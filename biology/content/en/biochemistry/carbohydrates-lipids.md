Proteins do most of the work of a cell, but they are built and powered by three other families of molecule. **Carbohydrates** are the fuel that most cells burn first, the store of energy in seeds and livers, and the bulk of the biosphere's structural material: there is more cellulose on Earth than any other organic compound. **Lipids** form the barriers of every membrane, store energy more densely than anything else an organism can make, and act as hormones and signals. **Nucleotides** are the monomers of DNA and RNA, the energy currency ATP, the core of the coenzymes that carry electrons and acyl groups, and the second messengers of signalling.

This chapter treats each family by asking what organisms do with it — store, build, transport, recognise, signal — and why particular molecules were chosen for particular jobs. The structural chemistry (D and L sugars, ring formation and anomers, glycosidic bonds, the packing of lipids into micelles and bilayers, the structure of the double helix) is in [Chemistry Atlas: carbohydrates, lipids and membranes](/learn/chemistry/lesson.php?c=biochemistry&l=carbohydrates-lipids) and [Chemistry Atlas: nucleic acids](/learn/chemistry/lesson.php?c=biochemistry&l=nucleic-acids); membranes are developed in [[cell-biology/membranes]].

## Sugars

::: definition Monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides {#def-sugars}
A **monosaccharide** is a polyhydroxy aldehyde (aldose) or ketone (ketose) with the formula $(\mathrm{CH_2O})_n$, usually $n = 3$–7: trioses, pentoses (ribose), hexoses (glucose, fructose, galactose). In water, pentoses and hexoses exist mostly as five- or six-membered rings, in two forms (α and β anomers) that differ in the orientation of the hydroxyl at the former carbonyl carbon. Two monosaccharides joined by a **glycosidic bond** — a link from the anomeric carbon of one to a hydroxyl of the other, with loss of water — form a **disaccharide**; long chains form **polysaccharides** (glycans), which can be linear or branched, and homo- or heteropolymers.
:::

### Why glucose?

D-Glucose is the most abundant monosaccharide in nature, the main circulating sugar of vertebrates, the building block of starch, glycogen and cellulose, and the starting point of glycolysis. Several features suit it to the role.

- **Stability.** In the β-pyranose ring of glucose every bulky substituent (each hydroxyl and the CH₂OH) can be equatorial, making it the most stable of the aldohexoses.
- **Low reactivity.** Open-chain aldehydes react non-enzymatically with amino groups of proteins (glycation, the first step of the Maillard reaction). Glucose spends only a tiny fraction of its time in the open-chain form, and H. F. Bunn and P. J. Higgins (*Science* 213, 222, 1981) showed that it reacts with haemoglobin more slowly than other common hexoses — a plausible reason, they suggested, that evolution settled on glucose as the blood sugar.
- **Availability.** Glucose is the immediate product of photosynthetic carbon fixation in most plants and the monomer of their storage and structural polymers.

### Transport and storage sugars

Organisms move sugars as disaccharides that are less reactive than their monomers. **Sucrose** (glucose α1↔β2 fructose), in which both anomeric carbons are locked in the bond, is a non-reducing sugar: chemically inert enough to be carried in the phloem of plants at concentrations of several hundred millimoles per litre ([[plant-biology/water-transport]]). **Trehalose** (glucose α1↔α1 glucose), also non-reducing, is the main sugar in the haemolymph of insects, a reserve in fungi and yeasts, and a protectant against desiccation ([[biochemistry/water-and-bonds]]). **Lactose** (galactose β1→4 glucose) is the sugar of mammalian milk.

::: example Sucrose and pressure in the phloem {#ex-phloem}
Phloem sap of many plants contains about 0.5 mol L⁻¹ sucrose (with smaller amounts of amino acids and K⁺). (a) Treating sucrose as an ideal solute, what osmotic pressure does it contribute at 20 °C? (b) If the same carbon were carried as free glucose, how much would the osmotic pressure be?
::: solution
(a) By [[biochemistry/water-and-bonds#eq-vanthoff]], $\Pi = 0.5\times0.08314\times293.15 = 12.2$ bar. (b) Each sucrose carries 12 carbons, two hexoses' worth, so the same carbon as glucose would be 1.0 mol L⁻¹ and give 24.4 bar. This osmotic pressure, generated by loading sugar into the phloem in the leaves and unloading it in roots and fruits, drives the bulk flow of sap (the pressure-flow hypothesis of Ernst Münch, 1930; [[plant-biology/water-transport]]). Carrying a disaccharide halves the osmotic load per carbon and, being non-reducing, sucrose does not react with the proteins along the way.
:::
:::

::: application Lactase persistence: a biochemical trait shaped by recent evolution
Infant mammals digest lactose with the intestinal enzyme lactase, whose production normally falls after weaning. In some human populations lactase production continues into adulthood. In Europeans this **lactase persistence** is associated mainly with a single nucleotide change about 14 kb upstream of the lactase gene *LCT*, in an enhancer within the neighbouring gene *MCM6* (N. S. Enattah and colleagues, 2002); in pastoralist populations of East Africa and the Middle East different variants in the same enhancer region have the same effect (S. A. Tishkoff and colleagues, 2007). The alleles spread over the last several thousand years, in step with dairying — one of the strongest signals of recent natural selection in the human genome and a clear case of convergent evolution at the molecular level ([[evolution/human-evolution]]). The clinical aspects of lactose intolerance belong to [Medicine Atlas: gastrointestinal](/learn/medicine/course.php?c=gastrointestinal).
:::

## Polysaccharides: stores and structures

The same monomer, glucose, gives both the most digestible and the least digestible polymers in nature. The difference lies entirely in the glycosidic bond.

| polymer | monomer and linkage | organisms | role |
|---|---|---|---|
| amylose | glucose, α1→4, unbranched helix | plants | storage (starch) |
| amylopectin | glucose, α1→4 with α1→6 branches about every 24–30 residues | plants | storage (starch) |
| glycogen | glucose, α1→4 with α1→6 branches about every 8–12 residues | animals, fungi, bacteria | storage |
| cellulose | glucose, β1→4, straight chains in hydrogen-bonded microfibrils | plants, some algae and bacteria, tunicates | structure (cell walls) |
| chitin | N-acetylglucosamine, β1→4 | arthropods, fungi | structure (exoskeleton, cell walls) |
| peptidoglycan | alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid, cross-linked by peptides | bacteria | structure (cell wall) |

The α1→4 bond puts a bend between successive residues, so amylose coils into a loose helix that enzymes can attack from its ends; the β1→4 bond makes each residue flip 180° relative to the last, giving straight chains that line up side by side, hydrogen bonded into crystalline microfibrils stronger, weight for weight, than steel wire. Most animals have no enzyme that cleaves β1→4 glucose links. Cattle, termites and many other herbivores digest cellulose through symbiotic bacteria, protists and fungi in their guts — so the biochemistry of a single bond shapes the ecology of grazing ([[microbiology/microbes-and-hosts]]).

Peptidoglycan is the target of the β-lactam antibiotics, which block the cross-linking enzymes, and of lysozyme, which cleaves its glycan chains (Alexander Fleming, 1922); see [[microbiology/antimicrobials]].

### Glycogen: an optimised fuel tank

Why store glucose as a polymer at all? Because a cell's osmotic pressure depends on the number of dissolved particles, not their size: the glucose in a liver cell's glycogen, if free, would be at about 0.4 mol L⁻¹ and draw in water until the cell burst (worked out in [Chemistry Atlas](/learn/chemistry/lesson.php?c=biochemistry&l=carbohydrates-lipids)). As a polymer it contributes almost nothing. And why branch it? Glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase act only at the non-reducing ends of chains, so the more ends, the faster glucose can be released or stored.

E. Meléndez-Hevia, T. G. Waddell and E. D. Shelton (*Biochemical Journal* 295, 477, 1993) treated glycogen as an engineering problem: choose the chain length and branching that maximise the amount of glucose that can be released quickly, per unit volume, while leaving room for the enzymes. Their model is a tiered tree.

::: proposition The tiered model of glycogen {#prop-glycogen}
Suppose every chain is $g$ glucose residues long, every inner chain carries $r$ branches, and the molecule has $t$ tiers, starting from a single chain attached to the protein glycogenin. Then the numbers of chains, of glucose residues and of outer (non-reducing-end) chains are

$$
C_t = \frac{r^t - 1}{r - 1},\qquad G_t = g\,C_t,\qquad C_\mathrm{outer} = r^{t-1}.
$$ {#eq-glycogen}

For $r = 2$ the outer tier contains just over half of all the glucose, and only that outer glucose can be released by phosphorylase before a debranching enzyme must act. Assumptions: a regular tree with identical chains; real glycogen is irregular and has a range of chain lengths.
:::

::: proof
Tier 1 has one chain, tier 2 has $r$ chains, tier $k$ has $r^{k-1}$; summing the geometric series over $k = 1,\dots,t$ gives $C_t = (r^t - 1)/(r - 1)$, and the outermost tier is $k = t$. For $r = 2$, the fraction of chains in the outer tier is $2^{t-1}/(2^t - 1)$, which is slightly more than $1/2$.
:::

::: example How big is a glycogen molecule? {#ex-glycogen}
Meléndez-Hevia and colleagues found that the optimum is close to $g = 13$, $r = 2$ — matching measured average values — and that a molecule can grow to about 12 tiers before the outer tier becomes too crowded for enzymes to reach. How many glucose residues and non-reducing ends does a 12-tier molecule have, and what fraction of its glucose is immediately available to phosphorylase?
::: solution
$C_{12} = 2^{12} - 1 = 4095$ chains, so $G_{12} = 13\times4095 = 53\,235$ glucose residues, with $2^{11} = 2048$ non-reducing ends. The outer tier holds $2048\times13 = 26\,624$ residues, or $26\,624/53\,235 = 50.0$ % of the total (phosphorylase stops a few residues short of each branch point, so in reality somewhat less is released before debranching). Measured "β-particles" of muscle and liver glycogen are about 20–30 nm across and contain of the order of 55,000 glucose residues, in agreement with the model — a nice case of a biological structure that can be predicted from an optimisation argument.
:::
:::

### Sugars as information: glycans on the cell surface

Most proteins on the outside of eukaryotic cells, and many lipids, carry branched oligosaccharides (glycans), added in the ER and Golgi ([[cell-biology/protein-sorting]]). Because sugars can be linked through several hydroxyls, in α or β, and branched, a few monosaccharides can make an enormous variety of structures. Glycans serve in folding quality control, as recognition signals between cells, as receptors for viruses and toxins, and as markers of self. The **ABO blood groups** are an example. The O antigen is a basic oligosaccharide; the A and B antigens add N-acetylgalactosamine or galactose to it, using two forms of a glycosyltransferase encoded by alleles of one gene that differ at four amino acids; the O allele is a frameshift mutation that encodes no active enzyme (F. Yamamoto and colleagues, 1990). The clinical significance belongs to [Medicine Atlas: blood](/learn/medicine/course.php?c=blood).

## Lipids

**Lipids** are defined by solubility rather than structure: they are the biological molecules that dissolve in non-polar solvents. They include fatty acids and their esters, phospholipids and sphingolipids, sterols, terpenoids and waxes.

::: definition Fatty acids and their notation {#def-fatty-acid}
A **fatty acid** is a carboxylic acid with a long unbranched hydrocarbon chain, usually with an even number of carbons (12–24) because it is built two carbons at a time ([[biochemistry/metabolic-integration]]). It is **saturated** if the chain has no carbon–carbon double bonds and **unsaturated** if it has one (monounsaturated) or more (polyunsaturated); natural double bonds are almost always *cis*. The notation $18{:}2\ \Delta^{9,12}$ means 18 carbons with two double bonds, starting at carbons 9 and 12 counted from the carboxyl carbon (C-1). Nutritionists also count from the methyl end: in an **ω-3** (n-3) fatty acid the last double bond starts three carbons from the end.
:::

Chain length and unsaturation set the physical state of a lipid. A *cis* double bond puts a kink of about 30° in the chain, preventing close packing, so unsaturated fats melt at much lower temperatures than saturated ones of the same length.

This explains why animals living at different temperatures, or a single fish moved from warm to cold water, change the fatty-acid composition of their membranes (homeoviscous adaptation; [[cell-biology/membranes]]), why the fat in the legs of reindeer and in the extremities of other cold-climate mammals is more unsaturated than fat in the body core, and why plant oils, rich in unsaturated chains, are liquid while beef fat is solid at room temperature.

### Essential fatty acids and the ω-3 food web

Animals can insert a double bond at Δ⁹ (making oleic acid from stearic acid) but vertebrates cannot insert double bonds beyond Δ⁹ toward the methyl end, because they lack the Δ¹² and Δ¹⁵ (ω-3) desaturases that plants have. They must therefore obtain linoleic acid (18:2, ω-6) and α-linolenic acid (18:3, ω-3) in their diet; George and Mildred Burr discovered in 1929 that rats fed fat-free diets develop a deficiency syndrome cured by linoleic acid. From these precursors animals make the longer polyunsaturated acids arachidonic acid (20:4, ω-6), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5, ω-3) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:6, ω-3), which are concentrated in neuronal and retinal membranes. The ultimate source of most ω-3 fatty acids in marine food webs is microalgae; fish accumulate them by eating algae and the animals that eat algae. Some invertebrates, such as the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, do have ω-3 desaturases, showing that the loss in vertebrates is a lineage-specific event.

### Fat as a store of energy

Triacylglycerols — three fatty acids esterified to glycerol — are the main energy store of animals and of many seeds. They are better than glycogen for two reasons. Their carbons are more reduced (fewer oxygens per carbon), so oxidising them releases more energy per gram; and, being hydrophobic, they are stored in nearly anhydrous droplets, while glycogen holds several times its own mass of water.

::: proposition Energy density of fuels (empirical) {#prop-energy-density}
The metabolisable energy obtained from complete oxidation is approximately 37 kJ g⁻¹ for fat, 17 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrate and 17 kJ g⁻¹ for protein (the Atwater factors of 9, 4 and 4 kcal g⁻¹, used in nutrition since W. O. Atwater's calorimetric studies of the 1890s). Glycogen in cells is hydrated with roughly 3 g of water per gram (estimates range from about 2 to 4 g), so a stored gram of hydrated glycogen yields only about $17/(1+w)$ kJ, where $w$ is the water-to-glycogen mass ratio. These are average values; individual fats and sugars differ by a few per cent.
:::

::: example Why we store fat {#ex-fat}
A lean adult human stores about 15 kg of triacylglycerol and about 0.5 kg of glycogen (liver and muscle together). (a) Compare their energy content. (b) If the energy in the fat were instead stored as hydrated glycogen ($w = 3$), how much would the store weigh?
::: solution
(a) Fat: $15\,000\ \mathrm{g}\times37\ \mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}} = 555$ MJ. Glycogen: $500\times17 = 8.5$ MJ — about 1.5 % of the fat's energy. At a resting energy expenditure of about 8 MJ per day, the glycogen would last about a day and the fat two months.
(b) Hydrated glycogen yields $17/4 = 4.25\ \mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$, so 555 MJ would need $555\,000/4.25 = 131\,000$ g, or about 131 kg — the storage form alone would weigh nearly twice as much as the whole person. Fat is about 8.7 times more energy-dense than hydrated glycogen as stored, which is decisive for any animal that must move or fly with its reserves.
:::
:::

The advantage is greatest for migrants. Before their non-stop flight of more than 11,000 km from Alaska to New Zealand, bar-tailed godwits (*Limosa lapponica*) lay down fat amounting to roughly half their body mass and shrink their digestive organs (R. E. Gill and colleagues, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 276, 447, 2009). Glycogen, by contrast, is the fuel of choice where speed of mobilisation matters more than weight: for bursts of muscle activity and for the minute-to-minute regulation of blood glucose by the liver.

### Lipids of membranes and surfaces

Membrane lipids are amphipathic: glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids and sterols in eukaryotes; mostly glycerophospholipids in bacteria, some of which also make **hopanoids** (sterol-like pentacyclic molecules whose synthesis needs no oxygen); and isoprenoid chains ether-linked to glycerol in archaea. The archaeal lipids are built on the opposite stereoisomer of glycerol phosphate (sn-glycerol-1-phosphate) from bacterial and eukaryotic ones (sn-glycerol-3-phosphate), by unrelated enzymes — the "lipid divide", which bears on how the first cells and the eukaryotic cell arose ([[diversity-of-life/origin-of-life]]). Membrane structure, fluidity and lipid rafts are covered in [[cell-biology/membranes]].

On the outside of organisms lipids form waterproofing layers. **Waxes** — esters of long-chain fatty acids with long-chain alcohols — coat the cuticles of leaves and insects, limiting water loss, and form the comb of honeybees. The plant cuticle, with its cutin polymer and waxes, was one of the innovations that let plants colonise dry land ([[plant-biology/plant-cells-tissues]]).

### Lipids as signals

Lipids also carry information. **Steroid hormones** (cortisol, oestradiol, testosterone, and the moulting hormone ecdysone of insects) are made from cholesterol and act on nuclear receptors that regulate transcription. **Eicosanoids** (prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes), made from arachidonic acid, are short-range signals in inflammation, blood clotting and reproduction. Membrane phosphatidylinositol lipids are cleaved to the second messengers diacylglycerol and inositol trisphosphate ([[cell-biology/cell-signalling]]). Plants use the lipid-derived hormone jasmonic acid in defence against herbivores, and brassinosteroids in growth ([[plant-biology/plant-hormones]]).

::: warning "Fat is just an inert energy store"
Adipose tissue is an active organ. Its fat is continuously broken down and re-formed (a substantial fraction of stored triacylglycerol in humans turns over within a year), its release of fatty acids is tightly controlled by hormones, and adipocytes secrete hormones of their own, such as leptin, that signal the size of the fat store to the brain. In hibernating mammals and in newborns, brown adipose tissue burns fat to make heat ([[biochemistry/oxidative-phosphorylation]]). Treating fat as a passive deposit misses most of its biology.
:::

## Nucleotides: monomers, currency and coenzymes

::: definition Nucleosides and nucleotides {#def-nucleotide}
A **nucleoside** is a nitrogenous base — a purine (adenine, guanine) or a pyrimidine (cytosine, thymine, uracil) — joined by an N-glycosidic bond to C-1′ of ribose or 2′-deoxyribose. A **nucleotide** is a nucleoside with one or more phosphate groups esterified to the sugar, usually at C-5′: for example adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP). In nucleic acids, nucleotides are linked by 3′,5′-phosphodiester bonds.
:::

Nucleotides play four roles.

1. **Monomers of nucleic acids.** DNA uses dA, dG, dC and dT; RNA uses A, G, C and U ([[molecular-biology/dna-structure]]).
2. **Energy currency.** ATP links energy-releasing to energy-requiring processes; GTP drives protein synthesis and signalling switches; UTP and CTP activate sugars and lipids for biosynthesis (UDP-glucose for glycogen, CDP-diacylglycerol for phospholipids). Their energetics are the subject of [[biochemistry/bioenergetics]].
3. **Coenzymes.** NAD⁺, NADP⁺, FAD and coenzyme A all contain an adenosine diphosphate unit that takes no part in their chemistry. H. B. White (1976) suggested these are molecular fossils of an RNA world, in which catalysts were RNA and coenzymes were nucleotides joined to it.
4. **Signals.** Cyclic AMP, discovered by Earl Sutherland in liver extracts in 1957–58 as the mediator of adrenaline's effect on glycogen breakdown (Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1971), is a second messenger in animals and a regulator of catabolite repression in bacteria. Bacteria signal starvation with guanosine tetraphosphate (ppGpp), the "magic spot" found by Michael Cashel and Jonathan Gallant in 1969, which shuts down ribosome synthesis; cyclic di-GMP controls the switch between swimming and biofilm life.

::: example How fast is ATP recycled? {#ex-atp-turnover}
A resting adult consumes about 0.25 L of O₂ per minute. Taking 22.4 L mol⁻¹ for a gas at standard conditions and about 5 ATP made per O₂ reduced (2.5 per pair of electrons, [[biochemistry/oxidative-phosphorylation]]), estimate the daily ATP turnover. The body contains about 25 L of intracellular water with roughly 4 mmol L⁻¹ ATP (molar mass 507 g mol⁻¹). How many times is each ATP molecule recycled per day, and how long would the pool last without resynthesis?
::: solution
O₂ per day: $0.25\times1440 = 360$ L, or $360/22.4 = 16.1$ mol. ATP made: $16.1\times5 = 80$ mol per day, or $80\times507 = 41\,000$ g — about 40 kg, more than half the body's own mass, every day at rest.
The pool is $25\times0.004 = 0.10$ mol (about 50 g), so each ATP is recycled about $80/0.10 = 800$ times a day. The pool would last $0.10/(80/86\,400) \approx 110$ s at the resting rate, and only seconds in working muscle. ATP is a currency in rapid circulation, not a store; stores are fat, glycogen and, for seconds, phosphocreatine ([[biochemistry/bioenergetics]]).
:::
:::

Nucleotides are made in two ways: **de novo**, from amino acids, CO₂, one-carbon units and ribose 5-phosphate (via phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate), and by **salvage** of bases and nucleosides released when nucleic acids are degraded. Their breakdown products are excreted: purines end as uric acid in humans, other apes, birds and reptiles (whose ancestors lost or inactivated urate oxidase, or who use uric acid as their main nitrogenous waste), and as allantoin or further products in most other mammals ([[biochemistry/metabolic-integration]]). Because rapidly dividing cells need large amounts of nucleotides, many anticancer, antiviral and antimicrobial drugs are nucleotide analogues or inhibitors of nucleotide synthesis — the logic, not the use, is biology's concern here.

::: quiz
Which statement about the storage forms of glucose is correct?
- [ ] Cellulose is a storage polysaccharide in plants that animals cannot digest because it is too highly branched.
- [x] Glycogen is more highly branched than amylopectin, which gives it more non-reducing ends for rapid mobilisation.
- [ ] Starch and glycogen are stored because polymers exert more osmotic pressure than free glucose.
- [ ] Fat stores more energy than glycogen per gram mainly because fat molecules are larger.
::: solution
Glycogen branches every 8–12 residues, amylopectin every 24–30, so glycogen has many more ends for phosphorylase and synthase. Cellulose is structural, unbranched, and indigestible because of its β1→4 links. Polymers exert far *less* osmotic pressure than the same mass of monomers. Fat's advantage comes from its more reduced carbons and its lack of water of hydration, not molecular size.
:::
:::

### Methods for sugars and lipids

Carbohydrates and lipids are harder to study than proteins and nucleic acids, because they are not made from a template and their structures cannot be read from a gene. Classical methods include colour reactions (iodine stains amylose blue-black, glycogen red-brown; the periodic acid–Schiff stain marks glycogen in tissue sections), thin-layer and gas chromatography (fatty acids are analysed as their volatile methyl esters), and enzymatic assays using specific enzymes coupled to NADH or NADPH, whose absorbance at 340 nm can be measured ([[biochemistry/enzymes]]). Today **lipidomics** and **glycomics** use mass spectrometry to profile hundreds of molecular species at once, and isotope tracers (¹³C-glucose, ²H₂O) followed by mass spectrometry or NMR measure how fast stores are made and used in living organisms.

::: history
Emil Fischer worked out the configurations of glucose and its isomers between 1884 and 1894 (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1902), and Norman Haworth established the ring structures of sugars in the 1920s (Nobel Prize, 1937). Claude Bernard discovered glycogen in liver in 1857, showing that animals make sugar as well as consume it. Friedrich Miescher isolated "nuclein" — DNA with protein — from pus cells in 1869. Wilbur Atwater's human calorimeter experiments of the 1890s gave the energy values of foods. George and Mildred Burr found essential fatty acids in 1929. Fritz Lipmann's concept of the "energy-rich phosphate bond" (1941) made ATP the cell's currency, and Sutherland's discovery of cyclic AMP (1957–58) began the study of second messengers.
:::

## Where this leads

The fuels of this chapter are burned in [[biochemistry/glycolysis-tca]] and [[biochemistry/oxidative-phosphorylation]] and made and stored in [[biochemistry/metabolic-integration]]; the energy carriers ATP, NADH and NADPH are the subject of [[biochemistry/bioenergetics]]. Enzymes that act on them follow in [[biochemistry/enzymes]].

::: summary
- Glucose is the universal fuel: stable, slow to glycate, and the product of photosynthesis. Sucrose and trehalose, non-reducing disaccharides, are transport and protective sugars; lactose is the milk sugar, and lactase persistence evolved convergently in dairying peoples.
- α1→4 glucose polymers (starch, glycogen) are digestible stores; β1→4 polymers (cellulose, chitin) are structural and need microbial symbionts to digest.
- Glycogen's branching ($g \approx 13$, $r = 2$, up to 12 tiers, about 53,000 glucose) is close to an optimum for rapid release; polymers avoid the osmotic load of free glucose.
- Fatty acids are named by length and double bonds ($18{:}2\ \Delta^{9,12}$, ω-6); *cis* double bonds lower melting points. Vertebrates must eat linoleic and α-linolenic acids.
- Fat yields 37 kJ g⁻¹ and is stored anhydrous; hydrated glycogen yields about 4 kJ g⁻¹ as stored. A lean human stores 555 MJ in fat and 8.5 MJ in glycogen.
- Nucleotides are nucleic-acid monomers, the energy currency (a resting human recycles about 40 kg of ATP a day), parts of coenzymes and second messengers (cAMP, ppGpp).
:::

::: exercise Branching and mobilisation {level=2}
Glycogen synthase adds glucose only to non-reducing ends, and glycogen phosphorylase removes it only from them. A mutant enzyme makes glycogen with branches every 30 residues instead of every 13 (as in amylopectin). Predict, with reasons, the effect on (a) the maximum rate of glucose release, (b) the solubility of the polymer, and (c) the amount of glucose stored per molecule of a given size.
::: solution
(a) Fewer branches mean fewer non-reducing ends per molecule, so fewer enzyme molecules can act at once and the maximal rate of release falls. (b) Long unbranched chains tend to form helices that pack and crystallise (like amylose and the outer chains of amylopectin, which form insoluble starch granules); the polymer becomes less soluble. Indeed, in human glycogen storage disease type IV, lack of branching enzyme produces poorly branched, insoluble glycogen. (c) Longer chains pack more glucose in a given volume, so storage density can rise — the trade-off between density and speed that Meléndez-Hevia analysed. Plants, which store for months and mobilise slowly, chose the dense form (starch); animals, which need fast release, the branched one.

::: exercise Essential fatty acids {level=2}
A vertebrate cell can make oleic acid (18:1 ω-9) from glucose but not linoleic acid (18:2 ω-6). Explain why, and why a diet of pure saturated fat plus sugar eventually produces a deficiency even though the body can desaturate fatty acids. Why can a cow, whose diet contains little fat, still obtain essential fatty acids?
::: solution
Vertebrates have Δ⁹ desaturase, which converts stearate to oleate, but no Δ¹² or Δ¹⁵ desaturase, so they cannot introduce double bonds in the methyl-end half of the chain; desaturases that act toward the carboxyl end (Δ⁵, Δ⁶) and elongases can extend existing ω-6 and ω-3 chains but cannot create the ω-6 or ω-3 bond. Saturated fat and sugar supply only chains that can become ω-9 or ω-7. A cow eats grass, whose chloroplast membranes are rich in α-linolenic acid (18:3 ω-3), so even a low-fat diet supplies it (although rumen microbes hydrogenate much of it, which is why ruminant fat is relatively saturated).

::: exercise Flying on fat {level=3 check="15.4"}
A migrating shorebird carries 200 g of fat and flies at 60 km h⁻¹ with a metabolic power of 8.0 W (illustrative values). (a) Using 37 kJ g⁻¹, how far could it fly, in thousands of km (three significant figures), if all the fat were used? (b) How much farther could it fly than a bird carrying the same mass of hydrated glycogen ($w = 3$)? (c) Give two reasons real birds fly less far than (a) predicts.
::: solution
(a) Energy $200\times37 = 7400$ kJ. Time $= 7.4\times10^6/8.0 = 9.25\times10^5$ s $= 257$ h. Distance $= 257\times60 = 15\,400$ km, i.e. 15.4 thousand km.
(b) Hydrated glycogen gives $4.25\ \mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$, so 200 g supports $257/8.71 = 29.5$ h, about 1,770 km — fat gives almost nine times the range.
(c) Not all fat can be used (some is structural and a reserve must remain on arrival); some protein is also burned; the power needed falls as the bird gets lighter but rises with headwinds and the costs of thermoregulation; and the flight speed relative to the ground depends on wind. Energy budgets of this kind, refined with measured flight power, do predict the observed non-stop flights of godwits within reasonable bounds.

::: exercise Interpreting a glycation experiment {level=3}
In an experiment of the kind reported by Bunn and Higgins (1981), haemoglobin is incubated with equal concentrations of different sugars, and the rate of formation of sugar–protein adducts is measured (relative to glucose = 1; illustrative values): glucose 1, mannose 5, galactose 5, fructose 7, ribose 30, glyceraldehyde 200. The percentage of each sugar present in the open-chain (carbonyl) form at equilibrium is in the same order. (a) What hypothesis connects the two sets of data? (b) Why would this property matter for a sugar that circulates in blood at about 5 mmol L⁻¹ for a lifetime? (c) Suggest one further experiment that could test whether open-chain content, rather than some other property, controls the rate.
::: solution
(a) Glycation begins with the reaction of the open-chain carbonyl group with a protein amino group to form a Schiff base. If only the open-chain form reacts, the rate is proportional to its concentration, so sugars with more open-chain form glycate faster. Glucose, whose stable β-pyranose ring makes it the least open-chain of these sugars, reacts slowest. (b) Glycation damages proteins slowly and irreversibly; a circulating fuel at millimolar concentrations for decades must be as unreactive as possible, which favours glucose over sugars that would otherwise serve equally well as fuels. (c) Measure the open-chain fraction of each sugar directly (for instance by NMR or circular dichroism) at the same temperature, and test whether the rate constant per open-chain molecule is the same for all sugars; or compare sugars of similar size but different ring stability, such as glucose and its 2-deoxy analogue, or use modified sugars locked in the ring form, which should not react.

::: exercise Interpreting a melting-point comparison {level=3}
Fats from the hooves and lower legs of reindeer are reported to have lower melting points than fat from near the body core, and contain more unsaturated fatty acids. Using the figure for melting points, (a) explain how the composition affects the melting point, (b) explain why the difference would be adaptive, and (c) propose how you would test whether it is a response to local temperature or a fixed developmental pattern.
::: solution
(a) *Cis* double bonds kink the chains and prevent them packing into a solid, lowering the melting point by tens of degrees for each double bond (stearic acid 70 °C, oleic 13 °C). Shorter chains also melt lower. (b) The lower legs of a reindeer standing in snow are kept far below core temperature (to save heat by countercurrent exchange). Saturated fat would solidify there, making tissues stiff; unsaturated fat stays fluid and keeps membranes and padding functional. (c) Compare tissues from animals raised in warm and cold conditions, or measure composition along the leg in relation to measured tissue temperature; or experimentally warm or cool a limb region (in a suitable animal model, under approved ethical protocols) and follow fatty-acid composition over time. A response to local temperature would point to regulated desaturase expression, as in homeoviscous adaptation of membranes.
