A herring gull chick, a few hours out of the egg, pecks at the tip of its parent's bill, and the parent regurgitates food for it. Why does it peck? The question has more than one answer, and the answers are not rivals. The chick pecks because a particular visual pattern — a red spot near the tip of a long, thin, downward-pointing yellow bill — triggers a pecking movement through circuits in its visual and motor systems. It pecks because it hatched with a crude version of the response that sharpens with practice over its first days. It pecks because chicks that pecked effectively were fed and survived, and chicks that did not, did not. And it pecks in a way it shares with related gulls, because the response was inherited from a common ancestor and modified along each lineage. Niko Tinbergen, who studied these chicks with Albert Perdeck in the late 1940s, argued that a complete understanding of any behaviour needs all four answers.

This chapter sets out that framework and the practical methods built on it. We begin with Tinbergen's four questions and the distinction between proximate and ultimate explanation. We then look at the classical ethology from which the framework grew — sign stimuli and fixed action patterns — and at how behaviour is described and measured: ethograms, sampling and recording rules, and the reliability of observers. The second half is about inference: experiments, controls, blind observation, pseudoreplication, and the comparative method for testing evolutionary hypotheses across species. We end with the logic of optimality models and adaptationist reasoning, which the rest of the course uses constantly, and with the ethics of studying animals. The evolutionary tools are those of [[evolution/natural-selection]].

## Tinbergen's four questions

In 1963 Tinbergen published "On aims and methods of ethology" in the *Zeitschrift für Tierpsychologie*. He proposed that biologists asking "why?" about a behaviour are really asking one of four different questions.

::: definition Tinbergen's four questions {#def-four-questions}
An explanation of a behaviour answers one of four questions:

1. **Mechanism (causation)**: what internal and external factors cause the behaviour to occur — stimuli, sense organs, neural circuits, hormones, genes acting in the individual?
2. **Development (ontogeny)**: how does the behaviour arise and change over the life of the individual, through the interaction of its genes, its environment and its experience, including learning?
3. **Function (survival value, adaptive significance)**: how does the behaviour contribute to the survival and reproduction of the individual and its relatives — what is it *for*, in the sense of why selection maintains it?
4. **Evolution (phylogeny)**: how did the behaviour arise in the history of the lineage, from what ancestral state, and how has it changed?

Questions 1 and 2 concern the individual and are called **proximate**; questions 3 and 4 concern populations over evolutionary time and are called **ultimate**. Questions 1 and 3 can be asked of a behaviour as it is now (static); questions 2 and 4 ask how it came to be (historical).
:::

The proximate–ultimate distinction was made sharply by Ernst Mayr in 1961, in an essay in *Science* called "Cause and effect in biology". Mayr's example was the autumn migration of a warbler: it leaves because shortening days trigger physiological changes (a proximate cause), and because ancestors that left before winter survived to breed, while those that stayed starved (an ultimate cause). Tinbergen's contribution was to split each of Mayr's categories into a static and a historical half, giving four boxes:

| | static (the behaviour now) | historical (how it came to be) |
|---|---|---|
| **proximate** | mechanism | development |
| **ultimate** | function | evolution |

::: example Four answers about birdsong {#ex-song}
A male great tit (*Parus major*) sings from a perch in his territory at dawn in spring. Give an answer to each of Tinbergen's questions, based on what is known about songbirds.
::: solution
**Mechanism.** Lengthening days stimulate the release of gonadotropins and a rise in testosterone; testosterone and its metabolite oestradiol act on a set of interconnected nuclei in the forebrain (the song system, including the nuclei HVC and RA), which drive the muscles of the syrinx and respiratory system. Hearing a rival's song increases singing.

**Development.** Oscine songbirds learn their songs. In species studied in detail, such as the white-crowned sparrow (*Zonotrichia leucophrys*), a young male memorises songs heard from adults during a sensitive period early in life, then practises, matching his output to the memorised template by auditory feedback until the song "crystallises". Great tits also learn their song types from neighbours.

**Function.** Song defends a territory and attracts mates. John Krebs (1977) removed territorial male great tits in Wytham Woods near Oxford and replaced them with loudspeakers: territories where the speakers played great tit song were reoccupied by new males more slowly than territories with a speaker playing a control sound or silence.

**Evolution.** Vocal learning of this kind is found in only three bird groups — oscine passerines, parrots and hummingbirds — and phylogenetic analysis indicates that it evolved more than once (whether two or three times is still debated, because parrots and passerines are sister groups). Within oscines, song structure and repertoire size have diverged among lineages, partly under sexual selection.

None of these four answers replaces another. A complete account of dawn song needs all of them.
:::
:::

::: warning "The real reason is…"
The commonest confusion in behavioural biology is to treat answers to different questions as competing. "Birds don't sing to attract mates; they sing because their testosterone is high" mixes a mechanism with a function and pretends that one excludes the other. Both are true. Two explanations compete only when they answer the *same* question: "song functions mainly in territory defence" and "song functions mainly in mate attraction" are genuine alternatives, and can be tested against each other. When you meet a debate about behaviour, first ask which of the four questions each side is answering.
:::

::: quiz
A study shows that mice lacking a functional copy of the gene *Fosb* fail to retrieve their pups to the nest. Which of Tinbergen's questions does this result mainly address?
- [x] Mechanism
- [ ] Development
- [ ] Function
- [ ] Evolution
::: solution
It identifies a gene whose product is needed, in the individual, for the behaviour to be performed — a proximate, causal explanation. (Jennifer Brown and colleagues reported this in 1996.) It says nothing about why retrieving pups is favoured by selection (function), how retrieval develops, or how it evolved; though if the knockout disrupted the behaviour only when the gene was removed early in life, it would bear on development too.
:::
:::

Note that "function" means the effect of the behaviour that explains why selection maintains it, not any effect it happens to have: song also makes a male conspicuous to sparrowhawks, but that is a cost, not its function. And the four questions, though logically distinct, inform one another — if song defends territories, expect its mechanism to respond to rivals; if song is learned, cultural transmission adds a second inheritance system on top of genes.

## Classical ethology: sign stimuli and action patterns

The four questions grew out of **ethology**, the biological study of behaviour developed in Europe in the 1930s to 1950s by Konrad Lorenz, Niko Tinbergen, Karl von Frisch and their students. They studied animals in natural or near-natural conditions, compared closely related species, and looked for the units of which behaviour is built.

::: definition Sign stimulus and fixed action pattern {#def-fap}
A **sign stimulus** (when given by another animal, a **releaser**) is a simple, specific feature of the environment that elicits a particular behaviour, out of all the features the animal could perceive. A **fixed action pattern** (now usually called a **modal action pattern**, to acknowledge its variability) is a stereotyped, species-typical sequence of movements which, once triggered, tends to run to completion even if the stimulus is removed. A stimulus that elicits a stronger response than the natural one is a **supernormal stimulus**.
:::

The classic examples are still instructive.

- **Pecking in gull chicks.** Tinbergen and Perdeck (1950) presented newly hatched herring gull chicks (*Larus argentatus*) with cardboard models of adult heads and counted pecks. The colour of the head hardly mattered; the presence and contrast of the red spot on the bill, the shape of the bill and its downward orientation mattered a great deal. A thin red rod with white bands near its tip elicited more pecks than a realistic model of the parent's head — a supernormal stimulus.
- **Egg-rolling in geese.** A greylag goose (*Anser anser*) rolls an egg that has left the nest back with her bill. Lorenz and Tinbergen (1938) found that if the egg was removed partway through, the goose often completed the retrieving movement with nothing under her bill, while the sideways corrections, which depend on feedback from the egg, stopped.
- **Aggression in sticklebacks.** Territorial male three-spined sticklebacks (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) in breeding condition, which have red throats and bellies, attack crude models with a red underside more readily than accurate models without red.

Ethologists interpreted such results with hydraulic models of "drive" released by sign stimuli. These are no longer accepted as neural mechanisms, and the strict separation of "innate" from "learned" behaviour proved untenable ([[behaviour/mechanisms-of-behaviour]]). But the discoveries stand: animals respond selectively to small subsets of the information available, and many behaviour patterns are as species-typical, and as useful for comparison, as anatomical structures.

The functional half of the four questions was also tested experimentally in this period. Black-headed gulls (*Chroicocephalus ridibundus*) pick up the empty eggshell after a chick hatches and carry it away from the nest. Tinbergen and colleagues (1962) laid out single eggs on the dunes at Ravenglass in Cumbria, some with an empty shell beside them and some without. Eggs with a shell nearby were found and taken by carrion crows and herring gulls more often, and the effect weakened as the shell was placed further away. The white inside of the broken shell evidently draws predators to the well-camouflaged eggs and chicks, which explains why selection favours its removal — and why, since a parent away from the nest leaves the chick exposed to its own cannibalistic neighbours, the gulls wait an hour or two after hatching before removing it.

::: history
Darwin wrote about behaviour throughout his career, and *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872) treated displays and expressions as evolved characters. The young science of ethology took shape in the 1930s: Lorenz described imprinting in 1935 and, with Tinbergen, analysed egg-rolling in 1938; Tinbergen's *The Study of Instinct* (1951) became its textbook. Meanwhile von Frisch was decoding the dance of honeybees. Lorenz, Tinbergen and von Frisch shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1973. In the same decade the emphasis shifted towards function, with the rise of **behavioural ecology** and **sociobiology** (E. O. Wilson's *Sociobiology*, 1975), built on Hamilton's kin selection (1964), Maynard Smith and Price's game theory (1973) and optimal foraging theory.
:::

## Describing behaviour

Before testing anything about a behaviour, you must be able to say reliably when it happens. This sounds trivial and is not: behaviour is a continuous stream of movement, and dividing it into categories requires decisions that affect every later result.

::: definition Ethogram, events and states {#def-ethogram}
An **ethogram** is a catalogue of the behaviour patterns of a species (or of those relevant to a study), each with a precise operational definition that any trained observer could apply. Categories are either **events** — behaviours of negligible duration, such as a peck or a call, measured by their **frequency** (number per unit time) — or **states** — behaviours of appreciable duration, such as feeding or resting, measured by their **duration**, the **proportion of time** spent in them, and the number and length of **bouts**. The **latency** of a behaviour is the time from a defined starting point (such as the start of a trial) to its first occurrence.
:::

Good ethogram categories are defined by *what the animal does* (structure: "head lowered below shoulder level, bill touching substrate") or by *what it achieves* (consequence: "moves an object at least one body length"), never by inferred motives ("hungry", "afraid"). The categories should be mutually exclusive if they will be analysed as proportions of time, and exhaustive if every moment must be classified (a category "other" or "out of sight" ensures this).

### Sampling and recording rules

You cannot watch everything. Jeanne Altmann's 1974 paper "Observational study of behavior: sampling methods" codified the choices that every observational study must make, and her terms are universal.

::: definition Sampling and recording rules {#def-sampling}
**Sampling rules** say whom to watch and when:
- **ad libitum** sampling — recording whatever is visible and seems relevant (useful for pilot work, biased for analysis);
- **focal** sampling — watching one individual for a set period and recording all of its behaviour;
- **scan** sampling — at set times, rapidly recording the behaviour of every individual in a group;
- **behaviour** sampling — watching the whole group and recording every occurrence of one kind of behaviour (for rare, conspicuous events such as fights or matings), and who was involved.

**Recording rules** say how the behaviour is recorded:
- **continuous** recording — every occurrence, with its start and end times, giving true frequencies, durations and sequences;
- **instantaneous** (point) recording — the observation period is divided into sample intervals, and at the instant each interval ends the observer records whether the behaviour is occurring;
- **one-zero** recording — the observer records whether the behaviour occurred at any time during each sample interval.
:::

Continuous recording is the gold standard but is feasible only for one individual and a few categories at a time, unless you film and score afterwards. Instantaneous recording gives an unbiased estimate of the proportion of time spent in a state, provided the interval is short compared with typical bout lengths. One-zero recording is quick but biased: it scores the *number of intervals in which the behaviour occurred*, which is neither a frequency nor a duration.

Scan samples give a time budget as a proportion: if a meerkat is foraging in 42 of 120 scans, $\hat p = 0.35$, with binomial standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n} = 0.044$ ([[biostatistics/estimation]]). That standard error assumes successive scans are independent. If foraging bouts last longer than the interval between scans, consecutive scans catch the same bout, the effective sample size is smaller than 120 and the true uncertainty larger; space samples more widely than a typical bout, or treat days or individuals as the units of replication.

::: example How one-zero recording misleads {#ex-onezero}
During a 10-minute focal watch divided into twenty 30-second intervals, a bird performs six preening bouts of 5 seconds each, each in a different interval. Compare the true proportion of time spent preening with the one-zero score and with the expected value of instantaneous recording.
::: solution
The true proportion is $6\times5/600 = 0.05$. The one-zero score is the fraction of intervals in which preening occurred, $6/20 = 0.30$ — six times too high. With instantaneous recording at the end of each interval, the chance that a sample point falls in a preening bout equals the proportion of time spent preening, so the expected score is $0.05$ (though with only twenty points it will often be 0 or 0.05 or 0.10). One-zero scores overestimate short, frequent behaviours and underestimate long ones (a single bout lasting the whole session would score 1.0, the same as a twenty-fold more frequent but brief behaviour). They are sometimes defended as an index of "how much" behaviour occurred, but they confound frequency with duration and depend on the interval length chosen.
:::
:::

Sequences matter as well as amounts. A record of continuous behaviour can be summarised by the probabilities of moving from each state to each other in a fixed time step, giving a **Markov chain** model. Its stationary distribution predicts the long-run time budget, and the probability $p_{ii}$ of remaining in state $i$ gives a mean bout length of $1/(1 - p_{ii})$ steps if bouts end at random — a prediction you can check against observed bout lengths. Real bouts often deviate from this geometric distribution (for example, animals that have just started feeding are less likely to stop than those that have been feeding a while), and that deviation is itself informative about mechanism.

### Observers and their reliability

Behavioural data are only as good as the observer's categorisation. Two observers scoring the same video should agree; one observer should agree with themselves on re-scoring weeks later. Raw percentage agreement overstates reliability, because two observers who classified at random would still agree sometimes. **Cohen's kappa** corrects for this.

::: proposition Cohen's kappa {#prop-kappa}
If two observers classify the same $n$ items into $k$ categories, let $p_o$ be the observed proportion of agreements, and let $p_e = \sum_i r_i c_i / n^2$ be the proportion expected by chance, where $r_i$ and $c_i$ are the numbers of items that the first and second observer put in category $i$. Then

$$
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}.
$$ {#eq-kappa}

$\kappa = 1$ for perfect agreement and $\kappa = 0$ for agreement no better than chance, given each observer's frequencies of use of each category. Conventional (and arbitrary) guidelines treat $\kappa$ above about 0.6 as substantial and above 0.8 as excellent.
:::

The expected agreement $p_e$ assumes the two observers' classifications are independent given their marginal frequencies: the chance that both choose category $i$ is $(r_i/n)(c_i/n)$.

::: example Agreement between two observers {#ex-kappa}
Two observers independently score the same 100 video frames of a deer as foraging (F), vigilant (V) or resting (R):

| A (rows), B (columns) | F | V | R | total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F | 40 | 5 | 1 | 46 |
| V | 4 | 25 | 3 | 32 |
| R | 1 | 2 | 19 | 22 |
| total | 45 | 32 | 23 | 100 |

Calculate the percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa.
::: solution
Agreements lie on the diagonal: $40 + 25 + 19 = 84$, so $p_o = 0.84$. Chance agreement is

$$
p_e = \frac{46\times45 + 32\times32 + 22\times23}{100^2} = \frac{2070 + 1024 + 506}{10\,000} = 0.36.
$$

Then $\kappa = (0.84 - 0.36)/(1 - 0.36) = 0.48/0.64 = 0.75$. Agreement is substantial but not excellent; most disagreements involve "vigilant", suggesting that its definition (for example, how long the head must be raised) needs tightening before the main study.
:::
:::

Reliability is not validity: observers can agree perfectly on "vigilance" scored as head-up posture even if it includes chewing with the head raised. Validity is shown when the measure behaves as it should — vigilant deer should detect a model predator sooner.

Observers who know the hypothesis or the treatment also tend to score in the direction they expect (**observer bias**), and animals can read observers. In 1904 Clever Hans, a horse in Berlin, appeared to answer arithmetic questions by tapping a hoof; Oskar Pfungst showed (1907) that Hans stopped tapping in response to tiny, unconscious movements of the questioner, and failed when the questioner did not know the answer. Score blind wherever possible, and make sure the animal cannot read the experimenter.

## Experiments and inference

Observation reveals patterns; experiments test explanations of them. Suppose great tits with larger song repertoires hold better territories. This correlation could arise because song repertoires deter rivals, because high-quality males have both large repertoires and good territories for some other reason, or because good territories (with more food) allow males to develop or perform larger repertoires. Only manipulation — giving some territories more song and others less, as Krebs did with loudspeakers — separates these.

::: warning Correlation is not causation, even in the field
An association between a behaviour and a benefit, observed in an undisturbed population, does not show that the behaviour causes the benefit. Animals are not randomly assigned to behaviour: individuals in better condition typically do more of everything. A classic case is parental care: parents that feed their young more often may raise more young because of the feeding, or because they are better parents in other ways or hold richer territories. Brood-size manipulations (moving chicks between nests so that parents raise more or fewer than they laid) break the link between parental quality and brood size and are the standard way to test such hypotheses.
:::

Besides the usual features of any experiment ([[biostatistics/experimental-design]]) — random allocation, controls, replication — behavioural experiments need controls matched in everything but the factor of interest (Krebs's controls were real speakers playing a sound), balanced treatment orders, because animals habituate and learn, and attention to the animal's own state (hunger, season, rank), which often matters more than the treatment.

### Choice tests

Many questions about behaviour are posed as choices: does a female approach the speaker playing conspecific song or the one playing heterospecific song? Does a bee land on the blue flower or the yellow one? With one choice per individual, and independent individuals, the number choosing one option follows a binomial distribution under the null hypothesis of no preference.

::: example A two-choice playback test {#ex-binomial}
Twenty female crickets are each released once, midway between two loudspeakers, one playing the song of their own species and one the song of a related species, with the sides randomised. Fifteen go to the conspecific song. Is this evidence of a preference?
::: solution
Under the null hypothesis that each female chooses either speaker with probability $\tfrac12$, independently, the number $X$ choosing the conspecific song is $\mathrm{Bin}(20, \tfrac12)$. The probability of a result at least as extreme as 15 in the direction of the conspecific song is

$$
P(X \ge 15) = \frac{1}{2^{20}}\sum_{k=15}^{20}\binom{20}{k} = \frac{15\,504 + 4845 + 1140 + 190 + 20 + 1}{1\,048\,576} = \frac{21\,700}{1\,048\,576} = 0.0207.
$$

A two-sided test, appropriate if a preference for either song would have been of interest, doubles this: $p = 0.041$. At the conventional 5 % level we would reject the null hypothesis and conclude that females prefer the conspecific song. The conclusion depends on the trials being independent — each female tested once, the speakers' positions randomised, and the two songs played at the same amplitude, so that the preference is for the song and not for loudness or side.
:::
:::

With 20 females and a true preference of 75 %, the experiment detects the preference only about 62 % of the time; a "non-significant" result from such a study says little. Calculating power before the study, from a plausible effect size, is the way to decide how many animals to test ([[biostatistics/hypothesis-testing]]) — and in animal research it is an ethical requirement as well as a statistical one, since an underpowered study uses animals without being able to answer its question.

### Pseudoreplication

::: definition Pseudoreplication {#def-pseudo}
**Pseudoreplication** (Stuart Hurlbert, 1984) is the use of non-independent measurements as if they were independent replicates in a statistical test. The unit of replication must be the unit to which the treatment was independently applied: if one recording of song is played to twenty birds, the experiment has one replicate of "song" (with twenty measurements of response to it), not twenty.
:::

Pseudoreplication is endemic in behavioural studies, because individuals are measured repeatedly, groups share an environment, and stimuli are expensive to make. Donald Kroodsma (1989) pointed out that many bird-song playback experiments had used a single recording of each song type, so that any difference in response could be due to an idiosyncrasy of that recording rather than to the song type. The solutions are to use many independent stimuli (each bird hears a different exemplar), to average repeated measures within each individual before analysis, or to use statistical models that represent the nested structure explicitly (mixed models with individual or stimulus as a random effect).

## The comparative method

Experiments test function within a species. Questions about evolution — and many about function — are tested by comparing species. If sperm competition favours larger testes, species in which females mate with many males should have larger testes for their body size than species in which they do not. This prediction has been confirmed in primates, birds, butterflies and fishes. But comparisons across species have a statistical trap.

::: definition Phylogenetic non-independence {#def-nonindep}
Species are not independent data points. Closely related species resemble one another because they inherited traits from a common ancestor, so a correlation between two traits across many species may reflect only a few evolutionary events, each inherited by a whole clade. A **phylogenetic comparative method** analyses the data with the phylogeny explicitly, so that each independent evolutionary change counts once.
:::

Joseph Felsenstein's 1985 paper "Phylogenies and the comparative method" showed how badly naive analyses can mislead and proposed **phylogenetically independent contrasts**: differences between sister taxa (and between reconstructed ancestral values deeper in the tree), standardised by branch length, which under a Brownian-motion model of evolution are independent. Modern methods (phylogenetic generalised least squares) extend the idea. The principle can be seen in its simplest form with sister pairs.

::: example Counting independent changes {#ex-contrasts}
Eight bird species form four pairs of sister species. Within each pair, one species nests in colonies and the other solitarily. Researchers measure the rate of extra-pair paternity (the percentage of chicks sired by a male other than the social father). The illustrative data are:

| pair | colonial | solitary |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 22 | 12 |
| 2 | 15 | 9 |
| 3 | 8 | 7 |
| 4 | 30 | 18 |

Test the hypothesis that colonial nesting increases extra-pair paternity, treating each pair as one independent comparison.
::: solution
Within each pair, the two species have shared their history up to the split, so the difference between them reflects evolution *since* the split, independent of the other pairs. The four differences (colonial minus solitary) are $+10, +6, +1, +12$: all positive. Under the null hypothesis that colonial nesting has no effect, each difference is equally likely to be positive or negative, so the probability that all four are positive is $(\tfrac12)^4 = 0.0625$ (one-sided sign test). The data are suggestive but not significant at 5 %, and a study needs more independent pairs.

Treating the eight species as independent points would overstate the evidence. And if all the colonial species had belonged to one clade and all the solitary to another, the comparison would rest on a single evolutionary change, however many species were sampled.
:::
:::

The comparative method assumes an accurate phylogeny and a model of trait evolution, and it shows association, not causation; it is most persuasive when it agrees with experiments within species.

## Function, adaptation and optimality

Most of this course asks the third of Tinbergen's questions. The logic is that of **optimality modelling**: if natural selection has shaped a behaviour, we can predict its form by working out what would maximise fitness, given the constraints the animal faces, and compare the prediction with what animals actually do.

::: definition Ingredients of an optimality model {#def-optimality}
An optimality model specifies:
1. the **decision** — the behavioural variable assumed to be under selection (which prey to take, how long to stay in a patch, whether to fight);
2. the **currency** — the quantity assumed to be maximised, as a proxy for fitness (rate of energy intake, probability of surviving the winter, number of offspring raised);
3. the **constraints** — limits on what the animal can do or know (handling time, travel time, memory, perceptual errors), and how the decision and the environment determine the currency.

When the payoff of a decision depends on what others do, the optimum is replaced by an **evolutionarily stable strategy** ([[behaviour/conflict-game-theory]]).
:::

Alan Grafen (1984) called the working assumption behind such models the **phenotypic gambit**: we model behaviour *as if* it were controlled by a simple genetic system that allows any strategy to evolve, ignoring the details of inheritance. The gambit is a bet that genetic details rarely prevent populations from reaching the predicted optimum. It often pays off, and when it fails, the failure is informative.

Optimality models test hypotheses about selection; they do not prove that animals are optimal. If a prediction fails, the currency, the constraints or the assumption of adaptation may be wrong, and finding which is the scientific work. Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin (1979) criticised the "adaptationist programme" for replacing one failed adaptive story with another instead of considering by-products, history and constraint. The answer is to make adaptive hypotheses quantitative and to say in advance what result would refute them.

::: warning "For the good of the species"
Before the 1960s many biologists explained behaviour by its benefits to the group or species: animals restrain their aggression to avoid harming the species, or limit their breeding to avoid overexploiting food (V. C. Wynne-Edwards made this case in 1962). George Williams (*Adaptation and Natural Selection*, 1966) showed why such explanations usually fail: a gene for restraint among unrestrained individuals is out-reproduced and lost, whatever the effect on the species. Behaviour evolves through its effects on the reproductive success of individuals and their relatives ([[behaviour/cooperation]]).
:::

::: intuition The animal as a decision-maker
When a starling "decides" to leave a patch, the shorthand means that selection has favoured mechanisms — perhaps rules of thumb such as "leave after two minutes without a capture" — whose outputs approximate those of a rational calculator in the environments where they evolved. It lets us predict behaviour without knowing the mechanism, and misleads only if we forget that the mechanism may fail in novel environments.
:::

## The ethics of studying animals

Research on animal behaviour is regulated in most countries. William Russell and Rex Burch's *The Principles of Humane Experimental Technique* (1959) proposed the **3Rs**: **replacement** (non-animal methods where possible), **reduction** (the minimum number of animals that can answer the question, which requires power analysis) and **refinement** (minimising distress, for example by using playbacks and models rather than live opponents). The Association for the Study of Animal Behaviour and the Animal Behavior Society publish joint guidelines, revised periodically in *Animal Behaviour*. In the field, rings, tags and collars can change behaviour or survival, and observers can attract predators to nests; good practice is to measure such effects rather than assume they are absent.

::: application Behaviour in conservation and welfare
Ethograms and time budgets are used to monitor the welfare of farm, laboratory and zoo animals, where abnormal repetitive behaviours (stereotypies) indicate poor conditions. In conservation, captive-bred animals often lack the behaviour needed in the wild, and pre-release training to recognise predators has been tried in several species. Many conservation problems are mismatches between a behavioural mechanism (attraction to artificial light, for example) and a function it can no longer serve in a changed environment.
:::

::: summary
- Tinbergen's four questions — mechanism, development, function and evolution — are complementary; explanations compete only when they answer the same question. Mechanism and development are proximate; function and evolution are ultimate (Mayr, 1961).
- Classical ethology discovered sign stimuli, releasers, modal action patterns and supernormal stimuli, and pioneered field experiments on function, such as eggshell removal in gulls.
- An ethogram defines behaviour categories operationally; events are measured by frequency, states by duration and proportion of time.
- Altmann's sampling rules (ad libitum, focal, scan, behaviour) and recording rules (continuous, instantaneous, one-zero) determine what can be estimated; one-zero scores confound frequency with duration.
- Cohen's $\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)$ measures observer reliability beyond chance; blind scoring guards against observer bias (Clever Hans).
- Experiments with matched controls separate cause from correlation; choice tests are analysed with the binomial distribution, and power should be calculated beforehand.
- Pseudoreplication — treating non-independent measurements as replicates — must be avoided; the unit of replication is the unit independently treated.
- Species are not independent; comparative tests count independent evolutionary changes (independent contrasts, sister-pair comparisons).
- Optimality models specify a decision, a currency and constraints; they test hypotheses about selection and rest on the phenotypic gambit. Explanations "for the good of the species" usually fail.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Which question? {level=1}
Classify each statement by the Tinbergen question it answers. (a) Honeybee workers that perform waggle dances have elevated expression of certain genes in the brain. (b) Waggle dances occur in all species of *Apis*, but dances of the open-nesting dwarf honeybees are performed on a horizontal surface. (c) Colonies whose dances were experimentally disoriented collected less food in some habitats. (d) Young workers start to dance only after a period working inside the hive and following other dancers.
::: solution
(a) Mechanism (a proximate cause in the individual). (b) Evolution: the comparative distribution of the trait across the genus bears on its origin; horizontal dancing in the dwarf bees is often interpreted as close to the ancestral state, with dancing on vertical comb in the dark, using gravity to represent the sun's direction, derived. (c) Function: the effect on food collection is the survival value. (d) Development: how the behaviour arises over the life of the individual.
:::

::: exercise Kappa {level=1 check="0.8"}
Two observers agree on 90 % of 200 classifications, and the agreement expected by chance from their category frequencies is 50 %. What is Cohen's kappa?
::: solution
$\kappa = (0.9 - 0.5)/(1 - 0.5) = 0.4/0.5 = 0.8$.
:::

::: exercise Bout length {level=1 check="100"}
An animal is observed every 10 seconds. If it is resting, the probability that it is still resting 10 seconds later is 0.9, independently of how long it has rested. What is the mean length of a resting bout, in seconds?
::: solution
With a constant probability 0.9 of continuing each step, bout length in steps is geometric with mean $1/(1 - 0.9) = 10$ steps, that is $10\times10 = 100$ s.
:::

::: exercise A small choice test {level=2 check="0.0107"}
Nine of ten female fish, each tested once, spend more time near a male with a red throat than near a male without. Calculate the one-sided binomial $p$-value (three significant figures) for the null hypothesis of no preference.
::: solution
$P(X \ge 9) = \left[\binom{10}{9} + \binom{10}{10}\right]/2^{10} = 11/1024 = 0.0107$. Two-sided, $p = 0.0215$.
:::

::: exercise A standard error from scans {level=2 check="0.0324"}
In 200 independent scan samples a bird is vigilant in 60. Estimate the proportion of time spent vigilant and its standard error (three significant figures).
::: solution
$\hat p = 0.3$ and $\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{0.3\times0.7/200} = \sqrt{0.00105} = 0.0324$, giving an approximate 95 % interval $0.30 \pm 0.064$.
:::

::: exercise Choosing a design {level=2}
You want to know (a) what proportion of the day a group of 12 baboons spends feeding, (b) how often adult males fight, and (c) the exact sequence of behaviour that precedes a mother leaving her infant. For each, choose sampling and recording rules and justify the choice.
::: solution
(a) Scan sampling with instantaneous recording at regular intervals across the day: an unbiased time budget for all twelve animals at little cost. (b) Fights are rare and conspicuous: behaviour sampling of the whole group with continuous recording of each fight and its participants, noting observation hours so that rates can be calculated. (c) Focal sampling of mothers with continuous recording (ideally video), so that the order and timing of every act is captured.
:::

::: exercise Spot the pseudoreplication {level=2}
To test whether robins respond more strongly to songs of strangers than to songs of neighbours, a researcher records one neighbour's song and one stranger's song, and plays both to each of 30 territorial males in random order. Responses to the stranger song are significantly stronger ($p < 0.01$, paired test on 30 males). Explain why this does not establish the conclusion, and redesign the experiment.
::: solution
There is only one exemplar of each category, and the "neighbour" recording is in fact the neighbour of at most one or two of the 30 males; for the rest it is another stranger. Even ignoring that, any difference could be due to some feature of the two particular recordings (length, amplitude, recording quality, song type) rather than familiarity. The design must give each male his own neighbour's song and a stranger's song, recorded and edited in the same way, with different stranger and neighbour recordings for different males, matched in amplitude and played from the same position in random order. Then the 30 males are 30 independent replicates of the comparison between familiar and unfamiliar songs.
:::
:::

::: exercise Sister-pair comparisons {level=3 check="1/32"}
Five independent pairs of sister species of primates differ in mating system: in each pair, one species has females that mate with several males during an oestrus, and the other does not. In all five pairs the multi-male species has larger testes relative to body mass. What is the one-sided sign-test probability of this result if mating system had no effect? Why is this a better test than regressing relative testis mass on mating system across all ten species with ordinary least squares?
::: solution
Each pair contributes one independent comparison, positive with probability $\tfrac12$ under the null hypothesis, so $P = (\tfrac12)^5 = 1/32 = 0.031$. Ordinary least squares on ten species would treat them as independent, but sister species share most of their history; if larger testes and multi-male mating had evolved together once, early in a large clade, every species in it would carry both traits, and the regression would count one evolutionary event many times. The sister-pair test counts only changes that occurred independently, after each split. (A full analysis would use independent contrasts or phylogenetic least squares on the whole tree, with relative testis size measured as the residual from a log–log regression on body mass.)
:::

::: exercise One-zero recording {level=3 check="6"}
In [[#ex-onezero]], by what factor does the one-zero score overestimate the proportion of time spent preening? If the sample interval were halved to 15 seconds (forty intervals), and each bout still fell in a separate interval, what would the one-zero score be, and what general lesson follows?
::: solution
The one-zero score is 0.30 and the true proportion 0.05, a factor of 6. With forty intervals the score would be $6/40 = 0.15$, a factor of 3. The score depends on the interval length chosen, and approaches the true proportion only as the interval becomes short compared with a bout — at which point one-zero recording has become continuous recording. One-zero scores should not be interpreted as durations, and scores obtained with different intervals cannot be compared.
