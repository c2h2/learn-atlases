At the end of August 2007 a female bar-tailed godwit (*Limosa lapponica baueri*), carrying a satellite transmitter and known to researchers as E7, left the coast of western Alaska and flew south over the Pacific. About eight days later she landed in New Zealand, roughly 11,700 kilometres away, without having stopped to feed, drink or rest. Robert Gill and colleagues, who tracked her and other godwits, reported in 2009 that such nonstop flights were the norm for this population, and later tracking has recorded godwits flying more than 13,000 km nonstop. Before leaving Alaska, the birds had fattened until fat made up more than half their body mass, while their digestive organs shrank; they flew across an ocean with no landmarks, and arrived where they intended to.

Migration poses questions at every one of Tinbergen's levels ([[behaviour/studying-behaviour#def-four-questions]]). How do animals know which way to go, and how do they know where they are? How do young animals on their first journey find a destination they have never seen? Why migrate at all, and how much fuel should a migrant carry? How quickly can migratory routes evolve? This chapter starts with the classification of orientation and the map-and-compass model, then treats the compasses animals use — the sun, the stars, the Earth's magnetic field and polarised light — and path integration in insects. It then turns to the harder problem of the map: displacement experiments, olfactory and magnetic maps, and what is still unknown. The second half concerns the ecology and evolution of migration: the costs and benefits, optimal fuelling, the genetics of migratory direction in blackcaps, and the effects of climate change. The foraging theory of [[behaviour/foraging]] reappears in the decisions of migrants at stopover sites.

## Kinds of orientation

::: definition Migration {#def-migration}
**Migration** is persistent, directed movement that takes an animal away from its current home range, during which it is not distracted by resources that would halt other kinds of movement (such as food it would normally stop to eat), and which is typically followed by a return or by settlement in a new range. It is usually seasonal, and is accompanied by physiological preparation (fattening, changes in hormones and organs) and by migratory restlessness. Migration is distinguished from **dispersal** (a one-way move from birthplace to breeding site) and from **foraging** movements within a home range.
:::

Donald Griffin (1955) distinguished three levels of orientation ability, which remain useful:

::: definition Piloting, compass orientation and true navigation {#def-navigation}
**Piloting** is finding the way by familiar landmarks. **Compass orientation** is maintaining a fixed direction using a compass, without reference to the goal's position. **True navigation** is the ability to head towards a goal from an unfamiliar place, which requires a **map** (to determine position relative to the goal) as well as a **compass** (to choose and hold the direction). Gustav Kramer's **map-and-compass** model (1953) proposed that homing animals first determine the direction of home with the map, then fly in that direction with the compass.
:::

A further strategy, important for young migrants, is **vector navigation** (also "clock-and-compass" orientation): following an inherited programme of one or more compass directions for an inherited period, which brings the animal approximately to its population's wintering area without any knowledge of where it is.

## Compasses

### The sun compass

In the late 1940s and early 1950s, Kramer kept European starlings (*Sturnus vulgaris*) in circular cages during the migration season, when caged migrants become restless at the time they would be migrating (**migratory restlessness**, or *Zugunruhe*) and orient their hopping in the migratory direction. With the sky visible, starlings oriented correctly; under overcast skies their orientation deteriorated. When Kramer used mirrors to shift the apparent position of the sun by 90°, the birds' orientation shifted by about 90°. They were using the sun as a compass.

But the sun moves. Its azimuth (compass bearing) changes through the day, from roughly east in the morning through south at noon (in the northern hemisphere) to roughly west in the evening. To use it as a compass, an animal must know the time of day and compensate for the sun's movement, using its circadian clock ([[behaviour/mechanisms-of-behaviour]]). This was demonstrated by **clock-shift** experiments, first done with homing pigeons by Klaus Schmidt-Koenig in 1958. Pigeons kept for several days on a light–dark cycle shifted 6 hours out of phase with the natural day reset their circadian clocks; when released, they flew in a direction deviating from that of controls by roughly the angle the sun moves in 6 hours.

::: example Predicting a clock-shift deviation {#ex-clockshift}
Pigeons in the northern hemisphere are clock-shifted 6 hours slow (their subjective dawn is 6 hours after the real dawn) and released at real noon at a site south of their loft. Assume the sun's azimuth moves on average about 15° per hour, from 90° at 6:00 to 180° at noon. (a) In which direction will the pigeons fly? (b) What if they were shifted 6 hours fast?
::: solution
The birds want to fly north (0°, towards home). (a) At real noon the sun is at azimuth 180°. The birds' internal clock reads 6:00, when they expect the sun at about 90°. To fly north at 6:00 a bird would fly 90° anticlockwise from the sun — at 0° when the sun is at 90°. Applying the same angle to the real sun at 180° sends the bird to $180° - 90° = 90°$, east. The deviation is 90° clockwise of the correct direction. (b) Shifted fast, the internal clock reads 18:00, when the sun is expected at about 270°; north is then 90° clockwise of the sun, so the bird flies at $180° + 90° = 270°$, west — 90° anticlockwise of the correct direction.

In practice, the deviations are often smaller than predicted, because the sun's azimuth does not move uniformly (it moves faster near noon in summer and at low latitudes) and because pigeons also use other compass cues, such as the magnetic field. The direction of the deviation, however, is consistently as predicted, which shows that the sun compass is time-compensated.
:::
:::

Time-compensated sun compasses are widespread: in birds, fishes, turtles, and many insects. In the eastern North American population of monarch butterflies, which migrate thousands of kilometres to overwintering sites in the mountains of central Mexico, the clock used for sun compensation is located partly in the antennae: Christine Merlin, Robert Gegear and Steven Reppert (2009) showed that monarchs without antennae, or with antennae painted to block light, failed to orient correctly, while those with antennae painted with clear paint oriented normally.

### The star compass

Many songbirds migrate at night. Stephen Emlen (1967) tested indigo buntings (*Passerina cyanea*) in a planetarium, in funnel-shaped cages lined with paper that recorded their footprints. Under the planetarium sky, birds oriented in the appropriate migratory direction; when the stars were turned off, they did not. Covering particular constellations showed that the birds used the pattern of stars within about 35° of the celestial pole — the region around Polaris — rather than any single star. Most strikingly, Emlen (1970) raised young buntings under a planetarium sky that rotated around Betelgeuse, a star in Orion, instead of Polaris. In autumn these birds oriented as if Betelgeuse marked north. Buntings are not born knowing the constellations; they learn, as nestlings, which part of the sky is the centre of rotation, and that defines north.

### The magnetic compass

Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko showed in 1972 that caged European robins (*Erithacus rubecula*) oriented in the migratory direction in the absence of visual cues, and that their orientation changed when the magnetic field around the cage was altered with Helmholtz coils. Their key finding was that the robin's compass is an **inclination compass**. Reversing the horizontal component of the field (so that magnetic north pointed south) reversed the birds' orientation, as expected for any compass; but reversing only the vertical component also reversed it, which a polarity compass (like a human compass needle) would not. The birds distinguish "poleward" (where the field lines dip steeply into the ground) from "equatorward", not north from south.

The mechanism is not settled. The leading hypothesis for the bird compass is a light-dependent **radical-pair** mechanism in the retina, in which a magnetically sensitive chemical reaction in the protein cryptochrome (probably Cry4) is influenced by the direction of the field relative to the eye. Experiments showing that weak radio-frequency fields disrupt the compass support a radical-pair mechanism; Jingjing Xu, Henrik Mouritsen, Peter Hore and colleagues (2021) showed that purified robin Cry4 forms magnetically sensitive radical pairs. A separate system based on iron-containing magnetite particles has been proposed for detecting field intensity, though candidate magnetite receptors in the pigeon beak proved to be macrophages. Magnetic compasses are known in many animals besides birds, including sea turtles, salmon, newts, mole-rats and insects.

::: intuition Why carry several compasses?
Each compass has weaknesses: the sun is hidden by cloud and its use requires an accurate clock; stars are visible only at night and in clear weather; the magnetic compass gives only the axis of inclination and is unreliable near the magnetic poles and the magnetic equator, and the difference between magnetic and geographic north (declination) varies from place to place. Using several compasses, and calibrating them against one another — for example, by recalibrating the magnetic compass at sunset using polarised-light patterns near the horizon — gives a robust system. How birds rank and calibrate their compasses is still debated, because different experiments have given conflicting results.
:::

### Path integration

Desert ants (*Cataglyphis*) forage individually on hot, featureless salt pans in North Africa, wandering on tortuous paths for tens or hundreds of metres from the nest, then, after finding food, run straight home. They do this by **path integration** (dead reckoning): continuously summing the distance and direction of every segment of their outward path, so that they always "know" the vector pointing home. Direction comes from a celestial compass based on the polarisation pattern of skylight, which ants and bees detect with specialised photoreceptors in the dorsal rim of the eye (Rüdiger Wehner's work); distance comes from a step counter. Matthias Wittlinger, Rüdiger Wehner and Harald Wolf (2006) glued stilts to the legs of ants that had reached a feeder, or shortened their legs; ants on stilts overshot the nest on the way home, and those with shortened legs undershot it, as predicted if they count steps.

::: proposition The home vector {#prop-homevector}
If an animal's outward journey consists of segments with displacements $\mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2, \dots, \mathbf{d}_n$ (vectors in the plane), its position relative to home is $\mathbf{D} = \sum_i \mathbf{d}_i$, and the **home vector** is $-\mathbf{D}$. In components (east, north), with segment $i$ of length $\ell_i$ at compass bearing $\theta_i$ (measured clockwise from north),

$$
D_E = \sum_i \ell_i\sin\theta_i, \qquad D_N = \sum_i \ell_i\cos\theta_i,
$$

the distance home is $\sqrt{D_E^2 + D_N^2}$, and the bearing of home is the direction of $(-D_E, -D_N)$. Errors in each segment's estimated direction or length accumulate, so path integration becomes less accurate over long, winding paths, and animals rely on landmarks near the goal.
:::

::: example Running home {#ex-pathintegration}
A desert ant walks 10 m north, then 20 m east, then 5 m south, and finds food. What are the distance and compass bearing of the straight path home?
::: solution
Summing displacements: east $0 + 20 + 0 = 20$ m, north $10 + 0 - 5 = 5$ m. The ant is at $(20, 5)$ relative to the nest. The home vector is $(-20, -5)$: distance $\sqrt{400 + 25} = \sqrt{425} = 20.6$ m, and bearing $\operatorname{atan2}(-20, -5) = 256°$ (measured clockwise from north), a little south of west. An ant displaced by the experimenter just after picking up the food runs 20.6 m on bearing 256° from wherever it is released, and then searches in widening loops — showing that it follows the vector rather than any landmark.
:::
:::

## Maps

A compass is useless for homing unless the animal knows where it is relative to home. The classic experiment revealing the difference between compass orientation and true navigation was done on wild migrating birds.

Each autumn, starlings breeding around the Baltic migrate west-south-west through the Netherlands to winter in southern England, northern France and Belgium. In the 1950s, A. C. Perdeck caught some 11,000 starlings on migration in the Netherlands, ringed them and transported them by air to Switzerland, where they were released. Recoveries of the ringed birds showed a sharp difference between age classes. Juveniles on their first migration continued to fly west-south-west, as if they were still in the Netherlands, and ended up in France and Spain, outside their population's normal wintering area. Adults corrected for the displacement and flew north-west, towards the normal wintering area. Perdeck's 1958 paper showed that juveniles used vector navigation (a compass direction), while adults, with experience, had a map. Later displacement experiments with tracked birds, such as white-crowned sparrows displaced across North America (Kasper Thorup, Martin Wikelski and colleagues, 2007), gave the same result.

How does the map work? Two hypotheses have the most support.

**Olfactory map.** Floriano Papi and colleagues (from 1972) found that homing pigeons deprived of smell, by cutting the olfactory nerve or anaesthetising the olfactory mucosa, homed poorly from unfamiliar sites, though their compass orientation was intact. Pigeons are thought to learn, at the loft, the association between odours and the wind directions that bring them, building a map of gradients in atmospheric chemicals. The idea was long controversial, partly because results differed between pigeon populations, but experiments with tracked seabirds (shearwaters made anosmic failed to home over the sea, while controls did) have extended it.

**Magnetic map.** The Earth's magnetic field varies systematically over the surface: its intensity increases and its inclination steepens from the magnetic equator towards the poles.

::: proposition Inclination and intensity of a dipole field {#prop-dipole}
Approximating the Earth's field as that of a dipole aligned with the rotation axis, at magnetic latitude $\lambda$ the **inclination** $I$ (the angle of the field below the horizontal) and the total **intensity** $F$ satisfy

$$
\tan I = 2\tan\lambda, \qquad F = F_0\sqrt{1 + 3\sin^2\lambda},
$$ {#eq-dipole}

where $F_0$ is the intensity at the magnetic equator (about 30 μT). The intensity at the poles is twice that at the equator. The real field departs from the dipole substantially and changes slowly over years (secular variation), so lines of equal inclination and equal intensity are not parallel to lines of latitude, and in many regions they cross at an angle, which in principle allows a bicoordinate map.
:::

::: example Inclination as a latitude cue {#ex-inclination}
Using [[#eq-dipole]], find the inclination at magnetic latitudes 30° and 50°, and the change in inclination per degree of latitude near 50°. A sea turtle can detect differences in inclination of a fraction of a degree; how precisely could inclination alone locate it in latitude?
::: solution
At 30°: $\tan I = 2\tan30° = 1.155$, $I = 49.1°$. At 50°: $\tan I = 2\tan50° = 2.384$, $I = 67.2°$. Between 49.5° and 50.5°, $I$ changes by about 0.72°, so one degree of latitude (about 111 km) corresponds to about 0.7° of inclination. If an animal could detect differences of, say, 0.2° (an illustrative value), inclination alone would place it within about 30 km in latitude — but only along the north–south axis. Longitude needs a second coordinate, such as intensity, whose isolines run at an angle to those of inclination.
:::
:::

Experiments with animals in artificial fields support magnetic maps in several species. Kenneth Lohmann and colleagues tested young loggerhead turtles (*Caretta caretta*) from Florida, which as hatchlings ride the North Atlantic gyre around the Sargasso Sea. Hatchlings exposed to the magnetic field of a location on the edge of the gyre (for example, off northern Portugal) swam in the direction that would keep them within the gyre. Juvenile green turtles (*Chelonia mydas*) exposed to the field of a site 337 km north of their capture site swam south, and those exposed to the field of a site the same distance south swam north (Lohmann and colleagues, 2004). Adult Eurasian reed warblers (*Acrocephalus scirpaceus*) physically displaced about 1000 km east changed their orientation to compensate (Nikita Chernetsov, Dmitry Kishkinev and Henrik Mouritsen, 2008), and in a later "virtual displacement" experiment in which only the magnetic declination around the cage was altered, they reoriented as if they had been displaced (Chernetsov and colleagues, 2017), suggesting that declination serves as an east–west cue. How general magnetic maps are, and what senses underlie them, are active questions.

::: warning "Animals know where they are going" and "migration is innate"
Young migrants on their first journey usually do not know their destination: they follow an inherited vector, which works only if they start from the usual place, as Perdeck's juvenile starlings showed. Adults have learned maps from experience. Conversely, saying that migration is "innate" overlooks that young birds learn which part of the night sky is north (Emlen's buntings), calibrate their compasses against one another, and, in some species such as cranes and geese, learn routes from their parents. As with all behaviour, migratory programmes develop through the interaction of inherited predispositions and experience ([[behaviour/mechanisms-of-behaviour]]).
:::

::: quiz
Young birds of a migratory species are caught in their breeding area before their first migration and transported 1000 km east, then released in autumn. If they navigate by an inherited vector, where should they end up?
- [ ] At their population's usual wintering area
- [x] About 1000 km east of their population's usual wintering area
- [ ] At the release site, because they cannot orient there
- [ ] Back in their breeding area
::: solution
With vector navigation, the bird flies the inherited direction for the inherited distance from wherever it starts. The whole route is shifted by the displacement, so the birds end up about 1000 km east of the normal wintering area, as Perdeck's juvenile starlings ended up south-west of theirs. Adults with a map would correct.
:::
:::

## Why and how far to migrate

Migration is costly: the energy for the journey, the risk of storms, predators and starvation on the way, and the time spent travelling instead of breeding. It is favoured when the benefits of exploiting seasonally abundant resources in one place, and escaping seasonal scarcity in another, outweigh these costs. Many populations are **partially migratory**: some individuals migrate and others stay. Whether an individual migrates often depends on its condition, age, sex or dominance, so that the decision is a conditional strategy; in European blackbirds and robins, for instance, females and young birds are more likely to migrate than adult males, which may benefit from holding breeding territories early.

### Fuelling and the speed of migration

Long-distance migrants alternate flights with **stopovers**, during which they feed and store fat. How much fuel should a migrant load before leaving a stopover site? Thomas Alerstam and Åke Lindström (1990) treated this as an optimisation problem.

::: proposition The range of a migrant {#prop-range}
From aerodynamic theory, the flight range of a bird carrying a fuel load $f$ (fuel mass as a fraction of lean body mass) is approximately

$$
Y(f) = c\Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + f}}\Bigr),
$$ {#eq-range}

where $c$ is a constant depending on the bird's size, shape and flight efficiency. Range increases with fuel load but with diminishing returns, because fuel adds weight that must itself be carried. This is an approximation derived under simplifying assumptions (constant flight speed relative to the optimum, fat as the only fuel).
:::

A migrant that minimises the **total time** of migration — plausible for birds racing to secure the best breeding territories — should choose its departure fuel load to maximise the overall **speed of migration**: distance covered per unit of total time, including the time spent fuelling. If fuel is deposited at a constant rate $k$ (fraction of lean mass per day), after an initial settling period $t_0$, the problem has exactly the form of the marginal value theorem ([[behaviour/foraging#thm-mvt]]): the migrant should leave when the marginal gain in range per unit of time spent fuelling falls to the overall speed of migration.

::: example How much fuel to carry? {#ex-fuel}
(a) Using [[#eq-range]], by what factor does the range increase if a bird doubles its fuel load from $f = 0.5$ to $f = 1.0$? (b) A time-minimising migrant fuels at rate $k = 0.04$ of lean mass per day after a settling period of $t_0 = 3$ days. Measuring time in units of fuel ($kt_0 = 0.12$), the speed of migration is proportional to $Y(f)/(f + 0.12)$. Find the optimal departure fuel load. (c) How should the optimum change if the settling period were longer?
::: solution
(a) $Y(0.5)/c = 1 - 1/\sqrt{1.5} = 0.184$ and $Y(1.0)/c = 1 - 1/\sqrt2 = 0.293$; the ratio is 1.60. Doubling the fuel increases range by only 60 %.

(b) Maximising $(1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + f})/(f + 0.12)$ numerically gives $f^* \approx 0.41$: the bird should leave when its fuel reaches about 41 % of lean mass. (This is the point where the line from $(-0.12, 0)$ is tangent to the range curve, exactly as in the patch model.)

(c) A longer settling period is like a longer travel time between patches; the optimal stay, and so the departure fuel load, increases. With $kt_0 = 0.3$ the optimum is $f^* \approx 0.66$. Equally, the theory predicts that birds at sites with higher fuel deposition rates should leave with larger fuel loads, a prediction confirmed in several species by experimentally providing food at stopover sites.
:::
:::

### The evolution of migratory behaviour

Migratory behaviour can evolve fast. Peter Berthold and colleagues at the Max Planck research station in Radolfzell, southern Germany, showed in a long programme of breeding experiments with blackcaps (*Sylvia atricapilla*) that the amount of migratory restlessness, its timing and the migratory direction are all heritable; a few generations of artificial selection produced migratory and non-migratory lines from a partially migratory population. Cross-breeding blackcaps from populations that migrate south-west (through Spain) and south-east (through the Balkans) produced offspring with intermediate, southward orientation (Andreas Helbig, 1991).

The most dramatic case happened in nature. Since the 1960s, increasing numbers of blackcaps have wintered in Britain and Ireland rather than around the Mediterranean. Berthold, Helbig and colleagues (1992) caught blackcaps wintering in Britain, bred them in aviaries in Germany, and tested the offspring in orientation cages: the young birds, which had never migrated, oriented west-north-west — the direction from central Europe to Britain — rather than south-west like the offspring of German birds wintering in Iberia. A new migratory route with a genetic basis had evolved within about 30 years. Milder winters, garden bird-feeding in Britain, and a shorter journey that allows earlier return to the breeding grounds probably favoured it, and later work found that birds wintering in Britain and Iberia breeding in the same area tended to mate with partners of their own migratory type, possibly the start of divergence.

::: application Migration, climate change and conservation
Migrants depend on several places, and on timing. Many long-distance migrants time their departure from tropical wintering grounds by internal annual clocks and day length, which do not track warming in the breeding area. In the Netherlands, Christiaan Both and Marcel Visser (2001) found that pied flycatchers (*Ficedula hypoleuca*) had not advanced their arrival from Africa as much as the spring peak of caterpillars on which their nestlings depend had advanced; in areas where the caterpillar peak had advanced most, flycatcher populations declined by about 90 % over two decades (Both and colleagues, 2006). Migrants are also exposed to habitat loss at stopover sites — the mudflats of the Yellow Sea, a critical refuelling area for godwits and other shorebirds of the East Asian–Australasian Flyway, have lost a large fraction of their area since the mid-twentieth century — and to collisions with lit buildings at night, which are being reduced in some cities by turning off lights during migration peaks. Tracking technology, from geolocators to satellite and radio tags (including the Motus network of automated receivers), now allows the whole annual cycle of individual animals to be studied, which is essential for conserving them.
:::

::: history
Aristotle believed that some birds hibernated or transformed rather than migrated, an idea that persisted into the eighteenth century. Ringing, introduced by Hans Christian Mortensen in Denmark in 1899, revealed migratory routes. Kramer demonstrated the sun compass in starlings around 1950 and formulated the map-and-compass model in 1953; Schmidt-Koenig's clock-shift experiments with pigeons followed in 1958, as did Perdeck's displacement of starlings. Franz and Eleonore Sauer studied star orientation in warblers in the 1950s, and Emlen's planetarium experiments with buntings appeared in 1967 and 1970. The Wiltschkos demonstrated the inclination compass in robins in 1972, and Papi proposed the olfactory map the same year. Berthold's blackcap studies, from the 1970s onwards, showed the genetic basis of migratory behaviour. Since 2000, miniaturised tracking has revealed journeys — the godwits' nonstop Pacific crossings, the Arctic terns' pole-to-pole migrations — that earlier researchers could only infer.
:::

::: summary
- Migration is persistent, directed, seasonal movement with physiological preparation. Orientation ranges from piloting by landmarks through compass orientation to true navigation, which needs a map and a compass.
- The sun compass is time-compensated: clock-shifted pigeons deviate by roughly 15° per hour of shift (clockwise for slow shifts in the northern hemisphere). Monarchs use an antennal clock.
- Night migrants learn the centre of celestial rotation as nestlings and use it as a star compass (Emlen's buntings).
- The avian magnetic compass is an inclination compass, probably based on light-dependent radical pairs in cryptochrome.
- Desert ants integrate their path, using a polarised-light compass and a step counter, to compute the home vector $-\sum\mathbf{d}_i$.
- Displacement experiments separate vector navigation (juveniles) from true navigation with a map (adults). Candidate maps use odours and the magnetic field ($\tan I = 2\tan\lambda$, $F = F_0\sqrt{1 + 3\sin^2\lambda}$), with experimental support in pigeons, seabirds, turtles and warblers.
- Optimal fuelling theory treats departure fuel loads like patch residence times; range rises with diminishing returns as $1 - (1 + f)^{-1/2}$.
- Migratory direction is heritable and can evolve within decades (blackcaps wintering in Britain); climate change creates timing mismatches for long-distance migrants.
:::

An ant walks 4 m east, then 3 m north, then 8 m west, then 2 m north. How far is it from the nest, in metres?
::: solution
East: $4 - 8 = -4$; north: $3 + 2 = 5$. Distance $\sqrt{16 + 25} = \sqrt{41} \approx 6.40$ m. The home vector points to $(4, -5)$, bearing $\operatorname{atan2}(4, -5) \approx 141°$, south-east.

::: exercise Inclination at the equator and pole {level=2}
Using [[#eq-dipole]], what is the inclination at the magnetic equator and at the magnetic pole? Explain why an inclination compass fails at the magnetic equator, and what a migrant crossing it must do.
::: solution
At $\lambda = 0$, $\tan I = 0$, so $I = 0$: field lines are horizontal. At $\lambda = 90°$, $\tan I \to \infty$, so $I = 90°$: field lines are vertical. An inclination compass distinguishes "poleward" from "equatorward" by the direction in which field lines dip; at the magnetic equator they do not dip, so the compass gives only an axis without polarity. A trans-equatorial migrant must switch its magnetic preference when it crosses the equator (flying "equatorward" until the equator and then "poleward"), or rely on other compasses there. Experiments with garden warblers suggested that experiencing a horizontal field is what triggers this reversal.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a displacement experiment {level=2}
Adult and juvenile birds of a migratory population are captured in autumn on migration and displaced 800 km north-east before release. Recoveries show that juveniles continue in the population's normal migratory direction, while adults head towards the normal wintering area. (a) What does this show? (b) Why might the adults' corrected direction be imperfect? (c) What would you conclude if adults also continued in the normal direction?
::: solution
(a) Juveniles use vector navigation (an inherited compass direction); adults use true navigation, which requires a map, learned through experience of at least one migration. (b) Their map may be inaccurate outside the region they have experienced, the cues (odours, magnetic gradients) may be weaker or ambiguous at the release site, and wind drift after release may affect the tracks. (c) That adults, like juveniles, rely on a compass direction in this situation — either they have no map that extends to the release site, or the displacement was beyond the range over which their map works. This would not show that adults lack a map altogether.

::: exercise Optimal departure fuel load {level=2}
Explain, with reference to the tangent construction, why a time-minimising migrant at a stopover site with a high fuel deposition rate should leave with a larger fuel load than one at a poor site, even though at the poor site it takes longer to reach any given load.
::: solution
Measuring time in units of fuel deposited, the settling time $t_0$ corresponds to a distance $kt_0$ to the left of the origin in the tangent construction. A higher deposition rate $k$ moves this point further left (the settling period, in fuel units, is "longer"), so the tangent line from it is shallower and touches the range curve at a higher fuel load. Intuitively, at a rich site each extra day adds a lot of fuel, so it pays to stay longer and load up; at a poor site, extra days add little, and it pays to leave sooner with less fuel and hope for a better site further on. In addition, the overall speed of migration is higher for a migrant using rich sites.
:::

::: exercise The blackcap experiment {level=3}
Berthold and colleagues bred blackcaps that had wintered in Britain and tested their aviary-raised offspring in orientation cages, finding west-north-west orientation. (a) Why was it important that the offspring were raised in aviaries in Germany without contact with adults? (b) What additional evidence would show that the change is genetic rather than a maternal or early-environment effect? (c) Why might selection have favoured wintering in Britain, given that it is colder than Iberia?
::: solution
(a) To exclude learning from parents or experience of the route: the offspring had never migrated and had not been with adults of either type, so their orientation reflected their inherited programme (plus any common rearing effects, which were the same for all groups). (b) Cross-breeding and common-garden experiments: offspring of crosses between British-wintering and Iberian-wintering birds should show intermediate orientation; offspring of British-wintering birds raised by foster parents of the other type should retain their orientation; and the difference should persist over several generations in the aviary. Helbig's crosses between south-west and south-east migrants showed exactly such intermediate inheritance. (c) Possible advantages: a shorter journey with lower mortality; abundant food at garden bird feeders; and earlier return to the breeding grounds in spring, because British-wintering birds respond to the earlier lengthening of days at higher latitudes and arrive first, obtaining the best territories and mates — a benefit reinforced by assortative mating among birds of the same wintering type.
:::

::: exercise Is it navigation? {level=3}
Green turtles nest on Ascension Island, a speck of land about 2000 km from their feeding grounds off Brazil. Propose two hypotheses for how they find it, and design one experiment or observation that could distinguish them.
::: solution
Hypothesis 1, a magnetic map: turtles imprint on the magnetic signature (inclination and intensity) of their natal beach as hatchlings and later navigate along magnetic gradients towards it. Hypothesis 2, an olfactory or chemical cue: turtles home using chemical cues carried downstream from the island by the prevailing current, perhaps after first navigating to the right general region by a magnetic map. To distinguish them: attach small magnets to some turtles (with controls carrying non-magnetic weights) and track them with satellite tags as they migrate — if magnets impair the final approach, magnetic cues are important; or track turtles displaced upstream versus downstream of the island, since an odour-based hypothesis predicts poorer homing from upstream, where no plume reaches. (Paolo Luschi, Susanne Åkesson and colleagues did magnet and tracking experiments of this kind; the results suggested a role for wind- or water-borne cues in the final approach, though the question remains open.)
