A single cell can live on its own; a tissue is a society. In the lining of your gut, cells are bound to their neighbours by junctions that seal the gaps between them, so that the contents of the intestine stay out of the body; they rest on a sheet of extracellular matrix that they themselves secrete; they are born from stem cells at the bottom of small pits, migrate upwards over a few days, and then die and are shed at the tips of the villi — tens of billions of them every day. Multicellular life depends on cells that stick together in the right way, build and read the material around them, divide when replacements are needed, and die when they are told to.

This chapter, the last of the course, steps out from the single cell to the tissue. We look at the junctions that join cells, the extracellular matrix that surrounds them, the stem cells that renew tissues and the arithmetic of balance between birth and death, and finally the programmes of **cell death** — apoptosis above all — that remove cells cleanly. The signalling of [[cell-biology/cell-signalling]], the cytoskeleton of [[cell-biology/cytoskeleton]] and the cell cycle of [[cell-biology/cell-cycle]] all come together here.

## Cell junctions

Animal tissues are of two broad kinds. In **epithelia**, cells are packed tightly into sheets, joined to each other by junctions and to an underlying **basal lamina**; they line every surface and cavity of the body. In **connective tissues** — bone, cartilage, tendon, the dermis — cells are scattered within an abundant extracellular matrix, which bears most of the mechanical load. Junctions are best seen in epithelia.

::: definition Classes of cell junction {#def-junctions}
- **Occluding junctions** — in vertebrates, **tight junctions** — seal neighbouring cells together near their apical surface, so that molecules cannot leak between them, and separate the apical and basolateral domains of the plasma membrane. They are made of strands of the transmembrane proteins **claudins** and occludin.
- **Anchoring junctions** link the cytoskeleton of one cell to that of its neighbour or to the matrix. **Adherens junctions** join actin filaments of adjacent cells through **cadherins**; **desmosomes** join intermediate (keratin) filaments through desmosomal cadherins; **focal adhesions** and **hemidesmosomes** join actin and intermediate filaments, respectively, to the extracellular matrix through **integrins**.
- **Channel-forming junctions** — **gap junctions** in animals, **plasmodesmata** in plants — connect the cytoplasms of adjacent cells, letting ions and small molecules pass directly.
- **Signal-relaying junctions** include chemical synapses and the contacts through which Notch and Delta signal ([[cell-biology/cell-signalling]]).
:::

### Cadherins and the sorting of cells

Cadherins are single-pass transmembrane proteins whose extracellular parts consist of repeated domains that become rigid only when Ca²⁺ ions bind between them. Masatoshi Takeichi found in 1977 that cultured cells adhered to one another by a calcium-dependent mechanism that was protected from protease digestion by Ca²⁺, and went on to identify E-cadherin (epithelial) and N-cadherin (neural). Most cadherins bind **homophilically**: a cadherin on one cell binds the same kind of cadherin on another. Inside the cell, cadherins connect through β-catenin and α-catenin to actin filaments, so that a sheet of cells joined by adherens junctions forms a mechanically continuous network — contraction of the circumferential actin belt can fold a whole epithelial sheet into a tube, as in the formation of the vertebrate neural tube.

Homophilic adhesion explains a classic observation. In 1907 Henry Van Peters Wilson showed that sponges forced through a fine cloth into a suspension of single cells reaggregate into sponges; when cells of two sponge species are mixed, they sort out into separate aggregates. In 1955 Philip Townes and Johannes Holtfreter dissociated tissues from amphibian embryos and mixed them: the cells reaggregated randomly at first and then sorted out, epidermis to the outside and mesoderm and neural tissue inside, recreating something like their normal arrangement. Malcolm Steinberg's **differential adhesion hypothesis** (1963) proposed that cell populations behave like immiscible liquids, sorting so as to maximise the strength of adhesive bonds, with the most cohesive tissue in the middle. Cells expressing different amounts or types of cadherin sort out in just this way. (Later work by Carl-Philipp Heisenberg and others has shown that cortical tension generated by actomyosin contributes as much as adhesion itself.)

### Gap junctions

A gap junction is a cluster of channels, each formed by two **connexons** — hexamers of the protein connexin, one contributed by each cell — that meet across a narrow gap of about 3 nm. The aqueous pore, about 1.2–1.5 nm wide, passes inorganic ions and molecules up to about 1 kDa — sugars, amino acids, nucleotides, cAMP, IP₃ — but not proteins or nucleic acids. Gap junctions couple cells electrically (heart muscle cells, so that a wave of contraction spreads; some neurons, at "electrical synapses") and metabolically. They close when cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises or the pH falls, isolating a damaged cell from its neighbours. Invertebrates use an unrelated protein family, the innexins, for the same purpose.

::: warning "Cells in a tissue are simply glued together"
Adhesion is not passive glue. Junctions are dynamic structures, assembled and remodelled constantly, linked to the cytoskeleton and to signalling pathways. β-catenin, for example, is both a structural component of adherens junctions and the key transcriptional effector of the Wnt pathway; integrins and cadherins transmit force and signal to the cell how stiff its surroundings are. When epithelial cells lose E-cadherin and become migratory — the **epithelial–mesenchymal transition** — they change their gene expression, shape and behaviour, in normal development (gastrulation, the neural crest) and in the spread of carcinomas.
:::

## The extracellular matrix

The **extracellular matrix** (ECM) is the network of proteins and polysaccharides that cells secrete into the space around them. In bone it is mineralised and rigid; in tendon it is ropelike; in cartilage it is a resilient gel; in the cornea it is transparent; in the basal lamina it is a thin, tough sheet. Four groups of molecules make it.

- **Collagens**, the most abundant proteins in mammals (about a quarter of total protein). Humans have 28 types. The fibrillar collagens (types I, II, III) form fibrils with high tensile strength; type IV forms the sheet-like network of basal laminae.
- **Glycosaminoglycans** (GAGs) and **proteoglycans**: long, unbranched, negatively charged polysaccharides — hyaluronan, chondroitin sulfate, heparan sulfate — often attached to core proteins. They attract cations and water, forming hydrated gels that resist compression.
- **Elastin**, which forms rubber-like fibres that let skin, lungs and arteries recoil.
- **Adhesive glycoproteins** such as **fibronectin** and **laminin**, which link cells to the matrix and the matrix components to each other. Fibronectin binds integrins through the tripeptide Arg-Gly-Asp (**RGD**), identified by Michael Pierschbacher and Erkki Ruoslahti in 1984.

::: example How long is a collagen molecule? {#ex-collagen}
The triple-helical region of each α1 chain of type I collagen contains 1014 amino acids, with glycine at every third position (the repeat Gly-X-Y, where X and Y are often proline and hydroxyproline). In the triple helix each residue advances the molecule by 0.286 nm. (a) How many glycines are in the helical region of one chain? (b) How long is the triple helix? (c) Why must every third residue be glycine?
::: solution
(a) $1014/3 = 338$ Gly-X-Y triplets, so 338 glycines.

(b) $1014\times0.286\ \mathrm{nm} = 290\ \mathrm{nm}$, consistent with the measured length of the collagen molecule, about 300 nm, and its diameter of about 1.5 nm — an exceptionally long, thin rod.

(c) In the triple helix the three chains wind round a common axis, and every third residue of each chain lies on the inside, packed against the other two chains. Only glycine, with a hydrogen atom as its side chain, fits there. A substitution of a single glycine by a bulkier residue disrupts the helix locally; in type I collagen such substitutions cause the brittle-bone disease osteogenesis imperfecta. The hydroxylation of proline, which stabilises the helix, requires vitamin C, which is why its deficiency (scurvy) weakens connective tissues; clinical aspects are in [Medicine Atlas](/learn/medicine/course.php?c=musculoskeletal).
:::
:::

Collagen is secreted as a soluble precursor, procollagen, with propeptides at both ends that prevent it assembling inside the cell; they are cut off outside, and the molecules then self-assemble into staggered fibrils, cross-linked covalently by lysyl oxidase. The fibrils are oriented by the cells that make them — in tendon in parallel bundles along the line of tension, in skin in a wickerwork — and are remodelled by matrix metalloproteinases.

### Integrins: reading the matrix

Cells attach to the matrix through **integrins**, heterodimers of an α and a β subunit (humans have 18 α and 8 β subunits, forming 24 distinct integrins). An integrin is a two-way switch. Its extracellular head can be folded and low-affinity, or extended and high-affinity; binding of the cytoskeletal adaptor talin to the β-subunit tail inside the cell extends it (**inside-out activation**), and binding of matrix outside promotes the assembly of actin and signalling proteins inside (**outside-in signalling**). Integrin clusters connected to actin through talin and vinculin form **focal adhesions**, which both transmit the traction forces of crawling cells ([[cell-biology/cytoskeleton]]) and signal through kinases such as focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and Src. Most normal cells need integrin signalling to survive and divide: deprived of matrix, epithelial cells die by a form of apoptosis called **anoikis** — a control that cancer cells must evade to spread.

Plants solve the same problems with a different solution: their cells are cemented to one another by the pectin-rich middle lamella between their **cell walls**, built of cellulose microfibrils in a matrix of hemicelluloses and pectins, and they communicate through **plasmodesmata**, membrane-lined channels through the walls that join the cytoplasms of neighbouring cells. Because plant cells cannot move, plant development relies on oriented division and expansion rather than migration ([[plant-biology]]).

## Stem cells and tissue renewal

Many adult tissues are continually renewed: the epithelium of the small intestine every 3–5 days, the epidermis of human skin every few weeks, red blood cells every 120 days. Others, such as most of the brain and heart muscle, renew very little.

::: definition Stem cell and potency {#def-stem-cell}
A **stem cell** is a cell that can both **self-renew** — divide to produce more stem cells — and give rise to differentiated cell types. Stem cells are classified by their **potency**, the range of cell types they can produce: a **totipotent** cell (the zygote and, in mammals, early blastomeres) can form the entire organism including extra-embryonic tissues; a **pluripotent** cell (cells of the inner cell mass of the blastocyst, embryonic stem cells) can form every cell type of the body; a **multipotent** cell (a haematopoietic stem cell) can form several cell types of one tissue; a **unipotent** cell, one. Stem cells often divide rarely and produce **transit-amplifying cells**, which divide a limited number of times before differentiating.
:::

The existence of adult stem cells was demonstrated in 1961 by James Till and Ernest McCulloch in Toronto. They injected bone-marrow cells into mice whose own blood-forming system had been destroyed by irradiation, and found nodules on the spleen, each in proportion to the number of cells injected; in 1963, with Andrew Becker, they used chromosomal markers to show that each nodule was a clone from one cell, containing several blood-cell lineages. Embryonic stem cells were derived from mouse blastocysts by Martin Evans and Matthew Kaufman, and independently by Gail Martin, in 1981, and from human embryos by James Thomson and colleagues in 1998 — work that is regulated in most countries, because it uses human embryos.

Two discoveries showed that differentiation is reversible. In 1962 John Gurdon transplanted nuclei from intestinal cells of tadpoles of the frog *Xenopus laevis* into enucleated eggs and obtained swimming tadpoles, some of which grew into adults: a differentiated nucleus still contains all the genetic information needed to make a whole animal. In 2006 Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka reprogrammed mouse fibroblasts into **induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells** by introducing just four transcription factors (Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc). Gurdon and Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

### The arithmetic of homeostasis

A tissue at steady state must make cells exactly as fast as it loses them. For a stem-cell population, this imposes a precise constraint on how stem cells divide.

::: proposition Balance of stem-cell fates {#prop-stem-balance}
Suppose each division of a stem cell produces two stem cells with probability $p$, two differentiating cells with probability $q$, and one of each (asymmetric division) with probability $1 - p - q$, independently of other divisions. Then the expected number of stem cells produced per division is

$$
2p + 1\cdot(1 - p - q) + 0\cdot q = 1 + p - q,
$$ {#eq-stem}

so after $n$ rounds of division the expected stem-cell number is multiplied by $(1 + p - q)^n$. The population is maintained on average only if $p = q$; if $p > q$ it grows exponentially, and if $p < q$ it is exhausted. Assumptions: fates are independent and identically distributed; all stem cells divide at the same rate; no stem cells die or are lost by other means.
:::

Two strategies satisfy $p = q$. Every division could be asymmetric ($p = q = 0$), as in many invertebrate stem cells. Or divisions could be symmetric but balanced at the level of the population ($p = q > 0$): individual stem cells then drift at random, some lineages expanding and others being lost. Lineage tracing in the mouse intestine — marking single stem cells with heritable fluorescent labels — showed in 2010 (Hugo Snippert, Hans Clevers and colleagues; Carlos Lopez-Garcia, Allon Klein, Benjamin Simons and Douglas Winton) that crypt stem cells follow the second pattern: each crypt has about 14–16 equipotent stem cells (marked by the gene *Lgr5*), and over weeks to months one clone takes over the crypt by **neutral drift**. The figure shows how small an imbalance is needed to tip a stem-cell population into growth or decline.

::: example Replacing red blood cells {#ex-rbc}
An adult human has about $2.5\times10^{13}$ red blood cells, each living about 120 days. At what rate must new red cells be made? If each committed progenitor yields about $2^{12}$ red cells after a series of transit-amplifying divisions (an illustrative figure), how many progenitors must enter the pathway per second?
::: solution
At steady state, production equals removal: $2.5\times10^{13}/(120\times86\,400\ \mathrm{s}) = 2.4\times10^6$ cells per second. With $2^{12} = 4096$ cells per progenitor, about $2.4\times10^6/4096 \approx 600$ progenitors per second must start differentiating. Transit amplification means that a relatively small number of stem and early progenitor cells, dividing rarely, can sustain an enormous output — and that a mutation in one of them is propagated to many descendants.
:::
:::

Tissue size is regulated by feedback. The total number of cells $N$ of a tissue that are produced at rate $s$ and die with a per-cell rate $d$ obeys $dN/dt = s - dN$, approaching a steady state $N^* = s/d$ with time constant $1/d$ — formally the same as a signalling molecule in [[cell-biology/cell-signalling#prop-response-time]]. Real tissues sense their own size and adjust $s$ and $d$: after two-thirds of a rat's liver is removed, the remaining hepatocytes re-enter the cell cycle and restore the original mass within about a week, and then stop. Cell death is as much part of the control as division.

::: example A renewing epithelium at steady state {#ex-steady-tissue}
The cells produced by a group of intestinal crypts feed one villus. Suppose they deliver $s = 300$ new cells per hour (an illustrative figure) and each cell, once on the villus, is shed with a constant probability per unit time corresponding to a mean lifetime of 4 days. How many cells does the villus epithelium hold at steady state, and how long does it take to recover after half its cells are suddenly lost?
::: solution
The per-cell loss rate is $d = 1/(96\ \mathrm{h})$, so $N^* = s/d = 300\times96 = 28\,800$ cells. After a loss, $N(t) = N^* + (N_0 - N^*)e^{-dt}$: the deficit decays with time constant $1/d = 96$ h, so the epithelium is halfway back after $96\ln2 \approx 67$ h and within 5 % after $96\ln20 \approx 290$ h, about 12 days — unless the tissue senses the loss and increases $s$, as real injured epithelia do.
:::
:::

## Programmed cell death

::: definition Apoptosis and necrosis {#def-apoptosis}
**Apoptosis** is a genetically controlled form of cell death in which the cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses and its DNA is cut between nucleosomes, its plasma membrane forms blebs, and it breaks into membrane-bounded **apoptotic bodies** that expose phosphatidylserine and are rapidly engulfed by neighbouring cells or phagocytes, without spilling their contents or provoking inflammation. **Necrosis**, by contrast, is cell death in which the cell and its organelles swell and the plasma membrane ruptures, releasing the contents and triggering inflammation; it was long regarded as accidental, but several forms are now known to be regulated.
:::

The term apoptosis — Greek for "falling off", as of leaves — was introduced by John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie in 1972, who recognised the same characteristic morphology in many normal and diseased tissues and argued that it was a controlled, active process complementary to mitosis.

Apoptosis is part of normal life. In the developing vertebrate nervous system about half of many classes of neuron die, those that fail to get enough survival factors from their targets (the "neurotrophic hypothesis" that led Rita Levi-Montalcini to NGF). The digits of mammalian and bird embryos are separated by the death of the cells between them; a tadpole's tail is removed by it at metamorphosis; lymphocytes with useless or self-reactive receptors are eliminated by it. In the adult it balances division to keep tissue size constant and removes damaged, infected and potentially dangerous cells.

### Death in the worm

The genetics of apoptosis came from the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*. John Sulston and Robert Horvitz traced in the 1970s the complete lineage of every cell of the worm, by watching living embryos and larvae under the microscope; in the hermaphrodite, 1090 somatic cells are generated and exactly 131 of them die, always the same cells at the same time (Sulston and Horvitz, 1977; Sulston and colleagues, 1983). Horvitz's laboratory then isolated mutants in which these deaths did not occur. Hilary Ellis and Horvitz (1986) found that loss of either of two genes, *ced-3* or *ced-4* (cell death abnormal), allowed all 131 cells to survive; Michael Hengartner, Ellis and Horvitz (1992) found that loss of *ced-9* caused many cells to die that should have lived, killing the embryo, and that this extra death required *ced-3* and *ced-4*. CED-9 is therefore an inhibitor of a killing machine of which CED-3 and CED-4 are parts, and EGL-1 (found later) an inhibitor of CED-9. Sydney Brenner, who chose *C. elegans* as a model in the 1960s, Horvitz and Sulston shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

::: example Counting deaths in *C. elegans* {#ex-elegans}
What fraction of the somatic cells generated in a *C. elegans* hermaphrodite die by programmed cell death, and how many survive? In a *ced-3* loss-of-function mutant, how many somatic cells would you expect, and what does the near-normal appearance of such worms suggest?
::: solution
$131/1090 = 12.0\ \%$ die; $1090 - 131 = 959$ somatic cells survive in the adult. In a *ced-3* mutant, all 131 survive, so about 1090 somatic cells are present. Such worms are viable and look almost normal; extra cells are found, some of which differentiate into functional neurons. Programmed death in the worm is precise but largely dispensable under laboratory conditions — whereas in mammals, loss of the corresponding caspases causes severe defects in brain development in mice, showing that the importance of the pathway differs between organisms.
:::
:::

### The mammalian machinery

The worm genes have mammalian counterparts, discovered almost at the same time.

- **Caspases** (cysteine-dependent aspartate-directed proteases) are the executioners: CED-3 is a caspase (Junying Yuan, Horvitz and colleagues, 1993). Caspases are made as inactive precursors. **Initiator caspases** (caspase-8, caspase-9) are activated by being brought together on adaptor platforms, and cleave and activate **executioner caspases** (caspase-3, caspase-7), which cut hundreds of cellular proteins — lamins, cytoskeletal proteins, the inhibitor of the nuclease that then fragments DNA — producing the features of apoptosis. Because each caspase activates many others, the cascade amplifies itself and is irreversible.
- **The Bcl-2 family** corresponds to CED-9 and EGL-1. Bcl-2 itself was found as the gene activated by a chromosomal translocation in human follicular lymphoma; David Vaux, Suzanne Cory and Jerry Adams showed in 1988 that it promotes cell *survival* rather than proliferation, revealing a new class of oncogene. The family includes anti-apoptotic members (Bcl-2, Bcl-xL), pro-apoptotic effectors (Bax, Bak) and **BH3-only** proteins (Bid, Bim, Puma, Noxa) that respond to stress and tip the balance.
- **Apaf-1** corresponds to CED-4 and assembles the **apoptosome**.

There are two main pathways:

1. **The intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway** responds to internal damage — DNA damage acting through p53 ([[cell-biology/cell-cycle]]), growth-factor withdrawal, ER stress. BH3-only proteins activate Bax and Bak, which form pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane (**mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation**). **Cytochrome *c*** escapes from the intermembrane space into the cytosol, where it binds Apaf-1 and triggers assembly of the wheel-shaped apoptosome, which activates caspase-9. Xiaodong Wang's group discovered in 1996, in cell-free extracts of human cells, that cytochrome *c* — an electron carrier of the respiratory chain — is required for caspase activation: a striking dual role for a mitochondrial protein.
2. **The extrinsic (death-receptor) pathway** responds to signals from other cells. Ligands such as Fas ligand and TNF bind **death receptors** on the target cell, which recruit the adaptor FADD and procaspase-8 into a death-inducing signalling complex, activating caspase-8. Immune cells use this route to kill infected or abnormal cells.

### Other forms of regulated death

Apoptosis is not the only programme. **Necroptosis**, triggered when caspase-8 is inhibited, is mediated by the kinases RIPK1 and RIPK3 and the pore-forming protein MLKL, and ruptures the cell. **Pyroptosis**, in immune cells responding to infection, depends on caspase-1 and related caspases cleaving gasdermin D, which forms pores in the plasma membrane and releases inflammatory signals. **Ferroptosis**, named by Scott Dixon, Brent Stockwell and colleagues in 2012, is caused by iron-dependent peroxidation of membrane lipids and is prevented by the enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4. These lytic forms of death alert the immune system, whereas apoptosis is usually silent. **Autophagy** ([[cell-biology/organelles]]) is mainly a survival mechanism, but in some contexts accompanies or contributes to death.

::: quiz
Which experimental observation would best distinguish an apoptotic cell from a necrotic one in a cell culture?
- [ ] The cell is dead.
- [x] Early in the process the cell exposes phosphatidylserine on its outer surface while its plasma membrane still excludes impermeant dyes, and its DNA is cut into fragments of multiples of about 200 bp.
- [ ] The cell's mitochondria have stopped making ATP.
- [ ] The cell has detached from the dish.
::: solution
Apoptotic cells expose PS (detected with fluorescent annexin V, which binds it) while their membranes remain intact, so they exclude dyes such as propidium iodide; their DNA is cleaved between nucleosomes into a "ladder" of fragments. Necrotic cells lose membrane integrity early and admit the dye. Death, ATP loss and detachment occur in both. Late apoptotic cells in culture, where no phagocyte removes them, eventually also lose membrane integrity ("secondary necrosis"), so timing matters.
:::
:::

::: application Cell death and disease
Too little apoptosis contributes to cancer and autoimmunity; too much to neurodegeneration and to the tissue damage of strokes and heart attacks. Tumour cells evade death by overexpressing Bcl-2 or losing p53, and drugs that mimic BH3-only proteins ("BH3 mimetics", such as venetoclax) restore the cell's ability to die; their clinical use is described in [Medicine Atlas: cancer biology](/learn/medicine/lesson.php?c=pathology&l=cancer-biology), and cell injury and necrosis in tissues in [Medicine Atlas: cell injury](/learn/medicine/lesson.php?c=pathology&l=cell-injury). Many viruses carry genes that block apoptosis of the cells they infect, a sign of how important the programme is in defence.
:::

::: history
Carl Vogt described cell death in the developing notochord of midwife toads in 1842, and Walther Flemming the morphology of dying cells in 1885 ("chromatolysis"). Henry Van Peters Wilson's sponge reaggregation experiment appeared in 1907, Townes and Holtfreter's amphibian cell-sorting experiments in 1955, and Steinberg's differential adhesion hypothesis in 1963. James Till and Ernest McCulloch demonstrated haematopoietic stem cells in 1961; John Gurdon cloned frogs from tadpole intestinal nuclei in 1962. John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie named apoptosis in 1972. Masatoshi Takeichi described calcium-dependent adhesion in 1977. Sulston and Horvitz published the post-embryonic cell lineage of *C. elegans* in 1977, and Sulston's group the embryonic lineage in 1983; Ellis and Horvitz's *ced-3* and *ced-4* mutants followed in 1986. Evans and Kaufman and Martin derived mouse embryonic stem cells in 1981, Vaux, Cory and Adams showed that Bcl-2 promotes survival in 1988, Xiaodong Wang's group found the role of cytochrome *c* in 1996, Thomson's group derived human embryonic stem cells in 1998, and Takahashi and Yamanaka made iPS cells in 2006.
:::

## Looking back

This course began with the cell as a small, crowded volume bounded by a membrane, and has ended with cells that cooperate, specialise, replace one another and die for the good of the whole. The same few principles ran through every chapter: the hydrophobic effect that makes membranes; electrochemical gradients that store energy and carry signals; sorting signals that direct proteins; dynamic polymers and motors that build and move; switches, feedback and timers that make decisions; and the arithmetic of diffusion, binding and growth that sets how fast all of it can happen. The next courses take these ideas in different directions: [[molecular-biology]] into the genes that encode the machinery, [[microbiology]] into the cells that live alone, [[developmental-biology]] into how cells build an embryo, and [[animal-physiology]] into how tissues work as organs.

::: summary
- Tight junctions (claudins) seal epithelia and separate apical from basolateral membranes; adherens junctions (cadherins–catenins–actin) and desmosomes (cadherins–keratin) join cells mechanically; focal adhesions and hemidesmosomes join cells to the matrix through integrins; gap junctions (connexins) pass ions and molecules up to about 1 kDa.
- Homophilic, Ca²⁺-dependent cadherin binding and cortical tension sort cells into tissues (differential adhesion).
- The ECM is made of collagens (Gly-X-Y triple helices about 300 nm long), proteoglycans and GAGs, elastin, fibronectin (RGD) and laminin; integrins read it, transmit force and signal in both directions; matrix-deprived cells die by anoikis.
- Stem cells self-renew and differentiate; potency ranges from totipotent to unipotent. Homeostasis requires the probabilities of symmetric renewal and symmetric differentiation to be equal ($p = q$); intestinal stem cells achieve this at the population level, by neutral drift.
- Tissues replace cells at enormous rates (about 2.4 million red cells per second in an adult) through transit-amplifying divisions, and regulate their size by feedback on birth and death.
- Apoptosis is controlled death by caspases: intrinsic (Bcl-2 family, Bax/Bak, cytochrome *c*, Apaf-1, caspase-9) and extrinsic (death receptors, FADD, caspase-8) pathways. It is silent and non-inflammatory, unlike necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis.
- *C. elegans* genetics (131 of 1090 somatic cells die; *ced-3*, *ced-4*, *ced-9*) revealed a conserved death programme.
:::

After an injury stops production, a population of cells dies with a per-cell rate of 0.010 per day. How many days does it take to fall to half its initial size (three significant figures)?
::: solution
$N(t) = N_0e^{-dt}$, so $t_{1/2} = \ln2/d = 0.693/0.010 = 69.3$ days.
:::

::: exercise Sorting out {level=2}
Two populations of a cell line that normally does not adhere are engineered to express E-cadherin: population A at high levels, population B at low levels. They are labelled with different dyes, mixed and allowed to aggregate. (a) Predict the arrangement after a day, according to the differential adhesion hypothesis. (b) What result would you expect if instead A expressed E-cadherin and B expressed N-cadherin at similar levels? (c) How would you test whether Ca²⁺ is required, and what result would confirm that cadherins mediate the sorting?
::: solution
(a) The more cohesive population A forms a central core, surrounded by B, like a droplet of a liquid with higher surface tension inside one with lower — as Steinberg's group found with cadherin-transfected cells (Steinberg and Takeichi, 1994).
(b) Homophilic binding predicts that A and B sort into separate aggregates or domains, with each cell type adhering preferentially to its own kind (in practice E- and N-cadherin can bind each other weakly, so sorting may be incomplete).
(c) Repeat in a medium without Ca²⁺ (for example with a chelator): cadherins lose their rigid conformation and the cells should not aggregate or sort. Blocking antibodies against E-cadherin should have the same effect on population A, and adding back Ca²⁺ should restore aggregation.
:::

::: exercise Apoptosis or necrosis? {level=2}
Cells are treated with three agents and stained after 4 h with annexin V (which binds exposed phosphatidylserine) and propidium iodide (PI, a DNA dye that enters only cells with damaged plasma membranes). The percentages of cells in each class are:

| treatment | annexin⁻ PI⁻ | annexin⁺ PI⁻ | annexin⁺ PI⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | 95 | 3 | 2 |
| agent X | 40 | 50 | 10 |
| agent Y | 45 | 5 | 50 |
| agent X + caspase inhibitor | 90 | 5 | 5 |

(a) Which agent causes apoptosis and which necrosis? (b) What does the last row show? (c) Why would you also want a later time point for agent X?
::: solution
(a) Agent X produces many annexin⁺ PI⁻ cells — PS exposed but membranes intact — the signature of early apoptosis. Agent Y produces mostly annexin⁺ PI⁺ cells without a large annexin⁺ PI⁻ intermediate: membranes are damaged early, as in necrosis (annexin V also stains PS on the inner leaflet once the membrane is permeable).
(b) Inhibiting caspases prevents X's effect, confirming that the death is caspase-dependent apoptosis.
(c) In culture, apoptotic cells that are not engulfed eventually lose membrane integrity and become PI⁺ (secondary necrosis), so the annexin⁺ PI⁻ cells at 4 h should move into the double-positive class later; following the time course distinguishes this from primary necrosis, in which the double-positive population appears directly.
:::

::: exercise Ordering genes in a death pathway {level=3}
In *C. elegans*: *ced-3* and *ced-4* loss-of-function (lf) mutants have no programmed cell death; *ced-9* lf mutants die as embryos with massive ectopic cell death; a gain-of-function (gf) *ced-9* mutation blocks programmed cell death; and *ced-9(lf); ced-3(lf)* double mutants are viable and have no cell death. (a) What does the double-mutant phenotype tell you about the order of action of *ced-9* and *ced-3*? (b) Is CED-9 an activator or an inhibitor of death? (c) Write the simplest pathway consistent with the data, and indicate where the mammalian Bcl-2, Apaf-1 and caspase-9 proteins fit.
::: solution
(a) The double mutant shows the *ced-3* phenotype (no death): *ced-3* is epistatic to *ced-9*, so *ced-3* acts downstream. The ectopic death caused by losing *ced-9* requires *ced-3*.
(b) An inhibitor: losing it causes too much death, and overactivating it blocks death.
(c) CED-9 ⊣ CED-4 → CED-3 → death (with EGL-1 ⊣ CED-9 upstream, triggered in cells fated to die). In mammals, Bcl-2-family anti-apoptotic proteins correspond to CED-9, Apaf-1 to CED-4 and caspase-9 (with the downstream executioners) to CED-3; the mammalian pathway adds the mitochondrial step — Bax/Bak and cytochrome *c* release — between Bcl-2 and Apaf-1.
:::

::: exercise Neutral drift in a crypt {level=3 check="0.0625"}
An intestinal crypt contains $N = 16$ equivalent stem cells that replace one another at random by neutral drift. (a) What is the probability that the descendants of one particular stem cell, marked at time zero, eventually occupy the whole crypt? (b) If one marker is induced in each of 1000 crypts, about how many crypts would you expect to become entirely labelled? (c) How would a mutation that gives a stem cell a competitive advantage change the answer, and why is this relevant to the early stages of cancer?
::: solution
(a) Under neutral drift every stem cell is equally likely to be the eventual ancestor of the whole crypt, so the probability is $1/N = 1/16 = 0.0625$. (This is the same as the fixation probability of a neutral allele in a population of size $N$, a result of [[population-genetics]].)
(b) About $1000/16 \approx 63$ crypts; the remaining clones are lost. The fraction of monoclonal labelled crypts rises over weeks and then plateaus at this value, which is how the stem-cell number per crypt was estimated.
(c) An advantaged clone (one that replaces its neighbours more often than it is replaced) has a fixation probability greater than $1/N$, so mutant clones take over crypts more often and faster. Cancer-associated mutations, such as loss of the tumour suppressor *Apc*, give exactly such an advantage, and crypts fully mutant for them are a first step in intestinal tumour formation — natural selection operating among the cells of one body.
:::
