A drop of pond water, a scraping from the inside of your cheek and a slice of an onion look nothing alike, yet under a microscope all three resolve into the same kind of unit: a small volume of watery, crowded chemistry enclosed by a membrane, able to take in matter and energy, to copy itself and to pass on the instructions for doing so. That unit is the **cell**. Every organism we know is either a single cell or a community of cells descended from one, and nearly everything biology studies — metabolism, inheritance, development, behaviour, evolution — happens in or between cells.

This chapter sets up the course. We state the cell theory and see how it was won; compare the two great plans of cellular organisation, prokaryotic and eukaryotic; and then do something biology textbooks often skip — put numbers on cells. How big are they, how many molecules do they hold, and how long does it take a molecule to get from one side to the other? Those numbers explain a surprising amount of cell biology, including why cells are small. Finally we look at how we see cells at all: the physics that limits a light microscope, the tricks that get round it, and the electron microscope.

## The cell theory

The idea that organisms are built of cells is so familiar that it is easy to miss how strong a claim it is. It took about two centuries, from the first microscopes to the middle of the nineteenth century, to assemble.

::: definition The cell theory {#def-cell-theory}
1. Every living organism consists of one or more **cells**.
2. The cell is the smallest unit that shows the properties of life: it maintains itself by metabolism, responds to its surroundings and reproduces.
3. Every cell arises from a pre-existing cell by division. Cells are not assembled from non-living matter in present-day organisms.
:::

The third clause is the one with the deepest consequences. If every cell comes from an earlier cell, then every cell alive today sits at the tip of an unbroken lineage stretching back some four billion years to the earliest life. Inheritance, in this light, is the continuity of cells; evolution is change along their lineages.

Viruses are the instructive boundary case. A virus particle carries genes and can direct its own replication, but only inside a host cell, using the host's ribosomes and metabolism. It does not grow, metabolise or divide on its own, so by [[#def-cell-theory]] it is not a cell, and most biologists do not count it as an independent living organism — while recognising that viruses evolve, and that the question of whether they are "alive" depends on the definition chosen.

::: quiz
A red blood cell of an adult mammal has no nucleus and cannot divide. Which statement is consistent with the cell theory?
- [ ] It is not a cell, because it cannot reproduce.
- [x] It is a cell; it arose by division of a precursor cell and lost its nucleus while maturing.
- [ ] It shows that cells can be assembled from non-living matter.
- [ ] It contradicts the cell theory, which must therefore be abandoned.
::: solution
Mammalian erythrocytes descend from dividing precursors in the bone marrow and extrude their nucleus as they mature. They are terminally differentiated cells: the theory requires that every cell *comes from* a cell, not that every cell can still divide. Many specialised cells — most neurons, skeletal muscle fibres — have also stopped dividing.
:::
:::

## Two plans of cellular organisation

Living cells come in two architectural plans, distinguished above all by how they organise their DNA.

::: definition Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells {#def-pro-eu}
A **prokaryotic cell** has no nucleus: its chromosome, usually a single circular DNA molecule, lies in a region of the cytoplasm called the **nucleoid**, and it generally lacks membrane-bounded organelles. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotic.

A **eukaryotic cell** encloses its chromosomes in a **nucleus** bounded by a double membrane, the nuclear envelope, and contains an internal system of membrane-bounded compartments (endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, endosomes, lysosomes) together with mitochondria and, in plants and algae, plastids. Animals, plants, fungi and the many lineages of protists are eukaryotes.
:::

The table compares typical members of each plan. The values are representative; real cells vary widely.

| feature | prokaryote (e.g. *Escherichia coli*) | eukaryote (e.g. a human fibroblast) |
|---|---|---|
| typical linear size | 0.5–5 µm | 10–100 µm |
| DNA | one circular chromosome in a nucleoid, plus plasmids | several linear chromosomes in a nucleus, packed into chromatin on histones |
| ribosomes | 70S | 80S in the cytosol; 70S-like in mitochondria and plastids |
| internal membranes | usually none (some specialised compartments) | extensive: ER, Golgi, endosomes, lysosomes, peroxisomes |
| cytoskeleton | homologues of actin (MreB), tubulin (FtsZ) | actin filaments, microtubules, intermediate filaments, motor proteins |
| transcription and translation | coupled in the same compartment | separated: RNA is made in the nucleus and translated in the cytosol |
| division | binary fission | mitosis (or meiosis) and cytokinesis |

Two cautions keep this table honest. First, "prokaryote" names a plan of organisation, not a single evolutionary group. In 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox compared ribosomal RNA sequences and found that the organisms then called bacteria fall into two lineages as distinct from each other as either is from eukaryotes: the **Bacteria** and the **Archaea**. Archaea look like bacteria under the microscope but differ in their membrane lipids (isoprenoid chains joined to glycerol by ether bonds, rather than fatty acids joined by ester bonds), in their cell walls, and in a transcription and translation machinery that resembles that of eukaryotes. Woese, Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis proposed the three domains — Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya — in 1990. Genomes of the Asgard archaea, described from 2015 onwards, now suggest that eukaryotes arose from within the Archaea, by the merger of an archaeal host with a bacterium that became the mitochondrion; this two-domain picture is widely, though not universally, accepted. We return to the merger in [[cell-biology/organelles]].

Second, "no internal membranes" is a generalisation. Cyanobacteria have photosynthetic thylakoid membranes, magnetotactic bacteria make membrane-bounded magnetite crystals, and planctomycetes and the giant sulfur bacterium *Thiomargarita magnifica* — a single cell about a centimetre long, described by Jean-Marie Volland and colleagues in 2022 — have membrane-bounded compartments around their DNA. Bacteria also have a cytoskeleton, protein-shelled compartments such as carboxysomes, and precisely positioned proteins. The prokaryotic cell is organised; it is organised differently.

::: warning "Prokaryotes are primitive"
It is tempting to rank prokaryotes below eukaryotes as an earlier, simpler stage. Both are modern: today's bacteria and archaea have evolved for exactly as long as we have. They dominate the planet's chemistry — fixing nitrogen, cycling sulfur, and carrying out much of global photosynthesis and decomposition — and they occupy habitats from hot springs above 100 °C to the deep subsurface. "Simpler in architecture" is fair; "less evolved" is not.
:::

Despite their differences, every cell shares a core: a plasma membrane that separates inside from outside; DNA as the genetic material, read into RNA and translated into protein on ribosomes using nearly the same genetic code; ATP as the energy currency; and a common set of central metabolic reactions. This shared core is the strongest evidence that all life descends from a common ancestor.

## How big is a cell?

Numbers anchor intuition. The figures below come from Milo and Phillips, *Cell Biology by the Numbers* (2015), and the BioNumbers database they draw on; all are representative values with real spread.

| cell | characteristic size | volume |
|---|---|---|
| *Mycoplasma* (among the smallest free-living cells) | 0.2–0.3 µm diameter | ≈ 0.005–0.01 fL |
| *E. coli* | ≈ 1 µm × 2 µm | ≈ 1 fL |
| budding yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (haploid) | ≈ 4 µm diameter | ≈ 40 fL |
| human red blood cell | 7–8 µm disc, ≈ 2 µm thick | ≈ 90 fL |
| HeLa cell (cultured human cell line) | 15–20 µm when spread | ≈ 3000 fL |
| *Xenopus laevis* oocyte | ≈ 1.2 mm diameter | ≈ 1 µL $= 10^9$ fL |

Here $1\ \mathrm{fL} = 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{L} = 1\ \mathrm{\mu m^3}$, a convenient identity to remember. Linear size spans a factor of a few thousand, volume a factor of about $10^{11}$. The human body contains about $3\times10^{13}$ human cells — about 84 % of them red blood cells by number — and a comparable number of bacteria (Sender, Fuchs and Milo, 2016).

::: example The volume and surface of *E. coli* {#ex-ecoli-volume}
Model an *E. coli* cell as a cylinder of diameter 1 µm and length 1 µm capped at each end by a hemisphere of the same diameter, for a total length of 2 µm. Calculate its volume, surface area and surface-to-volume ratio.
::: solution
With radius $r = 0.5\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and cylinder length $L = 1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, the two caps make one sphere:

$$
V = \pi r^2 L + \tfrac43\pi r^3 = 0.785 + 0.524 = 1.31\ \mathrm{\mu m^3},\qquad
A = 2\pi r L + 4\pi r^2 = 3.14 + 3.14 = 6.28\ \mathrm{\mu m^2}.
$$

So $V \approx 1.3\ \mathrm{fL}$ and $A/V = 4.8\ \mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}$. Measured volumes depend on growth rate (fast-growing cells are larger) and range from about 0.6 to 1.5 fL, so "1 fL" is a good round number. Every square micrometre of membrane serves only about 0.2 fL of cytoplasm.
:::
:::

### Surface and volume

Why are most cells so small? One reason is geometry.

::: proposition Surface-to-volume scaling {#prop-sv}
For a sphere of radius $r$, the surface area is $4\pi r^2$ and the volume $\tfrac43\pi r^3$, so

$$
\frac{A}{V} = \frac{3}{r}.
$$ {#eq-sv}

For any family of cells of the same shape scaled by a linear factor $\lambda$, area grows as $\lambda^2$ and volume as $\lambda^3$, so $A/V \propto 1/\lambda$.
:::

Everything a cell imports — nutrients, oxygen — and everything it exports — waste, heat — must cross its surface, but its demand is set by the amount of cytoplasm, which scales with volume. Doubling the radius halves the membrane available per unit volume. Cells that are large in one dimension escape the constraint by changing shape: neurons and fungal hyphae are long and thin, intestinal epithelial cells fold their apical surface into microvilli, and large plant cells push their cytoplasm into a thin layer around a central vacuole.

### Molecules per cell

A concentration and a number of molecules are related by the volume: $n = c V N_\mathrm{A}$, with Avogadro's constant $N_\mathrm{A} = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$. In a cell this conversion is constantly useful, because many important molecules are present in small numbers.

::: example One nanomolar in a bacterium {#ex-nanomolar}
How many molecules does a concentration of $1\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$ correspond to in an *E. coli* cell of volume 1 fL? And in a HeLa cell of 3000 fL?
::: solution
$$
n = cVN_\mathrm{A} = (10^{-9}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})(10^{-15}\ \mathrm{L})(6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}) = 0.60.
$$

In *E. coli*, **1 nM is about one molecule per cell** — a rule of thumb worth memorising. A transcription factor present at ten copies is at about 17 nM, and "a concentration of 0.1 nM" is not a meaningful description of a single bacterium: such a molecule is present in one cell in ten. In a HeLa cell the same 1 nM is $0.60\times3000 \approx 1800$ molecules, and one molecule corresponds to only $0.6\ \mathrm{pM}$.
:::
:::

The low copy numbers have real consequences. The lac repressor of *E. coli* is present at roughly ten tetramers per cell; when such small numbers bind and unbind DNA, random fluctuations make genetically identical cells in the same medium behave differently. Cell-to-cell variability, "noise", is a theme of [[systems-biology]].

At the other extreme, an *E. coli* cell holds about $3\times10^6$ protein molecules (a total protein concentration of a few millimolar), and its cytoplasm is crowded: macromolecules occupy some 20–30 % of its volume. The cell interior is not a dilute solution but a gel-like, densely packed medium in which proteins are on average only a few nanometres apart.

## Diffusion: how long things take

Molecules in the cytoplasm are buffeted by water molecules and wander in random walks. Diffusion is fast over short distances and hopelessly slow over long ones, and the crossover is at roughly the size of a cell.

::: proposition Mean squared displacement of a diffusing particle {#prop-diffusion}
A particle with diffusion coefficient $D$ that starts at the origin has, after time $t$, a mean squared displacement along any one axis of

$$
\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt,
$$ {#eq-msd}

and in three dimensions $\langle r^2\rangle = \langle x^2+y^2+z^2\rangle = 6Dt$. The typical time to diffuse a distance $L$ along one axis is therefore

$$
t \approx \frac{L^2}{2D}.
$$ {#eq-tdiff}

Assumptions: the medium is homogeneous and the particle's steps are independent of each other (a random walk with no drift, no binding and no barriers), and $t$ is long compared with the time between molecular collisions.
:::

::: proof Random walk
Let the particle take a step of length $\delta$, left or right with equal probability, every $\tau$ seconds. After $N = t/\tau$ steps its position is $x = s_1 + \dots + s_N$ with independent $s_i = \pm\delta$. Then $\langle x\rangle = 0$ and

$$
\langle x^2\rangle = \sum_i \langle s_i^2\rangle + \sum_{i\neq j}\langle s_i\rangle\langle s_j\rangle = N\delta^2 + 0 = \frac{\delta^2}{\tau}\,t.
$$

Defining $D = \delta^2/(2\tau)$ gives $\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt$. The motions along the three axes are independent, so their contributions add to give $6Dt$. The same result follows from the diffusion (heat) equation, whose solution from a point source is a Gaussian of variance $2Dt$ — see [Maths Atlas: the heat equation](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=pde&l=heat-equation).
:::

The essential feature is that distance grows only as the *square root* of time: to go ten times further takes a hundred times longer. Diffusion coefficients for representative molecules (at 20–37 °C; from BioNumbers and the studies cited) are: a small metabolite such as glucose in water, about $600\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$; green fluorescent protein in water, about $90\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$, but about $25\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$ in the cytoplasm of a mammalian cell and $3$–$8\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$ in *E. coli* (Elowitz and colleagues, 1999); a lipid in a membrane, about $1\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$.

::: example Across a bacterium, across a cell, along an axon {#ex-diffusion-times}
Using [[#eq-tdiff]], estimate how long a protein with $D = 10\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$ takes to diffuse (a) 1 µm, across an *E. coli* cell; (b) 10 µm, across a human cell; (c) 1 m, the length of the longest motor-neuron axons in the human leg.
::: solution
(a) $t = (1\ \mathrm{\mu m})^2/(2\times10\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}) = 0.05\ \mathrm{s}$.

(b) $t = 100/20 = 5\ \mathrm{s}$.

(c) $L = 10^6\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, so $t = 10^{12}/20 = 5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{s}$, about 1600 years.

Inside a bacterium diffusion mixes the cytoplasm many times a second, and no transport machinery is needed. Across a eukaryotic cell it takes seconds — still adequate for many purposes, but too slow for others. Along an axon it is useless: proteins and organelles made in the cell body must be carried by motor proteins walking on microtubules at about $1\ \mathrm{\mu m\,s^{-1}}$, which brings the journey down to some ten days ([[cell-biology/cytoskeleton]]).
:::
:::

::: intuition Why diffusion favours the small
Combine [[#prop-sv]] and [[#prop-diffusion]]. A small cell has a large surface per unit volume *and* short internal distances, so it can feed itself by diffusion alone. A large cell has neither advantage; it must either stay thin, or invest in active transport — motors, cytoplasmic streaming, a circulatory system in a multicellular animal. Many features of eukaryotic cells, from the cytoskeleton to internal membranes that bring reactions together, can be read as solutions to the problem of being big.
:::

## Seeing cells: the light microscope

Most cells are below the resolution of the naked eye, about 0.1 mm at reading distance. The light microscope extends our vision by about a thousandfold, and the limit it reaches is set not by the quality of the lenses but by the wave nature of light.

::: definition Resolution and numerical aperture {#def-resolution}
The **resolution** of an imaging system is the smallest distance $d$ between two point objects at which they can still be distinguished as two. The **numerical aperture** of an objective is $\mathrm{NA} = n\sin\alpha$, where $n$ is the refractive index of the medium between specimen and lens and $\alpha$ is half the angle of the cone of light the objective collects. Dry objectives have $\mathrm{NA} < 1$; oil-immersion objectives ($n \approx 1.52$) reach about 1.4–1.5.
:::

A lens cannot focus light from a point to a point. Diffraction spreads it into a blurred spot, the point-spread function, whose width is about the wavelength divided by the numerical aperture. When two spots overlap too much, they merge.

::: proposition Diffraction limit (Abbe; Rayleigh) {#prop-abbe}
For light of wavelength $\lambda$ and an objective of numerical aperture $\mathrm{NA}$, the smallest resolvable separation is approximately

$$
d_\text{Abbe} = \frac{\lambda}{2\,\mathrm{NA}}, \qquad d_\text{Rayleigh} = \frac{0.61\,\lambda}{\mathrm{NA}}.
$$ {#eq-abbe}

The two forms come from different criteria — Abbe's from the finest periodic grating whose diffraction orders the objective can still collect, Rayleigh's from placing the first dark ring of one point's diffraction pattern on the centre of the other — and differ by about 20 %. Both assume conventional (linear, far-field) imaging of incoherent or partially coherent light.
:::

The derivation belongs to optics; see [Physics Atlas: diffraction](/learn/physics/lesson.php?c=optics&l=diffraction). The biological consequence is what matters here.

::: example The best a light microscope can do {#ex-abbe}
Calculate the Abbe and Rayleigh limits for green light ($\lambda = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$) with (a) a dry objective of $\mathrm{NA} = 0.25$ and (b) an oil-immersion objective of $\mathrm{NA} = 1.4$. Which of these can be resolved with (b): two mitochondria 1 µm apart; two ribosomes 30 nm apart; the two membranes of the nuclear envelope, about 40 nm apart?
::: solution
(a) $d_\text{Abbe} = 500/(2\times0.25) = 1000\ \mathrm{nm}$; $d_\text{Rayleigh} = 0.61\times500/0.25 = 1220\ \mathrm{nm}$.

(b) $d_\text{Abbe} = 500/2.8 = 179\ \mathrm{nm}$; $d_\text{Rayleigh} = 305/1.4 = 218\ \mathrm{nm}$.

With (b) the limit is about 0.2 µm. Mitochondria 1 µm apart are easily resolved. Ribosomes (about 25 nm across) and the two membranes of the nuclear envelope are far below the limit: a single ribosome can still be *detected* if it is made to fluoresce, but appears as a blur 0.2 µm wide, and the double membrane looks like a single line.
:::
:::

::: warning Magnification is not resolution
A photograph of a cell can be enlarged as much as you like, but enlarging a blurred image just gives a larger blur. Useful magnification in a light microscope stops at about 1000× (objective × eyepiece): beyond that, "empty magnification" reveals no new detail. What determines the finest detail is the numerical aperture and the wavelength, [[#eq-abbe]].
:::

### Contrast

Resolution is useless without contrast, and most cells are nearly transparent: their components differ only slightly in refractive index. Three families of methods supply contrast.

- **Stains.** Fixed and sectioned tissue is coloured with dyes that bind particular components: haematoxylin stains acidic structures such as chromatin blue-purple, eosin stains most proteins pink. Staining is the basis of histology but requires killing the cell.
- **Optical contrast.** Frits Zernike's **phase-contrast** microscope (1930s; Nobel Prize in Physics 1953) converts the tiny phase delays that light suffers in passing through denser parts of a cell into differences of brightness, so living, unstained cells can be watched. **Differential interference contrast** (DIC) does something similar using polarised light and gives cells a relief-like appearance.
- **Fluorescence.** A fluorophore absorbs light at one wavelength and emits at a longer one; filters pass the emission and block the excitation, so only labelled molecules shine against a dark background. Fluorescent antibodies (immunofluorescence) label proteins in fixed cells. The **green fluorescent protein** of the jellyfish *Aequorea victoria*, isolated by Osamu Shimomura in 1962, was cloned by Douglas Prasher in 1992 and expressed in *E. coli* and the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* by Martin Chalfie and colleagues in 1994; Roger Tsien's laboratory engineered it into a palette of colours. Because GFP is encoded by a gene, it can be fused to almost any protein and followed in living cells. Shimomura, Chalfie and Tsien shared the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

**Confocal microscopy**, patented by Marvin Minsky in 1957, places a pinhole in front of the detector so that only light from the focal plane is recorded; scanning the spot builds sharp optical sections that can be stacked into three-dimensional images. Two-photon and light-sheet microscopes reduce damage to living samples further.

### Beyond the diffraction limit

[[#prop-abbe]] limits *conventional* imaging. Since the 1990s several methods have broken it — not by violating optics but by exploiting properties of fluorescent molecules:

- **STED** (stimulated emission depletion), proposed by Stefan Hell and Jan Wichmann in 1994, uses a doughnut-shaped beam to switch off fluorescence everywhere except at the very centre of the excitation spot.
- **Single-molecule localisation** — PALM (Eric Betzig, Harald Hess and colleagues, 2006) and STORM (Xiaowei Zhuang's group, 2006) — switches on only a sparse random subset of fluorophores at a time. Each isolated molecule gives a diffraction-limited spot, but the *centre* of that spot can be located far more precisely than its width: with $N$ detected photons, the precision is roughly $\sigma/\sqrt N$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the spot. Thousands of frames are combined into one image.
- **Expansion microscopy** (Fei Chen, Paul Tillberg and Edward Boyden, 2015) embeds the specimen in a swellable gel and expands it physically about fourfold before imaging it with an ordinary microscope.

These methods now reach 10–50 nm in favourable cases. Betzig, Hell and William Moerner shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

## The electron microscope

The way to a much smaller diffraction limit is a much shorter wavelength. Electrons accelerated through a potential difference $V$ have a de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$; because at the voltages used they move at a large fraction of the speed of light, the momentum must include a relativistic correction:

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_\mathrm{e}eV\left(1 + \dfrac{eV}{2m_\mathrm{e}c^2}\right)}}.
$$ {#eq-electron}

::: example The wavelength of a 300 kV electron {#ex-electron}
Compute $\lambda$ for electrons accelerated through $V = 300\ \mathrm{kV}$, as in a modern cryo-electron microscope, and compare it with green light.
::: solution
With $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, $m_\mathrm{e} = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $eV = 4.807\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{J}$, the correction term is $eV/(2m_\mathrm{e}c^2) = 4.807\times10^{-14}/(1.637\times10^{-13}) = 0.294$. Then

$$
\lambda = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(4.807\times10^{-14})(1.294)}} = 1.97\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m} = 1.97\ \mathrm{pm}.
$$

That is about 250,000 times shorter than 500 nm light. In practice electron lenses have such large aberrations that only very small apertures can be used, and the attainable resolution is about 0.1 nm for materials and, for biological specimens, limited mainly by radiation damage and specimen preparation rather than by wavelength.
:::
:::

The first electron microscope was built by Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll in Berlin in 1931; Ruska shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics. Applying it to cells took two more decades of work on specimen preparation. Because the beam travels in a vacuum and is scattered by any thickness of matter, classical **transmission electron microscopy** (TEM) uses chemically fixed specimens, embedded in plastic, cut into sections 50–100 nm thick and stained with heavy metals (osmium, uranium, lead) that scatter electrons. In 1945 Keith Porter, Albert Claude and Ernest Fullam published the first electron micrograph of a whole cultured cell, and in the 1950s George Palade, Porter, Fritiof Sjöstrand and others used thin sections to describe the endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, the Golgi apparatus and the cristae of mitochondria. **Scanning electron microscopy** (SEM) rasters a beam over a metal-coated surface and records scattered electrons, giving vivid images of surfaces.

Chemical fixation and staining can produce artefacts. **Cryo-electron microscopy** avoids them by freezing the specimen so fast (in liquid ethane, cooled by liquid nitrogen) that water forms a glass rather than ice crystals, a method developed by Jacques Dubochet and colleagues in the early 1980s. Averaging images of many identical molecules, using methods pioneered by Joachim Frank, and the direct electron detectors introduced around 2012, now give structures of proteins and complexes at near-atomic resolution; Richard Henderson's 1990 structure of bacteriorhodopsin was an early landmark. Dubochet, Frank and Henderson shared the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. **Cryo-electron tomography** applies the same idea to thin regions of whole frozen cells, tilting the specimen to reconstruct molecular machines in their native setting. Structure determination is treated in [[structural-biology]].

::: application Correlating light and electrons
Light and electron microscopy answer complementary questions: fluorescence shows *which* molecule is *where* in a living cell, and how it moves, but at 0.2 µm resolution; electron microscopy shows ultrastructure at nanometre resolution, but in dead, fixed or frozen cells, and with little molecular identity. **Correlative light and electron microscopy** images the same cell both ways — for example, locating a rare fluorescent event in a living cell, then freezing the cell and imaging that spot by cryo-electron tomography. Pathology laboratories still use TEM of biopsies for some kidney and muscle diseases; the clinical side belongs to [Medicine Atlas: diagnostic pathology](/learn/medicine/lesson.php?c=pathology&l=diagnostic-pathology).
:::

::: history
In *Micrographia* (1665) Robert Hooke described the little boxes he saw in thin slices of cork and called them "cells"; they were the empty walls of dead plant cells. From 1673 onwards the Delft draper Antonie van Leeuwenhoek sent letters to the Royal Society of London describing "animalcules" — protists, sperm, and in 1676 and 1683 what were almost certainly bacteria — seen through single-lens microscopes he ground himself. Robert Brown named the nucleus in orchid cells in 1831. The generalisation came in 1838, when Matthias Schleiden argued that plants are built of cells, and in 1839, when Theodor Schwann extended the claim to animals in his *Mikroskopische Untersuchungen*. Both still thought that cells could crystallise out of an unstructured fluid. Robert Remak's observations of dividing cells in frog embryos (1852) and Rudolf Virchow's *Cellularpathologie* and its slogan *omnis cellula e cellula* (1855–58) established that cells come only from cells, and Louis Pasteur's experiments of the early 1860s disposed of spontaneous generation of microbes. Ernst Abbe's theory of image formation (1873), working with Carl Zeiss's workshop in Jena, explained the resolution limit and led to oil-immersion objectives. The terms *procaryote* and *eucaryote* were introduced by Édouard Chatton in 1925 and given their modern definition by Roger Stanier and Cornelis van Niel in 1962, fifteen years before Woese and Fox split the prokaryotes in two.
:::

- The cell theory: all organisms are made of cells, the cell is the smallest living unit, and every cell arises from a pre-existing cell.
- Prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea) lack a nucleus and usually lack internal membranes; eukaryotic cells have a nucleus, an endomembrane system and mitochondria. Archaea are a separate domain, and eukaryotes probably arose from within them.
- Cells span about $10^{11}$ in volume, from $\approx0.01$ fL (*Mycoplasma*) through $\approx1$ fL (*E. coli*) and $\approx3000$ fL (a HeLa cell) to $\approx1$ µL (a frog oocyte); $1\ \mathrm{fL} = 1\ \mathrm{\mu m^3}$.
- Surface-to-volume ratio falls as $3/r$; in *E. coli*, 1 nM is about one molecule per cell.
- Diffusion distances grow as $\sqrt{t}$: $\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt$. Diffusion crosses a bacterium in milliseconds to tens of milliseconds and an animal cell in seconds, but would take centuries along an axon.
- The light microscope is limited by diffraction to $d \approx \lambda/(2\,\mathrm{NA}) \approx 0.2\ \mathrm{\mu m}$; fluorescence (with GFP), confocal and super-resolution methods extend what it can show.
- Electron microscopes use wavelengths of picometres; cryo-EM and cryo-tomography image proteins and whole cells without chemical fixation.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Surface-to-volume ratio {level=1 check="1.5"}
A spherical yeast cell has a radius of 2 µm. Calculate its surface-to-volume ratio in µm⁻¹, and compare it with the *E. coli* value of [[#ex-ecoli-volume]].
::: solution
By [[#eq-sv]], $A/V = 3/r = 3/2 = 1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}$, about a third of the *E. coli* value of $4.8\ \mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}$. Each square micrometre of yeast plasma membrane serves about 0.67 fL of cytoplasm, against about 0.2 fL in *E. coli*.
:::

::: exercise A diffraction limit {level=1 check="200"}
Calculate the Abbe limit $d = \lambda/(2\,\mathrm{NA})$ in nanometres for light of wavelength 520 nm and an objective of numerical aperture 1.3.
::: solution
$d = 520/(2\times1.3) = 520/2.6 = 200\ \mathrm{nm}$.

::: exercise From copy number to concentration {level=1 check="166"}
An *E. coli* cell of volume 1 fL contains 100 copies of a protein. What is its concentration in nmol L⁻¹ (to three significant figures)?
::: solution
$c = n/(VN_\mathrm{A}) = 100/(10^{-15}\times6.022\times10^{23}) = 1.66\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} = 166\ \mathrm{nM}$, consistent with the rule of thumb of [[#ex-nanomolar]] (about 0.6 molecules per nM).
:::

::: exercise A metabolite crosses a cell {level=2 check="0.4"}
A metabolite with $D = 500\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$ is released at one side of a cell 20 µm across. Estimate, with [[#eq-tdiff]], the time in seconds for it to diffuse to the other side. Why is this only an estimate?
::: solution
$t \approx L^2/(2D) = 400/1000 = 0.4\ \mathrm{s}$. The formula gives the time for the root-mean-square displacement to reach $L$; individual molecules arrive over a broad spread of times, some much earlier and some later. Real cytoplasm is crowded, contains membranes and binding sites, and is not homogeneous, so effective diffusion coefficients are often several times smaller than in water.
:::
:::

::: exercise Is it resolved? {level=2}
Two fluorescently labelled proteins sit 150 nm apart in a cell imaged at an emission wavelength of 500 nm with a 1.4 NA oil-immersion objective. (a) Are they resolved in a conventional fluorescence image? (b) A student enlarges the digital image tenfold to separate them. Does it help? (c) Suggest a method that could separate them, and explain why it is not limited by [[#prop-abbe]].
::: solution
(a) No. The Abbe limit is 179 nm and the Rayleigh limit 218 nm (see [[#ex-abbe]]); at 150 nm the two spots merge into one elongated blur.
(b) No. Enlargement does not add information that the optics did not collect; it is empty magnification.
(c) Single-molecule localisation (PALM or STORM) would switch the two fluorophores on at different times so each appears alone; the centre of each isolated spot can be located to a few tens of nanometres or better, so their separation can be measured. STED would also work, by shrinking the effective excitation spot. Both exploit the ability to switch fluorophores between bright and dark states, which conventional imaging does not use.
:::
:::

::: exercise Classifying an unknown cell {level=2}
A newly isolated microorganism from a hot spring has the following properties: cells about 1 µm across; no nuclear envelope in electron micrographs; 70S ribosomes; membrane lipids made of isoprenoid chains joined to glycerol by ether bonds; a DNA-dependent RNA polymerase with many subunits that resembles eukaryotic RNA polymerase II. To which domain does it most probably belong? Which observation rules out a eukaryote, and which point away from Bacteria?
::: solution
It is most probably an archaeon. The absence of a nucleus and the 70S ribosomes show prokaryotic organisation, ruling out a eukaryote. Ether-linked isoprenoid lipids are the signature of archaeal membranes (bacteria and eukaryotes use ester-linked fatty acids), and a multi-subunit, eukaryote-like RNA polymerase is also characteristic of archaea. Size and the habitat are not diagnostic: many bacteria also live in hot springs. The decisive modern test would be the sequence of its 16S ribosomal RNA, as in Woese and Fox's work.
:::
:::

::: exercise Precision of a single-molecule localisation {level=2 check="3.2"}
A single fluorescent molecule gives an image spot with a standard deviation $\sigma = 100\ \mathrm{nm}$. Ignoring background and pixel size, the precision with which its centre can be located from $N$ detected photons is approximately $\sigma/\sqrt N$. What is the precision in nanometres when $N = 1000$? How many photons would be needed for a precision of 1 nm?
::: solution
$100/\sqrt{1000} = 100/31.6 = 3.2\ \mathrm{nm}$. For 1 nm, $\sqrt N = 100$, so $N = 10^4$ photons. Precision improves only as the square root of the photon count — the same statistics as the standard error of a mean — which is why bright, photostable fluorophores matter so much. In practice background noise and the finite pixel size make the real precision several-fold worse, and the size of the label (an antibody is about 10 nm) sets a further limit on how accurately the *protein* is located.
:::

::: exercise How large can a cell feeding on oxygen be? {level=3 check="49"}
A spherical cell of radius $R$ takes up oxygen by diffusion and consumes it uniformly throughout its volume at a rate $q$ (mol per unit volume per unit time). At steady state the concentration inside obeys $D\nabla^2 c = q$, and the concentration at the surface is held at $c_\mathrm{s}$.
(a) Show that $c(r) = c_\mathrm{s} - \dfrac{q}{6D}(R^2 - r^2)$ satisfies the equation and the boundary condition, where in spherical symmetry $\nabla^2 c = \dfrac{1}{r^2}\dfrac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\dfrac{dc}{dr}\right)$.
(b) Hence find the largest radius for which the centre is not starved of oxygen.
(c) Evaluate it in micrometres for $D = 2000\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$ (oxygen in water), $c_\mathrm{s} = 0.2\ \mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and an illustrative consumption rate $q = 1\ \mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$.
::: hint
For (b), require $c(0) \ge 0$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $dc/dr = qr/(3D)$, so $r^2\,dc/dr = qr^3/(3D)$, whose derivative is $qr^2/D$; dividing by $r^2$ gives $\nabla^2 c = q/D$, as required. At $r = R$ the bracket vanishes and $c = c_\mathrm{s}$.

(b) The concentration is lowest at the centre, $c(0) = c_\mathrm{s} - qR^2/(6D)$. It stays non-negative if $R \le R_\text{max} = \sqrt{6Dc_\mathrm{s}/q}$.

(c) The concentrations are in the same units, so $R_\text{max} = \sqrt{6\times2000\times0.2/1}\ \mathrm{\mu m} = \sqrt{2400}\ \mathrm{\mu m} = 49\ \mathrm{\mu m}$.

The answer depends on $q$ only as $q^{-1/2}$, so even a tenfold change in the assumed consumption rate moves $R_\text{max}$ by only a factor of about 3. Actively respiring cells much larger than about 0.1 mm are hard to supply by diffusion alone, and multicellular animals larger than a few millimetres need circulatory systems. Insects push the limit by piping air directly to the tissues through tracheae.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a size distribution {level=3}
A laboratory measures the volumes of 1000 *E. coli* cells in an exponentially growing culture and finds that they range from about 0.7 to 1.5 fL, with newborn cells clustered near the low end and cells about to divide near the high end. The same strain grown in a richer medium has a mean volume about twice as large. (a) Explain why a growing population must contain a spread of sizes even if every cell divided at exactly the same size. (b) What does the medium dependence say about using "1 fL" in calculations such as [[#ex-nanomolar]]? (c) If a protein's copy number per cell doubles in the richer medium, has its concentration changed?
::: solution
(a) Each cell grows from its birth size to about twice that size before dividing into two daughters, so at any moment the population contains cells at every stage between — roughly a factor of two in volume — even without any variability between cells. (Real cells also vary in division size.)
(b) "1 fL" is an order-of-magnitude figure, adequate for estimates but wrong by a factor of up to two or more in particular conditions. Calculations that convert copy numbers to concentrations should use the measured volume for the condition in question.
(c) Not necessarily. If the volume has also doubled, the concentration $n/(VN_\mathrm{A})$ is unchanged. Copy number and concentration must be distinguished: biochemical rates depend on concentration, while noise and the number of binding events depend on copy number.
:::
