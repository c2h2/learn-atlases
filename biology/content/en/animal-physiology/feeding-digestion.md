A blue whale lunges into a swarm of krill and, in a few seconds, engulfs a volume of water comparable to its own body, then strains it out through its baleen. A vampire bat can drink blood amounting to about half its body mass in under half an hour. A Burmese python swallows a meal a quarter of its own mass and, within two days, has doubled the size of its intestine to digest it. A cow, which cannot make a single enzyme that digests cellulose, lives on grass by keeping a fermentation vat of microbes in its stomach. Animals are heterotrophs: everything they are made of and every joule they spend, as counted in [[animal-physiology/energy-metabolism]], comes from food. How they get it, and how they turn it into molecules small enough to cross a cell membrane, varies more than almost any other aspect of their physiology.

This chapter surveys the ways animals feed, the organisation of digestive systems, the chemistry and transport of digestion and absorption, and the quantitative logic that links gut design to diet and body size. The membrane transporters that absorb nutrients are described in [[cell-biology/membrane-transport]], and the metabolic fate of absorbed fuels in [[biochemistry/metabolic-integration]].

## Ways of feeding

Animals are often classified by what they eat — herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, detritivores — but for physiology the more useful classification is by *how* food is obtained, because that determines what the gut must do.

::: definition Feeding mechanisms {#def-feeding}
- **Suspension feeding** (filter feeding): straining small particles or organisms from water, using cilia and mucus (sponges, bivalves, sea squirts), setae (barnacles, many crustaceans), gill rakers (some fishes) or baleen (baleen whales).
- **Deposit feeding**: ingesting sediment or soil and digesting the organic matter in it (earthworms, many polychaetes, sea cucumbers).
- **Fluid feeding**: sucking nutrient-rich fluids — nectar, plant sap, blood — usually with piercing mouthparts (aphids, mosquitoes, ticks, hummingbirds, vampire bats).
- **Bulk feeding**: ingesting large pieces of food, which are captured, torn or ground by jaws, teeth, beaks or radulae (most vertebrates, cephalopods, many insects).
- **Symbiotic nutrition**: obtaining nutrients from symbiotic microorganisms that live inside the animal's tissues, such as photosynthetic dinoflagellates in reef-building corals or chemoautotrophic bacteria in the trophosome of the hydrothermal-vent tubeworm *Riftia pachyptila*, which has no mouth or gut as an adult.
:::

Each mechanism has its own physical problems. A suspension feeder must move a large volume of water past its filter: a mussel a few centimetres long pumps a few litres of water an hour through its gills, and a population of bivalves can filter the whole volume of a shallow bay in days, clearing it of phytoplankton. A fluid feeder takes in a dilute food: nectar is mainly sugar and water, plant phloem sap is rich in sugar but poor in amino acids, and blood is rich in protein but poor in B vitamins. Both have to cope with excess water — vampire bats begin to excrete dilute urine within minutes of starting to feed, lightening themselves for take-off — and many rely on microbial symbionts to supply what the food lacks. Aphids depend on the bacterium *Buchnera aphidicola*, housed in specialised cells, to make the essential amino acids missing from phloem sap; tsetse flies depend on *Wigglesworthia* for B vitamins missing from blood.

## Digestive systems

**Digestion** is the breakdown of large food molecules (polysaccharides, proteins, fats, nucleic acids) into small ones (monosaccharides, amino acids and small peptides, fatty acids and monoacylglycerols, nucleosides) that can be absorbed. It is hydrolysis, catalysed by enzymes.

::: definition Intracellular and extracellular digestion; types of gut {#def-gut}
In **intracellular digestion** food particles are taken into cells by phagocytosis and digested inside lysosome-derived vacuoles (sponges; partly in cnidarians, flatworms and bivalves). In **extracellular digestion** enzymes are secreted into a cavity where food is broken down before absorption. A **gastrovascular cavity** (cnidarians, flatworms) has a single opening through which food enters and waste leaves. A **tubular gut** (through-gut), found in most bilaterian animals, has a mouth and an anus, so food moves in one direction through a series of specialised regions, typically: a region for ingestion and storage (mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, crop), one for mechanical and initial chemical processing (gizzard, stomach), one for digestion and absorption (small intestine in vertebrates, midgut in insects), and one for water and ion recovery and, in many herbivores, fermentation (large intestine, caecum, hindgut).
:::

A one-way tube has the great advantage of allowing different conditions in different regions. The vertebrate stomach secretes hydrochloric acid, reaching pH 1–2 in mammals, which denatures proteins and kills many microbes, and the protease pepsin, which works at that pH; the small intestine, after bicarbonate from the pancreas has neutralised the acid, provides the near-neutral conditions for pancreatic enzymes and the bile salts that emulsify fats. Food can be processed as a production line rather than in a single vat.

Not every vertebrate has an acid stomach. Many teleost fishes, including carp and their relatives, the lungfishes, the chimaeras and the monotremes (platypus and echidnas) have no gastric glands; the loss has happened independently many times, and genome studies show that the genes for pepsinogens and the gastric proton pump have been lost along with the stomach in several of these lineages.

### The chemistry of digestion

The enzymes of extracellular digestion are broadly similar across animals.

| food | enzymes (examples) | products |
|---|---|---|
| protein | endopeptidases (pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase) cut within chains; exopeptidases (carboxypeptidases, aminopeptidases) remove terminal residues | amino acids, di- and tripeptides |
| starch and glycogen | α-amylase; then brush-border maltase, isomaltase | glucose |
| disaccharides | brush-border sucrase, lactase, trehalase | monosaccharides |
| fat (triacylglycerols) | lipase, with bile salts or other emulsifiers | fatty acids, 2-monoacylglycerol |
| nucleic acids | nucleases, phosphatases | nucleosides, bases, phosphate |

Proteases are secreted as inactive precursors (zymogens), so that the gland that makes them is not digested: pepsinogen is activated by acid, and trypsinogen by enteropeptidase on the intestinal surface, after which trypsin activates the other pancreatic zymogens. Enzymes that act on disaccharides and short peptides are anchored in the membrane of the absorbing cells, the **brush border**, so that their products are released right next to the transporters that absorb them.

Expression of these enzymes tracks diet. Lactase, which digests the sugar of milk, is expressed in young mammals and declines after weaning in most species; persistence into adulthood in some human populations is a well-studied case of recent evolution ([[evolution/natural-selection]]). Cats, obligate carnivores, have low activities of some carbohydrases and cannot taste sweetness, because their gene for one subunit of the sweet-taste receptor is a pseudogene (Xia Li and colleagues, 2005). Hummingbirds and nectar-feeding bats have very high intestinal sucrase activities.

### Absorption

Absorption needs surface area. The vertebrate small intestine amplifies its area at three scales: circular folds of the wall, finger-like **villi** about a millimetre high, and **microvilli** about a micrometre long on each absorptive cell (enterocyte). Insect midgut cells have microvilli too, and many invertebrates use blind sacs (caeca) or a spiral valve, as in sharks and rays, to increase area in a short gut.

::: example How much surface does a gut need? {#ex-surface}
A small intestine is modelled as a smooth tube 3.0 m long and 2.5 cm in diameter. (a) What is its smooth inner surface area? (b) Folds, villi and microvilli multiply the area by factors of about 3, 10 and 20. What is the total? (c) Recent careful morphometry of human intestine (H. F. Helander and L. Fändriks, 2014) estimated the total mucosal area of the whole human gut, including microvilli, at about 30 m², much less than the 200–300 m² ("a tennis court") long quoted in textbooks. What does this tell you about such multiplication factors?
::: solution
(a) $\pi d L = \pi \times 0.025 \times 3.0 = 0.24$ m².

(b) $0.24 \times 3 \times 10 \times 20 = 0.24 \times 600 = 141$ m².

(c) The result depends entirely on the multipliers, each of which varies along the gut and between methods of measurement (fixation shrinks or swells tissue, and the microvillar factor depends on exactly how microvillus density and length are measured). Multiplying several uncertain factors compounds their errors, so a figure quoted to impress — a "tennis court" — can be several-fold too high. The principle is not in doubt: villi and microvilli increase the absorptive area by one to two orders of magnitude over a smooth tube.
:::
:::

Absorption itself is carried out by the transporters described in [[cell-biology/membrane-transport]]. Glucose and galactose enter enterocytes across the brush border with Na⁺ through the symporter SGLT1, which can move them against a large concentration gradient ([[cell-biology/membrane-transport#ex-sglt]]); fructose enters by facilitated diffusion through GLUT5; all three leave across the basolateral membrane through GLUT2. Amino acids enter through several Na⁺-coupled and other carriers, and di- and tripeptides through the H⁺-coupled peptide transporter PepT1. Fatty acids and monoacylglycerols, carried to the surface in bile-salt micelles, diffuse into enterocytes, are re-esterified to triacylglycerols, and leave packaged in lipoprotein particles (chylomicrons in mammals), which enter the lymph rather than the blood.

Some nutrients also pass **between** the cells, through the tight junctions, carried by water flow (paracellular absorption). In mammals this route is minor for glucose, but in small flying birds and bats it accounts for a large share — in some species most — of glucose absorption (Enrique Caviedes-Vidal and colleagues, 2007). Flying animals have shorter intestines than non-flying mammals of the same mass, which saves weight; a leaky intestine makes up for the smaller area, at the cost of also absorbing unwanted water-soluble compounds.

## The gut as a chemical reactor

Why are guts the length they are? Deborah Penry and Peter Jumars (1986, 1987) proposed that the answer can be found in chemical engineering, by treating guts as reactors. Engineers distinguish three ideal reactor types. A **batch reactor** is filled, allowed to react, and emptied: a gastrovascular cavity. A **continuous-flow stirred-tank reactor** (CSTR) is fed and emptied continuously while its contents are kept well mixed, so that the outflow has the composition of the whole tank: a rumen or a large caecum. A **plug-flow reactor** (PFR) is a tube through which material moves without mixing along its length, so that each parcel reacts for the same time: approximately a small intestine.

::: proposition Yield of a plug-flow and a stirred-tank reactor {#prop-reactors}
Suppose food is digested by first-order kinetics, with a rate constant $k$ (so that in isolation a fraction $\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$ remains undigested after time $t$), and that it passes through a reactor of volume $V$ at a volumetric flow rate $Q$, giving a mean **retention time** $\tau = V/Q$. At steady state the fraction of food digested by the time it leaves is

$$
X_\mathrm{PFR} = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-k\tau}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
X_\mathrm{CSTR} = \frac{k\tau}{1 + k\tau}.
$$ {#eq-reactors}

For the same $k$ and $\tau$, the plug-flow reactor always achieves the higher yield. Assumptions: first-order kinetics (the reaction rate is proportional to the amount of undigested substrate, as when enzyme or microbes are in excess); ideal flow (no mixing along a PFR, perfect mixing in a CSTR); and no absorption of the substrate before it is digested.
:::

::: proof
**Plug flow.** Each parcel spends exactly $\tau$ in the tube and reacts as in isolation, so the undigested fraction leaving is $\mathrm{e}^{-k\tau}$.

**Stirred tank.** Let $S_\mathrm{in}$ be the concentration of substrate in the inflow and $S$ in the tank, which by perfect mixing also equals that in the outflow. At steady state, substrate in = substrate out + substrate digested: $QS_\mathrm{in} = QS + kSV$. Dividing by $Q$ gives $S_\mathrm{in} = S(1 + k\tau)$, so the undigested fraction is $S/S_\mathrm{in} = 1/(1+k\tau)$ and the digested fraction $1 - 1/(1+k\tau) = k\tau/(1+k\tau)$.

**Comparison.** For $x = k\tau > 0$ we need $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-x} > x/(1+x)$, equivalently $\mathrm{e}^{-x} < 1/(1+x)$, i.e. $\mathrm{e}^{x} > 1 + x$, which holds for all $x > 0$.
:::

The stirred tank is worse because mixing sends some freshly arrived food straight to the outlet while other food stays much longer than needed. Why, then, do herbivores ferment in large, well-mixed chambers? Because a microbial fermentation is not a fixed enzyme reaction: the microbes themselves must be retained and must grow, and a well-mixed tank with continuous inflow keeps a stable population inoculating each new meal. Penry and Jumars argued that a CSTR followed by a PFR — a fermentation chamber followed by an absorptive tube — gives the best yield when the reaction is autocatalytic, as microbial digestion is. That is the layout of a ruminant (rumen, then small intestine) and, in reverse order, of a hindgut fermenter (small intestine, then caecum and colon), whose order has its own consequences, as we shall see.

::: example Choosing a reactor {#ex-reactor}
A substrate is digested with first-order rate constant $k = 0.20$ h⁻¹, and the gut section has a retention time of 10 h. (a) What fraction is digested in a plug-flow gut and in a stirred-tank gut? (b) What retention time would each need to digest 90 %? (c) If the stirred tank were divided into two equal, well-mixed compartments in series with the same total retention time, what fraction would be digested?
::: solution
(a) $k\tau = 2.0$. PFR: $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-2.0} = 0.865$. CSTR: $2.0/3.0 = 0.667$.

(b) PFR: $\mathrm{e}^{-k\tau} = 0.10$, so $\tau = \ln 10/0.20 = 11.5$ h. CSTR: $k\tau/(1+k\tau) = 0.90$, so $k\tau = 9$ and $\tau = 45$ h — four times as long, which would require a gut four times the volume at the same intake.

(c) Each compartment has $k\tau_i = 1.0$ and leaves a fraction $1/(1+1.0) = 0.5$ undigested; in series, $0.5^2 = 0.25$ remains and 0.75 is digested. Dividing a stirred tank into compartments in series brings it closer to plug flow (a long series of tiny tanks *is* a plug-flow reactor). The multi-chambered stomachs of ruminants and kangaroos, and the sacculated (haustrated) colons of horses, may work partly in this way.
:::
:::

## Herbivores and fermentation

Plant cell walls are made mainly of cellulose, hemicelluloses and lignin. Most animals cannot digest cellulose with their own enzymes: vertebrates have no cellulase genes, and in most invertebrates that digest wood or leaves the work is done partly or largely by symbiotic microbes. (Some invertebrates — termites, some crustaceans and molluscs, and some beetles — make cellulases of their own; Hirofumi Watanabe and colleagues showed in 1998 that a termite produces an endoglucanase in its salivary glands, but lower termites still depend heavily on flagellate protists in their hindgut.) Herbivorous vertebrates house dense populations of bacteria, archaea, protists and fungi in an enlarged, slow-moving region of the gut where fibre is fermented.

::: definition Foregut and hindgut fermentation {#def-fermentation}
In **foregut fermentation** the fermentation chamber lies before the acid stomach and small intestine: the rumen and reticulum of ruminants (cattle, sheep, deer, antelopes), the multi-chambered stomachs of camels, kangaroos, colobine monkeys and sloths, and, uniquely among birds, the enlarged crop of the hoatzin (*Opisthocomus hoazin*; Alejandro Grajal and colleagues, 1989). In **hindgut fermentation** the chamber lies after the small intestine, in an enlarged caecum or colon: horses, rhinoceroses, elephants, rabbits and many rodents, and many herbivorous lizards and birds. In both, microbes ferment carbohydrates anaerobically to **short-chain (volatile) fatty acids** — mainly acetate, propionate and butyrate — plus CO₂ and methane; the host absorbs the fatty acids and oxidises them as its main fuel.
:::

The ruminant arrangement has two advantages. Food is fermented before the host's own digestion, so the microbes, which grow on the fermented plant material and on non-protein nitrogen such as urea recycled from the host's blood, are themselves washed into the abomasum (the true, acid stomach) and small intestine and digested as high-quality protein. And ruminants regurgitate and re-chew the fibrous cud, reducing particle size and greatly accelerating fermentation; a sorting mechanism in the reticulum lets only finely ground particles leave, so coarse fibre is retained until it is digested. In cattle and sheep, short-chain fatty acids supply most of the animal's energy, about 70 % according to E. N. Bergman's review (1990), and glucose for the brain and for milk sugar must be made by gluconeogenesis from propionate in the liver. A cost of foregut fermentation is that the microbes ferment the easily digested sugars and proteins too, wasting energy and amino acids the host could have absorbed directly, and that some energy is lost as methane.

Hindgut fermenters digest easily available nutrients with their own enzymes first, then ferment the fibre that remains. They lose the microbial protein in their faeces — unless they eat it. Rabbits and many rodents practise **caecotrophy**: the caecum produces soft pellets of microbial material, which are eaten directly from the anus and digested, recovering protein and B vitamins. Horses do not, but they compensate with high intake: a horse processes fibrous food fast, extracting less from each kilogram but taking in more kilograms, and can thrive on forage too poor for a ruminant, whose slow, selective retention limits how much it can eat.

::: warning "Herbivores digest grass with their own enzymes" and "a longer gut always digests more"
No vertebrate makes a cellulase: the energy a cow gets from grass comes from its microbes, which ferment the cellulose and leave the cow their fermentation products. Without its microbial community a ruminant would starve on a diet of grass. A second misconception is that a longer gut or longer retention always means more nutrition. For a given gut volume, longer retention means slower throughput, so less food can be eaten; [[#prop-reactors]] shows that yield per unit food rises with retention time but with diminishing returns, while intake falls. The best retention time is a compromise that depends on the food: easily digested food is best processed fast, and a nectar-feeding bird holds its food for minutes, not days.
:::

### Body size and diet quality

Large herbivores eat poorer food than small ones. Elephants, rhinos and buffalo eat coarse grass and browse; small antelopes such as dik-diks pick out buds, fruits and young leaves; the smallest mammalian herbivores, voles and rabbits, are also selective or rely on caecotrophy. This pattern, the **Jarman–Bell principle** (after Peter Jarman's and Richard Bell's studies of African antelopes in the 1960s and 1970s), has an allometric explanation.

::: proposition Retention time and body size (Demment and Van Soest) {#prop-jarman-bell}
If the volume of the fermentation chamber scales isometrically with body mass, $V \propto M^{1.0}$, and the rate of food intake scales with metabolic requirement, $Q \propto M^{3/4}$ ([[animal-physiology/energy-metabolism]]), then the mean retention time scales as

$$
\tau = \frac{V}{Q} \propto M^{1/4}.
$$ {#eq-retention}

A larger herbivore therefore holds each unit of food longer, can ferment slowly digested fibre more completely, and can tolerate a lower-quality diet; a small herbivore must select food that can be digested quickly. This argument (M. W. Demment and P. J. Van Soest, 1985) rests on the empirical exponents, which vary between groups; measured gut capacities scale close to $M^{1.0}$, and measured retention times rise with body size, though more weakly than $M^{1/4}$ in some data sets.
:::

::: example Retention time across sizes {#ex-retention}
A 5 kg herbivore retains fibre in its gut for an average of 20 h. Using [[#eq-retention]], estimate the retention time of a geometrically similar 500 kg herbivore. If fibre is fermented with $k = 0.05$ h⁻¹ in a well-mixed chamber, what fraction does each digest?
::: solution
Retention scales by $(500/5)^{1/4} = 100^{1/4} = 3.16$, so $\tau \approx 63$ h. Using the CSTR result of [[#eq-reactors]]: small, $k\tau = 1.0$, $X = 0.50$; large, $k\tau = 3.16$, $X = 3.16/4.16 = 0.76$. The large animal extracts half as much again from the same fibre, which is why it can live on food the small one cannot.
:::
:::

## Budgets, efficiencies and the cost of digestion

Not all the energy in food becomes available to the animal. Physiologists divide it as follows.

::: definition The energy budget of feeding {#def-energy-budget}
Of the **gross energy** $I$ ingested, part is lost in faeces ($F$), so that $I - F$ is **digestible** (or assimilated) **energy**; the ratio $(I - F)/I$ is the **apparent digestibility**, or assimilation efficiency. Of the digestible energy, part is lost in urine ($U$, as urea, uric acid and other nitrogenous waste) and, in fermenting animals, as combustible gases ($G$, mainly methane), leaving the **metabolisable energy** $I - F - U - G$. Metabolisable energy is spent on maintenance (including the heat increment of feeding), activity, thermoregulation, growth and reproduction.
:::

Assimilation efficiencies of carnivores eating meat are commonly 80–95 %; of herbivores, from about 30 % on poor forage to 80 % on seeds or fruit; of detritivores, often lower still. Digestion itself costs energy: after a meal metabolic rate rises for hours or days, the **specific dynamic action** (SDA) or heat increment of feeding, which reflects the costs of digestion, absorption and above all of synthesising proteins and storing nutrients. SDA is commonly 10–30 % of the energy of a meal and is highest for protein.

::: example A sheep's energy budget {#ex-budget}
A sheep eats hay containing 400 MJ of gross energy over a week. Its faeces contain 160 MJ, its urine 15 MJ, and it belches methane containing 24 MJ. (a) Calculate its apparent digestibility and its metabolisable energy as a fraction of gross energy. (b) Which loss would you target to improve the efficiency of feeding, and how?
::: solution
(a) Digestible energy $= 400 - 160 = 240$ MJ; apparent digestibility $= 240/400 = 0.60$. Metabolisable energy $= 240 - 15 - 24 = 201$ MJ, or $0.50$ of gross energy. (b) The largest loss is in the faeces, as undigested fibre; better forage (younger grass with less lignin), grinding or chemical treatment of straw, or longer retention would increase digestibility. Methane, 6 % of gross energy here (typical values are a few to about 10 %), is both an energy loss and a greenhouse gas; feed additives that inhibit methanogenic archaea in the rumen are being tested for that reason. (Illustrative values typical of a sheep on moderate-quality hay.)
:::
:::

### A python's meal

The most dramatic responses to feeding are in animals that eat rarely. Stephen Secor and Jared Diamond (from 1995) found that Burmese pythons (*Python bivittatus*, then *P. molurus bivittatus*) that had fasted for a month, then ate a rodent of a quarter of their body mass, increased their metabolic rate by an order of magnitude or more for several days. The small intestine, kept small and inactive during fasting, roughly doubled in mass within a day or two, its enterocytes swelling and its nutrient transporters increasing many-fold; the heart, liver and kidneys grew too. After digestion everything shrank back. Downregulating the gut between meals saves the considerable cost of maintaining it, which in a frequently feeding mammal is a substantial fraction of resting metabolism; snakes that feed frequently show much smaller responses.

::: example The cost of a python's meal {#ex-sda}
A 1.0 kg python eats a 250 g rat containing about 7 kJ per gram. Over the following 10 days its extra O₂ consumption above the fasting rate totals 23 L. Estimate the SDA as a fraction of the meal's energy (20.1 kJ L⁻¹ O₂).
::: solution
Meal energy $= 250 \times 7 = 1750$ kJ. SDA $= 23 \times 20.1 = 462$ kJ, which is $462/1750 = 0.26$ of the meal. About a quarter of the meal pays for its own digestion and assimilation. (Illustrative figures of the order measured for large meals in pythons; SDA coefficients for snakes are commonly 20–35 %.)
:::
:::

## Control of digestion

Digestion is coordinated by nerves and hormones. The enteric nervous system, a network of neurons in the gut wall, generates the waves of contraction (peristalsis) that move food along and the mixing movements that churn it, and it can do so even when cut off from the brain. Hormones released by endocrine cells in the gut lining link one region to the next. In mammals, **gastrin** from the stomach stimulates acid secretion; **secretin**, released by the small intestine when acid arrives, stimulates the pancreas to secrete bicarbonate; **cholecystokinin** (CCK), released in response to fat and protein, stimulates release of pancreatic enzymes and contraction of the gall bladder, and slows emptying of the stomach. Secretin was the first hormone to be discovered, by William Bayliss and Ernest Starling in 1902 ([[animal-physiology/endocrine-control]]). Similar peptides regulate feeding and digestion in invertebrates; insect midguts, for example, have endocrine cells that secrete peptides related to the vertebrate tachykinins and neuropeptide F.

::: quiz
A hindgut-fermenting herbivore and a ruminant of the same size eat the same grass. Which statement is most likely to be correct?
- [ ] The hindgut fermenter digests more of the fibre in each kilogram of grass.
- [x] The ruminant digests more of the fibre in each kilogram, but the hindgut fermenter can eat more kilograms per day.
- [ ] Both digest cellulose with cellulases made by their own intestinal cells.
- [ ] The ruminant cannot use microbial protein, because the microbes are downstream of the small intestine.
::: solution
The ruminant retains coarse fibre selectively in its rumen and re-chews it, so it extracts more from each kilogram, but its slow throughput limits intake. The hindgut fermenter processes food faster, extracting less per kilogram but eating more. Neither makes its own cellulases. It is the hindgut fermenter whose microbes lie downstream of the small intestine, which is why rabbits eat their caecotrophs.
:::
:::

::: application Feeding livestock and feeding the planet
Ruminant nutrition is one of the oldest applications of digestive physiology. Feed formulation balances the energy and protein needs of the animal with those of its rumen microbes; non-protein nitrogen such as urea can be added to poor forage because the microbes turn it into protein. Enteric methane from ruminants is a significant part of agricultural greenhouse-gas emissions, and reducing it — by diet, by additives that inhibit methanogens, and by breeding — is an active area of research. The human gastrointestinal system and its disorders are treated in [Medicine Atlas: gastrointestinal](/learn/medicine/course.php?c=gastrointestinal).
:::

::: history
René Réaumur (1752) had a pet kite swallow perforated metal tubes containing meat and recovered them partly digested, and Lazzaro Spallanzani (1780) repeated the experiments with many animals and himself, showing that digestion is chemical, not just grinding or putrefaction. William Beaumont observed digestion through the gastric fistula of the trapper Alexis St Martin from 1822 and published his observations in 1833. Theodor Schwann described pepsin in 1836. Ivan Pavlov's studies of the nervous control of digestive secretion in dogs earned the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1904, and Bayliss and Starling's discovery of secretin in 1902 founded endocrinology. The study of rumen microbiology began in earnest with Robert Hungate's anaerobic culture methods in the 1940s and 1950s. Penry and Jumars applied chemical-reactor theory to guts in 1986–87, and Secor and Diamond's work on pythons in the 1990s made gut plasticity a central topic of digestive physiology.
:::

## Where this leads

Absorbed nutrients are carried by the circulation ([[animal-physiology/circulation]]); their nitrogen ends up in excretory products ([[animal-physiology/water-salt-balance]]); and their storage and mobilisation are controlled by insulin, glucagon and other hormones ([[animal-physiology/endocrine-control]]). The ecology of foraging, how animals decide what and where to eat, is in [[behaviour/foraging]], and the microbial communities of the gut in [[microbiology/microbes-and-hosts]].

::: summary
- Animals feed by suspension, deposit, fluid and bulk feeding, or through symbionts; fluid feeders often depend on microbes for nutrients missing from their food.
- Digestion is enzymatic hydrolysis, intracellular or extracellular; a one-way gut allows specialised regions (acid stomach, neutral intestine, fermentation chamber).
- Proteases are secreted as zymogens; brush-border enzymes finish digestion next to the transporters (SGLT1, GLUT5, GLUT2, PepT1); fats are absorbed via micelles and exported in lipoproteins. Flying vertebrates rely heavily on paracellular absorption.
- Treated as reactors, a plug-flow gut digests more than a stirred tank at the same retention time, $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-k\tau}$ against $k\tau/(1+k\tau)$ ([[#prop-reactors]]); fermentation chambers are stirred tanks followed by plug-flow intestines.
- No vertebrate makes cellulase: foregut and hindgut fermenters rely on microbes and absorb short-chain fatty acids; ruminants also digest their microbes, rabbits eat caecotrophs.
- Retention time scales roughly as $M^{1/4}$, so large herbivores tolerate poorer food (the Jarman–Bell principle).
- Gross, digestible and metabolisable energy are separated by losses in faeces, urine and methane; specific dynamic action costs 10–30 % of a meal.
- Infrequent feeders such as pythons shrink and regrow their guts between meals.
:::

::: solution
$(500 - 75)/500 = 0.85$.

::: exercise Retention time {level=1 check="8"}
A gut compartment holds 2.0 L of digesta, and digesta flow through it at 0.25 L h⁻¹. What is the mean retention time in hours?
::: solution
$\tau = V/Q = 2.0/0.25 = 8$ h.

::: exercise Matching guts to diets {level=1}
For each animal, say whether you would expect a foregut fermenter, a hindgut fermenter, or neither, and give one physiological reason: (a) a lion; (b) a horse; (c) a kangaroo; (d) a hummingbird; (e) a rabbit.
::: solution
(a) Neither: meat is easily digested by the animal's own enzymes, and a lion's gut is short and simple. (b) Hindgut (caecum and colon): it digests sugars, starch and protein first and ferments fibre afterwards, relying on high intake. (c) Foregut: kangaroos have a large, tubular, multi-chambered stomach in which fibre is fermented, convergent with ruminants though they do not ruminate in the same way. (d) Neither: nectar sugars are absorbed rapidly in the small intestine, with short retention times. (e) Hindgut, with caecotrophy, which recovers microbial protein and vitamins that would otherwise be lost.
:::

::: exercise Plug flow to 95 % {level=2 check="15"}
In a plug-flow gut, a substrate is digested with first-order rate constant $k = 0.20$ h⁻¹. What retention time (to the nearest hour) is needed to digest 95 % of it?
::: solution
$\mathrm{e}^{-k\tau} = 0.05$, so $\tau = \ln 20/0.20 = 3.00/0.20 = 15$ h.

::: exercise Scaling of gut capacity {level=2}
Gut capacity of herbivorous mammals scales approximately as $M^{1.0}$, metabolic rate as $M^{0.75}$. (a) Show that the ratio of gut capacity to daily energy need scales as $M^{0.25}$. (b) A 20 kg and a 2000 kg herbivore eat food of the same quality. How many times longer can the larger animal retain the food, if intake matches energy need? (c) What other options does the small herbivore have if it must eat poor food?
::: solution
(a) $M^{1.0}/M^{0.75} = M^{0.25}$. (b) $(2000/20)^{0.25} = 100^{0.25} = 3.2$ times longer. (c) It can select the most digestible parts of plants (buds, seeds, young leaves, fruit); re-ingest its microbial products (caecotrophy); detoxify or avoid plant defences that larger animals can dilute; or switch to a mixed diet including insects. It may also reduce its metabolic rate, lowering its requirement.
:::

::: exercise Why acid? {level=2}
Explain three functions of the acidic stomach of mammals and suggest why it has been lost so many times in teleost fishes and in monotremes. What physiological consequences would you predict for an agastric fish?
::: solution
Functions: (1) acid denatures proteins, unfolding them for proteases; (2) it activates pepsinogen to pepsin, which works best at low pH; (3) it kills or inhibits many ingested microbes, acting as a barrier against infection; it also helps release minerals such as calcium and iron from food. The losses are not fully explained; suggestions include diets in which acid digestion gives little advantage (small invertebrates, plant material, or food with high buffering capacity such as shells), the high cost of making acid (it requires the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase and must be neutralised again with bicarbonate), and chance loss of the genes in lineages where the stomach was little used. An agastric fish would rely entirely on pancreatic and intestinal proteases at neutral pH, digest protein more slowly at the start of the gut, and depend on other defences against pathogens in food.
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a feeding experiment {level=2}
Two groups of mice are fed for three weeks either a low-fibre or a high-fibre diet of the same energy content per gram. The high-fibre mice eat 30 % more food and, at the end, their small intestine and caecum are heavier. Explain both findings and predict what happens if the high-fibre mice are then returned to the low-fibre diet.
::: solution
High-fibre food has lower digestibility, so the mice must eat more to obtain the same metabolisable energy; intake increases to compensate (compensatory feeding). Processing more food requires more absorptive and fermentative capacity, so the gut grows — a reversible acclimation (gut phenotypic plasticity), similar in kind to, though slower and smaller than, the python's response. If they are returned to the low-fibre diet, intake should fall to its original level over days, and the gut should shrink back over days to weeks, because maintaining excess gut tissue is costly.
:::

::: exercise Two compartments in series {level=3}
(a) Show that $n$ equal, well-mixed compartments in series, with total retention time $\tau$ and first-order rate constant $k$, digest a fraction $X_n = 1 - (1 + k\tau/n)^{-n}$. (b) Calculate $X_n$ for $k\tau = 2$ and $n = 1, 2, 5, 50$. (c) What is the limit as $n \to \infty$, and what does it mean?
::: hint
Apply the stirred-tank result of [[#prop-reactors]] to each compartment in turn.
:::
::: solution
(a) Each compartment has retention time $\tau/n$ and passes on a fraction $1/(1 + k\tau/n)$ of the substrate it receives undigested. In series the undigested fractions multiply: $(1 + k\tau/n)^{-n}$, so $X_n = 1 - (1 + k\tau/n)^{-n}$. (b) $n = 1$: $1 - 1/3 = 0.667$; $n = 2$: $1 - 1/4 = 0.750$; $n = 5$: $1 - 1.4^{-5} = 1 - 0.186 = 0.814$; $n = 50$: $1 - 1.04^{-50} = 1 - 0.141 = 0.859$. (c) Since $(1 + x/n)^{n} \to \mathrm{e}^{x}$, $X_n \to 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-k\tau} = 0.865$, the plug-flow result. A tube with no mixing is the limit of infinitely many tiny stirred tanks in series, which is how chemical engineers model real tubular reactors with some mixing.
:::

::: exercise A vampire's problem {level=3}
A 30 g common vampire bat (*Desmodus rotundus*) drinks 15 g of blood in one meal. Blood is about 80 % water, and its dry matter is mostly protein. (a) Why must the bat begin excreting water while still feeding? (b) What problem does the protein-rich diet pose for its excretory system? (c) Why might blood feeding be difficult to sustain on a fluctuating food supply? (Vampire bats can starve to death within about two to three days without a meal.)
::: solution
(a) The meal adds half its body mass; the bat must be able to fly away from the host, so it rapidly absorbs water from the stomach and intestine and produces copious dilute urine within minutes, shedding much of the plasma water. (b) The protein is broken down for energy, producing a large amount of nitrogen to excrete as urea, which requires concentrating the urine; so after the initial dilute diuresis the bat switches to producing very concentrated urine, conserving water while eliminating urea — its kidneys must handle both extremes ([[animal-physiology/water-salt-balance]]). (c) Blood contains little fat or carbohydrate to store, and a small endotherm has small reserves and a high mass-specific metabolic rate ([[animal-physiology/energy-metabolism]]), so a bat that fails to feed for a couple of nights is in danger; field studies show that well-fed vampire bats often share regurgitated blood with roost-mates that failed to feed, a much-studied example of reciprocal cooperation ([[behaviour/cooperation]]).
:::
