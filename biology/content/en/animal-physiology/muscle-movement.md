A midge beats its wings about a thousand times a second, faster than any nerve can fire. A cheetah accelerates from rest to over 20 m s⁻¹ in a few seconds. A scallop holds its shell clamped shut for hours with a muscle that uses almost no energy, and a flea leaps a hundred times its own length in under a millisecond, faster than any muscle could contract. All of these movements are produced by the same basic machine: myosin motors pulling on actin filaments, powered by ATP. What differs is how the machine is switched on and off, how its parts are arranged, and how it is coupled to springs, levers and the environment.

The molecular mechanism — the sliding of thick and thin filaments, the cross-bridge cycle and its regulation by Ca²⁺ through troponin and tropomyosin — is set out in [[cell-biology/cytoskeleton]]. This chapter builds on it to compare the kinds of muscle animals have, to analyse how muscles produce force, work and power, and to see how body size and the physics of walking, running and jumping shape locomotion. The energetic cost of moving was introduced in [[animal-physiology/energy-metabolism#def-cot]].

## Three kinds of muscle

Vertebrates have three kinds of muscle, which differ in structure, in how contraction is triggered and in how it is controlled.

::: definition Skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle {#def-muscle-types}
- **Skeletal muscle** is made of long, multinucleate fibres formed by fusion of many cells; its sarcomeres are aligned in register, giving a **striated** appearance. Each fibre is activated by a motor neuron, and contraction is under voluntary control in vertebrates.
- **Cardiac muscle** is also striated, but is made of individual, branched cells joined end to end by intercalated discs containing gap junctions, so that electrical activity spreads from cell to cell; contraction is initiated by pacemaker cells within the heart ([[animal-physiology/circulation]]).
- **Smooth muscle** consists of small, spindle-shaped single cells without sarcomeres; actin and myosin filaments are arranged obliquely and anchored to dense bodies. It lines blood vessels, the gut, airways, the bladder and the uterus, contracts slowly and can maintain tension for long periods with little energy.
:::

The same three kinds are not universal across animals. Many invertebrates have **obliquely striated** muscle, in which the sarcomeres are staggered in a helix (in nematodes, annelids and the mantle of squid), and molluscs and arthropods show regulatory and mechanical specialisations, discussed below, that have no counterpart in vertebrates.

## Excitation–contraction coupling

In all muscle, contraction is triggered by a rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ concentration, from about 0.1 µmol L⁻¹ at rest to about 1–10 µmol L⁻¹. The steps that link an electrical signal at the cell membrane to that rise — **excitation–contraction coupling** — differ between muscle types.

**Skeletal muscle.** An action potential from the motor neuron spreads over the fibre membrane and down the **transverse tubules** (T-tubules), invaginations that carry it deep into the fibre. In the T-tubule membrane, voltage-sensing **dihydropyridine receptors** (DHPRs, L-type Ca²⁺ channels, Ca_V1.1) change conformation when the membrane depolarises. They are physically coupled to **ryanodine receptors** (RyR1), Ca²⁺ release channels in the membrane of the adjacent sarcoplasmic reticulum, the fibre's store of Ca²⁺ ([[cell-biology/organelles]]). The conformational change opens RyR1 directly, releasing Ca²⁺ into the cytosol. Remarkably, vertebrate skeletal muscle can contract normally without any Ca²⁺ entering from outside the cell: the DHPR acts as a voltage sensor, not as a channel. Martin Schneider and Knox Chandler (1973) detected the tiny charge movement produced by these sensors in frog muscle, and later structural work showed DHPRs arranged in groups of four facing alternate RyR1 tetramers, as the mechanical-coupling model requires.

**Cardiac muscle.** Here DHPRs (Ca_V1.2) do let Ca²⁺ enter during the action potential, and this Ca²⁺ opens nearby ryanodine receptors (RyR2) — **calcium-induced calcium release**, characterised by Alexandre Fabiato in the 1970s and early 1980s. Heart muscle therefore depends on extracellular Ca²⁺, as Sydney Ringer found in 1883 when frog hearts stopped beating in saline made up without it. The amount of Ca²⁺ released, and hence the force of the beat, can be modulated by hormones and nerves, which is part of how the heart adjusts its output.

**Smooth muscle.** Ca²⁺ enters through membrane channels or is released from internal stores in response to electrical, hormonal or stretch signals. It binds **calmodulin**, which activates **myosin light-chain kinase**; phosphorylation of the regulatory light chain of myosin then allows myosin to interact with actin. Smooth muscle is thus regulated on the *thick* filament, not the thin filament. When light chains are dephosphorylated while still attached, cross-bridges detach very slowly, so tension is held with little ATP use — the **latch state**, which lets the walls of arteries maintain tone around the clock.

Relaxation in all muscle requires Ca²⁺ to be removed, mainly by the sarco-endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) pumping it back into the store, which consumes a substantial part of the ATP used by contracting muscle. In fast fish and frog muscles, the soluble Ca²⁺-binding protein **parvalbumin** speeds relaxation by mopping up Ca²⁺ quickly.

### Invertebrate variations

- **Myosin-linked regulation.** In scallops and other molluscs, Ca²⁺ binds directly to the essential light chain of myosin and switches it on, as Andrew Szent-Györgyi and colleagues showed in the 1970s; many invertebrates use both thin- and thick-filament regulation.
- **Catch muscle.** The adductor muscles of bivalves can hold their shells closed for hours or days in a **catch** state, maintaining force at an energy cost far below that of normal contraction. Catch is entered after a contraction and released by serotonin, which acts through cAMP and protein kinase A to phosphorylate the giant protein twitchin; how dephosphorylated twitchin holds the filaments together is still being worked out.
- **Asynchronous flight muscle.** In flies, bees, beetles and true bugs, the flight muscles contract many times for each nerve impulse. J. W. S. Pringle showed in 1949 that their contractions are triggered by stretch: when one set of muscles shortens, it stretches the antagonistic set, which then contracts after a short delay, stretching the first set again. The thorax and wings form a resonant mechanical system, Ca²⁺ stays elevated throughout flight, and the frequency is set by the mechanics, not the nervous system. This allows wingbeat frequencies of several hundred per second in flies and bees and about 1000 per second in the smallest midges.

## From twitch to tetanus

A single action potential in a skeletal muscle fibre produces a brief contraction, a **twitch**. If a second action potential arrives before the muscle has relaxed, the second twitch adds to the first (**summation**), because more Ca²⁺ is released while the contractile machinery is still active and the elastic elements in series with the filaments are already stretched. At high stimulation frequencies the twitches fuse into a smooth, maximal contraction, a **tetanus**.

Muscles are controlled through **motor units**: a motor neuron and all the fibres it innervates, which contract together. Fine muscles of the eye have motor units of a few fibres; large leg muscles have units of hundreds or thousands. The nervous system grades force in two ways: by recruiting more motor units, which is done in order of size, from small to large (the **size principle**, Elwood Henneman, 1957), and by increasing the firing rate of each.

## Force, length and velocity

### Force and cross-sectional area

The force a muscle can produce is proportional to the number of sarcomeres working side by side, and so to its **physiological cross-sectional area** (the area perpendicular to the fibres). The maximal isometric force per unit area — the **specific tension** — is similar in vertebrate skeletal muscles of all sizes, roughly 200–300 kPa (20–30 N cm⁻²). Muscle length, by contrast, determines how far and how fast it can shorten: each sarcomere shortens by a similar amount and at a similar speed, so a muscle twice as long, with twice as many sarcomeres in series, shortens twice as far and twice as fast. Many muscles are **pennate**, with fibres attached at an angle to the tendon, which packs more fibres into the same volume — more force, less shortening.

The relation between force and sarcomere length — maximal over a plateau of filament overlap, falling at long and short lengths — is analysed in [[cell-biology/cytoskeleton#exr-length-tension]]. It is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the sliding-filament theory, and it explains the Frank–Starling relation of the heart ([[animal-physiology/circulation]]).

### The force–velocity relation

A muscle that shortens produces less force the faster it shortens; at its maximum velocity it produces none, and when held still it produces its maximal isometric force. A. V. Hill measured this relation precisely in frog sartorius muscle in 1938, with a lever that let the muscle lift loads of different sizes, and found that the data fitted a rectangular hyperbola.

::: proposition Hill's force–velocity equation (empirical) {#prop-hill-muscle}
For a fully activated muscle shortening at a steady velocity $v$ against a force $F$,

$$
(F + a)(v + b) = (F_0 + a)\,b,
$$ {#eq-hill-muscle}

where $F_0$ is the maximal isometric force and $a$ and $b$ are constants with the dimensions of force and velocity. Equivalently

$$
v = b\,\frac{F_0 - F}{F + a},
\qquad
v_\mathrm{max} = \frac{bF_0}{a},
$$ {#eq-hill-velocity}

so the curve is determined by $F_0$, $v_\mathrm{max}$ and the curvature $a/F_0$, which is typically 0.15–0.4 (0.25 is a common value for vertebrate skeletal muscle). The equation describes steady shortening of maximally activated muscle; it does not apply to lengthening (where force rises above $F_0$), and it is an empirical fit, although A. F. Huxley's cross-bridge model (1957) explained its shape from the rates of attachment and detachment of myosin heads.
:::

The **power** a muscle delivers is $P = Fv$. It is zero in an isometric contraction ($v = 0$) and at maximum velocity ($F = 0$), and has a maximum in between.

::: example Optimal load and maximal power {#ex-power}
A muscle has $a/F_0 = 0.25$. (a) Write its velocity as a fraction of $v_\mathrm{max}$, as a function of $f = F/F_0$. (b) At what fraction of $F_0$ is power maximal, and what is the maximal power as a fraction of $F_0 v_\mathrm{max}$? (c) What is the velocity at half the isometric force?
::: solution
(a) From [[#eq-hill-velocity]] with $k = a/F_0$ and $v_\mathrm{max} = bF_0/a = b/k$: $v/v_\mathrm{max} = k(1 - f)/(f + k) = (1 - f)/(1 + f/k)$.

(b) Power $p = f(1 - f)/(1 + f/k)$. Setting $\deriv{p}{f} = 0$ gives $f^2 + 2kf - k = 0$, so $f^* = \sqrt{k^2 + k} - k = \sqrt{0.3125} - 0.25 = 0.309$. Then $v/v_\mathrm{max} = 0.691/(1 + 1.236) = 0.309$ and $p_\mathrm{max} = 0.309 \times 0.309 = 0.095$. Maximal power, about a tenth of $F_0 v_\mathrm{max}$, is reached at about 31 % of maximal force and 31 % of maximal velocity. (The two fractions are equal because Hill's hyperbola is symmetric about the line $f = v/v_\mathrm{max}$.)

(c) $v/v_\mathrm{max} = 0.5/(1 + 2) = 0.17$.
:::
:::

### Matching muscle to task

Because power and efficiency peak at intermediate velocities, a muscle works best when it shortens at about a third of its maximum velocity. Animals achieve this by having different kinds of muscle fibre for different tasks.

| fibre type (mammals) | myosin isoform | speed | metabolism | fatigue | typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| slow oxidative (type I) | slow MyHC | slow | oxidative, many mitochondria, much myoglobin (red) | resistant | posture, sustained activity |
| fast oxidative–glycolytic (type IIa) | fast MyHC-2A | fast | oxidative and glycolytic | moderately resistant | sustained running |
| fast glycolytic (type IIx, IIb) | fast MyHC-2X, -2B | fastest | glycolytic, few mitochondria (white) | fatigues quickly | sprints and bursts |

Fishes show the division most clearly: a thin layer of red, slow, oxidative muscle along the side of the body powers steady cruising, while the bulk of the myotome, white, fast, glycolytic muscle, is used only for bursts. Lawrence Rome and colleagues (1988) showed in carp that at each swimming speed the muscle fibres in use shorten at velocities close to those that maximise power and efficiency — about 0.17–0.36 of $v_\mathrm{max}$ — and that the fish switches from red to white muscle at the speed at which red muscle would have to shorten too fast. A fish has different fibre types, the authors argued, for the same reason a car has gears.

Some muscles are specialised for extreme speed. The sound-producing muscles of the toadfish swim bladder, the shaker muscle of the rattlesnake's tail (about 90 contractions per second for hours) and the vocal muscles that bats use to produce echolocation calls (up to about 200 per second; C. P. H. Elemans and colleagues, 2011) achieve their speed with very fast Ca²⁺ cycling — abundant sarcoplasmic reticulum and SERCA — and fast cross-bridge kinetics, at the price of producing very little force.

::: warning "Muscles push as well as pull" and "contraction shortens the filaments"
Muscles can only pull. A muscle develops tension by shortening (or resisting stretch); it cannot actively lengthen. Every movement that needs to be reversed is produced by antagonistic pairs (biceps and triceps), by an elastic antagonist (the hinge ligament that opens a bivalve shell), or by a hydrostatic skeleton in which contraction of one muscle layer squeezes fluid to lengthen another region (the circular and longitudinal muscles of an earthworm, or the haemolymph pressure that extends spiders' legs, [[animal-physiology/circulation]]). And during contraction the actin and myosin filaments do not shorten; they slide past each other, as the constant width of the A band showed in 1954.
:::

## Size, strength and jumping

### Strength and scale

Consider animals of the same shape but different size, with linear dimension $L$. Muscle force is proportional to cross-sectional area, $L^2$, but body weight is proportional to volume, $L^3$. Relative strength, force per unit weight, therefore falls as $L^{-1}$, or $M^{-1/3}$. An ant can carry many times its own weight, and a grasshopper scaled up to the size of a horse would be crushed by its own weight. Large animals compensate by changing shape and posture: elephants stand with straight, column-like legs, which lowers the muscle force needed to support the body, and the skeletons of large mammals are proportionally thicker. Andrew Biewener (1989) showed that across mammals from chipmunks to horses, a more upright limb posture in larger animals keeps the peak stresses in bones and muscles roughly constant with body size.

::: example Muscle force and body weight {#ex-strength}
A muscle with a physiological cross-sectional area of 10 cm² has a specific tension of 25 N cm⁻². (a) What force can it produce? (b) A geometrically similar animal is 10 times longer. By what factors are its muscle force and body weight multiplied, and how does its relative strength compare?
::: solution
(a) $10 \times 25 = 250$ N, enough to support about 25 kg against gravity.

(b) Force scales with area, $10^2 = 100$; weight with volume, $10^3 = 1000$. Relative strength is $100/1000 = 0.1$ of the smaller animal's. For the same muscle mass fraction, the larger animal can support only a tenth as many multiples of its own weight.
:::
:::

### Jumping

Remarkably, small and large animals of similar build can jump to roughly the same height. Giovanni Borelli made the argument in *De Motu Animalium* (1680), and A. V. Hill restated it in 1950.

::: proposition Jumping height and body size {#prop-jump}
Suppose the work a muscle can do in one contraction is proportional to its mass (force ∝ cross-section times distance shortened ∝ length, so work ∝ volume), and the jumping muscles are a fixed fraction of body mass. A jump that converts this work $W$ into potential energy reaches a height $h$ given by $W = Mgh$. Since $W \propto M$,

$$
h = \frac{W}{Mg} = \frac{w\,\phi}{g},
$$ {#eq-jump}

independent of body size, where $w$ is the work per kilogram of muscle and $\phi$ the fraction of body mass in jumping muscles. Assumptions: geometric similarity, the same work per unit mass of muscle, and negligible air resistance — which fails for very small animals, and the assumption that muscles can deliver their work in the time available fails too, as the example shows.
:::

::: example How does a flea jump? {#ex-flea}
(a) A muscle can do about 100 J per kilogram in one contraction, and an animal's jumping muscles make up 10 % of its mass. What height does [[#eq-jump]] predict? (b) A flea jumps about 10 cm high, accelerating over a distance of about 0.5 mm. Calculate its take-off speed, the time over which it accelerates, and the power per kilogram of body mass required. (c) The maximum power output of muscle is a few hundred watts per kilogram of muscle. What does the comparison imply?
::: solution
(a) $h = 100 \times 0.10/9.81 = 1.0$ m. Bushbabies and some frogs, with larger jumping muscles and elastic help, jump about twice this.

(b) Take-off speed $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.10} = 1.40$ m s⁻¹. With uniform acceleration over $d = 0.5$ mm, the time is $t = 2d/v = 0.71$ ms. Power per kilogram of body $= \tfrac12 v^2 / t = 0.98/0.00071 = 1400$ W kg⁻¹.

(c) Even if all of the flea's body were muscle, it could not deliver 1400 W kg⁻¹ — and no muscle can contract and relax within a fraction of a millisecond. The flea must store energy slowly beforehand and release it suddenly, like a catapult. Henry Bennet-Clark and E. C. A. Lucey (1967) showed that fleas compress a pad of the rubber-like protein **resilin** in the thorax with their muscles and hold it with a catch; when the catch releases, the pad recoils and drives the legs. Locusts, froghoppers and many small jumpers use similar catapults. The size-independence of jump height is limited for small animals by power, not work.
:::
:::

## Walking and running

### Two mechanisms of terrestrial locomotion

Giovanni Cavagna, Norman Heglund and Richard Taylor (1977), measuring the forces under the feet of animals moving across force platforms, showed that walking and running save energy in two different ways.

- In **walking**, the body vaults over a stiff leg like an **inverted pendulum**: it rises as it slows down over the supporting foot and falls as it speeds up again, so kinetic and gravitational potential energy are exchanged twice in each stride, recovering up to about 60–70 % of the mechanical energy at the optimal walking speed.
- In **running**, trotting and hopping, the body bounces on legs that behave like **springs**: at mid-stance the body is at its lowest and slowest, and its kinetic and potential energy are both stored as elastic strain in stretched tendons and ligaments, to be returned in the second half of the stance. The Achilles tendon of a running human and the long tendons in the hind legs of kangaroos and horses are the main springs. Energy stored elastically does not have to be provided by muscle work, which is why kangaroos can hop faster at almost no extra metabolic cost ([[animal-physiology/energy-metabolism]]).

### Dynamic similarity and the Froude number

Animals of different sizes move in similar ways when they move at the same **Froude number**, the ratio of inertial to gravitational forces.

::: proposition Froude number and the walk–run transition {#prop-froude}
For an animal of leg length (hip height) $L$ moving at speed $v$, the **Froude number** is

$$
\mathrm{Fr} = \frac{v^2}{gL}.
$$ {#eq-froude}

(a) An animal walking as an inverted pendulum cannot exceed $\mathrm{Fr} = 1$: its body moves on an arc of radius $L$ over the foot, which requires a centripetal acceleration $v^2/L$ supplied by gravity, so $v^2/L \le g$. (b) Empirically, animals from small mammals and birds to horses and humans switch from walking to running at $\mathrm{Fr} \approx 0.5$, and quadrupeds and bipeds moving at the same Froude number use similar gaits, duty factors and relative stride lengths — **dynamic similarity** (R. McNeill Alexander, 1970s and 1980s). Part (a) is a mechanical limit assuming a stiff leg and a point mass; part (b) is an empirical regularity.
:::

::: proof
For (a): during the stance of a walking step, the body's centre of mass moves on an arc of radius $L$ about the foot. At the top of the arc, its acceleration towards the foot is $v^2/L$. The only downward force available is gravity, $Mg$ (the leg can push but not pull the body down towards the ground), so the motion is possible only if $Mv^2/L \le Mg$, i.e. $\mathrm{Fr} \le 1$. At higher speeds the body would leave the ground: the animal must run.
:::

::: example When does a cat start to run? {#ex-froude}
Using $\mathrm{Fr} = 0.5$ for the walk–run transition, estimate the transition speed for (a) a person with leg length 0.90 m, (b) a cat with leg length 0.25 m. (c) What is the theoretical maximum walking speed of the person? (Olympic race walkers exceed it; how?)
::: solution
(a) $v = \sqrt{0.5 \times 9.81 \times 0.90} = 2.1$ m s⁻¹, close to the observed transition speed in humans, about 2 m s⁻¹.

(b) $v = \sqrt{0.5 \times 9.81 \times 0.25} = 1.1$ m s⁻¹.

(c) At $\mathrm{Fr} = 1$, $v = \sqrt{9.81 \times 0.90} = 3.0$ m s⁻¹. Race walkers reach about 4 m s⁻¹ by rotating and dropping the pelvis, which flattens the path of the centre of mass so that its effective radius of curvature exceeds leg length, and by keeping the stance leg straight. Their gait is not a simple inverted pendulum, which is why it looks so peculiar.
:::
:::

The same principle lets palaeontologists estimate the speeds of extinct animals from fossil trackways. Alexander (1976) combined the empirical relation between relative stride length (stride length $\lambda$ divided by hip height $h$) and Froude number in living animals into a formula,

$$
v \approx 0.25\,g^{0.5}\,\lambda^{1.67}\,h^{-1.17},
$$ {#eq-alexander}

with $v$ in m s⁻¹ and lengths in metres, with hip height estimated as about four times footprint length. A relative stride below about 2.0 indicates walking; above about 2.9, running.

::: example A dinosaur trackway {#ex-trackway}
A trackway of a bipedal dinosaur has footprints 0.50 m long and a stride length of 3.0 m. Estimate its hip height, whether it was walking or running, and its speed.
::: solution
Hip height $h \approx 4 \times 0.50 = 2.0$ m. Relative stride $\lambda/h = 3.0/2.0 = 1.5$, below 2.0, so it was walking. Speed $v = 0.25 \times 9.81^{0.5} \times 3.0^{1.67} \times 2.0^{-1.17} = 0.25 \times 3.13 \times 6.26 \times 0.445 = 2.2$ m s⁻¹ (8 km h⁻¹), a brisk walk. Most dinosaur trackways give walking speeds of this order; a few indicate running. The estimate inherits the uncertainties of both the hip-height estimate and the empirical relation, and should be treated as accurate only to within tens of per cent.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A small and a large mammal of similar shape run at the same Froude number. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] They run at the same speed.
- [x] The larger animal runs faster, in proportion to the square root of its leg length, and both use similar gaits and relative stride lengths.
- [ ] The smaller animal runs faster, because its muscles are relatively stronger.
- [ ] The Froude number says nothing about gait.
::: solution
At equal $\mathrm{Fr} = v^2/gL$, $v \propto \sqrt{L}$: a mammal with legs four times longer moves twice as fast. Dynamic similarity means that at equal Froude number animals of different size use similar gaits, duty factors and stride lengths relative to their leg length.

::: application Muscle in agriculture and medicine
In pigs, a single point mutation in the skeletal-muscle ryanodine receptor gene *RYR1* (Arg615Cys) causes the **porcine stress syndrome**: affected pigs respond to stress or to the anaesthetic halothane with uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release, muscle rigidity and a lethal rise in body temperature, and their meat is pale and watery. The mutation, identified by J. Fujii and colleagues in 1991, had spread because it was associated with leaner carcasses; DNA testing has since allowed breeders to eliminate it. Related *RYR1* variants cause malignant hyperthermia in humans, a matter for [Medicine Atlas: musculoskeletal](/learn/medicine/course.php?c=musculoskeletal) and the human muscle physiology in [Medicine Atlas: muscle](/learn/medicine/lesson.php?c=physiology&l=muscle).
:::

::: history
Luigi Galvani reported in 1791 that frog leg muscles contract when touched with metals, launching the study of animal electricity. Sydney Ringer showed in 1883 that frog hearts need Ca²⁺ to beat, and Lewis Heilbrunn and Florian Wiercinski showed in 1947 that injecting Ca²⁺ into frog muscle fibres makes them contract. A. V. Hill measured the heat and mechanics of contraction from the 1910s and published the force–velocity relation in 1938 (he shared the 1922 Nobel Prize with Otto Meyerhof for earlier work on muscle heat and metabolism). The sliding-filament theory came from Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson and from Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke in 1954, and Andrew Huxley's cross-bridge model followed in 1957. Setsuro Ebashi discovered troponin in the 1960s, completing the picture of thin-filament regulation. J. W. S. Pringle described asynchronous insect flight muscle in 1949, and Elwood Henneman the size principle in 1957. Cavagna, Heglund and Taylor's force-platform studies (1977) and R. McNeill Alexander's work on dynamic similarity (from the 1970s) founded the modern biomechanics of locomotion.
:::

## Where this leads

Muscle is controlled by motor neurons and the circuits that coordinate them, the subject of [[neuroscience/motor-systems]]. Its energy demand, and how it scales, links back to [[animal-physiology/energy-metabolism]], and its hormonal regulation — including growth and the switch of fibre types with training and season — to [[animal-physiology/endocrine-control]].

::: summary
- Skeletal muscle is striated and nerve-activated; cardiac muscle is striated, myogenic and electrically coupled; smooth muscle is non-striated, slow and economical. Invertebrates add obliquely striated, catch and asynchronous flight muscles.
- Contraction is triggered by cytosolic Ca²⁺: in skeletal muscle by mechanical coupling of DHPR voltage sensors to RyR1; in cardiac muscle by Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release; in smooth muscle via calmodulin and myosin light-chain kinase. SERCA removes Ca²⁺ for relaxation.
- Force is graded by recruitment of motor units (size principle) and by summation towards tetanus.
- Force is proportional to physiological cross-section (specific tension 200–300 kPa); shortening speed and distance to fibre length.
- Hill's hyperbola $(F + a)(v + b) = (F_0 + a)b$ describes the force–velocity relation ([[#prop-hill-muscle]]); power peaks near a third of $F_0$ and $v_\mathrm{max}$, and animals use fibre types like gears to stay near it.
- Relative strength falls as $M^{-1/3}$, but jump height is roughly independent of size when limited by work ([[#prop-jump]]); small jumpers such as fleas are power-limited and use elastic catapults.
- Walking is an inverted pendulum, running a bouncing spring; the walk–run transition occurs near $\mathrm{Fr} = v^2/gL \approx 0.5$, and walking is impossible above $\mathrm{Fr} = 1$ ([[#prop-froude]]).
:::

::: exercise Velocity at a given load {level=2 check="0.49"}
For the muscle of the exercise *Maximum velocity* ($F_0 = 100$ N, $a = 25$ N, $b = 2.0$ s⁻¹), at what velocity (fibre lengths per second) does it shorten against a load of 75 N, and what fraction of its maximum power does it then deliver? Give the fraction to two significant figures.
::: solution
$v = b(F_0 - F)/(F + a) = 2.0 \times 25/100 = 0.50$ s⁻¹. Power $= 75 \times 0.50 = 37.5$ (N × fibre lengths s⁻¹). Maximum power, from [[#ex-power]] with $k = 0.25$: $0.0955 \times F_0 v_\mathrm{max} = 0.0955 \times 100 \times 8 = 76.4$. Fraction $= 37.5/76.4 = 0.49$. A heavy load (75 % of $F_0$) is moved slowly and delivers only half the muscle's peak power.
:::

::: exercise Gears in a fish {level=2}
A fish swims at increasing speeds. At low speed only its red muscle is active; above a certain speed white muscle is recruited. Using the force–velocity and power relations, explain (a) why the red muscle is not simply driven harder at high speed, (b) why white muscle is not used at low speed, and (c) why white muscle can only be used briefly.
::: solution
(a) As swimming speed rises, the body bends faster and every muscle fibre must shorten faster. Red fibres have a low $v_\mathrm{max}$; at high speeds they would have to shorten close to $v_\mathrm{max}$, where their force and power fall towards zero and efficiency is poor. (b) White fibres have a high $v_\mathrm{max}$; at low swimming speeds they would shorten at a small fraction of it, producing force inefficiently, and their glycolytic metabolism would waste fuel and produce lactate. Each fibre type works best near a third of its own $v_\mathrm{max}$ (Rome and colleagues, 1988). (c) White fibres have few mitochondria and little myoglobin and capillary supply; they rely on phosphocreatine and anaerobic glycolysis, which deplete stores and accumulate lactate and H⁺ within seconds to minutes, and recovery takes much longer.
:::

::: exercise A hydrostatic skeleton {level=2}
An earthworm has circular and longitudinal muscle layers around a fluid-filled body cavity whose volume stays constant. (a) Explain how contraction of the circular muscle lengthens a segment. (b) If a cylindrical segment of radius 2.0 mm and length 5.0 mm contracts its circular muscle so that its radius falls to 1.6 mm, what is its new length? (c) Why do soft-bodied animals need antagonistic muscle layers?
::: solution
(a) Contracting circular muscle reduces the radius; because the fluid is incompressible and the segment's volume is constant, the segment must lengthen. (b) $\pi r^2 L$ constant: $L_2 = 5.0 \times (2.0/1.6)^2 = 5.0 \times 1.5625 = 7.8$ mm. (c) Muscles can only pull. Longitudinal muscle shortens and widens the segment; circular muscle narrows and lengthens it; each re-extends the other through the fluid, which acts as the skeleton. Peristaltic waves of alternating contraction push the worm through soil, with bristles (chaetae) anchoring the stationary segments.
:::

::: exercise Scaling of strength {level=2}
A 5 mg ant can carry a load of 50 mg in its jaws. (a) If a 70 kg person had the same relative strength and the same shape scaled up isometrically, what load could they carry? (b) Using the scaling of muscle force with cross-sectional area, what load would you actually predict? (c) Comment.
::: solution
(a) Ten times body weight: 700 kg. (b) Linear scale factor $L_2/L_1 = (70/5\times10^{-6})^{1/3} = (1.4\times10^7)^{1/3} = 241$. Force scales as $L^2$ and weight as $L^3$, so relative strength falls by a factor of 241: the person could carry $10/241 = 0.041$ times body weight, about 3 kg. (c) The ant's apparently prodigious strength is mostly an effect of size, not of better muscles. Real people can carry roughly their own weight, much more than this simple prediction, because humans are not scaled-up ants: they have relatively much more muscle, different leverage and an upright posture that transfers weight through bones rather than muscles.

::: exercise Interpreting a force-platform record {level=3}
Force-platform recordings from a trotting dog show that, during each stance, the centre of mass is lowest at mid-stance, and that its horizontal kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy are both minimal at mid-stance. Recordings from the same dog walking show potential energy maximal and kinetic energy minimal at mid-stance. (a) Interpret both records in terms of the inverted-pendulum and spring-mass models. (b) Where is the energy stored at mid-stance in the trot? (c) Why does walking become expensive at high speeds and running expensive at low speeds?
::: solution
(a) In walking, kinetic and potential energy fluctuate out of phase: the body rises and slows over the stance leg, converting kinetic to potential energy, then falls and speeds up — the inverted pendulum, which recovers much of the energy without muscle work. In trotting they fluctuate in phase, both minimal at mid-stance, so they cannot be exchanged with each other; instead both are stored together. (b) As elastic strain energy in the stretched tendons and ligaments of the legs (and in the stretched muscles themselves), returned as the leg extends in the second half of the stance. (c) At high walking speeds the pendulum exchange becomes less effective and the Froude-number limit approaches, so muscles must do more work; at low running speeds the bouncing gait involves high forces and short contact times relative to the distance travelled, wasting energy, while the pendulum would work well. Animals choose the gait that minimises metabolic cost at each speed; ponies on a treadmill trained to use unusual gaits at given speeds used more oxygen than in their preferred gait (D. F. Hoyt and C. R. Taylor, 1981).
:::

::: exercise Asynchronous flight {level=3}
(a) Explain why a nerve cannot drive a muscle directly at 500 contractions per second, considering the time course of Ca²⁺ release and removal. (b) How does stretch activation solve the problem? (c) Why does the wingbeat frequency of an insect with asynchronous muscle change when its wings are clipped shorter? (d) What would you predict about the amount of sarcoplasmic reticulum in asynchronous compared with synchronous flight muscle, and why?
::: solution
(a) Each contraction triggered by a nerve requires Ca²⁺ release and removal; removal by SERCA, and detachment of cross-bridges, take several milliseconds at least, so at 500 Hz (2 ms per cycle) the muscle would be unable to relax between impulses and would go into tetanus. (b) In asynchronous muscle Ca²⁺ remains elevated throughout flight, maintained by a slow train of nerve impulses; contraction in each cycle is triggered by stretch, with a delay, so the antagonistic muscles and the elastic thorax form a self-sustaining oscillator whose frequency is set by the mechanics, not by Ca²⁺ cycling. (c) The frequency is the resonant frequency of the thorax–wing system, which depends on its stiffness and on the inertia of the wings; clipping the wings reduces their moment of inertia and raises the frequency, as was found experimentally. (d) Less: since Ca²⁺ does not cycle with each beat, asynchronous flight muscle needs little sarcoplasmic reticulum and can devote more of its volume to myofibrils and mitochondria, which helps explain its high power output. Synchronous flight muscles (as in locusts and dragonflies, at lower frequencies) have abundant sarcoplasmic reticulum.
