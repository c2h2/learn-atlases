To see something you need radiation with a wavelength no larger than the detail you want to see. Visible light, at 400–700 nm, can show a bacterium but not a protein, whose atoms are 1–2 Å apart. X-rays of wavelength around 1 Å are the right size, but no lens can focus them to form an image. X-ray crystallography gets round this by recording the scattered waves themselves and reconstructing the image by computation. The price is that the molecules must be arranged in a crystal, and that half of the information — the phases of the scattered waves — is lost in the measurement and must be recovered by ingenuity.

For seven decades crystallography was the main source of atomic structures in biology. It gave us myoglobin and haemoglobin, lysozyme and the serine proteases, the double helix of the Dickerson dodecamer, tRNA, the photosynthetic reaction centre, potassium channels, G-protein-coupled receptors and the ribosome, and crystal structures still make up the majority of entries in the Protein Data Bank (as of 2026). This chapter explains how a crystal structure is obtained and, just as important, how to judge one. The physics of diffraction — interference, Bragg's law, the reciprocal lattice and the structure factor — is developed in the Physics Atlas ([Diffraction](/learn/physics/lesson.php?c=optics&l=diffraction)) and the Chemistry Atlas ([X-ray diffraction](/learn/chemistry/lesson.php?c=materials-chemistry&l=diffraction)); we recall what we need and concentrate on what is special about large, weakly diffracting biological molecules. The Fourier series used throughout is introduced in Maths Atlas ([Fourier series](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=pde&l=fourier-series)).

## Why a crystal?

X-rays are scattered by electrons. A single protein molecule scatters so weakly, and is destroyed by the radiation so quickly, that its diffraction cannot be recorded with conventional sources. In a crystal, about $10^{12}$–$10^{15}$ identical molecules in identical orientations scatter in phase in particular directions, so the scattered waves add up into strong, discrete spots — **reflections** — and the signal is amplified enormously. The crystal acts both as an amplifier and as a sampler: it concentrates the scattering of the molecule into reflections, at the cost of measuring it only at those points.

::: definition Crystal, unit cell and asymmetric unit {#def-crystal}
A **crystal** is a three-dimensional periodic arrangement of molecules. The **unit cell** is the parallelepiped, with edges $a$, $b$, $c$ and angles α, β, γ, whose translations along the three edges generate the whole crystal (the **lattice**). The unit cell may contain several molecules related by **symmetry operations** (rotations and screw axes) of the crystal's **space group**; the smallest part of the cell from which the whole cell can be generated by these operations is the **asymmetric unit**, which may contain one or more molecules. Because biological molecules are chiral, only the 65 space groups without mirror or inversion symmetry (the Sohncke groups) are possible.
:::

Protein crystals are unlike crystals of salt. They are soft, fragile and wet: typically 30–70 % of their volume is solvent, filling channels between molecules that touch only at a few small patches. This is useful as well as inconvenient. It means that the molecule in the crystal is surrounded by water, much as in solution, and that small molecules — substrates, inhibitors, heavy-atom compounds — can be soaked into the crystal and diffuse to binding sites.

::: proposition The Matthews coefficient (empirical) {#prop-matthews}
For a crystal with unit-cell volume $V$ (Å³) containing $Z$ molecules of molar mass $M$ (Da), the **Matthews coefficient** is

$$
V_\mathrm M = \frac{V}{ZM}\quad (\text{Å}^3\,\text{Da}^{-1}),
$$ {#eq-matthews}

and the fraction of the cell occupied by solvent is approximately

$$
f_\mathrm{solvent} \approx 1 - \frac{1.23}{V_\mathrm M},
$$ {#eq-solvent}

where 1.23 Å³ Da⁻¹ is (approximately) the partial specific volume of protein expressed per dalton. B. W. Matthews found in 1968 that $V_\mathrm M$ lies between about 1.7 and 3.5 Å³ Da⁻¹ for most protein crystals, with a typical value near 2.4. The range is an empirical observation, used to guess how many molecules are in the asymmetric unit.
:::

::: example How many molecules in the asymmetric unit? {#ex-matthews}
A 25 kDa protein crystallises in space group $P2_12_12_1$ ($Z = 4$ asymmetric units per cell) with $a = 50$, $b = 60$, $c = 70$ Å, all angles 90°. How many molecules are in the asymmetric unit, and what is the solvent content?
::: solution
$V = 50\times60\times70 = 2.10\times10^5$ Å³. With one molecule per asymmetric unit, $Z M = 4\times25\,000 = 10^5$ Da and $V_\mathrm M = 2.1$ Å³ Da⁻¹, inside the normal range; the solvent fraction is $1 - 1.23/2.1 = 0.41$. With two molecules per asymmetric unit, $V_\mathrm M = 1.05$ Å³ Da⁻¹ and the solvent fraction would be negative — impossible. So there is one molecule per asymmetric unit and about 41 % solvent, a fairly tightly packed protein crystal.
:::
:::

### Growing crystals

Crystallisation is the most empirical step. A pure, homogeneous protein solution at a few mg mL⁻¹ is brought slowly to supersaturation, typically by vapour diffusion of water from a drop containing protein and a precipitant (a salt, a polyethylene glycol, an organic solvent) to a reservoir of higher precipitant concentration. In the supersaturated region of the phase diagram crystals may nucleate and grow; too far, and the protein precipitates as amorphous aggregate. Robots now screen hundreds of conditions with drops of 100 nL or less. Flexible termini and loops often prevent crystallisation and are removed by protein engineering; surface lysines may be mutated to reduce surface entropy; antibody fragments or designed binders may be added as "crystallisation chaperones".

Membrane proteins are harder still, because they must be extracted from the bilayer into detergent micelles. Two advances made them tractable: **lipidic cubic phase** crystallisation, in which the protein is reconstituted into a continuous, curved lipid bilayer that forms a three-dimensional lattice (E. M. Landau and J. P. Rosenbusch, 1996), and the fusion or co-crystallisation of receptors with stable partner proteins, which gave the first structures of a ligand-activated G-protein-coupled receptor, the human β₂-adrenergic receptor, in 2007 (V. Cherezov, D. M. Rosenbaum, B. K. Kobilka, R. C. Stevens and colleagues; S. G. F. Rasmussen and colleagues).

## Diffraction

A crystal is a stack of lattice planes. Waves reflected from successive planes interfere constructively only when the extra path length between them is a whole number of wavelengths.

::: proposition Bragg's law {#prop-bragg}
X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$ are diffracted by a set of lattice planes of spacing $d$ when

$$
2d\sin\theta = n\lambda ,
$$ {#eq-bragg}

where $\theta$ is the angle between the incident beam and the planes (so the diffracted beam is deflected through $2\theta$) and $n$ is an integer. Each reflection is labelled by the **Miller indices** $(hkl)$ of its planes, which absorb $n$, so we may take $n = 1$ with $d = d_{hkl}$. (W. L. Bragg and W. H. Bragg, 1913. Assumptions: specular reflection from planes of equivalent scatterers, elastic scattering, a perfect lattice.)
:::

Reflections at larger angles come from planes with smaller spacings and carry information about finer details. The smallest spacing for which reflections are measurable is the **resolution** $d_\mathrm{min}$ of the data, quoted in ångströms: a "2.0 Å structure" is one calculated from reflections out to $d = 2.0$ Å. Lower numbers mean better resolution.

::: example Resolution at the edge of a detector {#ex-bragg}
(a) At what angle $2\theta$ is a reflection with $d = 2.0$ Å observed using X-rays of $\lambda = 1.00$ Å? (b) A flat detector of radius 150 mm is centred on the beam 200 mm from the crystal. What is the best resolution recorded at its edge?
::: solution
(a) $\sin\theta = \lambda/2d = 0.25$, so $\theta = 14.5°$ and $2\theta = 29.0°$. (b) The edge subtends $2\theta = \arctan(150/200) = 36.9°$, so $\theta = 18.4°$ and $d_\mathrm{min} = \lambda/(2\sin\theta) = 1.00/(2\times0.316) = 1.58$ Å. To record higher resolution the detector is moved closer — at the cost of crowding the spots together, which matters for crystals with long cell edges, such as virus or ribosome crystals.
:::
:::

### Collecting data

In the standard **rotation method** the crystal is rotated in the beam in small steps (0.1–1°) while images are recorded, until a range of orientations sufficient to record every unique reflection has been covered. Each image shows a pattern of spots. Software **indexes** the spots (finds the unit cell and orientation), **integrates** their intensities, and **scales** and merges the measurements of each reflection and its symmetry equivalents. Quality is summarised by the **completeness** (fraction of possible reflections measured), the **multiplicity** (average number of measurements per unique reflection), the signal-to-noise ratio $\langle I/\sigma(I)\rangle$ and the correlation $\mathrm{CC}_{1/2}$ between intensities from two random halves of the data (P. A. Karplus and K. Diederichs, 2012), which is now used to decide how far in resolution the useful data extend.

Data are collected with the crystal cooled to about 100 K in a nitrogen stream, which slows **radiation damage** by limiting the diffusion of radicals. Even so, a protein crystal survives only a limited dose. R. Henderson estimated in 1990 that diffraction would be halved after about $2\times10^7$ Gy (J/kg) at cryogenic temperature; measurements by R. L. Owen, E. Garman and colleagues (2006) led to a recommended limit of about $3\times10^7$ Gy. Specific damage — breaking of disulfides, decarboxylation, reduction of metal centres — sets in at lower doses and can create artefacts at active sites.

Most data today are collected at **synchrotrons**, whose undulators produce X-ray beams many orders of magnitude brighter than laboratory sources, tunable in wavelength. **X-ray free-electron lasers** (XFELs), since 2009, deliver pulses of a few femtoseconds that are so intense a microcrystal is vaporised by each one — but only after the diffraction has left it, "diffraction before destruction" (R. Neutze, J. Hajdu and colleagues, 2000). **Serial femtosecond crystallography** merges single snapshots from thousands of tiny crystals streamed through the beam (H. N. Chapman and colleagues, photosystem I, 2011) and allows **time-resolved** studies in which a reaction is started by a laser flash and followed over femtoseconds to milliseconds.

## The structure factor and the phase problem

Each reflection is a wave with an amplitude and a phase. Its value is the sum of the waves scattered by all the atoms in the unit cell.

::: definition Structure factor and electron density {#def-structure-factor}
For atoms $j$ at fractional coordinates $(x_j, y_j, z_j)$ in the unit cell with **atomic scattering factors** $f_j$ (equal to the number of electrons at zero angle, falling at higher angles because atoms are not points), the **structure factor** of reflection $(hkl)$ is the complex number

$$
F_{hkl} = \sum_j f_j\,e^{2\pi i(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j)} = \lvert F_{hkl}\rvert\,e^{i\varphi_{hkl}} .
$$ {#eq-structure-factor}

Conversely the **electron density** at $(x, y, z)$ is the Fourier synthesis

$$
\rho(x, y, z) = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{h,k,l} F_{hkl}\,e^{-2\pi i(hx + ky + lz)} .
$$ {#eq-density}

The measured intensity is $I_{hkl} \propto \lvert F_{hkl}\rvert^2$: the amplitude is measured, but the **phase** $\varphi_{hkl}$ is not.
:::

[[#eq-density]] is the whole of crystallography in one line: if we knew both the amplitudes and phases of all reflections we could compute the electron density and see the molecule. The amplitudes come from the measured intensities. The phases are lost, because detectors record only the energy of the wave — and without phases [[#eq-density]] cannot be evaluated. This is the **phase problem**.

::: intuition Phases carry more of the picture than amplitudes
In a well-known demonstration (Kevin Cowtan's *Book of Fourier*), the Fourier transforms of a picture of a duck and a picture of a cat are computed; the amplitudes of the duck are then combined with the phases of the cat and the result transformed back. The image that appears is a cat. Phases encode where things are — the positions of the waves' crests — while amplitudes encode only how strongly each spacing is represented. This is why a map computed with good amplitudes and poor phases looks like the source of the phases, a danger known as **model bias**.
:::

The figure shows the idea in one dimension: a unit cell containing three atoms, represented by just five Fourier terms. You can change the phases.

### Solving the phase problem

Three families of methods recover phases for macromolecules.

- **Molecular replacement** (M. G. Rossmann and D. M. Blow, 1962). If a model of a similar structure is available — a homologue, another crystal form, or since 2021 a predicted model ([[structural-biology/structure-prediction]]) — it is placed in the unit cell by searching for the rotation and translation that best reproduce the observed intensities, and its calculated phases are used as a starting point. Most new crystal structures are now solved this way, and the arrival of accurate predicted models has made it possible for many targets that previously had no suitable template.
- **Isomorphous replacement**. Heavy atoms (Hg, Pt, Au, U) are bound to the protein, by soaking, without changing its structure. The heavy atoms change the intensities; from the differences, their positions can be found (they are few, so a **Patterson function** — the Fourier synthesis of the intensities themselves, which shows interatomic vectors — reveals them), and their known contributions fix the protein phases up to an ambiguity resolved by a second derivative. This multiple isomorphous replacement (MIR) method was introduced for proteins by D. W. Green, V. M. Ingram and M. F. Perutz in 1954 for haemoglobin, and gave the first protein structures.
- **Anomalous scattering**. Near an absorption edge, an atom's scattering acquires an imaginary component, which breaks the equality of the intensities of reflections $(hkl)$ and $(\bar h\bar k\bar l)$ (Friedel's law). Measuring these small differences at one or more wavelengths (single- and multi-wavelength anomalous diffraction, SAD and MAD) locates the anomalous scatterers and gives phases from a single crystal. W. A. Hendrickson's introduction of selenomethionine-labelled proteins (W. A. Hendrickson, J. R. Horton and D. M. LeMaster, 1990), in which every methionine carries a selenium atom, made MAD and then SAD routine at synchrotrons; even the weak anomalous signal of the sulfur atoms of native cysteines and methionines is now often enough.

For small molecules, **direct methods** use statistical relations between the phases of strong reflections to solve the phase problem from intensities alone (H. A. Hauptman and J. Karle; Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1985). They require atomic resolution and are rarely applicable to proteins except small ones diffracting beyond about 1.2 Å.

::: example Two atoms in one dimension {#ex-two-atoms}
A one-dimensional cell contains two identical point atoms ($f = 1$) at $x_1 = 0.10$ and $x_2 = 0.35$. (a) Calculate $F_1$ and $F_2$ from [[#eq-structure-factor]]. (b) Show that shifting both atoms by $\Delta = 0.25$ leaves every $\lvert F_h\rvert$ unchanged but changes the phases.
::: solution
(a) $F_h = e^{2\pi ih(0.10)} + e^{2\pi ih(0.35)}$. For $h = 1$: $e^{0.2\pi i} + e^{0.7\pi i} = (0.809 + 0.588i) + (-0.588 + 0.809i) = 0.221 + 1.397i$, so $\lvert F_1\rvert = 1.414$ and $\varphi_1 = 81°$. For $h = 2$: $e^{0.4\pi i} + e^{1.4\pi i} = (0.309 + 0.951i) + (-0.309 - 0.951i) = 0$: the two atoms are half a period of this wave apart and cancel. (b) A shift multiplies every term by $e^{2\pi ih\Delta}$, so $F_h' = F_h e^{2\pi ih\Delta}$: the amplitude is unchanged and the phase increases by $360°\times h\Delta$ — by 90° for $h = 1$. Intensities alone cannot tell where the origin is, nor distinguish the structure from its mirror image ($x \to -x$ gives the complex conjugate, with the same amplitude). Phasing methods must fix both.
:::
:::

## From map to model

With initial phases an electron-density map is calculated and improved by **density modification** — flattening the solvent region, which should be featureless, and averaging copies of the molecule related by non-crystallographic symmetry. A model of the molecule is then built into the map, residue by residue, today largely automatically, and **refined**: the coordinates and the **displacement parameters** of each atom are adjusted to minimise the discrepancy between the observed amplitudes and those calculated from the model, subject to **restraints** that keep bond lengths, angles and planarity near standard values. New phases from the improved model give a better map, and the cycle repeats.

::: example Data and parameters {#ex-parameters}
For the crystal of [[#ex-matthews]] (cell volume $2.1\times10^5$ Å³, point-group symmetry of order 4), estimate the number of unique reflections to 2.0 Å resolution. The 25 kDa protein has about 1,800 non-hydrogen atoms, each refined with $x$, $y$, $z$ and an isotropic $B$. How many observations are there per parameter?
::: solution
Reflections are the points of the reciprocal lattice, with one point per volume $1/V$ of reciprocal space. Those within resolution $d$ lie in a sphere of radius $1/d$, so their number is

$$
N \approx \frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{V}{d^3} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\times\frac{2.1\times10^5}{8} = 1.10\times10^5 .
$$

Friedel's law halves this, and the four-fold point-group symmetry divides it by 4, leaving about 13,700 unique reflections. The parameters number $4\times1800 = 7,200$, so there are only about 1.9 observations per parameter. At 3 Å the ratio would be $(2/3)^3$ of this, about 0.6. Unrestrained refinement would be hopeless; the restraints on stereochemistry, which effectively add thousands of observations, are what make macromolecular refinement possible.
:::
:::

### Judging the fit: R-factors

::: definition R-factor and free R-factor {#def-rfactor}
The **crystallographic R-factor** measures the disagreement between observed and calculated structure-factor amplitudes:

$$
R = \frac{\sum_{hkl}\bigl\lvert\,\lvert F_\mathrm{obs}\rvert - \lvert F_\mathrm{calc}\rvert\,\bigr\rvert}{\sum_{hkl}\lvert F_\mathrm{obs}\rvert} .
$$ {#eq-rfactor}

The **free R-factor** $R_\mathrm{free}$ is the same quantity computed for a randomly chosen test set of reflections (typically 5 %) that are never used in refinement (A. T. Brünger, 1992). A random structure gives $R \approx 0.59$ (acentric reflections); well-refined macromolecular structures typically have $R_\mathrm{work}$ of 0.15–0.20 and $R_\mathrm{free}$ of 0.20–0.25 at 2 Å resolution, with $R_\mathrm{free}$ a few per cent higher than $R_\mathrm{work}$.
:::

With few observations per parameter, a model can be adjusted to fit noise: $R_\mathrm{work}$ falls even when the model gets worse. $R_\mathrm{free}$, by contrast, measures how well the model *predicts* data it was not fitted to — cross-validation, exactly as in statistics ([Maths Atlas: regression](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=statistics&l=regression)). A large gap between $R_\mathrm{work}$ and $R_\mathrm{free}$ is a sign of overfitting.

### Displacement parameters and what resolution shows

Atoms are not fixed. They vibrate, and different molecules in the crystal differ slightly; both effects smear the electron density and weaken the high-angle reflections.

::: proposition The B-factor {#prop-bfactor}
If the displacement of an atom from its mean position along any direction has mean square $\langle u^2\rangle$ (isotropic, Gaussian), its contribution to the scattering is multiplied by $\exp(-B\sin^2\theta/\lambda^2) = \exp\bigl(-B/(4d^2)\bigr)$, where

$$
B = 8\pi^2\langle u^2\rangle .
$$ {#eq-bfactor}

The intensity of a reflection, proportional to $\lvert F\rvert^2$, is therefore reduced by $\exp\bigl(-B/(2d^2)\bigr)$. $B$ (Å²) is reported for every atom in a structure. It combines thermal motion, static disorder between unit cells and errors of the model.
:::

::: example What a B-factor means {#ex-bfactor}
(a) A side-chain atom has $B = 30$ Å². What is its root-mean-square displacement along a given direction? (b) If the whole crystal has an overall $B$ of 30 Å², by what factor is the average intensity at 2.0 Å weakened relative to an ideal, motionless crystal, and at 3.0 Å?
::: solution
(a) $\langle u^2\rangle = 30/(8\pi^2) = 0.380$ Å², so the r.m.s. displacement is $0.62$ Å along any one direction (and $\sqrt3\times0.62 = 1.07$ Å in three dimensions). (b) At $d = 2.0$ Å the factor is $e^{-30/8} = e^{-3.75} = 0.024$; at 3.0 Å it is $e^{-30/18} = 0.19$. High-resolution intensities fall off steeply, which is why disorder limits resolution: a crystal with $B = 60$ Å² gives only $e^{-7.5} = 5.5\times10^{-4}$ of the ideal intensity at 2 Å, usually too weak to measure.
:::
:::

Increase $B$ — a more disordered or flexible crystal — and see the useful data shrink towards lower resolution; it is mainly this fall-off, not the X-ray wavelength, that limits the resolution of a macromolecular crystal.
:::

The resolution of a map determines what can be seen in it:

| resolution | features visible |
|---|---|
| 6 Å | rods of density for α-helices; overall shape |
| 4–3.5 Å | β-strands separate; bulky side chains; sequence register uncertain |
| 3–2.5 Å | most side chains; main-chain carbonyl groups as bumps; ordered water molecules start to appear |
| 2.0 Å | side-chain conformations and ligands well defined; many waters |
| 1.5 Å and better | individual atoms resolved; alternative conformations; at ≲ 1.0 Å, some hydrogen atoms |

::: warning A crystal structure is a model, not a photograph
The coordinates in a PDB file are an interpretation of an electron-density map, which in turn depends on phases that came partly from the model itself. At modest resolution, side chains in weak density, loops, and especially bound ligands may be placed where there is little evidence for them; wishful interpretation of noise as a ligand has produced published errors. Before relying on a detail, look at the map (the PDB provides the data, and validation reports for every entry), check the local B-factors and real-space fit, and remember that the structure is an average over $10^{12}$ molecules and over the duration of the experiment, in a crystal lattice whose contacts can distort flexible regions.
:::

::: quiz
Two structures of the same enzyme are deposited. Structure A: 1.8 Å, $R_\mathrm{work} = 0.17$, $R_\mathrm{free} = 0.21$. Structure B: 2.9 Å, $R_\mathrm{work} = 0.16$, $R_\mathrm{free} = 0.31$. Which statement is best supported?
- [ ] B is better, because its $R_\mathrm{work}$ is lower.
- [x] A is more reliable; B's large gap between $R_\mathrm{work}$ and $R_\mathrm{free}$ suggests it has been overfitted for its resolution.
- [ ] They are equally reliable, because both $R_\mathrm{work}$ values are below 0.20.
- [ ] B is better, because low-resolution structures have fewer errors.
::: solution
At 2.9 Å there are few observations per parameter, and an $R_\mathrm{work}$ of 0.16 is suspiciously low; the gap of 0.15 to $R_\mathrm{free}$ indicates that the model fits the working data far better than it predicts the free set, the signature of overfitting. Structure A has higher resolution, a typical gap of 0.04 and a reasonable $R_\mathrm{free}$.
:::
:::

## Beyond the standard experiment

- **Neutron crystallography** uses the fact that neutrons are scattered by nuclei, and deuterium scatters as strongly as carbon. With deuterated crystals it locates hydrogen atoms and so determines protonation states in active sites — information X-rays rarely give — but needs large crystals and long exposures.
- **Room-temperature and time-resolved** crystallography, at synchrotrons and XFELs, shows conformational heterogeneity hidden by cryocooling and follows reactions such as the photocycle of photoactive yellow protein, the isomerisation of retinal in bacteriorhodopsin and the water-splitting cycle of photosystem II.
- **Microcrystal electron diffraction** (MicroED, since 2013) records diffraction from crystals a few hundred nanometres thick in a cryo-electron microscope, connecting crystallography to [[structural-biology/cryo-em]].
- **Small-angle X-ray scattering** (SAXS) from molecules in solution, without a crystal, gives low-resolution information — the radius of gyration ([[structural-biology/protein-architecture]]), the maximum dimension and the overall shape — and is useful for flexible and disordered proteins.

::: application Structure-based drug design
Crystal structures of drug targets with bound inhibitors, determined in series, guide medicinal chemists in deciding which groups to add or remove. The HIV-1 protease inhibitors of the 1990s were among the first drugs designed in this way, from the structure of the dimeric aspartic protease and its complexes with peptide-mimetic inhibitors. **Fragment screening** soaks crystals with hundreds of very small molecules (a few hundred daltons) and detects weak binders directly in the electron density, giving starting points to be grown into potent compounds; several approved drugs began as fragments. Medicinal chemistry itself is a course of the Chemistry Atlas ([Medicinal chemistry](/learn/chemistry/course.php?c=medicinal-chemistry)).
:::

::: history
Max von Laue, Walter Friedrich and Paul Knipping observed X-ray diffraction from a copper sulfate crystal in 1912, and William Henry and William Lawrence Bragg used it to solve the first crystal structures, beginning with NaCl, in 1913. Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin solved penicillin (1945), vitamin B₁₂ (1956) and insulin (1969). In 1934 J. D. Bernal and Crowfoot showed that wet pepsin crystals diffract to high resolution, but the phase problem kept protein structures out of reach until Max Perutz showed in 1953–54 that heavy-atom derivatives of haemoglobin change the intensities measurably. Using this, John Kendrew's group solved sperm-whale myoglobin in 1958 and Perutz's horse haemoglobin followed in 1959–60 (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1962). Lysozyme (David Phillips, 1965) was the first enzyme. Synchrotron radiation, cryocooling, area detectors and MAD phasing transformed the speed of the method in the 1980s and 1990s, and the ribosome structures of 2000 marked the high point of what crystallography alone could achieve for large assemblies.
:::

## Where this leads

Crystallography needs crystals and gives averaged, static pictures. [[structural-biology/nmr-spectroscopy]] works with molecules in solution and measures their motions; [[structural-biology/cryo-em]] needs no crystals and copes with large, flexible assemblies, using the same Fourier ideas with electrons instead of X-rays. Crystal structures in the PDB were the training data of the prediction methods of [[structural-biology/structure-prediction]].

::: summary
- A crystal amplifies the weak scattering of single molecules into discrete reflections. Protein crystals are 30–70 % solvent; the Matthews coefficient $V_\mathrm M = V/(ZM)$ (typically 1.7–3.5 Å³ Da⁻¹) estimates the contents of the asymmetric unit.
- Bragg's law $2d\sin\theta = \lambda$ relates each reflection to a plane spacing; resolution is the smallest $d$ measured.
- The electron density is the Fourier synthesis of the structure factors [[#eq-density]], but only their amplitudes are measured: the phase problem, solved by molecular replacement, isomorphous replacement or anomalous scattering.
- Models are refined against the amplitudes under stereochemical restraints; $R_\mathrm{free}$ cross-validates the fit. B-factors, $B = 8\pi^2\langle u^2\rangle$, describe disorder and limit resolution.
- Radiation damage, crystal contacts and averaging limit what a crystal structure shows; read the map and validation reports, not only the coordinates.
:::

::: exercise A Bragg angle {level=1 check="22.5"}
Copper Kα radiation has $\lambda = 1.5418$ Å. At what diffraction angle $2\theta$ (degrees, three significant figures) is a reflection of spacing 3.95 Å observed?
::: solution
$\sin\theta = 1.5418/(2\times3.95) = 0.19516$, so $\theta = 11.25°$ and $2\theta = 22.5°$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Solvent content {level=1 check="0.53"}
A crystal has $V_\mathrm M = 2.6$ Å³ Da⁻¹. Estimate its solvent fraction (two significant figures).
::: solution
$1 - 1.23/2.6 = 0.527$, about 53 %.

::: exercise r.m.s. displacement {level=1 check="1.01"}
A surface loop has $B = 80$ Å². What is the r.m.s. displacement of its atoms along one direction, in Å (three significant figures)?
::: solution
$\sqrt{80/(8\pi^2)} = \sqrt{1.013} = 1.01$ Å — large enough that the loop's density is weak and its conformation uncertain.
:::

::: exercise Counting reflections {level=2}
For the same crystal as [[#ex-parameters]], how many unique reflections are there to 1.5 Å and to 3.0 Å? What does this say about the observations-to-parameters ratio at each resolution?
::: solution
The number scales as $1/d^3$. From 13,700 at 2.0 Å: at 1.5 Å, $13\,700\times(2/1.5)^3 = 32\,500$; at 3.0 Å, $13\,700\times(2/3)^3 = 4\,070$. With 7,200 parameters the ratios are about 4.5 and 0.57. At 1.5 Å the structure is well over-determined and individual anisotropic B-factors begin to be justified; at 3 Å the model relies heavily on restraints and prior knowledge, and its details (side chains, waters, ligand poses) are much less certain.
:::
:::

::: exercise Friedel pairs and anomalous scattering {level=2}
Show from [[#eq-structure-factor]] that if all $f_j$ are real, $\lvert F_{hkl}\rvert = \lvert F_{\bar h\bar k\bar l}\rvert$. Explain why an imaginary component of $f_j$ for some atoms breaks this equality and how that helps phasing.
::: solution
If all $f_j$ are real, $F_{\bar h\bar k\bar l} = \sum_j f_j e^{-2\pi i(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j)}$ is the complex conjugate of $F_{hkl}$, so the two have equal amplitudes (Friedel's law). If some atoms have $f = f_0 + f' + if''$ with $f'' \neq 0$, their contribution to $F_{\bar h\bar k\bar l}$ is not the conjugate of their contribution to $F_{hkl}$ (the $if''$ term rotates the same way in both), so the amplitudes differ slightly. These "Bijvoet differences" depend on the positions of the anomalous scatterers, which can be found from them (as in a Patterson synthesis), and their known contribution then constrains the phase of the whole structure factor, from a single crystal and, with MAD, several wavelengths.
:::
:::

::: exercise Overfitting {level=2}
A crystallographer refining a 2.8 Å structure adds 400 water molecules and finds that $R_\mathrm{work}$ falls from 0.24 to 0.20 while $R_\mathrm{free}$ rises from 0.29 to 0.30. Should the waters be kept? What would have happened if no test set had been set aside?
::: solution
No. Adding parameters always lowers $R_\mathrm{work}$, because the model can fit more of the noise; the waters improved the fit to the working set without improving (in fact, slightly worsening) the prediction of the free set, so they are not supported by the data. At 2.8 Å relatively few ordered waters are expected to be visible, and only those in clear, chemically sensible positions should be modelled. Without a test set there would be no way to detect this: $R_\mathrm{work}$ alone rewards overfitting, which is exactly why Brünger introduced $R_\mathrm{free}$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a soaking experiment {level=2}
Crystals of an enzyme are soaked in a solution of its inhibitor and data are collected to 2.2 Å. A difference map ($F_\mathrm{obs} - F_\mathrm{calc}$, computed with phases from the inhibitor-free model) shows a positive peak in the active site at 3σ, with a shape that fits about half the inhibitor; the other half lies in a region with no density. (a) What possibilities should be considered? (b) What additional evidence would you look for before claiming that the inhibitor binds as modelled?
::: solution
(a) The inhibitor may bind with low occupancy or in several orientations; only part of it may be ordered (the rest flexible and smeared out); the peak may be a buffer or cryoprotectant molecule, an ion or ordered water that happens to fit part of the ligand; or the crystal contacts may block access to part of the site. (b) A clear, continuous difference density at a reasonable contour that matches the whole ligand, preferably in an omit map calculated without any ligand in the model; refined occupancy and B-factors comparable with the surrounding protein; chemically sensible contacts (hydrogen bonds, no clashes); consistency with binding data (affinity, mutations, structure–activity relationships); and ideally a co-crystal structure or a second crystal form showing the same pose. Anomalous scattering from a halogen or sulfur atom in the ligand can confirm its position.
:::
:::

::: exercise A Patterson for two heavy atoms {level=3}
In a one-dimensional cell of length $a = 100$ Å, two mercury atoms are bound at $x = 12$ Å and $x = 47$ Å. The Patterson function $P(u) = \sum_h \lvert F_h\rvert^2\cos(2\pi hu)$ has peaks at the interatomic vectors. (a) Where are its peaks (within one cell, $0 \le u < 100$ Å)? (b) Why does the Patterson give the positions only up to an origin shift and inversion? (c) Why is the Patterson of the whole protein uninterpretable while that of a few heavy atoms is useful?
::: solution
(a) The vectors between all pairs, including each atom with itself: $u = 0$ (two self-vectors, a large origin peak), $u = 47 - 12 = 35$ Å and $u = 12 - 47 = -35 \equiv 65$ Å. So peaks at 0, 35 and 65 Å. (b) The Patterson depends only on differences of positions, which are unchanged by adding the same shift to all atoms and by inverting all positions ($x\to-x$ swaps 35 and 65 but leaves the set the same). The heavy-atom sites at (12, 47), (0, 35) or (−12, −47) give identical Pattersons. (c) A protein of $N$ atoms gives $N(N-1)$ vectors (millions), which overlap into a featureless map; two or a few heavy atoms, each with ~80 electrons, give a handful of strong, isolated peaks in a difference Patterson computed from the intensity changes they cause.
:::
:::

::: exercise A resolution limit from the B-factor {level=3}
Suppose a reflection is measurable if its average intensity, relative to the ideal motionless crystal, is at least $10^{-3}$. Using $\exp(-B/(2d^2))$, find the best resolution $d$ achievable for overall B-factors of 20, 50 and 100 Å², and comment.
::: solution
Setting $B/(2d^2) = \ln 1000 = 6.91$ gives $d = \sqrt{B/13.8}$. For $B = 20$ Å², $d = 1.20$ Å; for 50 Å², 1.90 Å; for 100 Å², 2.69 Å. Resolution scales as $\sqrt B$: halving the disorder (or the B-factor) improves resolution by a factor of $\sqrt2$. The threshold is arbitrary, and real intensity also depends on crystal size, beam brightness and background, but the strong dependence on disorder explains why improving crystal order (by removing flexible regions or finding a better crystal form) usually does more for resolution than a brighter source.
:::
