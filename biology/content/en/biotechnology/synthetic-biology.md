By about 2000 it was possible to build almost any DNA molecule one wanted, and still very hard to predict what a cell carrying it would do. Synthetic biology began as a response to that gap. Its founding claim was that biological engineering had the character of electronics before standardisation: every circuit was a one-off, built by a specialist who knew its quirks, with no parts catalogue, no datasheets, no units and no way to combine two working designs into a third. The remedy proposed was borrowed wholesale from engineering — **abstraction** into a hierarchy of parts, devices and systems; **standardisation** of interfaces and of measurement; and **decoupling**, so that a designer could work at one level without reasoning about the one below.

Twenty-five years later the programme has delivered real capabilities and a sober view of its own difficulty. Circuits of a dozen components work reliably enough to be used in production strains and in diagnostics; whole genomes have been synthesised and recoded. At the same time, the hoped-for modularity turned out to be weaker than expected, because cells are not passive substrates: parts interact through shared resources, through the growth rate and through noise, and a costly construct evolves away. This chapter develops the quantitative framework — Hill functions, steady states, response times — then analyses the canonical devices, and finally takes seriously the ways in which cells refuse to behave like circuit boards. The modelling is extended in [[systems-biology/network-motifs]] and [[systems-biology/switches-oscillators]]; the application of circuits to production is [[biotechnology/metabolic-engineering]]; construct assembly is assumed from [[biotechnology/recombinant-dna]].

## Parts, devices and the problem of composition

::: definition The abstraction hierarchy {#def-hierarchy}
In the usual scheme, **parts** are the smallest functional sequences — a promoter, a ribosome binding site, a coding sequence, a terminator, a degradation tag; **devices** are combinations of parts with a defined input–output behaviour, such as an inverter (a promoter driving a repressor that acts on another promoter) or a sensor; and **systems** are combinations of devices, such as a two-input logic gate or an oscillator. The interface between levels is a signal: in most designs, the concentration of a transcription factor or a small molecule, or the activity of a promoter.

**Standardisation** applies to both the physical interface (a defined arrangement of restriction or type IIS sites, so that any two parts can be joined by the same reaction — the BioBrick convention of Tom Knight, 2003, and its successors) and to measurement. Reporting promoter activity in arbitrary fluorescence units makes results incomparable between laboratories, instruments and days, so activity is reported **relative to a reference standard** measured in the same experiment — relative promoter units (Jason Kelly and colleagues, 2009), or absolute calibration against fluorescent beads and a reference dye.
:::

The weak point of the scheme is **composition**. Parts are not independent, for reasons that are entirely mechanistic:

- The sequence at a junction changes the behaviour of the parts it joins. The translation initiation rate at a ribosome binding site depends on the mRNA secondary structure around it, which depends on the coding sequence that follows and on the 5′ untranslated region that precedes it — so the same ribosome binding site gives different expression in different constructs, routinely by ten-fold or more.
- Transcription does not stop cleanly. Read-through past a terminator drives the downstream part; convergent transcription interferes; supercoiling generated by transcribing one gene affects its neighbour's promoter.
- A repressor in a circuit is also a load on its own promoter and on anything else that promoter controls, and binding sites sequester the factor, so adding a downstream component changes the upstream signal — **retroactivity**, the biological analogue of loading an electrical output.

Two engineering responses are standard. **Insulation** places a defined element at each junction so that the parts cannot see each other: a self-cleaving ribozyme just after the transcription start site removes the variable 5′ end of the mRNA and makes the downstream ribosome binding site behave consistently, and a "bicistronic" design puts the regulated start codon downstream of a short leader gene whose translation opens the structure. **Characterisation in context** gives up some of the abstraction: parts are measured in the configuration in which they will be used, and libraries of variants are screened for the combination that works, which is the design–build–test–learn cycle rather than design alone.

## The quantitative core

Nearly all circuit reasoning rests on two equations: a transfer function describing how a regulator's concentration sets a promoter's activity, and a balance equation describing how a protein's concentration follows from its promoter.

::: proposition The Hill transfer function {#prop-hill}
Let a repressor at concentration $x$ bind a promoter's operator, and let the promoter be active only when unbound. If binding is cooperative with effective stoichiometry $n$ and dissociation constant such that half-maximal effect occurs at $x = K$, then the promoter's transcription rate is

$$
f(x) = \beta\,\frac{1}{1 + (x/K)^{n}} \qquad\text{(repression)},
$$ {#eq-hill-rep}

and for an activator

$$
f(x) = \beta\,\frac{(x/K)^{n}}{1 + (x/K)^{n}} \qquad\text{(activation)} .
$$ {#eq-hill-act}

Here $\beta$ is the maximal rate, $K$ the **threshold** at which the response is half-maximal, and $n$ the **Hill coefficient**, which measures steepness: $n = 1$ for simple binding, $n > 1$ for cooperative binding, which is what makes a sharp, switch-like response possible. A useful measure of steepness is the ratio of the concentrations giving 90 % and 10 % of the response, which for the activating form is $81^{1/n}$ — a factor of 81 at $n = 1$, of 9 at $n = 2$ and of 3 at $n = 4$.

The Hill form is a **phenomenological** fit, not a mechanism. It can be derived exactly for the simultaneous binding of $n$ identical ligands in the strong-cooperativity limit, and it approximates many real regulatory responses well, with fitted $n$ that need not be an integer and need not equal any molecular stoichiometry. Real promoters also have **leak**: a basal rate $\beta_0 > 0$ even when fully repressed, so [[#eq-hill-rep]] becomes $\beta_0 + (\beta - \beta_0)/(1+(x/K)^n)$, and the ratio $\beta/\beta_0$ — the **dynamic range** — is one of the most important numbers in a part's datasheet.
:::

::: proposition Steady state and response time of one gene {#prop-response}
Let a protein be produced at rate $f$ (molecules per unit volume per unit time) and removed by dilution through growth at specific growth rate $\mu$ and by degradation at rate constant $k_d$, and write $\gamma = \mu + k_d$. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t} = f - \gamma p , \qquad p(t) = p_\mathrm{ss}\left(1 - e^{-\gamma t}\right) + p(0)e^{-\gamma t}, \qquad p_\mathrm{ss} = \frac{f}{\gamma} .
$$ {#eq-one-gene}

The **response time**, conventionally the time to reach half the new steady state, is

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\gamma} ,
$$ {#eq-response-time}

and reaching 90 % takes $\ln 10/\gamma = 3.3$ times longer than reaching 50 %. Two consequences shape every dynamic design:

1. **Response time is set by removal, not by production.** Raising the promoter's strength raises the steady state and leaves the time to get there unchanged. The only ways to make a circuit faster are to grow faster, to add a degradation tag ($k_d$ up), or to use a regulatory structure that overshoots — which is what negative autoregulation does, by producing at a high rate until the product represses itself.
2. **For a stable protein in a bacterium, $\gamma \approx \mu$,** so the circuit's clock is the cell cycle. At $\mu = 1.39$ h⁻¹ (30 min doubling) the half-time is 30 min and 90 % of steady state takes 1.7 h; at $\mu = 0.35$ h⁻¹ it is 2.0 h and 6.6 h. No circuit built from stable proteins in a slowly growing cell can respond in minutes.
:::

::: example Reading a part's datasheet {#ex-datasheet}
A repressible promoter has maximal activity $\beta = 400$ molecules cell⁻¹ h⁻¹ of a stable reporter, leak $\beta_0 = 8$, threshold $K = 50$ nM and $n = 2$. Cells grow with $\mu = 0.7$ h⁻¹. (a) What are the fully induced and fully repressed steady-state levels? (b) What repressor concentration gives half-maximal output, and what gives 10 % of the way from leak to maximum? (c) How long after removing the repressor does the output reach 90 % of its new level? (d) The reporter is given a degradation tag with $k_d = 2.1$ h⁻¹. What happens to (a) and (c)?
::: solution
(a) From [[#eq-one-gene]], $p_\mathrm{ss} = f/\gamma$ with $\gamma = \mu = 0.7$ h⁻¹: induced, $400/0.7 = 571$ molecules per cell; repressed, $8/0.7 = 11$. The dynamic range is 50-fold, the ratio $\beta/\beta_0$, unchanged by the dilution term.

(b) Half-maximal output of the promoter is at $x = K = 50$ nM by definition. For 10 % of the way up from leak, we need $1/(1+(x/K)^2) = 0.1$, so $(x/K)^2 = 9$ and $x = 3K = 150$ nM. For 90 %, $(x/K)^2 = 1/9$ and $x = K/3 = 17$ nM — the 9-fold span predicted by $81^{1/n}$ at $n=2$.

(c) $\ln 10/\gamma = 2.303/0.7 = 3.3$ h.

(d) Now $\gamma = 0.7 + 2.1 = 2.8$ h⁻¹, four times larger. The steady states fall four-fold, to 143 and 2.9 molecules per cell, and the 90 % time falls four-fold to 0.82 h. This is the fundamental trade-off of dynamic design: speed is bought with signal, and a tagged reporter at 2.9 molecules per cell in the repressed state is in the regime where counting noise dominates ([[systems-biology/noise]]).
:::
:::

## Canonical devices

### Inverters and cascades

The elementary device is an **inverter**: an input promoter drives the synthesis of a repressor, which switches off an output promoter. Chaining inverters gives a cascade, which sharpens the overall response — each stage's Hill coefficient multiplies the effective steepness — at the cost of adding one response time per stage. A cascade of three stages with $t_{1/2} = 30$ min each takes a couple of hours to propagate, which is why designs needing speed use direct protein interactions, RNA devices or allosteric sensing rather than cascades of transcription factors.

::: example What a second stage buys and what it costs {#ex-cascade}
Take two identical repressing stages in series, each with output $A/(1+x^{2})$ in units of the next stage's threshold, with $A = 4$. (a) Compute the composite transfer function's 10–90 % input span numerically and compare it with one stage's. (b) What effective Hill coefficient does that correspond to? (c) What is the cost in time, in cells doubling every 30 minutes with stable proteins?
::: solution
(a) The composite output $z(x) = A/\bigl(1+(A/(1+x^{2}))^{2}\bigr)$ rises monotonically from $0.235$ at $x = 0$ to $3.954$ as $x$ grows. Reaching 10 % of that range needs $x = 0.83$ and 90 % needs $x = 3.27$, a span of $3.27/0.83 = 3.9$ — against 9 for a single Hill stage with $n = 2$.

(b) From $81^{1/n} = 3.9$ we get $n_\mathrm{eff} = \ln 81/\ln 3.9 = 3.2$. So the steepness has increased by a factor of about 1.6, not the factor of 2 that naive multiplication of Hill coefficients would suggest — composition sharpens the response, but less than a diagram implies, because the second stage does not operate entirely within its steep region.

(c) Each stage contributes its own response time, $\ln2/\mu = 30$ min to half and $\ln 10/\mu = 100$ min to 90 %. Two stages in series take roughly 3.3 h to settle rather than 1.7 h. Steepness bought with cascade depth is paid for in delay, at about one doubling time per stage.
:::
:::

Logic follows from combining inputs at a promoter: two operators for different repressors give a NOR gate, which is functionally complete, so in principle any logic can be built from inverters alone. In practice the limit is the number of mutually non-interfering repressors available, which motivated the construction of large orthogonal part libraries and, later, the use of guide RNAs with dCas9, where orthogonality comes free because the specificity is in a 20-nucleotide sequence.

::: example Margins in a NOR gate {#ex-nor}
A promoter with two independent operators is repressed by either of two inputs, so its activity is $\beta_0 + (\beta-\beta_0)/\bigl[(1+(x_1/K)^{n})(1+(x_2/K)^{n})\bigr]$. Take $\beta = 400$, $\beta_0 = 8$, $K = 1$, $n = 2$, and inputs that are either "low" at $0.1K$ or "high" at $5K$. (a) Tabulate the four outputs. (b) What is the gate's on–off margin? (c) Is the gate cascadable, and what does the answer depend on?
::: solution
(a) With $(x_1,x_2)$ in units of $K$:

| $x_1$ | $x_2$ | output |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.1 | 392 |
| 0.1 | 5 | 22.9 |
| 5 | 0.1 | 22.9 |
| 5 | 5 | 8.6 |

which is the NOR truth table: high output only when both inputs are low.

(b) The worst case is the lowest "on" output against the highest "off" output, $392/22.9 = 17$-fold. Note that the two "off" states differ three-fold from each other: with one input high the other operator is still only partly occupied, so the output sits well above the leak of 8.6. The nominal dynamic range of 50-fold ($\beta/\beta_0$) overstates the usable margin by a factor of three.

(c) Cascadable only if the next stage's threshold lies inside the margin and its own steepness restores a clean separation — that is, if $K_\text{next}$ sits between 23 and 392, and comfortably so, since both numbers vary between cells. This is the central practical difficulty of building large circuits from transcriptional logic: each stage must convert a degraded signal back into a clean one, and the margin available for doing it shrinks as leak rises and as repression becomes less complete. Raising the "high" input from $5K$ to $20K$ would push the single-high state down to about 9.0 and widen the margin to 44-fold, which is why gates are driven hard.
:::
:::

### The toggle switch

::: proposition Bistability in mutual repression {#prop-toggle}
Let two repressors at concentrations $x$ and $y$ each repress the other's promoter, with identical parameters. Non-dimensionalising concentrations by the threshold $K$ and time by $1/\gamma$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{a}{1+y^{n}} - x , \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{a}{1+x^{n}} - y ,
$$ {#eq-toggle}

where $a = \beta/(\gamma K)$ measures how strongly each gene is expressed relative to the threshold of the promoter it controls. Steady states satisfy $x = a/(1+y^n)$ and $y = a/(1+x^n)$, which always admits a symmetric solution $x = y = x^{*}$ where $x^{*}(1+x^{*n}) = a$.

The symmetric state is unstable, and the system **bistable**, when the loop gain exceeds one. Writing $F(y) = a/(1+y^n)$ for the map from one variable to the other, the condition is $\lvert F'(x^{*})\rvert > 1$, that is

$$
\frac{a\,n\,x^{*\,n-1}}{\left(1+x^{*\,n}\right)^{2}} > 1 .
$$ {#eq-bistability}

Two conclusions follow. **Cooperativity is necessary:** at $n = 1$ one can show $\lvert F'\rvert < 1$ for every $a$, so a pair of non-cooperative repressors cannot be bistable however strongly they are expressed. And **expression must be strong enough:** at $n = 2$ the condition reduces to $a > 2$ exactly (at $a=2$ the symmetric point sits at $x^{*}=1$ with $\lvert F'\rvert = 1$). Above the threshold the system has two stable states — one repressor high and the other low, or the reverse — and a transient input pushes it from one to the other, where it stays after the input is removed. That is cellular **memory** built from two genes.

Leak weakens the switch: a promoter that cannot be repressed below $\beta_0$ puts a floor under the "low" state, which both narrows the parameter range for bistability and makes the two states less distinguishable.
:::

### Oscillators

A ring of three repressors, each switching off the next, has no stable state it can rest in and can oscillate: the **repressilator** (Michael Elowitz and Stanislas Leibler, 2000). Sustained oscillation requires enough **delay** around the loop and enough nonlinearity: with first-order protein dynamics and shallow Hill functions the system spirals into the symmetric steady state instead. The original repressilator oscillated, but noisily — the period varied between cells and phase was lost within a few cycles — which was itself an important result, because it showed that the stochasticity of gene expression is a first-order design constraint rather than a detail. Later designs restored regularity by adding a slow negative feedback loop, by coupling cells through a diffusible quorum signal so that a population synchronises, and by reducing the sources of noise; the mathematics is developed in [[systems-biology/switches-oscillators]].

### Sensors and reporters

A **biosensor** has three parts: a sensing element that responds to the analyte (a ligand-binding transcription factor, a two-component system, a riboswitch, an aptamer), a transducing circuit that may amplify or threshold the signal, and an output that can be read (fluorescence, colour, luminescence, an electrochemical signal). Two design pressures dominate. **Sensitivity**: the analyte concentration of interest must sit near the sensor's threshold $K$, which is why engineering a sensor often means shifting $K$ rather than improving the output. And **specificity**: natural regulators respond to families of related molecules, so cross-reactivity has to be measured, not assumed.

Cell-free formats — the circuit freeze-dried on paper with a cell extract, rehydrated by the sample — avoid the questions of containment and viability that a living sensor raises, respond in minutes rather than hours because there is no growth to wait for, and are a well-developed route to field diagnostics.

::: application A heavy-metal biosensor, and what its numbers have to be
Arsenic contamination of well water is a large-scale public-health problem, and arsenic-responsive transcription factors exist naturally in bacteria that must cope with it. A sensor built on one of them couples the factor's promoter to a visible reporter. For the device to be useful, its threshold must lie at the concentration that matters — guideline values for drinking water are around 10 μg L⁻¹, which for arsenite is about 0.13 μmol L⁻¹ — so the sensing promoter's $K$ must be engineered to within a factor of a few of that value, and the response must be steep enough that a user can distinguish "above" from "below" by eye. From [[#prop-hill]], an $n$ of 1 would need an 81-fold concentration change to move from 10 % to 90 % of full output, which is useless for a threshold test; $n \approx 3$ needs only a factor of 4. Hence the characteristic architecture of such sensors: a sensing stage whose threshold is tuned, followed by a thresholding stage (a toggle, a cascade or a sequestering decoy) that sharpens the response. The remaining engineering is mostly about the things that have nothing to do with circuits — shelf life, interference by other ions, reproducibility between batches, and whether the result is trusted by the person reading it.
:::

## Why circuits fail: the cell is not a chassis

::: definition Burden and resource competition {#def-burden}
**Burden** is the reduction in host growth rate caused by expressing a construct. Its origin is not mysterious: transcription and translation of a construct consume RNA polymerases, ribosomes, charged tRNAs, nucleotides, amino acids and ATP, all of which are finite and all of which the cell was already using for its own growth. A strongly expressed, useless protein can slow growth by tens of per cent.

Burden creates two effects that no circuit diagram shows. First, **resource competition couples every component to every other**: inducing one gene reduces the expression of an unrelated gene elsewhere in the cell, because they draw on the same ribosome pool. Measured in engineered strains, inducing one strong construct can depress an unrelated reporter by a half. Second, because the dilution rate $\gamma$ in [[#eq-one-gene]] contains $\mu$, slowing growth *raises* the steady-state concentration of every stable protein, so a burden-induced slowdown feeds back positively on the concentration of the very protein causing it.
:::

Three further realities complete the picture.

**Noise.** Transcription and translation involve small numbers of molecules and bursty events, so genetically identical cells in identical conditions differ several-fold in the expression of the same gene. A circuit that is bistable on average will have cells that flip spontaneously; a sensor with a threshold will have cells on the wrong side of it. Noise can be designed around (more molecules, feedback, filtering through a slow stage) and occasionally designed *with*, in circuits that deliberately diversify a population ([[systems-biology/noise]]).

**Evolutionary instability.** A construct that costs the cell growth is selected against. Mutants that delete, truncate or silence it arise at the usual rates and, if they grow faster, take over.

::: example How long does a costly circuit last? {#ex-instability}
A construct reduces the growth rate by 5 %, so a cell that has inactivated it has a selective advantage $s = 0.05$ per generation. Inactivating mutations arise at about $10^{-6}$ per cell per generation and are initially present at roughly that frequency. (a) After how many generations do the non-functional cells reach half the population? (b) A fermentation involves about 30 generations from the seed vial to harvest. Is this circuit safe? (c) A second design costs 0.5 %. (d) What design choices change the answer qualitatively?
::: solution
(a) While the mutant is rare its frequency grows roughly as $q(t) = q_0 e^{st}$, so reaching $q = 0.5$ takes $t = \ln(0.5/10^{-6})/0.05 = \ln(5\times10^{5})/0.05 = 13.1/0.05 = 262$ generations.

(b) Over 30 generations the mutant frequency rises from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-6}e^{1.5} = 4.5\times10^{-6}$ — negligible. The circuit is entirely safe at this scale, and the arithmetic matters because a result of this kind is often asserted rather than calculated.

(c) At $s = 0.005$ the half-takeover time is 2620 generations. But note the asymmetry: the time scales as $1/s$ while the damage is done over the whole run, so a lower cost buys proportionally more time and nothing else changes.

(d) Qualitative changes come from changing the sign of selection, not its size. Couple the construct to something the cell needs — place an essential gene inside it, or make the product satisfy an auxotrophy — and a cell that loses the circuit dies rather than outgrowing its neighbours. The other qualitative change is to remove the repeats and homologies that make deletion easy, since a tandem duplication or a repeated promoter raises the mutation rate at that locus by orders of magnitude above the baseline used here.
:::
:::

**Context.** The same construct behaves differently in a different strain, at a different temperature, in rich rather than minimal medium, in exponential rather than stationary phase, and in a 10 m³ vessel rather than a shake flask. Much of the gap between a published circuit and a usable one consists of this.

::: warning Modularity is an engineering goal, not a property of cells
It is tempting to read the parts–devices–systems hierarchy as a description of how biology works. It is not: it is a description of how we would like to be able to think. Parts interact through mRNA structure at their junctions, through competition for polymerases and ribosomes, through the growth rate, through supercoiling, through the proteases and chaperones they load, and through the fitness cost they impose. Characterising a part in one context therefore does *not* predict its behaviour in another, and the measured failure rate of naive composition is high. The useful conclusions are practical: insulate junctions deliberately, measure in context, keep expression as low as the function allows, include a reference standard in every experiment, and expect to iterate. A design that works first time is evidence of a well-characterised context, not of a general law.
:::

::: quiz
A three-stage transcriptional cascade is built to detect an input and switch on a reporter. The response is correctly inverted at each stage but takes four hours, and the designers want it in under one hour, in cells doubling every 40 minutes. Which change is most likely to achieve it?
- [ ] Use stronger promoters at each stage.
- [x] Add degradation tags to the intermediate repressors, and shorten the cascade.
- [ ] Increase the plasmid copy number.
- [ ] Increase the Hill coefficient of each stage.
::: solution
Response time is set by the removal rate $\gamma = \mu + k_d$ ([[#eq-response-time]]), not by the production rate, so stronger promoters and higher copy number raise the output levels and leave the timing almost unchanged. Degradation tags raise $k_d$ and thereby shorten every stage's response time, and removing a stage removes its delay entirely. Steeper Hill functions sharpen the input–output relation but do not speed up the approach to steady state. The costs of the winning option are lower signal (the levels fall by the same factor as the times) and the loss of whatever the removed stage contributed to steepness.
:::
:::

## Writing at genome scale

The ambition of designing from first principles returns at the level of whole genomes, where the engineering is more tractable precisely because the objective is clearer.

**Minimal genomes.** Starting from the already small genome of *Mycoplasma mycoides*, the team that had synthesised it in 2010 systematically removed genes to produce JCVI-syn3.0 in 2016: a self-replicating cell with 473 genes and about 531 kb. The result is as much a question as an achievement, since the function of roughly a sixth of the retained genes was unknown — a minimal cell turns out to be a demonstration of how much we do not know rather than of how much we do.

**Synthetic chromosomes.** The Sc2.0 project has rebuilt the chromosomes of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* in synthetic form, removing introns, transposons and repeats, recoding stop codons and inserting recombination sites between genes so that the genome can be deliberately scrambled to generate variation. The yeast tolerates these changes remarkably well, which is itself informative about how much of a genome's sequence is required.

**Recoding.** Because the genetic code is redundant, a genome can be rewritten to use fewer codons. A strain of *E. coli* in which all occurrences of two serine codons and one stop codon were replaced throughout the genome — 18,214 substitutions across 4 Mb, reported by Julius Fredens, Jason Chin and colleagues in 2019 — grows using 61 codons. Freeing codons makes room for non-canonical amino acids ([[biotechnology/protein-production]]), and a recoded organism that no longer reads certain codons is also resistant to viruses that depend on them, and cannot express genes it acquires by horizontal transfer in the usual way. That last property has been proposed as a form of genetic **biocontainment**, alongside synthetic auxotrophy, in which an organism depends on a nutrient supplied only in the laboratory. Containment of this kind is one of the few engineered safeguards whose failure rate can be measured, which is why it is discussed at length in [[biotechnology/biosafety-ethics]].

::: intuition Why synthesis changes the kind of question that can be asked
As long as a genome could only be mutated, the experiments available were local: change one gene, see what breaks. When a genome can be written, global questions become experimental. Is this intron needed? Are these 20 codons needed? Would the organism work with the genes in a different order, or with all the tRNA genes gathered in one place? These are not questions about mechanism but about design — which features of a genome are requirements and which are historical accidents — and until the 2010s there was no way to ask them.
:::

::: history
Synthetic biology's modern phase began with two papers in *Nature* in January 2000: Timothy Gardner, Charles Cantor and James Collins built a bistable toggle switch from two mutually repressing promoters in *E. coli*, and Michael Elowitz and Stanislas Leibler built the repressilator, an oscillator from a three-repressor ring, and reported both its oscillation and its noisiness. Tom Knight proposed the BioBrick standard for physical composition of parts in 2003, the Registry of Standard Biological Parts was established at MIT, and the iGEM competition began in 2004, which did much to spread the discipline's vocabulary. Jason Kelly and colleagues proposed relative promoter units as a measurement standard in 2009. Jay Keasling's reconstruction of a terpenoid pathway in microbes from 2003 onwards showed what the approach could produce ([[biotechnology/metabolic-engineering]]). Daniel Gibson and colleagues transplanted a chemically synthesised 1.08 Mb genome into a recipient cell in 2010, and the minimal cell JCVI-syn3.0 followed in 2016. Christopher Voigt's laboratory published the Cello design compiler in 2016, which took a logic specification and produced DNA sequences for circuits that worked on first build at a useful rate — the clearest demonstration so far that the engineering programme can be made to deliver. Julius Fredens, Jason Chin and colleagues reported the 61-codon *E. coli* strain Syn61 in 2019. Throughout, the field has been accompanied by its own governance literature, since writing genomes raises questions about biosecurity and about the ownership of biological designs that the technical work does not answer.
:::

::: summary
- Synthetic biology applies abstraction (parts, devices, systems), standardisation (physical interfaces and relative units) and decoupling to biological engineering, and proceeds by design–build–test–learn cycles rather than design alone.
- Composition is the weak point: junction sequences change ribosome binding, transcription reads through terminators, and downstream components load upstream signals (retroactivity). Insulating elements and in-context characterisation are the standard responses.
- Regulation is modelled with Hill functions ([[#eq-hill-rep]], [[#eq-hill-act]]): threshold $K$, maximal rate $\beta$, leak $\beta_0$, Hill coefficient $n$. The 10–90 % span is $81^{1/n}$, so cooperativity is what makes switching possible.
- For one gene, $p_\mathrm{ss} = f/\gamma$ and $t_{1/2} = \ln2/\gamma$ with $\gamma = \mu + k_d$ ([[#eq-one-gene]], [[#eq-response-time]]): stronger promoters change levels, not speed; only faster growth, degradation tags or feedback change speed.
- Mutual repression is bistable when the loop gain exceeds one ([[#eq-bistability]]): impossible at $n=1$, and at $n=2$ requiring $a = \beta/(\gamma K) > 2$. A three-repressor ring can oscillate given enough delay and nonlinearity, noisily.
- Cells are not passive chassis: burden couples all components through shared ribosomes and through the growth rate, expression is noisy, costly constructs are selected against (half-takeover in roughly $\ln(1/q_0)/s$ generations), and behaviour depends on strain, medium and scale.
- At genome scale, synthesis allows minimal genomes (JCVI-syn3.0, 473 genes), synthetic chromosomes (Sc2.0) and codon recoding (Syn61), the last of which also offers measurable genetic containment.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Steepness {level=1 check="9"}
By what factor must an activator's concentration change to move a Hill-type promoter with $n = 2$ from 10 % to 90 % of its maximal activity?
::: solution
$81^{1/n} = 81^{1/2} = 9$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Response time {level=1 check="1.98"}
A stable protein is expressed in cells growing at $\mu = 0.35$ h⁻¹. How long, in hours, to reach half of a new steady state after induction? Two decimal places.
::: solution
$\gamma = \mu = 0.35$ h⁻¹ and $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/0.35 = 1.98$ h.
:::
:::

::: exercise Dynamic range {level=1 check="40"}
A promoter has maximal activity 320 and leak 8 in the same units. What is its dynamic range (fold-change)?
::: solution
$320/8 = 40$-fold. Note that the dilution rate cancels in the ratio, so the fold-change of the steady-state protein level is the same 40 as the fold-change of promoter activity — provided the protein is removed the same way in both states.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bistability by calculation {level=2}
For the symmetric toggle of [[#eq-toggle]] with $n = 2$, find the symmetric steady state at $a = 4$ and test [[#eq-bistability]]. Then repeat at $a = 1.5$ and interpret.
::: solution
At $a = 4$: solve $x^{*}(1+x^{*2}) = 4$, that is $x^{*3}+x^{*}-4 = 0$, giving $x^{*} = 1.379$. Then

$$
\frac{a n x^{*}}{(1+x^{*2})^{2}} = \frac{4\times2\times1.379}{(1+1.901)^{2}} = \frac{11.03}{8.42} = 1.31 > 1 ,
$$

so the symmetric state is unstable and the system is bistable, consistent with $a > 2$.

At $a = 1.5$: $x^{*3}+x^{*}-1.5 = 0$ gives $x^{*} = 0.835$, and the gain is $1.5\times2\times0.835/(1+0.697)^{2} = 2.50/2.88 = 0.87 < 1$. The symmetric state is stable and is the only steady state: the circuit has no memory. Both repressors sit at an intermediate level, each partly repressing the other — a design that would look correct on a diagram and do nothing useful.
:::
:::

::: exercise Speed against signal {level=2 check="4"}
A reporter is given a degradation tag that raises $k_d$ from $\approx 0$ to $3\mu$. By what factor does the response time shorten, and by what factor does the steady-state level fall?
::: solution
$\gamma$ goes from $\mu$ to $\mu + 3\mu = 4\mu$, so $t_{1/2} = \ln2/\gamma$ falls four-fold and $p_\mathrm{ss} = f/\gamma$ also falls four-fold. The product $p_\mathrm{ss}\,t_{1/2}$ is invariant, which is the clearest statement of the trade-off: at fixed promoter strength, dynamic range in time can only be bought with dynamic range in signal.
:::
:::

::: exercise Circuit stability over a fermentation {level=2 check="380"}
A construct costs 3 % in growth rate, so cells that inactivate it have $s = 0.03$ per generation. Starting from a mutant frequency of $10^{-7}$, after how many generations do the non-functional cells reach 1 % of the population? Round to two significant figures.
::: solution
While the mutant is rare, $q(t) \approx q_0 e^{st}$, so

$$
t = \frac{1}{s}\ln\frac{q}{q_0} = \frac{\ln(10^{-2}/10^{-7})}{0.03} = \frac{11.51}{0.03} = 384 \approx 380\ \text{generations}.
$$

The time scales as $1/s$, so halving the cost doubles the time; and because a typical production run is a few tens of generations, this construct is stable over a run but not over a long continuous culture.
:::
:::

::: exercise Resource competition, diagnosed {level=2}
Two independent cassettes are in one cell: a constitutive red reporter and an inducible green one. As the green inducer is raised, green rises and red falls by 40 %. The designers conclude that the green construct represses the red promoter. (a) Give a simpler explanation. (b) Design an experiment that distinguishes the two. (c) What does the result imply for a circuit of ten components?
::: solution
(a) Resource competition: translating a large amount of green protein diverts ribosomes (and other shared resources) from all other mRNAs including red's, and the induced burden slows growth, which changes dilution for both proteins. No regulatory interaction is needed.

(b) Replace the green coding sequence with one encoding a different, equally expressed but functionally unrelated protein — or with a non-functional variant of the same length. If red still falls, the effect is burden and not regulation. Complementary tests: express green from a different promoter with the same output level and see whether the effect tracks the output rather than the promoter; measure growth rate and plot red against growth rate rather than against inducer; and use a capacity monitor, a constitutive reporter whose only purpose is to report the cell's spare expression capacity.

(c) That the components cannot be designed independently. Every added component reduces the output of all the others, so a circuit that works when its parts are tested singly may fail when assembled, and the total expression budget — not any individual part's strength — becomes the design constraint. This is the main practical reason that successful large circuits use the weakest expression that gives the required function.
:::
:::

::: exercise Designing a thresholding sensor {level=3}
You need a device that turns on sharply when a metabolite exceeds 50 μM. The available sensing transcription factor has $K = 5$ μM and $n = 1$. (a) Why is the sensor unusable as it stands, on both counts? (b) Describe two distinct ways to move the threshold and two to sharpen the response, saying what each costs. (c) How would you test whether the finished device has the intended threshold?
::: solution
(a) The threshold is ten-fold too low, so at 50 μM the sensor is already saturated and cannot distinguish 50 from 500 μM; and with $n = 1$ the response spans a factor of 81 in concentration between 10 % and 90 % output, so there is no sharp "on" at any concentration.

(b) Moving the threshold: mutate the factor's ligand-binding site to weaken binding (direct, but each variant must be characterised, and weakened binding often means a weaker maximal response); or add a competing sink that absorbs the metabolite — a binding protein or a consuming enzyme — so that the free concentration reaching the sensor is a tenth of the total (easy to implement, but it consumes the analyte and introduces a time dependence). Sharpening: put the sensor's output through a toggle or another positive-feedback stage, which converts a graded input into a near-step response and adds memory (at the cost of irreversibility, which may be unwanted); or add a sequestering decoy — a protein or set of binding sites that soaks up the output regulator until it is saturated, giving an apparent threshold (cheap and effective, but the threshold depends on the decoy's expression level, which varies with cell state).

(c) Build a dose–response curve: many concentrations spanning two decades, measured in the final format at steady state, with a reference standard in every run so that the numbers are comparable; fit $K$ and $n$ to [[#eq-hill-act]] rather than reading them off by eye. Then test the things the model omits: reproducibility between batches, interference by chemically related metabolites, behaviour in the real matrix rather than in defined medium, and the spread between single cells — a population mean of "on" can hide a bimodal distribution in which half the cells are off.
:::
:::

::: exercise A circuit that works in a flask and fails in a fermenter {level=3}
An inducible production circuit gives the designed behaviour in 50 mL shake flasks and fails at 500 L: output is a third as high, and a quarter of the cells have lost the plasmid by harvest. List four mechanistic explanations and, for each, a measurement that would support or refute it.
::: solution
Possibilities, with tests: (i) **Growth-rate change.** The large culture grows more slowly (oxygen limitation, mixing, pH control), and since $p_\mathrm{ss} = f/\gamma$ with $\gamma \approx \mu$, output should then *rise*, not fall — so measuring $\mu$ in both scales distinguishes the direction and may exclude this. (ii) **Gradients.** A large vessel is not well mixed: cells experience fluctuating inducer, oxygen and substrate concentrations as they circulate, and a circuit with a response time of hours integrates these in ways a flask never tests. Measure the mixing time and compare with the circuit's $t_{1/2}$; test in a flask with deliberately pulsed inducer. (iii) **Plasmid loss under weaker selection.** Antibiotic degrades over a long run and selection lapses; combine this with a few per cent fitness cost and [[#ex-instability]] gives substantial loss over the extra generations from a 500 L inoculum train. Measure plasmid-bearing fraction and antibiotic concentration against time, and count the generations from vial to harvest. (iv) **Mutational inactivation rather than loss.** Sequence the construct from end-of-run isolates: cells that retain a plasmid with a deleted promoter look plasmid-positive and produce nothing. (v) **Burden interacting with the longer run**: measure a capacity reporter at both scales.

The general lesson is that scale changes the *environment* of the circuit — number of generations, uniformity, and the duration over which selection acts — rather than any of its parts, which is why [[biotechnology/bioprocessing]] treats these as engineering variables in their own right.
:::
