A motor neuron in your spinal cord has a cell body about a twentieth of a millimetre across, yet its axon runs to a muscle in your foot, a metre away. Every protein that axon needs at its far end is made in or near the cell body and must be carried there. The same cell receives thousands of contacts from other neurons on its dendrites, adds up their effects within a few milliseconds, and decides whether to send an electrical impulse down the axon. Around it, and outnumbering it in many parts of the brain, are glial cells that wrap its axon in insulation, clear away the transmitter it releases, buffer the potassium it loses, and clean up after injury.

This chapter introduces the two kinds of cells that make up nervous systems, **neurons** and **glia**: their shapes, their parts and what each part does, how they were discovered to be separate cells, and the scales of size, number and time that the rest of the course works with. The general machinery of eukaryotic cells (nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, cytoskeleton) is in [[cell-biology/organelles]] and [[cell-biology/cytoskeleton]]; here we ask what is special about cells whose job is to carry signals.

## The neuron doctrine

In the middle of the nineteenth century the nervous system was a puzzle under the microscope. Nerve tissue is soft, densely packed and almost transparent, and the stains of the day showed a tangle of fibres in which no one could follow a single cell from end to end. Many anatomists concluded that nerve fibres fuse into a continuous network, the **reticulum**, through which nervous activity flows as it would through a system of pipes.

In 1873 the Italian physician Camillo Golgi published a method, which he called the *reazione nera* ("black reaction"), in which tissue hardened in potassium dichromate and then soaked in silver nitrate develops black deposits of silver chromate. For reasons still not fully understood, the reaction stains only a small fraction of the neurons in a block of tissue, perhaps one in a hundred, but stains each of those completely, with all its branches. Against an unstained background, single neurons stood out for the first time.

Golgi himself remained a reticularist. The Spanish histologist Santiago Ramón y Cajal took up his stain in 1887 and, working first on the cerebellum of birds and on embryonic tissue, where cells are smaller and less myelinated, argued from 1888 onwards that the fibres of each nerve cell end freely, close to other cells but not fused with them. In the following decades Cajal described almost every region of the vertebrate nervous system and drew thousands of cells. He and Golgi shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1906 and used their lectures to disagree.

::: definition The neuron doctrine {#def-neuron-doctrine}
The **neuron doctrine** holds that:

1. the nervous system is made of discrete cells, **neurons**, each with its own membrane, which are the structural and functional units of the system;
2. neurons communicate at specialised points of contact, **synapses**, where their membranes come close but remain separate;
3. each neuron is **polarised**: signals normally enter through the dendrites and cell body and leave through the axon (Cajal's *law of dynamic polarisation*).
:::

The name *neuron* was coined by Wilhelm Waldeyer in 1891, in a review that made Cajal's work widely known, and Charles Sherrington introduced *synapse* in 1897 for the functional junction between neurons, which he inferred from the delay and one-way conduction of spinal reflexes. Direct proof of separation had to wait for the electron microscope: in 1955 Sanford Palay and George Palade, and independently Eduardo De Robertis and H. Stanley Bennett, showed a gap of about 20 nm between the membranes at chemical synapses, the **synaptic cleft**, with clusters of small vesicles on one side.

::: warning "The neuron doctrine has no exceptions"
The doctrine is an excellent first approximation, not a law. Many neurons are joined by **gap junctions**, channels that connect the cytoplasm of two cells and pass current directly ([[neuroscience/synapses]]). Dendrites can release transmitter onto other dendrites, and some axons receive synapses. The giant axon of the squid, the model for most of the next two chapters, is formed by the fusion of the axons of hundreds of small neurons, and in 2023 Pawel Burkhardt and colleagues reported that the subepithelial nerve net of the ctenophore *Mnemiopsis leidyi* is a true syncytium, with continuous membranes between neurons. Golgi was not entirely wrong; Cajal was right about the great majority of neurons.
:::

## The parts of a neuron

Neurons come in a bewildering variety of shapes, but almost all share four regions, each specialised for one step in handling a signal.

::: definition Regions of a neuron {#def-neuron}
- The **cell body** (soma, perikaryon) contains the nucleus and most of the protein-synthesising machinery. The rough endoplasmic reticulum of neurons is so abundant that it stains as clumps, the **Nissl bodies**.
- **Dendrites** are branched, tapering processes that receive synaptic input. In many neurons they carry **dendritic spines**, protrusions about 1 µm long on which most excitatory synapses are made.
- The **axon** is a single, usually long process of nearly constant diameter that carries action potentials away from the cell body. It begins at the **axon hillock** and **axon initial segment** (AIS), a stretch of some tens of micrometres with a very high density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, where action potentials are normally initiated. It may branch into **collaterals** and ends in **terminals** (boutons) that form synapses.
- **Presynaptic terminals** contain synaptic vesicles filled with transmitter, mitochondria, and the proteins that dock the vesicles and fuse them with the membrane when Ca²⁺ enters.
:::

The cell body and dendrites form the **receptive** part of the neuron, the axon hillock and initial segment the **integrative** part, the axon the **conductive** part and the terminals the **output** part. The division is functional as well as anatomical: the membrane of each region carries different ion channels, receptors and adhesion molecules, and the cell keeps them apart. A diffusion barrier at the initial segment, organised by the scaffold protein ankyrin-G and the cytoskeletal protein βIV-spectrin, prevents membrane proteins of the soma and dendrites from drifting into the axon, much as tight junctions separate the apical and basolateral membranes of an epithelial cell.

Neurons are classified in several overlapping ways.

- **By the number of processes.** **Multipolar** neurons have many dendrites and one axon; most vertebrate neurons are of this kind. **Bipolar** neurons have one dendrite and one axon, as in the retina and the olfactory epithelium. **Pseudounipolar** neurons, such as the sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglia, have a single process that divides into a peripheral branch running to the skin or muscle and a central branch entering the spinal cord; the impulse passes from one branch to the other without going through the cell body. Most invertebrate neurons are **unipolar**: one process leaves the cell body and branches into dendritic and axonal parts, and the cell bodies sit in a rind around the outside of the ganglion, away from the synaptic neuropil in the centre.
- **By function.** **Sensory** (afferent) neurons carry signals from receptors towards the central nervous system; **motor** (efferent) neurons carry them to muscles and glands; **interneurons** connect neurons with one another and make up the great majority of cells in any brain. Interneurons whose axons stay within a local region are **local circuit neurons**; those whose axons leave it are **projection neurons**.
- **By transmitter and effect.** Neurons are **excitatory** or **inhibitory** according to the effect of their main transmitter on the cells they contact. In the vertebrate brain, most projection neurons release glutamate and are excitatory, and most local interneurons release GABA or glycine and are inhibitory.
- **By shape and position.** The **pyramidal cells** of the cerebral cortex and hippocampus have a triangular cell body, a long apical dendrite running towards the surface and a skirt of basal dendrites. The **Purkinje cells** of the cerebellum spread a flat, fan-shaped dendritic tree that receives up to about 200,000 synapses from parallel fibres. The tiny **granule cells** of the cerebellum, with four or five short dendrites each, are the most numerous neurons in the vertebrate brain.

Since about 2015, single-cell RNA sequencing has added a further classification by the genes each cell expresses. Atlases of the mouse brain now distinguish thousands of transcriptomic cell types, grouped into a few hundred broader classes. How these molecular types map onto shapes, connections and functions is one of the active questions of the field.

::: quiz
A neuron in a dorsal root ganglion has a single process that splits into a branch going to the skin and a branch entering the spinal cord. Where is its receptive region, and where does its impulse pass the cell body?
- [ ] Its dendrites are on the cell body, and the impulse is generated there.
- [x] Its receptive endings are in the skin; the impulse travels along the peripheral branch and continues into the central branch, bypassing the cell body.
- [ ] The cell body is the receptive region, and the impulse travels outward to the skin.
- [ ] It has no axon, because it has only one process.
::: solution
This is a pseudounipolar sensory neuron. Its sensory endings in the skin are the receptive part; the impulse they set up runs along the peripheral branch, which behaves as an axon, and continues into the central branch at the T-junction. The cell body hangs to the side of the pathway and need not be invaded by the impulse at all. Its job is to maintain the cell, not to integrate.
:::
:::

## The neuron as a long, thin cell

What makes neurons unusual among cells is their geometry. A typical animal cell is roughly compact, ten or twenty micrometres across in every direction. Neurons extend processes over millimetres or metres, and most of their volume and membrane is far from the nucleus.

::: example How much of a motor neuron is axon? {#ex-volume}
Model a human spinal motor neuron as a spherical cell body of diameter 50 µm and an unbranched cylindrical axon of diameter 10 µm and length 1.0 m, ignoring dendrites and terminals. Compare the volumes and the membrane areas of the cell body and the axon.
::: solution
Cell body: radius $R = 25$ µm, so volume $\tfrac43\pi R^3 = 6.5\times10^4\ \mu\mathrm{m}^3$ and area $4\pi R^2 = 7.9\times10^3\ \mu\mathrm{m}^2$.

Axon: radius $r = 5$ µm, length $L = 10^6$ µm, so volume $\pi r^2 L = 7.9\times10^7\ \mu\mathrm{m}^3$ and area $2\pi r L = 3.1\times10^7\ \mu\mathrm{m}^2$.

The axon has about $1200$ times the volume of the cell body and about $4000$ times its membrane area. In other words, more than 99.9 % of this cell's cytoplasm is in its axon, a metre from the nucleus that makes all its messenger RNA. Real motor neurons also have large dendritic trees, but the conclusion stands: the cell body is a small factory supplying a very long, thin structure.
:::
:::

This geometry creates a supply problem. Proteins, lipids and organelles made in the cell body must reach the terminals, and worn-out components and signals from the target must come back. Could diffusion do the job?

::: proposition Diffusion time grows with the square of distance {#prop-diffusion}
A molecule with diffusion coefficient $D$, starting at a point, has a mean squared displacement along one axis after time $t$ of

$$
\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt,
$$ {#eq-diffusion}

so the time typically needed to diffuse a distance $x$ along a thin process is of the order of

$$
t \approx \frac{x^2}{2D}.
$$

The result holds for free random motion in one dimension (a long, thin cylinder) with no flow, binding or barriers. Binding to fixed structures, crowding and obstacles all slow diffusion further, so the formula is a lower bound on the time.
:::

The squared dependence is the crucial point. Diffusion is fast over short distances and hopeless over long ones. Doubling the distance quadruples the time.

::: example Diffusion against transport in an axon {#ex-transport}
A protein of medium size has a diffusion coefficient in cytoplasm of about $D = 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ (10 µm² s⁻¹). Estimate how long it would take to travel by diffusion (a) across a cell body, 10 µm; (b) along 1 mm of axon; (c) along a 1 m axon. Compare (c) with **fast axonal transport** at 400 mm per day and **slow axonal transport** at 1 mm per day.
::: solution
From [[#prop-diffusion]], $t \approx x^2/2D$.

(a) $x = 10^{-5}$ m: $t \approx 10^{-10}/(2\times10^{-11}) = 5$ s.

(b) $x = 10^{-3}$ m: $t \approx 10^{-6}/(2\times10^{-11}) = 5\times10^{4}$ s, about 14 hours.

(c) $x = 1$ m: $t \approx 1/(2\times10^{-11}) = 5\times10^{10}$ s, about 1600 years.

Fast transport covers 1 m in $1000/400 = 2.5$ days, slow transport in about 1000 days, nearly three years. Diffusion is adequate within the cell body and in short processes, but every long axon depends on active, motor-driven transport.
:::
:::

**Axonal transport** comes in two broad speeds. **Fast transport**, at roughly 50–400 mm per day (up to about 5 µm s⁻¹), carries membrane-bound cargo, such as vesicle precursors, mitochondria and endosomes, along microtubules. In the axon nearly all microtubules point the same way, with their fast-growing plus ends towards the terminal, so **kinesin** motors, which walk towards plus ends, carry cargo outwards (**anterograde** transport), and **cytoplasmic dynein**, which walks towards minus ends, carries it back (**retrograde** transport). Retrograde transport returns damaged organelles for degradation and carries signals from the target, such as the neurotrophins that keep many neurons alive ([[neuroscience/neural-development]]). **Slow transport**, at roughly 0.2–8 mm per day, moves cytoskeletal proteins and soluble enzymes; it is now understood as fast movements interrupted by long pauses, giving a slow average. The motors themselves are described in [[cell-biology/cytoskeleton]].

Even with $D = 50$ µm² s⁻¹, faster than most proteins move in cytoplasm, diffusion falls behind slow transport after a few millimetres.

Neurons also make some proteins locally. Messenger RNAs for cytoskeletal and synaptic proteins are carried into dendrites and, in many neurons, into axons, and are translated near the synapses that need them. Local translation in dendrites is required for some forms of lasting synaptic change ([[neuroscience/plasticity-learning]]).

::: application When transport fails
Because the longest axons are the most dependent on transport, defects in motor proteins or microtubules tend to affect them first. Several inherited peripheral neuropathies in humans are caused by mutations in kinesins, dynein or the proteins that link cargo to them, and the first symptoms typically appear in the feet, at the ends of the longest axons. The clinical side is covered in [Medicine Atlas: neuromuscular disorders](/learn/medicine/lesson.php?c=nervous-system&l=neuromuscular); the biological point is that geometry makes neurons vulnerable in ways that compact cells are not.
:::

### The cost of a nervous system

Neurons are expensive. Pumping back the Na⁺ and K⁺ that cross the membrane during signalling uses most of their ATP ([[neuroscience/resting-potential]]). The adult human brain is about 2 % of body mass but accounts for roughly 20 % of the resting body's oxygen consumption. Insects pay similar costs in proportion: the photoreceptors and visual neurons of the blowfly retina consume a substantial fraction of the fly's resting energy budget. Energy is one reason why nervous systems use sparse codes, in which few neurons are active at once ([[neuroscience/neural-coding]]), and why the brains of animals that lose the need for a sense, such as cave fish that lose their eyes, shrink the corresponding regions.

## How many neurons?

Nervous systems range from a few hundred neurons to hundreds of billions.

| animal | neurons (approximate) | source of the number |
|---|---|---|
| nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* (hermaphrodite) | 302 | complete reconstruction by electron microscopy (White and colleagues, 1986) |
| fruit fly *Drosophila melanogaster*, adult brain | about 140,000 | FlyWire whole-brain connectome (2024) |
| honeybee *Apis mellifera* | about 1 million | cell counts |
| laboratory mouse *Mus musculus*, brain | about 70 million | isotropic fractionator counts |
| human, brain | about 86 billion | isotropic fractionator counts (Azevedo and colleagues, 2009) |
| African elephant, brain | about 257 billion | isotropic fractionator counts (2014) |

The human figure, which replaced the frequently quoted "100 billion", comes from the **isotropic fractionator** method of Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues: the tissue is dissolved into a suspension of free nuclei, the nuclei are counted, and the fraction belonging to neurons is found by staining for a neuron-specific nuclear protein (NeuN). Of the human brain's 86 billion neurons, about 69 billion are in the cerebellum, most of them granule cells, and only about 16 billion in the cerebral cortex. The elephant brain has three times as many neurons as ours, but almost all of the excess is in its cerebellum; its cerebral cortex has about a third as many neurons as the human cortex.

The *C. elegans* and *Drosophila* numbers come from **connectomes**, complete wiring diagrams reconstructed from serial electron micrographs. The worm's connectome took more than a decade of manual tracing; the fly's, completed in 2024 by the FlyWire consortium with machine learning and thousands of human proofreaders, contains roughly 50 million synapses.

## Glia

Rudolf Virchow named the non-neuronal cells of the brain *Nervenkitt*, "nerve glue", in the 1850s, and the Greek word for glue, *glia*, has stuck to them. They are now known to do far more than hold neurons together.

::: definition The main classes of glial cells {#def-glia}
In vertebrates:

- **Astrocytes** (CNS) are star-shaped cells whose fine processes surround synapses and whose **endfeet** cover blood vessels. They take up K⁺ and the transmitter glutamate from the extracellular space, supply metabolic substrates to neurons, and help to form and maintain the blood–brain barrier.
- **Oligodendrocytes** (CNS) wrap segments of several axons, often dozens, in myelin.
- **Schwann cells** (PNS) each myelinate a single segment of one axon, or ensheathe several small unmyelinated axons without myelinating them.
- **Microglia** (CNS) are the resident macrophages of the brain and spinal cord: they survey the tissue, engulf debris, dying cells and some synapses, and respond to injury and infection.
- **Oligodendrocyte precursor cells** (OPCs, also called NG2 glia) persist throughout life, divide, and can make new oligodendrocytes.
- **Ependymal cells** line the ventricles and central canal, and **radial glia** are the elongated progenitor cells of the developing brain, which give rise to most neurons and glia and guide migrating neurons.

Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPCs, ependymal cells and Schwann cells are derived, like neurons, from the embryonic ectoderm; microglia are not.
:::

### Astrocytes

Astrocytes are the most numerous glia in most regions of the mammalian brain. In the rodent cortex each astrocyte occupies its own territory, with little overlap with its neighbours (a pattern called **tiling**), and its processes contact of the order of 100,000 synapses. Three functions are well established.

1. **Potassium buffering.** Every action potential releases K⁺ into the narrow extracellular space, and a small volume of space means that even a modest efflux raises the local K⁺ concentration appreciably. The membranes of glial cells are permeable almost only to K⁺, so they take it up where it is high and release it where it is low, a process called **spatial buffering**. Richard Orkand, John Nicholls and Stephen Kuffler showed in 1966, recording from glia in the optic nerve of the mudpuppy *Necturus maculosus*, that the glial membrane potential follows the Nernst potential for K⁺ almost exactly ([[neuroscience/resting-potential]]) and depolarises when nearby axons fire.
2. **Transmitter uptake.** Glutamate released at excitatory synapses is removed mainly by astrocytic transporters (EAAT1 and EAAT2 in humans, known in rodents as GLAST and GLT-1), which couple its uptake to the inward movement of Na⁺. Inside the astrocyte, glutamate is converted to glutamine, which is returned to neurons and turned back into glutamate (the **glutamate–glutamine cycle**).
3. **Vascular and barrier functions.** Astrocyte endfeet ensheathe almost all the brain's capillaries. The **blood–brain barrier** itself, which keeps most blood-borne molecules out of the brain, is formed by tight junctions between the capillary endothelial cells, but astrocytes and the pericytes that sit on the capillaries induce and maintain it. In insects the arrangement is different: the barrier between the haemolymph and the nervous system is formed by glia themselves, sealed by septate junctions.

Beyond these, astrocytes respond to transmitters with rises in internal Ca²⁺, and many studies since the late 1990s have proposed that they release transmitters of their own, such as glutamate, ATP and D-serine, forming with the pre- and postsynaptic neurons a **tripartite synapse**. How large and how general this **gliotransmission** is remains disputed, partly because the methods used to stimulate astrocytes can have unintended effects. Treat it as an active hypothesis rather than a textbook fact.

### Myelinating glia

Many vertebrate axons are wrapped in **myelin**, a spiral of compacted glial membrane. The idea behind it is the one behind insulating a wire: it reduces the leakage of current across the axonal membrane and the capacitance that must be charged, so that an impulse travels further and faster for a given axon diameter. The physics is in [[neuroscience/action-potential]].

::: definition Myelin, internodes and nodes of Ranvier {#def-myelin}
A **myelin sheath** is formed when a Schwann cell or an oligodendrocyte process wraps an axon many times and squeezes out the cytoplasm between the turns, leaving tightly stacked layers of membrane. Each glial wrap covers one **internode**, typically 0.2–2 mm long. Between successive internodes is a short gap of bare axon, about 1 µm long, the **node of Ranvier**, where voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated. The ratio of the axon diameter to the outer diameter of the myelinated fibre is the **g-ratio**, typically 0.6–0.8.
:::

Myelin membrane is unusual: about 70–80 % of its dry mass is lipid (much cholesterol and galactolipids), against about 50 % in most membranes. Proteins hold the layers together, including myelin basic protein on the cytoplasmic side and, in the CNS, proteolipid protein; in the PNS the adhesion protein P0 does much of the work. X-ray diffraction of fresh nerve shows that each wrap, two membranes thick, adds about 17–18 nm to a peripheral sheath and about 15–16 nm to a central one.

::: example Counting the turns of a myelin sheath {#ex-myelin}
A peripheral axon has a diameter of 6 µm and a g-ratio of 0.6. Assuming a repeat of 17.6 nm per turn, (a) what is the outer diameter of the fibre, (b) how thick is the sheath, and (c) about how many times did the Schwann cell wrap the axon?
::: solution
(a) $g = d/D$, so $D = d/g = 6/0.6 = 10$ µm.

(b) The sheath occupies the difference between the radii: $(D - d)/2 = (10 - 6)/2 = 2$ µm.

(c) $2\ \mu\mathrm{m} / 17.6\ \mathrm{nm} = 2000/17.6 \approx 114$ turns. A large fibre thus carries of the order of a hundred layers of glial membrane, each turn an extension of a single cell.
:::
:::

Myelin is a vertebrate invention, but not a uniquely vertebrate one. Among living vertebrates only the jawed ones have it; lampreys and hagfishes do not. Myelin-like sheaths evolved independently in several invertebrate groups, including some annelid worms, prawns and copepods, which also conduct impulses unusually fast for their axon size. That convergence is good evidence that fast conduction in a thin axon is worth a great deal to an animal.

### Microglia

Microglia have a different origin from all other glia. Fate-mapping experiments in mice (Florent Ginhoux and colleagues, 2010) showed that they descend from progenitors in the embryonic yolk sac that colonise the brain early in development and then renew themselves for life, rather than from blood monocytes. In the healthy brain their fine processes move continuously, sampling the surroundings. In the developing visual system of the mouse, microglia engulf synapses that have been tagged by proteins of the complement system and are less active than their neighbours (Dorothy Schafer, Beth Stevens and colleagues, 2012), helping to remove inappropriate connections. When the brain is injured, microglia migrate to the site, proliferate and release inflammatory signals; the role of these responses in human neurological disease is covered in [Medicine Atlas: neuroinflammation](/learn/medicine/lesson.php?c=nervous-system&l=neuroinflammation).

### How many glia?

It is often said that glia outnumber neurons by ten to one in the human brain. They do not.

::: example Glia-to-neuron ratios {#ex-ratio}
The isotropic fractionator counts of Azevedo and colleagues (2009) for adult human brains gave, in round numbers (billions):

| region | neurons | non-neuronal cells |
|---|---|---|
| cerebral cortex (with white matter) | 16 | 61 |
| cerebellum | 69 | 16 |
| rest of brain | 0.7 | 8 |

Non-neuronal cells include glia and the endothelial cells of blood vessels. (a) What is the non-neuronal-to-neuron ratio of the whole brain? (b) Of the cerebral cortex and of the cerebellum? (c) What might explain the difference between the two regions?
::: solution
(a) Totals: neurons $16 + 69 + 0.7 \approx 86$ billion; non-neuronal cells $61 + 16 + 8 = 85$ billion. The ratio is about $85/86 \approx 1.0$: there is roughly one non-neuronal cell per neuron.

(b) Cerebral cortex: $61/16 \approx 3.8$. Cerebellum: $16/69 \approx 0.23$.

(c) The ratio follows neuron size rather than brain "sophistication". The cerebellum is dominated by tiny granule cells, each with a small volume of cytoplasm and short unmyelinated axons, so fewer glia are needed per neuron. The cerebral cortex has larger neurons and a mass of myelinated white matter, populated by oligodendrocytes. Comparisons across mammals show the same pattern: the glia-to-neuron ratio rises with average neuron size, not with intelligence. Note that "non-neuronal" is not the same as "glial", because blood-vessel cells are included.
:::
:::

::: warning "Glia outnumber neurons ten to one" and "we use only 10 % of our brains"
Both claims are popular and both are false. The ten-to-one ratio appears in older textbooks without a measurement behind it; direct counts give about one non-neuronal cell per neuron in the whole human brain, with large regional differences. The "10 %" myth has no basis at all: imaging and recording show activity in all parts of the brain over the course of a day, and damage to almost any region has measurable consequences. What is true is that at any instant only a small fraction of neurons fire, because neural codes are sparse and spikes are expensive.
:::

### Glia in invertebrates

The nematode *C. elegans* has 56 glial cells in the hermaphrodite, most associated with its sense organs, and when particular glia are removed by laser ablation, the sensory neurons they ensheathe lose their normal shape and function. *Drosophila* has several classes of glia, including surface glia that form the blood–brain barrier, cortex glia around neuronal cell bodies, ensheathing glia that engulf debris, and astrocyte-like glia whose processes infiltrate the neuropil and take up transmitter. Many of the genes for glial functions, such as transporters and the engulfment receptor Draper (related to mammalian MEGF10), are shared between flies and vertebrates, which suggests that a division of labour between neurons and supporting cells is ancient.

::: history
Golgi's black reaction (1873), Cajal's studies of avian cerebellum (1888) and Waldeyer's review (1891) established the cellular view of the nervous system; Golgi and Cajal shared the Nobel Prize in 1906. Sherrington named the synapse in 1897, and electron microscopy confirmed the synaptic cleft in 1955. Rudolf Virchow described *Nervenkitt* in 1856; Pío del Río Hortega, a student of Cajal's, distinguished microglia and oligodendrocytes in the late 1910s and early 1920s with silver-carbonate stains. Theodor Schwann described the sheath cells of peripheral nerve in 1839, and Louis-Antoine Ranvier the nodes in 1878. The isotropic fractionator counts of the human brain were published by Frederico Azevedo, Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues in 2009. The complete connectome of *C. elegans* was published by John White, Eileen Southgate, Nichol Thomson and Sydney Brenner in 1986, and that of the adult *Drosophila* brain by the FlyWire consortium in 2024.
:::

## Where this leads

The next two chapters explain the electrical signals that neurons carry: the resting potential across their membranes ([[neuroscience/resting-potential]]) and the action potential that travels along their axons ([[neuroscience/action-potential]]). Then [[neuroscience/synapses]] shows how one neuron affects the next. Glia will reappear wherever they matter: in K⁺ buffering, in the conduction velocity of myelinated axons, in the clearance of transmitter, and in the development and pruning of circuits.

::: summary
- The neuron doctrine (Cajal, from 1888, using Golgi's stain) holds that nervous systems are made of discrete, polarised cells communicating at synapses; gap junctions, dendrodendritic synapses and a few syncytial nerve nets are exceptions.
- A neuron has a cell body, dendrites (often with spines), an axon that begins at the initial segment, where impulses start, and presynaptic terminals; a diffusion barrier at the initial segment keeps axonal and somatodendritic membranes distinct.
- Neurons are classified by number of processes, by function (sensory, motor, interneuron), by transmitter and effect, by shape and, now, by gene expression.
- Long axons cannot be supplied by diffusion ($t \approx x^2/2D$); fast (≈ 50–400 mm per day) and slow (≈ 0.2–8 mm per day) axonal transport, driven by kinesins and dynein, carry cargo along microtubules.
- The human brain has about 86 billion neurons (69 billion in the cerebellum) and about as many non-neuronal cells; the glia-to-neuron ratio varies with neuron size.
- Astrocytes buffer K⁺, take up glutamate and support the blood–brain barrier; oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells make myelin (g-ratio ≈ 0.6–0.8, nodes about 1 µm long); microglia, of yolk-sac origin, are the brain's macrophages and prune synapses.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Diffusion along a dendrite {level=1 check="50"}
A second messenger with $D = 100\ \mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ is produced at a spine. Using $t \approx x^2/2D$, roughly how long (in seconds) does it take to spread 100 µm along the dendrite?
::: solution
$t \approx (100)^2/(2\times100) = 10{,}000/200 = 50$ s. Over tens of micrometres even a small, fast molecule takes many seconds, which is one reason why biochemical signals tend to stay local to the synapse that produced them, whereas electrical signals spread along a dendrite in milliseconds.
:::

::: exercise Transport times {level=1 check="10"}
A giraffe's longest motor axons are about 4 m long. How many days would fast axonal transport at 400 mm per day take to carry a vesicle from the cell body to the terminal?
::: solution
$4000\ \mathrm{mm} / 400\ \mathrm{mm\,day^{-1}} = 10$ days. Slow transport at 1 mm per day would take about 11 years, comparable with the animal's lifetime, so the terminals of such axons depend on long-lived proteins and on local synthesis.

::: exercise Naming the parts {level=1}
For each structure, say which cell type it belongs to and what it does: (a) Nissl bodies; (b) the axon initial segment; (c) astrocyte endfeet; (d) the node of Ranvier; (e) a dendritic spine.
::: solution
(a) Neuron: clumps of rough endoplasmic reticulum in the cell body, the site of synthesis of membrane and secreted proteins. (b) Neuron: the first few tens of micrometres of the axon, with a high density of Na⁺ channels, where action potentials are normally initiated, and a diffusion barrier that separates axonal from somatodendritic membrane. (c) Astrocyte: processes that cover blood vessels, involved in inducing the blood–brain barrier, regulating blood flow and taking up K⁺ and water. (d) Myelinated axon: a short gap between internodes with concentrated Na⁺ channels, where the action potential is regenerated. (e) Neuron: a small protrusion from a dendrite, the site of most excitatory synapses in many brain regions, which compartmentalises chemical signals such as Ca²⁺.
:::

::: exercise A myelin sheath in the CNS {level=2 check="133"}
A central axon of diameter 4 µm has a g-ratio of 0.5. With a repeat of 15 nm per turn, how many turns of myelin surround it (to the nearest whole number)?
::: solution
Outer diameter $D = d/g = 8$ µm; sheath thickness $(8 - 4)/2 = 2$ µm $= 2000$ nm. Turns $= 2000/15 \approx 133$.

::: exercise Interpreting a Golgi stain {level=2}
Explain why the Golgi method, which stains only about one neuron in a hundred, was more useful for establishing the neuron doctrine than a stain that coloured every neuron. Then explain why the Golgi method alone could never have proved the doctrine.
::: solution
A stain that coloured every cell would show the dense tangle of processes that had led anatomists to the reticular theory: with every process dark, one cannot tell which fibre belongs to which cell or whether two fibres are continuous. Staining a sparse random subset, but each cell completely, isolates single neurons against a clear background, so their entire shape, including the free endings of axons, can be followed. However, light microscopy cannot resolve a gap of 20 nm, so a Golgi preparation cannot distinguish a contact from a fusion; and because unstained cells are invisible, the method cannot show what an ending touches. Proof that membranes remain separate at synapses required the electron microscope (1955). The sparse-labelling principle survives in modern genetic methods such as Brainbow and sparse viral labelling, used for the same reason.
:::

::: exercise Neuron counts and brain size {level=2}
A rodent brain of 0.4 g has 70 million neurons, a primate brain of 80 g has 6 billion neurons, and a human brain of 1500 g has 86 billion neurons. (a) Calculate the neuronal density (neurons per gram) of each. (b) What does the comparison suggest about how neuron number scales with brain mass in rodents and primates, and what additional data would you need to test it?
::: solution
(a) Rodent: $7\times10^7/0.4 = 1.75\times10^8$ g⁻¹. Primate: $6\times10^9/80 = 7.5\times10^7$ g⁻¹. Human: $8.6\times10^{10}/1500 = 5.7\times10^7$ g⁻¹.

(b) Density falls with brain size, but more slowly between the primate and the human than between the rodent and the primate. Herculano-Houzel's comparative data show that in rodents, neurons get larger as brains get larger, so neuronal density drops steeply, whereas in primates neuron size changes little, so a large primate brain packs many more neurons than a rodent brain of the same mass would. Three species cannot establish a scaling law, especially when they come from different orders: you would need many species within each order, fitted on log–log axes, with phylogenetically independent comparisons ([[animal-physiology/homeostasis]]).
:::

::: exercise Potassium in the extracellular space {level=3}
The extracellular space of the brain is about 20 % of tissue volume. Suppose that a burst of activity releases K⁺ equivalent to 0.5 mmol per litre of **tissue**, and that the resting extracellular K⁺ concentration is 3 mmol L⁻¹. (a) If no K⁺ were removed, what would the extracellular K⁺ concentration become? (b) Using $E_\mathrm{K} = 61.5\log_{10}([\mathrm{K}]_\mathrm{o}/[\mathrm{K}]_\mathrm{i})$ mV with $[\mathrm{K}]_\mathrm{i} = 140$ mmol L⁻¹, by how much would $E_\mathrm{K}$ change? (c) Explain how astrocytes limit the change.
::: hint
The released K⁺ is spread over the extracellular volume only, which is a fifth of the tissue.
:::
::: solution
(a) The release corresponds to $0.5/0.2 = 2.5$ mmol L⁻¹ of extracellular fluid, so [K⁺]ₒ rises from 3 to 5.5 mmol L⁻¹.

(b) $E_\mathrm{K}$ at 3 mmol L⁻¹: $61.5\log_{10}(3/140) = 61.5\times(-1.669) = -102.6$ mV. At 5.5 mmol L⁻¹: $61.5\log_{10}(5.5/140) = 61.5\times(-1.406) = -86.4$ mV. A change of about 16 mV in the depolarising direction, enough to change the excitability of nearby neurons substantially.

(c) Astrocytes have membranes permeable mainly to K⁺ and are coupled to one another by gap junctions into a network. Where extracellular K⁺ rises, the local $E_\mathrm{K}$ becomes less negative than the membrane potential of the glial network, so K⁺ flows into the astrocytes there; current carried through the coupled network drives K⁺ out where extracellular K⁺ is low, including near blood vessels. This is spatial buffering. Astrocytes also take up K⁺ with the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and with Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporters. Real rises in [K⁺]ₒ during intense activity are therefore smaller, typically reaching a ceiling of about 10–12 mmol L⁻¹ even in seizures.
:::

::: exercise Why insulate? {level=3}
Myelin evolved independently in jawed vertebrates, some annelids and some crustaceans, while squid and cockroaches use giant unmyelinated axons for their fastest escape responses. (a) What does independent evolution suggest about the advantage of myelin? (b) Suggest a reason why an animal might use a giant axon rather than myelin. (c) A mammalian myelinated fibre of 10 µm outer diameter conducts at about 60 m s⁻¹, while the unmyelinated squid giant axon, 500 µm across, conducts at about 20 m s⁻¹. If unmyelinated conduction velocity rises as the square root of diameter, how wide would an unmyelinated axon have to be to conduct at 60 m s⁻¹, and what would that mean for a nerve containing thousands of such fibres? (Ignore the difference in temperature.)
::: solution
(a) Convergent evolution of the same specialised structure in distant lineages suggests a strong and general advantage: fast conduction for a given axon diameter, which saves space and the energy of signalling in many axons. (b) A giant axon needs no specialised glial machinery and can evolve from existing cells, simply by enlarging or fusing axons; for a single escape pathway, where only a few fast axons are needed, its volume is affordable. Invertebrate giant axons are typically few in number. (c) Velocity $\propto \sqrt{d}$, so tripling the velocity from 20 to 60 m s⁻¹ needs $3^2 = 9$ times the diameter: about 4.5 mm, 450 times the diameter of the myelinated fibre and some 200,000 times its cross-sectional area. A nerve of a few thousand such axons would be centimetres thick, and the brain could not hold its white matter. (The squid axon works at sea temperature; at 37 °C it would be somewhat faster, but the conclusion is unchanged.) Myelin is what makes the vertebrate brain compact.
:::
