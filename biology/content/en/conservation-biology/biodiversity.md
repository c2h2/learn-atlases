In 1982 the entomologist Terry Erwin fogged the crowns of nineteen trees of a single species, *Luehea seemannii*, in a forest in Panama with a fast-acting insecticide and collected everything that fell onto sheets spread below. He sorted more than 1200 species of beetle from those nineteen trees, most of them unnamed. Erwin then did something audacious: he multiplied. If a tropical tree species supports about 160 beetle species found on no other kind of tree, and there are some 50 000 species of tropical tree, and beetles are about two-fifths of all arthropods, then the tropics might hold not the one or two million species of animal that most biologists then assumed, but something like 30 million. The number was almost certainly too large — later work has revised it downwards several times — but it changed the question. Biologists realised that they did not know, even to within a factor of ten, how many kinds of organism share the planet, and that many of them were disappearing before anyone had described them.

This course is about that disappearance and what can be done about it. Its first task is to say exactly what is being lost. **Biodiversity** is a deliberately broad word, and conservation needs to measure it: to count species that have not all been seen, to compare the diversity of different places, to recognise that some species carry far more unique evolutionary history than others, and to ask why any of this matters to people. This chapter takes these in turn. It builds directly on the measures of richness, evenness and diversity introduced in [[ecology/communities]], which we use rather than re-derive, and on the species–area relationship of [[ecology/biogeography]].

## What biodiversity is

The phrase "biological diversity" was used by conservationists such as Thomas Lovejoy and Elliott Norse around 1980, and the contraction *biodiversity* was coined by Walter Rosen in planning the 1986 National Forum on BioDiversity in Washington, whose proceedings, edited by E. O. Wilson, appeared in 1988. Six years later the word had a legal definition.

::: definition Biodiversity {#def-biodiversity}
The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992, Article 2) defines **biological diversity** as "the variability among living organisms from all sources including, inter alia, terrestrial, marine and other aquatic ecosystems and the ecological complexes of which they are part; this includes diversity within species, between species and of ecosystems." Biodiversity is therefore recognised at three main levels:

- **genetic diversity** — variation in the genes and genomes of individuals within a species, both within and among its populations;
- **species diversity** — the number and relative abundance of species, and their evolutionary relationships;
- **ecosystem diversity** — the variety of communities, habitats and ecological processes in a region.

Other facets are measured alongside these: **functional diversity** (the range of ecological traits and roles — body size, diet, rooting depth, nitrogen fixation) and **phylogenetic diversity** (the amount of evolutionary history represented, [[#def-pd]]).
:::

The levels are nested but not interchangeable. Two forests may contain the same tree species and still differ in their genetic diversity, if one was replanted from a handful of seed sources. A region may lose an entire ecosystem type — a seasonal wetland, a native grassland — while every species in it survives somewhere else. Conservation needs all three levels because each can be lost independently, and because each supports the others: genetic variation is the raw material for adaptation ([[evolution/natural-selection]]), species interactions make up ecosystems, and ecosystems provide the habitats in which populations persist.

::: warning "Biodiversity just means the number of species"
Species richness is the most familiar measure, but it is only one facet. Two communities with 20 species each can differ enormously in evenness (one dominated by a single species), in the evolutionary distinctiveness of their members (20 closely related grasses or 20 species from different orders), in functional roles and in the genetic variation within each species. A conservation decision based only on counting species can protect a place rich in common, closely related species while ignoring a place with fewer, unique ones. Always ask *which* facet a measure captures.
:::

### Ecosystem diversity

Ecosystems are harder to delimit than species, because they grade into one another, but a common classification is needed to report what is being lost. The **IUCN Global Ecosystem Typology** (David Keith and colleagues, 2022) organises the world's ecosystems hierarchically into five realms (terrestrial, freshwater, marine, subterranean and atmospheric, with transitional realms between them), 25 biomes and 110 **ecosystem functional groups** such as "tropical heath forests" or "seagrass meadows", defined by the processes that structure them. The **Red List of Ecosystems** applies criteria analogous to those for species (see [[conservation-biology/extinction]]) to assess the risk of ecosystem "collapse" — a transformation so profound that the ecosystem's defining biota and processes are lost.

## How many species are there?

About 2.1–2.2 million species of eukaryote have been described and catalogued (Catalogue of Life checklists, mid-2020s). The total is far larger and very uncertain. Several features of the problem make it hard:

- **Undescribed species are concentrated where few taxonomists work** — in tropical forests, the deep sea, soils, and among small organisms such as mites, nematodes, fungi and parasitic wasps.
- **Species boundaries are not always clear.** Many morphologically identical "species" have turned out, with DNA sequencing, to be complexes of cryptic species; conversely, some described species are synonyms. Which species concept is used ([[evolution/speciation]]) changes the count.
- **Prokaryotes are not counted in the same way.** Bacteria and archaea are classified by genome similarity rather than by reproductive isolation, most cannot yet be cultured, and estimates of their diversity range over many orders of magnitude; they are usually reported separately ([[diversity-of-life/bacteria-archaea]]).

Estimates for eukaryotes have used three main approaches. **Extrapolation from samples**, as in Erwin's calculation, scales up the richness of a well-studied sample by ratios such as host specificity. **Ratios between groups** use a well-known group as a yardstick: if in Britain there are about two species of insect for every species of vascular plant, and the world's plants are reasonably well known, the world's insects can be scaled accordingly. **Patterns in the taxonomic hierarchy** use the fact that higher taxa (phyla, classes, orders) are much better known than species: Camilo Mora and colleagues (2011) found that the number of taxa at each rank increases regularly from phylum to genus, extrapolated the pattern to the species level and arrived at about 8.7 million (± 1.3 million) eukaryotic species, of which they estimated that about 86 % of those on land and 91 % of those in the ocean remained undescribed. Most current estimates for eukaryotes fall between about 5 and 10 million, with insects the largest component.

::: example Erwin's estimate, step by step {#ex-erwin}
Erwin collected about 1200 beetle species from *Luehea seemannii* and, by assigning each beetle group a guess at its degree of host specificity, estimated that about 163 of them were specific to that tree species. Reconstruct his estimate of the number of tropical arthropod species from these assumptions: (i) 163 host-specific beetle species per tree species; (ii) 50 000 species of tropical tree; (iii) beetles make up 40 % of arthropod species; (iv) the canopy holds twice as many arthropod species as the forest floor, and the two faunas are distinct. Then identify the assumption you would most want to test.
::: solution
Host-specific canopy beetles: $163\times50\,000 = 8.15\times10^6$ species.

All canopy arthropods: $8.15\times10^6/0.40 = 2.04\times10^7$, about 20 million.

Adding the ground fauna, half as rich as the canopy: $2.04\times10^7\times1.5 = 3.06\times10^7$, about 30 million species.

Every factor is uncertain, and they multiply, so a twofold error in each of two factors gives a fourfold error in the answer. The weakest is the first: host specificity was guessed rather than measured. Later work with feeding records showed that many tropical herbivorous insects feed on several related tree species, so the effective number of "host-specific" species per tree species is much lower. Using a host-specificity estimate based on such data, Vojtech Novotny and colleagues (2002) argued that tropical arthropods number in the low millions rather than tens of millions. The lesson is not that Erwin was wrong to multiply, but that an extrapolation is only as good as its least-tested ratio.
:::
:::

### Estimating richness from incomplete samples

Even in a single forest, a survey never finds every species. The number of species observed, $S_\mathrm{obs}$, rises with sampling effort and approaches the true number $S$ only slowly, because the rarest species are encountered last. Plotting $S_\mathrm{obs}$ against the number of individuals or samples gives a **species accumulation curve**; its shape tells you whether sampling was nearly complete (the curve flattens) or far from it (the curve is still climbing steeply).

Two consequences follow. First, **raw species counts from samples of different sizes cannot be compared**: a larger sample will contain more species even from the same community. **Rarefaction** solves this by computing the expected number of species in a random subsample of a fixed size $n$ from each sample, so that communities are compared at equal effort. Second, the information about undetected species lies in the rarest detected ones. If many species have been seen only once, many more probably have not been seen at all.

::: proposition The Chao1 estimator of species richness {#prop-chao1}
Let $f_1$ be the number of species represented by exactly one individual in a sample (**singletons**) and $f_2$ the number represented by exactly two (**doubletons**). Suppose that individuals of each species are detected independently, species $i$ being detected a Poisson number of times with mean $\lambda_i$. Then the expected number of undetected species $f_0$ satisfies

$$
\E[f_0] \;\ge\; \frac{\E[f_1]^2}{2\,\E[f_2]},
$$

which motivates the **Chao1 estimator** (Anne Chao, 1984) of total richness,

$$
\hat S_\mathrm{Chao1} = S_\mathrm{obs} + \frac{f_1^2}{2f_2}.
$$ {#eq-chao1}

It is a **lower bound**: with strong heterogeneity in detectability the true richness may be higher still. When $f_2 = 0$ the bias-corrected form $S_\mathrm{obs} + f_1(f_1-1)/2$ is used.
:::

::: proof
Under the Poisson model, species $i$ appears exactly $k$ times with probability $e^{-\lambda_i}\lambda_i^k/k!$, so the expected numbers of species seen 0, 1 and 2 times are

$$
\E[f_0] = \sum_i e^{-\lambda_i}, \qquad \E[f_1] = \sum_i \lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i}, \qquad \E[f_2] = \tfrac12\sum_i \lambda_i^2 e^{-\lambda_i}.
$$

Write $\lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i} = \bigl(e^{-\lambda_i/2}\bigr)\bigl(\lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i/2}\bigr)$ and apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\bigl(\sum a_ib_i\bigr)^2 \le \sum a_i^2 \sum b_i^2$:

$$
\E[f_1]^2 = \Bigl(\sum_i e^{-\lambda_i/2}\,\lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i/2}\Bigr)^2 \le \sum_i e^{-\lambda_i}\,\sum_i \lambda_i^2 e^{-\lambda_i} = \E[f_0]\cdot 2\E[f_2].
$$

Rearranging gives the inequality. Equality holds when every $\lambda_i$ is the same, which is why Chao1 is exact for equally detectable species and an underestimate otherwise.
:::

::: example Estimating the bird species of a forest {#ex-chao}
A year of mist-netting in a forest reserve caught 40 species of bird. Twelve species were caught only once and six exactly twice. Estimate the total number of species present and the proportion detected.
::: solution
From [[#eq-chao1]], $\hat S = 40 + 12^2/(2\times6) = 40 + 144/12 = 52$ species. The survey detected about $40/52 = 77$ % of the species estimated to be present; at least a dozen more species probably use the reserve but were not caught. The bias-corrected form $40 + 12\times11/(2\times7) = 49.4$ is a little lower; the difference is a reminder that the estimate itself has substantial uncertainty (confidence intervals for Chao1 are usually wide and asymmetric). Mist nets also miss canopy species altogether — a violation of the assumption that every species is catchable — so the true number is likely higher than either estimate.
:::
:::

## Diversity across space: alpha, beta and gamma

Robert Whittaker, studying plant communities along gradients in the Siskiyou Mountains of Oregon (1960), distinguished diversity *within* a site from the turnover of species *between* sites. The distinction is now central to conservation planning, because the diversity of a region depends as much on how different its sites are as on how rich each is.

::: definition Alpha, beta and gamma diversity {#def-beta}
For a region divided into $N$ sites (samples, habitats or communities):

- **alpha diversity** $\alpha$ is the diversity within a site; $\bar\alpha$ is its mean over the sites;
- **gamma diversity** $\gamma$ is the diversity of the whole region (all sites pooled);
- **beta diversity** measures how much the sites differ in composition. In Whittaker's multiplicative form,

$$
\beta = \frac{\gamma}{\bar\alpha}.
$$ {#eq-beta}

When diversities are measured as effective numbers of species (Hill numbers, [[ecology/communities]]), $\beta$ ranges from 1 (all sites identical) to $N$ (no species shared) and can be read as the **effective number of distinct communities** in the region (Lou Jost, 2007). Pairwise dissimilarity between two sites with $a$ and $b$ species, $c$ of them shared, is often measured by the **Sørensen similarity** $2c/(a+b)$ or the **Jaccard similarity** $c/(a+b-c)$.
:::

::: example Three ponds {#ex-ponds}
A survey of dragonflies in three ponds finds 12, 9 and 15 species; 24 species are found in total. Ponds 1 and 2 share 6 species. Calculate $\bar\alpha$, $\beta$ and the Sørensen and Jaccard similarities of ponds 1 and 2. If only one pond can be protected, what does $\beta$ tell you?
::: solution
$\bar\alpha = (12 + 9 + 15)/3 = 12$, $\gamma = 24$, so $\beta = 24/12 = 2$: the region holds as many species as two completely distinct ponds of average richness. (With richness as the measure the Hill-number interpretation holds exactly only when sites are equally weighted, as here.)

Sørensen: $2\times6/(12 + 9) = 0.571$. Jaccard: $6/(12 + 9 - 6) = 0.400$.

Since $\beta = 2$ is well above 1, the ponds are far from interchangeable: protecting even the richest pond (15 species) would leave 9 of the region's 24 species unprotected. High beta diversity is the reason why conservation networks need many sites, chosen for **complementarity** — how many *new* species each adds — rather than for their individual richness ([[conservation-biology/protected-areas]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Region X has ten sites, each with 30 species, and 40 species in total. Region Y has ten sites, each with 15 species, and 90 species in total. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] X has the higher beta diversity because its sites are richer.
- [x] Y has the higher beta diversity, and protecting a single site captures a smaller fraction of its species.
- [ ] Their beta diversities are equal because both have ten sites.
- [ ] Beta diversity cannot be calculated without abundances.
::: solution
$\beta_X = 40/30 = 1.33$ and $\beta_Y = 90/15 = 6$. In Y the sites differ greatly in composition: one site holds only $15/90 = 17$ % of the regional species, compared with $30/40 = 75$ % in X. Richness-based beta diversity needs only presence–absence data.
:::
:::

## Phylogenetic diversity

All species are not equal in the evolutionary history they represent. The tuatara (*Sphenodon punctatus*) of New Zealand is the only surviving species of the order Rhynchocephalia, whose lineage separated from that of lizards and snakes about 250 million years ago; losing it would prune from the tree of life a branch about a quarter of a billion years long. Losing one of several hundred closely related species of a single genus of cichlid fish would remove a far shorter branch. Daniel Faith (1992) proposed a measure that captures this.

::: definition Phylogenetic diversity and evolutionary distinctiveness {#def-pd}
Given a dated or otherwise branch-length phylogeny, the **phylogenetic diversity** (PD) of a set of species is the sum of the lengths of all branches of the tree that connect those species to the root. The **evolutionary distinctiveness** (ED) of a species is its share of the tree's total branch length, usually calculated by the **fair proportion** rule: each branch's length is divided equally among the species descended from it, and a species' ED is the sum of its shares along its path to the root. The EDs of all species sum to the PD of the whole tree.
:::

::: example Which species to save? {#ex-pd}
Four species form the tree ((A:2, B:2):6, (C:5, D:5):3) — that is, A and B are sisters on branches of 2 million years each, joined by a stem branch of 6 million years to the root; C and D are sisters on branches of 5 million years, with a stem of 3 million years. (a) Find the PD of the whole tree and of each pair of species. (b) If only two species can be conserved, which pair maximises PD? (c) Calculate each species' ED.
::: solution
(a) Total PD $= 2 + 2 + 6 + 5 + 5 + 3 = 23$ million years. For pairs: $\{A,B\} = 2 + 2 + 6 = 10$; $\{C,D\} = 5 + 5 + 3 = 13$; any cross pair such as $\{A,C\} = 2 + 6 + 5 + 3 = 16$ (likewise $\{A,D\}$, $\{B,C\}$, $\{B,D\}$).

(b) Any pair with one species from each clade, with PD 16 — saving one member of each clade keeps both deep branches. Saving the two members of the same clade loses a stem branch entirely.

(c) Fair proportion: A $= 2 + 6/2 = 5$, B $= 5$, C $= 5 + 3/2 = 6.5$, D $= 6.5$. Check: $5 + 5 + 6.5 + 6.5 = 23$. C and D are slightly more distinctive because their terminal branches are longer.
:::
:::

The **EDGE** programme of the Zoological Society of London (Nick Isaac and colleagues, 2007) combines distinctiveness with extinction risk: in its original form a species' score is $\ln(1 + \mathrm{ED}) + \mathrm{GE}\ln 2$, where GE runs from 0 for Least Concern to 4 for Critically Endangered on the Red List ([[conservation-biology/extinction]]), so that each step up in threat counts as a doubling of extinction risk. Species that are both evolutionarily distinct and globally endangered — the aye-aye, the long-beaked echidnas, the Chinese giant salamander — head the list. A revised method (EDGE2, 2023) uses probabilities of extinction directly and accounts for the threat to close relatives, since a branch is lost only when all its descendants are.

::: intuition The tree of life as a piece of string
Imagine the phylogeny drawn with string, each branch cut to its length in years. PD is the total length of string you keep if you pick up the species you have saved and keep every piece connecting them to the root. A species on a long, lonely branch drags a lot of string with it; one with many close relatives drags very little, because its relatives already hold most of its branch. ED is a fair way of sharing the string among the species that hang from it.
:::

Phylogenetic diversity is often used as a proxy for **feature diversity** — the variety of traits, chemistry and genes that future generations might find useful — on the argument that more distant relatives differ in more features. The correlation between PD and functional diversity is real but imperfect; convergent evolution and rapid radiations can break it.

## Why biodiversity matters

Measuring biodiversity is a technical problem. Deciding why it should be conserved is a question of values, and conservation biologists have been explicit about that since the field was founded. In his essay "What is conservation biology?" (1985) Michael Soulé set out the "normative postulates" of the discipline — that diversity of organisms is good, that untimely extinction is bad, that ecological complexity is good, that evolution is good, and that biotic diversity has intrinsic value. Science can tell us what is being lost and what the consequences are; it cannot by itself say that the loss is wrong. Most arguments fall into three families.

::: definition Values of biodiversity {#def-values}
- **Intrinsic value**: species and ecosystems have value in themselves, independent of their usefulness to people.
- **Instrumental value**: value as a means to human ends. Economists divide it into **direct use** value (food, timber, medicines, ecotourism), **indirect use** value (the regulation of climate, water and pests by ecosystems), **option** value (the possibility of future use) and **non-use** value (the satisfaction of knowing that a species exists, *existence* value, or will exist for one's descendants, *bequest* value).
- **Relational value**: value arising from relationships between people and nature — identity, sense of place, cultural and spiritual practice, responsibility and care (Kai Chan and colleagues, 2016).
:::

The **Millennium Ecosystem Assessment** (2005) popularised the language of **ecosystem services**, the benefits people obtain from ecosystems, divided into *provisioning* services (food, fibre, fresh water, genetic resources), *regulating* services (climate regulation, flood control, pollination, disease regulation), *cultural* services (recreation, inspiration, spiritual value) and *supporting* services (soil formation, nutrient cycling, primary production) that underlie the rest. The Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) later broadened this to **nature's contributions to people**, which includes contributions valued in non-economic terms and the knowledge systems of Indigenous peoples and local communities, and its *Values Assessment* (2022) argued that decisions dominated by market values systematically neglect the others. The economics of ecosystem services is developed in [[conservation-biology/people-and-policy]].

::: application Medicines, enzymes and the option value of species
Many medicines originated in organisms that were not studied for that purpose. The cancer drug paclitaxel was first isolated from the bark of the Pacific yew *Taxus brevifolia*, and vincristine and vinblastine from the Madagascar periwinkle *Catharanthus roseus*. The heat-stable DNA polymerase that made the polymerase chain reaction practical came from *Thermus aquaticus*, a bacterium isolated by Thomas Brock and Hudson Freeze (1969) from a hot spring in Yellowstone. None of these uses could have been predicted when the organisms were first described, which is the essence of **option value**: a species that goes extinct removes possibilities nobody has yet thought of. The same reasoning applies to the wild relatives of crops, whose genes have supplied resistance to diseases and pests; their conservation is part of food security. The rights of the countries and communities from which such genetic resources come are governed by the Nagoya Protocol (2010) of the Convention on Biological Diversity.
:::

### Biodiversity and ecosystem functioning

Does the number of species in an ecosystem affect how well it works? For most of the twentieth century this was argued from observation, until a series of experiments in the 1990s assembled communities of known diversity. The best known is at the Cedar Creek Ecosystem Science Reserve in Minnesota, where David Tilman and colleagues sowed plots with 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 species of prairie plant drawn at random from a pool, and followed them for decades. More diverse plots produced more above-ground biomass, took up more of the soil's nitrate, were less invaded by weeds and — in a decade-long analysis (Tilman, Reich and Knops, 2006) — varied less from year to year in total biomass. Similar results came from the European BIODEPTH experiment and the Jena Experiment in Germany. Reviewing hundreds of such experiments, Bradley Cardinale and colleagues (2012) concluded that biodiversity loss reduces the efficiency with which communities capture resources and produce biomass, and that the effect is typically **saturating**: the first losses from a diverse community matter little, because other species compensate, but losses accelerate as diversity falls.

Two mechanisms produce the positive effect. **Complementarity**: species differ in the resources they use (deep- and shallow-rooted plants, legumes fixing nitrogen alongside grasses), so a mixture exploits the environment more fully than any monoculture. **Selection** (or sampling): a more diverse mixture is more likely to include a highly productive species that comes to dominate. Michel Loreau and Andy Hector (2001) showed how to separate the two statistically from the yields of species in mixture and monoculture; both operate, and complementarity tends to strengthen with time.

### The insurance and portfolio effects

Diversity also stabilises. In a variable environment, species respond differently to the same weather, so when one declines another may increase. Shigeo Yachi and Michel Loreau (1999) called this the **insurance hypothesis**. Its simplest form is borrowed from finance: a portfolio of many assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated is less variable than any one of them.

::: proposition The portfolio effect {#prop-portfolio}
Let the abundances (or yields) of $n$ species or populations be random variables $X_1, \dots, X_n$, each with mean $\mu$, standard deviation $\sigma$ and coefficient of variation $\mathrm{CV} = \sigma/\mu$, and with the same correlation $\rho$ between every pair. Then the total $T = \sum_i X_i$ has coefficient of variation

$$
\mathrm{CV}_T = \mathrm{CV}\,\sqrt{\frac{1 + (n-1)\rho}{n}}.
$$ {#eq-portfolio}

With independent fluctuations ($\rho = 0$) the total becomes less variable as $1/\sqrt n$; with positive correlation it falls towards a floor of $\mathrm{CV}\sqrt\rho$ however many species are added; and with negative correlations (compensatory dynamics) it can fall faster still.
:::

::: proof
The mean of $T$ is $n\mu$. Its variance is the sum of all variances and covariances:

$$
\Var(T) = \sum_i \Var(X_i) + \sum_{i\neq j}\Cov(X_i, X_j) = n\sigma^2 + n(n-1)\rho\sigma^2 .
$$

Hence $\mathrm{CV}_T^2 = \Var(T)/(n\mu)^2 = (\sigma^2/\mu^2)\bigl(1 + (n-1)\rho\bigr)/n$, and taking square roots gives [[#eq-portfolio]]. As $n\to\infty$ the factor under the root tends to $\rho$.
:::

::: example Ten salmon stocks {#ex-portfolio}
A fishery is supplied by ten salmon populations spawning in different rivers. Each population's annual return has a coefficient of variation of 0.5. What is the coefficient of variation of the total catch if the populations fluctuate (a) independently, (b) with pairwise correlation 0.2? (c) What is the least variable the total could be with $\rho = 0.2$, however many populations there were?
::: solution
(a) $\mathrm{CV}_T = 0.5\sqrt{1/10} = 0.158$.

(b) $\mathrm{CV}_T = 0.5\sqrt{(1 + 9\times0.2)/10} = 0.5\sqrt{0.28} = 0.265$.

(c) $0.5\sqrt{0.2} = 0.224$. Even a modest correlation limits the benefit of adding populations: with $\rho = 0.2$, ten populations already achieve most of the possible stabilisation. What matters is not only how many populations there are but how *differently* they respond to the environment — which is why the loss of distinct local populations erodes stability even when the species survives.
:::
:::

::: application The sockeye salmon of Bristol Bay
The sockeye salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) fishery of Bristol Bay, Alaska, is supplied by hundreds of distinct populations spawning in different lakes, rivers and beaches, which differ in timing, body size and response to climate. Different populations dominate the catch in different decades. Daniel Schindler and colleagues (2010) estimated that if the fishery depended on a single homogeneous population, its year-to-year variability would be about 2.2 times higher and fishery closures would occur about ten times as often. This is the portfolio effect at work in a real economy, and it is an argument for conserving *population* diversity — the genetic and life-history variation within a species — and not merely the species itself. The genetic side of this argument is developed in [[conservation-biology/conservation-genetics]].
:::

::: warning "More diverse ecosystems are always more stable"
Stability has several meanings — constancy of total biomass, resistance to disturbance, speed of recovery, persistence of every species — and diversity does not improve them all. Experiments consistently find that diversity stabilises **aggregate** properties such as total biomass; but Robert May's analysis of random food webs ([[ecology/communities]]) showed that, other things being equal, more species and more interactions make the **populations of individual species** less stable. Both can be true at once: in a diverse community individual species may fluctuate wildly while the total stays steady because their fluctuations compensate. The claim "diversity begets stability" must always say which stability.
:::

::: history
The idea that nature should be conserved for its own sake runs through the writings of Henry David Thoreau and John Muir in the nineteenth century, and Aldo Leopold's *A Sand County Almanac* (1949) argued for a "land ethic" extending moral concern to soils, waters, plants and animals. Conservation biology as a scientific discipline emerged in the late 1970s: the first International Conference on Conservation Biology was held in San Diego in 1978, Michael Soulé and Bruce Wilcox's edited volume *Conservation Biology* appeared in 1980, and the Society for Conservation Biology was founded in 1985, the year of Soulé's essay describing it as a "crisis discipline". Erwin's canopy-fogging estimate (1982), the National Forum on BioDiversity (1986) and Wilson's volume *Biodiversity* (1988) brought the scale of the problem to public attention, and the Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) gave biodiversity a legal definition. Whittaker's partition of diversity (1960), Chao's estimator (1984), Faith's phylogenetic diversity (1992) and the biodiversity–ecosystem function experiments of the 1990s supplied the tools to measure it.
:::

::: summary
- **Biodiversity** ([[#def-biodiversity]]) is variability at the genetic, species and ecosystem levels, plus functional and phylogenetic facets; each level can be lost independently.
- About 2.1–2.2 million eukaryotic species are described (mid-2020s); most estimates of the total lie between 5 and 10 million (8.7 million by Mora et al., 2011). Extrapolations such as Erwin's 30 million are only as good as their weakest ratio ([[#ex-erwin]]).
- Samples underestimate richness; compare samples by **rarefaction** and estimate undetected species from singletons and doubletons with the **Chao1** lower bound $S_\mathrm{obs} + f_1^2/2f_2$ ([[#prop-chao1]]).
- **Alpha, beta and gamma** diversity ([[#def-beta]]): $\beta = \gamma/\bar\alpha$ is the effective number of distinct communities; high beta diversity means conservation needs many complementary sites.
- **Phylogenetic diversity** sums branch lengths; **evolutionary distinctiveness** shares them among species ([[#def-pd]]); EDGE combines distinctiveness with extinction risk.
- Biodiversity has **intrinsic, instrumental and relational** values ([[#def-values]]); ecosystem services and nature's contributions to people describe the instrumental ones.
- Experiments show saturating positive effects of diversity on productivity (complementarity and selection), and the **portfolio effect** $\mathrm{CV}_T = \mathrm{CV}\sqrt{(1 + (n-1)\rho)/n}$ ([[#prop-portfolio]]) explains why diversity stabilises aggregate properties.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Chao1 {level=1 check="33"}
A survey of moths records 25 species, of which 8 are singletons and 4 are doubletons. Estimate the total species richness with the Chao1 estimator.
::: solution
$\hat S = 25 + 8^2/(2\times4) = 25 + 64/8 = 33$ species.
:::
:::

::: exercise Beta diversity {level=1 check="4"}
Five sites in a region hold on average 15 species of plant each, and 60 species are recorded in the region as a whole. Calculate Whittaker's beta diversity.
::: solution
$\beta = \gamma/\bar\alpha = 60/15 = 4$: the region is as diverse as four completely distinct sites of average richness.
:::
:::

::: exercise Effective number of species {level=1 check="1/0.38"}
A grassland has three species with proportions 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. What is its effective number of species based on the Simpson index, $1/D$ with $D = \sum p_i^2$?
::: solution
$D = 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04 = 0.38$, so $1/D = 2.63$: the grassland is as diverse as 2.6 equally abundant species. (Its richness is 3; the shortfall reflects the dominance of the first species.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Losing a species from a tree {level=2}
For the tree of [[#ex-pd]], ((A:2, B:2):6, (C:5, D:5):3), calculate the PD lost by the extinction of (a) A alone, (b) A and B together, (c) C and D together. Explain why the loss in (b) is much more than twice the loss in (a).
::: solution
(a) Losing A removes only its terminal branch: 2 million years (PD falls from 23 to 21).

(b) Losing both A and B removes their terminal branches and the stem they shared: $2 + 2 + 6 = 10$ million years.

(c) $5 + 5 + 3 = 13$ million years.

While either A or B survives, the shared stem branch of 6 million years is still represented. Only when the last descendant of a branch disappears is the branch lost, so the loss of PD accelerates as a clade is whittled down. This is why EDGE2 scores take into account the threat status of a species' close relatives.
:::
:::

::: exercise Portfolio with negative correlation {level=2 check="0.5*sqrt(0.7/4)"}
Four populations each have a coefficient of variation of 0.5. Their fluctuations are partly compensatory, with pairwise correlation $\rho = -0.1$. Calculate the coefficient of variation of their total. What is the most negative common correlation possible for four populations?
::: solution
$\mathrm{CV}_T = 0.5\sqrt{(1 + 3\times(-0.1))/4} = 0.5\sqrt{0.175} = 0.209$, compared with 0.25 for independent fluctuations.

The variance $n\sigma^2(1 + (n-1)\rho)$ cannot be negative, so $\rho \ge -1/(n-1) = -1/3$. At $\rho = -1/3$ the total is perfectly constant. Equal negative correlations among many populations are impossible: compensation is necessarily limited when there are many species.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rarefaction and a misleading comparison {level=2}
An ecologist reports that a logged forest has more species of dung beetle (48) than a nearby unlogged forest (41), and concludes that logging increased diversity. The logged forest was sampled with 60 traps and the unlogged forest with 25. (a) Why can the raw counts not be compared? (b) Rarefied to 25 traps, the logged forest is expected to contain 33 species. What do you conclude? (c) Name one further reason why richness alone might be a poor measure of the effect of logging here.
::: solution
(a) Species accumulate with sampling effort; with more than twice as many traps, the logged forest would show more species even if the two forests were identical.

(b) At equal effort (25 traps) the logged forest has fewer species (33) than the unlogged one (41). The original conclusion is reversed: logging appears to have reduced dung beetle richness. (Confidence intervals for the rarefied value would show whether the difference is larger than sampling error.)

(c) Logging may replace forest specialists by widespread open-habitat species, so richness can stay similar or even rise while the forest fauna is lost. Comparing composition (beta diversity between the forests, or the number of forest specialists) would reveal this. Abundance and evenness, functional roles such as dung burial, and the identity of the species lost are all invisible to a species count.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a biodiversity experiment {level=2}
In a grassland experiment, plots sown with 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 species produced mean above-ground biomass of 180, 260, 340, 400 and 430 g m⁻² after five years. The most productive monoculture produced 390 g m⁻². (a) Describe the shape of the relationship. (b) Is the 16-species yield consistent with a pure selection effect? (c) What does the shape imply for the effect of losing species from rich and poor communities?
::: solution
(a) Biomass increases with richness, but with diminishing returns: each doubling adds less (+80, +80, +60, +30 g m⁻²). The relationship is saturating.

(b) Under a pure selection effect, the best a mixture could do in the long run is approach the yield of its most productive species in monoculture, 390 g m⁻². The 16-species plots exceed it (430 g m⁻²) — **transgressive overyielding** — which requires complementarity: the mixture uses resources that no single species exploits fully. (A formal partition, as in Loreau and Hector's method, needs the yields of each species in mixture.)

(c) Losing species from a rich community (16 → 8) costs little biomass (30 g m⁻²), but losing them from a poor one (2 → 1) costs much more (80 g m⁻² for a single species). The steepest consequences of biodiversity loss come at low diversity — which is where many intensively managed landscapes already are.
:::
:::

::: exercise Revising Erwin {level=3}
Suppose that instead of being specific to one tree species, a typical "specialist" beetle in a tropical forest feeds on about five related tree species. (a) Recalculate Erwin's estimate ([[#ex-erwin]]) under this assumption, keeping his other ratios. (b) Mora et al.'s method gives about 8.7 million eukaryotic species in total, about 1 million of which are described insects. Discuss whether your revised figure in (a) is compatible with it. (c) What kind of data would test the host-specificity ratio directly?
::: solution
(a) If each specialist species is shared among five tree species, the number of distinct specialist beetle species is $163\times50\,000/5 = 1.63\times10^6$. Dividing by 0.4 gives $4.1\times10^6$ canopy arthropods, and multiplying by 1.5 for the ground gives about $6.1\times10^6$ tropical arthropod species. (This still ignores the non-specialist beetles, which add a little.)

(b) Six million tropical arthropods is compatible with a total of 5–10 million eukaryotes if arthropods are the majority, as most estimates assume; Erwin's original 30 million is not. The revised figure is closer to estimates such as Novotny's, showing how strongly the answer depends on one ratio.

(c) Feeding records — rearing larvae from foliage, or feeding trials offering each insect species several tree species — or DNA metabarcoding of insect gut contents, giving the number of host species per insect species. Sampling many tree species at the same site, so that the overlap of their beetle faunas can be measured (a beta-diversity calculation among host trees), gives the same information.
:::
:::

::: exercise Values in a decision {level=3}
A government must decide whether to allow a mine in a small upland valley that is the only known locality of an endemic species of freshwater snail. The mine would provide 300 jobs for 20 years. (a) List arguments for protecting the valley drawn from each of the three families of value in [[#def-values]]. (b) Which of these could an economic cost–benefit analysis capture, and how? (c) What scientific information would most improve the decision, and what part of the decision can science not settle?
::: solution
(a) *Intrinsic*: the snail is a unique product of evolution with a right to exist; its extinction would be an irreversible loss regardless of use. *Instrumental*: the stream ecosystem may regulate water quality and flow for users downstream (indirect use); the snail and its microbiota have unknown option value; many people value the existence of endemic species (non-use). *Relational*: local people may identify with the valley as a place, use it for recreation or hold it to be culturally significant.

(b) Direct and indirect use values can be estimated from markets and avoided costs (for example, the cost of treating water if the catchment is degraded); option value is very uncertain; existence value can be estimated with stated-preference surveys (willingness to pay), whose reliability is debated. Intrinsic value, by definition, is not a monetary quantity, and relational values are poorly captured by prices.

(c) Science can establish whether the snail really is restricted to this valley (surveys of other streams, perhaps with environmental DNA), whether it could survive the mine (habitat requirements, tolerance to sediment and metals), whether translocation is feasible, and what the mine would do to downstream water. It cannot decide how to weigh 300 jobs against the irreversible loss of a species: that is a judgement of values for the society concerned, which science can inform but not replace.
:::
