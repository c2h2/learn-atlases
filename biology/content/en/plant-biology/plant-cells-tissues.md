Look at a thin slice of a potato tuber, an onion skin or a young leaf under a light microscope and the first thing you see is not the cells themselves but their boundaries: a honeycomb of walls. Robert Hooke saw the same honeycomb in cork in 1665 and called the compartments cells; what he saw were in fact empty walls, the living contents long gone. The wall is the defining feature of the plant cell. It lets a plant cell hold an internal pressure several times that of a car tyre, it fixes each cell in place for life, it decides the shape into which a cell can grow, and in wood it remains as a load-bearing skeleton after the cell has died. Most of the carbon that plants have fixed and that is now held in the land biosphere is wall material: cellulose, hemicelluloses and lignin.

This chapter builds the plant body from the bottom up. We begin with the three features that distinguish plant cells from the animal cells of [[cell-biology/organelles]] — the **cell wall**, the **plastids** and the large central **vacuole** — and with the **plasmodesmata**, the channels that connect the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells through their walls. We then meet the handful of cell types from which every plant organ is made, and the three **tissue systems** — dermal, vascular and ground — into which they are arranged. The quantitative thread is mechanical: how turgor pressure stretches the wall, why a cylindrical cell is stressed twice as much around its girth as along its length, and how plants exploit that to choose the direction in which they grow. The evolutionary background — how land plants arose from green algae and acquired a cuticle, stomata and vascular tissue — is in [[diversity-of-life/land-plants]], and we build on it here.

## What makes a plant cell different

A typical cell of a leaf or a young stem contains everything a eukaryotic cell needs: a nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus (in plants usually many separate stacks, or dictyosomes, that move through the cytoplasm), mitochondria, peroxisomes, ribosomes and a cytoskeleton of microtubules and actin filaments ([[cell-biology/organelles]], [[cell-biology/cytoskeleton]]). Three additions transform it.

1. A **cell wall** outside the plasma membrane, a composite of cellulose fibres embedded in a matrix of other polysaccharides and proteins. The wall resists the cell's internal pressure and determines its shape.
2. **Plastids**, organelles with two envelope membranes and their own genome, descended from a cyanobacterium ([[cell-biology/organelles#def-endosymbiosis]]). Chloroplasts are the photosynthetic form; other forms store starch, oils or pigments.
3. A large **central vacuole**, bounded by the **tonoplast** membrane, which in a mature cell commonly fills 80–90 % or more of the cell's volume and squeezes the cytoplasm into a thin layer against the wall.

Plant cells also lack some things animal cells have: there are no centrioles (microtubules are nucleated from many sites, without a single centrosome), no intermediate filaments of the animal type, and no extracellular matrix of collagen; the wall takes the place of all of these mechanically.

::: warning "Plants have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria"
A common error is to think that because plants photosynthesise they do not respire. Every living plant cell has mitochondria and carries out glycolysis, the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation ([[biochemistry/oxidative-phosphorylation]]), day and night. Roots, seeds, flowers, fruits and the non-green cells of stems have no working chloroplasts at all and rely entirely on respiration of sugar imported from leaves. Even a leaf respires in the light; in the dark it is a net consumer of oxygen. Chloroplasts make sugar; mitochondria burn it.
:::

## The cell wall

### Primary and secondary walls

::: definition Primary wall, secondary wall and middle lamella {#def-wall}
The **primary wall** is the thin (typically 0.1–1 µm), extensible wall laid down by a cell while it is still growing. It consists of **cellulose microfibrils** embedded in a hydrated matrix of **hemicelluloses** and **pectins**, with a small proportion of structural proteins. The **middle lamella** is the pectin-rich layer, formed at cell division, that glues the primary walls of neighbouring cells together. After growth has stopped, many cells deposit a **secondary wall** inside the primary wall: thicker, richer in cellulose, poorer in pectin, often in several layers with different microfibril orientations, and in many cell types impregnated with **lignin**.
:::

**Cellulose** is an unbranched chain of glucose residues joined by β(1→4) bonds ([[biochemistry/carbohydrates-lipids]]). Because each residue is rotated by 180° relative to its neighbour, the chain is flat and straight, with hydroxyl groups along both edges; neighbouring chains bond to each other by many hydrogen bonds and stack into a nearly crystalline **microfibril**. Each glucose adds about 0.515 nm to the length of a chain. In the primary walls of flowering plants microfibrils are about 3 nm across and are thought to contain 18 chains (the number has been debated, with older models proposing 36), each with a degree of polymerisation — number of glucose residues — of several thousand. A microfibril has a tensile stiffness comparable with steel per unit weight, and it is the reinforcing fibre of the wall composite, in the way steel rods reinforce concrete.

Cellulose is not made inside the cell and exported, like the matrix polysaccharides. It is spun at the plasma membrane by **cellulose synthase complexes**, rosettes of six lobes visible by freeze-fracture electron microscopy, each lobe containing several CESA proteins. Each CESA takes UDP-glucose from the cytosol and extrudes a growing glucan chain through the membrane; the chains from one complex crystallise together into one microfibril. As the chains are fixed in the wall, the complex is pushed forward through the membrane, so the complexes move. In 2006 Alex Paredez, Chris Somerville and David Ehrhardt imaged fluorescently tagged CESA6 in living *Arabidopsis thaliana* hypocotyl cells and saw the complexes travelling along tracks defined by the cortical **microtubules** just beneath the membrane, at a few hundred nanometres per minute. This is how the cytoskeleton steers the orientation of the microfibrils, and through them, as we shall see, the direction of growth.

::: example How long does it take to spin a cellulose chain? {#ex-cesa}
A cellulose synthase complex moves through the membrane at about 300 nm min⁻¹. (a) How many glucose residues does each of its chains gain per second? (b) How long does it take to make a chain with a degree of polymerisation of 8,000, and how long is that chain?
::: solution
(a) Each residue adds 0.515 nm to the chain, and the complex advances by the length of the chain it has deposited, so each chain gains $300/0.515 = 583$ residues per minute, or about **10 glucose residues per second** per CESA.

(b) The chain is $8000\times0.515\text{ nm} = 4120\text{ nm} \approx 4.1$ µm long, and takes $4120/300 = 13.7$ minutes, about **14 minutes**, to make. A microfibril is longer than any one chain, because chains start and finish at staggered positions. For comparison, a cell 40 µm in diameter has a circumference of $\pi\times40 = 126$ µm; a microfibril wrapping around it is made of many overlapping chains.
:::
:::

The **matrix** is made in the Golgi apparatus and delivered by secretory vesicles. In the primary walls of most flowering plants other than grasses, the main **hemicellulose** is **xyloglucan**, a β(1→4)-glucan backbone with xylose side chains, which binds to the surface of cellulose and may link microfibrils at a limited number of sites. The **pectins** are a family of acidic polysaccharides rich in galacturonic acid. The simplest, **homogalacturonan**, is secreted with most of its carboxyl groups methyl-esterified; in the wall, pectin methylesterases remove the methyl groups, and the freed carboxylates are cross-linked by Ca²⁺ ions into a gel. The primary walls of grasses and their relatives differ: they contain less pectin and xyloglucan, and more glucuronoarabinoxylan and mixed-linkage (1→3),(1→4)-β-glucan. Very roughly, a eudicot primary wall is a quarter to a third cellulose by dry mass, a quarter hemicellulose and a third pectin, with a few per cent protein; the proportions vary widely between tissues and species.

**Lignin** is a hydrophobic polymer of phenylpropanoid units (monolignols), polymerised in the wall by peroxidases and laccases into an irregular, cross-linked network. It fills the spaces between polysaccharides, displaces water and makes the wall rigid, waterproof and very hard to digest. Lignin evolved with the vascular plants; it is what lets xylem conduits resist the tension of water being pulled through them ([[plant-biology/water-transport]]) and lets trees stand 100 m tall. Two other hydrophobic polymers are deposited in particular places: **cutin**, which with waxes forms the cuticle on the outer surface of the epidermis, and **suberin**, which seals cork cells and the endodermis of roots.

### Walls under pressure

A plant cell takes up water by osmosis until the inward push of its wall balances the osmotic tendency of water to enter. The resulting hydrostatic pressure inside the cell, the **turgor pressure** $P$, is typically 0.3–1 MPa (3–10 times atmospheric pressure) and can exceed 2 MPa in guard cells. It can be estimated from van 't Hoff's relation between osmotic pressure and concentration ([[biochemistry/water-and-bonds]]); the full treatment of water potential is in [[plant-biology/water-transport]].

::: example Turgor from the difference in solute concentration {#ex-turgor}
A cell whose sap contains 0.30 osmol kg⁻¹ of solutes sits in tissue water containing 0.05 osmol kg⁻¹. Estimate its turgor at 20 °C, assuming it has come to equilibrium and the solutions are dilute.
::: solution
At equilibrium the turgor balances the difference in osmotic pressure. With $\Delta c = 0.25$ osmol kg⁻¹ $\approx 250$ mol m⁻³,

$$
P = \Delta c\,RT = 250\times8.314\times293.15\ \text{Pa} = 6.09\times10^5\ \text{Pa} \approx 0.61\ \text{MPa},
$$

about six atmospheres. (Van 't Hoff's relation strictly holds for ideal dilute solutions; at these concentrations it is accurate to a few per cent for sugars and less so for salts, whose osmotic coefficients are below 1.)
:::
:::

How much force does this pressure exert on the wall? Think of an elongated plant cell as a thin-walled cylinder of radius $r$ with closed ends.

::: proposition Wall stress in a pressurised cylinder {#prop-hoop}
Let a cylindrical cell of radius $r$ and wall thickness $t \ll r$ hold a turgor pressure $P$ (above the outside pressure). If the wall carries the load uniformly through its thickness, the tensile stress in the wall around the circumference (the **hoop stress**) and along the axis (the **axial stress**) are

$$
\sigma_\text{hoop} = \frac{P\,r}{t}, \qquad \sigma_\text{axial} = \frac{P\,r}{2t}.
$$ {#eq-hoop}

For a spherical cell the stress is the same in every direction, $\sigma = P\,r/(2t)$.
:::

::: proof
*Hoop stress.* Cut a length $L$ of the cylinder in half along a plane through its axis. The pressure acting on the inside of one half pushes it away from the other half with a net force equal to $P$ times the projected area of the cut, $P\cdot 2rL$. This force is resisted by the wall across the two cut strips, each of area $tL$, carrying stress $\sigma_\text{hoop}$: $2tL\,\sigma_\text{hoop} = 2rLP$, so $\sigma_\text{hoop} = Pr/t$.

*Axial stress.* Cut the cylinder across its axis. The pressure on the end cap pushes with force $P\pi r^2$, resisted by the annulus of wall of area $2\pi r t$ (for $t \ll r$): $2\pi r t\,\sigma_\text{axial} = \pi r^2 P$, so $\sigma_\text{axial} = Pr/(2t)$. For a sphere, the same cut through the centre gives $2\pi rt\,\sigma = \pi r^2 P$ in every orientation.
:::

The assumptions matter: the wall must be thin compared with the radius, uniform, and the cell must be roughly cylindrical. Real cells have neighbours that share the load, walls of varying thickness, and corners; but the order of magnitude, and above all the factor of 2, are robust.

::: example The stress in a growing cell wall {#ex-hoop}
A cylindrical cell in a growing stem has radius 20 µm, a primary wall 0.2 µm thick and a turgor of 0.6 MPa. Find the hoop and axial stresses.
::: solution
By [[#eq-hoop]], $\sigma_\text{hoop} = 0.6\times20/0.2 = 60$ MPa and $\sigma_\text{axial} = 30$ MPa. The wall, though only a fifth of a micrometre thick, carries stresses of tens of megapascals — a hundred times the turgor itself, because the ratio $r/t = 100$ amplifies the pressure. This is why a plant cell needs a wall reinforced by fibres as strong as cellulose, and why loosening the wall even slightly lets the cell expand.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why cells grow long, not fat
If the wall were equally stiff in all directions, a cylindrical cell would expand more around its girth than along its length, because the hoop stress is twice the axial stress: it would get fatter. Growing plant cells usually do the opposite, elongating many-fold while scarcely widening. They do it by laying cellulose microfibrils predominantly around the circumference, like hoops on a barrel. The hoops resist radial expansion, and the matrix between them can slide apart, allowing growth along the axis. The orientation of the hoops follows the cortical microtubules that guide the cellulose synthase complexes. Disrupt the microtubules — with the herbicide oryzalin, for instance — and newly deposited microfibrils are disordered; the cells then swell into balloons instead of elongating. The direction of growth is a mechanical consequence of how the wall is built.
:::

Expansion requires the wall to yield. Growing walls are loosened by **expansins**, proteins discovered by Simon McQueen-Mason and Daniel Cosgrove in 1992 in cucumber hypocotyls, which are active at acidic pH and are thought to disrupt the non-covalent binding between cellulose and matrix polysaccharides, letting the wall creep under the stress of turgor. Pumping protons into the wall, which auxin promotes, therefore loosens it — the **acid-growth** response taken up in [[plant-biology/plant-hormones]]. How turgor and wall extensibility together set the growth rate is the subject of the Lockhart equation in [[plant-biology/plant-form]].

## Plastids and the vacuole

### Plastids

All plastids of a plant develop from small, undifferentiated **proplastids** in meristematic cells (or, in some tissues, by conversion of one mature type into another), and they divide by fission. Which form they take depends on the cell and its environment.

::: definition Plastid types {#def-plastids}
- **Chloroplasts** contain chlorophyll in a system of internal **thylakoid** membranes, stacked into **grana** and connected by unstacked stromal lamellae, and carry out photosynthesis. A mesophyll cell of *Arabidopsis* contains of the order of a hundred.
- **Etioplasts** are the plastids of seedlings grown in the dark: they contain a lattice-like **prolamellar body** of membrane and a chlorophyll precursor, and turn into chloroplasts within hours of illumination.
- **Amyloplasts** store starch, in storage organs such as the potato tuber and cereal endosperm; in the root cap and in shoot endodermis, dense amyloplasts sediment under gravity and act as gravity sensors ([[plant-biology/plant-responses]]).
- **Chromoplasts** accumulate carotenoid pigments, colouring ripe tomatoes, peppers, carrots and many petals orange, yellow or red.
- **Leucoplasts** is a general term for colourless plastids, including **elaioplasts** that store lipids.
:::

Plastids are not only for photosynthesis. Every plant cell depends on them, because plastids are the site of synthesis of fatty acids, of most amino acids, of purines and pyrimidines, of chlorophyll and haem, and of carotenoids and the hormones derived from them. A cell without plastids is not viable. Chloroplast structure and the light reactions are developed in [[plant-biology/photosynthesis]].

### The vacuole

::: definition Vacuole and tonoplast {#def-vacuole}
The **central vacuole** of a mature plant cell is a single large compartment bounded by the **tonoplast**. Its lumen is acidified to about pH 5–5.5 (as low as pH 2–3 in citrus fruit) by two proton pumps in the tonoplast, the V-type ATPase and the vacuolar H⁺-pyrophosphatase, and the resulting proton gradient drives the accumulation of ions, sugars, organic acids and secondary metabolites by H⁺-coupled antiporters. **Lytic vacuoles** combine this role with the hydrolytic functions of animal lysosomes; **protein storage vacuoles**, found in seeds, store reserve proteins.
:::

The vacuole has several jobs. It **stores**: ions such as K⁺ and Cl⁻, sugars, organic acids (the malate of CAM plants, [[plant-biology/c4-and-cam]]), pigments (the anthocyanins of red petals and autumn leaves are dissolved in the vacuole), and defensive compounds such as alkaloids and glucosinolates, kept away from the enzymes in the cytoplasm that would activate or be poisoned by them. It **digests**: autophagy delivers worn-out organelles to it. And it **inflates** the cell: by accumulating solutes and water, it generates turgor and occupies most of the volume of a large cell with a cheap solution of salts, sugars and water rather than an expensive cytoplasm full of proteins and ribosomes.

::: example How thin is the cytoplasm? {#ex-vacuole}
A cylindrical leaf cell has radius 20 µm and length 100 µm; its vacuole fills 90 % of its volume. If the cytoplasm forms a uniform layer lining the side walls (ignore the ends), how thick is that layer?
::: solution
The cytoplasm occupies $10\,\%$ of the cylinder, so the vacuole is a cylinder of radius $r - d$ with $\pi(r-d)^2L = 0.9\,\pi r^2 L$. Hence

$$
d = r\left(1 - \sqrt{0.9}\right) = 20\times(1 - 0.9487)\ \text{µm} = 1.03\ \text{µm}.
$$

The living cytoplasm is a film only about a micrometre thick, in which chloroplasts (each about 5 µm across) lie pressed against the wall. Two consequences follow. A protein diffusing at 10 µm² s⁻¹ crosses the layer in a fraction of a second, so a large plant cell is, for diffusion, effectively a thin sheet ([[cell-biology/cells-and-microscopy]]). And chloroplasts sit next to the wall, where CO₂ arrives, rather than buried in the cell.
:::
:::

The vacuole is also how plant cells grow so fast and so large without making much new cytoplasm. A cell elongating in a root or stem takes up water into its vacuole and expands its volume ten- to a hundred-fold, while the amount of cytoplasm increases far less. The energy cost of plant growth is mostly in the wall, not in filling the cell.

## Plasmodesmata: a connected cytoplasm

When a plant cell divides, the new wall — the **cell plate** — is assembled from Golgi vesicles in the middle of the cell, guided by the **phragmoplast**, an array of microtubules and actin, and grows outwards until it fuses with the parent wall ([[cell-biology/cell-cycle]]). Strands of endoplasmic reticulum trapped in the forming cell plate become the core of **plasmodesmata**.

::: definition Plasmodesma, symplast and apoplast {#def-symplast}
A **plasmodesma** (plural plasmodesmata) is a membrane-lined channel, typically 20–50 nm in diameter, that crosses the cell wall and joins the plasma membranes of two neighbouring cells into one continuous membrane. Through its centre runs the **desmotubule**, a narrow tube of compressed endoplasmic reticulum; the **cytoplasmic sleeve** between the desmotubule and the plasma membrane is the path for molecules moving between cells. The cytoplasms of all cells connected by plasmodesmata form the **symplast**. Everything outside the plasma membranes — cell walls, intercellular air spaces and the lumens of dead xylem conduits — forms the **apoplast**.
:::

Plasmodesmata let small molecules — sugars, amino acids, ions, hormones — move from cell to cell without crossing a membrane. The largest molecule that passes freely through a resting plasmodesma, its **size exclusion limit**, is often quoted as about 1 kDa, but it is regulated. Plants narrow plasmodesmata by depositing the polysaccharide **callose** (a β(1→3)-glucan) in the wall around the neck of the channel, and widen them by removing it. In developing and sink tissues the limit can be much larger, and certain proteins — transcription factors such as SHORT-ROOT and KNOTTED1, and viral movement proteins that plant viruses use to spread — move through actively.

::: example What can pass through a plasmodesma? {#ex-sel}
The minimum radius of a globular protein of mass $M$ (in daltons) is roughly $0.066\,M^{1/3}$ nm. The cytoplasmic sleeve of a resting plasmodesma is estimated to allow particles up to about 1–1.5 nm in radius. Compare sucrose (342 Da; hydrodynamic radius about 0.5 nm), a 1 kDa peptide and green fluorescent protein (GFP, 27 kDa).
::: solution
A 1 kDa molecule has $r \approx 0.066\times1000^{1/3} = 0.66$ nm; sucrose is about 0.5 nm; both pass. GFP has $r \ge 0.066\times27000^{1/3} = 1.98$ nm (its real hydrodynamic radius is larger, about 2.4 nm, because it is not a perfect sphere and carries a hydration layer), so it should be excluded from a resting plasmodesma. Experiments agree: Karl Oparka and colleagues (1999) found that GFP expressed in one cell of a tobacco leaf spread freely between cells in young, importing (sink) leaves but stayed put in mature, exporting (source) leaves. The size exclusion limit is not a fixed property of the channel but changes with development.
:::
:::

Plasmodesmata divide the plant into **symplastic domains**. Mature guard cells lose theirs, becoming isolated so that they can change turgor independently of their neighbours. The phloem's sieve elements are linked to their companion cells by branched plasmodesmata, but in many species the companion-cell–sieve-element complex is almost isolated from surrounding cells, a fact of great importance for how sugar is loaded into the phloem ([[plant-biology/water-transport]]).

## Cell types

A plant is built from a surprisingly small set of cell types. Botanists sort them by their walls and by whether they are alive at maturity.

::: definition The basic cell types {#def-cell-types}
- **Parenchyma** cells have thin primary walls and are alive at maturity. They photosynthesise (mesophyll), store (potato tuber, seed cotyledons), secrete and heal wounds, and they can resume division. Most of the soft tissue of a plant is parenchyma.
- **Collenchyma** cells are alive, elongated, with unevenly thickened primary walls rich in pectin. They support young, growing organs — the ribs of a celery petiole are collenchyma — because their walls are strong but can still extend.
- **Sclerenchyma** cells have thick, usually lignified secondary walls and are often dead at maturity. **Fibres** are long and slender (the commercial fibres of flax, hemp and jute); **sclereids** are short, irregular cells (the grit in pear flesh, the stone of a peach).
- **Xylem** conducting cells — **tracheids** and **vessel elements** — are elongated cells with lignified secondary walls that die at maturity, leaving hollow pipes for water. Tracheids are closed at their ends and pass water through **pits**, thin areas of wall; vessel elements lose their end walls, partly or entirely, and join end to end into **vessels** that can be centimetres to metres long.
- **Phloem** conducting cells — **sieve elements** — are alive but lose their nucleus, vacuole and most organelles; they are joined end to end through **sieve plates** perforated by enlarged, callose-lined pores into **sieve tubes**. In flowering plants each sieve-tube element is served by one or more **companion cells**, its sister cells, which supply proteins and energy.
:::

Note the paradox of the two vascular tissues. The xylem conducts through cells that have died, so that their lumens are empty and the flow meets no membranes or cytoplasm; the phloem conducts through cells that are alive, because, as we shall see in [[plant-biology/water-transport]], phloem transport is driven by membrane transport of sugar into and out of the conducting cells. Xylem cell death is programmed: it follows the deposition of the secondary wall and involves vacuolar rupture and release of hydrolases that clear the cell contents.

::: quiz
Which of the following cells are dead when they carry out their function?
- [x] A vessel element in the xylem of an oak
- [ ] A sieve-tube element in the phloem of a bean
- [x] A fibre in a flax stem
- [ ] A guard cell
- [ ] A collenchyma cell in a celery petiole
::: solution
Vessel elements and (most) fibres function as dead cell walls: the vessel as a pipe, the fibre as a strut. A sieve-tube element is alive, though it has lost its nucleus and depends on its companion cell; guard cells and collenchyma are living cells whose function depends on turgor and on growing walls. (Some fibres remain alive for years, so "most" is the safe word.)
:::
:::

## Tissue systems

Cells are grouped into tissues, and tissues into three systems that run continuously through every organ — root, stem and leaf. This continuity is one of the simplifying ideas of plant anatomy: whatever the organ, you can find the same three systems, arranged differently.

::: definition The three tissue systems {#def-tissue-systems}
- The **dermal tissue system** is the outer covering. In the primary body it is the **epidermis**, usually one cell layer thick, covered on aerial parts by the **cuticle** and containing specialised cells — **guard cells** that form **stomata**, **trichomes** (hairs), and in roots **root hairs**. Where an organ thickens by secondary growth the epidermis is replaced by the **periderm**, made of cork.
- The **vascular tissue system** consists of **xylem**, which carries water and dissolved minerals upwards from the roots, and **phloem**, which carries sugars, amino acids and signals from sources to sinks, together with associated parenchyma and fibres.
- The **ground tissue system** is everything else: mostly parenchyma, with collenchyma and sclerenchyma. In leaves it forms the photosynthetic **mesophyll**; in stems and roots, the **cortex** and the **pith**.
:::

The epidermis is a remarkable tissue. Its outer walls are thick and covered by a cuticle of cutin and waxes that is almost impermeable to water — the cuticular conductance of a leaf is typically one to two orders of magnitude below the conductance of its open stomata. Gas exchange therefore goes through the stomata, each a pore flanked by two **guard cells** whose turgor opens and closes it. The guard-cell walls are thickened and their microfibrils arranged radially, like the hoops of [[#prop-hoop]] turned around the cell, so that as turgor rises the cells lengthen and bow apart, opening the pore. Stomatal density varies from a few tens to many hundreds per square millimetre of leaf surface; how stomata are regulated is taken up in [[plant-biology/water-transport]] and [[plant-biology/plant-hormones]].

In the root, the innermost layer of the cortex, the **endodermis**, has a band of lignin and suberin in its radial and transverse walls, the **Casparian strip**, which blocks the apoplast. Water and solutes moving inwards through the walls must cross an endodermal plasma membrane to reach the xylem, which gives the root control over what it takes up ([[plant-biology/mineral-nutrition]]).

How these systems are arranged in roots, stems and leaves, and how they are produced by meristems, is the subject of [[plant-biology/plant-form]]. Here is the bare plan:

| organ | dermal | vascular | ground |
|---|---|---|---|
| young root | epidermis with root hairs, no cuticle | central cylinder (stele) of xylem and phloem in alternating strands, surrounded by pericycle | cortex, with endodermis as its innermost layer |
| young stem | epidermis with cuticle and stomata | vascular bundles: a ring in eudicots, scattered in monocots | cortex outside the bundles, pith inside |
| leaf | upper and lower epidermis, stomata mostly on the lower side in many species | veins (vascular bundles), often surrounded by a bundle sheath | mesophyll: palisade and spongy parenchyma |

::: application Walls we use
The plant cell wall is the most abundant biological material on Earth and one of the most useful. Wood is mostly secondary wall: roughly 40–50 % cellulose, 20–30 % hemicelluloses and 20–30 % lignin by dry mass, the proportions differing between conifers and hardwoods. Paper is cellulose fibres from which most of the lignin has been removed by pulping. A cotton fibre is a single epidermal cell of the seed coat, up to about 3 cm long, whose secondary wall is almost pure cellulose. Linen comes from the phloem fibres of flax. In the diet, cell walls are **dietary fibre**, indigestible by human enzymes but fermented by gut bacteria. And because walls hold most plant carbon, converting them to fuels and chemicals is a major goal of biotechnology; the obstacle is lignin, which shields cellulose from enzymes. Plants engineered to make less lignin, or lignin that breaks down more easily under mild chemical treatment, are one approach to this "recalcitrance" problem; too little lignin, however, makes xylem vessels collapse under tension, a trade-off seen in the *irregular xylem* mutants of *Arabidopsis*.
:::

::: history
Robert Hooke described and named cells in cork in *Micrographia* (1665), and Nehemiah Grew (*The Anatomy of Plants*, 1682) and Marcello Malpighi (*Anatome plantarum*, 1675–1679) described plant tissues — Grew introduced the word "parenchyma" into botany. Robert Brown described the nucleus in orchid epidermal cells in 1831. Anselme Payen isolated and named cellulose in 1838; Hugo von Mohl named the living contents "protoplasm" in 1846. Eduard Tangl saw cytoplasmic connections through the walls of endosperm cells in 1879, and Eduard Strasburger named them plasmodesmata in 1901. The rosette cellulose synthase complexes were seen by freeze fracture by Malcolm Brown and others in the 1970s and 1980s; the first plant *CESA* genes were identified in cotton in 1996 (Pear and colleagues); and live imaging of CESA complexes moving along microtubules (Paredez, Somerville and Ehrhardt, 2006) linked the cytoskeleton directly to wall architecture.
:::

## Summary and outlook

::: summary
- Plant cells have a **cell wall**, **plastids** and a **central vacuole**, and connect their cytoplasm through **plasmodesmata**. They also have mitochondria and respire continuously.
- The **primary wall** is cellulose microfibrils in a matrix of hemicelluloses (xyloglucan in eudicots) and pectins; **secondary walls** are thicker and often lignified. Cellulose is spun at the plasma membrane by CESA complexes guided by cortical microtubules, at about 10 glucose residues per second per chain.
- Turgor (0.3–1 MPa) loads a cylindrical wall with hoop stress $Pr/t$ and axial stress $Pr/2t$, tens of megapascals. Transverse microfibrils make cells elongate rather than swell; expansins and acidification loosen the wall for growth.
- Plastids — chloroplasts, etioplasts, amyloplasts, chromoplasts, leucoplasts — arise from proplastids and make fatty acids, amino acids, pigments and hormone precursors as well as carrying out photosynthesis.
- The acidic vacuole stores ions, sugars, acids and defensive compounds, digests cell components, and fills most of the cell volume, leaving a cytoplasmic layer about 1 µm thick.
- The **symplast** (cytoplasm connected by plasmodesmata) and **apoplast** (walls and dead conduits) are the two pathways of transport; the size exclusion limit of plasmodesmata is regulated by callose.
- Cell types: parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma (fibres, sclereids), xylem (tracheids, vessel elements; dead) and phloem (sieve elements with companion cells; alive). They form the **dermal**, **vascular** and **ground** tissue systems that run through every organ.
:::

With the cell and its tissues in hand, [[plant-biology/plant-form]] turns to how meristems produce and arrange them, and to the anatomy of roots, stems and leaves.

## Exercises

::: exercise Hoop stress {level=1 check="50"}
A cylindrical parenchyma cell has radius 25 µm, wall thickness 0.25 µm and turgor 0.5 MPa. What is the hoop stress in its wall, in MPa?
::: solution
$\sigma_\text{hoop} = Pr/t = 0.5\times25/0.25 = 50$ MPa ([[#eq-hoop]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Turgor at equilibrium {level=1 check="0.74"}
A cell with sap of 0.40 osmol kg⁻¹ is at equilibrium with a solution of 0.10 osmol kg⁻¹ at 25 °C. Estimate its turgor in MPa, to two decimal places, using van 't Hoff's relation.
::: solution
$P = \Delta c\,RT = 300\ \text{mol m}^{-3}\times8.314\times298.15 = 7.44\times10^5$ Pa $= 0.74$ MPa.
:::
:::

::: exercise A fuller vacuole {level=1 check="0.76"}
A cylindrical cell of radius 30 µm has a vacuole filling 95 % of its volume. How thick, in µm to two decimal places, is the cytoplasmic layer lining its side walls?
::: solution
$d = r(1 - \sqrt{0.95}) = 30\times(1 - 0.97468) = 0.76$ µm.
:::

::: exercise Naming the parts {level=1}
For each of the following, name the cell type or tissue and say whether it is part of the dermal, vascular or ground tissue system: (a) the strings in a celery stalk that support it while it is still growing; (b) the gritty particles in a pear; (c) the pores on the underside of a leaf and the cells around them; (d) the hollow tubes, open end to end, in the wood of an oak; (e) the cells of a potato tuber that store starch.
::: solution
(a) Collenchyma (ground tissue system; the vascular bundles in celery are also accompanied by collenchyma strands near the epidermis). (b) Sclereids, a type of sclerenchyma (ground). (c) Stomata, formed by guard cells (dermal). (d) Vessels, made of vessel elements (vascular — xylem). (e) Storage parenchyma containing amyloplasts (ground).
:::

::: exercise Building a chain {level=2 check="14.7"}
A cellulose synthase complex moves at 350 nm min⁻¹. How many minutes does it take to make a cellulose chain of degree of polymerisation 10,000? Give one decimal place.
::: solution
Chain length $= 10000\times0.515 = 5150$ nm; time $= 5150/350 = 14.7$ minutes.

::: exercise Swollen roots {level=2}
Seedlings of *Arabidopsis* are grown on medium containing oryzalin, which depolymerises microtubules. After a day, the cells in the elongation zone of the root have stopped elongating and swelled radially, so that the root tip looks bulbous. (a) Explain this result in terms of [[#prop-hoop]] and the deposition of cellulose. (b) Predict the phenotype of a mutant whose CESA complexes are present but cannot move along microtubules in a guided way, and explain why its cells might still make cellulose.
::: solution
(a) Microtubules guide CESA complexes so that new microfibrils are laid down transversely. Without microtubules, the complexes still make cellulose but in random orientations. The wall becomes mechanically isotropic, so the larger hoop stress ($Pr/t$, twice the axial stress) dominates and the cells expand more in girth than length: radial swelling.
(b) Such a mutant would make cellulose (the catalytic activity is intact) but in disordered orientation, giving a similar, if milder, swelling phenotype and shorter organs. Mutants of this kind have been found — for example in proteins that link CESA complexes to microtubules (cellulose synthase interactive protein 1, CSI1) — and show reduced anisotropic growth while still producing cellulose.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reading the GFP experiment {level=2}
In the experiment of [[#ex-sel]], GFP moved between cells in sink leaves but not source leaves. A fusion of GFP to a 23 kDa protein (total 50 kDa) also moved in sink leaves. (a) What does this say about the size exclusion limit of plasmodesmata in sink tissue? (b) Suggest two mechanisms by which the plant could reduce the limit as a leaf matures. (c) Why might it be advantageous for a mature leaf, which exports sugar through its phloem, to have more restrictive plasmodesmata?
::: solution
(a) The limit in sink tissue must be at least 50 kDa — far above the textbook 1 kDa — at least for these non-targeted proteins moving by diffusion. The limit is set by the tissue's developmental state.
(b) Deposition of callose around the neck of the plasmodesmata narrows the cytoplasmic sleeve; and during leaf maturation simple plasmodesmata are replaced by branched ones with narrower channels. (Changes in the proteins of the sleeve and in the tightness of membrane attachment are also implicated.)
(c) A source leaf must keep sucrose at high concentration in its phloem and prevent it leaking back into the mesophyll; restricting the symplastic connections of the phloem and of other cells lets the leaf establish separate compartments, and also limits the spread of viruses and of proteins and RNAs that control cell identity in specific domains.
:::
:::

::: exercise Spheres and cylinders {level=2}
(a) A spherical cell and a cylindrical cell have the same radius, wall thickness and turgor. Which has the larger maximum wall stress, and by what factor? (b) A spherical yeast-like cell of radius 3 µm with a 0.1 µm wall holds 1 MPa turgor; compare its wall stress with that of [[#ex-hoop]], and explain why small cells can have thin walls.
::: solution
(a) In the sphere every direction carries $Pr/2t$; in the cylinder the hoop stress is $Pr/t$, twice as large. The cylinder is more highly stressed for the same dimensions.
(b) $\sigma = Pr/2t = 1\times3/(2\times0.1) = 15$ MPa, a quarter of the 60 MPa hoop stress in the larger plant cell even though the turgor is higher. Stress scales with $r/t$: a small cell can hold a high pressure with a thin wall, a large one needs a proportionally thicker wall.
:::
:::

::: exercise Plasmolysis {level=3}
Onion epidermal cells are mounted in a series of sucrose solutions. In 0.30 mol L⁻¹ sucrose and above, the protoplasts shrink away from the walls (plasmolysis); in 0.25 mol L⁻¹ and below they do not. (a) What does the threshold tell you about the osmotic pressure of the cell sap, and why is incipient plasmolysis a useful measuring point? (b) Estimate the sap's osmotic pressure at 20 °C. (c) In plasmolysed cells, strands of membrane (Hechtian strands) remain attached to the wall at many points. What might anchor them, and why might the anchoring matter to a cell exposed to drought?
::: solution
(a) At incipient plasmolysis the protoplast just fills the wall without pressing on it, so turgor is zero; the external solution's osmotic pressure then equals that of the sap. Measuring at zero turgor removes the unknown pressure term.
(b) Taking the threshold as about 0.275 mol L⁻¹ sucrose (between the two concentrations), $\Pi = cRT = 275\times8.314\times293.15 = 6.7\times10^5$ Pa ≈ 0.67 MPa (with an uncertainty of about ±0.06 MPa from the spacing of the series).
(c) The strands are attached where the plasma membrane is linked to the wall, plausibly at plasmodesmata and at sites where membrane proteins (for example arabinogalactan proteins or wall-associated kinases) bind wall polymers. Keeping the membrane linked to the wall may help the cell re-expand without tearing when water returns, preserve plasmodesmatal connections, and allow the cell to sense that it has lost contact with its wall — a signal of water stress.
:::
:::

::: exercise Dead pipes and living pipes {level=3}
Explain, using what you know of membranes and flow, why it is an advantage for xylem conduits to be dead and for sieve elements to be alive. In your answer, consider (a) what would happen to water flow through a xylem vessel if it were full of cytoplasm and crossed by plasma membranes at each end of each cell; (b) what drives flow in the phloem and why that requires living cells; (c) why sieve elements nevertheless lose their nucleus and vacuole.
::: solution
(a) Water crossing a membrane must pass through aquaporins or the lipid bilayer, a large resistance compared with flow down an open tube; and cytoplasm, with its cytoskeleton and organelles, would greatly increase the viscous resistance. Hollow, dead conduits with perforated or absent end walls give the lowest possible resistance for the tension-driven flow of the transpiration stream; their lignified walls also resist collapse under tension, which a living, turgid cell's wall would not need to do.
(b) Phloem flow is driven by a pressure difference generated osmotically: sugar is actively loaded into sieve elements at the source and removed at the sink. Active loading requires membranes and energy — proton pumps and sucrose–H⁺ symporters in the plasma membrane — and keeping sugar inside the conduit requires an intact, selectively permeable membrane. So the conduit must be alive.
(c) Organelles and a large vacuole in the lumen would obstruct the flow; by losing them the sieve element approaches the open-pipe geometry of the xylem while keeping its plasma membrane. The proteins and ATP it needs are supplied by the companion cell through plasmodesmata — a division of labour between a conduit cell and a metabolic cell.
:::
