In the 1640s the Flemish physician Jan Baptist van Helmont planted a willow shoot weighing 5 pounds in a tub holding 200 pounds of dried soil, watered it with rainwater for five years, and then weighed both again. The willow now weighed about 169 pounds; the soil had lost only about 2 ounces. Van Helmont concluded that the tree had been made from water. He was half right: we now know that most of the dry mass came from carbon dioxide in the air, and much of the rest from water. But the 2 ounces matter too. They were the mineral nutrients — nitrogen (which in nature comes mostly from soil organic matter and microbes), phosphorus, potassium, calcium, iron and a dozen others — without which no plant can grow, and whose supply limits plant growth in most of the world's ecosystems and in all intensive agriculture.

This chapter is about those elements. We start with what makes an element **essential**, what each does and how deficiency shows itself. We then follow nutrients from the soil to the root surface, where depletion and diffusion set the limits, across the root's membranes by pumps, channels and carriers whose kinetics we can measure, and into the xylem. Finally we meet the two great symbioses that extend the plant's reach: **mycorrhizae**, in which fungi act as the roots' roots, and **nitrogen fixation**, in which bacteria living in root nodules turn atmospheric N₂ into ammonia. Membrane transport ([[cell-biology/membrane-transport]]) and the transpiration stream that carries nutrients upwards ([[plant-biology/water-transport]]) are prerequisites.

::: warning "Plants get their food from the soil"
Many people, like van Helmont's contemporaries, think that plants build their bodies from soil. In fact the bulk of plant dry mass — about 45 % carbon, 42 % oxygen and 6 % hydrogen — comes from CO₂ and water. Mineral elements together make up only a few per cent of dry mass. They are nonetheless indispensable, and because soils supply them slowly they usually limit growth more than carbon does. A related misconception is that plants feed on "organic" fertiliser directly: with minor exceptions (some amino acids and urea are taken up), roots absorb nutrients as inorganic ions — nitrate, ammonium, phosphate, potassium — that microbes release from organic matter.
:::

## Essential elements

How can we tell which elements a plant needs? In the 1860s Julius Sachs and Wilhelm Knop grew plants with their roots in solutions of known salts, without soil — the first **hydroponic** culture — and showed that a small set of elements suffices for a complete life cycle. Later work, with ever purer chemicals and vessels, added elements needed in such small amounts that impurities had previously supplied them.

::: definition Essential element {#def-essential}
An element is **essential** for a plant if (Daniel Arnon and Perry Stout, 1939): (1) the plant cannot complete its life cycle without it; (2) its function cannot be replaced by another element; and (3) it is directly involved in the plant's metabolism, as a constituent of an essential molecule or as a required cofactor. Essential elements needed in relatively large amounts (above about 0.1 % of dry mass) are **macronutrients**; those needed in small amounts (below about 100 mg per kg dry mass) are **micronutrients**. Elements that promote growth in some species but are not essential for all, such as sodium, silicon, cobalt and selenium, are called **beneficial**.
:::

Besides carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, there are fourteen generally recognised essential mineral elements: the macronutrients nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulfur, and the micronutrients iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine and nickel. Nickel was the last added, after Patrick Brown, Ross Welch and Earle Cary showed in 1987 that barley grown without it could not produce viable seed; it is a cofactor of urease.

### Deficiency symptoms and mobility

Each element's deficiency produces characteristic symptoms, and where they appear first depends on whether the element can be moved in the phloem from old leaves to young ones. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium are **mobile**: a deficient plant scavenges them from its older leaves — nitrogen from degraded Rubisco and chloroplast proteins — so symptoms appear first on old leaves. Calcium, iron and boron are **immobile**: once deposited in a leaf they stay, so a deficiency shows first in young leaves, growing tips and fruits. Calcium enters organs only in the xylem, which is why fruits, which transpire little, are prone to calcium deficiency disorders such as blossom-end rot in tomato and bitter pit in apple, even in soil rich in calcium.

Common symptoms are **chlorosis** (yellowing, from impaired chlorophyll synthesis or degradation of chloroplasts), often uniform in nitrogen deficiency and between the veins in magnesium and iron deficiency; **necrosis** (dead patches), at the leaf margins in potassium deficiency; dark green or purplish leaves in phosphorus deficiency, as anthocyanins accumulate; and death of the shoot tip in boron and calcium deficiency.

::: solution
Interveinal chlorosis points to iron or magnesium (both needed for chlorophyll). Its appearance in *young* leaves points to an immobile element: iron. Magnesium is phloem-mobile, so its deficiency appears first in old leaves; nitrogen deficiency gives uniform chlorosis of old leaves; potassium deficiency gives marginal scorching of old leaves. Iron deficiency is common on alkaline, calcareous soils, where iron is present but insoluble.

## From soil to root surface

Soil is a mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air and organisms. Clay particles and humus carry negative surface charges and hold cations — K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, NH₄⁺ — loosely on their surfaces, from which they can be exchanged for other cations; the total amount a soil can hold is its **cation exchange capacity**. Roots exploit this by releasing protons, which displace bound cations into the soil solution. Anions such as nitrate are not held and move freely with water, which is why nitrate is easily leached from soils into groundwater. Phosphate is the opposite: it forms insoluble compounds with calcium in alkaline soils and with iron and aluminium in acid soils, so the soil solution typically contains only micromolar phosphate even where the total phosphorus content is high.

Soil pH controls availability. Iron, manganese and zinc become insoluble at high pH; aluminium becomes soluble and toxic below about pH 5, damaging root tips; phosphate is most available in slightly acid soils, around pH 6–7. 

Nutrients reach the root surface by two processes. **Mass flow** carries dissolved ions with the water drawn to the root by transpiration; for nitrate, calcium and magnesium in many soils it supplies much of the demand. **Diffusion** brings ions down concentration gradients towards the root as uptake depletes the soil around it; for phosphate and potassium, which are present at low concentrations in the solution and strongly bound, it is the main supply. Diffusion through soil is slow, because ions must follow tortuous paths through water films and are repeatedly adsorbed to particles.

::: proposition How far does a depletion zone extend? {#prop-depletion}
If a nutrient diffuses through soil with effective diffusion coefficient $D_e$ (which accounts for the tortuosity of the water paths and for adsorption on soil particles), then in time $t$ a depletion zone spreads from the root surface to a characteristic distance

$$
\ell \approx \sqrt{2D_e t}.
$$ {#eq-depletion}

This is the root-mean-square displacement of one-dimensional diffusion ([[cell-biology/cells-and-microscopy]]). Effective diffusion coefficients in moist soil are of the order of $10^{-10}$ m² s⁻¹ for nitrate and $10^{-12}$ to $10^{-15}$ m² s⁻¹ for phosphate.
:::

::: example Depletion zones of nitrate and phosphate {#ex-depletion}
Estimate the distance from a root over which it can draw nutrients in one day, for nitrate with $D_e = 10^{-10}$ m² s⁻¹ and phosphate with $D_e = 10^{-13}$ m² s⁻¹.
::: solution
In one day, $t = 86{,}400$ s. For nitrate, $\ell = \sqrt{2\times10^{-10}\times86400} = 4.2\times10^{-3}$ m, about 4 mm. For phosphate, $\ell = \sqrt{2\times10^{-13}\times86400} = 1.3\times10^{-4}$ m, about 0.13 mm.

A root can drain nitrate from a cylinder of soil several millimetres across, so neighbouring roots compete for it; but it can draw phosphate only from a sheath a tenth of a millimetre thick, comparable with the length of its root hairs. To get more phosphate the plant must grow new root surface into fresh soil — hence root hairs, clusters of fine roots, and above all mycorrhizal fungi, whose hyphae, a few micrometres thick, extend centimetres beyond the depletion zone.
:::
:::

## Across the membranes

Once at the root surface, ions enter the apoplast of the epidermis and cortex, and must cross a plasma membrane — at the latest at the endodermis, whose Casparian strip blocks the apoplast ([[plant-biology/plant-form]]). Inside, they move symplastically to the stele and are released into the xylem by other transporters.

The energy for uptake comes from the **plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase**, which pumps protons out of the cell, making the cytoplasm electrically negative by −120 to −200 mV relative to the outside and the apoplast about 1.5–2 pH units more acidic than the cytosol. This proton-motive force powers uptake in two ways ([[cell-biology/membrane-transport]]).

- **Channels** let cations flow down their electrochemical gradient. Because the cytoplasm is so negative, K⁺ can enter passively through inward-rectifying channels (such as AKT1 in *Arabidopsis*) even when the soil concentration is far below that in the cell.
- **Proton-coupled symporters** move anions — nitrate, phosphate, sulfate — and sugars or amino acids into the cell against the membrane potential, each molecule accompanied by one or more protons running down their gradient.

::: example When can potassium enter passively? {#ex-nernst-k}
A root epidermal cell holds 100 mmol L⁻¹ K⁺ in its cytosol and has a membrane potential of −180 mV at 20 °C. Below what external K⁺ concentration can K⁺ no longer enter through channels?
::: solution
K⁺ flows inwards through a channel only if the membrane potential is more negative than the potassium equilibrium potential, $E_K = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\frac{[K^+]_o}{[K^+]_i}$ (the Nernst equation, [[cell-biology/membrane-transport#prop-nernst]]). The limit is $E_K = V_m$, so

$$
[K^+]_o = [K^+]_i\exp\!\left(\frac{FV_m}{RT}\right) = 100\times\exp\!\left(\frac{-180}{25.3}\right)\ \text{mmol L}^{-1} = 0.080\ \text{mmol L}^{-1},
$$

using $RT/F = 25.3$ mV at 20 °C. Channels can supply K⁺ down to about 80 µmol L⁻¹ outside. Below that, uptake requires active, proton-coupled transporters (in *Arabidopsis*, the HAK5 transporter). This matches the experiments: *akt1* mutant seedlings grow normally at high K⁺ but poorly at low K⁺ in conditions that block the high-affinity transporter (Hirsch and colleagues, 1998).
:::
:::

### Uptake kinetics

In the 1950s Emanuel Epstein and Carl Hagen measured the rate at which excised barley roots absorbed radioactive potassium and rubidium from solutions of different concentration, and found that uptake rose and saturated like an enzyme reaction.

::: proposition Michaelis–Menten uptake {#prop-mm-uptake}
If uptake of an ion is mediated by a saturable transport system, with the transporter in rapid equilibrium with the ion at the membrane surface, the rate of uptake per unit root mass is

$$
v = \frac{V_{\max}\,c}{K_\mathrm{m} + c},
$$ {#eq-mm-uptake}

where $c$ is the external concentration, $V_{\max}$ the maximum rate (proportional to the number of active transporters) and $K_\mathrm{m}$ the concentration at half-maximal rate — the same form as the Michaelis–Menten equation for enzymes ([[biochemistry/enzymes]]). The equation assumes a single transport system, a well-stirred solution (no depletion near the root) and no efflux.
:::

Epstein's later measurements (Epstein, Rains and Elzam, 1963) over a wider concentration range showed a second phase of uptake at higher concentrations: a **high-affinity transport system** (HATS) with $K_\mathrm{m}$ of about 20 µmol L⁻¹ for K⁺, saturating below 0.2 mmol L⁻¹, and a **low-affinity system** (LATS) that kept rising up to tens of millimoles. The "dual isotherm" is now known to reflect different transporters and channels. For nitrate, the high-affinity transporters belong to the NRT2 family, and the transporter NRT1.1 (also called NPF6.3) switches between low and high affinity when it is phosphorylated, and also acts as a nitrate sensor — a **transceptor** (Yi-Fang Tsay and colleagues, 1993–2009).

::: example A dual uptake isotherm {#ex-dual}
A root absorbs K⁺ by a high-affinity system with $V_{\max} = 10$ µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹ and $K_\mathrm{m} = 20$ µmol L⁻¹, and a low-affinity system with $V_{\max} = 20$ µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹ and $K_\mathrm{m} = 5$ mmol L⁻¹. Calculate the total uptake rate at 10 µmol L⁻¹, 100 µmol L⁻¹, 1 mmol L⁻¹ and 10 mmol L⁻¹, and the fraction contributed by the high-affinity system in each case.
::: solution
Writing concentrations in µmol L⁻¹ and summing two terms of [[#eq-mm-uptake]]:

| $c$ (µmol L⁻¹) | HATS | LATS | total (µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹) | HATS fraction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | $10\times10/30 = 3.33$ | $20\times10/5010 = 0.04$ | 3.37 | 99 % |
| 100 | $10\times100/120 = 8.33$ | $20\times100/5100 = 0.39$ | 8.73 | 95 % |
| 1000 | $9.80$ | $20\times1000/6000 = 3.33$ | 13.1 | 75 % |
| 10000 | $9.98$ | $20\times10^4/1.5\times10^4 = 13.3$ | 23.3 | 43 % |

At the low concentrations typical of soil solutions, the high-affinity system does almost all the work. At high concentrations (in a fertilised field or a nutrient solution) it is saturated and the low-affinity system takes over. Because roots in soil usually face micromolar concentrations at their surface, the high-affinity transporters are the targets of most efforts to improve nutrient-use efficiency.
:::

### Iron: two strategies

Iron is abundant in soils but almost insoluble as Fe³⁺ oxides and hydroxides at neutral and alkaline pH. Plants have evolved two ways to get it. **Strategy I**, used by all flowering plants except grasses, acidifies the rhizosphere with H⁺-ATPases, releases phenolic compounds that chelate Fe³⁺, reduces Fe³⁺ to the more soluble Fe²⁺ with a plasma-membrane ferric chelate reductase (FRO2 in *Arabidopsis*), and takes up Fe²⁺ through the transporter IRT1. **Strategy II**, used by grasses, secretes **phytosiderophores** — mugineic acids, non-protein amino acids that bind Fe³⁺ strongly — and takes up the Fe³⁺–phytosiderophore complexes whole through transporters of the YELLOW STRIPE 1 family (first identified in maize). Strategy II works better on calcareous soils, one reason why grasses tolerate them.

## Assimilating nitrogen

Nitrogen enters plants mainly as nitrate in aerobic agricultural soils and as ammonium in acid, waterlogged or forest soils. Nitrate must be reduced before use: **nitrate reductase**, a cytosolic molybdenum enzyme, reduces it to nitrite with NAD(P)H, and **nitrite reductase** in plastids reduces nitrite to ammonium with reduced ferredoxin — eight electrons in all per nitrogen atom. Ammonium, whether taken up or produced by reduction, is toxic in excess and is incorporated at once into amino acids by the **glutamine synthetase–glutamate synthase (GS–GOGAT)** cycle: glutamine synthetase adds ammonium to glutamate to make glutamine, using ATP, and glutamate synthase transfers the amide group of glutamine to 2-oxoglutarate, yielding two glutamates. Glutamate and glutamine then donate nitrogen to all other amino acids and nucleotides. In leaves, nitrate reduction runs partly on the reductant of photosynthesis; in roots, on respiration.

## Mycorrhizae

Most land plants do not take up nutrients through their roots alone. Their roots are colonised by fungi that form **mycorrhizae** ("fungus roots"), mutualisms in which the fungus supplies mineral nutrients — above all phosphate, and also nitrogen, zinc and water — and receives sugars and lipids from the plant ([[diversity-of-life/fungi]]).

::: definition Mycorrhiza {#def-mycorrhiza}
A **mycorrhiza** is a symbiotic association between a fungus and the roots of a plant in which nutrients and carbon are exchanged across specialised interfaces. In **arbuscular mycorrhizae** (AM), fungi of the Glomeromycotina grow inside the root cortex and form highly branched **arbuscules** inside cortical cells, surrounded by the plant's plasma membrane, where exchange takes place. In **ectomycorrhizae** (ECM), fungi of the Basidiomycota and Ascomycota form a sheath, the **mantle**, around short roots and a network of hyphae between the cortical cells, the **Hartig net**, without entering the cells. Other types include ericoid and orchid mycorrhizae.
:::

Arbuscular mycorrhizae are the commonest: a compilation by Mark Brundrett and Leho Tedersoo (2018) estimated that about 72 % of vascular plant species form them, about 2 % form ectomycorrhizae (but these include the dominant trees of temperate and boreal forests — pines, oaks, beeches, birches — and the dipterocarps of South-East Asia), about 10 % form orchid mycorrhizae and 1–2 % ericoid mycorrhizae, and about 7–8 % are non-mycorrhizal, including most members of the cabbage family (Brassicaceae, to which *Arabidopsis* belongs). The association is ancient: arbuscule-like structures are preserved in plants of the 407-million-year-old Rhynie chert, and the plant genes needed for it are found in liverworts and their relatives as well as flowering plants, so the symbiosis probably helped plants colonise land.

The fungal hyphae extend several centimetres from the root, far beyond the phosphate depletion zone of [[#ex-depletion]], and their small diameter gives them a large surface per unit of carbon invested. In return the plant supplies the fungus with sugars and, as discovered in the 2010s, with fatty acids, which AM fungi cannot make for themselves; estimates of the cost range from a few per cent to about a fifth of the plant's photosynthate. Plants regulate the partnership: well-supplied with phosphate, they suppress colonisation, and they appear to deliver more carbon to roots colonised by fungi that deliver more phosphate.

The dialogue begins with chemical signals. Roots exude **strigolactones**, carotenoid-derived compounds that stimulate hyphal branching in AM fungi (Kohki Akiyama, Hideo Hayashi and colleagues, 2005) — and that are also plant hormones controlling shoot branching ([[plant-biology/plant-hormones]]), and germination cues exploited by parasitic witchweeds (*Striga*). The fungi reply with chitin-derived signals that the plant perceives with receptor kinases. Inside the root, the signal is relayed by a **common symbiosis signalling pathway** that is shared, remarkably, with the root-nodule symbiosis described next: a receptor-like kinase, nuclear ion channels, oscillations of calcium concentration in and around the nucleus, and a calcium- and calmodulin-dependent kinase (CCaMK) that decodes them.

## Nitrogen fixation

Nitrogen is the nutrient that most often limits plant growth, although the air is 78 % N₂. The triple bond of N₂ is so stable that only one enzyme in nature breaks it: **nitrogenase**, found only in bacteria and archaea.

::: proposition The nitrogenase reaction {#prop-nitrogenase}
Nitrogenase, a complex of an iron protein and a molybdenum–iron protein, reduces dinitrogen to ammonia with the obligatory evolution of hydrogen, with the limiting stoichiometry

$$
\mathrm{N_2 + 8\,H^+ + 8\,e^- + 16\,MgATP \;\longrightarrow\; 2\,NH_3 + H_2 + 16\,MgADP + 16\,P_i}.
$$ {#eq-nitrogenase}

The stoichiometry is empirical: it is the minimum measured under optimal conditions, and real cells use more ATP and electrons. Nitrogenase is irreversibly inactivated by oxygen.
:::

::: example The energy cost of fixing nitrogen {#ex-nfix-cost}
Estimate the minimum cost of symbiotic nitrogen fixation in glucose, assuming that each pair of electrons supplied to nitrogenase costs the equivalent of 2.5 ATP (one NADH) and that complete respiration of glucose yields 30 ATP. Express the answer as grams of carbon respired per gram of nitrogen fixed.
::: solution
Per NH₃ (half of [[#eq-nitrogenase]]): 8 ATP and 4 electrons. The 4 electrons are 2 pairs, costing $2\times2.5 = 5$ ATP-equivalents. Total: 13 ATP per N. Glucose needed: $13/30 = 0.43$ mol per mol N. Carbon: $0.43\times72$ g $= 31$ g C per 14 g N, or **2.2 g C per g N**.

This is a theoretical minimum. Measured costs, which include building and maintaining nodules, feeding the bacteria and protecting nitrogenase from oxygen, are substantially higher, commonly put at several grams of carbon per gram of nitrogen. Assimilating nitrate from the soil also costs energy (8 electrons per N for reduction), but less — which is why legumes given ample nitrate suppress nodulation.
:::
:::

Some free-living bacteria fix nitrogen, and cyanobacteria such as *Anabaena* do so in **heterocysts**, specialised cells that lack photosystem II and so make no oxygen. But the largest biological inputs on land come from symbioses, above all between legumes (Fabaceae) and bacteria collectively called **rhizobia** (genera *Rhizobium*, *Sinorhizobium*, *Bradyrhizobium*, *Mesorhizobium* and others). A smaller group of trees and shrubs, the actinorhizal plants such as alder (*Alnus*), forms nodules with the actinobacterium *Frankia*.

::: definition Root nodule symbiosis {#def-nodule}
The legume–rhizobium symbiosis develops in steps. (1) Legume roots release **flavonoids**, which activate the bacterial *nod* genes. (2) The bacteria produce **Nod factors**, lipochitooligosaccharides whose structure is specific to each partnership, perceived by LysM receptor kinases on root hairs. (3) The root hair curls around attached bacteria and forms an **infection thread**, a tube of plant wall material through which the multiplying bacteria grow inwards. (4) Meanwhile cortical cells divide to form a nodule primordium. (5) Bacteria are released from the infection threads into nodule cells, enclosed in plant membranes, and differentiate into nitrogen-fixing **bacteroids** inside **symbiosomes**. (6) The plant supplies the bacteroids with dicarboxylic acids (malate, succinate) and receives ammonia, which it assimilates by GS–GOGAT.
:::

The nodule solves nitrogenase's oxygen problem in a striking way. Bacteroids need oxygen for respiration to make ATP, but nitrogenase is destroyed by it. Nodule cells are packed with **leghaemoglobin**, a plant haemoglobin that gives active nodules their pink interior. It binds O₂ with high affinity and delivers it to the bacteroids' high-affinity terminal oxidases while holding the free O₂ concentration in the infected cells in the nanomolar range, and a barrier of tightly packed cells around the infected zone restricts oxygen diffusion into the nodule. Plants whose leghaemoglobin genes are silenced form nodules that do not fix nitrogen. The plant also polices its partners: soybean plants reduce the oxygen supply to nodules whose bacteria do not fix nitrogen, and those bacteria multiply less (Toby Kiers and colleagues, 2003).

::: application Feeding the world with fixed nitrogen
Before the twentieth century, farmers could add nitrogen to soils only by manuring, by rotating crops with legumes, or by importing guano and Chilean saltpetre. In 1909 Fritz Haber showed that ammonia could be synthesised from N₂ and H₂ at high pressure and temperature over an iron catalyst, and by 1913 Carl Bosch had scaled the process up at BASF. The **Haber–Bosch process** now fixes more nitrogen than all natural terrestrial processes combined (David Fowler and colleagues, 2013, estimated about 120 Tg N per year by Haber–Bosch against about 58 Tg by natural terrestrial fixation), and it has been estimated that crops grown with synthetic fertiliser feed roughly half of the world's population (Jan Willem Erisman and colleagues, 2008). The cost is large: ammonia synthesis uses on the order of 1–2 % of the world's energy supply, and much of the applied nitrogen is lost as nitrate leaching into rivers, where it drives eutrophication, and as nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. Hence the interest in improving nitrogen-use efficiency of crops, in legume rotations, and in the long-term goal of engineering nitrogen-fixing symbioses — or nitrogenase itself — into cereals.
:::

::: history
Carl Sprengel (1828) and Justus von Liebig (1840) argued that plants take mineral elements from the soil, and Liebig popularised the idea that growth is limited by the scarcest essential nutrient (the "law of the minimum", first stated by Sprengel). Julius Sachs (1860) and Wilhelm Knop (1865) grew plants in nutrient solutions. Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth (1886, published 1888) showed that legumes, but not cereals, grow on nitrogen-free sand if their roots form nodules, and that sterilising the soil prevents nodulation; Martinus Beijerinck isolated the nodule bacteria in 1888. Albert Bernhard Frank named mycorrhizae in 1885. Arnon and Stout (1939) defined essentiality, and Dennis Hoagland's nutrient solutions became the laboratory standard. Epstein and Hagen (1952) brought enzyme kinetics to ion uptake. The molecular era began with the cloning of the first plant nutrient transporters in the early 1990s — the K⁺ channels KAT1 and AKT1 in 1992 and the nitrate transporter CHL1 (NRT1.1) in 1993 — and the identification of Nod factors by Jean Dénarié and colleagues in 1990.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
- Plants need 14 mineral elements: macronutrients N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S and micronutrients Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni. Essentiality is defined by Arnon and Stout's criteria; amounts range over a million-fold (N : Mo ≈ 10⁶ atoms).
- Deficiency symptoms appear first in old leaves for phloem-mobile elements (N, P, K, Mg) and in young tissues for immobile ones (Ca, Fe, B).
- Nutrients reach roots by mass flow and diffusion; depletion zones spread as $\ell \approx \sqrt{2D_et}$ — millimetres for nitrate, a tenth of a millimetre for phosphate in a day.
- The plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase energises uptake: cations enter through channels down the membrane potential (K⁺ passively above about 80 µmol L⁻¹ at −180 mV), anions by H⁺-coupled symport.
- Uptake follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics; dual isotherms reveal high-affinity and low-affinity systems. Iron is obtained by reduction (Strategy I) or phytosiderophores (Strategy II, grasses).
- Nitrate is reduced to ammonium (8 electrons) and assimilated by GS–GOGAT.
- Arbuscular mycorrhizae (about 72 % of species) and ectomycorrhizae extend the root's reach for phosphate and nitrogen in exchange for sugars and lipids; strigolactones and a common symbiosis signalling pathway initiate them.
- Nitrogenase: $\mathrm{N_2 + 8H^+ + 8e^- + 16ATP \to 2NH_3 + H_2}$, oxygen-sensitive; minimum cost about 2 g C per g N. Legume nodules form via flavonoids, Nod factors and infection threads; leghaemoglobin buffers O₂.
:::

With water and minerals supplied, the plant can do what makes it a plant: capture light. [[plant-biology/photosynthesis]] begins with the light reactions.

::: exercise Diagnosing a crop {level=2}
For each case name the most likely deficiency and justify it from mobility and function: (a) a maize field whose older leaves turn uniformly pale yellow from the tip down the midrib, while young leaves stay green; (b) celery whose growing hearts turn black, in a dry summer, on soil well supplied with lime; (c) an oat crop on an alkaline soil with grey-brown spots on its young leaves (a disorder called grey speck); (d) cabbages whose older leaves are scorched brown at the margins.
::: solution
(a) Nitrogen: mobile (remobilised from old leaves), needed for chlorophyll and proteins; uniform chlorosis starting in old leaves is the classic sign.
(b) Calcium: immobile, supplied only by the xylem; in dry weather transpiration-driven delivery to enclosed, slowly transpiring tissues (the heart) is inadequate even when soil calcium is plentiful (black heart).
(c) Manganese: immobile, insoluble at high pH, needed for photosystem II and superoxide dismutase; grey speck of oats is the classic manganese deficiency.
(d) Potassium: mobile, so first in old leaves; marginal scorch arises as K⁺, the main osmoticum, is withdrawn, and the leaf edges dry and die.
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a mycorrhiza experiment {level=2}
Leek plants are grown in sterilised, phosphate-poor soil with or without an arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus, and at three levels of added phosphate. After eight weeks: without phosphate, mycorrhizal plants have 4 times the shoot mass and 6 times the shoot phosphorus of non-mycorrhizal plants; at the highest phosphate, the two groups do not differ, and root colonisation by the fungus has fallen from 60 % to 10 % of root length. (a) Explain the result at low phosphate. (b) Explain the loss of benefit and of colonisation at high phosphate. (c) Why use sterilised soil, and what control would you add to show that the growth benefit is due to phosphate rather than something else the fungus provides?
::: solution
(a) The hyphae reach beyond the root's thin phosphate depletion zone ([[#ex-depletion]]) and deliver phosphate to the plant; phosphorus limits growth, so growth rises with phosphate supply.
(b) With ample phosphate, the roots alone can absorb enough, so the fungus no longer adds phosphate but still costs carbon; the plant suppresses colonisation (phosphate status down-regulates strigolactone release and symbiosis genes), so the fungal benefit and colonisation both decline. At high phosphate, mycorrhizae can even reduce growth slightly because of their carbon cost.
(c) Sterilising removes native mycorrhizal fungi and other microbes so that the non-mycorrhizal treatment is genuinely uncolonised. A control is to give non-mycorrhizal plants enough extra phosphate to match the phosphorus content of mycorrhizal plants: if their growth then matches too, the benefit is due to phosphate. Adding a filtrate of the fungal inoculum (microbes other than the fungus) to the non-mycorrhizal pots controls for other organisms introduced with the inoculum.
:::
:::

::: exercise Nodulation mutants {level=2}
Explain the expected phenotype of each legume mutant when grown without fixed nitrogen, and say whether it would be rescued by adding nitrate: (a) a mutant lacking the LysM receptor that recognises Nod factors; (b) a mutant lacking CCaMK; (c) a mutant whose nodules lack leghaemoglobin; (d) a mutant that forms many more nodules than normal (hypernodulating), even in the presence of nitrate.
::: solution
(a) No root-hair curling or infection threads, no nodules; nitrogen-starved (pale, stunted); rescued by nitrate. Since this receptor is specific to Nod factors, mycorrhization is normal.
(b) CCaMK decodes calcium spiking in the common symbiosis pathway, so neither nodules nor arbuscular mycorrhizae form; rescued by nitrate (and by phosphate for the mycorrhizal defect).
(c) Nodules form but are white and do not fix nitrogen, because free O₂ inactivates nitrogenase (and bacteroid respiration lacks a high-affinity O₂ supply); starved of N; rescued by nitrate.
(d) A defect in the systemic **autoregulation of nodulation** (a shoot-derived feedback, involving CLE peptides and a shoot receptor kinase, that limits nodule number) or in nitrate inhibition; plants are often stunted because nodules drain carbon. Nitrate does not suppress nodulation in such mutants.
:::

::: exercise Liebig's barrel {level=3}
Liebig's law of the minimum states that growth is limited only by the scarcest resource relative to need, like water in a barrel whose height is set by its shortest stave. A fertiliser trial gives the following grain yields (t ha⁻¹): no fertiliser 2.0; N only 3.0; P only 2.4; N + P 5.5. (a) Would Liebig's law predict this pattern? (b) What does the result suggest about how N and P limit growth together? (c) Suggest a physiological reason why adding N could increase the plant's ability to obtain P.
::: solution
(a) Under strict Liebig limitation, adding the limiting nutrient raises yield until the other becomes limiting, and adding the non-limiting nutrient does nothing. Here both N alone and P alone increase yield, and together they increase it more than the sum of their separate effects ($+1.0 + 0.4 = 1.4$, against $+3.5$ observed). This is not Liebig's pattern.
(b) The crop is **co-limited** by N and P, with a synergistic interaction: each nutrient's effect depends on the level of the other. Co-limitation is common in ecosystem fertilisation experiments, which is why Liebig's law is regarded as a useful first approximation, not a law.
(c) Nitrogen allows more growth of roots and root hairs, more synthesis of phosphate transporters and of phosphatases (enzymes, rich in N, that release phosphate from organic compounds), and more photosynthate to feed mycorrhizal fungi; thus N supply improves P acquisition, and vice versa, P supports the ATP-intensive assimilation of N.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why fix nitrogen at all? {level=3}
Given the cost in [[#ex-nfix-cost]], explain (a) why legumes are common pioneers on nitrogen-poor soils and after fires, (b) why legumes become less competitive when soils are fertilised with nitrogen, and (c) why engineering cereals to fix their own nitrogen would not make fertiliser free, even if it worked perfectly.
::: solution
(a) On nitrogen-poor soils, fixation provides a resource that competitors cannot get; its carbon cost is affordable when light is plentiful (open, disturbed sites) and nitrogen is the factor that limits growth most.
(b) With abundant soil nitrogen, absorbing nitrate or ammonium is cheaper than fixing N₂, and legumes suppress nodulation; they lose their special advantage and must compete for soil nitrogen with grasses, which are often better at it.
(c) The energy for fixation would come from the crop's own photosynthate — at least about 2 g C per g N and in practice several times more — so yield would fall by the carbon diverted, unless photosynthesis were also improved. The cost of nitrogen would be paid in sugar rather than in natural gas, which may still be worthwhile, especially for farmers who cannot buy fertiliser, but it is not free.
:::
