Every lineage on the tree of [[diversity-of-life/classifying-life]] traces back to a single root, the last universal common ancestor, LUCA. LUCA was already a sophisticated cell: it had a genetic code, ribosomes, a membrane with proteins embedded in it and probably hundreds of enzymes. Before LUCA there must have been simpler systems, and before those, chemistry with no biology at all. How did chemistry become biology? This is the problem of the **origin of life** (abiogenesis). It is unsolved, but it is no longer a matter of speculation alone: experiments have shown how many of life's building blocks form without life, how RNA can act as both gene and catalyst, and how simple membranes grow and divide; and the rock record dates the first traces of life to within the first billion years of Earth's history.

This chapter treats the problem as a scientist would — by dividing it into steps that can be tested. We first ask what has to be explained, and what the early Earth was like. We then follow three lines of research: prebiotic chemistry, the RNA world with its quantitative limit (Eigen's error threshold), and protocells. Finally we turn to the evidence of the rocks — microfossils, stromatolites and isotopes — and to what can be inferred about LUCA and the first two billion years of life. The geology is treated in more depth in the Earth Atlas ([early Earth](/learn/earth/lesson.php?c=earth-history&l=early-earth) and [oxygen and the Proterozoic](/learn/earth/lesson.php?c=earth-history&l=oxygen-proterozoic)).

## What has to be explained

::: definition A working definition of life {#def-life}
There is no universally agreed definition of life. A widely used working definition, adopted by NASA's exobiology programme following Gerald Joyce (1994), is: life is **a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution**. "Self-sustaining" means that the system obtains energy and materials from its environment and maintains itself; "Darwinian evolution" requires heredity (information copied to offspring), variation (imperfect copying) and selection (differential reproduction). All known life consists of **cells**: compartments bounded by a lipid membrane, containing a genome of DNA, transcribed into RNA and translated into protein by ribosomes, and a metabolism powered by chemical or light energy, usually through ion gradients across a membrane.
:::

The definition emphasises what makes the origin of life hard. Modern cells are built from three kinds of polymer — DNA, RNA and protein — that depend on each other: DNA is copied and transcribed by proteins; proteins are made by ribosomes according to RNA instructions; and ribosomes are made of RNA and protein. Which came first? Any account must explain the origin of (1) the building blocks (nucleotides, amino acids, lipids), (2) their polymers, (3) a system of replication that allows evolution, (4) compartments that keep replicators and their products together, (5) a supply of energy and (6) the genetic code linking nucleic acids to proteins. These may have arisen in different orders and in different places.

One historical question was settled long ago. Until the seventeenth century it was commonly believed that maggots arose from rotting meat and microbes from broth — **spontaneous generation**. Francesco Redi (1668) showed that maggots appear only where flies can lay eggs, and Louis Pasteur (1859–1862), with flasks whose S-shaped necks let air in but trapped dust, showed that boiled broth stays sterile indefinitely. Life today comes only from life. Its origin was a one-time historical event under the very different conditions of the early Earth.

## The early Earth

The solar system formed 4.567 billion years ago (the age of the oldest solids in meteorites), and the Earth reached roughly its present size within a few tens of millions of years, its age being usually quoted as **4.54 ± 0.05 Ga** (Ga, billions of years ago). A giant impact soon after formed the Moon. The oldest terrestrial minerals, zircon grains from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, are up to 4.4 billion years old, and their oxygen isotopes indicate that they crystallised from magma that had interacted with liquid water (Simon Wilde and colleagues, 2001): there were probably oceans by about 4.4–4.3 Ga. The early atmosphere is thought to have been dominated by CO₂ and N₂ with some water vapour and only traces of free oxygen; how much hydrogen, methane and ammonia it contained is uncertain and matters for prebiotic chemistry. Large impacts continued, and whether a "late heavy bombardment" around 3.9 Ga sterilised the surface is now doubted, since the evidence for a sharp spike in impacts has weakened.

The window for the origin of life is therefore bounded below by the establishment of stable oceans (about 4.4–4.3 Ga) and above by the oldest convincing evidence of life (about 3.5 Ga, discussed below) — some hundreds of millions of years. Molecular clocks for LUCA, which are uncertain, have been pushed earlier still (Edmund Moody and colleagues, 2024, estimated about 4.2 Ga). If correct, life appeared quickly once conditions allowed.

## Prebiotic chemistry

In the 1920s Aleksandr Oparin (1924) and J. B. S. Haldane (1929) independently proposed that organic molecules formed abiotically in a reducing early atmosphere and accumulated in the oceans as a "prebiotic soup", from which the first living systems emerged. Darwin had anticipated the idea in a private letter of 1871 to Joseph Hooker, imagining "some warm little pond with all sorts of ammonia and phosphoric salts, light, heat, electricity &c. present".

### The Miller–Urey experiment

In 1953 Stanley Miller, a graduate student of Harold Urey at the University of Chicago, tested the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis directly. He circulated methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water vapour through a glass apparatus past an electric spark (simulating lightning), condensed the products into a flask of water (the "ocean") and boiled it to keep the cycle going. Within a week the water turned brown, and paper chromatography identified glycine, alanine and other amino acids. Reanalysis of Miller's original archived samples with modern methods (Adam Johnson and colleagues, 2008) found more than twenty amino acids, especially in a variant of the apparatus with a steam jet resembling a volcanic eruption.

The result's significance has shifted. If the early atmosphere was mainly CO₂ and N₂ rather than strongly reducing, spark discharges give far lower yields, although yields recover if the experiment is buffered against acidity or if hydrogen was more abundant than once thought, and local reducing environments (volcanic plumes, impact sites) may have mattered. What the experiment established firmly is that the building blocks of proteins form readily from simple gases under plausible energy sources — a fact confirmed by meteorites.

### Nucleotides, sugars and other building blocks

The nucleobase adenine is a pentamer of hydrogen cyanide, C₅H₅N₅ = (HCN)₅, and Joan Oró (1960) produced it by heating concentrated ammonium cyanide. Sugars, including ribose, form from formaldehyde in the alkaline **formose reaction** (Aleksandr Butlerov, 1861), but as part of a tarry mixture of many sugars, and ribose decomposes rapidly in water. Assembling a nucleotide from a sugar, a base and phosphate is hard: joining ribose to the pyrimidine bases in water essentially fails. In 2009 Matthew Powner, Béatrice Gerland and John Sutherland found a way around this, making activated pyrimidine ribonucleotides from cyanamide, cyanoacetylene, glycolaldehyde, glyceraldehyde and phosphate by a route that never passes through free ribose or free bases; ultraviolet light destroyed the unwanted by-products. Sutherland's group later showed that precursors of nucleotides, amino acids and lipids can all be made from hydrogen cyanide, hydrogen sulfide and UV light ("cyanosulfidic" chemistry, Bhavesh Patel and colleagues, 2015). How such chemistry would have been organised on the early Earth — which reagents, in which pools, in what sequence — is an active debate.

Extraterrestrial material shows that this kind of chemistry happens in nature. The Murchison meteorite, which fell in Australia in 1969, contains dozens of amino acids, many of them non-biological, with a near-racemic mixture of left- and right-handed forms and isotope ratios that rule out terrestrial contamination (Keith Kvenvolden and colleagues, 1970). Samples returned by spacecraft from the asteroids Ryugu (Hayabusa2) and Bennu (OSIRIS-REx) contain uracil (Yasuhiro Oba and colleagues, 2023) and, in Bennu, 14 of the 20 amino acids used in proteins and all five nucleobases of DNA and RNA (Daniel Glavin and colleagues, 2025). Organic building blocks are made abiotically throughout the solar system; the hard part is getting from them to systems that copy themselves.

### Where?

Several settings are argued for, with different strengths.

- **Surface pools and hot springs** offer wet–dry cycles, which concentrate solutes and drive condensation reactions (forming peptide and phosphodiester bonds releases water, which drying removes), as well as UV light for photochemistry. David Deamer and Bruce Damer have argued for volcanic hot-spring fields; Sutherland's chemistry also needs surface conditions.
- **Deep-sea hydrothermal vents**, discovered in 1977 on the Galápagos Rift, supply chemical energy. Alkaline vents such as the Lost City field on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (Deborah Kelley and colleagues, 2001), in which seawater reacts with mantle rock (serpentinisation), produce hydrogen and natural pH and redox gradients across thin mineral walls. Michael Russell and William Martin have argued that these gradients could have powered carbon fixation and anticipated the chemiosmotic membranes of all modern cells. A difficulty is that polymers such as RNA tend to hydrolyse in water and are diluted in the open ocean.
- **Mineral surfaces** concentrate and orient molecules. The clay montmorillonite catalyses the joining of activated nucleotides into RNA chains of 30–50 units (James Ferris and colleagues, 1996). Günter Wächtershäuser's "iron–sulfur world" (1988) proposed that metabolism began on iron sulfide surfaces, without genes.

These are not exclusive; different steps may have happened in different places, connected by transport.

## The RNA world

The circularity of DNA, RNA and protein is broken if one molecule once did both jobs. RNA is the natural candidate: like DNA it stores sequence information that can be copied by base pairing, and like a protein it folds into shapes that can catalyse reactions. The idea was raised by Carl Woese, Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel independently in the late 1960s; Walter Gilbert named it the **RNA world** in 1986.

::: definition The RNA world hypothesis {#def-rna-world}
The **RNA world hypothesis** proposes that at an early stage of life, RNA (or a similar nucleic acid) served both as the genetic material and as the main catalyst, replicating itself by RNA-catalysed copying; proteins and DNA were later additions, proteins as better catalysts and DNA as a more stable store of information. An RNA molecule with catalytic activity is a **ribozyme**.
:::

The evidence is of three kinds.

1. **Ribozymes exist.** Thomas Cech's group found that an intron in the ribosomal RNA of the ciliate *Tetrahymena thermophila* splices itself out without proteins (Kelly Kruger and colleagues, 1982), and Sidney Altman's group showed that the RNA component of ribonuclease P is the catalyst that cuts precursor tRNAs (Cecilia Guerrier-Takada and colleagues, 1983). Cech and Altman shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
2. **The ribosome is a ribozyme.** The atomic structures of the large ribosomal subunit (from the archaeon *Haloarcula marismortui*; Nenad Ban, Poul Nissen, Peter Moore, Thomas Steitz and colleagues, 2000) showed that no protein lies within about 18 Å of the site where peptide bonds form: the core of protein synthesis in every cell is catalysed by RNA. Proteins, on this view, were invented by RNA.
3. **Molecular fossils.** Many coenzymes — ATP, NAD⁺, FAD, coenzyme A — contain nucleotides that play no part in their chemistry, as if they were relics of RNA catalysts (Harold White, 1976). DNA is made from RNA nucleotides (deoxyribonucleotides are synthesised by reducing ribonucleotides), and DNA replication starts from RNA primers. Both are what we would expect if RNA came first.

Experiments have tested what ribozymes can do. In **in vitro evolution** (Craig Tuerk and Larry Gold, and Andrew Ellington and Jack Szostak, 1990), pools of $10^{13}$–$10^{15}$ random RNA sequences are screened for a function, the active molecules are amplified with error-prone copying, and the cycle is repeated: selection in a test tube. David Bartel and Szostak (1993) evolved a ribozyme that joins RNA strands (a ligase) from random sequences. From such ligases, laboratory evolution produced **RNA polymerase ribozymes** that copy RNA templates by adding nucleotides one at a time: the first extended a primer by 14 nucleotides (Wendy Johnston and colleagues, 2001); later versions copy templates of 100 or more nucleotides and even other ribozymes, though none yet copies a sequence as long as itself. Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce (2009) made two RNA ligases that assemble each other from four smaller RNA pieces, which grew exponentially with a doubling time of about an hour as long as the pieces were supplied — self-sustained replication, with heritable variation and competition between variants.

::: example Exponential replication in a test tube {#ex-joyce}
Cross-replicating RNA ligases double in number about every hour while substrates last. (a) How long does it take to amplify a population a billion-fold? (b) A variant that doubles every 50 minutes starts at a frequency of 1 %. What is its frequency after 24 hours?
::: solution
(a) A billion-fold increase needs $\log_2 10^9 = 9/\log_{10}2 = 29.9$ doublings, about 30 hours.

(b) In 24 hours the variant doubles $24\times60/50 = 28.8$ times and the original 24 times, so their ratio grows by $2^{4.8} = 27.9$. Starting from 1 : 99, it becomes $27.9 : 99$, a frequency of $27.9/(27.9 + 99) = 0.22$. A 20 % advantage in rate takes the variant from 1 % to 22 % in a day: selection is fast when replication is exponential. This is Darwinian evolution in a system with no proteins at all.
:::
:::

### How long can a genome be? Eigen's error threshold

Copying is never perfect, and a primitive replicase would have made many errors. Manfred Eigen (1971) showed that the error rate sets a limit on how much information can be maintained by selection.

::: proposition The error threshold {#prop-error-threshold}
Consider a population of replicating sequences of length $L$. A "master" sequence replicates $\sigma > 1$ times faster than all its mutants (its **superiority**), and each copy of it is error-free with probability $Q = (1 - \mu)^L \approx e^{-\mu L}$, where $\mu$ is the error rate per nucleotide. Ignoring back-mutation of mutants to the master, the master persists at equilibrium only if $\sigma Q > 1$, that is, if

$$
L < \frac{\ln\sigma}{-\ln(1 - \mu)} \approx \frac{\ln\sigma}{\mu}.
$$ {#eq-error-threshold}

Below this length, the master's equilibrium frequency is

$$
x^* = \frac{\sigma Q - 1}{\sigma - 1}.
$$ {#eq-master}

Above it, the master is lost and the population becomes a cloud of mutants that no longer preserves the information.
:::

::: proof
Let the master be at frequency $x$, with growth rate $\sigma$, of which a fraction $Q$ of offspring are exact copies; mutants have growth rate 1 and (ignoring back-mutation) produce only mutants. With the population kept at constant size by dilution at the mean growth rate $\bar w = \sigma x + (1 - x)$, the master's frequency changes as

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = \sigma Qx - \bar wx = x\big(\sigma Q - 1 - (\sigma - 1)x\big).
$$

The equilibria are $x = 0$ and $x^* = (\sigma Q - 1)/(\sigma - 1)$. The non-zero equilibrium is positive, and stable, only when $\sigma Q > 1$; otherwise $x$ declines to 0. Taking logarithms of $\sigma(1 - \mu)^L > 1$ gives $L < \ln\sigma/(-\ln(1 - \mu))$, and for small $\mu$, $-\ln(1 - \mu) \approx \mu$.
:::

The threshold is strikingly simple: the maximum length is roughly the reciprocal of the error rate, multiplied by a modest factor ($\ln\sigma$ is only 2.3 for $\sigma = 10$ and 4.6 for $\sigma = 100$). Information cannot be maintained if each copy, on average, contains more than about $\ln\sigma$ errors.

::: example The error threshold for a primitive replicator {#ex-error-threshold}
A ribozyme replicase makes errors at $\mu = 0.01$ per nucleotide. (a) If the master sequence has a tenfold advantage ($\sigma = 10$), what is the longest sequence that can be maintained? (b) For $L = 100$, what is the equilibrium frequency of the master? (c) What error rate would be needed to maintain a genome of 2,000 nucleotides with the same $\sigma$?
::: solution
(a) $L_{\max} \approx \ln 10/0.01 = 2.303/0.01 = 230$ nucleotides (229 with the exact form $-\ln 0.99 = 0.01005$).

(b) $Q = 0.99^{100} = 0.366$, so $\sigma Q = 3.66$ and $x^* = (3.66 - 1)/9 = 0.296$: under 30 % of the population carries the exact master sequence, the rest being its mutants.

(c) $\mu < \ln 10/2000 = 1.15\times10^{-3}$, a fidelity of 99.9 % per nucleotide. Here is **Eigen's paradox**: a replicase accurate enough to copy a large genome would itself need a long sequence to encode it, but a long sequence cannot be maintained without an accurate replicase. Proposed ways out include cooperating sets of short replicators (Eigen and Peter Schuster's **hypercycle**, 1977), compartments that let groups of cooperating molecules be selected together, and the fact that real fitness landscapes are not single sharp peaks: many mutants of a ribozyme are nearly as good as the master.
:::
:::

### Problems and alternatives

The RNA world is the leading hypothesis for an early stage of life, but it has well-known difficulties: ribose and activated nucleotides are unstable; RNA strands, once copied, tend to stay paired with their templates; and no ribozyme has yet replicated itself from monomers. Alternatives and refinements include a **pre-RNA world** in which a simpler nucleic acid (with a different sugar, such as threose in TNA, or a peptide-like backbone, as in PNA) came first; **metabolism-first** scenarios, in which self-sustaining networks of small-molecule reactions, perhaps on mineral surfaces or in vents, preceded genes; and **coevolution** of RNA and peptides from the beginning, in which short peptides stabilised RNA and RNA templated peptides. The translation system itself, with tRNAs and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, must have evolved within an RNA–protein world; how the genetic code acquired its assignments is a separate, partly unsolved problem.

::: warning "Life by chance is impossibly improbable"
A common argument computes the probability that a particular protein of 100 amino acids forms by random assembly — $20^{-100} \approx 10^{-130}$ — and concludes that life could not arise by chance. The calculation is correct and irrelevant. No researcher proposes that a modern protein or cell assembled at random. Chemistry is not random: some molecules form far more readily than others, and many different sequences perform any given function (in vitro selection finds functional ribozymes in pools of $10^{14}$ random sequences, although such pools contain a vanishing fraction of all possible sequences). Above all, once replication begins, **selection accumulates improvements step by step**, exactly as in the evolution of the eye ([[evolution/macroevolution#ex-eye]]). The question is how the first, simple replicators arose, not how complex proteins assembled in one step.
:::

## Protocells

Replicators must be kept together with their products. A ribozyme replicase floating in a pond copies any RNA it meets, including **parasites** — short sequences that are copied quickly but do nothing — which outcompete the replicase. In Sol Spiegelman's experiments with the RNA of bacteriophage Qβ, copied by its replicase in a test tube (Donald Mills, Roy Peterson and Spiegelman, 1967), selection for speed alone shrank the RNA, generation after generation, to a small fraction of its original length: everything not needed for being copied was lost. Compartments solve this problem by linking genotype to phenotype. A vesicle containing a good replicase makes more copies of its own contents and grows faster than vesicles with parasites, so selection acts on the compartment as a unit.

::: definition Protocell {#def-protocell}
A **protocell** is a hypothetical (or experimental) compartment, bounded by a self-assembled membrane, that encloses a replicating genetic system and can grow and divide. Modern membranes are bilayers of phospholipids ([[cell-biology/membranes#def-bilayer]]); the first were probably made of simpler single-chain amphiphiles such as fatty acids, which self-assemble into vesicles in water above a critical concentration and are found in meteorites and made in abiotic syntheses.
:::

Jack Szostak's laboratory showed that fatty-acid vesicles have several properties that suit them to be early compartments. Clay particles (montmorillonite) accelerate vesicle formation and can be encapsulated with RNA bound to them (Martin Hanczyc, Shelly Fujikawa and Szostak, 2003). Vesicles grow by absorbing fatty acid from micelles and from other vesicles, and those containing more RNA, which raises their internal osmotic pressure, take up fatty acid at the expense of their neighbours (Irene Chen, Richard Roberts and Szostak, 2004) — a purely physical form of competition in which a better replicator would make its compartment grow faster. Growing vesicles become filamentous and divide under gentle shear (Ting Zhu and Szostak, 2009). Fatty-acid membranes are permeable to nucleotides but not to RNA polymers, so monomers could enter from outside and polymers would be retained.

## The oldest evidence of life

The rock record of the first billion years is sparse: few rocks survive unaltered, and most of those have been heated and deformed. Four kinds of evidence are used, each with characteristic pitfalls.

- **Microfossils**: cell-like structures in rocks, usually chert. Abiotic processes can make similar shapes, so a claim needs evidence of biological composition (organic carbon in cell walls), size distributions, taphonomy and context.
- **Stromatolites**: layered sedimentary structures built by microbial mats trapping sediment and precipitating minerals, still forming today in places such as Shark Bay, Western Australia. Some layered structures form without life.
- **Isotopic signatures**: enzymes discriminate between isotopes, so organic carbon made by life is depleted in ¹³C relative to carbonate (below), and similar effects occur in sulfur and nitrogen.
- **Biomarkers**: molecular fossils such as steranes and hopanes, the remains of membrane lipids characteristic of particular groups. They are reliable only in rocks that have not been heated much and have not been contaminated, which in practice limits them to the past 1.6–1.7 billion years or so.

::: definition Isotopic fractionation and the δ notation {#def-delta}
The **δ¹³C** value of a sample is its ratio $R = {}^{13}\mathrm{C}/{}^{12}\mathrm{C}$ relative to a standard (Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite, VPDB, $R_\mathrm{std} = 0.011180$), expressed in parts per thousand (‰, "per mil"; the factor 1000 converts the fractional difference to per mil):

$$
\delta^{13}\mathrm{C} = \left(\frac{R_\mathrm{sample}}{R_\mathrm{std}} - 1\right)\times1000.
$$ {#eq-delta}

Carbon fixation by the enzyme RuBisCO, and by other autotrophic pathways, favours ¹²C, so the organic matter produced is depleted in ¹³C by roughly 20–30 ‰ relative to the inorganic carbon it came from. Marine carbonates have δ¹³C near 0 ‰, and sedimentary organic carbon typically −20 to −35 ‰, a difference that has persisted through most of the rock record.
:::

::: example A carbon isotope measurement {#ex-delta}
Graphite in a 3.7-billion-year-old metasediment has $^{13}\mathrm{C}/^{12}\mathrm{C} = 0.010956$. What is its δ¹³C, and what does it suggest?
::: solution
$\delta^{13}\mathrm{C} = (0.010956/0.011180 - 1)\times1000 = (0.97996 - 1)\times1000 = -20.0$ ‰ (using $R_\mathrm{std} = 0.0111802$, $-20.05$ ‰). This is in the range of biological carbon fixation, and values like it in graphite from Isua, Greenland, were interpreted as a trace of life (Minik Rosing, 1999). But metamorphism alters isotope ratios, and some abiotic reactions (such as Fischer–Tropsch-type synthesis in hydrothermal systems) also produce ¹³C-depleted carbon. An isotope value alone is suggestive, not conclusive; it becomes convincing in combination with geological context and other evidence.
:::
:::

Isotopes also record how much carbon life buried over time. Carbon enters the ocean–atmosphere system from the mantle and volcanoes with $\delta_\mathrm{in}$ of about −5 ‰ and leaves it by burial as carbonate (δ_carb) and as organic matter (δ_org).

::: proposition Carbon isotope mass balance {#prop-mass-balance}
At steady state, if a fraction $f_\mathrm{org}$ of the carbon buried is organic and $1 - f_\mathrm{org}$ is carbonate, conservation of each isotope requires $\delta_\mathrm{in} = f_\mathrm{org}\,\delta_\mathrm{org} + (1 - f_\mathrm{org})\,\delta_\mathrm{carb}$, so

$$
f_\mathrm{org} = \frac{\delta_\mathrm{carb} - \delta_\mathrm{in}}{\delta_\mathrm{carb} - \delta_\mathrm{org}}.
$$ {#eq-forg}

(The δ notation can be treated as linear for mixing at these small ratio differences.)
:::

::: example How much carbon did life bury? {#ex-burial}
Take $\delta_\mathrm{in} = -5$ ‰, $\delta_\mathrm{carb} = 0$ ‰ and $\delta_\mathrm{org} = -25$ ‰. (a) What fraction of buried carbon is organic? (b) During a positive excursion, carbonates reach $+5$ ‰ with the other values unchanged. What fraction is then organic, and what might that imply for oxygen?
::: solution
(a) By [[#eq-forg]], $f_\mathrm{org} = (0 - (-5))/(0 - (-25)) = 5/25 = 0.2$: about a fifth of buried carbon is organic.

(b) $f_\mathrm{org} = (5 + 5)/(5 + 25) = 10/30 = 0.33$. A third of the carbon is buried as organic matter. Each mole of organic carbon buried leaves behind a mole of O₂ produced when it was fixed by photosynthesis, so sustained positive excursions are read as periods of enhanced oxygen release — the largest being the Lomagundi excursion of about 2.2–2.06 Ga, which followed the Great Oxidation Event.
:::
:::

Remarkably, carbonates have had δ¹³C near 0 ‰ for more than 3.5 billion years, with large excursions at a few times (around the Great Oxidation Event and in the Neoproterozoic), suggesting that biological carbon fixation on a large scale was established very early. Each kilogram of organic carbon buried leaves behind the oxygen produced in fixing it, so the same balance links carbon burial to the oxygenation of the atmosphere.

The main claims for early life and their status as of the mid-2020s are:

| age (Ga) | locality | evidence | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| about 4.1 | Jack Hills, Australia | ¹³C-depleted graphite inside a zircon (Elizabeth Bell and colleagues, 2015) | intriguing, single occurrence; origin uncertain |
| ≥ 3.77 (possibly older) | Nuvvuagittuq, Canada | haematite tubes and filaments in vent deposits (Matthew Dodd and colleagues, 2017) | contested; abiotic origins proposed |

The broad consensus is that life existed by about 3.5 Ga and probably by 3.7 Ga; older claims remain open.

## LUCA and the first two billion years

What was LUCA like? Its properties can be inferred from what all its descendants share — and, with more uncertainty, from genes whose phylogenies trace back to the root. All living cells share the genetic code (with minor variants), DNA and RNA polymerases, ribosomes with about 30–40 universally conserved proteins, ATP synthase and membrane-based energy conservation. Madeline Weiss, William Martin and colleagues (2016) identified 355 protein families whose trees suggested presence in LUCA, and reconstructed it as an anaerobic, hydrogen-dependent, nitrogen-fixing thermophile using the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway of carbon fixation — consistent with a hydrothermal setting. A larger analysis (Moody and colleagues, 2024) estimated that LUCA lived about 4.2 billion years ago (with a wide uncertainty interval), had a genome of perhaps 2.5–3 million base pairs encoding some 2,600 proteins, and was already part of an ecosystem with other organisms and viruses. Two cautions apply: horizontal gene transfer makes it hard to tell genes inherited from LUCA from genes spread later between domains, and LUCA was the last *common* ancestor, not the first cell — it had relatives whose lineages died out.

The subsequent history of life is the subject of the rest of this course. Its milestones in the Precambrian, with approximate dates that are mostly contested to some degree, are:

| approx. age | event |
|---|---|
| ≥ 3.5 Ga | microbial mats, anoxygenic photosynthesis, sulfur metabolism, probably methanogenesis |
| ≥ 2.7–3.0 Ga (debated) | oxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria |
| 2.4–2.2 Ga | **Great Oxidation Event**: free O₂ accumulates in the atmosphere, recorded by the disappearance of mass-independent fractionation of sulfur isotopes (James Farquhar, Huiming Bao and Mark Thiemens, 2000) |
| ≥ 1.6–1.7 Ga | eukaryotic microfossils ([[diversity-of-life/protists]]) |
| about 1.05 Ga | *Bangiomorpha pubescens*, a multicellular red alga with sexual reproduction |
| 0.72–0.635 Ga | "Snowball Earth" glaciations; rise of algae in the plankton |
| about 0.57 Ga | Ediacaran macroscopic organisms; animals ([[diversity-of-life/invertebrates]]) |

For three billion years, then, life was mostly microbial — the subject of [[diversity-of-life/bacteria-archaea]].

::: application Searching for life elsewhere
Origin-of-life research shapes the search for life beyond Earth. If life arose quickly on the early Earth, it may arise readily wherever liquid water, energy and organic chemistry meet: on early Mars, and in the subsurface oceans of Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus, whose plumes contain water, salts, organic molecules and phosphate. The difficulty is recognising a biosignature. The claim of fossil bacteria in the Martian meteorite ALH84001 (David McKay and colleagues, 1996) has not been accepted, since all the features have abiotic explanations — the same problem as with Earth's oldest rocks. NASA's Perseverance rover collected in 2024 a rock sample from Jezero crater with features that the mission team described as a potential biosignature, to be tested only if the sample is returned to Earth. Explicit criteria for evaluating such claims, step by step, have been proposed for exactly this reason.
:::

::: history
Redi (1668) and Pasteur (1859–1862) disproved spontaneous generation. Darwin speculated in 1871 about a "warm little pond"; Oparin (1924) and Haldane (1929) proposed the prebiotic soup. Stanley Miller and Harold Urey's spark-discharge experiment (1953) made amino acids from simple gases, and Joan Oró (1960) made adenine from cyanide. Woese, Crick and Orgel suggested in the late 1960s that RNA preceded proteins; Manfred Eigen (1971) derived the error threshold; Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman discovered ribozymes (1982–1983), and Walter Gilbert named the RNA world (1986). In 2000 the structure of the ribosome showed that peptide bonds are made by RNA. Matthew Powner, Béatrice Gerland and John Sutherland (2009) synthesised pyrimidine ribonucleotides under prebiotic conditions, and Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce (2009) demonstrated self-sustained replication of RNA enzymes. The fossil record of early life was pushed back by J. William Schopf's Apex chert microfossils (1993) and Abigail Allwood's Strelley Pool stromatolites (2006), amid lasting controversy over claims older than 3.5 Ga.
:::

::: summary
- Life can be defined as a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution. Its origin requires building blocks, polymers, replication, compartments, energy and eventually a genetic code.
- Earth formed 4.54 Ga and had oceans by about 4.4–4.3 Ga; widely accepted evidence of life (stromatolites, microfossils, isotopes) dates to about 3.5 Ga, probably 3.7 Ga, so life arose within some hundreds of millions of years.
- Prebiotic chemistry makes amino acids (Miller–Urey, 1953), nucleobases from HCN, sugars by the formose reaction and nucleotides by Sutherland's routes; meteorites and asteroid samples contain many of the same molecules.
- The RNA world hypothesis is supported by ribozymes, the RNA-catalysed ribosome and nucleotide coenzymes; in vitro evolution has produced ligases, polymerase ribozymes and self-sustained RNA replication.
- Eigen's error threshold: a sequence of length $L$ is maintained only if $L \lesssim \ln\sigma/\mu$; at $\mu = 0.01$ and $\sigma = 10$, about 230 nucleotides. Escaping Eigen's paradox needs cooperation, compartments or forgiving fitness landscapes.
- Fatty-acid protocells self-assemble, grow, compete and divide, and link genotype to phenotype, protecting replicators from parasites.
- δ¹³C $= (R/R_\mathrm{std} - 1)\times1000$; biological fixation depletes ¹³C by 20–30 ‰; mass balance $f_\mathrm{org} = (\delta_\mathrm{carb} - \delta_\mathrm{in})/(\delta_\mathrm{carb} - \delta_\mathrm{org}) \approx 0.2$. LUCA was a complex cell, perhaps an anaerobic thermophile, about 4.2 Ga by one recent estimate.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Reading a δ value {level=1 check="-25.1"}
Organic carbon in a shale has $^{13}\mathrm{C}/^{12}\mathrm{C} = 0.010900$. Taking $R_\mathrm{std} = 0.0111802$, what is its δ¹³C in ‰, to one decimal place?
::: solution
$(0.010900/0.0111802 - 1)\times1000 = (0.974938 - 1)\times1000 = -25.1$ ‰, typical of organic matter made by RuBisCO-based carbon fixation.
:::

::: exercise A length limit {level=1 check="277"}
A replicator copies with error rate $\mu = 0.005$ per nucleotide, and the master sequence is four times as fast as its mutants. Using $L_{\max} \approx \ln\sigma/\mu$, what is the longest sequence that can be maintained, to the nearest nucleotide?
::: solution
$\ln 4/0.005 = 1.3863/0.005 = 277.3$, so 277 nucleotides.
:::

::: exercise Sorting the evidence {level=1}
Classify each of the following as a microfossil, stromatolite, isotopic or biomarker line of evidence, and name one way each could be misleading: (a) domed, finely laminated carbonate layers; (b) organic carbon with δ¹³C = −28 ‰; (c) 2α-methylhopanes extracted from a shale; (d) chains of hollow, carbon-walled spheres 5 µm across.
::: solution
(a) Stromatolite: layered structures also form by abiotic precipitation or deformation. (b) Isotopic: abiotic synthesis and metamorphism can produce or shift similar values. (c) Biomarker (hopanoids, once linked to cyanobacteria): contamination by younger oil or by modern laboratory material, and the realisation that other bacteria also make these lipids. (d) Microfossil: minerals and organic matter can self-organise into cell-like shapes (pseudofossils).
:::

Suppose $\delta_\mathrm{in} = -5$ ‰, carbonates are at $+1$ ‰ and organic matter at $-24$ ‰. What fraction of buried carbon is organic?

::: exercise A billion-fold {level=2 check="29.9"}
Self-replicating ribozymes double every hour. How many hours, to one decimal place, are needed to amplify them a billion-fold?
::: solution
$\log_2 10^9 = 9\ln 10/\ln 2 = 29.9$ hours.

::: exercise A minimal RNA genome {level=3 check="5.76"}
Suppose the simplest RNA-based cell needed a genome of 4,000 nucleotides (enough for a replicase and a few other ribozymes), and its master sequence had $\sigma = 10$. What is the maximum error rate per nucleotide, in units of $10^{-4}$, to two decimal places? Compare it with the error rate of the best laboratory polymerase ribozymes (about $10^{-2}$) and discuss two ways in which the requirement could be relaxed.
::: solution
$\mu < \ln 10/4000 = 2.3026/4000 = 5.76\times10^{-4}$, a fidelity of 99.94 %. That is about twenty times more accurate than present ribozyme polymerases. The requirement is relaxed if (1) the genome is split into several shorter, separately replicated molecules held together in a compartment (each needs only $L < \ln\sigma/\mu$ for its own length, if the compartment is selected as a unit); (2) the fitness landscape is flat around the master, so that many mutants retain function (neutral mutations do not count as errors); (3) selection is stronger (higher $\sigma$, though $\ln\sigma$ grows slowly); or (4) the replicase evolves higher fidelity step by step as genome length increases, since even modest improvements allow a slightly longer and better replicase.
:::
:::

::: exercise A fast or a slow origin? {level=3}
Life is widely accepted by 3.5 Ga, probably existed by 3.7 Ga, and stable oceans are inferred by about 4.4 Ga. (a) What is the longest interval available for the origin of life? (b) Some authors argue that a rapid origin on Earth implies that life arises easily wherever conditions allow. Give one reason to accept and two reasons to be cautious about this argument. (c) How would a robust detection of an independent origin of life on Mars change the argument?
::: solution
(a) About $4.4 - 3.5 = 0.9$ billion years at most, perhaps $4.4 - 3.7 = 0.7$ — less if the early surface was repeatedly sterilised, and much less if LUCA really lived 4.2 Ga.

(b) For: the earlier life appears relative to the opening of the window, the less likely it is that its origin needed an extremely improbable event (a version of this argument was made by Charles Lineweaver and Tamara Davis, 2002). Cautions: (1) we have only one example, and an observer effect applies — we could only exist on a planet where life did arise, and an early origin may have been needed for intelligent life to evolve within the Sun's lifetime; (2) the dates are uncertain, and life might have originated elsewhere (panspermia only moves the problem). The early start constrains, but does not measure, the probability.

(c) A second, independent origin in the same solar system would show that abiogenesis is not a one-off fluke, strongly supporting a high probability wherever conditions are similar. (Life on Mars sharing our biochemistry, however, could reflect exchange by meteorites rather than a second origin.)
:::
