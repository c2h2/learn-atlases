Look at a drop of pond water under a microscope and you will see cells unlike any bacterium: a *Paramecium* sweeping food into its oral groove with thousands of beating cilia, a diatom gliding in a glass box ornamented like a cathedral window, an amoeba flowing around a smaller cell and engulfing it. These are **protists** — eukaryotes that are not animals, plants or fungi. They are not a natural group; they are everything left over when the three familiar multicellular kingdoms are taken out, and they include most of the evolutionary diversity of eukaryotes. The kelp forests of temperate coasts, the chalk of the White Cliffs of Dover, the potato blight that caused the Irish famine and the parasites that cause malaria and sleeping sickness are all protists.

This chapter first asks how the eukaryotic cell itself arose, by a merger of an archaeal host with a bacterium that became the mitochondrion, and how plastids spread through eukaryotes by primary and then secondary endosymbiosis. It then sets out the current picture of the eukaryotic tree as a set of **supergroups**, as summarised by Fabien Burki and colleagues (2020), and tours the major protist lineages. Two quantitative threads run through it: the physics of life at low Reynolds number, which governs how all single cells move, and the curious arithmetic of diatom cell division. The cell biology of organelles is in [[cell-biology/organelles]]; here the emphasis is on evolution and diversity.

## The origin of the eukaryotic cell

::: definition Eukaryote and LECA {#def-leca}
A **eukaryote** is a cell with a nucleus — chromosomes enclosed in a double-membraned envelope perforated by nuclear pores — and typically an **endomembrane system** (endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, endosomes, lysosomes), a cytoskeleton of actin filaments and microtubules with motor proteins, **mitochondria** or organelles derived from them, linear chromosomes with telomeres, genes interrupted by spliceosomal introns, and a cell cycle with mitosis and, in most lineages, meiosis. The **last eukaryotic common ancestor** (LECA) already had essentially all of these features; the **first eukaryotic common ancestor** (FECA) is the point at which the eukaryotic lineage split from its closest archaeal relatives. Everything distinctively eukaryotic evolved on the stem between FECA and LECA.
:::

Comparative genomics shows that LECA was not a simple cell. Reconstructions of its gene content include thousands of gene families: a full set of endomembrane trafficking machinery, a nuclear pore complex, spliceosomes and introns, the meiotic recombination machinery, and a cilium (flagellum) built on a 9 + 2 arrangement of microtubules. The stem leading to it is a long branch with no surviving side branches, so its stages cannot be read directly from living organisms. Three facts constrain them.

1. **The host was an archaeon.** As explained in [[diversity-of-life/classifying-life#prop-two-domains]], most current phylogenomic analyses place eukaryotes within the Asgard archaea, whose genomes encode homologues of eukaryotic actin, small GTPases, ESCRT membrane-remodelling proteins and ubiquitin systems. The eukaryotic information-processing machinery (transcription, translation, replication) is archaeal in origin.
2. **The mitochondrion was an alphaproteobacterium.** Mitochondria have their own small genomes, ribosomes of bacterial type and double membranes, and divide by fission ([[cell-biology/organelles#def-endosymbiosis]]); phylogenies of their genes group them with the Alphaproteobacteria (whether within that class or as its sister is still debated). Every eukaryote studied either has mitochondria or has organelles derived from them — the hydrogenosomes of some anaerobic protists and the mitosomes of *Giardia* — or descends from ancestors that had them. In 2016 Anna Karnkowska and colleagues showed that the gut protist *Monocercomonoides exilis* has lost mitochondria entirely, the first eukaryote known to have done so; the loss was secondary.
3. **Many "bacterial" genes in eukaryotes, including those for membrane lipids and much of metabolism, came from bacteria** — probably mostly from the mitochondrial symbiont, partly by other transfers.

How did an archaeon come to contain a bacterium? Archaea do not engulf other cells, and a classical view held that the host was already a primitive phagocytic eukaryote. The discovery that Asgard archaea are prokaryotic cells, and the first culture of one — '*Candidatus* Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum', a small cell with long protrusions that lives in syntrophy with hydrogen-consuming bacteria (Hiroyuki Imachi and colleagues, 2020) — supports **symbiogenic** models in which a metabolic partnership came first. William Martin and Miklós Müller's **hydrogen hypothesis** (1998) proposed that an archaeal host that consumed hydrogen became dependent on a bacterial partner that produced it; Imachi's group suggested that protrusions of the host could have entangled and eventually enclosed the partner. On such models the mitochondrion was acquired early, and its energy supply made the later expansion of the genome and of the endomembrane system possible (an argument developed by Nick Lane and Martin, 2010). Other researchers favour a later acquisition, after a phagocytic cell had evolved. The question is open.

### When?

Molecular clocks place the crown group of eukaryotes (the diversification from LECA) somewhere between about 1.0 and 1.9 billion years ago, depending on calibrations and models. The oldest fossils accepted as eukaryotic are organic-walled microfossils with complex wall structure and processes from rocks about 1.65–1.6 billion years old in northern China and northern Australia; multicellular forms appear in rocks of about 1.6 Ga. The oldest fossil that can be assigned confidently to a living group is *Bangiomorpha pubescens*, a filamentous red alga from Arctic Canada with differentiated cells and evidence of sexual reproduction (Nicholas Butterfield, 2000), now dated to about 1.05 Ga (Timothy Gibson and colleagues, 2018). Biomarkers tell a complementary story: sterol-like lipids of "stem" eukaryotes occur from about 1.6 Ga, but steranes characteristic of crown-group algae become abundant only around 650 million years ago, after the Cryogenian glaciations, when algae replaced cyanobacteria as the main primary producers of the ocean (Jochen Brocks and colleagues, 2017, 2023). Eukaryotes may thus have existed for nearly a billion years as minor members of microbial ecosystems before they became ecologically dominant.

## Plastids and endosymbiosis

::: definition Primary, secondary and tertiary endosymbiosis {#def-endosymbiosis-types}
In **primary endosymbiosis** a eukaryote engulfs a free-living cyanobacterium, which becomes a **plastid** (chloroplast) bounded by two membranes, derived from the cyanobacterium's inner and outer membranes. In **secondary endosymbiosis** a eukaryote engulfs a eukaryotic alga that already contains a primary plastid; the plastid is then surrounded by three or four membranes (the extra ones derived from the engulfed alga's plasma membrane and the host's phagosomal membrane), and sometimes a vestige of the alga's nucleus, the **nucleomorph**, survives between them. In **tertiary endosymbiosis** a eukaryote acquires a plastid by engulfing an alga with a secondary plastid. In each case most of the symbiont's genes are lost or transferred to the host nucleus (**endosymbiotic gene transfer**), and the proteins they encode are imported back into the organelle by targeting sequences.
:::

Primary plastids arose once, as far as the evidence shows, in the common ancestor of the **Archaeplastida**: the glaucophytes, whose plastids (cyanelles) still have a peptidoglycan wall between their two membranes; the red algae (Rhodophyta); and the green algae with their descendants, the land plants (Chloroplastida). A second, independent primary endosymbiosis occurred much more recently in the rhizarian amoeba *Paulinella chromatophora*, whose photosynthetic "chromatophores" derive from a different group of cyanobacteria (Birger Marin, Eva Nowack and Michael Melkonian, 2005), perhaps 90–140 million years ago. In 2024 Tyler Coale, Jonathan Zehr and colleagues showed that the nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium UCYN-A in the marine haptophyte alga *Braarudosphaera bigelowii* divides in step with its host and imports host-encoded proteins: a nitrogen-fixing organelle, or **nitroplast**, in the process of formation.

Secondary endosymbioses then spread photosynthesis across the tree:

| plastid source | lineages with secondary (or tertiary) plastids | membranes | nucleomorph |
|---|---|---|---|
| green alga | euglenids (Discoba) | 3 | no |
| green alga | chlorarachniophytes (Rhizaria) | 4 | yes |
| red alga | cryptophytes (Cryptista) | 4 | yes |
| red alga | haptophytes; ochrophytes (diatoms, brown algae and other stramenopiles); most photosynthetic dinoflagellates; apicomplexans (non-photosynthetic apicoplast) | 3–4 | no |

How many separate red-algal secondary endosymbioses there were, and whether some lineages acquired their plastids from each other by "serial" or tertiary endosymbiosis, is debated; it is clear that plastid trees and host trees do not match, so plastids moved between lineages more than once. Dinoflagellates are the champions: various species have replaced their original plastid with ones taken from green algae, haptophytes, diatoms or cryptophytes, and some steal plastids temporarily from their prey (**kleptoplasty**), as do some sea slugs.

The **apicoplast** of *Plasmodium* and *Toxoplasma* is a non-photosynthetic plastid of red-algal origin, discovered in the 1990s, which still makes fatty acids, isoprenoids and haem for the parasite (Geoffrey McFadden and colleagues, 1996). Because its machinery is bacterial in origin, it is a target for antibiotics; for malaria itself see [Medicine Atlas](/learn/medicine/course.php?c=infectious-diseases).

::: example How much of a cyanobacterium survives? {#ex-gene-transfer}
The plastid genome of *Arabidopsis thaliana* is about 154 kb and encodes about 120 genes, whereas free-living cyanobacteria such as *Synechocystis* have about 3,200 genes. William Martin and colleagues (2002) estimated that about 4,500 of the roughly 25,000 nuclear protein-coding genes of *Arabidopsis* were acquired from the cyanobacterial ancestor of plastids. (a) What fraction of the nuclear genes is of cyanobacterial origin? (b) What fraction of a cyanobacterium's gene number survives in the plastid genome? (c) Why does the number of transferred nuclear genes exceed the number of genes in a modern cyanobacterium's genome?
::: solution
(a) $4500/25{,}000 = 0.18$: about 18 % of the nuclear genes.

(b) About $120/3200 = 0.04$, or 4 %, survives in the organelle.

(c) The ancestral cyanobacterium may have had more genes than *Synechocystis*; transferred genes were duplicated after transfer and diversified within the plant nucleus; and the phylogenetic method counts genes whose closest relatives are cyanobacterial, which includes some acquired from cyanobacteria by other routes or misassigned. Many transferred genes now serve functions outside the plastid. The broad point is robust: the endosymbiont's genome was dismantled, and most of what survived did so in the host's nucleus.
:::
:::

## The eukaryotic tree

The old division of protists into "protozoa" (animal-like, motile, heterotrophic), "algae" (plant-like, photosynthetic) and "fungus-like" forms was an ecological classification: the tree shows that photosynthesis, multicellularity, amoeboid movement and parasitism each evolved many times. Since the early 2000s phylogenomic analyses of hundreds of genes have reorganised eukaryotes into a few large clades. The following summary follows the review by Fabien Burki, Andrew Roger, Matthew Brown and Alastair Simpson (*Trends in Ecology & Evolution*, 2020); the uncertainties it lists are real, and some groupings have changed since.

::: definition The eukaryotic supergroups (as of the 2020s) {#def-supergroups}
- **Diaphoretickes**, a large clade comprising:
  - **TSAR**: Telonemia, **Stramenopiles** (heterokonts: diatoms, brown algae, golden algae, oomycetes), **Alveolata** (ciliates, dinoflagellates, apicomplexans) and **Rhizaria** (foraminifera, radiolarians, cercozoans including chlorarachniophytes and *Paulinella*);
  - **Haptista** (haptophytes, including coccolithophores; centrohelids);
  - **Cryptista** (cryptophytes, katablepharids);
  - **Archaeplastida** (glaucophytes, red algae, green algae and land plants), whose monophyly with respect to Cryptista is uncertain in some analyses.
- **Amorphea**: **Amoebozoa** (lobose amoebae, *Entamoeba*, slime moulds such as *Dictyostelium* and *Physarum*) and **Obazoa**, which comprises the breviates, apusomonads and **Opisthokonta** — the clade of **animals** and their unicellular relatives (choanoflagellates, filastereans, ichthyosporeans) and of **fungi** and theirs (nucleariid amoebae).
- **Discoba**: Euglenozoa (euglenids, kinetoplastids such as *Trypanosoma*), Heterolobosea (*Naegleria*) and Jakobida (*Reclinomonas*).
- **Metamonada**: anaerobic flagellates with reduced mitochondria (*Giardia*, *Trichomonas*, *Monocercomonoides*).
- Several smaller "orphan" lineages whose positions are being resolved: CRuMs (allied with Amorphea), Hemimastigophora (Gordon Lax and colleagues, 2018), Ancyromonadida, Malawimonadida and **Provora** (Denis Tikhonenkov and colleagues, 2022).
:::

Two older supergroups have dissolved. "Excavata", defined by a feeding groove, is no longer recovered as a clade: Discoba and Metamonada are separate, and the excavate cell form may be ancestral for eukaryotes. "Chromalveolata", which united all lineages with red-algal secondary plastids on the hypothesis of a single endosymbiosis, was broken up when Rhizaria were found to nest among them and plastid and host trees disagreed.

Where is the **root** of the eukaryotic tree? The two-domains tree does not help much, because the archaeal outgroup lies at the end of the long FECA–LECA branch. Proposed roots include one between Amorphea (with CRuMs and others) and Diaphoretickes plus excavates ("Opimoda" against "Diphoda", Romain Derelle and colleagues, 2015), and roots within or beside the excavate-like lineages Discoba or Metamonada. The root is one of the main open problems in eukaryotic phylogeny.

::: warning "Protists are primitive eukaryotes" and "algae are a group"
Living protists are not ancestors of plants and animals; each lineage has evolved for as long as any other since LECA, and some protist cells — ciliates with two kinds of nucleus and thousands of coordinated cilia, dinoflagellates with eye-like ocelloids — are among the most complex cells known. Nor are "algae" a clade: photosynthesis entered eukaryotes once by primary endosymbiosis and spread to unrelated lineages several times by secondary endosymbiosis, so a brown alga is more closely related to a non-photosynthetic oomycete or ciliate than to a red or green alga. "Protist" and "alga" are useful words for grades of organisation, not for groups of relatives.
:::

## A tour of protist diversity

### Stramenopiles

Stramenopiles are named for the tripartite hairs (mastigonemes) on one of their two flagella. They include:

- **Diatoms** (Bacillariophyta), unicellular algae enclosed in a two-part silica wall, the **frustule**, whose halves fit like the lid and base of a Petri dish. They are among the most important primary producers on Earth: David Nelson and colleagues (1995) estimated that diatoms account for about 40 % of marine primary production. Their frustules form the sediment diatomite.
- **Brown algae** (Phaeophyceae), multicellular seaweeds including the giant kelp *Macrocystis pyrifera*, which can exceed 40 m in length and grow tens of centimetres a day, forming forests that support rich coastal ecosystems. Multicellularity evolved independently here, with tissues, a stem-like stipe, gas-filled floats and sieve-tube-like conducting cells.
- **Oomycetes** ("water moulds"), heterotrophs with filamentous growth that were long classified with fungi. *Phytophthora infestans* causes late blight of potato; its spread through Ireland from 1845 destroyed the potato harvest repeatedly and contributed to a famine in which about a million people died and as many emigrated.

::: example How much of the world's photosynthesis do diatoms do? {#ex-diatom-npp}
Field and colleagues (1998) estimated global net primary production at about 104.9 Pg C per year, of which 48.5 Pg C is marine. If diatoms account for about 40 % of marine production, what fraction of global production is that? How does it compare with all the world's rainforests, at roughly 18 Pg C per year?
::: solution
$0.40\times48.5 = 19.4$ Pg C per year, and $19.4/104.9 = 0.185$: diatoms, single cells a few tens of micrometres across, fix roughly a fifth of all the carbon fixed on Earth — about as much as all tropical rainforests. The estimate is uncertain (the 40 % comes from silica budgets and regional measurements), but the order of magnitude is robust, and explains why changes in ocean stratification and nutrient supply matter for the global carbon cycle.
:::
:::

::: example How diatoms shrink {#ex-diatom}
When a diatom divides, each daughter keeps one half of the parental frustule as its larger half (the epitheca) and builds a new, smaller half inside it. The daughter inheriting the parental epitheca stays the same size; the one inheriting the hypotheca becomes smaller by a fixed amount $\delta$ (the MacDonald–Pfitzer rule, 1869). A clone starts from a cell 50 µm long, and $\delta = 0.4$ µm. (a) After $n$ divisions, what is the distribution of cell sizes among the $2^n$ descendants? (b) For $n = 40$, give the mean, the smallest possible size and the standard deviation. (c) How do diatoms escape shrinking indefinitely?
::: solution
(a) Follow one lineage from the founder to a descendant: at each of the $n$ divisions it either keeps its size or shrinks by $\delta$. The number of descendants that shrank $k$ times is the number of ways of choosing which $k$ of the $n$ divisions were "shrinking" ones, $\binom{n}{k}$. So the fraction of cells of size $50 - k\delta$ is $\binom{n}{k}/2^n$: $k$ follows a binomial distribution with parameters $n$ and $\tfrac12$.

(b) The mean of $k$ is $n/2 = 20$, so the mean size is $50 - 20\times0.4 = 42$ µm. The smallest cells, with $k = 40$, are $50 - 16 = 34$ µm, but they are a fraction $2^{-40} \approx 10^{-12}$ of the population. The standard deviation of $k$ is $\sqrt{n/4} = 3.16$, so that of size is $0.4\times3.16 = 1.26$ µm.

(c) When cells fall below a critical size, typically 30–40 % of the maximum, they become competent for sexual reproduction. The zygote develops into an **auxospore**, which swells to the maximum size before building a new frustule, restoring the clone. The size–sex link acts as a cell-cycle clock: in many species, small cells *must* undergo sex or die.
:::
:::

### Alveolates

Alveolates have flattened membrane sacs (alveoli) beneath the plasma membrane.

- **Ciliates** such as *Paramecium*, *Tetrahymena* and *Stentor* have many cilia and two kinds of nucleus: a diploid germline **micronucleus**, used in sexual conjugation, and a polyploid somatic **macronucleus**, which expresses genes. The macronucleus develops from a micronucleus by massive DNA elimination and amplification. Ribozymes and telomerase were discovered in *Tetrahymena* ([[diversity-of-life/origin-of-life]]).
- **Dinoflagellates** have two flagella in perpendicular grooves, enormous genomes (up to more than 100 Gb) packaged without typical nucleosomes, and diverse nutrition. Symbiotic dinoflagellates (family Symbiodiniaceae) live in reef-building corals and supply most of their energy; when stressed by high temperature, corals expel them and **bleach**. Bloom-forming species cause "red tides", some producing toxins that accumulate in shellfish.
- **Apicomplexans** are parasites of animals with an apical complex used to invade host cells: *Plasmodium* (malaria), *Toxoplasma*, *Cryptosporidium*. Their relatives include the photosynthetic coral symbiont *Chromera velia* (Robert Moore and colleagues, 2008), which shows that the apicoplast is a reduced photosynthetic plastid.

### Rhizaria

Rhizaria are mostly amoeboid cells with thin, threadlike pseudopodia. **Foraminifera** build chambered shells (tests) of calcium carbonate or cemented particles; their fossils are used to date marine sediments and their oxygen isotopes to reconstruct past ocean temperatures. **Radiolarians** make intricate skeletons of silica. **Cercozoa** include the chlorarachniophytes with their nucleomorphs and *Paulinella*.

### Haptophytes and cryptophytes

**Coccolithophores**, such as *Emiliania huxleyi*, are haptophyte algae covered with calcite plates (coccoliths); they form blooms visible from space, and their accumulated remains make chalk, as in the Cretaceous chalk of southern England. **Cryptophytes** are flagellates with plastids of red-algal origin, four membranes and a nucleomorph.

### Excavate lineages: Discoba and Metamonada

**Euglenids** include photosynthetic *Euglena* (with a green plastid gained by secondary endosymbiosis) and many heterotrophs. **Kinetoplastids** such as *Trypanosoma brucei* (sleeping sickness) and *Leishmania* have a single huge mitochondrion with a mass of interlocked DNA circles, the kinetoplast, whose transcripts are extensively edited by insertion and deletion of uridines (Rob Benne and colleagues, 1986). *Naegleria* switches between amoeboid and flagellated forms; jakobids such as *Reclinomonas americana* have the most bacteria-like mitochondrial genomes known, with 97 genes including a bacterial-type RNA polymerase (B. Franz Lang and colleagues, 1997). **Metamonads** such as *Giardia* and *Trichomonas* are anaerobes with mitochondrion-derived mitosomes or hydrogenosomes.

### Amoebozoa and the relatives of animals and fungi

Amoebozoa include free-living amoebae and the **slime moulds**. In the cellular slime mould *Dictyostelium discoideum*, starving amoebae aggregate in response to pulses of cyclic AMP into a multicellular slug, which forms a fruiting body in which about a fifth of the cells die to make a stalk that lifts the others as spores: a model for the evolution of cooperation and multicellularity. Among the opisthokonts, the **choanoflagellates**, collared flagellates that resemble the feeding cells of sponges, are the closest living relatives of animals; some, such as *Salpingoeca rosetta*, form colonies whose development is triggered by bacterial signals (Nicole King and colleagues) — a window on the origin of animal multicellularity ([[diversity-of-life/invertebrates]]). The nucleariid amoebae are relatives of fungi ([[diversity-of-life/fungi]]).

::: quiz
A newly described single-celled alga has a plastid surrounded by four membranes with a small nucleus-like body between the second and third membranes, and its host nuclear genes group it with Rhizaria. What is the most likely history of its plastid?
- [ ] Primary endosymbiosis of a cyanobacterium
- [x] Secondary endosymbiosis of a eukaryotic alga, whose nucleus survives as a nucleomorph
- [ ] Inheritance from the common ancestor of all eukaryotes
- [ ] Kleptoplasty from prey eaten each generation
::: solution
Four membranes and a nucleomorph are the signature of a secondary plastid that retains a remnant of the engulfed alga's nucleus, as in chlorarachniophytes (green-algal origin, within Rhizaria) and cryptophytes (red-algal origin). Primary plastids have two membranes; LECA had no plastid; stolen plastids are not maintained with a nucleomorph.
:::
:::

## Life at low Reynolds number

Protists, like bacteria, live in a physical world unlike ours. For a swimmer, the important number is the ratio of inertial forces (which keep a body moving) to viscous forces (which resist motion).

::: definition The Reynolds number {#def-reynolds}
For an object of length $L$ moving at speed $v$ through a fluid of density $\rho$ and dynamic viscosity $\eta$,

$$
\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho vL}{\eta}.
$$ {#eq-reynolds}

For water at 20 °C, $\rho \approx 1000$ kg m⁻³ and $\eta \approx 1.0\times10^{-3}$ Pa s. When $\mathrm{Re} \gg 1$, inertia dominates (a fish coasts after a stroke); when $\mathrm{Re} \ll 1$, viscosity dominates and motion stops almost instantly when propulsion stops.
:::

::: proposition Coasting at low Reynolds number {#prop-coasting}
A sphere of radius $r$ and density $\rho$ moving at speed $v_0$ in a fluid of viscosity $\eta$, with $\mathrm{Re} \ll 1$, experiences the Stokes drag force $F = 6\pi\eta rv$. If its propulsion stops, its speed decays exponentially, $v(t) = v_0e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = 2\rho r^2/9\eta$, and it coasts a total distance

$$
d = v_0\tau = \frac{2\rho v_0r^2}{9\eta}.
$$ {#eq-coast}
:::

::: proof
Newton's second law with mass $m = \tfrac43\pi r^3\rho$ gives $m\,dv/dt = -6\pi\eta rv$, so $v = v_0e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = m/(6\pi\eta r) = \tfrac43\pi r^3\rho/(6\pi\eta r) = 2\rho r^2/(9\eta)$. The distance travelled is $\int_0^\infty v\,dt = v_0\tau$. (Strictly, the added mass of entrained fluid should be included; it changes $\tau$ by a factor of order 1.)
:::

::: example Swimming in syrup {#ex-reynolds}
Compute $\mathrm{Re}$ for (a) a bacterium 2 µm long swimming at 30 µm s⁻¹, (b) a *Paramecium* 200 µm long at 1 mm s⁻¹, and (c) a 30 cm fish at 1 m s⁻¹. (d) How far does the bacterium (radius about 1 µm) coast when its flagella stop?
::: solution
(a) $\mathrm{Re} = 1000\times30\times10^{-6}\times2\times10^{-6}/10^{-3} = 6\times10^{-5}$. (b) $1000\times10^{-3}\times2\times10^{-4}/10^{-3} = 0.2$. (c) $1000\times1\times0.3/10^{-3} = 3\times10^5$.

(d) By [[#eq-coast]], $d = 2\times1000\times30\times10^{-6}\times(10^{-6})^2/(9\times10^{-3}) = 6.7\times10^{-12}$ m — less than a tenth of the diameter of an atom, over about $2\times10^{-7}$ s. As Edward Purcell put it in his 1977 lecture "Life at low Reynolds number", a bacterium that stops swimming stops dead. At low Re, a reciprocal stroke (one that retraces its path in reverse, like opening and closing a scallop's shell) produces no net movement whatever its speed — the "scallop theorem". Cells must therefore use non-reciprocal motions: rotating helices (bacterial flagella) or the asymmetric power and recovery strokes of cilia and eukaryotic flagella.
:::
:::

::: application Protists, climate and the carbon cycle
Marine protists are central to the global carbon cycle. Phytoplankton, mostly diatoms, haptophytes, dinoflagellates and cyanobacteria, carry out about half of the planet's net primary production, roughly 50 Pg of carbon a year (Christopher Field and colleagues, 1998). A fraction of this carbon sinks as dead cells, faecal pellets and the mineral shells of coccolithophores, foraminifera and diatoms — the **biological pump** — and is buried, removing CO₂ from the atmosphere over geological time. Chalk, limestone and diatomite are protist rocks, and the isotope ratios in foraminiferal shells are one of the main archives of past climate ([Earth Atlas](/learn/earth/)). Changes in ocean temperature, stratification and acidity affect these organisms and so feed back on climate.
:::

::: history
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek described ciliates and other "animalcules" in 1674–1677. Ernst Haeckel proposed the kingdom Protista in 1866 and illustrated radiolarians in his monographs (1862, 1887) and *Kunstformen der Natur* (1899–1904). Konstantin Mereschkowsky (1905, 1910) proposed that plastids were endosymbiotic cyanobacteria, and Ivan Wallin (1925) that mitochondria were bacteria; the idea was revived by Lynn Margulis (as Lynn Sagan, 1967) and confirmed by ribosomal RNA and genome sequences in the 1970s–1990s. Secondary endosymbiosis was proposed in the 1970s and the cryptophyte nucleomorph was shown to be a vestigial nucleus by Susan Douglas and colleagues (2001, genome sequence). Thomas Cavalier-Smith's many classifications (1981–2022) shaped the modern framework; phylogenomics produced the supergroups (Sandra Baldauf, 2003; Adl and colleagues, 2005, 2012, 2019) and the current tree (Burki and colleagues, 2020).
:::

::: summary
- Protists are eukaryotes other than animals, plants and fungi — a paraphyletic grade holding most of eukaryotic diversity.
- Eukaryotes arose from an Asgard-related archaeal host and an alphaproteobacterial endosymbiont (the mitochondrion); LECA already had a nucleus, endomembranes, cilia, meiosis and mitochondria. Crown eukaryotes date to roughly 1.0–1.9 Ga by clocks; eukaryotic fossils appear about 1.65–1.6 Ga, *Bangiomorpha* about 1.05 Ga, and algae became dominant about 0.65 Ga.
- Primary plastids (two membranes) arose in Archaeplastida, and independently in *Paulinella*; secondary plastids (three or four membranes, sometimes a nucleomorph) spread to euglenids, chlorarachniophytes, cryptophytes, haptophytes, stramenopiles and alveolates. Most endosymbiont genes moved to the nucleus (about 18 % of *Arabidopsis* nuclear genes are of cyanobacterial origin).
- Supergroups (Burki et al., 2020): Diaphoretickes (TSAR, Haptista, Cryptista, Archaeplastida), Amorphea (Amoebozoa, Obazoa with Opisthokonta), Discoba, Metamonada and orphan lineages; the root is unresolved.
- Diatoms shrink by the MacDonald–Pfitzer rule: sizes follow a binomial distribution with mean loss $n\delta/2$; sex and auxospores restore size.
- At low Reynolds number, $\mathrm{Re} = \rho vL/\eta \ll 1$, viscosity dominates: a bacterium coasts $2\rho vr^2/9\eta \approx 10^{-11}$ m, and swimming requires non-reciprocal strokes.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise A ciliate's Reynolds number {level=1 check="0.05"}
A ciliate 100 µm long swims at 0.5 mm s⁻¹ in water ($\rho = 1000$ kg m⁻³, $\eta = 10^{-3}$ Pa s). What is its Reynolds number?
::: solution
$\mathrm{Re} = 1000\times5\times10^{-4}\times10^{-4}/10^{-3} = 0.05$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Counting membranes {level=1}
How many membranes surround the plastid of (a) a red alga, (b) a glaucophyte, (c) a cryptophyte, (d) *Euglena*, and (e) a diatom? Which of these plastids are primary?
::: solution
(a) Two; (b) two, with a peptidoglycan layer between them; (c) four, with a nucleomorph; (d) three; (e) four. The red-algal and glaucophyte plastids are primary; the others are secondary.
:::
:::

::: exercise Placing organisms {level=1}
Assign each organism to a supergroup: (a) *Phytophthora infestans*; (b) *Plasmodium falciparum*; (c) *Dictyostelium discoideum*; (d) a choanoflagellate; (e) *Trypanosoma brucei*; (f) *Giardia intestinalis*; (g) a foraminiferan; (h) *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*.
::: solution
(a) Stramenopiles, in TSAR (Diaphoretickes). (b) Alveolata, in TSAR. (c) Amoebozoa (Amorphea). (d) Opisthokonta (Amorphea). (e) Discoba. (f) Metamonada. (g) Rhizaria, in TSAR. (h) Archaeplastida (green algae), in Diaphoretickes.
:::

::: exercise A shrinking clone {level=2 check="52.5"}
A diatom clone starts at 60 µm and each shrinking division reduces length by 0.5 µm. What is the mean cell length after 30 divisions, in µm?
::: solution
The mean number of shrinking divisions is $30/2 = 15$, so the mean length is $60 - 15\times0.5 = 52.5$ µm.
:::

::: exercise Time to sex {level=2 check="120"}
For the clone of the previous exercise, after how many divisions does the *mean* cell length reach a critical size of 30 µm?
::: solution
The mean falls by $\delta/2 = 0.25$ µm per division, so $(60 - 30)/0.25 = 120$ divisions. (Some lineages get there sooner: the fastest-shrinking ones need only 60.)
:::

::: exercise Genes moved to the nucleus {level=2 check="18"}
If 4,500 of 25,000 nuclear genes of a plant are of cyanobacterial origin, what percentage is that? Name two reasons why genes might move from an organelle to the nucleus and stay there.
::: solution
$4500/25{,}000 = 18\,\%$. Organelle genomes are asexual and, in a small population of genome copies, accumulate harmful mutations (Muller's ratchet), so a nuclear copy, which recombines, is maintained better; organelles produce mutagenic reactive oxygen species; and once a gene has been transferred and its product is imported, the organelle copy can be lost without harm, a one-way ratchet. Some genes may stay in organelles because their hydrophobic products are hard to import or because local control of their expression by the organelle's redox state is advantageous (the CoRR hypothesis of John Allen).
:::
:::

::: exercise Coasting *Paramecium* {level=2 check="2.2"}
Model a *Paramecium* as a sphere of radius 100 µm with density 1000 kg m⁻³, swimming at 1 mm s⁻¹ in water. How far, in µm to one decimal place, does it coast after its cilia stop?
::: solution
$d = 2\rho v r^2/9\eta = 2\times1000\times10^{-3}\times10^{-8}/(9\times10^{-3}) = 2.2\times10^{-6}$ m $= 2.2$ µm, about 1 % of its length. Even at $\mathrm{Re} \approx 0.2$, motion stops almost immediately.
:::

::: exercise Reading the plastid trees {level=3}
Phylogenies of plastid genes from red algae, cryptophytes, haptophytes, stramenopiles and dinoflagellates group the plastids of these lineages together, nested within red algae. Phylogenies of their nuclear genes place cryptophytes with Archaeplastida in some analyses, haptophytes elsewhere in Diaphoretickes, and stramenopiles and dinoflagellates within TSAR, with Rhizaria (which mostly lack plastids) among them. (a) Why did these data sink the Chromalveolata hypothesis of a single secondary endosymbiosis? (b) Describe one alternative history consistent with both trees. (c) What kind of evidence could discriminate between alternatives?
::: solution
(a) A single secondary endosymbiosis in a common ancestor predicts that the host lineages form a clade, and that the plastid tree matches the host tree. The host tree instead interleaves photosynthetic lineages with non-photosynthetic ones (Rhizaria, telonemids, others), requiring many independent plastid losses, and the plastid and host trees disagree.

(b) Serial endosymbiosis: one lineage (for example the cryptophytes) acquired a red-algal plastid by secondary endosymbiosis, and others acquired theirs from it, or from each other, by tertiary or "higher-order" endosymbioses — say ochrophytes from a cryptophyte, haptophytes from an ochrophyte. This keeps a single origin of the plastid from red algae while letting it jump between unrelated hosts.

(c) The genes for plastid-targeted proteins in each host nucleus (whose phylogenies should reflect the donor of each transfer), the protein-import machinery of the extra membranes (shared derived components would suggest common acquisition), molecular dating of host and plastid splits (a donor must be older than its recipient's plastid), and genomic remnants of nucleomorphs or of lost plastids in non-photosynthetic relatives.
:::

::: exercise The scallop theorem {level=3}
(a) Explain why, in Stokes flow ($\mathrm{Re} \to 0$), the motion of a swimmer at each instant depends only on its current shape and the rate at which its shape is changing, not on its past motion. (b) Use this to argue that a reciprocal stroke (shape sequence A → B → A) gives zero net displacement, whatever the speeds of the two halves. (c) Describe how a eukaryotic cilium avoids reciprocity.
::: solution
(a) With $\mathrm{Re} \to 0$, the inertial terms in the fluid's equations of motion vanish; the fluid's response to the body's boundary motion is instantaneous and linear in the boundary velocities, and, with no inertia, the body's own motion satisfies force balance (zero total force and torque) at each instant. So its velocity at any moment is a linear function of the rate of change of its shape, depending on the current shape.

(b) Displacement over a stroke is then an integral of (shape-dependent coefficient) × (rate of shape change) over time, which equals a line integral over the path in shape space. Changing the speed reparametrises the path without changing the integral. A reciprocal stroke traverses a path and then the same path backwards, so the second half cancels the first exactly: zero net motion.

(c) A cilium beats in two phases that trace different paths in shape space: a fast, extended power stroke and a slower recovery stroke in which it bends close to the cell surface. Because the shape sequences differ, the path encloses an area in shape space and the net displacement is non-zero. (Drag on a slender filament is also anisotropic — about twice as great moving sideways as lengthways — which allows a beating or rotating flagellum to push.)
:::
