Darwin's fifteen pairs of maize plants gave a mean difference in height of 20.9 eighths of an inch in favour of the cross-fertilised plants ([[biostatistics/data-and-variation#ex-darwin-location]]). Had he raised fifteen different pairs, he would have obtained a different number. How different? Is the true advantage of crossing, the one we would find with unlimited plants, probably close to 21, or could it be zero, or twice as large? A single number from a sample is an **estimate**, and an estimate is useless without a statement of how precise it is.

This chapter is about that statement. It starts with how samples should be taken, so that statistics estimate what we intend; then introduces the **sampling distribution** of a statistic, the **standard error** that measures its spread, and the **central limit theorem** that makes the sampling distribution of a mean approximately normal; then builds **confidence intervals** for means and proportions using Student's t distribution; and ends with the bootstrap, which does the same job by computation. The underlying theory is in Maths Atlas ([sampling](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=statistics&l=sampling), [estimation](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=statistics&l=estimation) and [confidence intervals](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=statistics&l=confidence-intervals)).

## Samples and populations {#sampling}

A statistic estimates a parameter of the population from which the sample was drawn ([[biostatistics/data-and-variation#def-population]]). Two quite different things can go wrong.

::: definition Sampling error, bias and precision {#def-bias}
The **sampling error** of an estimate is its difference from the parameter, which varies from sample to sample. An estimation procedure is **unbiased** if its estimates are correct on average over repeated samples, and **biased** if they are systematically too high or too low. It is **precise** if repeated estimates cluster closely together. **Accuracy** requires both: small bias and high precision.
:::

Precision improves with the size of the sample; bias does not. A thousand fish caught by a net that lets small fish escape give a precise estimate of the wrong mean length. The cure for bias is the design of the sampling, and the basic design is the **random sample**: every unit in the population has the same chance of being included, and units are chosen independently of one another.

Biologists rarely have a list of the population to draw from, so random sampling takes practical forms: quadrats placed at random coordinates generated by a computer rather than thrown over the shoulder; animals selected from a cage by a random number rather than by which one comes to the hand first (the slowest or the boldest); fields chosen at random from a map. Common sources of bias in biological sampling include:

- **Convenience sampling**: sampling near roads, paths or the field station, where conditions differ.
- **Volunteer or detection bias**: sampling those individuals that are easiest to see, catch or trap — trap-happy animals, conspicuous plants, birds that sing.
- **Length-biased sampling**: longer-lived or larger units are more likely to be caught in a snapshot, as long-lasting infections are over-represented in a one-day survey.
- **Survivorship bias**: museum specimens, fossils and long-term study plots record only what survived to be measured.

When the population is heterogeneous, **stratified random sampling** — dividing it into strata (habitats, age classes) and sampling randomly within each — gives more precise estimates for the same effort. Its logic is the same as that of blocking in an experiment ([[biostatistics/experimental-design]]).

## Sampling distributions and the standard error {#sampling-distribution}

Imagine repeating a study many times, each time with a new random sample of the same size $n$, and calculating the sample mean $\bar x$ each time. The distribution of these means is the **sampling distribution** of $\bar x$. It is not the distribution of the data: it describes how the *estimate* would vary.

::: proposition Mean and standard error of a sample mean {#prop-se}
If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent observations from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the sample mean $\bar X$ has mean $\mu$ and standard deviation
$$
\sigma_{\bar X} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.
$$ {#eq-se}
This is the **standard error of the mean**. Estimated from the sample, it is $\mathrm{SE}_{\bar x} = s/\sqrt n$.
:::

::: proof
$\E[\bar X] = \frac1n\sum\E[X_i] = \mu$. By independence the variances add: $\Var\big(\sum X_i\big) = n\sigma^2$, so $\Var(\bar X) = n\sigma^2/n^2 = \sigma^2/n$.
:::

The square root is the key to sample sizes. To halve the standard error you need four times as many observations; to reduce it tenfold, a hundred times as many. And note the assumption of independence: if the observations are correlated — ten measurements on the same leaf, five tanks of fish sharing one water supply — the effective sample size is smaller than $n$ and [[#eq-se]] is too optimistic. This is the statistical core of pseudoreplication ([[biostatistics/experimental-design]]).

::: definition Standard deviation versus standard error {#def-sd-se}
The **standard deviation** $s$ describes the variability of individual observations; it does not shrink as more data are collected (it converges to $\sigma$). The **standard error** $s/\sqrt n$ describes the uncertainty of the sample mean; it shrinks towards zero as $n$ grows. Report the standard deviation to describe a population, the standard error (or better, a confidence interval) to describe the precision of an estimate.
:::

## The central limit theorem {#clt}

[[biostatistics/probability-distributions#prop-normal]] showed that the mean of normal observations is exactly normal. Remarkably, the mean of almost *any* observations is approximately normal when $n$ is large enough.

::: proposition Central limit theorem {#prop-clt}
If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are independent and identically distributed with mean $\mu$ and finite standard deviation $\sigma$, then for large $n$ the distribution of
$$
Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
$$
is approximately standard normal, whatever the shape of the distribution of the $X_i$. Precisely, $\Prob(Z \le z) \to \Phi(z)$ as $n \to \infty$, for every $z$.
:::

The proof (by characteristic functions) is in [Maths Atlas: limit theorems](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=probability&l=limit-theorems). How large is "large"? It depends on how far the population is from normal: for a symmetric population $n = 10$ is usually plenty; for a strongly skewed one, such as parasite loads or species abundances, $n$ may need to be 30, 50 or more. Try it.

::: widget clt
dist: skewed
n: 5
samples: 2000
caption: Two thousand sample means, each of $n$ draws from a right-skewed (log-normal) population, as a histogram, against the normal curve with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$ that the central limit theorem predicts. With $n = 5$ the histogram of means is still skewed to the right. Increase $n$ to 30 and it becomes nearly symmetric and close to the curve, and also narrower, by the factor $1/\sqrt n$. Switch to the bimodal or Bernoulli population to see that even very non-normal populations give nearly normal means.
:::

::: example Mean parasite load in a sample of fish {#ex-clt}
In a lake, the number of parasites per fish has mean 6 and standard deviation 9: most fish carry few parasites and a few carry very many, so the distribution is strongly right-skewed. A researcher examines a random sample of 50 fish. Approximately, what is the probability that the sample mean exceeds 8, and within what range will the sample mean fall in 95 % of such samples?
::: solution
By [[#prop-se]] the standard error is $9/\sqrt{50} = 1.273$. By the central limit theorem $\bar X$ is approximately $\Normal(6, 1.273^2)$, even though individual loads are far from normal. Then $z = (8 - 6)/1.273 = 1.571$ and $\Prob(\bar X > 8) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.571) = 0.058$. The central 95 % of sample means lie within $6 \pm 1.96\times1.273$, from 3.5 to 8.5.

Two cautions. With such strong skew, $n = 50$ is about the smallest sample for which the normal approximation is reasonable; the true sampling distribution is still slightly right-skewed, so large means are a little more likely than the approximation says. And with a sample of only 5 fish the standard error would be 4.0 and the approximation useless — a single heavily infected fish would dominate the mean.
:::
:::

The central limit theorem is why the normal distribution dominates statistics even though few biological variables are normal. It applies to sums as well as means, to proportions (which are means of 0–1 variables), and, in modified forms, to regression coefficients and many other estimates.

## Student's t distribution {#t-distribution}

If we knew $\sigma$, an interval $\bar x \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n$ would contain $\mu$ for 95 % of samples. But $\sigma$ is never known; we have only $s$, which is itself uncertain, especially in small samples. Replacing $\sigma$ by $s$ makes the standardised mean more variable than a normal variable, and the correct distribution was found by William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, who published under the pseudonym "Student" in 1908.

::: definition Student's t distribution {#def-t}
If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent $\Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)$ observations with mean $\bar X$ and standard deviation $S$, then
$$
T = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{S/\sqrt n}
$$ {#eq-t}
has **Student's t distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom**, written $t_{n-1}$. It is symmetric about zero and bell-shaped but has heavier tails than the standard normal; as the degrees of freedom increase it approaches $\Normal(0, 1)$.
:::

The heavier tails are the price of estimating $\sigma$. The two-sided 95 % critical values $t_{0.975,\,\nu}$, which cut off 2.5 % in each tail, show how fast the price falls:

| degrees of freedom $\nu$ | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 | 14 | 30 | 100 | ∞ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $t_{0.975,\,\nu}$ | 12.706 | 4.303 | 2.571 | 2.228 | 2.145 | 2.042 | 1.984 | 1.960 |

With two observations ($\nu = 1$) the 95 % multiplier is more than six times its large-sample value; by $\nu = 30$ the difference is small. (These values were computed for this lesson from the regularised incomplete beta function and agree with standard tables.)

::: widget distribution
dist: t
params: nu=3
normal: true
caption: The density of Student's t distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom, with the standard normal curve overlaid. With $k = 3$ the t curve is lower in the middle and has much heavier tails: extreme values of $T$ are far more likely than the normal predicts, because $s$ is sometimes much smaller than $\sigma$ in a small sample. Increase $k$ towards 30 and the two curves become almost indistinguishable.
:::

Strictly, the t distribution requires normal data. In practice the t procedures are **robust**: by the central limit theorem they work well for moderately non-normal data once $n$ is more than about 15–30, except for severe skew or outliers. For small samples from skewed populations, transform the data first ([[biostatistics/data-and-variation#transformations]]), or use methods that do not assume normality ([[biostatistics/comparing-groups]]).

## Confidence intervals for a mean {#ci-mean}

::: definition Confidence interval {#def-ci}
A **confidence interval** with confidence level $1 - \alpha$ (often 95 %) is an interval calculated from the data by a procedure that, over repeated samples, contains the true parameter with probability $1 - \alpha$. For the mean of a normal population, the $1 - \alpha$ interval is
$$
\bar x \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\; n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
$$ {#eq-ci-mean}
:::

::: proof Why the interval works
By [[#eq-t]], $\Prob\big(-t^* \le (\bar X - \mu)/(S/\sqrt n) \le t^*\big) = 1 - \alpha$ when $t^* = t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}$. Rearranging the inequalities to isolate $\mu$ gives $\Prob\big(\bar X - t^*S/\sqrt n \le \mu \le \bar X + t^*S/\sqrt n\big) = 1 - \alpha$. The probability statement is about the random endpoints, computed before the data are seen.
:::

::: example Darwin's maize: a confidence interval for the advantage of crossing {#ex-darwin-ci}
The fifteen differences (crossed minus selfed, eighths of an inch) are 49, −67, 8, 16, 6, 23, 28, 41, 14, 29, 56, 24, 75, 60, −48. Calculate a 95 % confidence interval for the mean difference, and also the 90 % and 99 % intervals.
::: solution
Because each pair grew in the same pot, the natural unit of analysis is the within-pair difference $d$. From the data, $\bar d = 314/15 = 20.93$, $s_d = 37.74$, and $\mathrm{SE} = 37.74/\sqrt{15} = 9.746$. With $\nu = 14$:

- 95 %: $t^* = 2.145$, so $20.93 \pm 2.145\times9.746 = 20.93 \pm 20.90$, giving **0.03 to 41.84**.
- 90 %: $t^* = 1.761$, giving 3.77 to 38.10.
- 99 %: $t^* = 2.977$, giving −8.08 to 49.94.

In inches the 95 % interval is 0.004 to 5.2 inches. The data are compatible with an advantage of crossing anywhere from essentially nothing to more than five inches. The 95 % interval only just excludes zero, which corresponds to the borderline P-value of 0.0497 that we shall obtain in [[biostatistics/hypothesis-testing]]; the 99 % interval includes zero. Using the normal multiplier 1.96 instead of $t$ would give 1.83 to 40.03 — too narrow, understating the uncertainty of a 15-pair experiment.
:::
:::

What does the 95 % mean? Not that there is a 95 % probability that $\mu$ lies between 0.03 and 41.84: $\mu$ is a fixed (unknown) number and either does or does not lie there. The 95 % describes the *method*: if Darwin's experiment were repeated many times and an interval computed each time by [[#eq-ci-mean]], 95 % of the intervals would contain the true mean difference. Our interval is one of them, and we do not know whether it is one of the lucky 95 %. (A Bayesian **credible interval** does carry the direct probability interpretation, given a prior; see [Maths Atlas: Bayesian inference](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=statistics&l=bayesian). With vague priors the two intervals are often numerically similar.)

::: warning "A 95 % confidence interval contains 95 % of the data"
A confidence interval for a mean describes where the *population mean* plausibly lies, and it shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$. The range containing 95 % of individual observations — a reference range, about $\bar x \pm 2s$ for normal data — does not shrink with $n$. With Darwin's differences, the confidence interval for the mean is 0.03 to 41.8, but individual pairs ranged from −67 to +75, and two of the fifteen selfed plants were *taller* than their crossed partners. A significant mean advantage does not mean that every crossed plant beats every selfed one.
:::

### Planning precision

Inverting [[#eq-se]] tells us how many observations we need to estimate a mean to within a margin $E$ with 95 % confidence: approximately
$$
n \approx \left(\frac{1.96\,\sigma}{E}\right)^2,
$$ {#eq-n-precision}
where $\sigma$ must be guessed from a pilot study or the literature. Because the t multiplier exceeds 1.96 for small $n$, the result should be rounded up and checked. Planning for the power of a test, rather than the width of an interval, is treated in [[biostatistics/hypothesis-testing]] and [[biostatistics/experimental-design]].

::: example How many flowers to measure? {#ex-n-precision}
A botanist wants to estimate the mean sepal length of an *Iris* population to within ±0.05 cm (95 % confidence). Pilot data suggest a standard deviation of about 0.35 cm, like Anderson's *I. setosa*. How many flowers are needed?
::: solution
$n \approx (1.96\times0.3525/0.05)^2 = (13.82)^2 = 190.9$, so 191 flowers. With $\nu = 190$ the t multiplier is 1.973, which would raise this to about 194; measure at least 194 to be safe (and more, to allow for damaged flowers). Halving the margin to ±0.025 cm would require four times as many, about 770.
:::
:::

## Confidence intervals for a proportion {#ci-proportion}

A proportion $\hat p = x/n$ — the fraction of infected hosts, of germinating seeds, of offspring with a phenotype — is a mean of 0–1 variables, so by [[biostatistics/probability-distributions#prop-binomial-moments]] and the central limit theorem it is approximately normal with standard error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$.

::: definition Wald and Wilson intervals for a proportion {#def-ci-prop}
The simple **Wald interval** is $\hat p \pm z^*\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n}$, with $z^* = 1.96$ for 95 %. The **Wilson score interval** (Edwin Wilson, 1927) is
$$
\frac{\hat p + \dfrac{z^{*2}}{2n} \pm z^*\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \dfrac{z^{*2}}{4n^2}}}{1 + \dfrac{z^{*2}}{n}}.
$$ {#eq-wilson}
:::

The Wald interval is fine for large samples with $\hat p$ not near 0 or 1. For small samples or extreme proportions it behaves badly: its actual coverage can be far below 95 %, and it can extend below 0 or above 1. The Wilson interval, obtained by inverting the normal test rather than plugging in $\hat p$, stays within $[0, 1]$ and has close to nominal coverage; Alan Agresti and Brent Coull (1998) and others recommend it, or the similar Agresti–Coull interval, for routine use.

::: example Two proportions, large and small {#ex-ci-prop}
(a) Mendel recorded 1850 wrinkled seeds among 7324. Give a 95 % interval for the proportion wrinkled. (b) In a pilot survey, 3 of 20 frogs carry a chytrid fungal infection. Give the Wald and Wilson 95 % intervals for the prevalence.
::: solution
(a) $\hat p = 1850/7324 = 0.2526$, $\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{0.2526\times0.7474/7324} = 0.00508$, so the Wald interval is $0.2526 \pm 0.0100$, i.e. **0.243 to 0.263**. With $n$ this large the Wilson interval is the same to three decimal places. The interval comfortably contains ¼, the Mendelian expectation.

(b) $\hat p = 0.15$. Wald: $0.15 \pm 1.96\sqrt{0.15\times0.85/20} = 0.15 \pm 0.156$, i.e. −0.006 to 0.306 — an impossible negative lower limit. Wilson, from [[#eq-wilson]] with $z^{*2}/n = 0.1921$: centre $(0.15 + 0.0960)/1.1921 = 0.2064$, half-width $1.96\sqrt{0.006375 + 0.002401}/1.1921 = 0.1540$, giving **0.052 to 0.360**. Twenty frogs say only that the prevalence is probably somewhere between 5 % and 36 %.
:::
:::

When none of $n$ units shows the trait, $\hat p = 0$ and the Wald interval collapses to a point. A useful approximation, the **rule of three**, says that the upper 95 % limit is about $3/n$: if no infected frog is found among 20, the prevalence could still be as high as 15 % (Wilson gives 16 %). Absence from a small sample is weak evidence of absence.

## The bootstrap {#bootstrap}

For a mean or a proportion there are formulas for the standard error. For a median, a ratio, a correlation or a diversity index there often are not, or they rely on assumptions we cannot check. Bradley Efron's **bootstrap** (1979) estimates the sampling distribution by computation: treat the sample as a stand-in for the population, draw many **resamples** of the same size *with replacement* from it, compute the statistic for each, and use the spread of these values.

::: algorithm Percentile bootstrap confidence interval {#alg-bootstrap}
1. From the data $x_1, \dots, x_n$, draw $n$ values at random with replacement.
2. Compute the statistic $\hat\theta^*$ for this resample.
3. Repeat steps 1–2 a large number $B$ of times (say 10,000).
4. The standard deviation of the $B$ values estimates the standard error of $\hat\theta$, and their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles form an approximate 95 % confidence interval.
:::

```python
import random, statistics
d = [49, -67, 8, 16, 6, 23, 28, 41, 14, 29, 56, 24, 75, 60, -48]  # Darwin's differences
random.seed(1)
boot = sorted(statistics.median(random.choices(d, k=len(d))) for _ in range(10_000))
print(statistics.median(d), boot[249], boot[9749], statistics.stdev(boot))
# 24  14  49  8.05   -> median 24, 95% percentile interval 14 to 49, bootstrap SE about 8
```

For Darwin's differences the median is 24, with a bootstrap standard error of about 8 and a 95 % percentile interval from 14 to 49 (exact values depend on the random seed). The interval is lopsided, reflecting the lumpy distribution of fifteen differences. The same resamples, used for the mean, give a percentile interval of 1.5 to 37.9, a little narrower than the t-interval of [[#ex-darwin-ci]] — a known tendency of the simple percentile bootstrap in small samples. The bootstrap is not magic: with $n = 15$ the sample is a rough stand-in for the population, and it cannot repair a biased or non-independent sample. Refinements such as the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) interval improve its accuracy.

::: quiz
A study reports the mean wing length of 25 birds as 72.0 mm with a 95 % confidence interval of 70.0 to 74.0 mm. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] About 95 % of birds in the population have wings between 70 and 74 mm.
- [ ] There is a 95 % probability that the next bird measured will fall between 70 and 74 mm.
- [x] The interval was produced by a method that captures the population mean in 95 % of samples; the standard deviation of wing length is roughly 4.8 mm.
- [ ] If the study were repeated, 95 % of the new sample means would lie between 70 and 74 mm.
::: solution
The interval is about $\bar x \pm 2.06\,s/\sqrt{25}$, so its half-width 2.0 mm gives $s \approx 2.0\times5/2.064 = 4.8$ mm. Individual birds therefore vary over a range of roughly $72 \pm 9.7$ mm, far wider than the interval. The last option is also wrong: new sample means vary around $\mu$, not around this sample's $\bar x$, so fewer than 95 % of them (on average about 83 %) would fall in this particular interval.
:::
:::

::: application Estimating population size by mark–recapture
Ecologists estimate the size $N$ of an animal population by marking $M$ animals, releasing them, and later catching $n$ animals, of which $m$ are marked. If the second sample is random, the marked fraction $m/n$ estimates $M/N$, giving the Lincoln–Petersen estimate $\hat N = Mn/m$. Its assumptions are those of this chapter: a closed population, no loss of marks, and — crucially — equal catchability, so that the recapture sample is random with respect to marking. Trap-shy or trap-happy animals bias $\hat N$ up or down, however many animals are caught. A standard error and confidence interval follow from the binomial variability of $m$; Chapman's modification $\hat N = (M + 1)(n + 1)/(m + 1) - 1$ reduces bias when $m$ is small. Population estimation is developed in [[ecology]] and [[conservation-biology]].
:::

::: history
The standard error had been used in astronomy as the "probable error" since the early nineteenth century, but always with large samples. William Sealy Gosset, analysing small experiments on barley and hops for the Guinness brewery, found that the normal theory gave intervals that were too narrow; his paper "The probable error of a mean", published as "Student" in *Biometrika* in 1908, derived the t distribution (partly by simulation, shuffling 3000 measurements of criminals' finger lengths and heights on cards), and Ronald Fisher gave the general proof and the modern form in terms of degrees of freedom in the 1920s. Jerzy Neyman formalised confidence intervals in 1937; Edwin Wilson had published his interval for proportions in 1927. Bradley Efron introduced the bootstrap in 1979, one of the first statistical methods made possible by cheap computing.
:::

::: summary
- Bias is a property of how a sample is taken and is not reduced by larger samples; random (and stratified random) sampling is the defence.
- The sampling distribution of $\bar x$ has mean $\mu$ and standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$, estimated by $s/\sqrt n$, provided the observations are independent.
- The central limit theorem makes means approximately normal for large $n$, whatever the population, more slowly for skewed populations.
- With $\sigma$ estimated, $(\bar x - \mu)/(s/\sqrt n)$ follows Student's $t_{n-1}$; the 95 % interval is $\bar x \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1}\,s/\sqrt n$.
- Darwin's maize: mean advantage 20.9 eighths of an inch, 95 % interval 0.03 to 41.8.
- A confidence level describes the long-run success of the method, not the probability that this particular interval is right; confidence intervals for means are much narrower than the spread of the data.
- For proportions prefer the Wilson interval; with zero successes in $n$, the upper 95 % limit is about $3/n$.
- The bootstrap estimates standard errors and intervals by resampling the data with replacement.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise A standard error {level=1 check="1.5"}
A sample of 36 snails has shell lengths with standard deviation 9.0 mm. What is the standard error of the mean, in mm?
::: solution
$9.0/\sqrt{36} = 9.0/6 = 1.5$ mm.
:::
:::

::: exercise Quadrupling the sample {level=1 check="0.5"}
By what factor does the standard error of a mean change when the sample size is quadrupled (all else equal)?
::: solution
$\mathrm{SE} \propto 1/\sqrt n$, so it changes by $1/\sqrt4 = 0.5$: it halves.
:::
:::

::: exercise A t-interval {level=1}
Ten samples of pond water have nitrate concentrations with mean 4.80 mg L⁻¹ and standard deviation 0.90 mg L⁻¹. Calculate the 95 % confidence interval for the mean.
::: answer
4.16 to 5.44 mg L⁻¹.
:::
::: solution
$\mathrm{SE} = 0.90/\sqrt{10} = 0.2846$; with $\nu = 9$, $t_{0.975,9} = 2.262$, so $4.80 \pm 2.262\times0.2846 = 4.80 \pm 0.644$: 4.16 to 5.44 mg L⁻¹.
:::
:::

::: exercise The upper limit for zero {level=2 check="0.05"}
No individuals carrying a particular allele are found among 60 sampled. Using the rule of three, what is the approximate upper 95 % confidence limit for its carrier frequency?
::: solution
$3/n = 3/60 = 0.05$. The data are consistent with a carrier frequency of up to about 5 %. (The rule comes from solving $(1 - p)^n = 0.05$, i.e. $p \approx -\ln(0.05)/n = 3.0/n$.)
:::
:::

::: exercise Sample size for a proportion {level=2 check="385"}
How many animals must be sampled to estimate a prevalence that is probably around 50 % to within ±5 percentage points with 95 % confidence? Use $n = z^{*2}p(1-p)/E^2$ and round up.
::: solution
$n = 1.96^2\times0.25/0.05^2 = 3.8416\times0.25/0.0025 = 384.2$, so 385 animals. Because $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$, this is the worst case; for a prevalence near 10 % about 139 would do.
:::
:::

::: exercise Recovering the standard deviation {level=2 check="4.50"}
A paper reports a mean of 31.2 with a 95 % confidence interval of 28.8 to 33.6, from $n = 16$. Assuming a t-interval, what was the sample standard deviation? Give three significant figures.
::: solution
The half-width is $2.4 = t_{0.975,15}\,s/\sqrt{16}$, with $t_{0.975,15} = 2.131$, so $s = 2.4\times4/2.131 = 4.50$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Darwin without pairing {level=3}
Suppose Darwin's crossed and selfed plants had *not* been paired in pots, so that the two groups were independent samples. (a) Calculate the standard error of the difference between the group means, $\sqrt{s_\mathrm{C}^2/15 + s_\mathrm{S}^2/15}$, using $s_\mathrm{C} = 28.94$ and $s_\mathrm{S} = 16.41$. (b) Compare it with the paired standard error, 9.746, and explain the difference. (c) What does this suggest about the value of pairing in this experiment?
::: solution
(a) $\sqrt{837.3/15 + 269.4/15} = \sqrt{55.82 + 17.96} = \sqrt{73.78} = 8.59$.
(b) Surprisingly, the unpaired standard error is *smaller* than the paired one. The variance of a difference is $\Var(C) + \Var(S) - 2\Cov(C, S)$; pairing helps only when partners are positively correlated, so that the covariance term subtracts. Here the within-pair correlation of crossed and selfed heights is actually negative ($r \approx -0.34$): in pairs 2 and 15, for example, a very short crossed plant shared its pot with an ordinary selfed one. The paired variance $37.74^2 = 1424.6$ exceeds $837.3 + 269.4 = 1106.7$.
(c) In this experiment pairing within pots did not reduce the noise — the plants' heights were not driven mainly by shared pot conditions. But the analysis must follow the design: because the plants *were* paired, the paired analysis is the valid one, whichever standard error is smaller. Fisher's own randomisation analysis ([[biostatistics/hypothesis-testing]]) respects the pairing. Choosing the analysis after seeing which gives the smaller P-value would be cheating.
:::
:::

::: exercise What the confidence level promises {level=3}
A laboratory routinely reports 95 % confidence intervals. Over a year it publishes 200 intervals from independent studies. (a) How many would you expect *not* to contain the true value, and what is the standard deviation of that number? (b) The laboratory then publishes only the 40 intervals that excluded zero (its "positive findings"). Explain why the 95 % coverage guarantee no longer applies to the published set, and what kind of error the published intervals will tend to show.
::: solution
(a) The number of misses is $\Bin(200, 0.05)$: mean 10, standard deviation $\sqrt{200\times0.05\times0.95} = 3.08$. Between about 4 and 16 misses would be unremarkable.
(b) Coverage is a property of the procedure applied to every study. Selecting intervals because they excluded zero selects samples whose estimates happened to be far from zero, and those include, disproportionately, samples where chance exaggerated the effect. The published intervals will therefore tend to overestimate the size of effects, and some will be from studies where the true effect is zero (false positives). This is the "winner's curse" and part of the reproducibility problem discussed in [[biostatistics/hypothesis-testing]] and [[biostatistics/experimental-design]].
