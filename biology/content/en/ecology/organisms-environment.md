The saguaro cactus grows across the Sonoran Desert of Arizona and north-western Mexico, but stops abruptly a little way north and east of it, and climbs no higher than about the elevation at which hard frosts last through a whole day. The barnacle *Chthamalus stellatus* covers the upper zone of rocky shores in western Scotland and is almost absent a few tens of centimetres lower down, where conditions look more benign. The wood frog freezes solid each winter in Alaska and thaws in spring; a coral a few degrees too warm expels its algae and starves. Each of these facts is about where an organism can live and how many of it there are — the **distribution and abundance** of organisms, which is the subject matter of ecology.

Ecology as a whole asks why organisms are found where they are, in the numbers they are, and what they do there. Answers come at several levels: the physiology of individuals, the dynamics of populations, the interactions between species and the flows of energy and matter through ecosystems. This first chapter starts at the bottom, with a single organism and the environment it faces. We distinguish the physical **conditions** that act on an organism from the **resources** it consumes, describe how climate sets the pattern of both across the globe, quantify how performance depends on temperature, and end with the **niche** — the concept that ties an organism's requirements to its place in the world and that the following chapters on competition, predation and communities will keep returning to. Natural selection, which shapes every tolerance and requirement discussed here, was introduced in [[evolution/natural-selection]].

## Conditions and resources

An organism interacts with its physical and biological surroundings in two fundamentally different ways. Some environmental factors act on it without being used up; others are taken up and consumed, so that what one organism takes is no longer available to another.

::: definition Conditions and resources {#def-conditions-resources}
A **condition** is a physical or chemical feature of the environment — temperature, relative humidity, pH, salinity, water flow, wind speed, the concentration of a pollutant — that affects the performance of organisms but is not consumed or depleted by them. A **resource** is anything consumed by an organism, so that its quantity available to others is reduced: light and carbon dioxide for a plant, mineral nutrients, water, food, oxygen, and space such as nest holes or a place on a rock.
:::

The distinction matters because only resources can be **competed** for. Two plants side by side both experience the same air temperature, and one plant's experience does not diminish the other's; but every photon absorbed by the upper leaf is unavailable to the leaf beneath it, and every nitrate ion taken up by one root is lost to its neighbour. A resource therefore links the fates of the organisms that use it, and its supply can be driven down by consumption — the basis of the models of competition in [[ecology/competition]].

Some factors are both. Water is a resource for a plant that takes it up, but water flow over a stream bed is a condition for a mayfly larva clinging to a stone. Oxygen is a resource for animals, yet its concentration in a pond is lowered by their respiration in a way that then acts as a condition for everything else living there. The classification is a statement about the relationship between a factor and an organism, not a property of the factor itself.

::: quiz
For a seedling growing on the floor of a beech wood, which of the following are resources (in the sense of [[#def-conditions-resources]])? Select all that apply.
- [x] Light reaching the forest floor
- [ ] Air temperature
- [x] Nitrate in the soil
- [ ] Soil pH
- [x] Soil water
::: solution
Light, nitrate and water are taken up and consumed: a neighbouring plant using them reduces what the seedling can get. Air temperature and soil pH are conditions — they affect the seedling's growth, but the seedling does not deplete them and neighbours do not compete for them (although organisms can modify conditions, as roots acidify the soil around them).
:::
:::

## How climate sets the scene

The large-scale pattern of conditions and resources on Earth is set by the input of solar energy and by the circulation of the atmosphere and oceans that redistributes it.

**Solar radiation and latitude.** The Sun delivers about 1361 W m⁻² to a surface perpendicular to its rays at the top of the atmosphere (the "solar constant"); averaged over the whole spherical surface and over day and night this is a quarter, about 340 W m⁻². Near the equator the noon Sun is high all year and its energy is concentrated on a small area; towards the poles the same beam strikes the surface obliquely, is spread over a larger area, and passes through more atmosphere. Because the Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.4° relative to its orbit, the latitude of most direct sunlight moves between the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn over the year, which produces the **seasons** — weak near the equator, extreme at high latitudes.

**Atmospheric circulation.** Air heated at the equator rises, cools as it expands, and drops its water as the heavy rain of the tropical rain forests. The dry air then flows poleward at altitude, sinks at around 30° north and south, and warms as it descends, absorbing moisture: this descending limb of the **Hadley cells** is why the great hot deserts — the Sahara, the Arabian, the Kalahari, the Australian interior — lie near those latitudes. The band of rising air and rain near the equator, the intertropical convergence zone, follows the Sun north and south, giving many tropical regions a wet and a dry season instead of a warm and a cold one.

**Oceans and mountains.** Water has a high heat capacity, so coastal climates are milder than continental ones at the same latitude, and ocean currents move heat over thousands of kilometres: the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift keep north-western Europe several degrees warmer than eastern Canada at the same latitude. Mountains force moist air upwards, where it cools and rains on the windward slope, leaving a **rain shadow** on the lee side — the dry interior of the western United States east of the Sierra Nevada and Cascades, or the Patagonian steppe east of the Andes.

**Altitude.** Rising air expands and cools. In the standard atmosphere the temperature falls by about 6.5 °C per kilometre of altitude (the actual **lapse rate** varies with humidity, season and place, from roughly 4 to 9.8 °C km⁻¹). Climbing a mountain therefore takes an organism through a sequence of climates similar to those met by travelling towards the poles, and vegetation zones on mountains echo the latitudinal sequence of biomes described in [[ecology/biogeography]].

::: example How far upslope must a species move? {#ex-lapse}
A species of alpine plant has its lower limit on a mountain at 1800 m, set by summer temperature. The mountain is roughly a cone with its summit at 2800 m. If the regional climate warms by 1.5 °C with an unchanged lapse rate of 6.5 °C km⁻¹, by how much does the lower limit move, and what fraction of the species' former area remains?
::: solution
A given temperature is now found higher up by

$$
\Delta h = \frac{1.5\ \text{°C}}{6.5\ \text{°C km}^{-1}} = 0.231\ \text{km} \approx 231\ \text{m},
$$

so the lower limit moves from 1800 m to about 2031 m. For a cone, the horizontal area above altitude $h$ is a circle whose radius is proportional to the height remaining to the summit, $H - h$. The area therefore scales as $(H - h)^2$, and the fraction remaining is

$$
\left(\frac{2800 - 2031}{2800 - 1800}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{769}{1000}\right)^2 \approx 0.59.
$$

A warming of 1.5 °C removes about 41 % of the species' habitat, even though the band it lost is only 231 m deep. Real mountains are not cones and plants do not track isotherms instantly, but the geometry is general: habitat near summits shrinks as it moves up, and species already near the top have nowhere to go.
:::
:::

**Microclimate.** The climate an organism experiences may differ greatly from the regional climate recorded by a weather station 1.5 m above short grass. The south-facing slope of a hill in the northern hemisphere receives more sunlight than the north-facing one; the air a few millimetres above a dark rock in sunshine can be 20 °C warmer than the air a metre higher; the inside of a rotting log or a burrow is buffered against both heat and cold. Small organisms live in these microclimates, and many animals choose among them by behaviour — a lizard shuttling between sun and shade can hold its body temperature within a few degrees over a day in which air temperature varies by twenty.

## Responding to conditions: tolerance and performance

How does an organism's performance — its growth rate, survival, fecundity, or the speed at which a lizard can sprint — depend on a condition such as temperature?

::: definition Tolerance range and performance curve {#def-tolerance}
For a given condition, the **performance curve** of an organism plots a measure of performance against the level of the condition. Performance is greatest at an **optimum** and falls on either side. The **tolerance range** is the range of the condition over which the organism can survive; within it, a narrower range allows growth, and a still narrower one allows reproduction. The limits of survival are called the **critical minimum** and **critical maximum** (for temperature, $CT_\mathrm{min}$ and $CT_\mathrm{max}$).
:::

The idea that every species has a range of tolerance for each factor, with limits at both ends, was put forward by Victor Shelford in 1913 as the "law of tolerance". Thermal performance curves of ectotherms (animals whose body temperature follows the environment) have a characteristic asymmetric shape: performance rises slowly with temperature, reaches its optimum, then collapses over a few degrees as proteins and membranes begin to fail. The optimum therefore lies much closer to the critical maximum than to the critical minimum, which has an unwelcome consequence: a small warming above the optimum costs far more than the same cooling below it.

Ectotherms that live in the tropics often have body temperatures already close to their optimum and critical maximum, with small **thermal safety margins**, whereas many temperate species currently live below their optimum. Studies comparing the thermal performance curves of insects and lizards across latitude have therefore concluded that tropical ectotherms may be more vulnerable to a given degree of warming than temperate ones, although such predictions depend on how far the animals can use shade, microclimate and behaviour to buffer themselves.

### Temperature and the rates of biological processes

Within the tolerance range, most biological rates increase with temperature because the rates of the underlying chemical reactions do. A convenient summary is the temperature coefficient.

::: proposition The temperature coefficient Q₁₀ {#prop-q10}
If the rate $R$ of a process is multiplied by the same factor $Q_{10}$ for every increase of 10 °C, then

$$
R(T) = R(T_0)\,Q_{10}^{\,(T - T_0)/10}, \qquad Q_{10} = \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^{10/(T_2 - T_1)},
$$ {#eq-q10}

where $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the rates measured at temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$. For most metabolic processes of organisms between about 5 and 35 °C, $Q_{10}$ lies between about 2 and 3.
:::

This is an **empirical description**, not a law. Taking logarithms, $\ln R$ is a straight line in $T$ with slope $(\ln Q_{10})/10$, which is a good approximation over a moderate range; over wider ranges $Q_{10}$ itself changes, falling at high temperatures and collapsing beyond the optimum, as the performance curve shows. The more mechanistic Arrhenius (Boltzmann) form, $R \propto e^{-E/kT}$ with $T$ in kelvin and an activation energy $E$, underlies the metabolic theory of ecology, but it too is a description of average behaviour over a range rather than a mechanism for any one organism. The chemistry of reaction rates is treated in the [Chemistry Atlas](/learn/chemistry/course.php?c=kinetics), and enzymes in [[biochemistry/enzymes]].

::: example Q₁₀ of a lizard's metabolism {#ex-q10}
A lizard consumes 0.20 mL of oxygen per hour at a body temperature of 20 °C and 0.52 mL h⁻¹ at 30 °C. Calculate $Q_{10}$, and predict the oxygen consumption at 35 °C.
::: solution
The interval is exactly 10 °C, so $Q_{10} = 0.52/0.20 = 2.6$. From [[#eq-q10]],

$$
R(35) = R(30)\times 2.6^{(35 - 30)/10} = 0.52 \times 2.6^{0.5} = 0.52\times 1.612 = 0.84\ \text{mL h}^{-1}.
$$

The prediction is only as good as the assumption of a constant $Q_{10}$: if 35 °C is near the lizard's optimum, the true value may be lower.
:::
:::

A practical consequence is that the development of ectotherms depends on accumulated warmth rather than on calendar time. For many insects and plants, development proceeds at a rate roughly proportional to the temperature above a **developmental threshold** $T_\mathrm{th}$, and completing a stage takes a fixed **thermal constant** $K$, measured in **degree-days**: one degree-day is one day spent 1 °C above the threshold. This linear model is an approximation that holds over the middle of the temperature range; it is used routinely to time the emergence of pests and the flowering of crops.

::: example Degree-days and the number of generations {#ex-degree-days}
An aphid has a developmental threshold of 10 °C and needs 250 degree-days to develop from birth to reproduction. How long does development take at a constant 15 °C and at 20 °C? A growing season lasts 150 days with a mean temperature of 18 °C; how many generations fit into it, and how many if the season warms by 2 °C?
::: solution
At 15 °C, each day contributes $15 - 10 = 5$ degree-days, so development takes $250/5 = 50$ days. At 20 °C each day contributes 10, so it takes 25 days: a 5 °C warming halves the generation time.

Over the season the accumulated degree-days are $150\times(18 - 10) = 1200$, enough for $1200/250 = 4.8$ generations. With a 2 °C warming the season accumulates $150\times 10 = 1500$ degree-days, or 6 generations. Because each generation multiplies the population, an extra 1.2 generations of an insect whose numbers increase several-fold per generation can mean a much larger population by the end of the season — one route by which warming affects pest outbreaks.
:::
:::

Real temperatures fluctuate, and degree-days are summed day by day. The simplest method uses the mean of the daily maximum and minimum:

```python
import numpy as np

t_max = np.array([22, 25, 19, 17, 24, 28, 26])   # daily maximum, °C (shape: 7 days)
t_min = np.array([ 9, 12, 10,  6, 11, 15, 14])   # daily minimum, °C
threshold = 10.0                                  # developmental threshold, °C

daily_mean = (t_max + t_min) / 2
degree_days = np.clip(daily_mean - threshold, 0, None)   # no development below threshold
print(degree_days)          # [ 5.5  8.5  4.5  1.5  7.5 11.5 10. ]
print(degree_days.sum())    # 49.0 degree-days in the week
```

The averaging method underestimates development on days whose mean is below the threshold but whose afternoon is warm; more careful methods integrate a sine curve fitted between the minimum and maximum.

### Coping with extremes

Organisms deal with conditions outside their preferred range in three broad ways. They can **tolerate** them physiologically: the wood frog *Rana sylvatica* (*Lithobates sylvaticus* in some classifications) survives the freezing of much of its body water by loading its cells with glucose. They can **avoid** them in time or space: by dormancy (seeds, bacterial endospores, the diapause of insects), by hibernation, or by migration. Or they can **regulate** their internal state against the external one, as endotherms do for temperature and as marine fishes do for salt; the physiology of thermoregulation is treated in [[animal-physiology/thermoregulation]].

The extremes life can tolerate are remarkable. Some archaea grow above 100 °C in hydrothermal vents — one strain of *Methanopyrus kandleri* was reported in 2008 to reproduce at 122 °C under high pressure — while some bacteria and algae grow in brine below 0 °C. Few eukaryotes complete their life cycles above about 60 °C. These are the edges of the possible; most species tolerate a far narrower range, set not by what proteins can survive but by the trade-offs of living in a particular place.

::: warning "Organisms are adapted to the conditions where they are found"
It is tempting to assume that the conditions where a species lives must be its optimum. Often they are not. The barnacles and plants discussed below live where competitors and predators allow them to, which may be far from their physiological optimum; species can persist for a time in places where their populations are declining; and many species have not yet tracked recent changes in climate. The range of conditions occupied in nature is a combination of physiology, interactions and history — the distinction between the fundamental and the realised niche made precise later in this chapter.
:::

### Acclimation and local adaptation

Tolerance is not fixed. Within a lifetime, an individual moved to a new condition often adjusts its physiology over days or weeks — **acclimation** (in the laboratory) or **acclimatisation** (in the field) — for instance by changing the composition of its membrane lipids or the isoforms of its enzymes. Across generations, populations of a widespread species living in different conditions often evolve genetic differences: they become **ecotypes**. The classic demonstration came from Jens Clausen, David Keck and William Hiesey, who from the 1930s grew clones of yarrow (*Achillea*) and other plants collected along a transect across the Sierra Nevada of California in common gardens at three altitudes. Plants from high altitude were short and remained short even when grown at low altitude, showing that much of the difference between populations was genetic, while a share of it was plastic. Common-garden and reciprocal-transplant experiments of this kind remain the standard way to separate local adaptation from plasticity.

## Resources

Resources are the things an organism consumes in order to grow and reproduce. For green plants they are solar radiation, carbon dioxide, water and mineral nutrients; for animals they are other organisms, alive or dead, as food, together with water, oxygen and space.

**Solar radiation.** Plants use radiation of wavelengths 400–700 nm, **photosynthetically active radiation** (PAR). The rate of photosynthesis of a leaf rises with irradiance and then saturates, and leaves of plants grown in shade saturate at lower irradiance than sun leaves but use dim light more efficiently. Light is a strange resource: it cannot be stored, so a photon not captured when it arrives is lost, and its supply changes from second to second as clouds and wind-blown leaves pass. Light-response curves and their dependence on C₃ and C₄ photosynthesis are treated in [[plant-biology/photosynthesis]].

**Water.** Plants lose water through their stomata whenever they take in carbon dioxide, so water and carbon gain are inseparable. Soil water is held more tightly as the soil dries, and its availability is measured as **water potential**, a negative pressure in megapascals; by convention, many crop plants wilt permanently when soil water potential falls to about −1.5 MPa, though desert plants can extract water from much drier soils. Water movement through plants is covered in [[plant-biology/water-transport]].

**Mineral nutrients.** Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, calcium, magnesium, iron and a dozen others must be taken up from the soil (or, for aquatic plants, the water). Their availability is uneven in space and time, and their cycling through ecosystems is the subject of [[ecology/nutrient-cycles]].

**Food.** For heterotrophs, the resource is the bodies of other organisms, which vary greatly in quality. Plant tissue is rich in carbon but poor in nitrogen — leaves typically have carbon-to-nitrogen mass ratios of 20 to 50, and wood several hundred, whereas animal tissue has ratios of about 5 to 8 — and is defended by cellulose, lignin and toxins. Animal prey is nutritionally closer to the predator but has to be caught.

### Combining several resources

When an organism needs several resources, how do their supplies combine to determine its growth?

::: definition Essential and substitutable resources {#def-essential}
Two resources are **essential** (or complementary) if neither can replace the other: growth is limited by whichever is in shortest supply relative to need, as nitrogen and phosphorus are for a plant. They are **substitutable** if one can take the place of the other: a fall in one can be compensated by more of the other, as two species of seeds for a finch. Intermediate cases exist: resources may be partly substitutable, or **complementary** in the sense that a mixture gives more growth than either alone.
:::

For essential resources, the agricultural chemists of the nineteenth century noticed that adding a nutrient raised crop yields only if that nutrient was the one in shortest supply. The idea is associated with Justus von Liebig, who popularised it in 1840 (it had been stated earlier by Carl Sprengel), and is known as **Liebig's law of the minimum**: growth is controlled not by the total of resources available but by the scarcest one relative to requirements.

::: example The law of the minimum {#ex-liebig}
A field can supply 80 % of the nitrogen, 45 % of the phosphorus and 120 % of the potassium a wheat crop needs to reach a target yield of 8 t ha⁻¹. Under the law of the minimum, what yield do you expect? What happens if (a) potassium fertiliser is added, (b) phosphorus is added to meet the full requirement?
::: solution
Yield is set by the most limiting nutrient, phosphorus at 45 %: about $0.45\times 8 = 3.6$ t ha⁻¹.

(a) Adding potassium, already in excess, changes nothing: still 3.6 t ha⁻¹.

(b) With phosphorus at 100 %, nitrogen becomes limiting, at 80 %: the yield rises to $0.80\times 8 = 6.4$ t ha⁻¹, not to 8. The limiting factor shifts as each limitation is removed.
:::
:::

The law of the minimum is a useful first model, but nature is less tidy. Plants adjust their allocation, growing more roots when soil nutrients are scarce and more leaf when light is, so that several resources tend to become limiting together; and a meta-analysis by Stanley Harpole and colleagues in 2011, which pooled hundreds of nutrient-addition experiments in terrestrial, freshwater and marine systems, found that adding nitrogen and phosphorus together often increased producer biomass more than either alone, a pattern called **co-limitation**.

## The ecological niche

We now have the ingredients for the most important concept in this chapter. A species can live in a place only if the conditions there lie within its tolerance and the resources it needs are available. The set of all such requirements defines its niche.

The word has had three meanings. Joseph Grinnell, writing about the California thrasher in 1917, used it for the habitat requirements that limit a species' distribution. Charles Elton, in *Animal Ecology* (1927), used it for the species' functional role in its community — what it eats and what eats it. G. Evelyn Hutchinson, in 1957, gave the definition used today, which absorbs both.

::: definition The Hutchinsonian niche {#def-niche}
Consider every condition and resource that affects a species, each as an axis of an $n$-dimensional space. The **fundamental niche** of the species is the region (the "$n$-dimensional hypervolume") of this space in which, in the absence of other species, its populations can grow or at least maintain themselves — that is, in which births at least balance deaths. The **realised niche** is the part of the fundamental niche that the species actually occupies in the presence of competitors, predators, parasites and mutualists.
:::

Three points about this definition are worth making explicit. First, the niche is defined by **population growth**, not by individual survival: an individual may survive conditions in which its population cannot persist. Second, it is a property of the **species**, not of a place: the niche is a set of requirements, and the habitat is where they are met. A useful way to say this is that a habitat is a species' "address" and its niche its "profession". Third, the niche can be drawn only one or two axes at a time, but it has as many dimensions as there are factors that matter. With one condition (say temperature) the niche is an interval; with two (temperature and humidity) it is a region of a plane; with three it is a volume.

### Fundamental and realised niches in the field

The clearest demonstration of the difference between the fundamental and the realised niche is an experiment on barnacles by Joseph Connell, published in 1961. On the rocky shore at Millport, on the Isle of Cumbrae in the Firth of Clyde, adults of the small barnacle *Chthamalus stellatus* occupied a band high on the shore, above the zone of the larger *Balanus balanoides* (now *Semibalanus balanoides*). Yet *Chthamalus* larvae settled lower down as well. Connell removed *Balanus* from some stones in the lower zone and left it on others. Where *Balanus* was removed, *Chthamalus* survived well; where it remained, *Balanus* smothered, undercut and crushed the young *Chthamalus*. In the upper zone, by contrast, *Balanus* died of desiccation at low tide, while *Chthamalus* tolerated it.

The interpretation is that the fundamental niche of *Chthamalus* extends down into the *Balanus* zone, but competition restricts its realised niche to the upper shore; *Balanus*, meanwhile, is excluded from the upper shore by a physical condition, not by a competitor. Exactly this pattern — the upper limit set by physical stress, the lower limit set by biotic interactions — has since been found on rocky shores around the world.

::: quiz
In Connell's experiment, what does the survival of *Chthamalus* in the lower zone after *Balanus* is removed show?
- [ ] That *Chthamalus* is better adapted to the lower zone than *Balanus*
- [x] That the lower zone lies within the fundamental niche of *Chthamalus*, which is excluded from it by *Balanus*
- [ ] That *Balanus* cannot survive in the lower zone
- [ ] That desiccation sets the lower limit of *Chthamalus*
::: solution
Removing the competitor reveals where *Chthamalus* *could* persist on its own: its fundamental niche includes the lower zone. In the presence of *Balanus* it does not survive there, so its realised niche is narrower. The experiment says nothing about *Chthamalus* being better adapted to the lower zone than *Balanus* — when both are present *Balanus* wins there.
:::
:::

The realised niche is usually smaller than the fundamental niche, but not always. **Facilitation** — one species making conditions better for another — can extend a species into places it could not occupy alone: on salt marshes in New England, rushes shade the soil and reduce its salinity, allowing other plants to grow where they would otherwise fail. And a species can be found outside its fundamental niche altogether when individuals keep arriving from productive populations nearby: a **sink** population, in which deaths exceed births, persists only because it is replenished by immigrants from **source** populations, an idea developed by Ronald Pulliam in 1988. A list of the places where a species is found therefore includes both more and less than its fundamental niche.

::: intuition The niche as a filter
Picture all the species of a region as a pool of potential colonists for a given site. The site's conditions act as a first filter, admitting only species whose tolerances include them. Its resources are a second filter. The species already present, with their competitive, predatory and mutualistic effects, are a third. And dispersal decides which species even reach the site. What we find there is what passed all four filters — which is why the presence of a species tells us about all of them at once, and the absence of a species about none of them in particular.
:::

### Mapping niches and predicting distributions

Because the fundamental niche is defined in environmental space and a species' range is defined in geographical space, the two can be connected by mapping. A **species distribution model** (or ecological niche model) relates records of where a species has been found to environmental variables at those places — typically climate, soil and land cover — and projects the fitted relationship back onto a map, predicting where conditions are suitable. Such models are widely used to predict where an invasive species might spread or where a species' climatically suitable range will lie under future climates.

They must be interpreted with care. A model fitted to where a species occurs describes something closer to its realised niche than its fundamental niche, since the records already reflect competitors, predators, dispersal barriers and history; and it assumes that the relationships fitted under present conditions will hold under new combinations of conditions. A complementary approach builds the niche from physiology — measured tolerances and energy budgets — and asks where a population could persist; combining the two is an active area of research.

::: application Species on the move
If species' distributions are set in part by climate, a warming climate should move them. A meta-analysis of studies on many groups of organisms, published by I-Ching Chen and colleagues in 2011, estimated that species' ranges had shifted to higher latitudes at a median rate of about 17 km per decade and to higher elevations at about 11 m per decade, with faster shifts in regions that had warmed more. The responses were highly variable between species — some had not moved, some had moved the "wrong" way — as one would expect when distributions are set by interactions and dispersal as well as by temperature tolerance. The conservation consequences of these shifts, and of the shrinking habitat near mountain summits worked out in [[#ex-lapse]], are taken up in [[conservation-biology/invasions-climate]].
:::

::: history
Ecology became a named science when Ernst Haeckel coined the word *Oekologie* in 1866 for the study of the relations of an organism to its surroundings, but natural historians had long recorded where species lived. Alexander von Humboldt's profile of the Andean volcano Chimborazo, published in 1807, showed vegetation zones arranged by altitude against temperature, effectively the first map of plant distribution against a condition. Liebig's law of the minimum (1840) and Shelford's law of tolerance (1913) gave the first general statements about how resources and conditions limit organisms. The niche was defined three times: by Joseph Grinnell (1917) as a species' habitat requirements, by Charles Elton (1927) as its role in the community, and by G. Evelyn Hutchinson (1957) as an $n$-dimensional hypervolume. Joseph Connell's barnacle experiments (1961) made the distinction between fundamental and realised niches a matter of field experiment rather than of definition.
:::

::: summary
- Ecology studies the distribution and abundance of organisms and the interactions that determine them.
- **Conditions** (temperature, pH, salinity) affect organisms without being consumed; **resources** (light, water, nutrients, food, space) are consumed and can therefore be competed for ([[#def-conditions-resources]]).
- Climate is set by the latitudinal gradient in solar radiation, the axial tilt (seasons), atmospheric circulation (rain at the equator, deserts near 30°), oceans, mountains (rain shadows) and altitude (about 6.5 °C cooler per km). Organisms live in microclimates that can differ greatly from the regional climate.
- Performance curves rise to an optimum and fall; for ectotherms they are skewed, collapsing just above the optimum. Biological rates typically have $Q_{10} \approx 2$–3 ([[#prop-q10]], an empirical rule), and ectotherm development can be modelled with degree-days.
- Organisms tolerate, avoid or regulate extremes; tolerance changes by acclimation within a lifetime and by local adaptation (ecotypes) across generations.
- Essential resources combine according to Liebig's law of the minimum as a first approximation; co-limitation is common.
- The **fundamental niche** is the region of condition–resource space in which a population can persist; the **realised niche** is what is left in the presence of other species ([[#def-niche]]). Connell's barnacles show competition restricting the realised niche; facilitation can expand it and source–sink dynamics can place species outside it.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise A temperature coefficient {level=1 check="2"}
The rate of respiration of a seedling is 3.0 μmol CO₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 15 °C and 6.0 μmol CO₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 25 °C. Calculate $Q_{10}$.
::: solution
The interval is 10 °C, so $Q_{10} = 6.0/3.0 = 2$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Predicting a rate {level=1 check="17.33"}
A process has $Q_{10} = 2.5$ and a rate of 10 units at 20 °C. Assuming $Q_{10}$ is constant, what is the rate at 26 °C? Give two decimal places.
::: solution
By [[#eq-q10]], $R(26) = 10\times 2.5^{(26-20)/10} = 10\times 2.5^{0.6} = 10\times 1.733 = 17.33$ units.
:::
:::

::: exercise Degree-days {level=1 check="40"}
A beetle has a developmental threshold of 8 °C and a thermal constant of 400 degree-days. How many days does it take to develop at a constant 18 °C?
::: solution
Each day at 18 °C contributes $18 - 8 = 10$ degree-days, so development takes $400/10 = 40$ days.
:::
:::

::: exercise Temperature on a mountain {level=1 check="12.25"}
The mean summer temperature at sea level near a mountain is 22 °C. Using a lapse rate of 6.5 °C km⁻¹, estimate the mean summer temperature at 1500 m.
::: solution
$22 - 1.5\times 6.5 = 22 - 9.75 = 12.25$ °C.
:::
:::

::: exercise Conditions or resources? {level=2}
Classify each of the following as a condition or a resource for the organism named, and justify any case you find ambiguous: (a) nest holes in old trees for a woodpecker; (b) salinity for an estuarine crab; (c) oxygen dissolved in water for a trout; (d) wind speed for a tree on a ridge; (e) nectar for a bumblebee.
::: solution
(a) Resource: a hole used by one pair is unavailable to another, so nest sites can be competed for (space is a resource). (b) Condition: the crab's presence does not alter salinity. (c) Resource: respiration consumes oxygen and lowers its concentration for other fish, especially in warm, still water; but its concentration also acts like a condition for animals that do not much deplete it, which is why oxygen is sometimes classed as both. (d) Condition: wind affects the tree's growth and shape but is not consumed. (e) Resource: nectar taken by one bee is unavailable to the next.
:::
:::

::: exercise Liebig with three nutrients {level=2}
A grassland can supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium at 60 %, 90 % and 75 % of the amounts needed for a maximum biomass of 10 t ha⁻¹. Assuming Liebig's law of the minimum, predict the biomass (a) as it stands, (b) after nitrogen fertilisation to meet requirements, (c) after nitrogen and potassium fertilisation. What result would suggest co-limitation instead?
::: solution
(a) Nitrogen is most limiting: $0.60\times10 = 6$ t ha⁻¹. (b) Potassium becomes limiting: 7.5 t ha⁻¹. (c) Phosphorus becomes limiting: 9 t ha⁻¹. Under strict Liebig limitation, adding phosphorus alone to the original grassland would have no effect. If an experiment found that adding phosphorus alone increased biomass, or that nitrogen and phosphorus together increased it by more than the sum of their separate effects, the plants would be co-limited — for instance because the community contains species limited by different nutrients, or because extra phosphorus lets plants take up more nitrogen.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reading a removal experiment {level=2}
On a rocky shore, two species of seaweed occupy adjacent zones: species U in the upper intertidal and species L in the lower. An ecologist clears species L from plots in the lower zone and transplants species U into them, and transplants species L into the upper zone. After a year: U survives well in cleared lower plots, but poorly in uncleared lower plots; L transplanted into the upper zone dies within weeks, whether or not U is removed. Interpret the results in terms of fundamental and realised niches.
::: solution
U can live in the lower zone when L is absent, so the lower zone is within U's fundamental niche; its realised niche is restricted to the upper zone by L (by competition or some other negative interaction). L dies in the upper zone even without U, so its upper limit is set by a physical condition — probably desiccation or heat at low tide — not by U. This is the same asymmetry Connell found in barnacles: upper limits set by physical stress, lower limits by biotic interactions.
:::
:::

::: exercise Thermal safety margins {level=3}
Two lizard species have the same thermal performance curve shape. Species T, in the tropics, has an optimum body temperature of 33 °C, a critical maximum of 38 °C and a typical field body temperature of 32 °C. Species M, at mid-latitude, has an optimum of 33 °C, a critical maximum of 41 °C and a field body temperature of 26 °C. (a) For each, what is the thermal safety margin ($CT_\mathrm{max}$ minus field temperature)? (b) Using the asymmetric shape of performance curves, explain which species is likely to gain and which to lose performance if its field body temperature rises by 3 °C. (c) Give two reasons why the real outcome might differ.
::: solution
(a) T: $38 - 32 = 6$ °C. M: $41 - 26 = 15$ °C.

(b) M at 26 °C is below its optimum; warming by 3 °C moves it to 29 °C, closer to the optimum, so its performance rises. T at 32 °C is just below its optimum; warming to 35 °C takes it past the optimum onto the steep falling side, 2 °C above the optimum and 3 °C from its critical maximum, so its performance falls, possibly sharply, and on hot days it may exceed its critical maximum. The same warming helps the temperate species and harms the tropical one.

(c) Lizards thermoregulate behaviourally and may hold body temperature down by using shade or being active at cooler times (though this costs foraging time); thermal tolerance can change by acclimatisation or evolution; and warming is uneven in time, so extremes may matter more than means. Interactions with other species, which also respond to temperature, can change outcomes too.
:::
:::

::: exercise Habitat loss on a mountain {level=3 check="1.9"}
An alpine species lives above 2400 m on a mountain that is approximately a cone with its summit at 3400 m. The lower limit tracks temperature with a lapse rate of 6.5 °C km⁻¹. What regional warming (in °C, to one decimal place) would halve the area available to it? Assume the summit is not a limit.
::: hint
For a cone, the area above altitude $h$ is proportional to $(H - h)^2$.
:::
::: solution
The area above the lower limit is proportional to $(3400 - h)^2$. Halving it requires $(3400 - h')^2 = \tfrac12(1000)^2$, so $3400 - h' = 1000/\sqrt2 = 707$ m and $h' = 2693$ m. The limit must rise by 293 m, which corresponds to a warming of $0.293\times 6.5 = 1.9$ °C.
:::
:::

::: exercise Niche or habitat? {level=3}
A species of fish is found in a lake where, by repeated sampling, deaths every year exceed births in the population living there. (a) How can the species persist in the lake? (b) Is the lake within its fundamental niche? (c) Why does this matter for a conservation manager who protects the lake as "the species' habitat"?
::: solution
(a) The population must be maintained by immigration, for instance from a stream or a connected lake where births exceed deaths: the lake is a **sink** and the other population a **source**.

(b) No. The fundamental niche is defined by conditions under which the population can at least maintain itself; in the lake, births do not balance deaths, so the lake lies outside it even though the species is found there.

(c) Protecting the sink alone will not conserve the species; if the source population declines or the connection is cut, the lake population will dwindle to extinction. The presence of a species is not proof that a place supports it, and management must identify and protect sources. Measuring demographic rates (births and deaths) rather than presence alone is the way to tell sources from sinks.
:::
:::
