A benchmark measures what a model can do on questions it has not seen. Language models are trained on trillions of tokens collected from the web, and the web contains benchmarks: test sets uploaded to repositories, questions discussed on forums with their answers, papers quoting examples, and pages generated by other models that were themselves trained on test data. When a test item, its answer, or a close paraphrase is in the training data, the evaluation partly measures **memorisation**, and the score overstates the capability. This is **test-set contamination**, introduced in [[pretraining/training-data]] as a data-pipeline problem; this chapter treats it as a measurement problem.

The chapter defines the kinds of contamination and what they do to a score, and shows how to estimate the clean score when contamination is present. It implements the classic detector — **$13$-gram overlap**, as in the GPT-3 analysis — in plain Python and derives how quickly it fails under paraphrase. It describes detection without access to the training data: **membership inference** with Min-K% Prob, a **permutation test** that can prove contamination statistically, and **canary strings**. It then turns to evidence that contamination and overfitting affect real scores, notably the **GSM1k** study, and to the main remedy, **dynamic and held-out benchmarks** such as LiveBench and LiveCodeBench, which also bear on the other way benchmarks die: saturation ([[evaluation/benchmarks#sec-lifecycle]]).

## Kinds of contamination {#sec-kinds}

::: definition Contamination {#def-contamination}
An evaluation item is **contaminated** for a model if information that determines its answer entered the model's training (pretraining, fine-tuning or reinforcement learning) in a form specific to that item. Distinguish:

- **verbatim input contamination**: the question appears, without its answer;
- **verbatim input–output contamination**: the question appears together with its answer or solution;
- **paraphrase or translation contamination**: a reworded or translated version appears, with its answer;
- **indirect contamination**: the item reaches the model through other models' outputs — synthetic training data generated by a model that had seen it, or data distilled from such a model;
- **evaluation-time contamination**: a model with search or retrieval finds the item or its answer online during the evaluation.

The **contamination rate** $c$ of a benchmark is the fraction of its items that are contaminated. A related but distinct problem is **distributional overfitting**: training on data so similar in style and template to a benchmark that the model learns the benchmark's quirks without having seen its items.
:::

Input-only contamination is not harmless — a model that has seen a question many times may find it easier to parse, and Magar and Schwartz (2022) found that models could exploit test examples seen during pretraining, without labels, on some tasks — but the most damaging kind supplies the answer. Contamination is also a matter of degree: an item seen once in a crawl of trillions of tokens may leave no measurable trace, while one repeated hundreds of times may be recited word for word ([[llm-society/privacy-memorisation]]).

### What contamination does to a score {#sec-effect}

Let each item $i$ have a score $Y_i^0$ that the model would achieve if the item were not in its training data, and $Y_i^1$ if it were — **potential outcomes** in the language of causal inference. Only one is observed: $Y_i^1$ for contaminated items, $Y_i^0$ for clean ones.

::: proposition Inflation and the clean-subset estimate {#prop-inflation}
Let $D_i\in\{0, 1\}$ indicate contamination, $c = \Prob(D = 1)$, and the observed score $Y_i = D_iY_i^1 + (1 - D_i)Y_i^0$. The quantity of interest is the uncontaminated score $\mu_0 = \E[Y^0]$. Then:

1. The overall score overstates it by
$$
\E[Y] - \mu_0 = c\,\big(\E[Y^1\mid D = 1] - \E[Y^0\mid D = 1]\big),
$$ {#eq-inflation}
the contamination rate times the average benefit of contamination to the contaminated items.
2. If contamination is independent of difficulty, $\E[Y^0\mid D = 1] = \E[Y^0\mid D = 0]$, then the **clean-subset score** $\E[Y\mid D = 0]$ equals $\mu_0$, and the dirty-minus-clean difference $\E[Y\mid D = 1] - \E[Y\mid D = 0]$ equals the average benefit.
3. If contaminated items are systematically easier (or harder), the dirty-minus-clean difference mixes the benefit of contamination with the difference in difficulty, and the clean-subset score estimates the score on the *kind* of items that happen to be clean.
:::

::: proof
(1) $\E[Y] = c\,\E[Y^1\mid D = 1] + (1 - c)\,\E[Y^0\mid D = 0]$ and $\mu_0 = c\,\E[Y^0\mid D = 1] + (1 - c)\,\E[Y^0\mid D = 0]$ by total expectation; subtracting gives [[#eq-inflation]]. (2) Under independence, $\E[Y\mid D = 0] = \E[Y^0\mid D = 0] = \E[Y^0] = \mu_0$, and $\E[Y\mid D = 1] - \E[Y\mid D = 0] = \E[Y^1\mid D = 1] - \E[Y^0\mid D = 1]$. (3) In general $\E[Y\mid D = 1] - \E[Y\mid D = 0] = \big(\E[Y^1 - Y^0\mid D = 1]\big) + \big(\E[Y^0\mid D = 1] - \E[Y^0\mid D = 0]\big)$, the benefit plus a difficulty gap that is zero only under independence.
:::

Part 3 is not a technicality. Items that leak onto the web tend to be the famous, easy or frequently discussed ones; items flagged by $n$-gram overlap tend to be long (more $n$-grams to match) and to use common phrasing. The GPT-3 authors (Brown et al., 2020), who reported clean-subset results for every benchmark, noted that for several benchmarks the dirty and clean subsets differed in ways unrelated to contamination, so that a small dirty-minus-clean difference was weak evidence either way.

::: example Estimating a clean score {#ex-clean}
A model scores $84\%$ overall on a benchmark. An overlap analysis flags $30\%$ of the items as contaminated, and the model scores $95\%$ on those. What is its score on the clean subset, and what does [[#prop-inflation]] say about the inflation?
::: solution
$0.84 = 0.3\cdot0.95 + 0.7\,s_{\text{clean}}$ gives $s_{\text{clean}} = (0.84 - 0.285)/0.7 = 0.793$. If flagged items are no easier than the others, the benefit of contamination is $0.95 - 0.793 = 0.157$ per contaminated item and the overall score is inflated by $0.3\cdot0.157 = 0.047$, about five points. If flagged items are also intrinsically easier — plausible, since popular items leak — some of the $0.157$ is difficulty, and the inflation is smaller. Either way, the honest report gives both numbers and the detection method.
:::
:::

## Detecting contamination with the training data {#sec-ngram}

When the training corpus is available — to the developer, or for open-data models to anyone — contamination can be searched for directly. The standard method looks for long shared word sequences.

::: algorithm $n$-gram overlap detection {#alg-ngram}
1. **Normalise and tokenise** each benchmark item and each training document: lower-case, remove punctuation, split into words (or use model tokens).
2. Build the set of all $n$-grams (runs of $n$ consecutive tokens) of the training corpus — in practice with a hash set, a Bloom filter or a suffix array, since the corpus is enormous.
3. For each item, compute its $n$-grams and the fraction found in the training set.
4. Flag the item as **dirty** if the fraction (or count) exceeds a threshold: any match, in the GPT-3 analysis, with $n = 13$ for most benchmarks and shorter $n$ for benchmarks with short items; at least $70\%$ of $8$-grams, in the PaLM analysis (Chowdhery et al., 2022).
5. Report the contamination rate and scores on dirty and clean subsets; optionally remove dirty documents from the training data (**decontamination**) before training.
:::

The choice of $n$ trades false positives against false negatives. Short $n$-grams such as "how many apples does she have" occur in many unrelated documents; very long ones miss items with any small change. Thirteen words is long enough that a chance match between unrelated texts is rare in natural language, and short enough to catch partial copies.

```python
import re

def tokens(text):
    """Lower-case word tokens with punctuation removed, as in the GPT-3 contamination analysis."""
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())

def ngrams(toks, n):
    return {tuple(toks[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(toks) - n + 1)}

test_items = {
    "q1": "A baker makes 24 muffins in the morning and twice as many in the afternoon. "
          "She sells 50 muffins. How many muffins does she have left at the end of the day?",
    "q2": "Tom has 3 boxes with 12 pencils in each box. He gives 7 pencils to his sister "
          "and loses 4 more. How many pencils does Tom have now?",
    "q3": "A train travels 60 miles per hour for 2 hours and then 45 miles per hour for "
          "3 hours. What is the total distance the train travels?",
    "q4": "A garden has 8 rows of tomato plants with 15 plants in each row. If 10 plants "
          "die, how many tomato plants are left in the garden?",
}
training_docs = [
    # a forum post that copies q1 verbatim, with its answer
    "Can someone help with my homework? A baker makes 24 muffins in the morning and twice "
    "as many in the afternoon. She sells 50 muffins. How many muffins does she have left at "
    "the end of the day? I got 22.",
    # a paraphrase of q2: same problem, different wording
    "Tom owns three boxes, each holding a dozen pencils. After giving seven to his sister "
    "and losing four, how many pencils remain?",
    # a page that quotes part of q3 only
    "Speed problems: a train travels 60 miles per hour for 2 hours and then 45 miles per "
    "hour, so what is its average speed overall?",
    "The tomato is a plant in the nightshade family. Gardeners grow it in rows in the garden.",
]
n = 13
train = set()
for doc in training_docs:
    train |= ngrams(tokens(doc), n)

for name, text in test_items.items():
    grams = ngrams(tokens(text), n)
    hit = grams & train
    print(f"{name}: {len(tokens(text)):2d} tokens, {len(grams):2d} {n}-grams, "
          f"{len(hit):2d} found in training data ({len(hit) / len(grams):.0%}) -> "
          f"{'DIRTY' if hit else 'clean'}")
```

```text
q1: 32 tokens, 20 13-grams, 20 found in training data (100%) -> DIRTY
q2: 28 tokens, 16 13-grams,  0 found in training data (0%) -> clean
q3: 27 tokens, 15 13-grams,  4 found in training data (27%) -> DIRTY
q4: 27 tokens, 15 13-grams,  0 found in training data (0%) -> clean
```

The detector behaves as designed and shows its two failure modes. The verbatim copy of q1 — with its answer — is caught. The paraphrase of q2, which gives away the same problem, is missed entirely: not one $13$-gram survives the rewording. And q3 is flagged although the training document asks a *different* question (average speed rather than total distance) and contains no answer: a partial quotation is a false positive for the purpose that matters. q4 shares individual words ("tomato", "rows", "garden") with a training document but no long sequence, and is correctly clean.

### How fast detection fails under paraphrase {#sec-paraphrase}

Paraphrase can be modelled crudely as editing each token of an item independently with probability $r$. An $n$-gram survives if none of its $n$ tokens is edited.

::: proposition Survival of $n$-grams under edits {#prop-survival}
An item of $L\ge n$ tokens is edited, each token independently with probability $r$, and an edited token never matches. The number $S$ of the item's $n$-grams still present verbatim in the training copy has

$$
\E[S] = (L - n + 1)(1 - r)^n,\qquad \Prob(\text{detected}) = \Prob(S\ge1)\le\min\big(1, (L - n + 1)(1 - r)^n\big).
$$ {#eq-survival}
:::

::: proof
The $j$-th $n$-gram survives if its $n$ tokens are all unedited, with probability $(1 - r)^n$ by independence; summing the indicator over the $L - n + 1$ positions gives $\E[S]$ by linearity. The overlapping $n$-grams are not independent, but $\Prob(S\ge1)\le\E[S]$ by Markov's inequality (equivalently, the union bound over positions), and a probability is at most $1$.
:::

The bound shows the exponential sensitivity in $n$: at an edit rate of $30\%$, a $40$-token item has $\E[S] = 28\cdot0.7^{13} = 0.27$ surviving $13$-grams, so detection is at most $27\%$ likely. The exact probability of at least one surviving run, computed by dynamic programming over the length of the current run of unedited tokens, is lower still.

```python
def p_detect(L, n, r):
    """Exact probability that an item of L tokens, each edited independently with
    probability r, still contains a run of n unedited tokens (one surviving n-gram)."""
    # state: length of the current run of unedited tokens (0..n-1); absorbing once it reaches n
    probs = [1.0] + [0.0] * (n - 1)
    found = 0.0
    for _ in range(L):
        new = [0.0] * n
        for run, p in enumerate(probs):
            new[0] += p * r                      # this token edited: run resets
            if run + 1 == n:
                found += p * (1 - r)             # run completes: the n-gram survives
            else:
                new[run + 1] += p * (1 - r)
        probs = new
    return found

L = 40
for n in (8, 13):
    for r in (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3):
        bound = min(1.0, (L - n + 1) * (1 - r) ** n)
        print(f"n = {n:2d}, edit rate {r:.2f}: P(detect) = {p_detect(L, n, r):.3f}, "
              f"expected surviving n-grams {(L - n + 1) * (1 - r) ** n:6.2f}, bound {bound:.3f}")
```

```text
n =  8, edit rate 0.05: P(detect) = 1.000, expected surviving n-grams  21.89, bound 1.000
n =  8, edit rate 0.10: P(detect) = 0.990, expected surviving n-grams  14.21, bound 1.000
n =  8, edit rate 0.20: P(detect) = 0.839, expected surviving n-grams   5.54, bound 1.000
n =  8, edit rate 0.30: P(detect) = 0.510, expected surviving n-grams   1.90, bound 1.000
n = 13, edit rate 0.05: P(detect) = 0.962, expected surviving n-grams  14.37, bound 1.000
n = 13, edit rate 0.10: P(detect) = 0.791, expected surviving n-grams   7.12, bound 1.000
n = 13, edit rate 0.20: P(detect) = 0.332, expected surviving n-grams   1.54, bound 1.000
n = 13, edit rate 0.30: P(detect) = 0.087, expected surviving n-grams   0.27, bound 0.271
```

With one word in five changed, a $13$-gram detector finds only a third of contaminated $40$-token items; an $8$-gram detector finds most, at the price of more chance matches. Real paraphrases are not independent edits — they reorder clauses and change every few words — and real models can be helped by a paraphrase as much as by a copy. Yang et al. (2023) showed this directly: fine-tuning a model on rephrased or translated test sets raised its benchmark scores dramatically while $n$-gram decontamination detected almost none of the rephrased items; they proposed using an embedding search followed by a model judge to find semantic duplicates.

## Detecting contamination without the training data {#sec-blackbox}

For most commercial models and many open-weight ones, the training data is not public. Contamination must then be inferred from the model's behaviour.

### Membership inference: Min-K% Prob {#sec-mink}

A **membership inference** test decides from a model's outputs whether a given text was in its training data. The idea is that training lowers the loss on the training texts, so they look more probable to the model than comparable unseen texts. Raw loss is a poor signal, because some texts are simply predictable (common phrases, simple language) whether or not they were seen.

::: definition Min-K% Prob {#def-mink}
For a text $x_1, \dots, x_T$ and a model $p_\theta$, compute the token log-probabilities $\ell_t = \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})$, take the set $\mathcal{M}$ of the $\lceil kT\rceil$ smallest (the least likely tokens), and define

$$
\text{Min-}k\%(x) = \frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{M}\rvert}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{M}}\ell_t.
$$

The text is predicted to be a training member if $\text{Min-}k\%(x)$ exceeds a threshold. Shi et al. (2024), who proposed it, used $k = 20\%$ and evaluated it on **WikiMIA**, a set of Wikipedia passages about events before (members) and after (non-members) the models' training cut-offs.
:::

The rationale is that an unseen text usually contains a few **outlier tokens** with very low probability — an unusual name, a specific number — while a text seen in training has had those tokens pushed up. Averaging only the worst tokens focuses on them, and ignores the many easy tokens that every model predicts well.

::: example Computing Min-K% Prob {#ex-mink}
A $10$-token passage has token log-probabilities $-0.1, -0.3, -2.5, -0.2, -4.0, -0.05, -1.2, -0.4, -3.1, -0.6$. Compute its mean log-probability and its Min-$20\%$ Prob.
::: solution
The mean is $-12.45/10 = -1.245$. The lowest $20\%$ ($2$ tokens) are $-4.0$ and $-3.1$, so Min-$20\%$ is $-3.55$. A passage that the model had memorised would typically have its two hardest tokens much closer to zero, and its Min-$20\%$ would rise more than its mean, since the mean is dominated by easy tokens that change little.
:::
:::

Membership inference against large language models is harder than early results suggested. Duan et al. (2024) evaluated several methods, including Min-K% Prob, on models trained on the Pile with members and non-members drawn from the *same* distribution, and found performance barely above chance in most settings; they attributed much of the apparent success on benchmarks like WikiMIA to a **distribution shift** between members and non-members (the non-members, being about later events, differ in content and wording). Since a benchmark's items are not distinguished from comparable unseen items by date, this matters for contamination: a membership score is suggestive evidence about particular items, strongest when an item has been seen many times, and rarely proof.

### A permutation test for verbatim contamination {#sec-permutation}

Oren et al. (2023) found a way to obtain *provable* evidence of one kind of contamination from log-probabilities alone. Benchmarks are distributed as files with their items in a fixed **canonical order**. If a model has trained on the file, it may have learned that order: item $17$ tends to follow item $16$. If it has not, the canonical order is no more likely than any other.

::: proposition An exchangeability test {#prop-permutation}
Let $X = (x_1, \dots, x_m)$ be a benchmark in canonical order, and suppose under the null hypothesis $H_0$ that the canonical order is exchangeable from the model's point of view: for every permutation $\sigma$, the sequence $X_\sigma = (x_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, x_{\sigma(m)})$ has the same distribution of log-probability under the model as $X$. Draw $B$ random permutations, compute $L_0 = \log p_\theta(X)$ and $L_b = \log p_\theta(X_{\sigma_b})$ for $b = 1, \dots, B$, and let

$$
\hat p = \frac{1 + \#\{b : L_b\ge L_0\}}{B + 1}.
$$

Then under $H_0$, $\Prob(\hat p\le\alpha)\le\alpha$ for every $\alpha\in(0, 1)$: $\hat p$ is a valid $p$-value.
:::

::: proof
Under $H_0$ the $B + 1$ values $L_0, L_1, \dots, L_B$ are exchangeable: the canonical order is just one more uniformly random ordering as far as the model is concerned. The rank of $L_0$ among them (counting ties against $L_0$) is therefore at least as likely to be large as under a uniform distribution on $\{1, \dots, B + 1\}$. $\hat p\le\alpha$ requires that at most $\alpha(B + 1) - 1$ of the $L_b$ reach $L_0$, i.e. that $L_0$ be among the top $\lfloor\alpha(B + 1)\rfloor$ values, which has probability at most $\lfloor\alpha(B + 1)\rfloor/(B + 1)\le\alpha$.
:::

A small $p$-value proves that the model prefers the canonical order — which is strong evidence that it has seen the benchmark file, or something derived from it in that order. The test has no false positives beyond its stated level (given exchangeability, which can fail if the items have a natural order, such as increasing difficulty), but it has false negatives: a model that saw the items shuffled, scattered across web pages, or only a few times will not reveal it. Oren et al. split benchmarks into shards to make the test practical and reported evidence of contamination for some benchmarks in some open models, but not most.

### Canary strings and elicitation {#sec-canaries}

Benchmark authors can make detection easier in advance. The BIG-bench authors (Srivastava et al., 2022) put a **canary string** — a fixed sentence asking that the data not be used for training, with a unique random identifier (a GUID) — in every file of the benchmark. Developers can filter out any document containing the canary; and if a model can complete the identifier when prompted with its beginning, it was almost certainly trained on files containing it. The GPT-4 technical report (OpenAI, 2023) noted that portions of BIG-bench had been inadvertently mixed into the training set. Canaries only work if the benchmark is copied with the canary intact and if developers filter for it.

A related approach asks the model to reproduce a test item from its beginning, or to generate an item given the benchmark name and split (Golchin and Surdeanu, 2023). Exact or near-exact reproduction is evidence of contamination; failure is not evidence of its absence, since a model can be helped by data it cannot recite.

## Evidence of overfitting: GSM1k {#sec-gsm1k}

Detection methods identify particular contaminated items. A different question is whether published scores on a benchmark are inflated *overall*, for whatever reason — contamination, paraphrased leakage, or training on data so similar to the benchmark that its quirks are learned. The cleanest test is to build a **new test set from the same distribution** and see whether scores hold.

Zhang et al. (2024) did this for GSM8K. They commissioned **GSM1k**, $1{,}250$ new grade-school mathematics problems written by human annotators without the help of language models, designed to match GSM8K in style, difficulty distribution (measured by the number of steps and by human solve rates) and answer format, and kept private to avoid contamination. They then evaluated many open and closed models on both. Their reported findings were these. Some model families showed accuracy drops on GSM1k relative to GSM8K across almost all their sizes — evidence of systematic overfitting to GSM8K — of up to several percentage points; many others, including the frontier models they tested, showed little or no drop. They also found a positive relationship between a model's per-character log-likelihood of generating GSM8K test examples (a membership-style signal) and the size of its GSM8K–GSM1k gap, consistent with contamination being part of the cause, while noting that it did not explain all of the variation, so other forms of overfitting are likely also involved. The study is evidence that some reported GSM8K scores overstate the corresponding capability, not that all do, and its conclusions are specific to the models and time it covered.

::: example Is a gap real? {#ex-gap}
A model scores $80\%$ on the $1{,}319$-item GSM8K test set and $72\%$ on the $1{,}250$-item GSM1k. Treating the two scores as independent binomial proportions, is the $8$-point gap larger than sampling noise? What assumption carries the conclusion?
::: solution
Standard error of the difference: $\sqrt{0.8\cdot0.2/1319 + 0.72\cdot0.28/1250} = \sqrt{0.000121 + 0.000161} = 0.0168$. The gap is $0.08/0.0168 = 4.8$ standard errors, far beyond noise. The test, however, only shows that the two item sets differ *for this model*; attributing the difference to overfitting requires that the sets be equally hard for an uncontaminated model. That is why GSM1k was matched on difficulty and validated with human solve rates, and why the informative quantity is the gap *relative to other models*: if most models score the same on both sets and a few drop, the drop is about those models, not about the sets.
:::
:::

::: warning Absence of detected contamination is not absence of contamination
A report that says "we checked for contamination and found little" usually means that one detector, with one threshold, found few exact overlaps with the benchmark's text. It does not cover paraphrases and translations ([[#prop-survival]]), solutions posted separately from questions, synthetic data produced by models that had seen the benchmark, or fine-tuning data written in the benchmark's style. Conversely, a flagged overlap does not prove that the score was inflated ([[#prop-inflation]]). Read contamination statements for their method and their coverage, and give more weight to results on held-out items created after the model was trained.
:::

## Dynamic and held-out benchmarks {#sec-dynamic}

Detection is a patch; the structural remedy is to evaluate on items the model cannot have seen. Two designs do this, and both also address saturation, because the item pool can be renewed as models improve.

::: definition Held-out and dynamic benchmarks {#def-dynamic}
A **held-out** (private) benchmark keeps its test items secret, releasing at most a small public sample; evaluation is run by the maintainer, who sees the model's outputs but does not publish the items. A **dynamic** benchmark adds new items over time, each stamped with its **release date**; a model is evaluated on items released after its training-data cut-off, which cannot have been in its training data, and scores are compared on matched time windows.
:::

**LiveBench** (White et al., 2024) releases new questions regularly, drawn from recent sources — newly published mathematics competitions, recent arXiv papers and news articles, new datasets — across mathematics, coding, reasoning, data analysis, language and instruction following, and scores them automatically against objective ground-truth answers, avoiding human or model judges. **LiveCodeBench** (Jain et al., 2024) continuously collects programming problems from competitive programming sites (LeetCode, AtCoder and Codeforces) together with their publication dates, and evaluates code generation and related skills, such as self-repair and predicting the output of code, on problems from chosen time windows. Its authors reported that some models' performance dropped markedly on problems published after their training cut-offs compared with earlier ones, which is the signature of contamination (or of easier earlier problems, which matched-window comparisons control for).

Held-out sets include private test splits kept by benchmark maintainers and evaluation organisations, sets of questions written for one-off studies (like GSM1k), and the private test set of competitions. Their weaknesses are the mirror image of their strength: results cannot be independently reproduced without the maintainer, the maintainer must be trusted, and a held-out set used repeatedly by many developers through the maintainer's interface leaks information through the scores themselves — a form of adaptive overfitting that Dwork et al. (2015) analysed and proposed to limit by adding noise to the answers or restricting the number of queries.

Dynamic benchmarks have their own costs. Fresh items differ from old ones in topic and possibly difficulty, so scores on different time windows are not directly comparable; a model's score depends on which window it is evaluated on; and building a steady stream of high-quality, verifiable items is expensive. Matched windows — comparing two models only on items released after both of their cut-offs — address the first two.

::: example A time-split analysis {#ex-timesplit}
A model with a training cut-off at the end of month $0$ solves $46\%$ of $500$ coding problems released in months $-6$ to $0$ and $31\%$ of $400$ problems released in months $1$ to $6$. A second model with a cut-off at month $6$ solves $40\%$ and $39\%$ of the same two sets. What do the numbers suggest?
::: solution
For the first model the drop after its cut-off is $15$ points; for the second, whose cut-off is after both windows, the two windows look equally hard ($40\%$ against $39\%$). So the later window is not intrinsically harder, and the first model's drop is most plausibly due to having seen (or seen discussions of) the earlier problems. Comparing the two models fairly requires using only the window after both cut-offs — months $1$ to $6$ would not qualify for the second model, so a further window after month $6$ is needed. On the earlier window the first model appears $6$ points better than the second; this comparison is contaminated.
:::
:::

### Contamination, saturation and the life of a benchmark {#sec-life}

Contamination and saturation reinforce each other. As a benchmark becomes popular, its items are copied, discussed and solved online, so contamination grows with time; as scores rise, the remaining items are the hardest and most ambiguous, and label errors dominate. A benchmark is most informative early in its life and loses validity on both counts as it ages. Good practice for benchmark builders follows: release items with a canary and in a format hard to crawl (some benchmarks distribute test sets in encrypted or password-protected archives); keep a private split; date every item; plan for renewal; and publish a procedure for retiring the benchmark when its ceiling is reached. For readers, the corresponding habit is to ask, of any score, how old the benchmark is, whether the items were public before the model was trained, and whether the result holds on items written afterwards.

::: history
Concern about test data in training sets is as old as large-scale pretraining. The GPT-2 report (Radford et al., 2019) measured $8$-gram overlap between WebText and common test sets, and the GPT-3 report (Brown et al., 2020) made $13$-gram contamination analysis and clean-subset results a standard part of a model report — and noted that a bug had left some overlaps in its training data. Dodge et al. (2021) documented benchmark data inside the C4 corpus. BIG-bench (Srivastava et al., 2022) introduced canary strings. In 2023 Sainz et al. warned that contamination threatened much of NLP evaluation, Golchin and Surdeanu proposed eliciting test items from models, Oren et al. gave a provable black-box test, and Yang et al. showed that rephrased test data evades $n$-gram decontamination. Shi et al. (2024) proposed Min-K% Prob, Duan et al. (2024) questioned how well membership inference works on large models, and Zhang et al. (2024) built GSM1k to measure overfitting directly. LiveBench and LiveCodeBench (2024) made dated, renewable items the default answer.
:::

::: summary
- An item is **contaminated** when item-specific information entered training: verbatim (with or without the answer), paraphrased, translated, indirectly via other models' outputs, or at evaluation time via search ([[#def-contamination]]).
- Contamination inflates scores by $c$ times the average benefit to contaminated items ([[#eq-inflation]]); the clean-subset score is unbiased only if contaminated items are as hard as clean ones ([[#prop-inflation]]).
- **$n$-gram overlap** ($13$-grams in the GPT-3 analysis) needs the training data; it catches copies and partial quotations and misses paraphrases, with detection falling like $(L - n + 1)(1 - r)^n$ under edits ([[#prop-survival]]).
- Without training data: **Min-K% Prob** averages the least likely tokens ([[#def-mink]]) but is often near chance on same-distribution data; the **permutation test** gives valid $p$-values for having learned a benchmark's canonical order ([[#prop-permutation]]); **canaries** and elicitation detect copying.
- **GSM1k** found accuracy drops for some model families relative to GSM8K, consistent with overfitting, and little drop for others.
- **Held-out** and **dynamic** benchmarks (LiveBench, LiveCodeBench) evaluate on items the model cannot have seen; compare models on matched time windows.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Counting matches {level=1 check="0.271"}
A benchmark item has $60$ tokens and a training document contains $25$ consecutive tokens of it verbatim. What fraction of the item's $13$-grams appear in the document (three decimal places)?
::: solution
The item has $60 - 13 + 1 = 48$ $13$-grams; the shared run contains $25 - 13 + 1 = 13$ of them. The fraction is $13/48 = 0.271$.
:::
:::

::: exercise A clean-subset score {level=1 check="0.65"}
A model scores $70\%$ overall. A fifth of the items are flagged as contaminated, and it scores $90\%$ on them. What is its clean-subset score?
::: solution
$0.70 = 0.2\cdot0.90 + 0.8\,s$, so $s = (0.70 - 0.18)/0.8 = 0.65$.
:::
:::

::: exercise A permutation $p$-value {level=1 check="0.02"}
For a benchmark, $199$ random permutations are scored, and $3$ of them have a log-probability at least as high as the canonical order. What is the $p$-value of [[#prop-permutation]]?
::: solution
$\hat p = (1 + 3)/(199 + 1) = 0.02$. The canonical order ranks fourth of $200$; under the null hypothesis this happens with probability at most $0.02$.
:::
:::

::: exercise A detection bound {level=2 check="0.428"}
Using [[#eq-survival]], bound the probability that a $30$-token item, with each token edited independently with probability $0.25$, is still detected by a $13$-gram detector. Give three decimal places.
::: solution
$(30 - 13 + 1)\cdot0.75^{13} = 18\cdot0.02376 = 0.428$. At most $43\%$ of such items would be caught.
:::
:::

::: exercise Min-K% by hand {level=2 check="-2.5"}
A $15$-token text has token log-probabilities $-0.2, -0.1, -1.8, -0.4, -0.3, -3.3, -0.2, -0.6, -2.4, -0.1, -0.5, -0.9, -0.3, -0.2, -0.7$. Compute Min-$20\%$ Prob.
::: solution
$20\%$ of $15$ is $3$ tokens; the three lowest are $-3.3$, $-2.4$ and $-1.8$, with mean $-7.5/3 = -2.5$. The mean over all tokens is $-12.0/15 = -0.8$; the Min-$20\%$ score looks only at the three surprising tokens, which are the ones that training on this text would raise most.
:::
:::

::: exercise Inflation for strong and weak models {level=2}
Two models have uncontaminated accuracy $0.9$ and $0.5$ on every item. Both have seen $20\%$ of the benchmark, and each recalls the answer on a contaminated item it would otherwise get wrong with probability $0.8$. Using the model of the figure in [[#sec-effect]], compute both observed scores and the observed gap. What does this imply for comparisons?
::: solution
Inflation is $c(1 - a)m$: $0.2\cdot0.1\cdot0.8 = 0.016$ for the strong model and $0.2\cdot0.5\cdot0.8 = 0.08$ for the weak one. Observed scores $0.916$ and $0.58$; the gap shrinks from $0.40$ to $0.336$. Contamination helps weak models more, because they have more wrong answers to correct, so equal contamination compresses differences; unequal contamination can reverse them. A comparison is only safe on items clean for both models.
:::
:::

::: exercise Designing a dynamic benchmark {level=2}
You are building a dynamic benchmark for question answering over news. List the design decisions needed to make scores (a) uncontaminated, (b) comparable across models with different cut-offs, and (c) automatically gradable, and name one weakness that remains.
::: solution
(a) Write questions only about events after a stated date; stamp each item with its creation date; keep a private reserve; do not publish answers until a window closes. (b) Report scores per time window and compare models only on windows after both cut-offs; keep the item-writing process and difficulty targets constant over time, and include anchor items (answered by all models) to detect drift in difficulty. (c) Use questions with short, verifiable answers (names, numbers, dates) and normalised matching ([[evaluation/benchmarks#def-em-f1]]), avoiding judges. Remaining weaknesses: models with web search can look up the answers (evaluation-time contamination), so the protocol must fix tool access; recent-news questions favour models with later cut-offs, conflating recency with capability; and difficulty may still drift between windows.
:::
:::

::: exercise Exact detection probability for a small case {level=3 check="0.5"}
For an item of $L = 4$ tokens, a $2$-gram detector, and edit rate $r = \frac12$, compute exactly the probability that at least one $2$-gram survives, by counting edit patterns. Compare with the bound of [[#eq-survival]].
::: solution
Each of the $16$ edit patterns is equally likely. Detection fails when no two adjacent tokens are both unedited: writing $1$ for unedited, the patterns of length $4$ with no "$11$" number $F_6 = 8$ (the Fibonacci count: $0000$, $0001$, $0010$, $0100$, $1000$, $0101$, $1010$, $1001$). So $\Prob(\text{detected}) = 1 - 8/16 = 0.5$. The bound is $(4 - 2 + 1)(1/2)^2 = 0.75$: valid but loose, because overlapping $2$-grams survive together (if $x_2x_3$ survives, $x_1x_2$ shares a token with it), so the expected count overstates the probability of at least one.
:::
:::

::: exercise Longest common run {level=3}
Extend the detector of [[#sec-ngram]] to report, for each item, the length of the longest run of consecutive tokens it shares with any training document, using dynamic programming over token positions. Flag items whose longest run is at least $50\%$ of their length, and compare this rule with "any $13$-gram" on the four items of the listing.
::: solution
```python
import re

def tokens(text):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())

def longest_common_run(a, b):
    """Length of the longest common contiguous token sequence of lists a and b."""
    best, prev = 0, [0] * (len(b) + 1)
    for i in range(1, len(a) + 1):
        cur = [0] * (len(b) + 1)
        for j in range(1, len(b) + 1):
            if a[i - 1] == b[j - 1]:
                cur[j] = prev[j - 1] + 1
                best = max(best, cur[j])
        prev = cur
    return best

items = {
    "q1": "A baker makes 24 muffins in the morning and twice as many in the afternoon. "
          "She sells 50 muffins. How many muffins does she have left at the end of the day?",
    "q2": "Tom has 3 boxes with 12 pencils in each box. He gives 7 pencils to his sister "
          "and loses 4 more. How many pencils does Tom have now?",
    "q3": "A train travels 60 miles per hour for 2 hours and then 45 miles per hour for "
          "3 hours. What is the total distance the train travels?",
    "q4": "A garden has 8 rows of tomato plants with 15 plants in each row. If 10 plants "
          "die, how many tomato plants are left in the garden?",
}
docs = [
    "Can someone help with my homework? A baker makes 24 muffins in the morning and twice "
    "as many in the afternoon. She sells 50 muffins. How many muffins does she have left at "
    "the end of the day? I got 22.",
    "Tom owns three boxes, each holding a dozen pencils. After giving seven to his sister "
    "and losing four, how many pencils remain?",
    "Speed problems: a train travels 60 miles per hour for 2 hours and then 45 miles per "
    "hour, so what is its average speed overall?",
    "The tomato is a plant in the nightshade family. Gardeners grow it in rows in the garden.",
]
for name, text in items.items():
    t = tokens(text)
    run = max(longest_common_run(t, tokens(d)) for d in docs)
    print(f"{name}: longest shared run {run:2d} of {len(t)} tokens "
          f"({run / len(t):.0%}) -> {'DIRTY' if run >= 0.5 * len(t) else 'clean'}")
```

```text
q1: longest shared run 32 of 32 tokens (100%) -> DIRTY
q2: longest shared run  4 of 28 tokens (14%) -> clean
q3: longest shared run 16 of 27 tokens (59%) -> DIRTY
q4: longest shared run  3 of 27 tokens (11%) -> clean
```

The proportional rule agrees with the $13$-gram rule here: it still flags q3's partial quotation (a run of $16$ tokens, the question's first sentence) and still misses q2's paraphrase, whose longest shared run is only $4$ tokens ("to his sister and"). Changing the threshold trades one error for the other; neither surface-matching rule can detect that q2's *problem* leaked. That requires semantic matching — embeddings or a judge — as proposed by Yang et al. (2023).
:::
