A language model produces its answer one token at a time, and each token comes out of a single forward pass through a fixed number of layers. Ask a model "What is $17\times 24 - 39$?" and require the answer immediately, and the whole calculation must happen inside the forward passes that emit the three digits. Let it first write "$17\times24 = 408$, and $408 - 39 = 369$", and each part of the calculation gets its own tokens, its own forward passes, and a written record that later tokens can read. That second behaviour, writing out intermediate steps before the answer, is called **chain of thought** (CoT). It is the starting point of everything in this course.

This chapter covers the discovery and the main variants of chain-of-thought prompting: scratchpads trained into small models, few-shot and zero-shot prompts for large ones, decompositions such as least-to-most prompting, and program-aided reasoning, in which the model writes code and an interpreter runs it. It then asks *why* intermediate tokens help. Part of the answer can be proved: a Transformer of fixed depth that writes $m$ tokens before answering can perform computations with $m+1$ times as many sequential steps, and published theorems show that, under standard complexity assumptions, this makes certain problems solvable that are not solvable in a single pass. Other parts of the answer are empirical and still debated. The chapter ends with the step from *prompting* for reasoning to *training* models that reason at length, which the rest of the course develops. Basic prompting technique is in [[prompting-rag/prompt-design#sec-cot]]; here we go much deeper.

## Scratchpads and chains of thought {#sec-origins}

The idea that a model should show its working predates large language models. Ling et al. (2017) collected algebra word problems with written **rationales** (the AQuA dataset) and trained a sequence-to-sequence model to generate the rationale and then the answer. Nye et al. (2021) trained Transformers to write intermediate results to a **scratchpad** — the partial sums of a long addition, or the values of variables line by line as a Python program executes — before writing the final output. On addition of numbers with more digits than seen in training and on program execution, models trained with scratchpads were far more accurate than models trained to output the answer directly. Cobbe et al. (2021) released GSM8K, $8{,}500$ grade-school mathematics word problems whose reference solutions are written out step by step in natural language, and trained models on those solutions.

These approaches needed training data with intermediate steps. Wei et al. (2022) showed that a sufficiently large pretrained model does not: a handful of demonstrations in the prompt, each consisting of a question, a written-out reasoning chain and the answer, is enough to make the model produce such chains for new questions.

::: definition Chain of thought {#def-cot}
A **chain of thought** for an input $x$ is a sequence of tokens $z = (z_1,\dots,z_m)$ generated by the model after $x$ and before a final answer $y$, intended to contain intermediate steps of the solution. The model samples from

$$
p_\theta(z, y\mid x) = p_\theta(z\mid x)\,p_\theta(y\mid x, z),
$$ {#eq-cot-factor}

and the answer is extracted from the end of the output (for example the text after "The answer is"). **Few-shot CoT** places $k$ demonstrations $(x_i, z_i, y_i)$ before $x$; **zero-shot CoT** adds only an instruction such as *Let's think step by step*; a **reasoning model** is trained to produce $z$ by itself, usually between special delimiters.
:::

The factorisation [[#eq-cot-factor]] is simply the chain rule of probability applied to the output: the chain of thought is part of what the model generates, not a separate mechanism. That observation matters twice in this course. In [[reasoning/self-consistency]] we will *marginalise* over $z$, treating the reasoning as a latent variable; in [[reasoning/faithfulness-limits]] we will ask whether $z$ actually reflects the computation that produced $y$.

### What Wei et al. found {#sec-wei}

Wei et al. evaluated few-shot CoT on arithmetic (GSM8K, SVAMP, AQuA and others), commonsense (CommonsenseQA, StrategyQA) and symbolic tasks (concatenating last letters of words, tracking whether a coin is still heads up after people flip it or not). Three findings defined the field.

1. **Large gains on multi-step problems.** With the $540$-billion-parameter PaLM model, GSM8K accuracy rose from $17.9\%$ with standard few-shot prompting (question, answer) to $56.9\%$ with eight chain-of-thought demonstrations.
2. **Dependence on scale.** The gains appeared only for the largest models they tested, around $100$ billion parameters and up; for small models, chain-of-thought prompting gave fluent but wrong reasoning and often *lower* accuracy than answering directly. The authors described chain of thought as an *emergent* ability of scale — a description later work has qualified, since it depends on the metric and on how models were trained ([[pretraining/in-training-evaluation]]).
3. **The content of the chain matters.** Ablations that replaced the chain by an equation only, or by a matched number of dots, or that put the reasoning *after* the answer, recovered little of the gain. The intermediate steps had to come before the answer and be written in language.

The demonstrations were short — eight hand-written solutions for most arithmetic tasks — and the same eight worked across several datasets, which is part of why the result was so striking.

::: example A few-shot chain-of-thought prompt {#ex-fewshot}
Write a one-demonstration chain-of-thought prompt for the question *A shop sells pens in packs of $12$. Mia buys $7$ packs and gives away $30$ pens. How many does she have left?* Count the tokens the model must generate with and without the chain, assuming about $1.3$ tokens per English word and $1$ token per number.
::: solution
A prompt with one demonstration:

> Q: Tom has 3 boxes of 8 eggs. He uses 5 eggs. How many are left?
> A: Tom has 3 × 8 = 24 eggs. After using 5 he has 24 − 5 = 19. The answer is 19.
>
> Q: A shop sells pens in packs of 12. Mia buys 7 packs and gives away 30 pens. How many does she have left?
> A:

The expected continuation is *Mia has 7 × 12 = 84 pens. After giving away 30 she has 84 − 30 = 54. The answer is 54.* That is about $16$ words and $6$ numbers, roughly $16\cdot1.3 + 6\approx27$ tokens; a direct answer "54" is $1$ token. The chain multiplies the output by about $27$ here, and by far more on hard problems, where reasoning models routinely write thousands of tokens. Every one of those tokens costs a forward pass — the price side of chain of thought, which we return to in [[#sec-cost]] and [[reasoning/test-time-scaling]].
:::
:::

## Zero-shot chain of thought {#sec-zeroshot}

Kojima et al. (2022) removed the demonstrations. Their **zero-shot CoT** prompt has two stages. In the first, *reasoning extraction*, the question is followed by "A: Let's think step by step." and the model writes a chain. In the second, *answer extraction*, the question, the trigger, the generated chain and a format-specific phrase such as "Therefore, the answer (arabic numerals) is" are fed back, and the model's short continuation is parsed as the answer. With an instruction-following GPT-3 model (text-davinci-002), accuracy on the MultiArith benchmark rose from $17.7\%$ to $78.7\%$ and on GSM8K from $10.4\%$ to $40.7\%$. Few-shot CoT with good demonstrations remained better, but the trigger alone recovered much of the gain. Kojima et al. compared many trigger phrases; instructive ones ("Let's think step by step", "First,") helped, while misleading or irrelevant ones did not.

The two-stage design exposes a practical point that remains true in 2026: the answer must be *extracted*, and extraction is an error source. A chain that ends "so the answer is probably 54, or perhaps 56" must be resolved to one value before it can be scored, voted on ([[reasoning/self-consistency]]) or rewarded ([[reasoning/verifiable-rewards]]). Reasoning benchmarks therefore fix an answer format (a boxed number, an option letter) and a parser.

::: warning "Let's think step by step" is not a universal improvement
Chain of thought helps most on problems with several dependent steps — arithmetic word problems, symbolic manipulation, logic. Sprague et al. (2024) analysed more than a hundred papers and ran their own evaluations on twenty datasets, and found that the gains of CoT prompting were concentrated in mathematics and symbolic reasoning; on most other tasks they were small, and on knowledge questions of the MMLU kind most of the benefit came from questions involving equations. CoT can also hurt: on some perceptual or intuitive judgements, writing out reasoning lowers accuracy, and on simple questions it only adds cost. For models already trained to reason, an explicit CoT instruction usually changes little. Test it on your task rather than adding it by reflex.
:::

## Why intermediate tokens help {#sec-why}

Three explanations are offered, and they are complementary rather than exclusive.

- **More computation.** Each generated token is another forward pass, and its result is written where every later pass can read it. A chain of thought therefore buys *serial* computation that a single forward pass does not have. This part can be made precise ([[#prop-depth]] and [[#sec-expressivity]]).
- **Decomposition into familiar steps.** A hard question becomes a sequence of easy ones, each resembling text the model has seen often. Prystawski, Li and Goodman (2023) gave a formal version: when training data consist of local clusters of variables that co-occur, a model can estimate the relation between two variables that never appear together by chaining through intermediate ones, and reasoning helps exactly in that setting.
- **Conditioning on a good trajectory.** Text with a correct worked solution followed by an answer is more common in pretraining data than a bare correct answer to a hard problem. Producing the solution first moves the model into the part of its distribution where the answer is right.

### Serial depth {#sec-depth}

Consider a decoder-only Transformer with $L$ layers ([[transformers/transformer-block]]). At position $t$, layer $\ell$ reads, through attention, the layer-$(\ell-1)$ states of positions $1,\dots,t$. Information flows *up* the layers and *forward* in position, but a state at layer $\ell$ can never feed a state at a lower layer — except through the output: the top layer at position $t$ produces a token, and that token is the input at position $t+1$, where computation restarts at layer $1$.

::: proposition Chain of thought multiplies serial depth {#prop-depth}
In an $L$-layer decoder-only Transformer that reads an input of length $n$, generates $m$ tokens of chain of thought and then one answer token, define a *serial path* as a sequence of layer applications each of which takes the output of the previous one as an input. The longest serial path ending at the answer token has length $L(m+1)$. Without the chain ($m = 0$) it has length $L$, however long the input.
:::

::: proof
Let the prompt occupy positions $1,\dots,n$ and the chain positions $n+1,\dots,n+m$; the top layer at position $t$ produces the token at position $t+1$, so the answer token is produced by the top layer at position $n+m$. A serial path uses two kinds of edges: from $(s,\ell-1)$ to $(t,\ell)$ with $s\le t$ (attention or the residual stream), which raises the layer by one, and from $(t, L)$ to $(t+1, 1)$ through a *generated* token, which is possible only for $t+1\in\{n+1,\dots,n+m\}$ — prompt tokens are given, not computed. Between two token edges a path climbs through at most $L$ layer applications. There are at most $m$ token edges on a path, hence at most $m+1$ climbs, and at most $L(m+1)$ layer applications in total. The bound is attained by the path that climbs the full stack at position $n$, passes through the token it emits to position $n+1$, climbs the full stack there, and so on up to position $n+m$. With $m = 0$ there are no token edges and the bound is $L$.
:::

The proposition is about *dependency structure*, not about what a model actually does with its extra depth. But it explains a basic limitation: the number of sequential layer applications available for the answer to a direct question is fixed by the architecture, while the length of a calculation — the number of moves in a game, the number of digits in a multiplication — grows with the input. Width, more heads or a longer input add parallel computation, not serial steps.

::: example Serial depth of a reasoning model {#ex-depth}
A model has $L = 64$ layers. It answers a question directly, or after a chain of thought of $5{,}000$ tokens. How long is the longest serial path in each case, and how many forward passes are made after the prompt?
::: solution
Directly: $L = 64$ sequential layer applications, one forward pass for the answer token. With the chain: $64\cdot5{,}001 = 320{,}064$ sequential layer applications and $5{,}001$ forward passes. The chain does not make any individual pass deeper; it makes the *composition* of passes deep, with the tokens as the only channel between them. That channel is narrow — one token, about $17$ bits for a vocabulary of $10^5$ — which is why the chain must write down the state that later steps need.
:::
:::

### Expressivity results {#sec-expressivity}

Theoretical computer science makes "fixed serial depth" precise with circuit classes. $\mathsf{TC}^0$ is the class of problems solvable by families of Boolean circuits of constant depth and polynomial size with threshold (majority) gates; $\mathsf{NC}^1$ allows logarithmic depth with bounded fan-in gates. It is known that $\mathsf{TC}^0\subseteq\mathsf{NC}^1$, and it is widely believed — but not proved — that the inclusion is strict.

::: theorem Transformers with and without a chain of thought (published results, stated informally) {#thm-expressivity}
1. (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023.) A Transformer with a constant number of layers whose numbers have $O(\log n)$ bits of precision on inputs of length $n$ can be simulated by uniform $\mathsf{TC}^0$ circuits. Hence, if $\mathsf{TC}^0\ne\mathsf{NC}^1$, such a Transformer answering in one pass cannot solve $\mathsf{NC}^1$-complete problems, such as evaluating the composition of a sequence of permutations of five elements (Barrington, 1989).
2. (Feng et al., 2023.) Under the same assumption, bounded-depth log-precision Transformers that output the answer directly cannot evaluate arithmetic expressions or solve systems of linear equations unless their size grows super-polynomially with the input length, while Transformers of *constant* size can solve these tasks by generating a chain of thought of polynomial length.
3. (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024.) Allowing a Transformer to generate $t(n)$ intermediate tokens increases its power with $t$: logarithmically many steps extend it only slightly beyond $\mathsf{TC}^0$; linearly many let it recognise every regular language (so it can track the state of any finite automaton); polynomially many (with a mild architectural assumption) make it recognise exactly the problems solvable in polynomial time.
4. (Li et al., 2024.) With $T$ steps of chain of thought, constant-depth Transformers with constant-bit precision can compute any function computable by Boolean circuits of size $T$.
:::

We do not prove these theorems; their proofs occupy the cited papers. What they establish, and what they do not, should be stated carefully. They are results about **expressivity** — whether *some* setting of the weights computes the function — under idealised assumptions about precision and attention. They do not say that gradient descent will find those weights, that a trained model uses its chain of thought the way the constructions do, or that natural-language reasoning has the structure of the formal problems. They also rest on the unproved conjecture $\mathsf{TC}^0\ne\mathsf{NC}^1$. Within those limits they give a clean answer to "can intermediate tokens add capability, or only make it visible?": they can add capability, because they add serial steps.

Experiments are consistent with this picture on synthetic tasks. Models trained to track the state of a permutation sequence or a finite automaton directly tend to learn shortcuts that fail on longer inputs (Liu et al., 2023), while models trained to write the intermediate states generalise further. The next example makes the idea concrete.

::: example The shell game {#ex-shell}
A ball starts under cup $0$ of five cups; a sequence of $n$ moves each swaps two cups. Where is the ball at the end? Write the scratchpad for the moves $(3,4), (0,2), (4,3), (3,2), (3,2), (4,1)$, and explain why this task is a natural test of [[#thm-expressivity]].
::: solution
Track the cup holding the ball. Start at $0$. $(3,4)$ does not touch cup $0$: still $0$. $(0,2)$ moves it to $2$. $(4,3)$: still $2$. $(3,2)$: to $3$. $(3,2)$: back to $2$. $(4,1)$: still $2$. The scratchpad is $0, 2, 2, 3, 2, 2$ and the answer is cup $2$.

Each step needs only the previous state and one move, so a chain of thought that writes the state after each move turns an $n$-step composition into $n$ easy steps. Answering directly requires composing $n$ permutations of five elements in one pass; following the ball distinguishes the identity from a five-cycle, so this is the $\mathsf{NC}^1$-complete problem of part 1 of [[#thm-expressivity]], and a bounded-depth Transformer cannot solve it for all $n$ in one pass unless $\mathsf{TC}^0 = \mathsf{NC}^1$.
:::
:::

### Errors compound {#sec-compound}

Serial computation has a cost: every step can go wrong. If each step of a chain is correct with probability $q$, independently, a chain of $s$ steps is entirely correct with probability $q^s$. Even $q = 0.99$ gives $0.99^{100}\approx0.37$ for a hundred steps. Errors are not always fatal — a later step may notice and repair an earlier slip, and reasoning models trained with reinforcement learning do this visibly ([[reasoning/verifiable-rewards]]) — but the compounding of small error rates is the basic reason long chains need checking, sampling and verification, which the next chapters develop.

::: widget plot
f: q^x; 1/5 + (4/5)*(1 - 5*(1 - q)/4)^x
x: 0, 200
y: 0, 1
sliders: q=0.98:0.9:0.999:0.001
labels: all $s$ steps correct, $q^s$; final answer correct (shell game, $5$ cups)
caption: The probability that a chain of $s$ steps, each correct with probability $q$, is entirely right ($q^s$), and the probability that the final answer of the five-cup shell game is right when an erroneous step puts the ball under a random wrong cup (derived in an exercise). Later errors can undo earlier ones, so the answer is right more often than the chain, but both decay towards their floor — $0$ for the chain, the guessing rate $1/5$ for the answer. Move $q$ from $0.98$ to $0.999$ to see how much per-step reliability long reasoning needs.
:::

## Decomposition {#sec-decomposition}

A chain of thought decides its own steps. Several methods instead make the decomposition explicit.

**Least-to-most prompting** (Zhou et al., 2023) works in two stages. First the model is prompted to break the problem into a list of simpler subproblems; then it solves them in order, each solution appended to the prompt for the next. On the SCAN benchmark of translating commands such as "jump around left twice" into action sequences, whose test split requires longer compositions than training, least-to-most prompting with $14$ demonstrations reached about $99\%$ accuracy with a code-trained GPT-3 model, against about $16\%$ for chain-of-thought prompting. The gain comes from **easy-to-hard generalisation**: the demonstrations show how to reduce a problem, and the reduction applies to longer problems than any shown.

**Self-ask** (Press et al., 2023) makes the model pose and answer explicit follow-up questions ("Follow up: Who was the president of the US when the iPhone was released?") before the final answer, which also gives natural places to call a search engine. Press et al. defined the **compositionality gap** as the fraction of two-hop questions a model gets wrong although it answers both single-hop sub-questions correctly, and found that the gap did not shrink as models grew; eliciting the sub-questions narrowed it.

::: algorithm Least-to-most prompting {#alg-ltm}
**Input:** question $x$; a decomposition prompt $P_d$ and a solving prompt $P_s$ with demonstrations.
1. Generate subquestions $(u_1,\dots,u_k)\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid P_d, x)$, ordered from simplest to the original question ($u_k = x$).
2. Set the context $C\leftarrow (P_s, x)$.
3. For $i = 1,\dots,k$: generate an answer $v_i\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid C, u_i)$ and append $(u_i, v_i)$ to $C$.
4. Return $v_k$.
:::

::: example Decomposing a word problem {#ex-ltm}
*Elsa has $5$ apples. Anna has $2$ more apples than Elsa. How many apples do they have together?* Give a least-to-most decomposition and its solution.
::: solution
Decomposition: $u_1$ = "How many apples does Anna have?", $u_2$ = "How many apples do they have together?". Solving: $v_1$ = "Anna has $5 + 2 = 7$." Then with $(u_1, v_1)$ in context, $v_2$ = "Together they have $5 + 7 = 12$." The answer is $12$. The decomposition step did the planning; each solving step is a single operation whose inputs are already written down — exactly the structure that [[#prop-depth]] says a fixed-depth model benefits from.
:::
:::

## Offloading computation: program-aided reasoning {#sec-pal}

Language models make arithmetic slips that no calculator would. **Program-aided language models** (PAL; Gao et al., 2023) and the closely related **program of thoughts** (Chen et al., 2022) separate the two parts of a solution: the model writes the reasoning as a program, and an interpreter executes it. The model's chain of thought becomes code whose variables name the quantities of the problem; the answer is whatever the program prints. Gao et al. reported that PAL with a code model outperformed chain-of-thought prompting of a much larger general model on GSM8K, by about $15$ points, and that the advantage grew on a version of the benchmark with larger numbers (GSM-Hard), where a model's mental arithmetic fails and the interpreter's does not.

::: example A program-aided solution {#ex-pal}
Write a PAL-style solution for: *A train leaves at 09:40 and travels $372$ km at $93$ km/h, then waits $25$ minutes, then travels $108$ km at $72$ km/h. When does it arrive?*
::: solution
```python
leave = 9 * 60 + 40                 # minutes after midnight
leg1 = 372 / 93 * 60                # 240.0 minutes
wait = 25
leg2 = 108 / 72 * 60                # 90.0 minutes
arrive = leave + leg1 + wait + leg2 # 935.0
print(f"{int(arrive // 60):02d}:{int(arrive % 60):02d}")   # 15:35
```

The model's job is the *translation* of the problem into code — which quantities exist and how they combine — and the interpreter's is the arithmetic. If the model wrote the wrong formula the program would be wrong too, so PAL removes calculation errors but not reasoning errors. The same division of labour reappears as tool use in [[agents]], and as the automatic checking of answers that makes reinforcement learning on mathematics and code possible ([[reasoning/verifiable-rewards]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
A model with $L = 48$ layers is asked to compose a sequence of $500$ permutations. Which statements are correct? (Select all that apply.)
- [x] Answering directly, the longest serial path to the answer has length $48$, whatever the input length.
- [x] Writing the intermediate state after each permutation gives a serial path of length up to $48\cdot501$.
- [ ] A long enough prompt containing the permutations adds serial depth, because attention reads all of it.
- [ ] Expressivity theorems prove that a trained model will learn to use its chain of thought to track the state.
::: solution
By [[#prop-depth]] the serial depth is $L$ without a chain and $L(m+1)$ with $m$ generated tokens, so the first two are correct. A longer prompt adds parallel computation (more positions in each layer), not serial steps, because input tokens are given rather than computed. Expressivity results ([[#thm-expressivity]]) say that suitable weights exist, not that training finds them.
:::
:::

## A numerical illustration {#sec-code}

The listing simulates the shell game with five cups as a toy model of a fixed-depth network: in one forward pass it can apply at most $h = 4$ moves reliably and otherwise guesses, and each pass errs with probability $\varepsilon = 0.02$. A chain of thought writes the ball's position after every block of $h$ moves. This is an illustration of [[#prop-depth]], not a model of a real Transformer.

```python
import random
rng = random.Random(0)

# A shell game: a ball starts under cup 0 of 5; each move swaps two cups.
def moves(n):
    return [tuple(rng.sample(range(5), 2)) for _ in range(n)]

def trace(ms, start=0):
    """The scratchpad a chain of thought would write: the ball's cup after each move."""
    pos, out = start, []
    for a, b in ms:
        pos = b if pos == a else a if pos == b else pos
        out.append(pos)
    return out

ms = moves(6)
print("moves:", ms)
print("scratchpad:", trace(ms))

# Toy model of a fixed-depth network: in one forward pass it can apply at most h moves
# reliably; beyond that it guesses (1/5). Each pass errs with probability eps.
def direct(ms, h, eps):
    if len(ms) > h:
        return rng.randrange(5)
    true = trace(ms)[-1]
    return true if rng.random() > eps else rng.choice([c for c in range(5) if c != true])

def with_cot(ms, h, eps):
    pos = 0
    for i in range(0, len(ms), h):                 # one written token per h moves
        chunk = ms[i:i + h]
        true = trace(chunk, pos)[-1]
        pos = true if rng.random() > eps else rng.choice([c for c in range(5) if c != true])
    return pos

h, eps, trials = 4, 0.02, 20000
print(" moves  direct  with CoT  CoT tokens  (1-eps)^tokens")
for n in [2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64]:
    d = c = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        ms = moves(n); ans = trace(ms)[-1]
        d += direct(ms, h, eps) == ans
        c += with_cot(ms, h, eps) == ans
    k = -(-n // h)
    print(f"{n:5d}  {d/trials:6.3f}  {c/trials:8.3f}  {k:10d}  {(1-eps)**k:14.3f}")
```

```text
moves: [(3, 4), (0, 2), (4, 3), (3, 2), (3, 2), (4, 1)]
scratchpad: [0, 2, 2, 3, 2, 2]
 moves  direct  with CoT  CoT tokens  (1-eps)^tokens
    2   0.978     0.980           1           0.980
    4   0.979     0.980           1           0.980
    8   0.200     0.960           2           0.960
   16   0.202     0.923           4           0.922
   32   0.200     0.855           8           0.851
   64   0.203     0.731          16           0.724
```

The first lines reproduce [[#ex-shell]]. Within its depth budget, the direct answer is as good as the chain; beyond it, it falls to the guessing rate $0.2$ at once. The chain keeps working but decays slowly, because each of its $\lceil n/h\rceil$ written states is another chance to err; its accuracy is slightly *above* $(1-\varepsilon)^k$ because an erroneous step occasionally puts the ball back where it belongs. A later exercise derives the exact curve.

## From prompting to trained reasoning {#sec-trained}

Chain-of-thought *prompting* asks a model trained for other purposes to produce reasoning. From 2022 onwards, reasoning was increasingly *trained in*.

- **Instruction tuning with rationales.** Chung et al. (2022) found that instruction-tuning mixtures without chain-of-thought data degraded a model's CoT ability, and included nine CoT datasets in the Flan collection to preserve it ([[post-training/instruction-tuning]]).
- **Bootstrapping.** STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) samples rationales, keeps those that reach the correct answer, and fine-tunes on them, repeatedly — a form of reinforcement learning with a checkable reward, analysed in [[reasoning/verifiable-rewards]].
- **Reasoning models.** From September 2024 several developers released models trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to produce long chains of thought before answering. OpenAI's announcement of o1 reported that performance improved with both more reinforcement-learning compute and more thinking time, without disclosing the method; the DeepSeek-R1 report (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) described an open recipe in detail and released the weights. Such models write their reasoning between delimiters (for example `<think>` and `</think>`), and some deployments show users only a summary of it.

For reasoning models, the methods of this chapter change role. Explicit CoT instructions add little, because the model reasons anyway; few-shot demonstrations of reasoning can even hurt, as the DeepSeek-R1 report noted for its model. What carries over is the analysis: why the chain helps ([[#prop-depth]]), why it costs ([[#sec-cost]]), why errors compound ([[#sec-compound]]), and why the answer must be extracted and checked.

## What reasoning costs {#sec-cost}

Every token of a chain of thought is an output token: it takes one forward pass, adds an entry to the key–value cache ([[inference/kv-cache]]) and is billed at the output-token price by API providers. Three quantities follow directly.

::: proposition Cost of a chain of thought {#prop-cost}
For a model with $N$ parameters generating a chain of $m$ tokens and an answer of $a$ tokens after a prompt of $n$ tokens, ignoring the attention terms:

- the generation compute is about $2N(m + a)$ FLOPs (two FLOPs per parameter per token, [[scaling]]);
- at a decoding speed of $v$ tokens per second, the added latency is $m/v$ seconds;
- at a price of $c_{\text{out}}$ per output token, the added cost is $m\,c_{\text{out}}$.

The key–value cache grows linearly in $n + m$, and attention over it adds compute proportional to $n + m$ per token, which matters when $m$ reaches tens of thousands of tokens.
:::

::: proof
A forward pass for one token through a dense model multiplies the token's activations by every weight matrix once, which is one multiply and one add per parameter; $m+a$ generated tokens need $m+a$ passes. The latency and price statements are the definitions of decoding speed and per-token price.
:::

::: example The price of thinking {#ex-cost}
A service answers $10^6$ questions a day. Answering directly takes $30$ output tokens; with a reasoning model, $2{,}000$ thinking tokens plus $30$. Output tokens cost $\$10$ per million and are decoded at $60$ tokens per second. Compare daily cost and per-question latency.
::: solution
Direct: $30\times10^6$ tokens, $\$300$ a day; latency $30/60 = 0.5$ s. With reasoning: $2{,}030\times10^6$ tokens, $\$20{,}300$ a day, and $2{,}030/60\approx34$ s per question. The chain multiplies cost and latency by about $68$. Whether that is worth it depends on how much accuracy it buys on this workload — the trade-off studied quantitatively in [[reasoning/test-time-scaling]] and, from the serving side, in [[inference]].
:::
:::

::: history
Ling et al. (2017) generated rationales for algebra problems; Nye et al. (2021) trained models to use scratchpads; Cobbe et al. (2021) released GSM8K with natural-language solutions. In January 2022 Wei et al. posted *Chain-of-thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models* (NeurIPS 2022), and in May 2022 Kojima et al. showed the zero-shot trigger *Let's think step by step* (NeurIPS 2022). Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022), least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al., 2022), program-aided models (Gao et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022) and self-ask (Press et al., 2022) followed within months; most appeared at conferences in 2023. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) trained on the model's own successful rationales. Theoretical analyses of chain of thought as added computation appeared in 2023 and 2024 (Feng et al.; Merrill and Sabharwal; Li et al.). OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) made long, trained reasoning the default way of solving hard problems.
:::

::: summary
- A **chain of thought** is generated text $z$ between the input and the answer, $p_\theta(z,y\mid x) = p_\theta(z\mid x)p_\theta(y\mid x,z)$; it can be elicited with demonstrations (few-shot), an instruction (zero-shot) or by training.
- Wei et al. (2022) raised PaLM 540B on GSM8K from $17.9\%$ to $56.9\%$; the gains depended on model scale and on the reasoning coming before the answer. Kojima et al. (2022) obtained much of the gain with *Let's think step by step*.
- Generating $m$ tokens multiplies the **serial depth** of an $L$-layer Transformer from $L$ to $L(m+1)$; expressivity theorems show that, if $\mathsf{TC}^0\ne\mathsf{NC}^1$, chains of thought let Transformers solve problems they cannot solve in one pass — results about what weights can compute, not what training finds.
- Decomposition (least-to-most, self-ask) makes the steps explicit and supports easy-to-hard generalisation; **program-aided** reasoning hands arithmetic to an interpreter.
- Errors compound: $s$ steps of reliability $q$ give $q^s$, motivating sampling, verification and checking.
- Chains cost about $2N$ FLOPs, one decoding step and one output-token price per token; reasoning models write thousands of them.
:::

::: exercise The bill for a benchmark {level=1 check="40"}
A reasoning model writes on average $400$ chain-of-thought tokens per question (ignore the answer and the prompt). Output tokens cost $\$10$ per million. How many dollars does it cost to run a benchmark of $10{,}000$ questions once?
::: solution
$400\times10{,}000 = 4\times10^6$ tokens, and $4\times10^6\times\$10/10^6 = \$40$.

::: exercise Retrying checked steps {level=2 check="22.22"}
Suppose each step of a $20$-step solution can be checked exactly (for instance by an interpreter), and a failed step is regenerated until it passes. Each attempt at a step succeeds with probability $0.9$, independently. What is the expected total number of step attempts? Give two decimal places.
::: solution
The number of attempts for one step is geometric with success probability $0.9$, with mean $1/0.9$. By linearity of expectation the total is $20/0.9 = 22.22$. Compare with the probability $0.9^{20}\approx0.12$ that an unchecked chain is right: checking turns exponential decay into a linear overhead, which is the motivation for process rewards ([[reasoning/process-outcome-rewards]]) and step-level search ([[reasoning/search]]).
:::

::: exercise Few-shot or zero-shot? {level=2}
You must solve $5{,}000$ customer questions about invoice arithmetic with a general instruction-tuned model that does not reason by default. Describe an experiment to choose between direct answering, zero-shot CoT and few-shot CoT, including what you measure and how you extract answers.
::: solution
Take a labelled sample of a few hundred representative questions (held out from those used to write demonstrations). Fix an answer format — for example "Final answer: <amount>" — and a parser that extracts the last amount in that format, and count unparseable outputs as wrong. Run the three prompts (with a fixed decoding setting, e.g. greedy) and measure accuracy, the rate of parse failures, the mean number of output tokens and latency. Compare accuracies with a paired test, since the same questions are used (as in [[prompting-rag/prompt-design#sec-testing]]). Choose the cheapest prompt whose accuracy is not significantly worse than the best; if few-shot wins, check that its gain is not specific to the demonstrations by trying a second set.
:::

::: exercise A decomposition {level=2}
Give a least-to-most decomposition ([[#alg-ltm]]) for: *A tank holds $240$ litres. It is filled at $12$ litres per minute and drained at $4$ litres per minute at the same time, starting empty. After how many minutes is it three-quarters full?* Solve it.
::: solution
Subquestions: $u_1$ "What is the net filling rate?" $u_2$ "How many litres is three-quarters full?" $u_3$ "How many minutes does it take?" Answers: $v_1$ $12 - 4 = 8$ litres per minute; $v_2$ $0.75\times240 = 180$ litres; $v_3$ $180/8 = 22.5$ minutes. Each answer uses only the question and the previous answers.

::: exercise The exact shell-game curve {level=3 check="0.7336"}
In the toy model of the listing, the chain of thought writes $k$ states. Each written state is correct with probability $1-\varepsilon$ given a correct previous state; on an error it is a uniformly random cup among the four that are not the correct next position. Show that the final answer is correct with probability

$$
P_k = \tfrac15 + \tfrac45\big(1 - \tfrac54\varepsilon\big)^k,
$$

and evaluate it for $k = 16$, $\varepsilon = 0.02$ to four decimal places. Compare with the simulation.
::: solution
Let $P_j$ be the probability that the $j$-th written state is correct, $P_0 = 1$. If the previous state is correct (probability $P_{j-1}$), the next is correct with probability $1-\varepsilon$. If the previous state is wrong, the step applied to it gives some cup different from the correct one (the moves are a bijection); with probability $1-\varepsilon$ that wrong cup is written, and with probability $\varepsilon$ a uniformly random cup other than it is written, which is the correct one with probability $1/4$. So

$$
P_j = (1-\varepsilon)P_{j-1} + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}(1 - P_{j-1}) = \tfrac{\varepsilon}{4} + \big(1 - \tfrac54\varepsilon\big)P_{j-1}.
$$

The fixed point is $P^* = \frac{\varepsilon/4}{5\varepsilon/4} = \frac15$, and $P_j - \frac15 = (1-\frac54\varepsilon)(P_{j-1} - \frac15)$, so $P_k = \frac15 + \frac45(1-\frac54\varepsilon)^k$. For $k = 16$, $\varepsilon = 0.02$: $0.975^{16} = 0.6670$, and $P_{16} = 0.2 + 0.8\cdot0.6670 = 0.7336$. The simulation gave $0.731$ with $20{,}000$ trials, within its standard error of about $0.003$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Parity with and without a scratchpad {level=3}
The parity of $n$ bits is $1$ if an odd number of them are $1$. (a) Write a chain of thought for parity that needs only one easy operation per generated token. (b) Parity *is* in $\mathsf{TC}^0$ (a threshold circuit can count), so [[#thm-expressivity]] does not forbid one-pass solutions. Yet trained Transformers are known to struggle to learn parity directly for long inputs. Explain why this does not contradict the theorem, and what it suggests about the gap between expressivity and learnability.
::: solution
(a) Write the running parity: after reading bit $b_i$, emit $s_i = s_{i-1}\oplus b_i$ with $s_0 = 0$; the answer is $s_n$. Each token requires an XOR of two bits — the previous emitted state and the next input bit, which attention can retrieve by position.

(b) The theorem concerns whether weights exist; a one-pass parity network exists but requires finely tuned weights (for example attention that counts ones precisely and a sharp threshold on the count's parity), and the loss landscape gives little gradient signal toward it: changing any single input bit flips the output, so no low-order statistic of the input correlates with the label. With the scratchpad, each target token depends on only two inputs and is easy to learn. Expressivity is necessary for a capability but not sufficient: what gradient descent finds from data is a separate question, and chains of thought can help *learnability* even where they are not needed for expressivity.
:::
:::

::: exercise A scratchpad for addition {level=3}
Write a plain-Python function that, given two non-negative integers as strings, produces a scratchpad for their sum in the style of Nye et al.: one line per digit position from the right, showing the two digits, the incoming carry, the digit written and the outgoing carry, and finally the sum. Run it on $5678 + 9876$.
::: solution
```python
def scratchpad_add(a, b):
    a, b = a[::-1], b[::-1]
    carry, digits, lines = 0, [], []
    for i in range(max(len(a), len(b))):
        x = int(a[i]) if i < len(a) else 0
        y = int(b[i]) if i < len(b) else 0
        s = x + y + carry
        lines.append(f"pos {i}: {x} + {y} + carry {carry} = {s} -> write {s % 10}, carry {s // 10}")
        digits.append(str(s % 10)); carry = s // 10
    if carry:
        digits.append(str(carry)); lines.append(f"final carry {carry}")
    lines.append("answer " + "".join(reversed(digits)))
    return "\n".join(lines)

print(scratchpad_add("5678", "9876"))
```

```text
pos 0: 8 + 6 + carry 0 = 14 -> write 4, carry 1
pos 1: 7 + 7 + carry 1 = 15 -> write 5, carry 1
pos 2: 6 + 8 + carry 1 = 15 -> write 5, carry 1
pos 3: 5 + 9 + carry 1 = 15 -> write 5, carry 1
final carry 1
answer 15554
```

Each line depends only on two input digits and the previous carry, so a model trained on such traces must learn a single-digit operation, not a long-range dependency across all digit positions at once.
:::
