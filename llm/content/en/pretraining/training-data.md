The objectives of [[pretraining/pretraining-objectives]] say *how* a model learns from text; the training data say *what* it learns. A large language model is shaped more by its corpus than by almost any architectural choice: what languages and subjects it covers, how it writes, which facts it repeats and which errors it inherits. Building that corpus is mostly engineering under uncertainty. Trillions of tokens must be collected, cleaned, filtered for quality, stripped of duplicates and checked against evaluation sets, with decisions that are validated by training models rather than by proofs.

This chapter follows a document from the web to a training batch. It describes where pretraining text comes from (web crawls, code, books, papers and curated sources), the heuristic, classifier-based and perplexity-based filters applied to it, and **deduplication**, where we derive the MinHash estimate of Jaccard similarity and the collision probability $1-(1-s^r)^b$ of locality-sensitive hashing and implement both in plain Python. It then surveys public datasets with their sizes, and the problems of benchmark contamination and of licensing, consent and personal information. How the cleaned sources are weighted against one another is the subject of [[pretraining/data-mixtures]].

## Sources of pretraining text {#sec-sources}

Pretraining corpora combine a very large, noisy source with smaller, cleaner ones.

**Web crawls.** Most tokens come from the public web. **Common Crawl**, a non-profit founded in 2007, has released crawls since 2008, roughly monthly in recent years; each snapshot holds a few billion web pages as raw HTML (WARC files) and as extracted plain text (WET files). It is the starting point of most open web corpora. Crawls are broad but messy: navigation menus, cookie notices, spam, machine-generated pages, near-identical copies and boilerplate dominate the raw data.

**Code.** Public source repositories supply programming languages, configuration files and technical prose. Code data are usually filtered by licence and file type and deduplicated aggressively, because forks and vendored libraries repeat the same files many times. The Stack (Kocetkov et al., 2022) and The Stack v2 (Lozhkov et al., 2024) are open code datasets assembled this way, with a procedure for developers to opt out.

**Curated sources.** Encyclopaedias such as Wikipedia, scientific papers (for example arXiv and open-access literature), books (Project Gutenberg's public-domain books are a common source), patents, legal and government documents, question-answering forums and mathematics. They are small compared with the web but are often given more weight than their share of tokens ([[pretraining/data-mixtures]]).

**Synthetic data.** Increasingly, some pretraining text is generated by other models — rewritten web pages, textbook-style explanations, exercises. Its use and risks are discussed in [[pretraining/data-mixtures]].

::: definition Document, corpus and token budget {#def-corpus}
A **document** is a unit of text with metadata (source, URL, date, language, licence). A **corpus** is a multiset of documents; its size is measured in **tokens** under a stated tokeniser, since bytes, words and tokens differ by factors of about $4$ to $5$ for English ([[text-and-language/tokenisation]]). The **token budget** $D$ of a training run is the number of tokens the model processes; it may exceed the corpus size if some data are repeated over several **epochs**.
:::

Token counts in this chapter are quoted as the dataset authors report them, with the tokeniser they used; they are not directly comparable across datasets.

## From HTML to text {#sec-extraction}

The first step turns a web page into clean text. Common Crawl's WET files contain all visible text of a page, including menus and footers, and corpora built from them (C4, for example) inherit that boilerplate. Later pipelines such as RefinedWeb and FineWeb extract text from the raw HTML with tools like `trafilatura`, which try to keep only the main content. Penedo et al. (2024) found that models trained on text extracted from HTML in this way performed better than models trained on the WET text of the same pages.

Next comes **language identification**, usually with a fast character-n-gram classifier such as fastText's language-ID model, keeping documents whose predicted language and confidence pass a threshold. English-focused corpora keep only English; multilingual corpora keep many languages and record the prediction so that languages can later be balanced ([[pretraining/data-mixtures]]).

## Filtering {#sec-filtering}

Most of a crawl is not text one would want a model to imitate. Filters remove it in three broad ways.

### Heuristic filters {#sec-heuristics}

Rule-based filters compute simple statistics of a document and discard documents outside a plausible range. Published rule sets include:

- **C4** (Raffel et al., 2020): keep only lines that end in terminal punctuation and have at least five words; discard pages with fewer than three sentences, pages containing words from a public list of obscene words, pages with the string *lorem ipsum* or a curly brace (to remove code), and lines mentioning JavaScript; keep pages that a language detector classifies as English with probability at least $0.99$.
- **Gopher / MassiveText** (Rae et al., 2021): keep documents of $50$ to $100{,}000$ words with mean word length between $3$ and $10$ characters; discard documents with a ratio of hash or ellipsis symbols to words above $0.1$, with more than $90\%$ of lines starting with a bullet or more than $30\%$ ending with an ellipsis, with fewer than $80\%$ of words containing an alphabetic character, or with fewer than two of a list of common English words (*the, be, to, of, and, that, have, with*); also discard documents with too many repeated lines, paragraphs or $n$-grams.

These rules are cheap and transparent, but blunt: the C4 obscene-word filter, for example, was found to remove disproportionately many documents written in African-American English and documents about LGBTQ+ identities (Dodge et al., 2021).

::: warning "Clean" does not mean "good", and every filter has a bias
A filter that removes spam also removes some legitimate text that happens to look like spam: dialects, informal registers, lists and tables, non-English passages, poetry, code in prose. A filter defined by resemblance to Wikipedia or books prefers the topics, styles and authors those sources over-represent. These effects are invisible in the aggregate token count. Before trusting a filter, look at samples of what it removes as well as what it keeps, and measure its effect on downstream evaluations of the populations and tasks you care about.
:::

### Quality classifiers {#sec-classifiers}

A **quality classifier** is a model trained to separate documents that resemble a chosen reference set from random crawl documents, and its score is used to keep or weight documents.

::: definition Quality classifier {#def-quality-classifier}
Let $R$ be a reference set of documents considered high quality and $U$ a sample of unfiltered documents. A **quality classifier** is a probabilistic classifier $g(\text{doc})\in[0, 1]$ trained to predict membership of $R$ against $U$. A document is kept if $g$ exceeds a threshold, or with a probability that increases with $g$.
:::

GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020) trained a logistic-regression classifier on hashed word features, with WebText, Wikipedia and books as positives and raw Common Crawl as negatives, and kept each document if a draw from a Pareto (Lomax) distribution with shape $\alpha = 9$ exceeded $1 - g$. The randomness keeps some lower-scoring documents for diversity. More recent pipelines label a sample of web pages with a large language model, for example asking it to rate each page's educational value, and train a small classifier to reproduce those ratings on the whole crawl: FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., 2024) used ratings by an open-weight model on a $0$–$5$ scale and kept pages scoring at least $3$, and DCLM (Li et al., 2024) trained a fastText classifier whose positives were instruction-following and question-answering data. Both reported clear gains on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks for models trained on the filtered data.

::: example Pareto-based sampling {#ex-pareto}
In the GPT-3 scheme, a document with classifier score $g$ is kept if $X > 1 - g$, where $X$ has the Lomax distribution with $\Prob(X > x) = (1 + x)^{-\alpha}$ and $\alpha = 9$. Compute the probability of keeping documents with $g = 1$, $0.5$ and $0.1$.
::: solution
$\Prob(\text{keep}) = (1 + 1 - g)^{-9} = (2 - g)^{-9}$. For $g = 1$: $1^{-9} = 1$. For $g = 0.5$: $1.5^{-9} = 0.026$. For $g = 0.1$: $1.9^{-9} = 0.0031$. High-scoring documents are almost always kept, a document the classifier rates as a coin toss survives about one time in $38$, and low-scoring documents survive about one time in $300$, so the filter is soft but steep.
:::
:::

### Perplexity filters {#sec-perplexity-filters}

A **perplexity filter** scores each document by the perplexity of a small language model trained on a reference corpus ([[text-and-language/perplexity]]). Documents that look like the reference have low perplexity. CCNet (Wenzek et al., 2020) trained a $5$-gram model on Wikipedia for each language and split the crawl into *head*, *middle* and *tail* thirds by perplexity; the first LLaMA models (Touvron et al., 2023) used CCNet-processed data. Perplexity filters share the bias of quality classifiers — they prefer text like the reference — and a further one: very repetitive or formulaic text can have low perplexity too, so they are combined with repetition filters.

## Deduplication {#sec-dedup}

Web crawls are full of repetition: the same article syndicated to many sites, boilerplate licence text, product pages generated from templates, mirrors and successive snapshots of the same page. Duplicates waste compute, and they harm models. Lee et al. (2022) found in C4 a $61$-word English sentence repeated more than $60{,}000$ times; after deduplicating, their models emitted memorised training text about ten times less often and reached the same or better accuracy in fewer steps. Repeated text is memorised more easily (Carlini et al., 2023), and duplicates between training and test sets inflate evaluation scores.

::: definition Exact and near-duplicate detection {#def-dedup}
**Exact deduplication** removes documents (or lines, paragraphs or substrings) whose normalised text is identical to another's, typically by comparing cryptographic hashes; **exact substring** deduplication removes any long repeated token sequence, found with a suffix array (Lee et al. used a threshold of $50$ tokens). **Near-duplicate** detection removes documents whose similarity to another exceeds a threshold, usually measured by the Jaccard similarity of their **shingles**: the sets of their word or character $n$-grams.
:::

::: definition Jaccard similarity {#def-jaccard}
The **Jaccard similarity** of two finite sets $A$ and $B$ (not both empty) is

$$
J(A, B) = \frac{\lvert A\cap B\rvert}{\lvert A\cup B\rvert}\in[0, 1].
$$

For documents, $A$ and $B$ are their shingle sets; $J = 1$ for identical shingle sets and $J = 0$ for documents with no $n$-gram in common.
:::

Comparing every pair of documents is impossible at web scale: $10^9$ documents form about $5\times10^{17}$ pairs. **MinHash** (Broder, 1997) compresses each shingle set to a short signature whose agreement estimates the Jaccard similarity, and **locality-sensitive hashing** (LSH) uses the signatures to find likely similar pairs without comparing all of them.

### MinHash {#sec-minhash}

::: definition MinHash signature {#def-minhash}
Let $\mathcal{U}$ be the universe of shingles and $\pi$ a random permutation of $\mathcal{U}$ (in practice a random hash function into a large range, with ties having negligible probability). The **MinHash** of a non-empty set $A\subseteq\mathcal{U}$ is

$$
h_\pi(A) = \min_{a\in A}\pi(a).
$$

A **signature** of length $K$ is $\big(h_{\pi_1}(A), \dots, h_{\pi_K}(A)\big)$ for $K$ independent permutations.
:::

::: theorem MinHash collision probability {#thm-minhash}
For non-empty sets $A$ and $B$ and a uniformly random permutation $\pi$ of $\mathcal{U}$,

$$
\Prob\big(h_\pi(A) = h_\pi(B)\big) = J(A, B).
$$ {#eq-minhash}

Consequently the fraction $\hat J$ of agreeing positions in two signatures of length $K$ is an unbiased estimator of $J(A, B)$ with variance $J(1-J)/K$.
:::

::: proof
Consider the element $m$ of $A\cup B$ that $\pi$ maps to the smallest value. Because $\pi$ is a uniformly random permutation, every element of $A\cup B$ is equally likely to be this one, with probability $1/\lvert A\cup B\rvert$. If $m\in A\cap B$, then $m$ is the minimiser for both sets and $h_\pi(A) = h_\pi(B) = \pi(m)$. If $m$ lies in only one of the sets, say $A$, then $h_\pi(A) = \pi(m)$ while $h_\pi(B) > \pi(m)$, because every element of $B$ is in $A\cup B$ and is mapped above $\pi(m)$; so the MinHashes differ. Hence the MinHashes agree exactly when $m\in A\cap B$, which has probability $\lvert A\cap B\rvert/\lvert A\cup B\rvert$. For $K$ independent permutations the indicators of agreement are independent Bernoulli$(J)$ variables, so their mean $\hat J$ has expectation $J$ and variance $J(1-J)/K$.
:::

With $K = 128$ hashes, the standard deviation of $\hat J$ at $J = 0.8$ is $\sqrt{0.8\cdot0.2/128} = 0.035$, accurate enough to separate near-duplicates from merely related documents, and the signature of any document, however long, is $128$ numbers.

### Locality-sensitive hashing {#sec-lsh}

To avoid comparing all pairs of signatures, LSH splits each signature into $b$ **bands** of $r$ values each ($K = br$) and hashes each band. Two documents become a **candidate pair** if they agree on all $r$ values of at least one band. Only candidate pairs are checked further (or simply merged).

::: proposition Banding collision probability {#prop-banding}
If two documents have Jaccard similarity $s$ and their signatures use $K = br$ independent hash functions in $b$ bands of $r$, the probability that they become a candidate pair is

$$
P(s) = 1 - \big(1 - s^{r}\big)^{b}.
$$ {#eq-banding}

$P$ increases from $0$ to $1$ as $s$ goes from $0$ to $1$, and its steepest rise is near $s^\ast\approx(1/b)^{1/r}$.
:::

::: proof
By [[#thm-minhash]] each of the $r$ positions in a band agrees with probability $s$, independently, so the whole band agrees with probability $s^r$ and differs with probability $1 - s^r$. The $b$ bands use disjoint hash functions and are independent, so all of them differ with probability $(1 - s^r)^b$, and at least one agrees with probability $1 - (1 - s^r)^b$. The function is increasing because $s^r$ is. For the location of the threshold, $P(s) = 1/2$ when $(1 - s^r)^b = 1/2$; for small $s^r$, $(1 - s^r)^b\approx e^{-bs^r}$, so $s^r\approx(\ln2)/b$, which is close to $1/b$, giving $s^\ast\approx(1/b)^{1/r}$ as a rule of thumb.
:::

The banding turns the linear relation [[#eq-minhash]] into an S-curve: pairs well below the threshold are almost never compared, pairs well above it almost always are. Increasing $r$ moves the threshold up and sharpens it; increasing $b$ moves it down.

::: example Choosing bands and rows {#ex-bands}
FineWeb's deduplication used $112$ MinHashes in $b = 14$ bands of $r = 8$. Compute the probability that documents with Jaccard similarity $0.5$, $0.75$ and $0.9$ become candidates, and the approximate threshold $(1/b)^{1/r}$.
::: solution
For $s = 0.75$: $s^8 = 0.1001$, $(1 - 0.1001)^{14} = 0.8999^{14} = 0.2284$, so $P = 0.772$. For $s = 0.5$: $0.5^8 = 0.0039$, $P = 1 - 0.9961^{14} = 0.053$. For $s = 0.9$: $0.9^8 = 0.4305$, $P = 1 - 0.5695^{14} = 0.9996$. The threshold is $(1/14)^{1/8} = 0.719$. Pairs at $75\%$ similarity are caught about three times in four, pairs at $90\%$ essentially always, and pairs at $50\%$ only one time in twenty.
:::
:::

::: example Why not compare all pairs? {#ex-pairs}
A corpus has $N = 2\times10^9$ documents. How many pairs does all-pairs comparison require? If MinHash signatures with $K = 112$ values are hashed into $b = 14$ bands, how many band hashes must be computed and grouped?
::: solution
All pairs: $N(N-1)/2\approx2\times10^{18}$ comparisons — at $10^9$ per second, $63$ years. LSH needs one hash per band per document, $14N = 2.8\times10^{10}$ band keys, which are grouped by sorting or a distributed shuffle; only documents sharing a key are compared, and for a corpus of mostly distinct documents these groups are small. The cost is dominated by computing the $112$ MinHashes over each document's shingles, which is linear in the corpus size.
:::
:::

### MinHash in plain Python {#sec-code}

The listing normalises six short documents, removes exact duplicates by hashing, computes the Jaccard similarity of their character $5$-gram shingles, builds MinHash signatures with $K = 32$ hash functions $h(x) = (ax + c) \bmod P$ on a $61$-bit prime $P$, and runs LSH with $b = 8$ bands of $r = 4$.

```python
import hashlib, random, re
from collections import defaultdict
from itertools import combinations

docs = {
    "a": "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog near the river bank today.",
    "b": "The quick brown fox jumped over the lazy dog near the river bank today!",
    "c": "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog near the river bank today.",
    "d": "A slow green turtle walks under the busy bridge across the old town square.",
    "e": "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy cat near the river bank yesterday.",
    "f": "Click here to subscribe to our newsletter for the latest deals and offers.",
}

def normalise(s):                                   # lower-case, drop punctuation, squash spaces
    return " ".join(re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9 ]", " ", s.lower()).split())

# 1. exact deduplication: hash the normalised text
seen, exact_dups = {}, []
for k, s in docs.items():
    h = hashlib.sha1(normalise(s).encode()).hexdigest()
    if h in seen: exact_dups.append((seen[h], k))
    else: seen[h] = k
print("exact duplicates after normalisation:", exact_dups)

# 2. shingles and true Jaccard similarity
def shingles(s, n=5):                               # character 5-grams of the normalised text
    t = normalise(s)
    return {t[i:i + n] for i in range(len(t) - n + 1)}
S = {k: shingles(s) for k, s in docs.items()}
def jaccard(A, B): return len(A & B) / len(A | B)

# 3. MinHash signatures with K = b * r hash functions h(x) = (a*x + c) mod P
P = (1 << 61) - 1
b, r = 8, 4
K = b * r
rng = random.Random(0)
coeffs = [(rng.randrange(1, P), rng.randrange(0, P)) for _ in range(K)]
def base(x): return int.from_bytes(hashlib.md5(x.encode()).digest()[:8], "little")
def signature(sh):
    xs = [base(x) for x in sh]
    return [min((a * x + c) % P for x in xs) for a, c in coeffs]
sig = {k: signature(v) for k, v in S.items()}

print("pair  Jaccard  MinHash estimate  P(candidate)")
for u, v in combinations(docs, 2):
    J = jaccard(S[u], S[v])
    est = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(sig[u], sig[v])) / K
    if J > 0.05:
        print(f"{u}-{v}   {J:.3f}    {est:.3f}            {1 - (1 - J ** r) ** b:.3f}")

# 4. LSH: documents that agree on all r values of some band become candidate pairs
buckets = defaultdict(set)
for k, s in sig.items():
    for i in range(b):
        buckets[(i, tuple(s[i * r:(i + 1) * r]))].add(k)
cands = sorted({tuple(sorted(p)) for g in buckets.values() for p in combinations(g, 2)})
print("candidate pairs from LSH:", cands)
print("threshold (1/b)^(1/r) =", round((1 / b) ** (1 / r), 3))
```

```text
exact duplicates after normalisation: [('a', 'c')]
pair  Jaccard  MinHash estimate  P(candidate)
a-b   0.841    0.906            0.996
a-c   1.000    1.000            1.000
a-e   0.646    0.625            0.783
b-c   0.841    0.906            0.996
b-e   0.541    0.562            0.512
c-e   0.646    0.625            0.783
candidate pairs from LSH: [('a', 'b'), ('a', 'c'), ('b', 'c')]
threshold (1/b)^(1/r) = 0.595
```

Documents *a* and *c* differ only in capitalisation, so exact hashing after normalisation catches them. Document *b* (*jumped*, *!*) is a near-duplicate with $J = 0.84$; with only $32$ hashes the MinHash estimate $0.906$ is off by about two standard deviations ($\sqrt{0.84\cdot0.16/32} = 0.065$), which is why production systems use more. Document *e* changes two words and has $J = 0.65$, just above the threshold of $0.595$; it would become a candidate with probability $0.78$, and with this seed it happens not to, a false negative of the kind the S-curve predicts. Unrelated documents (*d*, *f*) share almost no shingles and are never compared.

Two practical questions remain. **What to do with a cluster**: candidate pairs are joined into connected components (with union–find) and one document per component is kept. **At what granularity**: documents, paragraphs, lines and substrings all repeat. FineWeb deduplicated each Common Crawl snapshot separately rather than across all snapshots, after finding that global deduplication removed so much that the remaining older data were of lower quality; deduplication interacts with filtering and is itself a tuned hyperparameter, not an operation with one right answer.

::: quiz
Two documents have Jaccard similarity $0.6$. With LSH using $b = 20$ bands of $r = 5$, what is the probability that they become a candidate pair, to two decimal places?
- [ ] $0.08$
- [ ] $0.60$
- [x] $0.80$
- [ ] $0.99$
::: solution
$s^r = 0.6^5 = 0.0778$, $(1 - 0.0778)^{20} = 0.198$, so $P = 0.80$ by [[#prop-banding]]. The threshold $(1/20)^{1/5} = 0.55$ lies just below $0.6$, so the pair is on the rising part of the S-curve.
:::
:::

## Public datasets {#sec-datasets}

Many large models have been trained on undisclosed data, but a series of public datasets documents what a pretraining corpus looks like. Sizes below are those reported by the authors, with their tokenisers.

| dataset | year | contents | reported size |
|---|---|---|---|
| C4 (Raffel et al.) | 2019 | English web text from one Common Crawl snapshot (April 2019), heuristic filters | about $750$ GB; $156$ billion tokens (Dodge et al., 2021) |
| The Pile (Gao et al.) | 2020 | $22$ sources: filtered web, books, arXiv, PubMed, GitHub, Stack Exchange, legal, subtitles … | $825$ GiB; about $300$ billion tokens with the GPT-NeoX tokeniser (as used for Pythia) |
| RefinedWeb (Penedo et al.) | 2023 | web only, from Common Crawl: HTML extraction, heuristics, extensive deduplication | about $5$ trillion tokens; $600$ billion released |
| Dolma (Soldaini et al.) | 2024 | web, code, social media, scientific papers, books, encyclopaedia; built for OLMo | about $3$ trillion tokens |
| FineWeb (Penedo et al.) | 2024 | $96$ Common Crawl snapshots (2013–2024), extraction, filters, per-snapshot MinHash | $15$ trillion GPT-2 tokens; FineWeb-Edu $1.3$ trillion |

The progression shows two trends: corpora grew by two orders of magnitude in five years, and the documentation of how they were built — and ablations showing which steps help — became a research contribution in itself. Datasets are also revised and withdrawn: subsets are removed after complaints about their content or provenance, and versions differ, so a paper's "trained on dataset X" should be read with the version and date. RedPajama (Together Computer, 2023) and DCLM (Li et al., 2024; a filtered baseline of about $4$ trillion tokens) are further open examples.

::: example A filtering funnel {#ex-funnel}
An illustrative pipeline (numbers invented for the exercise, though of the order reported for web corpora) starts with $200$ trillion tokens of extracted crawl text. Language identification keeps $45\%$, heuristic filters keep $60\%$ of that, deduplication keeps $40\%$, and a quality classifier keeps the top $30\%$. How many tokens remain, and what fraction of the original is it?
::: solution
$200\cdot0.45 = 90$; $90\cdot0.6 = 54$; $54\cdot0.4 = 21.6$; $21.6\cdot0.3 = 6.48$ trillion tokens — $3.24\%$ of the start, since $0.45\cdot0.6\cdot0.4\cdot0.3 = 0.0324$. Multiplicative funnels like this explain why even a vast crawl yields a corpus of only a few trillion high-quality tokens, and why the strictness of each step trades quality against the risk of running out of data ([[pretraining/data-mixtures#sec-epochs]]).
:::
:::

## Contamination {#sec-contamination}

Web crawls contain copies of evaluation benchmarks: test questions posted on forums, solutions in repositories, papers that quote examples. A model trained on them may have seen the answers.

::: definition Contamination {#def-contamination}
An evaluation example is **contaminated** if it, its answer, or a close paraphrase appears in the training data. **Contamination** of a benchmark is the fraction of its examples that are contaminated, usually estimated by searching the training corpus for long $n$-gram overlaps with each example.
:::

GPT-3's authors searched for $13$-gram overlaps between their training data and their benchmarks, removed what they found before training where possible, and reported results on "clean" subsets; later reports used $8$- to $50$-token overlap criteria. $n$-gram matching misses paraphrases and translations and flags common phrases, so it gives only an estimate, and the honest practice is to report the method, the measured overlap and results on clean subsets. **Decontamination** removes training documents that overlap known benchmarks; it protects the benchmarks one knows about. The measurement of contamination and its effect on scores is treated in [[evaluation/contamination]].

::: example Counting overlapping n-grams {#ex-ngram-overlap}
A benchmark question has $40$ tokens. How many $13$-grams does it contain? A training document shares a run of $20$ consecutive tokens with it; how many of the question's $13$-grams appear in that document?
::: solution
A sequence of $L$ tokens has $L - n + 1$ $n$-grams: $40 - 13 + 1 = 28$. A shared run of $20$ tokens contains $20 - 13 + 1 = 8$ of them, so $8/28 = 29\%$ of the question's $13$-grams match — enough to flag it under a rule that marks any question with at least one matching $13$-gram.
:::
:::

## Licensing, consent and personal information {#sec-rights}

Pretraining data raise legal and ethical questions on which reasonable people, courts and legislatures disagree, and which are still developing as of 2026. This section states the issues; it does not settle them.

**Copyright and licensing.** Much web text, code and book content is under copyright or a licence with conditions. Whether training a model on such material without permission is lawful — under fair use in the United States, text-and-data-mining exceptions in the European Union, Japan and elsewhere, or other doctrines — is the subject of lawsuits and policy debates in several jurisdictions, with no settled general answer as of 2026. Developers take different approaches: some train only on public-domain or permissively licensed data (Common Pile, Kandpal et al., 2025, is an example), some license data from publishers, and some rely on legal exceptions. Code datasets often filter by licence and, like The Stack, offer opt-out.

**Consent and opt-out.** Website owners can signal preferences to crawlers through `robots.txt`, and some crawlers used for AI training identify themselves so they can be blocked. Longpre et al. (2024) measured a rapid increase in such restrictions on the domains that common corpora draw on. Whether a robots.txt preference made after a crawl applies to earlier snapshots, and whether opt-out rather than opt-in is an adequate standard, are contested. The European Union's AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models to publish a summary of the content used for training, with obligations applying from August 2025.

**Personal information.** Crawls contain names, email addresses, phone numbers and other personal data, and models can reproduce memorised training text: Carlini et al. (2021) extracted verbatim sequences, including personal contact details, from GPT-2. Pipelines therefore remove or mask **personally identifiable information** (PII) with pattern matching and classifiers, deduplicate (which reduces memorisation), and filter sources known to hold sensitive data. PII detection is imperfect in both directions, and privacy law (for example the EU's GDPR) applies differently in different places.

**Harmful content.** Filters for toxic, sexual or hateful content reduce what a model learns to reproduce, but they also remove text needed to recognise and discuss such content, and they carry the biases discussed above. Some teams keep such data but tag it, so that later training can control its effect. The trade-offs here are values decisions as much as technical ones ([[llm-society]]).

::: history
Shannon's 1951 experiments used a book as the corpus; the Brown Corpus (1961) and the British National Corpus (1994) were carefully sampled collections of a million and a hundred million words. Web-scale text arrived with Google's 2006 release of a trillion-word $n$-gram collection, and Common Crawl began releasing crawls in 2008. Broder (1997) introduced MinHash at DEC to find near-duplicate web pages for the AltaVista search engine, and Indyk and Motwani (1998) formalised locality-sensitive hashing. For language models, Radford et al. (2019) built WebText from pages linked from Reddit; Raffel et al. (2019) released C4; Wenzek et al. (2020) built CCNet with perplexity filtering; Gao et al. (2020) assembled the Pile; and Brown et al. (2020) described GPT-3's classifier filter and contamination analysis. Rae et al. (2021) published the Gopher heuristics, Dodge et al. (2021) documented C4 and its biases, and Lee et al. (2022) showed the benefits of deduplication. RefinedWeb (2023), Dolma (2024), FineWeb (2024) and DCLM (2024) made the construction of large web corpora an openly documented, ablated research subject.
:::

::: summary
- Pretraining text comes mostly from **web crawls** (Common Crawl), plus code, books, papers, encyclopaedias and other curated sources; sizes are quoted in tokens with a stated tokeniser.
- Pipelines extract main text from HTML, identify the language, apply **heuristic filters** (length, symbol ratios, repetition, stop words), **quality classifiers** trained against a reference set, and **perplexity filters**; every filter has biases that should be inspected.
- **Deduplication** removes exact copies by hashing and near-duplicates by Jaccard similarity of shingles; it reduces memorisation and improves training efficiency (Lee et al., 2022).
- A **MinHash** agrees between two sets with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity; **LSH** with $b$ bands of $r$ rows makes a pair a candidate with probability $1-(1-s^r)^b$, an S-curve with threshold near $(1/b)^{1/r}$.
- Public corpora grew from C4 ($156$ billion tokens, 2019) to FineWeb ($15$ trillion, 2024), with increasingly documented pipelines.
- **Contamination** with evaluation data must be measured and reported; **copyright, consent and personal information** raise open legal and ethical questions that pipelines address with licensing choices, opt-outs, PII removal and documentation.
:::

::: exercise Jaccard by hand {level=1 check="4/7"}
Document $A$ has word $2$-grams $\{$*the cat*, *cat sat*, *sat on*, *on the*, *the mat*$\}$ and document $B$ has $\{$*the cat*, *cat sat*, *sat on*, *on a*, *a mat*, *the mat*$\}$. Compute $J(A, B)$.
::: solution
The intersection is $\{$*the cat*, *cat sat*, *sat on*, *the mat*$\}$, with $4$ elements. $A$ has $5$ elements and $B$ has $6$, so the union has $5 + 6 - 4 = 7$ and $J = 4/7 = 0.571$.

::: exercise Estimator accuracy {level=2 check="400"}
How many MinHash functions $K$ are needed for the standard deviation of $\hat J$ to be at most $0.02$ when $J = 0.8$? Give the smallest integer.
::: solution
By [[#thm-minhash]], $\sqrt{J(1-J)/K}\le0.02$ requires $K\ge0.16/0.0004 = 400$. With $K = 400$ the standard deviation is exactly $0.02$.

::: solution

::: exercise Tuning the S-curve {level=2 check="286"}
You want near-duplicates at $s\ge0.85$ to be caught with probability at least $0.99$ and pairs at $s\le0.5$ to become candidates with probability at most $0.02$. Try $(r, b) = (8, 16)$, $(10, 12)$ and $(9, 14)$, all with $K = br\le128$, and show that none meets both conditions. Then find, by a short search over integers, the smallest number of hashes $K = br$ that does.
::: hint
$P(s)$ is increasing in $b$ and decreasing in $r$ for fixed $s$; for each $r$, find the smallest $b$ meeting the first condition and test the second.
:::
::: solution
$(8, 16)$: $P(0.85) = 1 - (1 - 0.85^8)^{16} = 0.994$, but $P(0.5) = 0.061$ — too many false positives. $(10, 12)$: $P(0.5) = 0.012$, but $P(0.85) = 0.928$ — too many misses. $(9, 14)$: $P(0.85) = 0.975$, $P(0.5) = 0.027$ — both fail. A search

```python
P = lambda s, r, b: 1 - (1 - s ** r) ** b
print(min((r * b, r, b) for r in range(1, 40) for b in range(1, 400)
          if P(0.85, r, b) >= 0.99 and P(0.5, r, b) <= 0.02))     # (286, 11, 26)
```

finds $r = 11$, $b = 26$, $K = 286$: $P(0.85) = 0.991$ and $P(0.5) = 0.0126$. Increasing $r$ cuts false positives but loses true duplicates; increasing $b$ does the opposite; only more hashes make the curve steeper. In practice false positives are cheap if every candidate pair is verified by computing its exact Jaccard similarity, so a smaller $K$ with a lower threshold plus verification is often preferred.
:::
:::

::: exercise Exact substring deduplication {level=2}
Lee et al. removed every repeated substring of at least $50$ tokens. Explain why document-level hashing misses a boilerplate paragraph that appears in $10^5$ otherwise different documents, why near-duplicate detection on whole documents may miss it too, and what a suffix array provides.
::: solution
Document hashes differ because the documents differ elsewhere, so exact document deduplication sees no duplicate. Their Jaccard similarity is low if the boilerplate is a small part of each document, so MinHash on whole documents does not flag them either. A suffix array of the concatenated corpus sorts all suffixes, so any repeated substring appears as a shared prefix of adjacent suffixes; scanning adjacent pairs finds every substring of length $\ge50$ that occurs more than once, in time roughly linear in corpus size after sorting. The boilerplate can then be cut out of each document while the rest is kept.
:::
:::

::: exercise Contamination counts {level=2 check="8"}
In [[#ex-ngram-overlap]], how many of the question's $13$-grams appear in the training document that shares a $20$-token run with it?
::: solution
A run of $20$ tokens contains $20 - 13 + 1 = 8$ thirteen-grams, all of which are $13$-grams of the question.

::: exercise MinHash is unbiased: a simulation {level=3}
Write a plain-Python simulation that checks [[#thm-minhash]] with true random permutations: take $A = \{0, \dots, 59\}$ and $B = \{20, \dots, 99\}$ in a universe of $100$ elements, draw $20{,}000$ random permutations with `random.shuffle` (seed $0$), and compare the agreement frequency with $J(A, B)$.
::: solution
```python
import random
rng = random.Random(0)
A, B = set(range(60)), set(range(20, 100))
J = len(A & B) / len(A | B)
hits, N = 0, 20000
perm = list(range(100))
for _ in range(N):
    rng.shuffle(perm)                       # perm[x] is the rank of element x
    hits += min(perm[a] for a in A) == min(perm[b] for b in B)
print(f"J = {J:.4f}, agreement frequency = {hits / N:.4f}, sd = {(J * (1 - J) / N) ** 0.5:.4f}")
```

```text
J = 0.4000, agreement frequency = 0.4002, sd = 0.0035
```

$\lvert A\cap B\rvert = 40$ and $\lvert A\cup B\rvert = 100$, so $J = 0.4$; the simulated frequency is well within one standard deviation of it.
:::

::: exercise A heuristic filter {level=3}
Implement in plain Python a function `keep(doc)` with three Gopher-style rules: between $50$ and $100{,}000$ words; mean word length between $3$ and $10$ characters; at least two of the words *the, be, to, of, and, that, have, with*. Apply it to (a) the Dickens passage of [[pretraining/pretraining-objectives#sec-code]], (b) the string `"BUY NOW!!! " * 20`, (c) a $60$-word list of product codes like `"SKU-48213"`. Discuss one kind of legitimate text the rules would wrongly remove.
::: solution
```python
STOP = {"the", "be", "to", "of", "and", "that", "have", "with"}
def keep(doc):
    words = doc.split()
    if not 50 <= len(words) <= 100_000: return False, "length"
    mean = sum(map(len, words)) / len(words)
    if not 3 <= mean <= 10: return False, "mean word length"
    if len(STOP & {w.lower().strip(".,;:!?") for w in words}) < 2: return False, "stop words"
    return True, "ok"

dickens = ("it was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was "
           "the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it "
           "was the season of light, it was the season of darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was "
           "the winter of despair, we had everything before us, we had nothing before us, we were all "
           "going direct to heaven, we were all going direct the other way")
print(keep(dickens), keep("BUY NOW!!! " * 20), keep(" ".join(f"SKU-{48213 + i}" for i in range(60))))
```

```text
(True, 'ok') (False, 'length') (False, 'stop words')
```

The Dickens passage ($85$ words) passes. The spam string has only $40$ words and fails the length rule (repeated to $50$ words or more it would still fail, on stop words); the product list passes length ($60$ words, mean length $9$) but fails the stop-word rule. Legitimate text that fails: short poems or captions (under $50$ words), text in other languages (no English stop words — such rules are language-specific), tables of data, and code. A rule set designed for English prose silently removes all of these.
:::
