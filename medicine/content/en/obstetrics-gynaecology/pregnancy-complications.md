Most complications of pregnancy fall into a small number of groups. Some are caused by the placenta: pre-eclampsia, fetal growth restriction and placental abruption all start with a placenta that implanted poorly. Some are disorders of maternal metabolism unmasked by pregnancy, such as gestational diabetes and intrahepatic cholestasis. Some are mechanical or timing problems: a placenta lying over the cervix, membranes that rupture too early, labour that starts at 26 weeks. Knowing the group helps you predict what else to look for. A woman with pre-eclampsia needs her baby's growth checked; a woman with a small baby needs her blood pressure watched.

These conditions also share a central clinical decision. For most of them the definitive treatment is birth, and the question is when. Birth cures pre-eclampsia but may expose the baby to the risks of prematurity. Waiting gives the baby time but exposes both of them to the progressing disease. Much of this chapter is about how that balance is assessed.

The chapter covers the hypertensive disorders, diabetes in pregnancy, intrahepatic cholestasis, bleeding in the second half of pregnancy, preterm labour and ruptured membranes, fetal growth restriction and multiple pregnancy. Normal physiology and the antenatal schedule are in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]].

## Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy

Hypertensive disorders affect about one in ten pregnancies and remain a leading cause of maternal and perinatal death worldwide. UK practice follows NICE NG133 (*Hypertension in pregnancy*, 2019, updated 2023).

::: definition The hypertensive disorders {#def-hypertension}
Hypertension in pregnancy is a blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg or more. **Severe hypertension** is 160/110 mmHg or more.

- **Chronic hypertension**: hypertension present before pregnancy or diagnosed before 20 weeks.
- **Gestational hypertension**: new hypertension after 20 weeks without the features of pre-eclampsia.
- **Pre-eclampsia**: new hypertension after 20 weeks with one or more of the following new features:
  - proteinuria (urine protein:creatinine ratio of 30 mg/mmol or more, or albumin:creatinine ratio of 8 mg/mmol or more);
  - maternal organ dysfunction: kidney (creatinine 90 µmol/L or more), liver (raised transaminases, with or without right upper quadrant or epigastric pain), neurological (eclampsia, altered mental state, blindness, stroke, clonus, severe headache, persistent visual scotomata), or haematological (platelets below 150 × 10⁹/L, haemolysis or disseminated intravascular coagulation);
  - uteroplacental dysfunction: fetal growth restriction, abnormal umbilical artery Doppler or stillbirth.
- **Superimposed pre-eclampsia**: pre-eclampsia developing in a woman with chronic hypertension.
- **Eclampsia**: a tonic–clonic seizure in a woman with pre-eclampsia.
- **HELLP syndrome**: **H**aemolysis, **E**levated **L**iver enzymes and **L**ow **P**latelets. It is a severe form of pre-eclampsia and can occur with only modestly raised blood pressure.
:::

Proteinuria is no longer required for the diagnosis. A woman with new hypertension at 34 weeks, no proteinuria and a platelet count of 90 × 10⁹/L has pre-eclampsia.

### Why the placenta causes the disease

::: theorem Pre-eclampsia is a placental disease with a maternal syndrome {#thm-two-stage}
Pre-eclampsia develops in two stages. In the first, the placenta implants abnormally and is poorly perfused. In the second, the stressed placenta releases factors into the maternal circulation that damage the endothelium throughout the body. The maternal signs, including hypertension, proteinuria, oedema, liver injury and seizures, all follow from this widespread endothelial dysfunction. The disease resolves only when the placenta is delivered.
:::

::: proof
In a normal pregnancy, the trophoblast invades the maternal spiral arteries in the first half of pregnancy and replaces their muscular walls. The arteries become wide, low-resistance channels that deliver a high flow of blood to the placenta regardless of maternal vascular tone.

In pre-eclampsia this remodelling is incomplete. The spiral arteries keep their muscle, stay narrow and deliver intermittent, low-volume flow. The placenta is exposed to ischaemia and reperfusion, and it responds by releasing anti-angiogenic proteins, chiefly **soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1 (sFlt-1)** and soluble endoglin. sFlt-1 binds and neutralises vascular endothelial growth factor and **placental growth factor (PlGF)**, which the endothelium needs to stay healthy.

The result is a maternal endothelial dysfunction affecting every organ. The vessels constrict, raising blood pressure. The glomerular endothelium swells, causing proteinuria and a fall in filtration. Capillaries leak, causing oedema and, in severe cases, pulmonary oedema. Hepatic sinusoids are damaged, causing raised transaminases and pain from stretching of the liver capsule. Platelets are consumed on damaged endothelium. The brain loses its ability to regulate blood flow, which leads to headache, visual disturbance and seizures. Because the source is the placenta, only delivery of the placenta removes the cause, although the disease can take days to settle and can first appear after birth.

The same reasoning explains two clinical tools. A low maternal PlGF level is a marker of placental dysfunction, and NICE recommends PlGF-based testing between 20 and 36+6 weeks to help rule out pre-eclampsia in women with suspected disease. And aspirin, started early in pregnancy before the second stage develops, reduces the risk of preterm pre-eclampsia.
:::

### Management

Assessment of a woman with hypertension after 20 weeks includes repeated blood pressure measurement, urine protein quantification, blood tests (full blood count, kidney and liver function), a search for symptoms of severe disease, and fetal assessment with ultrasound for growth and umbilical artery Doppler and a CTG.

**Antihypertensive treatment.** NICE recommends treating blood pressure above 140/90 mmHg, aiming for 135/85 mmHg or below. Labetalol is first line; nifedipine is used if labetalol is unsuitable, and methyldopa is a third option. ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers are avoided in pregnancy. Treatment controls blood pressure and prevents severe hypertension and stroke; it does not treat the underlying disease.

**Severe hypertension** of 160/110 mmHg or more is an emergency, because it carries a risk of intracerebral haemorrhage. It needs treatment in hospital with oral or intravenous labetalol, oral nifedipine or intravenous hydralazine, following a local protocol, with close monitoring of mother and fetus.

**Magnesium sulfate** prevents and treats eclamptic seizures. It is given to women with eclampsia and is considered in women with severe pre-eclampsia, particularly when birth is planned within 24 hours. A typical regimen, based on the Collaborative Eclampsia Trial, is a loading dose of 4 g intravenously over 5–10 minutes followed by an infusion of 1 g per hour for 24 hours; recurrent seizures are treated with a further bolus. Local protocols must be followed. Magnesium is cleared by the kidneys, and toxicity causes loss of the patellar reflexes, then respiratory depression and finally cardiac arrest. Women receiving it need hourly checks of reflexes, respiratory rate and urine output, and the antidote, calcium gluconate, must be available.

**Timing of birth.** NICE NG133 recommends that, for pre-eclampsia before 34 weeks, the pregnancy usually continues with close monitoring, and birth is planned if there are maternal or fetal indications: uncontrollable blood pressure, worsening blood tests, neurological features, HELLP syndrome, placental abruption, pulmonary oedema or fetal compromise. Between 34+0 and 36+6 weeks, the decision weighs maternal benefits against the neonatal risks of earlier birth. From 37+0 weeks birth should be initiated within 24–48 hours. Antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium for fetal neuroprotection are given where indicated if birth is planned preterm.

**After birth.** Blood pressure often peaks again 3–6 days after birth, and pre-eclampsia and eclampsia can first appear after delivery. Women need blood pressure checks after discharge, antihypertensives that are compatible with breastfeeding (such as enalapril, nifedipine, amlodipine or labetalol), and advice to return with headache or visual symptoms. Pre-eclampsia also signals a long-term increase in the woman's risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease, so she should be told and her blood pressure reviewed in primary care.

::: example Indigestion at 32 weeks {#ex-hellp}
A 27-year-old woman in her first pregnancy attends at 32 weeks with epigastric pain for two days, which she thought was indigestion. Her blood pressure is 148/96 mmHg. Urine protein:creatinine ratio is 85 mg/mmol. Blood tests: haemoglobin 104 g/L, platelets 78 × 10⁹/L, ALT 142 U/L, lactate dehydrogenase 890 U/L. Ultrasound shows the fetal abdominal circumference on the 5th centile with raised umbilical artery resistance. What is the diagnosis and what should happen next?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** New hypertension after 20 weeks, proteinuria, low platelets, raised transaminases and a raised LDH (suggesting haemolysis) make this **pre-eclampsia with HELLP syndrome**. The fetal findings show uteroplacental dysfunction. Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain is a classic presentation of HELLP and is often mistaken for indigestion. The blood pressure is only moderately raised, which does not make the disease mild.

**Next steps.**
1. Admit to the labour ward and involve the consultant obstetrician and anaesthetist immediately.
2. Control the blood pressure according to protocol.
3. Give magnesium sulfate to prevent eclampsia.
4. Give antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity, since she is under 34 weeks, if there is time.
5. Check clotting, a blood film and group and save; monitor fluid balance carefully, as women with pre-eclampsia are at risk of pulmonary oedema from fluid overload.
6. Plan birth once she is stabilised. HELLP syndrome is an indication for birth whatever the gestation. The mode of birth depends on the cervix, the fetal condition and the speed of deterioration. A low platelet count affects the choice of anaesthesia, as regional anaesthesia may be unsafe.
:::
:::

### Preventing pre-eclampsia

Low-dose aspirin is the only widely used preventive treatment. NICE advises aspirin 75–150 mg daily from 12 weeks until birth for women with one high-risk factor or two or more moderate-risk factors ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]). The best evidence comes from the ASPRE trial (2017), in which women identified as high risk by first-trimester screening were given aspirin 150 mg at night or placebo from 11–14 weeks until 36 weeks. Preterm pre-eclampsia occurred in 1.6% of the aspirin group and 4.3% of the placebo group.

How much a woman gains from a preventive treatment depends on her starting risk. The **number needed to treat (NNT)** is the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction. If the relative risk with treatment is $r$ and the baseline risk is $p$, the absolute risk reduction is $p(1-r)$ and
$$
\text{NNT} = \frac{1}{p\,(1-r)}.
$$ {#eq-nnt}

::: widget plot
f: 100/(x*(1-r))
x: 1, 10
y: 0, 250
sliders: r=0.4:0.2:0.9:0.02
labels: \text{NNT}
caption: Number needed to treat (vertical axis) against baseline risk in per cent (horizontal axis), for a treatment that multiplies risk by r. With r = 0.4, roughly the effect of aspirin on preterm pre-eclampsia in ASPRE, about 37 women at 4.5% baseline risk must be treated to prevent one case, but about 170 at 1% risk. The same relative effect means much less to a low-risk woman, which is why aspirin is targeted at women with risk factors.
:::

::: example The number needed to treat in ASPRE {#ex-nnt}
Using the ASPRE results (preterm pre-eclampsia in 1.6% with aspirin and 4.3% with placebo), calculate the absolute risk reduction and the number needed to treat.
::: solution
The absolute risk reduction is $4.3\% - 1.6\% = 2.7\%$, or 0.027. The NNT is $1/0.027 \approx 37$. About 37 high-risk women need to take aspirin through pregnancy to prevent one case of preterm pre-eclampsia. The relative risk is $1.6/4.3 \approx 0.37$, close to the reported odds ratio of 0.38. Because aspirin is cheap and safe in these doses, this NNT is considered very worthwhile.
:::
:::

## Diabetes in pregnancy

### Gestational diabetes

**Gestational diabetes (GDM)** is hyperglycaemia first recognised in pregnancy that does not meet the criteria for overt diabetes. It arises because the insulin resistance of late pregnancy ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal#thm-new-normal]]) outstrips the woman's capacity to secrete insulin.

::: definition Diagnosing gestational diabetes {#def-gdm}
NICE NG3 (*Diabetes in pregnancy*, 2015, updated 2020) diagnoses GDM on a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 24–28 weeks if the **fasting plasma glucose is 5.6 mmol/L or more** or the **2-hour value is 7.8 mmol/L or more**.

The WHO (2013) and IADPSG criteria, used in many other countries, are lower and include a 1-hour value: fasting 5.1–6.9 mmol/L, 1-hour 10.0 mmol/L or more, or 2-hour 8.5–11.0 mmol/L. Higher values diagnose overt diabetes in pregnancy.
:::

Maternal hyperglycaemia crosses the placenta, while insulin does not. The fetal pancreas responds with hyperinsulinaemia, which drives fat deposition and growth. The consequences are **macrosomia** (with a higher risk of shoulder dystocia and birth injury), polyhydramnios, neonatal hypoglycaemia after the cord is cut, and an increased risk of stillbirth.

Treatment starts with diet and physical activity. NICE glucose targets are fasting below 5.3 mmol/L and 1 hour after meals below 7.8 mmol/L (or 2 hours below 6.4 mmol/L). If the targets are not met within one to two weeks, metformin is offered, and insulin is added or substituted if needed. Insulin is started at diagnosis if the fasting glucose is 7.0 mmol/L or more. Fetal growth is monitored by ultrasound. NICE advises birth no later than 40+6 weeks for women with uncomplicated GDM, earlier if there are complications.

GDM usually resolves after birth, but up to half of women develop type 2 diabetes within about ten years. They should have a fasting glucose or HbA1c after birth (HbA1c from 13 weeks postpartum) and an HbA1c every year.

### Pre-existing diabetes

Women with type 1 or type 2 diabetes face higher risks: congenital anomalies (especially cardiac and neural tube defects), miscarriage, pre-eclampsia, macrosomia and stillbirth. The risk of anomalies rises with the HbA1c around conception. Pre-pregnancy care aims for an HbA1c below 48 mmol/mol if it can be achieved safely, folic acid 5 mg daily, review of medicines (stopping statins and ACE inhibitors) and retinal and renal assessment. During pregnancy, insulin requirements rise steeply in the second and third trimesters. Continuous glucose monitoring is offered to women with type 1 diabetes. See [[endocrine/diabetes]] for the underlying disease.

::: example An OGTT result {#ex-ogtt}
A 33-year-old woman of South Asian family origin has a 75 g OGTT at 26 weeks. Fasting glucose is 5.4 mmol/L and the 2-hour value is 8.1 mmol/L. Does she have gestational diabetes under NICE criteria, and under WHO 2013 criteria?
::: solution
**NICE.** The fasting value (5.4) is below 5.6 mmol/L, but the 2-hour value (8.1) is above 7.8 mmol/L. She has GDM.

**WHO 2013.** The fasting value is at least 5.1 mmol/L, so she has GDM even though the 2-hour value is below 8.5 mmol/L.

Both systems diagnose GDM here, but by different values. The difference matters at the margins: a woman with fasting 5.2 mmol/L and 2-hour 7.0 mmol/L has GDM by WHO criteria but not by NICE criteria. This is why GDM prevalence figures from different countries cannot be compared without knowing the criteria.
:::
:::

## Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy presents with **itching without a rash**, typically on the palms and soles and worse at night, usually in the third trimester. It is diagnosed when itching is accompanied by a raised serum bile acid concentration (19 µmol/L or more under RCOG guidance, 2022), after other causes of liver disease have been excluded. It is more common in women of South Asian and South American family origin. The main concern is stillbirth, and the risk is concentrated in women with very high bile acids: a peak of 100 µmol/L or more carries a clearly increased risk, and planned birth at around 35–36 weeks is considered, while women with lower peaks can usually wait until later in the third trimester. Ursodeoxycholic acid may ease the itch, but the PITCHES trial (2019) showed that it does not reduce adverse perinatal outcomes. The condition resolves after birth, and liver tests should be checked to confirm this.

## Bleeding in the second half of pregnancy

::: definition Antepartum haemorrhage {#def-aph}
**Antepartum haemorrhage (APH)** is bleeding from the genital tract from 24+0 weeks until the birth of the baby. Its main causes are:
- **Placental abruption**: separation of a normally sited placenta before birth.
- **Placenta praevia**: a placenta lying wholly or partly over the internal cervical os. A placenta whose edge is within 20 mm of the os is called **low-lying**.
- **Vasa praevia**: fetal vessels running in the membranes over the cervix, unprotected by placenta or cord.
- Local causes such as cervical ectropion, polyps, infection or, rarely, cervical cancer.
:::

| | Placental abruption | Placenta praevia |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Usually present, often constant | Usually painless |
| Uterus | Tender, may be hard ("woody") | Soft, non-tender |
| Bleeding | May be small or absent (concealed); shock may be out of proportion to visible loss | Visible bleeding, often recurrent, matches the clinical state |
| Fetal lie | Usually normal | Often abnormal or high presenting part |
| Fetal heart | Often abnormal | Usually normal unless the mother is shocked |
| Risk factors | Pre-eclampsia, smoking, cocaine, abdominal trauma, previous abruption | Previous caesarean section, multiple pregnancy, assisted conception, smoking |

Placenta praevia is usually identified on the anomaly scan. Most low placentas move away from the cervix as the lower segment forms, so a low placenta is rescanned at about 32 weeks and, if still low, at about 36 weeks. A woman with a placenta praevia and a previous caesarean section is at risk of **placenta accreta spectrum**, in which the placenta invades the uterine wall and does not separate at birth. This is a cause of massive haemorrhage and needs planned birth in a specialist centre.

::: warning Never examine the cervix digitally before excluding placenta praevia
A digital vaginal examination in a woman with an unknown placental site can provoke catastrophic bleeding from a praevia. Check the anomaly scan report or perform an ultrasound first. A gentle speculum examination is safe and helps to assess the amount and source of bleeding.
:::

::: example Bleeding at 34 weeks {#ex-aph}
A 35-year-old woman in her third pregnancy, with two previous caesarean sections, presents at 34 weeks with a sudden painless loss of about 200 mL of bright red blood. Her pulse is 96 and blood pressure 118/72 mmHg. The uterus is soft and non-tender, the baby is in a transverse lie and the CTG is normal. Her anomaly scan report says "placenta anterior, low". What is the likely diagnosis, and what are the priorities?
::: solution
Painless bleeding with a soft uterus, an abnormal lie and a low anterior placenta point to **placenta praevia**. With two previous caesarean sections and an anterior praevia, **placenta accreta spectrum** is a serious concern.

Priorities:
1. Resuscitation: large-bore intravenous access, full blood count, clotting, crossmatch (not just group and save) and the major haemorrhage protocol available.
2. No digital vaginal examination; a speculum examination and ultrasound to confirm the placental site.
3. Antenatal corticosteroids, since birth before 35 weeks is likely, and anti-D if she is RhD-negative.
4. Senior obstetric, anaesthetic and haematology input, and urgent specialist imaging for accreta if she is stable.
5. If bleeding continues or the mother or fetus is compromised, caesarean birth by a senior team prepared for massive haemorrhage and possible hysterectomy.
:::
:::

## Preterm labour and ruptured membranes

**Preterm birth** (before 37+0 weeks) occurs in about 7–8% of births in England and is the leading cause of death and disability in young children worldwide. About two-thirds follow spontaneous preterm labour or preterm prelabour rupture of membranes (PPROM); the rest are planned for maternal or fetal reasons. Risk factors include a previous preterm birth or late miscarriage, cervical surgery such as large loop excision, multiple pregnancy, uterine anomalies, infection and smoking.

**Prediction and prevention.** Women at high risk are offered transvaginal measurement of cervical length in the second trimester. A short cervix (25 mm or less between 16 and 24 weeks) can be treated with vaginal progesterone or a cervical suture (cerclage), depending on the history.

**Diagnosis of preterm labour.** Painful contractions alone are not enough, because many women with threatened preterm labour do not deliver. NICE NG25 (*Preterm labour and birth*, 2015, updated 2022) recommends using transvaginal cervical length or fetal fibronectin, a protein that leaks into the vagina when the membranes begin to separate, to identify women unlikely to deliver soon. A cervical length above 15 mm or a fibronectin below 50 ng/mL makes preterm labour unlikely.

**Interventions that improve the baby's outcome.**
- **Antenatal corticosteroids** accelerate fetal lung maturation and reduce neonatal death, respiratory distress syndrome and intraventricular haemorrhage. NICE recommends offering them between 24+0 and 33+6 weeks when preterm birth is expected, and considering them at 34+0 to 35+6 weeks. The benefit is greatest when birth occurs between 24 hours and 7 days after the first dose.
- **Magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection** reduces the risk of cerebral palsy. It is offered between 24+0 and 29+6 weeks when birth is expected within 24 hours, and considered between 30+0 and 33+6 weeks.
- **Tocolysis** with nifedipine (or atosiban if nifedipine is unsuitable) can delay birth for long enough to complete steroids or transfer the mother to a unit with appropriate neonatal care. It has not been shown to improve outcomes by itself.
- **Birth in the right place**: transfer to a hospital with a neonatal intensive care unit before birth (in utero transfer) is safer than transfer of the baby afterwards.
- Antibiotics against **group B streptococcus** in labour, and optimal cord management and thermal care at birth ([[paediatrics/newborn]]).

**PPROM.** Rupture of the membranes before 37 weeks and before labour is diagnosed by speculum examination showing pooling of fluid, with a bedside test of vaginal fluid if the diagnosis is unclear. The risks are preterm labour, **chorioamnionitis** (infection of the membranes and amniotic fluid), cord prolapse and, if very early, pulmonary hypoplasia. Women are given erythromycin for 10 days or until labour, steroids, and magnesium if appropriate, and are monitored for infection. If there is no infection or other problem, NICE advises considering expectant management until 37 weeks. Chorioamnionitis is an indication for birth whatever the gestation.

::: example Membranes rupture at 26 weeks {#ex-pprom}
A 30-year-old woman at 26+2 weeks reports a gush of fluid. Speculum examination shows a pool of clear fluid. She is apyrexial, her pulse is 84 and the CTG is reassuring. Outline her management, and say how it changes if, the next day, her temperature is 38.2 °C, her pulse 112, the uterus is tender and the fluid is offensive.
::: solution
**At presentation.** This is PPROM at 26 weeks. She is admitted to a unit with neonatal intensive care (or transferred to one). She is given erythromycin, antenatal corticosteroids, and magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection if birth becomes imminent. Observations, including temperature, pulse and fetal heart, are recorded regularly, and blood tests including C-reactive protein and white cell count are tracked as trends. The neonatal team meets the parents to discuss what birth at this gestation would mean. If she remains well, the plan is expectant management.

**The next day.** Fever, tachycardia, uterine tenderness and offensive fluid indicate **chorioamnionitis**, which can progress to maternal sepsis. She needs sepsis management with blood cultures and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics according to local guidance, and **birth**, because the infected uterus cannot be treated while the pregnancy continues. Labour is usually induced or allowed to proceed; caesarean section is reserved for the usual obstetric indications. The neonatal team must be present at the birth.
:::
:::

## Fetal growth restriction

A baby is **small for gestational age (SGA)** if its estimated weight or abdominal circumference is below the 10th centile. Many SGA babies are constitutionally small and healthy. **Fetal growth restriction (FGR)** means the baby has failed to reach its growth potential, usually because of placental insufficiency. An international consensus (Gordijn and colleagues, 2016) defines early FGR (before 32 weeks) as an abdominal circumference or estimated weight below the 3rd centile, absent end-diastolic flow in the umbilical artery, or a size below the 10th centile combined with abnormal Doppler findings. FGR is the single largest identifiable risk factor for stillbirth.

Ultrasound distinguishes the two. In placental FGR, serial scans show the growth curve crossing centiles downward, amniotic fluid falls, and Doppler studies show increasing resistance in the **umbilical artery**: first a raised pulsatility index, then **absent** and finally **reversed end-diastolic flow**. The fetus redistributes blood to the brain, lowering resistance in the middle cerebral artery. Late changes in the **ductus venosus** waveform signal cardiac decompensation. Timing of birth balances the worsening fetal condition against prematurity, using the Doppler findings and computerised CTG, and follows specialist guidance (for example the RCOG guideline on the SGA fetus).

## Multiple pregnancy

Twins occur in about 1 in 65 births in the UK, more often after assisted conception. The key concept is **chorionicity**, which is determined on ultrasound in the first trimester. **Dichorionic** twins each have their own placenta. **Monochorionic** twins share one placenta, with vascular connections between the two circulations. About 10–15% of monochorionic diamniotic pregnancies develop **twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome**, in which net flow through the connections leaves one twin overloaded with polyhydramnios and the other underperfused with oligohydramnios. It is treated by fetoscopic laser ablation of the connecting vessels. Monochorionic twins therefore have scans every two weeks from 16 weeks. All twin pregnancies carry higher risks of pre-eclampsia, anaemia, GDM, preterm birth and FGR. NICE NG137 (2019) recommends planned birth from 37+0 weeks for uncomplicated dichorionic twins and from 36+0 weeks for uncomplicated monochorionic diamniotic twins.

::: quiz
A woman at 36 weeks with pre-eclampsia is receiving magnesium sulfate. Two hours after the infusion started, her patellar reflexes are absent and her respiratory rate is 14 per minute. Her urine output over the last two hours has been 30 mL. What is the most appropriate immediate action?
- [ ] Increase the infusion rate, because absent reflexes suggest an imminent seizure.
- [x] Stop the infusion, check the magnesium level and assess her urgently; calcium gluconate is given if there is respiratory depression.
- [ ] Continue the infusion and recheck in four hours.
- [ ] Give furosemide to increase urine output and continue the infusion.
::: solution
Loss of the patellar reflexes is the first sign of magnesium toxicity, and oliguria explains why the drug is accumulating: magnesium is excreted by the kidneys. The infusion should be stopped, a serum magnesium level taken and a senior review arranged. Respiratory depression or arrest is treated with calcium gluconate and airway support. Absent reflexes do not predict seizures.
:::
:::

::: history
Eclampsia, the convulsions that complicated a small proportion of pregnancies and killed many of the women affected, was described long before its cause was known. The antenatal clinics that spread in the 1920s and 1930s were set up largely to detect the rising blood pressure and proteinuria that preceded it. In 1995 the Collaborative Eclampsia Trial showed that magnesium sulfate prevented recurrent seizures better than diazepam or phenytoin, and in 2002 the Magpie trial showed that it roughly halved the risk of eclampsia in women with pre-eclampsia. Aspirin prevention ran from the first small trials of the mid-1980s through two decades of discordant results to NICE's 2010 recommendation (CG107) of aspirin 75 mg daily from 12 weeks for women with one high-risk or two moderate-risk factors, later reinforced by the ASPRE trial (2017) and carried into NICE NG133 (2019) at 75–150 mg. For preterm birth, the decisive discovery came from New Zealand: Graham Liggins noticed in 1969 that lambs delivered prematurely after fetal glucocorticoid infusion had unexpectedly aerated lungs, and Liggins and Ross Howie's randomised trial, published in 1972, showed that antenatal corticosteroids reduced respiratory distress and death in preterm human babies. It took more than twenty years, and a meta-analysis by Patricia Crowley in 1990, before steroids were used routinely.
:::

List the signs of magnesium toxicity in the order they usually appear, and explain why women with oliguria are at particular risk.
::: solution
Loss of the deep tendon reflexes (usually tested at the patella) appears first, followed by respiratory depression and then cardiac arrest. ::: exercise Itching at 34 weeks {level=1}
A woman at 34 weeks has severe itching of her palms and soles, worse at night, with no rash. What condition do you suspect, what test confirms it, and what is the main risk?
::: solution
**Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy.** The diagnosis is made by finding a raised serum bile acid concentration (19 µmol/L or more) after excluding other liver disease, such as viral hepatitis, gallstones and pre-eclampsia. The main risk is stillbirth, which is concentrated in women with peak bile acids of 100 µmol/L or more. Ursodeoxycholic acid may help the itching but does not reduce adverse perinatal outcomes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Number needed to treat {level=2 check="round(1/(0.03*(1-0.4)))"}
A woman's risk of preterm pre-eclampsia without aspirin is 3%. If aspirin multiplies the risk by 0.4, what is the number needed to treat? Round to the nearest whole number.
::: hint
Use [[#eq-nnt]] with $p = 0.03$ and $r = 0.4$.
:::
::: solution
The absolute risk reduction is $0.03 \times (1 - 0.4) = 0.018$. The NNT is $1/0.018 \approx 55.6$, so about **56** women at this risk must take aspirin to prevent one case.
:::

::: exercise Abruption or praevia? {level=2}
A woman at 37 weeks who has pre-eclampsia develops sudden severe abdominal pain. She has lost about 100 mL of dark blood vaginally, her pulse is 125 and blood pressure 95/60 mmHg. The uterus is hard and tender, and the CTG shows a fetal heart rate of 80 beats per minute. What is the likely diagnosis, what explains the discrepancy in her observations, and what are the immediate priorities?
::: solution
**Placental abruption.** Pre-eclampsia is a risk factor, and pain, a hard tender uterus and fetal bradycardia are typical. Most of the bleeding is concealed behind the placenta and within the uterine wall, so her shock is much greater than the 100 mL visible loss suggests. Note also that her "normal" blood pressure is low for a woman with pre-eclampsia. Abruption also consumes clotting factors and can cause disseminated intravascular coagulation.

**Priorities.** Call for help and activate the major haemorrhage protocol; secure two large-bore cannulae; send full blood count, clotting including fibrinogen, crossmatch; resuscitate with blood and blood products. With fetal bradycardia and a live baby, an immediate category 1 caesarean birth is usually indicated, with correction of coagulopathy alongside. Anticipate postpartum haemorrhage.
:::
:::

::: exercise Twins {level=2}
At a 12-week scan, twins are found with a single placenta and a thin dividing membrane (a "T-sign"). What is the chorionicity, what complication is specific to this type, and how does it change antenatal care?
::: solution
A single placenta with a T-sign indicates **monochorionic diamniotic** twins (a "lambda sign", where placental tissue extends into the base of the membrane, indicates dichorionic twins). Because the twins share a placenta with vascular connections, they are at risk of **twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome**, as well as selective growth restriction and, if one twin dies, acute injury or death of the other. Care is in a specialist clinic with ultrasound every two weeks from 16 weeks, looking for differences in amniotic fluid and growth, and planned birth from 36+0 weeks if uncomplicated.
:::
:::

::: exercise Doppler interpretation {level=3}
A fetus at 29 weeks has an estimated weight below the 3rd centile. The umbilical artery Doppler shows absent end-diastolic flow; the middle cerebral artery pulsatility index is low; the ductus venosus waveform is normal; the computerised CTG is normal. Explain each finding and the management principles.
::: solution
**Findings.** The estimated weight below the 3rd centile meets the definition of FGR. Absent end-diastolic flow in the umbilical artery means placental resistance is so high that no forward flow occurs in diastole, which reflects loss of a large part of the placental vascular bed. The low middle cerebral artery pulsatility index shows **brain-sparing**: the fetus is redistributing blood to the brain. A normal ductus venosus waveform and normal computerised CTG mean the fetus has not yet decompensated.

**Management.** Care is in a fetal medicine unit with neonatal intensive care. Antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium sulfate are given when birth becomes likely. Surveillance with frequent Doppler and computerised CTG continues, and birth is planned when the ductus venosus or CTG becomes abnormal, or at a gestation set by specialist guidance even if they do not, because absent end-diastolic flow is itself associated with stillbirth. Birth at this stage is usually by caesarean section, since the compromised fetus tolerates labour poorly. The mother is also assessed for pre-eclampsia, which often accompanies placental FGR.
:::
:::

::: exercise A headache after birth {level=3}
A 34-year-old woman had a straightforward vaginal birth five days ago. Her pregnancy was normal. She returns with a severe headache and seeing flashing lights. Her blood pressure is 168/108 mmHg and there is 2+ protein on dipstick. Give a differential diagnosis and the first steps of management.
::: solution
**Differential diagnosis.** **Postpartum pre-eclampsia**, with imminent eclampsia, is the most likely and the most urgent: pre-eclampsia can first present after birth, and blood pressure commonly peaks 3–6 days postpartum. Other important causes of severe postpartum headache include **post-dural-puncture headache** (if she had an epidural; postural and without hypertension), **cerebral venous sinus thrombosis** (pregnancy and the puerperium are prothrombotic), **subarachnoid or intracerebral haemorrhage**, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, and migraine (a diagnosis of exclusion here).

**First steps.** Treat the severe hypertension urgently according to protocol; give magnesium sulfate because of the neurological symptoms; check full blood count, kidney and liver function and urine protein:creatinine ratio; and perform a neurological examination. If the headache is atypical, there are focal signs, or symptoms persist despite treatment, arrange urgent brain imaging. A medical or obstetric senior review is needed now, not in the morning.
:::
