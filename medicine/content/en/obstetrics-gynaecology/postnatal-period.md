The hours and weeks after birth are when most maternal deaths happen. In the first day the danger is bleeding; over the following weeks it is thrombosis, infection, hypertension, heart failure and, towards the end of the first year, suicide. Most of these conditions start with symptoms that are easy to attribute to the ordinary exhaustion of looking after a newborn: tiredness, breathlessness, a headache, low mood, a sore leg. Good postnatal care depends on knowing which symptoms are expected and which are warning signs.

The **postnatal period**, or **puerperium**, is conventionally the six weeks after birth, during which the reproductive organs return to their non-pregnant state. UK reports on maternal deaths also count **late deaths** up to one year after the end of pregnancy, because some of the most important causes, including suicide, occur after six weeks.

This chapter covers normal recovery, postpartum haemorrhage, infection, thromboembolism, perinatal mental health, breastfeeding and contraception after birth. The third stage of labour is described in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/labour-birth]], and postnatal hypertension in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-complications]].

## Normal recovery

**Involution of the uterus.** Immediately after birth the contracted uterus can be felt at about the level of the umbilicus. It shrinks rapidly as muscle cells reduce in size, and by about 10–14 days it can no longer be felt above the symphysis pubis. A uterus that is larger than expected, soft or tender suggests retained tissue or infection.

**Lochia.** The vaginal discharge after birth is red for the first few days, then becomes brownish and then pale, and usually stops by about four to six weeks. Heavy fresh bleeding, passing large clots or an offensive smell are not normal.

**Physiology.** Blood volume and cardiac output return towards non-pregnant values over several weeks. The clotting system stays prothrombotic for about six weeks, and the risk of VTE in the first weeks after birth is higher than at any point in pregnancy. Glucose tolerance usually returns to normal within days, which is why insulin for gestational diabetes is stopped at birth.

**Routine care.** NICE NG194 (*Postnatal care*, 2021) recommends that every woman has an individualised care plan, contacts with a midwife at home or in clinic in the first days, a review of her physical and mental health at each contact, and a review by her GP at six to eight weeks after birth. At each contact the woman should be asked about her mood and should be told the symptoms that need urgent help: heavy bleeding, fever, chest pain or breathlessness, a painful swollen leg, severe headache, and thoughts of self-harm.

## Postpartum haemorrhage

::: definition Postpartum haemorrhage {#def-pph}
**Primary postpartum haemorrhage (PPH)** is blood loss of 500 mL or more from the genital tract within 24 hours of birth. RCOG guidance (2016) divides it into **minor** (500–1000 mL) and **major** (more than 1000 mL), with major PPH further divided into moderate (1001–2000 mL) and severe (more than 2000 mL).

**Secondary PPH** is abnormal or excessive bleeding from the genital tract between 24 hours and 12 weeks after birth.

The 2025 WHO, FIGO and ICM guidelines recommend starting treatment earlier: PPH is diagnosed when measured blood loss reaches 300 mL with any abnormal sign (pulse above 100, shock index above 1, or low blood pressure), or 500 mL regardless of signs.
:::

The shift to earlier diagnosis reflects two observations. Visual estimates of blood loss are inaccurate and usually too low, so loss should be measured, by weighing swabs or using a calibrated drape. And a young, fit woman with a large blood volume can lose a lot of blood before her pulse and blood pressure change; by the time she is hypotensive she may have lost a third of her circulating volume.

### Causes: the four Ts

::: theorem The four Ts of postpartum haemorrhage {#thm-four-ts}
Haemostasis at the placental site depends mainly on **contraction of the myometrium**, which kinks and compresses the spiral arteries that supplied the placenta, and secondarily on clotting. PPH therefore has four groups of causes:
- **Tone**: uterine atony, the commonest cause (about 70% of cases).
- **Trauma**: tears of the perineum, vagina or cervix, uterine rupture, or extension of a caesarean incision.
- **Tissue**: retained placenta, placental fragments or membranes, which prevent effective contraction; also placenta accreta spectrum.
- **Thrombin**: coagulopathy, either pre-existing (such as von Willebrand disease) or acquired (abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, sepsis, pre-eclampsia, dilution from massive transfusion).
:::

::: proof
At term, about 500–700 mL of blood flows through the placental bed every minute. When the placenta separates, the open spiral arteries could exsanguinate the mother within minutes. Their walls were remodelled in early pregnancy into wide, muscle-free channels ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-complications#thm-two-stage]]), so they cannot constrict effectively themselves. Instead, the interlacing fibres of the myometrium act as "living ligatures", tightening around the vessels when the uterus contracts. Anything that prevents contraction (a tired muscle after a long labour or oxytocin infusion, an overdistended uterus from twins or polyhydramnios, retained tissue, or a full bladder) causes bleeding from an intact placental bed. Trauma bleeds despite a well-contracted uterus. Coagulopathy prevents the clots that seal vessels and is both a cause and a consequence of massive haemorrhage, since bleeding consumes clotting factors and especially fibrinogen. This is why a contracted uterus with continued bleeding points to trauma or coagulopathy, and why the uterus is always checked first.
:::

Risk factors include previous PPH, placenta praevia or accreta, multiple pregnancy, macrosomia, polyhydramnios, prolonged or induced labour, operative birth, pre-eclampsia, anaemia and obesity. Many PPHs occur in women with no risk factors, so every unit must be ready to treat it.

### Management

Prevention starts in the third stage: active management with oxytocin roughly halves the risk ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/labour-birth]]). Treating antenatal anaemia means a woman starts with more reserve.

When PPH occurs, the response runs several actions in parallel:

1. **Call for help** and communicate the problem clearly. In a major PPH, the senior midwife, obstetrician, anaesthetist and haematology and blood bank are involved, and the major haemorrhage protocol is activated if needed.
2. **Resuscitate**: airway, breathing and high-flow oxygen; two large-bore intravenous cannulae; blood for full blood count, clotting including fibrinogen, crossmatch and blood gas with lactate; warmed fluids, then blood and blood products as soon as they are needed. Keep the woman warm and monitor pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and urine output.
3. **Measure** the blood loss and keep a running total.
4. **Stop the bleeding** by finding the cause:
   - **Tone**: rub up a contraction by fundal massage, empty the bladder, and give uterotonic drugs in sequence: oxytocin (bolus and infusion), ergometrine (avoided in hypertension, because it causes vasoconstriction), carboprost, a prostaglandin F2α analogue given intramuscularly (avoided in asthma, because it causes bronchospasm), and misoprostol. **Bimanual compression** of the uterus is a holding measure.
   - **Trauma**: examine the genital tract with good light and analgesia, and repair tears.
   - **Tissue**: check the placenta for completeness; remove retained tissue manually in theatre.
   - **Thrombin**: correct coagulopathy guided by laboratory or point-of-care tests, keeping fibrinogen above about 2 g/L in ongoing bleeding.
5. **Tranexamic acid**: the WOMAN trial (2017), in 20,060 women with PPH, showed that 1 g intravenously reduced death from bleeding, with the benefit seen when it was given within 3 hours of birth. It is now part of standard care.
6. **Surgical and radiological measures** if bleeding continues: intrauterine balloon tamponade, compression sutures such as the B-Lynch suture, uterine or internal iliac artery ligation, interventional radiological embolisation, and, if all else fails, hysterectomy. The decision to perform a hysterectomy should be made by a senior obstetrician before the woman is moribund.

The **E-MOTIVE** trial (2023), a cluster-randomised trial in about 210,000 vaginal births in Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa and Tanzania, combined early detection by a calibrated drape with a treatment bundle given at once rather than step by step: uterine massage, oxytocic drugs, tranexamic acid, intravenous fluids, examination and escalation. Severe bleeding, laparotomy or death from bleeding fell by about 60%. The 2025 WHO guidelines recommend this bundle approach.

::: example Bleeding after a long labour {#ex-pph}
A 29-year-old woman gives birth vaginally after a 14-hour labour augmented with oxytocin. Ten minutes after the placenta is delivered, the midwife notices continuing bleeding; the measured loss is 900 mL. The uterus is soft and above the umbilicus. Her pulse is 112 and blood pressure 108/70 mmHg. What is happening and what should be done?
::: solution
**Assessment.** A blood loss of 900 mL is a PPH by every definition, and her tachycardia means she is compensating. A soft, enlarged uterus after a long, oxytocin-augmented labour indicates **atony**.

**Actions, in parallel:**
1. Call for help: senior midwife, obstetrician and anaesthetist.
2. Resuscitate: oxygen, two large-bore cannulae, full blood count, clotting and fibrinogen, crossmatch; start warmed fluids and request blood.
3. Treat the atony: fundal massage to rub up a contraction, catheterise the bladder, oxytocin according to protocol, then ergometrine if she is not hypertensive, carboprost if she does not have asthma, and misoprostol.
4. Give tranexamic acid 1 g intravenously, according to local protocol, since she is within 3 hours of birth.
5. Check the placenta for completeness and examine the genital tract for trauma once the uterus is contracted.
6. Continue measuring blood loss. If bleeding continues despite these measures, she goes to theatre for examination under anaesthesia and, if necessary, balloon tamponade or surgical measures.

After the emergency, she is monitored closely in a high-dependency setting, her haemoglobin and clotting are rechecked, VTE prophylaxis is considered (haemorrhage and transfusion increase VTE risk), and the event is explained to her.
:::
:::

::: example A shock index {#ex-shock-index}
A woman who has just given birth has a heart rate of 120 beats per minute and a systolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg. Calculate her shock index and interpret it.
::: solution
The **shock index** is heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure: $120 / 90 \approx 1.33$. In healthy women after birth it is usually about 0.7–0.9. A value above 1, the threshold used in the WHO 2025 definition, indicates significant blood loss even if the visible loss seems small, and a value this high suggests she may have lost a large proportion of her blood volume. She needs immediate assessment for haemorrhage, including concealed bleeding.
:::
:::

## Infection

**Puerperal sepsis** remains an important cause of maternal death. The commonest sites are the uterus (**endometritis**), the urinary tract, a caesarean wound, the breast and the chest. Invasive **group A streptococcus** (*Streptococcus pyogenes*) is the organism most associated with rapid deterioration and death; it can be acquired from a household contact, such as a child with a sore throat.

Endometritis presents with fever, lower abdominal pain, a tender uterus and offensive or heavy lochia. Risk factors include caesarean section, prolonged rupture of membranes, manual removal of the placenta and retained tissue. It is treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics following local guidance, and an ultrasound may be needed to look for retained products.

Signs of sepsis in a postnatal woman, such as fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypotension, confusion, reduced urine output or a raised lactate, require immediate escalation, blood cultures and intravenous antibiotics within an hour, as in [[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]]. A **maternity early warning score**, a chart that scores physiological observations against pregnancy-specific ranges, helps staff recognise deterioration. Severe pain that seems out of proportion, diarrhoea and vomiting, or a rash may be the first signs of invasive group A streptococcal infection.

::: warning Do not explain away tachycardia after birth
A persistent tachycardia in a postnatal woman should be explained. "She is anxious", "she is in pain" and "she has just had a baby" are diagnoses of exclusion. Tachycardia is often the earliest sign of haemorrhage, sepsis, pulmonary embolism or cardiac disease, and in UK reviews of maternal deaths an unexplained tachycardia recorded hours before collapse is a recurring finding.
:::

## Venous thromboembolism

Thrombosis and thromboembolism were the leading direct cause of maternal death in the UK in 2021–23, according to the MBRRACE-UK report published in 2025. The risk is highest in the first weeks after birth, especially after caesarean section.

Prevention depends on **risk assessment** at booking, on any admission and after birth, following RCOG Green-top Guideline 37a. After birth, women with risk factors are offered LMWH for **10 days**, or for **6 weeks** if they are at high risk (for example a previous VTE, or several risk factors). Early mobilisation and good hydration matter for everyone. LMWH and warfarin are both compatible with breastfeeding.

**Diagnosis.** A deep vein thrombosis typically causes a painful, swollen leg, often the left, because the left common iliac vein is crossed by the right common iliac artery. Pulmonary embolism may cause breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, tachycardia or collapse. D-dimer is not useful in pregnancy or soon after birth because it is raised normally. Suspected DVT is investigated with compression duplex ultrasound; suspected PE with a chest X-ray and then a CT pulmonary angiogram or ventilation–perfusion scan. Treatment with therapeutic LMWH should start while waiting for imaging, unless it is contraindicated.

**Peripartum cardiomyopathy** is heart failure with a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (usually below 45%) that develops towards the end of pregnancy or in the months after birth, with no other identifiable cause. Breathlessness, orthopnoea, ankle swelling and tiredness are easily mistaken for normal postnatal symptoms. It is diagnosed by echocardiography and treated as heart failure, with drugs chosen for compatibility with breastfeeding and with anticoagulation if the ejection fraction is very low. See [[cardiovascular/heart-failure]].

::: example Breathless at three weeks {#ex-breathless}
A 31-year-old woman, three weeks after an emergency caesarean section, tells her health visitor she is breathless walking to the shops and cannot lie flat at night. She has palpitations. Her oxygen saturation is 95% on air, pulse 110 and blood pressure 104/66 mmHg, and there are crackles at both lung bases. What are the main possibilities and how should she be investigated?
::: solution
**Main possibilities.** Orthopnoea and bilateral crackles suggest **heart failure**, and at three weeks postpartum **peripartum cardiomyopathy** must be considered first. **Pulmonary embolism** is the other critical diagnosis, because she is in the highest-risk period after a caesarean section. Other possibilities include pneumonia, anaemia after haemorrhage and, rarely, pre-existing heart disease unmasked by pregnancy. Her symptoms must not be attributed to anxiety or tiredness without investigation.

**Investigations.** Same-day hospital assessment: ECG, chest X-ray, full blood count, kidney function, troponin and natriuretic peptide (BNP or NT-proBNP), and an **echocardiogram**. If there is any suspicion of pulmonary embolism, therapeutic LMWH should be started unless contraindicated, and CT pulmonary angiography arranged. If peripartum cardiomyopathy is confirmed, she needs cardiology care, heart failure treatment compatible with breastfeeding, and counselling about the high risk of recurrence in a future pregnancy.
:::
:::

## Perinatal mental health

Mental illness is common in pregnancy and after birth, and suicide is the leading cause of maternal death between six weeks and one year after birth in the UK. Conditions range from mild and self-limiting to life-threatening.

::: definition Mental health conditions after birth {#def-mental}
- **Baby blues**: tearfulness, irritability and emotional lability starting around day 3–5 after birth, affecting about half of women. It resolves within about two weeks without treatment. Symptoms persisting beyond two weeks suggest depression.
- **Postnatal depression**: a depressive episode in the first year after birth, affecting about 10–15% of women. Symptoms are those of depression, often with guilt about not coping or not loving the baby, and anxiety about the baby's health. Anxiety disorders, obsessive–compulsive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder after a traumatic birth are also common.
- **Postpartum (puerperal) psychosis**: an acute psychotic illness, usually starting within the first two weeks after birth, affecting about 1–2 in 1,000 women. Typical features are rapidly changing mood (mania, depression or mixed states), confusion, delusions and hallucinations, often involving the baby. It is a **psychiatric emergency**.
:::

The strongest risk factor for postpartum psychosis is **bipolar disorder**, or a previous episode of postpartum psychosis: about one in five women with bipolar disorder has a severe relapse after birth. Women with a history of severe mental illness should have a pre-conception or early pregnancy plan made with a perinatal mental health team, covering medication, sleep and early warning signs.

**Recognition.** At each antenatal and postnatal contact, NICE recommends asking about mood, for example with the two **Whooley questions**: "During the past month, have you often been bothered by feeling down, depressed or hopeless?" and "During the past month, have you often been bothered by having little interest or pleasure in doing things?". The **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)** is a ten-item questionnaire widely used to assess symptoms further. Red flags that require urgent specialist assessment include recent significant change in mental state, new thoughts or acts of violent self-harm, and new and persistent expressions of incompetency as a mother or estrangement from the baby.

**Management.** Mild to moderate depression is treated with psychological therapy, with antidepressants for moderate to severe illness; many antidepressants, including sertraline, can be used in breastfeeding. Postpartum psychosis needs urgent admission, ideally to a **mother and baby unit**, where the mother and baby can stay together.

If you are affected by any of the issues in this section, support is available from your GP, midwife or health visitor, from NHS 111, and in the UK from the Samaritans (116 123).

::: example A rapid change in mental state {#ex-psychosis}
A 28-year-old woman with a history of bipolar disorder, who stopped her lithium in early pregnancy, gave birth eight days ago. Her partner says that for two days she has hardly slept, talks very fast, believes the baby has been switched in hospital and seems confused at times. What is the diagnosis and the immediate management?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** A rapid onset of manic symptoms, delusions about the baby and confusion in the first two weeks after birth, in a woman with bipolar disorder who has stopped her mood stabiliser, is **postpartum psychosis** until proved otherwise.

**Immediate management.** This is a psychiatric emergency: same-day assessment by the crisis or perinatal mental health team, a physical examination and blood tests to exclude organic causes such as infection, thyroid disease or a postpartum complication causing delirium, and an assessment of risk to herself and the baby. Admission, usually to a mother and baby unit, is needed. Treatment is with antipsychotic and mood-stabilising medication chosen with attention to breastfeeding, and her partner and family need support and information. With treatment, most women recover fully, but the risk of recurrence after a future birth is high, so a plan for any future pregnancy is essential.
:::
:::

## Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding provides ideal nutrition and protection against infection for the baby, and reduces the mother's risk of breast and ovarian cancer. The WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for six months.

**Physiology.** After the placenta is delivered, the fall in progesterone allows **prolactin** to start full milk production, usually two to four days after birth. Suckling stimulates prolactin release from the anterior pituitary, which sustains production, and **oxytocin** release from the posterior pituitary, which contracts the myoepithelial cells around the alveoli and causes the **let-down reflex**. Oxytocin also contracts the uterus, which is why breastfeeding causes cramping in the first days. Milk supply depends on frequent and effective removal of milk.

**Problems.** Sore or cracked nipples usually reflect poor attachment, which a skilled midwife or feeding specialist can correct. **Mastitis** is inflammation of the breast, often from milk stasis, with a painful, red, hot area and fever; continuing to feed or express from the affected breast helps, and antibiotics are given if symptoms persist or infection is likely. A **breast abscess** presents as a fluctuant, tender lump and needs drainage.

**Drugs and breastfeeding.** Most drugs pass into breast milk in small amounts and are compatible with breastfeeding. A few are not, or need caution, so prescribers check a specialist source such as the UK Drugs in Lactation Advisory Service.

## Contraception after birth

Ovulation can return as early as about **day 21** after birth in women who are not breastfeeding, so contraception is needed from three weeks. Breastfeeding delays ovulation: the **lactational amenorrhoea method** is more than 98% effective if the baby is under six months old, the woman is fully or nearly fully breastfeeding and she has had no periods since birth.

The options include:
- **Progestogen-only methods** (implant, progestogen-only pill, injection) can be started at any time after birth, including while breastfeeding.
- An **intrauterine device or system** can be inserted within 48 hours of birth, or from four weeks after birth.
- **Combined hormonal contraception** is avoided in the first weeks because of the VTE risk: it is not used in the first three weeks after birth, or in the first six weeks if breastfeeding.

A short interval between pregnancies is associated with preterm birth and other adverse outcomes, so contraception is discussed before discharge. Methods are covered in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/contraception-fertility]].

Tearfulness and emotional lability starting around day 3–5 is typical of the baby blues, which affects about half of women and resolves by about two weeks. ::: history
In 1795 Alexander Gordon of Aberdeen argued that puerperal fever was carried from one woman to the next by the doctors and midwives attending them. In 1843 Oliver Wendell Holmes made the same argument in Boston, and in 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis in Vienna showed that requiring examiners to wash their hands in chlorinated lime solution sharply reduced deaths from puerperal fever on his ward. The cause, the streptococcus, was demonstrated by Louis Pasteur in 1879. Mortality from puerperal sepsis fell dramatically only after the introduction of sulphonamides in the 1930s. In 1829 James Blundell reported a successful transfusion of human blood to a woman bleeding after childbirth. The Confidential Enquiries into Maternal Deaths in England and Wales began in 1952 and have continued, now as MBRRACE-UK, as one of the longest-running audits in medicine. Their findings have repeatedly led to changes in practice, including the routine use of thromboprophylaxis, early warning scores and specialist perinatal mental health services.
:::

::: solution
Her blood volume is $55 \times 100 = 5500$ mL. The loss is $1500/5500 \approx 0.273$, so about **27%**. This is approaching the level at which hypotension develops; in a smaller woman, the same absolute loss is more dangerous.

::: exercise Fever after caesarean section {level=2}
A woman has a fever of 38.6 °C on the third day after an emergency caesarean section for prolonged labour. List the possible sources of infection and the examination and investigations you would do.
::: solution
**Sources**: endometritis (most likely after caesarean for prolonged labour), wound infection, urinary tract infection (after catheterisation), chest infection (after anaesthesia), mastitis, infected haematoma, and VTE (which can cause fever). Invasive group A streptococcal infection must be considered if she is unwell.

**Examination**: full observations with an early warning score; chest; breasts; abdomen and wound; uterus for size and tenderness; lochia; calves.

**Investigations**: blood cultures, full blood count, C-reactive protein, lactate, kidney function; urine culture; wound and high vaginal swabs; sputum if relevant; ultrasound for retained products or a collection if indicated. If she meets sepsis criteria, intravenous antibiotics following local guidance should start within an hour.
:::

::: exercise Planning for a woman with bipolar disorder {level=2}
A woman with bipolar disorder, stable on medication, is 16 weeks pregnant. What should her perinatal mental health plan include?
::: solution
Referral to a specialist perinatal mental health team; a review of her medication, weighing the risks of each drug in pregnancy and breastfeeding against the high risk of relapse if she stops (valproate is avoided); a written plan, shared with the woman, her partner, midwife, obstetrician, GP and health visitor, covering early warning signs, who to contact, and what to do if she becomes unwell; measures to protect sleep after birth; a longer postnatal stay or early review; and arrangements for close monitoring in the first weeks, when the risk of postpartum psychosis is highest. Her own wishes about feeding and medication should be recorded.
:::

::: exercise A massive haemorrhage {level=3}
During a caesarean section for placenta praevia, a woman loses 3000 mL. The uterus is contracted, but bleeding continues from the placental bed. Results: haemoglobin 68 g/L, platelets 72 × 10⁹/L, fibrinogen 1.2 g/L, prothrombin time prolonged. Interpret the results and describe the management.
::: solution
**Interpretation.** The uterus is contracted, so the bleeding is from the lower-segment placental bed (which contracts poorly, particularly with a praevia or accreta) and from **coagulopathy**: a fibrinogen of 1.2 g/L, low platelets and a prolonged prothrombin time reflect consumption and dilution. Low fibrinogen is a strong predictor of progression to severe PPH.

**Management.** Activate the major haemorrhage protocol. Transfuse red cells, and give fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate to raise the fibrinogen above about 2 g/L, platelets, and fresh frozen plasma, guided by laboratory or point-of-care testing and haematology advice. Give tranexamic acid if not already given. Keep her warm and correct calcium and acidosis. Surgically, apply measures to the placental bed (sutures, balloon tamponade), then consider uterine artery ligation, interventional radiology if available and she is stable, and hysterectomy if bleeding continues. Senior obstetricians, anaesthetists and haematologists should all be involved. Afterwards she needs critical care, VTE prophylaxis once bleeding is controlled, and a debrief.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a symptom list {level=3}
A woman telephones the maternity triage line ten days after birth. Which of the following require same-day assessment, and why? (a) lochia that has changed from red to brown; (b) a severe headache unlike her usual headaches; (c) pain and swelling in the left calf; (d) feeling tired; (e) a red, painful area on one breast with a temperature of 38.5 °C; (f) thoughts that her baby would be better off without her.
::: solution
**Same day**: (b) a new severe headache may indicate postpartum pre-eclampsia, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis or intracranial haemorrhage; (c) suspected DVT needs assessment and probably imaging and anticoagulation; (e) mastitis with fever needs assessment for sepsis and possibly antibiotics; (f) thoughts that the baby would be better off without her suggest severe depression with possible suicidal thinking and need urgent mental health assessment, handled with compassion.

**Not urgent by themselves**: (a) a change from red to brown lochia is normal; (d) tiredness is expected, although it should be explored if severe or accompanied by breathlessness, palpitations or low mood.
:::
