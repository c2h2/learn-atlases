The gynaecological cancers differ greatly in how they behave and how they can be caught early. Cervical cancer develops slowly through precancerous stages caused by a virus, so it can be prevented by vaccination and by screening. Endometrial cancer usually announces itself early with postmenopausal bleeding, so the key is to investigate that symptom promptly. Ovarian cancer usually causes vague symptoms until it has spread, and no screening test has been shown to reduce deaths, so the focus is on recognising symptoms and identifying women with inherited high risk. Vulval cancer is rare and occurs mainly in older women, often in skin affected by a chronic condition.

This chapter covers each cancer in turn: how it arises, how it presents, how it is detected, and the principles of treatment. It also uses each one to illustrate a general idea: what screening can and cannot do, how a test for a symptom works as a rule-out test, and how risk scores combine information. The general principles of cancer biology are in [[pathology/cancer-biology]] and of screening programmes in [[public-health/screening]]. UK practice follows NICE NG12 (*Suspected cancer: recognition and referral*, first published 2015 and updated since) and the NHS screening programmes.

## Cervical cancer

### HPV and the natural history

Almost all cervical cancers are caused by persistent infection with high-risk types of **human papillomavirus (HPV)**. HPV is sexually transmitted and very common; most infections are cleared by the immune system within two years. In a minority, infection with a high-risk type, especially HPV 16 and 18, which together cause about 70% of cervical cancers, persists. The viral oncoproteins **E6** and **E7** bind and inactivate the host tumour suppressor proteins **p53** and **retinoblastoma protein (pRb)**, allowing uncontrolled cell division and accumulation of genetic damage.

The process starts at the **transformation zone**, where the columnar epithelium of the endocervix meets the squamous epithelium of the ectocervix and undergoes metaplasia. The changes pass through precancerous stages over years, which is what makes screening effective.

::: definition Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia {#def-cin}
**Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN)** is a precancerous change in the squamous epithelium of the cervix, graded by how much of the epithelium's thickness contains abnormal cells:
- **CIN 1**: the lower third. Usually reflects transient HPV infection and regresses; managed by surveillance.
- **CIN 2**: up to two-thirds. Often treated, although observation is an option for some younger women.
- **CIN 3**: more than two-thirds or the full thickness (carcinoma in situ). Treated, because a substantial proportion progress to invasive cancer over years if untreated.

Glandular precancer (**cervical glandular intraepithelial neoplasia**, CGIN) is less common and harder to detect by cytology.
:::

### Prevention

**HPV vaccination** prevents infection with the vaccine types. In the UK, it has been offered to girls at 12–13 years since 2008, to boys since 2019, and, since 2023, as a single dose. The effect has been dramatic: a study in England published in 2021 found that cervical cancer rates were about 87% lower in women offered the vaccine at age 12–13 than in unvaccinated cohorts. The WHO's global strategy (2020) aims for elimination of cervical cancer as a public health problem, defined as fewer than 4 cases per 100,000 women a year.

**Cervical screening** in England invites women and people with a cervix aged 25 to 64. The sample is tested first for high-risk HPV (**HPV primary screening**), and only HPV-positive samples are examined by cytology. Since July 2025, people aged 25 to 49 who test HPV-negative are recalled after five years instead of three, the interval already used for those aged 50 to 64 and in Scotland and Wales. The pathway is:

- **HPV negative**: return to routine recall.
- **HPV positive, cytology abnormal**: referral to **colposcopy**.
- **HPV positive, cytology normal**: repeat test in 12 months. If HPV is still positive at 12 months with normal cytology, a further repeat at 24 months; if still positive at 24 months, colposcopy.

At **colposcopy**, the cervix is examined under magnification after applying acetic acid, which turns abnormal epithelium white, and iodine. Abnormal areas are biopsied. CIN 2 and CIN 3 are usually treated by **large loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ)**, which removes the abnormal area with an electrical wire loop, usually under local anaesthetic. Deep or repeated excisions slightly increase the risk of preterm birth in a later pregnancy, which is one reason for avoiding overtreatment.

::: example Following an HPV-positive result {#ex-screening}
A 32-year-old woman's cervical screening sample is HPV positive with normal cytology. She is worried that she has cancer and asks whether her partner has been unfaithful. How would you explain the result and the next steps?
::: solution
**Meaning.** HPV is very common, and most people who have it clear it within one to two years. A positive test with normal cytology means no abnormal cells were seen. It does not mean she has cancer or is likely to develop it soon. HPV can remain undetectable for years after infection, so a positive result does not show when or from whom it was acquired, and it does not imply infidelity.

**Next steps.** Under the England pathway, she will be invited for a repeat test in 12 months to see whether her body has cleared the virus. If HPV is still present at 12 months with normal cytology, she will be retested at 24 months, and if still positive then, or if cytology becomes abnormal at any point, she will be referred for colposcopy. Stopping smoking helps clearance of HPV. She should be told to see her GP promptly if she develops bleeding after intercourse or between periods, which should not wait for the next screening test.
:::
:::

### Presentation and treatment

Cervical cancer is most common in women aged 30 to 45. Most are squamous cell carcinomas; about a fifth are adenocarcinomas. Early cancer may be found only by screening. Symptoms include **postcoital bleeding**, **intermenstrual bleeding**, postmenopausal bleeding and an offensive discharge; advanced disease causes pelvic pain, leg swelling and kidney failure from ureteric obstruction. A woman with symptoms needs examination of the cervix and, if it looks abnormal, referral on the suspected cancer pathway; a cervical screening test is not a diagnostic test and should not be used to investigate symptoms.

Staging follows the **FIGO 2018** system, which uses clinical examination, imaging (usually MRI) and pathology. Stage I is confined to the cervix; stage II extends beyond the uterus but not to the pelvic side wall or lower third of the vagina; stage III involves the lower vagina or pelvic wall, causes hydronephrosis, or involves pelvic or para-aortic lymph nodes; stage IV involves the bladder or rectal mucosa or distant sites.

Treatment depends on the stage. Very early cancers can be treated by cone biopsy, and women who want to preserve fertility may have a **radical trachelectomy** (removal of the cervix with the uterus preserved). Other early cancers are treated by radical hysterectomy with pelvic lymph node assessment. Locally advanced cancers are treated with **chemoradiotherapy** (external beam radiotherapy with cisplatin) followed by brachytherapy.

::: example Bleeding after intercourse {#ex-pcb}
A 38-year-old woman has had bleeding after intercourse for three months. Her last cervical screening test, four years ago, was HPV negative. She asks whether she can simply have another screening test. How should she be assessed?
::: solution
**Not a screening test.** Screening is for people without symptoms. A woman with symptoms needs a diagnostic assessment, and a previous negative screen, or a new one, does not exclude cancer: a small proportion of cancers, particularly adenocarcinomas, are missed by screening.

**Assessment.** A pregnancy test if relevant; a speculum examination to inspect the cervix; swabs for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, since cervicitis is a common cause of postcoital bleeding; and a bimanual examination. Common benign findings include a cervical **ectropion** (columnar epithelium on the ectocervix, which bleeds easily) or a polyp.

**Referral.** If the cervix looks suspicious of cancer, NICE recommends referral on a suspected cancer pathway. If the cervix looks normal but bleeding persists, she should be referred for colposcopy. A visible ectropion should not be assumed to be the cause without these steps, because an early cancer can look similar.
:::
:::

## Endometrial cancer

Endometrial cancer is the commonest gynaecological cancer in the UK, and its incidence has risen with increasing obesity. It occurs mainly after the menopause, with a peak in women in their sixties.

**Unopposed oestrogen** is the central risk factor. Oestrogen stimulates endometrial proliferation, and progesterone, produced after ovulation, stops proliferation and transforms the endometrium so that it is shed. Any situation with oestrogen but little progesterone increases risk:
- **obesity**, because adipose tissue converts androgens to oestrogen through aromatase; this is the most important modifiable risk factor;
- **anovulation**, as in PCOS;
- **oestrogen-only HRT** in a woman with a uterus;
- **tamoxifen**, which acts as an oestrogen agonist on the endometrium;
- early menarche, late menopause and nulliparity.

Type 2 diabetes is also a risk factor. **Lynch syndrome**, an inherited defect in DNA mismatch repair genes, gives a high lifetime risk of endometrial and colorectal cancer. Combined hormonal contraception, the LNG-IUS and pregnancy are protective.

**Endometrioid adenocarcinoma**, the commonest type, usually develops through endometrial hyperplasia and is often low grade with a good prognosis. Serous and clear cell carcinomas are less common, unrelated to oestrogen, and more aggressive. Since 2023, FIGO staging includes a **molecular classification** that groups tumours by their genetics: **POLE**-mutated (excellent prognosis), **mismatch repair deficient**, **p53 abnormal** (poor prognosis) and **no specific molecular profile**. NICE recommends testing all endometrial cancers for mismatch repair deficiency, which also identifies women who may have Lynch syndrome.

### Postmenopausal bleeding

::: definition Postmenopausal bleeding {#def-pmb}
**Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB)** is vaginal bleeding occurring 12 months or more after the last menstrual period. About 1 in 10 women with PMB has endometrial cancer, so PMB always needs investigation. NICE NG12 recommends referral on a suspected cancer pathway for women aged 55 or over with PMB, and consideration of referral for women under 55.
:::

The first test is **transvaginal ultrasound** to measure the **endometrial thickness**. In a woman not taking HRT, a thin endometrium (less than 4 mm in UK practice) makes cancer unlikely. A thicker endometrium, or a persistent bleed despite a thin endometrium, leads to tissue sampling: an outpatient pipelle biopsy or hysteroscopy with directed biopsy.

::: theorem A good rule-out test can have a poor positive predictive value {#thm-rule-out}
For a test with high sensitivity, a negative result greatly reduces the probability of disease, even if the specificity is only moderate. A positive result from the same test may still leave most people without the disease. Such a test is used to **rule out** disease and to select who needs a more definitive test.
:::

::: proof
The negative predictive value is $\text{NPV} = \dfrac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN} + \text{FN}}$. When sensitivity is high, false negatives (FN) are few, so the NPV is close to 1 regardless of the specificity. The positive predictive value is $\text{PPV} = \dfrac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}$. When the prevalence is modest and the specificity moderate, false positives (FP) from the large disease-free group outnumber true positives, and the PPV is low. Endometrial thickness in PMB behaves exactly like this: many benign conditions, such as polyps and fibroids, thicken the endometrium, but almost all cancers thicken it. The figure and [[#ex-pmb]] show the numbers.
:::

::: example Investigating postmenopausal bleeding {#ex-pmb}
Using the values in the figure, imagine 1,000 women with PMB. How many have endometrial cancer, how many have an endometrial thickness of 4 mm or more, and what are the positive and negative predictive values?
::: solution
With a prevalence of 9%, **90** women have cancer and 910 do not.

Of the 90 with cancer, $90 \times 0.95 = 85.5$ have a thickened endometrium (true positives) and 4.5 do not (false negatives).

Of the 910 without cancer, $910 \times 0.55 = 500.5$ have a thin endometrium (true negatives) and $910 \times 0.45 = 409.5$ have a thickened one (false positives).

Positive results: $85.5 + 409.5 = 495$. **PPV** $= 85.5/495 \approx 17\%$.

Negative results: $500.5 + 4.5 = 505$. **NPV** $= 500.5/505 \approx 99.1\%$.

So about half of the women can be reassured by a scan alone, with a residual risk of under 1%, and the other half go on to biopsy, of whom about 1 in 6 has cancer. A woman with a thin endometrium who continues to bleed still needs further investigation, because the test is not perfect.
:::
:::

### Treatment

Most endometrial cancers are diagnosed at stage I and treated by **total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy**, usually laparoscopically, with lymph node assessment (increasingly by sentinel node biopsy) depending on the risk. Adjuvant radiotherapy (often vaginal brachytherapy) or chemotherapy is given according to stage, grade and molecular class. Advanced or recurrent disease may be treated with chemotherapy, hormonal therapy (progestogens) and, for mismatch repair-deficient tumours, immunotherapy. Women with **atypical endometrial hyperplasia**, a precursor with a substantial risk of coexisting or future cancer, are usually offered hysterectomy; a progestogen such as the LNG-IUS with close biopsy surveillance is an alternative for those who wish to preserve fertility or are unfit for surgery.

## Ovarian cancer

About 90% of ovarian cancers are **epithelial**. The commonest and most lethal type, **high-grade serous carcinoma**, often starts in the distal fallopian tube as serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma, which then spreads to the ovary and peritoneum. This discovery explains why removing the tubes reduces risk. Other types include endometrioid and clear cell carcinomas (associated with endometriosis), mucinous tumours, borderline tumours, and, in young women, germ cell and sex cord-stromal tumours.

**Risk factors** are age, a family history, and especially inherited mutations: women with a **BRCA1** mutation have a lifetime risk of ovarian cancer of about 40%, and with **BRCA2** about 15–20%; Lynch syndrome also increases the risk. Factors that reduce ovulation or block the tubes are protective: combined hormonal contraception, pregnancy, breastfeeding, tubal ligation and salpingectomy.

**Presentation.** Because the pelvis is large and the peritoneal cavity accommodates tumour spread, most women present at an advanced stage, with symptoms that are common and nonspecific. NICE (CG122, 2011) advises that ovarian cancer should be considered in women, especially those aged 50 or over, with persistent or frequent symptoms (particularly more than 12 times a month) of **abdominal distension or bloating**, **early satiety or loss of appetite**, **pelvic or abdominal pain**, or **urinary urgency or frequency**. New symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome in a woman over 50 should also raise the question. Ascites or a pelvic or abdominal mass needs urgent referral.

**Investigation.** In primary care, a serum **CA-125** is measured; if it is 35 IU/mL or more, an ultrasound of the abdomen and pelvis follows. In secondary care, the **risk of malignancy index (RMI)** combines the ultrasound findings, menopausal status and CA-125 to decide who should be managed by a specialist gynaecological oncology team.

::: definition The risk of malignancy index {#def-rmi}
$$
\text{RMI} = U \times M \times \text{CA-125},
$$ {#eq-rmi}
where CA-125 is the serum level in IU/mL, and:
- $U$ is the **ultrasound score**: one point for each of multilocular cyst, solid areas, metastases, ascites and bilateral lesions; $U = 0$ for no features, $U = 1$ for one feature, and $U = 3$ for two to five features.
- $M$ is the **menopausal score**: $M = 1$ for premenopausal and $M = 3$ for postmenopausal women.

NICE CG122 recommends referral to a specialist multidisciplinary team for women with an RMI of 250 or more.
:::

CA-125 is not specific: it is raised by endometriosis, fibroids, pregnancy, menstruation, pelvic infection, liver disease, and any condition irritating the peritoneum or pleura. This is why it is used in combination with ultrasound and menopausal status, and why a raised CA-125 in a premenopausal woman means less than in a postmenopausal woman.

::: example Calculating an RMI {#ex-rmi}
A 64-year-old woman has six months of bloating and early satiety. Ultrasound shows bilateral multilocular ovarian masses with solid areas and a small amount of ascites. CA-125 is 180 IU/mL. Calculate her RMI and state what should happen next.
::: solution
**Ultrasound score.** She has multilocular cysts, solid areas, ascites and bilateral lesions: four features, so $U = 3$.

**Menopausal score.** She is postmenopausal, so $M = 3$.

**RMI** $= 3 \times 3 \times 180 = 1620$.

This is far above 250. She should be referred urgently to the gynaecological oncology multidisciplinary team, with CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis for staging. Ascites may be sampled for cytology and a tissue biopsy obtained to confirm the diagnosis before treatment, if primary surgery is not planned.
:::
:::

**Treatment** of advanced epithelial ovarian cancer combines **cytoreductive surgery**, aiming to remove all visible disease, with **platinum-based chemotherapy** (carboplatin with paclitaxel), either after surgery or before and after it (neoadjuvant chemotherapy) when complete removal is not initially possible. Maintenance treatment with **PARP inhibitors**, such as olaparib and niraparib, has improved survival, especially in women with BRCA mutations or other defects in homologous recombination repair. PARP inhibitors exploit **synthetic lethality**: cells that lack BRCA-mediated repair depend on PARP-mediated repair, and blocking both kills them. All women with non-mucinous epithelial ovarian cancer are offered germline genetic testing, because finding a BRCA mutation guides treatment and allows relatives to be tested ([[genetics/cancer-genetics]]).

**Screening.** The UK Collaborative Trial of Ovarian Cancer Screening (UKCTOCS), which randomised over 200,000 postmenopausal women to annual CA-125-based screening, ultrasound screening or no screening, reported in 2021 that screening detected some cancers earlier but did **not reduce deaths** from ovarian cancer. There is therefore no population screening programme. Women at high inherited risk are offered **risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy** once their family is complete: for BRCA1 carriers, usually between 35 and 40, and for BRCA2 carriers between 40 and 45. Surgical menopause has consequences, and HRT is usually offered until the natural age of menopause to women without breast cancer.

::: warning Do not dismiss bloating in older women as irritable bowel syndrome
Irritable bowel syndrome rarely starts for the first time after the age of 50. A woman over 50 with new, persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic pain or urinary frequency should have a CA-125, and an ultrasound if it is raised, before the symptoms are attributed to IBS. A normal CA-125 does not completely exclude ovarian cancer, and symptoms that persist should be reassessed.
:::

## Vulval cancer

Vulval cancer is uncommon and mostly affects women over 60. Most are squamous cell carcinomas, which arise by two routes. In younger women, they are associated with HPV and a precursor called **vulval intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) of usual type**. In older women, more often, they arise without HPV in skin affected by **lichen sclerosus**, a chronic inflammatory skin condition that causes itching and white, thin, fragile skin, through a precursor called differentiated VIN. Treating lichen sclerosus with potent topical corticosteroids controls symptoms and may reduce the risk of cancer, and women with it need long-term follow-up and advice to report any new lump, ulcer or non-healing area.

Vulval cancer presents with a lump, ulcer, bleeding, or persistent itching or pain. Any unexplained vulval lump, ulcer or bleeding should be referred on a suspected cancer pathway and biopsied. Treatment is surgical: wide local excision with assessment of the groin lymph nodes, using sentinel node biopsy for small tumours, and radiotherapy for advanced disease.

::: quiz
A 58-year-old woman has a single episode of light vaginal spotting, two years after her last period. She is not taking HRT and feels well. What is the most appropriate action?
- [ ] Reassure her, because a single light episode is common after the menopause.
- [ ] Arrange a cervical screening test.
- [x] Refer on a suspected cancer pathway for transvaginal ultrasound and, if indicated, endometrial sampling.
- [ ] Check CA-125.
::: solution
Any bleeding 12 months or more after the last period is postmenopausal bleeding and must be investigated, because about 1 in 10 women with PMB has endometrial cancer. NICE recommends a suspected cancer referral for women aged 55 or over. Transvaginal ultrasound measures endometrial thickness, with biopsy if it is thickened or bleeding persists. A cervical screening test is not a diagnostic test for symptoms, and CA-125 is a test for ovarian, not endometrial, cancer.
:::

::: history
Georgios Papanicolaou reported in 1928 that cancer cells could be seen in vaginal smears, and with Herbert Traut he published in 1943 a monograph showing that cervical cytology could detect cancer before symptoms. Organised cytology screening, introduced in British Columbia in the late 1940s and in the UK as a national call-and-recall programme in 1988, greatly reduced cervical cancer incidence and mortality. In the late 1970s and early 1980s Harald zur Hausen proposed that papillomaviruses caused cervical cancer, and his group isolated HPV 16 in 1983 and HPV 18 in 1984 from cervical cancer tissue, work recognised by the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2008. Ian Frazer and Jian Zhou's development of virus-like particles in the early 1990s led to the HPV vaccines licensed from 2006. In ovarian cancer, Mary-Claire King mapped a breast cancer susceptibility gene to chromosome 17 in 1990, and *BRCA1* was cloned in 1994, opening the way to genetic testing, risk-reducing surgery and, in 2014, the first approvals of PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutated ovarian cancer.
:::

## Where this leads

Abnormal bleeding before the menopause is in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/menstrual-pelvic-disorders]]. Inherited cancer syndromes, including BRCA and Lynch syndrome, are in [[genetics/cancer-genetics]]. HRT and the endometrium are in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/menopause]]. Screening as a population intervention is in [[public-health/screening]], and diagnostic test accuracy in [[epidemiology-ebm/diagnostic-tests]].

::: summary
- Persistent high-risk HPV infection causes almost all cervical cancers; E6 and E7 inactivate p53 and pRb. HPV 16 and 18 cause about 70%.
- CIN 1 usually regresses; CIN 2 and 3 are usually treated by LLETZ.
- England uses HPV primary screening for ages 25 to 64; since July 2025, HPV-negative results lead to recall after five years at all ages. HPV-positive with normal cytology is retested at 12 and 24 months; abnormal cytology leads to colposcopy.
- HPV vaccination, now a single dose for adolescents in the UK, has sharply reduced cervical cancer in vaccinated cohorts.
- Endometrial cancer is driven by unopposed oestrogen: obesity, anovulation, oestrogen-only HRT and tamoxifen. Postmenopausal bleeding must always be investigated, first with transvaginal ultrasound.
- An endometrial thickness under 4 mm is a good rule-out test: high NPV despite a low PPV.
- Ovarian cancer presents late with nonspecific symptoms; CA-125 then ultrasound in primary care; RMI $= U \times M \times$ CA-125, with referral at 250 or more.
- Screening the general population for ovarian cancer did not reduce deaths in UKCTOCS; BRCA carriers are offered risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy.
- Vulval cancer arises through HPV-related VIN in younger women and through lichen sclerosus in older women; any persistent vulval lump or ulcer needs biopsy.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise An RMI calculation {level=1 check="270"}
A 60-year-old postmenopausal woman has a unilateral ovarian cyst with solid areas and no other features. Her CA-125 is 90 IU/mL. Calculate her RMI.
::: solution
One ultrasound feature (solid areas), so $U = 1$; postmenopausal, so $M = 3$. RMI $= 1 \times 3 \times 90 = 270$. This exceeds 250, so she should be referred to the specialist multidisciplinary team.
:::

::: exercise CIN grades {level=1}
A colposcopic biopsy shows CIN 1 in a 27-year-old woman. What does this mean, and how is it usually managed?
::: solution
Abnormal cells are confined to the lower third of the epithelium. CIN 1 usually reflects a transient HPV infection and most cases regress without treatment, so it is managed by surveillance (repeat testing) rather than excision. Treating it would expose her to the small risks of LLETZ, including a slightly increased risk of preterm birth, with little benefit.
:::
:::

::: exercise Endometrial cancer risk {level=1}
List five risk factors and three protective factors for endometrial cancer, and explain the common mechanism of most of them.
::: solution
**Risk factors**: obesity, PCOS and anovulation, oestrogen-only HRT in a woman with a uterus, tamoxifen, nulliparity, early menarche and late menopause, type 2 diabetes, Lynch syndrome. **Protective factors**: combined hormonal contraception, the LNG-IUS, pregnancy, combined (oestrogen plus progestogen) HRT compared with oestrogen-only HRT. **Mechanism**: most of these increase or reduce the exposure of the endometrium to oestrogen without the opposing effect of progesterone. Lynch syndrome acts through defective DNA mismatch repair instead.
:::
:::

::: exercise Positive predictive value {level=2 check="round(100*85.5/495)"}
In [[#ex-pmb]], what percentage of women with an endometrial thickness of 4 mm or more have endometrial cancer? Give the nearest whole per cent.
::: solution
PPV $= 85.5/495 \approx 0.173$, so **17%**. Most women with a thickened endometrium have benign causes, such as polyps or atrophy with fluid, which is why tissue sampling, not ultrasound, makes the diagnosis.
:::

::: exercise CA-125 in a premenopausal woman {level=2}
A 34-year-old woman with painful periods has a 5 cm ovarian cyst with "ground-glass" echoes and a CA-125 of 95 IU/mL. Calculate her RMI and explain why a raised CA-125 is less worrying here.
::: solution
A unilocular cyst with "ground-glass" contents has none of the five ultrasound features, so $U = 0$; she is premenopausal, so $M = 1$. Her RMI is $0 \times 1 \times 95 = 0$, and even a single feature ($U = 1$) would give only 95, well below 250. The clinical picture suggests an **endometrioma**: endometriosis commonly raises CA-125, as do menstruation, fibroids and pelvic infection in premenopausal women, so the marker is much less specific before the menopause. Germ cell tumours are a consideration in young women with solid masses, and markers such as AFP and hCG are checked if the imaging suggests one.
:::
:::

::: exercise A BRCA1 carrier {level=2}
A 33-year-old woman has just learned that she carries a pathogenic *BRCA1* variant. She has one child and may want another. What would you discuss about her ovarian cancer risk?
::: solution
Her lifetime ovarian (and tubal) cancer risk is about 40%, rising steeply from her forties, and there is no effective screening. **Risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy** is recommended once her family is complete, usually between 35 and 40 for BRCA1 carriers; it greatly reduces the risk of ovarian and tubal cancer. It causes surgical menopause, so HRT until about 51 is usually recommended if she has not had breast cancer, and bone and cardiovascular health need attention. Taking the combined pill reduces ovarian cancer risk in the meantime. Her breast cancer risk (screening with MRI, risk-reducing mastectomy) and the implications for relatives, who can be offered testing, should be addressed through a clinical genetics service ([[genetics/cancer-genetics]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Why UKCTOCS matters {level=3}
In UKCTOCS, screening detected more cancers at an early stage in the screened groups, yet ovarian cancer deaths were not reduced. Explain how both can be true, and what this teaches about evaluating screening.
::: solution
Earlier stage at diagnosis is a surrogate outcome. Screening can shift stage without changing death because of **lead-time bias** (diagnosis is earlier, so survival from diagnosis looks longer, but death occurs at the same time), **length bias** (screening preferentially detects slower-growing tumours that have a better prognosis anyway), and **overdiagnosis** of tumours that would not have caused harm. In UKCTOCS, the reduction in stage IV disease was modest and did not translate into fewer deaths, perhaps because high-grade serous cancer spreads early from the tube, before it is detectable by CA-125 or ultrasound. The lesson is that screening programmes must be judged by randomised trials with **disease-specific or all-cause mortality** as the outcome, not by stage shift or survival from diagnosis. See [[epidemiology-ebm/bias-confounding]].
:::
:::

::: exercise Vulval symptoms {level=3}
A 74-year-old woman has had vulval itching for years, treated intermittently with antifungal creams. On examination there are white, thin, wrinkled areas around the vulva and perianal skin, and a 1 cm firm, ulcerated lesion on the left labium majus. What are the likely diagnoses and the management?
::: solution
The white, thin, wrinkled skin in a figure-of-eight around the vulva and anus is typical of **lichen sclerosus**, which has been misdiagnosed as thrush. The ulcerated lump in an older woman with lichen sclerosus is **vulval squamous cell carcinoma** until proved otherwise. She needs referral on a suspected cancer pathway and a biopsy of the lesion. If cancer is confirmed, treatment is usually surgical excision with groin node assessment (sentinel node biopsy for small tumours), planned by a specialist team. Her lichen sclerosus should be treated with a potent topical corticosteroid and followed up long term. Chronic vulval itching in an older woman that does not respond to antifungal treatment should be examined, not treated repeatedly by telephone.
:::
:::
