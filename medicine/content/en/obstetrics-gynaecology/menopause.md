The menopause is a normal stage of life, not a disease, but its symptoms can be severe and long-lasting, and its hormonal changes affect bone, the cardiovascular system, the genitourinary tract and mood. For about two decades after 2002, many women were denied effective treatment because of fears about hormone replacement therapy (HRT) based on an incomplete reading of the evidence. Current guidance aims to give women accurate information about benefits and risks in absolute numbers, so that they can make their own choices.

This chapter explains the physiology of the menopause, its diagnosis, short-term and long-term effects, premature ovarian insufficiency, and the hormonal and non-hormonal treatments. UK practice follows NICE NG23 (*Menopause: identification and management*, 2015, updated 2024 and amended since) and FSRH guidance on contraception. The hormonal background is in [[endocrine/reproductive-endocrinology]].

## Physiology and definitions

A woman is born with about one to two million primordial follicles, a number that falls to around 300,000–400,000 by puberty and continues to fall throughout reproductive life, mostly by atresia rather than ovulation. When the pool approaches exhaustion, ovarian function becomes erratic and then stops.

::: definition Stages of the menopause {#def-stages}
- **Menopause** is the permanent end of menstruation resulting from loss of ovarian follicular activity. It is diagnosed retrospectively, after **12 months without a period** (in the absence of other causes). The average age in the UK is 51.
- **Perimenopause** is the time from the start of irregular cycles and other symptoms until 12 months after the last period. It typically lasts several years.
- **Postmenopause** is the time after the menopause.
- **Early menopause** is menopause between 40 and 45.
- **Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)** is loss of ovarian function before 40. It affects about 1 in 100 women.
:::

::: theorem Feedback explains the hormone pattern of the menopause {#thm-feedback}
As follicles are depleted, the ovary produces less **inhibin B** and, eventually, less **oestradiol**. Both normally restrain FSH secretion by negative feedback, so **FSH rises**, first intermittently and then persistently. In the perimenopause, high FSH can drive some remaining follicles to produce bursts of oestradiol, so oestrogen levels swing between high and low. After the menopause, oestradiol is low and FSH and LH are persistently high.
:::

::: proof
Granulosa cells of developing follicles secrete inhibin B and oestradiol. Inhibin B selectively suppresses FSH release from the pituitary, and oestradiol suppresses both GnRH and gonadotrophins. With fewer follicles, inhibin B falls first, while oestradiol may still be normal, so FSH starts to rise early in the perimenopause. The higher FSH recruits remaining follicles faster and can cause exaggerated oestradiol peaks and short cycles; in other cycles no follicle responds, leading to long cycles without ovulation. This fluctuation explains why symptoms can come and go, why a single FSH level is unreliable in women over 45, and why perimenopausal women can still ovulate and conceive. When the follicles are exhausted, oestradiol production by the ovary stops; a small amount of oestrogen continues to be made in fat by aromatisation of adrenal androgens, which is why obese postmenopausal women have higher oestrogen levels and a higher risk of endometrial cancer.
:::

## Symptoms and long-term effects

Most women notice symptoms, which vary greatly in severity and duration.

- **Vasomotor symptoms**: hot flushes and night sweats affect about three-quarters of women. They last a median of about seven years and can continue for much longer in some women. They arise because falling oestrogen narrows the thermoregulatory zone in the hypothalamus, partly through increased signalling by **neurokinin B** neurones, so that small rises in core temperature trigger heat-loss responses.
- **Psychological symptoms**: low mood, anxiety, irritability, poor concentration and memory ("brain fog"), and poor sleep, partly due to night sweats.
- **Genitourinary syndrome of menopause**: vaginal dryness, soreness, pain during intercourse, urinary frequency and urgency, and recurrent urinary tract infections, caused by thinning of oestrogen-dependent vaginal and urethral tissue. Unlike hot flushes, these symptoms tend to progress unless treated.
- **Musculoskeletal symptoms**: joint and muscle aches.
- Reduced sexual desire.

**Long-term effects.** Oestrogen restrains bone resorption by osteoclasts. Loss of oestrogen accelerates bone loss for several years after the menopause, increasing the risk of **osteoporosis** and fragility fractures ([[endocrine/bone-calcium]]). Cardiovascular risk also increases after the menopause, and earlier menopause is associated with higher risk of cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis and, in POI, reduced life expectancy if untreated.

## Diagnosis

NICE NG23 recommends diagnosing the perimenopause and menopause **clinically, without laboratory tests**, in otherwise healthy women aged over 45:
- **perimenopause**: vasomotor symptoms and irregular periods;
- **menopause**: no period for at least 12 months (if not using hormonal contraception), or symptoms in women without a uterus.

**FSH should not be used** to diagnose the menopause in women over 45, because levels fluctuate. It can be considered in women aged 40 to 45 with symptoms or a change in their cycle, and in women under 40 with suspected POI. FSH is also unreliable in women taking combined hormonal contraception or high-dose progestogens, which suppress it.

::: example Hot flushes at 49 {#ex-perimenopause}
A 49-year-old woman has had irregular periods for a year, with gaps of up to three months, and hot flushes several times a day that wake her at night. She is tired and irritable at work. She asks for a blood test "to check if it is the menopause". What would you advise?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** She is over 45 with vasomotor symptoms and irregular periods, so under NICE guidance she can be diagnosed with the **perimenopause** clinically. An FSH test is not needed and could mislead, because FSH fluctuates in the perimenopause and a normal result would not exclude it. Thyroid function may be checked if there are other features of thyroid disease.

**Discussion.** She should be given information about the perimenopause, the likely course of her symptoms, and the options: HRT, which is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms; non-hormonal options; and lifestyle measures, such as regular exercise, reducing alcohol and caffeine, and stopping smoking. Because she still has periods, a **sequential combined** HRT regimen (oestrogen continuously, with a progestogen for part of each cycle) or oestrogen with an LNG-IUS would be appropriate if she has a uterus. She should be told she still needs contraception: HRT is not a contraceptive, and she may still ovulate.
:::
:::

## Premature ovarian insufficiency

POI is the loss of ovarian function before 40. It is diagnosed in a woman under 40 with menopausal symptoms, including no or infrequent periods, and **raised FSH**. NICE recommends two FSH measurements four to six weeks apart; the ESHRE guideline (2024) accepts a single raised FSH (above 25 IU/L) when the clinical picture is typical, with a repeat if uncertain.

Most cases have no identified cause. Known causes include chemotherapy, pelvic radiotherapy and ovarian surgery; autoimmune disease (including autoimmune thyroid and adrenal disease); genetic causes such as **Turner syndrome** and the **fragile X premutation** (*FMR1*); and infections. Women with POI are offered karyotyping and fragile X testing, and testing for adrenal and thyroid autoimmunity.

POI has important consequences beyond symptoms: loss of fertility, early bone loss and increased cardiovascular risk. Women are offered **hormone replacement**, either HRT or combined hormonal contraception, **at least until the average age of menopause**, about 51, to replace what their ovaries would normally have produced. In this group, HRT restores physiology rather than adding risk, and the breast cancer risks quoted for older women do not apply in the same way. Ovarian function is intermittent in some women, and about 5% conceive spontaneously, so women who do not want to conceive need contraception. Women who want to conceive are usually offered IVF with donor eggs. The diagnosis can be distressing, and psychological support should be offered.

::: example A 36-year-old with no periods {#ex-poi}
A 36-year-old woman has had no periods for six months, with hot flushes and vaginal dryness. A pregnancy test is negative. Her FSH is 68 IU/L and, five weeks later, 72 IU/L; oestradiol is low; prolactin and TSH are normal. What is the diagnosis and what should she be offered?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Amenorrhoea, menopausal symptoms and two raised FSH levels four to six weeks apart, under the age of 40, confirm **premature ovarian insufficiency**.

**Investigations.** Karyotype (for Turner syndrome mosaicism) and *FMR1* premutation testing; adrenal and thyroid antibodies; and a bone density scan (DXA) as a baseline.

**Treatment.** **HRT** (or combined hormonal contraception if she prefers and has no contraindications) at least until about 51, with a progestogen to protect the endometrium as she has a uterus. Vaginal oestrogen can be added for dryness. Lifestyle measures for bone and cardiovascular health. She should be told that her ovaries may occasionally still work, so pregnancy is possible though unlikely; if she wants to avoid pregnancy, she needs contraception, and if she wants to conceive, she should be referred to a fertility service. If the *FMR1* premutation is found, genetic counselling for her and her relatives is needed. She should be offered psychological support and information about patient organisations.
:::
:::

## Hormone replacement therapy

HRT replaces oestrogen. Women who have a uterus also need a **progestogen**, because oestrogen alone stimulates the endometrium and increases the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer.

::: definition HRT regimens {#def-hrt}
- **Oestrogen-only**: for women who have had a hysterectomy.
- **Sequential (cyclical) combined**: oestrogen every day with a progestogen for 10 to 14 days of each month, causing a monthly withdrawal bleed. Used in the perimenopause and in the first year after the last period, when continuous progestogen tends to cause irregular bleeding.
- **Continuous combined**: oestrogen and progestogen every day, aiming for no bleeding. Used once a woman has been without periods for a year, or after a year of sequential HRT.
- The **LNG-IUS** can provide the progestogen and contraception at the same time.
- **Tibolone**, a synthetic steroid with oestrogenic, progestogenic and androgenic effects, is an alternative in postmenopausal women.
:::

**Routes.** Oestrogen can be given orally, or **transdermally** as a patch, gel or spray. Oral oestrogen is absorbed through the gut and passes through the liver first, where it increases the synthesis of clotting factors. Transdermal oestrogen avoids this first-pass effect, and at standard doses it is not associated with an increased risk of VTE or stroke. Transdermal oestrogen is therefore preferred for women with a higher baseline risk of VTE, such as those with obesity, and for women with migraine.

**Vaginal oestrogen** in low doses treats genitourinary symptoms with minimal systemic absorption. It does not need a progestogen, can be used long term, and can be used alongside systemic HRT. For women with a history of breast cancer, its use is discussed with their oncology team.

### Benefits and risks

**Benefits.** HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms, and NICE describes it as first-line for them. It often improves mood, sleep and joint symptoms, and treats genitourinary symptoms. It prevents bone loss and reduces fracture risk while taken.

**Risks.** NICE NG23 (2024) presents the risks in absolute terms, and they depend on the type of HRT, the route, the woman's age and the duration of use:
- **Breast cancer**: **combined** HRT is associated with an increased risk of breast cancer, which rises with duration of use and falls after stopping, although some excess risk persists for years. **Oestrogen-only** HRT is associated with little or no change in breast cancer risk.
- **VTE**: oral HRT increases the risk; transdermal HRT at standard doses does not.
- **Stroke**: oral oestrogen is associated with a small increase in risk; transdermal oestrogen is not. Because the baseline risk of stroke is low in women under 60, the absolute increase is small.
- **Cardiovascular disease**: HRT started in women under 60 does not increase cardiovascular disease risk.
- **Endometrial cancer**: increased by oestrogen-only HRT in a woman with a uterus; not increased by appropriate combined HRT.
- **Dementia**: HRT does not affect the risk of dementia when started around the menopause.

Absolute risk is what matters to an individual. If a woman's baseline risk of an event over a given period is $b$ and HRT multiplies it by a relative risk $R$, the number of **extra cases** is
$$
\text{extra cases} = b\,(R - 1).
$$ {#eq-extra}

The same relative risk produces few extra cases when the baseline risk is low and more when it is high. With R below 1, the treatment prevents cases, and the line falls below zero. This illustrative figure is a way of understanding the numbers in a discussion aid, not a calculator for an individual woman.
:::

::: example Turning a relative risk into a number {#ex-absolute}
Suppose that, in an illustrative group of women in their fifties, 30 per 1000 would be diagnosed with breast cancer over ten years without HRT, and that a particular combined HRT regimen used for five years is associated with a relative risk of 1.3 over that period. How many extra cases per 1000 women would this represent? How would you describe it?
::: solution
Using [[#eq-extra]], extra cases $= 30 \times (1.3 - 1) = 30 \times 0.3 = 9$ per 1000 women. Described in words: "Of 1,000 women like you who did not take HRT, about 30 would be diagnosed with breast cancer over ten years. Of 1,000 who took this HRT for five years, about 39 would be, so about 9 more. That means about 961 of the 1,000 would not." This framing also allows comparison with other risks, such as the effect of alcohol or obesity on breast cancer. The numbers are illustrative; in practice, clinicians use the absolute figures in the NICE discussion aid, which differ by age, type and duration of HRT.
:::
:::

::: warning HRT is not a contraceptive
A perimenopausal woman taking HRT can still ovulate and become pregnant, because the doses of hormone are too low to suppress ovulation reliably. Contraception is needed until the age of 55, or, if the last natural period occurred at 50 or over, until one year after it, and if it occurred under 50, until two years after it. The LNG-IUS can provide both contraception and the progestogen component of HRT. Women using hormonal contraception cannot rely on the absence of periods to judge when they have reached the menopause.
:::

::: example Choosing a regimen {#ex-regimen}
A 52-year-old woman's last period was 18 months ago. She has severe night sweats and low mood. Her BMI is 34 and she has migraine without aura. She has a uterus and no personal or family history of VTE or breast cancer. What HRT would be suitable?
::: solution
She is postmenopausal (more than 12 months since her last period), so a **continuous combined** regimen is appropriate, aiming for no bleeding. Because of her BMI (a risk factor for VTE) and her migraine, **transdermal oestradiol** is preferred over oral, as it does not increase VTE or stroke risk at standard doses. The progestogen can be oral micronised progesterone or another progestogen, or an LNG-IUS. Any unscheduled bleeding after the first few months should be investigated. She should be given absolute risk information, including that combined HRT is associated with a small increase in breast cancer risk that depends on duration. Her low mood may improve with HRT; if it does not, or if it is severe, it should be assessed as possible depression. A review at three months and then yearly is usual.
:::
:::

## Non-hormonal treatments

Some women cannot or do not want to take HRT, for example after breast cancer. Options for vasomotor symptoms include:
- **cognitive behavioural therapy** designed for menopausal symptoms, which NICE recommends considering for vasomotor symptoms, low mood and sleep problems, alongside or instead of HRT;
- **SSRIs and SNRIs**, such as venlafaxine, which reduce hot flushes; paroxetine and fluoxetine should not be used by women taking tamoxifen, because they inhibit the enzyme CYP2D6 that converts tamoxifen to its active form;
- **clonidine** and **gabapentin**;
- **fezolinetant**, a neurokinin 3 receptor antagonist that acts on the hypothalamic thermoregulatory pathway described above; it was licensed in the UK in 2023 and appraised by NICE, which has considered it for women in whom HRT is unsuitable;
- lifestyle measures such as weight loss, regular exercise, and avoiding triggers such as alcohol and caffeine.

Herbal remedies such as black cohosh and St John's wort have uncertain effects and quality, and St John's wort interacts with many drugs, including tamoxifen and hormonal contraception. **Testosterone** can be considered for women with low sexual desire if HRT alone has not helped.

::: quiz
A 47-year-old woman on sequential combined HRT, whose periods were irregular before she started it, asks whether she can stop using condoms. Which answer is correct?
- [ ] Yes, because HRT prevents ovulation.
- [ ] Yes, once she has had 12 months without a natural period.
- [x] No; HRT is not contraceptive. She needs contraception until 55, or until two years after her last natural period if that was under 50, and an LNG-IUS could serve both purposes.
- [ ] No; she must stop HRT for a year to find out whether she is postmenopausal.
::: solution
The hormone doses in HRT do not reliably prevent ovulation, and the regular bleeds on sequential HRT do not show whether natural periods have stopped. FSRH guidance is to continue contraception until 55, or for one year after the last natural period at 50 or over, or two years if under 50. An LNG-IUS provides contraception and endometrial protection.
:::
:::

::: history
The word "ménopausie" was introduced by the French physician Charles de Gardanne in 1821. Oestrogens were isolated in the 1920s and 1930s, by Edgar Allen and Edward Doisy among others, and conjugated equine oestrogens became available in North America in 1942. In the 1960s Robert Wilson's book *Feminine Forever* promoted oestrogen as a way to preserve youth, and its use grew rapidly until the mid-1970s, when it was linked to endometrial cancer; adding a progestogen for women with a uterus solved that problem. In 2002 the combined HRT arm of the Women's Health Initiative, a large American trial in women with an average age of 63, was stopped early because of an increase in breast cancer and cardiovascular events, and in 2003 the UK Million Women Study reported an association with breast cancer. HRT use fell sharply around the world. Later analyses showed that the risks depended strongly on age at starting, the type and route of HRT, and duration, and that for women under 60 with symptoms the benefits usually outweigh the risks. The Collaborative Group on Hormonal Factors in Breast Cancer published a large meta-analysis of the breast cancer risk in 2019, and these data underpin the absolute risk figures used in current NICE guidance.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Extra cases {level=1 check="6"}
The baseline risk of an event over ten years in a group of women is 20 per 1000. A treatment has a relative risk of 1.3. How many extra cases per 1000 women would be expected?
::: solution
$20 \times (1.3 - 1) = 20 \times 0.3 = 6$ extra cases per 1000 women over ten years.
:::
:::

::: exercise When is the menopause? {level=1}
A 53-year-old woman had her last period 10 months ago. Is she postmenopausal? What HRT regimen would be appropriate if she has a uterus and wants treatment?
::: solution
Not yet by definition: the menopause is diagnosed after 12 months without a period, so she is still perimenopausal. A **sequential combined** regimen is appropriate now; she can move to continuous combined HRT once she has been without periods for a year (or after a year of sequential HRT). Using continuous combined HRT too early tends to cause irregular bleeding.
:::
:::

::: exercise Who needs a progestogen? {level=1}
Which of the following need a progestogen with systemic oestrogen: (a) a woman who has had a total hysterectomy for fibroids; (b) a woman who has had an endometrial ablation; (c) a woman who has had a subtotal hysterectomy, with the cervix retained; (d) a woman using only low-dose vaginal oestrogen?
::: solution
(a) No; without a uterus, oestrogen-only HRT is used. (b) **Yes**; islands of endometrium may remain after ablation and could develop hyperplasia. (c) **Yes**, usually; some endometrial tissue can remain in the cervical stump, so a progestogen is generally advised (specialist advice may vary). (d) No; low-dose vaginal oestrogen has minimal systemic absorption and does not need a progestogen.
:::
:::

::: exercise Absolute benefit {level=2 check="round(40*(1-0.7))"}
Suppose that in an illustrative group of women, 40 per 1000 would have a fragility fracture over five years, and HRT reduces this by a relative risk of 0.7. How many fractures per 1000 women would HRT prevent over five years? Give the nearest whole number.
::: solution
Extra cases $= 40 \times (0.7 - 1) = -12$, so HRT would **prevent 12 fractures** per 1000 women over five years. Expressing benefits and harms in the same units ("per 1000 women over five years") lets a woman weigh them directly.
:::
:::

::: exercise Breast cancer and vasomotor symptoms {level=2}
A 50-year-old woman who has completed treatment for oestrogen receptor-positive breast cancer and is taking tamoxifen has severe hot flushes. What options are there?
::: solution
Systemic HRT is generally not recommended after breast cancer, and any discussion involves her oncology team. Non-hormonal options include **CBT**; **venlafaxine** or another SNRI, or an SSRI such as citalopram, but **not paroxetine or fluoxetine**, which inhibit CYP2D6 and reduce the conversion of tamoxifen to its active metabolite, endoxifen; **gabapentin** or **clonidine**; and lifestyle measures. Fezolinetant may be an option depending on its licence and local guidance. For vaginal dryness, non-hormonal moisturisers and lubricants are first line; low-dose vaginal oestrogen can be considered after discussion with her oncologist. St John's wort should be avoided because it interacts with tamoxifen.
:::
:::

::: exercise Vaginal symptoms {level=2}
A 64-year-old woman, ten years past her menopause, has vaginal dryness, pain with intercourse and three urinary tract infections in the past year. She took HRT for three years in her fifties and does not want to take it again. What would you suggest?
::: solution
These are features of **genitourinary syndrome of menopause**, which tends to progress without treatment. **Low-dose vaginal oestrogen** (cream, pessary or ring) is effective, has minimal systemic absorption, does not need a progestogen, and can be continued long term; it also reduces the risk of recurrent urinary tract infections. She can use vaginal moisturisers and lubricants in addition or instead. Any postmenopausal bleeding while using it must be investigated. Her concerns about systemic HRT do not apply in the same way to low-dose vaginal oestrogen, and this should be explained.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting an FSH {level=3}
A 47-year-old woman taking a combined oral contraceptive pill asks whether she has reached the menopause. Her GP measured FSH during the pill-free week and found it to be 12 IU/L. What does this result mean, and how would you advise her about contraception and the menopause?
::: solution
The result is uninterpretable. Combined hormonal contraception suppresses FSH, and although levels rise in the pill-free week, they may not reach postmenopausal values; in addition, FSH fluctuates in the perimenopause. A normal FSH does not mean she is not perimenopausal, and a raised FSH would not prove she is postmenopausal. In any case, NICE does not recommend FSH testing to diagnose the menopause in women over 45.

**Advice.** She can continue combined hormonal contraception until age 50 if she has no risk factors (it also controls perimenopausal symptoms), and then switch to a progestogen-only method, such as the LNG-IUS, the implant or the progestogen-only pill, until 55, when contraception can stop because natural fertility is negligible. If she develops vasomotor symptoms after switching, HRT can be added to a progestogen-only method (with the LNG-IUS providing endometrial protection, or with a separate progestogen otherwise).
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting the WHI {level=3}
In 2002, the combined HRT arm of the Women's Health Initiative was stopped early because of increased breast cancer and cardiovascular events. Explain why its results led to undertreatment of younger symptomatic women, and how current guidance interprets them.
::: solution
The WHI enrolled women with an average age of 63, many more than ten years past the menopause, most without significant symptoms, and used one specific oral regimen (conjugated equine oestrogens with medroxyprogesterone acetate). Cardiovascular risk from starting oral HRT is concentrated in older women with established atherosclerosis; in women under 60 or within ten years of the menopause, later analyses found no increase in coronary heart disease. The breast cancer findings were reported mainly as relative risks, which sounded alarming without the small absolute numbers. The results were then generalised to all women and all types of HRT, and prescribing fell sharply, leaving many women with untreated symptoms and bone loss. Current guidance (NICE NG23) considers age at starting, type, route and duration; describes HRT as first-line treatment for vasomotor symptoms; presents risks in absolute numbers; and notes that transdermal oestrogen does not increase VTE or stroke risk. The general lessons are to check whether a trial's population matches the patient in front of you, and to communicate absolute, not just relative, risks.
:::
:::
