Bleeding or pain in the first trimester is one of the commonest reasons women attend emergency gynaecology services. Most of these pregnancies will either continue normally or end in a miscarriage that needs support rather than urgent treatment. A small number are ectopic pregnancies, which can rupture and cause life-threatening internal bleeding. The skill of early pregnancy care is to identify that small number reliably without subjecting every woman to unnecessary tests, while recognising that a miscarriage is a loss that deserves care in its own right.

Two tools do most of the work: **transvaginal ultrasound**, which shows where the pregnancy is and whether it is developing, and **serum hCG**, the hormone made by the trophoblast, whose level and rate of change give information when the scan cannot. This chapter explains how they are used together, and covers miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, gestational trophoblastic disease, hyperemesis gravidarum and recurrent miscarriage. UK practice follows NICE NG126 (*Ectopic pregnancy and miscarriage*, 2019, updated 2023) and RCOG guidance.

## The normal first trimester

After fertilisation in the fallopian tube, the embryo travels to the uterus over about five days and implants into the endometrium (the **decidua**) at around day 6–7. The outer cells, the **trophoblast**, invade the decidua and produce **human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG)**. hCG acts on the corpus luteum to maintain progesterone production until the placenta takes over at about 8–10 weeks.

In a normal early pregnancy, the serum hCG level rises steeply, roughly doubling every two days in the first weeks, then peaks at about 8–11 weeks and falls to a lower plateau. Urine pregnancy tests detect hCG from about the time of the missed period.

On transvaginal ultrasound, a normally developing intrauterine pregnancy shows a recognisable sequence:

| Approximate gestation | Finding |
|---|---|
| About 4+3 to 5 weeks | Gestational sac, a small fluid collection within the decidua |
| About 5+3 weeks | Yolk sac within the gestational sac (confirms an intrauterine pregnancy) |
| About 6 weeks | Fetal pole with visible cardiac activity |

These timings vary with the accuracy of the dates, which is why a single scan rarely proves that a pregnancy has failed, and why the criteria below use sizes rather than dates.

## Pregnancy of unknown location

If a woman has a positive pregnancy test and no pregnancy can be seen in the uterus or elsewhere on transvaginal ultrasound, she has a **pregnancy of unknown location (PUL)**. This is a description, not a diagnosis. It has three possible outcomes: an intrauterine pregnancy too early to see, a failing pregnancy (either intrauterine or ectopic) that will resolve, or an ectopic pregnancy too small to see.

::: theorem The hCG ratio in a pregnancy of unknown location {#thm-hcg-ratio}
NICE NG126 recommends measuring serum hCG twice, 48 hours apart, in a woman with a PUL who is clinically stable:
- A **rise of more than 63%** suggests a developing intrauterine pregnancy, although it does not exclude an ectopic pregnancy. A transvaginal scan is offered within 7–14 days, or earlier if the hCG is 1500 IU/L or more.
- A **fall of more than 50%** suggests a failing pregnancy. A urine pregnancy test is done after 14 days; if it is negative, nothing further is needed.
- A **change between these** (a rise of 63% or less, or a fall of 50% or less) means an ectopic pregnancy is possible, and the woman is reviewed in the early pregnancy unit within 24 hours.

Symptoms override the numbers: a woman with significant pain or signs of bleeding needs urgent assessment whatever her hCG.
:::

::: proof
A viable intrauterine pregnancy has a growing mass of healthy trophoblast, which secretes hCG at an increasing rate, so the level rises steeply. In a failing pregnancy the trophoblast is dying and hCG is cleared with a half-life of about a day or two, so the level falls rapidly. An ectopic pregnancy usually implants in a site with a poor blood supply, such as the tube, so the trophoblast grows, but grows poorly: its hCG level typically rises slowly, plateaus or falls slowly. The cut-offs of 63% and 50% are chosen so that few viable pregnancies are wrongly classified as non-viable; the price is that a minority of ectopic pregnancies show a "normal" rise, which is why a rise of more than 63% does not exclude one.
:::

::: example Interpreting two hCG values {#ex-pul}
A 28-year-old woman has light bleeding at about 5 weeks by dates. She is well, with mild cramping only. A transvaginal scan shows no intrauterine or extrauterine pregnancy and no free fluid. Her hCG is 1200 IU/L, and 48 hours later it is 2100 IU/L. Interpret the result.
::: solution
The rise is $2100/1200 = 1.75$, an increase of **75%**, which is more than 63%. This suggests a developing intrauterine pregnancy that was too early to see on the first scan. Because a rise of this size does not exclude an ectopic pregnancy, she is offered a repeat transvaginal scan; since her hCG is now above 1500 IU/L, the scan should be done soon rather than waiting 7–14 days. She is given written information and told to return immediately with increasing pain, shoulder-tip pain, fainting or heavy bleeding.
:::
:::

## Miscarriage

::: definition Types of miscarriage {#def-miscarriage}
**Miscarriage** is the loss of a pregnancy before 24 weeks. About one in five recognised pregnancies ends in miscarriage, most before 12 weeks; the commonest cause is a chromosomal abnormality in the embryo. Clinical types are:
- **Threatened miscarriage**: bleeding with a viable intrauterine pregnancy and a closed cervix. Most continue normally.
- **Inevitable miscarriage**: bleeding with an open cervix; the pregnancy will be lost.
- **Incomplete miscarriage**: some pregnancy tissue has been passed, but some remains in the uterus.
- **Complete miscarriage**: all pregnancy tissue has been passed; bleeding settles and the uterus is empty. If no intrauterine pregnancy was ever seen on a scan, a "complete miscarriage" cannot be diagnosed with confidence, and the woman is managed as a PUL.
- **Missed (delayed or silent) miscarriage**: an intrauterine pregnancy that has stopped developing, found on a scan, often without symptoms.
- **Septic miscarriage**: miscarriage complicated by infection of the uterine contents.
:::

**Diagnosing a missed miscarriage.** Because a mistaken diagnosis could end a wanted pregnancy, NICE uses conservative ultrasound criteria. On transvaginal scan:
- If the **crown–rump length is 7 mm or more** with no visible heartbeat, the finding is confirmed by a second operator or on a repeat scan at least 7 days later.
- If the **mean gestational sac diameter is 25 mm or more** with no visible fetal pole, the same applies.
- Below these sizes, a repeat scan is done at least 7 days later before making the diagnosis.

**Management.** Women with a confirmed miscarriage are offered a choice:
- **Expectant management**: waiting for the miscarriage to happen naturally, usually for 7–14 days, is the first-line option for most women. A urine pregnancy test three weeks later confirms completion.
- **Medical management**: for a missed miscarriage, NICE recommends oral mifepristone followed 48 hours later by misoprostol; for an incomplete miscarriage, misoprostol alone. Pain relief and anti-emetics are provided.
- **Surgical management**: removal of the pregnancy tissue by **manual vacuum aspiration** under local anaesthetic in an outpatient setting, or by electric vacuum aspiration in theatre. Surgery is preferred if there is heavy bleeding, infection or a previous traumatic experience, or if the woman chooses it.

Each option has advantages. Expectant and medical management avoid an operation but involve bleeding and pain at home and sometimes need to be followed by surgery. Surgery is quicker and more predictable but carries small risks of infection, uterine perforation and intrauterine adhesions. The woman's choice is central.

**Anti-D.** NICE recommends anti-D prophylaxis for RhD-negative women who have **surgical** management of a miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy. It is not recommended for medical management alone, threatened miscarriage, complete miscarriage or PUL.

**Emotional care.** Many women experience grief, anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress after a miscarriage. Staff should use clear, compassionate language ("your pregnancy has stopped developing", not "products of conception" when talking to the woman), explain what will happen, and offer follow-up and information about support organisations.

**Threatened miscarriage and progesterone.** Following the PRISM trial (2019), NICE (2021) recommends offering vaginal micronised progesterone to women with vaginal bleeding in early pregnancy and a confirmed intrauterine pregnancy who have had a previous miscarriage, continuing until 16 weeks. The benefit was seen mainly in women with previous miscarriages.

::: example A pregnancy that has stopped developing {#ex-missed}
A 34-year-old woman at 9 weeks by dates attends for an early scan because of brown spotting. Transvaginal ultrasound shows an intrauterine gestational sac with a fetal pole of crown–rump length 8 mm and no cardiac activity. How is the diagnosis confirmed, and what options does she have?
::: solution
A crown–rump length of 7 mm or more without a heartbeat meets the NICE criterion for **missed miscarriage**, but the diagnosis must be confirmed by a second operator, or by a repeat scan at least 7 days later, before acting on it. She should be told in a quiet room, with time to ask questions.

If confirmed, she is offered **expectant**, **medical** (mifepristone then misoprostol 48 hours later) or **surgical** (manual vacuum aspiration under local anaesthetic, or vacuum aspiration in theatre) management, with written information about each, including what to expect, pain relief, when to seek help (heavy bleeding, fever, severe pain) and how completion will be confirmed. Anti-D is needed only if she is RhD-negative and has surgical management. She should be told that a first miscarriage is very common, is usually due to a chromosomal problem, and does not usually reduce her chance of a future successful pregnancy.
:::
:::

## Ectopic pregnancy

An **ectopic pregnancy** is a pregnancy implanted outside the uterine cavity. It affects about 1 in 90 pregnancies in the UK. About 95% are in the fallopian tube, most in the **ampulla**; others are in the cervix, the interstitial (intramural) part of the tube, a caesarean section scar, the ovary or the abdomen. As the pregnancy grows, it can rupture the tube and cause severe intraperitoneal haemorrhage. Ectopic pregnancy remains a cause of maternal death in the UK, usually when the diagnosis is missed.

**Risk factors** are anything that damages the tube or impairs its transport: previous ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease (especially chlamydial), previous tubal surgery including sterilisation, assisted conception, smoking, and conception with an intrauterine device or progestogen-only contraception in place. About a third of women have no risk factor.

**Presentation** is variable. The classic features are abdominal or pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding and a missed period, but women may present with diarrhoea, dizziness or shoulder-tip pain (from blood irritating the diaphragm). NICE emphasises that ectopic pregnancy should be considered in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain, and that a pregnancy test should be done in every woman of reproductive age with these symptoms. **Rupture** causes severe pain, peritonism, and signs of hypovolaemic shock; young women compensate well, so tachycardia and pallor may come before hypotension.

**Diagnosis** is by transvaginal ultrasound, which may show an adnexal mass separate from the ovary, sometimes containing a yolk sac or fetal pole, with an empty uterus. Free fluid in the pouch of Douglas suggests bleeding. If nothing is seen, the woman is managed as a PUL with serial hCG.

::: definition Choosing treatment for a tubal ectopic pregnancy {#def-ectopic-treatment}
Following NICE NG126:
- **Expectant management** can be offered to women who are clinically stable and pain-free with a small unruptured tubal ectopic (less than 35 mm), no visible heartbeat, and an hCG of 1500 IU/L or less that is falling.
- **Methotrexate** is offered first line to women who can return for follow-up and have no significant pain, an unruptured tubal ectopic with an adnexal mass smaller than 35 mm and no visible heartbeat, an hCG below 1500 IU/L, and no intrauterine pregnancy.
- **Surgery** is first line for women who cannot return for follow-up or who have significant pain, a mass of 35 mm or more, a visible fetal heartbeat, or an hCG of 5000 IU/L or more.
- Between 1500 and 5000 IU/L, women who otherwise meet the criteria for methotrexate can choose methotrexate or surgery.
:::

**Methotrexate** is a folate antagonist that stops the rapidly dividing trophoblast. hCG is measured on days 4 and 7 after treatment and then weekly until negative; a fall of less than 15% between days 4 and 7 suggests treatment has failed, and a second dose or surgery may be needed. Abdominal pain is common after methotrexate and must be assessed, because it may be rupture. Women are advised to avoid pregnancy for at least three months afterwards because of the drug's teratogenicity.

**Surgery** is usually laparoscopic. A **salpingectomy** (removal of the tube) is preferred if the other tube is healthy. A **salpingotomy** (opening the tube to remove the pregnancy) may be offered if the other tube is damaged, to preserve fertility, but around 1 in 5 women then need further treatment for persistent trophoblast, so hCG must be followed up.

::: example Choosing a treatment {#ex-ectopic}
A 31-year-old woman at 6 weeks by dates has mild left iliac fossa pain. Transvaginal ultrasound shows an empty uterus and a 22 mm left adnexal mass with no fetal heartbeat, and a small amount of free fluid in the pouch of Douglas. Her hCG is 1100 IU/L. She is stable, lives near the hospital and has no contraindications to methotrexate. What are her options?
::: solution
She has an unruptured tubal ectopic pregnancy: a mass smaller than 35 mm, no heartbeat, an hCG below 1500 IU/L, no significant pain, and she can attend follow-up. She meets the NICE criteria for **methotrexate** as first-line treatment, after checking full blood count, liver and kidney function. Surgery is an alternative if she prefers it. Expectant management is possible only if her hCG is falling on repeat testing.

If she chooses methotrexate, she needs hCG on days 4 and 7 and then weekly until negative, clear advice to return with increasing pain or dizziness, advice to avoid alcohol and folate-containing supplements during treatment, and advice to avoid pregnancy for three months. Anti-D is not needed for medical management.
:::
:::

::: example Collapse in the emergency department {#ex-rupture}
A 26-year-old woman is brought in after fainting at work. She has severe lower abdominal pain and pain at the tip of her right shoulder. Her last period was seven weeks ago. Pulse 128, blood pressure 86/50 mmHg, abdomen rigid. A urine pregnancy test is positive. What is the diagnosis and the management?
::: solution
The diagnosis is a **ruptured ectopic pregnancy with haemorrhagic shock** until proven otherwise. Shoulder-tip pain reflects blood in the peritoneal cavity irritating the diaphragm.

Management: call the senior gynaecologist and anaesthetist and alert theatre immediately; two large-bore cannulae, crossmatch, full blood count and clotting; activate the major haemorrhage protocol; and give blood. Resuscitation should not delay surgery, because the bleeding will not stop until it is controlled surgically. A bedside ultrasound showing free fluid supports the diagnosis but is not required before surgery. She needs **emergency laparoscopy or laparotomy** (depending on stability and the surgeon's experience) and salpingectomy. Anti-D is given if she is RhD-negative, since this is surgical management.
:::
:::

## Gestational trophoblastic disease

**Gestational trophoblastic disease** comprises abnormal proliferations of trophoblast. The commonest form is the **hydatidiform mole** (molar pregnancy), which occurs in about 1 in 600 pregnancies in the UK.

- A **complete mole** usually has a diploid, entirely paternal karyotype (most commonly 46,XX), arising when an egg with no maternal chromosomes is fertilised by one sperm that duplicates, or by two sperm. There is no fetus, and the villi are swollen throughout.
- A **partial mole** is usually triploid (for example 69,XXY), with two sets of paternal chromosomes and one maternal. Some fetal tissue may be present.

Moles may present with bleeding in early pregnancy, a uterus larger than expected, severe vomiting or, rarely, hyperthyroidism (hCG stimulates the TSH receptor). The hCG level is often very high. Many are now diagnosed on histology after a miscarriage. Ultrasound may show a "snowstorm" appearance in a complete mole. Treatment is removal by suction evacuation. In the UK, all women are then registered with a specialist screening centre (Charing Cross Hospital in London, Sheffield or Dundee) for hCG monitoring until it is normal. About 15% of complete moles and 0.5–1% of partial moles develop **gestational trophoblastic neoplasia**, persistent or invasive disease (including choriocarcinoma), which is detected by rising or plateauing hCG. It is highly sensitive to chemotherapy, usually methotrexate, with cure rates close to 100% when treated through the specialist centres.

## Nausea, vomiting and hyperemesis gravidarum

Nausea and vomiting affect most women in early pregnancy, usually starting at around 6 weeks and settling by about 16–20 weeks. **Hyperemesis gravidarum** is at the severe end of the spectrum. RCOG guidance (2024) describes it as protracted nausea and vomiting in pregnancy with weight loss of more than 5% of pre-pregnancy weight, dehydration and electrolyte imbalance. Other causes of vomiting, such as urinary tract infection, gastroenteritis, molar pregnancy and thyroid disease, must be considered, particularly if vomiting starts after 10 weeks.

Severity can be scored with the **PUQE score** (Pregnancy-Unique Quantification of Emesis). Treatment is stepwise and can usually be given as a day case: intravenous fluids (normal saline with potassium chloride as needed), anti-emetics (first-line options include antihistamines such as cyclizine and promethazine, phenothiazines such as prochlorperazine, and doxylamine with pyridoxine; second-line options include metoclopramide and ondansetron), and corticosteroids for refractory cases under specialist supervision. **Thiamine** is given to women with prolonged vomiting to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy, and dextrose-containing fluids are avoided until thiamine has been given. VTE prophylaxis is considered for admitted women because dehydration and immobility raise the risk. The psychological burden is considerable, and women should be asked about their mental health.

::: warning Women with hyperemesis are often under-treated
Women with severe nausea and vomiting in pregnancy are often reluctant to take medicines, and clinicians are sometimes reluctant to prescribe them. Untreated hyperemesis causes dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, Wernicke's encephalopathy, VTE, malnutrition and severe distress, and some women have ended wanted pregnancies because of it. The anti-emetics used in pregnancy have extensive safety data; treating early and adequately is safer than not treating.
:::

## Recurrent miscarriage

The RCOG (Green-top Guideline 17, 2023) defines **recurrent miscarriage** as three or more first-trimester miscarriages, which need not be consecutive. Clinicians may investigate after two losses if there is reason to suspect an underlying cause, and a second-trimester loss is investigated in its own right. Recurrent miscarriage affects about 1% of couples. Investigations include:
- **antiphospholipid antibodies** (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies) on two occasions at least 12 weeks apart. In **antiphospholipid syndrome**, treatment with low-dose aspirin and heparin in a future pregnancy improves the live birth rate;
- **thyroid function** and thyroid antibodies;
- **pelvic ultrasound** for uterine anomalies such as a septate uterus;
- **cytogenetic analysis** of pregnancy tissue from the third and later miscarriages, with parental karyotyping if an unbalanced structural abnormality is found.

In about half of couples no cause is found. Their chance of a successful next pregnancy is still good, and supportive care in an early pregnancy clinic is itself associated with better outcomes.

## Abortion care

Induced abortion is legal in Great Britain under the Abortion Act 1967, which permits it before 24 weeks on specified grounds when two doctors agree, and without a gestational limit in some circumstances. In Northern Ireland it was decriminalised in 2019 and is regulated by separate legislation from 2020. Most abortions in Great Britain are early medical abortions using mifepristone followed by misoprostol, and since 2022 women in England and Wales can take both medicines at home up to 9+6 weeks after a telemedicine consultation. Care follows NICE NG140 (2019). The ethical and legal framework is discussed in [[medical-ethics-law/beginning-of-life]].

::: quiz
A woman has a positive pregnancy test and an empty uterus on transvaginal ultrasound, with no adnexal mass. She is well. Her hCG is 800 IU/L, and 48 hours later it is 900 IU/L. What is the next step?
- [ ] Reassure her: a rising hCG means a normal intrauterine pregnancy.
- [ ] Repeat the urine pregnancy test in 14 days.
- [x] Review her in the early pregnancy unit within 24 hours, because a suboptimal rise means an ectopic pregnancy is possible.
- [ ] Give methotrexate immediately.
::: solution
The rise is $900/800 = 1.125$, or 12.5%, which is between the thresholds of a 63% rise and a 50% fall. Following [[#thm-hcg-ratio]], an ectopic pregnancy is possible and she needs senior review in the early pregnancy unit within 24 hours, with a further scan and hCG measurements. Methotrexate is not given without a diagnosis, or after careful senior assessment in a persisting PUL.
:::
:::

::: history
The first recorded surgical treatment of an ectopic pregnancy was by John Bard in New York in 1759, who removed a dead fetus from the abdomen of a woman who survived. In 1883 Robert Lawson Tait in Birmingham performed the first successful operation for a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, ligating the tube, and he reported a series of patients who survived what had been an almost uniformly fatal condition. The second revolution was diagnostic: radioimmunoassays for the β subunit of hCG, developed by Judith Vaitukaitis, Glenn Braunstein and Griff Ross at the US National Institutes of Health in 1972, made it possible to detect a pregnancy before any symptoms, and transvaginal ultrasound in the 1980s made it possible to see where it was. Together they changed ectopic pregnancy from a surgical emergency diagnosed at rupture to a condition usually diagnosed early, and made medical treatment with methotrexate, first reported for ectopic pregnancy in 1982, a realistic option. Choriocarcinoma, once almost always fatal, became the first solid tumour cured by chemotherapy alone, after Min Chiu Li and Roy Hertz used methotrexate at the National Cancer Institute in 1956.
:::

::: summary
- A pregnancy of unknown location is managed with two hCG values 48 hours apart: a rise of more than 63% suggests a developing intrauterine pregnancy, a fall of more than 50% a failing pregnancy, and anything in between needs review within 24 hours for possible ectopic pregnancy.
- A missed miscarriage is diagnosed when the CRL is 7 mm or more without a heartbeat, or the mean sac diameter is 25 mm or more without a fetal pole, and the finding is confirmed by a second operator or a repeat scan after 7 days.
- Miscarriage can be managed expectantly, medically (mifepristone then misoprostol for missed miscarriage) or surgically; the woman's choice is central. Anti-D is given only with surgical management.
- Ectopic pregnancy affects about 1 in 90 pregnancies; consider it in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain and do a pregnancy test.
- Methotrexate is first line for a stable woman with a small unruptured ectopic, no heartbeat and hCG below 1500 IU/L; surgery for significant pain, a mass of 35 mm or more, a heartbeat or hCG of 5000 IU/L or more. A ruptured ectopic needs immediate surgery.
- Complete moles are usually diploid and paternal; partial moles are triploid. All need hCG follow-up at a specialist centre; persistent disease responds very well to chemotherapy.
- Hyperemesis gravidarum causes weight loss, dehydration and electrolyte imbalance; treat early with fluids, anti-emetics and thiamine.
- Recurrent miscarriage (three or more first-trimester losses) is investigated for antiphospholipid syndrome, uterine anomalies and parental chromosomal rearrangements.
:::

A woman with a pregnancy of unknown location has an hCG of 1400 IU/L, and 48 hours later 1850 IU/L. Calculate the percentage change to the nearest whole number, and state what it means under NICE guidance.
::: solution
$1850/1400 = 1.321$, a rise of **32%**. This is less than 63%, and not a fall, so it is in the intermediate range: an ectopic pregnancy is possible and she should be reviewed in the early pregnancy unit within 24 hours.

For each, name the type of miscarriage: (a) bleeding at 8 weeks, cervix closed, scan shows a fetus with a heartbeat; (b) heavy bleeding with clots, cervix open, tissue visible at the os; (c) scan at 12 weeks shows a fetus with CRL 25 mm and no heartbeat, with no symptoms.
::: solution
(a) **Threatened miscarriage**: most such pregnancies continue. (b) **Inevitable or incomplete miscarriage**: the open cervix with tissue at the os means the pregnancy cannot continue; removing tissue from the os can relieve cervical shock (bradycardia and hypotension from vagal stimulation). For RhD-negative women, which of the following situations call for anti-D under NICE NG126: (a) threatened miscarriage at 9 weeks; (b) medical management of a missed miscarriage at 10 weeks; (c) surgical management of a missed miscarriage at 10 weeks; (d) laparoscopic salpingectomy for an ectopic pregnancy; (e) methotrexate for an ectopic pregnancy?

::: exercise Molar karyotypes {level=1}
Explain how a complete mole can have a normal-looking karyotype of 46,XX and yet be abnormal.
::: solution
In a complete mole, both sets of chromosomes come from the father: an egg that has lost its maternal chromosomes is fertilised by a sperm that duplicates its genome (giving 46,XX), or by two sperm. The count is normal, but there is no maternal genome. Because some genes are expressed only from the maternal or paternal copy (**genomic imprinting**), a purely paternal genome drives overgrowth of trophoblast with no embryo. See [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]].
:::
:::

::: exercise Hyperemesis {level=2}
A woman at 9 weeks has lost 7 kg from a pre-pregnancy weight of 62 kg. She has 3+ ketones in her urine, sodium 131 mmol/L and potassium 3.1 mmol/L, and cannot keep fluids down. Calculate her percentage weight loss and outline her management.
::: solution
Weight loss: $7/62 \approx 11\%$, well above 5%. With dehydration and electrolyte imbalance, she has **hyperemesis gravidarum**.

Management: admit or treat as a day case depending on response; check urine for infection, thyroid function, and arrange an ultrasound to confirm the pregnancy and exclude multiple or molar pregnancy; intravenous normal saline with potassium chloride, guided by repeated electrolytes; **thiamine** before any dextrose; regular anti-emetics, starting with a first-line drug and adding a second-line drug if needed; VTE risk assessment and prophylaxis; and support for her mental health. If she does not respond, corticosteroids may be considered by a senior clinician. She should have a plan for early treatment if symptoms recur.
:::
:::

::: exercise Methotrexate follow-up {level=2}
A woman treated with methotrexate for an ectopic pregnancy has an hCG of 1300 IU/L on day 4 and 1250 IU/L on day 7. Is this satisfactory? What next?
::: solution
The fall from day 4 to day 7 is $(1300 - 1250)/1300 \approx 3.8\%$, well below the expected 15%. Treatment has probably not been effective. She should be reassessed clinically and with ultrasound, and offered a second dose of methotrexate or surgery, according to her symptoms, findings and preference. If at any time she develops significant pain or signs of rupture, she needs urgent surgical assessment.
:::

::: exercise A differential for pain and a positive test {level=3}
A 22-year-old presents with right iliac fossa pain for 12 hours, nausea and a low-grade fever. Her last period was six weeks ago, and her pregnancy test is positive. Give a differential diagnosis and explain how you would distinguish the main possibilities.
::: solution
**Differential diagnosis.** Ectopic pregnancy; miscarriage; acute appendicitis (which occurs in pregnancy and is not excluded by a positive test); a corpus luteum cyst that has bled or ruptured; ovarian torsion (more likely with an enlarged corpus luteum); urinary tract infection or pyelonephritis; and renal colic.

**Distinguishing them.** Transvaginal ultrasound is the key test: it shows whether there is an intrauterine pregnancy, an adnexal mass, free fluid, an ovarian cyst or abnormal ovarian blood flow. Serum hCG helps if the scan is inconclusive. Urinalysis and culture assess for infection. Inflammatory markers and examination (migration of pain, guarding at McBurney's point) help with appendicitis, and ultrasound or MRI of the appendix can be used in pregnancy. Ectopic pregnancy must remain on the list until an intrauterine pregnancy is confirmed. Heterotopic pregnancy (simultaneous intrauterine and ectopic pregnancies) is rare after natural conception but more common after assisted conception.
:::
:::

::: exercise A third miscarriage {level=3}
A 35-year-old woman has had three first-trimester miscarriages. She and her partner ask what will be investigated and what can be done. Outline your answer.
::: solution
**Investigations**: antiphospholipid antibodies on two occasions 12 weeks apart; thyroid function and antibodies; pelvic ultrasound (ideally three-dimensional) for uterine anomalies; cytogenetic testing of tissue from this miscarriage, with parental karyotyping if an unbalanced translocation is found. Lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, BMI) are reviewed.

**Treatment** depends on findings: aspirin and heparin for antiphospholipid syndrome; hysteroscopic resection of a uterine septum in selected cases; referral to clinical genetics for a parental translocation. For unexplained recurrent miscarriage, there is no proven drug treatment, although vaginal progesterone is offered if she bleeds in a future pregnancy (following PRISM). She should be offered supportive care in an early pregnancy unit with early reassurance scans, and told that most couples with unexplained recurrent miscarriage go on to have a successful pregnancy. The emotional impact should be acknowledged and psychological support offered.
:::
:::
