Heavy periods, painful periods, irregular periods and pelvic pain are among the commonest reasons women see a doctor, and among the conditions most often dismissed. Many women are told that heavy or painful periods are normal, and wait years for a diagnosis of endometriosis or for treatment of the iron deficiency that their bleeding has caused. The conditions in this chapter are rarely fatal, but they affect work, education, relationships and fertility, and most of them are treatable.

The approach is the same for each symptom. First establish what is normal, then exclude pregnancy and, where relevant, cancer, then use a classification to work through the causes, and finally offer treatment that matches the woman's priorities: control of bleeding, relief of pain, contraception or fertility. The hormonal control of the cycle is described in [[endocrine/reproductive-endocrinology]]; gynaecological cancers are in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/gynaecological-cancer]].

## The normal menstrual cycle

The FIGO system (2018) describes normal menstruation by four features:

| Feature | Normal |
|---|---|
| Frequency | Every 24 to 38 days |
| Duration | Up to 8 days |
| Regularity | Shortest and longest cycles differ by no more than 7 to 9 days |
| Volume | Not felt by the woman to be heavy |

Bleeding between periods (**intermenstrual bleeding**) and bleeding after intercourse (**postcoital bleeding**) are not normal and need assessment, because they can be caused by cervical or endometrial pathology, including cancer.

## Abnormal uterine bleeding

::: definition Heavy menstrual bleeding and PALM–COEIN {#def-hmb}
**Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB)** is excessive menstrual blood loss that interferes with a woman's physical, social, emotional or material quality of life (NICE NG88, *Heavy menstrual bleeding*, 2018, updated 2021). It is defined by its impact, not by a measured volume. In research, a loss of more than 80 mL per cycle has been used.

The FIGO **PALM–COEIN** system classifies causes of abnormal uterine bleeding in women of reproductive age:
- **Structural (PALM)**: **P**olyp, **A**denomyosis, **L**eiomyoma (fibroid), **M**alignancy and hyperplasia.
- **Non-structural (COEIN)**: **C**oagulopathy, **O**vulatory dysfunction, **E**ndometrial disorders, **I**atrogenic (for example anticoagulants or hormones), and **N**ot otherwise classified.
:::

### Assessment

The history covers the pattern and impact of bleeding (flooding, clots, changing protection at night, time off work), symptoms of anaemia, pain, pressure symptoms, intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding, contraception and plans for pregnancy, medicines, and symptoms of a bleeding disorder. NICE recommends:

- a **full blood count** for all women with HMB, and treatment of iron deficiency;
- testing for **coagulation disorders**, particularly von Willebrand disease, in women who have had HMB since their first period or who have a personal or family history suggesting a bleeding disorder;
- **pelvic ultrasound** if the uterus is palpable abdominally, there is a pelvic mass on examination, or symptoms suggest a structural cause such as fibroids or adenomyosis;
- **outpatient hysteroscopy** if there is suspicion of submucosal fibroids, polyps or endometrial pathology, such as persistent intermenstrual bleeding;
- **endometrial biopsy** in women with risk factors for endometrial cancer, such as persistent intermenstrual bleeding or HMB with treatment failure in women aged 45 or over, or obesity and anovulation at a younger age.

### Iron deficiency

HMB is the commonest cause of iron deficiency anaemia in women of reproductive age, and the arithmetic explains why. Each millilitre of blood with a haemoglobin of 130 g/L contains about 0.45 mg of iron. A woman also loses about 1 mg of iron a day from skin, gut and urine. Her diet typically allows her to absorb only about 1–2 mg a day.

::: example Heavy bleeding and anaemia {#ex-hmb}
A 42-year-old woman has had increasingly heavy periods for two years, with flooding through clothes and clots. She is tired and breathless on stairs. Her periods are regular and she has no intermenstrual bleeding. Her haemoglobin is 92 g/L, mean cell volume 72 fL and ferritin 6 µg/L. On examination the uterus is not palpable abdominally. She does not want more children. How should she be assessed and treated?
::: solution
**Assessment.** She has HMB with **iron deficiency anaemia**. Her regular cycles, absence of intermenstrual bleeding and non-palpable uterus make a major structural cause less likely, but because she is over 40 with symptoms, a pelvic ultrasound is reasonable to assess for fibroids or adenomyosis. If the ultrasound suggests a submucosal fibroid or polyp, outpatient hysteroscopy is indicated. An endometrial biopsy is needed if she has risk factors for endometrial cancer or if first-line treatment fails.

**Treatment.**
1. **Iron**: oral iron (once daily or on alternate days improves absorption and tolerance), with dietary advice; intravenous iron if she cannot tolerate or absorb oral iron or needs rapid correction.
2. **Bleeding**: if the uterus is normal or fibroids are small, NICE recommends the **LNG-IUS** first line; it reduces blood loss substantially and also provides contraception. Alternatives are tranexamic acid or an NSAID such as mefenamic acid during periods (non-hormonal), combined hormonal contraception if she has no contraindications, or cyclical oral progestogens.
3. If medical treatment fails or she prefers surgery, **endometrial ablation** or **hysterectomy** can be discussed.

Her heavy bleeding has caused a measurable health problem, which should be treated rather than accepted as normal.
:::
:::

## Fibroids and adenomyosis

**Fibroids (uterine leiomyomas)** are benign tumours of the myometrium. They are very common, increasing with age until the menopause, and are more common, larger and earlier in onset in Black women. They are described by their position: **submucosal** (projecting into the cavity), **intramural** (within the wall) and **subserosal** (projecting from the outer surface, sometimes on a stalk). Their growth depends on oestrogen and progesterone, so they shrink after the menopause.

Many fibroids cause no symptoms. When they do, the main problems are **heavy menstrual bleeding** (especially with submucosal fibroids), **pressure symptoms** (urinary frequency, constipation, an abdominal mass), and **subfertility or miscarriage**, mainly with submucosal fibroids that distort the cavity. In pregnancy, a fibroid can outgrow its blood supply and undergo **red degeneration**, causing acute pain.

Treatment depends on symptoms, size and position, and whether the woman wants to keep her fertility:
- **Medical**: the treatments for HMB above; **GnRH agonists** shrink fibroids by suppressing ovarian hormones but cause menopausal symptoms and bone loss, so they are used short term, often before surgery; **relugolix combination therapy** (an oral GnRH antagonist with add-back oestradiol and norethisterone) is recommended by NICE for moderate to severe symptoms. Ulipristal acetate is restricted in the UK because of rare severe liver injury.
- **Surgical and radiological**: **hysteroscopic resection** of submucosal fibroids; **myomectomy** (removal of fibroids, preserving the uterus); **uterine artery embolisation**, performed by an interventional radiologist; and **hysterectomy**, which is definitive.

**Adenomyosis** is the presence of endometrial tissue within the myometrium. It causes an enlarged, tender, "boggy" uterus, heavy and painful periods, and is most common in parous women in their forties. It is diagnosed by ultrasound or MRI. Treatment is similar to HMB, with the LNG-IUS often effective; hysterectomy is the only definitive treatment.

## Endometriosis

::: definition Endometriosis {#def-endometriosis}
**Endometriosis** is the presence of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterus, causing a chronic inflammatory reaction. It most often affects the pelvic peritoneum, the ovaries (where it forms cysts called **endometriomas**), the uterosacral ligaments and the pouch of Douglas, and sometimes the bowel, bladder or distant sites. It affects about 1 in 10 women of reproductive age.
:::

::: theorem Endometriosis is oestrogen-dependent {#thm-endometriosis}
Endometriotic lesions grow and bleed in response to the cyclical ovarian hormones, like the endometrium. Treatments that suppress ovulation or menstruation therefore relieve symptoms, and symptoms usually settle after the menopause. Hormonal treatment suppresses the disease but does not cure it, so symptoms often return when treatment stops.
:::

::: proof
The commonest explanation of how endometrial tissue reaches the pelvis is **retrograde menstruation**: menstrual fluid flowing backwards through the fallopian tubes, which happens in most women. In some women, because of immune, genetic or local factors, these cells implant and survive. Other theories, including metaplasia of the peritoneum and spread through blood or lymph, explain lesions at distant sites. Once established, the lesions express oestrogen receptors and, unusually, their own aromatase, which makes oestrogen locally. Each cycle, they proliferate and bleed into the surrounding tissue. The blood and inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins, cause pain, fibrosis and adhesions that tether the pelvic organs together. Removing the cyclical stimulus by suppressing ovulation or inducing amenorrhoea stops this cycle of bleeding and inflammation, which is why combined hormonal contraception, progestogens, the LNG-IUS and GnRH analogues all help. The lesions, however, remain and can be reactivated when ovarian function returns.
:::

**Symptoms** include severe dysmenorrhoea, often starting before the period and lasting throughout it; **deep dyspareunia** (pain deep in the pelvis during intercourse); chronic pelvic pain; painful bowel movements or urination during periods; and subfertility. Some women have no symptoms. The severity of symptoms does not correlate well with the extent of disease. In the UK, the average time from first symptoms to diagnosis is about eight to nine years.

**Diagnosis.** NICE NG73 (*Endometriosis*, 2017, updated 2024) recommends that clinicians suspect endometriosis on symptoms, offer treatment without waiting for a definitive diagnosis, and refer women with severe, persistent or recurrent symptoms. Transvaginal ultrasound can detect endometriomas and deep disease, but a normal scan does not exclude endometriosis. The definitive diagnosis is by **laparoscopy**, ideally with biopsy, which also allows treatment.

**Treatment** depends on the woman's priorities:
- **Pain**: analgesia (NSAIDs and paracetamol); hormonal treatment with combined hormonal contraception (often taken continuously), progestogens or the LNG-IUS; GnRH agonists with add-back HRT if these fail; and laparoscopic **excision or ablation** of lesions. Women with deep endometriosis involving the bowel, bladder or ureter should be referred to a specialist centre.
- **Fertility**: hormonal treatments prevent pregnancy and do not improve fertility. Surgery for minimal or mild disease may improve natural conception rates; IVF is often needed for more severe disease.

::: example Painful periods since school {#ex-endometriosis}
A 27-year-old woman has had painful periods since her teens, now so severe that she misses two days of work each month despite ibuprofen. She also has deep pain during intercourse and painful bowel movements during her periods. She has been trying to conceive for six months. Pelvic examination is tender in the posterior fornix, and transvaginal ultrasound shows a 4 cm cyst with homogeneous low-level echoes in the right ovary. What is the likely diagnosis, and how would you plan her care?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Severe dysmenorrhoea, deep dyspareunia, dyschezia during periods and posterior fornix tenderness suggest **endometriosis**, and the ovarian cyst with "ground-glass" echoes is typical of an **endometrioma**. Painful bowel movements and posterior tenderness raise the possibility of deep disease affecting the rectovaginal septum or bowel.

**Plan.** She should be referred to a gynaecology service with expertise in endometriosis, and specialist imaging (expert ultrasound or MRI) is arranged to map deep disease. Because she is trying to conceive, hormonal treatments that prevent pregnancy are not appropriate at present; pain is managed with analgesia. Her ovarian reserve should be assessed before any ovarian surgery, because removing endometriomas can reduce it. The options of laparoscopic surgery (diagnosis, excision of disease and possibly the endometrioma) or referral for fertility assessment and IVF should be discussed with her and her partner. Her experience of years of pain should be acknowledged, and she should be given information about support organisations.
:::
:::

## Dysmenorrhoea

**Primary dysmenorrhoea** is painful menstruation with no pelvic pathology. It usually starts within a year or two of menarche, once cycles become ovulatory. It is caused by endometrial prostaglandins, which cause uterine contractions and ischaemia. It is treated with NSAIDs, which inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, and hormonal contraception. **Secondary dysmenorrhoea** is caused by pelvic pathology, such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids or pelvic inflammatory disease. Features that suggest a secondary cause include onset years after menarche, pain that is getting worse, deep dyspareunia, intermenstrual bleeding, and failure to respond to NSAIDs and the pill.

## Polycystic ovary syndrome

**Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)** is the commonest endocrine disorder in women of reproductive age, affecting about 10–13%. It combines ovulatory dysfunction, androgen excess and insulin resistance.

::: definition The Rotterdam criteria {#def-pcos}
PCOS is diagnosed in adults when **two of three** features are present, after other causes have been excluded:
1. **Oligo-ovulation or anovulation**, usually shown by irregular cycles.
2. **Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism**: hirsutism, acne or androgenic hair loss, or a raised androgen level such as free testosterone.
3. **Polycystic ovarian morphology** on ultrasound (20 or more follicles in at least one ovary with modern equipment), or, under the 2023 international evidence-based guideline, a raised anti-Müllerian hormone level in adults.

In adolescents, both irregular cycles and hyperandrogenism are required, and ultrasound and AMH are not used, because multiple follicles are normal in the years after menarche.
:::

Other conditions that can present similarly must be excluded: thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia, non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing's syndrome and, if virilisation is rapid or testosterone very high, an androgen-secreting tumour.

The mechanism involves a vicious circle. Insulin resistance leads to high insulin levels, which stimulate ovarian theca cells to produce androgens and reduce the liver's production of sex hormone-binding globulin, increasing free androgen. Increased LH pulse frequency also drives androgen production. High local androgen levels arrest follicle development, so many small follicles accumulate and ovulation does not occur.

**Long-term health.** Women with PCOS have an increased risk of type 2 diabetes and gestational diabetes, cardiovascular risk factors, sleep apnoea, depression and anxiety, and **endometrial hyperplasia and cancer**, because without ovulation the endometrium is exposed to oestrogen without the protective effect of progesterone.

**Management** depends on the woman's concerns:
- **Lifestyle**: weight loss of 5–10% in women with excess weight can restore ovulation and improve metabolic risk.
- **Irregular cycles and endometrial protection**: combined hormonal contraception, cyclical progestogens or the LNG-IUS. Women with very infrequent periods should have a withdrawal bleed induced at least every three to four months, and ultrasound and possibly endometrial biopsy if they have not bled for a long time.
- **Hirsutism and acne**: combined hormonal contraception, cosmetic methods, topical eflornithine for facial hair, and anti-androgens (which must be used with effective contraception because they can feminise a male fetus).
- **Metabolic risk**: regular screening for diabetes (HbA1c or a glucose tolerance test) and cardiovascular risk factors; metformin in selected women.
- **Fertility**: letrozole for ovulation induction ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/contraception-fertility]]).

::: example Irregular periods and acne {#ex-pcos}
A 24-year-old woman has periods every two to four months, persistent acne and dark coarse hair on her chin that she plucks daily. Her BMI is 31. Total testosterone is mildly raised; TSH, prolactin and 17-hydroxyprogesterone are normal; a pregnancy test is negative. Does she have PCOS, and what are the priorities?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** She has oligo-ovulation (cycles of two to four months) and clinical and biochemical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, raised testosterone). That is two of the three Rotterdam criteria, so she has **PCOS** without needing an ultrasound, provided the alternatives are excluded: thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia and non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia have been excluded by her tests. A mildly raised testosterone with a slow history does not suggest a tumour.

**Priorities.** Ask what matters most to her: the hair and acne, her periods, future fertility or her weight. A combined pill would regulate bleeding, protect the endometrium and improve hyperandrogenic symptoms, if she has no contraindications. Lifestyle support for weight loss improves all features. She should be screened for type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular risk factors, and asked about mood and sleep. She should be told that PCOS does not mean she cannot have children: if she wants to conceive later, ovulation induction is usually effective.
:::
:::

## Amenorrhoea

**Primary amenorrhoea** is the absence of periods by age 15 in a girl with normal secondary sexual characteristics, or by 13 with no secondary sexual development. **Secondary amenorrhoea** is the absence of periods for three months in a woman with previously regular cycles, or six months with previously irregular cycles.

The first test is always a **pregnancy test**. The causes can then be organised by level:
- **Hypothalamus**: low body weight, excessive exercise and stress (functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea), with low or normal gonadotrophins and low oestrogen.
- **Pituitary**: hyperprolactinaemia (prolactinoma or drugs such as antipsychotics), and, rarely, other pituitary disease, including Sheehan's syndrome after a severe postpartum haemorrhage.
- **Ovary**: PCOS, premature ovarian insufficiency ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/menopause]]) and, in primary amenorrhoea, Turner syndrome.
- **Uterus and outflow tract**: intrauterine adhesions after surgery or infection (Asherman syndrome), and in primary amenorrhoea congenital anomalies such as an imperforate hymen or absent uterus.

Initial tests are FSH, LH, oestradiol, prolactin, TSH and testosterone, with further tests depending on the results.

::: example No periods for eight months {#ex-amenorrhoea}
A 20-year-old university student who runs about 60 km a week has had no periods for eight months; before that her cycles were regular. Her BMI is 17.5. A pregnancy test is negative. FSH and LH are low-normal, oestradiol is low, and prolactin, TSH and testosterone are normal. Interpret the results and outline the management.
::: solution
**Interpretation.** Secondary amenorrhoea (eight months after regular cycles) with low oestradiol and low-normal gonadotrophins points to a problem above the ovary. With normal prolactin and thyroid function, and a history of low weight and heavy training, the likely diagnosis is **functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea**: low energy availability suppresses the pulsatile release of GnRH. The pattern differs from premature ovarian insufficiency, where FSH is high, and from PCOS, where oestrogen is usually normal and androgens are often raised.

**Management.** The main concern is **bone health**: low oestrogen at an age when bone mass should be increasing leads to low bone density and stress fractures. Management aims to restore energy availability, through increased intake and reduced training, with dietetic support and, where relevant, assessment for an eating disorder, which should be asked about sensitively. A bone density scan is considered if amenorrhoea has lasted six months or more. If periods do not return, specialist advice is needed about hormone replacement; the combined pill produces withdrawal bleeds but does not reliably protect bone and masks recovery. She also needs contraception if pregnancy is to be avoided, because ovulation can return before the first period.
:::
:::

## Vaginal discharge and pelvic inflammatory disease

Physiological discharge varies through the cycle. A change in discharge has three common infective causes: **bacterial vaginosis** (a thin, white, fishy-smelling discharge with a vaginal pH above 4.5; an overgrowth of anaerobes, not a sexually transmitted infection), **vulvovaginal candidiasis** (thick white discharge with itching and soreness; pH normal) and **trichomoniasis** (frothy yellow-green discharge; sexually transmitted). Discharge can also be the presenting feature of chlamydia and gonorrhoea infecting the cervix.

**Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** is infection ascending from the cervix to the endometrium, tubes and ovaries. *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* are important causes, along with *Mycoplasma genitalium* and anaerobes, but often no organism is found. Symptoms are lower abdominal pain, deep dyspareunia, abnormal bleeding and discharge, with cervical motion tenderness and adnexal tenderness on examination. Because the consequences of untreated PID, which include **tubal infertility**, **ectopic pregnancy** and **chronic pelvic pain**, are serious and no test excludes it, treatment is started on clinical suspicion, using an antibiotic regimen that covers the likely organisms, following national guidance (BASHH). Pregnancy must be excluded, and sexual partners need testing and treatment. Severe disease, a tubo-ovarian abscess or pregnancy require admission. See [[infectious-diseases/sexually-transmitted-infections]].

::: warning Ovarian torsion can occur with normal Doppler flow
**Ovarian torsion**, twisting of the ovary on its pedicle, causes sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain, often with nausea and vomiting, and usually occurs in an ovary enlarged by a cyst. It is a surgical emergency because the ovary can infarct. Ultrasound may show an enlarged ovary, but blood flow on Doppler can be normal in a torted ovary, because the twist may obstruct venous and lymphatic drainage before arterial inflow, or may be intermittent. When the clinical picture suggests torsion, the patient needs diagnostic laparoscopy, and the ovary should usually be untwisted and preserved, not removed, even if it looks dark.
:::

## Pelvic organ prolapse

**Pelvic organ prolapse** is the descent of the uterus, bladder (cystocele), rectum (rectocele) or vaginal vault into the vagina. It results from weakening of the pelvic floor muscles and connective tissue supports, with childbirth, ageing, menopause, obesity, chronic cough and constipation as risk factors. Symptoms include a feeling of a lump or dragging in the vagina, urinary and bowel symptoms, and sexual difficulties. Treatment ranges from **pelvic floor muscle training** and vaginal pessaries to surgical repair. Transvaginal mesh for prolapse has been restricted in the UK since 2018 because of serious complications. Urinary incontinence is discussed in [[renal/urinary-tract]].

::: quiz
A 30-year-old woman has a 10-day history of lower abdominal pain, a new vaginal discharge and pain during intercourse. She has a temperature of 37.8 °C, cervical motion tenderness and bilateral adnexal tenderness. A pregnancy test is negative. What is the best next step?
- [ ] Wait for the results of chlamydia and gonorrhoea tests before starting treatment.
- [x] Take swabs and start empirical antibiotic treatment for pelvic inflammatory disease according to national guidance, and arrange partner notification.
- [ ] Arrange a diagnostic laparoscopy before treatment.
- [ ] Reassure her, because negative swabs would exclude PID.
::: solution
This is a typical presentation of PID. Treatment should start on clinical suspicion, after taking swabs, because delay increases the risk of tubal damage, and negative swabs do not exclude the diagnosis. She should be reviewed after about 72 hours, or admitted if she is very unwell, and her partners should be tested and treated.
:::
:::

::: history
Endometriosis was described under various names in the nineteenth century, including by Carl von Rokitansky in 1860, but the modern understanding began with John Sampson in New York, who in 1927 proposed retrograde menstruation as the cause and named endometriomas. The polycystic ovary was described by Irving Stein and Michael Leventhal in Chicago in 1935, in seven women with amenorrhoea, hirsutism and enlarged ovaries, whom they treated by wedge resection of the ovaries; the 2003 Rotterdam consensus of the European and American reproductive medicine societies produced the diagnostic criteria still used, refined in the 2018 and 2023 international guidelines. Uterine artery embolisation for fibroids was first reported by Jacques Ravina in Paris in 1995. The FIGO PALM–COEIN classification, led by Malcolm Munro, was published in 2011 and revised in 2018, to replace inconsistent terms such as "menorrhagia" and "dysfunctional uterine bleeding".
:::

A 35-year-old woman has had painless periods all her life but over the last two years has developed increasingly painful periods, heavier bleeding and an enlarged, tender, globular uterus on examination. What is the likely diagnosis and how is it confirmed?
::: solution
Onset of pain years after menarche, progressive symptoms and an enlarged globular tender uterus point to **secondary dysmenorrhoea**, most likely from **adenomyosis**, possibly with fibroids. ::: exercise Endometrial protection in PCOS {level=2}
A 29-year-old woman with PCOS has had two periods in the last year and does not want hormonal contraception. Why is this a concern, and what would you offer?
::: solution
Without ovulation, there is no progesterone to transform and shed the endometrium, so it is exposed to unopposed oestrogen, increasing the risk of **endometrial hyperplasia and cancer**. She should be offered a progestogen to induce a withdrawal bleed at least every three to four months, or an LNG-IUS, which protects the endometrium without systemic oestrogen. Because she has had long gaps between periods, a transvaginal ultrasound to measure endometrial thickness is reasonable, with endometrial biopsy if it is thickened. Weight loss, if appropriate, may restore ovulation.
:::

::: exercise A woman with fibroids {level=2}
A 38-year-old woman with a 12-week-size fibroid uterus has heavy periods, urinary frequency and an intramural fibroid of 7 cm. She wants to keep the option of pregnancy. Discuss her options.
::: solution
The LNG-IUS is less effective and more often expelled when fibroids are large or distort the cavity, and medical treatments for bleeding (tranexamic acid, NSAIDs, CHC) do not address the pressure symptoms. **Relugolix combination therapy** or a GnRH agonist can shrink the fibroid and reduce bleeding, but are not a long-term solution for someone wanting pregnancy, since they prevent ovulation. Because she wants to keep her fertility, **myomectomy** is usually the preferred definitive option; it carries risks of bleeding (rarely leading to hysterectomy) and of fibroid recurrence, and a later pregnancy may need planned caesarean birth depending on the extent of surgery. **Uterine artery embolisation** preserves the uterus but its effect on fertility is less certain, so it is generally less preferred for women planning pregnancy. Hysterectomy is not appropriate if she wants to keep her fertility.
:::
:::

Using the model in the figure (basal iron loss 1 mg per day, about 0.45 mg of iron per mL of blood, and a 28-day cycle), find the menstrual blood loss per cycle at which the average daily requirement equals an absorption of 1.5 mg per day. Give the answer in mL to the nearest whole number. Comment on what it means.
::: hint
Solve $1 + 0.45x/28 = 1.5$ for $x$.
:::
::: solution
$0.45x/28 = 0.5$, so $x = 0.5 \times 28 / 0.45 \approx 31.1$, about **31 mL** per cycle. Above this level, the model says she loses more iron than she absorbs from a typical diet. Many women lose about this amount, which is why iron deficiency is so common in women of reproductive age even without HMB, and why women with HMB, often losing over 80 mL, so often become anaemic. In reality absorption increases as stores fall, which delays but does not prevent depletion when losses are large.

::: exercise Acute pelvic pain {level=3}
A 19-year-old woman presents with sudden severe right-sided pelvic pain that started while she was playing football, with vomiting. Her pregnancy test is negative. She is afebrile. Ultrasound shows a 7 cm right ovarian cyst with normal arterial Doppler flow. Give a differential diagnosis and your management.
::: solution
**Differential diagnosis.** **Ovarian torsion** is the most important and most likely: sudden severe pain with vomiting, during exertion, with an enlarged ovary containing a large cyst. Normal Doppler flow does not exclude it. Others are haemorrhage into or rupture of the cyst, appendicitis, and, less likely with a negative pregnancy test, ectopic pregnancy. PID is unlikely with this sudden onset and no fever.

**Management.** Analgesia and antiemetics, intravenous access and bloods (full blood count, CRP, group and save), and urgent gynaecological review. Because the clinical picture suggests torsion, she needs **diagnostic laparoscopy** without delay. If the ovary is twisted, it is untwisted, and the cyst may be removed (cystectomy), preserving the ovary even if it looks dusky, because most recover. The cyst is sent for histology. She should be told that the aim is to preserve her ovary and her fertility.
