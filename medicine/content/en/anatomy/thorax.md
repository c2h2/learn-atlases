The thorax is two balloons and a pump in a box, and almost everything clinical about it follows from three facts: the lungs work against a pressure they do not generate, the heart sits between them with its own water-tight compartment, and the box's walls, floor and lid are shared with the abdomen, the neck and the upper limb in ways that mislead clinicians who forget the sharing. Pleural fluid lets the lungs slide; the costodiaphragmatic recess is where fluid collects; the mediastinum is a list of compartments each with its own emergencies; and the diaphragm's three holes are the three most examinable relationships in the body.

This chapter covers the thoracic wall and pleural cavity, the lungs and their segments, the mediastinum, the heart and pericardium, the great vessels, and the diaphragm — with the imaging conventions from [[anatomy/anatomical-language]] applied throughout.

## The thoracic wall and the pleural cavity

::: definition The thoracic wall {#def-wall}
The wall is the rib cage (12 ribs, sternum, thoracic vertebrae), its intercostal muscles in **three layers** — external (fibres down and forwards, "hands in pockets"), internal (down and back), innermost (transverse thoracis and intercostalis intimi) — the **endothoracic fascia** and **parietal pleura**, and outside them the neurovascular plane. Each intercostal space carries a **vein, artery and nerve** (VAN, superior to inferior) in the **costal groove along the inferior border of the upper rib**, with a smaller collateral branch running along the superior border of the lower rib. The space is drained anteriorly by the internal thoracic (mammary) artery behind the costal cartilages and posteriorly by the azygos system — a relationship that makes the internal thoracic the chest's most dangerous haemorrhage and the coronary surgeon's most useful conduit.
:::

The parietal pleura is densely innervated by the intercostal nerves (peripheral parts) and the phrenic nerve (central diaphragm and mediastinal pleura); the visceral pleura and lung parenchyma have no pain fibres, which is why a biopsied lung edge is silent while a pleural peel hurts, and why pleuritic pain is sharp, localised, and — when the central diaphragm is involved — **referred to the C3–5 dermatome over the shoulder** via the phrenic nerve (Kehr's sign, the same referral as a subphrenic abscess or a ruptured spleen, reached from below the diaphragm by the same nerve).

::: definition Pleural reflections and the recesses {#def-pleura}
The **costal pleura** lines the chest wall, the **mediastinal pleura** the mediastinum, the **diaphragmatic pleura** the diaphragm; the reflections are where two parietal regions meet. The lung's borders fall short of the pleural reflections, and the gap — the **costodiaphragmatic recess** — contains nothing but potential space and a thin film of lubricating fluid normally, and is where free fluid collects first when the patient is upright. The classic surface lines (midclavicular, midaxillary, paravertebral): the **lower border of the lung** crosses rib 8, 10 and 12 respectively; the **pleural reflections** cross rib 6 (or the xiphoid/2nd costal junction level medially), 8 and 10 — **two ribs lower than the lung throughout**, which is why "the chest is dry on auscultation" and "the recess is empty" are different statements. The **costomediastinal recess** behind the sternum does the same service for air and small fluid collections.
:::

::: example Safe thoracentesis {#ex-thoracentesis}
A 2 L right pleural effusion is to be drained in the medical ward.
::: solution
The anatomy writes the checklist. Sit the patient up and lean forward — fluid falls into the **dependent costodiaphragmatic recess**. Find the **upper border of the rib**, not the lower border of the space above it: the intercostal nerve, artery and vein run in the costal groove at the **inferior border of the rib above**, so the needle that rides the rib's upper edge passes under the neurovascular bundle, not through it (the collateral branch along the lower rib's upper border is small and variable — accept that residual risk). Aim for the **posterior axillary or scapular line at the 7th–9th space**, ultrasound-confirmed: ultrasound both picks the pocket and measures the fluid depth, and the British Thoracic Society practice is to tap nothing that is not imaged, since a blind tap of a basal collection risks the **liver or spleen through the elevated diaphragm** — the diaphragm on the affected side may sit at the level of the nipple. Aspirate slowly and stop at pain, cough or the manometer floor: **re-expansion pulmonary oedema** after >1.5 L in one sitting is the reason guidelines cap the volume and the reason the patient's cough is a useful sign. The needle's route, from outside in, is the lesson's syllabus: skin → superficial fascia → serratus/extrinsic muscle layer → three intercostal layers → endothoracic fascia → parietal pleura → pleural cavity, traversing — at that rib's upper border and only there — the intercostal space free of its bundle.
:::
:::

::: history
Empyema is among the oldest operated diseases: the Hippocratic corpus describes counter-opening and drainage of collections of the chest, and military surgery kept thoracic drainage alive through centuries in which penetrating chest injury was a near-certain death from infection. The modern rules — the rib's upper border, the dependent recess, ultrasound guidance, capped volumes — were each paid for: the intercostal drain's place in trauma surgery was settled by the world wars' chest units, and the shift from blind to ultrasound-guided pleural procedures by the controlled series of the 1990s and 2000s, which showed how many "safe" landmarks hide a raised diaphragm or a shrunken lung.
:::

## Lungs, airways and bronchopulmonary segments

::: definition The lungs and their airways {#def-lungs}
The **right main bronchus** is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left — the reason aspirated objects prefer the right side, and the reason the right lung handles marginally more ventilation. The trachea divides at the **sternal angle (T4/5)**; the right bronchus gives off an **eparterial (superior lobar) bronchus** above the pulmonary artery before entering the hilum — "eparterial" is the definition, and the left superior lobar bronchus lies below (hyparterial) its artery. Each main bronchus subdivides into **bronchopulmonary segments** — 10 on the right, 8–10 on the left (many pairs share a common stem) — pyramidal units with their own segmental bronchus, artery and connective-tissue septa, lying between segments that have **no arterial anastomoses**; the veins, by contrast, run **in the intersegmental planes** and drain two segments each. The lung parenchyma itself is a branching epithelial tree — conducting airways to the terminal bronchioles (no gas exchange), respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts to the ~300 million alveoli — with its own blood supply, innervation and lymphatics described in [[respiratory/mechanics-breathing]].
:::

The segmental architecture is the reason thoracic surgeons can remove a lung's cancer with its segment and leave the rest: a **segmentectomy** follows the plane between segments — outlined by inflating the lung after dividing the segmental bronchus (the affected segment stays collapsed, "inflation-deflation line") and by the intersegmental veins that mark the boundary. It is also why infections tend to respect segment boundaries and why a postero-basal consolidation on the chest film maps to a named segment and a named bronchus to be chased at bronchoscopy.

::: example The aspirated peanut {#ex-aspiration}
A two-year-old is brought 20 minutes after choking on a peanut; intermittent cough, reduced air entry right side, wheeze heard best over the right lower chest. Chest X-ray is reported "normal; no air trapping seen on expiration".
::: solution
The X-ray does not exclude the diagnosis; the anatomy predicts where to look and the physiology says why the film can be normal early. The peanut has most likely passed the right main bronchus and sits in the **right bronchus intermedius or a lower-lobe segmental bronchus**: a child's airways are small enough for a peanut to occlude a lobar bronchus yet the obstruction can act as a **ball-valve**, letting air in past the peanut during the positive transpulmonary pressures of inspiration while blocking expiration — hyperinflation that appears on expiratory or lateral decubitus films, not on the inspiratory PA view. Persistent unilateral hyperinflation or a fixed wheeze unresponsive to bronchodilator in a young child is **rigid or flexible bronchoscopy** territory: the extraction of organic foreign bodies (a peanut's oil causes a brisk chemical bronchitis within days, making later extraction harder and the segment more damaged). The lesson: aspiration is a diagnosis of history and anatomy, and "the X-ray was normal" answers a question no one asked.
:::
:::

## The mediastinum and its compartments

::: definition Mediastinal divisions and contents {#def-mediastinum}
The **mediastinum** — the space between the two pleural sacs from sternum to vertebræ — is divided at the **transverse thoracic plane (sternal angle to the T4/5 disc)** into **superior** (above: great vessels, trachea, oesophagus, thoracic duct, thymus remnant) and **inferior**, the latter subdivided by the pericardium into **anterior** (loose tissue, remnants, lymphatics — and the site of the classic "4 Ts": thymoma, teratoma/germ-cell tumour, "terrible" lymphoma, thyroid), **middle** (heart, pericardium, lung roots, phrenic nerves), and **posterior** (descending aorta, azygos and hemiazygos system, oesophagus, thoracic duct, vagus nerves, sympathetic trunks). The plane is not academic: it is the level at which the arch begins, the trachea bifurcates, the thoracic duct crosses to the left, and the azygos vein arches over the right lung root to enter the superior vena cava.
:::

The lung **roots** are held by reflections continuous with mediastinal pleura; the arrangement of their contents is a named exam favourite and a surgical orientation: on the **right**, bronchus posterior, pulmonary artery anterior, pulmonary veins inferior and anterior (**RALS**: on the **R**ight the pulmonary **A**rtery is **A**nterior to the bronchus; on the **L**eft it is **S**uperior); hilum structures are surrounded by **bronchopulmonary lymph nodes**, which is why TB and sarcoid enlarge them and why a carcinoma here meets the mediastinum's nodal stations on its path.

::: example A tumour in the apex {#ex-pancoast}
A 62-year-old smoker has three months of right shoulder and scapular pain, wasting of the right hand's small muscles, a drooping right eyelid with a small constricted pupil, and hoarseness.
::: solution
Each sign localises to one mediastinal-limb interface and the set localises to one square centimetre — the **superior sulcus (Pancoast) tumour** of the lung apex. **Horner's syndrome** (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) is the **stellate ganglion / cervical sympathetic chain**, C8–T1 level, invaded posteromedially; **C8–T1 pain and hand intrinsic wasting** is the **lower trunk of the brachial plexus** as it crosses the neck of the first rib; **hoarseness** is the **right recurrent laryngeal nerve** hooking the subclavian artery in the groove behind the clavicle (on the left, the nerve recurs under the aortic arch, so left vocal-cord palsy raises aortic and mediastinal causes — a lesion in the aortopulmonary window). The diagnosis is an apical lung carcinoma until proven otherwise, and the staging logic is mediastinal: nodal stations and the great vessels define what the surgeon can reach. Pancoast tumours are treated by combined chemoradiotherapy and, when resectable, extended resection involving chest wall and occasionally vertebral bodies — so the anatomist's three names (sympathetic chain, lower trunk, recurrent laryngeal) are also the prognosis: lid and pupil can be excised; a paralysed hand and hoarse cord are the boundaries of curative surgery.
:::
:::

::: warning The thoracic duct and chylothorax
The **thoracic duct** rises from the **cisterna chyli** (L2 level, behind the crura), enters the posterior mediastinum through the aortic hiatus between aorta and azygos vein, ascends on the right crossing to the left at about T5, and drains into the **junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins**. Injury gives **chyle** in the chest — white, triglyceride-rich, and immunologically costly — classically after oesophagectomy or thoracic trauma, with the side of the collection following the duct's crossing (right below T5, left above). Recognising it is a fluid-analysis exercise (pleural triglycerides) and managing it is surgical: thoracic duct ligation is one of the operations where the pre-operative anatomy is a line on a diagram and the operating field is the posterior mediastinum as described above.
:::

## Heart, pericardium and the auscultation areas

::: definition The heart in the chest {#def-heart}
The heart lies in the **middle mediastinum**, rotated so that the **right ventricle forms most of the anterior (sternocostal) surface**, the **left ventricle the left border and apex** (left 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line — the apex beat), the **left atrium the posterior surface** (immediately in front of the oesophagus, hence dysphagia from a huge left atrium and the transoesophageal echocardiographic view), and the **right atrium the right border**. The **base** (posterosuperior, mainly left atrium) receives the pulmonary veins; the **apex** is the ventricular tip. The four valves lie in one plane facing forwards and rightward, but blood flows through them in the direction of the oncoming stream, so their **auscultation areas** are where the flow reaches the chest wall — aortic focus right 2nd intercostal space (the ascending aorta's line of flow), **pulmonary** left 2nd intercostal space, **tricuspid** left lower sternal border, **mitral** the apex.
:::

::: theorem You listen downstream, not at the door {#thm-downstream}
Valve sounds are conducted along the direction of the jet they generate, so the surface marking of a valve (its anatomic ring, projected on the chest behind the sternum) and its auscultation area (downstream in the bloodstream) differ. The aortic valve lies behind the middle of the sternum at the level of the 3rd costal cartilage; aortic stenosis is best heard in the right second space and radiates to the carotids because that is where the systolic jet is heading.
:::

::: proof
Take the two extremes. The **mitral valve** ring projects behind the sternum around T7–8 in the recumbent patient; yet its lesion is judged at the **apex**, because the regurgitant jet of mitral incompetence is driven posteriorly and laterally by ventricular systole — and to the **axilla and back**, because in flail posterior leaflet lesions the jet is directed anteriorly into the left atrium's body and out through its ostium, and the axilla and the interscapular region is where that stream's turbulence is heard. **Aortic stenosis** radiates upward along the carotids — the same physical fact that lets an echocardiographer put a Doppler cursor in the neck when the cardiac windows are poor — while the systolic murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, generated along the left-ventricular outflow tract towards the base but modified by the late-peaking dynamic obstruction, is heard between them and increases with the manoeuvres that reduce preload. Surface anatomy, in other words, is the physics of a jet projected onto the chest wall. The one true rule is the auscultator's map: aortic 2nd right space, pulmonary 2nd left, tricuspid 4th–5th left sternal, mitral 5th left midclavicular — and the reason each name sits where it does is the theorem, not the valve's geography.
:::

::: definition The pericardium {#def-pericardium}
The **fibrous pericardium** is an inextensible bag continuous with the central tendon of the diaphragm (below) and the great-vessel adventitia (above), lined by **serous pericardium** — parietal layer (with the fibrous layer) and visceral layer (**epicardium**, on the heart) — with a potential space holding 10–50 mL of fluid. Its inextensibility is the whole of tamponade physiology: fluid accumulating **fast** (blood after trauma, free rupture, dissection into the pericardium) raises intrapericardial pressure steeply, right atrial and ventricular diastolic filling fail, and **Beck's triad** — hypotension, muffled heart sounds, raised jugular venous pressure — describes the collapse; fluid accumulating **slowly** (malignancy, uraemia, hypothyroidism) allows litres to collect with only dyspnoea, and the earliest electrical signs (low voltage, **electrical alternans** as the heart swings in its fluid sac) precede the physiology. Pain arises from the **phrenic-innervated parietal pericardium**, which is why acute pericarditis refers to the trapezius ridge — pathognomonic, because only phrenic referral reaches that patch of C3–5 skin.
:::

::: example The neck veins that say "not a pneumothorax" {#ex-tamponade}
A road-traffic collision, blunt chest trauma, SBP 80 mmHg, HR 130, equal air entry both sides, distended neck veins, muffled sounds.
::: solution
This is **obstructive shock with the tamponade triad**, and the anatomy decides the immediate action. Equal air entry excludes tension pneumothorax (the other obstructive-shock emergency — which has the same hypotension and neck veins but **absent breath sounds and displaced trachea** on the affected side); no dullness of a haemothorax; and the muffled heart sounds complete the triad. The treatment is **pericardial decompression** — needle pericardiocentesis (subxiphoid, aimed at the left shoulder, the needle's path through central tendon to the right ventricle's anterior surface avoiding the lung's anterior borders and the internal thoracic vessels) as a bridge, and **thoracotomy or resuscitative sternotomy** if the patient arrests, because blunt or penetrating trauma with tamponade usually means a bleeding chamber needing suture, not drainage. The surgically relevant facts behind this: the heart's anterior surface is reachable without entering either pleura (the pericardium lies bare behind the lower sternum — the "bare area" that makes subxiphoid and anterior thoracotomy routes possible), and the chambers under the sternum are right ventricle first — which is where the needle and the surgeon's finger arrive.
:::
:::

The heart's own blood supply follows the grooves: the **right coronary artery** in the right atrioventricular groove gives the **inferior (posterior) descending** in 85% (right dominance) and supplies the **SA node** in ~60% and the **AV node** in ~90% — the anatomical reason **inferior myocardial infarction** presents with bradycardia and heart block; the **left anterior descending** down the anterior interventricular groove supplies most of the septum (its occlusion causes the anterior infarct with bundle-branch block and apical aneurysm), and the **left circumflex** in the left AV groove supplies the lateral wall (its occlusion is the "electrically quiet" lateral infarct). The venous return is mostly via the **coronary sinus** in the posterior AV groove to the right atrium — the cannulation target for left-sided pacing leads and cardioplegia.

## Great vessels, lymphatics and the diaphragm

The **arch of the aorta** (T4/5 plane, from right brachiocephalic vein's formation to the descending aorta at the ligamentum arteriosum) gives, in order, **brachiocephalic, left common carotid, left subclavian**; its branches and relations explain the aortic-aneurysm catalogue of pressure effects — SVC obstruction, dysphagia (oesophagus), hoarseness (left recurrent laryngeal), and rib erosion. The **thoracic aorta** descends left of the midline, crosses to midline by T12, and gives posterior intercostals (whose dilated collaterals cause rib notching in coarctation), bronchial, oesophageal and pericardial branches. Aortic coarctation sits, in the common adult form, at or just beyond the **isthmus** near the ligamentum arteriosum — hence the collaterals that enlarge the intercostals, and the radio-radial and radio-femoral delay whose pulse comparison is literally the arch's branches versus its descending continuation.

::: definition The diaphragm and its openings {#def-hiatus}
The muscular diaphragm has a **central tendon** (the future tendon comes from the septum transversum), **sternal, costal and lumbar parts** (lumbar fibres forming the **median, medial and lateral arcuate ligaments** over psoas and quadratus lumborum), and is innervated by the **phrenic nerves (C3–5)** with sensory innervation of the central surface also phrenic and the periphery intercostal. The three great openings:

| Opening | Level | Passes |
|---|---|---|
| **Caval (central tendon)** | **T8** | Inferior vena cava, right phrenic nerve |
| **Oesophageal (right crus)** | **T10** | Oesophagus, vagal trunks |
| **Aortic (behind median arcuate ligament)** | **T12** | Aorta; thoracic duct and azygos vein pass behind the ligament, not "through" the hiatus |

The mnemonic: the **vena cava** (8 letters) at **T8**, the **oesophagus** (10 letters) at **T10**, the **aortic hiatus** (12 letters) at **T12** — with the caveat that the aorta is not truly "through" the muscle, which is why aortic injury at the hiatus is a retroperitoneal/mediastinal affair and why the thoracic duct and azygos join the aorta behind the ligament.
:::

::: example Shoulder pain and a hole in the wrong place {#ex-hiatus}
A 70-year-old has chronic post-prandial retrosternal burning and regurgitation; a barium swallow shows a gastric fold above the diaphragm; a different patient, 40 years post-blunt trauma, presents with bowel sounds in the chest.
::: solution
The first is a **hiatus hernia**, whose two types are the anatomy of the oesophageal hiatus itself: in the **sliding** hernia (95%) the gastro-oesophageal junction migrates up through a lax phrenic-oesophageal membrane, taking the cardia with it — the anti-reflux machinery (the sling of left crus, the acute angle of His, the intra-abdominal segment) is defeated, and reflux follows; in the **rolling (para-oesophageal)** hernia the junction stays put and the fundus herniates beside it — reflux is not the danger, **gastric volvulus and strangulation** are, and that is why a large symptomatic para-oesophageal hernia is repaired while an asymptomatic slide is not. The second patient's "bowel sounds in the chest" after a remote motor accident is a **diaphragmatic rupture**, most often left-sided (the right is protected by the liver), missed at the original injury because the defect is a hole in a muscle that ultrasound and plain films do not interrogate well, and declaring itself years later when abdominal viscera migrate into the thorax and strangulate: a chest film with a nasogastric tube coiling above the diaphragm ends the debate, and the repair needs the abdomen or thorax, not an endoscope. Both cases turn on the T10 hiatus's design: a muscular ring that is competent only when its crura, its membrane and its pressures work together.
:::

::: quiz
On a postero-anterior chest radiograph, which structure forms the dominant part of the left heart border at its inferior third?
- [ ] Left atrial appendage
- [x] Left ventricle
- [ ] Right ventricle
- [ ] Pulmonary trunk
::: solution
The PA film's left border from above down: **aortic knuckle, pulmonary trunk, left auricle (small), left ventricle** — the ventricle dominates the lower two-thirds and its apex is the lowest point. The **right** border is **right atrium** (lower half) and SVC (upper), which is why cardiac enlargement on a frontal film means "left atrium pushing into the right side" (double contour) or a ventricle extending the left border; and the reason the right ventricle — largest part of the anterior surface — is **not seen on the border at all** but only as retrosternal filling on the lateral film. The auscultation rule applies again to imaging: the surface silhouette is where the chamber lies against the lung, not where the chamber sits in space.
:::

::: summary
- The intercostal bundle runs in the costal groove along the **inferior border of the upper rib**: ride the lower rib's upper edge and you miss it — the basis of safe drainage and nerve blocks.
- The pleura reflects two ribs lower than the lung: the recess is a real space, fluid collects there first, and the diaphragm's height makes the "safe" lines unsafe without ultrasound.
- Airway anatomy is destiny for aspiration (right, vertical, wide) and for surgery (segments with their own bronchus and artery, veins in between); bronchoscopy and segmentectomy are applied segmental anatomy.
- The mediastinum is a compartment list, and its syndromes are pressure effects: Pancoast at the apex, recurrent laryngeal in the aortopulmonary window, chylothorax from the duct's crossing, dysphagia from the left atrium behind the oesophagus.
- The heart's surfaces, chambers, auscultation areas and ECG silhouettes are all the same rotated organ seen from different directions; listen downstream, image the border that touches the lung, and refer pain by nerve (phrenic → shoulder, T1 → forearm, intercostal → belt).
- Diaphragm: T8 cava in its tendon, T10 oesophagus in the right crus, T12 aorta behind the median arcuate ligament — and the hiatus's design explains both reflux and the strangulating para-oesophageal hernia.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise The nerve's path {level=1}
Trace the left recurrent laryngeal nerve from nucleus to larynx, and list three lesions that paralyse the left vocal cord.
::: solution
Nucleus in the **nucleus ambiguus** of the medulla → vagus through the jugular foramen → in the carotid sheath → crosses the aortic arch **posterior to the ligamentum arteriosum**, where it hooks under the arch and ascends in the tracheo-oesophageal groove → supplies all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid (superior laryngeal) → sensory below the vocal cords. Three left-cord lesions: **aortic arch aneurysm or dissection** at the ligamentum, **apical/mediastinal lung or nodal malignancy** in the aortopulmonary window, and **thyroid surgery** (the nerve's groove relation to Berry's ligament — surgically the most famous relation in endocrine anatomy). Unilateral palsy gives hoarseness and a weak cough; bilateral palsy is an airway emergency with cords paramedian.
:::
:::

::: exercise Chest-film borders {level=1}
Name the structure whose enlargement produces: (a) double density on the right heart border; (b) prominent left hilar "knuckle" shadow; (c) loss of the right tracheal angle with retrosternal opacity on the lateral film; (d) splaying of the carina.
::: solution
(a) **Left atrium** — the chamber behind the oesophagus projects to the right as a double contour (and its appendage bulges the left border's middle segment in mitral stenosis); confirm with a lateral/barium view showing oesophageal compression. (b) **Main or left pulmonary artery** dilation (pulmonary hypertension, left-to-right shunts). (c) **Right ventricular enlargement** filling the retrosternal space on the lateral film, and (d) splayed carina (>100°) again means **left atrial enlargement** — the carina is the roof of the left atrium's neighbourhood — or subcarinal nodes (TB, lymphoma, metastases). Each answer is a chamber or vessel read through its lung interface: the radiograph sees surfaces, and this chapter's chamber geography turns surfaces back into volumes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Where does the tube go? {level=1}
A chest drain is inserted in the right 5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line, "over the rib". For a simple effusion this is acceptable for air and fluid; describe what your tube's path traverses and the two structures at risk if the insertion is too low and one if too high.
::: solution
The path: skin → superficial fascia → latissimus/serratus territory of the thoracic wall → external, internal and innermost intercostals → endothoracic fascia → parietal pleura → pleural cavity, riding the **superior border of the lower rib** to miss the VAN bundle. Too low (below the level of the dome, which in a distressed patient may sit at the 5th space mid-axillary line): **liver** on the right, **spleen** on the left; too high and anterior, **lung parenchyma** is the risk when the tube is pushed into a consolidated or adherent lung rather than a free space — hence the finger-first rule (the examining finger distinguishes lung surface and adhesions from empty space) and the imaging rule (the dome is where the diaphragm says it is, not where the textbook says it is). The same answer also names the collateral's residual risk at the lower rib's upper border: a small vessel, a larger haematoma in a coagulopathic patient, and the reason intercostal drainage is done with the patient's clotting corrected when time allows.
:::
:::

::: exercise Territory and rhythm {level=2}
An inferior STEMI with ST elevation in II, III and aVF develops 2:1 AV block with a junctional escape at 45/min. Which artery is implicated, and what is the anatomical explanation for the block?
::: solution
**Right coronary artery** (in the ~85% right-dominant circulation, the inferior wall, and in ~90% the **AV node artery** arising at the crux). The AV node is supplied by the AV-nodal branch from the posterior descending/posterior AV-groove region, so inferior infarction produces **nodal ischaemia and block at the node** — narrow-complex, junctional or low-atrial escape, usually transient and atropine-responsive, often vagally reinforced (Bezold-Jarisch). Contrast: anterior infarction with new right bundle-branch block means **infra-Hisian** damage (septal perforators), wide escape, and a poor prognosis requiring pacing — the same rhythm label ("heart block") with two anatomical stories, which is why the ECG's QRS width and the infarct territory, not the block ratio, decide the management.
:::
:::

::: exercise The widened mediastinum {level=2}
A blunt trauma film shows a widened superior mediastinum. List the anatomically plausible differential (five), and say which single anatomical fact makes traumatic aortic injury the first thing to exclude.
::: solution
Differential: **aortic injury at the isthmus**; mediastinal haematoma from sternal/rib fractures or vertebral injury; distended great veins (supine, tense patient — a positional pseudo-widening); thymus (young children); lymphadenopathy (incidental); oesophageal rupture with mediastinal gas. The anatomical fact: the aorta is fixed at the **ligamentum arteriosum** and the diaphragm, so deceleration concentrates shear at the isthmus — the classic site of traumatic rupture — and its contained mediastinal haematoma is the radiographic sign before free rupture into the pleura kills on the ward. Management follows the anatomy: CT angiography (contrast in the arch, the isthmus interrogated in the arterial phase) and, once confirmed, blood-pressure control (reduce dP/dt) before endovascular repair — the arch's branch list deciding how the stent graft lands and which left subclavian has to be covered or revascularised.
:::
:::

::: exercise A pulseless chest {level=2}
A 30-year-old has BP 190/100 in the arms, BP 120 systolic in the legs, radio-femoral delay, systolic murmur to the left interscapular area; the chest film shows inferior notching of ribs 6–8. Explain each finding with one sentence each.
::: solution
This is **coarctation of the aorta**, typically just beyond the left subclavian at the isthmus: (1) hypertension in the arms is the upstream pressure the narrowing forces the left ventricle to generate; (2) relative hypotension in the legs is the downstream pressure; (3) radio-femoral delay is the pulse arriving through the collaterals and the narrow segment; (4) the posterior-interscapular murmur is flow across the coarctation and its collaterals heard where the aorta runs; (5) rib notching is **dilated, pulsatile posterior intercostal arteries** (aorta to aorta via internal thoracic → anterior intercostals → posterior intercostals bypassing the obstruction) eroding the rib's undersides — the radiograph's own record of collateral anatomy, which is also why these patients bleed torrentially at thoracotomy and why the surgeon's pre-operative map is the intercostal story this chapter drew.
:::

::: exercise Pain localisation by nerve {level=2}
Explain the referral by naming the nerve and segment: (a) central diaphragmatic irritation → shoulder tip; (b) myocardial ischaemia → left arm medial side; (c) lower lobe pleurisy → upper abdomen; (d) a Pancoast tumour → medial arm.
::: solution
(a) **Phrenic nerve, C3–5** — the shoulder's suprapclavicular skin is C3–4 (supraclavicular nerves), the same segments, hence Kehr's sign (splenic rupture, subphrenic abscess, pericarditis). (b) Cardiac **T1–T5 (mainly T1–4 via the sympathetic chain and the middle/inferior cervical ganglia to the stellate)** with the medial arm's **T1/intercostobrachial, medial cutaneous nerve of arm (T1–2)** territory overlapping — pain crosses to the ulnar forearm and little finger because those dermatomes share the cord segments. (c) The parietal pleura of the costal lower chest is supplied by the **lower intercostal nerves (T7–12)**, and those segments supply the abdominal wall and are conflated with gut pain in the cortex — pleurisy above the 6th rib rarely refers to the abdomen (the upper intercostal nerves stay in thoracic dermatomes), which is the diagnostic border to remember. (d) The tumour invades the **lower trunk of the brachial plexus (C8–T1)**, whose territory is the medial forearm and hand — the same segment set as cardiac referral, but with **wasting, Horner's syndrome and a palpable apex lesion** marking it as a surgical not visceral problem.
:::

::: exercise Plan the procedure {level=3}
An 18-year-old with a 30% primary spontaneous pneumothorax is managed with aspiration then, after failure, a chest drain and pleurodesis. Justify the anatomy at each step: needle site, tube site, the pleurodesis requirement, and the two long-term anatomical risks of the alternative (surgical bleb resection via thoracoscopy).
::: solution
Needle aspiration for air: **2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line** — apical free air in an upright patient, and the lung's anterior border at the sternum's edge means a low or medial needle enters the costomediastinal recess not the cavity, while the internal thoracic vessels sit one finger-breadth lateral to the sternal border (stay outside that line). Tube: **5th space, anterior to mid-axillary line, in the "triangle of safety"** (medial border pectoralis major's edge laterally, the lateral border of latissimus posteriorly, the apex at the nipple line (4th space in men, inframammary in women) — a region of chest wall where the intercostal artery's anterior branch is absent, no axillary tail to damage, and a drain sits in the pleural space's dependent-for-air zone even when supine). Pleurodesis works only if the **lung fully re-expands** to appose visceral and parietal pleura — the space must be obliterated before talc can inflame two surfaces together — hence the drain's position and suction, and the failed re-expansion ("trapped lung") that forces decortication. Surgical risks in the same anatomical frame: thoracoscopic resection of apical blebs risks the **stellate ganglion and sympathetic chain (Horner's)** posteriorly and the **internal thoracic vessels** medially; and post-operative intercostal neuralgia along the drain's tract or port site is a known price of the approach — a named nerve, in a named space, following the same rule that has governed every needle in this chapter: know the bundle, ride the rib, respect the diaphragm.
:::
