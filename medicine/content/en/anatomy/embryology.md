Embryology is the anatomy of time. Every structure described in this course — the four chambers that came from one tube, the gut that spun through its own blood supply, the testis that crossed the abdomen to reach the scrotum — is an arrested or completed stage of a process that runs, in outline, over eight weeks, and goes wrong in ways that have proper names and recurrence risks. Around one in thirty-three children is born with a clinically significant congenital anomaly; a further fraction of adult disease (bicornuate uteri found at caesarean, a duplicated collecting system found on imaging, a divided heart found in middle age) is embryology discovered late. And the obstetrician, the paediatric surgeon, the geneticist and the teratologist all speak the same language: "organogenesis", "neural crest", "week six".

This lesson walks the process in its clinical order: germ layers, folding and tube closure, the pharyngeal apparatus, the organs whose errors present most often, the fetal-placental circulation at birth, and the rules governing what harms the embryo when.

## From zygote to implantation: the first fortnight

::: definition Fertilisation through implantation {#def-fertilisation}
**Fertilisation** — fusion of gametes — normally occurs in the **ampulla of the fallopian tube**. The zygote cleaves during its 3–4 day transit to the uterus (morula, 16 cells), fluid enters to form the **blastocyst**: an outer **trophoblast** (future placenta) and an inner **embryoblast** (future embryo) with a primitive cavity. The blastocyst **implants** in the uterine fundus or body around **day 6–7**, trophoblast differentiating into cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast, the latter eroding endometrial capillaries and secreting **hCG**, which rescues the corpus luteum and its progesterone output — the biochemical basis of every pregnancy test and of the luteal phase's maintenance until the placenta takes over around week 8–10. By day 14 the bilaminar disc (epiblast, hypoblast) is in place, and the embryonic — not fetal — period proper begins.
:::

::: example Twinning, and why it is an obstetric emergency question {#ex-twins}
An ultrasound at 12 weeks reports "dichorionic, diamniotic twins". At 26 weeks a different scan reports "monochorionic, diamniotic twins" in another patient, and the team begins fortnightly surveillance.
::: solution
Chorionicity — how many placentas — is set by the **timing of splitting**, and it determines everything that follows. Division within 72 hours of fertilisation (before the trophoblast forms) gives **dichorionic, diamniotic** twins: separate placentas (or fused but separate territories), each in its own sac; this is also the pattern of every triplet-or-more reduction question and of the shared-placenta-but-separate cases that are not truly shared. Division on days 4–8, after trophoblast but before amnion formation, gives **monochorionic, diamniotic** twins — one shared placenta with vascular anastomoses in 95% or more of cases, which is the soil for **twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome** (unbalanced arterio-venous shunting: donor hypovolaemic and oliguric with oligohydramnios, recipient volume-overloaded with polyhydramnios) and for the selective growth restriction that fortnightly surveillance exists to catch. Division after day 8 gives monoamnionic twins (rare, dangerous cord entanglement), and division after day 13 is incomplete — conjoined twins. Two practical consequences: the **chorion count must be established by 14 weeks** (after that, fused placentas cannot be reliably separated on ultrasound, and the label "dichorionic" read from a thick lambda peak is the whole surveillance plan for the pregnancy); and **monozygotic does not equal identical in the medicolegal sense** — monochorionic twins share DNA yet differ epigenetically and can differ in phenotype, an important counselling point when "identical" twins are discordant for a malformation.
:::
:::

## Gastrulation and the three-line constitution

::: definition Gastrulation {#def-gastrulation}
At the start of **week 3**, cells of the epiblast migrate through the **primitive streak** on the bilaminar disc's surface, displacing and replacing hypoblast to form the **three primary germ layers**: **ectoderm** (surface and nervous system), **mesoderm** (muscle, bone, connective tissue, blood and endothelium, kidney and gonad stroma), and **endoderm** (gut lining and its glandular outgrowths — respiratory, liver, pancreas, thyroid). The cranial end of the streak thickens as the **notochordal process**, whose tip — the **prechordal plate** — marks the future mouth, and alongside it the neural plate is induced to form the nervous system: **induction**, one tissue's signal dictating another's fate, the central concept of the field (demonstrated in the classic transplantation experiments of Spemann and Mangold, 1924).
:::

::: theorem Germ-layer destiny explains adult pathology {#thm-germlayers}
A lesion's tissue composition and location betray its layer of origin: teratomas contain derivatives of all three layers (they arise from totipotent cells); a Meckel's diverticulum contains ectopic gastric mucosa because it is a remnant of the vitelline duct, an endodermal tube; branchial cleft cysts line the neck because the pharyngeal apparatus is endoderm and ectoderm meeting; and carcinomas arise from epithelium (endoderm or ectoderm) while sarcomas arise from mesoderm. When pathology seems to ignore anatomy, ask what the tissue was doing at week four.
:::

::: proof
The rule works as a diagnostic generator. A midline neck mass that moves with swallowing and tongue protrusion is a **thyroglossal cyst** because the thyroid gland descends from the foramen cecum at the tongue base through the thyroglossal duct, dragging its attachment; the same embryological statement explains why the surgery (Sistrunk procedure) removes the midportion of the hyoid bone — the duct passes with it. An anterior mediastinal mass in a young adult that contains hair, teeth and epithelium is a **teratoma** because totipotent cells (from the primitive streak region, or migrated germ cells) can organise all three layers into exactly that chaos. A cyst in the lateral neck along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid, discovered in adulthood after an upper respiratory infection, is a **branchial cleft cyst** — a buried pocket of the second pharyngeal cleft, whose normal fate is to be overgrown by the second arch and obliterated; its adult presentation is textbook because its embryology is a countdown to disappearance. And the **distribution of a teratoma's tissues** follows the migration routes of primordial germ cells (midline structures: sacrococcygeal, mediastinal, pineal), an observation that turned a curiosum into a map of cell migration. None of these inferences needs a textbook's illustrations; each follows from the germ-layer constitution plus the migration routes that this lesson's remaining sections list.
:::

## Neurulation, folding, and the tube defects

::: definition Neurulation and body folding {#def-neurulation}
Under notochordal influence the dorsal ectoderm thickens into the **neural plate**, whose grooves elevate, converge and zip — beginning at the future cervical level and running both ways — into the **neural tube** by the end of **week 4**; the crest cells that pinch off from the folding edges form the **neural crest**, whose derivatives (peripheral ganglia and Schwann cells, melanocytes, craniofacial mesenchyme including the odontoblasts and much of the facial skeleton, the **cardiac outflow tract septum**, the aortopulmonary septum and parathyroid and C cells via the pharyngeal apparatus, adrenal medulla, enteric ganglia) are the shortest list in medicine that still has to be memorised. Simultaneously the flat disc folds in both planes — craniocaudally, bringing the oropharyngeal membrane to the head end and the cloaca to the tail, and laterolaterally, rolling the endoderm-lined yolk sac into the **tubular gut** enclosed by the closing body wall.
:::

Failure of the cranial end produces **anencephaly** (incompatible with life; elevated maternal and amniotic AFP with absent cranial vault on ultrasound); failure of the caudal end produces **spina bifida** in its closed (spina bifida occulta — a hairy dimple, common, benign) and open forms (**meningocele, myelomeningocele** — the meningomyelocele lesion determines the level of the subsequent paralysis and bladder/bowel pattern: a lumbar lesion spares hip flexion, a sacral lesion leaves the lower limbs mobile but the bladder denervated). The protective effect of **periconceptional folic acid (400 µg/day before conception through week 4)** — reduction of recurrence risk by roughly two thirds in the trial evidence, and of first-occurrence risk substantially in population data after food fortification — makes this the single most preventable structural defect category, and the one where the embryological fact (closure by day 28) outruns most patients' awareness of being pregnant.

::: example A defect in the midline, and its arithmetic {#ex-ntd}
A woman with one previous pregnancy affected by myelomeningocele asks, at 5 weeks' gestation in a subsequent pregnancy, what to do and what the risks are.
::: solution
Two numbers and one deadline. The empirical **recurrence risk after one affected pregnancy is 3–5%** (population risk ~0.1%; higher in some populations and with more affected relatives — quote the genetic counselling figure, not the background one). The intervention is **folic acid 5 mg daily**, started before conception if possible — she is already 5 weeks, so start today and continue through the first trimester, recognising that the neural tube closes at day 25–28 post-conception, roughly the time of a missed period: the drug's target window is the one the patient almost always misses, which is exactly why the advice belongs in every preconception conversation, not only this one. The surveillance: **AFP at 16–18 weeks** (open defects leak alpha-fetoprotein into maternal plasma and amniotic fluid) plus the **anomaly ultrasound**, where modern imaging detects most open defects and many closed ones. The deeper counselling point: most neural tube defects are multifactorial, not single-gene — recurrence risk figures, not Mendelian ratios, are the honest currency; and the population data on fortified flour belongs in the answer because it lets her see that the risk she is managing is partly environmental.
:::
:::

::: warning The dates problem
Obstetric dates run from the **last menstrual period** (LMP: a 4-week "gestational age" is a 2-week embryo); embryological events are dated from **fertilisation**. A teratogen exposure "at 6 weeks" means nothing until you know which clock — a drug taken at 6 weeks by LMP was taken at 4 weeks developmentally, during gastrulation and neurulation, when the same dose and the same molecule could do damage it could no longer do at 8 weeks. Convert the clock before you counsel: LMP minus two weeks is the embryological time.
:::

## The pharyngeal apparatus: the head's segments

::: definition Pharyngeal arches {#def-pharyngeal}
Five arches appear in week 4 (the first, second, third, fourth and sixth — the second transiently overgrows the third and fourth, burying them in the **cervical sinus**); each has an **artery**, a **cartilage/bone set**, a **nerve** and a muscle set. The arches and their nerves:

| Arch | Nerve | Skeletal/muscular derivatives | Artery |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (mandibular) | Trigeminal (V3) | Meckel's cartilage → malleus, incus, mandible, maxilla; muscles of mastication, mylohyoid, anterior digastric, tensor tympani | Maxillary artery region |
| 2 (hyoid) | Facial (VII) | Stapes, styloid, lesser horn of hyoid; muscles of facial expression, stapedius | Stapedial artery |
| 3 | Glossopharyngeal (IX) | Greater horn of hyoid; stylopharyngeus | Common/internal carotid region |
| 4 | Vagus (superior laryngeal) | Thyroid and most cricoid cartilages, pharyngeal constrictors | Arch of aorta (left) |
| 6 | Vagus (recurrent laryngeal) | Cricoid, arytenoid; all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid | Pulmonary arteries, ductus arteriosus |

The **clefts** between arches are ectodermal (the first forms the external acoustic meatus), the **pouches** inside are endodermal: pouch 1 → middle ear cavity and tube; pouch 2 → palatine tonsil; pouch 3 → **inferior parathyroids and thymus**; pouch 4 → **superior parathyroids** and parafollicular C cells. That the inferior parathyroids come from the higher-looking arch 3 and end up below those from arch 4 is a fact with surgical consequences: the thymus descends from the neck into the mediastinum dragging its parathyroid with it, so an ectopic inferior parathyroid may sit anywhere from the angle of the mandible to the mediastinum — the reason parathyroid surgery is an embryology exam.
:::

::: example When the fourth pouch fails {#ex-diGeorge}
A neonatologist sees a term infant with hypocalcaemic seizures, a conotruncal cardiac defect (truncus arteriosus), and a cleft palate.
::: solution
The triad points to one embryological unit — the **pharyngeal pouches 3 and 4 and the cardiac outflow tract**, all populated by **neural crest cells** — and to one test: chromosomal microarray or FISH for **22q11.2 deletion** (**DiGeorge/velocardiofacial syndrome**, thymic and parathyroid aplasia/hypoplasia, conotruncal anomalies, palatal dysfunction, learning and psychiatric risk). The reasoning is the theorem in action: hypocalcaemia is parathyroid absence (pouch 4), truncus is outflow septation failure (cardiac neural crest), and crest migration is a single process that ties them together. Immediate management is calcium and vitamin D, and — because the thymus is the arch 3 derivative — checking T-cell function before any live vaccine and using irradiated, leucodepleted blood if transfusion is needed; long-term management is the syndrome's adult literature (palatal repair, cardiac follow-up, and the schizophrenia risk that belongs in adolescent counselling). The point of the exercise is the habit: **a syndrome is an embryological field defect**, and recognising the field (here, crest → 3rd/4th pouch → outflow tract) is how a list of findings becomes one diagnosis.
:::
:::

## Organs and their characteristic errors

**The heart.** A single tube — a mesodermal sheet that tubularises by week 3 and starts beating around **day 22** — loops rightward (dextro-looping; failure gives **dextrocardia with situs inversus** when ciliary laterality fails, Kartagener spectrum) and septates by week 5–7: atrioventricular cushions, the **septum primum and secundum** with the **foramen ovale** that lets right-atrial blood bypass the fluid-filled lung, and the **aorticopulmonary septum** — spiral, which is why the aorta ends up posterior-right of the pulmonary trunk and why malalignment produces the Fallot/together-type anatomy. The commonest clinically important defect categories: **ventricular septal defect** (most common overall; small ones close), **atrial septal defect ostium secundum type** (fixed split S2), **patent ductus arteriosus** (the vessel that should have constricted at birth), **tetralogy of Fallot** (anterior-superior displacement of the infundibular septum: VSD, overriding aorta, pulmonary stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy — the "blue" physiology of exertional squatting).

**The gut.** Midgut herniates into the umbilicus in week 6, rotates 270° anticlockwise around the superior mesenteric artery, and returns to fix the caecum low in the right iliac fossa; failure of any phase gives **omphalocele** (midline, membrane-covered), **gastroschisis** (right-sided, no membrane, bowel in amniotic fluid), **non-rotation with midgut volvulus** (the surgical emergency of bilious vomiting in a neonate — Ladd's procedure), **malposition of the caecum**. Vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct remnants give the **rule of 2s** Meckel's diverticulum: 2% of the population, 2 feet from the ileocaecal valve, 2 inches long, 2 kinds of common ectopia (gastric, pancreatic), presenting before age 2 and 2-fold male-predominant for complications — painless rectal bleeding from acid ulceration of normal ileum opposite ectopic gastric mucosa, diagnosed by a technetium-99m pertechnetate scan that images gastric mucosa anywhere it is. Hind abnormalities give **Hirschsprung's disease** — absence of **enteric ganglia** (neural crest, again) from distal colon because crest cells migrate head-to-tail and the rectum is last: a tone without inhibition, proximal dilatation ("Hirschsprung" is the rectosigmoid segment that never relaxes), rectal biopsy for diagnosis, pull-through for treatment.

**The urogenital system.** Three kidneys in sequence (pronephros, mesonephros, metanephros); the metanephric bud ascends from the pelvis to the loin and rotates, and failures produce **pelvic or horseshoe kidneys** (the isthmus catches on the inferior mesenteric artery's origin — a fact for any aortic surgery) and **duplex collecting systems** with the upper pole draining inferomedially (Weigert-Meyer: the upper pole obstructs, the lower refluxes). The **intermediate mesoderm** builds both sexes' ducts — paramesonephric (Müllerian) tubes form uterus, tubes and upper vagina; mesonephric (Wolffian) tubes form male epididymis and vas — and in the absence of a Y chromosome and Sry the gonad is female by default, which is the true sense of the tired phrase. Descent of the testis drags its vessels, its peritoneal sleeve (**processus vaginalis**, whose failure to close is a patent processus → indirect hernia or hydrocele) and its nerve's apparent course with it; failure gives **cryptorchidism** with its cancer and fertility stakes, and ectopic testes that are palpable but never in the canal.

::: example Painless bleeding in a two-year-old {#ex-meckel}
A 2-year-old boy has three painless maroon stools; haemoglobin 78 g/L; abdominal examination and coagulation screen normal; colonoscopy unrevealing; the paediatric surgeon considers a technetium scan.
::: solution
The rule of 2s makes the diagnosis in a sentence: painless significant rectal bleeding in a child under two with a normal upper and lower endoscopy is a **bleeding Meckel's diverticulum** until the pertechnetate scan (which concentrates in ectopic **gastric mucosa**) says otherwise, sensitivity ~85% in children. Note that the anatomy of the bleeding is also embryological: acid secreted by the diverticulum's gastric mucosa ulcerates the **adjacent normal ileal mucosa**, so the "lesion" is on the wall opposite the diverticulum, not inside it — the operation is segmental resection including the ulcerated area, not a simple diverticulectomy. The scan is the investigation because it images the embryological tissue, not the anatomy: a reminder that in this chapter's world, diagnosis is often the direct imaging of a developmental error.
:::
:::

::: example Bilious vomiting on day two {#ex-volvulus}
A two-day-old, previously well, suddenly vomits green-stained fluid after feeds; the abdomen becomes distended and tender; the baby is pale and tachycardic.
::: solution
**Bilious vomiting in a neonate is malrotation with midgut volvulus until an upper gastrointestinal contrast study proves otherwise**, and the embryology is the clinical reasoning. The midgut that failed to complete its rotation hangs on a narrow **superior mesenteric artery** pedicle with abnormal **Ladd's bands** crossing the duodenum; the bowel twists around that pedicle, and volvulus is an arterial occlusion of the whole midgut — the pale, shocked, acidotic baby is not a "gastroenteritis" but an ischaemic bowel. Time is measured in minutes of gut, not hours: an emergency laparotomy with untwisting and Ladd's procedure must follow within the ischaemia window, because the entire small bowel distal to the papilla and proximal to the mid-transverse colon is on that one artery. The phrase to keep is the embryological one — **midgut volvulus is a single-vessel catastrophe** — because it explains why "green vomit + blood in stool + shock in a neonate" is as surgical an emergency as a ruptured aneurysm is in an adult, and why the upper GI series (abnormal position of the duodenojejunal flexure relative to the ligament of Treitz) is the one investigation worth doing if the child is stable.
:::
:::

## Fetal circulation, and the transition at birth

::: definition The fetal circulatory shortcuts {#def-fetal-circ}
The fetus does not ventilate its lungs, and its circulation is wired accordingly. Oxygenated placental blood returns in the **ductus venosus**, which streams the best-oxygenated blood through the **foramen ovale** into the left atrium and so to the coronary and cerebral circulations (the right-to-left atrial shunt is the *normal* bypass of the unloaded lung); the remainder of inferior vena caval blood and all superior vena caval blood go round the right ventricle into the pulmonary trunk and then across the **ductus arteriosus** (aortic arch derivative 6, kept patent by low oxygen tension and placental/prostaglandin E₂) into the descending aorta, most of it returning via the **umbilical arteries** (two, in the cord) to the placenta. The umbilical **vein** is single. At birth: lung expansion drops pulmonary vascular resistance, pulmonary blood flow rises, left atrial pressure exceeds right and the **foramen ovale functionally closes** (its anatomical closure is incomplete in ~25% — patent foramen ovale, common and usually harmless); rising arterial oxygen and falling PGE₂ constrict the **ductus** (anatomical closure by 1–3 months); the umbilical vessels fibrose to the **medial umbilical ligaments** and the **ligamentum teres/venosum** in the free edge of the falciform ligament — landmarks that matter to hepatobiliary and transplant surgeons.
:::

::: theorem Some lesions are duct-dependent {#thm-duct}
In a set of critical congenital heart defects, survival past the first days of life depends on the ductus arteriosus staying open, because the circulation is otherwise interrupted or pulmonary blood flow is abolished: **duct-dependent pulmonary flow** (pulmonary atresia, critical Fallot), **duct-dependent systemic flow** (critical coarctation, interrupted aortic arch, hypoplastic left heart), and **parallel circuits requiring mixing** (complete transposition). Keeping them alive is pharmacology — prostaglandin E₁ (alprostadil) infusion, accepting its apnoea and fever — and diagnosis before closure is the purpose of newborn pulse-oximetry screening.
:::

::: proof
The physiology is a loop analysis of a two-circuit system with switches. Take **hypoplastic left heart**: the left ventricle cannot support the systemic circuit, so systemic output depends on right-ventricular output crossing the duct to the descending aorta, with the coronary and cerebral circulations depending on atrial-level mixing and a retrograde arch limb — when the duct constricts on day 2–3, systemic perfusion collapses and the baby presents in shock. The same arithmetic with the signs swapped gives **transposition of the great arteries**: aorta from the right ventricle and pulmonary trunk from the left make two parallel loops that would not circulate at all but for an atrial or ductal communication, so the immediate intervention is an atrial septostomy (a Rashkind balloon) to enlarge the mixing lesion, and definitive repair (arterial switch) within weeks. Take **coarctation**: the narrowing at the ductal insertion means the lower body is perfused through the duct prenatally; when it closes, afterload to the left ventricle rises and the legs go pale while the head stays perfused — the differential cyanosis and radio-femoral delay, and the reason infantile coarctation is an emergency and adult-type coarctation is a collateral disease (dilated intercostals eroding rib undersides, "rib notching", the textbook sign of a chronic bypass the fetus never needed). The theorem is a corollary of one sentence: *the newborn's ductus is the only bridge most of these lesions leave unburned*, which is why "start PGE₁, do not wait for the echo" is the emergency-medicine instruction, and why ductal-dependent lesions are the strongest argument in paediatrics for the principle that the right drug can buy a single surgical opportunity.
:::

::: quiz
A drug taken by a patient during the "week 5–6" of pregnancy (dated from LMP) corresponds to which period of embryological risk?
- [ ] Early organogenesis of limb buds and heart only
- [x] The gastrulation/neurulation window — the most sensitive period for major structural teratogenesis, with cardiac and neural tube structures vulnerable
- [ ] The early fetal growth period
- [ ] The all-or-none period before implantation
::: solution
Subtract two weeks. LMP week 5–6 is fertilisation week 3–4: gastrulation through neural tube closure — the period whose disruption produces the neural tube defects, cardiac septation errors and early facial and limb-field damage that define classical teratology. "Before implantation, all-or-none" describes the first two weeks; weeks 8 onward (developmentally) are the fetal period, where teratogens more often cause growth restriction and functional or minor structural defects than gross malformations. The conversion, not the list, is the knowledge: the teratogen's effect is read on the clock the embryo keeps, not the one the calendar keeps.
:::
:::

## Teratogens and the rules of timing

::: definition Critical periods {#def-teratogen}
A **teratogen** causes malformation when exposure falls within the organ's **critical period** — during organogenesis (developmental weeks 3–8), gross structural defects; during the fetal period, growth, functional and CNS effects. The dose–response is rarely all-or-none except in the first two weeks (where enough damage is lethal, and survivors are structurally normal: the "all-or-none" rule). The list worth knowing cold: **infections** (the TORCH group: toxoplasma, rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes; parvovirus B19 hydrops; zika); **drugs** (thalidomide — the 1957–61 disaster that created modern teratological regulation, limb reduction defects from a ~4–7 day window; isotretinoin — craniofacial, cardiac, CNS, the reason for mandatory pregnancy-prevention programmes; warfarin — nasal hypoplasia and stippling; valproate and other anticonvulsants — neural tube and cognitive; ACE inhibitors and NSAIDs in the third trimester — fetal renal; alcohol — the most common preventable cause of intellectual disability, no established safe dose); **maternal disease** (pregestational diabetes — caudal regression and cardiac defects, the risk tracking first-trimester glycaemia; phenylketonuria untreated; systemic lupus — anti-Ro/La neonatal heart block).
:::

::: history
The ideas are younger than the anatomy. William Harvey's embryological essays (1651) framed development as epigenesis; von Baer's laws (1828) put the germ layers and the "general before the special" ordering of development into biology; Weismann's germ-plasm doctrine (1892) separated the heredity line from the body's soma. The clinical era opened with the rubella epidemic of 1961–65 (Gregg's 1941 link of maternal rubella to cataracts and deafness vindicated), and the field's regulation was paid for by **thalidomide** — approved 1957, withdrawn 1961–62, and the direct ancestor of every modern teratogen-testing requirement, pregnancy-label rule and pharmacovigilance system; the 1988 isotretinoin-era pregnancy-prevention programmes and the 2016–17 zika emergency wrote the current chapters. The embryology itself was mapped on model organisms — Spemann and Mangold's organiser transplant (1924), Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus's *Drosophila* screens (1980), the knock-out mouse era after which the "hox code" explained why the spine's segments, the arches and the gut's territories rhyme with one another.
:::

::: summary
- Germ layers are the constitution: ectoderm nerve and surface, mesoderm muscle-bone-fluid-lines, endoderm gut-and-glands; lesion location, composition and syndromic grouping follow layer and migration route.
- The clock matters twice: neural tube closure by day 28 (folic acid before conception works), and LMP-versus-fertilisation dating changes every teratogen conversation by two weeks.
- Twins are classified by chorions, not faces: chorionicity is set by the day the embryo splits, and monochorionic placentation is the risk that surveillance is built around.
- The pharyngeal apparatus is a segmental map whose failures are fields, not lists: 22q11 is crest and pouches; parathyroid surgery and cardiac septation are its clinical subjects.
- Heart, gut, kidney and gonad each have a handful of characteristic movements; the malformation is the movement arrested, excessive or misdirected — volvulus, Meckel's, horseshoe kidney, cryptorchidism.
- The fetal circulation's three shunts close on schedule, and a critical congenital heart defect is usually a lesion that either needs one of them to stay open or needs the other to close: prostaglandin and pulse oximetry are the two modern answers to that sentence.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Convert the clock {level=1}
A patient took an over-the-counter medication at "4 weeks pregnant" (by LMP). Give the developmental stage at that moment and say whether major structural organogenesis was underway.
::: solution
LMP 4 weeks = about 2 weeks post-fertilisation: the blastocyst is implanting or just implanted and the bilaminar disc is forming — before gastrulation. This is the **all-or-none period** in its literal sense: significant insult tends to be lethal to the conceptus or produces no malformation, because organogenesis has not begun; the drug is very unlikely to have caused a structural defect. The counselling is still to take a proper history (some "4 week" patients are post-dated differently; ovulation timing varies; and a few teratogen windows — e.g. neural plate — open within days), and the reassurance should be given in the two-week language the patient can apply again.
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the remnant {level=1}
Identify the embryological origin of: (a) thyroglossal cyst; (b) urachus patent to the umbilicus; (c) epiploic appendage-like ileal diverticulum with gastric mucosa; (d) a cyst in the posterior triangle that enlarges with a cold; (e) the ligamentum arteriosum.
::: solution
(a) The thyroid's descent track from foramen cecum of the tongue to its lower neck position (the duct's remnant; moves with swallowing/tongue protrusion). (b) The **allantois/urogenital sinus** connection — the urachus running from bladder apex to umbilicus; a patent urachus leaks urine at the umbilicus; its median remnant is the median umbilical ligament. (c) The **vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct** remnant — a Meckel's diverticulum (the rule of 2s). (d) A **second branchial (cleft) cyst** — anterior border of sternocleidomastoid classically; the posterior-triangle atypical variant still arises from buried cleft epithelium, enlarging when lymphoid tissue in its wall reacts to infection. (e) The closed **ductus arteriosus**, the left sixth aortic arch's distal remnant — a structure whose persistence is a lesion and whose closure is a physiological event (see [[anatomy/thorax]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Recurrence-risk arithmetic {level=1 check="0.05"}
Neural tube defects recur in 5% of subsequent pregnancies after one affected pregnancy in the population this clinic serves (pretreat). If periconceptional high-dose folate reduces recurrence by two thirds, what is the treated recurrence risk?
::: solution
$0.05 \times (1 - 2/3) \approx 0.017$, about 1.7% — roughly one in sixty against the untreated one in twenty. The arithmetic belongs in counselling for two reasons: it is small enough to reassure a frightened couple and large enough to justify the surveillance (anomaly scan and AFP) and the daily tablet; and it illustrates the field's honest grammar — risks fall multiplicatively, and a preventive effect is a fraction, not a zero. (Cite the clinic's own numbers where audit data exist; population figures vary by region and fortification policy.)
:::

::: exercise Field diagnosis {level=2}
For each constellation, name the embryological field: (a) cleft lip + hypodontia + ear tags; (b) branchial fistula opening along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid; (c) right-sided aortic arch + truncus + hypocalcaemia; (d) bilateral renal agenesis with Potter sequence.
::: solution
(a) **First arch/first-and-second arch field**, mostly neural-crest derived (frontonasal and maxillary processes) — the orofacial-crest syndromes. (b) A **second pharyngeal cleft/pouch** track — the cervical sinus's failure to obliterate, giving the classic cleft-to-skin fistula along the muscle's anterior border (deep relation: between the internal and external carotid arteries, which is the surgical anatomy). (c) **Cardiac neural crest / conotruncal and pharyngeal pouch field** — think 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge spectrum): outflow septation, pouch derivatives, arch laterality all crest. (d) **Intermediate-mesoderm ureteric-bud induction failure**: no bud means no collecting system and no amniotic fluid (fetal urine is most of the second-half liquor), and oligohydramnios gives the Potter sequence — limb compression, pulmonary hypoplasia — bilateral renal agenesis being incompatible with extrauterine life because the lungs cannot grow without fluid.
:::

::: exercise Duct-dependent or not {level=2}
A newborn day-2 baby, blue, with a soft systolic murmur, has SpO₂ 84% on air. A prostaglandin infusion is started before any imaging. Justify the decision in one physiological sentence, and give the two findings that make PGE₁ the right drug rather than oxygen alone.
::: solution
Physiological sentence: if the pulmonary blood flow or the systemic output depends on the ductus, its closure is the lesion, and reopening it is the only drug that treats the pathophysiology; the decision costs nothing and buys hours. The findings: a **pre-ductal/post-ductal saturations difference** is not required (in parallel-circuit lesions like transposition both arms may be equally desaturated because mixing is atrial), and the specific pair that argues for duct-dependence is **desaturation that barely improves with 100% oxygen** (fixed right-to-left shunt or limited pulmonary flow — hyperoxia cannot open what is atretic) plus a **shock-prone or collapsing picture with acidosis** (systemic flow across the duct). In that context a hyperoxia test is a delay and echocardiography is the appointment, not the treatment; prostaglandin started on the clinical impression with transfer to a paediatric cardiac centre is the standard of care, its apnoea risk being the reason it is started in a monitored setting, not a reason to withhold it.
:::

::: exercise The scan that images a tissue, not an organ {level=2}
A technetium-99m pertechnetate scan is positive in the right lower quadrant in a child with obscure bleeding. Explain the diagnosis, the false-negative causes, and why angiography may be the alternative.
::: solution
Diagnosis: ectopic **gastric mucosa in a Meckel's diverticulum** — pertechnetate is taken up by gastric-type mucosa, so the scan images the embryological tissue rather than the anatomy (see [[anatomy/abdomen]]). False negatives: too little gastric mucosa or mucosa injured/sloughed by bleeding, rapid washout, and adult patients (yield falls outside early childhood). When bleeding is brisk and the scan is negative or unobtainable, the alternative is a **tagged-red-cell scan** (detects slower bleeding over hours) or **angiography** (faster bleeding, more precise, therapeutic potential), and if bleeding is massive a surgical or endoscopic decision must overtake imaging — which is why this question belongs in a paediatric surgical unit from the outset. The teaching point: embryology-based imaging is powerful precisely because it is mechanism-based, and fails precisely where the mechanism is absent — which is why the second test should target anatomy and flow instead.
:::

::: exercise Plan the parathyroid hunt {level=2}
At neck exploration for primary hyperparathyroidism, three normal-appearing glands are found but calcium and PTH remain high. Where do you look next, and what embryology justifies the search order?
::: solution
Look for a **fourth gland along the descent track of the third pouch**: from the angle of the mandible down the carotid sheath, into the superior and anterior mediastinum (thymic tongue — deliver the thymus and inspect it), within or behind the thyroid, in the tracheo-oesophageal groove, and the carotid sheath and thyroid parenchyma. The order is justified because **inferior parathyroids arise from pouch 3 with the thymus** and therefore travel farthest and are the commonest ectopic sites; the superior (pouch 4) glands are more constant, near the cricothyroid junction posterior to the upper pole. Supranumerary glands (10–15%) and the rare double inferior glands are the field's exceptions — the reason the operation ends when the biochemistry is explained, not when four sites have been seen; and if all four are normal, think ectopic or (rarely) a fifth gland, and consider imaging and, next time, the radiologist's localisation study, which is itself a map of the same embryology.
:::

::: exercise Counsel the exposure {level=3}
A woman at 7 weeks' gestation (by LMP) took a single dose of an NSAID and had one glass of wine at a wedding at 5 weeks (LMP dates in both), is worried, and asks whether the pregnancy is "damaged". You know that the teratogen windows she worries about run on the other clock. Structure the counselling.
::: solution
Convert the clock first: LMP 5 weeks ≈ fertilisation 3 weeks — gastrulation/neurulation, the peak structural-sensitivity window; LMP 7 weeks ≈ developmental 5 weeks — cardiac and limb fields active. Then take each exposure on its own evidence, not as undifferentiated dread. **Single-dose NSAID at 3–4 weeks**: the human signal (if any) is in later pregnancy (third-trimester ductal constriction, oligohydramnios via fetal renal prostaglandins, which is why NSAIDs are avoided after 30 weeks) and periconceptionally the data do not support meaningful structural risk from a single dose — but the ductal and renal facts are exactly why she must not take them regularly now. **One glass of wine**: no safe threshold is formally claimed for alcohol, but the fetal alcohol spectrum literature concerns sustained or heavy patterns; a single early dose is, in population terms, close to background risk — say that plainly and do not oversell the distinction, because credibility is what she needs to follow the advice that follows. Generalise: reassure with numbers where the evidence allows ("your description is the pattern we see in many normal pregnancies, and nothing you did in that pattern calls for a change in your care plan"), keep the routine anomaly scan at 18–20 weeks unchanged (do not order "extra" scans that medicalise the event), and reframe the conversation toward what does move risk now: folic acid, smoking and second-hand smoke, medications review, and diet. Close by naming the residual anxiety as normal, and by telling her where the advice lives in the notes, so the next clinician reassures her with the same facts — inconsistent reassurance is its own harm.
