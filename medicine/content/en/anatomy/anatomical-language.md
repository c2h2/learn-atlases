Anatomical language is the first clinical technology a medical student learns, and the only one used on every subsequent page of the medical record. When a surgeon writes "a 3 cm mass in the upper outer quadrant of the left breast, 4 cm from the nipple", a radiologist writes "an 8 mm stone at the junction of the right renal pelvis and ureter", and a physiotherapist writes "shoulder abduction restricted to 90° with painful arc", all three are performing the same act: localising a thing in space using a shared convention precise enough that someone in another building, in another discipline, on another continent can find it. The convention was invented — not discovered — by a profession that had repeatedly bled itself with imprecise questions, and it is worth learning as a designed system with a small number of primitives: a posture, three planes, a vocabulary of relations, a vocabulary of movements, and an honest set of rules for what to do when bodies depart from the book.

This lesson sets those primitives, their clinical misuse, and the modern extension of the language into imaging, where the conventions of the reading room are inherited from the dissection room but applied to pictures rather than bodies.

## The reference posture

::: definition The anatomical position {#def-anatomical-position}
The **anatomical position** is the reference frame of human anatomy: standing erect, head and eyes horizontal, feet together or slightly separated and pointing forwards, and upper limbs at the sides with the **palms facing forwards** and thumbs directed away from the body. Every directional term means "in the anatomical position", whatever the patient's actual posture. The thumb's outward rotation is the reason the forearm can be rotated: in the anatomical position the radius and ulna are parallel, and pronation crosses the radius over the ulna — so the palm-forward rule is not cosmetic but records the limb's rotational embryology.
:::

The rule that every term refers to the anatomical position, not the patient's current pose, resolves exactly the ambiguity it was written for. A supine patient's "anterior" surface is still anterior although it is now the surface facing the ceiling; the "medial" border of the foot is still medial although, crossed at the ankle, it faces laterally. The one structure family that regularly escapes the rule is the head, because the adult head is bent on the trunk at roughly a right angle from its embryonic alignment: in the head, **vertical** planes and **horizontal** planes are defined relative to the Frankfort horizontal plane (the line from the lower orbital margin to the upper ear canal), not to the floor, and "superior" in the brain means "towards the vertex" in an upright person — which is why the brainstem's own axes need their own names (rostral/caudal along the neuraxis).

::: definition Relational terms {#def-relational}
- **Superior/inferior** (cranial/caudal): towards the head end / tail end of the body axis.
- **Anterior/posterior** (ventral/dorsal): towards the front / back of the body — in the limbs the equivalent terms are **proximal/distal**, towards the attachment / away from it, and in the hand and forearm "anterior" is replaced by **palmar (volar)/dorsal** and in the foot by **plantar/dorsal**.
- **Medial/lateral**: towards / away from the median plane.
- **Superficial (external)/deep (internal)**: towards / away from the surface.
- **Ipsilateral/contralateral**: on the same / opposite side of the body.
- For the limbs specifically, **radial/ulnar** (thumb side / little-finger side) and **tibial/fibular** replace medial/lateral when rotation makes those terms confusing — the wrist may be supinated, pronated or between; the radius is always the radius.
:::

::: theorem No term is meaningful without the frame {#thm-frame}
Every correct statement of an anatomical relation is implicitly quantified: "in the anatomical position, on this side, relative to these two named landmarks." A report, examination finding or operative note that omits one of these three coordinates has not made a statement that might be wrong; it has failed to make a statement at all — the reader must silently supply a frame, and different readers supply different ones.
:::

::: proof
The failure modes are documented in the most literal way surgical audit allows. In the era before standardised posture and imaging conventions, a bullet's or a stone's "level" could be given relative to the nipple, the umbilicus, or a vertebral spine palpated in a patient who was sitting, lying or turned — and the same description would then point at different structures in different readers' hands. The fixed points the discipline chose are: vertebrae, numbered from the axis (C2) downward, with the prominent spine at the neck base as the clinical landmark (C7, vertebra prominens — the spinous process you can feel, T1's being lower and often deeper); ribs, counted down from the second, whose cartilage meets the sternal angle (the angle of Louis, the flat you can feel, which marks the T4/T5 disc, the bifurcation of the trachea and the arch of the aorta, and which is the reason the phrase "count ribs from the sternal angle" appears in every physical-examination manual); and the umbilicus, which is not reliable (it moves with distension and habitus) — which is why radiologists localise by named vertebrae, not by scars. Note how even the landmarks obey the theorem: the sternal angle is usable because it is *defined* by its relation to a vertebra, closing the loop between palpation and imaging. And note the epistemic hygiene embedded in the numbering conventions — "L3/4 disc level" is true whether or not the patient stood up for the scan, whereas "at the level of the umbilicus" changes meaning with posture: the language encodes which statements survive variation and which do not.
:::

## Planes, sections and axes of movement

::: definition Planes {#def-planes}
A **plane** is a flat surface through the body; three families dominate. The **median (midsagittal) plane** divides left from right equally; any parallel plane is **sagittal** (paramedian). A **coronal (frontal) plane** divides front from back; the one through the approximate centre of mass also earns the name **oblique plane** only in casual speech — proper usage keeps oblique for planes at a deliberate angle, as in oblique body-section imaging. A **transverse (axial, horizontal) plane** divides upper from lower. A **section** is a cut: a **transverse (axial) section** is the display of whatever lies in a transverse plane, and the cross-sectional shape of an organ is a fact about that organ — the liver's, the heart's, and the uterus's axial silhouettes are how the structures appear on the CT and MRI that now teach more anatomy than the cadaver does.
:::

Movements happen *around* axes that stand perpendicular to the plane in which they occur — flexion/extension sweeps the sagittal plane about a coronal axis, abduction/adduction the coronal plane about an anteroposterior axis, and rotation the transverse plane about a longitudinal axis. A **circumduction** is the composition of the first three at a condyloid or ball-and-socket joint, tracing a cone whose apex sits in the joint.

::: definition Movements, in the terms a clinician is asked to record {#def-movements}
**Flexion** and **extension** decrease and increase the angle between articulating bones (except at the knee, where flexion is posterior rotation of the leg and "extension" of the knee past neutral is hyperextension). **Abduction** and **adduction** move away from and towards the median plane; **lateral flexion** tilts the trunk or neck sideways. **Rotation** is **medial (internal)/lateral (external)** about the long axis; **supination** and **pronation** rotate the forearm so the palm faces up or down; **dorsiflexion** and **plantarflexion** tip the foot up and down, **inversion** and **eversion** turn the sole inwards and outwards. Specialised movements have their own names because they are common: **protraction/retraction** (jaw, shoulder girdle), **elevation/depression** (shoulder, mandible), **opposition** of the thumb (the saddle joint's composite of flexion, medial rotation and abduction that brings the pad of the thumb against the pad of the little finger — its loss is the clinical event in median nerve palsy), and the foot's combined **supination/pronation** (which for the subtalar joint mean roughly the union of eversion with abduction and inversion with adduction — a nomenclature trap worth memorising, because a chiropodist's "overpronation" does not mean the forearm movement).
:::

::: example A wrist, described properly {#ex-wrist}
A patient describes difficulty "bending the wrist down" after a fall on the outstretched hand. On examination, the hand is held in slight flexion with dorsal prominence proximal to the wrist ("dinner-fork" appearance on the lateral view), and passive movement reproduces pain.
::: solution
The fall on the outstretched hand with the "dinner-fork" deformity is the classic **Colles' fracture** — dorsally displaced fracture of the distal radius. Now read what the record must capture in the language rather than the picture: active **extension** at the wrist is lost and **flexion** partially preserved; the displacement itself is described as **dorsal (posterior) tilt and angulation** of the distal fragment with **radial (lateral) shortening** — which is why the hand appears shifted towards the thumb side. "Bending the wrist down" was, for a patient whose palm faced the thigh, actually a combination of flexion and pronation, and the physiotherapy referral that simply repeats it prescribes an ambiguity. The lesson generalises: every movement finding should name the joint, the plane/axis of movement, and side — "left wrist: extension 0° (normal 70°), flexion 60° (normal 80°), painful on passive supination" is a statement another clinician can check.
:::
:::

## Regions, quadrants, cavities and the inside of the trunk

::: definition Divisions of the body and its cavities {#def-regions}
The body is divided into **head, neck, trunk and limbs**; the trunk into **thorax** and **abdomen** separated by the **diaphragm**, with the **pelvis** below the abdomen, continuous with it through the pelvic brim. The **dorsal body cavity** (cranial cavity plus vertebral canal) and the much larger **ventral body cavity** are lined by serous membranes: the ventral cavity's thoracic part is lined by **parietal pleura** (chest wall), reflected as **visceral pleura** over the lung, with **costodiaphragmatic recesses** where parietal pleura of two surfaces meets and no lung lies — the recesses are why fluid can collect without the lung being "covered"; the abdomen's linings are **parietal peritoneum** and **visceral peritoneum**, with the **greater and lesser sacs**, and the pelvis gets peritoneal reflections that are the reason an inflamed appendix localises where it does and why free fluid tracks to the rectouterine pouch (pouch of Douglas). The **mesothelium** of these linings secretes just enough serous fluid for gliding — a fact that becomes visible as ascites or effusion when Starling forces go wrong.
:::

The clinical geography of the abdomen exists in two rival systems. The **four quadrants** (right and left upper and lower, divided by the umbilical planes) are quick and good for localising pain; the **nine regions** — right and left hypochondriac, lumbar (flank) and inguinal (iliac) regions with epigastric, umbilical and hypogastric (pubic) centrally — map pain and masses onto organs and are still how the abdomen is examined in most physical-diagnosis texts.

::: example Localising appendicitis twice {#ex-appendix}
A 19-year-old has had central peri-umbilical pain since 6 a.m., now localised to the right lower quadrant, with anorexia and one vomit after the onset of pain.
::: solution
Both descriptions are correct and each earns its detail from embryology. The **peri-umbilical** pain is **visceral**: the caecum and appendix are midgut derivatives, supplied by the superior mesenteric artery and its visceral afferents, which enter the spinal cord around T10 and refer pain to that dermatome around the umbilicus — midline, poorly localised, because the afferents arrive bilaterally and the cortex cannot separate sides. The later **right lower quadrant** pain is **somatic (parietal)**: inflamed appendix now irritates overlying parietal peritoneum, whose sensory nerves are segmental intercostal and lumbar nerves, giving sharp, localised, lateralised pain. The same event, described in quadrant language and in nerve-language, becomes the clinical reasoning: the sequence visceral → somatic in a midgut territory is appendicitis until proven otherwise, and the point of maximal tenderness approximates the inflamed organ (hence McBurney's point, one third of the way from the anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus — an eponym, but a useful one because it names the geometry, not a person).
:::
:::

::: warning The words that quietly mislead
Three families of terms cause disproportionate harm. **"Flank"** means the lateral chest/abdominal wall, but renal colic is described as "loin to groin" while pleurisy is "pleuritic, radiating to shoulder" — patients use all three; always re-ask in the body's own words and point. **"Perforated" vs "ruptured" vs "hernia"**: a perforation is a hole in a hollow viscus wall, a rupture is a burst of a solid organ or an aneurysm, and a hernia is a protrusion through a wall; "gut perforation" said of an obstruction is not the same emergency in the same anatomy. **Eponymous spaces and triangles** (McBurney's point, Calot's triangle, Petit's triangle) carry no geometric information to anyone who has not memorised them, and the current editorial style in anatomy — "name the boundaries, then the contents" — exists precisely because eponyms fail at exactly the moment precision is needed, in the operating theatre and in the medicolegal record.
:::

## Imaging: the language inherits the frame

::: definition Reading conventions of cross-sectional imaging {#def-imaging}
Computed tomography and magnetic resonance images are conventionally displayed **in the transverse plane as if viewed from the patient's feet**: the patient's **right is on the viewer's left**, anterior at the top, posterior at the bottom; "up" on the image stack is **cranial (cephalad)**. A chest radiograph is displayed **as if facing the patient** — again the patient's right on the viewer's left; a lateral chest film is shown with the patient facing the viewer's left and the label marking right or left. Contrast convention: on CT, bone is **hyperdense** (white), air **hypodense** (black); on MRI, signal is called **hyperintense/hypointense**, and because T1- and T2-weighted sequences invert the fat/fluid appearance, an MRI report's colour words mean nothing unless you know the sequence — a small, pure triumph of convention over intuition.
:::

::: example A report read in the correct frame {#ex-imaging}
An axial CT slice at the level of T10 is requested. "Where do I look for the inferior vena cava, the aorta, and what tells me the slice is at T10?"
::: solution
Use the frame, then the local anatomy. The frame: the image is transverse, viewed from below — the patient's right is your left, front is up, so anterior structures (the right ventricle's outflow, the ascending aorta's successors) sit towards the top of the screen. At T10 the vertebral body is a round button centrally and posteriorly; the **aorta** lies just left of and anterior to the vertebral body; the **inferior vena cava** lies anterior and to the right of the aorta, hugging the posterior margin of the liver, flattened by low central venous pressure into an ellipse or a slit — two facts, position and shape, that let you tell them apart at a glance even when both are grey. The slice is at T10 because the oesophagus pierces the diaphragm at that level (with the vagal trunks), the aorta passing through the aortic hiatus behind it at T12; and because at T10 you see lung on both sides with no heart between them (the diaphragmatic crura replace the heart as the central anterior structure). Reading cross-sectional anatomy is this: locate the frame, locate a level-marker structure, and derive the rest. Every organ's position in the next chapter follows the same two-step.
:::
:::

## Variation, naming, and the honesty problem

::: definition Anatomical variation {#def-variation}
An **anatomical variation** is a deviation from the standard description that falls within the range found in healthy people: not a malformation (which impairs function) and not (usually) a disease. Variations are numbered, not exceptional: the **hepatic artery** arises from the superior mesenteric artery or the aorta in roughly a fifth of bodies; a **left-sided inferior vena cava** occurs in a few per thousand; the **accessory left hepatic vein**, **azygos lobe** of the lung, **palmaris longus** absent in 10–15%, **thyroid ima artery** — the lists exist for every region because "standard anatomy" is a statistical summary, and the operative surgeon's mental model must carry a probability distribution over structures rather than a single diagram.
:::

::: proof
Why variation matters clinically is a consequence of the way variations arise: embryological alternatives that were viable and stayed viable. An aberrant hepatic artery from the superior mesenteric artery is a persistent embryonic vessel that grew in the wrong relative order; it matters because it runs near the common hepatic duct and can be clamped during cholecystectomy — so the language of operative cholecystectomy is a language of identifying structures by *relations* rather than by size and position (hence the critical view of safety: two and only two structures entering the liver, cystic duct and cystic artery, with the upper third of the gallbladder dissected from the cystic plate). Naming systems encode the same philosophy. The international standard, **Terminologia Anatomica** (1998, with subsequent revisions), prescribes Latin forms with English equivalents, retires eponyms where a descriptive term exists, and formally distinguishes "variants" from "anomalies"; the practical effect on a clinical note is a rule of composition: structures are named by their relations and contents (rectouterine pouch, lateral ventricle's anterior horn, first-order branch of the superficial temporal artery), which is information a reader who has never seen the eponym can use.
:::

::: example Absent muscle, present consequences {#ex-variation}
A hand surgeon plans a palmaris longus tendon transfer for a patient with median nerve palsy; the preoperative examination finds no palmaris longus tendon bilaterally.
::: solution
The absence of palmaris longus is one of the commonest "variations" (absent in 10–15% of people in many populations, higher in some), entirely harmless, and here decisive: the planned graft or transfer must be re-planned before the theatre, not discovered in it — which is the entire reason the preoperative examination of variants exists. The same thinking structures the language of living-donation surgery (a right or left lobe of liver, one kidney: the vessels' variation defines who may donate), and it explains why anatomy's honest register says "typically", "arises in 70% of cases", "may be duplicated" while the clinical consequence demands certainty: the resolution is that *named structures are defined by relationships that persist across the variation* — the cystic artery is what runs in Calot's triangle to the gallbladder, whichever vessel happens to fill that role in this patient.
:::
:::

::: history
The language is older than most institutions. Herodotus of Alexandria (c. 300 BCE) coined terms that survive — *duodenum* for the bowel's twelve-digit length, the word *mamma*; Galen's second-century Latin and Greek nomenclature dominated through the universities by translation and authority rather than dissection. Andreas **Vesalius** restored the scalpel with *De humani corporis fabrica* (1543), naming structures as he saw them and quietly correcting Galen over a thousand pages; Fabricus, Varolius, Falloppio, Eustachius and Arantius added the eponyms still carved into the bones and ducts; William **Croone** and then the eighteenth-century Leiden and Edinburgh schools taught anatomy through the arteries and the nerves. **Gray's Anatomy** (1858) fixed the English-language student's vocabulary; the **Basle, London and Bonn** conventions of 1895–1935 tried and half-succeeded in standardising Latin nomenclature; **Terminologia Anatomica** (1998) completed the attempt under the International Federation of Associations of Anatomists. The imaging conventions arrived last and fast: the transverse section first, then from 1972 (Hounsfield's CT) the decision to display from the feet — a choice as arbitrary and as necessary as the palm-forward rule, and equally load-bearing for patient safety.
:::

::: quiz
A wound lies "3 cm proximal to the wrist, on the ulnar aspect of the forearm, penetrating the skin and passing deep to the tendon that flexes the fingers but leaving the radial pulse intact". Which of the four listed inferences is safe?
- [ ] The lacerated tendon is flexor carpi radialis.
- [x] The described tendon lies on the little-finger side, where flexor digitorum superficialis/profundus pass — flexor carpi radialis would have been called radial-sided, and the radial pulse being intact says nothing about the ulnar artery.
- [ ] The radial nerve is injured.
- [ ] The patient cannot flex the fingers.
::: solution
The example is a translation test, and the whole point of the vocabulary: **ulnar** means little-finger side; **radial** means thumb side, and a wound on the ulnar side with an intact radial pulse excludes nothing on the ulnar side — the ulnar artery is precisely the vessel at risk there, which is why the record specifies the pulse but the hand must be assessed by Allen's test or perfusion of the digits. The named deep structure is on the ulnar side, so it cannot be flexor carpi radialis (radial-sided); an intact "flexors pass deep to it" statement about the wound floor localises depth, not nerve — the median nerve at the wrist is central, not ulnar; and "leaving the tendon that flexes the fingers deep" tells us it is *intact* or untouched, not divided, so finger flexion may be preserved. Reading anatomical description is a matter of noting what *was* and *was not* localised, in the language, before the knife or the scan.
:::

::: summary
- All anatomical statements are made in the anatomical position with palms forward; the patient's actual pose never changes a structure's directional terms.
- Three planes, one frame (right on the viewer's left), and vertebra- and rib-anchored landmarks convert palpation and imaging into one coordinate system; the umbilicus is not one of the landmarks.
- Movement names pair joints with planes and axes; precise documentation names joint, plane and side — and limb-specific terms (radial/ulnar, tibial/fibular) exist because rotation makes medial/lateral lie.
- Visceral pain is midline and vague, somatic pain is lateralised and sharp; quadrants and regions are dialects of the same geography, and the choice of dialect records which kind of pain is meant.
- Imaging conventions are inherited dissection-room conventions applied to pictures; MRI signal names mean nothing without the sequence, CT density names mean nothing without contrast state.
- Variation is normal within defined margins; name structures by relations and contents rather than by position or size, and describe what is there, what is not, and what that implies for the next person — in writing, in the standard terms, before anyone cuts.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Directional translation {level=1}
Restate in the standard terms: (a) "the gallbladder fundus, relative to the liver's inferior surface"; (b) "the appendix base, relative to the caecum"; (c) "the heart, relative to the oesophagus and to the body wall"; (d) "the common peroneal nerve, relative to the knee joint line and the fibular neck".
::: solution
(a) The fundus normally projects at the junction of the right and middle thirds of the sulcus on the liver's **inferior (visceral) surface**, and relative to the liver as an organ it is **inferior and anterior**, since it reaches the abdominal wall at the tip of the right ninth costal cartilage. (b) The three taeniae coli converge at the base, which is **posteromedial** on the caecum, at the junction of the caecum's middle and posterior thirds — the geometric reason the vermiform appendix is an appendage of the caecum's posteromedial wall and why its tip wanders (pelvic 30%, retrocaecal 65% — the percentages of surgical importance). (c) The heart lies **anterior** to the oesophagus, **posterior** to the body wall (behind the body of the sternum and the left 3rd–6th costal cartilages, which is the answer a stethoscope needs), and **medial** to the pleural cavities. (d) The common peroneal nerve winds **superficially and laterally (fibular side)** around the **neck of the fibula, a finger-breadth or so distal to the joint line** — a relationship that survives every rotation of the knee and is why a tight plaster at the fibular neck causes foot drop.
:::
:::

::: exercise Count the level {level=1}
A clinical note says "stony dullness and bronchial breathing from the apex to the level of the 6th rib anteriorly, left side". Which cavity is involved, how large an effusion is suggested, and what does "from the sternal angle count down" confirm?
::: solution
"Stony dullness" is the sign of fluid in a serous cavity — **left pleural space**, since the lung is compressed out of the way and the chest wall conducts sound from the consolidated/atelectatic lung above the fluid, giving bronchial breathing at its upper limit. Anteriorly to the 6th rib roughly means the effusion occupies the upper half of the left hemithorax — a substantial effusion (hundreds of millilitres to litres; the costodiaphragmatic recess alone can hold 300–500 mL before it declares itself on a chest film posteriorly). Counting from the sternal angle (the flat corresponding to the T4/5 disc and the second costal cartilage) confirms *which* rib is the sixth and therefore that the fluid's upper border is mid-thorax — and it excludes the commonest error, starting at the first palpable prominence (which is often the clavicle or T1's spinous, depending on which end of the body you are at).
:::
:::

::: exercise Planes of the spine {level=1}
A disc herniation is described as "posterolateral at L4/5". Draw the three planes through the disc and say which movement and which nerve root the phrase predicts.
::: solution
The transverse plane cuts the disc into upper and lower halves; the sagittal plane (coronal in conventional imaging of the spine, which displays both pedicles — the "north–south" views) divides left from right; and the posterolateral descriptor combines them: the herniation is behind the disc's centre (posterior — into the spinal canal's lateral recess/foraminal zone) and off to one side (lateral). The **L5 nerve root** is the one at risk: at each lumbar level the exiting root lies in the foramen below the corresponding pedicle and is spared by a central/posterolateral paracentral disc, which instead catches the **traversing** root of the level below — the single most tested relationship in spine anatomy, and it is pure relational language: "traversing versus exiting root", not size, decides the deficit. The predicted movement is **flexion** worsening the pain (pressing the nucleus posteriorly) and straight-leg raise reproducing it along the root's course — which is why the examination is described, like everything else, as relations between movement and nerve.
:::
:::

::: exercise Body-surface mapping {level=2}
Mark, and give the landmark-defined boundaries of: (a) McBurney's point; (b) the surface marking of the appendix in a pregnant woman at 34 weeks; (c) the ductus (vas) deferens palpation; (d) the apex beat in a patient with right ventricular enlargement.
::: solution
(a) One third of the way along a line from the **anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus**, in the right lower (iliac) quadrant — a line-and-fraction definition, not a point in space. (b) The base (caecal junction) stays roughly at the lateral junction of the pubic tubercle line, but the gravid uterus pushes the appendix **superolaterally**: by the third trimester maximal tenderness migrates towards the right flank or subcostal region — a mobile relation, and one reason appendicitis in pregnancy presents atypically and late. (c) In the posterior aspect of the spermatic cord at the **superficial inguinal ring, or above it within the inguinal canal**, a firm cord-like structure rollable under the skin — palpable because it is a tube of thick muscle with no important neighbours that feel the same, and the basis of vasectomy's anatomy. (d) The apex beat is **the most inferolateral point of cardiac impulse**; in right ventricular enlargement the heart rotates and translates — the apex becomes more anterior and may be impalpable while the left sternal border becomes active; the phrase to remember is "left parasternal heave, apex displaced laterally but not downwards in pure RV enlargement", because the description is of displacement *direction*, the language of the trunk's frame applied to a changing organ.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reading an MRI report {level=2}
An MRI report reads: "A 12 mm lesion in the right anterior horn of the lateral ventricle, hyperintense on T2, hypointense on T1, showing no diffusion restriction." Explain what each coordinate and each signal word contributes, and what the sentence cannot say without contrast images.
::: solution
Coordinates: **right** and **anterior horn** locate it in the ventricle's named chamber and division — and "of the lateral ventricle" already places it deep to the frontal lobe's white matter, because the ventricle is a space within the cerebrum. Signal words: **hyperintense on T2** with **hypointensity on T1** is the signature of free water (simple fluid — a cyst, oedema, cerebrospinal fluid), and the signal pattern therefore suggests but does not prove that the lesion is fluid-filled; **no diffusion restriction** argues against thick cellularity/pus/acute infarct. Without contrast, the report cannot say whether there is a **solid enhancing component** — the feature that separates a benign arachnoid/ependymal cyst from a cystic tumour; and sequence-relative signal is not enough to identify tissue, only its water content and barriers. The exercise's moral: the language is compositional — frame + chamber + sequence + behaviour — and a reader's competence is checking which of the four coordinates a given sentence leaves blank.
:::
:::

::: exercise Variation in the operating theatre {level=2}
During laparoscopic cholecystectomy the surgeon notes "a vessel of arterial calibre coursing from the region of the proper hepatic artery towards the liver's segment IV/VII hilum, medial to the expected cystic artery". What variations are in the differential, and what does the critical view of safety actually establish in terms of relational anatomy?
::: solution
The differential for an arterial vessel in the hepatocystic triangle includes a **replaced right hepatic artery** (from the superior mesenteric, running posterolateral to the bile duct), an **accessory right hepatic artery**, a **middle hepatic artery** from the left or right branch, and — the one this description with its medial course suggests — an arterial variant crossing towards the paramedian segments. The critical view of safety is a relational definition in surgical form: clear the hepatocystic triangle of fat and fibrous tissue, divide the serosa off the lower third of the gallbladder from the cystic plate, and confirm **exactly two structures, and only two, entering the gallbladder** — the cystic duct and cystic artery. What the view "establishes" is not that the anatomy is standard but that whichever vessels are present, only these two belong to the gallbladder and the rest belong to the liver and will be preserved. Naming structures by *where they go and what they feed* rather than by what they are called is the only nomenclature robust to variation — the theorem in practice.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write the finding {level=3}
Convert these three findings into precise anatomical notes, each with joint/plane/side or frame/landmark/level as appropriate: (a) a swollen first metacarpophalangeal joint; (b) a pulsatile abdominal mass; (c) pain on turning the head to the right.
::: solution
(a) "Right first metacarpophalangeal joint: swollen, warm, tender to passive flexion and extension (range 0–40° versus 0–90° left); no deviation at rest; first dorsal interosseous wasted" — joint named, side, plane of movement tested, comparison ipsilateral/contralateral. (b) "Pulsatile, expansile mass in the epigastrium and umbilical region, 7 cm across, extending from approximately 2 cm above the umbilicus towards the xiphisternum, non-tender, with bilateral femoral pulses present" — region names (epigastric/umbilical), a size in one line, level in landmarks, and the distal-pulses statement which is anatomical reasoning (an aneurysm embolises; a pulselessness localises). (c) "Rotation of the cervical spine to the right reproduces pain in the right paracervical region radiating to the right occiput, maximal at C2/3 rotation; Spurling's on the right reproduces the radicular pain" — movement, side, direction, level, and the distinction between local and referred/radicular, which is anatomical language for nerve pathology. The pattern to learn: **structure, side, relation, provoking movement** — every examination finding is a set of these four nouns.
:::

::: exercise Design the better report {level=3}
A radiology department proposes replacing all region descriptions in abdominal reports with vertebral-level coordinates ("mass at L2, 4 cm left of midline, 2 cm anterior to the left kidney"). List the gains and the losses of the proposal, and state where each system is the right one.
::: solution
Gains: the coordinate system is **pose-stable** (L2 is L2 supine or erect, unlike "at the umbilicus"), machine-readable, and consistent with how CT and MRI actually display the body; it removes dependence on examiner palpation and on habitus (obesity, ascites, pregnancy, which make surface landmarks lie). Losses: surgeons operate through surfaces — the surgeon converting an endovascular repair needs the *surface projection* of the aortic bifurcation (midline, roughly at the level of the sacral promontory, which is felt — a coordinate translated back into palpation), radiotherapists need surface marks for daily set-up, and clinicians examining patients think in regions because their information is surface-derived (tenderness, masses, reflexes). The right answer, and the standard practice of the better reports, is **dual coding**: state the internal coordinate (vertebral level, named relations) and the surface translation (regions, quadrants, palpable landmarks) so that the same structure can be found by hand and by machine. A language whose primitives are checkable by two independent methods is exactly what the record is for — this is the anatomical-position rule again, one level up.
