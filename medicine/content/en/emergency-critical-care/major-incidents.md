Most of medicine assumes that resources match need: every patient gets the best care available. A major incident breaks that assumption. A bomb in a crowded venue, a train crash, a building collapse, a chemical release, a flood or a cyberattack that disables a hospital's computers can produce more patients, or a greater disruption, than the normal system can absorb. The response depends far more on preparation, organisation and communication than on any single clinical skill.

This chapter covers how major incidents are defined and declared in the UK, the command and control structure, the principles of joint working between emergency services, triage of many casualties, the patterns of injury that are characteristic of mass-casualty events (blast, crush, penetrating injury, and chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear incidents), the ethics of rationing care, the role of the receiving hospital, and recovery and learning afterwards. UK arrangements are described (the Civil Contingencies Act 2004, the NHS England Emergency Preparedness, Resilience and Response framework, and the Joint Emergency Services Interoperability Principles); other countries use similar structures under different names.

## The system {#def-system}

::: definition Major incident {#def-mi}
For the NHS, a **major incident** is any occurrence that presents a serious threat to the health of the community, or causes such numbers or types of casualties, as to require special arrangements to be implemented. The key word is *special*: an incident is major when the normal way of working cannot cope.

Major incidents include **big bang** events (an explosion or crash), **rising tide** events that build over days or weeks (a pandemic, a heatwave, winter pressures), **cloud on the horizon** threats (an expected storm or industrial action), **headline news** events that generate public concern, **chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear and explosive (CBRNe)** incidents, **mass gatherings**, and **internal incidents** such as a fire, flooding, power or IT failure inside a hospital. A **mass casualty incident** is one in which the number of casualties is so large that the usual standard of care cannot be provided to all of them.
:::

In England, NHS organisations have legal duties under the **Civil Contingencies Act 2004**, which defines **Category 1 responders** (the emergency services, NHS trusts, local authorities and others who are at the core of the response) and **Category 2 responders** (organisations that cooperate and share information, such as utilities and transport companies). Category 1 responders must assess risk, maintain emergency plans and business continuity plans, share information and warn the public. NHS England's **Emergency Preparedness, Resilience and Response (EPRR)** framework sets out how NHS organisations meet these duties, including training and regular exercises.

### Declaration

A major incident can be declared by any of the emergency services or by a healthcare organisation. In NHS practice, a hospital may be notified of:

- **"Major incident – standby"**: an incident may need a major incident response; prepare.
- **"Major incident declared – activate plan"**: implement the plan.
- **"Major incident – stand down"**: return to normal working, which itself needs a plan.

The first emergency service crew on scene sends an initial report to its control room. In the UK the standard format is **METHANE**:

| Letter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **M** | Major incident declared or on standby |
| **E** | Exact location |
| **T** | Type of incident |
| **H** | Hazards, present and potential |
| **A** | Access and egress routes |
| **N** | Number, severity and type of casualties |
| **E** | Emergency services present and those required |

A METHANE message is repeated and updated as the situation changes. It gives every control room the same picture at the same time.

### Command and control

::: definition Command levels and the joint principles {#def-command}
UK emergency services use three levels of command:

- **Strategic (gold)**: sets the overall aims and policy of the response, allocates resources, and handles the wider consequences; usually away from the scene, in a strategic coordinating group.
- **Tactical (silver)**: plans and coordinates the response to achieve the strategic aims; often near the scene.
- **Operational (bronze)**: carries out the work at the scene, often in defined sectors or functions.

The **Joint Emergency Services Interoperability Principles (JESIP)** describe how commanders from different services work together. The five principles are to **co-locate**, **communicate**, **co-ordinate**, **jointly understand risk**, and achieve **shared situational awareness**. JESIP also provides a **Joint Decision Model** (gather information and intelligence, assess risks and develop a working strategy, consider powers, policies and procedures, identify options and contingencies, take action and review what happened, all centred on the shared aim of saving lives and reducing harm).
:::

Hospitals have their own command structure, mirroring the same levels: a strategic lead (often an executive director), a tactical **hospital control team** (a senior clinician, senior nurse and manager) and operational leads in each area, such as the emergency department, theatres and critical care. Every member of staff has an **action card** that says what to do in the first minutes.

## The clinical work {#def-clinical}

### Triage

In normal practice, the sickest patient is treated first. In a mass casualty incident, the aim changes.

::: theorem The principle of mass-casualty triage {#thm-triage}
When the number of casualties exceeds the resources available to treat them, care is prioritised to **do the greatest good for the greatest number**: casualties are sorted by the urgency of their need and the likelihood that treatment will change their outcome, rather than by severity alone, and the resources used on any single casualty are limited so that more people can be saved.
:::

::: proof
Each casualty can be thought of as having a probability of survival with treatment and a probability without, and a cost in resources (staff time, theatre time, blood, ventilators). In normal practice resources are effectively unlimited, so it makes sense to treat everyone, starting with the most severe. In a mass casualty incident the resources are fixed for the first hours. Spending them on a casualty whose chance of survival changes little whatever is done (either because the injuries are minor, or because they are almost certainly unsurvivable with the resources available) leaves less for casualties whose chance of survival depends critically on prompt treatment. Sorting casualties so that those with the largest gain per unit of resource are treated first maximises the expected number of survivors. This is why triage categories are about priority, why the categories are reassessed as resources change, and why the "expectant" category exists only when resources are overwhelmed.
:::

Triage is done repeatedly: at the scene (**primary triage**, rapid and simple), at the casualty clearing station and on arrival at hospital (**secondary triage**, more detailed), and again as patients' conditions and resources change. The categories used in the UK are:

| Priority | Colour | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Red | Immediate: needs life-saving intervention now |
| **P2** | Yellow | Urgent: needs intervention within a few hours |
| **P3** | Green | Delayed: walking wounded, can wait |
| **Expectant** | Blue (where used) | Injuries so severe that survival is unlikely with the resources available; given comfort care and reassessed when resources allow |
| **Dead** | White or black | Dead |

::: definition Primary triage: Ten Second Triage {#def-tst}
In 2023, NHS England announced that the triage sieve used by ambulance services since 2013 would be replaced by two tools, rolled out from 2023 to 2024: **Ten Second Triage (TST)**, designed so that any responder, including police and fire officers, can use it, and the **Major Incident Triage Tool (MITT)** for NHS clinical responders. TST asks, in order:

1. **Walking?** If yes, **P3**.
2. **Severe bleeding?** If yes, control it (pressure, packing, tourniquet) and categorise as **P1**.
3. **Talking?** If yes, check for a **penetrating injury** to the chest, abdomen, back or neck: if present **P1**, otherwise **P2**.
4. **Breathing?** (after opening the airway) If yes, place in the recovery position: **P1**. If no, the casualty is categorised as **not breathing**.

It uses no physiological measurements, so it is quick and can be taught to non-clinicians. Internationally, similar tools include **START** (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment), developed in California in the 1980s, and **SALT** (Sort, Assess, Lifesaving interventions, Treatment/Transport), developed in the USA in 2008.
:::

Triage is imperfect. **Over-triage** (assigning a higher priority than needed) wastes scarce resources on people who could wait; analyses of terrorist bombings have associated high over-triage rates with higher mortality among the critically injured, because hospitals are flooded with the less severely injured. **Under-triage** (assigning too low a priority) delays treatment for people who need it urgently. Every triage system trades one against the other.

### Patterns of injury

**Blast injury** is classified by mechanism:

- **Primary**: the pressure wave damages air-filled organs: tympanic membranes, lungs (**blast lung**, with haemorrhage and contusion that may appear hours later), and bowel.
- **Secondary**: fragments and debris cause penetrating injuries, often many, with contamination.
- **Tertiary**: the casualty is thrown against objects, causing blunt injury, fractures and traumatic amputation.
- **Quaternary**: everything else, including burns, crush injury, inhalation of smoke and dust, and exacerbation of illness.

Blast casualties frequently have multiple injuries, and the most visible injury is not always the most dangerous. Bombings may also involve deliberate secondary devices targeting responders, so scene safety is paramount.

**Crush injury** occurs when a person is trapped under heavy weight, for example in collapsed buildings. When the pressure is released, potassium, myoglobin and acids from damaged muscle enter the circulation (**crush syndrome**), causing hyperkalaemia, arrhythmias, hypovolaemia and acute kidney injury. Fluid resuscitation before release, cardiac monitoring and early renal replacement capacity are key.

**Penetrating injury** from firearms or knives predominates in marauding terrorist attacks. The principles of damage control (stop the bleeding, tourniquets, rapid transfer to surgery) are those of trauma care in [[surgery/trauma]].

**CBRN incidents**. In a chemical incident, casualties may arrive at the hospital without warning and contaminate the department. The UK **"Remove, Remove, Remove"** approach for the public and first responders is to **remove** yourself from the immediate area, **remove** outer clothing (which removes most contamination), and **remove** the substance from the skin by blotting and rinsing. Hospitals have plans and protective equipment for decontamination before patients enter the department. Specific antidotes, such as atropine and oximes for nerve agents, are described in [[emergency-critical-care/poisoning]]. Biological incidents behave more like rising-tide events, recognised through clusters of unusual illness.

### The receiving hospital

When a major incident is declared, a hospital typically:

1. Opens a **control room** and assigns roles from action cards.
2. **Clears the emergency department**: moves or discharges current patients to make space.
3. **Creates capacity** across the hospital: cancels elective operations, expedites discharges of patients who are well enough, opens additional critical care beds, and calls in staff according to the plan.
4. Sets up a **triage point** at the front door, led by a senior clinician, and areas for P1, P2 and P3 patients.
5. Applies **damage control** principles: limited, life-saving surgery first; definitive surgery later.
6. Establishes systems for **patient identification** (major incident numbering), tracking, a **casualty bureau** link with police, and information for relatives.
7. Keeps **business continuity** for the patients already in the hospital.

The effort needed is not only in the first hours: patients with complex injuries may need repeated operations, critical care and rehabilitation for weeks, and the hospital must plan for that sustained load.

::: warning Treating the first casualties to arrive as the sickest
In a major incident, the first patients to reach hospital are often the least injured: walking wounded who make their own way or are carried by bystanders. If they are absorbed into resuscitation bays and operating theatres, there may be no capacity for the critically injured who arrive later by ambulance after extrication. Hospital triage must keep the scale of the incident in mind, keep resuscitation capacity for those who need it, and redirect P3 patients to a separate area.
:::

### Ethics of mass-casualty care

In a mass casualty incident, the standard of care shifts from what is best for each individual to what is best for the population of casualties, often described as **crisis standards of care**. This is ethically uncomfortable but defensible when it is based on transparent, pre-agreed criteria, applied consistently, without discrimination on grounds irrelevant to clinical need (such as social status, disability as such, or ability to pay), reviewed regularly, and abandoned as soon as resources allow. The expectant category is the clearest example: it does not mean abandonment, but comfort and reassessment. Clinicians who must make these decisions need institutional support, and the decisions should be made by senior staff where possible. These principles are discussed further in [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]].

Children, older people, people with disabilities, pregnant women and people who do not speak English are more vulnerable in disasters, and plans should include them explicitly: paediatric equipment and dosing, accessible information and communication, interpreters, and safeguarding of unaccompanied children.

## The aftermath {#def-aftermath}

### Recovery and stand-down

Stand-down is a managed process, not a moment. The hospital restores normal services, catches up on cancelled work, replenishes supplies, and supports staff. A **hot debrief** immediately afterwards captures fresh lessons and checks on staff welfare; a **structured debrief** within weeks, and a **formal report**, identify what worked and what did not, and lead to changes in the plan.

### Psychological care

Many survivors, bereaved relatives and responders experience distress after a major incident; most recover with practical support and time. Evidence does not support routine single-session psychological debriefing for everyone, which may even be harmful; instead, current practice is **psychological first aid** (practical help, information and a sense of safety and connection), **active monitoring** to identify those who develop persistent symptoms, and evidence-based treatment, such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, for post-traumatic stress disorder. Staff welfare includes rest, food, recognition, and access to occupational health and peer support.

### Learning

Major incidents and their inquiries have shaped current practice. In the UK, the response to the **London bombings of 7 July 2005** led to reviews of communications between services, and the **Manchester Arena bombing of 22 May 2017** led to a public inquiry whose report on the emergency response (2022) identified serious failures of joint working and communication between the emergency services, including that fire and rescue crews did not arrive at the scene for around two hours, and concluded that one of the people who died would probably have survived with an adequate response. Such reports are why JESIP training, joint exercises and interoperable communications are emphasised. Regular **exercises** (table-top, command post and live exercises) test plans before they are needed.

## Worked cases

::: example Triage at the footbridge {#ex-triage}
A footbridge over a dual carriageway collapses during a festival. The first ambulance clinician finds 40 casualties. Using Ten Second Triage: 24 are walking; of the 16 who cannot walk, 3 have severe bleeding from limb injuries; of the remaining 13, 8 are talking, 2 of whom have penetrating injuries to the torso from debris; of the 5 who are not talking, 4 are breathing after the airway is opened and 1 is not. Assign the categories and count them.
::: solution
- Walking: **24 P3**.
- Severe bleeding: bleeding controlled with pressure or tourniquets, **3 P1**.
- Talking with a penetrating torso injury: **2 P1**; talking without: **6 P2**.
- Not talking but breathing: recovery position, **4 P1**.
- Not breathing after airway opening: **1 not breathing**.

Totals: P1 $= 3 + 2 + 4 = 9$; P2 $= 6$; P3 $= 24$; not breathing $= 1$; overall $9 + 6 + 24 + 1 = 40$. The METHANE report should give these numbers. The 24 P3 casualties should be directed to a separate area, so that they do not overwhelm the nearest emergency department, and all casualties will be reassessed at secondary triage.
:::
:::

::: example The Tuesday the systems went white {#ex-cyber}
At 09:15 on a weekday, a ransomware attack disables a hospital's electronic patient record, laboratory and radiology systems, and pathology results stop arriving. There are no casualties. Is this a major incident, and what should happen?
::: solution
Yes. A major incident is defined by the need for special arrangements, not by casualties. This is an **internal incident** threatening patient safety across the hospital: results cannot be reported, prescriptions and observations cannot be recorded electronically, and imaging cannot be viewed.

The hospital declares an internal critical or major incident, according to its plan, and sets up command and control. The response includes switching to **paper-based downtime procedures** (prepared in advance), telephoning critical results, prioritising urgent laboratory and imaging work, considering diversion of ambulances and transfer of time-critical patients, informing regional NHS teams and the national cyber security bodies, and communicating clearly with staff and patients. Recovery may take weeks, including reconciling paper records into the electronic system. Business continuity planning for exactly this scenario is part of EPRR.
:::
:::

::: example The exercise that found the generator {#ex-exercise}
During a live exercise simulating a power cut combined with a major incident, a hospital discovers that its backup generator supports the operating theatres but not the critical care ventilator sockets in a newly built extension, and that the major incident action cards still list staff who left two years ago. How should the hospital use these findings?
::: solution
This is exactly what exercises are for: finding failures safely. The findings are recorded in a formal **exercise report** with an action plan, owners and deadlines: urgent electrical work to put critical equipment on essential supplies (with interim mitigation, such as moving ventilated patients or providing uninterruptible power supplies); updating action cards and the call-in list, with a process for regular review; and re-testing after the changes. Reporting the findings to the board and the local resilience forum ensures accountability. Exercises that find nothing are usually not testing hard enough.
:::
:::

::: example The week after the storm {#ex-weekthree}
A severe storm floods part of a town. The acute phase lasts two days with a handful of injuries, but in the following weeks the local emergency department sees more older people with falls, infections and decompensated chronic illness, the community nursing service cannot reach patients in damaged homes, and two dialysis patients miss sessions. What does this illustrate?
::: solution
Many disasters are **rising tide** incidents in their health effects. Disruption of housing, power, transport, medicines, care services and social networks harms people with chronic illness and those who depend on regular care, such as dialysis, oxygen, insulin and home care. Planning should identify **vulnerable people** in advance (through registers held by local authorities, community services and utilities), maintain continuity for critical services such as dialysis (with alternative units and transport), and include mental health support. The recovery phase may produce more illness and death than the acute phase, so the stand-down of the acute response should not mean the end of the health response.
:::
:::

::: quiz
At the scene of an explosion, a responder using Ten Second Triage finds a man who cannot walk, has no severe bleeding, is talking, and has a small penetrating wound to the back of the chest. What category is he?
- [ ] P3, because he is talking.
- [ ] P2, because he is talking and not bleeding severely.
- [x] P1, because a talking casualty with a penetrating torso injury is prioritised as immediate.
- [ ] Not breathing.
::: solution
In TST, a casualty who is not walking, has no severe bleeding and is talking is checked for a penetrating injury to the chest, abdomen, back or neck. If present, they are P1, because such injuries can cause rapid deterioration from internal bleeding or tension pneumothorax even when the casualty looks well at first.
:::
:::

::: history
The idea of sorting the wounded by the urgency of their injuries rather than by rank is credited to **Dominique-Jean Larrey**, chief surgeon to Napoleon's armies, who in the 1790s introduced "flying ambulances" to bring the wounded quickly from the battlefield and treated them in order of need. In the First World War, the French word *triage* became standard military medical practice, and the wars of the twentieth century refined evacuation chains and damage control surgery. Civilian triage tools followed: **START** was developed in Newport Beach, California, in the early 1980s, and the UK's major incident training courses (such as MIMMS, Major Incident Medical Management and Support, first run in the 1990s) taught the triage sieve and command structures to a generation of clinicians. The **Civil Contingencies Act 2004** gave UK emergency planning its current legal framework, and **JESIP** was launched in 2012 after inquiries into incidents such as the 2005 London bombings had shown failures of joint working.
:::

::: summary
- A major incident is any event that needs special arrangements; it may be a big bang, rising tide, CBRN or internal incident, with or without casualties.
- UK duties come from the Civil Contingencies Act 2004 and the NHS EPRR framework; plans are tested by regular exercises.
- Declarations: standby, declared, stand down. The first report from the scene uses METHANE.
- Command is strategic (gold), tactical (silver) and operational (bronze); JESIP asks services to co-locate, communicate, co-ordinate, jointly understand risk and share situational awareness.
- Mass-casualty triage aims to do the greatest good for the greatest number. Ten Second Triage: walking (P3), severe bleeding (P1), talking (P2 unless penetrating torso injury, then P1), breathing (P1) or not breathing.
- Over-triage overwhelms hospitals; under-triage delays care for the critically injured.
- Blast injuries are primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary; crush syndrome causes hyperkalaemia and kidney injury; chemical incidents need "Remove, Remove, Remove" and decontamination.
- Hospitals clear the emergency department, create capacity, triage at the door and use damage control surgery; recovery, staff welfare, psychological first aid and learning continue long after stand-down.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Write the METHANE {level=1}
A coach carrying 30 schoolchildren and 3 adults has overturned on a motorway slip road in the rain; fuel is leaking. You are the first paramedic on scene and count roughly 8 casualties who cannot walk. Fire and police are not yet present. Write a METHANE message.
::: solution
**M**: Major incident declared. **E**: exact location (the motorway, junction number, slip road and direction, with what3words or a grid reference if available). **T**: overturned coach, multiple casualties, many children. **H**: leaking fuel (fire risk), live traffic, wet road, possible further instability of the vehicle. **A**: access via the slip road from the main carriageway; suggest closure and a rendezvous point. **N**: about 33 casualties, approximately 8 non-walking; numbers and severity to follow after triage; many paediatric patients. **E**: one ambulance present; require fire and rescue (extrication and fuel hazard), police (traffic and scene management), further ambulances, a senior clinician and a hazardous area response team; alert paediatric receiving hospitals.
:::
:::

::: exercise Count the categories {level=1 check="9"}
In the footbridge collapse in [[#ex-triage]], how many casualties were categorised as P1?
::: solution
$3$ (severe bleeding) $+ 2$ (talking with penetrating torso injury) $+ 4$ (not talking but breathing) $= 9$.
:::

::: exercise Surge capacity {level=2 check="105"}
A hospital has 900 adult beds, of which 95% are occupied when a major incident is declared. The plan expects to discharge 60 patients rapidly. How many beds will be available?
::: solution
Occupied beds: $0.95 \times 900 = 855$, so $900 - 855 = 45$ are free. Adding 60 rapid discharges gives $45 + 60 = 105$ beds. Beds are only part of capacity: staff, theatre time, critical care beds and blood products may be the real limits.
:::
:::

::: exercise Over-triage rate {level=2 check="67"}
After an incident, review shows that of 9 casualties triaged P1 at the scene, only 3 had injuries that required immediate life-saving intervention. What percentage of P1 casualties were over-triaged? Give the answer to the nearest whole percent.
::: solution
Over-triaged: $9 - 3 = 6$. Rate $= 6/9 = 0.667$, which is **67%**. High over-triage is common with simple primary triage tools and is accepted to keep under-triage low; secondary triage at the casualty clearing station and at hospital corrects it.
:::
:::

::: exercise Triage the school hall {level=2}
After a roof collapse at a school, apply Ten Second Triage to: (a) a teacher walking with a forearm laceration; (b) a 10-year-old lying still, not talking, breathing after the airway is opened; (c) a 14-year-old with spurting bleeding from the thigh, screaming; (d) a 12-year-old who cannot stand because of a leg injury, talking, no penetrating wounds.
::: solution
(a) Walking: P3.
(b) Not walking, no severe bleeding, not talking, breathing: recovery position, P1.
(c) Not walking, severe bleeding: apply a tourniquet or direct pressure, P1.
(d) Not walking, no severe bleeding, talking, no penetrating injury: P2.
Children who are too young to talk are treated as "not talking" in TST, which may raise their priority.
:::
:::

::: exercise Organise the receiving hospital {level=2}
You are the senior emergency physician on duty when your hospital is told "major incident declared – activate plan" after a stabbing attack at a shopping centre with an estimated 15 casualties. List your first ten minutes of actions.
::: solution
Take your action card; confirm the declaration and the expected numbers and arrival times with ambulance control; establish emergency department command and inform the hospital control team. Clear the department: move admitted patients to wards, discharge or redirect minor cases. Set up a triage point at the ambulance entrance led by a senior clinician, with P1, P2 and P3 areas and a separate area for walking wounded. Allocate teams (a senior doctor, nurse and support staff per resuscitation bay), alert theatres, critical care, radiology, blood bank (major haemorrhage packs) and the trauma team. Request staff according to the call-in plan. Ensure patient labelling with major incident numbers and a tracking board, and a relatives' area. Brief staff on penetrating trauma priorities (haemorrhage control, tourniquets, damage control surgery) and on security: the attack may not be over, and the hospital may itself be a target.
:::
:::

::: exercise Judge the standard of care {level=3}
During a pandemic surge, a hospital has two critical care beds left and five patients who would benefit from ventilation. A clinician proposes allocating them to the two youngest patients. Discuss whether this is ethically acceptable and what a better process looks like.
::: solution
Age alone is a crude and potentially discriminatory criterion. It may correlate with the likelihood of benefit, but it is not the same thing, and allocation decisions should be based on clinical need and the likelihood and amount of benefit from the scarce resource. A better process uses criteria agreed in advance and published (such as short-term survival probability from organ dysfunction scores and, carefully, frailty), applied consistently by senior clinicians or a triage team not directly treating the patients, with regular reassessment (including withdrawing a resource from a patient who is not improving, if the criteria say so), documentation, appeal routes, and good palliative care for those not allocated. The decisions should be transparent to the public, and the criteria should be relaxed as soon as resources allow. Fairness in process is as important as the criteria themselves.
:::
:::

::: exercise Build the vulnerable-population plan {level=3}
A local resilience forum asks the NHS to contribute to a plan for prolonged power cuts in winter. Identify three groups of people at particular health risk and one measure for each.
::: solution
1. **People dependent on electrically powered medical equipment** (home oxygen concentrators, home ventilators, home dialysis, powered beds): maintain registers shared with electricity companies (such as the Priority Services Register), and provide backup oxygen cylinders and plans for transfer to hospital or a community facility.
2. **Older and frail people living alone**: proactive welfare checks by community services and volunteers, warm spaces, and checking medicine supplies.
3. **People who need cold-chain medicines** (insulin, some biologics): advice on storage, and community pharmacy plans for replacement.
Other answers include people receiving home care, people with dementia or mental illness, and families with young children. Each measure needs a responsible organisation, a way of reaching people, and testing in an exercise.
:::
