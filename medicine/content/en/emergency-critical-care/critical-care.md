The intensive care unit is the hospital's argument that dying deserves engineering: one nurse to one or two patients, organs supported on schedules, and a monitoring technology so complete that the specialty's own diseases - delirium, weakness, post-intensive-care syndrome, the family's traumatic memories - had to be named and then unbundled. **This chapter covers the organisation and admission logic of the modern ICU (the graded critical-care tiers and outreach, the admission-escalation-triage decisions and their documentation), the daily practice that determines outcome more than any monitor (the ABCDEF-era bundles: analgesia-first sedation and daily interruption, spontaneous breathing trials, early mobilisation, delirium screening and prevention, sleep and nutrition and glycaemia and the prevention-of-harm bundles for lines, tubes, pressure and thrombosis), the organ-support menu with its indications and its ethical ceilings (ventilation, vasopressors and mechanical circulation, renal replacement, the escalation-to-refractory decisions), the communication and family-centred machinery (the daily update rhythm, the family conference, the ICU diary and the long tail of post-intensive-care syndrome), and the specialty's hardest competence - recognising when support has become prolonging and leading that conversation well.**

## Where this leads

The ventilator mechanics are in [[emergency-critical-care/airway-ventilation]], shock and vasoactive drugs in [[emergency-critical-care/shock-fluids]], delirium prevention and management in [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]], the palliative interface in [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]] and [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]], sepsis in [[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]], and organ-specific critical illness in [[respiratory/respiratory-failure]] and [[renal/acute-kidney-injury]].

::: history
**Intensive care was invented by an epidemic and a war. The First World War's shock and respiratory wounds produced Cushing's post-operative 'hospitals within hospitals' and the 1919-20 polio epidemic produced the iron lung; the Copenhagen polio epidemic of 1952-53, when Bjørn Ibsen and colleagues manual-ventilated paralytic patients around the clock with medical students and housewives and dropped case-fatality from about 90 to 40 percent, is the specialty's accepted birth, because it proved that prolonged artificial respiration could be organised as a unit rather than an improvisation. The London Polio Unit at the Queen Elizabeth, Birmingham, and the prescient notes of Florence Nightingale, who described the hospital ward as an intensive-care space decades earlier, and the anaesthetists who staffed it, along with the iron-lung wards of the United States - the National Foundation's wards, the 'Cavalier' respirator, and later the tank-respirator generations' memories - carried the model through the 1950s. Then monitoring made it a medicine rather than a nursing feat: Bjørk's and Swan and Ganz's catheters brought the circulation indoors in the 1970s, PEEP and blood-gas analysis the lungs, and the growth of renal replacement and parenteral nutrition made multi-organ support imaginable; the first coronary and surgical ICUs of the 1960s (the Belfast and Kansas City presences, Hughes and the shock-ward lineage) merged into general ICUs. The 1990s began the audit era - the APACHE and SOFA scores, the first standardised mortality-ratio league tables (the 1993 US Medicare/CMS ICU-reporting experiment, abandoned after its mortality predictions proved wildly imprecise at hospital level, a lesson every subsequent league table has had to relearn), and the UK ICNARC and Australian and New Zealand NZICU data systems. The 2000s brought the trials that changed practice: the low-tidal-volume ventilation ofARDS from the NHLBI ARDSNet (2000), the early goal-directed therapy tide and its later controlled-trial correction (ProCESS, ARISE, ProMISe after the 2014-19 SEMI-collapse), the tight-glycaemic-control reversal (NICE-SUGAR, 2009), the proning survival signal (PROSEVA, 2013), the sedation and mobilisation trials, and the 2016 redefinition of sepsis; and the harm discoveries - the 'ICU psychosis' of the 1960s renamed and measured as delirium, the ICU-acquired weakness literature from the 1990s, and post-intensive-care syndrome and PICS-F (the 2009-2011 consensus statements and IOM report) which forced the field to define success as who the patient becomes, not only whether the organ was supported. The ethical spine ran alongside: the 1976 Karen Ann Quinlan case and the President's Commission-era withdrawal-of-support jurisprudence, the UK's Bland (1993) and later Winship and James rulings on advance decisions and best interests, the Te Sun and the Brazilian and Italian constitutions' different answers to who decides, and the pandemic-era triage-protocol debates (the 'maximise lives saved' frameworks and the disability-rights objections) which will be a chapter of its own. The pandemic of 2020 supplied the specialty's newest epidemiology: ECMO's renaissance in severe respiratory failure with early-trial confusion and later observational clarity, the self-proning-at-home advice that came from the Australian and Argentine ED wards, the mortality comparisons across health systems and the moral-injury and workforce-exit data whose consequences ICUs are still paying for - and the recognition, now in every 2020s strategy document, that intensive care's ceiling is not technology but the number of trained people willing to do the night shift. Its history ends where every ICU ward round begins: with a nurse deciding today which of yesterday's supports can be removed.** - [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]], [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]], [[respiratory/respiratory-failure]].**
:::

::: summary
- The ICU is graded (ward outreach, step-down, intermediate and full critical care), with admission governed by reversibility and escalation-ceiling conversations documented daily, and triage in scarcity following published 'maximise lives saved' frameworks.
- Outcome tracks the daily bundles more than the monitors: analgesia-first light sedation with daily interruption and breathing trials, early mobilisation, delirium screening, sleep protection, nutrition within 48 hours, line and ventilator care bundles, and thromboprophylaxis.
- Organ support is a menu with indications and ceilings: lung-protective ventilation and proning, noradrenaline-first vasoactive strategies, CRRT, and mechanical circulation (ECMO) only with a destination.
- Families are second patients: scheduled plain-language updates, open visiting, conferences, the ICU diary; family PTSD is an outcome measure, not a soft one.
- Prognostication is probabilistic; time-limited trials of support and goals-of-care conversations (know-hope-worry-matters) replace verdicts, and withdrawal-with-dignity protocols are core ICU competence.
- Survival leaves sequelae - ICU-acquired weakness, cognitive and psychological morbidity (PICS) - so the post-ICU clinic and follow-up are part of the intervention, not an appendix.
:::
