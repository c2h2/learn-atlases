Every day an adult eats food containing several hundred grams of carbohydrate, protein and fat, together with about 2 litres of drink. The gut adds about 7 litres of its own secretions. By the time the residue leaves as stool, almost all of that water and nearly all of the nutrients have crossed into the body. The gastrointestinal tract achieves this with four functions working together: **motility** moves and mixes the contents, **secretion** supplies enzymes, acid, bicarbonate and bile, **digestion** breaks large molecules into small ones, and **absorption** carries the small molecules across the lining.

Most gastrointestinal symptoms are failures of one of these four functions. Diarrhoea is usually a failure of absorption or an excess of secretion. Malabsorption is a failure of digestion or absorption. Reflux and many ulcers are disorders of motility and acid secretion. If you understand the normal physiology, the diseases in the rest of this course become much easier to reason about.

This chapter covers the structure and control of the gut wall, motility, the main secretions and the hormones that control them, the digestion and absorption of each class of nutrient, the special cases of iron, vitamin B12 and bile salts, and finally the mechanisms of diarrhoea and malabsorption.

## The gut wall and its control

The tube from oesophagus to rectum has the same basic layers throughout. The **mucosa** is the epithelium with its supporting lamina propria and a thin muscle layer. The **submucosa** carries blood vessels, lymphatics and a nerve plexus. The **muscularis propria** has an inner circular and an outer longitudinal layer of smooth muscle. The **serosa** covers the outside where the gut lies within the peritoneum. Anatomical detail is in [[anatomy/abdomen]].

In the small intestine, circular folds, villi and microvilli multiply the absorptive surface several hundred times. Mature absorbing cells sit on the villi; dividing stem cells sit in the crypts. The epithelium is replaced every 3 to 5 days, which is why it is so sensitive to chemotherapy and radiation.

::: definition Motility and its control {#def-motility}
**Gastrointestinal motility** is the coordinated contraction of gut smooth muscle that mixes and propels the contents. It is controlled at three levels:

- **Pacemaker cells.** The interstitial cells of Cajal generate rhythmic **slow waves** of membrane potential. Slow waves set the maximum frequency of contraction: about 3 per minute in the stomach, about 12 per minute in the duodenum and fewer in the ileum.
- **The enteric nervous system.** The **myenteric (Auerbach) plexus** between the muscle layers mainly controls motility. The **submucosal (Meissner) plexus** mainly controls secretion and local blood flow. Together they contain hundreds of millions of neurons and can run reflexes without input from the brain.
- **Extrinsic nerves and hormones.** Parasympathetic fibres (vagus and pelvic nerves) generally increase motility and secretion. Sympathetic fibres generally reduce them and tighten sphincters. Gut hormones modulate both.
:::

A slow wave alone does not cause contraction; spike potentials triggered by stretch, acetylcholine or hormones must ride on it. The slow wave sets the rhythm and the nerves decide whether each beat is used.

Three motor patterns matter clinically. **Peristalsis** is a wave of contraction behind a bolus with relaxation in front of it. It is a local reflex triggered by stretch, and it is how the oesophagus clears swallowed food in a few seconds. **Segmentation** consists of stationary contractions that divide and remix the contents of the small intestine, which increases contact with the mucosa. The **migrating motor complex** (MMC) is a band of strong contractions that sweeps from stomach to ileum about every 90 minutes during fasting. It clears residue and bacteria between meals. Motilin triggers it and eating abolishes it; erythromycin acts on motilin receptors, which is why it can be used as a prokinetic.

The stomach has its own behaviour. Its upper part, the fundus, relaxes as food arrives (**receptive relaxation**), so a meal can be stored without a rise in pressure. The antrum grinds food against a closing pylorus and allows only particles smaller than about 2 mm to pass. Liquids leave the stomach quickly; solids leave in a roughly linear fashion over 2 to 4 hours. Fat and acid in the duodenum slow emptying through nerves and hormones, so a fatty meal stays in the stomach longer.

The colon moves its contents slowly, over 1 to 3 days, allowing water absorption and bacterial fermentation. A few times a day, often after a meal (the **gastrocolic reflex**), **mass movements** push contents over long distances.

## Secretions and gut hormones

The gut and its glands secrete about 7 litres a day. Each secretion has a job and a control system.

::: definition The main digestive secretions {#def-secretion}
| Secretion | Volume per day | Main contents | Main stimuli |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saliva | about 1–1.5 L | water, bicarbonate, amylase, mucus | parasympathetic nerves |
| Gastric juice | about 2 L | hydrochloric acid, pepsinogen, intrinsic factor, mucus | vagus, gastrin, histamine |
| Pancreatic juice | about 1.5 L | bicarbonate, proteases, lipase, amylase | secretin, CCK, vagus |
| Bile | about 0.5–1 L | bile salts, phospholipid, cholesterol, bilirubin | CCK (gallbladder contraction), secretin (ductal bicarbonate) |
| Intestinal secretion | about 1.5 L | water and electrolytes, mucus | local reflexes, CFTR chloride channel |

Volumes are approximate and vary with diet.
:::

### Gastric acid

The **parietal cells** of the gastric body and fundus secrete hydrochloric acid at a concentration of up to about 150 mmol/L, giving a luminal pH of 1 to 2. The final step is the **H⁺/K⁺-ATPase**, the proton pump, which exchanges hydrogen ions for potassium ions across the cell's apical membrane. Chloride follows through apical channels. For every hydrogen ion secreted, a bicarbonate ion leaves the basolateral side of the cell into the blood. After a meal this produces a small rise in blood bicarbonate, the **alkaline tide**.

Three stimuli act on the parietal cell, and they reinforce each other:

- **Acetylcholine** from vagal nerve endings acts on muscarinic M3 receptors.
- **Gastrin** from G cells in the antrum acts on CCK2 receptors. Gastrin also stimulates the **enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells** to release histamine.
- **Histamine** from ECL cells acts on H2 receptors. This is the strongest single amplifier, because both vagal and gastrin signals pass partly through it.

The brake is **somatostatin** from D cells, which is released when the antral pH falls below about 3. It inhibits gastrin release and acid secretion directly. This negative feedback means acid production limits itself.

Acid secretion has three phases. In the **cephalic phase**, the sight, smell and taste of food act through the vagus. In the **gastric phase**, stretch and peptides in the meal release gastrin; this phase produces most of the acid. In the **intestinal phase**, acid and fat in the duodenum mainly inhibit gastric secretion.

These pathways are the targets of acid-suppressing drugs. **H2-receptor antagonists** block the histamine signal. **Proton pump inhibitors** (PPIs) such as omeprazole and lansoprazole bind irreversibly to active pumps and block the final common step, so they suppress acid more completely. Because PPIs act only on pumps that are actively secreting, they work best when taken before a meal, when the pumps are being switched on. New pumps are synthesised over about a day, so the effect builds over several days and persists after a dose.

### Pancreatic juice and bile

The pancreas has two secretory systems. The **acinar cells** make digestive enzymes, most as inactive precursors. **Trypsinogen** is activated in the duodenum by **enteropeptidase** (enterokinase) on the brush border, and trypsin then activates the other proenzymes. This sequence protects the pancreas from digesting itself; when it fails inside the gland, the result is pancreatitis ([[gastrointestinal/pancreas-biliary]]). The **duct cells** secrete a large volume of bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralises gastric acid in the duodenum. The duct cell uses the **CFTR** chloride channel to drive this secretion, which is why pancreatic damage is so common in cystic fibrosis.

**Bile** is made by hepatocytes and modified by bile duct cells. Its key components are the **bile salts**, which are made from cholesterol and act as detergents. Between meals the sphincter of Oddi is closed and bile is stored and concentrated in the gallbladder. After a fatty meal, cholecystokinin contracts the gallbladder and relaxes the sphincter.

### Gut hormones

The gut is the largest endocrine organ in the body. Its hormone-secreting cells are scattered among ordinary epithelial cells and sense the contents of the lumen. Five hormones are worth knowing in detail.

| Hormone | Made by | Released by | Main actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gastrin | G cells, gastric antrum | peptides, stretch, vagus | stimulates acid secretion and mucosal growth |
| Cholecystokinin (CCK) | I cells, duodenum and jejunum | fat and protein | gallbladder contraction, pancreatic enzyme secretion, slows gastric emptying, satiety |
| Secretin | S cells, duodenum | acid | pancreatic and biliary bicarbonate, inhibits acid secretion |
| GIP and GLP-1 | K cells (duodenum) and L cells (ileum, colon) | glucose and fat | increase insulin release after a meal (the incretin effect); GLP-1 also slows gastric emptying and reduces appetite |
| Motilin | M cells, duodenum | fasting | triggers the migrating motor complex |

Somatostatin acts as a general brake on many of these. GLP-1 has become clinically important: GLP-1 receptor agonists are used in type 2 diabetes and obesity ([[endocrine/diabetes]], [[gastrointestinal/clinical-nutrition]]), and their slowing of gastric emptying explains their main side effects of nausea and fullness.

## Digestion and absorption of nutrients

::: definition Digestion and absorption {#def-digest}
**Digestion** is the breakdown of large food molecules into units small enough to be absorbed: polysaccharides into monosaccharides, proteins into amino acids and small peptides, triglycerides into fatty acids and monoglycerides. **Absorption** is the transfer of these units, together with water, electrolytes, vitamins and minerals, from the lumen across the epithelium into blood or lymph.

Digestion happens in two places. **Luminal digestion** uses enzymes in the gut lumen, mainly from the pancreas. **Brush-border (membrane) digestion** uses enzymes fixed to the enterocyte surface, which finish the job at the point of absorption.
:::

### Carbohydrate

Starch is broken down by salivary and pancreatic **amylase** into maltose, maltotriose and small branched fragments. Brush-border enzymes then complete digestion: maltase and isomaltase for starch fragments, **sucrase** for sucrose and **lactase** for lactose. The products are glucose, galactose and fructose.

Glucose and galactose enter the enterocyte through **SGLT1**, a transporter that carries two sodium ions with each sugar molecule. The low intracellular sodium created by the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase powers this uptake. Fructose enters by facilitated diffusion through **GLUT5**. All three sugars leave the cell into the blood through **GLUT2**.

Lactase activity falls after weaning in most of the world's adults. This is the normal ancestral pattern; **lactase persistence** into adulthood is a genetic variant common in northern European and some African and Middle Eastern populations. A person with low lactase who drinks a lot of milk leaves lactose in the lumen. The lactose holds water osmotically in the small bowel, and colonic bacteria ferment it to gas and short-chain fatty acids, causing bloating and diarrhoea.

### Protein

Pepsin begins protein digestion in the stomach, but most is done by pancreatic proteases and brush-border peptidases. Free amino acids enter through sodium-linked carriers specific to amino acid groups. Di- and tripeptides enter through **PepT1**, a proton-linked transporter, and are split inside the cell.

### Fat

Fat digestion is the most complicated, because fat does not dissolve in water. It needs three steps.

1. **Emulsification.** Gastric churning and bile salts break fat into small droplets, increasing the surface on which enzymes can act.
2. **Lipolysis.** Pancreatic **lipase**, anchored to the droplet surface by **colipase**, splits triglycerides into free fatty acids and 2-monoglycerides.
3. **Micelle formation.** Bile salts gather the products, along with cholesterol and fat-soluble vitamins, into **micelles**: tiny water-soluble aggregates that carry the fat across the unstirred water layer to the enterocyte surface.

Inside the enterocyte the fatty acids and monoglycerides are rebuilt into triglycerides and packaged with apolipoprotein B-48 into **chylomicrons**. Chylomicrons are too large to enter capillaries, so they pass into the lymphatic lacteals and reach the blood through the thoracic duct. Medium-chain fatty acids are the exception: they go directly into portal blood, which is why medium-chain triglyceride supplements help in fat malabsorption.

Because fat absorption depends on bile salts and pancreatic lipase, fat is the nutrient that suffers first when either is lacking. Unabsorbed fat produces **steatorrhoea**: pale, bulky, greasy, offensive stools that float and are hard to flush. Deficiencies of the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K follow.

### Water and electrolytes

About 9 litres of fluid enter the small intestine each day: about 2 litres from drink and about 7 litres from secretions. Of this, the small intestine absorbs about 7.5 litres and the colon about 1.3 litres, leaving only about 100 to 200 mL in the stool. The colon can increase its absorption to perhaps 4 to 5 litres a day when more fluid arrives, which buffers modest small-bowel losses.

::: theorem Water follows solute {#thm-water}
The intestinal epithelium has no pump for water. Water is absorbed or secreted only because solutes, mainly sodium, chloride and nutrients, are moved first and water follows the osmotic gradient they create.
:::

::: proof
The small-intestinal epithelium is leaky: water crosses easily through tight junctions and through aquaporins in the cell membranes. When sodium is pumped from the enterocyte into the lateral intercellular space by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, that space becomes slightly hypertonic and water follows it. Sodium enters the cell from the lumen by several routes: with glucose through SGLT1, with amino acids, and in exchange for hydrogen ions through NHE3. In the colon, sodium also enters through the ENaC channel, which aldosterone up-regulates.

Secretion works in reverse. Crypt cells take up chloride from the blood side through a Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ co-transporter and release it into the lumen through **CFTR** and other chloride channels. Sodium follows between the cells to keep electrical balance, and water follows the salt. Anything that holds CFTR open, such as the rise in cyclic AMP caused by cholera toxin, floods the lumen with salt and water.

It follows that a solute that cannot be absorbed will hold water in the lumen. Unabsorbed lactose, magnesium and polyethylene glycol all cause diarrhoea this way.
:::

This principle is the basis of **oral rehydration solution** (ORS). Even in severe cholera, the sodium–glucose co-transporter SGLT1 still works. Giving sodium together with glucose in the right proportions allows sodium, and therefore water, to be absorbed despite the toxin-driven secretion. The WHO reduced-osmolarity ORS contains 75 mmol/L of sodium and 75 mmol/L of glucose, with a total osmolarity of 245 mOsm/L. Its global impact is discussed in [[public-health/global-health]].

### Minerals and vitamins

Several micronutrients have their own address in the gut, and knowing where each is absorbed predicts the effect of disease or surgery.

| Nutrient | Main site of absorption | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | duodenum and upper jejunum | ferric iron is reduced to ferrous; enters by DMT1; leaves by ferroportin, which hepcidin blocks |
| Calcium | duodenum (active), whole small bowel (passive) | active uptake requires vitamin D |
| Folate | jejunum | deficiency common in coeliac disease |
| Vitamin B12 | terminal ileum | requires intrinsic factor from the stomach |
| Bile salts | terminal ileum | active uptake by the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter |
| Fat-soluble vitamins | small bowel, with fat | need bile salts and lipase |

**Iron** balance is controlled at absorption, because the body has no regulated route of iron loss. When stores are full, the liver makes **hepcidin**, which degrades ferroportin and traps iron in enterocytes that are later shed. Inflammation also raises hepcidin, part of the anaemia of chronic disease ([[blood/anaemia]]).

**Vitamin B12** has the most complex route. In the stomach it is released from food protein by acid and pepsin and binds to haptocorrin from saliva. In the duodenum pancreatic proteases digest haptocorrin, and B12 transfers to **intrinsic factor**, which the parietal cells secrete. The B12–intrinsic factor complex travels to the **terminal ileum**, where it is taken up by a specific receptor (cubilin). The liver holds stores of several years' worth, so deficiency develops slowly after absorption fails.

The B12 route can therefore break at five points: low intake (strict vegan diet), lack of acid or intrinsic factor (autoimmune gastritis, gastrectomy), pancreatic insufficiency, bacterial overgrowth that consumes B12, or terminal ileal disease or resection.

::: example A sore tongue and numb feet {#ex-b12}
A 64-year-old woman of Afro-Caribbean heritage has 6 months of tiredness, a sore red tongue and tingling in both feet. She has hypothyroidism. Haemoglobin is 92 g/L with a mean cell volume of 114 fL, and the blood film shows hypersegmented neutrophils. Serum B12 is low and folate is normal. Intrinsic factor antibodies are positive. Explain the diagnosis using the physiology of B12 absorption, and outline the treatment principle.
::: solution
**Step 1: classify the anaemia.** A high mean cell volume with hypersegmented neutrophils is a **megaloblastic** anaemia. The usual causes are deficiency of B12 or folate, both needed for DNA synthesis. The neuropathy points to B12, because B12 deficiency damages myelin and folate deficiency does not.

**Step 2: find where absorption fails.** Intrinsic factor antibodies are highly specific for **pernicious anaemia**, an autoimmune gastritis that destroys parietal cells. Without intrinsic factor, B12 cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum however much is eaten. Other autoimmune diseases, such as her hypothyroidism, are commonly associated. Pernicious anaemia is not confined to older white Europeans and is well described in people of African and Caribbean heritage, sometimes at younger ages.

**Step 3: choose the route of treatment.** Because intrinsic factor is missing, the usual treatment is intramuscular hydroxocobalamin, which bypasses the gut, continued for life; injections are the standard start when there are neurological features. Folate must not be given alone, because it can correct the anaemia while nerve damage progresses.

Autoimmune gastritis also carries an increased risk of gastric neoplasia ([[gastrointestinal/oesophagus-stomach]]). Management follows local guidelines.
:::
:::

## The enterohepatic circulation

Bile salts are expensive to make and the body recycles them. The total **bile salt pool** is only about 3 g, yet the liver secretes about 15 to 30 g of bile salts a day. The pool must therefore go round several times a day.

::: theorem Bile salts are recycled {#thm-enterohepatic}
About 95% or more of the bile salts secreted into the duodenum are reabsorbed, mainly by active transport in the terminal ileum, and returned to the liver in portal blood for re-secretion. This **enterohepatic circulation** cycles the pool roughly 4 to 12 times a day, more after meals. Faecal loss, about 0.5 g a day, is replaced by new synthesis from cholesterol.
:::

::: proof
Hepatocytes take up bile salts from portal blood with high efficiency, so very little reaches the systemic circulation. In the terminal ileum, the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT) moves bile salts into enterocytes and a basolateral carrier releases them into portal blood. When bile salts return, they inhibit their own synthesis: the ileum releases the hormone FGF19, which suppresses the hepatic enzyme cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase.

If the terminal ileum is diseased or removed, two things change. More bile salts reach the colon, where they stimulate chloride and water secretion. And the liver loses its feedback signal, so it increases synthesis. With a short ileal loss, increased synthesis keeps up and the main problem is **bile acid diarrhoea**. With a long loss, synthesis cannot keep up, the pool shrinks, micelles fail, and **steatorrhoea** follows.
:::

As a rule of thumb, losing less than about 100 cm of terminal ileum causes bile acid diarrhoea, which responds to sequestrants such as colestyramine; losing more causes fat malabsorption, which sequestrants make worse. The 100 cm figure is a guide, not a sharp cut-off.

::: example The ileal resection, three years on {#ex-stones-ileum}
A 34-year-old man with Crohn's disease had 60 cm of terminal ileum removed three years ago. He now has watery diarrhoea four to six times a day, a calcium oxalate kidney stone and a low serum B12. His Crohn's disease is in remission on colonoscopy. Explain each problem.
::: solution
**Diarrhoea.** With 60 cm of ileum gone, bile salt reabsorption is reduced. Unabsorbed bile salts reach the colon and stimulate secretion, giving **bile acid diarrhoea** ([[#thm-enterohepatic]]). Because the loss is under about 100 cm, the liver can make enough new bile salt to keep fat digestion going, so steatorrhoea is not prominent. A trial of a bile acid sequestrant is a reasonable diagnostic and therapeutic step; a SeHCAT scan, where available, measures bile acid retention directly.

**Kidney stone.** Normally dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut, and calcium oxalate is not absorbed. When fat is poorly absorbed, fatty acids bind calcium instead. Oxalate is left free and is absorbed in the colon, whose permeability is also increased by bile salts. Urinary oxalate rises and calcium oxalate stones form. This is **enteric hyperoxaluria**, and it requires an intact colon.

**Low B12.** The B12–intrinsic factor complex is absorbed only in the terminal ileum. After resection of this length, B12 replacement by injection is usually needed for life, and levels should be checked periodically.
:::
:::

## Diarrhoea and malabsorption

::: definition Diarrhoea and its mechanisms {#def-diarrhoea}
**Diarrhoea** is the passage of abnormally liquid or frequent stools, usually three or more loose stools a day. It is **acute** if it lasts less than 14 days and **chronic** if it lasts more than 4 weeks. There are four main mechanisms, and many diseases combine them:

- **Osmotic**: a poorly absorbed solute holds water in the lumen. Examples: lactose in lactase deficiency, magnesium salts, sorbitol. It **stops with fasting**.
- **Secretory**: active chloride secretion, or blocked sodium absorption, drives salt and water into the lumen. Examples: cholera and enterotoxigenic *E. coli*, bile acid diarrhoea, rare hormone-secreting tumours. It **continues with fasting**, and volumes can be large.
- **Inflammatory (exudative)**: a damaged mucosa leaks blood, protein and mucus and absorbs poorly. Examples: inflammatory bowel disease, invasive infection. Blood, fever and raised inflammatory markers are typical.
- **Altered motility**: fast transit reduces contact time. Examples: hyperthyroidism, irritable bowel syndrome, diabetic autonomic neuropathy.
:::

The **faecal osmotic gap** helps to separate osmotic from secretory diarrhoea. Stool in the colon is in osmotic balance with plasma, so its osmolality is close to 290 mOsm/kg. In secretory diarrhoea, almost all of this osmolality comes from sodium and potassium salts. In osmotic diarrhoea, much of it comes from an unmeasured solute. The gap estimates that unmeasured part:

$$
\text{faecal osmotic gap} = 290 - 2\left([\text{Na}^+] + [\text{K}^+]\right)
$$ {#eq-gap}

The factor of 2 counts the anions that accompany the cations. A gap above about 125 mOsm/kg suggests osmotic diarrhoea, and a gap below about 50 mOsm/kg suggests secretory diarrhoea. Values in between are indeterminate. The fixed value of 290 is used because measured stool osmolality rises after collection as bacteria keep fermenting; a measured value much above 290 suggests a delayed sample, and a value well below 290 suggests the sample has been diluted with water or dilute urine.

::: example Osmotic or secretory? {#ex-diarrhoea-types}
Two adults have watery diarrhoea. Calculate the faecal osmotic gap for each and classify the diarrhoea.

(a) A 26-year-old man of Chinese heritage develops bloating and diarrhoea after starting a high-protein diet that includes a litre of milk a day. Stool sodium is 35 mmol/L and potassium 45 mmol/L. The diarrhoea stops during a 48-hour fast.

(b) A 50-year-old woman has 3 litres of watery stool a day that continues while fasting. Stool sodium is 110 mmol/L and potassium 30 mmol/L.
::: solution
Use [[#eq-gap]].

(a) Gap $= 290 - 2(35 + 45) = 290 - 160 = 130$ mOsm/kg. This is above 125, so the diarrhoea is **osmotic**. Stopping with fasting agrees. The likely solute is lactose: low lactase activity in adulthood is the majority pattern in people of East Asian ancestry. A trial of lactose reduction, or a hydrogen breath test, would confirm it.

(b) Gap $= 290 - 2(110 + 30) = 290 - 280 = 10$ mOsm/kg. This is very low, so the stool's osmolality is almost all sodium and potassium salts: **secretory** diarrhoea. Persistence with fasting and the large volume agree. The differential includes bile acid diarrhoea, microscopic colitis, surreptitious stimulant laxative use and rare neuroendocrine tumours. Investigation would follow local guidance, starting with common causes.
:::
:::

::: warning Fasting does not exclude every osmotic cause
A fasting test only helps if the offending solute comes from food. Magnesium-containing antacids or laxatives, or sorbitol in "sugar-free" medicines, keep an osmotic diarrhoea going during a fast. Take a full drug history before interpreting the test.
:::

**Malabsorption** is defective absorption of one or more nutrients. It can arise at three stages:

- **Luminal (digestive) failure**: not enough pancreatic enzymes (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, pancreatic cancer) or not enough bile salts (cholestasis, ileal loss, bacterial overgrowth that breaks down bile salts).
- **Mucosal failure**: the absorbing surface is damaged or reduced, as in coeliac disease, Crohn's disease of the small bowel, or short bowel after resection ([[gastrointestinal/bowel-disease]]).
- **Post-mucosal failure**: the route out of the enterocyte is blocked, as in lymphatic obstruction or intestinal lymphangiectasia.

Malabsorption presents with weight loss, diarrhoea or steatorrhoea, and deficiencies that point to the site: iron and folate in proximal small-bowel disease, B12 in ileal disease, vitamins D and K in fat malabsorption. A low **faecal elastase** suggests exocrine pancreatic insufficiency.

::: example The toddler who will not grow {#ex-cf}
An 11-month-old boy is falling across the weight centiles. He has large, pale, greasy and very offensive stools, a distended abdomen and has had two chest infections this winter. His newborn screening result is not available because he was born abroad. Faecal elastase is very low, and a sweat test shows a sweat chloride of 96 mmol/L. Explain the malabsorption and outline the principles of management.
::: solution
**Step 1: localise.** Pale, bulky, offensive stools mean **steatorrhoea**, so fat is not being digested or absorbed. A very low faecal elastase places the failure in the lumen: the pancreas is not delivering enzymes.

**Step 2: explain the mechanism.** A sweat chloride of 60 mmol/L or more on a properly performed test supports **cystic fibrosis**, and 96 mmol/L is well above that. CFTR is the chloride channel that drives fluid secretion by pancreatic duct cells. Without it, pancreatic secretions are thick, ducts become blocked and the acinar tissue is destroyed, often before birth. Lipase delivery fails first in clinical terms, because fat digestion depends most on pancreatic enzymes. The chest infections reflect the same defect in the airways.

**Step 3: management principles.** Fat malabsorption causes energy loss and deficiencies of vitamins A, D, E and K. Care is led by a specialist cystic fibrosis team and follows national guidance. It includes **pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy** with every meal and snack, adjusted to the fat content and to symptoms and growth; a high-energy diet; supplements of fat-soluble vitamins; and respiratory care. CFTR modulator drugs, which improve the faulty channel's function in people with suitable variants, are now part of treatment ([[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
A person has had their stomach removed (total gastrectomy). Which absorption problem is most certain to develop without lifelong treatment?
- [ ] Fructose malabsorption
- [x] Vitamin B12 deficiency
- [ ] Bile acid diarrhoea
- [ ] Failure to absorb amino acids
::: solution
After total gastrectomy there are no parietal cells, so there is no **intrinsic factor**. Without it, vitamin B12 cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum, and deficiency is inevitable once liver stores run out after a few years. Lifelong B12 replacement is therefore standard. Fructose and amino acids are absorbed by small-bowel transporters that do not depend on the stomach. Bile acid diarrhoea is a problem of the terminal ileum, not the stomach. Iron and calcium absorption are also often reduced after gastrectomy, but less predictably.
:::

::: history
In 1822 a shotgun wound left the Canadian voyageur Alexis St Martin with a permanent opening from his stomach to the skin. The US army surgeon William Beaumont used it over the following decade to study digestion directly: he sampled gastric juice, showed that it contained acid and digested food outside the body, and recorded how emotion and illness changed it. He published his observations in 1833. In 1902 William Bayliss and Ernest Starling showed that acid in the duodenum caused pancreatic secretion even when the nerves were cut, because the duodenal lining released a substance into the blood. They called it **secretin**, and Starling later introduced the word "hormone". At about the same time Ivan Pavlov studied the nervous control of digestive secretion in dogs and received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1904.
:::

::: summary
- The gut moves, secretes, digests and absorbs. Slow waves from the interstitial cells of Cajal set the rhythm; the enteric nervous system, extrinsic nerves and hormones decide when contractions happen.
- Gastric acid comes from the parietal cell proton pump, driven by acetylcholine, gastrin and histamine and braked by somatostatin. PPIs block the final common step.
- Carbohydrate and protein are digested to monomers and absorbed by sodium-linked carriers. Fat needs bile salts, lipase and micelles, so it is the first nutrient to suffer when either bile or pancreatic enzymes are lacking.
- Water always follows solute. Sodium–glucose co-transport still works in secretory diarrhoea, which is why oral rehydration solution works.
- Iron is absorbed in the duodenum under hepcidin control, B12 in the terminal ileum with intrinsic factor, and bile salts in the terminal ileum through the enterohepatic circulation.
- Diarrhoea is osmotic, secretory, inflammatory or motility-related. The faecal osmotic gap, $290 - 2([\text{Na}^+]+[\text{K}^+])$, separates osmotic from secretory causes.
- Malabsorption can be luminal, mucosal or post-mucosal, and its deficiencies point to where the problem lies.
:::

## Where this leads

The structural diseases of each region follow: acid and motility disorders of the upper gut in [[gastrointestinal/oesophagus-stomach]], coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease in [[gastrointestinal/bowel-disease]], and bile and pancreatic disorders in [[gastrointestinal/pancreas-biliary]]. The liver's handling of absorbed nutrients and bilirubin is in [[gastrointestinal/liver-function]], and the practical use of these principles in feeding sick people is in [[gastrointestinal/clinical-nutrition]]. Infectious diarrhoea is covered in [[microbiology/bacterial-pathogenesis]].

## Exercises

::: exercise Draw the parietal cell {level=1}
List the three main stimuli of gastric acid secretion and the receptor each acts on. Name the main inhibitor and the stimulus for its release. Which drug class blocks the final step common to all stimuli?
::: solution
Acetylcholine acts on muscarinic M3 receptors; gastrin acts on CCK2 receptors (on parietal cells and on ECL cells, which then release histamine); histamine acts on H2 receptors. The main inhibitor is somatostatin from antral D cells, released when the antral pH falls below about 3. Proton pump inhibitors block the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase, the final common step ([[#def-secretion]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Where is it absorbed? {level=1}
State the main site of absorption of (a) iron, (b) folate, (c) vitamin B12 and (d) bile salts. Which two deficiencies would you expect after resection of the distal 120 cm of ileum?
::: solution
(a) Duodenum and upper jejunum. (b) Jejunum. (c) Terminal ileum, with intrinsic factor. (d) Terminal ileum, by active transport. After resection of 120 cm of distal ileum, expect **vitamin B12 deficiency** and **bile salt loss**. With more than about 100 cm lost, the bile salt pool usually falls enough to cause fat malabsorption, so deficiencies of the fat-soluble vitamins are also likely.
:::
:::

::: exercise The oral rehydration solution {level=1 check="245"}
The WHO reduced-osmolarity oral rehydration solution contains, in mmol/L: sodium 75, chloride 65, glucose 75, potassium 20 and citrate 10. Assuming each solute contributes one osmole per mole, calculate its total osmolarity in mOsm/L. Why is glucose included?
::: hint
Add the concentrations of all the solutes.
:::
::: solution
Osmolarity $= 75 + 65 + 75 + 20 + 10 = 245$ mOsm/L. Glucose is included because SGLT1 carries sodium into the enterocyte together with glucose. This co-transport is not affected by the toxins that drive secretory diarrhoea, so sodium is absorbed and water follows it ([[#thm-water]]). The slightly hypotonic solution also avoids drawing water into the lumen.
:::
:::

::: exercise Use the gap {level=2 check="150"}
A 45-year-old woman has chronic watery diarrhoea. Stool sodium is 40 mmol/L and potassium is 30 mmol/L. Calculate the faecal osmotic gap in mOsm/kg and classify the diarrhoea. Suggest two causes you would ask about in the history.
::: hint
Use $290 - 2([\text{Na}^+]+[\text{K}^+])$.
:::
::: solution
Gap $= 290 - 2(40 + 30) = 290 - 140 = 150$ mOsm/kg. This is above 125, so the diarrhoea is **osmotic**. Ask about magnesium-containing medicines or supplements, osmotic laxatives, sorbitol or other sugar alcohols in "sugar-free" products, and lactose intake. Carbohydrate malabsorption from mucosal disease, such as coeliac disease, can also contribute. A fasting trial would be expected to reduce the output.
:::
:::

::: exercise Recycle the bile {level=2 check="0.54"}
In an illustrative model, the bile salt pool is 3 g and it cycles 6 times a day. The terminal ileum reabsorbs 97% of the bile salt that passes through it on each cycle. Calculate the daily faecal loss of bile salt in grams, to two decimal places. What must the liver do to keep the pool constant?
::: hint
First find the total bile salt secreted per day, then the fraction not reabsorbed.
:::
::: solution
Daily secretion $= 3 \times 6 = 18$ g. Faecal loss $= 18 \times (1 - 0.97) = 18 \times 0.03 = 0.54$ g per day. To keep the pool constant the liver must synthesise 0.54 g of new bile salt a day from cholesterol. If the ileum is diseased and reabsorption falls, the loss rises, the FGF19 feedback signal falls, and the liver increases synthesis ([[#thm-enterohepatic]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Steatorrhoea without pancreatic disease {level=2}
A 58-year-old man with primary sclerosing cholangitis has pale, greasy stools and a prolonged prothrombin time. His faecal elastase is normal. Explain the mechanism of his steatorrhoea and the prolonged prothrombin time. Why would pancreatic enzymes not help?
::: hint
Which second ingredient, besides lipase, is needed for fat absorption?
:::
::: solution
His pancreas is producing enough lipase, as the normal faecal elastase shows. The problem is **bile**: cholestasis reduces bile salt delivery to the duodenum, so micelles do not form and the products of lipolysis cannot reach the enterocyte efficiently. Fat is malabsorbed, and with it the fat-soluble **vitamin K**. Vitamin K is needed for the gamma-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX and X, so the prothrombin time lengthens. Extra pancreatic enzymes cannot replace bile salts. Parenteral vitamin K should correct the prothrombin time if the liver's synthetic function is preserved ([[gastrointestinal/liver-function]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpret a chronic diarrhoea work-up {level=3}
A 39-year-old man has had 10 months of diarrhoea, 6 kg weight loss and bloating. Results: haemoglobin 108 g/L, mean cell volume 78 fL, ferritin 6 µg/L, serum folate low, B12 normal, albumin 33 g/L, corrected calcium 2.08 mmol/L, alkaline phosphatase mildly raised, faecal elastase normal, faecal calprotectin mildly raised. Where in the gut is the problem most likely, what is the most likely single diagnosis, and what test would you request first?
::: hint
Iron and folate are absorbed in the proximal small bowel; B12 in the terminal ileum.
:::
::: solution
**Localise.** Iron deficiency and folate deficiency with normal B12 point to the **proximal small bowel** (duodenum and jejunum), because that is where iron and folate are absorbed, while the terminal ileum, where B12 is absorbed, seems to be working. A normal faecal elastase makes pancreatic failure unlikely, so the defect is mucosal.

**Interpret the bone results.** Low calcium with raised alkaline phosphatase suggests vitamin D deficiency with osteomalacia, consistent with fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption and reduced calcium absorption in the duodenum. Low albumin reflects malnutrition and possibly protein loss.

**Diagnosis.** The most likely diagnosis is **coeliac disease**, which damages the proximal small bowel most. The first test is **tissue transglutaminase IgA with total IgA**, taken while the person is still eating gluten; further assessment follows national guidance ([[gastrointestinal/bowel-disease]]). The differential includes small-bowel Crohn's disease, bacterial overgrowth and giardiasis.
:::
:::

::: exercise Design a diarrhoea clinic question set {level=3}
You are writing a structured history for a new chronic diarrhoea clinic. Using the four mechanisms in [[#def-diarrhoea]], write one or two questions that point towards each mechanism, and list three "red flag" features that would require urgent investigation for cancer or inflammatory bowel disease.
::: hint
Think of the clinical feature each mechanism produces: response to fasting, volume, blood, transit.
:::
::: solution
**Osmotic:** Does the diarrhoea stop when you do not eat? Do you take milk, sugar-free products, magnesium or laxatives?

**Secretory:** Does it continue at night or when fasting? Is the volume large, and do you wake to open your bowels? Have you had gallbladder or ileal surgery, or do you take new medicines?

**Inflammatory:** Is there blood or mucus? Fever, mouth ulcers, joint pains, skin rashes or eye inflammation? Any family history of inflammatory bowel disease?

**Motility:** Is pain relieved by defecation, with alternating constipation? Any symptoms of an overactive thyroid, or long-standing diabetes?

**Red flags:** rectal bleeding, unintentional weight loss, iron deficiency anaemia, an abdominal or rectal mass, or a family history of colorectal cancer. Referral follows national guidance such as NICE NG12 ([[gastrointestinal/gi-bleeding-cancer]]).
:::
:::
