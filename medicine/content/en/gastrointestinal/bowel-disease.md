Disorders of the small and large bowel range from conditions that need an operation within hours to lifelong diseases managed over decades. Appendicitis and complicated diverticulitis are surgical emergencies. Coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease are chronic immune disorders that can be controlled but not yet cured. Irritable bowel syndrome is very common, causes real suffering and has no structural lesion at all.

These conditions overlap in their symptoms. Abdominal pain, diarrhoea, bloating and a change in bowel habit can occur in all of them. The clinician's task is to recognise which pattern fits, to use a few well-chosen tests to confirm it, and to avoid both missing serious disease and over-investigating functional symptoms.

This chapter covers coeliac disease, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, microscopic colitis, irritable bowel syndrome and constipation, diverticular disease and appendicitis. Colorectal cancer and polyposis syndromes are covered in [[gastrointestinal/gi-bleeding-cancer]].

## Coeliac disease

::: definition Coeliac disease {#def-coeliac}
**Coeliac disease** is a chronic immune-mediated disorder of the small intestine, triggered by dietary **gluten** (proteins in wheat, barley and rye) in genetically susceptible people. It causes **villous atrophy**, **crypt hyperplasia** and an increase in **intraepithelial lymphocytes**, mainly in the proximal small bowel, and it resolves on a strict gluten-free diet.
:::

Coeliac disease affects about 1 in 100 people in the UK, but most cases are undiagnosed. Almost everyone with it carries **HLA-DQ2** or **HLA-DQ8**. These HLA molecules present gluten peptides to T cells particularly well after the enzyme **tissue transglutaminase** (tTG) has modified the peptides, making them more negatively charged. Gluten-specific CD4 T cells then drive inflammation, and intraepithelial lymphocytes kill enterocytes. B cells make antibodies against tTG itself, which is the basis of the main blood test.

Because the HLA types are common, carrying DQ2 or DQ8 does not mean a person has the disease. Their absence, however, makes coeliac disease very unlikely, so HLA typing is useful mainly to rule it out.

### Presentation

The classic picture of diarrhoea, steatorrhoea and weight loss is now the minority. More often people present with:

- iron deficiency anaemia that does not respond to oral iron, or folate deficiency;
- fatigue, bloating, or symptoms resembling irritable bowel syndrome;
- osteoporosis or osteomalacia;
- **dermatitis herpetiformis**, an itchy blistering rash on the elbows, knees and buttocks;
- abnormal liver enzymes, infertility, or neurological symptoms such as ataxia or neuropathy;
- poor growth or delayed puberty in children.

Coeliac disease is more common in people with type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, Down syndrome and first-degree relatives of people with coeliac disease. NICE guideline NG20 lists groups who should be offered testing.

### Diagnosis

The first-line test is **IgA anti-tTG antibody**, measured together with **total IgA**. Total IgA is needed because about 2% of people with coeliac disease have IgA deficiency, which gives a false-negative tTG. In that case IgG-based tests are used.

Serology and biopsy are only reliable while the person is eating gluten. NICE advises gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing. Starting a gluten-free diet before testing is one of the commonest causes of diagnostic confusion.

In adults, the diagnosis has traditionally been confirmed by **duodenal biopsy** showing the typical histology. In children, European paediatric guidance (ESPGHAN 2020) allows diagnosis without biopsy when tTG-IgA is at least 10 times the upper limit of normal and endomysial antibodies are positive on a second sample. A 2025 British Society of Gastroenterology guideline extended a similar **no-biopsy option** to symptomatic adults assessed in secondary care with tTG-IgA at least 10 times the upper limit of normal, after shared decision-making, while endoscopy remains appropriate when there are red-flag features. Practice is changing, so local guidance should be checked.

### Management

Treatment is a lifelong, strict **gluten-free diet**, supported by a dietitian. Oats are tolerated by most people if uncontaminated. Other parts of care include checking and correcting iron, folate, B12, calcium and vitamin D; assessing bone density where indicated; and pneumococcal vaccination because of **hyposplenism**. Serology should fall over months on the diet and is used to monitor adherence.

Persistent symptoms despite treatment are usually due to continued gluten exposure, often unintended. A small number of people have **refractory coeliac disease**, which carries a risk of **enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma**. Long-standing untreated coeliac disease is also associated with small-bowel adenocarcinoma.

::: example Anaemia with a flat biopsy {#ex-coeliac}
A 29-year-old woman of Irish heritage has iron deficiency anaemia that has not improved after 3 months of oral iron. She has mild bloating and loose stools. Her sister has type 1 diabetes. tTG-IgA is 21 times the upper limit of normal and total IgA is normal. Explain the failure of oral iron, how the diagnosis is confirmed, and what follow-up she needs.
::: solution
**Step 1: why oral iron failed.** Iron is absorbed in the duodenum and upper jejunum ([[gastrointestinal/digestion-absorption]]). Coeliac disease damages exactly this region most severely, so even large doses of oral iron are poorly absorbed.

**Step 2: confirm the diagnosis.** A tTG-IgA above 10 times the upper limit of normal with normal total IgA makes coeliac disease very likely. Depending on local practice and her preferences, the diagnosis is confirmed either by duodenal biopsies while she is still eating gluten, or, under recent British adult guidance, without biopsy after specialist assessment and shared decision-making. She must not start a gluten-free diet until this is settled.

**Step 3: management and follow-up.** Lifelong gluten-free diet with dietitian support; iron replacement, intravenously if oral iron continues to fail; checks of folate, B12, calcium and vitamin D; a decision about bone density assessment; and pneumococcal vaccination for hyposplenism. Repeat serology over the following year should show a falling tTG. First-degree relatives, including her sister with type 1 diabetes, have a higher risk and can be offered testing.
:::
:::

## Inflammatory bowel disease

**Inflammatory bowel disease** (IBD) means two main chronic disorders: **Crohn's disease** and **ulcerative colitis** (UC). Both usually start in adolescence or young adulthood, both relapse and remit, and both arise from an abnormal immune response to gut bacteria in genetically susceptible people.

Over 200 genetic risk loci are known. In Crohn's disease, variants in *NOD2*, a sensor of bacterial cell wall products, and in autophagy genes point to defective handling of bacteria. Environmental factors matter too. Smoking increases the risk and severity of Crohn's disease but, for reasons that are not understood, is associated with a lower risk of UC. Incidence has risen in countries as they industrialise, which suggests changes in diet and the microbiota are involved ([[microbiology/microbiota]]).

::: definition Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis {#def-ibd}
| Feature | Crohn's disease | Ulcerative colitis |
|---|---|---|
| Site | anywhere from mouth to anus; terminal ileum and colon commonest | rectum, extending continuously up the colon |
| Distribution | patchy, with "skip lesions" | continuous from the rectum |
| Depth | transmural (full thickness) | mucosal |
| Histology | non-caseating granulomas in some cases | crypt abscesses, goblet cell depletion |
| Complications | strictures, fistulas, abscesses, perianal disease | toxic megacolon, severe bleeding |
| Typical symptoms | abdominal pain, diarrhoea, weight loss | bloody diarrhoea, urgency, tenesmus |
| Smoking | increases risk and severity | associated with lower risk |

About 10% of cases of colitis cannot be classified and are called **IBD unclassified**. Extent and behaviour are described with the **Montreal classification**.
:::

### Clinical features

Crohn's disease is defined by its depth. Because inflammation goes through the full thickness of the wall, it can cause **strictures** that obstruct, **fistulas** to other organs or the skin, and **abscesses**. Ileal disease causes right lower quadrant pain and may mimic appendicitis. Perianal disease with fissures, fistulas and abscesses is common. Ileal inflammation or resection leads to B12 deficiency and bile acid diarrhoea.

UC always involves the rectum. Proctitis causes rectal bleeding, urgency and **tenesmus**, the feeling of incomplete emptying. Extensive colitis causes frequent bloody diarrhoea. The most feared complication is **toxic megacolon**, a dilated colon with systemic toxicity that may perforate.

Both diseases have **extra-intestinal manifestations**: peripheral arthritis and spondyloarthritis, episcleritis and uveitis, erythema nodosum and pyoderma gangrenosum, and, particularly with UC, **primary sclerosing cholangitis** ([[gastrointestinal/liver-disease]]). Long-standing extensive colitis in either disease increases the risk of colorectal cancer, so colonoscopic surveillance is offered.

### Investigation

**Faecal calprotectin** is a protein released by neutrophils into the bowel lumen. A raised level indicates intestinal inflammation. NICE recommends it in primary care to help distinguish IBD from irritable bowel syndrome in adults with new lower gastrointestinal symptoms, when cancer is not suspected. A normal calprotectin makes IBD unlikely and spares many people a colonoscopy.

The diagnosis is made by **ileocolonoscopy** with biopsies. Small-bowel Crohn's disease is assessed with MR enterography or capsule endoscopy, and perianal disease with pelvic MRI. Stool tests for infection, including *Clostridioides difficile*, must be done in every flare, because infection can mimic or trigger relapse.

### Treatment

Treatment has two aims: to induce remission in a flare and to maintain it. Increasingly, the target is not only symptom control but **mucosal healing**, because it predicts fewer complications.

- **Aminosalicylates** (5-ASA, such as mesalazine) are first-line for mild to moderate UC, orally and rectally. They have little role in Crohn's disease.
- **Corticosteroids** induce remission in both diseases but are not used for maintenance, because of their adverse effects and because they do not maintain remission.
- **Thiopurines** (azathioprine, mercaptopurine) maintain remission. Thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) activity is checked before starting, because low activity causes severe myelosuppression.
- **Biologic drugs**: anti-TNF antibodies (infliximab, adalimumab), vedolizumab (blocks gut-specific lymphocyte homing through α4β7 integrin) and ustekinumab and newer anti-IL-23 agents. Screening for tuberculosis and hepatitis B is required before starting, because these can reactivate.
- **Small molecules**: Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors such as tofacitinib and upadacitinib, and S1P receptor modulators.
- **Surgery**: in UC, colectomy cures the colonic disease, and many people choose an ileal pouch–anal anastomosis. In Crohn's disease, surgery treats complications such as strictures, but disease tends to recur at the join.

Choice of drug follows national guidance (NICE technology appraisals and the British Society of Gastroenterology IBD guideline) and individual factors.

### Acute severe ulcerative colitis

Acute severe UC is a medical emergency. It is defined by the **Truelove and Witts criteria**: six or more bloody stools a day plus at least one sign of systemic toxicity, namely a temperature above 37.8 °C, a pulse above 90 per minute, haemoglobin below 105 g/L, or an ESR above 30 mm/h.

Standard management is admission, intravenous corticosteroids, venous thromboembolism prophylaxis (IBD flares are strongly prothrombotic), stool tests to exclude infection, a plain abdominal radiograph to look for colonic dilatation, and early joint care by gastroenterologists and colorectal surgeons. Response is assessed on **day 3**. In the original 1996 Oxford study, people with more than 8 stools a day, or 3 to 8 stools with a CRP above 45 mg/L, on day 3 mostly went on to need colectomy. These **Oxford criteria** are now used to trigger **rescue therapy** with infliximab or ciclosporin, or colectomy.

::: example The day-3 decision {#ex-asuc}
A 26-year-old man of Pakistani heritage with extensive UC is admitted with 9 bloody stools a day, pulse 104 per minute, temperature 38.1 °C, haemoglobin 96 g/L and CRP 98 mg/L. Stool tests for infection are negative. He receives intravenous hydrocortisone and enoxaparin. On day 3 he still has 7 bloody stools a day and his CRP is 62 mg/L. His abdominal radiograph shows no colonic dilatation. What does his day-3 status mean, and what are the options?
::: solution
**Step 1: confirm severity.** On admission he had more than 6 bloody stools a day with a fever, tachycardia and anaemia: **acute severe UC** by Truelove and Witts.

**Step 2: apply the day-3 assessment.** He has 7 stools a day (between 3 and 8) with a CRP above 45 mg/L. He meets the **Oxford criteria** for steroid failure, which predicts a high chance of colectomy without a change of treatment.

**Step 3: options.** Waiting longer on steroids alone increases the risk of complications. The options are **rescue medical therapy**, with infliximab or ciclosporin, which have similar effectiveness in trials, or **colectomy**. Before infliximab he needs screening for tuberculosis and hepatitis B. The decision is made with him, jointly with the colorectal surgeons, so that surgery is not delayed if rescue therapy fails. Daily review continues: worsening pain, colonic dilatation or signs of perforation would mean urgent colectomy.

**Step 4: explain the reasoning.** Colectomy is not a failure of care in this setting. It removes the diseased organ and is life-saving when medical therapy has not worked.
:::
:::

### Microscopic colitis

**Microscopic colitis** causes chronic watery, non-bloody diarrhoea with a normal-looking colon at colonoscopy; the diagnosis depends on biopsies showing either a thickened subepithelial collagen band (**collagenous colitis**) or increased intraepithelial lymphocytes (**lymphocytic colitis**). It is commonest in older women and is associated with drugs, notably NSAIDs, PPIs and SSRIs, and with smoking and coeliac disease. Oral budesonide is effective. Biopsies must be taken even when the colon looks normal in an older person with watery diarrhoea.

## Functional bowel disorders and constipation

::: definition Irritable bowel syndrome and constipation {#def-functional}
**Irritable bowel syndrome** (IBS) is a disorder of gut–brain interaction defined by the **Rome IV criteria**: recurrent abdominal pain on average at least one day a week in the last 3 months, associated with two or more of: pain related to defecation; a change in stool frequency; a change in stool form. Symptoms must have started at least 6 months before diagnosis. IBS is subtyped by the predominant stool pattern: constipation, diarrhoea, mixed or unclassified.

**Chronic constipation** is persistent difficult, infrequent or incomplete defecation. **Faecal impaction** is a mass of hard stool in the rectum or colon that the person cannot pass.
:::

IBS affects roughly 1 in 10 people, more often women. Its mechanisms include **visceral hypersensitivity** (a lower threshold for pain from gut distension), altered motility, changes in the gut microbiota, low-grade immune activation, and altered processing of gut signals in the brain. It sometimes begins after an episode of gastroenteritis.

IBS is diagnosed positively from the symptom pattern, not just by excluding everything else. In the UK, NICE guideline CG61 recommends a small set of tests in people who meet the criteria: full blood count, ESR or CRP, and coeliac serology. Faecal calprotectin helps exclude IBD, and CA125 is checked in women with symptoms that could suggest ovarian cancer. Further tests are needed only if there are **red flags**: unintentional weight loss, rectal bleeding, a family history of bowel or ovarian cancer, anaemia, an abdominal or rectal mass, or a change in bowel habit after age 60.

Management starts with a clear explanation and a confident diagnosis. Dietary advice includes regular meals, adjusting fibre and limiting caffeine, alcohol and fizzy drinks. A **low-FODMAP diet**, which restricts fermentable short-chain carbohydrates, can help but should be supervised by a dietitian. Drug treatment is targeted at the main symptom: antispasmodics for pain, laxatives such as ispaghula or polyethylene glycol for constipation (lactulose increases bloating), loperamide for diarrhoea, and low-dose tricyclic antidepressants as a second-line treatment for pain. Psychological therapies, including gut-directed hypnotherapy and cognitive behavioural therapy, help people with persistent symptoms.

Constipation in adults is usually functional, but medication is a common cause: opioids, anticholinergic drugs, iron, calcium channel blockers and aluminium antacids. Metabolic causes include hypothyroidism and hypercalcaemia. **Faecal impaction** is especially common in frail older people, and can present paradoxically as diarrhoea.

::: example Diarrhoea that is really constipation {#ex-impaction}
An 86-year-old woman in a care home has had liquid stool leaking several times a day for a week. Staff have started loperamide. She takes co-codamol for knee pain and has become more confused. Her abdomen is distended, and rectal examination finds a rectum packed with hard stool. Explain what is happening and outline management.
::: solution
**Step 1: recognise overflow.** Opioids slow colonic transit, and reduced mobility and fluid intake add to this. Hard stool accumulates in the rectum. Bacteria liquefy stool above the impaction, and this liquid leaks around it: **overflow diarrhoea**. The rectal examination is the key finding.

**Step 2: recognise the harm of the current treatment.** Loperamide slows transit further and worsens the impaction. It should be stopped.

**Step 3: management principles.** Clear the rectum with suppositories or enemas, then use an oral osmotic laxative such as polyethylene glycol until bowels are regular. Review the opioid, and use an alternative analgesic or add a regular laxative if it is still needed. Encourage fluids and mobility. Check for urinary retention, which often accompanies impaction.

**Step 4: the confusion.** Constipation is a common precipitant of **delirium** in older people. Her confusion should improve with treatment, but other causes, such as infection or drug effects, also need checking ([[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]]).
:::
:::

## Diverticular disease

::: definition Diverticular disease {#def-diverticular}
**Diverticula** are small pouches of mucosa that herniate through weak points in the colonic wall where blood vessels penetrate the muscle. The presence of diverticula is **diverticulosis**. **Diverticular disease** means diverticula causing symptoms. **Diverticulitis** is inflammation or infection of a diverticulum, and it is **complicated** when there is an abscess, perforation, fistula, obstruction or bleeding.
:::

In Western populations, diverticula occur mainly in the sigmoid colon and become very common with age: well over half of people over 80 have them. Low-fibre diets, obesity, smoking and NSAIDs increase the risk. In parts of Asia, diverticula are more often right-sided.

Acute diverticulitis typically causes left lower quadrant pain, fever and raised inflammatory markers. CT of the abdomen confirms the diagnosis and shows complications. Perforation is graded with the **Hinchey classification**, from a localised pericolic abscess (stage I) to faecal peritonitis (stage IV).

As of 2026, NICE guideline NG147 recommends considering a **no-antibiotic strategy** for people with acute diverticulitis who are systemically well, with simple analgesia such as paracetamol and advice to return if symptoms worsen. NSAIDs and opioids are best avoided because of the possible risk of perforation. Antibiotics are offered to people who are systemically unwell, immunosuppressed or have significant comorbidity. Abscesses may be drained under radiological guidance, and generalised peritonitis needs emergency surgery. Diverticular bleeding is discussed in [[gastrointestinal/gi-bleeding-cancer]].

::: example Left iliac fossa pain {#ex-diverticulitis}
A 64-year-old man of Black Caribbean heritage has 2 days of left lower abdominal pain. Temperature 37.6 °C, pulse 88 per minute, blood pressure normal. He has tenderness in the left iliac fossa without guarding. White cell count 13 × 10⁹/L, CRP 70 mg/L. He is otherwise well and has no other illnesses. What is the likely diagnosis, and how should it be managed according to NICE NG147?
::: solution
**Step 1: likely diagnosis.** Left iliac fossa pain with mild fever and raised inflammatory markers in a man in his sixties is typical of **acute diverticulitis**. The differential includes colorectal cancer with local perforation, colitis and, in older people, ischaemic colitis.

**Step 2: severity.** He is systemically well: no tachycardia, hypotension or peritonism, and no comorbidity. He can be managed in the community if he can take fluids and his pain is controlled.

**Step 3: management.** NICE suggests considering **no antibiotics**, with paracetamol for pain and clear advice to come back if the pain worsens, he develops a high fever, or he cannot keep fluids down. If he becomes unwell, a CT scan and antibiotics would be needed.

**Step 4: follow-up.** After recovery, the colon should be assessed according to local pathways if it has not been examined recently, because a colorectal cancer can present like diverticulitis.
:::
:::

## Appendicitis

::: definition Appendicitis {#def-appendix}
**Acute appendicitis** is inflammation of the appendix, usually caused by obstruction of its lumen by a faecolith or lymphoid hyperplasia, followed by bacterial invasion of the wall. Untreated, it can progress to gangrene and **perforation**, with abscess or generalised peritonitis.
:::

Appendicitis is the commonest surgical emergency in young people. The classic history is pain starting around the umbilicus and moving to the **right iliac fossa** over a day, with anorexia, nausea and mild fever. The early pain is poorly localised because it comes from visceral afferents of the midgut, which refer to the umbilical region. When inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum, which has somatic innervation, the pain localises.

Presentation is often atypical. A retrocaecal appendix may cause flank pain, and a pelvic appendix may cause diarrhoea or urinary symptoms. Young children, older adults and pregnant women often present late, with a higher perforation rate. Scores such as the Alvarado or Appendicitis Inflammatory Response score combine symptoms, signs and blood tests to estimate risk. Imaging choice depends on the person: ultrasound first in children and in pregnancy, and CT in most adults. In women of reproductive age, a pregnancy test is mandatory to exclude ectopic pregnancy.

Laparoscopic appendicectomy remains the standard treatment. Trials, including the CODA trial in 2020, have shown that **antibiotics alone** can treat uncomplicated appendicitis confirmed on CT in many people, but a proportion need appendicectomy later, and failure is more likely when an appendicolith is present. This is now an option discussed with suitable patients. Surgical aspects are covered in [[surgery/acute-abdomen]].

::: warning A normal white cell count does not exclude appendicitis
Inflammatory markers support the diagnosis but can be normal early on. Diagnosis rests on the history and repeated examination. Serial review over a few hours, with imaging where needed, is safer than relying on a single blood test.
:::

::: history
In 1888 Samuel Gee in London described "the coeliac affection" in children and suggested that diet was the key to treatment. The cause was found during the Second World War, when the Dutch paediatrician Willem-Karel Dicke noticed that children with coeliac disease improved when bread was scarce during the famine of 1944–45 and relapsed when it returned. With colleagues he showed in the early 1950s that wheat gluten was responsible. Inflammatory bowel disease was separated into its two forms over the same period: Samuel Wilks used the term "ulcerative colitis" in 1859, and Burrill Crohn, Leon Ginzburg and Gordon Oppenheimer described regional ileitis in New York in 1932.
:::

::: summary
- Coeliac disease is a gluten-driven, HLA-DQ2/DQ8-restricted enteropathy. Test with tTG-IgA and total IgA while the person eats gluten; treat with a lifelong gluten-free diet.
- Crohn's disease is patchy and transmural anywhere in the gut; ulcerative colitis is continuous and mucosal from the rectum.
- Faecal calprotectin separates inflammatory from functional disease; diagnosis of IBD needs ileocolonoscopy and biopsies.
- Acute severe UC is defined by Truelove and Witts; failure to respond by day 3 (Oxford criteria) triggers rescue therapy or colectomy.
- IBS is diagnosed positively with the Rome IV criteria and a few tests; red flags need investigation.
- NICE NG147 suggests no antibiotics for systemically well people with acute diverticulitis.
- Appendicitis is a clinical diagnosis supported by scores and imaging; laparoscopic appendicectomy is standard, with antibiotics alone an option for selected uncomplicated cases.
:::

::: exercise Long-run flares {level=2 check="20"}
In the illustrative model in the figure, a person in remission has a 0.1 chance of flaring each month and a person in a flare has a 0.4 chance of entering remission each month. In the long run, what percentage of months are spent in a flare?
::: hint
At equilibrium the flow from remission to flare equals the flow back: $0.1\,\pi_R = 0.4\,\pi_F$, with $\pi_R + \pi_F = 1$.
:::
::: solution
From $0.1\,\pi_R = 0.4\,\pi_F$ we get $\pi_R = 4\pi_F$. With $\pi_R + \pi_F = 1$, $5\pi_F = 1$, so $\pi_F = 0.2$. In the long run **20%** of months are spent in a flare. A maintenance drug that halved the monthly flare probability to 0.05 would give $\pi_F = 0.05/(0.05 + 0.4) = 0.111$, about 11%.
:::

::: exercise A right iliac fossa case {level=3}
A 24-year-old woman has 18 hours of lower abdominal pain, now worst in the right iliac fossa, with nausea and a temperature of 37.9 °C. Her last menstrual period was 6 weeks ago. Give a differential diagnosis, the essential first test, and explain how imaging would be chosen.
::: hint
Think about gynaecological as well as gastrointestinal causes.
:::
::: solution
**Differential:** acute appendicitis; **ectopic pregnancy** (her period is late); ovarian cyst rupture or torsion; pelvic inflammatory disease; mesenteric adenitis; terminal ileal Crohn's disease; and urinary tract infection or ureteric stone.

**Essential first test:** a **pregnancy test**. A positive test changes the diagnosis and management fundamentally, because ruptured ectopic pregnancy is life-threatening ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/early-pregnancy]]).

**Imaging:** if she is pregnant, pelvic ultrasound is first-line. If she is not pregnant, ultrasound can assess the ovaries and sometimes the appendix, and CT is often used in adults when the diagnosis remains uncertain. Repeated examination is important. If appendicitis is confirmed, laparoscopy allows both diagnosis and treatment, with antibiotics alone as an option for selected uncomplicated cases.
