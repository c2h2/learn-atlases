The gallbladder, the bile ducts and the pancreas share a single exit into the duodenum. The common bile duct and the main pancreatic duct usually join just before they open at the **ampulla of Vater**, guarded by the sphincter of Oddi. This shared anatomy explains why a small gallstone can cause pancreatitis, why a cancer in the head of the pancreas causes jaundice, and why one endoscopic procedure, ERCP, can treat disease of both organs.

The conditions in this chapter range from very common and usually benign, such as gallstones, to some of the most lethal cancers in medicine. Acute pancreatitis sits between the two: most attacks are mild and settle in days, but about one in five becomes severe, with organ failure and a substantial risk of death.

This chapter covers gallstones and their complications, acute and chronic pancreatitis, autoimmune pancreatitis, and cancers and cysts of the pancreas and bile ducts. Bile physiology is in [[gastrointestinal/digestion-absorption]] and the liver tests used throughout are in [[gastrointestinal/liver-function]].

## Gallstones

::: definition Gallstones and their complications {#def-stones}
**Gallstones** (cholelithiasis) are solid concretions in the gallbladder. Two main types occur:

- **Cholesterol stones**, the commonest type in Western countries, form when bile contains more cholesterol than bile salts and phospholipid can keep in solution.
- **Pigment stones** are made mainly of calcium bilirubinate. **Black** pigment stones form in the gallbladder in chronic haemolysis and cirrhosis. **Brown** pigment stones form in the bile ducts in association with infection and biliary stasis.

Most people with gallstones never have symptoms. Complications include **biliary colic**, **acute cholecystitis**, **choledocholithiasis** (stones in the common bile duct) with obstructive jaundice, **ascending cholangitis**, and **gallstone pancreatitis**.
:::

Gallstones are common: they are present in about one in ten adults in Western countries, with higher rates in women and with age. Cholesterol stones form when three conditions coincide: bile that is **supersaturated** with cholesterol, **nucleation** of cholesterol crystals, and **gallbladder hypomotility** that allows crystals to grow. Risk factors include female sex, pregnancy and oestrogens, obesity, rapid weight loss (including after bariatric surgery), diabetes, ileal disease or resection, and some ethnic groups, notably several Indigenous American populations.

Asymptomatic gallstones found incidentally are not usually removed. Each year only a small proportion of people with silent stones develop symptoms, and the risk of an operation outweighs the benefit for most.

### Biliary colic and cholecystitis

**Biliary colic** is caused by a stone temporarily blocking the gallbladder outlet (the cystic duct) while the gallbladder contracts. The pain is in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium, is steady rather than colicky despite the name, often follows a fatty meal, and lasts from about 30 minutes to several hours. There is no fever and blood tests are normal.

**Acute cholecystitis** develops when the obstruction persists, the gallbladder wall becomes inflamed and secondary infection may follow. The pain lasts longer, there is fever, and the **Murphy sign** is positive: the person stops breathing in as the examiner presses under the right costal margin and the inflamed gallbladder descends onto the hand. Ultrasound shows stones, a thickened wall and fluid around the gallbladder. The **Tokyo Guidelines** (2018) define the diagnosis by combining local signs, systemic inflammation and imaging, and grade severity from mild to severe according to organ dysfunction.

NICE guideline CG188 recommends laparoscopic cholecystectomy for people with symptomatic gallbladder stones, and for acute cholecystitis an operation **within a week of diagnosis**. Early surgery shortens total time in hospital and avoids recurrent attacks while waiting. People too unwell for surgery can have the gallbladder drained through the skin (percutaneous cholecystostomy).

### Stones in the common bile duct and cholangitis

A stone that passes into the common bile duct can cause **obstructive jaundice**, with a cholestatic pattern of liver tests and dilated ducts on ultrasound. It can also cause pancreatitis by obstructing the ampulla. Duct stones are confirmed with **MRCP** or **endoscopic ultrasound** and removed at **ERCP**, in which a side-viewing endoscope is passed into the duodenum, the duct is cannulated and the sphincter cut (sphincterotomy).

::: definition Acute cholangitis {#def-cholangitis}
**Acute cholangitis** is bacterial infection of an obstructed biliary tree, most often caused by a common bile duct stone, a stricture or a blocked stent. It classically causes **Charcot's triad**: fever with rigors, jaundice and right upper quadrant pain. Confusion and hypotension added to the triad make up **Reynolds' pentad**, which indicates severe disease with septic shock.
:::

Cholangitis is a surgical and endoscopic emergency because infected bile under pressure refluxes into the bloodstream. Treatment combines resuscitation and intravenous antibiotics with **biliary drainage**, usually by ERCP. Drainage is urgent in severe cases. If ERCP is not possible, the ducts can be drained through the liver by interventional radiology.

::: example Shaking chills {#ex-cholangitis}
An 81-year-old woman of Chinese heritage is brought in with rigors, confusion and yellow eyes. Temperature 39.2 °C, pulse 118 per minute, blood pressure 84/50 mmHg. She has right upper quadrant tenderness. Bilirubin 96 µmol/L, ALP 420 U/L, ALT 260 U/L, white cell count 19 × 10⁹/L, lactate 4.2 mmol/L. Ultrasound shows gallstones and a common bile duct of 12 mm. What is the diagnosis, and what is the priority?
::: solution
**Step 1: diagnosis.** Fever, jaundice and right upper quadrant pain are **Charcot's triad**. With confusion and hypotension she has **Reynolds' pentad**: severe **acute cholangitis** with septic shock. The dilated duct and gallstones point to an obstructing common bile duct stone. In people from East and South-East Asia, brown pigment stones forming in the ducts, sometimes linked to parasitic infection, are also possible.

**Step 2: resuscitation and antibiotics.** Follow the sepsis pathway: intravenous fluids, blood cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics chosen according to local guidance, and assessment for vasopressors in a critical care setting ([[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]]).

**Step 3: drainage is the definitive treatment.** Antibiotics cannot sterilise infected bile under pressure. She needs urgent **ERCP** to drain the duct, usually with stone removal or placement of a stent. If ERCP fails, percutaneous transhepatic drainage is the alternative.

**Step 4: later.** Once she recovers, cholecystectomy is considered to prevent recurrence, weighed against her age and fitness.
:::
:::

## Acute pancreatitis

::: definition Acute pancreatitis {#def-pancreatitis}
**Acute pancreatitis** is acute inflammation of the pancreas. The diagnosis requires **two of three** features:

1. typical abdominal pain: acute, severe, epigastric, often radiating to the back;
2. serum **lipase or amylase at least three times** the upper limit of normal;
3. characteristic findings on imaging (CT, MRI or ultrasound).

Under the **revised Atlanta classification** (2012), severity is:

- **mild**: no organ failure and no local or systemic complications;
- **moderately severe**: transient organ failure resolving within 48 hours, or local complications, or worsening of a comorbid disease;
- **severe**: **persistent organ failure** for more than 48 hours.
:::

The two commonest causes are **gallstones** and **alcohol**, together responsible for most cases. Others include hypertriglyceridaemia (usually above about 11 mmol/L), hypercalcaemia, ERCP, drugs (for example azathioprine, valproate and some diuretics), trauma, autoimmune pancreatitis, genetic variants (*PRSS1*, *SPINK1*, *CFTR*), and anatomical variants. A useful mnemonic exists, but the history (alcohol, drugs, family), ultrasound for gallstones, calcium and triglycerides find the cause in most people.

::: theorem Pancreatitis is premature activation of trypsin {#thm-trypsin}
Acute pancreatitis begins when trypsinogen is activated to trypsin **inside acinar cells** instead of in the duodenum. Trypsin then activates other digestive enzymes, and the gland begins to digest itself. The local injury triggers an inflammatory response that, if large enough, becomes systemic.
:::

::: proof
Normally several safeguards prevent activation inside the pancreas. Enzymes are made as inactive precursors and stored in granules separate from lysosomes; trypsin inhibitor (SPINK1) blocks small amounts of active trypsin; and trypsin can destroy itself by autolysis. Gallstone obstruction of the ampulla, alcohol and other insults disturb calcium signalling in acinar cells, cause fusion of zymogen granules with lysosomes, and allow cathepsin B to activate trypsinogen. Gain-of-function variants in *PRSS1* (cationic trypsinogen) make trypsin resistant to inactivation and cause hereditary pancreatitis, which strongly supports this model.

Injured acinar cells release cytokines and recruit neutrophils and macrophages. In most people inflammation stays local. In some, the **systemic inflammatory response** causes capillary leak, hypovolaemia, acute respiratory distress syndrome and kidney failure, which is why severe pancreatitis is defined by organ failure rather than by the appearance of the pancreas.
:::

### Assessing severity

Amylase and lipase confirm the diagnosis but **do not measure severity**. Amylase returns to normal within a few days, so lipase, which stays raised longer, is more useful in late presentations. Severity depends on organ failure, which evolves over the first days. Several tools help predict it:

- the **modified Glasgow (Imrie) score**, which gives one point for each of eight criteria within 48 hours: PaO₂ below 8 kPa, age over 55, white cell count above 15 × 10⁹/L, calcium below 2 mmol/L, urea above 16 mmol/L, LDH above 600 U/L (or AST above 200 U/L), albumin below 32 g/L and glucose above 10 mmol/L. A score of 3 or more predicts severe disease;
- **CRP above 150 mg/L** at 48 hours;
- signs of systemic inflammatory response syndrome that persist beyond 48 hours.

Contrast CT is not needed to diagnose typical pancreatitis. It is useful after about 72 hours if the person is not improving, to look for **necrosis** and collections.

### Management

Management is supportive, and in the early days most deaths come from organ failure.

- **Fluid resuscitation.** Intravenous crystalloid, usually a balanced solution such as Hartmann's or Ringer's lactate, guided by clinical response. The WATERFALL trial (2022) compared aggressive and moderate fluid resuscitation and stopped early because aggressive fluids caused more fluid overload without better outcomes. Moderate, goal-directed fluid therapy is now preferred.
- **Analgesia**, often with opioids.
- **Nutrition.** Early oral feeding is encouraged in mild disease when the person can tolerate it. In moderately severe or severe pancreatitis, NICE (NG104) recommends **enteral nutrition**, by nasogastric or nasojejunal tube if needed, started within 72 hours. Parenteral nutrition is used only if enteral feeding fails ([[gastrointestinal/clinical-nutrition]]).
- **Antibiotics** are not given prophylactically. They are used for proven or strongly suspected infection, such as cholangitis or infected necrosis.
- **ERCP** is needed early for gallstone pancreatitis with cholangitis, but not routinely for pancreatitis without it.
- **Cholecystectomy** for gallstone pancreatitis is recommended **during the same admission** in mild disease, because waiting risks recurrence. In severe disease it is delayed until inflammation settles.

::: example Severe on arrival {#ex-severe-panc}
A 47-year-old man who drinks heavily has 24 hours of severe epigastric pain radiating to the back and vomiting. Pulse 120 per minute, blood pressure 98/60 mmHg, respiratory rate 28 per minute, SpO₂ 91% on air. Lipase 9 times the upper limit of normal. Within 48 hours: PaO₂ 7.6 kPa, white cell count 17 × 10⁹/L, calcium 1.9 mmol/L, urea 18 mmol/L, LDH 520 U/L, albumin 30 g/L, glucose 8 mmol/L. Calculate his modified Glasgow score and outline his management.
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Typical pain plus lipase more than 3 times normal: **acute pancreatitis**, likely alcohol-related. Gallstones must still be excluded by ultrasound.

**Modified Glasgow score.** PaO₂ below 8 kPa: 1. Age over 55: 0. White cells above 15: 1. Calcium below 2: 1. Urea above 16: 1. LDH above 600: 0. Albumin below 32: 1. Glucose above 10: 0. Total $= 5$, which predicts **severe** disease.

**Severity by Atlanta.** He already has respiratory compromise and borderline circulation. If organ failure persists beyond 48 hours, the attack is classified as **severe**.

**Management.** Admit to a high-dependency or intensive care setting; oxygen and assessment for respiratory support; moderate, goal-directed intravenous fluids with close monitoring to avoid overload; analgesia; enteral nutrition within 72 hours; thiamine and monitoring for alcohol withdrawal; no prophylactic antibiotics. CT at 72 hours or later if he does not improve, to look for necrosis. Before discharge, offer support for alcohol use, because continued drinking causes recurrence and chronic pancreatitis.
:::
:::

### Local complications

Collections around the pancreas are classified by **time** and by the presence of **necrosis**:

| | Within 4 weeks | After 4 weeks (walled off) |
|---|---|---|
| No necrosis (interstitial oedematous pancreatitis) | acute peripancreatic fluid collection | **pseudocyst** |
| Necrosis present (necrotising pancreatitis) | acute necrotic collection | **walled-off necrosis** |

Most early collections resolve on their own. Intervention is reserved for collections that cause symptoms, obstruct the stomach or bile duct, or become **infected**. Infected necrosis is suspected when a person deteriorates, often in the second to fourth week, with fever and rising inflammatory markers, and sometimes gas within the collection on CT. The modern approach is a **step-up strategy**: antibiotics, then drainage (often endoscopic, through the stomach wall), then endoscopic or minimally invasive removal of necrotic tissue (necrosectomy) only if needed. The PANTER trial (2010) showed that this approach reduced major complications and death compared with open necrosectomy.

::: example A collection in week five {#ex-won}
A 39-year-old woman is recovering from necrotising gallstone pancreatitis. Five weeks after admission she has early satiety and vomiting. She has no fever. CT shows a 14 cm encapsulated collection containing fluid and solid debris, compressing the stomach. What is this collection, and how should it be managed?
::: solution
**Classification.** A collection more than 4 weeks after necrotising pancreatitis, with a defined wall and solid debris, is **walled-off necrosis**.

**Indication for treatment.** It is sterile (no fever, no gas), but it is large and causing **gastric outlet obstruction**, so intervention is indicated.

**Management.** Endoscopic ultrasound-guided drainage through the stomach wall, typically with a lumen-apposing metal stent, with endoscopic necrosectomy if the solid debris does not clear. This is part of the step-up approach and avoids open surgery. She also needs nutritional support and, once recovered, cholecystectomy to prevent further gallstone pancreatitis.
:::
:::

## Chronic pancreatitis

::: definition Chronic pancreatitis and autoimmune pancreatitis {#def-other-pancreas}
**Chronic pancreatitis** is progressive inflammation and fibrosis of the pancreas leading to permanent loss of exocrine and endocrine tissue. Its main features are **pain**, **exocrine insufficiency** with steatorrhoea, and **diabetes** (type 3c, pancreatogenic). Alcohol and smoking are the main risk factors; genetic variants, recurrent acute pancreatitis and duct obstruction also contribute.

**Autoimmune pancreatitis** type 1 is the pancreatic manifestation of **IgG4-related disease**, a fibro-inflammatory condition that can also affect the bile ducts, salivary glands, kidneys and retroperitoneum. It often causes painless jaundice and a diffusely swollen pancreas, can closely mimic cancer, and responds to corticosteroids.
:::

Chronic pancreatitis is diagnosed mainly by imaging: CT shows calcification, duct dilatation and atrophy, and MRI and endoscopic ultrasound detect earlier changes. **Faecal elastase** is a simple test of exocrine function. Management includes stopping alcohol and smoking; **pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy** (PERT), taken with every meal and snack and adjusted to the fat content of the food and to symptoms; replacement of fat-soluble vitamins and assessment of bone health; treatment of diabetes, which carries a high risk of hypoglycaemia because glucagon secretion is also lost; and pain management. Opioid dependence is a real risk in long-term pain, so a multidisciplinary pain approach is used, with endoscopic or surgical drainage for obstructed ducts in selected people. Chronic pancreatitis also increases the risk of pancreatic cancer.

::: example A swollen pancreas {#ex-ig4}
A 66-year-old man has painless jaundice and weight loss. CT shows a diffusely enlarged, "sausage-shaped" pancreas with a thin rim of low density around it and a narrowed distal common bile duct, but no discrete mass. Serum IgG4 is raised. He also has swollen submandibular glands. What is the likely diagnosis, and why must cancer still be considered carefully?
::: solution
**Likely diagnosis.** Diffuse pancreatic enlargement with a peripheral rim, a biliary stricture, raised IgG4 and salivary gland involvement suggest **type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis**, part of **IgG4-related disease**.

**Why cancer must still be considered.** Painless jaundice and weight loss in a man of this age are also the classic features of **pancreatic cancer**. IgG4 can be modestly raised in some cancers, and the treatments are completely different. Diagnosis rests on combining imaging, serology, other organ involvement and histology, usually from endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy. Starting corticosteroids for a missed cancer would be a serious error.

**Treatment once confirmed.** Corticosteroids usually produce a rapid response, which itself supports the diagnosis, with a biliary stent if needed. Relapse is common, and maintenance therapy or rituximab may be used under specialist care.
:::
:::

## Pancreatic and biliary cancer

::: definition Pancreatic and biliary tract cancers {#def-cancers}
- **Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma** makes up about 90% of pancreatic cancers. Most arise in the **head** of the pancreas, where they obstruct the bile duct and cause painless obstructive jaundice; tumours of the body and tail present later, with pain and weight loss.
- **Cholangiocarcinoma** arises from bile duct epithelium inside or outside the liver. Risk factors include primary sclerosing cholangitis and, in South-East Asia, chronic infection with liver flukes such as *Opisthorchis viverrini* and *Clonorchis sinensis*.
- **Gallbladder cancer** is associated with long-standing gallstones and is common in parts of South America and South Asia.
- **Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours** and **cystic neoplasms**, such as intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMN), are less common and generally have a better outlook.
:::

Pancreatic cancer has one of the worst survival rates of any cancer: fewer than one in ten people diagnosed in the UK survive 5 years, mainly because most present when the tumour is already unresectable. Risk factors include smoking, obesity, long-standing diabetes, chronic pancreatitis and a family history. **New-onset diabetes** in an older adult, especially with weight loss, can be an early sign.

Presentation includes painless jaundice, weight loss, epigastric or back pain, steatorrhoea and new diabetes. A palpable, non-tender gallbladder in a jaundiced person suggests malignant obstruction (**Courvoisier's law**). NICE NG12 recommends a suspected cancer pathway referral for people aged 40 and over with jaundice, and urgent CT for people aged 60 and over with weight loss and certain other symptoms, such as back pain, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, nausea or new-onset diabetes.

The key investigation is a **pancreas-protocol CT**, which shows the tumour and its relationship to the major vessels, and so determines resectability. The tumour marker **CA19-9** supports diagnosis and monitoring but is not a screening test: it is raised in benign obstruction and is not produced by people who lack the Lewis antigen. Only a minority have resectable disease. Surgery for a tumour in the head is a **pancreaticoduodenectomy** (Whipple procedure), followed by adjuvant chemotherapy. For people with jaundice who are not having immediate surgery, an endoscopic biliary stent relieves the obstruction and itching. Palliative care, enzyme replacement and nutritional support are central for most patients.

::: warning Do not stent before the surgeon has seen the scan
In a person with suspected resectable pancreatic cancer, routine pre-operative biliary drainage can increase complications. Decisions about stenting, biopsy and surgery should be made by the specialist multidisciplinary team after staging CT, unless cholangitis or other urgent indications force earlier drainage.
:::

::: quiz
A 34-year-old woman has mild gallstone pancreatitis. Her pain settles within 3 days and she is eating normally. What is the recommended plan for her gallbladder?
- [ ] Discharge with an outpatient surgical appointment in 3 months
- [x] Cholecystectomy during the same admission
- [ ] No surgery, because pancreatitis has resolved
- [ ] ERCP with sphincterotomy for every patient before discharge
::: solution
After **mild gallstone pancreatitis**, cholecystectomy during the **same admission** (or within a short interval) is recommended, because the risk of recurrent pancreatitis or other gallstone complications while waiting is substantial. ERCP is not routinely needed unless there is cholangitis or persistent bile duct obstruction. In severe pancreatitis, surgery is delayed until inflammation has settled.

::: history
In 1882 the German surgeon Carl Langenbuch performed the first cholecystectomy in Berlin. In 1889 the Harvard pathologist Reginald Fitz described acute pancreatitis as a distinct disease and argued for early recognition. Allen Whipple in New York reported pancreaticoduodenectomy for ampullary cancer in 1935, refining an operation first attempted by Walther Kausch. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy was introduced in the 1980s, with the first operations reported by Erich Mühe in Germany in 1985 and Philippe Mouret in France in 1987, and it rapidly became the standard operation.
:::

::: summary
- Gallstones are common and usually silent; biliary colic, cholecystitis, duct stones, cholangitis and pancreatitis are their complications.
- Acute cholecystitis is treated with laparoscopic cholecystectomy within a week of diagnosis (NICE CG188).
- Cholangitis presents with Charcot's triad; severe disease adds confusion and shock. Drainage, usually by ERCP, is the definitive treatment.
- Acute pancreatitis is diagnosed by two of: typical pain, lipase or amylase at least 3 times normal, and imaging. Severity is defined by organ failure (revised Atlanta).
- Management is supportive: moderate goal-directed fluids, analgesia, early enteral nutrition, no prophylactic antibiotics, and same-admission cholecystectomy after mild gallstone pancreatitis.
- Collections are classified by time and necrosis; infected necrosis is managed with a step-up approach.
- Chronic pancreatitis causes pain, exocrine insufficiency and diabetes; autoimmune pancreatitis mimics cancer.
- Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma usually presents late; pancreas-protocol CT determines resectability.
:::

Assume that a person with silent gallstones has a constant 2% chance each year of developing symptoms. What is the probability, to the nearest whole percent, of developing symptoms within 20 years?
::: hint
The probability of staying symptom-free for 20 years is $0.98^{20}$.

::: exercise Steatorrhoea after pancreatitis {level=3}
A 52-year-old man with alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis has lost 9 kg, has pale floating stools and has developed diabetes. Faecal elastase is very low. HbA1c is high and he has had two episodes of severe hypoglycaemia on insulin. Explain each problem and outline management principles.
::: hint
Think about both the exocrine and endocrine parts of the pancreas, including glucagon.
:::
::: solution
**Weight loss and steatorrhoea:** loss of acinar tissue means too little lipase reaches the duodenum. Fat is not digested, causing steatorrhoea, energy loss and deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins. The very low faecal elastase confirms **exocrine insufficiency**.

**Diabetes:** loss of islets causes **type 3c diabetes**. Both insulin-secreting β cells and glucagon-secreting α cells are lost. Without glucagon the counter-regulatory response to a low glucose is impaired, so hypoglycaemia on insulin is frequent and severe. Erratic absorption from untreated malabsorption adds to the instability.

**Management principles:** pancreatic enzyme replacement with every meal and snack, adjusted to symptoms and weight; dietitian support with normal fat intake rather than a low-fat diet; vitamins A, D, E and K; bone density assessment; insulin regimens designed to minimise hypoglycaemia, with education, continuous glucose monitoring where available and specialist diabetes input; support to stop alcohol and smoking; and awareness of the increased risk of pancreatic cancer.
:::
:::

::: exercise Painless jaundice: plan the pathway {level=3}
A 72-year-old woman has painless jaundice, 6 kg weight loss and new-onset diabetes. Bilirubin 180 µmol/L, ALP 640 U/L, CA19-9 raised. Ultrasound shows dilated intra- and extrahepatic ducts and a dilated pancreatic duct (the "double duct sign"). Give the differential diagnosis and the sequence of investigations and decisions, explaining the role of the multidisciplinary team.
::: hint
Dilatation of both ducts points to a lesion where they meet.
:::
::: solution
**Differential:** carcinoma of the head of the pancreas (most likely, given new diabetes and weight loss), ampullary carcinoma, distal cholangiocarcinoma, autoimmune pancreatitis, and less likely a common bile duct stone or chronic pancreatitis. The double duct sign points to a lesion at the ampulla or pancreatic head.

**Sequence:** urgent referral on the suspected cancer pathway; **pancreas-protocol CT** of chest, abdomen and pelvis for staging and assessment of vessel involvement; endoscopic ultrasound with biopsy if tissue is needed; IgG4 to help exclude autoimmune pancreatitis. CA19-9 supports the diagnosis but cannot confirm it, because biliary obstruction alone raises it.

**Decisions by the multidisciplinary team:** whether the tumour is resectable, borderline or unresectable; whether she is fit for a Whipple procedure; whether biliary drainage is needed before treatment (avoided if early surgery is planned, unless she has cholangitis); and chemotherapy before or after surgery. If the disease is unresectable, an endoscopic metal stent, palliative chemotherapy where appropriate, enzyme replacement, diabetes management and palliative care are planned with her.
:::
