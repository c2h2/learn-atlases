Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are the two common causes of airflow obstruction. Between them they affect several million people in the UK and hundreds of millions worldwide, and they account for a large share of emergency admissions, especially in winter. Both narrow the airways and both cause wheeze, cough and breathlessness, but they differ in cause, in mechanism, in how they behave over time and in how they are treated.

The short version is this. **Asthma** is a disease of **variable** airflow limitation driven by airway inflammation; it often starts in childhood, it can be well controlled, and between attacks lung function may be normal. **COPD** is a disease of **persistent**, usually progressive, airflow limitation caused mainly by long-term exposure to noxious particles, above all tobacco smoke; it starts in mid or later life, and the lung damage is largely irreversible. Many patients do not fit neatly into one box, and some have features of both.

This chapter covers each condition in turn: what goes wrong, how the diagnosis is made, how stable disease is managed and how exacerbations are treated. Management follows current UK guidance (the 2024 NICE/BTS/SIGN asthma guideline NG245 and NICE guideline NG115 for COPD) and the international GINA and GOLD reports, and is described as principle, not as instructions for individual patients.

## Asthma {#sec-asthma}

::: definition Asthma {#def-asthma}
**Asthma** is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways characterised by **variable respiratory symptoms** (wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, which vary over time and in intensity) together with **variable expiratory airflow limitation**. The airways are **hyperresponsive**: they narrow excessively in response to stimuli that have little effect on healthy airways.
:::

### What happens in the airway

In most people with asthma, especially those whose disease began in childhood, the inflammation is of a **type 2** pattern. Inhaled allergens such as house dust mite, pollen or animal dander are taken up by dendritic cells, which present them to T helper 2 (Th2) cells. These and the innate lymphoid cells of the airway release cytokines with distinct jobs: interleukin-4 drives B cells to make immunoglobulin E (IgE), interleukin-5 recruits and sustains eosinophils, and interleukin-13 increases mucus production and airway hyperresponsiveness. Damaged airway epithelium releases "alarmin" signals, such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), that amplify the response ([[immunology/hypersensitivity]]).

When a sensitised person inhales the allergen again, IgE on mast cells is cross-linked and the mast cells release histamine, leukotrienes and prostaglandins within minutes. Smooth muscle contracts and the mucosa swells: the **early asthmatic response**. Some hours later, recruited eosinophils and lymphocytes cause a **late response** with further narrowing and increased hyperresponsiveness. Over years, repeated inflammation can thicken the airway wall, increase smooth muscle mass and deposit collagen under the epithelium. This **remodelling** may make some airflow limitation fixed.

Not all asthma is type 2. Some adults, often with later onset, obesity or a smoking history, have little eosinophilic inflammation; this "type 2-low" asthma responds less well to corticosteroids. Markers of type 2 inflammation that are measured in practice are the **blood eosinophil count** and **FeNO** ([[respiratory/control-breathing-tests]]).

Common triggers include viral respiratory infections (the commonest cause of attacks in children), allergens, cold air, exercise, smoke and pollution, and occupational sensitisers such as flour, isocyanates and latex. Some drugs provoke attacks: **β-blockers**, including eye drops, can cause severe bronchospasm, and in a minority of adults with asthma and nasal polyps, **aspirin and other NSAIDs** trigger attacks by diverting arachidonic acid metabolism towards leukotrienes.

### Diagnosis

There is no single gold-standard test for asthma. The diagnosis rests on a typical history supported by objective evidence of variable airflow obstruction or type 2 inflammation. Objective testing matters: studies in several countries have found that a substantial proportion of adults labelled as having asthma do not have it when tested.

The 2024 NICE/BTS/SIGN guideline (NG245) sets out a sequence of tests for adults with a history suggestive of asthma. In outline:

1. Measure the **blood eosinophil count** or **FeNO**. A raised eosinophil count (above the laboratory reference range) or a FeNO of 50 ppb or more confirms the diagnosis.
2. If not, perform **spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility**. An increase in FEV₁ of 12% or more and 200 mL or more confirms asthma.
3. If not, or if spirometry is not available, measure **peak flow variability** twice daily for two weeks; a mean amplitude of 20% or more confirms asthma.
4. If the diagnosis is still unproven, refer for a **bronchial challenge test**.

Children aged 5 to 16 follow a similar pathway with a lower FeNO threshold (35 ppb). Under 5, objective testing is usually not possible, and treatment is based on symptoms and signs, with review when the child is old enough to be tested.

::: example A cough that comes back every winter {#ex-asthma-dx}
A 24-year-old woman has had episodes of wheeze and night-time cough for a year, worse after colds and around cats. She had eczema as a child. Examination between episodes is normal. Her FeNO is 62 ppb. How is the diagnosis made, and what treatment would NG245 suggest?
::: solution
The history is typical: episodic, variable symptoms, nocturnal cough, recognisable triggers and a personal history of atopy. Under NG245, a FeNO of 50 ppb or more in an adult with a suggestive history **confirms asthma** without further testing (spirometry is still useful as a baseline). Her raised FeNO also predicts a good response to inhaled corticosteroids.

For a newly diagnosed adult, NG245 recommends starting with **as-needed low-dose ICS/formoterol** (an inhaled corticosteroid combined with the fast-acting long-acting β₂ agonist formoterol in one inhaler), used for symptom relief: "anti-inflammatory reliever" (AIR) therapy. Each use of the reliever also delivers a dose of corticosteroid. If she has frequent symptoms or night waking at presentation, the guideline suggests starting directly on low-dose MART (below). She also needs a personalised written action plan, inhaler technique training and advice on triggers.
:::
:::

### Managing stable asthma

The goal is **control**: no or minimal symptoms, no attacks, no limitation of activity, and normal or near-normal lung function, with the fewest side effects. Uncontrolled asthma is usually assessed by asking about symptoms, reliever use and night waking, sometimes with a validated questionnaire, and by recording attacks needing oral corticosteroids.

::: definition Inhaled therapy for asthma {#def-asthma-drugs}
- **Inhaled corticosteroids** (ICS; for example budesonide, beclometasone, fluticasone) reduce airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness and the risk of attacks. They are the foundation of asthma treatment.
- **β₂ agonists** relax airway smooth muscle. Short-acting agents (SABA; salbutamol, terbutaline) act within minutes for 4–6 hours. Long-acting agents (LABA; salmeterol, formoterol, vilanterol) act for 12–24 hours. Formoterol has a fast onset, so it can also be used as a reliever.
- **MART** (maintenance and reliever therapy) uses a single ICS/formoterol inhaler both as regular maintenance and as needed for symptoms.
- Other add-on treatments include **leukotriene receptor antagonists** (montelukast), **long-acting muscarinic antagonists** (LAMA; tiotropium) and, in severe asthma, **biological therapies** directed at type 2 pathways.
:::

For adults and young people aged 12 and over, NG245 sets out a stepwise pathway. In outline: start with as-needed low-dose ICS/formoterol (AIR); if asthma is not controlled, move to low-dose MART; then moderate-dose MART. If still uncontrolled, check FeNO and blood eosinophils: if either is raised, refer to a specialist asthma service; if neither is raised, consider adding a leukotriene receptor antagonist or a LAMA for a trial period, and refer if control is not achieved. Children aged 5–11 follow a related pathway starting with a paediatric low-dose ICS. Exact doses, devices and licensing come from the guideline and a current formulary. The GINA strategy (updated annually) recommends a similar ICS/formoterol-based approach as its preferred track.

Before stepping up, the basics are checked: is the patient using the inhaler correctly, taking it regularly, and avoiding triggers (including smoking)? Is the diagnosis right? Are comorbidities such as rhinitis, obesity, reflux or dysfunctional breathing contributing?

::: warning Never treat asthma with a short-acting reliever alone
Regular or frequent use of a short-acting β₂ agonist without an inhaled corticosteroid relieves symptoms while leaving the inflammation untreated, and heavy SABA use is a marker of risk. The UK National Review of Asthma Deaths (2014) found that many people who died had been prescribed large numbers of reliever inhalers and few preventers. Current guidance (NG245 and GINA) no longer recommends SABA-only treatment for adults and adolescents. A patient who is collecting many reliever inhalers needs urgent review, not a repeat prescription.
:::

**Severe asthma** is asthma that remains uncontrolled despite high-dose treatment and attention to the basics, or that needs such treatment to stay controlled. It is managed in specialist centres. Biological therapies target type 2 inflammation: **omalizumab** (anti-IgE), **mepolizumab** and **reslizumab** (anti-interleukin-5), **benralizumab** (against the interleukin-5 receptor, depleting eosinophils), **dupilumab** (against the interleukin-4 receptor α, blocking interleukin-4 and -13 signalling) and **tezepelumab** (anti-TSLP). They reduce attacks and the need for oral corticosteroids, whose long-term harms include osteoporosis, diabetes, cataract and adrenal suppression.

### Acute asthma

An acute asthma attack is an emergency that can kill, including in young people with previously mild disease. Severity is judged by simple bedside measures. The BTS/SIGN criteria used in UK practice are summarised here.

::: definition Severity of acute asthma in adults {#def-acute-asthma}
- **Moderate**: increasing symptoms; PEF more than 50–75% of best or predicted; no features of acute severe asthma.
- **Acute severe**: any one of PEF 33–50% of best or predicted; respiratory rate 25/min or more; heart rate 110/min or more; inability to complete sentences in one breath.
- **Life-threatening**: in a patient with severe asthma, any one of PEF below 33% of best or predicted; $S_pO_2$ below 92%; $P_{aO_2}$ below 8 kPa; a "normal" $P_{aCO_2}$ (4.6–6.0 kPa); silent chest; cyanosis; poor respiratory effort; arrhythmia; exhaustion; altered conscious level; hypotension.
- **Near-fatal**: raised $P_{aCO_2}$, or the need for mechanical ventilation with raised inflation pressures.
:::

A normal $P_{aCO_2}$ is a danger sign in acute asthma. A patient with an attack should be hyperventilating and have a low $P_{aCO_2}$; a normal or rising value means they are tiring and can no longer maintain that ventilation.

Treatment in hospital follows the guideline and local protocols. The principles are: oxygen to a target saturation of 94–98%; high-dose inhaled short-acting β₂ agonist, repeated or continuous by oxygen-driven nebuliser in severe attacks; nebulised ipratropium added in severe or life-threatening attacks; systemic corticosteroids for every attack needing hospital assessment (in UK practice, typically oral prednisolone for at least five days); and, if there is a poor response, a single dose of intravenous magnesium sulfate. Patients with life-threatening or near-fatal features, or who are deteriorating, need early senior and critical care involvement. Before discharge, inhaler technique, preventer treatment and a written action plan are reviewed, with follow-up in primary care.

::: example Grading an attack {#ex-acute-asthma}
A 19-year-old student with asthma presents with breathlessness that began after a cold. She can speak only a few words between breaths. Her respiratory rate is 30/min, heart rate 124/min, PEF 160 L/min (her best is 450 L/min), $S_pO_2$ 93% on air. A blood gas shows $P_{aO_2}$ 9.1 kPa and $P_{aCO_2}$ 4.2 kPa. Classify the severity.
::: solution
PEF as a percentage of best: $160/450 = 0.356$, about 36%, in the 33–50% band. Respiratory rate above 25, heart rate above 110 and inability to complete sentences are all features of **acute severe asthma**. None of the life-threatening features is present: PEF is above 33%, $S_pO_2$ is above 92%, $P_{aO_2}$ is above 8 kPa, and her $P_{aCO_2}$ is low, as it should be. She needs immediate treatment in hospital: controlled oxygen, nebulised salbutamol, systemic corticosteroid and close reassessment. If her PEF falls below 33% or her $P_{aCO_2}$ rises into the normal range, she has moved into the life-threatening category.
:::
:::

## COPD {#sec-copd}

::: definition COPD {#def-copd}
**Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease** (COPD) is a heterogeneous lung condition with chronic respiratory symptoms (breathlessness, cough, sputum production, exacerbations) due to abnormalities of the airways (bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and/or the alveoli (emphysema) that cause **persistent, often progressive, airflow obstruction** (GOLD definition). In practice the diagnosis requires a **post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC below 0.70** in a person with compatible symptoms and risk factors.
:::

COPD is one of the leading causes of death worldwide; the WHO lists it fourth, with about 3.5 million deaths in 2021. In the UK the main cause is tobacco smoking. Worldwide, smoke from burning wood, dung and crop waste for cooking and heating (biomass fuel), occupational dusts and fumes, and outdoor air pollution are also important, which is why COPD is common in women in many low- and middle-income countries who have never smoked. Factors that limit lung growth in early life, such as preterm birth, childhood infections and maternal smoking, also raise the risk, because a person who never reaches normal peak lung function has less to lose.

**Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency** is an inherited cause. Alpha-1 antitrypsin, made in the liver, protects the lung against neutrophil elastase. People homozygous for the Z allele (PiZZ) have very low levels and develop emphysema early, typically in the lower lobes, especially if they smoke; some also develop liver disease ([[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]). Testing is recommended for people who develop COPD young or with little smoking history.

### What goes wrong

Tobacco smoke and other irritants cause chronic inflammation dominated by neutrophils, macrophages and CD8 T cells. Three processes follow, in different proportions in different people:

- **Chronic bronchitis**: hypertrophy of mucus glands and goblet cells, causing cough and sputum. Clinically it is defined as a productive cough on most days for at least three months in two consecutive years.
- **Small airway disease**: inflammation, fibrosis and loss of the small airways. This is the main site of airflow obstruction, and much of it occurs before symptoms appear.
- **Emphysema**: permanent enlargement of air spaces beyond the terminal bronchioles with destruction of their walls. **Centriacinar** emphysema, typical of smokers, affects the respiratory bronchioles in the upper zones; **panacinar** emphysema, typical of alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, affects the whole acinus, mainly in the lower zones. An imbalance between proteases (elastase from neutrophils and macrophages) and antiproteases, together with oxidative stress, drives the destruction.

The mechanical consequences were described in [[respiratory/mechanics-breathing]]: loss of elastic recoil and narrowed small airways limit expiratory flow, trap air and cause dynamic hyperinflation. Loss of alveolar walls reduces gas transfer. V/Q mismatch causes hypoxaemia; in advanced disease, hypercapnia, pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure (**cor pulmonale**) may follow. COPD is also a systemic disease, associated with muscle wasting, weight loss, osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, anxiety and depression, and lung cancer.

### Diagnosis and assessment

COPD should be considered in anyone over 35 with a risk factor (usually smoking) who has exertional breathlessness, chronic cough, regular sputum, frequent winter "bronchitis" or wheeze. Diagnosis needs **post-bronchodilator spirometry** showing FEV₁/FVC below 0.70. Other initial investigations in NICE guidance include a chest radiograph (to exclude other diagnoses, such as lung cancer), a full blood count (anaemia, polycythaemia) and calculation of body mass index. Smoking history is quantified in **pack-years**: the number of packs of 20 cigarettes smoked per day multiplied by the number of years smoked.

::: definition Assessing COPD severity {#def-gold}
- **Severity of airflow obstruction** (GOLD grades, by post-bronchodilator FEV₁ as % predicted, in people with FEV₁/FVC < 0.70): GOLD 1 mild, ≥80%; GOLD 2 moderate, 50–79%; GOLD 3 severe, 30–49%; GOLD 4 very severe, <30%.
- **Symptoms**: the modified MRC (mMRC) dyspnoea scale or the COPD Assessment Test (CAT).
- **Exacerbation history** in the past year.
- GOLD combines symptoms and exacerbations into groups **A** (few symptoms, low risk), **B** (more symptoms, low risk) and **E** (two or more moderate exacerbations, or one or more leading to hospital admission).
:::

FEV₁ alone correlates only loosely with symptoms and outcomes. Breathlessness, exacerbation frequency, exercise capacity and body weight all add prognostic information.

::: example How much has he smoked? {#ex-pack-years}
A 62-year-old man started smoking at 16. He smoked 30 cigarettes a day until he was 40 and 15 a day since. Calculate his pack-years.
::: solution
From 16 to 40 is 24 years at 30 a day: $24 \times 30/20 = 36$ pack-years.

From 40 to 62 is 22 years at 15 a day: $22 \times 15/20 = 16.5$ pack-years.

Total: $36 + 16.5 = 52.5$ pack-years. This is a heavy exposure. It puts him at high risk of COPD, lung cancer and cardiovascular disease; in England he would be eligible for the lung cancer screening programme ([[respiratory/lung-cancer-pleura]]). Stopping smoking now is still the single most useful intervention for every one of these risks.
:::
:::

### Managing stable COPD

The principles in NICE guideline NG115 are, in order of priority:

1. **Stop smoking.** Smoking cessation is the only intervention shown to slow the decline in lung function. Behavioural support combined with pharmacotherapy (nicotine replacement, varenicline or bupropion) roughly doubles or triples quit rates compared with willpower alone ([[public-health/prevention-promotion]]).
2. **Vaccinate**: annual influenza vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine, and other respiratory vaccines as recommended by the current national programme.
3. **Pulmonary rehabilitation** for people who are functionally limited by breathlessness (in NICE guidance, typically MRC grade 3 and above): a supervised programme of exercise training and education lasting at least six weeks. It improves exercise capacity and quality of life more than any inhaler, and after a hospital admission it reduces readmission.
4. **Self-management and optimisation**: an individualised plan for exacerbations, correct inhaler technique, treatment of comorbidities, nutrition and psychological support.
5. **Inhaled therapy** to relieve breathlessness and reduce exacerbations.

::: definition Inhaled therapy for COPD {#def-copd-drugs}
- **Short-acting bronchodilators** (SABA or short-acting muscarinic antagonist, SAMA) as needed for breathlessness.
- **LAMA + LABA**: for people who remain limited by breathlessness or have exacerbations, and who do not have asthmatic features. Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (tiotropium, umeclidinium, glycopyrronium, aclidinium) block M3 receptors on airway smooth muscle.
- **LABA + ICS**: considered in NICE guidance for people with **asthmatic features or features suggesting steroid responsiveness**: a previous diagnosis of asthma or atopy, a higher blood eosinophil count, substantial variation in FEV₁ over time (at least 400 mL) or substantial diurnal variation in peak flow (at least 20%).
- **Triple therapy** (LAMA + LABA + ICS) for people who still have exacerbations or troublesome symptoms on dual therapy.
:::

GOLD (2025 report) recommends LAMA + LABA as the usual initial treatment for groups B and E, considering triple therapy in group E when the blood eosinophil count is 300 cells/µL or more. Blood eosinophils predict benefit from inhaled corticosteroids. ICS increase the risk of pneumonia in COPD, so they are not used without an indication.

Other treatments for selected patients include **long-term azithromycin** to reduce exacerbations (specialist initiated, after checking the ECG for QT prolongation and excluding non-tuberculous mycobacteria), **roflumilast** (a phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitor) and mucolytics. Newer options in recent GOLD reports include **dupilumab** for people with frequent exacerbations and raised eosinophils, and the inhaled phosphodiesterase 3/4 inhibitor **ensifentrine**; availability varies by country. For selected patients with severe emphysema and hyperinflation, **lung volume reduction** (surgical, or bronchoscopic with one-way endobronchial valves) can improve breathlessness and exercise capacity, and lung transplantation is an option for a small number.

**Long-term oxygen therapy** (LTOT) prolongs life in COPD with chronic hypoxaemia. This was shown by two trials around 1980: the British Medical Research Council trial and the US Nocturnal Oxygen Therapy Trial. In NICE guidance, LTOT is offered to non-smokers with a $P_{aO_2}$ below 7.3 kPa when stable, or below 8 kPa with secondary polycythaemia, peripheral oedema or pulmonary hypertension, on two measurements at least three weeks apart, and it is used for at least 15 hours a day. Oxygen is not a treatment for breathlessness in people who are not hypoxaemic, and smoking near oxygen is a serious fire risk, so smoking is a strong reason not to prescribe it.

::: example A plan for stable COPD {#ex-copd-stable}
A 67-year-old woman with a 40-pack-year history is breathless walking at her own pace on the flat (mMRC 2, CAT 18). Post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC is 0.55 and FEV₁ 58% of predicted. She had one exacerbation treated at home last year. Her blood eosinophil count is 0.1 × 10⁹/L, and she has no history of asthma. She still smokes 10 a day. Classify her COPD and outline management principles.
::: solution
The ratio is below 0.70, confirming obstruction. FEV₁ 58% gives **GOLD grade 2** (moderate). She has significant symptoms (mMRC 2, CAT ≥10) and only one moderate exacerbation, so she is in **GOLD group B**.

Priorities: help to stop smoking with behavioural support and pharmacotherapy; influenza, pneumococcal and other recommended vaccines; referral to pulmonary rehabilitation, since she is limited by breathlessness; a self-management plan; and a check for comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, anxiety, depression, osteoporosis). Inhaled therapy: a short-acting bronchodilator as needed, and because she is breathless and has no asthmatic features and a low eosinophil count, a **LAMA + LABA** combination. An ICS is not indicated now. Inhaler technique should be checked at every review.
:::
:::

### Exacerbations of COPD

::: definition Exacerbation of COPD {#def-aecopd}
An **exacerbation** is a sustained worsening of symptoms (breathlessness, cough, sputum volume or purulence) beyond normal day-to-day variation, acute in onset, that requires a change in treatment. Most are triggered by respiratory viruses or bacteria (*Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*) or by air pollution; some have no identifiable cause.
:::

Exacerbations matter: they speed the decline in lung function, worsen quality of life, and those that need admission carry a substantial risk of death in the following year. Other causes of acute breathlessness in a person with COPD, such as pneumonia, pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, heart failure and arrhythmia, must be considered.

Principles of management, following NICE guidance and local protocols:

- Increase short-acting bronchodilator use.
- **Oral corticosteroid**: in UK practice, typically prednisolone 30 mg daily for 5 days. This shortens recovery and reduces treatment failure.
- **Antibiotics** when sputum is purulent or there are signs of infection, choosing according to local guidance (NICE NG114) and previous sputum results.
- In hospital, **controlled oxygen** to a target saturation of 88–92% if the patient is at risk of hypercapnic failure, with a blood gas to check.
- **Non-invasive ventilation** (NIV) for acute hypercapnic respiratory failure that persists after optimal initial medical treatment: in BTS/ICS guidance, pH below 7.35 with $P_{aCO_2}$ above 6.5 kPa. NIV reduces mortality and the need for intubation ([[respiratory/respiratory-failure]]).
- After the episode: review inhaled therapy, inhaler technique and smoking, and offer pulmonary rehabilitation (ideally starting within four weeks of discharge).

::: example The gas and the acid {#ex-aecopd}
A 71-year-old man with known COPD (GOLD 3) is admitted with three days of increasing breathlessness and green sputum. On a 28% Venturi mask, his $S_pO_2$ is 90%. His first gas shows pH 7.30, $P_{aCO_2}$ 8.1 kPa, bicarbonate 30 mmol/L. After nebulised bronchodilators, prednisolone, antibiotics and an hour of controlled oxygen, a repeat gas shows pH 7.28 and $P_{aCO_2}$ 8.6 kPa. What is the next step?
::: solution
He has an **acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory acidosis**: the raised bicarbonate indicates chronic retention, and the low pH shows an acute component. His saturation is on target (88–92%), so oxygen is not driving the problem; the acidosis has persisted, and slightly worsened, despite optimal medical treatment. With pH below 7.35 and $P_{aCO_2}$ above 6.5 kPa, he meets the criteria for **NIV**. Before starting, a decision is made and documented about what happens if NIV fails, whether invasive ventilation would be appropriate given his wishes and his condition, and a chest radiograph is checked for a pneumothorax. Blood gases are repeated within 1–2 hours of starting NIV; failure to improve prompts escalation according to the plan.
:::
:::

## Asthma or COPD? {#sec-distinguish}

Distinguishing the two matters because the treatments differ: ICS are the foundation of asthma treatment but can harm some people with COPD, while long-acting bronchodilators without ICS are first-line in COPD but dangerous as monotherapy in asthma.

| Feature | Asthma | COPD |
|---|---|---|
| Age at onset | often childhood or young adulthood | usually over 35 |
| Smoking or exposure | not required | almost always |
| Symptoms | variable, nocturnal, triggered | persistent, progressive |
| Atopy | common | not typical |
| Lung function between episodes | often normal | persistently abnormal |
| Large bronchodilator response, FeNO raised | supports | uncommon |
| Gas transfer | usually normal | often reduced (emphysema) |

Some people have features of both, especially smokers with long-standing asthma. Such patients are treated with inhaled corticosteroids as part of their regimen and generally do worse than people with either condition alone.

::: quiz
In a person with COPD and no features of asthma who remains breathless despite as-needed short-acting bronchodilators, which maintenance treatment is recommended first in NICE and GOLD guidance?
- [ ] An inhaled corticosteroid alone
- [ ] A long-acting β₂ agonist plus an inhaled corticosteroid
- [x] A long-acting muscarinic antagonist plus a long-acting β₂ agonist
- [ ] Long-term oxygen therapy
::: solution
Dual long-acting bronchodilation (LAMA + LABA) is recommended for people without asthmatic or steroid-responsive features ([[#def-copd-drugs]]). An ICS alone is not used in COPD, and LABA + ICS is reserved for those with asthmatic features. Oxygen is for chronic hypoxaemia, not breathlessness alone.
:::
:::

::: history
Henry Hyde Salter, a London physician who had asthma himself, described it in 1860 as a disorder of the bronchial muscle and recognised triggers such as cats. Charles Fletcher and Richard Peto followed London working men for eight years and in 1977 described the pattern now known by their names: only susceptible smokers lose lung function rapidly, and stopping smoking returns the rate of decline towards normal. In 1963 Carl-Bertil Laurell and Sten Eriksson discovered alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency from a missing band on serum electrophoresis. The Nocturnal Oxygen Therapy Trial in the United States (1980) and the British Medical Research Council trial (1981) showed that long-term oxygen prolongs life in hypoxaemic COPD. International strategies followed: the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) was launched in 1993 and published its first report in 1995, and the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) in 2001. In 2014 the UK National Review of Asthma Deaths showed that many deaths were preventable, most often because of underuse of preventers and overreliance on relievers.
:::

## Where this leads

The infections that trigger many exacerbations are in [[respiratory/respiratory-infections]]. When exacerbations lead to hypercapnic respiratory failure, the use of oxygen and NIV is developed in [[respiratory/respiratory-failure]]. COPD and lung cancer share their main cause and often coexist ([[respiratory/lung-cancer-pleura]]). The pharmacology of β₂ agonists and muscarinic antagonists is in [[pharmacology/autonomic-drugs]], and the type 2 immunology of asthma in [[immunology/hypersensitivity]].

::: summary
- Asthma: chronic airway inflammation with variable symptoms and variable airflow limitation; often type 2 (eosinophils, IgE, raised FeNO).
- NG245 (2024): diagnose adults with eosinophils or FeNO (≥50 ppb), then bronchodilator reversibility (≥12% and ≥200 mL), then PEF variability (≥20%), then bronchial challenge.
- Asthma treatment is based on ICS. NG245 starts adults on as-needed ICS/formoterol (AIR), then low- and moderate-dose MART. SABA-only treatment is no longer recommended.
- Acute severe asthma: PEF 33–50%, RR ≥25, HR ≥110, cannot complete sentences. A normal $P_{aCO_2}$ is a life-threatening sign.
- COPD: persistent airflow obstruction (post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70) from smoking and other exposures; small airway disease, emphysema and chronic bronchitis.
- Stable COPD: smoking cessation, vaccination, pulmonary rehabilitation, self-management, then LAMA + LABA; ICS only with asthmatic features or raised eosinophils; LTOT for chronic hypoxaemia ($P_{aO_2}$ < 7.3 kPa, or < 8 kPa with complications).
- Exacerbations: bronchodilators, prednisolone, antibiotics if infected, controlled oxygen (88–92%), and NIV for persistent respiratory acidosis.
:::

A man with an asthma attack has a PEF of 190 L/min; his best is 520 L/min. What percentage of his best is this (nearest whole number), and what severity band does it fall in on PEF alone?
::: solution
$190/520 = 0.365$, which is 37% to the nearest whole number. This lies in the 33–50% band, consistent with **acute severe** asthma; the other features (rate, pulse, speech, saturation, blood gases) must also be assessed for life-threatening signs.
:::
:::

::: exercise GOLD grade {level=1}
A 70-year-old man has post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC 0.48 and FEV₁ 34% of predicted. What is his GOLD grade? What other information is needed to choose treatment?
::: solution
FEV₁ 30–49% predicted with a ratio below 0.70 is **GOLD 3 (severe)**. Treatment choice also needs his symptom burden (mMRC or CAT), his exacerbation history over the past year, his blood eosinophil count, any asthmatic features, his smoking status, oxygen saturation (to consider assessment for LTOT) and comorbidities.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why formoterol? {level=2}
Explain why ICS/formoterol can be used as a reliever in asthma whereas ICS/salmeterol cannot.
::: solution
Formoterol has a rapid onset of bronchodilation (within a few minutes) as well as a long duration, so it relieves symptoms quickly; using the combination inhaler as a reliever also delivers a dose of ICS each time symptoms flare, treating inflammation when it increases. Salmeterol has a slow onset, so it does not provide fast relief and is not used as a reliever.
:::
:::

::: exercise Is it life-threatening? {level=2}
A 33-year-old man with acute asthma has a respiratory rate of 18/min, is drowsy, has a quiet chest on auscultation, $S_pO_2$ 91% on air and $P_{aCO_2}$ 5.6 kPa. A junior colleague says he looks "calmer" and his rate is normal. How do you respond?
::: solution
This is **life-threatening asthma**, and possibly near-fatal. The quiet (silent) chest, drowsiness (altered conscious level), saturation below 92% and a normal $P_{aCO_2}$ are all life-threatening features. A falling respiratory rate in this context means exhaustion, not improvement: he is no longer able to hyperventilate, and the normal $P_{aCO_2}$ shows it. He needs immediate high-dose bronchodilators, oxygen, systemic corticosteroid, intravenous magnesium and urgent senior and critical care review, as intubation may be needed.
:::
:::

::: exercise LTOT criteria {level=2}
A 74-year-old woman with COPD who stopped smoking five years ago has two stable arterial gases on air, four weeks apart: $P_{aO_2}$ 7.6 and 7.7 kPa. She has ankle oedema, and her echocardiogram suggests pulmonary hypertension. Does she meet NICE criteria for long-term oxygen therapy?
::: solution
Her $P_{aO_2}$ is above 7.3 kPa, so she does not meet the first criterion. But it is below 8 kPa, and she has peripheral oedema and evidence of pulmonary hypertension, so she meets the second criterion. She is a non-smoker. She should be offered LTOT for at least 15 hours a day, with assessment of her home safety and of her ability to use the equipment. The aim is to reduce the pulmonary vasoconstriction driving her pulmonary hypertension and to improve survival.
:::
:::

::: exercise Asthma or COPD? {level=3}
A 58-year-old man who smoked 25 pack-years and stopped 10 years ago has breathlessness and wheeze. He had "chest trouble" as a child. Post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC is 0.63; FEV₁ rises by 450 mL (22%) after salbutamol. FeNO is 48 ppb and blood eosinophils 0.45 × 10⁹/L. TLCO is normal. Discuss the likely diagnosis and how it affects treatment.
::: hint
Look at each feature in the table in [[#sec-distinguish]] and consider which direction it points.
:::
::: solution
Features of COPD: age, a substantial smoking history and persistent post-bronchodilator obstruction (ratio 0.63). Features of asthma: childhood respiratory symptoms, a very large bronchodilator response (450 mL, 22%), raised FeNO and raised blood eosinophils, and normal gas transfer (making significant emphysema unlikely). He probably has **asthma with fixed airflow obstruction**, perhaps with a COPD component from smoking. The practical consequence is that he must receive an **inhaled corticosteroid** as part of his treatment. A LABA without an ICS would be unsafe if asthma is present. In NICE COPD terms he has clear "asthmatic features", so LABA + ICS (or triple therapy) is appropriate, and specialist review is reasonable.
:::
:::

::: exercise Choosing the oxygen target {level=3}
For each patient, state the target oxygen saturation in hospital and give a reason: (a) a 30-year-old woman with acute severe asthma; (b) a 69-year-old man with COPD, previous hypercapnic respiratory failure and a raised bicarbonate on admission; (c) a 64-year-old woman with COPD whose admission blood gas shows $P_{aCO_2}$ 4.6 kPa and normal bicarbonate, admitted with pneumonia.
::: solution
(a) **94–98%.** She is not at risk of hypercapnic failure; hypoxaemia in asthma is dangerous and should be corrected.

(b) **88–92%.** He has documented chronic hypercapnia and previous hypercapnic failure, so uncontrolled oxygen risks worsening his acidosis ([[respiratory/control-breathing-tests#thm-o2-co2]]).

(c) Initially **88–92%** because she has COPD and is in a group at risk; but her blood gas shows no hypercapnia and a normal bicarbonate, so following BTS guidance the target can be revised to 94–98% if subsequent gases confirm she is not retaining carbon dioxide. The target is individual and reviewed, not fixed by diagnosis.
:::
