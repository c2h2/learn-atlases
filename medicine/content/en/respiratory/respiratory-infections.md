The lungs are exposed to the outside world with every breath: around 10,000 litres of air a day, carrying dust, droplets and micro-organisms. Most of the time the respiratory tract keeps the lower airways close to sterile. When those defences are overwhelmed, or a particularly virulent organism gets through, the result is a lower respiratory tract infection. Pneumonia remains one of the commonest reasons for admission to hospital and one of the leading infectious causes of death worldwide, particularly at the extremes of age.

This chapter starts with the defences of the lung and then deals with pneumonia: how it is classified, which organisms cause it, how severity is assessed and how antibiotics are chosen. It then covers the main viral infections, influenza, COVID-19 and respiratory syncytial virus, and ends with tuberculosis, which is treated in more depth in [[infectious-diseases/tuberculosis]]. Antibiotic recommendations are described as the principles of current UK guidance (NICE NG250, 2025, and NICE NG138); choices in practice follow local guidelines, which reflect local resistance patterns.

## Defences of the respiratory tract {#sec-defences}

::: definition Lung defences {#def-defences}
- **Mechanical**: nasal hairs and turbinates filter large particles; the cough reflex and glottic closure protect against aspiration.
- **The mucociliary escalator**: a layer of mucus traps particles and organisms, and cilia on the airway epithelium beat to move it upwards to the pharynx, where it is swallowed.
- **Humoral**: secretory IgA, surfactant proteins A and D (which bind and opsonise microbes), lysozyme, lactoferrin and antimicrobial peptides.
- **Cellular**: alveolar macrophages ingest particles and organisms that reach the alveoli and recruit neutrophils when they are overwhelmed.
:::

Almost every risk factor for pneumonia is a failure of one of these. Smoking paralyses cilia and damages macrophages. Viral infections such as influenza strip the epithelium and open the way for bacteria. Reduced consciousness (alcohol, sedatives, stroke, seizures) and swallowing problems allow aspiration. Cystic fibrosis and primary ciliary dyskinesia impair mucus clearance. Immunosuppression, whether from HIV, chemotherapy, transplant drugs or corticosteroids, weakens the cellular and humoral defences ([[immunology/immunodeficiency]]).

## Pneumonia {#sec-pneumonia}

::: definition Pneumonia and its settings {#def-pneumonia}
**Pneumonia** is infection of the lung parenchyma, with inflammatory exudate filling the alveoli. It is usually diagnosed by symptoms and signs of a lower respiratory tract infection together with new shadowing on a chest radiograph that has no other explanation. It is classified by where it is acquired:

- **Community-acquired pneumonia** (CAP): acquired outside hospital.
- **Hospital-acquired pneumonia** (HAP): developing 48 hours or more after admission, and not incubating at admission.
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia** (VAP): developing 48 hours or more after tracheal intubation.
- **Aspiration pneumonia**: following inhalation of oropharyngeal or gastric contents.
- **Pneumonia in the immunocompromised host**, where unusual organisms such as *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, fungi and cytomegalovirus must be considered.
:::

The distinction matters because the likely organisms differ. Pneumonia acquired in the community is usually caused by organisms that colonise the healthy upper airway. Pneumonia acquired in hospital, after days of antibiotics and invasive devices, is more often caused by Gram-negative bacilli and resistant organisms.

Pneumonia must be distinguished from **acute bronchitis**, an infection of the airways (usually viral) with cough but no consolidation. Acute bronchitis in otherwise healthy adults usually settles without antibiotics, and the cough can last three weeks.

### Causes of community-acquired pneumonia

- ***Streptococcus pneumoniae*** (the pneumococcus) is the commonest identified bacterial cause at all ages. It typically causes lobar pneumonia with an abrupt onset, high fever, rigors, pleuritic pain and rust-coloured sputum.
- ***Haemophilus influenzae*** is common in people with COPD.
- ***Mycoplasma pneumoniae*** affects younger adults and children, often in epidemics every few years. It causes a gradual illness with headache and dry cough, and sometimes extrapulmonary features such as haemolytic anaemia (cold agglutinins), rash and neurological complications.
- ***Legionella pneumophila*** is acquired by inhaling aerosols from contaminated water systems (cooling towers, showers, spas), not from other people. It can cause severe pneumonia with confusion, diarrhoea, hyponatraemia and deranged liver function.
- ***Staphylococcus aureus*** causes severe, sometimes cavitating pneumonia, classically after influenza. Strains producing Panton–Valentine leukocidin can cause rapidly fatal necrotising pneumonia in previously healthy young people.
- ***Klebsiella pneumoniae*** and other Gram-negative bacilli are more common in people with alcohol dependence, diabetes and frailty.
- ***Chlamydia psittaci*** (psittacosis) is acquired from birds.
- **Viruses**, including influenza, SARS-CoV-2 and respiratory syncytial virus, cause a large share of pneumonia, alone or with bacteria.

*Mycoplasma*, *Legionella* and *Chlamydia* species are often called "atypical" organisms. The term is clinically useful because they lack a conventional cell wall or live inside cells, so they do not respond to β-lactam antibiotics such as amoxicillin; they need a macrolide, a tetracycline or a fluoroquinolone. In practice, the clinical picture is a poor guide to the organism, and in about half of cases no organism is ever identified.

### Pathology and presentation

In lobar pneumonia, the classical sequence in an untreated patient moves through congestion (vascular engorgement and oedema), red hepatisation (alveoli filled with neutrophils, red cells and fibrin, so the lung looks like liver), grey hepatisation (red cells broken down, fibrin persists) and resolution, in which macrophages clear the exudate and the lung architecture returns to normal. **Bronchopneumonia** is patchy consolidation centred on bronchioles, often in both lower lobes, and is common in frail and older people ([[pathology/acute-inflammation]]).

The typical patient has fever, cough, sputum, breathlessness and pleuritic chest pain. Examination may show tachypnoea, reduced chest expansion, dullness to percussion, bronchial breathing and crackles over the affected area. Older people may present only with confusion, falls or reduced mobility, and may have no fever. Signs of sepsis (hypotension, a high respiratory rate, confusion, a raised lactate) indicate a serious illness ([[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]]).

### Investigations

- **Chest radiograph**: confirms consolidation and detects complications (effusion, cavitation). In primary care, a clinical diagnosis is often made without one. Radiographic changes lag behind clinical recovery, sometimes by several weeks.
- **Pulse oximetry and, if saturation is low or the patient is unwell, arterial blood gases.**
- **Blood tests**: full blood count, urea and electrolytes (urea is part of the severity score; hyponatraemia suggests *Legionella*), liver function and C-reactive protein. A point-of-care CRP test can help decide on antibiotics in primary care when the diagnosis is uncertain.
- **Microbiology** in people with moderate or high severity: blood cultures, sputum culture, urinary antigen tests for pneumococcus and *Legionella* (the *Legionella* test detects serogroup 1 only), and PCR for respiratory viruses and, when suspected, atypical organisms.

### Assessing severity

The decision whether to manage a patient at home or in hospital, and how intensively, rests on clinical judgement supported by a validated score.

::: definition CRB65 and CURB65 {#def-curb65}
One point is scored for each of:

- **C**onfusion (new disorientation in person, place or time, or an abbreviated mental test score of 8 or less);
- **U**rea above 7 mmol/L (CURB65 only, in hospital);
- **R**espiratory rate of 30/min or more;
- **B**lood pressure: systolic below 90 mmHg or diastolic 60 mmHg or less;
- age **65** or over.

In hospital, **CURB65** of 0–1 indicates low severity, 2 moderate and 3–5 high severity. In the community, **CRB65** (without urea) is used: in NICE NG250, 0 is usually low severity, 1–2 moderate and 3–4 high, and a score of 2 or more is an indication to consider hospital referral.
:::

Mortality rises steeply with the score: in the study that derived CURB65 (Lim and colleagues, 2003), it was under 3% for scores of 0–1 and around 40% or more for scores of 4–5. Scores support but do not replace judgement. They do not account for hypoxaemia, which on its own may require admission, or for pregnancy, frailty, social circumstances, an inability to take oral medicines or serious comorbidity.

::: example Scoring and deciding {#ex-curb}
A 77-year-old man is brought to the emergency department with three days of cough and fever. He is alert and oriented. Respiratory rate 26/min, blood pressure 102/58 mmHg, $S_pO_2$ 93% on air. Urea 9.4 mmol/L. The chest radiograph shows right lower lobe consolidation. Calculate his CURB65 score and describe the management principles.
::: solution
Confusion: no (0). Urea 9.4 > 7 (1). Respiratory rate 26, below 30 (0). Diastolic pressure 58 ≤ 60 (1). Age 77 ≥ 65 (1). **CURB65 = 3: high-severity pneumonia.**

He should be managed in hospital, assessed for sepsis, and considered for review by critical care if he deteriorates. Antibiotics are started promptly; for high-severity CAP, NICE guidance lists a broad-spectrum β-lactam combined with a macrolide (for example co-amoxiclav with clarithromycin), intravenously at first if he is very unwell, adjusted for allergy and local guidance. Blood and sputum cultures, urinary pneumococcal and *Legionella* antigen tests, and a respiratory virus PCR are sent. Oxygen is given to a target of 94–98% unless he is at risk of hypercapnia. Antibiotics are reviewed at 48 hours and switched to oral when he is improving; NICE recommends a total course of 5 days for most people with CAP, with a longer course only if clinically indicated.
:::
:::

### Treatment principles

Antibiotics should be started as soon as possible after the diagnosis is made, and within one hour if there are features of sepsis with high-risk criteria. Choices in NICE guidance for adults with CAP are, in outline: **low severity**, amoxicillin (with doxycycline or clarithromycin as alternatives in penicillin allergy); **moderate severity**, amoxicillin, adding a macrolide if an atypical organism is suspected; **high severity**, a β-lactam such as co-amoxiclav plus a macrolide. Doses, routes and alternatives come from the guideline and the formulary.

Supportive care is as important as antibiotics: oxygen, fluids, thromboprophylaxis, nutrition and early mobilisation. People are advised that fever usually settles within a week but that cough and fatigue can persist for several weeks.

If a patient does not improve, consider the possibilities in turn: the wrong antibiotic for the organism (atypical organism, resistance, *S. aureus*); a complication (empyema, lung abscess); a different diagnosis (pulmonary embolism, heart failure, lung cancer obstructing a bronchus, organising pneumonia, vasculitis); or a problem with the host (immunosuppression, aspiration). A follow-up chest radiograph is not routine but is considered for people whose symptoms persist and those at higher risk of an underlying cancer, such as smokers over 50.

### Complications

::: definition Parapneumonic effusion and empyema {#def-empyema}
A **parapneumonic effusion** is a pleural effusion associated with pneumonia. Most are sterile and resolve with antibiotics. A **complicated** parapneumonic effusion has been invaded by bacteria; it typically has a low pH (below 7.2), a low glucose and a high lactate dehydrogenase. **Empyema** is pus in the pleural space. Complicated effusions and empyema need drainage as well as antibiotics.
:::

Pleural infection should be suspected when fever persists, CRP fails to fall or pleuritic pain continues despite treatment. It is investigated by ultrasound-guided pleural aspiration and managed in line with the BTS pleural disease guideline (2023), as described in [[respiratory/lung-cancer-pleura]].

A **lung abscess** is a cavity containing pus, usually after aspiration (mixed anaerobic organisms), or with *S. aureus* or *Klebsiella*. It presents with swinging fever, weight loss and foul sputum, and the radiograph shows a cavity with an air–fluid level. It usually responds to a prolonged course of antibiotics. The differential includes cavitating lung cancer, tuberculosis and granulomatosis with polyangiitis.

Other complications include sepsis and septic shock, the acute respiratory distress syndrome ([[respiratory/respiratory-failure]]), and, in older people, acute coronary events and worsening heart failure.

::: example The fever that would not settle {#ex-empyema}
A 49-year-old woman with lobar pneumonia was treated with amoxicillin. On day 6 she still has fever and right-sided pleuritic pain, and her CRP has risen from 180 to 240 mg/L. Ultrasound shows a moderate effusion with septations. Pleural aspiration yields cloudy fluid with pH 7.05 and glucose 1.4 mmol/L. What is the diagnosis and the management principle?
::: solution
She has a **complicated parapneumonic effusion** progressing to empyema: the fluid is cloudy, acidic (pH below 7.2) and has a very low glucose, reflecting bacterial and neutrophil metabolism, and septations suggest it is becoming loculated. Antibiotics alone will not clear it. She needs **chest drain insertion** under ultrasound guidance, broadening of antibiotics to cover anaerobes and the organisms that cause pleural infection, with the choice guided by fluid culture and local guidance, and early discussion with a respiratory team. If drainage is incomplete, intrapleural tissue plasminogen activator with DNase, or surgery, is considered.
:::
:::

### Aspiration and hospital-acquired pneumonia

**Aspiration pneumonitis** is a chemical injury from inhaled gastric acid, often in a person with reduced consciousness; it may not need antibiotics. **Aspiration pneumonia** is a bacterial infection after aspiration of colonised oropharyngeal material, more common in people with dysphagia (after stroke, in Parkinson's disease or dementia) and poor dental health. It typically affects the dependent segments: the right lower lobe when upright, the posterior segments of the upper lobes and the apical segments of the lower lobes when lying flat, because of the anatomy of the right main bronchus, which is wider and more vertical than the left ([[anatomy/thorax]]).

**Hospital-acquired pneumonia** is a major healthcare-associated infection. Gram-negative bacilli (including *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Klebsiella*, *Escherichia coli*), *S. aureus* (including MRSA) and resistant organisms are more common, particularly in people who have had recent antibiotics or have been in hospital longer. Antibiotic choice depends on severity, time since admission, risk of resistance and local guidance. Prevention includes good oral care, sitting patients up, early mobilisation, careful swallow assessment and, in ventilated patients, a bundle of preventive measures ([[infectious-diseases/healthcare-infections]]).

## Viral respiratory infections {#sec-viral}

### Influenza

::: definition Influenza viruses and antigenic change {#def-influenza}
**Influenza A and B** are enveloped RNA viruses (orthomyxoviruses) with a segmented genome. Their surface carries **haemagglutinin** (H), which binds sialic acid receptors on respiratory epithelium, and **neuraminidase** (N), which releases new virions. **Antigenic drift** is the gradual accumulation of point mutations in H and N, producing new seasonal strains and the need for an updated vaccine each year. **Antigenic shift** occurs only in influenza A: reassortment of genome segments between human and animal (for example avian or swine) viruses produces a virus with a new H or N subtype to which few people are immune, which can cause a **pandemic**.
:::

Pandemics occurred in 1918 (H1N1, with an estimated tens of millions of deaths), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2) and 2009 (H1N1). Seasonal influenza causes abrupt fever, myalgia, headache, sore throat and dry cough. Most healthy adults recover within a week or two. Severe disease and death occur mainly in older people, people with chronic heart, lung, kidney, liver or neurological disease, people who are immunosuppressed, pregnant women and young children. Complications include primary viral pneumonia, secondary bacterial pneumonia (especially *S. pneumoniae* and *S. aureus*), exacerbations of asthma and COPD, myocarditis and encephalitis.

Treatment for people at increased risk of complications, when influenza is circulating in the community, is with a **neuraminidase inhibitor** (oseltamivir or zanamivir), ideally started within 48 hours of symptom onset, following NICE technology appraisal guidance and UKHSA advice. These drugs shorten illness modestly and may reduce complications in high-risk groups. Annual vaccination is offered in the UK to older adults, people with chronic conditions, pregnant women, health and social care workers and children ([[public-health/vaccination-programmes]]).

::: example A pneumonia after flu {#ex-flu}
A previously well 32-year-old man has an influenza-like illness. Five days later, after initial improvement, he becomes acutely unwell with high fever, haemoptysis and hypotension. His chest radiograph shows bilateral consolidation with early cavitation. What organism should be suspected and why?
::: solution
The pattern of initial improvement followed by sudden severe deterioration suggests **secondary bacterial pneumonia** after influenza. Influenza damages the respiratory epithelium and impairs neutrophil and macrophage function, giving bacteria access to the lung. Cavitation, haemoptysis and septic shock in a young person point to ***Staphylococcus aureus***, possibly a Panton–Valentine leukocidin-producing strain causing necrotising pneumonia. He needs urgent critical care assessment, blood and sputum cultures and broad antibiotic cover that includes activity against *S. aureus* (and MRSA if risk factors or local prevalence warrant), following local guidance and specialist advice. Public health and microbiology teams may be involved if PVL-producing *S. aureus* is confirmed.
:::
:::

### COVID-19

COVID-19 is caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), which enters cells through the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor. It emerged in late 2019 and the WHO declared a pandemic in March 2020. Infection ranges from no symptoms to a mild upper respiratory illness, to pneumonia with hypoxaemia, to acute respiratory distress syndrome and multi-organ failure. Severe disease is associated with older age, male sex, obesity, diabetes, immunosuppression and other chronic conditions, and also with social deprivation and occupational exposure. A notable feature in the early pandemic was "silent" hypoxaemia: some patients had very low saturations with little breathlessness.

Treatment has been shaped by large randomised trials, especially the UK RECOVERY platform trial. RECOVERY showed in 2020 that **dexamethasone** reduced mortality in hospitalised patients who needed oxygen or mechanical ventilation, but not in those who did not need oxygen; corticosteroids are therefore given to hospitalised patients with hypoxaemia, not to people with mild disease. Later trials showed benefit from blocking interleukin-6 (tocilizumab) in selected patients with hypoxaemia and systemic inflammation. Antiviral treatment (for example nirmatrelvir with ritonavir) is offered to people at highest risk of severe disease early in infection; ritonavir has many important drug interactions. Respiratory support with CPAP or high-flow nasal oxygen, and prone positioning, are discussed in [[respiratory/respiratory-failure]]. Vaccination greatly reduced severe disease and death. Some people have persistent symptoms for months after infection (post-COVID-19 condition, or "long COVID").

### Respiratory syncytial virus

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the commonest cause of bronchiolitis in infants, typically in the first year of life, with coryza, cough, wheeze, crackles and feeding difficulty ([[paediatrics/acute-childhood-illness]]). It also causes significant illness in older adults and people with chronic heart and lung disease. In September 2024 the UK began an RSV vaccination programme for older adults (initially aged 75–79) and for pregnant women, to protect their babies through transferred antibodies.

## Tuberculosis {#sec-tb}

::: definition Tuberculosis {#def-tb}
**Tuberculosis** (TB) is infection with *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, a slow-growing, acid-fast bacillus spread by airborne droplet nuclei from people with infectious pulmonary disease. **TB infection** (formerly "latent TB") is infection controlled by the immune system without symptoms or radiographic disease; it is not infectious. **TB disease** (active TB) causes symptoms and can be transmitted if the lungs or larynx are involved.
:::

According to the WHO, about 10.8 million people fell ill with TB in 2023 and about 1.25 million died, making it again the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. In the UK, most cases occur in people born in countries with a high incidence, and there is a strong association with social deprivation, homelessness, imprisonment and drug and alcohol dependence.

After inhalation, the bacilli are taken up by alveolar macrophages, in which they can survive. In most people a T-cell response, driven by interferon-gamma, contains the infection within **granulomas**: collections of activated macrophages, epithelioid cells and multinucleate giant cells surrounding central **caseous necrosis** ([[pathology/chronic-inflammation-repair]]). The initial lesion and the involved hilar lymph nodes form the **primary (Ghon) complex**. In about 5–10% of infected people with normal immunity, disease develops during their lifetime, about half of it in the first two years. The risk is much higher with HIV infection, immunosuppressive drugs (including anti-TNF agents and long-term corticosteroids), diabetes, chronic kidney disease, silicosis, malnutrition and in young children.

Post-primary pulmonary TB typically affects the apical and posterior segments of the upper lobes, which have a high ventilation–perfusion ratio and therefore a high oxygen tension. It causes cough for more than three weeks, haemoptysis, fever, night sweats and weight loss, and the radiograph shows upper zone consolidation and cavitation. TB can also affect lymph nodes, pleura, bones and spine, the meninges, the kidneys and almost any organ.

Diagnosis rests on microbiology. At least three sputum samples are sent for **smear microscopy** for acid-fast bacilli and for **culture**, which takes several weeks but remains the gold standard and allows full drug-susceptibility testing. A **nucleic acid amplification test**, such as one that also detects rifampicin resistance, gives a rapid result. TB infection is detected by an **interferon-gamma release assay** (IGRA) or a tuberculin skin test, neither of which can distinguish infection from active disease.

Treatment of drug-sensitive TB is a six-month regimen: **rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol** for two months (the intensive phase), followed by **rifampicin and isoniazid** for four months (the continuation phase). Multiple drugs are used together because resistance arises readily to any single drug. Important adverse effects include hepatotoxicity (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide), orange discoloration of urine and tears and potent induction of liver enzymes with many drug interactions, including hormonal contraceptives (rifampicin), peripheral neuropathy (isoniazid, prevented with pyridoxine) and optic neuritis (ethambutol). Adherence is supported by case management and, when needed, directly observed therapy. TB is a notifiable disease in the UK, and contacts are traced and screened. Drug-resistant TB, extrapulmonary disease and TB control programmes are covered in [[infectious-diseases/tuberculosis]].

::: example A cough for six weeks {#ex-tb}
A 34-year-old man who moved to the UK from a country with a high TB incidence three years ago has a six-week cough, night sweats and 6 kg weight loss. He lives in a shared house with five others. His chest radiograph shows a cavity in the right upper lobe. What should happen next?
::: solution
This is **suspected pulmonary TB** until proved otherwise; the cavity suggests he may be highly infectious. If admitted, he should be placed in a single room with respiratory isolation. Three sputum samples (ideally including an early morning sample) are sent for acid-fast smear, culture and a rapid nucleic acid amplification test that also detects rifampicin resistance. He should be offered an HIV test, as for all people with TB, and checked for diabetes. If the results support TB, standard four-drug treatment is started with baseline liver function and visual acuity tests, and the case is notified to the local health protection team, who will trace and screen his household contacts. Differential diagnoses of an upper lobe cavity include lung cancer (especially squamous carcinoma), lung abscess, non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection and granulomatosis with polyangiitis.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not start "a trial of antibiotics" with a fluoroquinolone when TB is possible
Fluoroquinolones such as levofloxacin and moxifloxacin are active against *M. tuberculosis*. Giving one for presumed bacterial pneumonia in a patient who actually has TB can cause a temporary improvement, delay the diagnosis, reduce the yield of sputum culture and select for fluoroquinolone resistance, which removes one of the key drugs for treating resistant TB. When TB is a realistic possibility, obtain sputum samples first and choose a non-quinolone antibiotic if one is needed.
:::

## Prevention {#sec-prevention}

The main measures that prevent respiratory infection at population level are: not smoking; reducing indoor air pollution from solid fuels; vaccination (pneumococcal, influenza, COVID-19, RSV, pertussis, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b and BCG for those at risk of TB, according to the national schedule); good infection prevention in healthcare (hand hygiene, respiratory isolation, ventilation); and early diagnosis and treatment of TB to stop transmission ([[public-health/communicable-disease-control]]).

::: quiz
A 45-year-old man returns from a hotel stay abroad with high fever, confusion, diarrhoea and right lower lobe consolidation. His sodium is 126 mmol/L. Which test would most quickly support the likely diagnosis?
- [ ] Sputum smear for acid-fast bacilli
- [x] Urinary *Legionella* antigen
- [ ] Interferon-gamma release assay
- [ ] Mycoplasma cold agglutinins
::: solution
Travel with a hotel stay (water systems), severe pneumonia with confusion, diarrhoea and hyponatraemia suggest **Legionnaires' disease**. The urinary antigen test gives a rapid answer for *L. pneumophila* serogroup 1, the commonest cause; a negative result does not exclude other serogroups, so culture and PCR of respiratory samples are also sent. Legionnaires' disease is notifiable in the UK, and public health teams investigate the possible source. Treatment needs an antibiotic active against intracellular organisms, such as a macrolide or a fluoroquinolone.
:::
:::

::: history
Robert Koch announced the discovery of the tubercle bacillus in Berlin on 24 March 1882, a date now marked as World TB Day. The pneumococcus was isolated independently by Louis Pasteur and George Sternberg in 1881, and in 1886 Albert Fraenkel established it as the main cause of lobar pneumonia. Streptomycin, discovered in Selman Waksman's laboratory in 1943, was tested in the British Medical Research Council trial of 1948, one of the first randomised controlled trials. That trial and its successors showed that resistance emerged quickly with a single drug, establishing combination therapy as the basis of TB treatment. Legionnaires' disease was recognised after an outbreak among people attending an American Legion convention in Philadelphia in 1976. The RECOVERY trial, launched in the UK in March 2020, reported the mortality benefit of dexamethasone in COVID-19 within about 100 days.
:::

::: summary
- Lung defences include the cough reflex, mucociliary clearance, secreted antimicrobials and alveolar macrophages; most risk factors for pneumonia impair one of these.
- Classify pneumonia by setting (community, hospital, ventilator, aspiration, immunocompromised), because the organisms differ.
- *S. pneumoniae* is the commonest bacterial cause of CAP; atypical organisms (*Mycoplasma*, *Legionella*, *Chlamydia*) need a macrolide, tetracycline or quinolone.
- CURB65 (hospital): 0–1 low, 2 moderate, 3–5 high severity. CRB65 in the community; 2 or more prompts consideration of hospital referral.

- Pleural fluid with pH below 7.2 in pneumonia indicates a complicated effusion that needs drainage.
- Influenza: antigenic drift causes seasonal epidemics, antigenic shift pandemics; neuraminidase inhibitors for high-risk patients; beware secondary *S. aureus* pneumonia.
- COVID-19: dexamethasone reduces mortality in patients who need oxygen.
- TB: granulomatous infection; diagnose with sputum smear, culture and NAAT; treat with 2 months of four drugs then 4 months of two; notify and trace contacts.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise CURB65 {level=1 check="2"}
A 68-year-old woman with pneumonia is alert, with respiratory rate 24/min, blood pressure 118/72 mmHg and urea 8.1 mmol/L. What is her CURB65 score?
::: solution
Confusion 0, urea above 7 (1), respiratory rate below 30 (0), blood pressure normal (0), age 65 or over (1). CURB65 = 2, moderate severity. Hospital assessment is appropriate, with management guided also by her oxygen saturation and clinical picture.
:::
:::

::: exercise Typical and atypical {level=1}
Why does amoxicillin not treat *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*? Name two classes of antibiotic that do.
::: solution
β-lactams such as amoxicillin act on the bacterial cell wall by binding penicillin-binding proteins; *Mycoplasma* has no cell wall. Macrolides (clarithromycin, azithromycin) and tetracyclines (doxycycline) are active, as are fluoroquinolones.
:::
:::

::: exercise Drift or shift? {level=1}
Explain the difference between antigenic drift and antigenic shift, and say which can occur in influenza B.
::: solution
Drift is the gradual accumulation of point mutations in the haemagglutinin and neuraminidase genes, producing new variants each season and requiring yearly vaccine updates; it occurs in influenza A and B. Shift is the sudden appearance of a new H or N subtype, usually by reassortment of genome segments between human and animal influenza A viruses; it can cause pandemics and occurs only in influenza A, because influenza B has no significant animal reservoir.
:::
:::

::: exercise Positive predictive value {level=2 check="round(100*0.08/(0.08+0.027))"}
An IGRA with sensitivity 80% and specificity 97% is used to test the household contacts of a person with smear-positive TB, among whom the prevalence of TB infection is 10%. What is the positive predictive value of a positive test, as a percentage to the nearest whole number?
::: hint
In 1,000 contacts, count the true positives and the false positives.
:::
::: solution
In 1,000 contacts, 100 are infected and 900 are not. True positives: $0.80 \times 100 = 80$. False positives: $0.03 \times 900 = 27$. PPV $= 80/(80 + 27) = 0.748$, or 75%. Compare with about 35% in a low-risk group with a 2% prevalence (the figure in this chapter): the same test is far more informative when used in a group with a high prior probability.
:::
:::

::: exercise Side effects of TB treatment {level=2}
A woman taking standard TB treatment reports tingling in her feet, and a month later blurred vision with difficulty distinguishing red from green. She also asks whether her combined oral contraceptive will still work. Which drugs are responsible, and what advice is appropriate?
::: solution
Tingling in the feet suggests **isoniazid** peripheral neuropathy, caused by pyridoxine deficiency; pyridoxine is given to prevent it, especially to people at risk. Blurred vision and red–green colour loss suggest **ethambutol** optic neuritis; the drug is stopped and she is seen urgently by an ophthalmologist. **Rifampicin** strongly induces liver enzymes and reduces the effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives; she should use a reliable alternative method, such as a copper intrauterine device, and her other medicines should be checked for interactions.
:::
:::

::: exercise Where did it aspirate? {level=2}
A 79-year-old man with Parkinson's disease who has had a fall and lay on the floor overnight develops pneumonia. Which lung segments are most likely to be affected, and why?
::: solution
Lying on his back, aspirated material flows by gravity into the dependent segments: the **posterior segments of the upper lobes** and the **apical (superior) segments of the lower lobes**, more often on the right because the right main bronchus is shorter, wider and more vertical. Standing or sitting, aspiration favours the lower lobes, particularly the right lower lobe. The organisms are usually a mix of oral bacteria, including anaerobes. His Parkinson's disease adds risk through dysphagia; a swallow assessment is part of management.
:::
:::

::: exercise The pneumonia that is not improving {level=3}
A 61-year-old smoker with CURB65 1 pneumonia in the right lower lobe has been treated with amoxicillin for 5 days. He still has a cough and low-grade fever. The repeat radiograph shows persistent right lower lobe shadowing with some volume loss. Give a structured differential and the key investigations.
::: solution
Consider four categories. **Wrong organism or wrong drug**: an atypical organism not covered by amoxicillin, a resistant organism, or TB. **Complication**: parapneumonic effusion or empyema (ultrasound and pleural aspiration if fluid is present), or lung abscess. **Wrong or additional diagnosis**: in a 61-year-old smoker with volume loss, a **tumour obstructing the bronchus** is a serious concern (post-obstructive pneumonia); also pulmonary embolism with infarction, organising pneumonia, or aspiration of a foreign body. **Host factors**: immunosuppression or recurrent aspiration.

Investigations: sputum cultures and acid-fast bacilli, urinary antigens, inflammatory markers, ultrasound of the chest for fluid, and **CT of the chest**, followed by **bronchoscopy** if CT suggests an endobronchial lesion. Persistent lobar shadowing with volume loss in a smoker over 50 should not be attributed to slow resolution without excluding cancer ([[respiratory/lung-cancer-pleura]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise A cluster of pneumonia {level=3}
Over two weeks, five adults are admitted to the same hospital with severe pneumonia. Three of them work in the same office block, which has a rooftop cooling tower. Two have hyponatraemia and one has deranged liver function. What is the likely cause, how is it confirmed, and what public health action follows?
::: solution
This suggests an outbreak of **Legionnaires' disease** from a common environmental source, probably the cooling tower. *Legionella* spreads through aerosols from contaminated water, not from person to person. Confirmation: urinary antigen tests (serogroup 1), and culture or PCR of respiratory samples, which allow typing of the organism so that patient isolates can be matched to environmental samples from the tower. Public health action: notification to the health protection team, an outbreak control team, inspection and sampling of potential sources, disinfection of the cooling tower, case finding among others exposed, and advice to clinicians to test people with pneumonia linked to the area. Patients need antibiotics active against *Legionella* (a macrolide or fluoroquinolone).
:::
:::
