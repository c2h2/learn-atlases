The airways get most of the attention in respiratory medicine, because asthma and COPD are common. This chapter is about the other two components of the lung: the **interstitium**, the thin supporting tissue of the alveolar walls, and the **pulmonary circulation**. Diseases of the interstitium thicken and scar the alveolar walls, stiffening the lung and blocking gas transfer. Diseases of the pulmonary vessels obstruct or narrow the circulation, wasting ventilation and loading the right ventricle.

The two groups share a physiological signature: breathlessness on exertion, a reduced gas transfer, hypoxaemia that worsens with exercise, and, in advanced disease, pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure. They also share a diagnostic trap, because early disease may leave the chest radiograph and spirometry looking normal. The chapter covers the interstitial lung diseases, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and sarcoidosis, then pulmonary embolism, then pulmonary hypertension.

## Interstitial lung disease {#sec-ild}

::: definition Interstitial lung disease {#def-ild}
**Interstitial lung disease** (ILD, also called diffuse parenchymal lung disease) is a group of more than 200 conditions that cause inflammation and/or fibrosis of the alveolar walls and surrounding tissue. They are grouped as:

- **ILD with a known cause or association**: connective tissue diseases (systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory myositis), drugs, occupational and environmental exposures (asbestos, silica, organic antigens in hypersensitivity pneumonitis) and radiation.
- **Granulomatous ILD**, principally sarcoidosis.
- **Idiopathic interstitial pneumonias**, of which **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis** is the commonest and most important.
- **Rare forms**, such as lymphangioleiomyomatosis and pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis.
:::

The physiology follows from [[respiratory/mechanics-breathing]] and [[respiratory/gas-exchange]]. Fibrosis makes the lung stiff, so lung volumes fall (a restrictive pattern with a normal or high FEV₁/FVC ratio) and breathing becomes rapid and shallow. Thickening of the alveolar walls and loss of capillaries reduce gas transfer (TLCO). Oxygen saturation may be normal at rest but falls on exertion, because transit time through the capillaries shortens. Typical findings are a dry cough, progressive breathlessness on exertion, fine "velcro" crackles at the bases and, in some conditions, finger clubbing.

**High-resolution CT** (HRCT) is the key investigation, because the pattern and distribution of abnormality often point to a specific disease. Diagnosis is made by a **multidisciplinary team** of respiratory physicians, radiologists and pathologists, combining the clinical history (exposures, drugs, symptoms of connective tissue disease), autoantibodies, HRCT and, when needed, bronchoalveolar lavage or lung biopsy.

### Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

::: definition Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis {#def-ipf}
**Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis** (IPF) is a chronic, progressive fibrosing lung disease of unknown cause, occurring mainly in people over 60, with the radiological and/or histological pattern of **usual interstitial pneumonia** (UIP). On HRCT, UIP shows reticulation and **honeycombing** (clustered cystic airspaces) that are **subpleural and basal**, with traction bronchiectasis. The diagnosis requires exclusion of known causes of ILD.
:::

IPF is thought to result from repeated injury to an ageing alveolar epithelium followed by abnormal repair, in which fibroblasts and myofibroblasts lay down excess collagen instead of restoring normal structure. Risk factors include older age, male sex, cigarette smoking, some occupational dusts and genetic variants (including in the *MUC5B* promoter and in telomere-related genes). It is more common in men.

The course is progressive but variable. Without treatment, median survival from diagnosis has been reported as about three to five years, worse than many cancers. Some patients have **acute exacerbations**, sudden deterioration with new bilateral ground-glass change, which carry a high mortality.

Treatment has changed considerably since 2014. Two **antifibrotic drugs**, **pirfenidone** and **nintedanib** (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor), each slow the decline in forced vital capacity by roughly half in clinical trials. Neither restores lost lung function or cures the disease. NICE technology appraisals set out which patients are eligible on the NHS, based on FVC as a percentage of predicted. In October 2025 the US Food and Drug Administration approved a third drug, **nerandomilast** (a preferential phosphodiesterase 4B inhibitor), on the basis of the FIBRONEER-IPF trial; its availability elsewhere depends on national regulators and appraisals. Supportive care is equally important: pulmonary rehabilitation, oxygen for hypoxaemia, treatment of cough and reflux, smoking cessation, vaccination, palliative care for breathlessness, and lung transplantation for a small number of suitable patients.

::: warning Do not treat IPF with immunosuppression
Because many interstitial diseases respond to corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, it was once standard to treat IPF the same way. The PANTHER-IPF trial (published 2012) stopped its triple-therapy arm (prednisolone, azathioprine and N-acetylcysteine) early because patients receiving it had more deaths and hospital admissions than those on placebo. IPF is a disorder of fibrosis rather than inflammation. This is why an accurate multidisciplinary diagnosis matters: the same HRCT appearance may be IPF, fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis or connective tissue disease, and the treatments differ.
:::

::: example The crackles that did not clear {#ex-ipf}
A 72-year-old retired joiner has had a dry cough and slowly worsening breathlessness for 18 months. He has finger clubbing and fine end-inspiratory crackles at both bases. He has no joint, skin or muscle symptoms, takes no regular drugs, keeps no birds and has not had significant exposure to asbestos. Spirometry: FEV₁ 2.2 L, FVC 2.6 L (68% predicted), FEV₁/FVC 0.85; TLCO 45% of predicted. His saturation falls from 95% to 86% during a six-minute walk. HRCT shows subpleural, basal-predominant reticulation with honeycombing and traction bronchiectasis. What is the likely diagnosis and how is it confirmed and managed?
::: solution
Restrictive spirometry (high ratio, reduced FVC), a markedly reduced TLCO and exertional desaturation indicate fibrotic ILD. With clubbing, basal crackles, age and a definite UIP pattern on HRCT, and no evidence of a known cause, the likely diagnosis is **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis**. The diagnosis is made by the ILD multidisciplinary team; with a typical UIP pattern and no alternative cause, a lung biopsy is usually not needed. Autoantibodies are checked to look for an unrecognised connective tissue disease, and his exposures are reviewed carefully.

Management: discuss the diagnosis and prognosis honestly; consider an antifibrotic, as his FVC falls within the range for which NICE guidance supports treatment; offer pulmonary rehabilitation; assess for ambulatory oxygen given his exertional desaturation; vaccinate; and plan for symptom control and advance care planning. Lung transplantation is unlikely to be suitable at 72, but this is assessed individually.
:::
:::

### Progressive pulmonary fibrosis

Many ILDs other than IPF can also become progressively fibrotic, including fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, connective tissue disease-associated ILD and unclassifiable ILD. An international guideline (ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT, 2022) defined **progressive pulmonary fibrosis** in a person with ILD other than IPF as at least two of the following within the past year, without another explanation: worsening symptoms; physiological progression (an absolute fall in FVC of 5% predicted or more, or in TLCO of 10% predicted or more); and radiological progression. Nintedanib slows FVC decline in this group too.

### Hypersensitivity pneumonitis

**Hypersensitivity pneumonitis** is an immune reaction in the small airways and alveoli to an inhaled antigen, typically organic: bird proteins (from pigeons, budgerigars, feather duvets), moulds (in damp homes, hay, compost, hot tubs) and some chemicals. It is classified as **non-fibrotic** or **fibrotic**. Non-fibrotic disease can cause recurrent episodes of fever, cough and breathlessness several hours after exposure. Fibrotic disease develops insidiously and can resemble IPF. Clues include an exposure history, mid- and upper-zone predominance on HRCT with mosaic attenuation and air trapping, and a lymphocytosis in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. The most important treatment is **identifying and removing the antigen**; corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants are used in some patients.

### Sarcoidosis

::: definition Sarcoidosis {#def-sarcoid}
**Sarcoidosis** is a multisystem disease of unknown cause characterised by **non-caseating granulomas** in affected organs. It most often involves the lungs and intrathoracic lymph nodes, but can affect the skin, eyes, heart, nervous system, liver, kidneys and bones. The diagnosis requires a compatible clinical picture, evidence of non-caseating granulomas (except in classical presentations such as Löfgren syndrome) and exclusion of other causes of granulomas, especially tuberculosis and fungal infection.
:::

Sarcoidosis typically presents between 20 and 50 years of age. It is more common and often more severe in people of African-Caribbean origin, and common in northern Europeans. Many people are found incidentally to have **bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy** on a chest radiograph. Chest radiograph appearances are described by the Scadding stages: 0 normal; I hilar lymphadenopathy only; II lymphadenopathy with pulmonary infiltrates; III infiltrates without lymphadenopathy; IV fibrosis.

**Löfgren syndrome** is an acute presentation with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, **erythema nodosum** (tender red nodules on the shins), arthritis or periarticular inflammation (especially of the ankles) and fever. It usually resolves spontaneously within months and has a good prognosis.

Extrapulmonary sarcoidosis matters because some organ involvement is dangerous:

- **Eyes**: anterior or posterior uveitis; all patients need eye assessment.
- **Heart**: granulomas in the myocardium can cause heart block, ventricular arrhythmias, sudden death and heart failure. Palpitations, syncope or an abnormal ECG need investigation with cardiac MRI or PET.
- **Calcium**: macrophages in granulomas express 1α-hydroxylase, converting vitamin D to its active form, which can cause hypercalciuria, hypercalcaemia and kidney stones.
- **Skin**: erythema nodosum, and lupus pernio (violaceous plaques on the nose and cheeks) in chronic disease.
- **Nervous system**: cranial nerve palsies (especially facial), meningitis, pituitary involvement.

Serum angiotensin-converting enzyme is often raised, but it is neither sensitive nor specific enough to diagnose sarcoidosis. Tissue is obtained from the most accessible site, often by **endobronchial ultrasound-guided needle aspiration** of mediastinal nodes. Many patients need no treatment, because the disease often resolves. Corticosteroids are used for organ-threatening disease (heart, eyes, nervous system, hypercalcaemia) and for progressive pulmonary disease with symptoms, with methotrexate and other agents as steroid-sparing options.

::: example Painful shins and a shadow {#ex-sarcoid}
A 29-year-old woman has a three-week history of painful, red, tender nodules on both shins, swollen painful ankles and a low-grade fever. She has a mild dry cough. The chest radiograph shows bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy. Her calcium is normal and an ECG is normal. What is the likely diagnosis, and does she need a biopsy?
::: solution
The combination of erythema nodosum, ankle periarthritis, fever and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy is **Löfgren syndrome**, an acute form of sarcoidosis. This presentation is so characteristic that a biopsy is usually not needed if the picture is typical and other causes (such as tuberculosis, lymphoma or streptococcal infection causing erythema nodosum) are considered unlikely. Treatment is symptomatic with NSAIDs and rest. She should have an eye examination and lung function tests as a baseline, and follow-up to confirm resolution, which usually occurs within six months to two years. Atypical features, such as persistent symptoms, weight loss or night sweats, should prompt tissue sampling.
:::
:::

### Occupational and drug-induced ILD

- **Asbestosis** is fibrosis caused by inhaled asbestos fibres, usually after heavy exposure decades earlier in shipbuilding, construction, insulation or pipe-lagging. It is basal and resembles IPF. **Pleural plaques** (benign, calcified thickening of the parietal pleura) mark exposure. Asbestos also causes mesothelioma and increases lung cancer risk, especially with smoking ([[respiratory/lung-cancer-pleura]]).
- **Silicosis** follows inhalation of crystalline silica in quarrying, sandblasting, stonemasonry and the cutting of engineered stone kitchen worktops, which has caused severe disease in young workers in several countries. It affects the upper zones, causes nodules and "eggshell" calcification of hilar nodes, and increases the risk of tuberculosis.
- **Coal workers' pneumoconiosis** is caused by coal dust.
- **Drugs** that can cause ILD include amiodarone, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin, bleomycin and other chemotherapy agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (pneumonitis). A drug history is essential, and stopping the drug is the first step ([[pharmacology/adverse-reactions]]).

In the UK, some occupational lung diseases qualify for Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit, and a thorough occupational history therefore has consequences for the patient beyond diagnosis ([[public-health/environmental-occupational]]).

## Pulmonary embolism {#sec-pe}

::: definition Venous thromboembolism and pulmonary embolism {#def-pe}
**Venous thromboembolism** (VTE) includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). A **pulmonary embolism** is obstruction of the pulmonary arterial tree, almost always by thrombus that has formed in the deep veins of the legs or pelvis and travelled through the right heart.
:::

::: theorem Virchow's triad {#thm-virchow}
Thrombosis results from a combination of three factors: **stasis** of blood flow, **injury to the vessel wall** (endothelial damage) and **hypercoagulability** of the blood.
:::

Risk factors map onto the triad: immobility, long journeys, surgery (especially orthopaedic, pelvic and abdominal), hospital admission, trauma, active cancer and its treatment, pregnancy and the postpartum period, oestrogen-containing contraception and hormone replacement, obesity, previous VTE and inherited or acquired thrombophilias such as factor V Leiden and antiphospholipid syndrome ([[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]], [[pathology/haemodynamics]]).

### What happens in the lung and heart

An embolus blocks blood flow to part of the lung, creating **alveolar dead space**: ventilation without perfusion. Blood is diverted to the rest of the lung, which is now overperfused relative to its ventilation, causing V/Q mismatch and hypoxaemia. Patients hyperventilate, so their $P_{aCO_2}$ is usually low. If the embolus reaches the periphery, it can cause **pulmonary infarction**, with pleuritic pain and haemoptysis.

The haemodynamic effect depends on how much of the circulation is blocked and on the patient's cardiorespiratory reserve. A large embolus suddenly increases the afterload of the right ventricle, which is thin-walled and not built for high pressures. The right ventricle dilates, its wall tension and oxygen demand rise, and the interventricular septum bows into the left ventricle, reducing left ventricular filling and cardiac output. This can lead to obstructive shock and cardiac arrest.

### Presentation and diagnosis

Typical symptoms are sudden breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, syncope and features of DVT. Signs include tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypoxaemia and, in a large PE, hypotension, a raised jugular venous pressure and a right ventricular heave. Many presentations are subtle, especially in older people.

The diagnostic strategy depends on the clinical probability, assessed with a validated score such as the two-level **Wells score** for PE, used in NICE guideline NG158.

::: definition Two-level Wells score for PE {#def-wells}
Points: clinical signs and symptoms of DVT (3); an alternative diagnosis less likely than PE (3); heart rate above 100/min (1.5); immobilisation for 3 days or more, or surgery in the previous 4 weeks (1.5); previous DVT or PE (1.5); haemoptysis (1); active cancer, treated within the last 6 months or palliative (1). A score **above 4** means PE is **likely**; 4 or less means PE is **unlikely**.
:::

In NG158, in outline: if PE is **likely**, arrange a CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA), giving interim anticoagulation if it cannot be done immediately. If PE is **unlikely**, perform a **D-dimer** test; a negative result makes PE very unlikely and other diagnoses should be considered, while a positive result leads to CTPA. NG158 allows an age-adjusted D-dimer threshold to be considered for people over 50. D-dimer, a breakdown product of cross-linked fibrin, is sensitive but not specific: it is raised by infection, cancer, pregnancy, surgery and older age, so it is useful only to rule out PE in people with low clinical probability. A ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) scan is an alternative to CTPA, for example in renal impairment, contrast allergy or, often, pregnancy.

Other tests are supportive. The ECG most often shows sinus tachycardia; the classic S1Q3T3 pattern is uncommon. The chest radiograph is often normal and helps exclude other diagnoses. In confirmed PE, troponin, natriuretic peptides and imaging of the right ventricle (on CT or echocardiography) help assess severity.

::: example Scoring a breathless woman {#ex-wells}
A 38-year-old woman is breathless and has pleuritic chest pain 10 days after an arthroscopy, after which she was immobile for a week. Heart rate 108/min, $S_pO_2$ 94%, no signs of DVT. She takes a combined oral contraceptive. There are no features of another diagnosis on examination, ECG or chest radiograph. Calculate her Wells score and outline the next step.
::: solution
Signs of DVT: no (0). Alternative diagnosis less likely than PE: yes (3). Heart rate above 100: yes (1.5). Surgery within 4 weeks/immobilisation: yes (1.5). Previous VTE: no. Haemoptysis: no. Cancer: no. Total **6: PE likely**.

She should have a CTPA. If it cannot be done promptly, interim anticoagulation is started while waiting, provided there is no contraindication. A D-dimer test is not useful here because a negative result would not reliably exclude PE in a person with a "likely" score. Her oral contraceptive is an additional (unscored) risk factor and would be stopped if PE is confirmed, with alternative contraception arranged, because anticoagulants such as DOACs and warfarin are harmful to a fetus.
:::
:::

### Severity and treatment

The European Society of Cardiology guideline (2019) stratifies PE by the risk of early death. **High-risk** PE causes haemodynamic instability (cardiac arrest, obstructive shock or persistent hypotension). **Intermediate-risk** PE has no instability but has adverse clinical features (for example a raised simplified PE Severity Index), with or without right ventricular dysfunction and a raised troponin. **Low-risk** PE has none of these, and selected patients can be managed as outpatients.

Principles of treatment:

- **Anticoagulation** is the treatment for almost all PE. It prevents clot extension and recurrence while the body's fibrinolytic system dissolves the existing clot. NICE (NG158) recommends a direct oral anticoagulant, **apixaban or rivaroxaban**, as first choice for most adults, with alternatives such as low-molecular-weight heparin followed by dabigatran, edoxaban or a vitamin K antagonist. DOACs are avoided in some situations, including antiphospholipid syndrome (especially with triple-positive antibodies), pregnancy and severe renal impairment.
- **Thrombolysis** (for example with alteplase) is given for high-risk PE with haemodynamic instability, unless contraindicated. It dissolves clot quickly and relieves the right ventricle, at the cost of a significant risk of major bleeding, including intracranial haemorrhage. Surgical or catheter-directed embolectomy are alternatives when thrombolysis is contraindicated or fails.
- **Duration**: at least 3 months. For PE provoked by a major transient risk factor such as surgery, treatment usually stops at 3 months. For unprovoked PE, longer-term anticoagulation is considered, weighing the risk of recurrence against the risk of bleeding.

Some people have persistent breathlessness after PE. A small proportion develop **chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension** (CTEPH), in which unresolved, organised thrombus obstructs the pulmonary arteries. It should be looked for in people with continuing breathlessness after three months of anticoagulation.

::: example A collapse in the ward corridor {#ex-pe-shock}
A 63-year-old man, 5 days after a colectomy for cancer, collapses while walking. He is pale and clammy, blood pressure 78/50 mmHg, heart rate 128/min, $S_pO_2$ 84% on high-flow oxygen, with distended neck veins. A bedside echocardiogram shows a dilated, hypokinetic right ventricle and a small, underfilled left ventricle. What is the likely diagnosis, and what are the treatment considerations?
::: solution
Sudden collapse, hypoxaemia, obstructive shock and an acutely dilated right ventricle after surgery strongly suggest **high-risk (massive) pulmonary embolism**. In an unstable patient in whom CTPA cannot be safely performed, bedside echocardiographic evidence of right ventricular overload is enough to justify reperfusion treatment.

The standard reperfusion treatment is systemic thrombolysis. But he had major abdominal surgery five days ago, which is a significant contraindication because of the risk of serious bleeding. The decision must be made quickly by senior clinicians, weighing the immediate risk of death from PE against bleeding. Options include surgical embolectomy or catheter-directed treatment where available. Meanwhile, he needs anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin (which can be stopped or reversed quickly), oxygen, careful fluid management (too much fluid can worsen right ventricular dilatation) and vasopressor support, with critical care involvement.
:::
:::

## Pulmonary hypertension {#sec-ph}

::: definition Pulmonary hypertension {#def-ph}
**Pulmonary hypertension** (PH) is defined, in the 2022 ESC/ERS guideline, as a **mean pulmonary artery pressure above 20 mmHg** at rest, measured by right heart catheterisation. **Pre-capillary** PH also has a pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP, an estimate of left atrial pressure) of 15 mmHg or less and a pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) above 2 Wood units. **Post-capillary** PH has a PAWP above 15 mmHg, reflecting raised pressure from the left heart.
:::

The pulmonary circulation is normally a low-pressure, low-resistance system that can accept the whole cardiac output with only a small rise in pressure, because unused vessels are recruited and distended. Pulmonary vascular resistance is calculated as:

$$
\text{PVR} = \frac{\text{mPAP} - \text{PAWP}}{\text{CO}}
$$ {#eq-pvr}

with pressures in mmHg and cardiac output in L/min, giving Wood units. (Multiply by 80 for dyn·s·cm⁻⁵.)

::: definition Clinical classification of PH {#def-ph-groups}
1. **Pulmonary arterial hypertension** (PAH): idiopathic; heritable (for example *BMPR2* variants); drug- and toxin-induced; associated with connective tissue disease (especially systemic sclerosis), HIV, portal hypertension or congenital heart disease.
2. **PH associated with left heart disease**: the commonest cause overall (heart failure, mitral and aortic valve disease).
3. **PH associated with lung disease and/or hypoxia**: COPD, ILD, sleep-disordered breathing, high altitude.
4. **PH associated with pulmonary artery obstructions**: mainly **chronic thromboembolic PH** (CTEPH).
5. **PH with unclear and/or multifactorial mechanisms**: for example haematological disorders, sarcoidosis and metabolic disorders.
:::

In PAH, the small pulmonary arteries are remodelled: smooth muscle and endothelial cells proliferate, the vessel walls thicken, and plexiform lesions form. Three signalling pathways are disturbed: too little nitric oxide and prostacyclin (vasodilators and inhibitors of proliferation) and too much endothelin (a vasoconstrictor and mitogen). These pathways are the targets of current drugs.

The presenting symptoms of PH are nonspecific: breathlessness on exertion, fatigue, exertional chest pain and syncope, and later ankle swelling. This leads to a typical delay of a year or more in diagnosis. Signs include a loud pulmonary component of the second heart sound, a right ventricular heave, tricuspid regurgitation, a raised JVP and oedema. **Echocardiography** estimates the probability of PH from the tricuspid regurgitant jet velocity and signs of right ventricular strain; **right heart catheterisation** confirms the diagnosis and measures the haemodynamics. Investigations also look for a cause: lung function, HRCT, V/Q scanning (the best screening test for CTEPH), sleep studies, autoimmune serology and HIV and liver tests.

Treatment depends on the group. In groups 2 and 3, the underlying heart or lung disease is treated, and oxygen corrects hypoxic vasoconstriction. CTEPH is treated with lifelong anticoagulation and, where possible, **pulmonary endarterectomy**, a potentially curative operation; balloon pulmonary angioplasty and the soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator riociguat are options for inoperable disease. PAH is managed in specialist centres (in the UK, designated national PH centres) with:

- **Endothelin receptor antagonists** (ambrisentan, bosentan, macitentan).
- **Nitric oxide pathway drugs**: phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) and riociguat.
- **Prostacyclin pathway drugs**: epoprostenol (continuous intravenous infusion), iloprost, treprostinil and selexipag.
- **Sotatercept**, an activin signalling inhibitor approved in 2024 for adults with PAH, which targets the imbalance in growth signalling.
- **Calcium channel blockers**, in high doses, only for the small minority of patients with idiopathic PAH who show a positive response to acute vasoreactivity testing.

Combination therapy is now common. Lung transplantation is considered for patients who deteriorate despite maximal treatment.

::: example Calculating vascular resistance {#ex-pvr}
A 52-year-old woman with systemic sclerosis has right heart catheterisation: mean pulmonary artery pressure 38 mmHg, PAWP 10 mmHg, cardiac output 3.5 L/min. Calculate her PVR and classify her PH.
::: solution
$\text{PVR} = (38 - 10)/3.5 = 28/3.5 = 8$ Wood units.

Her mPAP is above 20 mmHg, her PAWP is 15 mmHg or less and her PVR is above 2 Wood units, so she has **pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension**. In systemic sclerosis without significant ILD, this is most likely **pulmonary arterial hypertension** (group 1). Her cardiac output is low, a marker of right ventricular dysfunction and a poor prognostic sign. She should be managed in a specialist PH centre, usually with combination therapy.
:::
:::

::: history
Rudolf Virchow, working in Berlin in the 1840s and 1850s, coined the terms "thrombosis" and "embolism" and showed that clots in the lungs came from the veins. The three factors now called Virchow's triad were assembled from his work and that of later authors. In 1960 David Barritt and Simon Jordan published one of the first randomised controlled trials of anticoagulation, showing that heparin followed by an oral anticoagulant reduced death from PE. Sarcoidosis was described by Jonathan Hutchinson (skin lesions, 1877), Caesar Boeck (who named it sarcoid, 1899) and Jörgen Schaumann, and Sven Löfgren described the acute syndrome with erythema nodosum and hilar lymphadenopathy in 1953. The National Institutes of Health registry of primary pulmonary hypertension, begun in 1981, documented its poor prognosis and led to the first targeted drug, intravenous epoprostenol, approved in 1995.
:::

::: exercise Why is the PaCO2 low? {level=2}
A patient with an acute PE has $P_{aO_2}$ 8.2 kPa and $P_{aCO_2}$ 3.9 kPa breathing air. Explain both values, and calculate the A–a gradient (use $P_{IO_2}$ 20.0 kPa, $R$ 0.8).
::: solution
The low $P_{aO_2}$ results mainly from V/Q mismatch: blood diverted from the occluded vessels overperfuses the remaining lung relative to its ventilation; a fall in mixed venous saturation from reduced cardiac output adds to it. The low $P_{aCO_2}$ reflects hyperventilation, driven by stimulation of lung receptors, hypoxaemia and anxiety, which more than compensates for the dead space created by the embolus. $P_{AO_2} = 20.0 - 3.9/0.8 = 15.1$ kPa; gradient $= 15.1 - 8.2 = 6.9$ kPa, clearly raised, consistent with lung disease rather than hypoventilation.
:::

::: exercise Hypercalcaemia {level=2}
A 42-year-old man with known pulmonary sarcoidosis presents with thirst, polyuria and confusion. His corrected calcium is 3.2 mmol/L and his parathyroid hormone is suppressed. Explain the mechanism and the principles of management.
::: solution
Activated macrophages in sarcoid granulomas express 1α-hydroxylase, which converts 25-hydroxyvitamin D to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol) independently of parathyroid hormone. Calcitriol increases intestinal calcium absorption and bone resorption, causing hypercalcaemia, which suppresses PTH. Principles: intravenous fluids for dehydration, avoid vitamin D and calcium supplements and reduce sun exposure, and corticosteroids, which suppress granuloma activity and calcitriol production. Look for kidney stones and renal impairment ([[endocrine/bone-calcium]]).
:::

::: exercise Why not a D-dimer? {level=2}
Explain why a D-dimer test is useful to exclude PE in a 25-year-old with a low Wells score but much less useful in an 85-year-old inpatient recovering from pneumonia.
::: solution
D-dimer has high sensitivity but low specificity. A negative result effectively rules out PE when the pre-test probability is low, as in a young outpatient with a low score. D-dimer rises with age, infection, inflammation, cancer and hospital admission, so in an older inpatient with pneumonia it is likely to be positive whatever the cause; a positive result tells you little, and most such patients will still need imaging. Age-adjusted thresholds improve specificity somewhat in older people. If clinical probability is high, D-dimer should not be used to exclude PE at all.
:::

::: exercise A breathless woman with Raynaud's {level=3}
A 56-year-old woman with limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis has increasing breathlessness over a year. Spirometry and lung volumes are normal, but TLCO is 42% of predicted. Her six-minute walk distance has fallen and she desaturates to 88%. Her NT-proBNP is raised. Discuss the differential diagnosis, the investigations, and why the order of tests matters.
::: hint
Systemic sclerosis can cause both ILD and PAH, and the lung function pattern may help you choose between them.
:::
::: solution
The main possibilities are **pulmonary arterial hypertension** and **ILD**, both common in systemic sclerosis, and they can coexist. Normal lung volumes with an isolated, marked fall in TLCO and a raised natriuretic peptide favour PAH. Early ILD can also reduce TLCO before volumes fall, and left heart disease (diastolic dysfunction, myocardial involvement) can cause post-capillary PH.

Investigations: echocardiography to estimate the probability of PH and assess both ventricles; HRCT to look for ILD; V/Q scan to exclude CTEPH; and **right heart catheterisation** to confirm PH, measure PAWP and calculate PVR, which separates pre-capillary from post-capillary PH. The order matters because treatment differs completely: PAH-specific drugs help group 1 PH, may worsen oxygenation in group 3 PH (by releasing hypoxic vasoconstriction in poorly ventilated lung), and are not appropriate for group 2 PH. A specialist PH centre should be involved.
:::

::: exercise A trial of steroids? {level=3}
A 69-year-old man with a new diagnosis of fibrotic ILD is started on high-dose prednisolone by his GP "to see if it helps". HRCT shows a definite UIP pattern, and he has no exposures or features of connective tissue disease. Explain why this is a concern and what should happen instead.
::: solution
With a definite UIP pattern and no identified cause, the likely diagnosis is IPF. In IPF, corticosteroids (and other immunosuppression) do not improve outcomes and, combined with azathioprine and N-acetylcysteine in the PANTHER trial, increased death and hospitalisation. Prednisolone also causes hyperglycaemia, osteoporosis, myopathy, infection risk and weight gain. He should be referred to an ILD multidisciplinary team to confirm the diagnosis (and look again for hypersensitivity pneumonitis or connective tissue disease, which might respond to immunosuppression), and, if IPF is confirmed, be considered for antifibrotic therapy and supportive care. The steroids should be reviewed and tapered under supervision rather than stopped abruptly, because of the risk of adrenal suppression.
:::
