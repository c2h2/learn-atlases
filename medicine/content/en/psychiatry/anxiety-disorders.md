Anxiety is a normal and useful emotion. It prepares the body to deal with danger, sharpens attention and motivates action. An anxiety disorder is present when anxiety is out of proportion to the real threat, persists, and causes distress or gets in the way of everyday life. Taken together, anxiety and stress-related disorders are among the most common mental disorders. They are also among the most treatable, which makes recognising them worthwhile.

This chapter explains how normal fear becomes a disorder, describes the main anxiety, obsessive–compulsive and trauma-related disorders, and sets out the principles of treatment. It also covers the physical conditions that can look like anxiety, because missing them is a real danger.

## From normal fear to disorder

::: definition The threat response {#def-threat}
The **threat response** is the coordinated reaction of brain and body to perceived danger. The **amygdala** detects threat and, through the hypothalamus and brainstem, activates the **sympathetic nervous system** and the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis**. The result is a fast heart rate, rapid breathing, muscle tension, sweating, a dry mouth and heightened vigilance: the "fight or flight" response. The **prefrontal cortex** normally appraises the threat and damps the response once the danger has passed.
:::

Every symptom of a panic attack is part of this response. Rapid breathing lowers carbon dioxide in the blood, which causes tingling in the fingers and around the mouth, light-headedness and chest tightness. Understanding this helps people reinterpret frightening sensations as harmless.

What turns fear into a disorder? Vulnerability plays a part: anxiety disorders run in families, partly through genes and partly through learning, and childhood adversity raises risk. But the key to treatment is what **maintains** anxiety once it has started.

::: theorem Avoidance maintains anxiety {#thm-avoidance}
Anxiety disorders are kept going by the behaviours people use to protect themselves from the feared outcome. **Avoidance** (staying away from the feared situation), **escape** (leaving early) and **safety behaviours** (actions taken to prevent catastrophe, such as holding onto a rail or carrying medication "just in case") relieve anxiety in the short term but prevent the person from learning that the feared outcome does not happen or can be coped with. Effective treatment therefore involves facing the feared situation without these behaviours.
:::

::: proof
The explanation comes from learning theory. Fear can be learned by association, as when a panic attack on a bus makes buses frightening (classical conditioning). Avoiding the bus then brings immediate relief, and relief rewards avoidance, so the avoidance becomes stronger (negative reinforcement). Because the person never stays on the bus long enough for anxiety to fall naturally, the belief "I would have collapsed if I had not got off" is never tested. Safety behaviours work in the same way: if someone grips a rail during every attack and never faints, they credit the rail rather than concluding that panic does not cause fainting. Exposure therapy breaks the cycle. Repeated, prolonged contact with the feared situation, without escape or safety behaviours, allows new learning: the situation is safe, and anxiety rises, peaks and falls on its own.
:::

## The main disorders

::: definition Anxiety and fear-related disorders {#def-anx-disorders}
- **Generalised anxiety disorder** (GAD): excessive, hard-to-control worry about many everyday matters (health, money, work, family) on most days for at least several months (six months in DSM-5-TR), with symptoms such as restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension and disturbed sleep.
- **Panic disorder**: recurrent, unexpected **panic attacks** (sudden surges of intense fear peaking within minutes, with physical symptoms) followed by persistent worry about further attacks or a change in behaviour to avoid them.
- **Agoraphobia**: fear and avoidance of situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable if panic occurred, such as crowds, public transport, queues or being away from home alone.
- **Social anxiety disorder**: marked fear of social or performance situations because of worry about being judged negatively, embarrassed or humiliated.
- **Specific phobia**: marked fear of a particular object or situation, such as heights, spiders, injections or flying.
:::

In ICD-11, obsessive–compulsive disorder and the stress-related disorders are separate groups, but they share mechanisms and treatments with the anxiety disorders and are covered here.

**Obsessive–compulsive disorder** (OCD) is defined by obsessions, compulsions or, usually, both (see [[psychiatry/psychiatric-assessment]] for the definitions). Common themes are contamination and washing, harm and checking, symmetry and ordering, and unwanted sexual, religious or violent thoughts. The compulsions are time-consuming (often more than an hour a day) or cause distress or impairment. Insight varies; most people recognise their fears as excessive. Related disorders include **body dysmorphic disorder**, a preoccupation with perceived defects in appearance, and **hoarding disorder**.

**Stress-related disorders** follow an identifiable event:

- An **acute stress reaction** is a normal, transient response to an exceptionally stressful event, settling within days.
- **Post-traumatic stress disorder** (PTSD) follows exposure to an extremely threatening or horrifying event. ICD-11 requires three groups of symptoms: **re-experiencing** the event in the present (vivid intrusive memories, flashbacks or nightmares, with fear or horror); **avoidance** of thoughts, memories, activities or situations that are reminders; and a persistent sense of **current threat** (hypervigilance or an enhanced startle reaction). Symptoms last at least several weeks (more than one month in DSM-5-TR) and cause significant impairment.
- **Complex PTSD** (an ICD-11 diagnosis) is PTSD together with persistent problems with emotional regulation, a negative view of oneself (shame, guilt, worthlessness) and difficulty sustaining relationships. It usually follows prolonged or repeated trauma from which escape was difficult, such as childhood abuse, torture or domestic violence.
- **Adjustment disorder** is a reaction to a stressor, such as divorce or job loss, with preoccupation and failure to adapt, usually resolving within six months.

## Physical conditions that mimic anxiety

Anxiety symptoms are mostly physical, so physical conditions can look like anxiety disorders. A new presentation, particularly in someone over about 40 or without a previous history, needs a physical assessment.

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| Hyperthyroidism | Weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, tachycardia, goitre |
| Arrhythmia (for example supraventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation) | Sudden onset and offset of palpitations, irregular pulse, ECG changes |
| Hypoglycaemia | Episodes related to fasting or insulin and sulfonylureas, relieved by food |
| Asthma, pulmonary embolism | Breathlessness with wheeze, hypoxia, risk factors for thrombosis |
| Phaeochromocytoma (rare) | Paroxysms of headache, sweating, palpitations and hypertension |
| Substances | Caffeine, stimulants, cannabis; withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines |
| Medicines | Salbutamol, levothyroxine excess, corticosteroids, some decongestants |

Anxiety is also common in physical illness, and a person with a known anxiety disorder can still develop a cardiac or respiratory emergency.

::: example Chest pain at night {#ex-panic-cardio}
Ms Priya Desai, 29, attends the emergency department at 2 a.m. with sudden chest tightness, palpitations, tingling fingers and a feeling that she is about to die. The symptoms peaked within ten minutes and are now settling. This is her fourth attendance in two months. Her ECG, troponin and examination are normal. How should she be managed?

::: solution
**Exclude dangerous causes first, proportionately.** In a young woman with normal ECG, normal troponin, normal oxygen saturation and no risk factors, an acute coronary syndrome and pulmonary embolism are very unlikely; the assessment should still consider them and check for an arrhythmia (an ECG during symptoms is valuable) and hyperthyroidism.

**Recognise the pattern.** Sudden surges of intense fear with physical symptoms, peaking within minutes, recurring unexpectedly, with repeated help-seeking, suggest **panic disorder**. Ask about worry between attacks and avoidance: she now avoids the gym and the underground because "my heart might give out".

**Explain clearly.** Describe the threat response and how rapid breathing causes the tingling and chest tightness. Confirm that tests show her heart is healthy, and that the symptoms are real but not dangerous.

**Plan.** Advise against repeated investigations unless new features appear. Refer for CBT for panic disorder through primary care or NHS Talking Therapies. Advise reducing caffeine. A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) is an option if therapy is not available or preferred. Benzodiazepines are not recommended for panic disorder, because they are associated with a worse long-term outcome.
:::
:::

## Assessment and measurement

The history explores what the person fears, what happens in their body and mind, what they avoid, what safety behaviours they use, and how life has been affected. Always ask about low mood, alcohol and drug use (often used to manage anxiety), and suicidal thoughts. Depression and anxiety frequently occur together.

The **GAD-7** questionnaire scores seven symptoms of generalised anxiety over the previous two weeks, each from 0 to 3, giving 0–21. Scores of 5, 10 and 15 mark mild, moderate and severe anxiety. Its first two items form the **GAD-2**, a quick screen; a score of 3 or more is the usual cut-off. In the original primary care validation study, a GAD-2 of 3 or more had a sensitivity of about 0.86 and a specificity of about 0.83 for GAD.

::: example A GAD-7 and what follows {#ex-gad7}
Mr Luis Fernández, 45, completes a GAD-7 with item scores 3, 2, 2, 1, 2, 3, 1. Calculate his score and severity. Then calculate the positive predictive value of a GAD-2 of 3 or more (sensitivity 0.86, specificity 0.83) in a population where 20% have GAD, and comment.

::: solution
**GAD-7 total.** $3+2+2+1+2+3+1 = 14$, in the moderate range (10–14).

**GAD-2.** His first two items total $3+2 = 5$, above the cut-off.

**PPV at 20% prevalence.** True positives $= 0.86 \times 0.2 = 0.172$. False positives $= 0.17 \times 0.8 = 0.136$. PPV $= 0.172/(0.172 + 0.136) = 0.172/0.308 \approx 0.558$, about 56%.

**Comment.** Even in a high-prevalence group, nearly half of positive screens are false. The questionnaire identifies who needs a clinical assessment and gives a baseline for measuring progress; the diagnosis comes from the interview.
:::
:::

## Treatment

Treatment in the UK follows NICE guidelines: CG113 (2011) for GAD and panic disorder, CG159 (2013) for social anxiety disorder, CG31 (2005) for OCD and body dysmorphic disorder, and NG116 (2018) for PTSD. As of 2026 these remain the current guidelines. They share a **stepped-care** model: start with the least intensive effective treatment and step up if it does not help.

::: definition Treatment approaches in anxiety disorders {#def-anx-treatment}
- **Psychoeducation**: explaining the threat response and the avoidance cycle.
- **Low-intensity interventions**: guided self-help based on CBT, computerised CBT and psychoeducational groups.
- **Cognitive behavioural therapy** (CBT): identifying and testing frightening predictions, reducing safety behaviours, and graded **exposure** to feared situations. Disorder-specific forms include CBT for panic, the Clark and Wells model for social anxiety, and **exposure and response prevention** (ERP) for OCD, in which the person faces the trigger and refrains from the compulsion.
- **Trauma-focused therapies** for PTSD: **trauma-focused CBT** and **eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing** (EMDR).
- **Medication**: SSRIs are first-line drugs for most anxiety disorders and OCD. NICE recommends sertraline first for GAD. Alternatives include another SSRI, an SNRI (venlafaxine, duloxetine) or pregabalin.
:::

Important principles of drug treatment:

- **Early worsening.** SSRIs can make anxiety worse in the first week or two. Starting low and explaining this improves adherence.
- **Time to effect.** Benefit builds over weeks, and in OCD can take twelve weeks or more, often at higher doses than for depression (dose decisions follow the BNF and specialist advice).
- **Benzodiazepines.** These work quickly but cause tolerance, dependence, sedation, falls and impaired driving, and withdrawal can be severe. NICE advises against them for GAD and panic disorder except as short-term crisis treatment. Pregabalin also has potential for misuse and dependence and, since 2019, is a controlled drug in the UK.
- **No debriefing.** For PTSD, single-session psychological debriefing straight after a trauma is not recommended, as it does not prevent PTSD and may increase it. Early support, practical help and watchful waiting are preferred, with trauma-focused treatment for people who develop PTSD.

::: example Stopping a long-term benzodiazepine {#ex-benzo-taper}
Mrs Joan Fraser, 71, has taken diazepam every night for 15 years after it was started for anxiety following a bereavement. She has had two falls this year and her daughter is worried about her memory. She is anxious about stopping. Outline an approach.

::: solution
**Why change.** Long-term benzodiazepine use in an older person raises the risk of falls, fractures, cognitive impairment and road traffic accidents, and the original indication has passed.

**Shared decision.** Explain the reasons gently, acknowledge that she has done nothing wrong, and agree a plan she controls. Abrupt stopping is dangerous after long-term use: withdrawal can cause rebound anxiety, insomnia and, rarely, seizures.

**Gradual reduction.** Reduce the dose slowly, typically in small steps every two to four weeks, slowing further towards the end and pausing if withdrawal symptoms are troublesome, following the BNF and local guidance. Diazepam's long half-life makes it suitable for tapering as it is.

**Support.** Offer psychological help for anxiety and insomnia (CBT for insomnia), review other sedating medicines, and check for depression. Review regularly. Many people find that after withdrawal they sleep and think better than before.
:::
:::

::: example Checking that will not stop {#ex-checking-son}
Mr Ahmed Hassan, 35, takes two hours to leave home each morning because he checks that the cooker is off and the doors are locked, often 30 times. He knows this is excessive but feels unbearable anxiety that his family will die in a fire if he stops. Since his son was born, he also has intrusive images of harming the baby, which horrify him, and he avoids bathing the child. What is the diagnosis, and how should the intrusive thoughts about the baby be understood?

::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Obsessive–compulsive disorder: obsessions about harm (fire, harming the baby), compulsions (repeated checking), time consumed (over an hour a day) and impairment, with good insight.

**The intrusive images.** Unwanted thoughts of harming a baby are common in OCD, especially in new parents. They are **ego-dystonic**: the person finds them abhorrent, resists them and avoids situations that trigger them. They are not intentions or desires. There is no evidence that people with OCD act on such thoughts. Recognising this matters: treating him as a risk to his child would increase his shame and avoidance and could deter him from seeking help.

**Distinguish carefully.** The assessment should still be thorough, as any clinician must be when harm to a child is mentioned. Features that would raise concern are thoughts experienced as acceptable or desired, psychosis, intoxication, or a history of violence. None is present.

**Treatment.** CBT with exposure and response prevention: graded exercises such as leaving the house after a single check, and bathing his son, while not performing compulsions. An SSRI may be added if symptoms are severe or progress is slow.
:::
:::

::: warning Reassurance can become a compulsion
Families and clinicians often respond to anxiety by reassuring repeatedly: "The door is locked", "Your heart is fine". Reassurance relieves anxiety briefly, but it works like a safety behaviour or a compulsion and keeps the problem going (see [[#thm-avoidance]]). It is better to give clear information once, then support the person to tolerate uncertainty. Repeated tests to reassure a person with health anxiety have the same effect.
:::

::: example A fear of needles that matters {#ex-adult-phobia}
Ms Grace Adeyemi, 32, has type 1 diabetes diagnosed a year ago. She has missed several appointments and her HbA1c has risen. She admits that she faints at the sight of needles and avoids blood tests. Describe the type of phobia and how its treatment differs from that of other phobias.

::: solution
**Type.** A specific phobia of the **blood–injection–injury** type. Unlike other phobias, it often produces a two-phase response: an initial rise in heart rate and blood pressure, followed by a sudden fall (a vasovagal response), which can cause fainting.

**Treatment.** Graded exposure, starting with pictures of needles and moving step by step to blood tests and self-injection. Because of the fainting risk, therapists teach **applied tension**: tensing the large muscles of the arms, legs and trunk to raise blood pressure and prevent fainting during exposure.

**Wider care.** Work with the diabetes team to make procedures easier: lying down for blood tests, topical anaesthetic, and insulin pens with small needles. Her phobia is a barrier to medical care and deserves treatment.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A man with panic disorder always carries a bottle of water and sits near the exit in meetings "in case I feel ill". According to the cognitive behavioural model, what is the main effect of these behaviours?
- [ ] They gradually reduce his anxiety through habituation.
- [x] They prevent him from learning that panic is not dangerous, and so keep the disorder going.
- [ ] They are harmless habits and can be ignored.
- [ ] They show that his panic is caused by a physical illness.
::: solution
These are safety behaviours. They give short-term relief but mean that each time he does not collapse, he credits the water or the exit rather than learning that panic does not cause collapse. Treatment involves dropping them during planned exposure.
:::
:::

::: history
Descriptions of irrational fear are ancient, and Hippocrates described a man who could not pass a flute player at night. In 1871 Carl Westphal described *Agoraphobie*, and in 1895 Sigmund Freud separated "anxiety neurosis" from neurasthenia. Learning theory changed treatment in the mid-20th century: Joseph Wolpe developed systematic desensitisation in the 1950s, and Isaac Marks and others in London showed in the 1960s and 1970s that exposure was the active ingredient. Victor Meyer described exposure and response prevention for OCD in 1966. David Clark's cognitive model of panic (1986) explained attacks as the catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations. The diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder entered DSM-III in 1980, after campaigns by Vietnam veterans and clinicians, giving a name to reactions earlier called "shell shock" and "combat fatigue". ICD-11, adopted in 2019, added complex PTSD.
:::

## Where this leads

The autonomic basis of the threat response is in [[physiology/autonomic-nervous-system]]; the overlap with depression in [[psychiatry/mood-disorders]]; benzodiazepine dependence in [[psychiatry/substance-use]]; and the psychological therapies in more detail in [[psychiatry/treatments-mental-health-law]].

::: summary
- Anxiety is a normal threat response; a disorder is anxiety out of proportion to the danger, persistent and impairing.
- Avoidance, escape and safety behaviours relieve anxiety in the short term but maintain the disorder.
- The main disorders are GAD, panic disorder, agoraphobia, social anxiety disorder and specific phobias; OCD and the stress-related disorders (PTSD, complex PTSD, adjustment disorder) are closely related.
- Exclude physical causes, especially thyroid disease, arrhythmia, hypoglycaemia and substances, and look for coexisting depression and alcohol use.
- GAD-7 measures severity; GAD-2 is a quick screen whose predictive value depends on prevalence.
- Treatment is stepped: psychoeducation and guided self-help, then CBT with exposure (ERP for OCD, trauma-focused CBT or EMDR for PTSD).
- SSRIs are first-line drugs, with sertraline first for GAD; early worsening and delayed benefit should be explained.
- Avoid long-term benzodiazepines; do not offer single-session debriefing after trauma.
- Intrusive thoughts of harm in OCD are unwanted and resisted; they do not indicate a risk of acting on them.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Name the disorder {level=1}
Name the most likely diagnosis: (a) a woman who has avoided supermarkets and buses for a year because she fears having a panic attack where she cannot get out; (b) a man who for eight months has worried most days about his health, his job and his children, with poor sleep and muscle tension; (c) a student who cannot give a seminar presentation because she fears blushing and being thought stupid.
::: solution
(a) Agoraphobia (possibly with panic disorder). (b) Generalised anxiety disorder. (c) Social anxiety disorder.
:::
:::

::: exercise The physiology of a panic attack {level=1}
Explain why hyperventilation during a panic attack causes tingling of the fingers and lips.
::: solution
Rapid breathing blows off carbon dioxide, causing a fall in arterial $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ and a respiratory alkalosis. Alkalosis increases the binding of calcium to albumin, lowering ionised calcium, which increases the excitability of peripheral nerves and causes tingling around the mouth and in the fingers. Low $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ also causes cerebral vasoconstriction, contributing to light-headedness.
:::
:::

::: exercise PTSD symptom groups {level=1}
List the three core symptom groups of PTSD in ICD-11 and the three additional features of complex PTSD.
::: solution
PTSD: re-experiencing the trauma in the present (intrusive memories, flashbacks, nightmares); avoidance of reminders; and a persistent sense of current threat (hypervigilance, enhanced startle). Complex PTSD adds: problems with emotional regulation; a persistently negative self-concept (shame, guilt, worthlessness); and difficulties in sustaining relationships.
:::
:::

::: exercise A screening calculation {level=2 check="round(100*0.86*0.06/(0.86*0.06+0.17*0.94))"}
Using a GAD-2 sensitivity of 0.86 and specificity of 0.83, calculate the positive predictive value in a primary care population where 6% have GAD. Give the answer as a whole-number percentage.
::: solution
True positives $= 0.86 \times 0.06 = 0.0516$. False positives $= 0.17 \times 0.94 = 0.1598$. PPV $= 0.0516/(0.0516+0.1598) = 0.0516/0.2114 \approx 0.244$, so about **24%**. Three in four positive screens are false, so a positive GAD-2 leads to further assessment.
:::
:::

::: exercise Design an exposure hierarchy {level=2}
A 40-year-old man with agoraphobia has not used public transport for two years. Design a graded exposure hierarchy of six steps and state two rules for carrying out exposure.
::: solution
An example hierarchy, from least to most feared: (1) stand at the bus stop for 15 minutes; (2) travel one stop on a quiet bus with a companion; (3) travel one stop alone; (4) travel five stops alone at a quiet time; (5) travel five stops at rush hour; (6) take a 30-minute train journey alone. Rules: stay in the situation until anxiety falls substantially, or long enough to test the feared prediction, rather than escaping at the peak; drop safety behaviours (sitting by the door, carrying unneeded medication); repeat each step regularly until it is easy before moving on; record predictions and what actually happened.
:::
:::

::: exercise The worried well? {level=2}
A 55-year-old woman with a history of GAD presents with a month of anxiety, palpitations, weight loss of 4 kg, tremor and diarrhoea. Her GP increases her sertraline. What is wrong with this response, and what should be done?
::: solution
A new pattern of symptoms in a person with a known anxiety disorder still needs a physical assessment. Weight loss, tremor, diarrhoea and palpitations strongly suggest hyperthyroidism; atrial fibrillation must also be excluded. Assessment should include pulse rate and rhythm, signs of thyroid disease, thyroid function tests and an ECG. Attributing new physical symptoms to a known psychiatric diagnosis is called diagnostic overshadowing and is a common cause of missed illness in people with mental disorders.
:::
:::

::: exercise After the crash {level=3}
Three weeks after a serious motorway crash in which a friend was killed, a 24-year-old woman has nightmares, cannot drive, and is jumpy. Her employer arranged a single debriefing session the day after the crash. Her mother asks whether she now has PTSD and whether she should start an antidepressant. Discuss.
::: solution
Three weeks after the event, these symptoms are common and many people recover without specific treatment. ICD-11 PTSD requires symptoms lasting at least several weeks with significant impairment, and DSM-5-TR more than one month, so it is early to diagnose PTSD, although the symptoms may represent an acute stress response that becomes PTSD. NICE NG116 advises active monitoring and practical support, and reassessment within about a month; if PTSD develops (or if symptoms are severe now), trauma-focused CBT is offered, and individual trauma-focused CBT can be offered within the first month for people with clinically important symptoms. Single-session debriefing is not recommended because it does not prevent PTSD and may make it worse. Drug treatment is not first-line for preventing or treating PTSD; an SSRI or venlafaxine is considered later for adults who prefer it or cannot engage in therapy. Also check for depression, alcohol use and suicidal thoughts, and support her return to driving through graded exposure. Bereavement for her friend also needs space.
:::
:::

::: exercise A complex case {level=3}
A 38-year-old woman who experienced repeated abuse in childhood has nightmares and flashbacks, uses alcohol to sleep, has frequent intense rows with partners, and describes herself as "worthless and disgusting". She has self-harmed by cutting during periods of distress. Discuss the differential diagnosis and the priorities in treatment.
::: solution
The differential includes complex PTSD (PTSD symptoms with problems of emotional regulation, negative self-concept and relationships), PTSD with comorbid depression, borderline personality disorder (which overlaps substantially and may coexist), and alcohol use disorder. Careful assessment distinguishes them: in complex PTSD the self-concept is stably negative and the core is trauma symptoms, whereas in borderline personality disorder self-image is unstable and fear of abandonment is prominent. Priorities: safety first (self-harm, alcohol, current risk from others, including whether the abuser is still a threat); a stable therapeutic relationship; reduction of alcohol use, which interferes with therapy; stabilisation skills for managing distress and self-harm; then trauma-focused therapy, adapted and often extended for complex PTSD, as NICE NG116 suggests. Medication may help coexisting depression or sleep but is not the main treatment. Avoid benzodiazepines given alcohol use and self-harm.
:::
:::
