Everyone has days of low mood, and most people have periods of feeling unusually energetic. A mood disorder is different. The change in mood is more severe and lasts longer, it comes with a cluster of other symptoms, and it interferes with life. Depression is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide, and bipolar disorder, though less common, carries a high risk of suicide and of harm during episodes.

This chapter covers how depressive and manic episodes are recognised, how unipolar depression is distinguished from bipolar disorder, the main causes to consider, and the principles of treatment, including the safe use of lithium. Assessment skills from [[psychiatry/psychiatric-assessment]] are assumed.

## Depression

### The depressive episode

::: definition Depressive episode {#def-depression}
A **depressive episode** is a period of at least two weeks in which a person has **low mood** or **loss of interest or pleasure** (anhedonia) for most of the day, nearly every day, together with other symptoms. Both ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR require at least five symptoms in total from this list:

- low mood;
- loss of interest or pleasure;
- poor concentration or indecisiveness;
- feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt;
- hopelessness (listed in ICD-11);
- recurrent thoughts of death or suicide;
- change in sleep (insomnia, often with early-morning waking, or sleeping too much);
- change in appetite or weight;
- psychomotor agitation or retardation;
- fatigue or loss of energy.

The symptoms must cause distress or impair functioning, and must not be better explained by a substance or another medical condition.
:::

Severity is graded as **mild**, **moderate** or **severe**, according to the number and intensity of symptoms and the degree of impairment. Several patterns are worth recognising because they change management:

- **With psychotic features.** Delusions or hallucinations are present, usually in keeping with the low mood: delusions of guilt, poverty, illness or that the body is rotting (nihilistic delusions). This is a severe illness that needs combined antidepressant and antipsychotic treatment or ECT.
- **Melancholic.** Marked loss of pleasure, mood worse in the morning, early waking, marked psychomotor change and weight loss.
- **Atypical features.** Mood that brightens with good events, increased sleep and appetite, and sensitivity to rejection.
- **Seasonal pattern.** Episodes that recur at the same time of year, usually autumn and winter.
- **Peripartum onset.** Onset during pregnancy or after birth, discussed in [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]].

**Persistent depressive disorder** (dysthymia) is a milder but chronic low mood lasting at least two years.

A single depressive episode is **major depressive disorder, single episode**; further episodes make it **recurrent**. Depression is often recurrent: after one episode, about half of people have another, and the risk rises with each episode.

### What causes depression?

There is no single cause. The useful way to think is in layers:

- **Genetic liability.** Twin studies suggest that heritability is roughly 30–40%. The risk is spread across many genes of small effect. Early claims that a single gene variant, such as one in the serotonin transporter gene, interacted with stress to cause depression have not held up in large studies.
- **Stress and adversity.** Childhood abuse and neglect, bereavement, relationship breakdown, poverty and chronic illness all raise risk. Stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and many people with depression have raised cortisol.
- **Psychological patterns.** Aaron Beck described a **negative cognitive triad**: negative views of the self, the world and the future. These patterns keep depression going and are the target of cognitive therapy.
- **Biology of treatment.** The **monoamine hypothesis** proposed that depression is caused by too little serotonin or noradrenaline, because the first antidepressants increased these transmitters. Antidepressants raise monoamine levels within hours, yet improvement takes weeks. Current explanations therefore emphasise slower changes in neural plasticity, stress hormones and emotional processing. The simple "chemical imbalance" story is no longer accepted.

### Ruling out other causes

Low mood is a feature of many conditions, and a careful history protects the diagnosis.

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Bipolar disorder | A depressive episode in a person who has had mania or hypomania |
| Normal reactions | Grief after bereavement; adjustment disorder after a stressful event |
| Endocrine | Hypothyroidism, Cushing syndrome, Addison disease, hypercalcaemia |
| Neurological | Stroke, Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, dementia, brain tumour |
| Other medical | Anaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, obstructive sleep apnoea, cancer (notably pancreatic) |
| Substances | Alcohol, withdrawal from stimulants, cannabis |
| Medicines | Corticosteroids, interferon, some beta-blockers, levetiracetam, hormonal treatments |

Isotretinoin, used for severe acne, deserves a specific note. Depression, anxiety and, rarely, suicidal thoughts are listed adverse effects, although observational studies of a causal link give inconsistent results. In 2023 the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) introduced extra safeguards: mental health and sexual function are assessed before and during treatment, and for people under 18 two prescribers must agree to start it. A new low mood in someone taking isotretinoin should be taken seriously and discussed with the prescriber.

::: example Not every tired patient is depressed {#ex-not-depression}
Mrs Siobhan Kelly, 56, tells her GP she feels "flat and exhausted", has lost interest in gardening, and has gained 5 kg over six months. She feels cold and her skin is dry. She asks for an antidepressant. How should the GP proceed?

::: solution
**Assess the mood syndrome.** She has low mood, loss of interest and fatigue for several months, which might meet criteria for a depressive episode.

**Look for physical clues.** Weight gain with cold intolerance, dry skin and fatigue suggests hypothyroidism. Ask about constipation and menstrual changes, and examine for slow reflexes and goitre.

**Investigate.** Thyroid function shows a high TSH and a low free T4: primary hypothyroidism.

**Plan.** Treat the hypothyroidism and review mood when she is euthyroid, usually after several weeks. If low mood persists, it is then assessed and treated as depression in its own right. Starting an antidepressant first would have delayed the correct treatment.
:::
:::

### Measuring severity: the PHQ-9

The **Patient Health Questionnaire-9** (PHQ-9) asks about the nine DSM symptoms over the previous two weeks. Each item is scored 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), giving a total of 0–27. Conventional bands are 0–4 minimal, 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe and 20–27 severe. NICE guideline NG222 (2022) groups depression for treatment choice into **less severe** (roughly PHQ-9 below 16) and **more severe** (16 or above). Item 9, about thoughts of death or self-harm, must always be followed by a conversation, whatever the total.

A large individual-participant meta-analysis found that a PHQ-9 cut-off of 10 or more had a sensitivity of about 0.88 and a specificity of about 0.85 for major depression. As with any screen, its predictive value depends on prevalence.

:::

::: example Scoring a PHQ-9 {#ex-phq9}
Mr Kwame Mensah, 41, completes a PHQ-9 with item scores 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 0, 1 (item 9 is thoughts that he would be better off dead or of hurting himself). Calculate his total, give the conventional severity band and the NICE NG222 grouping, and say what item 9 requires.

::: solution
**Total.** $2+3+1+2+3+2+1+0+1 = 15$.

**Band.** 15 falls in the 15–19 band, "moderately severe".

**NICE grouping.** A score of 15 is just below 16, so it falls in "less severe" depression. The cut-off is a guide, not a rule: the clinician also considers functioning, duration and the person's history.

**Item 9.** A score of 1 ("several days") means he has had thoughts of death or self-harm. This needs a careful, direct conversation about suicidal thoughts, plans, protective factors and support, and a safety plan if needed. A questionnaire never replaces that conversation.
:::
:::

## Treatment of depression

Treatment is chosen together with the person, considering severity, previous response, preferences and other conditions. As of 2026, the main framework in England is NICE NG222 (2022).

::: definition Treatment options in depression {#def-treatments}
- **Psychological therapies.** Guided self-help based on CBT, group or individual **cognitive behavioural therapy** (CBT), **behavioural activation** (scheduling rewarding and purposeful activities), **interpersonal therapy** (IPT), counselling, short-term psychodynamic therapy, and group exercise or mindfulness programmes for less severe depression.
- **Antidepressants.** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (**SSRIs**, such as sertraline, citalopram, fluoxetine), serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (**SNRIs**, such as venlafaxine, duloxetine), mirtazapine, tricyclic antidepressants and monoamine oxidase inhibitors.
- **Combination** of individual CBT and an antidepressant, which NICE lists first for more severe depression.
- **Physical treatments** for severe or resistant illness: electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and, in some services, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation.
:::

For **less severe** depression, NICE does not recommend antidepressants routinely as first-line treatment unless the person prefers them; low-intensity psychological interventions come first. For **more severe** depression, a combination of individual CBT and an antidepressant is the first option listed, with individual CBT, behavioural activation or an antidepressant alone as alternatives.

### How antidepressants are used

Several principles are common to all antidepressants:

- **Onset.** Some benefit often appears in the first two weeks, but full effect takes four to six weeks or more. Side effects usually come first, so people need to know what to expect.
- **Review.** NICE advises reviewing people within two weeks of starting (and within one week for people under 25 or at increased risk of suicide), because of a small increase in suicidal thoughts in young people early in treatment.
- **Continuation.** After remission, the antidepressant should normally continue for at least six months to reduce the risk of relapse. People with recurrent episodes may benefit from longer maintenance treatment.
- **Stopping.** Antidepressants should be reduced gradually. Stopping suddenly can cause **discontinuation symptoms**: dizziness, "electric shock" sensations, anxiety, irritability, sleep disturbance and flu-like symptoms. These can be severe and prolonged for some people, and NICE (2022) advises tapering at a rate matched to the drug and the person.

Key adverse effects of SSRIs include nausea, gastrointestinal upset, sexual dysfunction, agitation early in treatment, **hyponatraemia** (especially in older people), an increased **bleeding risk** (especially combined with NSAIDs, aspirin or anticoagulants), and **QT prolongation** with citalopram and escitalopram. Any serotonergic drug can contribute to **serotonin syndrome** (agitation, tremor, clonus, hyperthermia) when combined with other serotonergic drugs such as tramadol, triptans, linezolid or monoamine oxidase inhibitors. Doses and interactions are always checked in the BNF or local formulary.

::: example The SSRI and the sodium {#ex-hyponatraemia}
Mrs Edith Clarke, 84, started sertraline for depression three weeks ago. She also takes bendroflumethiazide for hypertension. Her daughter reports she has become confused and unsteady. Her sodium is 121 mmol/L (normal 135–145). Explain what has happened and outline the response.

::: solution
**Mechanism.** SSRIs can cause the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, which leads to water retention and dilutional hyponatraemia. The risk is highest in the first weeks of treatment, in older people, in women, and with other drugs that lower sodium, such as thiazide diuretics.

**Assessment.** Confusion and unsteadiness with sodium of 121 mmol/L is moderate to severe hyponatraemia and needs urgent medical assessment, including fluid status, other causes and the risk of seizures or falls.

**Response.** Stop the sertraline and review the thiazide with the medical team; manage the hyponatraemia according to local guidelines, correcting sodium no faster than recommended. Once she has recovered, the depression still needs treatment: options include a psychological therapy or an antidepressant with lower hyponatraemia risk, such as mirtazapine, with sodium checked early. A baseline and follow-up sodium is advisable when starting an SSRI in an older person, especially with other risk factors.
:::
:::

### When depression does not respond

**Treatment-resistant depression** usually means an inadequate response to two antidepressants given at an adequate dose for an adequate time. Before calling depression resistant, check the basics: Is the diagnosis right (bipolar depression, an organic cause, substance use)? Has the person been taking the medicine? Are there ongoing stresses that treatment cannot address?

Next steps include adding or switching psychological therapy, switching to an antidepressant of a different class, or **augmentation**: adding lithium or an antipsychotic such as aripiprazole or quetiapine. **ECT** is the most effective treatment for severe depression, especially with psychotic features, life-threatening refusal of food and fluids, or stupor.

::: example Number needed to treat for maintenance {#ex-nnt}
A frequently cited meta-analysis of antidepressant continuation trials (Geddes and colleagues, 2003) found that over the follow-up period about 18% of people who continued an antidepressant relapsed, compared with about 41% of those switched to placebo. Calculate the absolute risk reduction and the number needed to treat (NNT) to prevent one relapse.

::: solution
**Absolute risk reduction.** $0.41 - 0.18 = 0.23$.

**NNT.** $1/0.23 \approx 4.35$. By convention NNT is rounded up, so about **5** people need to continue treatment for one to avoid a relapse.

**Interpretation.** This is a large effect by the standards of medicine. It supports the advice to continue an antidepressant after recovery. The decision is still individual: it weighs the person's history of relapse, side effects and their own preferences.
:::
:::

## Bipolar disorder

::: definition Mania, hypomania and the bipolar disorders {#def-bipolar}
A **manic episode** is a distinct period of elevated, expansive or irritable mood with increased activity or energy, lasting at least one week (or any duration if hospitalisation is needed). It comes with symptoms such as reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts or flight of ideas, grandiosity, distractibility and risky behaviour (spending, sexual activity, driving). It causes marked impairment and may include psychotic features.

A **hypomanic episode** has similar but milder symptoms, lasts at least four days in DSM-5-TR (ICD-11 says "several days"), and does not cause marked impairment or psychosis.

- **Bipolar I disorder**: at least one manic episode, usually with depressive episodes as well.
- **Bipolar II disorder**: at least one hypomanic episode and at least one depressive episode, with no manic episode.
- **Cyclothymic disorder**: at least two years of fluctuating mood with many periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet full criteria.
:::

Bipolar disorder affects roughly 1–2% of people over a lifetime, with onset usually in late adolescence or early adulthood. Heritability is high, around 60–80% in twin studies. Most people spend far more time depressed than manic, and the first episode is often depressive. Because of this, bipolar disorder is often first diagnosed as unipolar depression.

::: warning Always ask about hypomania before treating depression
Before starting an antidepressant, ask directly about past periods of unusually high energy, reduced sleep without feeling tired, unusual confidence, spending or risk-taking. People often remember these as "good times", so asking a relative helps. Clues to bipolarity include early onset of depression, many short episodes, postpartum onset, psychotic features and a family history of bipolar disorder. Treating bipolar depression with an antidepressant alone may trigger mania or rapid cycling.
:::

### Managing acute mania

Mania is a medical emergency for the person's finances, relationships, physical health and safety. Management principles are:

1. **Safety and setting.** Assess risk, consider admission, and reduce stimulation. Mental health legislation may be needed if the person lacks insight and is at serious risk.
2. **Stop antidepressants.**
3. **Antimanic medication.** NICE CG185 recommends an antipsychotic (haloperidol, olanzapine, quetiapine or risperidone) as first choice. If this is not effective, another antipsychotic from the list is tried, and lithium may be added. A short course of a benzodiazepine may help with agitation and sleep.
4. **Physical health.** Check for an organic cause (thyroid disease, drugs such as corticosteroids or stimulants), ensure adequate food, fluid and sleep.

### Long-term treatment and lithium

After an episode of mania, long-term treatment reduces the risk of relapse. **Lithium** is the first-line long-term treatment in NICE guidance. It is the most effective mood stabiliser for preventing relapse, and it is the only one with consistent evidence of reducing suicide.

::: theorem Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index {#thm-lithium}
The serum lithium concentration that prevents relapse is close to the concentration that causes toxicity. For maintenance, levels are typically aimed at 0.6–0.8 mmol/L, measured 12 hours after the last dose (higher levels, up to 1.0 mmol/L, may be used for some people). Toxicity becomes increasingly likely above about 1.2–1.5 mmol/L. Anything that lowers lithium excretion raises the level.
:::

::: proof
Lithium is not metabolised; it is excreted almost entirely by the kidneys. It is filtered at the glomerulus and most is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule along with sodium. When the body is depleted of sodium or water (vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, heat, reduced intake), the proximal tubule reabsorbs more sodium and lithium with it, so the level rises. Thiazide diuretics cause sodium loss and the same compensatory reabsorption. NSAIDs reduce renal blood flow and glomerular filtration, and ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers also reduce lithium clearance. Because the margin between effective and toxic levels is small, any of these can tip a stable person into toxicity.
:::

Lithium monitoring, summarised from NICE CG185 and the BNF, includes:

| What | Why | When (typical) |
|---|---|---|
| Serum lithium (12 h post-dose) | Efficacy and toxicity | Weekly after starting or dose change until stable; then every 3 months in the first year; then every 6 months, or 3-monthly in higher-risk groups |
| Renal function (eGFR) | Long-term risk of chronic kidney disease | Every 6 months |
| Thyroid function | Lithium causes hypothyroidism | Every 6 months |
| Calcium | Lithium can cause hyperparathyroidism | At baseline and periodically |
| Weight, ECG at baseline if cardiac risk | Weight gain; rare conduction effects | As indicated |

Common side effects are fine tremor, thirst and passing more urine (lithium can cause **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus**), weight gain and hypothyroidism. Signs of **toxicity** include coarse tremor, vomiting and diarrhoea, unsteadiness, slurred speech, confusion, and in severe cases seizures and reduced consciousness. Lithium is teratogenic and must be managed with specialist advice in pregnancy.

::: example Lithium meets an NSAID {#ex-lithium-interface}
Mr Tomasz Nowak, 48, has bipolar I disorder and has been stable on lithium for six years with levels of 0.6–0.7 mmol/L. After a sports injury he has taken ibuprofen for ten days. During a heatwave he has had diarrhoea. He presents with coarse tremor, vomiting and unsteadiness. His lithium level is 2.1 mmol/L and his creatinine has risen from 85 to 160 µmol/L. Explain what has happened and outline management.

::: solution
**Explanation.** Two mechanisms raised his lithium. Ibuprofen reduced renal clearance of lithium, and diarrhoea with heat caused sodium and water depletion, increasing proximal tubular reabsorption of lithium (see [[#thm-lithium]]). The rising creatinine shows acute kidney injury, which further reduces excretion.

**Severity.** A level of 2.1 mmol/L with neurological signs is significant toxicity and a medical emergency.

**Management.** Stop lithium and the ibuprofen. Seek urgent medical and toxicology advice; give intravenous fluids to correct dehydration and restore renal function; monitor levels, electrolytes, renal function and neurological state. Haemodialysis is considered in severe toxicity, especially with impaired renal function or serious neurological features, following local guidance and poisons information services.

**Prevention.** People taking lithium and their families should know the early signs of toxicity, that NSAIDs bought over the counter can be dangerous, and to seek advice about fluids and possibly pausing lithium during vomiting or diarrhoea. The decision to restart, and at what level, is made with the psychiatric team.
:::
:::

### Other mood stabilisers

- **Valproate** is effective for mania and maintenance but is highly teratogenic: it causes major malformations and neurodevelopmental disorders in children exposed in the womb. Since January 2024 the MHRA has required that valproate is not started in anyone under 55 unless two specialists independently document that no other effective or tolerated treatment is suitable, with a **Pregnancy Prevention Programme** for anyone who could become pregnant.
- **Lamotrigine** helps prevent depressive relapse more than manic relapse. It must be started slowly because of the risk of serious skin reactions, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome.
- **Antipsychotics** such as quetiapine, olanzapine and aripiprazole are used for acute episodes and for maintenance. Their metabolic effects require monitoring.

For **bipolar depression**, NICE CG185 recommends fluoxetine combined with olanzapine, or quetiapine alone, with olanzapine alone or lamotrigine as alternatives. Antidepressants are not used without an antimanic drug.

Psychological treatment is also important: psychoeducation, learning early warning signs of relapse, keeping regular sleep and daily routines, and family involvement all reduce relapse.

::: example A tired older man {#ex-late-life-mood}
Mr Harold Bennett, 74, a retired teacher, has become withdrawn over three months since his wife's death. He is eating little, has lost 6 kg, wakes at 4 a.m., and tells his GP that his bowels "have stopped working because my insides are rotting" and that he deserves to die for failing her. What is the likely diagnosis and why is it urgent?

::: solution
**Diagnosis.** This is a severe depressive episode with psychotic features. The nihilistic delusion (his insides rotting) and delusions of guilt are mood-congruent psychotic symptoms. Grief alone does not cause delusions, marked weight loss and pervasive guilt of this kind.

**Why urgent.** He is an older man, recently bereaved, with hopelessness, guilt and psychotic symptoms, and he is eating little. Each of these raises concern for suicide and for physical deterioration. Ask directly and compassionately about suicidal thoughts.

**Assessment.** Exclude physical causes of weight loss and constipation, including cancer, hypercalcaemia and hypothyroidism. Check his cognition.

**Management.** Urgent specialist referral. Treatment options are an antidepressant combined with an antipsychotic, or ECT, which works well in psychotic depression in older people and is especially considered if he stops eating and drinking. Admission may be needed for safety and nutrition.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 27-year-old woman is being treated for her third depressive episode. Which piece of history should most change the plan?
- [ ] She has had a previous good response to sertraline.
- [x] Her sister describes a week last year when she barely slept, spent her savings and started three new businesses.
- [ ] She has insomnia and poor appetite.
- [ ] Her mother had postnatal depression.
::: solution
The sister's account suggests a past manic or hypomanic episode, which would change the diagnosis to bipolar disorder. Treatment would then centre on a mood stabiliser or antipsychotic, and an antidepressant alone would be avoided. The other features are common in unipolar depression.
:::

::: history
Melancholia was described in Hippocratic writings, and the alternation of melancholia and mania was noted in antiquity by Aretaeus of Cappadocia. In 1854 Jean-Pierre Falret described *folie circulaire*, and in 1899 Emil Kraepelin grouped recurrent mood disorders as manic-depressive insanity, separating them from dementia praecox (schizophrenia) by their course. In 1949 John Cade in Melbourne reported that lithium calmed patients with mania; Mogens Schou's trials in Denmark in the 1950s and 1960s established its use. The first antidepressants were found by chance in the 1950s: iproniazid, a tuberculosis drug, lifted mood, and Roland Kuhn reported the antidepressant effect of imipramine in 1957. Fluoxetine, the first widely used SSRI, was approved in the United States in 1987. In parallel, Aaron Beck developed cognitive therapy for depression in the 1960s and 1970s.
:::

::: summary
- A depressive episode is at least two weeks of low mood or loss of interest with other symptoms, five in total, causing distress or impairment.
- Severity, psychotic features and pattern (melancholic, atypical, seasonal, peripartum) guide treatment.
- Always consider physical causes, medicines and substances, and always ask about past hypomania or mania.
- Screening questionnaires such as the PHQ-9 measure severity and support, but do not replace, clinical assessment; item 9 always needs a conversation.

- Mania is treated first with an antipsychotic; lithium is the first-line long-term treatment for bipolar disorder.
- Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index: dehydration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and thiazides raise levels; monitor level, renal, thyroid and calcium.
- Valproate must not be started in people under 55 without two specialists' agreement (MHRA 2024), and never without pregnancy prevention in those who could become pregnant.

List the core symptoms of a depressive episode and the minimum duration, and name four other symptoms that count towards the diagnosis.
::: solution
Core symptoms: low mood, and loss of interest or pleasure (at least one is needed). Duration: at least two weeks, most of the day nearly every day. Other symptoms (any four): poor concentration or indecisiveness; worthlessness or guilt; hopelessness; recurrent thoughts of death or suicide; sleep change; appetite or weight change; psychomotor agitation or retardation; fatigue. At least five symptoms in total, with distress or impairment.

::: exercise Mania or hypomania? {level=1}
Give two features that distinguish a manic episode from a hypomanic episode, and state the difference between bipolar I and bipolar II disorder.
::: solution
Mania lasts at least a week (or any duration if hospitalisation is needed), whereas hypomania lasts at least four days in DSM-5-TR. Mania causes marked impairment, may require admission and may include psychotic features; hypomania does not cause marked impairment or psychosis. Bipolar I requires at least one manic episode; bipolar II requires at least one hypomanic and one depressive episode and no mania.

::: exercise Lithium and its interactions {level=1}
Name three drugs or situations that raise serum lithium, and four signs of lithium toxicity.
::: solution
Raise lithium: NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, thiazide diuretics, and dehydration (vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, heat, poor intake); also falling renal function. Signs of toxicity: coarse tremor, vomiting and diarrhoea, ataxia, slurred speech, confusion, and in severe cases seizures and reduced consciousness.

In a maintenance trial in bipolar disorder, 30% of people taking lithium and 50% of those taking placebo relapsed over two years (illustrative figures). What is the number needed to treat to prevent one relapse over two years? Round up to a whole number.

::: exercise The first antidepressant {level=2}
A 35-year-old man with a first, more severe depressive episode and no past manic symptoms wants medication alongside therapy. He also takes regular naproxen for back pain. Discuss the choice of antidepressant class and what he should be told when it is started.
::: solution
An SSRI such as sertraline is a common first choice because of its tolerability and safety, combined with individual CBT as NICE lists first for more severe depression. The naproxen matters: SSRIs combined with NSAIDs increase the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, so the need for naproxen should be reviewed and gastroprotection with a proton pump inhibitor considered, following the BNF. He should be told that side effects such as nausea or restlessness often come first and usually settle; that benefit builds over several weeks; that he will be reviewed within about two weeks; to seek help early if he has suicidal thoughts or feels worse; that treatment usually continues for at least six months after recovery; and that stopping should be gradual and planned to avoid discontinuation symptoms.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bipolar disorder and pregnancy planning {level=3}
A 29-year-old woman with bipolar I disorder has been stable for three years on valproate, started at age 22 before the current restrictions. She now plans a pregnancy. Outline the issues and how they should be approached.
::: solution
Valproate in pregnancy carries a high risk of major congenital malformations and of neurodevelopmental disorders in the child, so it should not be used in pregnancy for bipolar disorder. Under MHRA rules she should already be on a Pregnancy Prevention Programme with annual specialist review. Planning should start before conception, with her psychiatrist and ideally a perinatal mental health service. Options include switching to an antipsychotic with more reassuring pregnancy data, or to lithium, which has a smaller and lower teratogenic risk than once thought but needs close level monitoring in pregnancy and around delivery. The switch should be gradual and the period after it monitored for relapse. She needs to know that the postpartum period carries a high risk of relapse and of postpartum psychosis, so a written perinatal plan and close support after birth are essential. Folic acid is advised. Valproate should not be stopped abruptly, as rapid withdrawal risks relapse.
:::
:::

::: exercise Is this depression resistant? {level=3}
A 52-year-old woman has had depression for 18 months. She has tried citalopram and then sertraline, each for four weeks, without benefit. She drinks about 40 units of alcohol a week and works long night shifts. Her GP describes her depression as "treatment-resistant" and asks about augmentation with lithium. Respond.
::: solution
She does not yet meet a reasonable definition of treatment-resistant depression. Four weeks is too short to judge an antidepressant; an adequate trial is usually at least four to six weeks at an adequate dose, with adherence confirmed. Two SSRIs in sequence also do not test different mechanisms. More important, 40 units of alcohol a week is enough to cause and maintain depression and to interfere with treatment; it also makes lithium less safe. Night shift work disrupts sleep and circadian rhythms. Other checks: the diagnosis (past hypomania, physical causes such as hypothyroidism or anaemia), and whether she has been offered psychological therapy. A sensible plan addresses alcohol use with support, offers CBT or behavioural activation, optimises a single antidepressant trial at adequate dose and duration, and reviews work patterns. Augmentation is considered only if depression persists after these steps, with specialist advice.
:::
:::
