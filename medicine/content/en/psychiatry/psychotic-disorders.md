Psychosis is a state in which a person's contact with shared reality is disturbed. They may hear voices that others cannot hear, hold beliefs that others find impossible, or think and speak in a way that is hard to follow. Psychosis is frightening for the person and for those around them, and it is often misunderstood. Most people with psychosis are not violent, many recover well, and the outcome depends a great deal on how quickly and how well they are helped.

This chapter covers the symptoms of psychosis, schizophrenia and the other psychotic disorders, the causes that must not be missed, the biology behind antipsychotic treatment, and the care that improves outcomes: early intervention, psychological and family work, careful prescribing, and attention to physical health.

## What psychosis is

::: definition Psychosis and its symptom domains {#def-psychosis}
**Psychosis** is a syndrome marked by **hallucinations**, **delusions** or **disorganised thinking**, usually with reduced insight. In schizophrenia and related disorders, symptoms are grouped into three domains:

- **Positive symptoms**: experiences added to normal mental life, such as hallucinations, delusions and disorganised speech or behaviour.
- **Negative symptoms**: loss of normal functions, such as reduced motivation (avolition), reduced speech (alogia), reduced emotional expression (blunted affect), reduced pleasure (anhedonia) and social withdrawal.
- **Cognitive symptoms**: problems with attention, working memory, processing speed and planning.
:::

Positive symptoms are the most dramatic and usually prompt the first contact with services. Negative and cognitive symptoms, though quieter, often have the greatest effect on work, study and relationships, and they respond least to medication.

Psychosis is a syndrome, not a diagnosis. It occurs in schizophrenia, but also in bipolar disorder and severe depression, in dementia and delirium, and with drugs. The first task in any presentation is to find out which.

## Schizophrenia and related disorders

### Schizophrenia

::: definition Schizophrenia {#def-schizophrenia}
In ICD-11, **schizophrenia** requires at least two of the following for most of the time over at least one month, at least one of them from the first four:

1. persistent delusions;
2. persistent hallucinations (most often auditory);
3. disorganised thinking (formal thought disorder);
4. experiences of influence, passivity or control (for example believing that thoughts are being inserted or withdrawn, or that actions are controlled by an outside force);
5. negative symptoms;
6. grossly disorganised behaviour;
7. psychomotor disturbances, including catatonia.

The symptoms must not be a manifestation of another health condition or the effect of a substance or medication. DSM-5-TR requires continuous signs of disturbance for at least six months, including at least one month of active symptoms.
:::

Schizophrenia usually begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, slightly earlier in men than in women. The lifetime risk is a little under 1%. The onset is often preceded by a **prodrome** of months or years with social withdrawal, falling performance at school or work, odd ideas and attenuated (milder) psychotic experiences.

The course varies. Some people have a single episode and recover fully; many have relapses with partial recovery between; a minority have persistent symptoms despite treatment. Factors linked to a better outcome include acute onset, an obvious trigger, good functioning before the illness, prominent mood symptoms, and a short **duration of untreated psychosis**.

### Other psychotic disorders

- **Schizoaffective disorder**: symptoms of schizophrenia and a mood episode (manic or depressive) present together during the same episode of illness.
- **Delusional disorder**: one or more persistent delusions, often of persecution, jealousy or a bodily (somatic) nature, without the other features of schizophrenia; the person's functioning outside the delusion is often well preserved.
- **Acute and transient psychotic disorder** (ICD-11): sudden onset of psychosis, with symptoms that change rapidly, usually resolving within weeks to a few months. DSM-5-TR has a similar **brief psychotic disorder** lasting under a month.
- **Substance-induced psychosis**: psychosis caused by intoxication with or withdrawal from a substance, such as cannabis, stimulants or alcohol.
- **Secondary psychosis** caused by a medical condition (discussed below).
- **Mood disorders with psychotic features**, covered in [[psychiatry/mood-disorders]].

### Causes and risk factors

Schizophrenia arises from a combination of genetic and environmental factors acting on the developing brain.

- **Genes.** Heritability is high, around 80% in twin studies. Risk is spread across many common variants of small effect, plus rarer copy-number variants such as the 22q11.2 deletion. Having a first-degree relative with schizophrenia raises lifetime risk about tenfold.
- **Early development.** Obstetric complications, such as hypoxia at birth, and maternal infection during pregnancy are associated with a modest increase in risk.
- **Social environment.** Growing up in a city, migration, being from an ethnic minority group (particularly where exposed to discrimination), and childhood adversity are associated with higher rates. In England, rates of psychosis are higher in some Black and minority ethnic groups, a finding that points to social causes such as discrimination and disadvantage rather than ethnicity itself.
- **Cannabis.** Regular cannabis use, especially in adolescence and of high-potency forms, is associated with a higher risk of psychosis. In a large European study, daily use of high-potency cannabis was associated with about five times the odds of a psychotic disorder compared with never using it.

## The dopamine hypothesis and antipsychotics

::: theorem The dopamine hypothesis of psychosis {#thm-dopamine}
Positive psychotic symptoms are associated with excess dopamine release in the striatum, driven by the mesolimbic and related pathways. All drugs that treat positive symptoms block dopamine D2 receptors, and their clinical potency correlates with how strongly they bind to D2 receptors. Drugs that increase dopamine, such as amphetamines, can cause psychosis.
:::

::: proof
Three lines of evidence support the hypothesis. First, from pharmacology: in the 1970s, the clinical doses of different antipsychotics were found to correlate closely with their affinity for D2 receptors, but not with their effects on other receptors. Second, from drug-induced psychosis: amphetamine and cocaine increase synaptic dopamine and can produce paranoid psychosis in healthy people, and they worsen symptoms in people with schizophrenia. Third, from brain imaging: PET studies have shown increased dopamine synthesis and release in the striatum of people with schizophrenia and of some people at high risk of psychosis. The hypothesis also explains the main adverse effects of antipsychotics, because the same D2 blockade acts in other dopamine pathways. Blockade in the **nigrostriatal** pathway causes movement disorders. Blockade in the **tuberoinfundibular** pathway removes dopamine's inhibition of prolactin release, causing hyperprolactinaemia. Reduced dopamine activity in the **mesocortical** pathway to the prefrontal cortex may contribute to negative and cognitive symptoms, which explains why D2 blockade helps these little or may worsen them. The hypothesis does not explain the whole illness: glutamate (NMDA receptor) dysfunction, altered brain development and cognitive changes are also involved.
:::

### Antipsychotic drugs

Antipsychotics are often divided into **first-generation** (typical) drugs, such as haloperidol and chlorpromazine, and **second-generation** (atypical) drugs, such as olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole, amisulpride and clozapine. The division is rough. The drugs differ more in their individual side-effect profiles than as two classes, and apart from clozapine they have broadly similar effectiveness for positive symptoms.

::: definition Major adverse effects of antipsychotics {#def-antipsychotic}
- **Extrapyramidal side effects** (from nigrostriatal D2 blockade): **acute dystonia** (sustained muscle spasm, such as oculogyric crisis or torticollis, within hours to days), **parkinsonism** (tremor, rigidity, slowness, within weeks), **akathisia** (an intensely distressing inner restlessness), and **tardive dyskinesia** (involuntary, often permanent movements of the mouth, tongue and face after months or years).
- **Hyperprolactinaemia**: menstrual disturbance, galactorrhoea, sexual dysfunction and, in the long term, reduced bone density. Common with risperidone, amisulpride and first-generation drugs.
- **Metabolic effects**: weight gain, dyslipidaemia and type 2 diabetes. Most marked with clozapine and olanzapine.
- **Cardiovascular effects**: QTc prolongation (relevant with haloperidol and several others), postural hypotension.
- **Sedation** and **anticholinergic** effects.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (NMS): a rare, life-threatening reaction with fever, severe rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability and a very high creatine kinase.
:::

The choice of drug is made with the person, considering which side effects matter most to them. Monitoring before and during treatment includes weight and waist circumference, blood pressure and pulse, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipids, prolactin where relevant, and an ECG when indicated (for example with cardiac risk factors or drugs that prolong the QT interval), as set out in NICE CG178 and local protocols.

::: warning Akathisia is easy to miss and dangerous
Akathisia looks like anxiety or agitation: the person cannot sit still, paces, shifts their weight, and feels driven to move. It is often mistaken for worsening psychosis, which leads to a higher antipsychotic dose and makes it worse. Akathisia is very distressing and has been associated with impulsive self-harm. Ask directly ("Do you feel restless inside? Do you have to keep moving?") whenever someone on an antipsychotic seems agitated, and review the drug and dose.
:::

### Clozapine

**Clozapine** is the only antipsychotic with clear evidence of superior effectiveness in **treatment-resistant schizophrenia**, which NICE defines in practice as failure to respond to at least two different antipsychotics, at least one of them a second-generation drug other than clozapine, each given at an adequate dose for an adequate time. It also reduces suicidal behaviour. Yet it is often offered late.

Clozapine needs careful monitoring because of serious adverse effects:

- **Neutropenia and agranulocytosis**: in the UK, a full blood count is required weekly for the first 18 weeks, then every two weeks to one year, then every four weeks, through a registered monitoring service.
- **Myocarditis and cardiomyopathy**: most often in the first two months; watch for fever, tachycardia, chest pain and breathlessness.
- **Constipation**, which can progress to life-threatening bowel obstruction (ileus) and must be asked about and treated actively.
- **Seizures** (dose-related), hypersalivation, sedation, weight gain and diabetes.
- **Interaction with smoking**: chemicals in tobacco smoke induce the liver enzyme CYP1A2, which metabolises clozapine. If a person stops smoking, for example on admission to a smoke-free ward, clozapine levels can rise sharply and cause toxicity. Nicotine replacement does not have this effect.

::: example A man who is "not getting better" {#ex-clozapine}
Mr Marcus Campbell, 31, has had schizophrenia for nine years. He has had adequate trials of risperidone and then olanzapine, with good adherence confirmed by a long-acting injection and blood levels. He still hears hostile voices daily and has been admitted three times in two years. Outline the next steps.

::: solution
**Confirm resistance.** He has had two antipsychotics, including at least one second-generation drug, at adequate doses and durations, with adherence confirmed. Substance use and the diagnosis have been reviewed. He meets the criteria for treatment-resistant schizophrenia.

**Offer clozapine.** Clozapine is the treatment of choice. Explain its benefits (a substantial chance of meaningful improvement and fewer admissions) and its burdens (blood tests, side effects), using written information.

**Baseline.** Full blood count, ECG, weight, blood pressure, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, and a record of smoking status. Register with the clozapine monitoring service.

**Start safely.** Begin at a low dose and increase slowly, monitoring pulse, blood pressure and temperature for the first weeks, because of the risk of hypotension, tachycardia and myocarditis. Ask about bowel habit at every review and prescribe a laxative proactively. Agree a plan for what happens if he stops smoking.

**Continue psychosocial care.** CBT for psychosis can help him cope with residual voices, and family intervention reduces relapse.
:::
:::

## Early intervention and the first episode

::: theorem Shorter untreated psychosis, better outcome {#thm-dup}
A longer **duration of untreated psychosis** (the time from the onset of psychotic symptoms to the start of adequate treatment) is associated with poorer symptomatic and functional outcomes. Services designed to detect and treat first-episode psychosis quickly, and to provide intensive multidisciplinary care in the first years, improve outcomes compared with standard care.
:::

In England, **early intervention in psychosis** (EIP) services work with people aged 14 to 65 with a first episode. The national access standard requires that at least 60% of people referred with suspected first-episode psychosis start NICE-recommended treatment within two weeks. EIP care includes an antipsychotic, **cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis** (CBTp), **family intervention**, support with education and employment (for example individual placement and support), and physical health care.

::: example A first episode {#ex-first-episode}
Ms Aisha Bello, 20, a university student, is brought by her flatmates to the GP. For two months she has stayed in her room, stopped attending lectures, and covered her laptop camera and window. She says neighbours are broadcasting her thoughts and that a voice comments on what she is doing. She smokes cannabis most days. How should she be assessed and managed?

::: solution
**Recognise psychosis.** She has persecutory delusions, thought broadcasting (a passivity experience), and a voice giving a running commentary, with social withdrawal: a probable first episode of psychosis.

**Assess risk and urgency.** Ask about suicidal thoughts, thoughts of harming others (particularly the neighbours), self-neglect, and her vulnerability. Ask about her understanding and what she would find helpful.

**Look for causes.** Physical examination; blood tests including full blood count, renal, liver and thyroid function, glucose and calcium; a urine drug screen; and consideration of HIV and syphilis serology. Neurological signs, seizures, confusion or a rapidly changing picture would prompt brain imaging, EEG and tests for autoimmune encephalitis.

**Refer urgently** to the early intervention in psychosis service. NICE advises against a GP starting an antipsychotic before specialist assessment unless in consultation with a psychiatrist.

**Management in EIP.** After baseline physical measures, an oral antipsychotic chosen with her, CBTp, family intervention with her consent, and support to stay at university. Discuss cannabis honestly and non-judgementally: continued use is linked to more relapses and poorer outcomes.
:::
:::

### Secondary (organic) psychosis

New psychosis can be caused by medical conditions. Features that point to a secondary cause include an older age at onset, rapid onset, confusion or fluctuating consciousness, visual or other non-auditory hallucinations, neurological signs, seizures, headache, and a poor response to treatment.

Causes include delirium, epilepsy (particularly temporal lobe), stroke, brain tumour, dementia (especially Lewy body dementia), Huntington disease, autoimmune encephalitis (such as anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, which typically presents in young women with psychosis followed by seizures, movement disorder and autonomic instability), HIV and neurosyphilis, systemic lupus erythematosus, thyroid disease, and drugs including corticosteroids, levodopa and stimulants.

## Long-term care and relapse prevention

After a first episode, NICE CG178 advises continuing antipsychotic treatment for a period (often at least one to two years) and discussing the risks and benefits of stopping. Relapse is common when antipsychotics are stopped, especially abruptly.

A large meta-analysis of maintenance trials (Leucht and colleagues, 2012) found that over about a year, 27% of people who continued an antipsychotic relapsed, compared with 64% of those switched to placebo.

Long-acting injectable (depot) antipsychotics can help people who prefer not to take daily tablets or who have relapsed because of non-adherence. Adherence improves when side effects are taken seriously, the person understands and agrees with the plan, and treatment fits their goals.

### Psychological and social care

- **CBT for psychosis** helps people make sense of their experiences, reduce distress from voices and beliefs, and develop coping strategies.
- **Family intervention** reduces relapse. It addresses **expressed emotion** (critical, hostile or over-involved attitudes in the household), which predicts relapse, and supports carers.
- **Individual placement and support** helps people into competitive employment.
- **Arts therapies** may help negative symptoms.
- **Housing, finances and social contact** are part of treatment, not extras.

### Physical health: the mortality gap

People with schizophrenia die on average about 15 years earlier than the general population. Most of the excess deaths are from physical illness, especially cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease and cancer. Contributing factors include high rates of smoking, the metabolic effects of antipsychotics, poverty, reduced physical activity, and **diagnostic overshadowing**, where physical symptoms are wrongly attributed to mental illness. Annual physical health checks, smoking cessation support, and early action on weight gain (lifestyle advice and, in some cases, switching antipsychotic or adding metformin under specialist guidance) are core parts of care.

::: example Weight gain on olanzapine {#ex-metabolic}
Mr Daniel Wright, 26, started olanzapine eight months ago for a first episode of psychosis and is now well. His weight has risen from 82 kg to 98 kg; he is 1.75 m tall. His HbA1c is 44 mmol/mol (normal below 42). Calculate his BMI and outline the response.

::: solution
**BMI.** $\text{BMI} = \text{weight}/\text{height}^2 = 98/1.75^2 = 98/3.0625 = 32.0$ kg/m². Before treatment it was $82/3.0625 \approx 26.8$. He has moved from overweight to obese, and his HbA1c is in the range indicating a raised risk of diabetes (42–47 mmol/mol).

**Response.** This is a common and important adverse effect, best addressed early. Discuss it openly with him. Offer a lifestyle programme of diet and physical activity. Review the antipsychotic: switching to a drug with lower metabolic risk, such as aripiprazole, can be considered with the specialist team, weighing the risk of relapse. Metformin is sometimes added to limit antipsychotic-associated weight gain under specialist advice. Check lipids and blood pressure, support him to stop smoking if he smokes, and repeat HbA1c. Rapid weight gain early in treatment predicts further gain, so weekly weight monitoring in the first weeks is advised.
:::

::: quiz
A 24-year-old man started haloperidol two days ago. He presents with his eyes fixed upwards and his neck twisted to one side, and he is very distressed. What is the most likely diagnosis?
- [x] Acute dystonia
- [ ] Tardive dyskinesia
- [ ] Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- [ ] Akathisia
::: solution
Sustained muscle spasm such as oculogyric crisis and torticollis within hours to days of starting a high-potency first-generation antipsychotic is acute dystonia. It responds rapidly to an anticholinergic drug such as procyclidine, given according to local guidance. Tardive dyskinesia appears after months or years; NMS involves fever, rigidity and altered consciousness; akathisia is restlessness.
:::
:::

::: example Voices in context {#ex-voices-culture}
Mrs Olufunke Adebayo, 58, has recently been widowed. She tells her GP that she sometimes hears her husband's voice calling her name in the evenings and occasionally sees him in his chair. She knows he has died, is grieving, but is otherwise functioning. Her church community says this is a sign he is at peace. Is this psychosis?

::: solution
**Probably not.** Brief hearing or seeing of a deceased loved one is common in bereavement across many cultures and is usually not a sign of mental illness. Key features: she knows her husband has died, the experiences are fleeting and related to her loss, she has no other psychotic symptoms, her functioning is preserved, and her community understands the experience in a way that she finds comforting.

**What would change the view.** Fixed false beliefs (for example that her husband is alive and being held somewhere), voices that are hostile or commanding, other psychotic symptoms, marked distress or impairment, or signs of severe depression or cognitive decline.

**Response.** Normalise the experience, support her grief, check for depression and suicidal thoughts, and invite her back if things change. Labelling ordinary grief as psychosis would be harmful.
:::
:::

::: history
Emil Kraepelin distinguished *dementia praecox* from manic-depressive illness in the 1890s, based on course and outcome. In 1911 Eugen Bleuler introduced the term *schizophrenia*, emphasising the splitting of mental functions and noting that not all patients deteriorated. Kurt Schneider described first-rank symptoms in the mid-20th century. In 1952 Jean Delay and Pierre Deniker in Paris reported the effect of chlorpromazine in psychosis, following Henri Laborit's observations of its calming action during surgery. Arvid Carlsson's work on dopamine in the late 1950s and 1960s, and the finding in the 1970s by Philip Seeman and Solomon Snyder's group that antipsychotic potency tracked D2 receptor binding, founded the dopamine hypothesis. In 1988 John Kane and colleagues showed clozapine's superiority in treatment-resistant schizophrenia, leading to its reintroduction with blood monitoring after earlier deaths from agranulocytosis. Early intervention services developed in the 1990s, notably through Patrick McGorry's work in Melbourne.
:::

::: summary
- Psychosis is a syndrome of hallucinations, delusions and disorganised thinking; in schizophrenia there are also negative and cognitive symptoms.
- Schizophrenia usually begins in early adulthood; diagnosis requires characteristic symptoms over at least a month (ICD-11) or six months of disturbance (DSM-5-TR), with other causes excluded.
- Risk reflects genes, early development, social adversity and cannabis use.
- All antipsychotics block D2 receptors; this explains both their effect on positive symptoms and their extrapyramidal and prolactin side effects.

- People with schizophrenia die about 15 years early, mostly from physical illness; physical health care is core treatment.

Classify each as a positive, negative or cognitive symptom: (a) hearing a voice commenting on one's actions; (b) spending all day in bed without motivation; (c) difficulty following a conversation and remembering instructions; (d) believing one's thoughts are being withdrawn by a machine; (e) speaking very little.
::: solution
(a) Positive (hallucination). (b) Negative (avolition). (c) Cognitive (attention and working memory). (d) Positive (passivity experience, thought withdrawal). (e) Negative (alogia).

::: exercise Dopamine pathways {level=1}
Name the four main dopamine pathways and the clinical effect of D2 blockade in each.
::: solution
Mesolimbic: reduction in positive symptoms (the therapeutic effect). Mesocortical: possible worsening of negative and cognitive symptoms. Nigrostriatal: extrapyramidal side effects (dystonia, parkinsonism, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia). Tuberoinfundibular: hyperprolactinaemia, because dopamine normally inhibits prolactin release.
:::

::: exercise Clozapine and smoking {level=1}
A man stable on clozapine is admitted to a smoke-free ward and stops smoking abruptly. Explain what may happen and what should be done.
::: solution
Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke induce CYP1A2, which metabolises clozapine. When smoking stops, enzyme activity falls over days, clozapine levels rise (often substantially) and toxicity may occur: sedation, hypersalivation, hypotension, constipation and seizures. Nicotine replacement does not induce CYP1A2. The team should monitor for toxicity, check clozapine levels, and reduce the dose as advised by a pharmacist or specialist. The reverse applies if he restarts smoking.
:::
:::

::: exercise Number needed to treat {level=2 check="3"}
In a maintenance meta-analysis, 27% of people on antipsychotics and 64% on placebo relapsed within a year. Calculate the number needed to treat to prevent one relapse in a year, rounded up to a whole number.
::: solution
Absolute risk reduction $= 0.64 - 0.27 = 0.37$. NNT $= 1/0.37 \approx 2.7$, rounded up to **3**. Three people need to continue an antipsychotic for a year to prevent one relapse, which is a large effect.
:::
:::

::: exercise BMI change {level=2 check="round(10*(95/1.68^2 - 78/1.68^2))/10"}
A woman 1.68 m tall gains weight from 78 kg to 95 kg on an antipsychotic. By how much has her BMI increased? Give the answer to one decimal place.
::: solution
Height squared $= 1.68^2 = 2.8224$. BMI before $= 78/2.8224 \approx 27.6$; after $= 95/2.8224 \approx 33.7$. Increase $= 17/2.8224 \approx 6.02$, so about **6.0** kg/m².
:::

::: exercise Recognise NMS {level=2}
A 40-year-old woman whose antipsychotic dose was increased a week ago becomes confused, with a temperature of 39.4 °C, generalised "lead-pipe" rigidity, sweating, a labile blood pressure and a creatine kinase of 18,000 U/L. Name the likely diagnosis, two differential diagnoses, and the principles of management.
::: solution
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Differentials: serotonin syndrome (onset over hours, clonus and hyperreflexia rather than lead-pipe rigidity, with serotonergic drugs), sepsis or encephalitis, malignant hyperthermia (after anaesthesia), and lethal catatonia. Management: stop the antipsychotic; transfer to medical or intensive care; supportive treatment with cooling, intravenous fluids and monitoring of renal function (rhabdomyolysis risk); benzodiazepines; specific agents such as dantrolene or bromocriptine in severe cases on specialist advice. Rechallenge with an antipsychotic is considered only after full recovery, cautiously, with a different drug.
:::
:::

::: exercise Organic or primary? {level=3}
A 23-year-old woman with no psychiatric history develops over two weeks paranoid ideas, agitation and auditory hallucinations, followed by a generalised seizure, abnormal movements of the mouth and fluctuating blood pressure. She is treated with haloperidol and becomes rigid and less responsive. What diagnosis should be considered, why, and what investigations are needed?
::: solution
Autoimmune encephalitis, especially anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, should be suspected. The clues are rapid onset of psychosis followed by seizures, orofacial dyskinesias, autonomic instability and reduced consciousness, and poor tolerance of antipsychotics (worsening rigidity, which could also be NMS). Investigations: serum and cerebrospinal fluid NMDA receptor antibodies, lumbar puncture (often lymphocytic pleocytosis), EEG (may show slowing or a characteristic pattern), MRI brain, and a search for an ovarian teratoma by pelvic ultrasound or MRI, because an associated tumour is common in young women. Management is neurological: immunotherapy and tumour removal if present, with the antipsychotic stopped. Early treatment improves outcome, so this diagnosis must be considered in any atypical first-episode psychosis.
:::
:::

::: exercise Planning with a family {level=3}
A 22-year-old man has recovered from a first episode of schizophrenia after six months of treatment. He wants to stop his antipsychotic because of weight gain and sexual side effects. His mother, with whom he lives, is very critical of his "laziness". Discuss the issues and a plan.
::: solution
His wish is reasonable and should be explored, not dismissed. Facts to share honestly: relapse rates after stopping are high, especially in the first years and after abrupt discontinuation, and relapse can set back education and relationships; but side effects are real and affect adherence and health. Options: switch to an antipsychotic with fewer metabolic and sexual side effects (for example aripiprazole), reduce the dose to the lowest effective level, address weight with a lifestyle programme, and, if he still wishes to stop after an informed discussion, agree a gradual reduction with close monitoring, a written relapse signature (his personal early warning signs) and a crisis plan, as NICE CG178 describes. The household atmosphere matters: high expressed emotion (criticism, hostility) predicts relapse. Offer family intervention, which educates his mother about negative symptoms (which can look like laziness), improves communication and reduces relapse. Support his return to work or study through individual placement and support. Document the shared decision.
:::
