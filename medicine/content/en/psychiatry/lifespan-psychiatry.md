Mental disorders look different at different stages of life. A child's anxiety may show as stomach aches and refusal to go to school. A new mother's depression may be hidden behind exhaustion and worry about the baby. An older person's depression may present as memory problems or physical complaints. The same principles of assessment and treatment apply, but they must be adapted to development, family context, physical health and the law.

This chapter covers three areas: child and adolescent psychiatry, perinatal mental health, and old-age psychiatry. For each, it highlights what is different, the conditions most often seen, and the safety issues that matter most.

## Child and adolescent mental health

About half of all lifetime mental disorders begin by the mid-teens. Recognising problems early, and helping the child within their family and school, can change the course of a life.

### What is different in children

- **Development.** Behaviour must be judged against what is expected for the child's age. Tantrums are normal at two but not at twelve; fears of the dark are normal in early childhood.
- **The system around the child.** A child lives within a family, a school and a community. Assessment involves parents or carers, teachers and often social care. Problems in the child may reflect problems in the system, such as parental illness, conflict or neglect.
- **Communication.** Young children may express distress through behaviour, play or physical symptoms rather than words.
- **Safeguarding.** Every assessment considers whether the child is at risk of abuse or neglect. Child protection is everyone's responsibility.
- **Consent.** Young people may be able to make their own decisions, depending on age and understanding.

::: definition Consent and competence in young people {#def-lifespan-adolescent}
In England and Wales, young people aged **16 and 17** are presumed to have capacity to consent to treatment, and the Mental Capacity Act 2005 applies to them for most purposes. A child **under 16** may consent to treatment if they have sufficient maturity and understanding to understand fully what is proposed; this is known as **Gillick competence**, after a 1985 House of Lords case. Parents can usually consent for children who lack competence. Refusal of treatment by young people raises complex legal questions, and specialist and legal advice is often needed.
:::

### Neurodevelopmental conditions

::: definition Neurodevelopmental conditions {#def-neurodev}
**Neurodevelopmental conditions** begin in the developmental period and involve differences in brain development that affect personal, social, academic or occupational functioning.

- **Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder** (ADHD): a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity–impulsivity, present before age 12 (DSM-5-TR), in more than one setting (for example at home and school), and causing impairment.
- **Autism spectrum disorder** (autism): persistent differences in social communication and social interaction, together with restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, which may include sensory sensitivities. Autism is lifelong and varies greatly in how it affects people; many autistic adults prefer identity-first language ("autistic person").
- **Intellectual disability** (ICD-11 "disorder of intellectual development"): significant limitations in intellectual functioning and adaptive behaviour, starting in the developmental period.
- **Tic disorders**, including **Tourette syndrome** (multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic for more than a year).
:::

Neurodevelopmental conditions often occur together, and with anxiety, depression and epilepsy. They are diagnosed by specialist multidisciplinary assessment, using history from parents and school, observation and standardised tools.

**ADHD treatment.** NICE NG87 (2018, updated 2019) recommends parent training and environmental support first for younger children, and medication when symptoms cause significant impairment despite these measures. **Methylphenidate** is the first-line drug for children aged 5 and over and young people; **lisdexamfetamine** is the next option. For adults, lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate is first line. Stimulants can reduce appetite and slow growth and raise heart rate and blood pressure, so monitoring includes height, weight, pulse and blood pressure before starting and regularly during treatment, with growth plotted on a centile chart.

**Autism.** There is no medication for autism itself. Support focuses on communication, adapting the environment, education, help for families, and treating coexisting conditions such as anxiety, sleep problems and ADHD. Antipsychotics are sometimes used for severe irritability or aggression, but only when other approaches have not worked and with careful monitoring (NICE CG170).

::: example A boy who cannot settle {#ex-adhd}
Leo Martin, 8, is referred because of poor progress at school. His teacher says he cannot stay in his seat, interrupts constantly and loses his belongings; his parents describe the same at home since he was a toddler. He is behind in reading. His mother asks whether he "just needs more discipline". How should he be assessed, and what should be considered?

::: solution
**Assessment.** A specialist assessment gathers a developmental history, reports from home and school (rating scales from both), observation, and a physical examination including hearing and vision. ADHD requires symptoms in more than one setting, onset in childhood and impairment, all of which appear present.

**Consider alternatives and coexisting conditions.** A specific learning disorder (such as dyslexia) may explain reading problems and frustration; anxiety, sleep problems (including obstructive sleep apnoea), hearing loss, autism, absence seizures and adverse experiences at home can all cause inattention or restlessness.

**Answer the question.** ADHD is not caused by poor parenting, although parenting strategies help. Explain this to reduce blame.

**Plan if ADHD is diagnosed.** Parent training, classroom adjustments and support for reading. If impairment remains significant, medication such as methylphenidate may be offered, after baseline height, weight, heart rate and blood pressure and a cardiovascular history, with regular monitoring of growth and cardiovascular measures.
:::
:::

### Emotional and behavioural problems

**Anxiety disorders** are the most common mental disorders in children. They may show as school refusal, clinginess, physical symptoms or avoidance. CBT, often involving parents, is the main treatment.

**Depression** becomes much more common after puberty, particularly in girls. In young people it may present with irritability rather than low mood. NICE NG134 (2019) recommends psychological therapies first. For moderate to severe depression not responding to therapy, **fluoxetine** may be offered alongside therapy after specialist review; it is the antidepressant with the best evidence in young people. All antidepressants carry a warning of increased suicidal thoughts in young people early in treatment, so close monitoring is needed.

**Conduct problems** (persistent defiance, aggression, rule-breaking) are best addressed through parent training programmes in younger children and multimodal interventions in adolescents.

## Perinatal mental health

Pregnancy and the year after birth bring physical, hormonal and social change, and they are times of increased vulnerability to mental illness. Mental illness at this time affects the mother, her relationship with her baby, and the baby's development. Suicide remains one of the leading causes of maternal death in the UK in the year after pregnancy.

::: definition Mental illness after childbirth {#def-perinatal}
- **Baby blues**: brief tearfulness, irritability and emotional lability, very common in the first days after birth (often around days 3–5), resolving without treatment within about two weeks.
- **Postnatal depression**: a depressive episode beginning in pregnancy or after birth, affecting roughly 1 in 10 women. Symptoms are those of depression, often with anxiety and worries about the baby or about being a bad mother.
- **Postpartum psychosis**: a severe psychiatric emergency affecting about 1–2 in 1,000 women after childbirth, usually beginning within the first two weeks. Features include rapidly changing mood (mania, depression or mixed states), confusion, delusions and hallucinations, often involving the baby. The risk is much higher in women with bipolar disorder or a previous episode.
:::

Perinatal anxiety disorders, OCD (often with intrusive thoughts of harm coming to the baby) and PTSD after a traumatic birth are also common. Fathers and partners can develop depression too.

### Screening and assessment

NICE CG192 (2014, updated 2020) advises asking about mental health at the first antenatal contact and in the early postnatal period, including past or present severe mental illness and a family history of perinatal mental illness. The **Whooley questions** (two questions about low mood and loss of interest) and a question about help are used for depression; the **GAD-2** for anxiety. The **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale** (EPDS), a 10-item questionnaire scored 0–30, is also widely used. Item 10 asks about thoughts of self-harm and always needs follow-up. In a large individual-participant meta-analysis, an EPDS cut-off of 11 or more had a sensitivity of about 0.81 and a specificity of about 0.88 for major depression.

::: example An EPDS score {#ex-epds}
Ms Sofia Rossi, 31, is seen by her health visitor six weeks after her first baby's birth. Her EPDS item scores are 2, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 0 (item 10 is self-harm). Calculate her total and explain what should happen next. Then calculate the positive predictive value of an EPDS of 11 or more with sensitivity 0.81 and specificity 0.88 at a prevalence of 10%.

::: solution
**Total.** $2+1+2+3+2+1+2+2+1+0 = 16$, above the cut-off.

**Next steps.** A positive screen leads to a clinical assessment of depression, anxiety, sleep, bonding with the baby, support, and safety. Item 10 is 0, but suicidal thoughts are still asked about directly. Many tired new mothers score highly, so the interview distinguishes depression from normal adjustment. If depression is confirmed, options include facilitated self-help, CBT or interpersonal therapy, and antidepressants where needed, with information on use in breastfeeding.

**PPV.** True positives $= 0.81 \times 0.1 = 0.081$; false positives $= 0.12 \times 0.9 = 0.108$; PPV $= 0.081/0.189 \approx 0.43$, about 43%. More than half of positive screens are false, which is why screening leads to assessment, not diagnosis.
:::
:::

### Medicines in pregnancy and breastfeeding

Decisions about psychotropic medicines in pregnancy weigh the risks of the medicine to the baby against the risks of untreated or relapsing illness to both mother and baby. Stopping effective treatment abruptly on discovering a pregnancy can cause relapse. Key principles:

- Plan before pregnancy where possible, with preconception advice for anyone of childbearing potential who takes psychotropic medication.
- Use the lowest effective dose of the drug with the best safety evidence, and avoid switching unnecessarily.
- **Valproate** must not be used in pregnancy for psychiatric conditions because of its high risk of malformations and neurodevelopmental harm. Under MHRA rules (January 2024), it should not be started in anyone under 55 unless two specialists independently agree there is no alternative, and those who could become pregnant must be on the Pregnancy Prevention Programme.
- **Lithium** in pregnancy needs specialist management, close monitoring of levels as kidney function changes, and planning for delivery.
- Information on specific medicines comes from specialist sources such as the UK Teratology Information Service and the BNF.

::: example A mother who is not herself {#ex-postpartum-psychosis}
Mrs Amara Okonkwo, 29, had her first baby eight days ago. Her husband calls the GP because she has not slept for three nights, is talking rapidly, believes the baby has been "swapped" in hospital, and at times seems confused. She has a history of a depressive episode at university. What is the likely diagnosis and what should happen?

::: solution
**Diagnosis.** This is probably **postpartum psychosis**: onset within two weeks of birth, severe insomnia, rapid speech, a delusion concerning the baby, and confusion. Her previous depression may have been part of an undiagnosed bipolar illness.

**Urgency.** This is a psychiatric emergency, because of the risks of suicide, harm to the baby (usually linked to delusions rather than intent), and rapid deterioration. Same-day assessment by the perinatal mental health or crisis team is needed.

**Rule out physical causes.** Infection, eclampsia, thyroid disease, anaemia, sleep deprivation-related delirium and drug effects should be considered.

**Management.** Admission to a **mother and baby unit**, where mother and baby can stay together safely, is preferred. Treatment usually involves an antipsychotic and sometimes a mood stabiliser, with attention to breastfeeding. Recovery is usually good, but the risk of recurrence after future pregnancies and of later bipolar episodes is high, so a written plan for future pregnancies is important.
:::
:::

## Old-age psychiatry

Old-age psychiatry deals with mental illness in later life, including the dementias. Physical illness, medication, sensory loss, bereavement and social isolation all shape presentations.

::: theorem The three Ds overlap {#thm-three-d}
In older people, **delirium**, **dementia** and **depression** each cause changes in cognition, mood and behaviour, and they often coexist. Delirium is acute and fluctuating with impaired attention; dementia is gradual and progressive with intact attention until late; depression causes subjective memory complaints and poor effort with often preserved performance when engaged. Because delirium is a medical emergency and depression is treatable, both must be actively sought in any older person with cognitive change.
:::

::: proof
The distinction follows from the mechanisms. Delirium is a disturbance of global brain function caused by an acute physical stressor (infection, drugs, metabolic disturbance, surgery). It impairs arousal and attention first, so it begins over hours to days and fluctuates through the day. Dementia results from progressive neurodegeneration or vascular damage. It erodes memory and other cognitive domains over months to years, while attention and alertness are preserved until late. Depression in later life impairs concentration, processing speed and motivation, so people may give "don't know" answers and complain of poor memory, but performance often improves with encouragement. Each condition also raises the risk of the others: dementia is the strongest risk factor for delirium, delirium can accelerate cognitive decline, and depression may be an early feature of dementia. A careful history from an informant about onset and course, an attention test such as the 4AT, a mood assessment, and a search for physical causes therefore form the core of assessment.
:::

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Acute (hours to days) | Gradual (months to years) | Weeks to months |
| Course | Fluctuating | Slowly progressive | Persistent, may vary with mood |
| Attention | Impaired | Usually preserved until late | Reduced concentration |
| Consciousness | Altered | Normal | Normal |
| Typical answers to tests | Inattentive, disorganised | Attempts, confabulates or covers up | "Don't know", poor effort |

### Depression in later life

Depression in older people is common and often missed, because it may present with physical symptoms, anxiety, memory complaints or withdrawal rather than sadness. It is linked to physical illness, pain, disability, bereavement and loneliness, and it increases mortality. Suicide rates are high in older men. Treatment follows the same principles as in younger adults, with attention to physical health, drug interactions, falls and hyponatraemia with SSRIs, and with ECT an effective option for severe or psychotic depression.

### Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia

::: definition Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia {#def-bpsd}
**Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia** (BPSD) include agitation, aggression, wandering, disturbed sleep, apathy, depression, anxiety, delusions and hallucinations. Many reflect unmet needs: pain, constipation, infection, hunger, thirst, boredom, fear, or a confusing environment.
:::

The first approach is to look for and treat causes and to use non-drug measures: person-centred care, structured activities, music, adjusting the environment and caregiver training. **Antipsychotics** increase the risk of stroke and death in people with dementia. NICE NG97 (2018) advises using them only when the person is at risk of harm to themselves or others or is experiencing severe distress from agitation, hallucinations or delusions, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time, with regular review. People with Lewy body dementia or Parkinson disease dementia can have severe, sometimes life-threatening, sensitivity reactions to antipsychotics.

::: example The cost of an antipsychotic {#ex-nnh}
A pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of atypical antipsychotics in dementia (Schneider and colleagues, 2005) found that over 10–12 weeks about 3.5% of people taking an antipsychotic died, compared with about 2.3% of those taking placebo. Calculate the absolute risk increase and the number needed to harm (NNH). How should this inform practice?

::: solution
**Absolute risk increase.** $0.035 - 0.023 = 0.012$, or 1.2 percentage points.

**NNH.** $1/0.012 \approx 83$. For roughly every 83 people with dementia treated with an atypical antipsychotic for about three months, one additional death occurred.

**Implications.** The benefit of antipsychotics for agitation in trials is modest. Against an additional death per 80 or so people treated, plus increased stroke risk, sedation and falls, antipsychotics should be reserved for severe distress or risk, used at low dose for short periods, and reviewed regularly, as NICE NG97 advises. Non-drug approaches come first.
:::
:::

### Late-onset psychosis

Psychosis beginning after the age of 60 is often linked to brain disease (dementia, especially Lewy body dementia, stroke, tumour), delirium, medication, sensory impairment (hearing loss is a recognised risk factor) or depression. A primary psychosis can also start late, often with persecutory delusions about neighbours or intruders in women living alone. Assessment includes cognitive testing, hearing and vision, and neuroimaging where indicated.

::: warning Do not assume that confusion in an older person is "just dementia"
A sudden change in an older person's thinking or behaviour, especially with drowsiness or fluctuation, is delirium until proven otherwise, even if they already have dementia. Delirium is common, frequently missed, and associated with high mortality. Check attention with a tool such as the 4AT, review medication, and look for infection, dehydration, urinary retention, constipation, pain and metabolic causes.
:::

::: quiz
A woman is seen four days after giving birth. She is tearful and irritable at times but sleeping when the baby sleeps, caring well for the baby and enjoying visitors. What is the most likely explanation?
- [x] Baby blues
- [ ] Postnatal depression
- [ ] Postpartum psychosis
- [ ] Bipolar disorder
::: solution
Brief tearfulness and emotional lability around days 3–5 after birth, without persistent low mood, loss of function, insomnia or psychotic features, are typical of the baby blues. They resolve within about two weeks. Persistence beyond two weeks or worsening symptoms would prompt assessment for postnatal depression.
:::

::: history
Child psychiatry emerged as a specialty in the 1930s, with Leo Kanner's textbook (1935) and his 1943 description of early infantile autism; Hans Asperger described a related group of children in Vienna in 1944. Michael Rutter's Isle of Wight studies in the 1960s gave the first rigorous estimates of childhood mental disorder. George Still described children with what would now be called ADHD in 1902. Louis-Victor Marcé published an early treatise on puerperal mental illness in 1858, and the Marcé Society for perinatal mental health is named after him. The first mother and baby units in the UK opened in the late 1940s and 1950s. Martin Roth's work in the 1950s distinguished depression and late-onset psychosis from dementia in older people, founding modern old-age psychiatry. In 1987 John Cox and colleagues published the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.
:::

- Half of lifetime mental disorders begin by the mid-teens; assessment in children considers development, family, school and safeguarding.
- Young people aged 16–17 are presumed to have capacity; younger children may be Gillick competent.
- ADHD, autism, intellectual disability and tic disorders are neurodevelopmental conditions; methylphenidate is first-line medication for ADHD in children, with monitoring of growth, pulse and blood pressure.
- Psychological therapy is first-line for depression in young people; fluoxetine may be added after specialist review.

- Treat BPSD by finding causes and using non-drug approaches first; antipsychotics in dementia increase stroke and mortality.

## Exercises

::: exercise Baby blues or depression? {level=1}
List three features that distinguish the baby blues from postnatal depression.
::: solution
Timing and duration: baby blues begin in the first few days and resolve within about two weeks, whereas postnatal depression lasts longer than two weeks and may begin later in the first year. Severity: blues are mild with preserved functioning, whereas depression involves persistent low mood or loss of interest with impaired functioning. Associated features: depression includes symptoms such as hopelessness, guilt, poor sleep even when the baby sleeps, and sometimes thoughts of self-harm.
:::
:::

::: exercise Delirium, dementia or depression? {level=1}
Give the typical onset, course and state of attention in delirium, dementia and depression.
::: solution
Delirium: acute onset over hours to days, fluctuating course, impaired attention and altered consciousness. Dementia: gradual onset over months to years, slowly progressive, attention usually preserved until late. Depression: onset over weeks to months, persistent course, reduced concentration but normal consciousness, often with "don't know" answers and poor effort.
:::
:::

::: exercise Gillick competence {level=1}
A 15-year-old asks her GP for treatment for anxiety and does not want her parents told. What must the GP assess?
::: solution
Whether she is Gillick competent for this decision: whether she has sufficient maturity and understanding to understand the nature, purpose, benefits and risks of the proposed treatment and the alternatives. The GP should encourage her to involve her parents but respect her confidentiality if she is competent, unless there is a safeguarding concern or serious risk of harm, which would justify sharing information with appropriate agencies. The assessment and reasoning should be documented.
:::
:::

::: exercise An EPDS total {level=2 check="13"}
A woman's EPDS item scores are 1, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 0. Calculate her total score.
::: solution
$1+1+2+2+1+2+1+2+1+0 = 13$. This is above a cut-off of 11 (and at the commonly used threshold of 13), so a clinical assessment for depression should follow. Item 10 is 0, but suicidal thoughts should still be asked about.
:::
:::

::: exercise Number needed to harm {level=2 check="1/(0.035-0.023)"}
Using death rates of 3.5% with antipsychotics and 2.3% with placebo over 10–12 weeks, calculate the number needed to harm. Give the exact value of $1/\text{ARI}$ (do not round).
::: solution
ARI $= 0.035 - 0.023 = 0.012$. NNH $= 1/0.012 \approx 83.3$. Conventionally this is reported as about 83 (some round NNH down to be conservative about harm). One additional death for every 80 or so people treated for three months.
:::

::: exercise Stimulant monitoring {level=2}
A 10-year-old starts methylphenidate for ADHD. Which measurements should be taken before starting and during treatment, and why?
::: solution
Before starting: height and weight plotted on a centile chart, heart rate and blood pressure plotted on a centile chart, and a cardiovascular history (palpitations, syncope, family history of sudden cardiac death), with an ECG or cardiology opinion if there are concerns. During treatment: height every six months and weight regularly (stimulants reduce appetite and may slow growth), heart rate and blood pressure after each dose change and every six months (stimulants can increase both), and review of sleep, mood, tics and appetite. Following NICE NG87 and local protocols.
:::
:::

::: exercise Perinatal planning {level=3}
A 32-year-old woman with bipolar I disorder, stable on lithium, is 8 weeks pregnant. She had postpartum psychosis after her first child. Outline a perinatal mental health plan.
::: solution
Refer urgently to the specialist perinatal mental health service. Do not stop lithium abruptly. Discuss the options: continuing lithium (with a small increase in risk of cardiac malformations, close monitoring of levels every few weeks as renal clearance rises in pregnancy, a fetal echocardiogram, and a delivery plan with level checks and fluid management because levels can rise sharply after birth), or switching to an antipsychotic with good pregnancy safety data, weighing relapse risk. Her risk of postpartum psychosis is very high given her history and bipolar disorder, so a written plan, shared with midwives, obstetricians, GP and family, should cover: early signs of relapse, protecting sleep after birth (night-time support), close contact in the first weeks, prompt access to a mother and baby unit, and decisions about feeding and medication after delivery. Involve her partner. Review the plan at intervals and make sure the maternity team knows to contact the perinatal service at delivery.
:::
:::

::: exercise A behaviour change in a care home {level=3}
An 84-year-old man with Alzheimer disease in a care home has become aggressive towards staff during personal care over the past week. The staff request "something to calm him down". Describe a structured approach.
::: solution
First look for causes, because new behaviour often signals an unmet need or physical illness: assess for delirium (attention, fluctuation), pain (for example from arthritis, pressure areas or dental problems), constipation, urinary retention or infection, new medication, poor sleep, hearing or vision problems, and depression. Look at the pattern: does aggression occur only during personal care? It may reflect fear, embarrassment, pain on movement, or misunderstanding. Non-drug measures: approach calmly, explain each step, use the same carers, allow more time, offer choices, adjust the timing, and treat pain. Involve his family to understand his history and preferences. Only if he is severely distressed or at risk of harming himself or others despite these measures should an antipsychotic be considered, at the lowest dose for the shortest time, after discussing the increased risks of stroke and death with him (as far as possible) and his family, and with review within about six weeks. Avoid antipsychotics if Lewy body dementia is possible. Document the reasoning.
:::
:::
