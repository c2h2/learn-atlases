This chapter covers two groups of conditions that are often misunderstood and sometimes treated with impatience. Eating disorders can look like a choice about food, yet anorexia nervosa has one of the highest mortality rates of any psychiatric disorder. Personality disorders can look like a matter of character, yet they are long-standing patterns of difficulty that cause great suffering and respond to the right help.

Both require doctors to combine careful physical care with a steady, respectful relationship. Both are made worse by stigma. The first half of the chapter covers the eating disorders and their medical risks; the second half covers personality disorders, with a focus on borderline personality disorder.

## Eating disorders

::: definition The main eating disorders {#def-eating}
- **Anorexia nervosa**: significantly low body weight for the person's height, age and stage of development (in adults, a BMI below 18.5 kg/m² in ICD-11), not explained by another condition, together with a persistent pattern of behaviour to prevent restoration of normal weight (restricting intake, excessive exercise, purging), usually driven by intense fear of weight gain and an overvalued concern with body shape or weight. ICD-11 adds a specifier for **dangerously low body weight** (BMI below 14 kg/m² in adults).
- **Bulimia nervosa**: frequent, recurrent episodes of **binge eating** (eating a large amount with a sense of loss of control) followed by repeated **compensatory behaviours** to prevent weight gain (self-induced vomiting, laxative misuse, fasting, excessive exercise), with excessive concern about shape or weight. Body weight is usually in or above the normal range.
- **Binge-eating disorder**: recurrent binge eating without regular compensatory behaviour, causing marked distress. It is the most common eating disorder and is often associated with obesity.
- **Avoidant–restrictive food intake disorder** (ARFID): restricted eating that leads to weight loss, nutritional deficiency or impaired functioning, driven not by concern about shape or weight but by, for example, sensory sensitivity to food, fear of choking or vomiting, or lack of interest in eating.
:::

Eating disorders affect people of all ages, sexes, ethnicities and body sizes. Anorexia nervosa most often begins in adolescence and is more commonly diagnosed in girls and women, but boys and men are affected and are diagnosed later. Eating disorders in people from ethnic minority groups and in people in larger bodies are often missed because they do not fit a stereotype.

### Causes and maintaining factors

Genetic factors contribute substantially; twin studies suggest moderate to high heritability for anorexia nervosa. Personality traits such as perfectionism, anxiety and obsessionality, adverse life events, and cultural pressure towards thinness all play a part. Dieting is a common trigger.

Once started, starvation itself helps maintain the illness. It causes preoccupation with food, rigidity, low mood, social withdrawal and slowed thinking, all of which make change harder. In bulimia nervosa, a cycle forms: strict dieting leads to hunger and loss of control, then a binge, then shame and compensatory behaviour, then renewed dieting.

### Physical effects

Starvation affects every organ system, and purging adds electrolyte disturbance.

| System | Effects |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Bradycardia, hypotension, postural drop, arrhythmia, QT prolongation, reduced cardiac mass |
| Metabolic | Hypoglycaemia; with purging, hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis; hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia |
| Endocrine and bone | Amenorrhoea, low sex hormones, delayed puberty, osteopenia and osteoporosis |
| Haematological | Anaemia, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia |
| Gastrointestinal | Delayed gastric emptying, constipation, raised liver enzymes; with vomiting, dental erosion, parotid swelling, oesophageal tears |
| Skin and other | Lanugo hair, dry skin, calluses on the knuckles from inducing vomiting (Russell's sign), hypothermia, proximal muscle weakness |

Blood tests can be normal even in severe illness, so a normal result never rules out danger. The Royal College of Psychiatrists' guidance **Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders** (MEED, 2022), which replaced the earlier MARSIPAN guidance, sets out a risk assessment for adults and young people. It rates several measures (BMI or percentage of median BMI, rate of weight loss, heart rate and blood pressure, ECG, blood tests, temperature, muscle strength and behaviour) as red, amber or green. For example, an adult BMI below 13 kg/m² is rated red. Several amber ratings together indicate high risk.

### BMI in children and young people

Because BMI changes with age during childhood, it is interpreted against growth references. The **percentage of median BMI** (%mBMI) compares a young person's BMI with the median BMI for their age and sex:

$$
\%\text{mBMI} = \frac{\text{BMI}}{\text{median BMI for age and sex}} \times 100
$$ {#eq-mbmi}

A %mBMI below about 70% indicates high risk.

::: example A referral from school {#ex-school-referral}
Chloe Patel, 15, is referred by her school nurse. She has lost weight over six months, stopped having periods, and runs every morning before school. She weighs 41.5 kg and is 1.65 m tall. Suppose the median BMI for a girl of her age is 20.0 kg/m² (an illustrative value; in practice it is read from the UK growth reference). Her heart rate is 44 lying down and 72 standing. Assess her risk and outline management.

::: solution
**BMI.** $41.5/1.65^2 = 41.5/2.7225 \approx 15.2$ kg/m².

**%mBMI.** $15.2/20.0 \times 100 \approx 76\%$. This is low, though above the 70% high-risk threshold.

**Other measures.** A resting heart rate of 44 is bradycardia, and a rise of 28 beats per minute on standing is a large postural change, suggesting cardiovascular compromise. Amenorrhoea indicates hormonal suppression and risk to bone. Compulsive exercise adds to risk.

**Overall.** She has probable anorexia nervosa with several concerning physical findings. She needs an urgent ECG, blood tests (electrolytes, phosphate, magnesium, glucose, full blood count, liver function), temperature and a sit-up/squat test of muscle strength, and same-day discussion with the paediatric and eating disorder teams to decide whether medical admission is needed.

**Treatment.** For children and young people, NICE recommends **anorexia-nervosa-focused family therapy** (FT-AN) as the first-line psychological treatment, delivered by a community eating disorder service. Her parents are central to restoring nutrition. Her school will need to adjust physical activity.
:::
:::

### Treatment

NICE guideline NG69 (2017, updated 2020) recommends psychological treatments tailored to the disorder and age:

- **Anorexia nervosa in adults**: individual eating-disorder-focused CBT (**CBT-ED**), the **Maudsley Anorexia Nervosa Treatment for Adults** (MANTRA) or **specialist supportive clinical management** (SSCM). In children and young people, FT-AN.
- **Bulimia nervosa**: in adults, guided self-help based on CBT first, then individual CBT-ED if needed; in young people, bulimia-nervosa-focused family therapy.
- **Binge-eating disorder**: guided self-help, then group or individual CBT-ED.

Medication is not a primary treatment for anorexia nervosa. Coexisting depression, anxiety and OCD are common and are assessed and treated in their own right. Weight restoration is essential, because many psychological changes of anorexia are effects of starvation and improve with nutrition.

### Refeeding syndrome

::: definition Refeeding syndrome {#def-refeeding}
**Refeeding syndrome** is a potentially fatal shift of fluid and electrolytes that can occur when nutrition is restarted after a period of starvation. During starvation, the body uses fat and protein for energy and intracellular phosphate, potassium and magnesium become depleted, although serum levels may be normal. When carbohydrate is reintroduced, insulin secretion drives glucose, phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells. Serum levels can then fall sharply, causing arrhythmia, heart failure, respiratory muscle weakness, confusion and seizures. Thiamine deficiency may also be unmasked.
:::

Prevention relies on identifying people at risk, measuring electrolytes before and regularly during refeeding, giving thiamine and other vitamins, and replacing phosphate, potassium and magnesium as needed. MEED guidance warns that **underfeeding** is also dangerous: starting nutrition too cautiously prolongs starvation and delays recovery. Refeeding is therefore done with careful monitoring and supplementation rather than with very low energy intake.

::: example Diabetes and weight loss {#ex-diabulimia}
Ms Hannah Brooks, 22, has type 1 diabetes. Her HbA1c has risen from 58 to 104 mmol/mol over a year, she has had two admissions with diabetic ketoacidosis, and she has lost weight. Her diabetes nurse finds she has been omitting insulin. She admits she is terrified of gaining weight. How should this be understood and managed?

::: solution
**Understanding.** Deliberately omitting insulin to lose weight is a form of compensatory behaviour. Without insulin, glucose is lost in the urine with its calories, so weight falls. This pattern, sometimes called "diabulimia", is not a separate diagnosis; it may form part of an eating disorder such as bulimia nervosa or an other specified feeding or eating disorder.

**Risks.** It greatly increases the risk of diabetic ketoacidosis, early microvascular complications (retinopathy, neuropathy, nephropathy) and death.

**Management.** Joint care between the diabetes team and the eating disorder service. Approach her with compassion, because shame is common. Avoid rapid tightening of glucose control, which can worsen retinopathy and trigger rapid fluid shifts; agree gradual, realistic insulin goals. Offer eating-disorder-focused psychological treatment. Screen for depression and suicidal thoughts. NICE NG69 and diabetes guidance advise that unexplained high HbA1c or recurrent DKA in a young person with type 1 diabetes should prompt questions about eating disorders and insulin omission.
:::
:::

### Screening

The **SCOFF** questionnaire asks five questions: Do you make yourself **S**ick because you feel uncomfortably full? Do you worry you have lost **C**ontrol over how much you eat? Have you recently lost more than **O**ne stone (6.35 kg) in three months? Do you believe yourself to be **F**at when others say you are too thin? Would you say **F**ood dominates your life? Two or more "yes" answers suggest a possible eating disorder. A meta-analysis found a pooled sensitivity and specificity of roughly 0.86 and 0.83.

::: warning A normal weight does not mean a low risk
Clinicians are often reassured by a BMI in the normal or high range. But people with bulimia nervosa usually have normal weight, and people who lose weight rapidly from a higher starting weight can have severe physical complications with a "normal" BMI. Assess risk from the rate of weight loss, purging behaviours, electrolytes, cardiovascular signs and the ECG, not from BMI alone. Never tell someone with a suspected eating disorder that they are "not thin enough" to have one.
:::

## Personality disorders

Everyone has a personality: a characteristic way of thinking, feeling and relating to others. A personality disorder is present when these patterns are rigid and cause persistent problems.

::: definition Personality disorder {#def-personality}
A **personality disorder** (ICD-11) is a long-standing disturbance in how a person experiences and interprets themselves, others and the world, shown in problems with **self-functioning** (identity, self-worth, self-direction) and **interpersonal functioning** (forming and keeping close, mutually satisfying relationships, managing conflict). The disturbance shows in maladaptive patterns of emotion, thinking and behaviour across many situations, has lasted for an extended period (typically two years or more), and causes distress or impairment.

ICD-11 rates **severity**: personality difficulty (not a disorder), and mild, moderate or severe personality disorder. It then describes prominent **trait domains**: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition and anankastia (perfectionism and rigidity), plus an optional **borderline pattern** specifier.

DSM-5-TR retains ten categorical personality disorders in three clusters: **cluster A** (odd or eccentric: paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal), **cluster B** (dramatic or erratic: antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic) and **cluster C** (anxious or fearful: avoidant, dependent, obsessive–compulsive).
:::

Personality disorder usually becomes apparent in adolescence or early adulthood, and many people improve over time, especially with treatment. About 1 in 20 adults may meet criteria for some personality disorder, and the proportion is much higher in mental health services and prisons. Personality disorder often coexists with depression, anxiety, eating disorders and substance use.

### Borderline personality disorder

**Borderline personality disorder** (BPD), also called emotionally unstable personality disorder, is the personality disorder most often seen in clinical services. Its features include:

- intense fear of abandonment and efforts to avoid it;
- unstable, intense relationships, alternating between idealisation and devaluation;
- an unstable sense of self;
- impulsivity in areas such as spending, sex, substances or eating;
- recurrent self-harm and suicidal behaviour;
- marked emotional instability, with rapid shifts of mood lasting hours;
- chronic feelings of emptiness;
- intense anger that is hard to control;
- transient, stress-related paranoid ideas or dissociation.

Causes include a temperamental tendency to intense emotional reactions combined with experiences that undermine emotional development, such as childhood abuse, neglect or an environment in which emotions were dismissed. Not everyone with BPD has a history of trauma.

People with BPD have a high risk of suicide. Their distress is real, and the label must never be used to dismiss it.

::: theorem Structure and consistency help; drugs do not treat the core {#thm-bpd}
The core features of borderline personality disorder respond best to structured, long-term psychological treatment delivered within a consistent, reliable relationship. No drug is licensed or recommended by NICE for the core features of borderline personality disorder, and polypharmacy causes harm.
:::

::: proof
Many difficulties in BPD arise in relationships: fear of abandonment, difficulty trusting, and emotional storms when others seem to withdraw. A treatment that offers a predictable framework (clear goals, regular sessions, an agreed crisis plan, a team that communicates) reduces the triggers for crisis and provides a setting in which new ways of relating can be learned. Effective therapies share this structure. **Dialectical behaviour therapy** (DBT) teaches skills for tolerating distress, regulating emotion, being mindful and handling relationships, and combines individual therapy, skills groups and telephone coaching. **Mentalisation-based treatment** (MBT) helps people understand their own and others' mental states, so that interactions are less often misread. Trials show that these therapies reduce self-harm and improve functioning. By contrast, drug trials for the core features have been small and inconsistent. In crises, medicines are often added one after another, leading to sedation, weight gain and dangerous amounts of medication at home. NICE CG78 (2009) therefore advises against drug treatment for BPD itself, while allowing short-term sedative use in a crisis (no longer than about a week) and treatment of coexisting conditions such as depression in their own right.
:::

::: example A frequent attender {#ex-bpd-spiral}
Ms Kayla Morgan, 24, has attended the emergency department eight times in two months after cutting her arms or taking overdoses following arguments with her partner. Staff describe her as "manipulative". She takes four psychotropic medicines started during different crises. How should her care be improved?

::: solution
**Change the language.** "Manipulative" is a stigmatising judgement. Her self-harm is a way of managing overwhelming emotion; it carries real risk and deserves a compassionate response.

**Each attendance.** Treat her injuries properly, with the same care and pain relief as for anyone else. Offer a psychosocial assessment each time, as NICE NG225 advises, rather than assuming nothing has changed.

**Review the diagnosis and plan.** If BPD is the working diagnosis, ensure she has been told and has had the chance to discuss it. Refer to the community mental health team or a specialist personality disorder service for a structured psychological therapy such as DBT or MBT.

**A crisis plan.** Develop with her a written crisis plan shared with the emergency department, GP and crisis team, describing triggers, what helps, and whom to contact.

**Medication.** Review all four medicines with the aim of reducing them; check for any that are dangerous in overdose, and limit quantities supplied. Treat any coexisting depression on its own merits.

**Consistency.** One named care coordinator, regular appointments not dependent on crisis, and good communication between services reduce the cycle of presentations.
:::
:::

### Antisocial personality disorder

**Antisocial personality disorder** is characterised by persistent disregard for and violation of the rights of others: deceitfulness, impulsivity, irritability and aggression, recklessness, irresponsibility and lack of remorse. DSM-5-TR requires evidence of conduct disorder before age 15. It is common in prisons and strongly associated with substance misuse. NICE CG77 recommends group-based cognitive and behavioural interventions for offending behaviour and states that people should not be excluded from health or social care because of the diagnosis or an offending history.

::: example The female athlete {#ex-female-athlete}
Ms Zara Ahmed, 19, a university athlete, presents with a stress fracture of the tibia. She has not had a period for eight months, trains twice a day, and eats a "clean" diet. Her BMI is 19.0 kg/m². She denies any concern about her weight. Discuss.

::: solution
**Recognise the pattern.** Amenorrhoea, a stress fracture and high training load suggest **relative energy deficiency in sport** (RED-S): energy intake is too low for the demands of training, leading to hormonal suppression and loss of bone density. Her BMI is normal, but that does not exclude low energy availability or an eating disorder.

**Assess.** Take a careful history of eating, weight change, exercise beyond training, compensatory behaviours, and thoughts about shape and weight. Screen for an eating disorder. Check heart rate, blood pressure and electrolytes, and arrange bone densitometry and endocrine assessment for the amenorrhoea.

**Manage.** Increase energy intake with a sports dietitian, adjust training with her coach and medical team, and monitor return of periods and bone health. If an eating disorder is present, refer to an eating disorder service. Explain that bone mass built in the late teens and twenties affects fracture risk for life.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 27-year-old woman with anorexia nervosa (BMI 12.5 kg/m²) is admitted and started on nasogastric feeding. On day 3 she becomes breathless and confused with a new arrhythmia. Which blood test result is most likely to explain this?
- [ ] Raised sodium
- [x] Low phosphate
- [ ] Raised calcium
- [ ] Low albumin
::: solution
This is refeeding syndrome. Insulin release after carbohydrate is reintroduced drives phosphate (together with potassium and magnesium) into cells. Severe hypophosphataemia causes respiratory and cardiac muscle weakness, arrhythmia and confusion. Electrolytes must be measured before and regularly during refeeding, and supplemented.
:::
:::

::: history
Richard Morton described a case of "nervous consumption" in 1689. In 1873 William Gull in London and Charles Lasègue in Paris independently described anorexia nervosa; Gull gave it its name. Gerald Russell described bulimia nervosa in 1979, naming the knuckle sign after himself. Hilde Bruch's work in the 1970s shaped the understanding of anorexia as a disorder of the sense of self and control. Family therapy for adolescent anorexia was developed at the Maudsley Hospital in the 1980s. For personality disorders, Adolph Stern introduced the term "borderline" in 1938. Marsha Linehan developed dialectical behaviour therapy in the 1980s and published her treatment manual in 1993; Anthony Bateman and Peter Fonagy developed mentalisation-based treatment in the late 1990s. In 2003 the English Department of Health's policy *Personality Disorder: No Longer a Diagnosis of Exclusion* challenged the practice of refusing care to people with this diagnosis.
:::

- Personality disorder is a long-standing disturbance of self and interpersonal functioning; ICD-11 rates severity and trait domains.

## Exercises

::: exercise BMI {level=1 check="round(10*36/1.6^2)/10"}
Calculate the BMI of a woman weighing 36 kg with a height of 1.60 m, to one decimal place, and say whether it falls in ICD-11's dangerously low range for adults.
::: solution
BMI $= 36/1.60^2 = 36/2.56 = 14.0625$, so **14.1** kg/m². This is just above the ICD-11 threshold for dangerously low body weight (BMI below 14 kg/m²), but is still very low and indicates high medical risk; the MEED framework would rate it amber, and other measures must be assessed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bulimia and potassium {level=1}
Explain why repeated self-induced vomiting causes hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis.
::: solution
Vomiting loses hydrochloric acid from the stomach, causing metabolic alkalosis. The loss of fluid causes volume depletion, which activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system; aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in the kidney. Alkalosis also shifts potassium into cells and increases renal potassium loss. Potassium lost directly in vomit contributes a little. The result is hypokalaemia, which can cause arrhythmia and muscle weakness.
:::
:::

::: exercise Clusters and domains {level=1}
Name the three DSM-5-TR personality disorder clusters with one example each, and list the five ICD-11 trait domains.
::: solution
Cluster A (odd or eccentric): paranoid, schizoid or schizotypal. Cluster B (dramatic or erratic): antisocial, borderline, histrionic or narcissistic. Cluster C (anxious or fearful): avoidant, dependent or obsessive–compulsive. ICD-11 trait domains: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition and anankastia.
:::
:::

::: exercise Percentage median BMI {level=2 check="round(100*14.2/18.6)"}
A 12-year-old boy has a BMI of 14.2 kg/m². Suppose the median BMI for his age and sex is 18.6 kg/m² (illustrative). Calculate his %mBMI as a whole number and comment.
::: solution
%mBMI $= 14.2/18.6 \times 100 \approx 76.3$, so **76%**. This is significantly underweight. It is above the high-risk threshold of about 70%, but risk depends also on the rate of weight loss, cardiovascular signs, blood tests and behaviour, which must be assessed.
:::

::: exercise Screening yield {level=2 check="round(100*0.86*0.05/(0.86*0.05+0.17*0.95))"}
Using a SCOFF sensitivity of 0.86 and specificity of 0.83, calculate the positive predictive value at a prevalence of 5%. Give the answer as a whole-number percentage.
::: solution
True positives $= 0.86 \times 0.05 = 0.043$. False positives $= 0.17 \times 0.95 = 0.1615$. PPV $= 0.043/(0.043+0.1615) = 0.043/0.2045 \approx 0.210$, so **21%**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Refeeding risk {level=2}
List four features that identify a person at high risk of refeeding syndrome, and outline the principles for preventing it.
::: solution
High-risk features include very low BMI, significant recent weight loss, little or no nutritional intake for many days, low potassium, phosphate or magnesium before feeding, and a history of alcohol misuse or drugs such as insulin, chemotherapy, antacids or diuretics. Prevention: measure electrolytes, glucose and ECG before feeding; give thiamine and a multivitamin; start nutrition with appropriate monitoring rather than very low intake (to avoid underfeeding); check phosphate, potassium, magnesium and glucose daily at first and replace them as needed; monitor fluid balance, heart rate and oedema; and involve a dietitian and the medical team, following MEED and local protocols.
:::
:::

::: exercise A diagnosis in dispute {level=3}
A 30-year-old man has been given four different diagnoses over ten years: depression, bipolar II disorder, ADHD and borderline personality disorder. He has rapid mood shifts lasting hours triggered by rejection, chronic emptiness, impulsive spending and repeated self-harm. Discuss how to distinguish bipolar disorder from borderline personality disorder, and why it matters.
::: solution
The key difference is the pattern of mood change. In bipolar disorder, mood episodes last days to weeks (hypomania at least four days), are a change from the person's usual self, and include sustained changes in sleep need and energy. In BPD, mood shifts are rapid, last hours, and are usually triggered by interpersonal events such as perceived rejection, against a background of chronic emptiness and unstable identity. His features fit BPD better, though the two can coexist, and ADHD can also contribute to impulsivity and needs separate assessment. It matters because treatment differs: bipolar disorder is treated with mood stabilisers, whereas BPD's core features respond to structured psychological therapy, and inappropriate mood stabilisers or antipsychotics expose him to side effects without benefit. A careful longitudinal history, collateral information and mood charting help. Whichever diagnosis is made should be discussed openly with him.
:::
:::

::: exercise A service design question {level=3}
An eating disorder service has a waiting list of six months and accepts referrals only for adults with BMI below 15 kg/m². Critique this policy using the principles in this chapter and current UK guidance.
::: solution
The BMI threshold excludes people with bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder and ARFID, and people with anorexia nervosa at higher weights or with rapid weight loss, many of whom are at serious medical and psychiatric risk. BMI is a poor marker of risk on its own; MEED emphasises a combined assessment of weight loss, cardiovascular signs, blood tests and behaviour. Long waits allow illness to become entrenched, whereas early intervention improves outcomes; national programmes in England have promoted rapid access to treatment for young people and early intervention for emerging adults with eating disorders. NICE NG69 states that people should not be excluded from treatment on the basis of BMI alone. A better policy: accept referrals by clinical need, triage by risk using the MEED framework, offer early intervention, provide guided self-help for bulimia and binge-eating disorder while people wait, and set clear shared-care arrangements with primary care for physical monitoring.
:::
:::
