Every doctor will meet people who have harmed themselves or who are thinking about suicide, in emergency departments, on wards, in general practice and in every specialty. How they are treated in those encounters matters. A compassionate, unhurried response can make it more likely that a person returns for help; a dismissive one can make it less likely.

This chapter covers what self-harm and suicide are, how common they are, why individual prediction is so difficult, how to assess and support a person in crisis, and what works to prevent suicide at the level of services and populations. In keeping with safe-messaging guidance, it does not describe methods or their lethality.

If you are affected by the topics in this chapter, support is available. In the UK and Ireland, Samaritans can be contacted free at any time on 116 123. Elsewhere, local crisis lines and emergency services can help.

## Definitions and language

::: definition Self-harm and suicide {#def-selfharm}
- **Self-harm**, as used in NICE guidance, is intentional self-poisoning or self-injury, **irrespective of the apparent purpose or motivation**. It includes acts with and without suicidal intent.
- **Suicidal ideation** means thoughts of ending one's life, ranging from passive wishes not to wake up to active thoughts and plans.
- A **suicide attempt** is a self-harming act carried out with at least some intent to die.
- **Suicide** is death caused by self-directed injurious behaviour with intent to die.
:::

NICE uses a broad definition of self-harm deliberately. The intent behind an act is often mixed, may change over time, and is hard to judge from outside. A person may harm themselves to manage overwhelming emotions, to punish themselves, to communicate distress, or to escape from an unbearable situation, and many people describe more than one reason. Treating some acts as "not serious" because they appear to lack suicidal intent is unsafe: self-harm of any kind is the strongest known risk factor for later suicide.

Language matters. Say "died by suicide", not "committed suicide", which implies a crime. Avoid "failed attempt" or "successful attempt". Avoid labels such as "attention-seeking" or "manipulative", which reflect frustration rather than understanding and can lead to people being denied care.

## How common are self-harm and suicide?

The World Health Organization estimates that about 727,000 people died by suicide worldwide in 2021. Suicide was the third leading cause of death among people aged 15–29, and most suicides occurred in low- and middle-income countries.

In England and Wales, 6,190 suicides were registered in 2024, a rate of 11.4 per 100,000 people. About three-quarters were men; the male rate was 17.6 and the female rate 5.7 per 100,000. Rates in the UK are highest in middle age. Self-harm is far more common than suicide, particularly among young people and especially young women. Most self-harm does not lead to hospital attendance.

Among people who attend hospital after self-harm, the risk of later suicide is greatly raised. A meta-analysis of follow-up studies found that roughly 1.6% died by suicide within a year, many times the rate in the general population.

## Why risk is hard to predict

Many factors are associated with suicide at the population level:

- previous self-harm, especially recent;
- mental illness, particularly depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, personality disorder and substance use disorders;
- male sex, middle age, and being divorced, widowed or living alone;
- unemployment, debt, and other financial difficulties;
- physical illness, especially chronic pain and conditions affecting independence;
- recent loss, relationship breakdown, bereavement (including bereavement by suicide), legal problems;
- hopelessness, and access to means;
- childhood adversity, being a victim of abuse or violence;
- certain occupations and groups: for example, people in contact with the criminal justice system, and some minority groups facing discrimination.

Protective factors include strong relationships, responsibility for children, a sense of purpose, religious or cultural beliefs about suicide, good problem-solving skills, and access to effective care.

::: theorem Low base rates defeat individual prediction {#thm-base-rate}
Because suicide is rare, even among people with several risk factors, any test or scale that classifies individuals as "high risk" or "low risk" has a low positive predictive value: most people classified as high risk will not die by suicide, and a substantial proportion of those who do die will have been classified as low risk. Risk factors describe groups; they cannot reliably say what one person will do.
:::

::: proof
The positive predictive value of any classification depends on prevalence as well as on sensitivity and specificity:

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sensitivity} \times p}{\text{sensitivity} \times p + (1-\text{specificity})(1-p)}
$$ {#eq-ppv-suicide}

Suppose that among people seen after self-harm, about $p = 0.016$ (1.6%) die by suicide within a year, and that a risk scale has a sensitivity of 0.8 and a specificity of 0.6, which would be a good performance for such tools. Per 1,000 people screened, about 16 will die by suicide; the scale labels $0.8 \times 16 = 12.8$ of them high risk and misses about 3. Of the 984 who will not die, it labels $0.4 \times 984 = 393.6$ high risk. The PPV is therefore $12.8/(12.8 + 393.6) \approx 0.031$, about 3%. Ninety-seven of every hundred people labelled high risk will not die by suicide in that year, while about one in five of those who do will have been labelled low risk. Systematic reviews of real risk scales have found performance of this kind or worse. A score cannot carry the weight of deciding who gets care.
:::

This is why the NICE guideline on self-harm (NG225, 2022) states that risk assessment tools and scales should not be used to predict future suicide or repetition of self-harm, and should not be used to determine who is offered treatment or who is discharged. It also advises against relying on global risk stratification into low, medium and high categories. Instead, the focus is on understanding the person's needs and on a plan to keep them safe.

## Assessing and supporting a person after self-harm

::: definition Psychosocial assessment and safety planning {#def-assessment}
A **psychosocial assessment** is a collaborative conversation, by a trained professional, that explores the person's circumstances, needs and mental health, the meaning and context of the self-harm, their current thoughts about suicide, the factors that make things worse or better, and what help they want. NICE NG225 recommends that everyone who presents to services after self-harm is offered one.

A **safety plan** is a short, personal, written plan made with the person. It typically lists: warning signs that a crisis may be developing; things they can do to help themselves; people and places that provide distraction or support; people to ask for help; professional and crisis contacts; and ways to make their environment safer.
:::

Key elements of good practice:

- **Respect and dignity.** Treat physical injuries with the same care, consent and pain relief as any other injury. Explain what is happening.
- **Physical treatment first where needed.** Medical treatment of self-poisoning or injury follows standard emergency care, with advice from poisons information services; it should never be delayed or withheld because the harm was self-inflicted.
- **Capacity.** If a person refuses treatment for self-harm, assess their capacity for that decision. A person with capacity can refuse; a person who lacks it may be treated in their best interests under the Mental Capacity Act. Urgent senior and legal advice may be needed.
- **Asking about suicide.** Ask directly and calmly. Explore current thoughts, plans, preparations, what has stopped them so far, and what they think will happen next. Asking does not increase risk.
- **Involving others.** With the person's consent, involve family, friends or carers, who often hold vital information and need support themselves. Where there is serious risk and the person declines, confidentiality may be breached in line with professional guidance, but this should be discussed and documented.
- **Follow-up.** Agree a plan for what happens next: who will follow up, when, and how the person can get help in a crisis. Communicate the plan to the GP.

::: example An adolescent after self-harm {#ex-adolescent-selfharm}
Maya Thompson, 15, attends the emergency department with her mother after cutting her forearm. The wounds are superficial. She says she has been self-harming for six months "to make the feelings stop" and that she was not trying to die. Her mother is angry and says she is "doing it for attention". How should the assessment proceed?

::: solution
**Treat the injury properly.** Clean and close the wounds with local anaesthetic, explaining each step.

**See her alone for part of the assessment.** Adolescents may not disclose bullying, abuse, sexuality, substance use or online harms in front of a parent. Explain confidentiality and its limits.

**Psychosocial assessment.** Explore the feelings she is trying to stop, what has been happening at home, school and online, her mood, sleep, eating, and any thoughts of suicide now or in the past. Ask about safeguarding concerns. Ask what helps.

**Support her mother.** Her mother's anger probably reflects fear and helplessness. Explain that self-harm is usually a way of coping with intense distress, not "attention-seeking", and that it is a sign Maya needs help. Give her information on how to respond and where to get support.

**Plan.** Make a safety plan with Maya, refer to the child and adolescent mental health service for follow-up, inform the GP and, with consent, the school's pastoral team. NICE NG225 recommends that children and young people who self-harm are assessed by professionals trained in their care, and that the risk of self-harm being repeated is not underestimated because the injury appears minor.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not judge the seriousness of intent from the seriousness of the injury
A minor injury may accompany strong suicidal intent, and a serious injury may follow an impulsive act with little planning. People also often misjudge the medical danger of what they have done. Assess intent through conversation about the person's thoughts before, during and after the act, and never use the physical severity of self-harm to decide that someone does not need a psychosocial assessment or follow-up.
:::

## High-risk times and groups

Some periods carry particularly high risk, and services can plan around them.

::: example The week after discharge {#ex-discharge-window}
Mr Ian Gallagher, 46, has been an inpatient for three weeks with a severe depressive episode after losing his job and separating from his wife. He is improving and is due for discharge on Friday to live alone in a flat. What should be in place?

::: solution
**Recognise the risk.** The National Confidential Inquiry into Suicide and Safety in Mental Health (NCISH) has consistently found that the risk of suicide is highest in the first weeks after discharge from psychiatric inpatient care, especially the first one to two weeks. He has several additional factors: male, middle-aged, recently separated and unemployed, and going home alone.

**Plan the discharge.** Avoid discharging on a Friday without weekend support where possible, or arrange specific weekend contact. Make sure follow-up is booked: in England, the standard is contact within 72 hours of discharge. Agree a written safety plan, including crisis contacts. Discuss practical problems (money, housing, work) and link him to support. With his consent, involve his family or friends.

**Medication.** Supply medication in appropriate quantities, and review whether any medicines would be particularly dangerous in overdose.

**Communicate.** Send a discharge summary to the GP and community team before or on the day of discharge, and make sure everyone knows who is responsible for the first contact.
:::
:::

Other high-risk times include the period after a first diagnosis of a serious physical or mental illness, after a bereavement, at times of legal or financial crisis, and after release from prison. Groups at particular risk in the UK include middle-aged men, people under the care of mental health services, people who self-harm, people with substance misuse, some occupational groups, and people bereaved by suicide.

::: example An early improvement {#ex-early-improvement}
Ms Lucy Evans, 33, started an antidepressant three weeks ago for severe depression. At review she seems calmer, brighter and more energetic, and says she has "made a decision" and feels "at peace". Her sister mentions that Lucy has been giving away some possessions. How should the doctor respond?

::: solution
**Do not be falsely reassured.** Improvement in energy before mood lifts, a sudden calm after a decision, and giving away belongings can indicate that a person has decided to end their life. Early in antidepressant treatment, energy may return before hopelessness lifts.

**Ask directly and kindly.** "You said you had made a decision. Can you tell me more about that? Have you been thinking about ending your life?"

**Respond to what she says.** If she discloses suicidal plans, this needs urgent action: same-day review by the mental health crisis team, consideration of a place of safety, involvement of her sister with consent, making the environment safer, and a clear plan for the next hours and days. If she lacks capacity or is at serious risk and declines help, mental health legislation may be considered.

**Lessons.** Antidepressants carry a warning about increased suicidal thoughts in young people early in treatment, and close review in the first weeks is advised for everyone. Information from family members is often crucial.
:::
:::

::: example An older man at the GP {#ex-older-male}
Mr Gerald Price, 78, recently widowed, attends his GP for a repeat prescription for pain from osteoarthritis. He says little but mentions he "doesn't see the point of anything any more" and that he "won't be a burden to anyone". How should the GP respond?

::: solution
**Take it seriously.** Older men have high suicide rates, and many see a GP in the weeks before death, often presenting with physical complaints. Bereavement, chronic pain, loss of independence and feeling a burden are important.

**Ask.** Gently and directly: "When you say you won't be a burden, have you had thoughts of ending your life?" Explore depression, loneliness, pain control, alcohol use and cognition.

**Act.** Treat depression if present (psychological therapy and, if appropriate, medication), improve pain management, connect him with bereavement support and community groups to reduce isolation, and involve family with consent. Review the quantity of medication supplied at home, especially medicines that are dangerous in overdose. Arrange early follow-up. If he has active plans, seek urgent mental health assessment.
:::
:::

## Prevention at the level of populations

::: definition Suicide prevention {#def-prevention}
**Suicide prevention** combines measures aimed at the whole population (universal), at groups at higher risk (selective), and at individuals who have shown suicidal behaviour (indicated). Effective components include restricting access to means, responsible media reporting, improving recognition and treatment of mental illness, follow-up after self-harm and discharge, support for people in crisis, and support for people bereaved by suicide (**postvention**).
:::

Evidence for population measures is strongest for **means restriction**. When a common method becomes less available, overall suicide rates often fall, because suicidal crises are frequently short and many people do not switch to another method. Examples include detoxification of domestic gas in Britain in the 1960s and 1970s, restrictions on highly toxic pesticides in several Asian countries, legislation in 1998 limiting pack sizes of a common over-the-counter painkiller in the UK, and safety barriers at known locations.

**Media reporting** influences suicide rates. Detailed reports of methods, or sensational and romanticised coverage, are associated with imitation, whereas stories of people coping with crisis may have a protective effect. Guidance from Samaritans and the WHO sets out safe reporting practices, which this chapter follows.

In England, the **suicide prevention strategy** (2023–2028) identifies priority groups, including middle-aged men, people who have self-harmed, children and young people, and people in contact with mental health services, and priority actions such as tackling online harms, improving support for people bereaved by suicide, and reducing access to means.

**Postvention** matters because people bereaved by suicide, including family, friends, colleagues and clinicians, have increased risk of suicide themselves and often feel isolated, guilty or stigmatised. Services such as Support After Suicide in the UK offer practical and emotional support.

### Looking after staff

Clinicians who lose a patient to suicide often experience grief, guilt and fear of blame. Organisations should offer support, protected time to reflect, and reviews focused on learning rather than blame. Doctors themselves have a raised suicide risk in some groups, and confidential support services exist for them.

::: history
For centuries suicide was treated as a crime or a sin in many countries. In England and Wales, suicide ceased to be a crime under the Suicide Act 1961, which made it an offence to assist suicide. Émile Durkheim's *Le Suicide* (1897) showed that suicide rates varied with social integration and regulation, establishing it as a subject for social science. Edwin Shneidman in the United States developed suicidology and the idea of "psychache", unbearable psychological pain. Chad Varah founded the Samaritans in London in 1953 as a telephone service for people considering suicide. Norman Kreitman in Edinburgh studied self-harm and, with colleagues, showed in 1976 how suicide rates fell when toxic domestic gas was replaced. The UK's National Confidential Inquiry into Suicide and Safety in Mental Health has collected data on suicides by people in contact with services since 1996. In 2012 Barbara Stanley and Gregory Brown published the safety planning intervention now widely used.
:::

::: solution
Self-harm is intentional self-poisoning or self-injury, irrespective of the apparent purpose or motivation. Intent is often mixed, changes over time and is hard to judge from outside; self-harm without suicidal intent still greatly raises the risk of later suicide; and defining some acts as "not real" self-harm leads to people being denied assessment and help.

Rewrite these phrases in non-stigmatising language: (a) "committed suicide"; (b) "attention-seeking overdose"; (c) "failed suicide attempt".
::: solution
(a) "Died by suicide" or "took their own life". (b) "Self-poisoning" or "an overdose as a way of coping with distress"; describe what happened rather than labelling motive. (c) "Suicide attempt" or "survived a suicide attempt".

::: exercise Components of a safety plan {level=1}
List six components of a safety plan.
::: solution
Warning signs of a developing crisis; internal coping strategies the person can use alone; people and social settings that provide distraction; people the person can ask for help; professional and crisis contacts (including services such as Samaritans and the local crisis team); and ways of making the environment safer. Many plans also include reasons for living.

::: exercise Predictive value {level=2 check="round(1000*0.75*0.01/(0.75*0.01+0.3*0.99))/10"}
A suicide risk scale has a sensitivity of 0.75 and a specificity of 0.70. In a population where 1% will die by suicide within the follow-up period, what percentage of people classified as high risk will die by suicide? Give your answer to one decimal place.
::: hint
Use [[#eq-ppv-suicide]] with $p = 0.01$.
:::
::: solution
True positives $= 0.75 \times 0.01 = 0.0075$. False positives $= 0.30 \times 0.99 = 0.297$. PPV $= 0.0075/(0.0075 + 0.297) = 0.0075/0.3045 \approx 0.0246$, so **2.5%**. About 97 or 98 of every 100 people labelled high risk will not die by suicide in that period.
:::
:::

::: exercise Missed by the scale {level=2 check="2.5"}
Using the same scale and population as the previous exercise, how many people per 1,000 screened will die by suicide despite being classified as low risk? Give your answer to one decimal place.
::: solution
Per 1,000 people, $1000 \times 0.01 = 10$ will die by suicide. The scale misses $1 - 0.75 = 0.25$ of them, so $10 \times 0.25 = $ **2.5** people per 1,000 who die will have been classified as low risk. This is why a "low risk" score must never be the reason someone is denied follow-up.
:::
:::

::: exercise Refusal of treatment {level=2}
A 28-year-old woman attends the emergency department after self-poisoning and refuses blood tests and treatment, saying she wants to leave. Outline the steps the doctor should take.
::: solution
Stay calm and try to understand her reasons; offer privacy and time. Explain the medical danger and the treatment in clear terms. Assess her capacity for the specific decision to refuse treatment: can she understand, retain and weigh the information and communicate a decision? Consider whether intoxication, a mental disorder or the overdose itself impairs this. If she has capacity, her refusal must be respected, but she should be encouraged to stay, offered a psychosocial assessment, given information on how to return, and the situation documented carefully; involve senior colleagues and, with consent, her family. If she lacks capacity, treatment can be given in her best interests under the Mental Capacity Act, using the least restrictive means, with senior input and clear documentation. Seek urgent senior, psychiatric and, if needed, legal advice; follow local policy.
:::
:::

::: exercise A service audit {level=3}
A mental health trust finds that 40% of its patient suicides occurred within three months of discharge, and that many patients were discharged on Fridays without a booked follow-up. Propose five changes, with the reasoning for each.
::: solution
(1) Ensure follow-up within 72 hours of discharge for everyone, because risk is highest in the first days and weeks. (2) Avoid Friday discharges without weekend arrangements, or set up proactive weekend contact, because services are thinner at weekends. (3) Make a collaborative written safety plan before discharge, shared with the GP and crisis team, so that the person and others know what to do if a crisis develops. (4) Involve families and carers in discharge planning with consent, because they often notice deterioration first. (5) Address practical stressors (housing, money, employment, substance misuse) before and after discharge, since these drive post-discharge crises. Also consider: reviewing medicines supplied, ensuring discharge summaries arrive promptly, monitoring and learning from each death without blame, and training staff in compassionate, needs-based assessment rather than risk scoring.
:::
:::

::: exercise Population strategy {level=3}
A health authority asks for advice on a local suicide prevention plan. Describe the main components, give examples of evidence-based measures, and explain why reducing access to means can lower overall suicide rates.
::: solution
Components: surveillance of local data (who, where, when) to target action; universal measures (means restriction, safe media reporting, school and workplace mental health programmes, reducing alcohol harm); selective measures for higher-risk groups (middle-aged men, people in contact with the criminal justice system, people with substance misuse, people bereaved by suicide); and indicated measures for people who have self-harmed or are in crisis (prompt psychosocial assessment, safety planning, follow-up after discharge, crisis services). Postvention for people bereaved by suicide is essential. Evidence-based measures include restrictions on access to commonly used means, physical barriers at identified locations, follow-up contact after self-harm and discharge, and working with media to follow safe-reporting guidance. Means restriction works because suicidal crises are often brief and ambivalent; if the immediate means is unavailable or harder to use, the crisis may pass, and many people do not substitute another method. National examples, such as the detoxification of domestic gas and restrictions on highly toxic pesticides, were followed by falls in overall suicide rates.
:::
