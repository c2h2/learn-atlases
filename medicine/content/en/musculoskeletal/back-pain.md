Low back pain is the single leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide, according to the Global Burden of Disease studies. Most adults have it at some point, and it is one of the commonest reasons for consulting a doctor and for time off work. Yet in the great majority of people, no specific structural cause can be found, and the outlook for an acute episode is good. The clinical task is therefore twofold: to pick out the small number of people with a serious or specific cause (cancer, infection, fracture, cauda equina syndrome, inflammatory disease), and to manage everyone else in a way that helps them recover and stay active rather than become disabled by pain and fear.

This chapter covers the anatomy that matters, a structured triage of back pain, the management of non-specific low back pain, sciatica and the nerve-root examination, cauda equina syndrome, spinal stenosis, serious specific causes, neck pain and the factors that turn acute pain into chronic disability. Management follows NICE guideline NG59 (low back pain and sciatica in over 16s) unless otherwise stated.

## The anatomy that matters {#sec-anatomy}

The lumbar spine has five vertebrae, each with a weight-bearing body in front and a bony arch behind, enclosing the spinal canal. Between the bodies lie the **intervertebral discs**. Each disc has a tough outer ring of fibrocartilage, the **annulus fibrosus**, around a gel-like centre, the **nucleus pulposus**. Behind, each pair of vertebrae articulates through two small synovial **facet joints**. Ligaments, including the ligamentum flavum lining the back of the canal, and the paraspinal muscles complete the structure.

The **spinal cord** ends at about the level of the L1–L2 disc in adults, as the **conus medullaris**. Below this, the canal contains the **cauda equina**, the bundle of lumbar and sacral nerve roots descending to their exits. The roots supplying the bladder, bowel and genitals (S2 to S4) run in the middle of the bundle.

Each lumbar nerve root leaves the canal through the intervertebral foramen **below the pedicle of the vertebra of the same number**: the L4 root exits between L4 and L5, the L5 root between L5 and S1. A typical **posterolateral disc herniation** at L4–L5 does not hit the L4 root, which has already left through the foramen above, but compresses the **L5** root as it travels down towards its own exit. A herniation at L5–S1 usually compresses **S1**. Far-lateral herniations are the exception, affecting the exiting root at the same level.

Many structures can generate back pain: discs, facet joints, sacroiliac joints, ligaments, muscles and the vertebral bodies. In practice it is rarely possible to say which one is responsible, and doing so seldom changes treatment. That is why the first step is triage, not anatomical diagnosis.

## Triage: three groups {#sec-triage}

::: definition The triage of back pain {#def-triage}
After history and examination, people with back pain fall into one of three groups:

1. **Non-specific (mechanical) low back pain**: pain in the lumbosacral region, which may radiate to the buttocks or thighs, without signs of nerve-root compression or serious disease. This accounts for the large majority, often quoted as about 90%.
2. **Radicular pain (sciatica)**: pain radiating below the knee in the distribution of a nerve root, often worse than the back pain, sometimes with numbness, weakness or reflex change. A minority of people with back pain, around 5 to 10%.
3. **Specific or serious pathology**: about 1 to 2% in primary care, including:
   - **cauda equina syndrome**;
   - **malignancy**: metastases (breast, prostate, lung, kidney and thyroid cancers commonly spread to bone) and myeloma;
   - **infection**: discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, spinal epidural abscess, tuberculosis;
   - **fracture**: osteoporotic vertebral fracture or trauma;
   - **inflammatory disease**: axial spondyloarthritis;
   - **referred pain** from outside the spine: abdominal aortic aneurysm, pancreatitis, kidney disease, peptic ulcer and gynaecological disease.
:::

The features that should raise suspicion of serious pathology are traditionally called **red flags**:

- **Cancer**: a history of cancer; unexplained weight loss; age over 50 with new pain; pain that is constant, progressive, worse at night or not relieved by rest; thoracic pain.
- **Infection**: fever; intravenous drug use; immunosuppression (HIV, steroids, chemotherapy); recent bacterial infection or spinal procedure; diabetes.
- **Fracture**: significant trauma; long-term corticosteroid use; known osteoporosis; older age with sudden onset after minor strain.
- **Cauda equina syndrome**: see [[#sec-ces]].
- **Neurological deficit**: progressive or widespread weakness, or signs of spinal cord involvement (gait disturbance, upper motor neuron signs).
- **Systemic illness**: feeling unwell, raised inflammatory markers, unexplained anaemia.

::: warning Red flags are not a checklist
Individual red flags have **poor accuracy**. Many, such as age over 50 or night pain, are common in people with ordinary back pain, so ticking a list produces large numbers of false alarms and unnecessary scans. Others are absent early in serious disease. The best single predictor of spinal malignancy is a **previous history of cancer**. Red flags work best as prompts to think, combined with the overall picture and its evolution over time: new neurological signs, failure to improve as expected, or worsening systemic illness should all prompt reassessment. A normal first consultation does not guarantee that nothing serious is present, so people need clear advice on when to return.
:::

## Non-specific low back pain {#sec-nslbp}

::: definition Non-specific low back pain {#def-nslbp}
**Non-specific low back pain** is pain between the lower ribs and the buttock creases, with or without referral into the leg above the knee, for which no specific cause (such as infection, cancer, fracture, inflammatory disease or nerve-root compression) has been identified. It is described as **acute** (under 6 weeks), **subacute** (6 to 12 weeks) or **chronic** (more than 12 weeks).
:::

Most acute episodes improve substantially within a few weeks, but recurrences are common and a minority of people go on to have persistent, disabling pain. Who these people are depends less on what the spine looks like than on psychological and social factors.

::: theorem Pain is not a measure of damage {#thm-pain-damage}
In back pain, the **intensity of pain and disability does not reflect the amount of structural damage** in the spine. Pain is produced by the nervous system in response to signals of potential threat, and is modulated by beliefs, mood, attention, sleep, previous experience and social context. Degenerative changes on imaging are common in people without pain and correlate poorly with symptoms.
:::

::: proof
Three lines of evidence support the principle. First, imaging studies of people **without** back pain show that "abnormalities" are the norm with age. A systematic review of MRI and CT in asymptomatic people (Brinjikji and colleagues, 2015) found disc degeneration in 37% of 20-year-olds rising to 96% of 80-year-olds, and disc bulges in 30% rising to 84%. Second, the factors that best predict which people develop persistent disabling pain are not imaging findings but **psychosocial** ones: fear that movement will cause harm, catastrophic thinking about pain, low mood, poor expectations of recovery, and work and compensation issues (sometimes called **yellow flags**). Third, **central sensitisation**, an increase in the responsiveness of nociceptive pathways in the spinal cord and brain, has been demonstrated in many people with chronic pain, explaining pain that persists or spreads beyond any tissue injury. Pain is real in all these people; it is the link between pain and damage that is weak.
:::

NICE NG59 sets out management of low back pain with or without sciatica:

- **Assessment**: exclude specific causes and consider alternative diagnoses at each review. **Risk stratification** with a tool such as the **STarT Back** questionnaire at first contact is considered, to match care to risk: simpler support (reassurance, advice to keep active, self-management) for people at low risk of a poor outcome, and more intensive support (exercise programmes, with or without manual therapy) for those at higher risk.
- **Imaging**: **not routinely offered** in non-specialist settings; in specialist settings, only if the result is likely to change management.
- **Self-management**: information about back pain and encouragement to **continue normal activities**. Bed rest is not advised.
- **Exercise**: a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind–body or a combination) is considered.
- **Manual therapy** (manipulation, mobilisation, massage) only as part of a package that includes exercise.
- **Not offered**: belts or corsets, foot orthotics, rocker-sole shoes, traction, acupuncture, ultrasound, TENS, PENS and interferential therapy.
- **Drugs**: an **oral NSAID** is considered, taking account of gastrointestinal, renal and cardiovascular risk, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time, with gastroprotection where appropriate. **Weak opioids** (with or without paracetamol) are considered for acute pain only if an NSAID is contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective. **Paracetamol alone is not offered**. **Opioids are not offered for chronic low back pain**. Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics) and **gabapentinoids or other antiepileptics are not offered** for low back pain.
- **Invasive treatments**: spinal injections are not offered for low back pain. **Radiofrequency denervation** is considered for selected people with chronic pain thought to arise from the facet joints, after a positive diagnostic medial branch block.
- **Work**: return to work and normal activities is promoted and facilitated.

NICE's earlier recommendations on psychological therapy and combined physical and psychological programmes were withdrawn in July 2026 after a surveillance review judged that they might be out of date; the importance of addressing fear, mood and expectations in people at high risk of persistent pain is not in doubt, but local guidance should be followed on how this is delivered.

::: example Acute back pain in a removals worker {#ex-acute}
A 38-year-old man felt sudden pain in his lower back while lifting a sofa two days ago. The pain is across the lower back and into the right buttock, worse on bending and eased by lying still. He has no leg pain below the knee, no numbness, no bladder or bowel change, no fever and no history of cancer. Examination shows paraspinal muscle spasm and reduced flexion, with normal leg neurology. He asks for an MRI "to see what's damaged" and for a sick note for a month. How would you manage him?
::: solution
This is **acute non-specific low back pain**: there are no features of radiculopathy, cauda equina syndrome or serious pathology.

**Imaging** is not indicated. Explaining why matters: an MRI would very likely show "degenerative" changes common in pain-free people of his age, which could alarm him without changing treatment ([[#thm-pain-damage]]).

**Explanation and reassurance**: most episodes like this improve considerably within a few weeks; the back is strong; hurt does not mean harm; gentle movement speeds recovery, while bed rest slows it.

**Stay active and plan return to work**: rather than a month off, discuss modified duties or a short absence with a planned return, because prolonged time off is itself a risk factor for chronic disability. A STarT Back questionnaire can identify whether he needs more than simple advice.

**Analgesia**: an oral NSAID such as ibuprofen at the lowest effective dose for a short period, if there are no contraindications. Paracetamol alone is not recommended. Heat may help.

**Safety-netting**: return urgently with numbness around the back passage or genitals, difficulty passing or controlling urine or stool, or new leg weakness; and return if the pain is not improving as expected, or new symptoms appear.
:::
:::

## Sciatica and radiculopathy {#sec-sciatica}

::: definition Radicular pain and radiculopathy {#def-radiculopathy}
**Radicular pain (sciatica)** is pain radiating from the back or buttock into the leg, typically below the knee, in the distribution of a lumbar or sacral nerve root. It is caused by irritation or compression of the root, most often by a **herniated intervertebral disc**, and less often by foraminal narrowing from degenerative change or spondylolisthesis. **Radiculopathy** means that there is also loss of nerve function: numbness, weakness or reduced reflexes in the territory of that root.

The roots most often affected, and their usual findings, are:

| Root | Pain and sensory loss | Weakness | Reflex |
|---|---|---|---|
| L3 | Anterior thigh to the knee | Knee extension, hip flexion | Knee jerk may be reduced |
| L4 | Anterior thigh to the medial leg and ankle | Knee extension; ankle dorsiflexion (tibialis anterior) | Knee jerk reduced |
| L5 | Lateral leg to the dorsum of the foot and big toe | Big toe extension; ankle dorsiflexion; hip abduction | Usually none reliable |
| S1 | Posterior leg to the sole and lateral border of the foot | Ankle plantarflexion; eversion | Ankle jerk reduced |

The **straight leg raise** (raising the straight leg reproduces leg pain below the knee between about 30 and 70 degrees) tests the L5 and S1 roots; the **femoral stretch test** (extending the hip with the knee flexed, patient prone) tests L3 and L4.
:::

The natural history of sciatica from a disc herniation is favourable. Herniated disc material is often resorbed by an inflammatory and macrophage response, and most people improve substantially over 6 to 12 weeks without surgery.

NICE NG59 advises that **gabapentinoids, other antiepileptics, oral corticosteroids and benzodiazepines are not offered** for sciatica, because there is no overall evidence of benefit and there is evidence of harm, and that **opioids are not offered for chronic sciatica**. NSAIDs may be used with awareness of their limited evidence and risks. **Epidural injections** of local anaesthetic and steroid are considered for acute, severe sciatica. Referral for **spinal decompression** (such as microdiscectomy) is considered when non-surgical treatment has not improved pain or function and imaging findings are consistent with the symptoms. Surgery gives faster relief of leg pain, although results at one to two years are broadly similar to continued conservative care in many trials. **Progressive or severe motor weakness** (such as a new foot drop) and **cauda equina syndrome** need urgent specialist assessment.

::: example Leg pain after gardening {#ex-sciatica}
A 44-year-old woman has had severe pain from her left buttock down the outer side of her calf to the top of her foot for two weeks, worse than her back pain, and tingling over the top of her foot. On examination, straight leg raising on the left reproduces leg pain at 40 degrees. Big toe extension and ankle dorsiflexion on the left are slightly weak (grade 4 out of 5). Knee and ankle reflexes are normal. Sensation is reduced over the dorsum of the left foot. Perianal sensation is normal, and she has no bladder or bowel symptoms. Which root is affected, what is the likely cause, and what is the plan?
::: solution
The pattern fits a **left L5 radiculopathy**: pain and sensory change over the lateral leg and dorsum of the foot, weakness of big toe extension and dorsiflexion, a positive straight leg raise, and normal reflexes (L5 has no reliable reflex). The likely cause is a **posterolateral disc herniation at L4–L5** compressing the traversing L5 root.

There are **no features of cauda equina syndrome**: she has unilateral symptoms, normal saddle sensation and normal sphincter function.

**Plan**: explanation of the good natural history; analgesia (an NSAID used with caution if suitable; not gabapentinoids or oral steroids, following NICE NG59); staying as active as possible; and physiotherapy. Mild weakness without progression can be observed, but she should be reviewed; if the weakness **worsens** (for example to foot drop), urgent specialist referral and MRI are needed. If severe pain persists despite conservative care, MRI and referral for consideration of an epidural injection or decompression are appropriate. She must be given clear **cauda equina safety-netting** advice, in writing if possible.
:::
:::

## Cauda equina syndrome {#sec-ces}

::: definition Cauda equina syndrome {#def-ces}
**Cauda equina syndrome (CES)** is compression of the lumbosacral nerve roots below the conus medullaris, usually by a large **central disc herniation**, and less often by tumour, infection, haematoma or severe stenosis. It produces some combination of:

- **bilateral** sciatica (or unilateral sciatica becoming bilateral);
- **saddle anaesthesia**: reduced or altered sensation in the perineum, buttocks and inner thighs;
- **bladder disturbance**: altered sensation of bladder filling, hesitancy, poor stream, then painless **urinary retention** with overflow incontinence;
- **bowel disturbance**: altered sensation, loss of control;
- **sexual dysfunction**: loss of genital sensation, erectile or ejaculatory dysfunction;
- reduced anal tone (a late sign).

It is described as **suspected** (CES-S: symptoms without objective deficit), **incomplete** (CES-I: altered bladder sensation, loss of desire to void or poor stream, but still voluntary control), and with **retention** (CES-R: painless retention and overflow). Outcomes are much better when surgery takes place before retention develops.
:::

Because outcomes depend on timing and the early symptoms are subtle, suspected CES requires **emergency MRI**. In England, the national suspected cauda equina pathway published by Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT) in 2023 sets the expectation of an MRI as soon as possible and certainly within four hours of the request, at any time of day, with immediate referral to the spinal surgical team if compression is confirmed for urgent decompression.

::: warning Do not wait for retention
The time to act is **before** the bladder fails. People are often unaware of subtle changes in bladder or saddle sensation unless asked directly. Everyone with back pain or sciatica should be asked specifically about saddle numbness, bladder and bowel function and sexual function, and given clear written advice to seek emergency care if these change. A bladder scan for post-void residual volume can support the assessment, but a normal result does not exclude early CES. Painless retention means the window for the best outcome has often already passed.
:::

::: example Back pain with numbness "down below" {#ex-ces}
A 39-year-old man with a three-year history of intermittent back pain has had severe pain down both legs for two days. Since this morning he has noticed numbness when wiping after opening his bowels, and he has had difficulty starting to pass urine. Examination shows reduced pinprick sensation in the perianal area and bilateral reduced ankle jerks. A bladder scan after voiding shows 350 mL residual. What is the diagnosis and what must happen now?
::: solution
This is **cauda equina syndrome**, at least **incomplete (CES-I)** with possible early retention: bilateral sciatica, **saddle anaesthesia**, **urinary hesitancy** with a large **post-void residual**, and bilateral S1 reflex loss. The likely cause is a large central disc herniation, probably at L4–L5 or L5–S1.

Actions:

1. **Emergency MRI** of the lumbar spine, as soon as possible and within four hours according to the national pathway, regardless of the time of day.
2. **Immediate referral to the on-call spinal surgical team**; if MRI confirms compression, urgent **surgical decompression** (discectomy).
3. Analgesia; catheterisation if in retention; careful documentation of perianal sensation, anal tone, lower-limb neurology and bladder findings, with times.

Delay worsens the chance of recovering bladder, bowel and sexual function. Cauda equina syndrome also generates many medicolegal claims, nearly all relating to delays in recognition, imaging or surgery.
:::
:::

## Spinal stenosis {#sec-stenosis}

**Lumbar spinal stenosis** is narrowing of the spinal canal or the lateral recesses and foramina, usually from degenerative change: disc bulging, facet joint hypertrophy and thickening of the ligamentum flavum, sometimes with degenerative spondylolisthesis. It typically affects people over 60 and causes **neurogenic claudication**: pain, heaviness, numbness or weakness in the buttocks and legs brought on by standing and walking and **relieved by sitting or bending forward**. Flexion enlarges the canal, which explains why people can often cycle, or walk leaning on a shopping trolley, much further than they can walk upright.

| Feature | Neurogenic claudication (stenosis) | Vascular claudication (peripheral arterial disease) |
|---|---|---|
| Brought on by | Standing as well as walking; walking downhill | Walking, especially uphill; not standing |
| Relieved by | Sitting or bending forward, over minutes | Stopping and standing still, within a minute or two |
| Cycling | Usually tolerated | Also causes pain |
| Pulses | Normal | Reduced or absent; low ankle–brachial pressure index |
| Skin | Normal | May show signs of chronic ischaemia |

Management starts with exercise (often flexion-based), analgesia and activity modification. Decompressive surgery is considered when symptoms substantially limit walking and quality of life despite conservative care, with MRI to define the anatomy. The two conditions often coexist in older people, so pulses and ankle–brachial pressure index are checked ([[cardiovascular/vascular-disease]]).

## Serious specific causes {#sec-serious}

**Metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC)** is an oncological emergency. It may be the first presentation of cancer. Warning features include thoracic or progressive back pain, pain worse on lying down or coughing, band-like radicular pain around the chest, and then limb weakness, sensory level, gait disturbance and sphincter problems. NICE NG234 (2023) asks services to provide MRI within 24 hours for people with suspected MSCC and neurological symptoms or signs, and recommends oral dexamethasone as soon as possible for people with neurological symptoms or signs, followed by definitive treatment with surgery or radiotherapy. The outcome depends on walking ability at the time of treatment.

::: example Back pain in a man with prostate cancer {#ex-mscc}
A 72-year-old man with prostate cancer treated two years ago has had mid-back pain for three weeks. It is constant, worse when lying flat and when he coughs, and now spreads around his chest like a band. Since yesterday his legs have felt heavy and he stumbled twice. Examination shows tenderness over T8, mild weakness of both legs, brisk knee reflexes and reduced pinprick sensation below the umbilicus. What is the likely diagnosis and what should happen now?
::: solution
The likely diagnosis is **metastatic spinal cord compression** at about the T8–T10 level: a history of cancer that commonly spreads to bone, thoracic pain that is worse lying down and on coughing, band-like radicular pain, and new **upper motor neuron** signs with a sensory level. This is an oncological emergency.

Actions, following NICE NG234 and the local MSCC pathway:

1. **Contact the MSCC coordinator or acute oncology team immediately**.
2. **Urgent MRI of the whole spine**, within 24 hours (sooner if needed to start treatment), because metastases are often present at more than one level.
3. **Dexamethasone** as soon as possible for people with neurological symptoms or signs, with gastroprotection and blood glucose monitoring.
4. **Analgesia**, and advice on positioning and spinal stability until imaging is reviewed.
5. **Definitive treatment** decided quickly by the multidisciplinary team: surgical decompression and stabilisation, or radiotherapy, according to his overall condition and the spine's stability.

The speed matters because people who are still walking when treatment starts are much more likely to keep walking afterwards. Earlier recognition, when he had only constant thoracic pain and a history of cancer, would have been better still.
:::
:::

**Spinal infection** (discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, epidural abscess) causes progressive, constant pain with local tenderness, often with fever, in people who inject drugs, have diabetes, are immunosuppressed or have had a recent bacteraemia or spinal procedure. Inflammatory markers are usually raised; MRI is the key test, and blood cultures and often biopsy identify the organism (most often *Staphylococcus aureus*; tuberculosis in endemic settings or high-risk groups). Epidural abscess can cause rapid paralysis.

**Osteoporotic vertebral fracture** causes sudden, severe thoracic or lumbar pain in an older person, sometimes after trivial strain, and often loss of height and kyphosis. Many vertebral fractures are not diagnosed. Each is a fragility fracture that should prompt osteoporosis assessment ([[musculoskeletal/bones-joints]]).

**Axial spondyloarthritis** causes inflammatory back pain in young adults and is described in [[musculoskeletal/inflammatory-arthritis]].

::: quiz
A 73-year-old man has pain in both buttocks and thighs after walking about 200 metres. He has to sit down for several minutes to get relief, and he can walk further pushing a supermarket trolley. Foot pulses are easily palpable. What is the most likely cause?
- [ ] Peripheral arterial disease
- [x] Lumbar spinal stenosis
- [ ] Acute L5 disc herniation
- [ ] Cauda equina syndrome
::: solution
Pain brought on by walking and relieved by sitting or flexing forward (the "shopping trolley" sign), with normal pulses, is typical of **neurogenic claudication** from lumbar spinal stenosis. In vascular claudication, standing still relieves the pain quickly and pulses are usually reduced. An acute disc herniation causes radicular pain that is not specifically related to walking distance, and cauda equina syndrome involves saddle and sphincter disturbance.
:::
:::

## Neck pain {#sec-neck}

Most neck pain is non-specific and managed on the same principles as low back pain: explanation, staying active, exercise and short-term analgesia. **Whiplash-associated disorder** after a road traffic collision usually improves over weeks; imaging is reserved for people who meet validated criteria for possible fracture (such as the Canadian C-spine rule) in the acute setting.

**Cervical radiculopathy** causes arm pain in a root distribution: C5 (shoulder and lateral upper arm; deltoid weakness; biceps reflex), C6 (lateral forearm and thumb; elbow flexion and wrist extension; biceps and brachioradialis reflexes), C7 (middle finger; triceps and wrist flexion; triceps reflex), C8 (little finger and medial forearm; finger flexion and hand intrinsics).

**Degenerative cervical myelopathy** is compression of the cervical spinal cord, usually by degenerative change in older adults. It is easily missed because it develops slowly. Features include clumsy, numb hands (difficulty with buttons and handwriting), an unsteady gait, and on examination **upper motor neuron signs**: hyperreflexia, a positive Hoffmann sign, upgoing plantar responses and clonus. It needs MRI and referral to a spinal surgeon, because decompression can halt progression but rarely reverses established deficits. People with **rheumatoid arthritis** can develop atlantoaxial subluxation, with similar consequences.

::: history
For most of the nineteenth century, sciatica was thought to be an inflammation of the sciatic nerve itself. The straight leg raise test is associated with the French physician **Charles Lasègue**, whose 1864 paper on sciatica inspired the sign later described in detail by his pupil **J. J. Forst**. In 1934 **William Mixter** and **Joseph Barr** in Boston showed in the *New England Journal of Medicine* that rupture of the intervertebral disc compressed nerve roots and could be relieved by surgery, beginning what has been called "the dynasty of the disc". The Scottish orthopaedic surgeon **Gordon Waddell** argued in 1987 for a biopsychosocial model of back pain, pointing out that rising disability from back pain could not be explained by any change in spinal pathology. In 2011 **Jonathan Hill**, **Nadine Foster** and colleagues at Keele University showed in a randomised trial in *The Lancet* that stratifying patients with the STarT Back tool and matching treatment to risk improved disability outcomes and reduced costs.
:::

::: summary
- Triage back pain into non-specific pain (most), radicular pain, and specific or serious pathology (about 1–2%): cauda equina syndrome, cancer, infection, fracture, inflammatory disease and referred pain.
- Red flags prompt thought rather than automatic imaging; a history of cancer is the most informative. Reassess when the course is unexpected.
- Pain does not measure damage: degenerative imaging findings are common without pain, and psychosocial factors predict chronic disability better than imaging.
- NICE NG59: no routine imaging in non-specialist settings; advice to stay active; exercise programmes; NSAIDs if suitable; not paracetamol alone; no opioids for chronic back pain; no antidepressants or gabapentinoids for back pain; no gabapentinoids, oral steroids or benzodiazepines for sciatica.
- Most sciatica from a disc herniation improves over 6–12 weeks. An L4–L5 posterolateral herniation compresses L5; L5–S1 compresses S1. Learn the root table.
- Cauda equina syndrome (bilateral sciatica, saddle anaesthesia, bladder, bowel or sexual dysfunction) needs emergency MRI, within four hours on the national pathway, and urgent decompression. Do not wait for retention; safety-net everyone.
- Neurogenic claudication from spinal stenosis is relieved by sitting or bending forward and spares the pulses.
- Metastatic cord compression, spinal infection and vertebral fracture are the main serious specific causes. In the neck, look for myelopathy: clumsy hands, gait disturbance and upper motor neuron signs.
:::

::: exercise Neurogenic or vascular? {level=2}
A 69-year-old smoker with diabetes describes calf pain on walking 100 metres uphill, relieved within a minute of standing still. He can stand for long periods without trouble and cycling also causes calf pain. Which type of claudication is this, and what examination and test would support it?
::: solution
This is **vascular claudication** from peripheral arterial disease: pain with walking (worse uphill), relief by standing still within a minute or two, pain with cycling and no problem on standing. He has major risk factors (smoking, diabetes). Examination would look for **reduced or absent foot pulses**, signs of chronic ischaemia and bruits; the key test is the **ankle–brachial pressure index**. Management includes cardiovascular risk reduction and supervised exercise ([[cardiovascular/vascular-disease]]).
:::

::: exercise Post-test probability of cancer {level=2 check="round(100*(0.007/0.993*15)/(1+0.007/0.993*15))"}
Assume, for illustration, that 0.7% of people consulting in primary care with back pain have spinal malignancy, and that a past history of cancer has a positive likelihood ratio of 15. Calculate the probability of spinal malignancy in a person with back pain and a past history of cancer, as a percentage rounded to the nearest whole number.
::: hint
Convert the pretest probability to odds, multiply by the likelihood ratio, then convert back to a probability.
:::
::: solution
Pretest odds $= 0.007/0.993 \approx 0.00705$. Post-test odds $= 0.00705 \times 15 \approx 0.1057$. Probability $= 0.1057/1.1057 \approx 0.096$, so about **10%**. A history of cancer turns a very unlikely diagnosis into a real possibility that must be investigated, but even so, most such people do not have spinal metastases. This is why red flags guide further assessment rather than automatically confirming disease.
:::
:::

::: exercise A clumsy pianist {level=2}
A 64-year-old retired music teacher has had difficulty playing the piano and doing up buttons for a year, and feels unsteady when walking in the dark. Her neck is stiff but not very painful. Examination shows brisk reflexes in all four limbs, a positive Hoffmann sign and upgoing plantar responses. What is the likely diagnosis, and why is it important not to attribute her symptoms to arthritis in her hands?
::: solution
The likely diagnosis is **degenerative cervical myelopathy**: cord compression in the neck producing clumsy, numb hands, gait unsteadiness (worse in the dark because of impaired proprioception) and **upper motor neuron signs** in arms and legs. Attributing hand symptoms to arthritis or carpal tunnel syndrome is a common cause of delay. Because the condition is progressive and decompression mainly prevents further deterioration rather than reversing established deficits, she needs **MRI of the cervical spine** and **referral to a spinal surgeon**. She should be advised to avoid activities with a high risk of neck trauma until assessed.
:::
:::

::: exercise A febrile back {level=3}
A 46-year-old man who injects heroin presents with three weeks of worsening constant lower back pain that keeps him awake. Today his legs feel weak. Temperature is 38.4 °C. There is marked tenderness over L3. Power in both legs is 4 out of 5. CRP is 186 mg/L. (a) What is the most likely diagnosis? (b) What investigations are needed, and how urgently? (c) What organism is most likely?
::: hint
Think about the combination of injecting drug use, fever, focal tenderness and a new neurological deficit.
:::
::: solution
(a) **Spinal infection**: vertebral osteomyelitis or discitis with a **spinal epidural abscess**, suggested by injecting drug use (bacteraemia), fever, constant progressive pain, focal tenderness, a very high CRP and new bilateral leg weakness.

(b) **Emergency MRI of the whole spine** with contrast, today, because epidural abscess with a neurological deficit can progress to paralysis within hours. **Blood cultures** (several sets) before antibiotics if this does not delay treatment; full blood count, renal function, HIV and hepatitis serology; **echocardiography** to look for endocarditis. Urgent referral to the spinal surgical team for decompression and drainage. Biopsy of disc or bone if blood cultures are negative.

(c) ***Staphylococcus aureus*** (including MRSA in some settings) is the most common organism. Empirical intravenous antibiotics follow local guidance, with a prolonged course guided by culture results.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why did it become chronic? {level=3}
A 41-year-old warehouse worker hurt his back lifting 14 months ago. He has not worked since. He believes his spine is "crumbling" because an MRI report described "multilevel degenerative disc disease". He takes codeine and pregabalin daily, sleeps poorly, rarely leaves the house and is in a dispute with his employer. Neurological examination is normal. (a) Identify the factors that are maintaining his disability. (b) Which of his medicines does NICE advise against for low back pain, and why? (c) Outline a management approach.
::: hint
Look for beliefs, behaviour, mood, medicines and social factors, and use [[#thm-pain-damage]].
:::
::: solution
(a) **Maintaining factors**: a frightening interpretation of normal age-related MRI findings ("crumbling spine"); fear of movement and avoidance, leading to deconditioning; prolonged absence from work and an employment dispute; poor sleep and probable low mood; and reliance on medicines that are unlikely to help. These are "yellow flags": psychosocial predictors of persistent disability.

(b) NICE NG59 advises **against opioids for chronic low back pain** (codeine), because evidence of benefit is lacking and the risks of dependence and harm are substantial; and **against gabapentinoids** (pregabalin) for low back pain, for lack of benefit and evidence of harm, including dependence and misuse. Any reduction must be planned and gradual, with explanation, following NICE guidance on medicines associated with dependence or withdrawal symptoms.

(c) **Management**: re-explain his MRI findings honestly (disc changes are very common in people without pain and do not mean his spine is crumbling); agree goals focused on function; a graded **exercise programme**, ideally group-based; address sleep and mood (screen for depression and treat if present); a structured, gradual plan to reduce codeine and pregabalin; and **vocational support** to plan a phased return to work, involving occupational health. Where available, a multidisciplinary pain or rehabilitation service that addresses physical, psychological and work factors together is appropriate. The aim is function and participation, not a pain score of zero.
:::
