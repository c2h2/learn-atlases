Back and neck pain is medicine's largest single source of disability and its most common complaint, and it is managed best by a paradox: **do a careful enough history to exclude the dangerous few, then resist the urge to find something, because in nine out of ten patients the problem is the spine's ordinary mechanics and the treatment is reassurance, movement and time.** The skill is not imaging, and never has been imaging; the skill is the red-flag interview, the neurological examination that means something, and the communication that prevents a normal ache from becoming a disabled life - a conversation every clinician has several times a week

This chapter covers mechanical back and neck pain, nerve-root and cord syndromes, the specific spinal diseases, and the chronic-pain system around them.

## The chronic pain system

Between five and eight per cent of people with back pain develop long-standing disabling pain, and the predictors are rarely spinal. The yellow-flag literature is now plain medicine: fear of movement and pain catastrophising predict disability better than MRI findings; belief that the spine is damaged and dangerous drives avoidance, deconditioning, opioid dependence and benefit withdrawal; and the single strongest occupational predictor of prolonged absence is the patient's own expectation of returning to work, shaped more by workplace culture, job satisfaction and compensation design than by lesion severity. This is not a claim that the pain is imaginary - nociceptive, inflammatory and nerve-related pain are real, and central amplification is a physiological phenomenon described in [[musculoskeletal/bones-joints]] and [[psychiatry/anxiety-disorders]] - but that successful management treats function as the endpoint and works through the psychological, sleep, mood, work and social layers alongside the tissue.

The service consequences follow. Stratified care - matching intensity of support to risk of chronicity using simple screening at first contact - outperforms one-size-fits-all physiotherapy; multidisciplinary rehabilitation for established disabling pain has a modest but real evidence base where opioids have none; and the conversations that matter most happen early, in plain language, with a named review date: how ordinary back pain behaves, what the scan does and does not mean, why movement is medicine, what the workplace can realistically change, and when re-assessment is warranted because the trajectory has slipped off the expected curve. The clinician's most powerful chronic-pain drug is an accurate, non-threatening explanation delivered well, and the referral that is never wrong is the one prompted by a changed story rather than a changed scan, and the most powerful diagnostic instrument is noticing, at follow-up, that this patient's story is no longer the story of a back.

Two groups deserve deliberate over-service in an under-service field. Older adults accumulate spinal disease silently - the stenosis blamed on "old age", the fracture never imaged after a "bad turn", the myelopathy attributed to "just unsteady" - and a fall, a height loss or a new hand-clumsiness in an octogenarian back-pain patient is a history question, not an age fact, and asking it costs ninety seconds. And the patients whose pain will not resolve need their diagnosis re-opened on a schedule, not defended: the sacroiliac septic joint that was "mechanical" for a month, the lymphoma that presented as "growing pains", the cauda equina whose bladder symptoms were read as retention from opioids. A pathway that is fast to reassure must be equally fast to re-question.

Recurrence is the other half of the epidemiology: about half of people who improve will flare again within a year or two, which is why the episode should be used as an education appointment - the person who has learned their own triggers, their own exercises and their own red flags self-manages the next flare in days instead of starting the imaging-referral-surgery cascade again. Prevention programmes that teach self-management and graded activity, and workplaces that keep modified duties available after any episode, are the only interventions proven to bend the recurrence curve, and both are cheaper than one MRI.

One safety footnote deserves repetition because it kills the occasionally: high-velocity manipulation of the neck carries a small risk of vertebral and carotid artery injury, and it is contraindicated outright in suspected instability, cord compression, fracture, infection, tumour, and connective-tissue and vascular disorders - the rheumatoid atlantoaxial joint and the os odontoideum belong on every referrer's exclusion list before anyone "cracks" a neck, and unwell patients sent to manipulation are a systems failure, not a therapist's error.

::: summary
- Non-specific mechanical pain is the diagnosis in ~85 %; the clinician's value lies in the small specific slice that needs different medicine.
- Red flags are interview findings, not blood tests: cancer history, night pain, infection context, trauma, and the bladder and saddle questions that gate emergencies.
- Imaging early usually harms: incidental degeneration labels normal ageing, and labels change behaviour, insurance and expectations.
- Radiculopathy mostly resolves; cauda equina and progressive motor loss are the exceptions the whole system is designed to catch.
- The neck hides dangerous mimics - myelopathy, tumour, plexopathy, vertebral artery symptoms - so the arm-pain examination is reflexes and gait as much as Spurling.
- Movement - walking, cycling, swimming, graded strength work - is the disease-modifying drug; the prescription, the follow-up and the workplace plan are its dose, review and refill.
:::
