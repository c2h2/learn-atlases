In inflammatory arthritis the problem is not the mechanics of the joint but the immune system or crystals attacking it. The synovium, normally a thin lining a few cells deep, becomes a thick, inflamed tissue full of immune cells and new blood vessels, and the inflammation can erode cartilage and bone within months. Unlike osteoarthritis, these diseases can affect the whole body, and unlike osteoarthritis, they respond dramatically to the right treatment. That combination makes speed of recognition the most important clinical skill in this chapter.

The chapter covers three groups: **rheumatoid arthritis**, the commonest autoimmune inflammatory arthritis; the **spondyloarthritides**, which share a link with HLA-B27 and a tendency to inflame entheses and the spine; and the **crystal arthritides**, gout and calcium pyrophosphate deposition. It builds on the joint anatomy and the inflammatory–mechanical distinction in [[musculoskeletal/bones-joints]] and on the immunology of [[immunology/autoimmunity]].

## Rheumatoid arthritis {#sec-ra}

::: definition Rheumatoid arthritis {#def-ra}
**Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)** is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease whose main feature is a **persistent, symmetrical, inflammatory polyarthritis** of the small joints of the hands and feet, which, if untreated, causes erosion of cartilage and bone, deformity and disability.

It affects roughly 0.5 to 1% of adults, women about two to three times as often as men, with onset most often between 30 and 60 years of age. Most people have autoantibodies: **rheumatoid factor (RF)**, an antibody (usually IgM) against the Fc portion of IgG, and **anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies**, also called anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA). RF is present in about 70% and is not specific (it occurs in other autoimmune diseases, chronic infections and some healthy older people). Anti-CCP antibodies have similar sensitivity but are much more specific for RA, typically above 95%.
:::

### Causes and mechanism

RA arises from an interaction of genes and environment. The strongest genetic risk lies in certain alleles of **HLA-DRB1** that share a sequence in the peptide-binding groove (the "shared epitope"). The best-established environmental risk is **smoking**, which promotes **citrullination** (conversion of arginine to citrulline in proteins) in the lungs. In genetically susceptible people, citrullinated proteins are treated as foreign and anti-CCP antibodies appear, often years before any joint symptoms.

In the joint, activated T cells, B cells and macrophages drive the synovium to proliferate. Synovial fibroblasts become invasive, and the thickened synovium, called **pannus**, grows over and into the cartilage and bone at the joint margins. The cytokines **tumour necrosis factor (TNF)**, **interleukin-6 (IL-6)** and **interleukin-1** drive inflammation and stimulate osteoclasts through RANKL, producing **erosions** where synovium meets bone. IL-6 also acts on the liver, producing the raised CRP, fatigue and anaemia of chronic disease. Each of these steps has become a drug target.

### Clinical features

Typically the onset is over weeks to months, with pain, swelling and **prolonged morning stiffness** (often more than an hour) in the **metacarpophalangeal (MCP)** and **proximal interphalangeal (PIP)** joints, the wrists and the **metatarsophalangeal (MTP)** joints, usually symmetrically. Squeezing across the MCPs or MTPs is painful. The distal interphalangeal joints are characteristically spared. Larger joints such as knees, shoulders, elbows and ankles may also be affected. Fatigue, low-grade fever and weight loss are common.

Untreated, longstanding disease produces characteristic deformities: **ulnar deviation** of the fingers at the MCPs, **swan-neck** and **boutonnière** deformities, and the **Z-shaped thumb**. In the neck, **atlantoaxial subluxation** can compress the spinal cord, which matters during intubation and general anaesthesia.

RA is a systemic disease. **Extra-articular features** include rheumatoid nodules, **interstitial lung disease** and pleural effusions, **scleritis** and episcleritis, secondary **Sjögren's syndrome** (dry eyes and mouth), anaemia, Felty's syndrome (RA with splenomegaly and neutropenia), rheumatoid vasculitis, **osteoporosis**, and a substantially increased risk of **cardiovascular disease**, which is the main cause of excess mortality. These features are now less common because inflammation is controlled earlier.

::: definition The 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria {#def-ra-criteria}
The American College of Rheumatology and EULAR classification criteria are applied to a person with at least one joint with definite clinical synovitis that is not better explained by another disease. Scores from four domains are added; a total of **6 or more out of 10** classifies the disease as definite RA.

| Domain | Category | Points |
|---|---|---|
| A. Joint involvement | 1 large joint | 0 |
| | 2–10 large joints | 1 |
| | 1–3 small joints (with or without large joints) | 2 |
| | 4–10 small joints (with or without large joints) | 3 |
| | More than 10 joints (at least 1 small joint) | 5 |
| B. Serology | Negative RF and anti-CCP | 0 |
| | Low-positive RF or anti-CCP (up to 3 times the upper limit of normal) | 2 |
| | High-positive RF or anti-CCP (more than 3 times the upper limit of normal) | 3 |
| C. Acute-phase reactants | Normal CRP and ESR | 0 |
| | Abnormal CRP or ESR | 1 |
| D. Duration of symptoms | Less than 6 weeks | 0 |
| | 6 weeks or more | 1 |

Small joints are the MCPs, PIPs, second to fifth MTPs, thumb interphalangeal joints and wrists. Large joints are the shoulders, elbows, hips, knees and ankles. Classification criteria were designed to identify uniform groups for research; in clinic they support, but do not replace, a specialist diagnosis.
:::

::: example Classifying early arthritis {#ex-classify}
A 44-year-old woman who smokes has had pain and swelling in her hands for 10 weeks. A rheumatologist finds synovitis in four MCP joints, two PIP joints and the right wrist. Anti-CCP antibody is 180 U/mL (upper limit of normal 7) and RF is negative. CRP is 24 mg/L (normal below 5). Apply the 2010 criteria.
::: solution
- **Joints**: 4 MCPs + 2 PIPs + 1 wrist = 7 small joints, which falls in "4–10 small joints": **3 points**.
- **Serology**: anti-CCP is more than 25 times the upper limit of normal, so high-positive: **3 points**.
- **Acute-phase reactants**: CRP raised: **1 point**.
- **Duration**: 10 weeks, which is 6 weeks or more: **1 point**.

Total $= 3 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 8$, which is 6 or more, so she meets the classification criteria for RA. Her high-titre anti-CCP and smoking history point to a higher risk of erosive disease, so the target of treatment should be **remission** (NICE NG100), and stopping smoking is part of treatment.
:::
:::

### Managing rheumatoid arthritis

The central idea in modern RA care is that early, sustained control of inflammation prevents joint damage.

::: theorem Treat early and treat to target {#thm-treat-to-target}
In inflammatory arthritis, **irreversible joint damage accumulates while inflammation is active**. Outcomes are therefore best when disease-modifying treatment starts as early as possible after symptoms begin, and when treatment is adjusted repeatedly to reach a defined target: **remission**, or **low disease activity** if remission cannot be reached.
:::

::: proof
Erosions can be seen on X-ray or MRI within months of onset, and damage, once done, does not heal. Trials comparing intensive "treat-to-target" strategies (frequent measurement of disease activity, with escalation whenever the target is missed) against usual care showed better control of inflammation, less radiographic progression and better function, at similar cost. The reasoning is like that for blood pressure: what is measured and acted upon improves. The early months may be a "window of opportunity" in which the immune process is more easily modified, so NICE recommends that people with suspected persistent synovitis are referred urgently and that a conventional DMARD is started ideally within three months of persistent symptoms.
:::

Disease activity is measured with composite scores. The most widely used in the UK is the **DAS28** (Disease Activity Score using 28 joints), which combines the number of tender joints (TJC) and swollen joints (SJC) out of 28 (shoulders, elbows, wrists, MCPs, PIPs and knees), the ESR in mm/h, and the patient's global assessment of health (GH) on a 0–100 scale:

$$\text{DAS28} = 0.56\sqrt{\text{TJC}} + 0.28\sqrt{\text{SJC}} + 0.70\ln(\text{ESR}) + 0.014\,\text{GH}$$ {#eq-das28}

A DAS28 below **2.6** indicates remission, 3.2 or below low disease activity, above 3.2 to 5.1 moderate activity, and above **5.1** high activity. A version using CRP instead of ESR is also used.

::: definition Drugs for rheumatoid arthritis {#def-ra-drugs}
**Conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs)**

- **Methotrexate**: the anchor drug. Given **once weekly** (orally or by subcutaneous injection) with folic acid on another day to reduce side effects. Adverse effects include nausea, mouth ulcers, bone marrow suppression, liver toxicity and pneumonitis; blood monitoring is required. It is **teratogenic** and must be stopped before a planned conception.
- **Sulfasalazine**: can be used in pregnancy; causes gastrointestinal upset, rash, and rarely marrow suppression.
- **Leflunomide**: inhibits pyrimidine synthesis; causes diarrhoea, hypertension and liver toxicity; teratogenic, with a very long half-life.
- **Hydroxychloroquine**: mild, used for mild or palindromic disease and in combinations; requires retinal screening for long-term use.

**Glucocorticoids**: short-term "bridging" treatment (oral, intramuscular or intra-articular) while a DMARD takes effect, which typically takes 6 to 12 weeks. Long-term use is avoided because of its many harms.

**Biological DMARDs (bDMARDs)**

- **TNF inhibitors**: adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, certolizumab pegol, golimumab.
- **IL-6 receptor blockers**: tocilizumab, sarilumab.
- **T-cell co-stimulation blocker**: abatacept.
- **B-cell depletion**: rituximab (anti-CD20).

**Targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs)**: the **Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors** tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib and filgotinib, taken orally.

Biological and targeted drugs increase the risk of infection. Before starting, people are screened for **latent tuberculosis**, **hepatitis B and C** and **HIV**, and vaccinations are updated; live vaccines are generally avoided during treatment. Because of trial data showing more major cardiovascular events and cancers with tofacitinib than with TNF inhibitors, UK regulators advise that JAK inhibitors are used in people aged 65 or over, current or long-time past smokers, and people with other cardiovascular or cancer risk factors only if there is no suitable alternative.
:::

In the UK, NICE NG100 (2018) recommends offering first-line **csDMARD monotherapy** with oral methotrexate, leflunomide or sulfasalazine as soon as possible, considering hydroxychloroquine as an alternative for mild or palindromic disease, considering short-term bridging glucocorticoids, and adding further csDMARDs in a step-up strategy if the target is not reached. During active disease, CRP and a composite score such as DAS28 are measured monthly in specialist care until the target is achieved. NICE technology appraisals set when biological and targeted drugs can be used, generally after an adequate trial of csDMARDs in people with persistently moderate or high disease activity measured by DAS28.

::: warning Methotrexate is a weekly drug
Methotrexate for RA is taken **once a week**, not once a day. Taking the weekly dose daily, through a prescribing, dispensing or patient error, has caused severe bone marrow suppression, mucositis and deaths. Safe practice includes prescribing a single strength of tablet, writing the day of the week on the prescription, giving clear patient information, and checking for interactions: **trimethoprim** and co-trimoxazole (additive antifolate effects) are particularly dangerous with methotrexate. Anyone taking methotrexate who develops a sore throat, mouth ulcers, bruising or fever needs an urgent blood count.
:::

::: example Measuring the response {#ex-das28}
A 52-year-old man starts methotrexate for RA. At baseline he has 9 tender and 7 swollen joints, an ESR of 42 mm/h and a global health score of 64. Three months later he has 2 tender and 1 swollen joint, an ESR of 12 mm/h and a global health score of 20. Calculate his DAS28 on each occasion and decide what to do next.
::: solution
Using [[#eq-das28]]:

Baseline: $0.56\sqrt{9} + 0.28\sqrt{7} + 0.70\ln 42 + 0.014 \times 64 = 1.68 + 0.741 + 2.616 + 0.896 \approx 5.93$. This is **high disease activity** (above 5.1).

Three months: $0.56\sqrt{2} + 0.28\sqrt{1} + 0.70\ln 12 + 0.014 \times 20 = 0.792 + 0.28 + 1.739 + 0.28 \approx 3.09$. This is **low disease activity** (3.2 or below) but **not remission** (below 2.6).

He has responded well, a fall of nearly 2.8 units. Whether to escalate depends on the agreed target. If the target is remission (as NICE suggests for people at higher risk of progression, such as those with anti-CCP antibodies or erosions), the next step would be to optimise the methotrexate dose or route and consider adding another csDMARD, with continued monthly review until the target is reached.
:::
:::

## The spondyloarthritides {#sec-spa}

::: definition Spondyloarthritis {#def-spa}
The **spondyloarthritides (SpA)** are a family of inflammatory diseases that share clinical, genetic and immunological features:

- inflammation of the **spine and sacroiliac joints** (axial disease);
- an **asymmetrical oligoarthritis**, often of the lower limbs (peripheral disease);
- **enthesitis**: inflammation where tendons and ligaments insert into bone, for example the Achilles tendon and plantar fascia at the heel;
- **dactylitis**: swelling of a whole finger or toe ("sausage digit");
- **extra-articular features**: acute anterior uveitis, psoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease;
- association with **HLA-B27**, and with the **IL-23/IL-17** pathway rather than the autoantibodies seen in RA. RF and anti-CCP are typically negative, which is why these diseases were once called "seronegative".

The main members are **axial spondyloarthritis** (including **ankylosing spondylitis**, in which X-rays show sacroiliitis, and non-radiographic axial SpA, in which they do not), **psoriatic arthritis**, **reactive arthritis**, and **arthritis associated with inflammatory bowel disease**.
:::

**Axial spondyloarthritis** usually starts in the late teens to thirties. The key symptom is **inflammatory back pain**: insidious onset before age 40 or so, lasting more than three months, worse with rest and better with exercise, with morning stiffness and pain that wakes the person in the second half of the night, often alternating buttock pain. In advanced disease, new bone bridges the vertebrae (syndesmophytes), producing the rigid "bamboo spine", loss of lumbar lordosis and a fixed thoracic kyphosis; chest expansion is reduced. The diagnosis is often delayed by years because back pain is so common; NICE NG65 (2017) lists features that should prompt referral to rheumatology. MRI of the sacroiliac joints shows early inflammation before X-rays change. Treatment combines **exercise and physiotherapy** with **NSAIDs**; if disease remains active, **TNF inhibitors** or **IL-17 inhibitors** (secukinumab, ixekizumab) are used. Conventional DMARDs such as methotrexate do not help axial disease, although sulfasalazine may help peripheral arthritis.

::: example Seven years of "bad back" {#ex-axspa}
A 31-year-old woman has had low back and buttock pain since she was 24. She has seen several practitioners and been told she has "mechanical back pain". The pain wakes her at about 4 a.m., she is stiff for an hour every morning, and she feels better after a swim. Last year she had a painful red left eye with blurred vision that was treated with steroid eye drops. Ibuprofen helps a great deal. Examination shows reduced lumbar flexion. What is the likely diagnosis, why has it been missed, and what should happen now?
::: solution
The likely diagnosis is **axial spondyloarthritis**. She has **inflammatory back pain** (onset in her twenties, lasting years, night pain in the second half of the night, prolonged morning stiffness, improvement with exercise), alternating buttock pain suggesting sacroiliitis, a history very suggestive of **acute anterior uveitis**, and a good response to NSAIDs.

It was missed because back pain is extremely common and most of it is mechanical, because inflammatory back pain is not always asked about specifically, and because axial SpA was once thought to affect mainly men; women are often diagnosed later. Average diagnostic delays of several years are well documented.

She should be **referred to rheumatology** (NICE NG65 lists features such as these as reasons to refer). Investigations will usually include **CRP**, **HLA-B27** and **MRI of the sacroiliac joints and spine**, since X-rays are often normal early in the disease. Treatment would combine a structured exercise programme with NSAIDs and, if disease remains active, a TNF or IL-17 inhibitor. She should be told to seek same-day eye review for any future painful red eye.
:::
:::

**Psoriatic arthritis** occurs in a substantial minority of people with psoriasis, often quoted as up to about a third. It can precede the skin disease. Patterns include asymmetrical oligoarthritis, a symmetrical polyarthritis resembling RA, predominant **distal interphalangeal** involvement (closely linked to **nail disease**: pitting and onycholysis), axial disease, and the rare destructive **arthritis mutilans**. Dactylitis and enthesitis are common. NICE advises annual screening of people with psoriasis for arthritis using a validated tool such as the Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool (PEST). Treatment uses csDMARDs (methotrexate also treats the skin), then biologics targeting TNF, IL-17 or IL-23, or other targeted drugs ([[musculoskeletal/skin]]).

**Reactive arthritis** is a sterile inflammatory arthritis that follows an infection elsewhere, usually 1 to 4 weeks after a genitourinary infection (*Chlamydia trachomatis*) or gastroenteritis (*Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter*, *Yersinia*). It typically causes an asymmetrical lower-limb oligoarthritis with enthesitis, sometimes with conjunctivitis or uveitis, urethritis, and skin lesions on the palms and soles. Most cases settle within six months, but some become chronic. The triggering infection should be sought and treated, and sexual contacts managed, where relevant ([[infectious-diseases/sexually-transmitted-infections]]).

## Crystal arthritis {#sec-crystals}

### Gout

**Gout** is caused by the deposition of **monosodium urate** crystals in and around joints. Urate is the end product of purine metabolism in humans, who lack the enzyme uricase. When serum urate is persistently above its saturation point (about 6.8 mg/dL, or roughly 400 µmol/L, at body temperature), crystals can form, especially in cooler peripheral joints. Most people with raised urate never develop gout, but the higher the urate, the greater the risk.

::: definition Gout {#def-gout}
**Gout** is an inflammatory arthritis caused by monosodium urate crystal deposition, resulting from persistently raised serum urate (**hyperuricaemia**). It presents as:

- **acute flares**: the rapid onset, often overnight, of severe pain, redness and swelling, classically in the **first metatarsophalangeal joint** (podagra), but also in the midfoot, ankle, knee, wrist, finger joints and olecranon bursa; a flare usually settles within one to two weeks even without treatment;
- **tophi**: deposits of urate crystals, often on the ears, elbows, fingers and around joints, in longstanding disease;
- **chronic gouty arthritis**: persistent joint inflammation and erosive damage after years of untreated hyperuricaemia.

Hyperuricaemia usually reflects **reduced renal excretion** of urate (about 90% of cases), and less often **overproduction**. Contributing factors include genetic variation in urate transporters, male sex, obesity, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, alcohol (especially beer and spirits), sugar-sweetened drinks and fructose, purine-rich foods, and drugs: **thiazide and loop diuretics**, low-dose aspirin and ciclosporin. Overproduction occurs with high cell turnover (myeloproliferative disease, tumour lysis) and rare enzyme defects.
:::

Inflammation in a flare is driven by the innate immune system: macrophages engulf urate crystals, which activate the **NLRP3 inflammasome** to release **interleukin-1β**, recruiting neutrophils in large numbers. This explains why colchicine (which impairs neutrophil function and inflammasome activation) and IL-1 blockers work.

In the UK, NICE NG219 (2022) recommends:

- **Diagnosis**: suspect gout with rapid-onset severe pain, redness and swelling of one or both first MTP joints, or tophi. Measure serum urate to confirm the clinical diagnosis: a level of **360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL) or more** supports it. Because urate can fall during a flare, a level below 360 µmol/L during a flare should be repeated at least two weeks after it settles. Joint aspiration and crystal microscopy are considered when the diagnosis is uncertain; if that is not possible, imaging (X-ray, ultrasound or dual-energy CT). **Septic arthritis** must always be considered in a hot joint.
- **Treating a flare**: an NSAID (with a proton pump inhibitor), **colchicine**, or a short course of an **oral corticosteroid**, chosen according to comorbidities, interactions and preference; a corticosteroid injection if these are unsuitable; an IL-1 inhibitor only after rheumatology referral when all else is unsuitable. Ice packs help.
- **Urate-lowering therapy (ULT)**: offered, using a treat-to-target strategy, to people with multiple or troublesome flares, chronic kidney disease stages 3 to 5, diuretic therapy, tophi or chronic gouty arthritis; discussed with anyone after a first or later flare. ULT starts at least 2 to 4 weeks after a flare has settled (or during a flare if flares are frequent), at a low dose, increased monthly according to serum urate. The target is below **360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL)**, or below **300 µmol/L (5 mg/dL)** for people with tophi, chronic gouty arthritis or continuing frequent flares. **Allopurinol or febuxostat** can be offered first line, but allopurinol is preferred in people with major cardiovascular disease. Colchicine (or a low-dose NSAID or corticosteroid) can be offered to prevent flares during titration. ULT is usually lifelong, with annual urate checks once at target.
- **Lifestyle**: no specific diet has been shown to prevent flares; a healthy diet, weight management and limiting alcohol are advised.

Two drug interactions and one genetic risk are worth knowing. **Allopurinol** inhibits xanthine oxidase, which also inactivates **azathioprine** and mercaptopurine; combining them can cause life-threatening bone marrow suppression unless the thiopurine dose is greatly reduced under specialist care. Allopurinol is a common cause of severe cutaneous adverse reactions (Stevens–Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis and DRESS); the risk is much higher in people carrying **HLA-B\*58:01**, an allele common in Han Chinese, Thai and Korean populations, in whom testing is recommended in some guidelines. **Colchicine** has a narrow therapeutic window and interacts with strong CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein inhibitors such as clarithromycin; toxicity causes diarrhoea, then marrow suppression and multi-organ failure.

::: example First flare or long-term treatment? {#ex-gout}
A 58-year-old man has had three flares of pain in the right big toe in the past year, each lasting about a week. He takes bendroflumethiazide for hypertension and has chronic kidney disease with an eGFR of 52 mL/min/1.73 m². Today, three weeks after his last flare, he is well, with no tophi. Serum urate is 498 µmol/L. Should he be offered urate-lowering therapy, and if so, how?
::: solution
**Yes.** Under NICE NG219 he meets several criteria for **offering** ULT: multiple flares, **CKD stage 3a** (eGFR 45–59) and **diuretic therapy**.

**Timing**: the flare settled three weeks ago, which meets the "2 to 4 weeks after a flare has settled" recommendation.

**Drug**: allopurinol or febuxostat; allopurinol is commonly chosen and would be preferred if he had major cardiovascular disease. It is started at a low dose (lower in CKD) and increased monthly guided by serum urate.

**Target**: below 360 µmol/L, since he has no tophi or chronic arthritis.

**Flare prophylaxis**: falling urate can mobilise crystals and trigger flares in the first months, so colchicine prophylaxis is discussed and offered if he wants it (with attention to his kidney function and drug interactions).

**Other measures**: review the thiazide, which raises urate; an alternative antihypertensive such as **losartan** (which has a mild uricosuric effect) or a calcium-channel blocker could be considered. Advise on alcohol, sugary drinks and weight, and explain that ULT is usually lifelong and that early flares during titration are not a sign that it is failing.
:::
:::

### Calcium pyrophosphate deposition

**Calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD)** disease is common in older people. Crystals form in cartilage, where they may be seen on X-ray as linear calcification (**chondrocalcinosis**), particularly in the knee menisci and the triangular fibrocartilage of the wrist. Acute **CPP crystal arthritis** (formerly "pseudogout") causes a hot, swollen joint, typically the knee or wrist, in an older person, sometimes triggered by illness or surgery. Aspiration shows **rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent** crystals and is essential to exclude infection. Treatment of a flare uses joint aspiration, intra-articular corticosteroid, NSAIDs or colchicine; there is no equivalent of urate-lowering therapy. CPPD in someone under about 60 should prompt tests for associated metabolic conditions: **hyperparathyroidism**, **haemochromatosis**, **hypomagnesaemia** and **hypophosphatasia**.

::: quiz
Which feature most strongly favours psoriatic arthritis over rheumatoid arthritis in a person with a new inflammatory arthritis?
- [ ] Symmetrical swelling of the MCP joints
- [ ] Positive anti-CCP antibodies
- [x] Dactylitis of one toe with nail pitting
- [ ] Morning stiffness lasting two hours
::: solution
Dactylitis (a "sausage" toe or finger) and nail pitting are typical of psoriatic arthritis. Symmetrical MCP swelling is typical of RA, although PsA can mimic it. Anti-CCP antibodies strongly favour RA. Prolonged morning stiffness occurs in both and simply indicates inflammatory disease.
:::

::: history
The French physician **Augustin Jacob Landré-Beauvais** described what is now recognised as rheumatoid arthritis in his doctoral thesis of 1800, and **Alfred Baring Garrod** in London showed in the late 1840s that the blood of people with gout contained excess uric acid, distinguishing gout from other arthritides. A factor in rheumatoid serum that agglutinated sensitised red cells was described by **Erik Waaler** in Norway in 1940 and by **Harry Rose** in New York in 1948: rheumatoid factor. In 1961 **Daniel McCarty** and **Joseph Hollander** identified urate crystals in gouty synovial fluid with polarised light microscopy. The strong association between **HLA-B27** and ankylosing spondylitis was reported in 1973. In 1985 **Michael Weinblatt** and colleagues published a controlled trial establishing low-dose weekly methotrexate for RA, and in 1994 **Marc Feldmann**, **Ravinder Maini** and colleagues showed in a randomised trial that blocking TNF with an antibody (later named infliximab) reduced RA activity, opening the era of biological therapy.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise RF or anti-CCP? {level=1}
Explain why anti-CCP antibodies are more useful than rheumatoid factor in confirming rheumatoid arthritis, and give two conditions other than RA in which rheumatoid factor may be positive.
::: solution
The two tests have broadly similar **sensitivity** (each is positive in roughly 60–70% of people with established RA), but anti-CCP antibodies are much more **specific** (typically above 95%), so a positive result is more likely to mean RA. Anti-CCP also predicts more erosive disease. RF can be positive in **Sjögren's syndrome**, **SLE**, chronic infections such as **hepatitis C** and infective endocarditis, cryoglobulinaemia, and in a proportion of healthy older people.
:::
:::

A 37-year-old man has had 8 weeks of joint pain. A rheumatologist finds synovitis in two MCPs, three PIPs and the left knee. Anti-CCP is 3.5 times the upper limit of normal; CRP is raised. Calculate his total 2010 ACR/EULAR score.
::: solution
Joints: 5 small joints (with one large joint) falls in "4–10 small joints": 3 points. Serology: anti-CCP more than 3 times the upper limit, so high-positive: 3 points. Acute-phase reactants abnormal: 1 point. Duration 8 weeks: 1 point. Total $= 3 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 8$, which meets the threshold of 6.

::: exercise A safe start for methotrexate {level=2}
List the main things that should be checked or explained before and soon after starting methotrexate for a 29-year-old woman with RA.
::: solution
**Before starting**: full blood count, renal and liver function, and often a baseline chest X-ray; hepatitis B and C (and often HIV) serology; vaccination status. **Pregnancy**: methotrexate is teratogenic, so discuss contraception and pregnancy plans; it must be stopped before a planned conception, and sulfasalazine or hydroxychloroquine are options compatible with pregnancy. **Dosing**: once weekly, on a named day, with folic acid on a different day. **Alcohol**: limited, because of combined liver toxicity. **Interactions**: avoid trimethoprim and co-trimoxazole; take care with NSAIDs and other drugs that reduce renal clearance. **Warning symptoms**: sore throat, mouth ulcers, fever, bruising or bleeding (marrow suppression) and new cough or breathlessness (pneumonitis) need urgent medical review. **Monitoring**: regular blood tests according to the shared-care protocol, more often in the first months. **Infection**: inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19) are recommended; live vaccines need specialist advice.
:::
:::

::: exercise A young man with a swollen knee {level=2}
A 24-year-old man develops a painful swollen left knee and right heel pain three weeks after a diarrhoeal illness on holiday. His eyes have been red and gritty. Aspiration of the knee shows 22,000 white cells per mm³ with no crystals and no organisms; culture is negative. What is the likely diagnosis, which organisms might have triggered it, and how is it managed?
::: solution
The likely diagnosis is **reactive arthritis**: an asymmetrical lower-limb oligoarthritis with **enthesitis** (heel pain at the Achilles insertion or plantar fascia) and **conjunctivitis**, after gastroenteritis, with sterile inflammatory synovial fluid. Triggers include *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter* and *Yersinia*; *Chlamydia trachomatis* is the main genitourinary trigger, so a sexual history and testing are appropriate. **Management**: exclude septic arthritis (done by aspiration and culture) and gonococcal arthritis; NSAIDs and intra-articular corticosteroid for the knee; treatment of any persisting infection (chlamydia, with partner notification). An ophthalmology review is needed if there is pain, photophobia or reduced vision, which suggest **anterior uveitis** rather than conjunctivitis. Most cases resolve within months; persistent disease may need sulfasalazine or methotrexate.
:::

::: exercise Gout and the transplant patient {level=3}
A 61-year-old man with a kidney transplant takes azathioprine, ciclosporin and prednisolone. He has recurrent gout. His GP proposes to start allopurinol. (a) What dangerous interaction must be considered? (b) Which of his drugs may be contributing to his gout? (c) What does NICE advise about referral?
::: hint
Think about the enzyme allopurinol inhibits and what else it metabolises.
:::
::: solution
(a) Allopurinol inhibits **xanthine oxidase**, which is a major route for inactivating **azathioprine** (via its metabolite 6-mercaptopurine). Combining them causes accumulation of active thiopurine metabolites and potentially fatal **bone marrow suppression**. If the combination is unavoidable, the azathioprine dose must be greatly reduced with close blood monitoring, or azathioprine switched to another drug (for example mycophenolate), under specialist guidance.

(b) **Ciclosporin** reduces renal urate excretion and is a well-known cause of hyperuricaemia and gout in transplant recipients. Reduced kidney function also contributes. Diuretics, if used, would add to this.

(c) NICE NG219 suggests considering referral to rheumatology for people with gout who have had an **organ transplant**, have CKD stages 3b to 5, or in whom treatment is contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective. Management should be coordinated with the transplant team. Colchicine also interacts with ciclosporin (raised colchicine levels and myotoxicity), so flare treatment needs care too.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which arthritis? {level=3}
Three people present with arthritis. Match each to the most likely diagnosis and give the single most useful confirmatory test. (a) An 82-year-old woman, two days after a hip operation, with a hot, swollen right knee; X-ray shows linear calcification in the menisci. (b) A 35-year-old woman with symmetrical swelling of MCPs and wrists for three months, positive anti-CCP. (c) A 28-year-old man with three years of low back pain that wakes him at night and improves with exercise, and an episode of painful red eye with photophobia.
::: hint
Look for one distinguishing feature in each: chondrocalcinosis, an antibody, and a combination of back pain with an eye problem.
:::
::: solution
(a) **Acute calcium pyrophosphate crystal arthritis** (CPPD), triggered by surgery. Chondrocalcinosis supports it, but the most useful test is **joint aspiration** to show positively birefringent rhomboid crystals and, crucially, to exclude **septic arthritis** by Gram stain and culture.

(b) **Rheumatoid arthritis**. The diagnosis is clinical, supported by anti-CCP; X-rays of hands and feet establish whether there are erosions, and baseline CRP/ESR allow disease activity to be measured. She needs urgent specialist care and early DMARD treatment.

(c) **Axial spondyloarthritis** with a history suggesting **acute anterior uveitis**. The most useful test is **MRI of the sacroiliac joints** (which shows inflammation before X-ray changes); HLA-B27 testing and CRP support the diagnosis. Any future red, painful eye with photophobia needs same-day ophthalmology review.
:::
