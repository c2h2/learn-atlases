Skin cancer is the paradigm of a preventable cancer that keeps rising: **ultraviolet radiation is a category-one carcinogen, most keratinocyte cancers and a large fraction of melanomas are its receipts, and the clinical skill-set is small enough to teach in an afternoon - look properly, excise properly, know which lesions need specialists, and spend the rest of your career on sunlight politics.**

This chapter covers carcinogenesis and risk, keratinocyte cancers, melanoma, appendageal and rare skin cancers, and prevention at population and individual level.

## Who should be seen soon

Referral triage in this chapter is short enough to memorise. Any pigmented lesion with a convincing history of change, any tumor that bleeds or crusts in one spot for a month, any new nodule that grows within weeks, any non-healing ulcer or scar, and any lesion that worries an experienced observer irrespective of criteria belong to a two-week-wait dermatology or plastic surgery pathway, and telemedicine photographs from primary care legitimately shortcut queues when used well ([[#def-melanoma]], [[public-health/screening]]). Urgent same-week review applies to immunosuppressed patients with rapid field degeneration, to facial lesions encroaching on the eye, nose, ear or lip where tissue-sparing surgery changes the reconstruction, and to any suspected Merkel cell carcinoma, whose doubling-time in weeks makes it the fastest skin cancer most clinicians will ever see ([[#def-rare]], [[#def-keratinocyte]]). Everyone else - the stable seborrhoeic keratoses, the ten-year-old symmetrical nevi, the lipid-crowned xanthelasmata - is managed with explanation, photography and time, which is the cheapest and most under-prescribed dermatological intervention in medicine ([[musculoskeletal/skin]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]).

## Practical horizons

Three working details separate a competent skin cancer service from a brave one. The first is margin strategy under real constraints. Mohs micrographic surgery examines 100 % of a lesion's margin while the patient waits, which is why it saves tissue and prevents recurrence in the high-risk facial tumours where every millimetre is worth money or beauty or both; where Mohs capacity is scarce, staged "slow Mohs" with overnight mapping, or brick-and-mortar stacked excisions with same-day margin assessment, achieve nearby goals with different resources, and a service that has neither should at least map high-risk lesions with serial transecting sections before reconstruction rather than discovering positive margins on a Monday report ([[pathology/diagnostic-pathology]], [[surgery/wounds-surgical-infection]]). The second is the adjuvant conversation itself. For resected stage III melanoma, modern adjuvant immunotherapy or targeted therapy reduces relapse risk by roughly a third in relative terms, which in individual absolute numbers can mean the difference between watching and treating, and patients deserve those numbers framed as ranges with uncertainty, the immune-toxicity list read as a schedule of things to report early rather than a horror inventory, and the endocrinology contact named before cycle one - because the hypophysitis and thyroiditis and adrenal insufficiency described in [[endocrine/hypothalamus-pituitary]] and [[endocrine/adrenal]] and [[endocrine/thyroid]] arrive on time and are miserable when a GP is surprised by them ([[immunology/adaptive-responses]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]). The third is data: skin cancer registers, linked pathology and staging datasets, and photographed sequential lesions are what turned this chapter from craft into epidemiology, and they are also the instruments that will one day show whether today's tanning bans and school shade programmes actually worked on the generation now turning forty, which is the only kind of proof that changes policy ([[public-health/screening]], [[epidemiology-ebm/measuring-disease]]).

## After the cure: surveillance and second primaries

Cure and surveillance are different skills, practised best by different people on different clocks, and conflating them is how follow-up becomes either theatre or neglect. A patient treated for one keratinocyte cancer carries a substantially elevated rate of the next one - roughly two to three per year in high-risk cohorts versus a small background rate - which is why transplant recipients and multiple-tumour patients get protocol total-skin-examinations rather than "come back if worried", and why each visit is simultaneously a lesion check, a field-treatment session, a sun-protection consultation and an adherence clinic for the topical regimen they are hating ([[#def-keratinocyte]], [[public-health/prevention-promotion]]). Melanoma surveillance leans on different numbers: most recurrences happen in the first two to three years, most second melanomas are found by the patient or their partner rather than in clinic, and the surveillance schedule exists to combine examination of the nodes and skin with teaching - which is why nurse-led follow-up with clear access routes performs as well as consultant outpatients and leaves scarcer clinic capacity for new lumps ([[#def-melanoma]], [[public-health/screening]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]). The psychological payload of "cancer surveillance" is real: fear of recurrence clusters around scan intervals and follow-up dates, and the honest service names it, schedules around it and offers the anxiety tools rather than adding another unlabelled scan to the calendar ([[psychiatry/anxiety-disorders]], [[epidemiology-ebm/diagnostic-tests]]).

::: summary
- Ultraviolet radiation is a carcinogen with two dose patterns: burns for melanoma, accumulation for keratinocyte cancer; both are preventable and neither respects "just a little".
- Actinic keratoses are field disease - treat fields and lesions and expectations, not just the angry one.
- Basal cell carcinoma almost never kills and regularly disfigures; subsite, subtype and margin strategy decide the face you get back.
- Squamous cell carcinoma risk is depth, site, differentiation, nerves and immunosuppression - and the transplant patient needs a negotiated, jointly owned immunosuppression plan.
- Melanoma outcome is written in millimetres and the first excision; ABCDE plus the back, the scalp and the nail.
- Checkpoint inhibitors and BRAF-MEK combinations turned stage IV melanoma from a hospice referral into a treatment decision with genuine, documented long-term survivors - and a new endocrine and immune follow-up workload.
