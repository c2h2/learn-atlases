Hormones are the body's slow, diffuse signalling system — the chemical counterpart of the autonomic nervous system, running the same reflex logic (sensor, integrator, effector, feedback) on timescales of minutes to days and over distances of centimetres rather than millimetres. Every endocrine disease is one of four things — too much hormone, too little, wrong timing, or target resistance — and all four read directly off the general physiology of this chapter: synthesis, storage, secretion control, transport, receptor action and feedback.

This chapter covers hormone chemistry and receptor mechanisms, the feedback organisation of the axes, pulsatile and rhythmic secretion, and the integrated control of fuel and stress.

## Chemistry: three classes, two signalling strategies

::: definition Hormone classes and their pharmacokinetic consequences {#def-classes}
**Peptides and proteins** (insulin, ACTH, GH, ADH, the glycoproteins): pre-pro hormones processed through the ER/Golgi, stored in secretory vesicles, released by exocytosis like any synapse ([[physiology/synapses]]' machinery in endocrine dress), water-soluble, **carrier-free**, minutes-long half-lives acting at membrane receptors — hence their fast onset and their oral destruction (insulin cannot be swallowed). **Steroids** (cortisol, aldosterone, sex steroids) and **thyroid hormone**: lipophilic, synthesised on demand from cholesterol (steroids) or tyrosine (T3/T4) by sequential cytochrome-P450 hydroxylations, **not stored** — secretion equals synthesis — carried in plasma bound (corticosteroid-binding globulin, albumin, thyroxine-binding globulin), the free fraction the active and filterable one, with the classic trap: any change in binding protein (pregnancy, liver disease, the pill) moves total hormone while free hormone, and therefore feedback and effect, hold steady. **Amines** split: catecholamines are water-soluble receptor-external hormones of the adrenal medulla, while thyroid hormone — an amine chemically — behaves as a lipophilic nuclear-receptor hormone. The division predicts the entire clinical pharmacology: which hormones can be taken by mouth, which need carriers, which show delayed effects and rebound, and which immunoassays measure the bound pool.
:::

::: definition Receptor mechanisms: five signal designs {#def-receptors}
Peptide hormones use membrane receptors of three designs: **cAMP/protein-kinase-A** (ACTH, TSH, LH/FSH, ADH-V₂, glucagon, β-adrenergic — the Gs family, with Gq/PLC-IP₃/Ca²⁺ its twin for TRH, GnRH, oxytocin, V₁, and Gi for somatostatin), the **tyrosine-kinase and JAK–STAT** receptors (insulin's α₂ tetramer autophosphorylating IRS-1; GH and prolactin and erythropoietin docking cytokine-receptor/JAK pairs), and the Notch, Hedgehog and Wnt families of developmental paracrine signals ([[anatomy/embryology]]'s vocabulary). Steroid and thyroid hormones cross the membrane (or enter by MCT8 transporter, in T3's case) to the **nuclear receptor superfamily** — one protein architecture: zinc-finger DNA binding, hormone domain, dimerisation interface — acting as ligand-activated transcription factors with **hours-to-days latency**, which is why cortisol's permissive and catabolic effects and T3's calorigenic ones take a day to appear and a day to fade, and why steroid excess or deficiency is never an hour's emergency at the receptor (the emergencies — the crash below — are the mineralocorticoid, catecholamine and glucose systems' faster edges). The general lesson is the synapse chapter's in reverse: signal speed lives in ion channels and phosphorylation; signal **capacity and breadth** live in gene transcription.
:::

## Feedback: the architecture of control

::: theorem Negative feedback and the pituitary axis {#thm-feedback}
A typical axis — hypothalamus (releasing hormone) → pituita (trophic hormone) → target gland (hormone) — is a **cascade of amplifiers with a negative-feedback path from the end product back to both upper tiers** (cortisol on CRH/ACTH, T4/T3 on TRH/TSH with its peculiar intrapituitary T4→T3 conversion by deiodinase-2, sex steroids on GnRH and gonadotropins, IGF-1 feeding back additionally). Three theorems fall out, each a diagnostic rule: (1) **the lesion localises by the pair** — low cortisol with **high** ACTH is adrenal (primary); low cortisol with low/normal ACTH is pituitary or hypothalamic (secondary) and the aldosterone system (renin-angiotensin-driven, axis-independent) survives the secondary lesion, the reason Addison's loses salt and skin-pigments (ACTH's MSH homology) while pituitary failure neither. (2) **The log-linear gain** — free T4 suppresses TSH log-linearly, so a 50% fall in T4 raises TSH ~3-fold while a 20% rise of T4 (early hyperthyroidism) buries it: the pituitary assay amplifies small glandular drifts into big trophic signals — why TSH is the screening test of thyroid state and why "inappropriately normal" TSH with a low T4 is central disease, not normality. (3) **Suppression is time-dependent**: exogenous glucocorticoid above ~7.5 mg prednisolone for three weeks suppresses the cortrotroph axis and the adrenal atrophies from disuse; abrupt withdrawal leaves the patient in the Addisonian crash the chapter's next example stages — recovery of the axis is the slowest pharmacology in medicine (6–12 months), and the steroid-taper and the sick-day rules are applied physiology.
:::

::: definition Pulsatility and rhythm {#def-pulsatile}
Releasing hormones reach the pituita through a portal circuit that **demands pulsatile delivery**: GnRH arrives in ~90-minute pulses and the gonadotroph decodes frequency (slow pulses favour FSH, fast LH; the continuous receptor occupancy **down-regulates** the GnRH receptor and switches the axis off — the therapeutic inversion behind GnRH agonist depriorisation of endometriosis, prostate cancer and central precocious puberty, a drug whose first-week flare must be covered). The same logic governs GH (pulses, amplified in slow-wave sleep — hence the single random GH is worthless and IGF-1, the pooled hepatic integrator, is the screening analyte), prolactin (pulse-locked and dopamine-inhibited — a tonic brake whose removal by any D₂ antagonist, or whose compression by a stalk lesion that interrupts dopamine flow, raises it), cortisol (circadian — the ACTH pulse amplitude driven by the suprachiasmatic clock over the SCN — highest before waking, which fixes the once-daily steroid dose at breakfast and confounds every random cortisol level: 8 am, not 4 pm), and melatonin (the dark-driven pineal output that entrains the rest). Seasonal, menstrual and stress modulation superimpose on all of them, and the clinical rule is general: **for a pulsatile hormone, one level is an anecdote; the axis is read by its integrators (IGF-1, 24-hour urinary cortisol, late-night salivary cortisol, HbA1c) or by dynamic tests.**
:::

::: example An antibody that reads as a hormone {#ex-ts}
A 28-year-old has weight loss, sweat and lid-retraction; TSH < 0.01 and free T4 high; the radioiodine uptake is diffusely increased — while her sister, three weeks post-partum, has identical hormones but an uptake near zero.
::: solution
Both are thyrotoxic, at two different points of the same axis. The sister of the first patient's disease sits one step above the gland: **thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSI)** — IgG autoantibodies that bind the TSH receptor's extracellular domain and, instead of clearing it, **occupy it constitutively**: a hormone-mimetic agonist the follicular cell reads as TSH, so cAMP rises, hormone synthesis and gland growth follow (the goitre), and the feedback loop's response (TSH crushed to nothing) cannot stop it because the antibody answers to no thermostat — the only common autoantibody whose action is stimulation rather than blockade, and the reason thyroid failure (blocking anti-TPO/destruction, the hashitoxicosis of the leak phase) and Graves' disease can run in one family. The post-partum sister's picture is **destructive thyroiditis** — immune-mediated follicular damage releasing stored hormone (a dump, not a drive: low synthesis, so **uptake is empty**, and the thyrotoxic phase is self-limited because stores exhaust); the treatment flows from the mechanism — the carbimazole (synthesis blockade) makes sense in Graves' and not in the leak (beta-blocker and time, with the hypothyroid follow-through the destroyed gland will produce). Add the pregnancy wrinkles the same axis teaches: TSI is IgG and crosses the placenta — the neonate's transient thyrotoxicosis for the weeks maternal antibody clears; and oestrogen's TBG rise makes total T4 climb while free stays measured. Two patients, one feedback loop, one receptor — and a nuclear-medicine scan that reads the receptor's intent.
:::

## Fuel integration: the fed–fast cycle

::: definition The insulin:glucagon ratio {#def-fuel}
The fed–fast cycle is run not by either hormone alone but by the **portal insulin:glucagon ratio** on the liver — the liver as the nutrient buffer sensing its own venous drainage. **Fed state**: insulin (β-cell, triggered by glucose metabolism closing K_ATP → depolarisation → Ca²⁺ exocytosis — the same synaptic mechanism, with sulfonylureas closing the channel and GLP-1 amplifying via cAMP) drives glycogen synthesis, lipogenesis, protein synthesis and glucose uptake in muscle and fat through **GLUT4 translocation** (the insulin-sensitive transporters' exercise-parallel route: contraction moves GLUT4 without insulin, one reason exercise lowers glucose in type 1 diabetes where insulin itself cannot rise). **Fasting**: falling insulin and rising glucagon switch on glycogenolysis (first 24 hours), then gluconeogenesis (amino acids from muscle proteolysis, glycerol, lactate), then — from day 2–3 and under insulin-permissive low ratios — **ketogenesis** in the liver with peripheral ketone use, brain adaptation to ~60% fuel by ketones at 40 days, sparing the protein that would otherwise be wasted (starvation's mild ketosis versus diabetic ketoacidosis: the same switch with the brake pedal of insulin completely gone, hence unopposed lipolysis, unopposed ketogenesis and the acidosis of [[endocrine/diabetes-complications]]). Growth hormone and cortisol rise in prolonged fasting as protein-sparing counter-regulators; the brain's glucose floor (~3 mmol/L) is defended by the counter-regulatory hierarchy — **falling insulin first, then adrenaline (the symptom generator and the hypoglycaemia unawareness target), then cortisol and GH (the slow rescue)** — and each recurrent episode resets the thresholds lower (hypoglycaemia-associated adaptive hypoglycaemic unawareness; the rationale of lenient glycaemic targets in the frail).
:::

::: example The morning collapse {#ex-steroid}
A woman stopped her prednisolone (for giant-cell arteritis) three days ago; she is now vomiting, hypotensive, febrile and confused, on day 4 of a chest infection.
::: solution
Two of this chapter's theorems collide: the **axis was suppressed** (three-plus months of exogenous steroid above physiological dose — corticotrophs asleep, adrenals atrophic, cortisol output zero at the moment of withdrawal) and **the stress response was needed** (infection demands a tenfold rise the normal adrenals deliver at the hour — she cannot deliver any). The collapse is the physiology itemised: no cortisol → lost permissive catecholamine effect on vessels (hypotension refractory to fluids), lost gluconeogenic and counter-regulatory tone (hypoglycaemia), lost free-water clearance (dilutional hyponatraemia — cortisol and ADH disinhibition), and the cytokine unmasking that produces the fever the infection alone might not have; the differential — sepsis alone, or both — is settled by treating both. Management is the chapter in an order: **blood cultures then hydrocortisone 100 mg intravenously** (replacement plus stress: 200 mg/day divided), aggressive saline (she is volume-depleted from lost mineralocorticoid-permissive tone even though aldosterone is often partly intact), glucose, treat the precipitant, then a taper back to the maintenance dose over days with the **steroid-card lesson** written on it: sick-day rules (double or triple the oral dose for fever; never stop), emergency injection and IM hydrocortisone kit, and the alert card — because the next collapse will present the same way and the same ten minutes.
:::
:::

::: example Three bladders, one hormone {#ex-di}
Three patients pass 8 L of dilute urine a day: a teenager after a head injury; a chronic lithium patient whose ADH level is high; and a 30-year-old psychiatric nurse with normal ADH response to dehydration.
::: solution
Polyuria with dilute urine means the collecting duct's water channel — **aquaporin-2**, inserted when ADH occupies V₂ receptors (cAMP/PKA — the hormone's second messenger of [[#def-receptors]]) — is not working; the three answers are the three failure sites of the axis. **(1) Cranial diabetes insipidus** — no ADH made or released (trauma, surgery, tumour, the infiltrations of [[renal/urinary-tract]]'s tubulointerstitial chapter; the posterior pituitary's bright spot gone on MRI, and the **water-deprivation test's** desmopressin response as the classical arbiter, now joined by copeptin stimulation as the practical marker). **(2) Nephrogenic DI** — ADH present and high, the duct deaf: lithium entering principal cells through ENaC and interfering with aquaporin-2 transcription (the resistance physiology — target, not axis — hence thiazide, amiloride for lithium's channel-specific entry, and prostaglandin inhibition: the paradoxical antidiuresis of a diuretic is always the chapter's volume-contraction mechanism, the proximal reabsorption of what little solute-free water remains). **(3) Primary polydipsia** — the axis is intact but chronically washed out: low-normal osmolality, low ADH, and the **water-loading** test's slow urinary concentration while the DI patients never dilute fully; distinguishing the dangerous hyponatraemia of polydipsia (water intoxication, the seizure threshold) from the DI pair is exactly the osmolality axis of [[physiology/homeostasis-body-fluids]] read at its two poles. One phenotype, three lesions, three tests, one hormone.
:::
:::
::: example The boy at the back of the class {#ex-growth}
A 14-year-old boy is the shortest in his year, has no pubic hair and a bone age of 11; his father "was late too" and grew at 16; growth velocity over the past year was 5 cm.
::: solution
The auxology separates the two common answers before any pituitary test: **constitutional delay ("late and late")** versus growth-hormone or other pathology. A velocity of 5 cm/year at 14 is prepubertal-normal but below the expectation for a boy whose peers have entered the pubertal GH–sex-steroid surge; the **bone age behind chronology** (here 11) with a family history of late growth says the epiphyses have time in hand — the child will grow after his classmates stop, and predicts an adult height consistent with the mid-parental target; a **pathologic pattern** would be velocity falling below the prepubertal 4–5 cm/year band, bone age either markedly delayed with dysmorphic features (hypothyroidism — check TSH — and the growth charts of [[endocrine/thyroid]]'s congenial screen) or advanced (precocious androgen excess), or a height velocity that fails to normalise once pubertary. The pituitary work-up then follows the chapter's pulsatility rule: **never a random GH** — screen with IGF-1 (the hepatic integrator, itself nutrition-sensitive, so a malnourished or hypothyroid child's low IGF-1 is not GH deficiency) and confirm with a provocative test (insulin-induced hypoglycaemia or arginine-glucagon — the clamp-stress release test) — and the MRI of the hypothalamo-pituitary region for the craniopharyngioma, septo-optic dysplasia and stalk lesion that hide in the "familial" group. The management answer is the diagnosis done properly: explain, chart every six months, and if delay is confirmed and the psychosocial price is heavy, a short course of low-dose testosterone to start the process the child's own axis, given time, will run.
:::

::: quiz
A random 10 a.m. growth-hormone level of 0.4 µg/L in a short child with normal IGF-1 is best interpreted as:
- [ ] Growth-hormone deficiency — start treatment
- [x] An uninterpretable value — GH is pulsatile and undetectable between pulses in normal people
- [ ] Normal — growth is adequate
- [ ] Indicative of hypopituitarism if TSH is normal
::: solution
GH secretion is pulsatile ([[#def-pulsatile]]) with undetectable troughs in healthy children; a single level can neither make nor exclude the diagnosis. The screen is **IGF-1** (the integrated output, adjusted for age, sex and nutritional state) and, when screening suggests deficiency, **provocative testing** — two stimulated peaks below the cut-off — with the auxiliary markers (IGFBP-3, the auxological velocity data that matter more than any single assay). The same rule in endocrine miniature governs prolactin (stress pulses), cortisol (circadian) and the aldosterone-renin ratio (sampling conditions) — the pulsatility doctrine, not the assay, defines what a number can mean.
:::

::: history
Starling coined "**hormone**" in 1905 from Bayliss and Starling's secretin — the first chemical messenger, found because a denied vagal explanation of pancreatic secretion forced a humoral one; Brown-Séquard's testicular extract (1889), Addison's and Cushing's and Beck's clinical nosologies (the glands before their chemistry), and Berthold's sterilised-cock testis-graft experiment (1849) had already located the sex hormones in the blood. The chemical era ran from Collip and McLeod's insulin (1921 — Banting and Macleod's Nobel the same decade, and the first deaths-from-diabetes year in the same ward) through Kendall's cortisol and the Hench anti-inflammatory prize (1950) to Guillemin and Schally's hypothalamic releasing hormones (1960s — the competition of a decade, settled by mass spectrometry in a pituitary-gland count measured in tens of thousands) and Yalow and Berson's **radioimmunoassay** (1960), the antibody-competition trick that turned hormones into numbers and won the Nobel of 1977 — the assay, not the hormone, being the actual discovery, the one that made the feedback loop visible as a curve, and made every theorem of this chapter measurable in the routine laboratory. Selye's stress triad (the alarm, resistance and exhaustion of the general adaptation syndrome) supplied the integrative frame the next sections still use, right or wrong in detail.
:::

## Where this leads

The axes in organ detail: [[endocrine/hypothalamus-pituitary]], [[endocrine/thyroid]], [[endocrine/adrenal]], [[endocrine/reproductive-endocrinology]]; calcium hormones in [[endocrine/bone-calcium]]; the fluid axis in [[renal/water-sodium]]; adrenal crisis in [[endocrine/endocrine-emergencies]]; the drug-receptor generalities in [[pharmacology/drug-receptor]].

::: summary
- Three chemistries, two signalling strategies: peptides act at membrane receptors within seconds through the G-protein and kinase second-messenger designs; steroids and thyroid hormone act on nuclear receptors with transcriptional latency; transport (vesicles vs carriers) follows from solubility.
- Axes are amplified negative-feedback cascades; the end-product's localisation logic (primary vs secondary, the aldosterone exception), the log-linear gain that makes trophic hormones the screening analytes, and the atrophy-and-rebound rules of suppression are general across cortisol, thyroid and gonadal systems.
- Secretion is pulsatile and rhythmic: random levels of pulse-transmitted hormones mislead (GH, cortisol, TSH-interpretation traps), frequency itself is the message (GnRH), and integrators or dynamic tests read an axis.
- Fuel physiology is the insulin:glucagon ratio operating the liver's fed–fast switches, defended against hypoglycaemia by a strict counter-regulatory hierarchy that diabetes treatment repeatedly dissects.
- Target resistance (insulin, thyroid, adrenal, renal ADH) completes the four-way symptom list — too much, too little, mistimed, unanswered.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Bound and free {level=1}
A pregnant woman on thyroxine 150 µg for Hashimoto disease has normal free T4 and normal TSH after conception, but her total T4 is up 40%. Why is no dose change needed, and when would one be?
::: solution
Oestrogen raises **thyroxine-binding globulin** (hepatic sialylation, slower catabolism), so total T4 climbs while the **free fraction — the species in feedback, in tissues, and in the assay's free analogues — is held** by the intact TSH loop; normal free T4 and TSH mean the tissues are euthyroid and the number to follow is unchanged. A dose increase becomes necessary when the **requirement** changes, not the binding: the increased volume of distribution and placental deiodinase-3 metabolism of pregnancy typically demand 25–30% more thyroxine from the first trimester (the guideline TSH targets of pregnancy apply to the free-hormone status, checked every 4–6 weeks), and similarly the pill, liver failure and nephrotic syndrome move the binding either way. The general rule of [[#def-classes]]: **interpret total levels with the binding protein in mind, and free levels with the feedback loop in mind.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Second messengers, sorted {level=1}
Assign the dominant signalling design (cAMP, IP₃/Ca²⁺, intrinsic tyrosine kinase, JAK–STAT, nuclear receptor) to: ACTH, TRH, insulin, GH, aldosterone, ADH at the duct, and glucagon — and give the pharmacological corollary for the cAMP pair at the airway and heart.
::: solution
**cAMP/PKA**: ACTH (MC2R), TSH, LH, FSH, glucagon, ADH-V₂; **IP₃/Ca²⁺**: TRH, GnRH, oxytocin, ADH-V₁, α₁; **tyrosine kinase**: insulin (IRS-1/PI3K/Akt — the metabolic arm, and the MAPK growth arm whose separation explains why insulin's mitogenic and metabolic effects can diverge in analogues); **JAK–STAT**: GH (→IGF-1 gene transcription), prolactin, EPO; **nuclear receptor**: aldosterone (MR), cortisol (GR), T3, steroids. The pharmacological corollary: the airway β₂ receptor is a cAMP machine (the bronchodilator's tremor, tachycardia and hypokalaemia via Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase are the same messenger in other tissues — and theophylline's attack on the messenger's destruction, PDE, is the non-selective older version), while the heart's β₁-cAMP is the inotrope's and the arrhythmia's route. One design, a whole formulary's dose–response symmetry.
:::
:::

::: exercise Localise the lesion {level=2}
Give the likely site (hypothalamus, pituitary, target gland, receptor) for each pattern: (a) high TSH, high free T4, no goitre, family history; (b) low free T4, normal TSH, post-partum haemorrhage; (c) high cortisol, low ACTH; (d) high testosterone, low LH, small testes.
::: solution
**(a) Receptor resistance** — a pituitary that cannot see T4 (THRB mutation) sets the axis at a higher thermostat: biochemically hyperthyroid-looking, clinically near-euthyroid, with a normal or raised TSH — the differential against the TSH-secreting adenoma (which has a high α-subunit and a mass) is the family history and the absence of thyrotoxic features; never treat the numbers with antithyroid drugs. **(b) Pituitary** — Sheehan syndrome: the enlarged gestational pituitary infarcts after the delivery haemorrhage, and the gonadotrophs and corticotrophs fail first (no lactation, no periods, then the crisis in the next infection); note the **normal TSH in hypothyroidism is the abnormality** ("inappropriately normal" = central). **(c) Target gland (adrenal cortex)** — an autonomous adenoma: cortisol high, ACTH crushed, the contralateral gland atrophic, and the mineralocorticoid and androgen systems follow the tumour's own enzyme profile (the pure-cortisol ones never salt-retain — aldosterone is renin's, not ACTH's, child). **(d) Exogenous androgen** — suppressed LH (feedback), testes shrunk by lost gonadotrophic stimulation, the diagnostic fingerprint of anabolic steroid use (or, rarely, an androgen-secreting tumour, which keeps the testes small but the testosterone far higher). All four are the same theorem — **read the trophic hormone against the target hormone** — applied in four directions.
:::
:::

::: exercise Counter-regulation in the laboratory {level=2}
During an experimental hypoglycaemic clamp, order the hormone responses by glucose threshold and explain why the patient with long-standing autonomic neuropathy loses the warning symptoms before the defences.
::: solution
Descending glucose: **insulin falls first** (~4.5 mmol/L, the primary defence — hepatic glucose output rises), **adrenaline and the symptom complex** (palpitation, tremor, anxiety — the autonomic, β-receptor-mediated warning) at ~3.8, **cortisol and GH** at ~3.5 (slow, permissive, and measured in hours), and **neuroglycopenia** (confusion, seizure, coma) below ~2.8 as the failure of the end-organ itself. Recurrent hypoglycaemia shifts every threshold **downward** (central adaptive resetting): the sympathoadrenal response now fires at a glucose low enough to have already caused neuroglycopenia, so the patient is unaware — and in **autonomic neuropathy** (diabetic, and the synucleinopathies of [[physiology/autonomic-nervous-system]]) the efferent limb is destroyed: the adrenaline response is blunted or absent (the pancreatic **noradrenaline** and the sympathetically driven adrenal medulla gone), the autonomic symptoms never appear (the clamp study shows the patient reporting nothing at 2.5 mmol/L), and the remaining defence is the direct cerebral-counter-regulatory and the glucagon response — which itself fails after years of type 1 diabetes as the intra-islet signal dies. This is the physiological basis of intensive-therapy risk (the DCCT's hypoglycaemia unknowability), of the beta-blocker caution (the tremor and palpitations blocked; the sweating survives, being cholinergic — one reason hypoglycaemia in the blocked patient announces itself only by sweat), and of the CGM alarm's place in modern practice.
:::
:::

::: exercise Permissiveness {level=2}
Define permissive interaction and use it for: (a) thyroid hormone and catecholamines; (b) cortisol's two vascular faces; (c) insulin's odd requirement for glucagon's ketogenesis.
::: solution
**Permissiveness**: hormone A, at its own concentration, provides the receptor or enzyme context in which hormone B works at normal concentration. **(a)** T3 up-regulates β-adrenergic receptor expression and Gs coupling in heart, muscle and adipocyte — hence hyperthyroidism's adrenergic phenotype (tachycardia, tremor, wide pulse pressure, anxiety) being largely **a catecholamine-sensitisation syndrome**, treated with β-blockade while the gland is reset, and thyroid deficiency's slow, blunt circulation read in the same currency. **(b)** Cortisol is required for vascular responsiveness to catecholamines (permissive — the Addisonian hypotension refractory to fluids and pressors until hydrocortisone is given) and, in excess, becomes itself pressor (MR cross-occupancy, up-regulated α₁, potentiated vasoconstrictor responses — Cushing's hypertension). **(c)** In fasted and diabetic fat loss, cortisol and GH's lipolysis proceed, but full ketogenesis requires the **low-insulin state** to leave the hepatic HMG-CoA route open and glucagon's portal signal to raise the acetyl-CoA throughput — permissiveness in the reverse sense, the permissive hormone withdrawn and the pathological pathway (ketoacidosis) running. Permissiveness is the reason endocrine syndromes over-read single-hormone maps: the effect is the **context of all the axes at once**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Design the suppression test {level=2}
The dexamethasone suppression test (1 mg overnight; 2 mg × 2 days; 8 mg high-dose) discriminates three states. Explain what each dose measures and how the results separate simple obesity, Cushing disease and an adrenal tumour, and where the false positives live.
::: solution
The test is the feedback theorem of [[#thm-feedback]] made diagnostic: **1 mg** asks "can any dose of synthetic glucocorticoid suppress the axis tonight?" — failure means endogenous drive is autonomous; false-positive non-suppression is common in obesity (increased volume of distribution and faster clearance — the dose per litre falls), in depression, alcohol, chronic illness and late-night salivary testing's confounders (the reason the overnight test is a screen with confirmatory second-line tests: late-night salivary cortisol, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, or the 2 mg/48-hour urinary-excretion version). **Failure to suppress → measure ACTH**: suppressed ACTH (< 5 pg/mL) means the adrenal is autonomous (tumour — imaged, and the high-dose test irrelevant); ACTH-dependent then asks "whose pituitary?" — the **8 mg** dose's partial suppression (the corticotroph adenoma retains some feedback set high) versus no change in ectopic ACTH (small-cell carcinoma, the completely deaf tumour), with CRH stimulation, inferior-petrosal-sinus sampling (the central-to-peripheral gradient — the definitive catheter test) and imaging for the tumour that has outgrown biochemistry. Obesity enters as the screen's false positive; the algorithm's structure is the chapter's, and its false-map edge — corticosteroid-binding globulin effects on serum cortisol, assay cross-reaction, assay standardisation across labs — belongs to the investigation of [[endocrine/adrenal]].
:::
:::

::: exercise Frequency as a message {level=2}
Explain why a GnRH agonist is used for both precocious puberty and prostate cancer, why the first week can worsen symptoms, and why a GnRH antagonist avoids that phase — the physiology of each clause.
::: solution
The gonadotroph decodes **GnRH pulse frequency**: ~90-minute pulses sustain LH/FSH transcription and release; **continuous receptor occupancy internalises and down-regulates the GnRH receptor** and silences the gonadotroph — the paradox that an agonist, given by daily injection or depot, is a chemical hypophysectomy (the medical history's cleanest example of frequency as a message). Precocious puberty (central, GnRH-driven) and prostate cancer (testosterone-driven) are both switched off at this step — before surgery existed for neither, the depot formulations' niche. The **first-week flare**: before down-regulation, the agonist stimulates — LH, then testosterone, rises, which in the prostate can worsen obstruction or bone-pain flares (covered by an antiandrogen for the first fortnight; in the rare precocious-puberty child it briefly accelerates rather than halts). The **antagonist** (receptor blockade — degarelix) never stimulates: immediate fall in LH and testosterone without the flare, at the price of injection-site reactions and (the clinical trials' signal) a possible loss of the cardio-metabolic protective plateau agonists seemed to enjoy — the same receptor-occupancy pharmacology in two formulations, and the reason the drug's kinetics, not its target, write the protocol.
:::
:::

::: exercise The appetite axis as an endocrine circuit {level=3}
Model body-weight regulation as a feedback system with adipose as the endocrine organ: identify sensor, afferent hormones, integrator, efferents; state the leptin experiment that founded the field; and explain why "leptin deficiency" treats almost no obese patient and what that implies for the feedback set point.
::: solution
**Sensor and afferent**: adipose mass secretes **leptin** proportionally to fat (the adipostat signal; the ob/ob mouse's absence of it and Coleman's parabiosis experiment, and Zhang's 1994 cloning, founded the field — plus insulin's smaller entry); the stomach secretes **ghrelin** pre-prandially (the meal signal); PYY, GLP-1 and CCK from the post-prandial gut terminate meals. **Integrator**: the **arcuate nucleus** — POMC/CART anorexigenic and NPY/AgRP orexigenic first-order neurons with leptin's JAK-STAT suppression of the latter and activation of the former, downstream melanocortin-4 receptors as the efferent route (its mutation the commonest monogenic obesity), the hypothalamic circuit hedonically overruled by limbic dopamine (the modern obesity pharmacology — GLP-1 agonists acting on this same integrator and the hindbrain — is [[endocrine/diabetes]]'s and [[gastrointestinal/clinical-nutrition]]'s clinical arm). **Efferents**: intake, and via the autonomic and thyroid axes, expenditure. **Why leptin fails**: human obesity is a state of **high leptin with central resistance** — the feedback loop runs at a raised adipostat, the pituitary-analogy of [[#ex-steroid]]'s receptor questions — so giving more ligand does what giving more insulin to an insulin-resistant patient does. The lesson closes the chapter's frame: an axis can be diseased at receptor or at set point, and the therapy of resistance is never more hormone; the whole discipline's general physiology — synthesis, feedback, pulsatility, resistance — reads in miniature on the obesity problem, where the feedback is intact, the hormone abundant, and the thermostat wrong.
:::
