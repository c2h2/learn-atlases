Exercise is physiology's integrative experiment: within one breath of starting a climb, the motor, cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, endocrine and thermoregulatory systems are re-set together, in proportion to the work, toward a new working steady state — and every one of those re-settings obeys the same control logic the separate chapters developed. To follow a litre of oxygen from the ambient air to the mitochondrion is to audit the whole course, and the system's ceiling (the maximal oxygen uptake) is set by the weakest of its links, which is why exercise physiology is also the clinical physiology of heart, lung and vessel limitation.

This chapter covers the integrated cardiovascular, respiratory and thermal responses to exercise; the special integrative stresses of altitude, immersion and heat; and the adaptations of training.

## The integrated response

::: definition Homeorhesis: exercise as a controlled disturbance {#def-homeorhesis}
The exercise steady state is not a return to rest but a **new regulated level** (Cannon's homeorhesis, "same flux"): central command from the motor cortex and brainstem feed-forwards the sympathetic surge before the first step (the heart rate rises with the intention, not after the metabolite — the proof of open-loop anticipation), while the working muscles' **mechanoreflexes (group III) and metaboreflexes (group IV — lactate, potassium, ATP, prostaglandins)** feedback the true demand, and the arterial baroreflex (**reset upward** — the exercise pressor operating point) holds the new plateau. The quantitative signature: **cardiac output rises linearly with work** to 4–5× rest in the untrained and 6–8× in the elite (25–35 L/min), distributed by a **massive redistribution** — resting muscle takes ~1 L/min of the 5, exercising muscle can take 20+ (fractional flow to quadriceps from 15% to 85%) because **sympathetic vasoconstriction in gut, kidney and inactive tissue and local metabolic vasodilatation (adenosine, K⁺, nitric oxide, shear, the pump) are simultaneous** — the integration par excellence, general vasoconstriction overridden only where work is being done. Mean arterial pressure rises modestly (systolic up, diastolic flat or down — total peripheral resistance falls by up to 70% in dynamic exercise and only cardiac output's rise keeps pressure up: the vasovagal faint after sudden stopping, when the muscle pump fails but the vessels remain dilated, is this arithmetic reversed).
:::

::: theorem The oxygen chain and its ceiling {#thm-oxygen-chain}
The **Fick principle** organises the whole system: VO₂ = cardiac output × arterial–venous oxygen difference. At rest (250 mL/min = 5 L/min × 0.05); at maximal exercise in a fit 70-kg man (4.4 L/min = 22 L/min × 0.20 — the extraction terms: mixed venous oxygen falls below 25 mmHg as muscle strips the blood, and the arterio-venous difference quintuples while the arterial content is unchanged). Each link can be the limiter, and the clinical ladder follows: **ventilation and diffusion** (limited only in elite athletes at peak — the arterial oxygen desaturation of maximal exertion in the very fit, and the exaggerated exercise desaturation of lung disease — [[respiratory/gas-exchange]]), **cardiac output** (the normal limiter — central fatigue; heart failure's peak VO₂ is its strongest prognostic number, and the anaerobic threshold — the work rate at which lactate begins to accumulate and ventilation to rise out of proportion, the **respiratory compensation point** — is its functional expression on the cardiopulmonary exercise test), and **peripheral extraction** (training's main gain at moderate workloads: capillary density — the Krogh capillary recruitment of 1918 — mitochondrial volume and dehydrogenase content, myoglobin, and the right-shifted oxygen dissociation of 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate and heat and pH). The ceiling, VO₂max, is ~85% genetically determined, declines ~10% per decade after 30 (faster after menopause and with detraining), and every 3.5 mL/kg/min of its loss predicts mortality across cardiac, surgical and oncological outcomes — the reason it now appears in guidelines as a vital sign and in prescriptions as "exercise dose".
:::

::: definition Heat: the integrative cost of metabolism {#def-heat}
Muscle is ~20–25% efficient; the rest of its ATP hydrolysis is heat — running a marathon releases ~2,500 W of heat in bursts of metabolism, enough to cook the core from 37 to 44 °C in an hour without disposal. Disposal is by the physics of the shell: **radiation, conduction and convection** all require the skin to be hotter than the environment (at dry air temperatures above ~35 °C they fail or reverse) and leave **evaporation — 2.4 kJ per gram of sweat** — as the only route, at a cardiovascular cost: skin blood flow (sympathetic cholinergic vasodilatation plus the local factors) rises from ~0.5 to **7–8 L/min**, competing with muscle for the cardiac output, which is why hot environments cap performance and why sweating's dehydration (1–2 L/hour, up to 3) must be partially replaced or plasma volume and stroke volume fall and the heart rate rises to hold the same output (**cardiovascular drift**). Humidity decides the margin: the theoretical human limit is a **wet-bulb temperature of ~35 °C** (the environment's evaporative capacity zeroing at skin temperature), and even below it, sustained work above the tolerance threshold produces **exertional heat illness** — cramps and syncope at the mild end, **heat stroke (core > 40 °C with confusion or coma) as the emergency**, in which cooling (cold-water immersion — the endurance-event protocol that has all but abolished deaths when applied before 30 minutes) precedes every other investigation, because the injury is time × temperature and the rhabdomyolysis, DIC and multi-organ failure that follow are the dose–response of the burn.
:::

::: example The marathon sodium problem {#ex-marathon}
After five hours in the finishing tent a marathoner is confused, vomiting and seizing; he drank "to every station", gained 3 kg of body weight, sodium is 121 mmol/L.
::: solution
This is **exercise-associated hyponatraemia**, the integrative physiology of [[physiology/homeostasis-body-fluids]] run into the ground deliberately: over-drinking **hypotonic fluid beyond sweat sodium losses** (sweat is ~40–60 mmol/L NaCl — a 2 L/hour sweater loses salt as well as water), plus the **non-osmotic arginine-vasopressin secretion** of prolonged exercise (pain, nausea, catecholamines and the ADH that survives a 3 L water load) and glycogen-associated water retention, dilutes plasma below the brain's adaptation capacity — cerebral oedema with seizures, the rare fatal cases from further hypotonic rescue fluids on the ward. The diagnostic distinction that changes treatment is volume state and speed: **acute dilutional hyponatraemia seizes before the brain has exported its osmolytes**, so the treatment is **hypertonic (3%) saline in a 100–150 mL bolus** repeated until neurological improvement — and the correction ceiling that protects chronic cases from osmotic demyelination is a looser constraint in the first hours (but still observed): the same physiology that makes over-fast correction dangerous makes over-slow treatment deadly. Prevention is the chapter's message to every distance-event medical tent: **drink to thirst, weigh in and out, replace weight loss's salt fraction in the ultra-endurance events** — and the same logic covers the opposite error of the dehydrated footballer with postural symptoms (the salt-loss type) and the infant with hypernatraemic dehydration: three populations, one bicompartmental equation ([[renal/water-sodium]]'s clinical faces).
:::
:::

::: example The lineman who stopped sweating {#ex-heatstroke}
On a 33 °C training day a large offensive lineman collapses in the fourth quarter, confused, combative, skin flushed and wet; rectal temperature 41.4 °C.
::: solution
Everything in [[#def-heat]] is present: high absolute heat production (his work is wrestling mass, and metabolic heat scales with muscle mass), a **low surface-area-to-mass ratio** (the same watts spread over less skin), equipment that blocks evaporation (uniform and pads trap the sweat boundary layer — the operative wet-bulb is the one the armour creates), and a day whose ambient heat shrinks the gradient. Collapse with **central nervous system failure (confusion, combativeness, seizure) at a core above 40 °C is heat stroke** — not simple faint (that is the cardiovascular drift of [[#def-heat]] with a normal mind), not hyponatraemia (the sodium check still belongs — [[#ex-marathon]]), not any exertional collapse at 39 °C. The mind is the defining organ and the clock the defining variable: **cool first — cold-water immersion, below 39 °C inside 30 minutes** — because the injury is the integral of temperature × time, exactly as in a burn, and the laboratory follow-up (CK, potassium, creatinine, platelets and INR for evolving disseminated intravascular coagulation, urine for myoglobin) runs while the ice is already on. The return-to-play decision, the acclimatisation programme (10–14 days — plasma expansion and dilute sweat, the trainable side of the drift) and the day's cancellation threshold are the same equations written as rules; the epidemiology is unforgiving: exertional heat stroke is among the leading causes of death in young athletes, and almost every recent fatality involved **delayed rectal measurement or delayed cooling** — a protocols disease.
:::

::: example The trekker's headache {#ex-ams}
Two days after flying to 3,800 m and trekking up, a 30-year-old has a throbbing headache, nausea and poor sleep unrelieved by paracetamol; her oxygen saturation is 82% at rest.
::: solution
**Acute mountain sickness** is acclimatisation failing to keep pace — hypobaric hypoxia (PAO₂ in the low 50s by [[#def-altitude]]'s equation) driving carotid-body ventilation, alkalosis and, in susceptible individuals and rapid ascent, the cerebral and pulmonary complications of the same responses: headache from cerebral vasodilatation and likely interstitial oedema (the mild end of the high-altitude cerebral oedema continuum, whose red flags are ataxia and confusion), poor sleep from periodic breathing — the unstable feedback loop between the CO₂ threshold and hypoxic drive that sleep's automatic control exposes — and the saturation of 82% expected at that altitude, diagnostic of exposure rather than of illness (the Lake Louise score is symptom-led). Management is the hierarchy of [[#def-altitude]]: **stop ascending** (the first and absolute rule), analgesia, acetazolamide 250 mg twice daily to hurry the renal bicarbonate diuresis, dexamethasone if descent is impossible or symptoms escalate — and for the cerebral or pulmonary forms, dexamethasone or nifedipine, oxygen and **descent now**, the portable chamber when the weather forbids walking. Re-ascent discipline follows: graded sleeping-altitude increments above 3,000 m, and the individual's history as the best predictor — the trekker who had it once will again unless the profile is slower. The case is the chapter's thesis in a day: one variable (inspired PO₂), a whole system re-set, and treatments ordered by the speed of the physiology they exploit — ventilation in minutes, renal compensation in days, erythropoietin in weeks, descent immediately.
:::

::: quiz
During sustained dynamic exercise (brisk cycling) in a healthy adult, the expected blood-pressure pattern is:
- [ ] Systolic and diastolic both rise steeply
- [x] Systolic rises, diastolic falls or is unchanged, pulse pressure widens
- [ ] Both fall as vessels dilate
- [ ] Mean arterial pressure falls and cardiac output holds rate
::: solution
Total peripheral resistance falls up to ~70% in dynamic exercise (metabolic vasodilatation in working muscle dwarfs sympathetic constriction elsewhere), so **diastolic pressure — the resistance term — falls or holds**, while the several-fold rise in cardiac output lifts systolic pressure and widens the pulse pressure; mean arterial pressure rises only modestly (the baroreflex reset point). A diastolic rise >15 mmHg or a systolic failure to rise are both test-abnormal findings (fixed cardiac output; excessive afterload responses) — and the isometric (handgrip) pressor response, where rate-pressure product rises with compressed intramuscular vessels and little vasodilatation, is the contrasting experiment the autonomic laboratory exploits ([[physiology/autonomic-nervous-system]]).
:::

## Altitude, immersion and the diving reflex

::: definition The hypoxic challenge {#def-altitude}
At 5,500 m barometric pressure is ~380 mmHg and inspired PO₂ ~70; the alveolar gas equation, **PAO₂ = PIO₂ − PACO₂/R**, gives an unacclimatised sea-dweller an alveolar PO₂ near 35 mmHg (arterial saturations in the 80s, desaturating to the 60s on exertion — exertional hypoxaemia worse than rest, the fact behind expedition pulse oximetry), which drives the **hypoxic ventilatory response** (carotid body — the only arterial oxygen sensing worth the name, and its failure in the blunted ventilator of sleep apnoea and obesity hypoventilation is a whole chapter of [[respiratory/control-breathing-tests]]): hyperventilation lowers PACO₂ (raising PAO₂ a few useful mmHg) at the price of **respiratory alkalosis**, which the kidney corrects over 2–4 days (bicarbonate excretion — the acetazolamide shortcut, a carbonic-anhydrase inhibitor that acidifies and drives ventilation, [[renal/tubular-function]]'s drug in clinical service); acclimatisation adds **erythropoietin-driven polycythaemia** (days–weeks — the haemoglobin mass of the altitude-trained, and the reason "blood doping" is the pharmacological version of the same axis: [[blood/haematopoiesis]]), 2,3-BPG right-shift, increased capillary density and ventilatory drive, and — for the rapid ascent that outruns all of it — the syndromes of **acute mountain sickness, high-altitude cerebral and pulmonary oedema** (the latter the hypoxic vasoconstriction of unevenly ventilated lung producing overperfusion oedema in the rested regions — a diffusion- and circulation-lecture in a tent), treated by the one physiological command: **descend** (with oxygen and dexamethasone for the cerebral form, nifedipine and a portable chamber for the pulmonary).
:::

::: example The diver's slow heart {#ex-dive}
A breath-hold diver's heart rate falls from 70 to 42 the instant her face enters the water; trained freedivers show more, and the spleen shrinks on the ultrasound at depth.
::: solution
The **diving reflex** is the mammalian aquatic solution — cold stimulation of trigeminal afferents (nerve V₁, the same nucleus that shares machinery with the swallowing and cough arcs) driving a **potent vagal bradycardia** (up to 50% fall) with, as breath-hold lengthens, **sympathetic peripheral vasoconstriction** that shunts the non-essential circulation (gut, kidney, inactive muscle) so the cardiac output is reserved for heart and brain — oxygen conservation by redistribution rather than storage, supplemented by the **blood shift** (thoracic blood and fluid engorgement protecting the lungs against depth compression) and the spleen's contraction (the autotransfusion of red cells that raises haematocrit during a dive train, measurable after competitive seasons). The reflex is present in infants (the neonatal apnoea of cold-face immersion — the drowned child's "cold-water miracle" and its protective vagal and cerebral-cooling physiology, the reason hypothermic drownings survive resuscitation that would be futile in normothermia), is trainable (conditioned apnoea increases the bradycardia — a classical conditioning experiment in elite athletes), and has a clinical shadow: the **divers' facial-immersion test** as a vagal probe, and the trigeminal–vagal bradyarrhythmias of cold-water swimming in susceptible hearts. Like exercise, the dive is an integrative stress whose signs — rate, distribution, blood composition — are ordinary autonomic and cardiovascular physiology read against an extraordinary environment.
:::
:::

::: history
Santorio of Padua's thermoscope and personal chair (1614) put exercise, sweat and "insensible perspiration" on numbers — the first metabolic physiology; Lavoisier and Laplace's ice calorimeter (1780) established that animal heat is combustion and that work increases it; and the modern exercise physiology of the oxygen chain belongs to **Lindhard** (Copenhagen, the steady-state work physiology of the bicycle), **A. V. Hill** (the Nobel of 1922, the oxygen debt, the exponential kinetics of [[#thm-oxygen-chain]) and **August Krogh** (the Nobel of 1920, capillary recruitment, and the diffusion–perfusion analysis of the working muscle). The altitude chapter opened with Paul Bert's chamber and **Mossó's** Italian alpine physiology of the 1890s (the first pulse oximeters of the ink-spot plethysmograph era), was quantified by the Haldane school's 1905 Monte Rosa expedition — Haldane and Guye's own measurements of alveolar gases and saturations at altitude, which is why the oxygen dissociation curve and mountain sickness were solved on the same summer's data; the 20th-century synthesis — Keys' Minnesota starvation and exercise studies (1944–45), Saltin's muscle-biopsy era of fibre recruitment and training adaptation, and the Holmgren–Åstrand clinical work-physiology tradition — supplies the ergometer, the cardiopulmonary exercise test and the "exercise is medicine" prescription the next century of guidelines now writes.
:::

::: summary
- Exercise is a feed-forward-and-feedback-regulated new steady state: central command plus the muscle metaborreflex, a baroreflex reset upward, and simultaneous sympathetic constriction (viscera) and local dilation (work).
- The Fick relation locates limitation: central (cardiac output — the normal ceiling and the clinical one in heart failure) or peripheral (extraction — the trainable compartment), with ventilation and diffusion limiting only at the elite or the diseased extremes.
- Heat is metabolism's tax: evaporation is the last route above 35 °C, costs litres of sweat and litres of skin perfusion, and fails at the wet-bulb limit — heat stroke is a time–temperature burn whose only specific therapy is rapid cooling.
- Altitude is a single variable (inspired PO₂) that tests the whole oxygen chain, acclimatized by renal bicarbonate excretion and erythropoietin, and treated by descent.
- The diving reflex shows the same autonomic and cardiovascular components re-combined for another stress — integrative physiology is one set of parts, rearranged.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Sweat the budget {level=1}
A foundry worker produces 400 W of net metabolic heat continuously for two hours; radiation and convection are negligible against the hot air, and all disposal is evaporative. How much water must he drink to stay isohydric, and why does a fan help above 40 °C when air-conditioning the same room to 35 °C barely would?
::: solution
Heat to remove: 400 J/s × 7,200 s = 2.88 MJ; at 2.4 kJ/g of sweat evaporated that is **~1.2 L/hour, 2.4 L over the shift** (in reality more, because none of the sweat may evaporate if the air is humid — drip-rate is not loss-rate). The fan helps because **evaporation depends on the vapour pressure gradient (the wet-bulb), not the dry-bulb**: moving air strips the saturated boundary layer and lets evaporation proceed even at 45 °C, whereas cooling the air to 35 °C restores only the dry gradients (radiation, convection) that a 35 °C air temperature zeroes — the wet-bulb, not the thermometer, runs the budget, which is why the hot-humid laundrette is dangerous at 33 °C and the hot-dry desert survivable at 48 °C with water. The same numbers size the endurance-event fluid stations and the acclimatisation programme's rising sweat rate (more output, saltier or — trained — more dilute, both trainable within [[#def-heat]]'s limits).
:::
:::

::: exercise Walk the oxygen {level=1}
List the steps of the oxygen chain from ambient air to mitochondrial consumption, and name one disease that limits each.
::: solution
**Ventilation** (COPD, obesity-hypoventilation, opioids) → **alveolar–capillary diffusion** (fibrosis, emphysema's surface loss — the exercise desaturation) → **perfusion and matching** (pulmonary embolism, any V/Q mismatch) → **haemoglobin carriage** (anaemia, carbon monoxide, methaemoglobin — content falls while the chain's later links stay intact, which is why they exhaust at high workloads, not at rest) → **cardiac output** (heart failure, valve disease, chronotropic incompetence from beta-blockers — the exercise test's failure to reach the predicted rate) → **muscle perfusion** (peripheral arterial disease — claudication sets the ceiling before heart or lung) → **extraction and mitochondria** (deconditioning, ageing, sepsis's cytopathic hypoxia — the "peripheral" limiter of chronic disease and the first to train). The chain is the differential diagnosis of breathlessness in order, and cardiopulmonary exercise testing reads it link by link ([[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]'s functional laboratory).
:::
:::

::: exercise Do the Fick arithmetic {level=1}
A patient's peak exercise: cardiac output 14 L/min, arterial O₂ content 19 mL/dL, mixed venous 9 mL/dL. Compute VO₂, compare with the 250 mL/min rest value, and say which abnormality — a venous oxygen of 15 mL/dL with the same output — would suggest.
::: solution
VO₂ = 14 L/min × (19−9) mL/dL = 14,000 mL/min × 0.10 = **1,400 mL/min** (5.6× rest — near-maximal for ordinary daily life). A mixed venous oxygen of 15 at the same output means either extraction has failed (arteriovenous shunting, mitochondrial poison — cyanide and the septic and carbon-monoxide pictures) or flow is excessive for demand (early high-output sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia's compensation) — the reason the **thermodilution and Fick outputs and the venous oxygen together** distinguish the failing low-output circulation (venous O₂ low, difference wide) from the distributive and hypermetabolic ones (venous O₂ high, difference narrow). One equation, two clinical worlds, the same arithmetic as [[#thm-oxygen-chain]].
:::
:::

::: exercise Cardiac drift and its causes {level=2}
At constant bicycle work in a warm room, heart rate rises 25 beats over 40 minutes while stroke volume falls. Explain, and give the three fluids-and-sodium lesions of endurance sport this drift predicts.
::: solution
The drift is **compensating tachycardia**: cutaneous vasodilatation plus sweat losses move blood to the skin and out of the circulation, venous return and end-diastolic volume fall ([[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]]'s Frank–Starling term), stroke volume drops, and rate holds the output that pressure reflexes demand — with dehydration, high ambient temperature, low fitness (smaller plasma volume, lower stroke reserve) and the postural load each steepening the slope. The three predictable lesions of endurance sport: **(1) exertional heat illness** when drift outpaces cooling and the core rises uncontrolled; **(2) postural hypotension and the "fainting room" of the finish line** (muscle-pump loss into dilated beds — the same as [[physiology/autonomic-nervous-system]]'s orthostatic arithmetic with heat added); **(3) the hydration-decision disorders** — under-drinking (the drift itself, hypernatraemic dehydration) and over-drinking (the hyponatraemia of [[#ex-marathon]]), the reason fluid guidance is expressed as body-mass change and sodium-awareness, not volume per se ([[renal/water-sodium]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Altitude arithmetic {level=2}
At sea level compute PAO₂ from PIO₂ 150 and PACO₂ 40 (R = 0.8); at 5,500 m with PIO₂ 70 and the acclimatised PACO₂ of 30, compute again; and explain why acetazolamide helps the ascent while hypoventilation from any cause (opioids, sleep) endangers it.
::: solution
Sea level: PAO₂ = 150 − 40/0.8 = **100 mmHg**. Altitude: PAO₂ = 70 − 30/0.8 ≈ **32 mmHg** — the hypoxaemia of 5,500 m is arithmetic first and poetry second, and the alveolar–arterial gradient remains near normal (the healthy lung is not the lesion). Hypoventilation enters the same equation twice as cruelly at altitude: at PIO₂ 70, a PACO₂ of 50 (a sedated or apnoeic night) drops PAO₂ to 70 − 62 = **8 mmHg** — numbers incompatible with consciousness, the reason opioids, sedatives and central hypoventilation are dangerous at altitude and in the recently arrived. Acetazolamide's help is the equation's lever: carbonic-anhydrase inhibition causes bicarbonate diuresis (a metabolic acidosis), which **raises ventilation and lowers PACO₂ at the price of pH**, pre-acclimatising the system (the same logic as [[renal/tubular-function]]'s tubular pharmacology) — while the real treatments remain gradual ascent, then descent, oxygen and the drugs of [[#def-altitude]].
:::
:::

::: exercise The transplant athlete {level=2}
Two weeks after a cardiac transplant a patient begins a walking programme. Predict the heart-rate and cardiac-output response to a fixed workload versus a control, and explain the physiology of each difference.
::: solution
The transplanted heart is **denervated**: no vagal brake (resting rate 90–110) and **no direct sympathetic chronotropic or inotropic connection** — exercise heart rate rises only with **circulating adrenaline**, which takes minutes to build, so the heart rate climbs **slowly, peaks late and falls slowly** — the rate–work loop is lagged. At a fixed workload the patient therefore has a higher-than-expected rate at steady state (the same output at a rate-driven rather than stroke-driven state), a lower peak output (stroke reserve only — the Frank–Starling and length-dependent augmentation, no contractile help — hence the blunted peak VO₂ of [[#thm-oxygen-chain]'s central limit in this population), and rate as a **poor prescribing variable** (training by perceived exertion or adjusted target rates). Added wrinkles of the same anatomy: **no angina** with graft disease (denervated afferents — the equivalent of diabetic silent ischaemia), exaggerated responses to direct-acting drugs (denervation supersensitivity — atropine does nothing, digoxin and sympathomimetics do more), and the cold-face-diving and Valsalva tests lose their parasympathetic limbs: the chapter's autonomic physiology read on a re-wired organ.
:::
:::

::: exercise The anaerobic threshold debate {level=2}
Define the lactate threshold and give the modern interpretation of lactate accumulation during incremental exercise (lactate as shuttle and signal), then explain why the threshold still marks the ventilatory rise.
::: solution
Classically, the **lactate threshold** (~2 mmol/L, the onset of chronic lactate elevation) and the **onset of blood lactate accumulation** (4 mmol/L) fall at 50–60% and 70–80% of VO₂max in the trained; the old reading was anaerobic glycolysis overflowing. The modern one (Brooks' **lactate-shuttle** doctrine): lactate is produced continuously and cleared continuously, and the rise marks the **increased set-point of the lactate transporter and oxidation machinery** — exercise recruits glycolytic fibres and adrenaline, raising production; the threshold is where production exceeds the oxidative capacity of heart, muscle and liver extraction. The **ventilatory rise** that identifies it at the breath-by-breath test is the same fact's chemical mirror: hydrogen ions from the dissociated lactic acid buffer onto bicarbonate, releasing extra **CO₂ beyond metabolic requirement** (the non-metabolic CO₂ that the respiratory compensation point of [[#thm-oxygen-chain]] vents), with the hyperventilation then driven by the metabolic acidosis's carotid and medullary chemoreception. The clinical harvest: the threshold (not VO₂max) predicts functional capacity and mortality in heart failure and is the variable that training moves most; the lactate curve has become the ergometer's acid–base trace — this chapter and the last meeting on the cycle ergometer.
:::

::: exercise Design the heat policy {level=3}
You advise a mass-participation event in 30 °C, 70% humidity. Using the wet-bulb concept, the heat-budget equation and the drift physiology, design: (a) the event's temperature cut-offs, (b) cooling-station spacing, (c) the medical protocol for a collapsed runner, and (d) the population at risk.
::: solution
**(a) Cut-offs from the budget:** metabolic heat ~1,000 W/m² at running pace; radiation, convection and conduction all fail once dry-bulb exceeds skin temperature (~35 °C) and wet-bulb is the hard limit (~35 °C theoretical, operationally **cancel above WBGT ~28–30 °C** for distance running — the governing bodies' cards use exactly the wet-bulb globe thermometer for its combined heat, humidity, radiation and wind terms; the 2019–21 world-championship marathon rewrites were this equation, not meteorological sentiment). **(b) Stations every 2–3 km** sized to partial replacement (gastric emptying caps ~0.8–1 L/hour, so stations deliver what the gut can, plus salt for the sweaters), ice-accessible cooling (cold-water immersion and ice-slurry ices — the phase-change physics), shade and misting for the non-runners. **(c) Collapse protocol ordered by the physiology:** rectal temperature **before** anything else (temporal-scan errors in sweaty, air-cooled athletes are the classic field mistake); if >40 °C with confusion — **cool first, transport second**, cold-water immersion to <39 °C in under 30 minutes, then fluids only after cooling has started (the "faint" is drift; the seizure is hyponatraemia until sodium says otherwise — [[#ex-marathon]]). **(d) Risk list read from the heat equations:** high surface-to-mass ratio problems (the heavy thrower and the under-hydrated endurance athlete at opposite ends), previous heat illness (the strongest single predictor — the physiology leaves a reset in its wake), medications that impair sweating (anticholinergics) or cardiac output (beta-blockers), stimulant and alcohol use, and the non-acclimatised (acclimatization over 10–14 days expands plasma volume, lowers sweat sodium and shifts the drift curve — the same trainable physiology as [[#def-heat]]'s cardiovascular cost). Every clause is this chapter; the cancellation decision is epidemiology's cost–benefit, and the protocol is physiology's.
:::
