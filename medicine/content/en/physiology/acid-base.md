Acid–base physiology is the discipline of a single number kept inside a narrow band — arterial pH 7.35–7.45, a hydrogen-ion activity of 35–45 nmol/L — by three systems running on different clocks: chemistry that acts in seconds, the lungs in minutes, the kidneys in days. It is also the branch of undergraduate physiology that most repays arithmetic, because its disorders are recognised by equations (the Henderson–Hasselbalch relation, the anion gap, the compensation rules) and its mixed forms — the kind that intensive-care and medical-ward patients actually present — are invisible without them.

This chapter covers acid production and the buffer systems, the respiratory and renal regulation of pH, and the quantitative method for reading a blood gas.

## The daily acid load and its first line

::: definition Acid production and pH control {#def-load}
Metabolism generates two acid streams: the **volatile acid** of respiratory carbonic-acid production — ~13 mol CO₂/day, potentially 400 L of gas exhaled, and acid because CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (the reaction catalysed by carbonic anhydrase everywhere the reaction must run fast: red cell, tubule, lung) — and the **fixed (non-volatile) acid** of ~0.7–1 mmol H⁺/kg/day from sulfur-containing amino acids (sulphuric, from methionine and cysteine), phosphoproteins (phosphoric), and incomplete organic-acid oxidation, offset by the daily alkali load of vegetable anions (citrate, malate — metabolised to bicarbonate, which is why vegetarian diets net-base-load and why the modern Western diet's net acid load of ~25–50 mmol/day matters more to bone and kidney physiology than the textbook's steak). Arterial pH is set by the **Henderson–Hasselbalch relation**, in its usable form **[H⁺] ≈ 24 × pCO₂ / [HCO₃⁻]** (pCO₂ in mmHg, bicarbonate in mmol/L): pH is a **ratio of lung to kidney**, and every disorder is a failure of numerator or denominator with the other term compensating — the equation is the whole diagnostic scheme in one line, and the bedside conversion (pH 7.40 ↔ 40 nmol/L; each 0.01 fall of pH ≈ +1 nmol/L near normal) makes it mental arithmetic.
:::

::: definition The buffer systems and the isohydric principle {#def-buffers}
Adding 10 mmol of strong acid to 5 L of **unbuffered** body fluid would kill; the buffers present absorb 100 mmol for a pH change of the tolerable kind. **Bicarbonate** is the ECF's workhorse despite its pK (6.1) lying far from physiological pH — because it is an **open system**: its acid component (CO₂) is vented by the lungs and its base component (HCO₃⁻) regenerated by the kidney, so unlike a closed beaker buffer it is never exhausted while the organs work, and 2/3 of daily H⁺ buffering is immediately or ultimately bicarbonate. **Phosphate** (pK 6.8 — the ideal intrarenal and intracellular buffer) and **protein** (the imidazole group of histidine, pK ~6.8, the great intracellular and haemoglobin buffer — **isohydric principle**: all buffer pairs in a common solution equilibrate to one [H⁺], so measuring one — bicarbonate — reports them all) do the rest; **haemoglobin** is the special case worth its own line, its histidines buffering the CO₂ of tissue metabolism (the **Haldane effect** — deoxygenated Hb binds H⁺ and thus carries more CO₂), while oxygenation in the lung reverses the reaction and dumps protons onto bicarbonate to be expelled as CO₂ ([[respiratory/gas-exchange]]'s transport chapter in chemistry). Exchangeable bone carbonate (the basic buffer of chronic acidosis, and its depletion the **buffer-release osteoporosis** of renal failure) is the fourth compartment and the reason chronic acidosis costs calcium.
:::

## Lungs and kidneys: the two regulators

::: definition Respiratory compensation and control {#def-lung}
The peripheral (carotid, aortic) and central (medullary, CSF-H⁺-sensing) chemoreceptors drive ventilation in proportion to [H⁺] and, more sluggishly, hypoxia, so metabolic acidosis is **hyperventilated within minutes** — the respiratory compensation rule of thumb is **expected pCO₂ = 1.5 × [HCO₃⁻] + 8 (± 2)** (Winter's formula), and a measured pCO₂ above the expectation means a hidden respiratory acidosis (the COPD patient whose Kussmaul breathing is not available) or below it a metabolic alkalosis or hyperventilatory drive (sepsis, salicylate, pregnancy). Conversely respiratory disorders are buffered chemically in minutes (bicarbonate rises ~1 mmol/L per 10 mmHg pCO₂ — the weak chemical buffering of a closed system) and then corrected by the kidney over 2–5 days (~3.5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg — the **acute-versus-chronic signature** that tells the ventilated patient's acidosis how old it is, and whose full expression requires chloride and volume repletion: hypercapnic patients are simultaneously chloride-deprived by the renal bicarbonate retention, the reason the COPD patient's compensation completes only with adequate salt and water).
:::

::: definition Renal handling: reclaim, then regenerate {#def-kidney}
The kidney's acid task has two shifts. **Reclamation**: essentially all filtered bicarbonate is reabsorbed (~85% proximal, via the **NHE3/H⁺-ATPase–carbonic-anhydrase-II** system — secreted H⁺ titrates filtered HCO₃⁻ to CO₂, which diffuses in and is reconstituted, so bicarbonate is *salvaged*, never *excreted*, while urine pH stays near 6.5 — the elegant trick, and the target of acetazolamide, whose bicarbonate diuresis and acidosis are the same blockade ([[renal/tubular-function]]'s transport map). **Generation of new bicarbonate**: the daily fixed acid must leave as something other than bicarbonate — **titratable acid** (H⁺ on phosphate, limited by filtered phosphate) and above all **ammonium** (glutamine-driven proximal **ammoniagenesis**: NH₄⁺ secreted in place of sodium, then trapped and recycled by the medullary machinery to be delivered where it can be excreted — the system that **doubles within days of acidosis** and whose failure (the renal tubular acidoses with hyperkalaemia — ammonium is a cation and its loss costs potassium and bicarbonate) is a failure of excretion with a normal serum creatinine. All of it is potassium-linked: **hypokalaemia causes alkalosis** (H⁺ shifts into cells and renal H⁺/K⁺-ATPase intercalated-cell switching and paradoxical aciduria) and **hyperkalaemia causes acidosis**, with mineralocorticoid excess driving both K⁺ loss and H⁺ loss, and its deficiency the reverse ([[renal/potassium-calcium-phosphate]] and [[renal/acid-base-disorders]] take the clinical forms).
:::

::: example The child who stays small {#ex-rta}
A two-year-old is failing to thrive with polyuria and dehydration episodes; pH 7.18, pCO₂ 18, HCO₃⁻ 7, Na⁺ 134, Cl⁻ 116, K⁺ 3.0, urine pH 6.0.
::: solution
Arithmetic first: Winter expects pCO₂ = 1.5×7+8 ≈ 18.5 — compensation appropriate; the gap = 134 − (116+7) = **11, normal** — a **hyperchloraemic, normal-gap metabolic acidosis**, and in a child that means bicarbonate lost (gut — the diarrhoea the history would name — or kidney) or acid excretion failed. The urine settles it: with plasma bicarbonate at 7 the normal kidney should acidify urine below 5.3 — a **urine pH of 6.0 in systemic acidosis is the diagnostic sin**, the distal (type 1) renal tubular acidosis: the α-intercalated cell cannot secrete H⁺, so acid is retained, potassium wastes through the intact aldosterone-driven exchange (the hypokalaemia), calcium citrate handling goes wrong (nephrocalcinosis and the polyuria's concentration defect), and **growth fails because chronic acidosis is a growth hormone–resistant, bone-buffering, potassium-losing state** — the failure to thrive may be the only presenting complaint. Treatment is the physiology: large oral alkali (citrate solution — the citrate of the last exercise's mechanism, given for its bicarbonate), potassium replacement, and the recognition that thiazide and the salt-losing variants need their own chapters ([[renal/acid-base-disorders]] takes the types, [[renal/potassium-calcium-phosphate]] the potassium). The proximal (type 2) variant — the bicarbonate threshold set low, glycosuria and phosphaturia in the syndrome of Fanconi — fails the same acid test differently: alkalinise the plasma toward normal and the urine acidifies again (the distal mechanism is intact), the finding that localises to the proximal tubule in one reagent strip.
:::

## Reading a blood gas: the quantitative method

::: theorem The five-step read {#thm-read}
(1) **Is there acidaemia or alkalaemia?** (pH 7.35/7.45; a normal pH with abnormal components means a **mixed** disorder). (2) **Which side moved?** pCO₂ > 45 acidotic, HCO₃⁻ < 22 acidotic — the matching side names the primary disorder(s). (3) **Is the compensation appropriate?** metabolic acidosis — Winter's formula; metabolic alkalosis — expected pCO₂ rises 0.7 per mmol/L of bicarbonate; respiratory acidosis — the acute 1:10 versus chronic 3.5:10 bicarbonate rules; respiratory alkalosis — 2:10 acute, 5:10 chronic (bicarbonate below 12–14 or a compensation outside the rules means a second disorder, **by definition** — that is what an inappropriate compensation is). (4) **In any metabolic acidosis, the anion gap:** AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻), normal ~12 ± 4 (albumin-corrected: **add 2.5 to the gap for every 1 g/dL of albumin below 4.0** — the hypoalbuminaemic ICU patient's gap is falsely friendly); a **high gap** = unmeasured anion (the mnemonic runs on the poison and perfusion list: lactate — shock, hypoxia, metformin, linezolid; ketones — diabetic, alcoholic, starvation; uraemia; ingestions — salicylate, glycolic/oxalic from ethylene glycol, formate from methanol); a **normal-gap hyperchloraemic acidosis** = bicarbonate lost (diarrhoea, ureterosigmoidostomy, acetazolamide) or renal acidification failed (the RTAs — [[renal/acid-base-disorders]]). (5) **The delta–delta** (ΔAG/ΔHCO₃⁻): in a pure high-gap acidosis, bicarbonate falls roughly as fast as the gap rises (ratio ~1–2); a **smaller-than-expected fall of bicarbonate with excess gap** exposes a coexisting metabolic alkalosis (the vomiting uremic, the NG-draining DKA patient) and a larger one a coexisting normal-gap acidosis — the method that turns three numbers into two diagnoses.
:::

::: example Three gases, one patient {#ex-series}
A 19-year-old with type 1 diabetes: **Gas 1** — pH 7.12, pCO₂ 15, HCO₃⁻ 5, Na⁺ 138, Cl⁻ 101, glucose 34. **Gas 2**, six hours into treatment — pH 7.28, pCO₂ 25, HCO₃⁻ 12, K⁺ 3.1. **Gas 3**, day 2, vomiting — pH 7.48, pCO₂ 30, HCO₃⁻ 22, Na⁺ 136, Cl⁻ 88, K⁺ 3.0.
::: solution
**Gas 1**: acidaemia; low bicarbonate — metabolic acidosis; Winter: expected pCO₂ = 1.5×5+8 = **15.5 ± 2** — measured 15, respiratory compensation maximal and appropriate; AG = 138 − (101+5) = **32** — high-gap, ketotic, with the glucose agreeing; Kussmaul breathing is the arithmetic made visible (the patient is breathing at his ventilatory ceiling). **Gas 2**: improving, but three treatment signals — the gap closing while bicarbonate lags is normal (the ketone anions are metabolised to bicarbonate, and chloride-rich fluids drag the gap down fast leaving a transient hyperchloraemic component), and the **potassium of 3.1 six hours in is the danger**: total-body potassium is depleted (acidosis shifted it out, urine took it) and insulin will drive it back in — replacement before the next insulin bottle, the same H⁺/K⁺ intercalated-cell logic as [[#def-kidney]]. **Gas 3**: pH alkalaemic, and neither term explains it alone — bicarbonate 22 is "normal" and pCO₂ 30 is low, and a low pCO₂ cannot be compensation for a normal bicarbonate, so this is **respiratory alkalosis** (pain, sepsis, the Kussmaul habit still partly running) **plus a metabolic alkalosis concealed in the normal bicarbonate** — the chloride of 88 and the vomiting history shout it, and the delta-delta's logic reversed: after a high-gap acidosis, a "normal" bicarbonate with hypochloraemia is a **new alkali-loaded state** (citrate and the vomited acid, plus the contracted chloride-depleted volume maintaining it — the saline-responsive alkalosis: [[renal/water-sodium]]'s chloride-wasting kidney with the gut doing the wasting). One admission, three complete pictures, and in each the treatment writes itself from the read.
:::
:::

::: example The aspirin picture {#ex-salicylate}
A 44-year-old after a suicide attempt: tinnitus, sweating, temperature 38.4°C, respiratory rate 30; pH 7.44, pCO₂ 21, HCO₃⁻ 14, Na⁺ 141, Cl⁻ 108, glucose 4.2, anion gap 19.
::: solution
The near-normal pH is the trap and the method the answer: pCO₂ 21 is far below anything needed to compensate a bicarbonate of 14 (Winter expects 29), and the gap is 19 with a history of ingestion — this is **two primary disorders in perfect pH opposition**: salicylate's direct medullary **respiratory-centre stimulation (primary respiratory alkalosis)** and its uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation with lactate and keto-acid accumulation (**primary high-gap metabolic acidosis**), each hiding the other's pH effect while the bicarbonate and the gap report both — the textbook demonstration that pH near normal is the *most* informative abnormality in medicine if the compensation rules are applied. Adults hypoventilate late (the fatigue of the respiratory drive, salicylate's own CNS depression) and then crash; children present with acidosis and die earlier; the management follows the physiology — **urine alkalinisation** (ion trapping of the weak-acid salicylate: pK 3.0 means that raising urine pH from 5 to 7.5 converts the anion-trapping ratio 100-fold in the tubule — [[renal/tubular-function]]'s non-ionised diffusion in reverse), potassium repletion (alkalinisation fails in hypokalaemia, the H⁺/K⁺ exchange of [[#def-kidney]]), glucose despite normal levels (the brain's un-coupled metabolism and the neuroglycopenic risk), and haemodialysis for the severe or deteriorating case, which corrects both limbs in an hour.
:::
:::
::: example The arrest gas {#ex-arrest}
During twenty minutes of ventricular fibrillation arrest: pH 6.88, pCO₂ 68, HCO₃⁻ 13, lactate 14.
::: solution
Both compartments of the equation have failed in the acidotic direction — a **mixed metabolic (lactic, anoxic) and respiratory (no ventilation, so CO₂ accumulates at ~3–6 mmHg/min with poor output) acidosis**, the pH of 6.88 the arithmetic of 24×68/13 ≈ 126 nmol/L. The clinical physiology is what the numbers forbid: with no cardiac output the "respiratory" component is partly a **transport failure** — CO₂ generated in arrested tissue does not reach the lungs (mixed venous pCO₂ soars while the arterial under-reads it: the gas from the arterial line understates the intracellular acidosis, which is why the central or venous gas is the better monitor of adequacy in CPR), and buffering is intracellular (the bicarbonate of the ECF is spent in minutes; proteins, phosphates and haemoglobin carry most of the load, and the lactate itself is partly the intracellular buffer's output — H⁺ exported with the monocarboxylate). Treatment follows the two lesions in order: **flow first** (compression quality restores CO₂ washout and lactate clearance better than any drug), ventilation with measurable capnography (the ETCO₂ of 2 kPa during arrest is the flowmeter that decides whether to keep going — the capnogram as the CPR monitor is [[emergency-critical-care/acute-assessment]]'s version of this equation), and **bicarbonate only for the specific indications** — prolonged arrest with documented severe metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, tricyclic and salicylate poisonings, the renal replacement ready for the refractory case — because giving bicarbonate without flow just makes more CO₂ the arrested lungs cannot remove: the open-system buffer, closed for the duration, and the chapter's central rule — **the acid–base read is a read of perfusion, ventilation and kidney at once.**
:::

::: history
Sørensen's pH (potenz Hydrogenii, 1909) gave the scale; Henderson's and then **Hasselbalch's** application of the mass-action equation to bicarbonate (1916–17) produced the relation, proved in the most physiology-scientific way — the Dane had himself wheeled on an ergometer bicycle through the streets of Copenhagen on a requisitioned hospital machine, measuring the fall of blood bicarbonate against the lactic acid of maximal exercise (the war's blackout of German journals made him publish in Swedish, and the result arrived in Paris already famous). The kidney's role — the urine as the final common path — was worked out in the 1930s with phosphate and ammonia analysis; Pitts and Alexander's demonstration (1945) that **ammonium excretion is the adaptive route** settled the mechanism; and Siggaard-Andersen's microblood pH electrodes, the **astrup** nomograms and the base-excess concept (1962) turned acid–base into the every-bedside arithmetic it is today, along with the still-live argument — the Stewart physical-chemistry account in which strong-ion difference and pCO₂, not bicarbonate, are the independent variables — one of physiology's most durable and instructive re-readings of the same equations.
:::

::: quiz
A patient with chronic CO₂ retention (pCO₂ 70 mmHg) is given furosemide and becomes alkalaemic at pH 7.52. The dominant explanation:
- [ ] Over-correction by mechanical ventilation
- [x] Contraction of a chloride-depleted volume removing the renal limit on bicarbonate retention, plus hypokalaemia — a metabolic alkalosis superimposed on compensated respiratory acidosis
- [ ] Loss of CO₂ buffering by albumin
- [ ] Salicylate contamination
::: solution
Her baseline was a **chronic respiratory acidosis with renal compensation** — bicarbonate high (~38) at the price of chloride wasting, the kidney's bicarbonate retention chloride-dependent ([[#def-lung]]). Furosemide now contracts the volume, wastes chloride and potassium further, and delivers the classic trio — **contraction, chloropenia, hypokalaemia** — each independently reabsorbing bicarbonate in the proximal tubule and secreting H⁺ in the collecting duct; the ventilatory drive that holds pCO₂ at 70 cannot lower bicarbonate, and the pH of 7.52 with a *high* bicarbonate and high pCO₂ is the mixed picture read correctly (a compensation rule violation, theorem step 3). Treatment names the mechanism: potassium and chloride (saline), stop the diuretic if possible, and accept the CO₂ — the mixed-disorder corollary that post-hypercapnic alkalosis is dangerous in its own right (reduced respiratory drive, seizures, arrhythmia), which is why the ventilated COPD patient's rate is lowered to *permit* CO₂ re-accumulation as bicarbonate still needs days to fall.
:::

## Where this leads

The tubule transport in [[renal/tubular-function]]; fluid and electrolyte co-management in [[renal/water-sodium]]; potassium in [[renal/potassium-calcium-phosphate]]; the clinical syndromes and the renal tubular acidoses in [[renal/acid-base-disorders]]; CO₂ transport in [[respiratory/gas-exchange]]; and the pre-analytical and perioperative pitfalls in [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]].

::: summary
- pH is held in a 0.04-unit band by buffers (seconds), lungs (minutes) and kidneys (days) against a volatile CO₂ load of ~13 mol/day and a fixed acid load of ~1 mmol/kg/day.
- Bicarbonate works because it is an open system; haemoglobin's histidines and urinary phosphate and ammonia do the rest, and the isohydric principle lets one buffer pair report them all.
- Compensation is quantitative and rule-bound: Winter's formula, the 1:10/3.5:10 hypercapnia rules and the chloride-dependent renal limits are the tests that unmask mixed disorders.
- The anion gap (albumin-corrected) splits metabolic acidosis into unmeasured anion versus bicarbonate loss, and the delta–delta splits the gap's story into its hidden alkalosis and normal-gap components.
- Potassium and chloride are acid–base ions as surely as hydrogen is: hypokalaemia alkalotises, hyperkalaemia acidotises, and chloride depletion maintains every alkalosis worth treating.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Convert and locate {level=1}
Use [H⁺] ≈ 24 × pCO₂/HCO₃⁻ for: (a) pH 7.20, pCO₂ 30 — find HCO₃⁻; (b) pH 7.55, HCO₃⁻ 32 — find pCO₂. Name the primary disorder of each.
::: solution
pH 7.20 ⇒ [H⁺] = 63 nmol/L: (a) HCO₃⁻ = 24×30/63 ≈ **11.4 mmol/L** — a metabolic acidosis with appropriate ventilation? Check Winter: expected pCO₂ = 25; measured 30 — **above** expectation, so a concomitant respiratory acidosis (the opioid on top of the metformin). (b) [H⁺] = 28: pCO₂ = 28×32/24 ≈ **37 mmHg** — for a chronic metabolic alkalosis compensation should be ~40 (0.7 rule gives 40: 0.7×(32−24)+40); a pCO₂ of 37 with alkalaemia means the low pCO₂ is **a second, respiratory process** (the salicylate or the ventilator). Two mixed disorders hidden by one decimal of compensation — the arithmetic, not the gestalt, is the diagnostic.
:::
:::

::: exercise Buffering clocks {level=1}
Order for speed of pH response: chemical buffer, lung, kidney, bone; and for capacity: bicarbonate, protein, phosphate, ammonia. Why do the orderings differ?
::: solution
Speed: **buffers (instant, ~20 mmol H⁺ to 0.1 pH unit), lung (minutes, up to 4–6× baseline ventilation), kidney (2–5 days for full amplitude), bone (days–weeks, the chronic reservoir)**; capacity is a different ranking: **bicarbonate first** (open system — its capacity is the lungs' and kidneys' ventilation and regeneration, not the mole count of the pair), **ammonia second** (adaptive — excretion rate can rise ten-fold in acidosis and is unlimited by intake), then protein and bone (capacity without rate), then phosphate (fixed by filtration — the classic exam contrast: urine pH can reach 4.4 against a 10⁵ gradient only because ammonia buffers the bladder-load first, free H⁺ alone would excrete a milliequivalent a day). The orderings differ because capacity in an open system is set by the **organ supplying the pair's missing arm**, not by the chemistry — the whole bicarbonate doctrine in one sentence.
:::
:::

::: exercise The vomiting gas {level=2}
A pyloric-stenosis baby: pH 7.52, pCO₂ 34, HCO₃⁻ 28, Na⁺ 136, Cl⁻ 88, K⁺ 3.0. Explain every number, including the urine's paradoxically low pH.
::: solution
Gastric loss of HCl removes acid (generation of the alkalosis — bicarbonate unopposed where chloride should be) and of salt and water; volume contraction up-regulates aldosterone (maintenance: enhanced distal Na⁺ reabsorption in exchange for K⁺ and H⁺) and hypochloraemia itself makes the proximal tubule reabsorb bicarbonate aggressively with the sodium — a **chloride-depletion, saline-responsive metabolic alkalosis** ([[renal/water-sodium]]'s two-alkalosis rule). Compensation for alkalosis is hypoventilation (expected pCO₂ ≈ 0.7 × 4 above baseline + 40 ≈ 43), and the measured 34 is below it — the hypovolaemic and hypokalaemic infant's drive is not free to slow, so a mild respiratory alkalosis rides on top; the case is already mixed before the paradoxical urine. The **paradoxical aciduria** is the chapter's most quoted transport fact: at the empty-chloride, potassium-depleted tubule, sodium must be reabsorbed with something, so the intercalated cell secretes H⁺ (acid urine, further alkalising the plasma) and the K⁺/H⁺ exchanger reabsorbs potassium while acidifying — alkali urine only after potassium is given. Treatment: **saline first (the chloride is the cure), potassium second, then the operation** (the pyloromyotomy that stops the acid being lost) — an old surgical dictation of this physiology.
:::
:::

::: exercise Lactate and its gaps {level=2}
A shocked patient has lactate 9 mmol/L but an anion gap of 16. Two mechanisms explain the mismatch, and one laboratory artefact explains the common opposite pattern. Give all three.
::: solution
First mechanism: the **gap and the lactate are different currencies** — one millimole of lactate one millimole of anion, but the gap is diluted across the **extracellular volume** (20 L) while the bicarbonate fall is shared across total buffer space; and early resuscitation **washes lactate from ischaemic beds** while its metabolism regenerates bicarbonate slowly. Second: **hypoalbuminaemia** masks ~2.5 of gap per g/dL below 4 (the septic patient with albumin 20 g/L has a hidden ~5-unit gap — correction is mandatory, [[#thm-read]] step 4). The artefact: arterial samples with **excess heparin** (the liquid-balloon syringe left at the bedside) dilute and add an anion — the falsely high gap and low bicarbonate on a gas; and the opposite classic — a falsely **high** bicarbonate on old air-exposed samples (CO₂ loss raises pH and lowers measured pCO₂ — the pre-analytical errors the acid–base laboratory exists to police). The interpretation rule: never audit a gap without the albumin, the chloride and the sampling history.
:::
:::

::: exercise The anion gap that is not {level=2}
Na⁺ 132, Cl⁻ 112, HCO₃⁻ 14, glucose 28, triglycerides 40 mmol/L, pH 7.24, lactate 1.1. The "gap" is 6 — hyperchloraemic acidosis with a "normal" gap. Yet this patient has a high anion gap. Explain the two deceptions.
::: solution
Both halves of the gap are lies. **Pseudonormalisation by hypoalbuminaemia** (assume albumin ~20 g/L ⇒ 5 units hidden — the correction of step 4) and **pseudohypochloraemia/hyperchloraemia by the analyser method**: the ion-selective-electrode indirect method (flame photometry-era sodium with lipid excess) — with triglycerides at 40 mmol/L the **pseudohyponatraemia** depresses the reported sodium (so the gap's numerator is falsely low) while chloride is often reported normally, collapsing a true gap of ~24 toward 6. Verify with **direct ISE or blood-gas electrolytes** (the fluid-phase measurement unaffected by the lipid volume) and by the lactate, ketone, osmolal-gap and salicylate panels — and the osmolal gap here (measure vs calculate) is the third screen this patient needs before the toxicologists are phoned. The clinical rule: in the shocked, the lipaemic, the hypoalbuminaemic and the massively transfused, the uncorrected gap is an unreliable number — [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]'s pre-analytical chapter, acid–base edition.
:::

::: exercise Design a compensation study {level=2}
You wish to measure the renal compensation of an acid load in conscious humans. What do you infuse, what do you measure, over what timescale, and what will the urine pH, ammonium and titratable acid do in the first hour versus the third day?
::: solution
Standardise the acid: **ammonium chloride** (oral or intravenous — metabolised to HCl with ammonia excreted, so it adds proton without adding an unmeasured anion; the older clinical test of renal acidification) or dietary acid models; fix salt and water so volume and chloride are not the variable. **Hour 1**: buffers dominate — bicarbonate falls ~2–4, pH falls, ventilation rises within minutes (Kussmaul), urine pH drops toward 5 (H⁺-ATPase activation is fast), but **ammonium excretion has not yet risen** — net acid excretion is only modestly increased and titratable acid is phosphate-limited. **Day 1–3**: the adaptive transcriptional programme of glutamine transporters (B⁰AT1) and ammoniagenic enzymes turns on, **ammonium excretion rises several-fold** (now the bulk of acid excretion, with its bicarbonate appearing in plasma — the slow "regeneration" component), urine pH to its floor ~4.4 with the new ammonia buffering it there (the free-H⁺ route contributing <0.1 mmol/day), and plasma bicarbonate recovers to a new steady state. Measured outputs: serial gases (bicarbonate, pH), urinary net acid excretion (pH-titratable acid + NH₄⁺ − HCO₃⁻) and balance frames; the curve's two phases — immediate urinary acidification, delayed ammonium adaptation — is the physiological proof that **secretion is fast and generation is adaptive**, the same distinction the proximal bicarbonate reclamation and distal acidification chapters made anatomically ([[renal/tubular-function]]).
:::

::: exercise Citrate, alkali and the stone {level=2}
Potassium citrate is given to patients with calcium-oxalate stones and low urinary citrate. Trace the physiology from the oral dose to the urine, and explain why the alkali load is the point while the potassium is a second benefit.
::: solution
Citrate is absorbed and, in the acidotic or hypokalaemic state, **reabsorbed even more avidly by the proximal tubule** (the sodium-dicarboxylate transporter NaDC1, up-regulated by acidosis via the intracellular acidification that increases its transcription and membrane insertion) — so low urinary citrate is a **marker of an intracellular acid load** (and of the potassium depletion that creates the same intracellular acid shift). Citrate in the tubular fluid is the urine's calcium chelator: it binds luminal calcium into a soluble complex, so **less ionised calcium is available to pair with oxalate** — hypocitraturia is thus a genuine lithogenic risk, and correcting it by giving citrate (metabolised to bicarbonate in the liver — each citrate yields three bicarbonates, an alkali load that removes the stimulus for proximal citrate reabsorption, so citrate now escapes into the urine where it does its chelating work) attacks both the marker and the mechanism. Potassium rather than sodium matters twice: the potassium salts correct the intracellular acidosis of potassium depletion (the H⁺/K⁺ shift of [[#def-kidney]]), and sodium loading would drag calcium with it (the salt–calcium coupling that is why the same logic underlies potassium-citrate over sodium-citrate, and thiazides' calcium lowering in the urine). Alkali also alkalinises urine, which helps uric-acid and cystine solubility (the same ion-trapping physics as [[#ex-salicylate]]) — so one molecule treats four stone diatheses. Renal physiology in a prescription.
:::

::: exercise The Stewart heresy {level=3}
Peter Stewart's physical-chemical approach holds that [H⁺] is set by the strong-ion difference, pCO₂ and total weak acid (A_tot) — and that bicarbonate is a dependent variable. Reconcile this with the Henderson–Hasselbalch teaching of this chapter, and give one clinical case where the Stewart view makes a correct prediction the bicarbonate view misses.
::: solution
The two accounts solve the **same equilibrium system** — a complete physico-chemical description (electroneutrality, mass action, conservation of mass) can be rearranged into either set of independent variables, and they predict identically; what differs is **which quantities you can actually hold fixed at the bedside**. Henderson–Hasselbalch treats pCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ as the independent pair, because lungs and kidneys control them and assays report them. Stewart's variables — the **strong-ion difference** ([Na⁺+K⁺+Ca²⁺+Mg²⁺] − [Cl⁻+lactate⁻+…], i.e. the net fixed charge that must be balanced by weak-acid anions and bicarbonate), **pCO₂**, and **A_tot** (albumin, phosphate) — are independent in the different sense that infusion choices set them. The case where the difference is clinical: **the post-cardiac-surgery patient whose pH will not correct despite "normal" bicarbonate** — the cause is the chloride load of saline (SID collapse, hyperchloraemic acidosis with an unremarkable-looking bicarbonate: the bicarbonate view calls the numbers near-normal, while the SID view predicts the acid fall straight from the electrolyte difference) and dilution of albumin — a **hypoproteinaemic alkalosis** as A_tot falls, hidden behind the chloride — modern resuscitation's choice of balanced crystalloids over 0.9% saline (the SMART and SPLIT trials' small-but-real acidosis and kidney-injury signals) is a **Stewart-prescription**: keep the strong-ion difference at ~38 mmol/L by giving acetate, gluconate or lactate as metabolisable anions instead of chloride. The reconciliation: bicarbonate is where physiology measures, strong-ion difference is where pharmacology acts — and any clinician who has alkalinised a patient with both bicarbonate and saline has been using both formulations in the same hour.
