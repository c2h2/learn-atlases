The autonomic nervous system is the body's automatic pilot: a set of spinal, brainstem and hypothalamic circuits that regulate the viscera below the level of consciousness, using the same transmitters as the somatic system (acetylcholine, noradrenaline) in different places and at different receptors. Its anatomy explains its pharmacology — where each drug acts follows from which neuron releases which transmitter onto which receptor — and its reflexes explain its pathology: almost every autonomic symptom is a reflex running normally against a lesion in one of its arcs.

This chapter covers the structural plan and chemical transmission of the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions, ganglionic and end-organ physiology, the central integration of cardiovascular control with the baroreflex, and the enteric and clinical divisions.

## The plan

::: definition The two-neuron efferent chain {#def-plan}
Preganglionic neurons (intermediolateral column, T1–L2 for the **sympathetic**; brainstem nuclei III, VII, IX, X and S2–S4 for the **parasympathetic**; the **enteric** system is a self-contained gut wall nervous system with its own reflex arcs and modulatory input from both) release **acetylcholine onto nicotinic (Nₙ) receptors** of postganglionic cells in ganglia; postganglionic **sympathetic** neurons release **noradrenaline** onto α and β adrenoceptors (except the sweat glands — sympathetic in anatomy, cholinergic (M₃) in transmission, and the adrenal medulla, a modified ganglion that secretes adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood). Postganglionic **parasympathetic** neurons release acetylcholine onto **muscarinic (M₃-dominant) receptors**. Consequences follow mechanically: sympathetic effects are **diffuse and mass** (short preganglionic fibres with many postganglionic targets; circulating catecholamines reach every cell; adrenoceptors are everywhere), parasympathetic are **discrete and fractionated** (long fibres to terminal ganglia serving one organ — the eye can constrict one muscle while the heart keeps beating); the **varicosity** of autonomic axons means transmitter acts volume-wise over a millimetre, not at a point junction, which is why both receptor subtypes and local blood flow shape the response, and why atropine's effects are organ-specific by parasympathetic tone, not by receptor distribution.
:::

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | T1–L2, lateral horn | Brainstem III, VII, IX, X; S2–S4 |
| Ganglion | Paravertebral/prevertebral, near cord | Near or in the target |
| Preganglionic transmitter | ACh → Nₙ | ACh → Nₙ |
| Postganglionic transmitter | NA (ACh at sweat, muscle vasodilator) | ACh → M |
| Adrenal medulla | Preganglionic directly to chromaffin cells (A, NA in blood) | — |
| Fibre length | Short pre, long post | Long pre, short post |
| Tone | Vascular sympathetic tone (vasoconstriction) runs the blood pressure | Vagal tone slows the heart at rest |

## Ganglia, receptors, co-transmission

::: definition Ganglionic transmission {#def-ganglion}
The autonomic ganglion is a integrative centre, not a relay: each postganglionic cell samples 10–50 preganglionic fibres, and its output is set by three conductances — the **fast EPSP** (ACh at Nₙ channels, the drug-sensitive nicotinic synapse whose block is hexamethonium, now clinical only in its side effects), the **slow EPSP** (muscarinic M₁, closing a K⁺ leak; minutes; the reason a ganglion can fire long after the preganglionic volley) and **long-lasting IPSPs** (DCN-containing LNPI neurons; dopamine and GABA from interstitial cells). Co-transmission is the rule in both divisions: **ATP with noradrenaline** at the vas deferens and bladder arterioles (α₁-independent purinergic component that matters in the human bladder and in ischaemia), **VIP with acetylcholine** in salivary and penile vasodilator nerves, and the **NANC (non-adrenergic non-cholinergic)** myenteric excitatory and inhibitory nerves whose transmitters are substance P/enkephalin and NO/VIP respectively — Dale's principle revised: one neuron releases all its transmitters at all its terminals, and the response is the target's receptor set. The pharmacology chapters exploit this anatomy one synapse at a time — [[pharmacology/autonomic-drugs]].
:::

## Central integration: the vasomotor system

::: definition The baroreflex arc {#def-baroreflex}
Arterial pressure is defended on a seconds timescale by high-pressure stretch receptors in the **carotid sinus** (nerve of Hering → glossopharyngeal) and **aortic arch** (vagus) firing rate-dependently with transmural pressure, projecting to the **nucleus tractus solitarius**, which excites the **caudal ventrolateral medulla** (GABAergic) and thereby inhibits the **rostral ventrolateral medulla** — the bulbospinal sympathotonic centre whose descending vasoconstrictor and cardiac-accelerating drive is tonically active at rest (sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone holds the capacitance vessels and sets total peripheral resistance) — and disinhibits the **nucleus ambiguus/dorsal motor vagal** neurons. The result: **pressure up → vagal bradycardia and sympathetic withdrawal** within two beats; **pressure down → the converse**. The arc's gain is high (an open-loop pressure change of a third produces a compensatory near-full correction), it resets over days to the prevailing pressure (which is why hypertension is not treated by the reflex and why the baroreceptor-bearing carotid sinus is surgically decompressed in resistant hypertension trials), and its failure defines the clinical syndromes below.
:::

::: theorem Reading a reflex in a patient {#thm-reflex}
The baroreflex is a quantitative clinical instrument. A phenylephrine bolus raises MAP from 90 to 120 mmHg and the heart falls from 75 to 55: **reflex gain = ΔHR/ΔMAP ≈ −0.67 beats per mmHg**; atropine abolishes the bradycardia (it is vagal), propranolol leaves the tachycardia of hypotension intact until it blocks the β-receptor itself (the efferent limb is both limbs). Orthostatic stress is the same arc at its limit: standing pools 500–700 mL in leg and splanchnic beds, venous return and stroke volume fall, systolic pressure dips, the unloading reflex fires — **heart rate up ≤20 and diastolic up, systolic within 20 mmHg** is normal; a fall of **≥20/10 mmHg within three minutes without heart-rate compensation** is neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (the reflex arc itself is broken — multiple system atrophy, diabetic autonomic neuropathy), and a rise of **≥30 bpm without blood-pressure fall** is postural tachycardia syndrome (arc intact, the compensation excessive — deconditioning, hypovolaemia, small-fibre neuropathy, hypermobile connective tissue). The same logic localises the lesion in autonomic failure: **plasma noradrenaline low supine and rising little on standing** = postganglionic (peripheral) failure; **normal-to-low with no rise, with intact ganglionic responses** = central/preganglionic (MSA). The tilt-table, the Valsalva (phase I pressure, IIa mechanical/IIb early recovery, III release, IV overshoot with reflex bradycardia — each phase an intact-arc test), the deep-breath heart-rate variation (E:I ratio, the earliest vagal neuropathy marker in diabetes) and quantitative sudomotor axon reflex testing are the reflex quantified at the bedside.
:::

::: example The pressure that comes in storms {#ex-pheo}
A 43-year-old has ten-minute episodes of pounding headache, palpitations, drenching sweat and pallor with pressures of 220/130; a beta-blocker given alone made the pressure worse.
::: solution
The spell is a **phaeochromocytoma** bolusing catecholamines — the triad (headache, sweat, palpitation) with paroxysmal hypertension, and the "spell" physiology of a tumour secreting into a circulation that keeps clearing and re-loading (the beta-blocker-alone catastrophe is this chapter's receptor logic as a commandment: with β₂-mediated vasodilatation and cardiac output blocked, α₁-constricted vessels face unopposed circulating adrenaline with nowhere to go — the pressure climbs; **alpha-block first (phenoxybenzamine, a covalent antagonist that outruns the catecholamine surges by its irreversibility), then beta-block for the reflex tachycardia, then salt-load and operate**). The diagnostic mirrors the mechanism — **plasma free metanephrines** (tumour catecholamines are metabolised intracellularly by COMT continuously, so metanephrine is the leak-proof marker), urine fractionated metanephrines, then CT/MRI and MIBG for the 10% bilateral, extra-adrenal, malignant and familial ones, with the RET/VHL/NF1/succinate-dehydrogenase genetics that make every case a family screen. The perioperative physiology is the whole chapter's clinical face: the intubation and tumour-handling surges (deepen anaesthesia, phentolamine or nitroprusside or magnesium for the crisis), then the clamped-tumour collapse (catecholamine-withdrawal into a chronically constricted, volume-depleted circulation — fluids and weaned alpha-block). Incidentally normotensive cases found on imaging ("incidentaloma") are tested the same way, because the untreated spell is also the stroke, dissection and pulmonary oedema literature calls the presentation.
:::
:::

::: example The pupil that spares itself {#ex-pupil-sparing}
A diabetic sixty-year-old's right eyelid drops and the eye sits down and out with diplopia — but the pupil reacts normally to light and accommodates.
::: solution
This is the anatomy of the third nerve as a vascular diagram: the **parasympathetic pupillomotor fibres run on the nerve's surface** in the pial vessel supply, while the somatic fibres to levator and the medial, superior and inferior recti lie deep within the fascicles. **Microvascular ischaemia** (diabetes, hypertension) infarcts the vasa nervorum's core — the somatic fibres die, the surface fibres are spared by their pial diffusion: painful ptosis and ophthalmoplegia **with a spared pupil**. A **compressive** lesion (the posterior communicating artery aneurysm, uncal herniation) hits from outside in — the pupil goes first (the surgical red line: a new third-nerve palsy with a large fixed pupil is imaging today, not observation), because the same surface location that saved the diabetic's pupil dooms this one. The pupillography is this chapter's doctrine applied: the efferent arc (Edinger-Westphal to the ciliary ganglion to postsynaptic M₃) is tested by light, by the near response and by the pharmacological drops — the dilated pupil of cholinergic block constricts poorly to pilocarpine, while the **tonic Adie pupil** of ciliary-ganglion denervation constricts to dilute methacholine that leaves the normal eye still, the supersensitivity test written by the same receptor-upregulation law that writes the orthostatic surges.

::: example The nose that runs and the eye that droops {#ex-horner}
A 58-year-old smoker has a left ptosis, a small pupil that dilates slowly in the dark and doesn't change with darkness as much as the right, and no sweating on the left face; the headache and arm pain came first.
::: solution
The three signs are one lesion of the **oculosympathetic pathway**: ptosis (Müller's muscle, the sympathetic tarsal smooth muscle — mild, unlike levator palsy), **miosis** (dilator pupillae paralysed while sphincter — parasympathetic, M₃ — runs free, hence the anisocoria larger in the dark: "sympathetic paresis shows in the dark"), and **anhidrosis** (sudomotor fibres travel with the carotid adventitia after the superior cervical ganglion — their presence or absence localises the lesion). A **Pancoast tumour at the apex** catches the pathway with the first thoracic sympathetic outflow (the C8–T1 arm pain is the root invasion) — the classical sequence the chapter's anatomy predicts and the chest CT must exclude; carotid dissection is the other emergency (pain, Horner, then the stroke the intimal tear was heralding). Pharmacological confirmation follows the transmission: **cocaine 4%** blocks noradrenaline reuptake and dilates a normal pupil but not one with no sympathetic drive to begin with (negative in Horner); **hydroxyamphetamine** releases store from the postganglionic terminal — dilation localises the lesion to the first or second neuron (preganglionic, the store intact), failure to dilate marks postganglionic. And the runny nose in the same patient set — vasomotor rhinitis, gustatory sweating (Frey syndrome after parotid surgery: aberrant regeneration of auriculotemporal parasympathetic fibres onto sweat glands, the aberrant-regeneration theme in one damp sign) — reminds that these are smooth-muscle and gland reflexes, built to be wrong when re-wired.
:::
:::
::: example The labile neck {#ex-barofailure}
Ten years after neck irradiation for laryngeal cancer a man has wild blood-pressure swings — 220 systolic with agitation, 90 after morphine — with tachycardia, and no orthostatic drop.
::: solution
This is **baroreflex failure**: irradiation (or carotid surgery, neck dissection, bilateral glomus tumours) destroys the afferent nerves or the sinus baroreceptors themselves, so the buffer that turns pressure excursions into corrections is gone — arterial pressure becomes **open-loop**, running with whatever central command and the humoral systems deliver (the agitation-surge, the analgesia-collapse pattern), with a fixed tachycardia from lost vagal buffering and **preserved** efferent sympathetic function (hence no orthostatic hypotension — the diagnostic contrast with the afferent-intact/efferent-dead syndromes of [[#thm-reflex]]). Testing shows the signature: absent or perverse heart-rate response to phenylephrine and nitroprusside (the baroreflex slopes flat), and treatment is the pharmacological buffering the centre cannot provide — clonidine and other central sympatholytics (the gain they restore is the brainstem's own, which is why benzodiazepines and control of afferent storms work too), long-acting agents over short, and the avoidance of the baroreceptor-nerve ablations designed for exactly the opposite physiology (their patients are made artificially into this one). The case is the reflex-arc method's negative image: every autonomic syndrome is named by which limb is silent, and the two-neuron plan is the grammar that makes the sentence readable.
:::
:::

::: history
The divisional anatomy is nineteenth-century — Goll's and Flexner's sympathetic chains, and **Langley's** precise 1898–1903 stimulation and degeneration experiments that defined the preganglionic/postganglionic split, named the autonomic, and even predicted its chemical transmission. **Loewi's** 1921 vagus perfusion proved the parasympathetic transmitter was a humoral substance (acetylcholine, with Dale's own earlier work); **the Euler's** 1946 identification of noradrenaline as the sympathetic one completed the chemical plan; **Cannon's** "emergency" hypothalamic mass-discharge doctrine and his denervation supersensitivity laws (the receptor-upregulation physiology behind the eye-drop tests and the orthostatic surges) dominated the integration era; and the modern period belongs to the **central autonomic network** — the anatomical tracing from the insula, amygdala and periaqueductal grey through the RVLM that explains why emotion, pain and respiration move the pulse and pressure, and why the hypothalamus, not the ganglion, owns the syndrome of autonomic dysreflexia.
:::

## The enteric division and the gut

::: definition The gut's own nervous system {#def-enteric}
The **myenteric (Auerbach)** plexus between the muscle layers runs motility (excitatory motor neurons: ACh and substance P onto smooth muscle; inhibitory: NO and VIP — the receptive relaxation of the orad stomach and the lower oesophageal sphincter's opening, whose loss gives the achalasia of [[gastrointestinal/oesophagus-stomach]]); the **submucosal (Meissner)** plexus runs secretion, absorption and local blood flow; **interstitial cells of Cajal** are the pacemakers whose slow waves (3/min in the stomach, ~8 in the duodenum, falling along the gut — the gradient that organises peristalsis) depolarise the syncytium toward threshold, with spikes only when excitation exceeds them. Intrinsic primary afferent neurons in the mucosa and muscle start the peristaltic reflex ("receptor" field of the gut), and the system contains ~20–100 million neurons — more than the cord — and most of the body's serotonin and much of its dopamine, the source of the "second brain" literature and of the enteropathy of serotonergic drugs. Vagal and sympathetic inputs **modulate, they do not command**: the gut's reflexes are complete without them, which is why transplant and denervation leave motility (disordered, but present) while intrinsic loss — the absence of ganglion cells in **Hirschsprung disease**, from failed neural-crest migration into the rectum and more proximally with longer aganglionic segments — gives the neonate who does not pass meconium (the rectal biopsy's gold standard: no submucosal plexus, hypertrophied nerve trunks; the pull-through operation).
:::

::: quiz
At the end-organ level, the differentiator between sympathetic and parasympathetic effects on the same organ is usually:
- [ ] Which spinal segment the preganglionic fibre enters
- [x] Which receptor subtype the target cell expresses and what tone it runs at rest
- [ ] Whether the postganglionic fibre is myelinated
- [ ] The presence of a ganglion near the organ
::: solution
Both divisions arrive with acetylcholine somewhere; the target decides by receptor set and resting tone: the SA node runs under **vagal tone at rest** (atropine raises rate; the sympathetic effect appears only under stress), the cutaneous vasculature under **sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone** (block causes vasodilatation and the orthostatic fall), the bronchus largely **without vagal tone at rest in the adult** (atropine does little; cholinergic bronchoconstriction is largely pathological, the vagal component of asthma's reflex arc) — and the stomach's parietal cell is stimulated by vagal ACh (M₃), gastrin and histamine converging on one calcium–cAMP integrator. In autonomic pharmacology the lesion of clinical relevance is the receptor or the tone, and the table above is a map of neither — [[pharmacology/autonomic-drugs]] works this out drug by drug.
:::

## Where this leads

The receptor and drug detail is [[pharmacology/autonomic-drugs]]; blood-pressure control's circulatory half is [[cardiovascular/circulation-blood-pressure]]; the adrenal medulla as an endocrine organ is [[endocrine/adrenal]]; the gut motor physiology returns in [[gastrointestinal/digestion-absorption]]; micturition's pontine arc is in [[renal/urinary-tract]]; the autonomic features of diabetes in [[endocrine/diabetes-complications]].

::: summary
- Two chains, one chemistry: ACh at nicotinic ganglia everywhere, then noradrenaline (sympathetic, diffuse, mass) or acetylcholine (parasympathetic, fractionated, discrete) at the target — with the exceptions (sweat glands, adrenal medulla, co-transmission) that define the clinical picture.
- The autonomic ganglion integrates (fast and slow EPSPs, inhibitory arcs), and varicose volume transmission means receptors, blood flow and tone — not junctional anatomy — set the effect.
- Arterial pressure is defended second-to-second by the baroreflex (TL→CVLM→RVLM→spinal, with vagal efferents); its gain is measurable, resets with chronic pressure, and its failure localises as orthostatic hypotension (neurogenic, adrenergic failure) or POTS (excessive compensation).
- Co-transmission (ATP, VIP, NO, NANC) makes the bladder, gut and sexual organs pharmacologically three-target organs, not two.
- The enteric nervous system is autonomous at the level of reflexes and commanded only in tone — its pacemakers, interneurons and crest-derived cell bodies make motility a nervous-system disease as much as a gut one.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Name the sites {level=1}
Give the transmitter and receptor at: ganglion, sympathetic vasculature of skin, sweat gland, heart SA node, bronchial muscle, detrusor, bladder neck/prostate, and skeletal-muscle vasodilator fibres.
::: solution
Ganglion: **ACh–nicotinic (Nₙ)**; skin vessels: **NA–α₁** (and ATP co-transmission in some beds); sweat gland: **ACh–M₃** (sympathetic cholinergic — the atropine test of sudomotor function); SA node: **ACh–M₂** (vagal, I_f and K⁺) with **NA–β₁** sympathetic; bronchus: **ACh–M₃** constrictor (mucus too) and **β₂** relaxant (the circulating-catecholamine route the lung gets from the adrenal); detrusor: **ACh–M₃** contraction (parasympathetic; with ATP purinergic component in the obstructed/bladder outflow); bladder neck and prostate: **NA–α₁** contraction (the tamsulosin target); skeletal muscle vasodilatation: **sympathetic cholinergic (M₃, plus the co-released vasodilator set)** in the defence reaction — the exercise "muscle pump" and the epinephrine β₂ component together, and the reason the alpha-blockade of the vasculature does not abolish exercise hyperaemia (which is local metabolite-driven).
:::

::: exercise Two drops {level=1}
A patient with anisocoria gets 4% cocaine in both eyes: the right dilates, the left does not. Then hydroxyamphetamine in the left: still nothing. Localise the lesion and give two causes.
::: solution
Cocaine failure to dilate proves **no sympathetic traffic** on the left (Horner — cocaine blocks reuptake, so it needs a terminal releasing noradrenaline to work). Hydroxyamphetamine releases **stored** noradrenaline from an intact postganglionic terminal; failure to dilate after it means the store is gone — a **postganglionic (third-order)** lesion. Causes: **carotid dissection** (pain, the urgent one), cavernous sinus or orbital apex disease (with the other cranial nerves), and the older lists' pancoast or mediastinal spread below the stellate for the preganglionic pattern (those would dilate after hydroxyamphetamine). The two remaining possibilities are excluded by the sequence: a normal pupil dilates to cocaine, and a first/second-order Horner dilates to hydroxyamphetamine — and the 0.1% adrenaline recheck (denervation supersensitivity of the chronically postganglionic lesion dilates the pupil with a dilute adrenaline dose that leaves the normal eye untouched).
:::
:::

::: exercise Tonic and phasic {level=2}
Explain, from the tone doctrine: (a) why atropine raises heart rate but rarely drops blood pressure; (b) why autonomic failure causes orthostatic hypotension but supine hypertension; (c) why the diabetic's resting tachycardia is an early sign.
::: solution
(a) The **heart runs under dominant vagal tone** at rest (the barostat's parasympathetic limb); the **circulation runs under sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone** with little parasympathetic vascular input — atropine removes the former (rate rises) and has no vascular tone to remove (pressure barely moves; the α-blockers' domain). (b) In neurogenic failure the baroreflex effector is dead: standing unloads without compensation — venous pooling, pressure falls (orthostatic hypotension); lying removes the gravitational gradient and the **denervation supersensitivity of the up-regulated α-receptors**, plus whatever residual humoral catecholamines act, over-constricts a now-centralised volume — supine hypertension, the treatment trap (head-up tilt at night, no dose at bedtime) of the midodrine era. (c) The vagus fails first in length-dependent neuropathy (the heart's long parasympathetic arc); losing the brake at rest leaves the sympathetic and intrinsic rate unopposed — the **resting tachycardia and the lost deep-breath variation (E:I ratio < 1.1, the earliest testable autonomic sign)**, before the postganglionic adrenergic failure that produces the orthostatic drop. Three signs, one doctrine: **function is tonic activity through the receptor set, so the lesion's order is the fibre's length.**
:::

::: exercise The dysreflexic spell {level=2}
A man with a T6 spinal cord injury gets a pounding headache, sweating above the lesion and a pressure of 240/130 after a femoral fracture — and bradycardia with it. Explain the whole picture arc by arc, and its treatment logic.
::: solution
Below an intact-spinal-baroreflex lesion, **no descending inhibition**: the fracture's nociceptive input fires the segmental sympathetic surge — massive vasoconstriction below the lesion and the pressure spike; the baroreflex senses the pressure and responds **above the lesion** (vagal bradycardia, vasodilatation and sweating in the head-neck arms — the "above-lesion flushing and sweat, below-lesion pale and dry" signature the patient learns to dread), but the cord cannot deliver the inhibition to the splanchnic outflow. Treatment is the arc in reverse: **sit the patient up** (orthostatic off-load), remove the trigger (the catheter for the bladder — the commonest cause — the fracture's spasm, the faecal impaction), then short-acting vasodilators (sublingual nifedipine historically, nitrates, hydralazine, diazoxide) to blunt the surge until the noxious stimulus is gone; long-term, avoid triggers, and the anaesthetic corollary (spinal/epidural above the lesion blocks the efferent arc) — the same spinal-anesthesia logic used for the vascular surgery below a sympathetic blockade.
:::
:::

::: exercise Purinergic and residual {level=2}
The obstructed human bladder responds poorly to muscarinic antagonists alone, and the α-blocker and β₃ strategies behave as if a second contractile system were involved; the diabetic's bladder is large and slack. Give the physiological reasons.
::: solution
The bladder has **three transmitter layers**: M₃ contraction of the detrusor (dominant in the normal human), an **ATP/purinergic (P2X)** component from subepithelial afferent-motor coupling that is small in the normal adult bladder but **greatly up-regulated with obstruction, denervation and age** (the "purinergic flare" that makes antimuscarinic therapy feel incomplete — and why the P2X3 antagonist class was pursued for overactivity, with its dry-mouth-free afferent target), and the **sensory limb** (urothelial ATP and TRPV1 signalling to afferents) that drives the sensation and the instability. The diabetic large slack bladder is another disease: **autonomic (and sensory) neuropathy** — lost afferent fullness (hence the huge capacity and the residual volume) and lost efferent contraction — so the therapy is behavioural (timed voiding, double voiding) and clean intermittent catheterisation, not drugs, while the overactive neuropathic detrusor (supra-lesional, e.g. after stroke) is the antimuscarinic/beta-3 (mirabegron-relaxant) and botulinum-toxin territory. The clinical pharmacology divides entirely on this chapter's question: **which limb of which arc is broken** — see [[renal/urinary-tract]].
:::

::: exercise Read the Valsalva {level=2}
Predict the four-phase blood-pressure and heart-rate trace in: (a) pure autonomic failure (postganglionic), (b) baroreflex failure with intact efferents, (c) heart failure with low output.
::: solution
The normal trace: phase I pressure transient; IIa fall with reflex tachycardia (mechanical); IIb continued low pressure with maintained tachycardia and rising total peripheral resistance; III release dip; **phase IV overshoot** (an over-constricted bed plus the refilled heart) **with reflex bradycardia** — IIb and IV are the adrenergic efferent test, the bradycardia the cardiovagal test. **(a) Autonomic failure:** pressure falls progressively through phase II (no vasoconstriction), no overshoot, no reflex bradycardia — the V-shaped trace where the square is absent; supine noradrenaline low. **(b) Baroreflex failure:** constriction and rate changes occur (efferents alive) but **unmodulated** — exaggerated, labile phase IIb and IV without the smoothing bradycardia; the trace's noise is the diagnosis. **(c) Heart failure:** overshoot blunted while the tachycardia is exaggerated — low stroke volume amplifies every phase, baroreflex gain is reduced (central resetting, carotid stiffness) and heightened muscle-nerve reflex drive adds rate, the pattern that tracks the sympathetic overactivity the β-blocker trials made prognostic. Three traces, one manoeuvre, the whole two-neuron arc interrogated in fifteen seconds at the bedside.
:::

::: exercise Cold, baro and ageing {level=2}
A 78-year-old on prazosin for BPH, with a stiff carotid sinus and a low-renin volume-expanded circulation, nearly falls after lunch. Assemble the four physiology items into one fall.
::: solution
Post-prandial hypotension: splanchnic vasodilatation (the **enteric and VIP/purinergic vasodilator** limb of digestion) pools 500–700 mL in the gut; normal subjects compensate by **baroreflex-mediated sympathetic activation** (heart rate and resistance up). Each element of this patient weakens the compensation: **age-stiffened baroreceptors and low gain** (less afferent firing per mmHg), **prazosin** blocking the α₁ efferent arm (the iatrogenic one, worst after the first dose and after a high-carbohydrate lunch — the glucose–insulin–splanchnic mechanism), and low-renin volume expansion making the pressure reflex the only effective lever (angiotensin-II support of the sympathetic output is off). Treatment reads off the same list: smaller low-carbohydrate meals, water before (the osmo-baroreflex volume load raises pressure), lying down after eating, and removing or moving the alpha-blocker to night with a uroselective agent. The case is the baroreflex theorem in geriatric prescription form.
:::

::: exercise Design the autonomic function laboratory {level=3}
Design a five-test battery that localises a lesion along this chapter's axis (afferent small fibre, central, preganglionic, postganglionic adrenergic, cholinergic sudomotor), with the expected pattern in pure autonomic failure (postganglionic), multiple system atrophy (central/preganglionic) and diabetic neuropathy (length-dependent mixed).
::: solution
(1) **Small-fibre afferents:** quantitative sensory testing and skin-punch intraepidermal nerve-fibre density and laser-evoked potentials. (2) **Cardiovagal (efferent parasympathetic):** deep-breath E:I ratio and Valsalva phase-IV bradycardia — vagal arc gain. (3) **Sympathetic adrenergic:** Valsalva phases II–III and the overshoot (intact efferent constriction), finger photoplethysmography, plus **plasma noradrenaline supine and standing** — the localisation lever: absent supine and no rise (postganglionic loss, **pure autonomic failure**) versus present-with-blunted-rise (central, **MSA**), where the supine pressure is often preserved-with-hypertension and the overshoot is present but the baroreflex slope is flat (afferent-intact, central-integration-dead, efferent alive) — the MSA/PAF discrimination in two lines. (4) **Sudomotor:** QSART (postganglionic cholinergic — reduced in length-dependent **diabetic neuropathy** from feet up, with cardiovagal loss early and adrenergic late) and thermoregulatory sweat test. (5) **Pupil and skin-write reflexes** as the localising extras — the sympathetic skin response and the cold-pressor pressor rise. Expected patterns: **PAF** = low supine NA, no overshoot, abnormal QSART (the ganglion-on disease), preserved cardiovagal early; **MSA** = normal-ish NA, blunted overshoot with supine hypertension, normal QSART (the periphery is intact) and abnormal brainstem reflexes; **diabetes** = length-dependent gradient across all arms, cardiovagal first. The battery works because the chapter's efferent chains are serially testable, and each segment's lesion leaves its neighbours' signs as the controls — the reflex-arc method the whole course keeps using.
:::
