Muscle is the body's motor: a molecular assembly that turns the chemical energy of ATP into force and shortening, controlled in one tissue type by the nerve that moves it, in another by its own pacemaker and the autonomic tone laid over it, and in a third by hormones, stretch and local chemistry. The three types — skeletal, cardiac, smooth — share one contractile mechanism (the actin–myosin sliding filament) and differ in everything wrapped around it: the trigger that raises the calcium, the architecture that directs the force, and the purpose the physics serves.

This chapter covers the sarcomere and the sliding-filament mechanism, excitation–contraction coupling in all three tissues, the mechanics of force and shortening, and the clinical disorders the mechanism explains.

## The sarcomere

::: definition The contractile apparatus {#def-sarcomere}
The **sarcomere** — Z disc to Z disc — is the repeating engine of striated muscle. The **thick filament** is ~300 myosin-II molecules, each with a coiled-coil tail and two heads carrying the ATPase and the actin-binding site; the heads project in the bare central zone and the **H zone**. The **thin filament** is a double helix of **actin** (G-actin–F-actin with tropomyosin in the groove and the **troponin complex TnC–TnI–TnT**: the calcium sensor, the inhibitor, the tropomyosin anchor), anchored at the Z disc by **α-actinin** and running inward; **nebulin** sets thin-filament length and **titin** — the giant spring from Z to M — centres the thick filament, provides passive stiffness and unfolds to prevent overstretch. Contraction is the **cross-bridge cycle**: myosin head (with ADP·Pᵢ bound, energised by the previous ATP hydrolysis) binds actin, releases the phosphate (power stroke), releases ADP, holds (**rigor**) until a new ATP binds and detaches the head; ~5 heads per half-sarcomere cycle asynchronously, and at maximal shortening the cycling rate falls and efficiency ~40–60% of the chemical energy becomes work and heat. The **A band** (thick-filament length) is constant; the **I band and H zone shorten** as the thin filaments slide — Huxley's 1954 proof in a single sentence, with the filament lengths themselves unchanged.
:::

::: theorem Length, velocity and load: the mechanics of a muscle {#thm-mechanics}
**Length–tension**: active force is maximal at the resting sarcomere length (~2.2 µm, optimal overlap) and falls as overlap decreases (stretch) or thin filaments collide (shortening) — the basis of the operating range of heart and limb, and of the descending limb of the cardiac function curve within the physiological range. **Force–velocity** (Hill's hyperbola): shortening velocity falls hyperbolically with load, V_max at zero load and zero velocity at **P₀** (isometric maximum); the product — **power** — peaks at about a third of P₀, which is why throwing and jumping movements load the limb lightly and contraction speed is trained with light, fast work. **Summation and tetanus**: a twitch (skeletal, ~10–100 ms) sums with itself if the next stimulus arrives before relaxation — **wave (temporal) summation** and then **tetanus**, fused and 3–5× a twitch — because the second calcium transient arrives on a still-saturated troponin and cross-bridge population; **staircase (treppe/Bowditch)** is a different, calcium-availability effect and belongs to the heart in [[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]]. **Recruitment**: motor units are added by the **size principle** (Henneman) — small, slow, fatigue-resistant units first, large fast-glycolytic last — because small axons have higher input resistance and reach threshold first; graded force is mostly recruitment plus rate coding, not more calcium per fibre.
:::

## Excitation–contraction coupling, three ways

::: definition Skeletal: mechanical coupling {#def-sect}
The motor end-plate potential sweeps the surface membrane and the **transverse (T) tubules** (at the A–I junction in mammals) past the **dihydropyridine receptor (DHPR, Cav1.1)**, whose voltage-sensor domains are **mechanically coupled** to the **ryanodine receptor RyR1** of the sarcoplasmic reticulum — depolarisation opens the release channel without needing extracellular calcium entry (the reason skeletal contraction survives calcium-free media for a while, and that malignant hyperthermia is an RyR1 disease — see the example below). Released calcium saturates troponin C (TnC), TnI's inhibition of actin–myosin is lifted, and contraction runs; relaxation requires **SERCA1/2a pumping calcium back (ATP-dependent)** against a steep gradient, the calcium-buffering **calsequestrin** inside the store, and the **Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger and plasma-membrane Ca-ATPase** clearing what leaked. Fast (type IIx/IIb) fibres have fewer mitochondria and SERCA kinetics matched to rapid relaxation; slow (type I) fibres are oxidative, fatigue-resistant, and dense with capillaries and myoglobin — and a motor unit is defined by its motoneurone, so its fibre type follows its firing pattern and training, the fibre-type switching of endurance training being physiology's most reproducible plasticity.
:::

::: definition Cardiac: calcium-induced calcium release {#def-cicr}
The cardiac action potential's plateau Ca²⁺ (L-type DHPR/Cav1.2) enters the T-tubules (wider, z-line level, one per sarcomere) and **triggers RyR2 opening by calcium itself** — **calcium-induced calcium release**, the spark summed into the transient that binds troponin C. Contraction's strength is therefore graded by the **trigger calcium's size** — the mechanism of inotropy: β-adrenergic phosphorylation of the L-type channel (more trigger), of **phospholamban** (relieving SERCA inhibition, faster reuptake and a bigger store), and of RyR2 (leakier, but timed) increases force and relaxation; **digoxin** acts one step downstream, inhibiting the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase so the raised internal Na⁺ slows **NCX** extrusion and each beat leaves more calcium behind — a pump inhibitor read as a calcium drug (see [[cardiovascular/heart-failure]]). The heart cannot be tetanised because the long action potential's refractory period spans the whole twitch — the guarantee that it fills — and **rest-potentiation and the force–frequency relation** (positive staircase in the intact heart, the reverse in failing) are the same calcium handling read at different rates.
:::

::: definition Smooth muscle: the calmodulin–MLC route {#def-smooth}
Smooth muscle has **no troponin and no sarcomere**: actin runs from **dense bodies** (functional Z discs) and dense plaques to myosin in a lattice that can rearrange, and contraction is initiated when calcium–**calmodulin** activates **myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK)**, which phosphorylates the myosin regulatory light chain, permitting cross-bridge cycling. Because free calcium is the trigger with no scaffolding for coincidence, tone is set by the **balance of MLCK and myosin light-chain phosphatase**, and modulated at a distance: kinases (Rho-kinase) inhibit the phosphatase, so the same calcium can produce more force (**calcium sensitisation** — the target of the vasospasm and asthma literature), and the **latch state** (dephosphorylated cross-bridges cycling slowly) maintains tone with a fraction of the energy, the secret of the arteriole that holds pressure all day on almost no ATP. Relaxation follows calcium removal and NO–cGMP–MLCK-desensitising pathways (the nitrate and sildenafil axis of [[cardiovascular/vascular-disease]]). Smooth muscle is **myogenic**: stretch increases tone (the Bayliss response, the basis of autoregulation), and many units contract as syncytia through gap junctions, with pacemaker slow waves in gut (the interstitial cells of Cajal — the GI motility chapters') and phasic tone in the bladder, uterus (oxytocin's endpoint) and large vessels.
:::

::: example The malignant heat {#ex-mh}
During halothane anaesthesia a child's end-tidal CO₂ rises, the masseter jerks, the temperature climbs one degree in ten minutes, the muscles become board-like, and the potassium and lactate pour out; the dantrolene infusion is started.
::: solution
**Malignant hyperthermia** is the skeletal RyR1 channel failing open under trigger (volatile anaesthetic or suxamethonium) in a susceptible mutation: the sarcoplasmic reticulum empties its calcium into the cytosol continuously, cross-bridges cycle without rest, SERCA pumps furiously to re-sequester the calcium — and both engines together consume ATP and produce heat faster than the body can shed it — the rigidity, the respiratory and mixed acidosis, the rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemia that kill. **Dantrolene** is the specific antidote precisely because its target is the defective channel: it binds RyR1 and reduces the open probability, cutting the release at the source (the dose is repeated to effect, with active cooling, treatment of the potassium and the acidosis, and dialysis if the muscle damage demands it). The differential that belongs in the same list — **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (antipsychotic dopamine-blockade, days of onset, rigidity with hyperthermia, treat with bromocriptine/dantrolene, a central set-point and dopaminergic problem, not a channelopathy) and **serotonin syndrome** (clonus, hyperreflexia, agitation, the serotonergic drugs) — shares the hyperthermia but not the mechanism; and the genetics closes with the chapter's recurring theme: the susceptibility alleles are RyR1 and CACNA1S variants, and the MH crisis is the presenting event of a **contractile-protein disease** in a child nobody had called myopathic. The same channels, the same coupling, and a family history is the only warning.
:::
:::

::: example The calf that will not lengthen {#ex-spindle}
Six weeks after a stroke a hemiplegic patient's calf is tight: the more briskly the ankle is dorsiflexed, the harder it resists, and the resistance gives way in a catch; the plantar response is extensor and the knee jerk is exaggerated.
::: solution
The resistance is **spasticity** — hyperexcitability of the **muscle spindle stretch reflex** below a lost corticospinal and reticulospinal set. The anatomy of the loop: Ia afferents from the nuclear-bag fibres of the spindle fire on stretch and lengthening velocity, synapse monosynaptically on the alpha motoneurone of the same muscle (the jerk) and, via one inhibitory interneuron, on its antagonist (reciprocal inhibition); **gamma motoneurones** set spindle sensitivity from the descending systems, and their release from supraspinal control after an upper-motor-neuron lesion biases the loop toward high gain — hence the velocity-dependent catch, the exaggerated jerk, and the clonus when the loop oscillates. Contrast the **rigidity** of Parkinson disease — velocity-independent, uniform through the range, a basal-ganglia set problem with the stretch reflex near-normal — and the **contracture** in which resistance is tissue elasticity, present at all speeds and without the jerk. Treatment targets the loop at its synapses: baclofen (GABA_B, presynaptic depression of afferent transmitter release and postsynaptic hyperpolarisation), diazepam and tizanidine (GABA_A and α₂), botulinum toxin into the overactive muscle (the [[physiology/synapses]] release machinery turned to therapy), stretching and loading programmes, and the intrathecal pump for the severe limb — each one a sentence of this chapter and the last.
:::

::: example The bronchus that tightens on nothing {#ex-airway-smooth}
An asthmatic's airways constrict to histamine at a quarter of the dose that affects a control subject, and salbutamol reverses it within minutes.
::: solution
Two smooth-muscle doctrines run the picture. **Hyperresponsiveness**: airway tone is set by the phosphorylation balance of [[#def-smooth]] — and chronic inflammation raises it by three routes: the sensory-neuron and vagal drive (the reflex arc that cold air and irritants exploit), the loss of the NO and prostacyclin brakes from a damaged epithelium, and the Rho-kinase–mediated **calcium sensitisation** by which the same calcium now produces more force; the result is a leftward shift of the dose–response (the methacholine challenge's PC₂₀ is exactly that curve measured). **Reversal**: β₂-agonists raise cAMP → PKA phosphorylates MLCK (lower activity for a given calcium), opens K⁺ channels (hyperpolarisation closes Cav), and drives the pump-leak handling of calcium — relaxation from the phosphorylation side, without ever lowering calcium much; the anticholinergics cut the IP₃ arm, and the corticosteroids, over days, rebuild the receptor and nitric-oxide machinery that the inflammation had eroded (and, in the chronically over-treated, β-receptor down-regulation is the pharmacological tolerance this chapter's receptor biology predicts). The bronchus is the general lesson of the chapter in one tube: **smooth muscle is a signalling integrator, and airway disease is its input set.**
:::

::: quiz
The immediate source of the energy for myosin head detachment from actin is:
- [ ] Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and phosphate
- [x] Binding of a new ATP molecule to the head
- [ ] Release of ADP from the head
- [ ] Calcium reuptake by SERCA
::: solution
The rigor state ends when **ATP binds** — binding detaches the head; hydrolysis then re-energises it; release of the hydrolysis products on re-binding drives the power stroke. This is why death causes **rigor mortis**: ATP synthesis stops, heads complete their last cycle and remain bound until proteolysis returns compliance hours later; and why a magnesium-free, ATP-depleted muscle in the physiologist's bath is a stick. The ATP is also required for relaxation (SERCA) — the whole cycle's economy, and its two clinical faces: the stiffness of death and the cramp of ATP depletion in metabolic myopathies.
:::

## From fibres to function

The whole-muscle force is the **physiological cross-sectional area** times specific tension (~20–30 N/cm²), because parallel-fibred and pennate muscles trade shortening distance (fibre length) for number of filaments in parallel (the pennation angle lets more fibres pack in, cos-loss in force transmission being the price). Excursions are set by fibre length, velocities by myosin isoform (the fast IIx myosin cycles ~8× slower-type I) and by the series elastic elements (tendon stiffness governs the storage and return of elastic energy — the stretch-shortening cycle of running, the kangaroo's economy, and the reason the gastrocnemius works like a spring while the soleus motors). The motor unit (one motoneurone and its fibres) is the unit of voluntary control; its size — from a dozen fibres in the lateral rectus to a thousand in the gastrocnemius — is the precision hierarchy, and its orderly recruitment by size (the [[#thm-mechanics]] rule) is why fine tasks use small units and why the electromyogram's recruitment pattern is a window on the nerve as well as the muscle.

::: example The child who cannot rise {#ex-duchenne}
A five-year-old boy uses his hands to climb up his own legs on standing from the floor; the calves are large and firm; CK is 12,000 U/L.
::: solution
This is **Duchenne muscular dystrophy** until the gene says otherwise — the **Gowers manoeuvre** (hip and knee extensors too weak to rise against gravity, so the trunk is levered up the thighs), the pseudohypertrophy of calf muscle replaced by fat and connective tissue, and the CK that reports membrane leak. The mechanism is structural: **dystrophin**, the actin-binding rod that links the cytoskeleton through the **dystrophin–glycoprotein complex** to the dystroglycan–laminin bond outside, is absent (X-linked, the reading-frame mutations that truncate the protein); without the shim, every contraction tears the sarcolemma, the fibres necrose, and the replacement fibrosis produces the contractures and the **cardiomyopathy** (the dystrophin isoforms of heart and diaphragm fail on the same clock) that kills in the third and fourth decade, now later with steroids, ACE inhibition and non-invasive ventilation. Becker disease is the in-frame mutation with partial protein — the same gene, a milder dose. The chapter's physiology is the exam answer: membrane fragility, not calcium handling or the contractile proteins themselves, and the diagnostic trail is CK → genetic testing (with deletion/duplication analysis first, since most mutations are deletions) → the rare muscle biopsy showing absent stain. Corticosteroids slow the decline; exon-skipping and reading-through drugs and gene therapy target the specific mutation class — a therapeutic family entirely organised around the mechanics described here.
:::

::: history
The sliding-filament story is one of the cleanest in physiology: Huxley and Hanson's electron microscopy of the contracted and relaxed fibril (1954) and Huxley's interpretation — filaments slide, filaments do not shorten — settled a century of accordion speculations in a few figures, with the interference-band evidence (unchanged A-band length) as the clincher. The cross-bridge chemistry came from Szent-Györgyi's actin and myosin and the ATPase link (the Nobel of 1937, already held by his vitamin-C years), Lohmann's and Meyerhof's muscle bioenergetics (1922), and Hill's force–velocity hyperbola and the thermodynamics of shortening that earned the 1922 prize with the muscle-heat experiments. The calcium trigger arrived with Heilbrunner's debates and then the structure era: the troponin complex (Ebashi), the DHPR–RyR mechanical coupling proved by the calcium-free contraction of skeletal muscle (Ríos, Brum, and the Melzer groups, 1980s) against cardiac CICR's sparks (Cheng, Stern, Lakatta and the N-calcium-imaging revolution of 1993). Henneman's size principle (1965) and the motor-unit physiology grew from the same electrophysiology that gave the EMG its clinical home.
:::

## Where this leads

Cardiac contraction in [[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]]; the respiratory pump muscles in [[respiratory/mechanics-breathing]]; smooth muscle's airway pharmacology in [[respiratory/asthma-copd]] and [[pharmacology/autonomic-drugs]]; the myopathies and myotonias and channelopathies in [[nervous-system/neuromuscular]]; and GI motility and its disorders in [[gastrointestinal/bowel-disease]].

::: summary
- One motor — the actin–myosin cross-bridge — with three bodies: troponin-controlled striated muscle (skeletal and cardiac) and calmodulin–MLC-controlled smooth muscle, each with its own calcium source and purpose.
- Skeletal coupling is mechanical (DHPR to RyR1), cardiac is calcium-induced calcium release (graded, modulated, tetanus-proof), smooth is phosphorylation-state and latch (economic, myogenic, drug-sensitive).
- Force–velocity and length–tension relations govern everything from the power peak of a throw to the operating range of the ventricle; recruitment by the size principle is the nervous system's volume control.
- ATP detaches the head and SERCA relaxes the fibre: the same molecule powers both ends of the cycle, and its absence explains rigor mortis, cramp and fatigue's periphery.
- The clinical list — malignant hyperthermia, myotonias, muscular dystrophies, myasthenic junctions — maps one-to-one onto the parts of the machine: channel, sensor, scaffold, or synapse.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Bands and lines {level=1}
During a concentric contraction, what happens to the A band, the I band, the H zone and the sarcomere length — and which molecular distance actually shortens?
::: solution
**A band unchanged** (thick-filament length); **I and H zones shorten**; sarcomere length falls from ~2.2 toward 1.5 µm. The thin filaments slide inward — no actin or myosin molecule shortens (the 1954 experiment's whole point); the distance between adjacent Z discs is what contracts, and the myosin heads' working strokes (a few nanometres each, tens per second) accumulate into it through the cycle's many repetitions.
:::
:::

::: exercise Recruit by size {level=1}
Explain why transcranial magnetic stimulation of the corticospinal tract preferentially activates large-diameter axons while voluntary contraction recruits small motor units first — and what each observation says about the mechanism of recruitment.
::: solution
TMS depolarises axons by the induced extracellular current: **threshold falls with diameter** (lower internal resistance, more captured membrane), so the biggest, fastest axons fire first — the reverse of nature. Voluntary drive arrives synaptically at the soma and dendrites: **small motoneurones have higher input resistance**, so the same synaptic current depolarises them more, and they reach threshold first (the size principle). The contrast is the proof that physiological recruitment order is a property of **synaptic integration, not axon excitability** — and it is why the EMG recruitment pattern (small units first, firing rate rising, then larger units) is a diagnostic window: in neuropathy the surviving units fire fast and sparse, and re-innervated units are large because sprouting has merged the pools.
:::
:::

::: exercise Inotropy inventory {level=2}
Rank the following for acute positive inotropy and give the molecular step each exploits: calcium chloride, dobutamine, digoxin, milrinone, and raising heart rate from 60 to 110 in the normal heart.
::: solution
All work on the trigger calcium, at four steps. **Calcium** raises the gradient for L-type entry directly (and is the dangerous quick fix in hypocalcaemia and hyperkalaemia). **Dobutamine** (β₁) — cAMP/PKA: L-type channel phosphorylation (more trigger calcium), phospholamban phosphorylation (faster SERCA uptake, bigger store, faster relaxation — the **lusitropic** bonus that distinguishes it from pure calcium). **Milrinone** (PDE3 inhibition) — the same cAMP pool not destroyed: inotropy plus vasodilatation ("inodilator"), useful in the low-output failing heart and its own hypotension. **Digoxin** — pump inhibition → internal Na⁺ up → NCX works less forward → more calcium per beat; slow onset, vagal and toxicity profile (the arrhythmias at toxic levels) that make it a chronic, not acute, drug. **Rate (60→110, normal heart)** — the **force–frequency relation**: more transients per minute and more calcium loading, the physiological staircase, whose failure (negative staircase) is an early sign of failing myocardium — the one item in the list that is a property of the machinery rather than a drug.
:::
:::

::: exercise Stiff muscle, soft muscle {level=2}
Myotonia congenita (ClC-1 chloride-channel loss) gives muscle that "will not let go" after grip; hypothyroidism gives the same delayed relaxation with normal channels. Explain the first and name what the second teaches.
::: solution
The **ClC-1** channel carries most of the resting skeletal-muscle conductance; its role is to shunt the depolarisation that K⁺ accumulation in the narrow T-tubules would otherwise produce during and after activity — losing it lets after-discharges fire the fibre repeatedly (the clinical grip myotonia, the EMG "dive-bomber" and the warm-up phenomenon of repeated exercise), while strength and contraction itself are near-normal — a disease of **hyperexcitability of the membrane, not of the contractile machinery**. Hypothyroid myotonia (and the pseudomyotonia of the slow relaxation after a reflex or grip) arises from **slowed SERCA-mediated calcium reuptake** (reduced thyroid-regulated isoform expression and ATP turnover), so the relaxation time constant lengthens while the membrane is normal: the teaching that "delayed relaxation" localises to two very different places — the surface membrane or the calcium pump — and the EMG separates them instantly (electrical after-activity versus silent slow lengthening), a bedside distinction worth the whole chapter's diagnostics in one manoeuvre.
:::
:::

::: exercise Latch and tone {level=2}
A noradrenaline constricts an arteriole to 30% of its diameter and holds it there for hours with little change in ATP use. Explain with the smooth-muscle phosphorylation logic, and predict what an endothelium-damaging injury changes.
::: solution
The initial constriction is receptor (α₁) → IP₃/DAG → calcium rise → MLCK phosphorylation of the regulatory light chain → cycling. The maintenance is the **latch state**: cross-bridges dephosphorylate while still attached and cycle very slowly (economy of tone), helped by **Rho-kinase inhibition of myosin phosphatase (calcium sensitisation)**, so force per calcium stays high and ATP per force falls to a fraction of the skeletal cycle — the arteriole's all-day hold, and the reason "tone" is not simply "contracted calcium". Endothelial damage removes the **NO/cGMP brake** (and the prostacyclin and hyperpolarising factors), so the same myocyte now runs at higher sensitised tone and the latch state is easier to enter — one mechanism of the vasospastic and remodelling vessel (with the growth-factor literature's long-term version), and the physiological foundation of the clinical fact that the denuded artery constricts to what a healthy one dilutes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Fibre type and disease {level=2}
Type I fibre predominance suits posture; type IIx suits a throw. Which pathologies selectively hit each type, and by what mechanism?
::: solution
**Type I (slow, oxidative)** are recruited continuously, so diseases of sustained energy supply and of the postsynaptic and membrane housekeeping declare themselves in the postural and oculobulbar muscles: the mitochondrial myopathies (the "ragged-red" oxidative fibres failing in the ptosis and ophthalmoplegia of chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia) and myasthenia's fatigable ocular and neck-extensor weakness (overused end-plates fail first). **Type II (fast glycolytic)** are the ones that atrophy with disuse, steroid excess and ageing (sarcopenia's speed and fall risk — the IIx myosin's loss), and the ones that cramp in bursts; the metabolic myopathies of glycolysis (McArdle's — myophosphorylase loss — the second-wind phenomenon and the exercise myoglobinuria) hit them because they run on glycogen. The diagnostic habit is the same for both: the distribution of weakness is the physiology of the fibre's job, and the biopsy's ATPase stain tells which job failed.
:::

::: exercise Design the fatigue experiment {level=2}
A patient fatigues abnormally. Separate central fatigue, neuromuscular-junction fatigue and peripheral (muscle) fatigue with three electrodiagnostic manoeuvres, and state what each localises.
::: solution
(1) ** voluntary activation with cervical or femoral magnetic stimulation (the twitch-overlay/twitch-interpolation technique)**: during a maximal voluntary contraction, a superimposed motor-nerve or cortical stimulus that produces extra force shows the central drive was incomplete — **central fatigue** (pain, motivation, CNS disease, deconditioning). (2) **Repetitive nerve stimulation and the post-exercise incremental test**: a decrement >10% at 3 Hz that recovers after rest, or the 10-second maximum-voluntary-contraction post-exercise **increment** (facilitation unmasking a low-probability presynaptic junction), localises to the **junction** (myasthenic and Lambert–Eaton patterns). (3) **Circular stimulation of the nerve before and after the contraction with the muscle twitch and the compound action potential compared**, or the simpler rest-vs-fatigued stimulation: if the muscle's own twitch (stimulated after the contraction, at rest) is reduced while the nerve and junction are intact, the failure is **peripheral — inside the fibre** (metabolic: phosphate accumulation, SR release failure, the ATP and calcium pumps described here, or substrate lack). The three localisations, the three treatments and diagnoses, and the fact that the chapter's machinery — drive, junction, coupling — is exactly the order of the tests.
:::

::: exercise The heart that cannot relax {level=3}
A patient with diastolic heart failure and a resting tachycardia is started on a rate-slowing strategy and improves. Using the excitation–contraction and lusitropy physiology, explain why relaxation is an active, ATP-dependent process, why tachycardia and ischaemia both worsen it, and why the improvement tracks the rate.
::: solution
Relaxation is the **removal of calcium** — SERCA2a pumping against a 10⁴-fold gradient (two Ca²⁺ transported per ATP hydrolysed), the dominant route, with NCX completing the exit — and the process is **ATP-consuming and temperature- and phosphorylation-dependent** (phospholamban's phosphorylation state, β-adrenergic and lusitropic). Diastolic failure is, mechanically, **incomplete or slowed calcium removal plus altered titin-based stiffness**, so the ventricle relaxes slowly and fills at high pressure. **Tachycardia** attacks it twice: less time in diastole for the same slow pump, and (at high rates) less calcium extrusion per beat with more sodium-load from the busy pump and exchanger — the failing heart's negative force–frequency relation. **Ischaemia** removes the ATP that both contraction and, disproportionately, relaxation need (relaxation fails first — the earliest diastolic abnormality of the ischaemic or hypertrophied heart), and acidosis worsens myofilament calcium sensitivity, slowing cross-bridge detachment. **Slowing the rate** lengthens diastole more than systole, restores the filling window, reduces the sodium-load accumulation and lets SERCA catch up, and so improves filling pressures and symptoms before any change in contractility — the reason rate control is a real therapy in the failing ventricle, and one of the clearest bedside demonstrations that relaxation is not the absence of contraction but its own, metabolically expensive, programme.
:::
