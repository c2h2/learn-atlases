Stroke is the sudden loss of brain function caused by a problem with its blood supply. It is one of the leading causes of death and the commonest cause of complex disability in adults. It is also one of the few neurological emergencies in which treatment given in the first hours changes the outcome. When a large artery is blocked, the brain tissue it supplies dies progressively, and the earlier blood flow is restored, the more is saved. This is why stroke pathways are organised around the clock.

This chapter describes the blood supply of the brain and the syndromes that follow its failure, transient ischaemic attack, the acute treatment of ischaemic stroke, intracerebral and subarachnoid haemorrhage, and secondary prevention and recovery. UK guidance is quoted from NICE NG128 (Stroke and transient ischaemic attack in over 16s, 2019, with later updates) and the 2023 National Clinical Guideline for Stroke for the UK and Ireland. Management always follows current local protocols.

## Definitions and types

::: definition Stroke and transient ischaemic attack {#def-stroke}
A **stroke** is a sudden focal (or sometimes global) neurological deficit of vascular origin. About 85% of strokes in the UK are **ischaemic**, caused by blockage of an artery; the rest are **haemorrhagic**, either **intracerebral haemorrhage** (bleeding into the brain substance) or **subarachnoid haemorrhage** (bleeding into the space around the brain).

A **transient ischaemic attack (TIA)** is a brief episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal ischaemia of the brain, spinal cord or retina, *without* infarction. The older definition used a time limit (symptoms resolving within 24 hours). The modern definition is based on tissue: if brain imaging shows an infarct, the event is a stroke, however quickly the symptoms resolved. In practice most TIAs last minutes.
:::

Ischaemic strokes are grouped by mechanism, because the mechanism decides prevention:

- **Large artery atherosclerosis**: plaque in the carotid or vertebral arteries or a major intracranial artery, which throws off emboli or blocks the vessel.
- **Cardioembolism**: clot from the heart, most often from the left atrium in **atrial fibrillation**, but also after myocardial infarction or from a prosthetic valve.
- **Small vessel disease**: occlusion of a small penetrating artery deep in the brain, typically in people with hypertension or diabetes, producing a **lacunar infarct**.
- **Other determined causes**: arterial dissection (an important cause in young adults), vasculitis, clotting disorders, and others.
- **Undetermined (cryptogenic)**: no cause found despite investigation.

## Arterial territories and stroke syndromes

The brain receives blood from the two internal carotid arteries (the **anterior circulation**) and the two vertebral arteries, which join to form the basilar artery (the **posterior circulation**). The two systems connect at the base of the brain through the **circle of Willis**, though it is often incomplete. The anatomy is set out in [[anatomy/neuroanatomy]].

::: definition The main stroke syndromes {#def-syndromes}
- **Middle cerebral artery (MCA)**: weakness and sensory loss of the opposite face and arm more than leg, homonymous hemianopia, and either **aphasia** (dominant, usually left, hemisphere) or **neglect** of the opposite side of space (non-dominant hemisphere). A blocked main stem (M1) causes the full syndrome with gaze deviation towards the side of the lesion.
- **Anterior cerebral artery (ACA)**: weakness of the opposite leg more than arm; with larger infarcts, apathy and urinary incontinence.
- **Posterior cerebral artery (PCA)**: homonymous hemianopia, often with macular sparing; memory loss if the medial temporal lobe is involved; and, with a dominant hemisphere lesion, difficulty reading.
- **Lacunar syndromes**: pure motor stroke, pure sensory stroke, sensorimotor stroke, ataxic hemiparesis and dysarthria with a clumsy hand. There are no cortical signs: no aphasia, neglect or field loss.
- **Posterior circulation (vertebrobasilar)**: combinations of vertigo, double vision, dysarthria, swallowing difficulty, ataxia, and **crossed signs** (cranial nerve signs on one side, limb signs on the other). **Lateral medullary syndrome** (from occlusion of the vertebral or posterior inferior cerebellar artery) gives vertigo, ipsilateral Horner syndrome, ipsilateral facial and contralateral body loss of pain sensation, ataxia and dysphagia. **Basilar artery occlusion** can cause coma or the **locked-in syndrome**, in which consciousness is preserved but only vertical eye movements and blinking remain.
:::

Posterior circulation strokes are missed more often than anterior ones, because vertigo and vomiting are attributed to inner ear disease and because CT shows the posterior fossa poorly. The HINTS examination for acute vertigo is described in [[nervous-system/sensory-systems]].

Several conditions **mimic stroke**: hypoglycaemia, seizures with post-ictal (Todd's) weakness, migraine with aura, functional neurological disorder, sepsis in someone with an old stroke, brain tumours and subdural haematoma. Capillary glucose is measured in every suspected stroke because hypoglycaemia is common, quick to find and easy to treat.

## The acute pathway for ischaemic stroke

::: theorem Time is brain {#thm-time}
In acute ischaemic stroke, the region of irreversibly damaged tissue (the **core**) is surrounded by a region of underperfused but still viable tissue (the **penumbra**). Without reperfusion, the core expands into the penumbra over minutes to hours, at a rate that depends on the collateral circulation. The benefit of restoring blood flow therefore falls steadily with time. One widely quoted estimate is that a typical large-vessel ischaemic stroke destroys about 1.9 million neurons for each minute it is untreated.
:::

::: proof Why some people still benefit at 24 hours
The rate at which the core grows varies from person to person. People with good **collateral circulation**, in which neighbouring arteries supply the territory through leptomeningeal connections, can keep a large penumbra for many hours ("slow progressors"). People with poor collaterals lose most of the territory within the first hour or two ("fast progressors"). The clock time since onset is therefore only a proxy for the real question, which is how much salvageable tissue remains. Perfusion CT or MRI estimates the core and the penumbra directly. Trials published in 2018 (DAWN and DEFUSE 3) used this imaging to select patients with a small core and a large mismatch between 6 and 24 hours after they were last known well, and showed large benefits from thrombectomy. The same logic underlies the use of MRI to treat some people who wake up with a stroke of unknown onset time.
:::

### Recognition and first assessment

Public campaigns teach **FAST** (Face, Arm, Speech, Time to call 999). Paramedics use structured tools, and ambulance services take suspected strokes directly to hyperacute stroke units. On arrival the team confirms the deficit, measures glucose, records the time the person was **last known well** (not the time they were found), checks for anticoagulant use and measures the severity on the **National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS)**, a 0–42 point scale that teams use as a common currency. Oxygen is given only if saturation is low. Swallowing is screened before anything is given by mouth.

The first imaging test is a **non-contrast CT** of the head, which excludes haemorrhage. **CT angiography** from the aortic arch to the vertex identifies a large vessel occlusion that might be removed, and is done at the same visit when thrombectomy might be appropriate. **CT perfusion** or MRI is added when the person presents late or with unknown onset.

### Thrombolysis

**Intravenous thrombolysis** uses a recombinant tissue plasminogen activator to dissolve the clot. Alteplase was licensed first; NICE technology appraisal TA990 (July 2024) recommends **tenecteplase** as an alternative to alteplase within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. Tenecteplase is given as a single bolus rather than a bolus followed by an hour's infusion, which simplifies treatment and transfer. Doses are weight-based and come from the current formulary and local protocol.

The core rules are:

- treatment within **4.5 hours** of the time last known well, once haemorrhage is excluded on imaging;
- the earlier, the better: the benefit within 3 hours is roughly twice that between 3 and 4.5 hours;
- in selected people beyond 4.5 hours, or who woke with symptoms, advanced imaging (MRI or perfusion imaging) can identify salvageable tissue, and specialists may treat;
- the main risk is **symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage**, which occurs in a few per cent of treated patients;
- important contraindications include haemorrhage on imaging, active bleeding, recent major surgery or head injury, therapeutic anticoagulation, and blood pressure that cannot be lowered below about 185/110 mmHg before treatment.

### Thrombectomy

**Mechanical thrombectomy** removes the clot with a stent retriever or aspiration catheter passed from the femoral or radial artery under imaging. In 2015, five trials published together showed that it greatly improves outcomes in people with a large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation. A pooled analysis of those trials (HERMES, 2016) found that 46% of treated patients were functionally independent at 90 days compared with 26.5% of controls.

In UK guidance (NICE NG128), thrombectomy is offered for proximal anterior circulation occlusion within 6 hours of onset, and between 6 and 24 hours when imaging shows salvageable tissue, in people with good previous function; it is considered for posterior circulation (basilar) occlusion within 24 hours. Thrombectomy is given together with thrombolysis if the person is eligible for both; it is not delayed to see whether thrombolysis has worked. Trials published since 2022 have extended benefit to some people with large established infarcts and to basilar artery occlusion, and guidance is evolving.

::: example A man found at breakfast {#ex-acute}
A 74-year-old man was last seen well at 06:10, when his wife heard him go to the bathroom. At 07:30 she found him slumped at the kitchen table, unable to speak, with a right facial droop and no movement of his right arm. Paramedics bring him to a hyperacute stroke unit at 08:15. Glucose is 6.4 mmol/L. Blood pressure is 172/94 mmHg. NIHSS is 19. He takes no anticoagulants. CT shows no haemorrhage. CT angiography shows occlusion of the left M1 segment.
::: solution
1. **Syndrome.** Aphasia, right facial and arm weakness, high NIHSS: a left MCA syndrome. The M1 occlusion confirms a large vessel occlusion.
2. **Clock.** The time last known well is 06:10, not 07:30. The 4.5-hour thrombolysis window ends at $06{:}10 + 4{:}30 = 10{:}40$. He is at 2 hours 5 minutes, well within it.
3. **Thrombolysis.** No haemorrhage, glucose normal, blood pressure below 185/110 mmHg, no anticoagulant: eligible. Treatment should start immediately.
4. **Thrombectomy.** Proximal anterior circulation occlusion within 6 hours: he should go straight to thrombectomy as well, not wait to see if thrombolysis works. If this hospital does not provide thrombectomy, transfer to the nearest centre is arranged at once.
5. **Communication.** Aphasia does not mean he lacks capacity, but it makes assessment difficult; treatment proceeds in his best interests if he cannot take part in the decision, with his wife informed. See [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]].

Afterwards he is managed on the stroke unit: blood pressure kept below 180/105 mmHg for 24 hours after thrombolysis, swallow screen, neurological observations, and a CT at 24 hours before antiplatelet therapy starts.
:::
:::

### The first days: the stroke unit

Care on a **stroke unit**, a dedicated ward with a specialist multidisciplinary team, reduces death and dependency for every type of stroke and is the single most widely applicable treatment. Its routine includes swallowing assessment and nutrition, positioning and early mobilisation, prevention of aspiration pneumonia, monitoring of glucose, temperature and oxygen, prevention of venous thromboembolism with intermittent pneumatic compression in immobile patients, continence care, and assessment by physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech and language therapy.

For ischaemic stroke not treated with thrombolysis, **aspirin** is started as soon as haemorrhage has been excluded and continued for two weeks, after which long-term antithrombotic treatment begins. People with a large MCA infarct who are under 60 may develop dangerous swelling (malignant MCA infarction) and are referred early for consideration of **decompressive hemicraniectomy**.

::: warning Do not lower blood pressure aggressively in acute ischaemic stroke
In acute ischaemic stroke, the penumbra depends on perfusion pressure, because the damaged vessels can no longer autoregulate. Lowering blood pressure sharply can extend the infarct. Blood pressure is therefore lowered only when it is very high, when there is another indication (such as aortic dissection or heart failure), or before and after thrombolysis (to below 185/110 mmHg before, and below 180/105 mmHg for the first 24 hours after). Intracerebral haemorrhage is different, as explained below; the first CT decides which rule applies.
:::

## Transient ischaemic attack

A TIA is a warning: the risk of stroke is highest in the first days. Current UK guidance (NICE NG128) is to give **aspirin** immediately unless contraindicated and to arrange specialist assessment within **24 hours** of onset for anyone with a suspected TIA in the last 7 days. Scores such as ABCD2 are no longer recommended for deciding urgency, because they miss important causes such as atrial fibrillation and carotid stenosis.

Assessment includes brain imaging (MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging when needed to determine the territory or to exclude a mimic), carotid imaging, an ECG and, when no cause is obvious, prolonged cardiac monitoring to look for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. In people with a minor stroke or high-risk TIA, a short course of **dual antiplatelet therapy** (aspirin and clopidogrel, typically for 21 days, started within 24 hours) reduces early recurrence, at the cost of a small increase in bleeding; this is based on trials such as CHANCE (2013) and POINT (2018).

::: example A blind spell and a narrow artery {#ex-carotid}
A 67-year-old woman, a smoker, had 10 minutes of painless loss of vision in her left eye yesterday, "like a shade being pulled down", which fully recovered. Today her examination is normal. Carotid ultrasound shows 80% stenosis of the left internal carotid artery.
::: solution
1. **Interpretation.** Transient monocular blindness (**amaurosis fugax**) is a retinal TIA, caused by an embolus to the ophthalmic or central retinal artery, a branch of the internal carotid. A severe ipsilateral stenosis is the likely source. It is a "symptomatic" stenosis.
2. **Risk.** The risk of stroke after a symptomatic carotid stenosis is highest in the first days and weeks.
3. **Treatment.** Antiplatelet therapy and a high-intensity statin start now, and smoking cessation support is offered. In UK guidance, people with a symptomatic stenosis of 50–99% (by NASCET criteria) are assessed for **carotid endarterectomy** within one week and have surgery within two weeks of symptoms, because the benefit of surgery falls quickly with delay. Carotid stenting is an alternative in selected people.
4. **Mimics and extras.** Giant cell arteritis is considered in anyone over 50 with visual loss (ask about headache and jaw claudication, check inflammatory markers). Blood pressure, lipids and diabetes are assessed, and she is advised not to drive until assessed, in line with DVLA rules.
:::
:::

## Intracerebral haemorrhage

**Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH)** accounts for about 10–15% of strokes but a larger share of deaths. Common causes are long-standing **hypertension** (bleeds deep in the basal ganglia, thalamus, pons or cerebellum), **cerebral amyloid angiopathy** (lobar bleeds in older people), **anticoagulant treatment**, and less often vascular malformations, tumours and drugs such as cocaine. It cannot be distinguished from ischaemic stroke without imaging.

::: definition Acute management of intracerebral haemorrhage {#def-ich}
The acute principles, as of 2026, are:

1. **Reverse anticoagulation** urgently. For warfarin, prothrombin complex concentrate and intravenous vitamin K; for dabigatran, idarucizumab; for factor Xa inhibitors, prothrombin complex concentrate or a specific reversal agent (andexanet alfa) where local policy supports it.
2. **Lower blood pressure** in people presenting within 6 hours with a systolic pressure of 150–220 mmHg. NICE NG128 (updated 2022) advises aiming for a systolic pressure of 140 mmHg or lower while ensuring the fall does not exceed 60 mmHg within the first hour of treatment.
3. **Refer to neurosurgery** people with hydrocephalus (often from cerebellar or intraventricular bleeding) or with a large cerebellar haematoma compressing the brainstem; surgery for most supratentorial bleeds has not shown clear benefit.
4. Stroke unit care, as for ischaemic stroke, with **intermittent pneumatic compression** for venous thromboembolism prevention.
5. An early, honest discussion of prognosis and goals with the person and family, without premature limitation of care.
:::

Together these elements form an "ABC" care bundle (anticoagulation reversal, blood pressure lowering, care pathway for neurosurgery), which was associated with reduced mortality when introduced in Salford, UK.

## Subarachnoid haemorrhage

Most spontaneous **subarachnoid haemorrhages (SAH)** come from rupture of a saccular ("berry") **aneurysm** in or near the circle of Willis. The classic presentation is a **thunderclap headache**: a severe headache reaching maximum intensity within about a minute, often described as the worst ever, sometimes with vomiting, neck stiffness, a brief loss of consciousness or seizures. Some people have a smaller "sentinel" bleed days before.

UK guidance (NICE NG228, 2022) recommends **CT of the head** as soon as possible. CT performed within 6 hours of headache onset is highly sensitive; if it is normal and was done within 6 hours, other diagnoses should be considered. If CT is done after 6 hours and is normal, **lumbar puncture** is considered, at least 12 hours after onset, looking for **xanthochromia** (yellow pigment from haemoglobin breakdown). CT angiography then looks for the aneurysm.

Treatment aims to prevent rebleeding by securing the aneurysm, usually by **endovascular coiling** (preferred when feasible since the ISAT trial, 2002) or by surgical **clipping**. **Nimodipine** reduces the risk of poor outcome from **delayed cerebral ischaemia**, which typically occurs between days 4 and 14. Other complications are hydrocephalus, seizures and hyponatraemia. See [[nervous-system/headache-pain]] for the wider approach to sudden headache.

::: example Headache at the gym {#ex-sah}
A 46-year-old woman develops a sudden, explosive occipital headache while lifting weights. It reached its peak within seconds. She vomits twice. On arrival 3 hours later she has a GCS of 15, neck stiffness and no focal signs. CT head shows blood in the basal cisterns.
::: solution
1. **Diagnosis.** Thunderclap headache with blood in the basal cisterns: subarachnoid haemorrhage, most likely aneurysmal.
2. **Immediate steps.** Neurosurgical referral; CT angiography to find the aneurysm; analgesia and antiemetics; stop any antithrombotic drugs; start nimodipine; monitor neurological status, sodium and fluid balance.
3. **Definitive treatment.** Securing the aneurysm as early as possible, usually by coiling, to prevent a rebleed, which carries a high mortality.
4. **Watch for complications.** Deterioration may be due to rebleeding, hydrocephalus (needing ventricular drainage) or delayed cerebral ischaemia between days 4 and 14. Hyponatraemia is common.
5. **Longer term.** Many survivors have fatigue, memory and mood problems even after a "good" recovery, and follow-up should include these, along with driving advice and smoking and blood pressure management.
:::
:::

## Secondary prevention and recovery

::: definition Secondary prevention after ischaemic stroke or TIA {#def-prevention}
The aim is to reduce the risk of a further event by treating the mechanism and the risk factors:

- **Antithrombotic therapy.** Clopidogrel is the usual long-term antiplatelet after ischaemic stroke in the UK. For **atrial fibrillation**, oral anticoagulation (usually a direct oral anticoagulant) replaces antiplatelets. Trials published in 2023–2024 (ELAN, TIMING and OPTIMAS) support starting anticoagulation earlier than was once usual, guided by the size of the infarct.
- **Blood pressure.** The 2023 National Clinical Guideline for Stroke recommends a target systolic pressure below 130 mmHg for most people, except those with severe bilateral carotid stenosis.
- **Lipids.** A high-intensity statin, with further lipid-lowering therapy if needed to reach the target.
- **Carotid revascularisation** for symptomatic stenosis, as in [[#ex-carotid]].
- **Lifestyle.** Stopping smoking, regular physical activity, a healthy diet, moderate alcohol and weight management; diabetes control.
:::

Recovery is fastest in the first weeks and months, driven by brain plasticity and rehabilitation. Rehabilitation is goal-directed and multidisciplinary: physiotherapy for movement and balance, occupational therapy for daily activities, speech and language therapy for communication and swallowing, and psychological support for the depression, anxiety, fatigue and cognitive problems that are common after stroke. Driving rules (from the DVLA in the UK) apply after stroke and TIA. Long-term outcome is often recorded on the **modified Rankin Scale**, from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (dead).

::: example Atrial fibrillation after a stroke {#ex-af}
A 78-year-old woman with hypertension and type 2 diabetes recovers well from a small left MCA infarct. Monitoring shows paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. Her estimated glomerular filtration rate is 52 mL/min/1.73 m². What is her CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, and what is the principle of her antithrombotic management?
::: solution
CHA₂DS₂-VASc counts: congestive heart failure 1, hypertension 1, age ≥75 years 2, diabetes 1, stroke or TIA or thromboembolism 2, vascular disease 1, age 65–74 years 1, female sex 1.

Her score: hypertension 1 + age ≥75 years 2 + diabetes 1 + stroke 2 + female 1 = **7**.

Her risk of further stroke is high, and anticoagulation is indicated (UK guidance, NICE NG196). A direct oral anticoagulant is usually chosen, with the dose adjusted for kidney function and age according to the product information and current formulary. Antiplatelet therapy is not continued alongside it unless there is a separate indication. Bleeding risk is assessed (for example with the ORBIT score) to identify modifiable risks such as uncontrolled blood pressure, not to withhold anticoagulation.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 68-year-old man arrives 2 hours after the onset of left-sided weakness. NIHSS is 14. CT shows no haemorrhage. He takes warfarin for atrial fibrillation and his INR is 2.6. CT angiography shows right M1 occlusion. Which is the most appropriate next step?
- [ ] Intravenous thrombolysis, because he is within 4.5 hours
- [ ] Reverse warfarin with prothrombin complex concentrate, then give thrombolysis
- [x] Proceed to mechanical thrombectomy without thrombolysis
- [ ] Start aspirin and admit to the stroke unit
::: solution
Therapeutic anticoagulation (an INR above 1.7 in most protocols) is a contraindication to thrombolysis, and reversing warfarin in order to give thrombolysis is not recommended. Anticoagulation does not prevent thrombectomy, and he has a proximal anterior circulation occlusion within 6 hours, so thrombectomy is the treatment. Aspirin alone would deny him an effective therapy.
:::
:::

::: history
In the 1950s C. Miller Fisher, working in Boston, described the transient ischaemic attack, the role of carotid artery disease and the lacunar syndromes, turning stroke from a vague "apoplexy" into a set of mechanisms. The North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET) and the European Carotid Surgery Trial established the benefit of surgery for severe symptomatic stenosis in 1991. In 1995 the NINDS trial showed that intravenous alteplase within 3 hours improved outcome, and ECASS III extended the window to 4.5 hours in 2008. The International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial (2002) established coiling. In 2015 five randomised trials (MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME and REVASCAT) showed the benefit of thrombectomy, and DAWN and DEFUSE 3 extended it to 24 hours in 2018.
:::

::: summary
- About 85% of strokes are ischaemic; the rest are intracerebral or subarachnoid haemorrhage. Only imaging can tell ischaemic stroke from ICH.
- TIA is defined by the absence of infarction, not by duration. Give aspirin immediately and arrange specialist assessment within 24 hours.
- Territories: MCA (face and arm, aphasia or neglect), ACA (leg), PCA (field loss), lacunar (pure motor or sensory, no cortical signs), posterior circulation (vertigo, diplopia, ataxia, crossed signs).
- Time is brain: the core grows into the penumbra. Thrombolysis (alteplase or tenecteplase) is given within 4.5 hours of last known well; thrombectomy for proximal anterior occlusion within 6 hours, and up to 24 hours with imaging selection.
- Stroke unit care reduces death and dependency for all stroke types.
- In ICH: reverse anticoagulation, lower systolic pressure to 140 mmHg or lower without a fall of more than 60 mmHg in the first hour, and refer hydrocephalus and large cerebellar bleeds to neurosurgery.
- Thunderclap headache needs CT; CT within 6 hours is highly sensitive; otherwise lumbar puncture at least 12 hours after onset. Secure the aneurysm and give nimodipine.
- Secondary prevention is mechanism-based: antiplatelet or anticoagulant, blood pressure below 130 mmHg systolic for most, high-intensity statin, carotid surgery when indicated, and lifestyle change.
:::

::: exercise The thrombolysis deadline {level=1}
A woman was last seen well at 21:40. She is found with a dense right hemiparesis at 23:10. By what clock time must thrombolysis start to be within 4.5 hours?
::: solution
The clock starts at the time last known well, 21:40, not the time she was found. $21{:}40 + 4{:}30 = 02{:}10$ the next morning. In practice the aim is to treat as soon as possible, with a door-to-needle time well under an hour.

Name the artery most likely blocked: (a) weakness of the right leg with little arm weakness; (b) left homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing and no weakness; (c) right face and arm weakness with expressive aphasia; (d) pure motor weakness of the left face, arm and leg.
::: solution
(a) Left anterior cerebral artery. (b) Right posterior cerebral artery. (c) Left middle cerebral artery (superior division). (d) A small penetrating artery supplying the right internal capsule (lacunar infarct).

Heart failure 1 + hypertension 1 + age 65–74 years 1 + diabetes 1 + TIA 2 = 6. (Male sex scores 0.)

::: exercise Number needed to treat for thrombectomy {level=2 check="5.1"}
In the HERMES pooled analysis, 46.0% of patients treated with thrombectomy and 26.5% of controls were functionally independent (modified Rankin 0–2) at 90 days. What is the number needed to treat for one additional person to be independent? Give the answer to one decimal place.
::: solution
Absolute risk difference $= 0.460 - 0.265 = 0.195$. NNT $= 1/0.195 = 5.13$, which is 5.1 to one decimal place. About one in every five people treated becomes independent who would not otherwise have done so, one of the largest treatment effects in medicine.
:::

::: exercise Blood pressure after a bleed {level=2}
A 63-year-old man presents 2 hours after the onset of left-sided weakness. CT shows a right putaminal haemorrhage. His blood pressure is 210/115 mmHg. He takes no anticoagulants. Describe the blood pressure target and explain why it differs from that in ischaemic stroke.
::: solution
He presents within 6 hours with a systolic pressure between 150 and 220 mmHg, so rapid lowering is advised. The NICE target is a systolic pressure of 140 mmHg or lower, without a fall of more than 60 mmHg in the first hour; a fall from 210 to about 150 mmHg in the first hour would therefore be the limit, with further lowering afterwards, and the target maintained for at least 7 days. In haemorrhage, high pressure is associated with expansion of the haematoma, which worsens outcome, while the surrounding tissue is not dependent on a penumbra in the way an ischaemic stroke is. In ischaemic stroke, by contrast, lowering pressure can reduce perfusion of the penumbra and extend the infarct, which is why the rule is reversed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Is it a TIA? {level=2}
A 58-year-old man had 30 minutes of numbness and weakness of his left hand yesterday, now completely resolved. MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging shows a small acute infarct in the right precentral gyrus. Is this a TIA? How does the answer affect management?
::: solution
No. By the tissue-based definition, an acute infarct on imaging makes this a minor ischaemic stroke, however brief the symptoms. Management is very similar in the first days: urgent investigation of the mechanism (carotid imaging, ECG and cardiac monitoring, blood tests), antiplatelet therapy (dual antiplatelet therapy for a limited period may be appropriate for minor stroke if started early), statin and risk factor control. The distinction matters for driving rules and for recording the diagnosis, and it reminds clinicians that symptom duration is a poor guide to tissue damage.
:::
:::

::: exercise The stroke that was not {level=3}
A 34-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes is brought in with sudden right-sided weakness and confusion. Her husband says she had skipped lunch after her insulin. Her NIHSS is 9. List the first three things to do, and discuss which conditions in the differential diagnosis would change management.
::: solution
First, check capillary glucose immediately: hypoglycaemia can cause focal weakness and is treated in minutes. Second, assess airway, breathing and circulation and record the time last known well. Third, if glucose is normal (or the deficit persists after correction), proceed urgently with the stroke pathway: CT and CT angiography, and a thrombolysis decision. The differential diagnosis includes hypoglycaemia (correct glucose and reassess before any thrombolysis decision), a seizure with Todd's paresis (witnessed jerking, tongue biting, an EEG later), migraine with aura (history of similar attacks, spreading positive symptoms), and stroke from a cause typical of young adults, such as arterial dissection (neck pain, recent trauma) or cardioembolism (including paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale). Stroke in young adults also prompts consideration of drug use, vasculitis and prothrombotic states.
:::
:::

::: exercise Planning a stroke service {level=3}
A rural region has one hospital that can provide thrombolysis and a thrombectomy centre 90 minutes away by road. Using the ideas in [[#thm-time]], discuss the trade-offs between taking patients with suspected large vessel occlusion directly to the thrombectomy centre ("mothership") and taking them to the nearest hospital for thrombolysis first ("drip and ship").
::: solution
Thrombolysis is time-critical and works best early; taking everyone to the distant centre would delay it for people without a large vessel occlusion, who are the majority, and for whom thrombolysis is the only reperfusion treatment. Thrombectomy is more effective than thrombolysis for large vessel occlusion, and its benefit also falls with time, so the extra delay of stopping at a local hospital and then transferring (often an hour or more, including the time to arrange transport) may cost tissue. The answer depends on how well paramedics can identify large vessel occlusion before arrival (prehospital scales have limited accuracy), on transport times, on door-in-door-out times at the local hospital, and on the availability of imaging and telemedicine. Many regions use the nearest centre for thrombolysis with fast imaging and immediate transfer for confirmed occlusions, and audit door-in-door-out times. Equity matters too: rural patients and those who live far from centres should not have systematically worse access.
