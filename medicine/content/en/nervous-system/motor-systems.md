Weakness is one of the commonest reasons people see a neurologist, and one of the most useful symptoms to examine, because the motor system is built in layers that fail in different ways. Damage to the motor cortex, the internal capsule, the spinal cord, a nerve root, a peripheral nerve, the neuromuscular junction or the muscle itself can all make a limb weak. But each produces weakness of a different *distribution*, with different tone, different reflexes and a different course over time. Reading these clues is the core skill of the neurological examination.

This chapter describes the organisation of the motor system from cortex to muscle, the distinction between upper and lower motor neuron lesions, the roles of the basal ganglia and cerebellum, the motor cranial nerves, and how the examination is put together to localise a lesion.

## The final common path

::: definition Motor neuron and motor unit {#def-motor-unit}
A **lower motor neuron** is a neuron whose cell body lies in the anterior horn of the spinal cord (or in a motor cranial nerve nucleus in the brainstem) and whose axon runs in a peripheral or cranial nerve to skeletal muscle. A **motor unit** is one lower motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres it supplies. Motor units range from a few fibres (in the extraocular muscles, giving fine control) to more than a thousand (in the large leg muscles, giving power). All the fibres of a unit contract together whenever the neuron fires.
:::

Charles Sherrington called the lower motor neuron the **final common path**: every influence on movement, whether from the cortex, the brainstem, the cerebellum or a spinal reflex, has to act through it. Force is graded in two ways. More motor units can be **recruited**, and those already active can fire faster (**rate coding**). Recruitment follows the **size principle** described by Elwood Henneman: small motor units, with small and easily excited neurons, are recruited first, and large units are added only as more force is needed. This gives fine control at low forces, where an extra unit adds only a little force.

The upper motor neurons control these lower neurons from above. The most important pathway is the **corticospinal tract**. Its fibres arise from the primary motor cortex in the precentral gyrus and neighbouring frontal and parietal areas. They descend through the **posterior limb of the internal capsule**, the cerebral peduncles, the base of the pons and the medullary pyramids. In the lower medulla about 85–90% of the fibres cross (the **pyramidal decussation**) and descend in the lateral corticospinal tract of the opposite side of the cord; the rest descend uncrossed in the anterior corticospinal tract. The motor cortex is arranged as a map of the body, the **motor homunculus**: the leg on the medial surface of the hemisphere, then the trunk, arm and hand, and the face lowest on the lateral surface.

Parallel **corticobulbar** fibres supply the motor cranial nerve nuclei. Most of these nuclei receive input from both hemispheres, which is why a one-sided cortical lesion rarely causes lasting difficulty with swallowing or weakness of the upper face.

## Upper and lower motor neuron signs

::: definition Upper and lower motor neuron lesions {#def-umn-lmn}
An **upper motor neuron (UMN) lesion** damages the descending pathways above the anterior horn cell. It causes weakness in a **pyramidal pattern** (in the arm the extensors are weaker than the flexors; in the leg the flexors are weaker than the extensors), increased tone of the **spastic** type, brisk tendon reflexes, sometimes clonus, and an **extensor plantar response** (the Babinski sign). Wasting is absent or mild and late.

A **lower motor neuron (LMN) lesion** damages the anterior horn cell, root or peripheral nerve. It causes weakness in the distribution of the damaged structure, **wasting**, reduced tone, reduced or absent reflexes, and often **fasciculations** (visible twitches of individual motor units). The plantar response is flexor or absent.
:::

| Sign | Upper motor neuron | Lower motor neuron | Neuromuscular junction | Muscle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distribution | Pyramidal; often one side or both legs | Root, nerve or anterior horn territory | Fatigable; eyes, bulbar, proximal | Usually proximal and symmetrical |
| Wasting | Little | Marked | None | Late, in proportion to weakness |
| Tone | Increased (spastic) | Reduced | Normal | Normal or reduced |
| Reflexes | Brisk | Reduced or absent | Normal | Normal, lost late |
| Plantar | Extensor | Flexor | Flexor | Flexor |
| Fasciculations | No | Yes (especially anterior horn) | No | No |
| Sensory loss | Depends on site | Present with root or nerve lesions | No | No |

::: proof Why upper motor neuron lesions cause spasticity
After an upper motor neuron lesion, the stretch reflex circuits in the spinal cord lose their descending control. The corticospinal tract runs alongside reticulospinal and other brainstem pathways that normally inhibit stretch reflex activity, and these are usually damaged together. Over days to weeks the spinal circuits reorganise: inhibition of stretch reflexes falls and motor neurons become more excitable. The result is **spasticity**, a velocity-dependent increase in resistance to passive stretch: the faster the examiner moves the limb, the greater the resistance, sometimes with a sudden give ("clasp-knife"). This takes time to develop, so an acute UMN lesion, for example in the first hours after a stroke or after spinal cord injury (spinal shock), can show reduced tone and absent reflexes at first.
:::

::: warning Early UMN lesions can look like LMN lesions
In the first hours or days after a large stroke or an acute spinal cord injury, the affected limbs may be flaccid with absent reflexes. A flaccid paralysis of both legs with a sensory level and urinary retention is a spinal cord lesion, not a neuropathy, whatever the reflexes show. The plantar response may be extensor even when the tendon reflexes are absent, and the sensory level and bladder are the more reliable clues. Mistaking acute cord compression for Guillain–Barré syndrome delays the MRI that could save the legs.
:::

### Grading power

Power is recorded with the **Medical Research Council (MRC) scale**: 0, no contraction; 1, a flicker of contraction; 2, movement with gravity eliminated; 3, movement against gravity but not resistance; 4, movement against some resistance; 5, normal power. Grade 4 covers a wide range and is often subdivided into 4−, 4 and 4+. Recording each muscle group separately makes the pattern visible and allows change to be measured.

The **tendon reflexes** test a single segment of the cord and its root. Each reflex is attached to a root: biceps C5–C6, supinator (brachioradialis) C5–C6, triceps C7, knee L3–L4, ankle S1. A reflex that is absent at one level while those above and below are normal or brisk points to a lesion at that level. **Clonus** is a sustained rhythmic contraction after a brisk stretch, usually tested at the ankle; more than a few beats is abnormal.

## Localising weakness level by level

The aim is to answer one question: where is the lesion? Each level has a recognisable pattern.

**Cortex.** Weakness of the opposite face and arm (middle cerebral artery territory) or leg (anterior cerebral artery territory), often with cortical signs: aphasia, neglect, visual field loss, or cortical sensory loss (inability to recognise objects by touch). Seizures can occur.

**Internal capsule.** The fibres for face, arm and leg are packed closely together, so a small lacunar infarct can cause equal weakness of the whole opposite side, face, arm and leg, without cortical signs: a **pure motor stroke**.

**Brainstem.** **Crossed signs**: a cranial nerve palsy on the side of the lesion with weakness of the opposite limbs. A lesion in the left ventral pons, for example, can cause left facial (VII) or abducens (VI) palsy with right hemiparesis.

**Spinal cord.** Weakness of both legs (paraparesis) or all four limbs (tetraparesis) with UMN signs below the lesion, a sensory level, and bladder and bowel involvement. LMN signs may appear at the level of the lesion, where anterior horn cells or roots are damaged.

**Root (radiculopathy).** Pain radiating in a dermatome, weakness of the muscles of that **myotome**, and loss of the corresponding reflex. The common examples are C6 and C7 in the neck and L5 and S1 in the back.

**Plexus.** A patchy mixture of root and nerve patterns in one limb, often painful (for example neuralgic amyotrophy of the brachial plexus).

**Peripheral nerve.** Weakness and sensory loss confined to the territory of one nerve (mononeuropathy), or distal and symmetrical in polyneuropathy.

**Neuromuscular junction.** Fatigable weakness, typically of the eyelids, eye movements, face, swallowing and proximal limbs, with normal reflexes and sensation. Myasthenia gravis is the main example.

**Muscle.** Symmetrical proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, lifting the arms above the head) without sensory loss, with reflexes preserved until late.

::: theorem Localise first, then name the disease {#thm-localise}
The neurological examination is used to answer the question "where?" before the question "what?". Once the level is known, the **time course** narrows the cause. Seconds to minutes suggests a vascular event or a seizure; hours to days suggests inflammation, infection or a compressive lesion that has suddenly decompensated; weeks to months suggests a tumour or a slowly progressive inflammatory disease; months to years suggests degeneration or a genetic disorder. Combining the site with the time course (and the person's age and background) usually leaves a short differential diagnosis.
:::

::: example The man who could not climb the stairs {#ex-cord}
A 68-year-old man with treated prostate cancer has had mid-back pain for six weeks, worse at night and on lying flat. Over three days his legs have become weak and stiff, and this morning he could not pass urine. Power is MRC grade 3 in hip flexion and ankle dorsiflexion on both sides, tone is increased, knee and ankle reflexes are brisk, and both plantars are extensor. Pinprick is reduced from just below the nipples downwards. Arms are normal.
::: solution
1. **Level.** Both legs are weak with UMN signs, the arms are spared, there is a sensory level and urinary retention: a thoracic spinal cord lesion.
2. **Height.** The sensory level is just below T4. Because the spinothalamic level can lie several segments below the true lesion, the lesion is at or above about T4–T6.
3. **Time course and background.** Weeks of night pain, then days of progressive weakness, in a man with prostate cancer: **metastatic spinal cord compression** until proved otherwise.
4. **Action.** Urgent MRI of the whole spine (not just the thoracic segment, because more than one level is often involved). Corticosteroids are commonly started to reduce cord oedema, and definitive treatment (surgical decompression or radiotherapy) is decided by the spinal and oncology teams according to local pathways (NICE NG234, 2023). Bladder catheterisation, thromboprophylaxis, pain control and pressure area care follow. The ability to walk at the start of treatment is the strongest predictor of walking afterwards, which is why delays are so costly.
:::
:::

## The basal ganglia and the cerebellum

Two large systems shape movement without projecting directly to the spinal cord. Damage to them causes disordered movement rather than weakness.

The **basal ganglia** (the caudate and putamen, which together form the striatum, the globus pallidus, the subthalamic nucleus and the substantia nigra) form loops with the cortex through the thalamus. Put simply, the **direct pathway** facilitates chosen movements and the **indirect pathway** suppresses competing ones; dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta facilitates the direct pathway and inhibits the indirect one. Loss of dopamine, as in Parkinson's disease, tips the balance towards suppression: movements become small and slow (**bradykinesia**), muscles become stiff with **rigidity** (increased tone that is the same throughout the range and does not depend on speed, unlike spasticity), and a rest tremor may appear. Damage to the indirect pathway, as in Huntington's disease, has the opposite effect, with excessive involuntary movements (**chorea**). These disorders are the subject of [[nervous-system/movement-disorders]].

The **cerebellum** compares intended movement with actual movement and corrects errors. It receives copies of motor commands from the cortex and sensory feedback from the spinal cord and vestibular system, and projects back to the motor cortex (through the thalamus) and the brainstem. A key rule: **a lesion of one cerebellar hemisphere affects the same side of the body**, because its outputs cross once and the corticospinal tract crosses again. Cerebellar signs include:

- **ataxia**: clumsy, poorly coordinated movement; a broad-based, unsteady gait;
- **dysmetria**: overshooting or undershooting a target, shown in the finger–nose and heel–shin tests;
- **intention tremor**: tremor that increases as the finger approaches the target;
- **dysdiadochokinesia**: difficulty with rapid alternating movements;
- **nystagmus** and **dysarthria** (slurred, "scanning" speech).

Lesions of the midline cerebellum (the vermis), typically from alcohol, mainly affect the trunk and gait, while hemisphere lesions affect the limbs on the same side.

## The motor cranial nerves

Several cranial nerves carry motor fibres, and their weakness has characteristic patterns.

**Eye movements.** The **oculomotor nerve (III)** supplies most of the extraocular muscles, the levator of the upper lid and the pupillary sphincter; a complete palsy leaves the eye "down and out" with ptosis and a dilated pupil. The **trochlear nerve (IV)** supplies the superior oblique, which depresses the eye when it is turned inwards; a palsy causes vertical double vision, worse when looking down (for example, going downstairs), and a compensatory head tilt away from the affected side. The **abducens nerve (VI)** supplies the lateral rectus; a palsy causes horizontal double vision worse on looking to the affected side. Sixth nerve palsy can be a "false localising sign" of raised intracranial pressure, because the nerve has a long intracranial course.

**The face.** The **facial nerve (VII)** supplies the muscles of facial expression. The pattern of facial weakness separates UMN from LMN lesions:

::: definition Upper and lower motor neuron facial weakness {#def-facial}
In an **upper motor neuron facial palsy** (for example after a stroke), weakness affects the lower face on the opposite side, and the forehead is relatively spared, because the part of the facial nucleus serving the upper face receives input from both hemispheres. In a **lower motor neuron facial palsy** (a lesion of the facial nucleus or nerve, for example Bell's palsy), the whole of one side of the face is weak, forehead included: the eyebrow cannot be raised, the eye does not close fully, and the eye rolls upwards on attempted closure (Bell's phenomenon).
:::

**Bell's palsy** is an acute, idiopathic LMN facial palsy that develops over hours to a couple of days. It is diagnosed only after other causes have been excluded: ear or parotid disease, herpes zoster (Ramsay Hunt syndrome, with vesicles in the ear or palate), Lyme disease, stroke (forehead spared) and bilateral facial palsy (which suggests sarcoidosis, Guillain–Barré syndrome or Lyme disease). UK guidance (NICE Clinical Knowledge Summaries) recommends oral corticosteroids for adults seen within 72 hours of onset, according to the current formulary, and protection of the eye, which may not close, with lubricants and taping at night. Most people recover fully within months.

**Bulbar muscles.** The glossopharyngeal (IX), vagus (X) and hypoglossal (XII) nerves control the palate, pharynx, larynx and tongue. Two patterns of weakness exist. In **bulbar palsy** (an LMN pattern, for example in motor neuron disease or myasthenia), the tongue is wasted and fasciculating, speech is nasal and the jaw jerk is normal. In **pseudobulbar palsy** (bilateral UMN lesions, for example multiple strokes or motor neuron disease), the tongue is small and stiff, speech is slow and strained, the jaw jerk is brisk, and there may be emotional lability. Both cause dangerous swallowing problems and aspiration.

::: example Crooked smile at the barber's {#ex-facial}
A 41-year-old man of South Asian heritage notices at the barber's that his mouth droops on the right. By evening he cannot close his right eye. There is no weakness elsewhere. He cannot wrinkle his right forehead. The ear canal and drum are normal, there are no vesicles, and the parotid gland is not enlarged. He has no rash and has not been walking in woodland.
::: solution
1. **UMN or LMN?** The forehead is involved and the eye will not close: a right **lower motor neuron** facial palsy. A stroke would usually spare the forehead and would rarely occur without other signs.
2. **Look for a cause.** Normal ear and drum and no vesicles make Ramsay Hunt syndrome less likely; a normal parotid argues against a tumour; no tick exposure makes Lyme disease less likely; one side only. No other cranial nerves are involved and no limb weakness.
3. **Diagnosis.** Bell's palsy, a diagnosis of exclusion.
4. **Management principles.** Oral corticosteroids, because he presents within 72 hours (according to the current formulary and local guidance); eye care to prevent corneal exposure, with lubricating drops by day and ointment and taping at night; review. He should be referred urgently if the eye becomes red or painful, if there is no improvement in three weeks, or if other signs appear. He can be told that most people make a full or near-full recovery.
:::
:::

## The examination as a tool

The motor examination follows a fixed order so that nothing is missed: **inspection** (posture, wasting, fasciculations, abnormal movements), **tone**, **power**, **reflexes**, and **coordination**, followed by **gait**. Two screening tests are particularly useful. In **pronator drift**, the person holds both arms straight out, palms up, eyes closed; a subtle UMN weakness makes one arm pronate and drift down. Watching someone walk often gives the diagnosis before any other test.

| Gait | What it looks like | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|
| Hemiparetic | Arm flexed, leg extended and swung round in a semicircle (circumduction) | Stroke |
| Spastic paraparetic ("scissoring") | Stiff legs, knees crossing, toes scraping | Cord lesion, cerebral palsy |
| Parkinsonian | Stooped, short shuffling steps, reduced arm swing, turning in many steps | Parkinson's disease |
| Cerebellar | Broad-based, unsteady, lurching | Cerebellar disease, alcohol |
| Sensory ataxic | Stamping, watching the feet, worse in the dark | Dorsal column or large-fibre neuropathy |
| Steppage (high-stepping) | Foot lifted high to clear a drop foot | Common fibular nerve palsy, L5 root, neuropathy |
| Waddling | Trunk sways side to side | Proximal muscle weakness |

The level of consciousness is part of the motor examination too. The **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** scores eye opening (1–4), verbal response (1–5) and the best motor response (1–6), for a total of 3 to 15. The motor score is the most informative component for prognosis. The full scale and its use in trauma are covered in [[emergency-critical-care/acute-assessment]].

::: example Working out a GCS {#ex-gcs}
A 23-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department after a fall from a horse. She opens her eyes only to pain, makes incomprehensible sounds, and when pressure is applied to the trapezius she reaches up towards the stimulus with her right hand. Her left arm extends at the elbow. What is her GCS, and what does the asymmetry suggest?
::: solution
1. **Eyes**: opening to pain = 2.
2. **Verbal**: incomprehensible sounds = 2.
3. **Motor**: the *best* response is scored. Reaching towards the stimulus is **localising** = 5. (The left arm's extension would score 2, but the best response counts.)

GCS = $2 + 2 + 5 = 9$, recorded as E2 V2 M5. A GCS of 8 or less usually indicates the need for airway protection, so she is close to that threshold and needs senior assessment of her airway now. The difference between the two sides (localising on the right, extension on the left) is a lateralising sign, suggesting a right hemisphere lesion such as an extradural or subdural haematoma. She needs urgent CT of the head and cervical spine and neurosurgical input.
:::
:::

::: example A slapping foot {#ex-footdrop}
A 52-year-old woman of Nigerian heritage, who lost a lot of weight after bariatric surgery and often sits with her legs crossed, has noticed her right foot slapping on the ground for two weeks. Ankle dorsiflexion and eversion are MRC grade 2 on the right; ankle inversion and plantar flexion are normal. The ankle reflex is present. Sensation is reduced over the dorsum of the foot and the lower lateral leg. There is no back pain.
::: solution
1. **Pattern.** Weak dorsiflexion (tibialis anterior) and eversion (peroneal muscles), with *normal inversion* (tibialis posterior).
2. **Nerve or root?** Tibialis anterior and the peroneal muscles are supplied by the **common fibular (peroneal) nerve**. Tibialis posterior is supplied by the tibial nerve, but it is also an L5 muscle. In an L5 radiculopathy inversion is weak too; here it is normal, so the lesion is in the common fibular nerve, not the L5 root.
3. **Reflex.** The ankle jerk (S1, tibial nerve) is preserved, consistent with a fibular nerve lesion.
4. **Cause.** Compression at the fibular head, where the nerve winds superficially around the bone. Rapid weight loss removes the protective fat pad, and leg crossing compresses the nerve.

Management: avoid leg crossing, ankle–foot orthosis for safe walking, and review. Nerve conduction studies can confirm the site and severity if recovery does not follow. Most compressive palsies recover over weeks to months. The [[nervous-system/neuromuscular]] chapter covers the nerve conduction findings.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which combination of findings points to a lesion of the left internal capsule rather than the left motor cortex?
- [ ] Right arm weakness with expressive aphasia
- [x] Equal weakness of the right face, arm and leg with no aphasia, neglect or visual field loss
- [ ] Left facial weakness with right arm and leg weakness
- [ ] Weakness of both legs with a sensory level
::: solution
In the internal capsule the motor fibres for face, arm and leg are packed together, so a small lesion gives equal weakness of all three without cortical signs (a pure motor stroke, typical of a lacunar infarct). A cortical lesion usually affects face and arm more than leg (or the reverse) and often comes with aphasia, neglect or field loss. Crossed signs indicate the brainstem, and a sensory level with both legs weak indicates the spinal cord.

::: history
In 1870 Gustav Fritsch and Eduard Hitzig showed that electrical stimulation of a dog's frontal cortex produced movements of the opposite limbs, and John Hughlings Jackson, studying the spread of focal seizures (the "Jacksonian march"), argued that the cortex contained an ordered map of movements. Joseph Babinski described the extensor plantar response as a sign of pyramidal tract disease in 1896. Charles Sherrington, whose work on reflexes and the motor neuron was summarised in *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System* (1906), introduced the term "final common path" and shared the Nobel Prize in 1932. Wilder Penfield and Edwin Boldrey mapped the human motor and sensory cortex during epilepsy surgery in 1937, giving the homunculus. In 1957 Elwood Henneman described the orderly recruitment of motor units by size, and in 1974 Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett published the Glasgow Coma Scale.
:::

## Where this leads

The disorders of the basal ganglia are covered in [[nervous-system/movement-disorders]]; stroke syndromes that combine motor and sensory loss in [[nervous-system/stroke]]; motor neuron disease, which damages both upper and lower motor neurons, in [[nervous-system/dementia-neurodegeneration]]; and diseases of the nerve, junction and muscle in [[nervous-system/neuromuscular]]. The sensory pathways that run beside the motor tracts are in [[nervous-system/sensory-systems]].

::: summary
- The lower motor neuron is the final common path; force is graded by recruitment (small units first, by the size principle) and by firing rate.
- The corticospinal tract runs through the internal capsule and brainstem and crosses in the lower medulla.
- Upper motor neuron lesions: pyramidal weakness, spasticity, brisk reflexes, extensor plantars, little wasting. Lower motor neuron lesions: wasting, fasciculation, reduced tone and reflexes.
- In the first hours after a stroke or cord injury, UMN lesions can be flaccid and areflexic; rely on the sensory level, the bladder and the plantar response.
- Localise level by level (cortex, capsule, brainstem, cord, root, plexus, nerve, junction, muscle), then use the time course to name the disease.
- The basal ganglia scale and select movements (loss of dopamine causes bradykinesia and rigidity); the cerebellum corrects them (lesions cause ipsilateral ataxia, dysmetria and intention tremor).
- A UMN facial palsy spares the forehead; an LMN palsy (Bell's palsy) does not. Bulbar palsy is LMN; pseudobulbar palsy is bilateral UMN.
- Gait and pronator drift are fast, sensitive screening tests; the GCS records conscious level, and its motor score is the best single predictor.
:::

::: exercise Nerve conduction arithmetic {level=2 check="55.6"}
In a nerve conduction study the median nerve is stimulated at the elbow and at the wrist. The response from abductor pollicis brevis appears 8.0 ms after elbow stimulation and 3.5 ms after wrist stimulation. The distance between the two stimulation points is 250 mm. What is the forearm conduction velocity in m/s, to one decimal place?
::: solution
Subtracting the latencies removes the time spent at the neuromuscular junction and in the muscle: $8.0 - 3.5 = 4.5$ ms. Velocity $= 250 \text{ mm} / 4.5 \text{ ms} = 55.6$ mm/ms $= 55.6$ m/s. This is within the normal range for the forearm median nerve (roughly above 50 m/s); marked slowing would suggest demyelination.
:::
:::

::: exercise Crossed signs {level=2}
A 66-year-old man suddenly develops double vision on looking to the left, weakness of the whole left side of the face including the forehead, and weakness of the right arm and leg with an extensor right plantar. Where is the lesion? Explain each sign.
::: solution
The lesion is in the left side of the caudal pons. The left abducens (VI) nucleus or fascicle is involved, giving weakness of left lateral rectus and double vision on left gaze. The left facial (VII) nucleus or fascicle, which loops around the abducens nucleus, is involved, giving an LMN facial palsy with forehead weakness. The corticospinal fibres in the left basis pontis have not yet crossed (they cross in the medulla), so the right arm and leg are weak with UMN signs. Ipsilateral cranial nerve palsy with contralateral hemiparesis is the hallmark of a brainstem lesion (here the pattern of Millard–Gubler syndrome); the most likely cause at his age is a small-vessel or basilar branch infarct.
:::
:::

::: exercise Proximal weakness {level=2}
A 58-year-old woman has had difficulty rising from low chairs and lifting her arms to wash her hair for three months. There is no sensory loss, reflexes are normal, there are no fasciculations and her eyelids and eye movements are normal. Where is the lesion likely to be, and what features of history and examination would help you identify the cause?
::: solution
Symmetrical proximal weakness without sensory loss, with preserved reflexes, suggests a myopathy. Helpful features: drugs (statins, glucocorticoids, alcohol); rash on the eyelids or knuckles (dermatomyositis); muscle pain; dark urine; dysphagia; breathlessness (interstitial lung disease with some inflammatory myopathies); symptoms of thyroid or adrenal disease; weight loss (cancer can be associated with dermatomyositis); family history; and whether the weakness fluctuates or fatigues (myasthenia can present with proximal weakness, even without eye signs). Initial tests include creatine kinase, thyroid function, inflammatory markers and electrolytes; myositis antibodies, electromyography, muscle MRI and biopsy follow as needed. See [[nervous-system/neuromuscular]].
:::
:::

::: exercise The acutely floppy legs {level=3}
A 35-year-old woman develops back pain, then over 18 hours flaccid weakness of both legs with absent knee and ankle reflexes. She has a band of tingling around the lower chest and has not passed urine for 10 hours. A colleague suggests Guillain–Barré syndrome. Discuss the differential diagnosis and say what single finding most strongly argues against that suggestion, and what you would do next.
::: solution
The urinary retention and the band-like sensory disturbance at a truncal level are the key findings: they point to the spinal cord. Guillain–Barré syndrome can cause autonomic disturbance but rarely a clear sensory level with early, persistent urinary retention. Absent reflexes are explained by spinal shock in an acute cord lesion. The differential diagnosis is acute cord compression (epidural abscess, haematoma, disc, tumour), transverse myelitis (idiopathic, multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, MOG antibody disease, infection), and spinal cord infarction (sudden onset, often with back pain, sparing of dorsal column sensation). The next step is urgent MRI of the whole spine; if compression is found, urgent neurosurgical referral. If not, MRI of the brain, CSF examination, and antibody testing (aquaporin-4, MOG) are done and acute treatment of myelitis begins. A bladder scan and catheter are needed now.
:::

::: exercise Weakness and time {level=3}
For each scenario, name the most likely level and the most likely cause, using [[#thm-localise]]: (a) a 72-year-old man with sudden right arm and face weakness and difficulty finding words, resolving in 20 minutes; (b) a 25-year-old woman with three days of ascending tingling and weakness in both legs, absent reflexes and no sensory level, two weeks after diarrhoea; (c) a 60-year-old man with a year of progressive weakness and wasting of the hands, fasciculations in the arms and legs, and brisk reflexes; (d) a 30-year-old woman with double vision and drooping eyelids that are worse in the evening.

(a) Left hemisphere cortex (face and arm weakness with aphasia); sudden and brief: transient ischaemic attack, which needs same-day specialist assessment. (b) Peripheral nerves, symmetrically and with roots: Guillain–Barré syndrome after *Campylobacter* infection; she needs monitoring of vital capacity and swallowing. (c) Upper and lower motor neurons together, without sensory loss, progressive over a year: motor neuron disease. A cervical myelopathy with root compression can mimic this and should be excluded. (d) Neuromuscular junction, fatigable: myasthenia gravis.
