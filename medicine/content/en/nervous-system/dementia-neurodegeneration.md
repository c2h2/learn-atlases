Neurodegenerative diseases are disorders in which particular groups of neurons die progressively, usually over years. Most are associated with the accumulation of a misfolded protein: amyloid-β and tau in Alzheimer's disease, α-synuclein in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, TDP-43 in most motor neuron disease and much frontotemporal dementia, and prion protein in Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease. Which neurons are affected decides the clinical picture: memory in Alzheimer's disease, behaviour and language in frontotemporal dementia, motor neurons in motor neuron disease, the cerebellum in the ataxias.

This chapter covers dementia (its assessment, the main types, reversible causes and treatment), motor neuron disease, the cerebellar ataxias and prion disease. UK management of dementia follows NICE NG97 (Dementia, 2018) and of motor neuron disease NICE NG42 (2016, updated 2019). The care of people living with dementia, including delirium, is developed further in [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]].

## Dementia

::: definition Dementia and mild cognitive impairment {#def-dementia}
**Dementia** (major neurocognitive disorder) is an acquired decline in one or more cognitive domains (memory, executive function, language, visuospatial function, attention, social cognition) that is severe enough to interfere with independence in everyday activities, and is not explained by delirium or another mental disorder. It is a syndrome with many causes, not a disease in itself.

**Mild cognitive impairment** is a decline in cognition greater than expected for age and education, shown on testing, while everyday independence is preserved. A proportion of people with mild cognitive impairment, especially those with evidence of Alzheimer pathology, progress to dementia each year; others remain stable or improve.
:::

Nearly a million people in the UK are estimated to live with dementia, and the number rises as the population ages. Age is the strongest risk factor. The 2024 Lancet Commission on dementia identified 14 potentially modifiable risk factors across the life course, including less education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, hypertension, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, diabetes, excessive alcohol, depression, social isolation, air pollution, traumatic brain injury and untreated vision loss, and estimated that nearly half of dementia might in principle be prevented or delayed by addressing them. Hearing loss and high LDL cholesterol carried the largest population-attributable fractions.

### Assessing a person with memory problems

The assessment has four aims: to confirm whether there is cognitive impairment; to describe its pattern; to find any reversible or contributing cause; and to understand what matters to the person and their family.

- **History**, from the person and from someone who knows them well: which abilities have changed, the order and speed of change, effects on daily life, mood, sleep, hallucinations, falls, driving, alcohol and medicines.
- **Cognitive testing** with a structured instrument such as the Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination (ACE-III, 100 points), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, 30 points) or, in primary care, shorter tools such as the 6-item Cognitive Impairment Test. Scores must be interpreted against age, education, language and culture; tests designed in one language and culture can underestimate the ability of people from others, and interpreters and culturally adapted tools should be used.
- **Physical and neurological examination**: parkinsonism, gait, focal signs, eye movements, primitive reflexes, signs of vascular disease.
- **Blood tests** to exclude reversible contributors: full blood count, urea and electrolytes, calcium, glucose or HbA1c, liver function, thyroid function, vitamin B12 and folate; syphilis and HIV serology when indicated.
- **Structural brain imaging** (MRI, or CT if MRI is unsuitable) to exclude other causes and to show patterns of atrophy and vascular disease.

Reversible or treatable contributors include depression, hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, alcohol, medicines with anticholinergic or sedative effects, obstructive sleep apnoea, **normal pressure hydrocephalus** (a triad of gait disturbance, urinary incontinence and cognitive slowing, with enlarged ventricles), subdural haematoma and brain tumours. Truly "reversible dementia" is uncommon, but contributing factors are common and worth treating.

::: warning Delirium is not dementia
**Delirium** is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, usually caused by an illness, drug or metabolic disturbance. People with dementia are at much higher risk of delirium, and the two often coexist. A sudden change in someone's thinking over hours to days is delirium until proved otherwise and needs a search for the cause (infection, drugs, retention, constipation, pain, hypoxia, electrolytes). A diagnosis of dementia should not be made during an episode of delirium; cognition is reassessed after recovery.
:::

### The main types

| Type | Typical early features | Pathology | Imaging |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alzheimer's disease | Gradual loss of recent memory (episodic memory), then language, visuospatial and executive problems; insight often reduced | Amyloid-β plaques, tau neurofibrillary tangles | Medial temporal (hippocampal) and parietal atrophy |
| Vascular dementia | Slowed thinking, executive dysfunction, gait problems, mood change; may be stepwise after strokes or gradual with small vessel disease | Infarcts, small vessel disease | Infarcts, extensive white matter change |
| Dementia with Lewy bodies | Fluctuating attention, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, parkinsonism; memory relatively spared early | α-synuclein (Lewy bodies) | Relative preservation of medial temporal lobes; reduced dopamine transporter uptake on DaTscan |
| Behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia | Disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive behaviours, dietary change; usually under 65 | Tau or TDP-43 | Frontal and anterior temporal atrophy |
| Primary progressive aphasias | Progressive language difficulty: effortful non-fluent speech, loss of word meaning (semantic variant), or word-finding pauses (logopenic, usually Alzheimer pathology) | Various | Left frontal, anterior temporal or temporoparietal atrophy |

Mixed pathology, especially Alzheimer's disease with vascular disease, is the rule rather than the exception in older people.

::: proof How amyloid and tau are thought to cause Alzheimer's disease
Amyloid precursor protein is a membrane protein normally cut by secretase enzymes. Cutting by β-secretase and then γ-secretase releases **amyloid-β** peptides, especially amyloid-β 42, which aggregate into soluble oligomers and then insoluble plaques. The **amyloid cascade hypothesis** proposes that amyloid accumulation is the initiating event: it begins 15–20 years before symptoms and, together with microglial inflammation, promotes the spread of abnormally phosphorylated **tau** inside neurons, forming neurofibrillary tangles. Tau pathology spreads in a predictable sequence from the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus to association cortex (Braak stages), and the burden of tau, rather than of amyloid, correlates best with neuronal loss and symptoms. Support for the hypothesis comes from genetics (mutations in *APP*, *PSEN1* and *PSEN2* cause early-onset familial Alzheimer's disease; people with Down syndrome, who have three copies of *APP*, almost all develop Alzheimer pathology), and from the modest clinical benefit of antibodies that clear amyloid. Its limits are shown by the many people with abundant amyloid and no dementia. The **APOE ε4** allele is the main genetic risk factor for late-onset disease: one copy raises risk several-fold, two copies more so.
:::

### Biomarkers

Alzheimer's disease can now be detected in life by measuring its pathology. **Amyloid PET** and **tau PET** show the proteins in the brain; **CSF** shows a low amyloid-β 42 (or a low amyloid-β 42/40 ratio) and raised phosphorylated tau; and **blood tests** for phosphorylated tau (especially p-tau217) have shown high accuracy in research and specialist settings. These tests answer the question "is Alzheimer pathology present?", not "is it causing these symptoms?", and their predictive value depends on who is tested: amyloid is present in a substantial minority of cognitively normal older people.

### Treatment and care

**Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) increase acetylcholine at cholinergic synapses, compensating partly for the loss of cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain. NICE NG97 recommends them for mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease, and for dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson's disease dementia (where the benefit is often larger). **Memantine**, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is recommended for moderate Alzheimer's disease when acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are not tolerated, and for severe disease, alone or in combination. The benefits are modest and symptomatic: on average a small improvement or slower decline in cognition and function. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors can cause nausea, diarrhoea, vivid dreams, bradycardia and syncope, so a pulse (and ECG when indicated) is checked. They are not recommended for frontotemporal dementia or for vascular dementia without Alzheimer's disease.

**Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies** (lecanemab and donanemab) remove amyloid plaques and, in trials of people with early symptomatic Alzheimer's disease and confirmed amyloid pathology, slowed decline on clinical scales by about a quarter to a third over 18 months. They require intravenous infusions and MRI monitoring because of **amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA)**, brain oedema or microhaemorrhages that are usually asymptomatic but occasionally serious, and more frequent in people with two copies of APOE ε4. In the UK, the MHRA licensed lecanemab and donanemab in 2024 (excluding APOE ε4 homozygotes), but NICE did not recommend them for NHS use in its 2025 guidance, on grounds of cost-effectiveness; after appeals, the appraisal was returned to NICE for reconsideration in 2026, and readers should check the current position.

**Non-drug care** is central: diagnosis given honestly and with time; information and a named care coordinator; cognitive stimulation therapy for mild to moderate dementia; support for carers; advance care planning, including lasting power of attorney while the person has capacity (see [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]]); assessment for driving; and attention to hearing, vision, pain, mood and sleep.

**Behavioural and psychological symptoms** (agitation, aggression, wandering, psychosis) are approached first by looking for causes (pain, infection, constipation, environment, unmet needs) and with person-centred non-drug approaches. Antipsychotics increase the risk of stroke and death in people with dementia and are used only when there is severe distress or risk of harm, at the lowest dose, for the shortest time and with regular review. In dementia with Lewy bodies, antipsychotics can cause severe, sometimes fatal, reactions.

::: example A retired nurse who keeps repeating herself {#ex-ad}
A 76-year-old retired nurse is brought by her daughter, who says that over two years her mother has begun repeating questions, forgetting recent conversations and appointments, and recently left the gas on twice. She manages her personal care but has stopped doing her own finances. She says her memory is "fine for my age". She takes amlodipine and oxybutynin for urinary urgency. ACE-III is 72/100, with marked deficits in delayed recall and verbal fluency. Examination is normal. Blood tests are normal. MRI shows bilateral hippocampal atrophy and mild small vessel disease.
::: solution
1. **Is there dementia?** Progressive decline over two years, abnormal cognitive testing, and loss of independence in complex activities (finances, kitchen safety): yes.
2. **Pattern.** Early, prominent loss of recent episodic memory with reduced insight, and hippocampal atrophy: typical **Alzheimer's disease**, with a minor vascular contribution.
3. **Contributing factors.** Oxybutynin is a strongly anticholinergic drug that can worsen cognition; it should be reviewed and, if possible, replaced by an alternative such as mirabegron. Hearing and vision are checked.
4. **Further tests?** In a typical presentation like this, the diagnosis can be made clinically. Biomarker tests (CSF or PET) are used when the diagnosis is uncertain, or when they would change management, for example eligibility for anti-amyloid treatment where available.
5. **Management principles.** Explain the diagnosis to her (with her consent and her daughter present if she wishes); offer an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor after checking her pulse; refer to post-diagnostic support; discuss lasting power of attorney while she can make that decision; review safety at home (gas safety devices); advise about driving and informing the DVLA; and plan regular review.
:::
:::

::: example Visual hallucinations and falls {#ex-dlb}
A 71-year-old man of Indian heritage has had a year of "good days and bad days", with spells of staring and confusion lasting hours. He sees small children in the garden who are not there, and is not frightened by them. His wife says that for years he has shouted and punched in his sleep. Examination shows mild bilateral rigidity and slowness. ACE-III shows prominent attention and visuospatial deficits with relatively preserved recall. After an episode of agitation in hospital he was given haloperidol and became rigid, feverish and unable to move.
::: solution
1. **Pattern.** Fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder and parkinsonism are the four core clinical features of **dementia with Lewy bodies** (fourth consensus report, 2017). Two or more core features make the diagnosis probable.
2. **Supporting tests.** A DaTscan showing reduced striatal dopamine transporter uptake, or a polysomnogram confirming REM sleep without atonia, would strengthen it.
3. **The haloperidol reaction.** Severe **antipsychotic sensitivity** is characteristic of DLB and can be life-threatening. It should be recorded prominently as an adverse reaction, and haloperidol and similar drugs avoided.
4. **Management principles.** An acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (often helps hallucinations and fluctuations); cautious levodopa for disabling parkinsonism, which may worsen hallucinations; management of postural hypotension, constipation and sleep; safety measures for REM sleep behaviour disorder; and support for his wife. If an antipsychotic ever became unavoidable, specialist advice is needed, and low-dose quetiapine or clozapine would be considered.
:::
:::

## Motor neuron disease

::: definition Motor neuron disease {#def-mnd}
**Motor neuron disease (MND)**, also called **amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)**, is a progressive neurodegenerative disease of upper and lower motor neurons. It causes a combination of **upper motor neuron signs** (spasticity, brisk reflexes, extensor plantars) and **lower motor neuron signs** (wasting, weakness, fasciculations) in the bulbar, cervical, thoracic and lumbosacral regions, with spread over time, and without sensory loss. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by electromyography, and requires exclusion of mimics; the Gold Coast criteria (2020) require progressive motor impairment with upper and lower motor neuron findings in at least one body region, or lower motor neuron findings in at least two regions.
:::

The lifetime risk is about 1 in 300. Onset is usually between 50 and 70 years. About two-thirds present with limb weakness (often asymmetric, for example a weak hand or a foot drop), and about a quarter with **bulbar** onset (slurred speech, swallowing difficulty), more often in older women. Sensation, bladder function and eye movements are typically spared. Up to half of people develop cognitive or behavioural change, and about 10–15% meet criteria for frontotemporal dementia; MND and FTD form a spectrum, linked by **TDP-43** pathology in almost all cases of MND. About 10% have a family history; the commonest genetic cause is a hexanucleotide repeat expansion in **C9orf72**, followed by mutations in **SOD1**. Median survival from symptom onset is about two to three years, usually ending in respiratory failure, but around 10% live more than ten years.

Mimics to exclude include cervical myelopathy with radiculopathy, multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block (treatable with immunoglobulin), myasthenia gravis, inclusion body myositis, Kennedy disease, and thyroid and parathyroid disorders.

Management is by a specialist multidisciplinary team (NICE NG42):

- **Riluzole**, a glutamate release inhibitor, prolongs survival modestly (by a few months on average). **Tofersen**, an antisense oligonucleotide that reduces SOD1 protein, was approved in the USA in 2023 and in the EU in 2024 for people with *SOD1* mutations.
- **Non-invasive ventilation** for respiratory insufficiency improves survival and quality of life. Respiratory function (forced vital capacity, sniff nasal inspiratory pressure, overnight oximetry, symptoms such as orthopnoea and morning headache) is monitored regularly.
- **Gastrostomy** to maintain nutrition and hydration, ideally placed before respiratory function falls too far.
- Speech and language therapy and **communication aids**, including voice banking early in the disease.
- Treatment of saliva, cramps, spasticity, pain, low mood and emotional lability; equipment and adaptations.
- Early, honest **advance care planning**, including decisions about ventilation and end-of-life care (see [[geriatrics-palliative/palliative-care]]).

::: example A weak hand and twitching muscles {#ex-mnd}
A 58-year-old builder has had 8 months of weakness in his right hand, now struggling with screws and buttons. He has noticed muscle twitches in both arms and thighs and has lost 6 kg. On examination there is wasting of the right first dorsal interosseous and thenar muscles, fasciculations in both arms, both thighs and the tongue, brisk reflexes in all four limbs and an extensor right plantar. Sensation is normal. His forced vital capacity is 2.1 L, against a predicted value of 4.2 L.
::: solution
1. **Pattern.** Lower motor neuron signs (wasting, fasciculations, including the tongue) together with upper motor neuron signs (brisk reflexes, extensor plantar) in several regions (bulbar, cervical, lumbosacral), with no sensory loss and progression over months: **motor neuron disease**.
2. **Mimics.** Cervical spondylotic myelopathy with root compression can cause wasted hands and brisk legs, but would not cause tongue fasciculations or widespread fasciculations in the legs; MRI of the cervical spine is still done. Nerve conduction studies exclude multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block; EMG shows widespread denervation and reinnervation. Thyroid function, calcium, and creatine kinase are checked.
3. **Respiratory function.** FVC is $2.1/4.2 = 0.50$, that is 50% of predicted, which indicates significant respiratory muscle weakness. He needs prompt respiratory assessment (including overnight oximetry and symptoms of nocturnal hypoventilation) and discussion of non-invasive ventilation.
4. **Next steps.** Diagnosis by a neurologist; referral to a specialist MND multidisciplinary team; riluzole; genetic testing where appropriate (offered to people with familial disease and increasingly to all, because results may affect treatment); early discussion of gastrostomy, communication aids, and advance care planning; and support for him and his family, including work and finances.
:::
:::

## The cerebellar ataxias

**Ataxia** is incoordination of movement. Cerebellar ataxia causes a broad-based unsteady gait, limb dysmetria and intention tremor, dysarthria and nystagmus (see [[nervous-system/motor-systems]]). Its causes are sorted by time course and family history.

- **Acute**: stroke or haemorrhage in the posterior fossa, drug toxicity (alcohol, phenytoin, lithium), Wernicke encephalopathy (with confusion and eye movement disorder, from thiamine deficiency), post-infectious cerebellitis in children, and Miller Fisher syndrome.
- **Subacute**: tumours, paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration (for example with anti-Yo antibodies in breast or ovarian cancer), immune-mediated ataxias (gluten ataxia, anti-GAD), and prion disease.
- **Chronic acquired**: alcohol-related cerebellar degeneration (mainly the vermis, giving gait ataxia), hypothyroidism, vitamin E deficiency, and the cerebellar form of multiple system atrophy.
- **Inherited**: **Friedreich ataxia** (autosomal recessive, GAA repeat expansion in *FXN*, onset in childhood or adolescence, with sensory neuropathy, absent reflexes, extensor plantars, scoliosis, cardiomyopathy and diabetes) and the autosomal dominant **spinocerebellar ataxias**, many of which are CAG repeat expansions like Huntington's disease.

::: example Unsteady after a binge {#ex-wernicke}
A 49-year-old man who drinks heavily and has eaten little for weeks is brought in confused and unable to walk without support. He has horizontal nystagmus, weakness of lateral gaze in both eyes, and a broad-based, lurching gait. His capillary glucose is 3.4 mmol/L.
::: solution
1. **Recognise.** Confusion, ophthalmoplegia (or nystagmus) and ataxia in a malnourished person who drinks heavily: **Wernicke encephalopathy**, caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency. Only a minority show all three features, so the diagnosis is made on suspicion.
2. **Treat at once.** High-dose **parenteral thiamine** (for example intravenous Pabrinex in the UK, according to the formulary) given before or together with glucose, because glucose metabolism consumes thiamine and can precipitate or worsen the encephalopathy. Correct hypoglycaemia, magnesium and other electrolytes.
3. **Look for other causes.** Head injury (CT head, since falls are common), hepatic encephalopathy, alcohol withdrawal, infection, and a posterior fossa stroke if the ataxia is asymmetric.
4. **Prognosis.** Eye signs often improve within days; ataxia and confusion more slowly. Untreated, it can progress to coma or to **Korsakoff syndrome**, a lasting amnesia with confabulation. Long-term support for alcohol use is part of the plan (see [[psychiatry/substance-use]]).
:::
:::

## Prion disease

::: definition Prion disease {#def-prion}
**Prion diseases** (transmissible spongiform encephalopathies) are fatal neurodegenerative disorders caused by a misfolded form of the host's own **prion protein** (PrP). The misfolded form acts as a template that converts the normal protein into more misfolded protein, a self-propagating process that produces neuronal loss and spongiform change. Prion disease can be **sporadic** (the commonest), **genetic** (mutations in the *PRNP* gene) or **acquired** (from contaminated tissue or instruments, or, in variant CJD, from bovine spongiform encephalopathy).
:::

**Sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD)** is the commonest form, affecting about 1–2 people per million per year, typically aged 55–75. It causes a **rapidly progressive dementia** over weeks to months, with myoclonus, ataxia, visual disturbance and akinetic mutism; most people die within a year. Investigations show cortical and basal ganglia restricted diffusion on MRI, periodic sharp wave complexes on EEG in some, and a positive **real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC)** test in CSF, which is highly specific. **Variant CJD**, linked to bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle, affected younger people, mainly in the UK, and began with psychiatric and sensory symptoms. There is no treatment. Suspected cases in the UK are referred to the National CJD Research and Surveillance Unit, and special infection control precautions apply to surgical instruments.

::: quiz
A 63-year-old man has become disinhibited over 18 months, making inappropriate remarks to strangers, spending money impulsively and eating large amounts of sweet food. He shows no concern about these changes. His memory for recent events is relatively good. Which diagnosis is most likely?
- [ ] Alzheimer's disease
- [x] Behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia
- [ ] Dementia with Lewy bodies
- [ ] Vascular dementia
::: solution
Early disinhibition, impulsivity, change in eating behaviour (often a craving for sweet foods) and loss of insight, with relatively spared memory, in a person in their early sixties, are characteristic of behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia. MRI typically shows frontal and anterior temporal atrophy. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are not helpful and may worsen behaviour. A family history should be taken, because a substantial minority of FTD is genetic (*C9orf72*, *MAPT*, *GRN*).
:::
:::

::: history
Alois Alzheimer described the plaques and tangles in the brain of Auguste Deter, who had died at 55 with progressive memory loss and paranoia, in 1906; Emil Kraepelin named the disease after him in 1910. Arnold Pick described focal frontal and temporal atrophy in 1892. Jean-Martin Charcot described amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in 1869. Nikolaus Friedreich described the hereditary ataxia that bears his name in 1863. Hans Gerhard Creutzfeldt (1920) and Alfons Jakob (1921) described the disease later named after them, and in 1982 Stanley Prusiner proposed that an infectious protein, the "prion", caused it, work recognised by a Nobel Prize in 1997. George Glenner and Caine Wong isolated amyloid-β in 1984, and in 1993 Allen Roses and colleagues identified APOE ε4 as the major genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's disease. In 2006 TDP-43 was identified as the main protein in the inclusions of MND and much frontotemporal dementia.
:::

## Where this leads

The care of people with dementia and delirium, including in hospital, is in [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]]; capacity and advance decisions in [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]]; Parkinson's disease and the movement disorders in [[nervous-system/movement-disorders]]; vascular contributions to cognitive decline in [[nervous-system/stroke]]; and the motor examination behind the diagnosis of MND in [[nervous-system/motor-systems]].

::: summary
- Dementia is an acquired cognitive decline that impairs everyday independence; mild cognitive impairment preserves independence. Delirium must be excluded first.
- Assessment combines a history from an informant, cognitive testing interpreted for language and culture, examination, blood tests for contributing causes, and brain imaging.
- Alzheimer's disease (episodic memory loss, hippocampal atrophy, amyloid and tau), vascular dementia (slowed thinking, executive and gait problems), dementia with Lewy bodies (fluctuation, visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, parkinsonism, antipsychotic sensitivity) and frontotemporal dementia (behaviour or language, younger onset) are the main types; mixed pathology is common.
- Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and memantine give modest symptomatic benefit. Anti-amyloid antibodies slow decline modestly, carry a risk of ARIA, and their NHS availability depends on NICE's current appraisal.
- About 14 modifiable risk factors, including hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, hypertension, smoking and inactivity, account for a large share of dementia risk.
- MND combines upper and lower motor neuron signs without sensory loss; riluzole, non-invasive ventilation, gastrostomy and multidisciplinary care improve survival and quality of life; tofersen targets *SOD1* MND.
- Cerebellar ataxia is classified by time course and inheritance; Friedreich ataxia is recessive (GAA repeat).
- Sporadic CJD causes rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus; CSF RT-QuIC is highly specific.
:::

::: exercise Biomarkers in primary care {level=2 check="0.5"}
A blood test for Alzheimer pathology has a sensitivity and specificity of 90%. It is used in a group of older people in primary care in which 10% have Alzheimer pathology. What is the positive predictive value? Give the exact decimal.
::: solution
Per 1,000 people: 100 have the pathology, of whom 90 test positive. Of the 900 without it, 90 test positive (10% false positives). PPV $= 90/(90 + 90) = 0.5$. Half of the positive results are false. In a memory clinic where half have the pathology, the same test would have a PPV of 0.9. This is why such tests belong in specialist assessment of people with symptoms, not in screening.
:::
:::

::: exercise Agitation in a care home {level=2}
An 84-year-old woman with moderate Alzheimer's disease in a care home has become agitated and hits out during personal care over the past week. Staff ask for "something to calm her down". How would you approach this?
::: solution
First, look for causes: a change over a week suggests delirium or an unmet need. Check for pain (for example arthritis, a fracture after an unwitnessed fall, dental pain), infection, constipation, urinary retention, new medicines, hearing aids or glasses not being used, and changes in staff or environment. Use a structured approach to describe the behaviour and its triggers (for example an ABC chart). Non-drug measures: explaining care before giving it, a consistent carer, adjusting the timing of personal care, music and meaningful activity. Treat any cause found, including pain (a trial of regular paracetamol can reduce agitation). Antipsychotics are used only if there is severe distress or risk of harm despite these measures, at the lowest dose, for a short period, with review within weeks, and after discussing the increased risks of stroke and death with her family or attorney.
:::
:::

::: exercise MND or a mimic? {level=2}
A 47-year-old man has a slowly progressive weakness of his left hand and right foot over three years, with wasting and some cramps, but no upper motor neuron signs and no bulbar symptoms. Nerve conduction studies show conduction block in the left ulnar and right fibular motor nerves, with normal sensory nerves. What is the likely diagnosis, and why does it matter?
::: solution
**Multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block**, an immune-mediated motor neuropathy (often with anti-GM1 antibodies) that mimics the lower motor neuron form of MND. Clues are slow progression, a pure lower motor neuron picture in the distribution of individual nerves (often starting in the arm), and conduction block on nerve conduction studies. It matters because it responds to intravenous immunoglobulin, whereas MND does not, and because the prognosis is entirely different.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rapid decline {level=3}
A 64-year-old woman has gone from independence to needing help with dressing in 10 weeks. She is confused, her vision is distorted, and she has jerky movements of her limbs and an unsteady gait. Discuss the differential diagnosis of a rapidly progressive dementia, the investigations, and the practical issues if prion disease is suspected.
::: solution
Rapidly progressive dementia (over weeks to months) has a broad differential, much of it treatable: autoimmune encephalitis (including anti-LGI1 and others), Hashimoto encephalopathy (steroid-responsive encephalopathy with thyroid antibodies), infections (HSV, HIV, syphilis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy), vasculitis, metabolic and toxic causes (hyponatraemia, Wernicke encephalopathy, lithium or bismuth toxicity), malignancy (primary CNS lymphoma, metastases, paraneoplastic syndromes), non-convulsive status epilepticus, and rapidly progressing neurodegenerative diseases including **sporadic CJD**, which her myoclonus, visual symptoms and ataxia suggest. Investigations: MRI including diffusion-weighted imaging (cortical ribboning and basal ganglia restriction in CJD), EEG (periodic sharp wave complexes; excludes non-convulsive status), CSF (cell count, protein, glucose, oligoclonal bands, neuronal antibodies, infection PCR, RT-QuIC, 14-3-3 protein), blood tests (sodium, calcium, thyroid function and antibodies, B12, HIV, syphilis, autoimmune screen, paraneoplastic antibodies) and a search for cancer. Practical issues if CJD is suspected: notify the National CJD Research and Surveillance Unit; follow infection control guidance for neurosurgical and other invasive procedures and instruments; sensitive communication with the family about prognosis; palliative care planning; and support. Treatable causes should not be dismissed because CJD seems likely.
:::
:::

::: exercise Dementia risk reduction {level=3}
A 52-year-old man asks what he can do to reduce his risk of dementia, as his mother has Alzheimer's disease. He smokes, drinks 30 units of alcohol a week, has untreated hypertension (152/96 mmHg), an LDL cholesterol of 4.6 mmol/L, a BMI of 31 kg/m², has noticed difficulty hearing in noisy places, and works long hours with little exercise. Which modifiable risks would you address, and what would you tell him about his family history?
::: solution
Address the factors identified in the 2024 Lancet Commission: stop smoking (with support); reduce alcohol to within 14 units a week; treat hypertension (aiming for a systolic pressure of 130 mmHg or lower in midlife, according to the Commission and national guidance); treat LDL cholesterol (lipid-lowering therapy according to his cardiovascular risk); increase physical activity; weight management; assess his hearing and provide hearing aids if needed (hearing loss is one of the largest modifiable risks); stay socially and cognitively active. These also reduce stroke and heart disease. About family history: having a parent with late-onset Alzheimer's disease raises risk modestly, partly through shared genes such as APOE ε4; genetic testing for APOE is not recommended for predictive purposes outside research, because it does not determine whether he will develop dementia and does not change the advice. If his mother's disease began unusually early (for example before 60) or several relatives are affected, referral to clinical genetics should be considered for possible familial Alzheimer's disease.
