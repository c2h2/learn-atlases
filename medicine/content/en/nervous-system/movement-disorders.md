Movement disorders are conditions in which movement is too little, too much, or of the wrong kind, without weakness. A person with Parkinson's disease has normal strength but cannot get movement started or keep it going at normal size and speed; a person with chorea has movements they did not choose flowing across the face and limbs. Most of these disorders come from the basal ganglia, a set of deep grey matter nuclei that select and scale movements. Many of them can be recognised by watching carefully, and several are treatable.

This chapter describes the basal ganglia circuit, Parkinson's disease and its treatment, the atypical and secondary forms of parkinsonism, and the hyperkinetic disorders: tremor, dystonia, chorea, tics and drug-induced movement disorders. UK management of Parkinson's disease follows NICE NG71 (Parkinson's disease in adults, 2017); the diagnosis follows the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical criteria (2015).

## The basal ganglia circuit

The **basal ganglia** comprise the **striatum** (caudate and putamen), the **globus pallidus** (external and internal segments), the **subthalamic nucleus** and the **substantia nigra** (pars compacta and pars reticulata). They receive input from almost the whole cortex and send their output, through the thalamus, back to the frontal cortex. The output nuclei, the internal globus pallidus and the substantia nigra pars reticulata, are inhibitory (they release GABA) and are tonically active: at rest they hold the thalamus, and therefore movement, in check.

::: theorem The direct and indirect pathways {#thm-pathways}
In the simplified classical model, the striatum influences the output nuclei through two routes. The **direct pathway** (striatum to internal pallidum) *inhibits* the output nuclei, releasing the thalamus and facilitating the chosen movement. The **indirect pathway** (striatum to external pallidum to subthalamic nucleus to internal pallidum) *increases* output-nucleus activity, suppressing competing or unwanted movements. **Dopamine** from the substantia nigra pars compacta excites direct-pathway neurons (through D1 receptors) and inhibits indirect-pathway neurons (through D2 receptors); both effects favour movement. Loss of dopamine therefore increases inhibition of the thalamus and produces **hypokinesia** (parkinsonism); loss of indirect-pathway function, as in Huntington's disease, reduces inhibition and produces **hyperkinesia** (chorea).
:::

The model is a simplification: the real circuits run in parallel loops (motor, oculomotor, cognitive and limbic), and the activity of their neurons is patterned in time as well as in rate. But it predicts the main features of disease and explains why deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus or internal pallidum helps in Parkinson's disease.

## Parkinson's disease

Parkinson's disease is the second commonest neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer's disease. Its prevalence rises steeply with age, and it is somewhat more common in men. It is caused by progressive loss of the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta; by the time motor symptoms appear, a large proportion of these neurons and an even larger proportion of striatal dopamine have already been lost. The surviving neurons contain **Lewy bodies**, inclusions of aggregated **α-synuclein**. α-Synuclein pathology is not confined to the substantia nigra: it is found in the olfactory system, the lower brainstem, the gut nervous system and, later, the cortex, which explains the many non-motor features.

::: definition Parkinsonism and Parkinson's disease {#def-parkinsonism}
**Parkinsonism** is defined (MDS, 2015) as **bradykinesia** in combination with **rest tremor**, **rigidity**, or both.

- **Bradykinesia** is slowness of movement *with a progressive reduction in speed or amplitude* as the movement is repeated (the "decrement" seen when tapping finger and thumb).
- **Rest tremor** is a 4–6 Hz tremor in a limb at rest, often "pill-rolling", that lessens with movement and reappears when a posture is held.
- **Rigidity** is increased resistance to passive movement throughout the range, independent of speed (unlike spasticity). A superimposed tremor gives "cogwheeling".

**Parkinson's disease** is diagnosed when parkinsonism is present and supportive features (a clear and dramatic response to dopaminergic treatment, levodopa-induced dyskinesia, rest tremor, a positive ancillary test such as loss of smell) outweigh the red flags and there are no absolute exclusion criteria (such as cerebellar signs, a vertical supranuclear gaze palsy, or treatment with a dopamine-blocking drug at the time of onset).
:::

The motor features are typically **asymmetric** at onset. Others include a soft voice (hypophonia), small handwriting (micrographia), reduced facial expression (hypomimia), reduced blinking, reduced arm swing, a stooped, shuffling gait with **freezing** and **festination**, and, later, impaired balance and falls.

The **non-motor features** are often more disabling than the motor ones. Some precede the motor signs by years: **REM sleep behaviour disorder** (acting out dreams), loss of smell, constipation, depression and anxiety. Later features include autonomic failure (postural hypotension, urinary urgency, erectile dysfunction), pain, fatigue, apathy, hallucinations and psychosis, and dementia, which develops in a large proportion of people after many years of disease.

::: example A quieter voice and a stiff arm {#ex-pd}
A 64-year-old retired electrician of Polish heritage notices that his right hand shakes when it rests on his lap while watching television. His wife says his voice has become quieter and that he no longer swings his right arm when walking. He has had constipation for years and has lost his sense of smell. For a decade he has punched and kicked in his sleep. On examination there is a 5 Hz rest tremor of the right hand, rigidity of the right wrist with cogwheeling, and progressive slowing and reduction in amplitude of finger tapping on the right. Eye movements, reflexes and plantars are normal. He takes no regular medicines.
::: solution
1. **Is there parkinsonism?** Yes: bradykinesia with decrement, plus rest tremor and rigidity.
2. **Supportive features.** Asymmetric onset, classic rest tremor, loss of smell, and a long history of probable REM sleep behaviour disorder and constipation, which are recognised prodromal features of Lewy body disease.
3. **Red flags and exclusions.** None: normal eye movements (no supranuclear gaze palsy), no cerebellar signs, no early falls or autonomic failure, and no dopamine-blocking drugs.
4. **Diagnosis.** Clinically probable Parkinson's disease. The diagnosis is clinical; NICE NG71 advises referral, before starting treatment, to a specialist (a neurologist or geriatrician with expertise in movement disorders). A dopamine transporter SPECT scan (DaTscan) is reserved for cases where essential tremor and parkinsonism cannot be clinically distinguished.
5. **Next steps.** Discussion of the diagnosis and its course; treatment when symptoms affect quality of life; physiotherapy and speech and language therapy; and advice on driving (he must inform the DVLA) and on how REM sleep behaviour disorder can injure his wife.
:::
:::

### Treating Parkinson's disease

Dopamine itself does not cross the blood–brain barrier. **Levodopa**, its precursor, does, carried by the large neutral amino acid transporter, and is converted to dopamine in the remaining nigral terminals and other cells. Given alone, most levodopa would be decarboxylated in the gut and blood, causing nausea and postural hypotension, so it is combined with a **peripheral dopa decarboxylase inhibitor** (carbidopa or benserazide), which does not enter the brain. Because levodopa competes with dietary amino acids for absorption and transport, large protein meals can blunt its effect.

The main classes of drug are:

- **Levodopa** (with carbidopa or benserazide): the most effective drug for motor symptoms.
- **Dopamine agonists** (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine patch): act directly on dopamine receptors; less effective than levodopa and more likely to cause hallucinations, sleepiness and **impulse control disorders** (pathological gambling, hypersexuality, binge eating, compulsive shopping).
- **MAO-B inhibitors** (rasagiline, selegiline, safinamide): reduce the breakdown of dopamine in the brain; modest effect.
- **COMT inhibitors** (entacapone, opicapone): reduce the peripheral breakdown of levodopa and prolong each dose.
- **Amantadine**: reduces levodopa-induced dyskinesia.

NICE NG71 advises offering levodopa to people in the early stage whose motor symptoms affect their quality of life, and considering a dopamine agonist, levodopa or an MAO-B inhibitor for those whose symptoms do not yet affect quality of life. Before starting a dopamine agonist, people (and their families) must be warned about impulse control disorders, which can damage finances and relationships, and about sudden onset of sleep, which affects driving. Doses are titrated slowly according to the current formulary.

::: proof Why motor fluctuations appear as the disease advances
Levodopa has a short plasma half-life, about 90 minutes. In early disease this hardly matters: the surviving nigral terminals take up the dopamine made from each dose, store it in vesicles and release it steadily, so a few doses a day give a smooth response. As neurons are lost, storage capacity falls, and brain dopamine increasingly follows the plasma level of levodopa. Two problems then emerge. As the level falls before the next dose, symptoms return (**wearing off**). At the peak of each dose, high and pulsatile stimulation of sensitised postsynaptic receptors produces involuntary writhing movements (**peak-dose dyskinesia**). The range of levels between "off" and "dyskinetic", the therapeutic window, narrows over the years. Treatments for fluctuations aim to make dopaminergic stimulation more continuous: smaller, more frequent doses; COMT or MAO-B inhibitors; long-acting agonists; continuous subcutaneous infusion of foslevodopa–foscarbidopa or apomorphine; levodopa gel delivered into the jejunum; and deep brain stimulation.
:::

::: example Wearing off after eight years {#ex-fluctuations}
A 69-year-old woman has had Parkinson's disease for eight years. She takes co-careldopa at 07:00, 11:00, 15:00 and 19:00. She says each dose "kicks in" after about 40 minutes, gives her about two and a half hours of good movement, and then her right hand starts to tremble and her walking slows for the hour before the next dose. Around an hour after each dose she has writhing movements of her neck and arms that her family find distressing, though she says she prefers them to being "off". She often takes her doses with meals.
::: solution
1. **Describe the problem.** Each dose works for about 2.5 hours, but the dosing interval is 4 hours, so she spends about $4 - (0.67 + 2.5) \approx 0.8$ hours, close to an hour, "off" before each dose: **end-of-dose wearing off**. She also has **peak-dose dyskinesia** about an hour after each dose.
2. **Mechanism.** Loss of presynaptic storage means brain dopamine now follows the short plasma half-life of levodopa, and the therapeutic window has narrowed (see the figure above).
3. **Simple measures.** Taking doses 30–60 minutes before meals, or redistributing protein towards the evening meal, improves absorption and makes onset more predictable.
4. **Smoothing the response.** Options include smaller, more frequent doses (for example five doses a day at a slightly lower size, which reduces both the peak and the trough), adding a COMT inhibitor (entacapone or opicapone) or an MAO-B inhibitor to prolong each dose, or adding a dopamine agonist. Adding a COMT inhibitor alone can worsen dyskinesia, so the levodopa dose may need to fall at the same time. Amantadine can reduce the dyskinesia.
5. **If these fail.** Referral to consider device-aided therapy: deep brain stimulation, apomorphine or foslevodopa–foscarbidopa subcutaneous infusion, or levodopa–carbidopa intestinal gel.
:::
:::

**Deep brain stimulation (DBS)** of the subthalamic nucleus or internal globus pallidus reduces motor fluctuations, dyskinesia and tremor in carefully selected people with good levodopa response and without significant cognitive impairment or uncontrolled psychiatric disease. It does not help symptoms that do not respond to levodopa, such as freezing that persists when "on", balance problems or dementia.

Multidisciplinary care matters as much as drugs: specialist nurses, physiotherapy (including cueing strategies for freezing), occupational therapy, speech and language therapy for voice and swallowing, attention to mood, sleep, bladder and bowel, and, later, advance care planning and palliative care.

::: warning Parkinson's medication in hospital
Levodopa must not be stopped or delayed abruptly. Missed doses in hospital, for example when someone is nil by mouth for surgery or cannot swallow after a stroke, cause rapid worsening of rigidity and immobility and can precipitate aspiration, falls and a rare but life-threatening reaction resembling the neuroleptic malignant syndrome (parkinsonism–hyperpyrexia syndrome). Parkinson's medicines are "time-critical": give them on time, and if the oral route is lost, use alternatives such as a rotigotine patch or a nasogastric tube, with specialist advice. Avoid dopamine-blocking drugs: most antipsychotics (haloperidol especially), metoclopramide and prochlorperazine can severely worsen parkinsonism. Domperidone, which does not cross the blood–brain barrier, is the antiemetic of choice, with attention to its cardiac risks.
:::

## Atypical and secondary parkinsonism

Not all parkinsonism is Parkinson's disease. Features that suggest another cause are a poor response to levodopa, symmetrical onset, early falls, early severe autonomic failure, early dementia or hallucinations, abnormal eye movements, and cerebellar or pyramidal signs.

| Condition | Key features | Pathology |
|---|---|---|
| Dementia with Lewy bodies | Dementia before or within a year of parkinsonism; fluctuating cognition; visual hallucinations; REM sleep behaviour disorder; severe sensitivity to antipsychotics | α-synuclein |
| Multiple system atrophy (MSA) | Parkinsonism and/or cerebellar ataxia with early, severe autonomic failure (postural hypotension, urinary retention or incontinence, erectile dysfunction); stridor; poor levodopa response | α-synuclein in glial cells |
| Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) | Falls (often backwards) in the first year, vertical supranuclear gaze palsy (especially downgaze), axial rigidity, dysarthria and dysphagia, frontal cognitive change | Tau |
| Corticobasal degeneration | Strikingly asymmetric rigidity and apraxia, cortical sensory loss, "alien limb", myoclonus | Tau |
| Drug-induced parkinsonism | Usually symmetrical; history of antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, sodium valproate; improves over weeks to months after stopping | None (receptor blockade) |
| Vascular parkinsonism | Mainly affects the legs and gait ("lower body parkinsonism"); vascular risk factors; white matter disease on MRI | Small vessel disease |

In a younger person (under about 50) with any movement disorder, **Wilson disease** must be excluded, because it is treatable: serum caeruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper and a slit-lamp examination for Kayser–Fleischer rings.

::: quiz
A 71-year-old woman has had parkinsonism for 18 months. She has fallen backwards several times, cannot look down voluntarily although her eyes move down when her head is passively tilted back, has a rigid neck held in extension, and has had little benefit from levodopa. What is the most likely diagnosis?
- [ ] Parkinson's disease
- [ ] Multiple system atrophy
- [x] Progressive supranuclear palsy
- [ ] Drug-induced parkinsonism
::: solution
Early backward falls, a vertical supranuclear gaze palsy (voluntary downgaze is lost, but reflex eye movements with head movement are preserved, which places the lesion above the ocular motor nuclei), axial rigidity and a poor levodopa response are typical of progressive supranuclear palsy, a tauopathy. Multiple system atrophy would usually show prominent early autonomic failure or cerebellar signs, and Parkinson's disease would respond well to levodopa and not cause early falls or a gaze palsy.
:::
:::

## Hyperkinetic movement disorders

### Tremor

**Tremor** is a rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a body part. It is classified first by when it appears.

- **Rest tremor**: present when the limb is supported and relaxed; typical of Parkinson's disease.
- **Postural tremor**: present when holding a posture, such as outstretched arms.
- **Kinetic and intention tremor**: present during movement; an **intention tremor** worsens as the target is approached and indicates cerebellar disease.

::: definition Essential tremor {#def-et}
**Essential tremor** is an isolated tremor syndrome of bilateral upper limb action tremor (postural and kinetic) of at least 3 years' duration, with or without tremor in other places such as the head, voice or legs, and without other neurological signs such as dystonia, ataxia or parkinsonism (MDS consensus statement, 2018). It is often familial and often improves temporarily with alcohol. It is the commonest cause of tremor in adults.
:::

Essential tremor affects tasks such as drinking from a cup, writing and using cutlery. When it is disabling, propranolol or primidone are the usual first drugs; deep brain stimulation of the thalamus or focused ultrasound thalamotomy are options for severe cases. **Enhanced physiological tremor** is a fine postural tremor made worse by anxiety, caffeine, thyrotoxicosis, hypoglycaemia, alcohol withdrawal and drugs such as salbutamol, lithium, valproate and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, so these should be looked for in anyone with a new tremor. **Functional tremor** varies in frequency, is distractible, and entrains to the rhythm of tapping with the other hand.

::: example Shaky handwriting {#ex-tremor}
A 58-year-old teacher has had a tremor of both hands for six years, making it difficult to write on the whiteboard and to drink tea without spilling. It is worse when she is nervous and improves for an hour after a glass of wine. Her father had a similar tremor. Examination shows a symmetrical 7 Hz postural and kinetic tremor of both hands and a slight "no-no" head tremor. There is no rest tremor, no bradykinesia, no rigidity, and no dystonia. Thyroid function is normal and she drinks two cups of coffee a day.
::: solution
1. **Type of tremor.** Postural and kinetic, bilateral, in the upper limbs, with head tremor; no rest tremor.
2. **Other signs.** None: no parkinsonism, dystonia or ataxia.
3. **Duration and context.** Six years; family history; alcohol responsiveness.
4. **Secondary causes.** Thyroid normal, modest caffeine; no tremor-causing drugs.
5. **Diagnosis.** **Essential tremor.** A DaTscan is not needed because there is no parkinsonism.
6. **Management principles.** Explanation and reassurance that this is not Parkinson's disease; practical aids (weighted cutlery, lidded cups); and, because it affects her work, a trial of propranolol (asthma and heart block must be excluded first) or primidone, according to the current formulary. She should be warned that alcohol is not a treatment.
:::
:::

### Dystonia

**Dystonia** is a movement disorder characterised by sustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing abnormal, often repetitive, movements, postures or both. It is often twisting, triggered or worsened by voluntary action, and may be eased by a **sensory trick** (geste antagoniste), such as touching the chin to relieve cervical dystonia. Dystonia may be **focal** (cervical dystonia, blepharospasm, writer's cramp, laryngeal dystonia), segmental or generalised. Generalised dystonia beginning in childhood is often genetic. Focal dystonias are treated with **botulinum toxin** injections into the overactive muscles; generalised dystonia may respond to anticholinergic drugs or deep brain stimulation of the internal pallidum. A trial of levodopa is given to every child or young adult with dystonia, because **dopa-responsive dystonia** (due to mutations in the *GCH1* gene) responds dramatically.

**Acute dystonic reactions** occur within hours or days of starting a dopamine-blocking drug, most often in young people: oculogyric crisis, torticollis, tongue protrusion or trismus. They are frightening but respond quickly to an anticholinergic drug such as procyclidine.

### Chorea and Huntington's disease

**Chorea** consists of irregular, unpredictable, brief movements that flow from one body part to another, giving a fidgety or dance-like appearance. Causes include Huntington's disease, drugs (excess levodopa in Parkinson's disease, oral contraceptives, phenytoin), autoimmune disease (systemic lupus erythematosus and antiphospholipid syndrome), **Sydenham chorea** after streptococcal infection in children, pregnancy (**chorea gravidarum**), hyperthyroidism, non-ketotic hyperglycaemia, polycythaemia, and stroke in the basal ganglia (hemichorea or hemiballism, from a lesion of the subthalamic nucleus).

::: definition Huntington's disease {#def-hd}
**Huntington's disease** is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat in the *HTT* gene on chromosome 4, encoding an expanded polyglutamine tract in the huntingtin protein. Interpretation of the repeat length:

- 26 or fewer: normal;
- 27–35: intermediate (the person will not develop the disease, but the repeat may expand in the next generation, especially through the father);
- 36–39: reduced penetrance (may or may not develop the disease);
- 40 or more: full penetrance.

Longer repeats are associated with earlier onset, and expansion during transmission, especially paternal, causes **anticipation**: earlier onset in successive generations.
:::

The disease usually starts between 30 and 50 years, with a triad of **chorea**, **cognitive decline** (executive dysfunction, slowness of thought) and **psychiatric features** (depression, irritability, apathy, obsessive behaviour, psychosis). Suicide risk is increased. Medium spiny neurons of the striatum, especially those of the indirect pathway, are lost early, which explains the chorea. There is no disease-modifying treatment as of 2026; chorea can be reduced with tetrabenazine (a VMAT2 inhibitor) or antipsychotics, and care is multidisciplinary. **Predictive testing** of at-risk adults follows a careful protocol of counselling before and after the test (see [[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]).

### Tics, myoclonus and drug-induced disorders

**Tics** are sudden, rapid, repetitive, non-rhythmic movements (motor tics) or sounds (vocal tics), usually preceded by an urge and temporarily suppressible. **Tourette syndrome** is diagnosed when multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic have been present for more than a year, beginning before the age of 18. It is often accompanied by attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and obsessive–compulsive disorder, and tics usually lessen in adulthood. Behavioural therapy (comprehensive behavioural intervention for tics) is first-line when treatment is needed.

**Myoclonus** consists of sudden, brief, shock-like jerks. It occurs normally on falling asleep (hypnic jerks), in epilepsy, in metabolic encephalopathies (uraemia, liver failure; often with **asterixis**, a negative myoclonus), after hypoxic brain injury, and in prion disease.

Dopamine-blocking drugs cause a family of disorders: **acute dystonia** (hours to days), **akathisia** (an inner restlessness, days to weeks), **drug-induced parkinsonism** (weeks to months), and **tardive dyskinesia** (repetitive mouth, tongue and facial movements after months to years, which may persist after the drug is stopped). The **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, reduced consciousness and a raised creatine kinase) is a medical emergency; it must be distinguished from **serotonin syndrome**, which develops faster and causes clonus and hyperreflexia rather than lead-pipe rigidity. See [[psychiatry/psychotic-disorders]] and [[emergency-critical-care/poisoning]].

::: example A young man with a new tremor and odd behaviour {#ex-wilson}
A 22-year-old student has become irritable and his grades have fallen over a year. His hands shake when he holds them out, with a coarse "wing-beating" quality, and his speech is slurred. His GP found mildly abnormal liver tests last year. There is no family history of neurological disease.
::: solution
1. **Pattern.** A young adult with a movement disorder (tremor, dysarthria), psychiatric change and liver abnormalities.
2. **Must not miss.** **Wilson disease**, an autosomal recessive disorder of copper transport (*ATP7B*), which causes copper deposition in the liver and the brain (especially the basal ganglia). It is treatable, and untreated it is fatal.
3. **Investigations.** Serum caeruloplasmin (usually low), 24-hour urinary copper (raised), slit-lamp examination for Kayser–Fleischer rings (present in almost all people with neurological involvement), liver function and imaging, MRI of the brain, and genetic testing. Liver biopsy with copper quantification may be needed.
4. **Treatment principles.** Specialist management with copper chelation (penicillamine or trientine) or zinc, avoiding high-copper foods; liver transplantation for acute liver failure. Neurological symptoms can worsen early in chelation, so treatment is started under specialist supervision. Siblings should be screened.
:::
:::

::: history
James Parkinson described six people with the "shaking palsy" in 1817, and Jean-Martin Charcot named the condition after him and recognised rigidity as distinct from weakness. George Huntington, a young doctor on Long Island, described hereditary chorea in 1872. Friedrich Lewy described the inclusions that bear his name in 1912. In 1957–1960 Arvid Carlsson showed that dopamine is a neurotransmitter and that reserpine-induced parkinsonism in animals is reversed by levodopa; Herbert Ehringer and Oleh Hornykiewicz found striatal dopamine depleted in Parkinson's disease in 1960, and George Cotzias established effective high-dose oral levodopa in 1967. In 1993 the Huntington's Disease Collaborative Research Group identified the CAG repeat expansion, and in 1997 Mihael Polymeropoulos and colleagues found an α-synuclein mutation in familial Parkinson's disease. Alim-Louis Benabid introduced deep brain stimulation for tremor in 1987.
:::

::: summary
- The basal ganglia select and scale movement. Dopamine favours movement by exciting the direct pathway and inhibiting the indirect pathway; its loss causes parkinsonism, and loss of the indirect pathway causes chorea.
- Parkinsonism is bradykinesia (with decrement) plus rest tremor and/or rigidity. Parkinson's disease is asymmetric, levodopa-responsive and accompanied by non-motor features (REM sleep behaviour disorder, anosmia, constipation, mood change, autonomic failure, later dementia).
- Levodopa with a decarboxylase inhibitor is the most effective drug; dopamine agonists carry risks of impulse control disorders and sleepiness. Motor fluctuations and dyskinesia arise as storage capacity is lost; continuous stimulation and deep brain stimulation help.
- Parkinson's medicines are time-critical in hospital; avoid dopamine-blocking drugs such as haloperidol and metoclopramide.
- Red flags for atypical parkinsonism: early falls, gaze palsy (PSP), early autonomic failure or ataxia (MSA), early dementia and hallucinations (DLB), marked asymmetry with apraxia (CBD), poor levodopa response. Consider drugs and Wilson disease.
- Essential tremor is a bilateral postural and kinetic tremor, often familial and alcohol-responsive; treat with propranolol or primidone.
- Dystonia: sustained contractions with abnormal postures, sensory tricks; botulinum toxin for focal forms; try levodopa in young-onset dystonia.
- Huntington's disease: autosomal dominant CAG expansion in *HTT* (40 or more fully penetrant, 36–39 reduced penetrance), chorea, cognitive decline and psychiatric illness.
- Dopamine blockers cause acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia and neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
:::

::: exercise Hospital admission {level=2}
A 78-year-old man with Parkinson's disease, taking co-careldopa five times a day, is admitted with a hip fracture and is nil by mouth awaiting surgery. Later he becomes agitated and hallucinating on the ward. Discuss how his Parkinson's medication should be managed, and how the agitation should be approached.
::: solution
His levodopa is time-critical. Before and after surgery, medication should be given on time; if he cannot take oral medicines, specialist advice (Parkinson's nurse or pharmacist) is sought for an alternative such as a rotigotine patch or levodopa via a nasogastric tube, with an equivalent dose calculated. Missed doses risk severe rigidity, aspiration, falls and the parkinsonism–hyperpyrexia syndrome. The agitation needs assessment for delirium: infection, pain, urinary retention, constipation, dehydration, hypoxia, missed or excess medication, and drugs such as opioids and anticholinergics. Non-drug measures come first. Haloperidol and other typical antipsychotics must be avoided because they can cause severe worsening and neuroleptic malignant syndrome; if an antipsychotic is unavoidable, low-dose quetiapine (or clozapine in specialist hands) is preferred, according to local guidance.
:::
:::

::: exercise Gambling losses {level=2}
A 56-year-old woman whose Parkinson's disease is well controlled on ropinirole has, according to her husband, lost several thousand pounds online in three months, which is completely unlike her. What is the likely cause, and what should be done?
::: solution
An **impulse control disorder** caused by the dopamine agonist. These occur in a substantial minority of people taking dopamine agonists, more often with higher doses and in younger people, and include gambling, compulsive shopping, binge eating and hypersexuality. Patients often do not report them because of embarrassment, so families should be warned beforehand and asked at reviews. Management is gradual reduction or withdrawal of the agonist (abrupt withdrawal can cause a dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome of anxiety, pain and low mood), with levodopa adjusted to maintain motor control, and support for the financial and relational consequences.
:::

::: exercise Choose the atypical parkinsonism {level=2}
A 61-year-old man has had slowness and stiffness for two years with little response to levodopa. He faints when standing, needs a catheter for urinary retention, snores loudly with nocturnal stridor, and has a wide-based unsteady gait. What is the likely diagnosis, and what features distinguish it from Parkinson's disease?
::: solution
**Multiple system atrophy** (parkinsonian and cerebellar features). Distinguishing features: early and severe autonomic failure (orthostatic hypotension, urinary retention or incontinence, erectile dysfunction), cerebellar ataxia, stridor, rapid progression, symmetrical onset and a poor or short-lived response to levodopa. In Parkinson's disease autonomic failure develops later and is milder, and levodopa produces a clear, sustained benefit. MRI may show atrophy of the pons and cerebellum (the "hot cross bun" sign) or of the putamen.
:::

::: exercise Risk after an unaffected life {level=3 check="1/6"}
A 60-year-old man's mother had Huntington's disease with 43 CAG repeats; he has not been tested and has no symptoms. Assume that 80% of people who inherit a fully penetrant allele show symptoms by the age of 60 (illustrative value). Using Bayes' theorem, what is the probability that he carries the allele? Give the answer as a fraction.
::: solution
Prior probability of carrying the allele: $1/2$. Probability of being symptom-free at 60 if a carrier: $0.2$; if not a carrier: $1$. Posterior:
$$P = \frac{0.5 \times 0.2}{0.5 \times 0.2 + 0.5 \times 1} = \frac{0.1}{0.6} = \frac{1}{6}.$$
His risk has fallen from 1 in 2 to about 1 in 6, and his children's risk of inheriting the allele from him is half of that, about 1 in 12. Such calculations help people decide whether to seek predictive testing, which would give a definite answer, after counselling.
:::

::: exercise A movement disorder after a new drug {level=3}
A 19-year-old woman attends the emergency department with her neck twisted to the right and her eyes fixed upwards, which began a few hours after she started an antiemetic for gastroenteritis. (a) What is the diagnosis and the likely drug? (b) How is it treated? (c) How would your assessment change if she had instead developed fever of 39.5 °C, generalised rigidity, sweating, fluctuating blood pressure and confusion three days after starting an antipsychotic? (d) What would point instead to serotonin syndrome?
::: solution
(a) An **acute dystonic reaction** (torticollis and oculogyric crisis) caused by a dopamine-blocking antiemetic, most likely metoclopramide or prochlorperazine. Young women are particularly susceptible. (b) Stop the drug and give an anticholinergic, such as procyclidine (intravenously or intramuscularly for rapid effect), according to the formulary; symptoms resolve within minutes. She should be advised to avoid that drug in future, and the reaction recorded as an adverse reaction. (c) This would suggest **neuroleptic malignant syndrome**, a medical emergency: stop the antipsychotic, measure creatine kinase (often very high), renal function and electrolytes, and give supportive care with cooling and fluids, usually in high dependency or intensive care; dantrolene or bromocriptine may be used with specialist advice. Watch for rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury. (d) Serotonin syndrome typically follows a serotonergic drug or combination, develops within hours, and causes clonus (especially inducible ankle clonus), hyperreflexia, tremor, agitation, diarrhoea and dilated pupils, with less rigidity than neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
