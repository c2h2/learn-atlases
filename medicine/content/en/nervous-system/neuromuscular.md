The neuromuscular system is the last part of the motor pathway: the lower motor neuron in the anterior horn, its axon running through the root, plexus and peripheral nerve, the neuromuscular junction where the nerve signal crosses to muscle, and the muscle itself. Disease at each level produces its own pattern of weakness, reflex change, sensory loss and time course. Recognising that pattern is the key skill, because it tells you which tests to request and which diseases to consider.

This chapter covers the approach to peripheral neuropathy and to nerve conduction studies and electromyography, Guillain–Barré syndrome and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, myasthenia gravis and the other junction disorders, the myopathies, and the genetic neuromuscular diseases. Motor neuron disease is covered in [[nervous-system/dementia-neurodegeneration]], and the upper and lower motor neuron examination in [[nervous-system/motor-systems]].

## Localising within the neuromuscular system

::: theorem The level decides the pattern {#thm-level}
Each level of the lower motor unit fails in a recognisable way.

- **Anterior horn cell** (for example spinal muscular atrophy, the lower motor neuron part of MND): weakness, wasting and fasciculations, with no sensory loss.
- **Root** (radiculopathy): pain radiating in a dermatome, sensory loss in that dermatome, weakness in the muscles of that myotome, and loss of the reflex served by the root.
- **Peripheral nerve**: a single nerve (mononeuropathy) gives weakness and sensory loss in that nerve's territory; many nerves give a length-dependent "glove and stocking" pattern (polyneuropathy) or a patchy pattern (mononeuritis multiplex). Reflexes are reduced or lost.
- **Neuromuscular junction**: **fatigable** weakness that worsens with use and improves with rest, often affecting the eyelids, eye muscles and bulbar muscles, with normal sensation and usually normal reflexes.
- **Muscle** (myopathy): usually **symmetrical proximal** weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, lifting the arms), with normal sensation and reflexes preserved until weakness is severe.
:::

Time course helps as well. Weakness that develops over hours to days suggests Guillain–Barré syndrome, botulism, a periodic paralysis, myasthenic crisis or a toxin; over weeks to months, an inflammatory neuropathy or myopathy; over years, a genetic or degenerative disease. A family history, drug history and a search for systemic disease complete the picture.

## Peripheral neuropathy

::: definition Peripheral neuropathy {#def-neuropathy}
**Peripheral neuropathy** is disease of the peripheral nerves. It is described by four features:

1. **Distribution**: a single nerve (**mononeuropathy**), several separate nerves (**mononeuritis multiplex**), or many nerves symmetrically (**polyneuropathy**, usually length-dependent, starting in the feet).
2. **Fibre type**: motor, sensory (large-fibre: vibration, joint position, reflexes; small-fibre: pain and temperature) or autonomic, or a combination.
3. **Pathology**: **axonal** (the axon degenerates) or **demyelinating** (the myelin sheath is damaged).
4. **Tempo**: acute, subacute or chronic.
:::

The commonest pattern is a **length-dependent sensory or sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy**. The longest nerves fail first, so symptoms start in the toes and feet and spread up the legs; when they reach about the knees, the fingertips become involved (the "glove and stocking" pattern). Ankle reflexes are lost early. Small-fibre involvement causes burning pain and allodynia; large-fibre involvement causes numbness, imbalance and a positive Romberg test.

In the UK, the commonest cause is **diabetes**. Others include alcohol, vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, drugs (for example vincristine, platinum compounds, taxanes and bortezomib in chemotherapy; isoniazid without pyridoxine; long-term metronidazole or nitrofurantoin), paraproteinaemia and amyloidosis, HIV, and inherited neuropathy. In many older people no cause is found.

Basic tests for a chronic polyneuropathy are HbA1c (or glucose tolerance), full blood count, vitamin B12 and folate, renal and liver function, thyroid function, and **serum protein electrophoresis** for a paraprotein. Nerve conduction studies are done when the pattern is atypical (asymmetric, motor-predominant, rapidly progressive or non-length-dependent) or the cause is unclear.

Red flags that suggest a treatable or dangerous neuropathy are rapid progression, asymmetry, prominent motor weakness, proximal as well as distal weakness, and systemic features. **Mononeuritis multiplex**, with painful, sequential lesions of individual nerves (for example a foot drop followed by a wrist drop), suggests **vasculitis** and needs urgent investigation (see [[musculoskeletal/connective-tissue-vasculitis]]).

**Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease** is the commonest inherited neuropathy (about 1 in 2,500 people). It causes slowly progressive distal weakness and wasting, foot deformity (high arches, hammer toes), thin calves and reduced reflexes, often with affected relatives who did not know they had it. The commonest type, CMT1A, is a demyelinating neuropathy caused by a duplication of the *PMP22* gene.

Neuropathic pain is treated with amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin (NICE CG173), with capsaicin cream for localised pain. Foot care is essential: people with diabetic neuropathy have their feet checked at least annually, using a 10 g monofilament, because unnoticed injuries lead to ulcers and amputation.

### Entrapment neuropathies

**Carpal tunnel syndrome**, compression of the median nerve at the wrist, is the commonest entrapment. It causes numbness and tingling in the thumb, index, middle and half of the ring finger, typically waking the person at night and relieved by shaking the hand. Later there is wasting of the thenar eminence and weakness of thumb abduction. Associations include pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, acromegaly and repetitive wrist use. Treatment is with wrist splints at night, a corticosteroid injection, or surgical decompression for persistent or severe symptoms. The **ulnar nerve** is commonly compressed at the elbow (numbness of the little finger and wasting of the small hand muscles), and the **common fibular nerve** at the fibular head (foot drop; see [[nervous-system/motor-systems]]).

### Nerve conduction studies and electromyography

**Nerve conduction studies** stimulate a nerve electrically and record the response from a muscle (motor) or from the nerve itself (sensory). They measure the **amplitude** of the response, which reflects the number of conducting axons, and the **conduction velocity** and **latency**, which reflect myelin.

- **Axonal** neuropathy: reduced amplitudes, with normal or only slightly reduced velocities.
- **Demyelinating** neuropathy: markedly slowed velocities, prolonged distal latencies and F-waves, and in acquired forms **conduction block** (the response falls sharply between two stimulation points) and temporal dispersion.

**Electromyography (EMG)** records the electrical activity of muscle through a needle electrode. In **neurogenic** disease, denervated fibres fire spontaneously (fibrillations and positive sharp waves), and surviving motor units enlarge as they reinnervate orphaned fibres, giving large, long motor unit potentials. In **myopathic** disease, motor units lose fibres, giving small, short, polyphasic potentials recruited early. **Repetitive nerve stimulation** and **single-fibre EMG** test the neuromuscular junction.

::: example A builder's numb hand {#ex-carpal}
A 54-year-old builder has numbness and tingling in his right thumb, index and middle fingers for six months. It wakes him at night and he shakes his hand to relieve it. Recently he has been dropping screws. He has type 2 diabetes. There is mild wasting of the thenar eminence and weakness of thumb abduction; sensation is reduced over the lateral three and a half fingers but normal over the thenar eminence itself. Tinel's sign at the wrist is positive.
::: solution
1. **Localise.** Sensory loss in the median nerve distribution of the fingers, with weakness and wasting of the median-innervated thenar muscles (abductor pollicis brevis), points to the median nerve. Sparing of sensation over the thenar eminence is important: that skin is supplied by the palmar cutaneous branch, which leaves the median nerve above the wrist and does not pass through the carpal tunnel. So the lesion is **at the wrist**: carpal tunnel syndrome.
2. **Differential.** A C6 or C7 radiculopathy (neck pain, reflex change, weakness outside the median nerve territory), or a diabetic polyneuropathy (bilateral, starting in the feet).
3. **Tests.** Nerve conduction studies confirm the diagnosis and its severity, and are particularly useful before surgery and when diabetes might be causing a coexisting neuropathy. Check thyroid function if not recently done.
4. **Management principles.** Because there is wasting and weakness (axonal loss), splinting alone is unlikely to be enough: he should be referred for surgical decompression. Optimise diabetes control. Discuss work adaptations, since vibrating tools can worsen symptoms.
:::
:::

## Guillain–Barré syndrome

::: definition Guillain–Barré syndrome {#def-gbs}
**Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)** is an acute, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy. It causes rapidly progressive, usually **symmetrical** weakness of the limbs with **reduced or absent reflexes**, reaching its worst (nadir) within **four weeks**, usually within two. Sensory symptoms (tingling in the hands and feet) are common but sensory signs are often mild. Back or limb pain is frequent and can be severe. Facial and bulbar weakness, **respiratory muscle weakness** and **autonomic dysfunction** (arrhythmias, blood pressure swings, ileus) are the main threats to life.
:::

GBS affects about 1–2 people per 100,000 each year. About two-thirds have an infection in the preceding weeks, most often *Campylobacter jejuni* gastroenteritis, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* or, in outbreaks, Zika virus. Antibodies made against the infection cross-react with gangliosides on nerve (**molecular mimicry**).

There are several subtypes. **Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy** is the commonest in Europe and North America. **Acute motor axonal neuropathy** is more common in East Asia and Central and South America and is associated with *Campylobacter* and anti-GM1 antibodies. **Miller Fisher syndrome** combines ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia, with anti-GQ1b antibodies.

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by **CSF** showing **albuminocytological dissociation** (raised protein with a normal cell count; protein may be normal in the first week) and nerve conduction studies. A CSF white cell count above 50/µL should make you reconsider the diagnosis (for example HIV, Lyme disease, lymphoma, poliomyelitis-like illnesses). Cord compression or transverse myelitis must be excluded when there is a sensory level or early sphincter disturbance.

::: proof Why breathing is monitored by numbers, not by oxygen saturation
In GBS and myasthenia the lungs are healthy; the problem is a weak pump. As the diaphragm and intercostal muscles weaken, tidal volume falls and the person breathes faster and more shallowly. Oxygen saturation stays normal until late, because the alveoli still exchange gas well and supplemental oxygen hides any shortfall. Carbon dioxide rises only when ventilation has failed. By the time saturation falls or the blood gas shows hypercapnia, the person may be close to respiratory arrest. The **forced vital capacity (FVC)**, measured with a bedside spirometer several times a day, falls earlier and more steadily. Reference points used in practice are the "20–30–40 rule": intubation is considered when the vital capacity falls below **20 mL/kg**, the maximal inspiratory pressure is weaker than **30 cmH₂O**, or the maximal expiratory pressure is below **40 cmH₂O**, or when these are falling rapidly. Inability to count to 20 in one breath, a weak cough, use of accessory muscles, paradoxical abdominal movement and difficulty swallowing secretions are warning signs. Facial weakness can make spirometry unreliable, so a tight-fitting face mask is used.
:::

Treatment is with **intravenous immunoglobulin** (IVIG, usually over five days) or **plasma exchange**, which are equally effective and speed recovery when started within two weeks of onset (plasma exchange up to four weeks) in people who cannot walk unaided. Corticosteroids do not help. Supportive care matters as much: thromboprophylaxis, pain control, monitoring of heart rhythm and blood pressure, bowel and bladder care, pressure care, physiotherapy and psychological support. The **Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score (EGRIS)** estimates the risk of needing ventilation in the first week from the speed of onset, facial or bulbar weakness and limb strength. About a fifth to a quarter of people need mechanical ventilation. Most recover over months: about 80% walk independently at six months, but 3–7% die and some are left with lasting weakness or fatigue.

::: example Tingling feet and then a weak face {#ex-gbs}
A 32-year-old man had diarrhoea three weeks ago. Five days ago his feet began tingling, and since then his legs have become weak; today he cannot climb stairs and has difficulty closing his eyes. He has severe low back pain. Power is 3/5 proximally and distally in the legs and 4/5 in the arms; all reflexes are absent; plantars are flexor; there is no sensory level. He weighs 75 kg. His FVC is 1.8 L, falling from 2.6 L yesterday. Pulse 110, blood pressure 165/100.
::: solution
1. **Recognise.** Rapidly progressive symmetrical weakness, areflexia, bilateral facial weakness and back pain, after diarrhoea (probably *Campylobacter*): **Guillain–Barré syndrome**.
2. **Respiratory risk.** His vital capacity is $1.8 / 75 \times 1000 = 24$ mL/kg, close to the 20 mL/kg threshold and falling fast (from about 35 mL/kg yesterday). With facial weakness and rapid onset, his EGRIS is high. He needs **urgent review by critical care** and admission to a monitored bed, with FVC checked every few hours.
3. **Autonomic dysfunction.** Tachycardia and hypertension suggest autonomic involvement. He needs cardiac monitoring; blood pressure swings are treated cautiously, because treatment can cause sudden hypotension.
4. **Tests.** Nerve conduction studies; lumbar puncture (protein may still be normal); blood tests including electrolytes; stool culture for *Campylobacter*. MRI of the spine if the picture is atypical.
5. **Treatment.** Start **IVIG** (dose by weight according to local protocol) now, since he cannot walk unaided and is within two weeks of onset. Thromboprophylaxis, analgesia for neuropathic and back pain, eye care for incomplete eye closure, swallowing assessment, and explanation to him and his family.
:::
:::

**Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP)** is the chronic relative of GBS. Weakness and sensory loss progress or relapse over **more than eight weeks**, with proximal as well as distal weakness and widespread areflexia. Nerve conduction studies show demyelination. It responds to IVIG, corticosteroids or plasma exchange (EAN/PNS guideline, 2021). It is important because it is treatable and is sometimes mislabelled as diabetic neuropathy. A subgroup with antibodies to proteins of the node of Ranvier (neurofascin-155, contactin-1), now called **autoimmune nodopathies**, responds poorly to IVIG but well to rituximab.

## The neuromuscular junction

::: definition Myasthenia gravis {#def-mg}
**Myasthenia gravis** is an autoimmune disease in which antibodies against the postsynaptic **acetylcholine receptor (AChR)**, or less often against **muscle-specific kinase (MuSK)** or LRP4, reduce the number of functioning receptors at the neuromuscular junction. Its hallmark is **fatigable weakness**: weakness that worsens with repeated or sustained use and improves with rest. It is **ocular** when confined to the eyelids and eye muscles (ptosis and double vision), and **generalised** when bulbar, limb or respiratory muscles are affected.
:::

Myasthenia has two peaks: women in their twenties and thirties, and men and women over 60 (now the larger group). About 85% of people with generalised disease have AChR antibodies, but only about half of those with purely ocular disease. About 10–15% have a **thymoma**, and many younger AChR-positive patients have thymic hyperplasia.

The normal junction has a large **safety factor**: each nerve impulse releases more acetylcholine than is needed to trigger a muscle action potential. With repeated firing, the amount released per impulse falls. Normally this does not matter, but when receptors are lost the end-plate potential falls below threshold in more and more fibres, so the muscle weakens with use.

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture and **antibody testing**. Neurophysiology shows a **decrement** of more than 10% in the compound muscle action potential with repetitive nerve stimulation at 3 Hz, and increased **jitter** on single-fibre EMG, the most sensitive test. The **ice pack test** (ice on a ptotic eyelid for two minutes improves the ptosis) is a useful bedside clue. CT of the chest looks for thymoma, and thyroid function is checked because autoimmune thyroid disease often coexists.

Treatment has several layers.

- **Symptomatic**: **pyridostigmine**, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that increases acetylcholine at the junction. Side effects are muscarinic: diarrhoea, cramps, sweating and salivation.
- **Immunosuppression**: **prednisolone**, started at a low dose and increased gradually (high starting doses can cause an early worsening), then a steroid-sparing drug such as azathioprine or mycophenolate. Rituximab is particularly effective in MuSK-positive disease.
- **Thymectomy**: for thymoma, and for AChR-positive generalised myasthenia in younger adults, in whom a randomised trial (MGTX, 2016) showed better outcomes and lower steroid needs.
- **Newer targeted treatments** for refractory generalised disease: complement inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan) and drugs that block the neonatal Fc receptor and so lower IgG levels (efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab). Their NHS availability depends on NICE appraisals; NICE recommended rozanolixizumab (TA1155) for antibody-positive generalised myasthenia not controlled by standard treatment.
- **Crisis and rapid worsening**: IVIG or plasma exchange.

A **myasthenic crisis** is weakness severe enough to need ventilation or to threaten the airway. It is often triggered by infection, surgery, pregnancy or drugs. Many drugs can worsen myasthenia: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and macrolides, intravenous magnesium, beta-blockers, neuromuscular blocking agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors. People with myasthenia should carry an alert card, and new prescriptions should be checked.

::: example Double vision in the evening {#ex-mg}
A 68-year-old woman has had six weeks of drooping of her left eyelid and double vision, both worse in the evening and after reading. In the past week she has choked on food twice and her voice becomes nasal when she talks for a long time. On examination there is left ptosis that worsens with sustained upward gaze for 60 seconds, variable diplopia, and weakness of neck flexion that worsens with repeated testing. Pupils and sensation are normal; reflexes are present.
::: solution
1. **Localise.** Fatigable ptosis, variable diplopia, bulbar symptoms and neck flexion weakness with normal pupils, sensation and reflexes: a disorder of the **neuromuscular junction**, most likely **generalised myasthenia gravis**. Normal pupils make a third nerve palsy unlikely.
2. **Danger.** Bulbar weakness (choking, nasal speech) puts her at risk of aspiration and respiratory failure. Measure FVC, assess swallowing, and admit if FVC is low or falling or swallowing is unsafe.
3. **Tests.** AChR antibodies (MuSK if negative), repetitive nerve stimulation or single-fibre EMG, CT of the chest for thymoma, and thyroid function.
4. **Treatment principles.** Pyridostigmine for symptoms; neurology-led immunotherapy with prednisolone started at a low dose and increased gradually, with bone protection and monitoring of glucose, then a steroid-sparing agent. If bulbar or respiratory weakness worsens, IVIG or plasma exchange. Thymectomy if a thymoma is found. Give her a list of drugs to avoid.
:::
:::

**Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)** is caused by antibodies against presynaptic **P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels**, which reduce acetylcholine release. About half of cases are associated with **small cell lung cancer**. It causes proximal leg weakness, reduced reflexes that briefly **increase after a few seconds of maximal contraction** (post-exercise facilitation), and autonomic symptoms such as a dry mouth and erectile dysfunction; eye signs are milder than in myasthenia. Repetitive nerve stimulation shows a low baseline response that rises by more than 100% after brief exercise. Treatment is of any tumour, with amifampridine to increase acetylcholine release and immunosuppression if needed.

**Botulism**, caused by botulinum toxin blocking acetylcholine release, produces a **descending** paralysis that starts with the eyes and bulbar muscles, often with dilated, unreactive pupils, and in the UK is seen mainly in people who inject drugs (wound botulism). It is treated with antitoxin and supportive care and is a notifiable disease.

## Muscle disease

::: definition Myopathy {#def-myopathy}
A **myopathy** is a disease of skeletal muscle. It usually causes **symmetrical proximal weakness**, with normal sensation. The **creatine kinase (CK)**, an enzyme released from damaged muscle fibres, is often raised. Myopathies are **acquired** (inflammatory, endocrine, drug-induced, toxic) or **genetic** (muscular dystrophies, myotonic dystrophy, metabolic and mitochondrial myopathies, channelopathies).
:::

**Inflammatory myopathies** (myositis) cause weakness over weeks to months.

- **Dermatomyositis**: proximal weakness with a violaceous rash on the upper eyelids (heliotrope rash), scaly papules over the knuckles (Gottron's papules) and a rash on sun-exposed skin. In adults it is associated with cancer, so a cancer screen is done at diagnosis.
- **Antisynthetase syndrome** (for example anti-Jo-1 antibodies): myositis with interstitial lung disease, arthritis, fever and cracked skin on the fingers ("mechanic's hands"); see [[respiratory/interstitial-vascular-disease]].
- **Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy**: severe weakness with a very high CK, associated with anti-SRP antibodies or anti-HMGCR antibodies, the latter often after **statin** exposure. It continues after the statin is stopped and needs immunotherapy.
- **Inclusion body myositis**: the commonest myopathy over 50, more common in men, causing slowly progressive, often asymmetric weakness of the **finger flexors** and **quadriceps**, with falls and later swallowing problems. It does not respond to immunosuppression.

Myositis is treated with corticosteroids and steroid-sparing drugs, with IVIG or rituximab in resistant cases.

**Other acquired myopathies** include **hypothyroidism** (with raised CK), Cushing's syndrome and long-term **corticosteroids** (proximal weakness with a normal CK), vitamin D deficiency (with bone pain), alcohol, and drugs. **Statins** commonly cause muscle aches with a normal CK; they rarely cause significant myopathy or rhabdomyolysis.

**Rhabdomyolysis** is the rapid breakdown of muscle with release of myoglobin, potassium and phosphate. Causes include crush injury, prolonged immobility after a fall, extreme exertion, seizures, drugs and toxins, and inherited metabolic myopathies. It causes muscle pain, dark urine (positive for blood on a dipstick without red cells on microscopy), a very high CK, hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury. Treatment is generous intravenous fluids, correction of potassium, and treatment of the cause (see [[renal/acute-kidney-injury]]).

### Genetic muscle disease

**Duchenne muscular dystrophy** is an X-linked recessive disease caused by mutations in the *DMD* gene, leading to absence of **dystrophin**, a protein that links the muscle cell's internal skeleton to its membrane. It affects about 1 in 3,500–5,000 boys. Boys are late to walk, fall often and use their hands to climb up their legs when rising from the floor (**Gowers' sign**), with enlarged calves (pseudohypertrophy). CK is greatly raised. Without treatment, walking is lost by about age 12. Corticosteroids prolong walking; respiratory support, cardiac treatment for the cardiomyopathy, spinal surgery and multidisciplinary care have extended survival into the thirties and beyond. Some genetic therapies (exon-skipping drugs, gene therapy) are approved in some countries. **Becker muscular dystrophy**, with reduced or abnormal dystrophin, is milder and later in onset. Female carriers can develop cardiomyopathy.

**Myotonic dystrophy type 1** is the commonest adult muscular dystrophy. It is autosomal dominant, caused by an expanded **CTG repeat** in the *DMPK* gene, and shows **anticipation**: the repeat tends to expand from one generation to the next, giving earlier and more severe disease; a severe congenital form occurs when it is passed on by an affected mother. Features include **myotonia** (slow relaxation after contraction, for example difficulty releasing a handshake), distal and facial weakness with ptosis and temporal wasting, frontal balding, **cataracts**, **cardiac conduction disease** (which can cause sudden death; regular ECGs are needed), insulin resistance, daytime sleepiness and swallowing problems. Sedatives and anaesthesia carry particular risks.

**Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)** is an autosomal recessive degeneration of anterior horn cells, usually caused by deletion of the *SMN1* gene. In its most severe form it causes profound weakness in infancy and, untreated, death from respiratory failure within two years. Three disease-modifying treatments (nusinersen, risdiplam and the gene therapy onasemnogene abeparvovec) have transformed the outlook, and they work best when given before symptoms appear, which is the argument for newborn screening.

**Periodic paralyses** are episodes of flaccid weakness caused by disorders of muscle ion channels or by shifts of potassium. **Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis**, with low potassium during attacks, occurs mainly in young men, particularly of East Asian heritage, and is triggered by carbohydrate-rich meals or rest after exercise. Potassium is replaced cautiously, because the body's potassium is not depleted but shifted into cells, and rebound hyperkalaemia can follow; the thyrotoxicosis is treated (see [[endocrine/thyroid]]).

::: example Difficulty getting up from the floor {#ex-myositis}
A 57-year-old woman has had three months of increasing difficulty climbing stairs, getting out of the bath and brushing her hair. She has a rash on her upper eyelids and red, scaly patches over her knuckles. She has also become breathless on exertion. She has no sensory symptoms; reflexes are normal. CK is 4,800 U/L (normal under about 200). Her chest has fine crackles at both bases.
::: solution
1. **Localise.** Symmetrical proximal weakness with normal sensation and reflexes, and a very high CK: a **myopathy**.
2. **Diagnosis.** Heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules: **dermatomyositis**. Breathlessness and crackles suggest **interstitial lung disease**, which can coexist (especially with antisynthetase or anti-MDA5 antibodies).
3. **Tests.** Myositis-specific antibodies; MRI of the thigh muscles (to show inflammation and guide biopsy); EMG; muscle (or skin) biopsy; high-resolution CT of the chest and lung function tests; swallowing assessment; and a **cancer screen** appropriate for her age and sex (for example CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis, mammography, and ovarian assessment), because dermatomyositis in adults is associated with malignancy, especially in the first few years.
4. **Treatment principles.** Corticosteroids with a steroid-sparing agent, chosen with the respiratory team because of the lung disease; IVIG for refractory disease or severe swallowing problems. With long-term steroids: bone protection, monitoring of glucose and blood pressure, and pneumocystis prophylaxis when combined immunosuppression is used. Physiotherapy once inflammation is controlled.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 61-year-old man who smokes has had three months of difficulty rising from a chair, a dry mouth and erectile dysfunction. His knee reflexes are absent at rest but appear after 10 seconds of maximal quadriceps contraction. Eye movements are normal. Which test is most likely to reveal the underlying cause?
- [ ] Acetylcholine receptor antibodies
- [ ] Creatine kinase
- [x] CT of the chest
- [ ] Muscle biopsy
::: solution
Proximal leg weakness, autonomic symptoms and reflexes that return after brief exercise (post-exercise facilitation) are typical of **Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome**. In a smoker, about half of cases are paraneoplastic, usually from **small cell lung cancer**, which may be found months to years after the neurological syndrome, so CT of the chest (and PET-CT if negative, with repeat screening) is the key test. The diagnosis itself is confirmed with voltage-gated calcium channel antibodies and repetitive nerve stimulation showing an increment after exercise.
:::
:::

::: warning Weak patients with normal oxygen saturation
In neuromuscular respiratory failure, a normal oxygen saturation and a normal blood gas are late and falsely reassuring. Measure the vital capacity, look at the breathing pattern, check the cough and swallow, and involve critical care early. Avoid sedatives in people with weak respiratory muscles, and check every new drug in myasthenia against a list of drugs that worsen it.
:::

::: history
In 1916 Georges Guillain, Jean-Alexandre Barré and André Strohl described two French soldiers with acute paralysis and areflexia whose spinal fluid had a high protein content but no cells. Guillaume-Benjamin Duchenne described pseudohypertrophic muscular paralysis in the 1860s, and William Gowers described the sign of climbing up the legs in 1879. In 1934 Mary Walker, a doctor at St Alfege's Hospital in London, noticed that myasthenia resembled curare poisoning and showed that physostigmine, an anticholinesterase, improved it. In 1973 Jim Patrick and Jon Lindstrom produced myasthenia in rabbits by immunising them with acetylcholine receptor, establishing its autoimmune basis. Jean-Martin Charcot, Pierre Marie and Howard Tooth described hereditary peroneal muscular atrophy in 1886. Louis Kunkel and colleagues identified the Duchenne gene in 1986, and Eric Hoffman and colleagues the dystrophin protein in 1987.
:::

::: summary
- The pattern of weakness localises: anterior horn (wasting, fasciculations, no sensory loss), root (dermatome and myotome), nerve (territory or glove and stocking), junction (fatigable, ocular and bulbar), muscle (symmetrical proximal, normal sensation).
- Polyneuropathy is described by distribution, fibre type, axonal or demyelinating pathology and tempo; in the UK diabetes is the commonest cause. Mononeuritis multiplex suggests vasculitis.
- Nerve conduction studies: reduced amplitude means axonal loss; slow velocity and conduction block mean demyelination. EMG separates neurogenic from myopathic disease.
- GBS: acute symmetrical weakness with areflexia after an infection; monitor vital capacity (20–30–40 rule) and autonomic function; treat with IVIG or plasma exchange; steroids do not help. CIDP is the chronic, treatable relative.
- Myasthenia gravis: fatigable ptosis, diplopia and bulbar weakness; AChR or MuSK antibodies; pyridostigmine, immunosuppression, thymectomy; avoid drugs that worsen it. LEMS is presynaptic and often paraneoplastic.
- Myopathies cause proximal weakness with raised CK; dermatomyositis has skin signs and a cancer association; inclusion body myositis affects finger flexors and quadriceps.
- Duchenne (X-linked, dystrophin), myotonic dystrophy (CTG repeat, cardiac conduction disease) and SMA (*SMN1*, now treatable) are key genetic diseases.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Vital capacity per kilogram {level=1 check="20"}
A woman with Guillain–Barré syndrome weighs 70 kg and has a forced vital capacity of 1.4 L. What is her vital capacity in mL/kg?
::: solution
$1.4 \text{ L} = 1400$ mL; $1400 / 70 = 20$ mL/kg. This is at the threshold at which intubation is usually considered, so critical care should already be involved.
:::

::: exercise Decrement {level=1 check="30"}
During repetitive nerve stimulation at 3 Hz, the first compound muscle action potential has an amplitude of 8.0 mV and the fourth an amplitude of 5.6 mV. What is the decrement, as a whole-number percentage?
::: solution
$(8.0 - 5.6) / 8.0 \times 100 = 30\%$. A decrement of more than 10% supports a disorder of neuromuscular transmission such as myasthenia gravis.

::: exercise Where is the lesion? {level=1}
Give the most likely level (anterior horn, root, nerve, junction or muscle) for each: (a) a 45-year-old with neck pain radiating to the thumb, weak elbow flexion and a reduced biceps reflex; (b) a 30-year-old with ptosis and diplopia that worsen through the day; (c) a 70-year-old with difficulty rising from chairs and a CK of 2,000 U/L; (d) a 60-year-old with numb feet and absent ankle reflexes; (e) a 55-year-old with wasting and fasciculations of the hands and no sensory loss.
::: solution
(a) Root (C6 radiculopathy). (b) Neuromuscular junction (myasthenia gravis). (c) Muscle (myopathy). (d) Peripheral nerve (length-dependent polyneuropathy). (e) Anterior horn cell (for example motor neuron disease); multifocal motor neuropathy, a nerve disorder, is the important treatable mimic.

::: exercise Axonal or demyelinating? {level=2}
Nerve conduction studies in two patients: (a) sural sensory amplitude absent, common fibular motor amplitude 0.8 mV (low), conduction velocity 41 m/s (near normal); (b) median motor conduction velocity 28 m/s (very slow), distal latency prolonged, and the motor response falls by 60% between wrist and elbow stimulation. Interpret each, and give a likely cause.
::: solution
(a) Reduced amplitudes with preserved velocity: an **axonal** neuropathy, for example a length-dependent diabetic or alcohol-related neuropathy. (b) Marked slowing, prolonged latency and **conduction block**: an **acquired demyelinating** neuropathy, such as GBS (if acute) or CIDP (if chronic). Uniform slowing without conduction block would instead suggest an inherited demyelinating neuropathy such as CMT1A.
:::

::: exercise Myasthenia and a chest infection {level=2}
A woman with generalised myasthenia gravis, stable on pyridostigmine and azathioprine, develops a chest infection. The admitting doctor plans to prescribe clarithromycin and intravenous magnesium for an arrhythmia. What concerns you, and what would you do?
::: solution
Infection is a common trigger of myasthenic worsening and crisis. Macrolides (clarithromycin), fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides can all worsen neuromuscular transmission, and intravenous magnesium reduces acetylcholine release and can precipitate crisis. Choose an antibiotic without this effect according to local guidance (for example amoxicillin if appropriate) and seek another treatment for the arrhythmia if possible. Monitor FVC, swallowing and bulbar function closely, inform the neurology team, and be ready to give IVIG or plasma exchange if she deteriorates. Do not stop her usual myasthenia treatment.
:::

::: exercise A family with myotonic dystrophy {level=2}
A 38-year-old man with mild myotonic dystrophy type 1 (difficulty releasing his grip and early cataracts) asks about his health risks and his plans to have children. What would you discuss?
::: solution
His health: regular ECGs (and Holter monitoring if needed) because cardiac conduction disease can cause sudden death and may need a pacemaker or defibrillator; screening for diabetes; assessment of daytime sleepiness and breathing; eye review for cataracts; and an anaesthetic alert, since sedatives, opioids and some anaesthetic drugs can cause prolonged respiratory depression. His children: the condition is autosomal dominant, so each child has a 50% chance of inheriting it. Because of anticipation, children may be more severely affected than him; the severe congenital form occurs almost only when the gene is inherited from the mother, so a child of an affected father is less likely to have it. Refer to clinical genetics for counselling and to discuss options such as prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic testing. Relatives may want testing too.
:::

::: exercise Ascending weakness, but not GBS {level=3}
A 40-year-old woman develops progressive weakness of both legs over four days, with numbness up to the level of her umbilicus and urinary retention. Reflexes in the legs are brisk. A junior colleague says "ascending weakness, so it's Guillain–Barré". Explain why this is unlikely, give the differential, and say what must happen first.
::: solution
A **sensory level** on the trunk, early **urinary retention**, and **brisk** reflexes point to the **spinal cord**, not the peripheral nerves. GBS causes areflexia, rarely a sensory level, and sphincter problems only late or mildly. (In the first hours of acute cord compression or myelitis, reflexes can be reduced by spinal shock, so a sensory level and sphincter involvement are the more reliable clues.) The differential is **compressive myelopathy** (metastatic cord compression, epidural abscess, disc, haematoma) and **transverse myelitis** (MS, NMOSD, MOG antibody disease, infection, sarcoidosis), and spinal cord infarction. The first step is an **urgent MRI of the whole spine**, because compression needs treatment within hours (see [[nervous-system/motor-systems]] and [[nervous-system/neuroinflammation]]). Catheterise for retention.
:::

::: exercise A falling man with weak fingers {level=3}
A 72-year-old retired teacher has had three years of slowly progressive difficulty gripping, especially with his left hand, and frequent falls because his knees "give way". He has started to cough when eating. There is wasting of the forearm flexors and quadriceps, worse on the left; finger flexion is weak; sensation is normal; reflexes are reduced at the knees. CK is 600 U/L. He has been offered prednisolone for "polymyositis". Discuss the likely diagnosis and its management.
::: solution
The pattern of slowly progressive, asymmetric weakness of the **finger flexors** and **quadriceps** in an older man, with falls, dysphagia and a moderately raised CK, is typical of **inclusion body myositis**. It is often misdiagnosed as polymyositis. The diagnosis is supported by MRI of the muscles, EMG, anti-cN1A antibodies (present in about a third to a half) and muscle biopsy (inflammation with rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates). Unlike other forms of myositis, it does **not respond** to corticosteroids or other immunosuppression, which carry real harms, so the steroids should not be started on the basis of a presumed polymyositis. Management is supportive: physiotherapy and exercise, falls prevention and aids (for example knee braces and walking aids), occupational therapy for grip, speech and language therapy assessment of swallowing (with options such as cricopharyngeal treatments for severe dysphagia), and support. Swallowing problems and aspiration are important causes of illness and death.
:::
