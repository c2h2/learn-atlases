A person who says "my foot feels numb" has given the clinician a clue, not a diagnosis. Numbness in one foot can come from a nerve trapped at the fibular head, a disc pressing on a root, a tumour in the spinal cord, or a small stroke in the thalamus. What separates these possibilities is the *pattern*: which parts of the body are affected, which kinds of sensation are lost, and what else goes with them. The sensory pathways run along fixed routes and cross the midline at fixed places, so each site of damage leaves a recognisable signature.

This chapter follows the main sensory systems from receptor to cortex: touch and joint position, pain and temperature, vision, hearing and balance. For each it asks the same two questions. Where does the pathway run? And what does a lesion at each level look like at the bedside?

## Receptors, fibres and the speed of a signal

Every sensory pathway begins with a **receptor**, a specialised ending that converts a stimulus (pressure, stretch, heat, light, sound) into a change in membrane potential. If the change is large enough, the first-order neuron fires action potentials, and the information travels to the central nervous system coded as a train of spikes. Stronger stimuli give higher firing rates, and different receptors respond to different features of the stimulus.

The **first-order neurons** of the body have their cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglia (or, for the face, in the trigeminal ganglion). Their fibres differ in diameter and myelination, and this decides how fast they conduct.

| Fibre group | Myelinated? | Typical conduction velocity | Carries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aα (Ia, Ib) | Yes, thick | 70–120 m/s | Muscle spindle and tendon organ signals |
| Aβ | Yes | 35–75 m/s | Touch, vibration, joint position |
| Aδ | Thin myelin | 5–30 m/s | Sharp, well-localised pain; cold |
| C | No | 0.5–2 m/s | Burning, poorly localised pain; warmth; itch |

The figures are approximate ranges from standard physiology texts; what matters is the order of magnitude. A touch on the toe reaches the spinal cord in a few tens of milliseconds, while a C-fibre signal from the same toe takes about a second. This is why a stubbed toe gives a sharp "first pain" followed by a duller, spreading "second pain".

::: definition Receptive field and two-point discrimination {#def-receptive}
The **receptive field** of a sensory neuron is the area of skin (or retina, or cochlea) whose stimulation changes that neuron's firing. Small, densely packed receptive fields give fine spatial resolution. **Two-point discrimination** is the smallest distance at which two simultaneous touches are felt as two points rather than one. It is about 2–5 mm on the fingertip and several centimetres on the back, which reflects the density of receptors and the size of their cortical representation.
:::

For bedside testing, each fibre type has a simple probe. Light touch (cotton wool) and vibration (a 128 Hz tuning fork) test large fibres; **proprioception**, the sense of joint position, is tested by moving the distal joint of a finger or toe up or down while the person's eyes are closed. Pinprick (a disposable neurological pin) and temperature (a cold tuning fork) test small fibres. Testing both groups matters, because diseases often pick one: vitamin B12 deficiency affects large fibres first, while early diabetic neuropathy often affects small fibres first.

::: example How long does the message take? {#ex-conduction}
A tall man steps on a drawing pin. The distance from the sole of his foot to the lumbar spinal cord is about 1.2 m. How long does an Aβ touch signal at 60 m/s take to arrive, and how long after the sharp Aδ pain (15 m/s) does the C-fibre pain (1 m/s) arrive?
::: solution
Time is distance divided by velocity.

- Aβ: $t = 1.2 / 60 = 0.020$ s, that is 20 ms.
- Aδ: $t = 1.2 / 15 = 0.08$ s.
- C: $t = 1.2 / 1 = 1.2$ s.

The C-fibre signal arrives $1.2 - 0.08 = 1.12$ s after the Aδ signal. He feels a sharp, well-localised prick, then just over a second later a burning ache. The same arithmetic explains why a demyelinating neuropathy, which slows conduction in large fibres, can make vibration sense disappear before pinprick does: slowing and dispersion of large-fibre signals spoil the timing that vibration detection needs.
:::
:::

## Two ascending pathways

Most of the clinical localisation of sensory loss rests on one anatomical fact: the two main somatosensory pathways cross the midline at different levels.

::: definition The dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway {#def-dcml}
The **dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway** carries fine touch, vibration and proprioception. Large first-order fibres enter the spinal cord and ascend *on the same side* in the dorsal columns: the **gracile fasciculus** (from the legs, medially placed) and the **cuneate fasciculus** (from the arms, added laterally above T6). They synapse in the gracile and cuneate nuclei of the lower medulla. The second-order fibres cross the midline there and ascend as the **medial lemniscus** to the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus. Third-order neurons project to the primary somatosensory cortex in the postcentral gyrus.
:::

::: definition The spinothalamic pathway {#def-stt}
The **spinothalamic tract** carries pain, temperature and crude touch. Small first-order fibres synapse in the dorsal horn soon after entry. The second-order fibres cross within one or two segments, in the anterior white commissure in front of the central canal, and ascend in the *opposite* anterolateral column to the VPL nucleus of the thalamus, and from there to the cortex. Within the tract the fibres are laminated: sacral fibres lie outermost and cervical fibres innermost.
:::

The face has its own system. Touch from the face travels in the trigeminal nerve to the principal sensory nucleus in the pons, while pain and temperature fibres descend in the **spinal trigeminal tract** as far as the upper cervical cord before synapsing. Both then cross and join the pathways to the thalamus (VPM nucleus). This descending tract explains why a lateral medullary stroke can cause loss of pain sensation on one side of the face and the other side of the body.

::: theorem Crossing points predict the pattern {#thm-crossing}
A lesion affects a pathway on the side where that pathway runs at the level of the lesion. Because the dorsal columns cross in the medulla and the spinothalamic fibres cross within a segment or two of entry, a one-sided lesion of the spinal cord causes loss of proprioception and vibration on the **same** side below the lesion and loss of pain and temperature on the **opposite** side below the lesion. Above the medulla both pathways lie on the side opposite the body they serve, so a one-sided lesion of the brainstem above the medulla, thalamus or cortex causes loss of all modalities on the opposite side.
:::

::: proof Why the spinal cord splits the modalities
Consider a hemisection of the cord on the right at T10. Right-sided dorsal column fibres from the right leg are ascending in the right dorsal column at T10 and are cut, so the right leg loses vibration and position sense. Spinothalamic fibres from the right leg crossed to the left side below T10, so they are spared; but fibres from the left leg crossed to the right anterolateral column below T10 and are now running through the lesion, so the left leg loses pain and temperature. The corticospinal tract has already crossed in the medulla and runs in the lateral column on the side of the body it controls, so the right leg is weak with upper motor neuron signs. This combination is the **Brown-Séquard syndrome**. Because spinothalamic fibres cross over one or two segments, the upper border of the pain loss sits a little below the level of the lesion.
:::

### Patterns of sensory loss

Recognising a pattern is quicker than reasoning from first principles each time. The common patterns are:

| Pattern | Typical cause | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Glove and stocking | Length-dependent polyneuropathy (diabetes, alcohol, B12, drugs) | Starts in the toes; reaches the fingertips when the leg loss reaches about the knees; ankle reflexes lost early |
| Single peripheral nerve | Entrapment or injury (median at the wrist, ulnar at the elbow, common fibular at the fibular head) | Loss confined to that nerve's territory, with weakness of its muscles |
| Dermatome | Root compression (disc, osteophyte) or shingles | Band of loss matching a root; pain radiating along it; reflex at that level may be lost |
| Sensory level | Spinal cord lesion | Loss below a horizontal level on the trunk, usually with weakness and bladder symptoms |
| Dissociated, cape-like | Central cord lesion (syringomyelia) | Pain and temperature lost over shoulders and arms; touch and position kept |
| Dorsal columns only | B12 deficiency, tabes dorsalis, copper deficiency | Loss of position and vibration, sensory ataxia, positive Romberg test |
| Hemisensory | Thalamus, internal capsule or cortex | Whole of one side, face included |
| Crossed face and body | Lateral medulla | Pain lost on the face on one side and the body on the other |

A few dermatomes are worth knowing by heart because they anchor the rest: C6 the thumb, C7 the middle finger, C8 the little finger, T4 the nipple line, T10 the umbilicus, L4 the medial leg, L5 the dorsum of the foot and great toe, S1 the lateral border and sole of the foot. The anatomy course covers the full maps in [[anatomy/neuroanatomy]].

The **Romberg test** asks the person to stand with feet together, first with eyes open and then closed. Someone with loss of joint position sense keeps balance with the eyes open, using vision, but sways or falls when the eyes close. A positive test points to a proprioceptive (sensory) ataxia. Someone with a cerebellar ataxia is unsteady with the eyes open as well, so the test is not "positive" in the same sense.

::: warning A sensory level is an emergency until proved otherwise
A horizontal sensory level on the trunk means the spinal cord is involved. When it is accompanied by leg weakness, urinary retention or new back pain, it may be cord compression from metastatic cancer, an epidural abscess or a disc, and outcome depends on how much function remains when treatment starts. The level found on examination is often several segments *below* the actual lesion, because of the lamination of the spinothalamic tract, so imaging must cover the whole spine, not just the segment suggested by the examination. UK guidance (NICE NG234, 2023, for metastatic spinal cord compression) asks for urgent whole-spine MRI. See [[musculoskeletal/back-pain]] for the wider approach to back pain with red flags.
:::

::: example The burning hands of a dentist {#ex-syrinx}
A 34-year-old dentist notices that she burnt both hands on hot plates without feeling it. Over a year she has developed wasting of the small muscles of both hands. On examination, pinprick and temperature sensation are lost over both shoulders and the outer arms and forearms, in a cape distribution. Light touch, vibration and joint position are normal everywhere. The legs are mildly brisk with extensor plantar responses.
::: solution
Work through the features one at a time.

1. **Dissociated sensory loss.** Pain and temperature are lost, touch and position are kept. That separates the spinothalamic fibres from the dorsal columns.
2. **Distribution.** The loss covers the cervical dermatomes on both sides but does not extend down the trunk. Fibres serving those dermatomes cross in the anterior white commissure, in front of the central canal, at cervical levels. A lesion centred on the central canal of the cervical cord interrupts exactly these crossing fibres on both sides while sparing the long tracts carrying fibres from lower down.
3. **Hand wasting.** Expansion into the anterior horns at C8–T1 damages the lower motor neurons of the intrinsic hand muscles.
4. **Brisk legs.** Pressure on the corticospinal tracts gives upper motor neuron signs below.

The pattern fits a **syringomyelia**, a fluid-filled cavity in the central cord, often associated with a Chiari malformation. MRI of the whole spine and craniocervical junction is the test. Painless burns are a typical and dangerous consequence, and she needs advice on protecting her hands while the neurosurgical opinion is sought.
:::
:::

## Pain as a sensory system

Pain is not simply the activation of nociceptors. The International Association for the Study of Pain (2020) defines it as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage. Nociceptive signals are modulated at every level: in the dorsal horn (the "gate" in which large-fibre input reduces transmission of small-fibre input), by descending pathways from the periaqueductal grey and rostral ventromedial medulla, and by attention, mood and expectation.

Damage to the sensory pathways themselves can cause pain. **Neuropathic pain** arises from a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system: post-herpetic neuralgia, painful diabetic neuropathy, trigeminal neuralgia and central post-stroke pain are examples. It is often described as burning, shooting or electric, and examination may show **allodynia** (pain from a stimulus that is not normally painful, such as clothing touching the skin) and **hyperalgesia** (excessive pain from a painful stimulus). The treatment of chronic pain and of headache is covered in [[nervous-system/headache-pain]].

## Vision: from retina to cortex

The visual pathway is the best example in the whole nervous system of how anatomy predicts symptoms. Because the fibres keep a precise map of the visual field all the way to the occipital cortex, the shape of a field defect points to the site of the lesion.

::: definition The visual pathway {#def-visual}
Light falling on the **retina** is converted by rods and cones; the signal passes through bipolar cells to **retinal ganglion cells**, whose axons form the **optic nerve**. At the **optic chiasm**, fibres from the nasal half of each retina (which see the temporal visual field) cross; fibres from the temporal half stay on the same side. Behind the chiasm, each **optic tract** therefore carries information about the opposite half of the visual field from both eyes. The tracts reach the **lateral geniculate nucleus** of the thalamus, and the **optic radiations** carry the signal to the **primary visual cortex** around the calcarine fissure. The lower fibres of the radiation loop forward through the temporal lobe (**Meyer's loop**) and carry the upper field; the upper fibres run through the parietal lobe and carry the lower field.
:::

The field defects follow directly:

| Site | Defect | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| Retina or optic nerve | Loss in one eye only (central scotoma in optic neuritis; altitudinal defect in ischaemic optic neuropathy) | Optic neuritis, ischaemia, glaucoma, compression |
| Chiasm | **Bitemporal hemianopia** | Pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma, meningioma |
| Optic tract | Incongruous homonymous hemianopia | Tumour, demyelination |
| Temporal radiation (Meyer's loop) | Contralateral **superior** quadrantanopia | Temporal lobe tumour, stroke, temporal lobectomy |
| Parietal radiation | Contralateral **inferior** quadrantanopia | Stroke, tumour |
| Occipital cortex | Congruous homonymous hemianopia, often with **macular sparing** | Posterior cerebral artery stroke |

"Homonymous" means the same half of the visual field is lost in both eyes. Macular sparing in occipital strokes is often attributed to a dual blood supply of the occipital pole (from the middle as well as the posterior cerebral artery), though the explanation is debated. Testing fields by confrontation is quick but insensitive; formal perimetry is needed when the answer matters, for example for driving.

### The pupil

The pupil is controlled by two opposing autonomic pathways, and each can be damaged in a characteristic way.

- **Constriction (parasympathetic).** Light activates retinal ganglion cells whose fibres leave the optic tract for the pretectal nuclei. Each pretectal nucleus projects to *both* Edinger–Westphal nuclei, so light in one eye constricts both pupils (the direct and consensual responses). Parasympathetic fibres travel with the **oculomotor nerve (III)** to the ciliary ganglion and the sphincter pupillae.
- **Dilatation (sympathetic).** A three-neuron chain runs from the hypothalamus down the brainstem to the upper thoracic cord (T1), out to the superior cervical ganglion, and up along the carotid artery to the dilator pupillae and to Müller's muscle in the upper lid.

::: definition Relative afferent pupillary defect {#def-rapd}
A **relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)** is present when one optic nerve (or, much less often, retina) conducts less light signal than the other. It is shown by the **swinging flashlight test**: the light is moved briskly from one eye to the other. When it reaches the affected eye, both pupils *dilate* slightly, because that eye sends a weaker signal than the good eye did. Pupils are equal in size at rest, because the efferent pathways are intact. An RAPD is the most objective bedside sign of optic nerve disease.
:::

The two classic efferent problems are opposite in appearance. A **third nerve palsy** gives ptosis, an eye that rests "down and out" and, when the parasympathetic fibres on the outside of the nerve are compressed, a large unreactive pupil. A painful third nerve palsy with pupil involvement is treated as an expanding posterior communicating artery aneurysm until vascular imaging shows otherwise. **Horner syndrome**, from interruption of the sympathetic chain, gives a small pupil, mild ptosis and, depending on the site, loss of sweating on that side of the face. A new painful Horner syndrome suggests carotid dissection.

::: example A curtain over one eye {#ex-visual-loss}
A 74-year-old woman of Caribbean heritage reports that the vision in her right eye "went dark like a blind coming down" this morning and has not recovered. For three weeks she has had a new headache, pain in the jaw when chewing, and aching shoulders. Visual acuity is counting fingers in the right eye and 6/9 in the left. There is a right RAPD.
::: solution
1. **Localise.** Loss in one eye with an RAPD means a prechiasmal lesion of the right retina or optic nerve.
2. **Time course.** Sudden, painless, persistent loss suggests a vascular cause: central retinal artery occlusion or anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy.
3. **Context.** In someone over 50, new headache, jaw claudication and shoulder girdle aching point strongly to **giant cell arteritis**, a vasculitis of medium and large arteries that can occlude the posterior ciliary arteries or the central retinal artery.
4. **Action.** This is an emergency because the other eye is at risk within days. Management follows local pathways (in the UK, the British Society for Rheumatology 2020 guideline): high-dose glucocorticoids are started immediately on clinical suspicion, without waiting for tests; inflammatory markers are taken; and urgent ophthalmology and a fast-track vasculitis assessment with temporal artery ultrasound or biopsy are arranged. Treatment begun the same day does not usually restore the lost vision, but it protects the remaining eye.

A detached retina is the main alternative for a "curtain", usually preceded by flashes and floaters; it is painless and needs same-day ophthalmic assessment. The pupil reaction and the fundus distinguish the two.
:::
:::

Not all sudden visual problems come from the nerve. **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** gives a painful red eye, haloes around lights, a fixed mid-dilated pupil, nausea and a rock-hard eye, and needs emergency treatment to lower pressure. **Papilloedema**, swelling of both optic discs from raised intracranial pressure, usually leaves acuity normal at first; it is a sign to be found, not a symptom, and it prompts urgent brain imaging. Optic neuritis, the painful loss of vision with an RAPD that is often the first event of multiple sclerosis, is covered in [[nervous-system/neuroinflammation]].

::: quiz
A 52-year-old man has had headaches for months. Confrontation testing suggests he cannot see the outer half of the field in either eye. Where is the lesion most likely to be?
- [ ] The left optic nerve
- [x] The optic chiasm
- [ ] The right optic tract
- [ ] The left occipital cortex
::: solution
Loss of both temporal fields (bitemporal hemianopia) means the crossing nasal retinal fibres are damaged, and those are together only at the chiasm. A pituitary adenoma pressing upward is the classic cause, so the next steps are formal perimetry, pituitary hormone tests and MRI of the pituitary region (see [[endocrine/hypothalamus-pituitary]]). An optic tract lesion gives a homonymous defect, and an optic nerve lesion affects one eye.
:::

## Hearing

Sound waves move the tympanic membrane and the ossicles, which match the impedance of air to the fluid of the inner ear. In the **cochlea**, the basilar membrane is tuned along its length, high frequencies at the base and low frequencies at the apex, and inner hair cells convert its movement into signals in the cochlear nerve. The central auditory pathway has many crossings, so a one-sided lesion above the cochlear nuclei rarely causes one-sided deafness. One-sided hearing loss therefore points to the ear or the cochlear nerve.

::: definition Conductive and sensorineural hearing loss {#def-hearing}
**Conductive hearing loss** is caused by a problem that stops sound reaching the cochlea: wax, otitis media with effusion, a perforated drum, or otosclerosis. **Sensorineural hearing loss** is caused by damage to the cochlea or cochlear nerve: age-related loss (presbycusis), noise, ototoxic drugs, Ménière disease, infection, or a vestibular schwannoma. The two tuning fork tests separate them. In the **Rinne test**, a 512 Hz fork is held on the mastoid (bone conduction) and then beside the ear (air conduction); normally air conduction is louder ("Rinne positive"). In the **Weber test**, the fork is placed on the forehead and the person says where the sound is heard.
:::

| | Rinne in the affected ear | Weber |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Air > bone (positive) | Central |
| Conductive loss | Bone > air (negative) | Heard in the affected ear |
| Sensorineural loss | Air > bone (positive, both reduced) | Heard in the better ear |

Two presentations need urgent action. **Sudden sensorineural hearing loss** (developing over 72 hours or less) needs same-day or next-day assessment by an ear, nose and throat service, because early corticosteroid treatment is offered in many guidelines. **Asymmetric sensorineural loss or one-sided tinnitus** needs MRI of the internal auditory meatus to look for a vestibular schwannoma.

## Balance and vertigo

The vestibular system detects head movement and position. Three **semicircular canals** in each inner ear sense rotation; the **utricle and saccule** sense linear acceleration and gravity. Their signals go to the vestibular nuclei in the brainstem and to the cerebellum, and from there to the eye muscles. The best-known reflex is the **vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR)**: when the head turns, the eyes turn the opposite way at the same speed, so that the image stays still on the retina.

**Vertigo** is an illusion of movement, usually spinning. It is useful to sort it by timing:

- **Brief episodes triggered by head position**, lasting seconds: **benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)**, caused by loose otoconia in a semicircular canal (usually the posterior). The Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre provokes it, and a repositioning manoeuvre (Epley) often cures it.
- **Recurrent spontaneous attacks lasting minutes to hours**: vestibular migraine; Ménière disease (with fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness); and, importantly, posterior circulation transient ischaemic attacks.
- **A single prolonged episode lasting days**, the **acute vestibular syndrome**: continuous vertigo, nausea, nystagmus and unsteadiness. Most cases are **vestibular neuritis**, but a minority are **posterior circulation strokes** in the cerebellum or brainstem, and these can look identical at first glance.

::: definition The HINTS examination {#def-hints}
**HINTS** is a three-part bedside examination for people with an acute vestibular syndrome who have nystagmus at the time of examination. Each part has a "peripheral" and a "central" answer.

1. **Head Impulse test.** The examiner turns the head quickly by about 15 degrees while the person fixes on the examiner's nose. In vestibular neuritis the VOR on the affected side fails, and the eyes are dragged off target and make a catch-up saccade (an *abnormal* test, which is reassuring). In a cerebellar stroke the VOR is intact, so a *normal* head impulse test is the worrying result.
2. **Nystagmus.** Peripheral nystagmus beats in one direction whichever way the person looks. Nystagmus that changes direction with gaze, or is purely vertical or torsional, is central.
3. **Test of Skew.** The eyes are covered alternately; a vertical correction movement (**skew deviation**) is central.

Any central sign, or a new hearing loss ("HINTS plus"), should be treated as a possible stroke. HINTS is not designed for people without nystagmus, for brief positional vertigo or for people with other clear neurological signs.
:::

The value of HINTS depends on who performs it. In the studies that defined it, experienced neuro-otologists and neurologists achieved a sensitivity for stroke well above 95%, better than early MRI, which misses a proportion of small posterior circulation infarcts in the first 48 hours. A 2020 systematic review found much lower accuracy when the test was performed by emergency physicians without specific training. ::: example Vertigo after the gym {#ex-hints}
A 61-year-old man with hypertension and type 2 diabetes woke with severe spinning vertigo, vomiting and inability to walk. Twelve hours later he still has continuous vertigo. He has left-beating nystagmus on looking left and right-beating nystagmus on looking right. The head impulse test is normal in both directions. There is no skew deviation. He cannot sit up without support. A CT scan of the head is normal.
::: solution
1. **Syndrome.** Continuous vertigo for hours with nystagmus, nausea and unsteadiness is an acute vestibular syndrome, so HINTS is appropriate.
2. **Head impulse.** Normal. In vestibular neuritis it would be abnormal on the affected side. This is a central sign.
3. **Nystagmus.** Direction-changing with gaze: a second central sign.
4. **Truncal ataxia.** Being unable to sit unsupported is severe for vestibular neuritis and suggests a cerebellar lesion.
5. **Normal CT.** CT is poor at showing early posterior fossa infarcts, so it does not exclude stroke.

The conclusion is a probable cerebellar or brainstem stroke. He needs urgent stroke assessment with MRI including diffusion-weighted imaging and vascular imaging of the vertebrobasilar system. Cerebellar infarcts can swell and compress the fourth ventricle and brainstem over the next few days, so he should be monitored in a stroke unit with access to neurosurgery. The vascular risk factors raise the prior probability; the examination raised it much further. See [[nervous-system/stroke]].
:::
:::

## Smell and taste

Smell is the only sense that reaches the cortex without relaying first in the thalamus. Olfactory receptor neurons in the nasal roof send axons through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb. Loss of smell (**anosmia**) follows head injury (shearing of these axons), upper respiratory infections (including COVID-19), nasal polyps and sinus disease. It is also an early, non-motor feature of Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease, sometimes appearing years before diagnosis. Taste from the front two-thirds of the tongue travels in the chorda tympani branch of the facial nerve, and from the back third in the glossopharyngeal nerve; much of what people call "taste" is in fact smell.

::: example A numb patch on the thigh {#ex-meralgia}
A 45-year-old man who has gained weight and started wearing a heavy tool belt describes a burning, numb patch on the outer front of his right thigh, worse when standing. Strength, reflexes and the rest of the examination are normal. Light touch and pinprick are reduced in an oval area on the anterolateral thigh, not crossing the knee.
::: solution
1. **Pure sensory loss without weakness or reflex change** means a purely sensory nerve, or the sensory part of a root without motor involvement.
2. **Distribution.** An oval area on the anterolateral thigh that respects the knee matches the territory of the **lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh** (lateral femoral cutaneous nerve), not a dermatome: an L2 or L3 root lesion would extend medially, and an L3 or L4 root lesion would usually reduce the knee reflex.
3. **Mechanism.** The nerve passes under or through the inguinal ligament near the anterior superior iliac spine, where tight belts, weight gain or pregnancy can compress it.

The diagnosis is **meralgia paraesthetica**. It is benign; management is explanation and removing the compression. Investigations are needed only if there is weakness, reflex change, progression, or a reason to suspect a pelvic mass.
:::
:::

::: history
Charles Bell (1811) and François Magendie (1822) established that the dorsal spinal roots are sensory and the ventral roots motor, the first clear separation of sensory and motor pathways. Charles-Édouard Brown-Séquard described the effects of cutting half the spinal cord in animals and patients in the 1840s and 1850s, explaining the crossed loss that now bears his name. Moritz Romberg described the test of standing with eyes closed in tabes dorsalis in his textbook of the 1840s. In 1898 Charles Sherrington mapped the dermatomes in monkeys, and Henry Head's studies of herpes zoster with Alfred Campbell (1900) helped map them in people. Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall's gate control theory (1965) changed how pain was understood, and the HINTS examination was described by Jorge Kattah, David Newman-Toker and colleagues in 2009.
:::

Vascular syndromes that combine sensory and visual loss are in [[nervous-system/stroke]], and optic neuritis in [[nervous-system/neuroinflammation]]. ::: summary
- Sensory fibres differ in size and speed: large myelinated fibres carry touch, vibration and position quickly; thin and unmyelinated fibres carry pain and temperature slowly.
- The dorsal columns ascend on the same side and cross in the medulla; the spinothalamic tract crosses within one or two segments of entry. A one-sided cord lesion therefore gives ipsilateral loss of position sense and contralateral loss of pain sense (Brown-Séquard).
- Patterns localise: glove and stocking (polyneuropathy), nerve territory, dermatome, sensory level (cord), cape-like dissociated loss (central cord), hemisensory loss (thalamus or cortex), crossed face and body (lateral medulla).
- A sensory level with weakness or bladder symptoms is a spinal emergency; image the whole spine.
- Visual field defects localise along the pathway: one eye (nerve), bitemporal (chiasm), homonymous (behind the chiasm), superior quadrant (temporal), inferior quadrant (parietal), macular sparing (occipital).
- An RAPD is the key sign of optic nerve disease. A painful pupil-involving third nerve palsy means aneurysm until proved otherwise; a painful Horner syndrome suggests carotid dissection.
- Rinne and Weber separate conductive from sensorineural hearing loss; sudden or asymmetric sensorineural loss needs urgent assessment.
- In acute vestibular syndrome, a normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus or skew deviation suggests stroke; HINTS is only as good as the examiner.
:::

Using the values in [[#ex-conduction]] (distance 1.2 m, Aδ 15 m/s, C 1 m/s), by how many seconds does C-fibre pain lag behind Aδ pain? Give the answer to two decimal places.
::: solution
C-fibre time $1.2/1 = 1.2$ s; Aδ time $1.2/15 = 0.08$ s. The difference is $1.2 - 0.08 = 1.12$ s.

::: exercise Name the field defect {level=1}
Name the visual field defect expected from: (a) a pituitary macroadenoma pressing on the chiasm from below; (b) an infarct in the right occipital lobe; (c) a tumour in the left temporal lobe; (d) optic neuritis of the left eye.
::: solution
(a) Bitemporal hemianopia, often starting in the upper temporal quadrants because the adenoma presses from below on the inferior crossing fibres. (b) Left homonymous hemianopia, often with macular sparing. (c) Right superior homonymous quadrantanopia, from damage to Meyer's loop. (d) Reduced acuity and a central scotoma in the left eye, with reduced colour vision and a left RAPD.

::: exercise Tuning forks {level=2}
A 30-year-old woman has gradually worsening hearing in her left ear. Rinne is negative on the left and positive on the right. Weber lateralises to the left. (a) What type of hearing loss does she have and in which ear? (b) Give two possible causes. (c) How would the results differ if she had a left vestibular schwannoma?
::: solution
(a) Left conductive hearing loss: bone conduction is louder than air conduction on the left, and Weber is heard in the affected ear because the conductive block reduces masking by ambient noise. (b) Wax, otitis media with effusion, a tympanic membrane perforation, or otosclerosis (which often presents in young adults and can worsen in pregnancy). (c) A schwannoma causes sensorineural loss: Rinne would be positive on both sides, and Weber would lateralise to the right, the better-hearing ear. One-sided sensorineural loss would prompt MRI of the internal auditory meatus.
:::

::: exercise The spinal cord stab wound {level=2}
A 22-year-old man is stabbed in the back. He has weakness of the left leg with brisk reflexes and an upgoing plantar, loss of vibration and position sense in the left leg, and loss of pinprick and temperature in the right leg up to just below the umbilicus. At what level and on which side is the lesion? Explain each finding.
::: solution
The lesion is a left hemisection of the cord at about T9–T10 (the umbilicus is T10, and the spinothalamic level sits one or two segments below the true lesion). Left leg weakness with upper motor neuron signs: the left lateral corticospinal tract (already crossed in the medulla) is cut. Left loss of vibration and position: the left dorsal column carries fibres from the left leg uncrossed. Right loss of pain and temperature: fibres from the right leg crossed to the left anterolateral column below the lesion and are cut there. This is the Brown-Séquard syndrome.
:::

::: exercise Peripheral or central vertigo? {level=2}
A 48-year-old woman has had continuous vertigo and vomiting for a day after a cold. She has right-beating nystagmus in all directions of gaze, a catch-up saccade when the head is turned quickly to the left, no skew deviation, normal hearing, and she can walk with some veering to the left. Interpret each finding and give the most likely diagnosis.
::: solution
Continuous vertigo for a day with nystagmus is an acute vestibular syndrome, so HINTS applies. The abnormal head impulse to the left shows a failing left vestibulo-ocular reflex (a peripheral sign). Unidirectional nystagmus beating away from the affected side (right-beating) is peripheral. No skew deviation and no new hearing loss. She can walk, veering towards the affected side. All features point to left vestibular neuritis. Management is supportive, with short-term antiemetics if needed and early vestibular rehabilitation; prolonged vestibular sedatives delay central compensation. She should be told to return if new neurological symptoms appear, because HINTS performance depends on the examiner and no test is perfect.
:::

::: exercise Predictive value of HINTS {level=3 check="83"}
In a group of 1,000 people with an acute vestibular syndrome, 100 have a stroke. HINTS is performed by examiners whose sensitivity is 83% and specificity 44%. (a) How many strokes are missed? (b) How many people without stroke have a "central" result? (c) What percentage of the 100 strokes are correctly identified? Give (c) as a whole number.
::: solution
(a) Missed strokes = $100 \times (1 - 0.83) = 17$. (b) False positives = $900 \times (1 - 0.44) = 504$. (c) Correctly identified = $100 \times 0.83 = 83$, that is 83% of strokes (which is simply the sensitivity). The positive predictive value is $83 / (83 + 504) \approx 14\%$: most "central" results are false alarms, and 17 strokes are still missed. The point of the exercise is that a test which performs well in expert hands can perform poorly elsewhere; training, and imaging for those with persistent concern, matter.
:::

::: exercise A patient with numb feet and falls {level=3}
A 70-year-old woman who has taken metformin for 12 years and a proton pump inhibitor for 8 years has numb feet, unsteadiness in the dark and two falls. Vibration is absent to the knees and joint position is lost at the toes. Romberg is positive. Ankle reflexes are absent but knee reflexes are brisk and both plantars are extensor. (a) What combination of lesions explains these signs? (b) What is the most likely cause and why is she at risk? (c) Outline the investigations and the principles of treatment, and explain why it is urgent.
::: solution
(a) Absent ankle reflexes with sensory loss suggest a peripheral neuropathy; brisk knees and extensor plantars suggest corticospinal tract involvement; loss of vibration and position with a positive Romberg indicates dorsal column dysfunction. The combination of dorsal column, corticospinal and peripheral nerve involvement is **subacute combined degeneration of the cord**. (b) Vitamin B12 deficiency. Both long-term metformin and acid suppression reduce B12 absorption; pernicious anaemia should also be considered. (c) Full blood count (macrocytosis may be absent), serum B12 and, where results are borderline, methylmalonic acid or homocysteine; intrinsic factor antibodies; folate and copper (copper deficiency causes an identical myelopathy). Spinal MRI may show signal change in the dorsal columns. Treatment is prompt intramuscular hydroxocobalamin according to the current formulary and local guidance, without waiting for every result if the clinical picture is clear, and folic acid must not be given alone first because it may worsen the neurological damage. Neurological recovery depends on how early treatment begins, so it is urgent. Falls prevention and a review of the long-term medicines complete the plan (see [[geriatrics-palliative/falls]]).
:::
