The brain and spinal cord are protected by the blood–brain barrier and were once thought to be largely hidden from the immune system. In fact immune cells patrol the central nervous system constantly, and when the immune response goes wrong, or when a pathogen gets through, the consequences can be severe. Inflammation of the nervous system ranges from multiple sclerosis, a lifelong condition of young adults, to bacterial meningitis, which can kill within hours.

This chapter covers multiple sclerosis and its diagnosis and treatment, optic neuritis and transverse myelitis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder and MOG antibody disease, autoimmune encephalitis, and the infections of the brain and meninges. UK management of multiple sclerosis follows NICE NG220 (2022), and of bacterial meningitis NICE NG240 (2024). Management always follows current local guidance.

## Multiple sclerosis

**Multiple sclerosis (MS)** is a chronic immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system in which inflammation causes patches of **demyelination**, with axonal loss and gliosis, scattered through the brain, optic nerves and spinal cord. It is the commonest cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in young adults. More than 130,000 people in the UK have MS. It usually starts between 20 and 40 years, and women are affected two to three times as often as men.

Risk reflects genes and environment. The strongest genetic factor is the **HLA-DRB1\*15:01** allele; more than 200 other variants each add a small risk. Environmental risks include **Epstein–Barr virus** infection (in a 2022 study of US military personnel, the risk of MS rose about 32-fold after EBV infection, and MS almost never occurred without it), low vitamin D and little sunlight exposure (MS is more common further from the equator), smoking, and obesity in adolescence.

::: proof How inflammation produces symptoms and then disability
In a relapse, autoreactive T and B lymphocytes cross the blood–brain barrier and start an inflammatory reaction around small veins, attracting macrophages that strip myelin from axons. Demyelinated axons conduct slowly or not at all, because the sodium channels needed for saltatory conduction are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier and the internodal membrane cannot sustain an action potential. This explains the symptoms of a relapse. Recovery follows as inflammation settles, sodium channels redistribute along the demyelinated segment, and some remyelination occurs. Partly demyelinated axons have a reduced safety margin for conduction, so a small rise in temperature (exercise, a hot bath, fever) can block them and temporarily bring back old symptoms (**Uhthoff's phenomenon**); stretching a demyelinated dorsal column in the neck can cause an electric sensation down the spine (**Lhermitte's sign**). Over years, chronic inflammation trapped behind the blood–brain barrier, loss of trophic support from myelin, and mitochondrial failure cause axons to degenerate. Axonal loss, not demyelination, is the main basis of permanent disability and of progressive disease.
:::

### Clinical course and presentations

Most people (about 85%) start with **relapsing–remitting MS**: episodes of new or worsening symptoms (**relapses**) developing over days, lasting at least 24 hours in the absence of fever or infection, and then improving over weeks to months. Many later enter a **secondary progressive** phase of gradually increasing disability with or without relapses. About 10–15% have **primary progressive MS**, with steadily increasing disability from onset, often as a spastic paraparesis in middle age. The first episode of demyelination is called a **clinically isolated syndrome**.

Typical first presentations are:

- **Optic neuritis**: subacute loss of vision in one eye over days, with pain on eye movement, reduced colour vision and a relative afferent pupillary defect (see below).
- **Partial myelitis**: numbness or tingling rising from the feet or in a band around the trunk, Lhermitte's sign, leg weakness or bladder urgency.
- **Brainstem syndromes**: double vision, especially from an **internuclear ophthalmoplegia** (failure of the adducting eye on lateral gaze, with nystagmus of the abducting eye, from a lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus); vertigo; facial numbness; trigeminal neuralgia in a young person.

Fatigue, cognitive difficulties, depression, bladder and bowel problems, sexual dysfunction, pain and spasticity are common in established disease and often matter more to people than visible disability.

### Diagnosis

::: definition Dissemination in space and time {#def-dis-dit}
The diagnosis of MS requires evidence that the disease affects more than one part of the central nervous system (**dissemination in space**) and is ongoing rather than a single event (**dissemination in time**), together with exclusion of better explanations. The **McDonald criteria** set out how MRI and cerebrospinal fluid can provide this evidence.

- **Dissemination in space** is shown by typical lesions in at least two of the characteristic regions: periventricular, cortical or juxtacortical, infratentorial, spinal cord, and, since the 2024 revision (published 2025), the **optic nerve**.
- **Dissemination in time** is shown by a new clinical attack, a new lesion on follow-up MRI, or the simultaneous presence of enhancing (active) and non-enhancing lesions. In the 2017 criteria, **CSF-specific oligoclonal bands** could substitute for dissemination in time; the 2024 revision also accepts a raised **kappa free light chain index** and, in some circumstances, allows diagnosis without dissemination in time when lesions are found in at least four of the five regions.

The 2024 revision also uses supportive MRI markers that increase specificity, the **central vein sign** (a vein running through the centre of a lesion) and **paramagnetic rim lesions**, and applies the same framework to progressive disease.
:::

Dissemination in space and time are not the only requirements: the clinical picture must be typical, and "no better explanation" is essential. Many conditions cause white matter lesions on MRI, including small vessel disease, migraine, and other inflammatory disorders (neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, MOG antibody disease, sarcoidosis, lupus, vasculitis), infections and vitamin B12 deficiency. Misdiagnosis of MS is a recognised problem, usually through over-reading non-specific MRI changes.

::: example A blurred eye before exams {#ex-on}
A 27-year-old woman of white British heritage, a postgraduate student, has had three days of blurred vision in her right eye and pain when she moves it. Colours look "washed out" in that eye. Acuity is 6/24 right and 6/6 left. There is a right relative afferent pupillary defect. The optic disc looks normal. Four years ago she had a month of tingling in both feet that went up to her waist and settled on its own; she did not see a doctor.
::: solution
1. **Localise.** Painful subacute monocular visual loss with reduced colour vision, an RAPD and a normal disc: **optic neuritis** (retrobulbar, so the disc looks normal; "the patient sees nothing and the doctor sees nothing").
2. **Typical or atypical?** Young woman, pain on eye movement, unilateral, moderate loss: typical demyelinating optic neuritis. Atypical features that would suggest NMOSD, MOG antibody disease or another cause include very severe loss, bilateral simultaneous involvement, no pain, marked disc swelling, failure to start improving within three weeks, and age over 50.
3. **Previous episode.** Ascending tingling to the waist four years ago suggests a previous partial myelitis: a second, earlier event in another part of the nervous system.
4. **Investigations.** MRI of the brain (and orbits, and usually spine) with contrast. Lesions in typical locations would show dissemination in space, and the history of two separate events in different places would establish dissemination in time clinically. Blood tests to exclude mimics, aquaporin-4 and MOG antibodies if any atypical feature is present, and CSF for oligoclonal bands if the diagnosis remains uncertain.
5. **Treatment principles.** The Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial (1992) showed that high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone speeds recovery but does not change the final visual outcome, so steroids are optional for typical optic neuritis. Most people recover good vision over weeks to months. If MS is confirmed, she is referred to the MS team to discuss disease-modifying therapy. Her exams can still go ahead, with adjustments.
:::

### Treating MS

Treatment has three parts.

**Relapses.** Not every relapse needs treatment. When a relapse is disabling, a short course of high-dose corticosteroids (oral or intravenous methylprednisolone, according to local guidance) speeds recovery without changing the long-term outcome. Infection, especially urinary infection, must be excluded first, because infection can cause a **pseudo-relapse** (worsening of old symptoms from fever).

**Disease-modifying therapies (DMTs)** reduce the frequency of relapses and new MRI lesions and slow accumulation of disability in relapsing disease. They act by modulating or depleting parts of the immune system and are prescribed through specialist MS services according to NHS England commissioning policy. In broad terms:

- **moderate-efficacy** drugs: interferon beta, glatiramer acetate, teriflunomide, dimethyl fumarate;
- **high-efficacy** drugs: natalizumab (blocks lymphocyte entry to the brain; carries a risk of **progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy** from JC virus, stratified by JC virus antibody status), the anti-CD20 antibodies ocrelizumab and ofatumumab, cladribine, alemtuzumab and sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulators such as fingolimod;
- **autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** for highly active disease in selected centres.

There is a trend towards earlier use of high-efficacy treatment. In progressive disease the options are more limited: ocrelizumab for early primary progressive MS with features of activity, and siponimod for active secondary progressive MS. Teratogenicity and the safety of each drug in pregnancy and breastfeeding are discussed before starting.

**Symptom management and rehabilitation** matter at every stage: physiotherapy and antispasticity drugs (baclofen, gabapentin, tizanidine, and botulinum toxin for focal spasticity), bladder management (antimuscarinics, intermittent self-catheterisation), treatment of fatigue (energy management, exercise, cognitive behavioural therapy), and support for mood, cognition, employment and sexual health. NICE NG220 advises supervised exercise and vitamin D supplementation only for deficiency, not as treatment for MS.

::: example Interpreting a trial result {#ex-arr}
In a trial of a new DMT in relapsing–remitting MS, the **annualised relapse rate** (total relapses divided by total patient-years of follow-up) was 0.50 in the comparator group and 0.30 in the treatment group over two years. What is the relative reduction in relapse rate, and what does it mean for a typical patient?
::: solution
1. **Relative reduction**: $(0.50 - 0.30)/0.50 = 0.40$, a 40% relative reduction.
2. **Absolute difference**: $0.50 - 0.30 = 0.20$ relapses per person per year, so on average one relapse prevented for every five patient-years of treatment.
3. **Interpretation**: a person who would otherwise have about one relapse every two years would have about one every three years and four months. Relapse rate is only one outcome; trials also measure new MRI lesions and **confirmed disability progression**, which matters more to most people. The decision also depends on the person's disease activity, side effects (including infection and monitoring burden), pregnancy plans and preferences. See [[epidemiology-ebm/randomised-trials]].
:::
:::

## Other inflammatory disorders of the central nervous system

### Transverse myelitis

**Transverse myelitis** is inflammation of the spinal cord, causing weakness, a sensory level and bladder disturbance developing over hours to days. Compression must be excluded first with urgent MRI. Causes include MS (usually a short, partial lesion), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (a **longitudinally extensive** lesion spanning three or more vertebral segments), MOG antibody disease, infections (varicella zoster, HIV, syphilis), sarcoidosis and systemic lupus. The approach to a cord syndrome is in [[nervous-system/motor-systems]].

### Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder and MOG antibody disease

::: definition Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder {#def-nmosd}
**Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD)** is an autoimmune astrocytopathy, usually caused by antibodies against **aquaporin-4 (AQP4)**, the water channel on astrocyte foot processes. Its core clinical characteristics (international consensus criteria, 2015) are optic neuritis (often severe or bilateral), acute myelitis (typically longitudinally extensive), the **area postrema syndrome** (unexplained hiccups or nausea and vomiting), and acute brainstem, diencephalic or cerebral syndromes. It is much more common in women than in men; relative to MS, it accounts for a larger share of demyelinating disease in people of East Asian and African ancestry; and it is associated with other autoimmune diseases.
:::

The distinction from MS matters because attacks of NMOSD are often severe with poor recovery, so prevention is essential, and some MS drugs (interferon beta, natalizumab, fingolimod) can make NMOSD worse. Acute attacks are treated with high-dose steroids and early plasma exchange. Preventive options include rituximab and the newer targeted antibodies (eculizumab and ravulizumab against complement C5, inebilizumab against CD19, satralizumab against the interleukin-6 receptor).

**MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD)** is caused by antibodies against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein. It is a separate disease from both MS and NMOSD and is diagnosed with criteria published in 2023. It is common in children, in whom it may present as **acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)**, a monophasic inflammatory illness with encephalopathy and widespread lesions, often after an infection. Adults typically have optic neuritis (often bilateral with disc swelling) or myelitis. Many people have a single episode.

### Autoimmune encephalitis

Antibodies against neuronal surface proteins cause treatable encephalitis. The best known is **anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis**, which typically affects young women and children: a psychiatric prodrome (anxiety, psychosis, behavioural change), then seizures, abnormal movements (especially of the mouth and face), reduced consciousness and autonomic instability. In young women it is associated with **ovarian teratoma**. **Anti-LGI1 encephalitis** affects older adults, especially men, with memory loss, hyponatraemia and **faciobrachial dystonic seizures** (brief stereotyped jerks of the face and arm). Diagnosis relies on antibody testing of serum and CSF, MRI, EEG and a search for tumours. Treatment is immunotherapy (steroids, intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange, then rituximab or cyclophosphamide if needed) and tumour removal. Autoimmune encephalitis should be considered in anyone with new psychosis plus neurological features, and in suspected viral encephalitis when HSV tests are negative.

::: example Psychosis that was not psychiatric {#ex-nmdar}
A 24-year-old woman of Black African heritage, with no previous mental illness, is admitted to a psychiatric unit after two weeks of insomnia, agitation and paranoid beliefs. Three days later she has a generalised seizure. She then becomes less responsive, with repetitive chewing and grimacing movements, fluctuating blood pressure and episodes of slow heart rate. A friend mentions she had a flu-like illness a month ago.
::: solution
1. **Red flags for an organic cause.** New psychosis with no previous history, followed by seizures, reduced consciousness, abnormal orofacial movements and autonomic instability. A primary psychiatric illness does not explain these features.
2. **Differential diagnosis.** Viral encephalitis (especially HSV), **anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis**, other autoimmune encephalitides, neuroleptic malignant syndrome if she has received antipsychotics, drug intoxication or withdrawal, and metabolic causes.
3. **Immediate steps.** Transfer to a medical or neurology ward; start intravenous aciclovir until HSV is excluded; MRI of the brain (often normal or showing mild changes in anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis); lumbar puncture (often a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, sometimes oligoclonal bands); EEG (diffuse slowing, sometimes an "extreme delta brush" pattern); and anti-NMDA receptor antibodies in **CSF** as well as serum, because CSF testing is more sensitive and specific.
4. **Look for a tumour.** Pelvic ultrasound or MRI for an ovarian teratoma.
5. **Treatment principles.** Start first-line immunotherapy (high-dose steroids, intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange) as soon as the diagnosis is likely, without waiting weeks for confirmation; remove any teratoma; second-line therapy (rituximab or cyclophosphamide) if there is no improvement. Many people need intensive care for weeks, but most recover well, often over months to years, especially with early treatment. Antipsychotics are used cautiously because these patients appear prone to severe reactions.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 34-year-old woman of Japanese heritage has had three weeks of intractable hiccups and vomiting, followed by weakness of both legs and urinary retention. MRI shows a spinal cord lesion extending from T2 to T9. Which is the most useful blood test?
- [ ] Oligoclonal bands
- [ ] Vitamin B12
- [x] Aquaporin-4 antibodies
- [ ] Anti-NMDA receptor antibodies
::: solution
Intractable hiccups and vomiting (area postrema syndrome) followed by a longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (more than three vertebral segments) are characteristic of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, which is diagnosed with serum aquaporin-4 antibodies. Oligoclonal bands are found in CSF, not serum, and are typical of MS rather than NMOSD. B12 deficiency causes a slowly progressive dorsal column myelopathy, and anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis presents with psychiatric symptoms, seizures and dyskinesias.
:::
:::

## Infections of the brain and meninges

### Bacterial meningitis

**Bacterial meningitis** is inflammation of the meninges caused by bacteria. In adults in the UK the main causes are *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Neisseria meningitidis*; *Listeria monocytogenes* is important in people over 60, in pregnancy and in immunosuppression; in neonates, group B streptococcus and *Escherichia coli*. Vaccination (Hib, meningococcal and pneumococcal; see [[public-health/vaccination-programmes]]) has transformed its epidemiology.

The classic features are fever, headache, neck stiffness and altered mental state, but fewer than half of adults have all of these, and young children and older people often have non-specific symptoms. **Meningococcal disease** can cause septicaemia with a non-blanching purpuric rash and shock, with or without meningitis.

::: theorem Antibiotics before investigations {#thm-antibiotics}
In suspected bacterial meningitis or meningococcal disease, mortality and neurological damage rise with every hour of delay in effective treatment. Antibiotics are therefore given immediately, and investigations must not delay them. In UK practice (NICE NG240, 2024), people with suspected meningococcal disease in the community receive parenteral benzylpenicillin before transfer if this does not delay transfer; in hospital, intravenous **ceftriaxone** (with amoxicillin added when *Listeria* is a risk) is given within an hour of arrival, together with **dexamethasone** for suspected or confirmed bacterial meningitis in adults and older children, which reduces hearing loss and, in pneumococcal meningitis, mortality. Lumbar puncture is performed if it can be done promptly and safely, ideally before antibiotics but never at the cost of delaying them; blood cultures and PCR remain informative after antibiotics have started.
:::

Lumbar puncture is deferred (and CT is performed first) when there are signs suggesting raised intracranial pressure or a mass lesion: reduced or fluctuating consciousness (for example a GCS below 9 or a fall of 3 or more), focal neurological signs, new seizures, papilloedema, or severe immunocompromise. It is also deferred in shock, coagulopathy or local skin infection.

| CSF finding | Bacterial | Viral | Tuberculous | Normal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Turbid | Clear | Clear or slightly turbid, may form a "cobweb" clot | Clear |
| White cells (per µL) | Hundreds to thousands, mainly neutrophils | Tens to hundreds, mainly lymphocytes | Tens to hundreds, mainly lymphocytes | Fewer than 5 |
| Protein | Raised, often above 1 g/L | Normal or mildly raised | Markedly raised | Below about 0.45 g/L |
| CSF:plasma glucose ratio | Low (below about 0.4) | Normal (about 0.6 or above) | Very low | About 0.6 or above |

The patterns overlap: early bacterial meningitis may show lymphocytes, partly treated bacterial meningitis may look viral, and *Listeria* often gives a mixed or lymphocytic picture. Gram stain, culture, PCR (meningococcus, pneumococcus, enteroviruses, herpesviruses) and blood cultures provide the specific diagnosis.

::: example A student with fever and a rash {#ex-meningitis}
An 18-year-old university student is brought to the emergency department by friends. Since the morning he has had fever, headache, vomiting and aching legs. He is drowsy but responds to voice (GCS 13). Heart rate is 128 beats per minute, blood pressure 92/54 mmHg, capillary refill 4 seconds, and his hands and feet are cold. There are a few purple spots on his legs that do not fade when a glass is pressed on them. He has mild neck stiffness.
::: solution
1. **Recognise.** Fever, a non-blanching rash and signs of shock (tachycardia, hypotension, prolonged capillary refill, cold peripheries) in a young adult: **meningococcal septicaemia**, possibly with meningitis. Aching legs and cold peripheries are early signs of septicaemia.
2. **Treat now.** Senior help; airway and oxygen; intravenous or intraosseous access; blood cultures and blood for meningococcal PCR, without delaying antibiotics; intravenous **ceftriaxone** immediately; fluid resuscitation; early involvement of critical care (see [[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]]).
3. **Lumbar puncture?** Not now. He is shocked and has reduced consciousness, so lumbar puncture is deferred. The diagnosis can be confirmed by blood PCR and culture.
4. **Dexamethasone** is not routinely given for meningococcal septicaemia without meningitis, but is given for suspected bacterial meningitis; senior judgement and local protocol decide.
5. **Public health.** Meningococcal disease is notifiable. The UK Health Security Agency arranges antibiotic prophylaxis for close contacts (household members and those with kissing contact), and vaccination where appropriate.
:::
:::

### Viral encephalitis

**Encephalitis** is inflammation of the brain substance. Fever and headache are accompanied by altered behaviour or consciousness, seizures or focal signs. The most important treatable cause in the UK is **herpes simplex virus (HSV) encephalitis**, which typically affects the temporal lobes: confusion, personality change, speech disturbance, olfactory or gustatory hallucinations, and seizures. Untreated, mortality is about 70%.

The rule is to start **intravenous aciclovir** as soon as viral encephalitis is suspected, without waiting for results. MRI shows temporal lobe signal change; CSF typically shows a lymphocytic pleocytosis with raised protein, sometimes red cells; and **HSV PCR** on CSF confirms the diagnosis. Other causes include varicella zoster virus, enteroviruses, HIV, and, in travellers, arboviruses such as Japanese encephalitis and West Nile virus. People with HIV and other immunocompromise are susceptible to opportunistic infections such as cryptococcal meningitis, toxoplasmosis and PML (see [[infectious-diseases/immunocompromised-host]]).

::: warning Do not wait for the scan or the tap
The commonest serious errors in central nervous system infection are delay of antibiotics while waiting for a CT scan or lumbar puncture, and failure to start aciclovir in a confused, febrile person because the presentation looks "psychiatric" or "just delirium". Treat first, investigate as soon as is safe. Cultures and PCR remain useful for hours to days after antibiotics or aciclovir have started, and treatment can always be stopped if another diagnosis is found.
:::

::: history
Jean-Martin Charcot, at the Salpêtrière in Paris, described "sclérose en plaques" clinically and pathologically in 1868, including the triad of nystagmus, intention tremor and scanning speech. In 1916 James Dawson in Edinburgh described the perivenular inflammation of MS plaques ("Dawson's fingers"). Devic and his student Gault described neuromyelitis optica in 1894; Vanda Lennon, Brian Weinshenker and colleagues identified the NMO antibody in 2004 and its target, aquaporin-4, in 2005. Gaspard Vieusseux described epidemic meningococcal meningitis in Geneva in 1805, and Anton Weichselbaum identified the meningococcus in 1887. Interferon beta-1b became the first disease-modifying therapy for MS in 1993, and Josep Dalmau and colleagues described anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis in 2007.
:::

::: exercise Internuclear ophthalmoplegia {level=2}
A 31-year-old man has double vision when looking to the right. On rightward gaze his left eye does not adduct beyond the midline, and his right eye shows nystagmus on abduction. Convergence is preserved. Where is the lesion, and what is the most likely cause at his age?
::: solution
This is a left **internuclear ophthalmoplegia**, caused by a lesion of the left medial longitudinal fasciculus, which carries signals from the right abducens nucleus (for rightward gaze) to the left oculomotor nucleus (to adduct the left eye). Preserved convergence shows that the left medial rectus and its nerve are intact. In a young adult, especially if bilateral, the most likely cause is demyelination in MS; in an older person, a small brainstem infarct is more likely.
:::
:::

::: exercise MS or not? {level=2}
A 52-year-old woman with migraine and hypertension has an MRI for headaches that shows a dozen small, non-specific white matter lesions in the deep and subcortical white matter. She has no neurological symptoms and a normal examination. Her friend has MS, and she is worried she has it too. What would you tell her?
::: solution
The diagnosis of MS is clinical and requires typical lesions in characteristic locations (periventricular, juxtacortical or cortical, infratentorial, spinal cord, optic nerve) and a clinical picture that fits, with no better explanation. Small deep and subcortical white matter lesions are common in people with migraine and with vascular risk factors such as hypertension, and become more common with age. They are not, on their own, evidence of MS. Without symptoms or typical lesions, she does not have MS. Over-reading such MRI findings is a common cause of misdiagnosis. Her blood pressure and vascular risk factors should be managed. If there were lesions with an MS-typical shape and location, the term radiologically isolated syndrome might be considered, with specialist review.
:::
:::

::: exercise Prophylaxis after meningococcal disease {level=2}
A university student is admitted with meningococcal meningitis. Which people need antibiotic prophylaxis, who arranges it, and why is it given?
::: solution
Prophylaxis is offered to close contacts in the 7 days before onset: people living in the same household (including shared kitchens in halls of residence), and those with intimate kissing contact. Health care workers need it only if they were directly exposed to respiratory secretions (for example during airway management without a mask). It is arranged by the UK Health Security Agency health protection team after notification, together with information for wider contacts and vaccination where the strain is vaccine-preventable. It is given to eradicate carriage of the organism among contacts, who are at increased risk of disease, and to stop onward transmission.
:::

::: exercise Confusion and fever {level=3}
A 63-year-old man is brought in by his wife after two days of fever, headache and strange behaviour. He has been talking nonsense and complained of a bad smell that no one else noticed. In the emergency department he has a focal seizure affecting the left side of his face. GCS is 13, temperature 38.6 °C. Outline your immediate management and investigations, and give the reasoning.
::: solution
The picture (fever, behavioural change, olfactory hallucinations, focal seizures) suggests encephalitis affecting the temporal lobes, most likely **HSV encephalitis**. Immediate management: airway, breathing and circulation; treat seizures; start **intravenous aciclovir immediately** (dose adjusted for renal function according to the formulary), and give empirical antibiotics for bacterial meningitis until it is excluded. Investigations: CT (or MRI if available promptly) before lumbar puncture because of the seizure and reduced consciousness; then lumbar puncture for cell count, protein, glucose, HSV and VZV PCR, enterovirus PCR and bacterial culture; EEG if consciousness remains impaired; blood cultures and HIV test. If HSV PCR is positive, aciclovir continues for at least 14 days (longer in immunocompromise) with a repeat CSF PCR in some protocols. If it is negative and the picture persists, consider autoimmune encephalitis (send antibodies) and, rarely, an early false-negative PCR, which may need a repeat lumbar puncture.
:::

::: exercise Choosing treatment for a young woman with MS {level=3}
A 29-year-old woman was diagnosed with relapsing–remitting MS a year ago. She has had two disabling relapses in that year, and her latest MRI shows four new lesions, two enhancing. She hopes to become pregnant within the next two or three years. Discuss the factors that shape the choice of disease-modifying therapy.
::: solution
She has **highly active** relapsing disease (frequent disabling relapses and new enhancing lesions), which argues for a high-efficacy therapy started early rather than escalating slowly, because early control of inflammation is associated with less long-term disability. Pregnancy plans are central: some drugs are teratogenic or need long washout periods (teriflunomide needs accelerated elimination; fingolimod and other S1P modulators should be stopped well before conception, with a risk of rebound disease); others have more favourable profiles (glatiramer acetate and interferon beta can be continued in pregnancy if needed; anti-CD20 antibodies can be given before conception with an interval; natalizumab is sometimes continued into pregnancy in very active disease; alemtuzumab and cladribine are given as courses, after which pregnancy is possible following a defined interval). Other factors: JC virus antibody status (PML risk with natalizumab), infection risk and vaccination status, monitoring burden, comorbidities, her preferences about route and frequency, and local commissioning rules. Relapse risk falls during pregnancy and rises in the first three months after delivery, which affects the plan for restarting treatment. The decision is shared, with the MS team, and revisited when her plans change.
