On 13 March 2006, six healthy young men took part in the first human trial of a new monoclonal antibody, TGN1412, at a research unit at Northwick Park Hospital in London. Each was paid to take part. Within about an hour of their infusions, all six became severely ill with a massive release of cytokines, and all needed intensive care for multi-organ failure. They survived, some with lasting harm. An expert review later recommended changes to how first-in-human doses are calculated and how quickly successive volunteers are dosed.

Research is how medicine learns what works. Every treatment in this curriculum exists because people agreed to take part in studies whose results were unknown. But research differs from treatment in a way that creates special ethical risks: its main aim is knowledge that will help future patients, not the person taking part. This chapter explains why research needs its own ethics, the history that shaped the rules, the requirements that make research ethical, consent and its exceptions, how research is regulated in the UK, and current issues in data, tissue and global research.

## Why research needs special ethics {#why-special}

In clinical care, the clinician's duty is to the patient: every decision should aim at that patient's good. In research, participants follow a protocol designed to answer a question. They may be randomised to a treatment the researchers hope is worse, given a placebo, or asked to undergo extra tests that will not help them. They accept risks partly for the benefit of others. This is ethically acceptable, but only with safeguards.

::: definition Research, audit and service evaluation {#def-research}
- **Research** aims to derive **generalisable** new knowledge, for example by testing a hypothesis or comparing interventions. It may involve randomisation or interventions beyond normal care. In the UK it requires review by a research ethics committee (REC) when it involves NHS patients, their tissue or data, among other cases.
- **Clinical audit** measures current care against a defined standard, to improve care locally. It does not involve randomisation or changes to treatment.
- **Service evaluation** describes current care or a service, to judge how well it works, without reference to a standard.

Audit and service evaluation do not need REC review, but they still require local approval, confidentiality protections and honesty in reporting. The Health Research Authority provides a decision tool to classify a project.
:::

A recurring problem is the **therapeutic misconception**: participants, and sometimes researchers, believe that the purpose of a trial is to benefit the individual participant, and underestimate the chance of receiving placebo or an inferior treatment. Good consent processes try to correct this.

## History and codes {#history-codes}

Research ethics was built largely in response to abuses. During the Second World War, German doctors carried out lethal experiments on concentration camp prisoners, including exposure to freezing water, high altitude and infections. After the war, 23 defendants, most of them doctors, were tried at Nuremberg. The judgment in 1947 set out ten principles, now called the **Nuremberg Code**, the first of which is that "the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential".

Abuse did not end there. In the **Tuskegee study** (1932–1972), the US Public Health Service followed about 600 Black men in Alabama, about 400 of whom had syphilis, to observe the natural history of the untreated disease. The men were not told they had syphilis, and they were not offered penicillin after it became the standard treatment in the 1940s. The study ended only after a whistleblower's concerns were reported in the press. At the **Willowbrook** State School in New York, from the 1950s, children with learning disabilities were deliberately infected with hepatitis, and admission was sometimes easier for families who agreed. In 1966 the anaesthetist Henry Beecher published, in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, 22 examples of ethically questionable studies from mainstream research.

In the UK, the discovery in the late 1990s that hospitals including Bristol Royal Infirmary and Alder Hey Children's Hospital had retained organs from children who had died, without their parents' knowledge, led to the **Human Tissue Act 2004**, which made consent the basis for storing and using human tissue.

The **Declaration of Helsinki**, first adopted by the World Medical Association in 1964, is the main international code for medical research. Its latest revision (October 2024) uses the word "participants" instead of "subjects", emphasises the vulnerability of individuals rather than whole groups, and adds requirements on community engagement, research waste and the secondary use of data and samples. In the United States, the **Belmont Report** (1979), written after Tuskegee, set out three principles that have shaped research ethics everywhere.

::: definition The Belmont principles {#def-belmont}
1. **Respect for persons:** treat individuals as autonomous agents, and protect those with diminished autonomy. Applied through **informed consent**.
2. **Beneficence:** do not harm; maximise possible benefits and minimise possible harms. Applied through **assessment of risks and benefits**.
3. **Justice:** distribute the burdens and benefits of research fairly. Applied through **fair selection of participants**, so that disadvantaged groups do not bear the risks of research that benefits others, and groups are not unfairly excluded from research that could benefit them.
:::

## The requirements of ethical research {#requirements}

In 2000, Ezekiel Emanuel, David Wendler and Christine Grady proposed a widely used set of requirements, which summarise the codes in a form that can be applied to any study.

::: theorem Seven requirements for ethical clinical research {#thm-requirements}
A study is ethical only if it has:

1. **Social or scientific value:** it asks a question whose answer could improve health or knowledge.
2. **Scientific validity:** its methods can answer the question reliably.
3. **Fair selection of participants:** participants are chosen for scientific reasons, not convenience or vulnerability.
4. **A favourable risk–benefit ratio:** risks are minimised and are reasonable in relation to the benefits to participants and the value of the knowledge.
5. **Independent review** by people without conflicts of interest.
6. **Informed consent** from participants, or a justified substitute.
7. **Respect for enrolled participants:** protecting their privacy, allowing withdrawal, monitoring their welfare, and informing them of results.
:::

::: proof
Each requirement answers a way in which research can wrong people. A study without value, or with methods too weak to answer its question, exposes participants to risk and inconvenience for nothing; it also wastes resources and can produce misleading evidence. This is why an underpowered trial, or one that will never be published, is an ethical problem, not just a scientific one.

Fair selection addresses the history: Tuskegee and Willowbrook chose participants because they were poor, Black or institutionalised. It also cuts the other way: excluding women, older people or ethnic minorities from trials leaves them with treatments tested on others.

Risk–benefit assessment and independent review exist because researchers have a conflict of interest: they want their study to go ahead. Consent respects autonomy. And because participants are owed respect throughout, not just at enrolment, the duties continue after consent: monitoring for harm, stopping a trial when one arm is clearly better, and sharing results.
:::

### Equipoise and placebo {#equipoise}

When is it ethical to randomise patients between treatments? Benjamin Freedman (1987) proposed **clinical equipoise**: a trial is ethical when there is genuine uncertainty within the expert medical community about which treatment is better. An individual researcher may have a hunch, but if the community of experts is divided or uncertain, no patient is knowingly given an inferior treatment.

Placebo controls are ethically straightforward when no effective treatment exists. When an effective treatment does exist, the Declaration of Helsinki says new interventions should be tested against the best proven intervention, except where no proven intervention exists, or where for compelling scientific reasons a placebo is necessary and patients receiving it will not be subject to additional risks of serious or irreversible harm.

A trial must also be large enough to answer its question. The figure shows how the power of a trial (the probability of detecting a real effect) depends on sample size and effect size ([[epidemiology-ebm/randomised-trials]]).

::: example A placebo-controlled trial {#ex-placebo-trial}
A company proposes a 12-week, placebo-controlled trial of a new drug for moderate depression in adults. Effective antidepressants and psychological therapies already exist. Is a placebo arm ethical?
::: solution
**The Helsinki test:** a proven intervention exists, so placebo needs a compelling scientific reason, and participants must not be exposed to additional risk of serious or irreversible harm.

**Scientific reason:** in depression, placebo response rates are high and variable, so trials comparing a new drug only with an existing one can fail to show whether either worked in that trial. Regulators often require placebo-controlled evidence. This can be a compelling reason.

**Risk:** the main risk of 12 weeks without active treatment is continued or worsening depression, including suicidal thoughts. The protocol must exclude people at high risk, monitor mood closely, have clear rules for withdrawing participants who deteriorate and offering them treatment, and keep the placebo period as short as possible. Including a third arm with an existing drug (active comparator) adds useful information.

**Consent:** participants must understand that they may receive placebo, that effective treatments exist outside the trial, and that they can withdraw at any time and receive standard care.

**Conclusion:** with these safeguards, a short placebo-controlled trial in moderate depression can be ethical. A long placebo trial in severe depression, or in a condition where untreated disease causes irreversible harm, would not be.
:::
:::

## Consent to research {#research-consent}

Consent to research is generally held to a higher standard than consent to treatment. Participants receive a written information sheet, have time to consider (often 24 hours or more for non-urgent studies), and give written consent. They must be told that they can withdraw at any time without giving a reason and without their care being affected. Information must cover the purpose, procedures, risks, possible benefits (and that there may be none), alternatives, confidentiality, use of data and samples, payment and compensation, and how results will be shared.

**Payment.** Reimbursing expenses and compensating time are acceptable. Payment becomes an **undue inducement** if it is large enough to lead people to take risks they would otherwise refuse, or to conceal information that would exclude them. RECs judge whether payments are appropriate.

::: warning A trial is not treatment, even when it offers hope
Patients with serious illness often enter trials hoping for benefit, and some do benefit. But a trial's design, including randomisation, placebo, fixed doses and extra tests, is chosen to answer a question, not to give each participant the best individual care. Researchers must say this plainly, avoid describing an experimental drug as "a new treatment" in a way that implies proven benefit, and make sure patients understand the alternatives. Clinicians who recruit their own patients have a particular duty to separate their roles, because patients may find it hard to say no to the doctor who treats them.
:::

**Adults who lack capacity.** Research involving adults who lack capacity is permitted in England and Wales only under specific legal conditions.

- For studies that are not trials of medicines, **sections 30–34 of the Mental Capacity Act 2005** apply. The research must be approved by an appropriate body (a REC), be connected with the person's impairing condition or its treatment, and be impossible to carry out as effectively with people who have capacity. It must either have the potential to benefit the person without disproportionate burden, or be intended to provide knowledge about the condition with negligible risk and no significant interference. The researcher must consult a **personal consultee** (such as a family member) or, if none is available, a **nominated consultee**, and must withdraw the person if they appear to object.
- For **clinical trials of investigational medicinal products** (CTIMPs), the Medicines for Human Use (Clinical Trials) Regulations 2004 apply instead, and consent is given by a **legal representative**.
- In **emergency research**, where treatment must start before anyone can be consulted, both frameworks allow enrolment under approved conditions, with consent sought from the participant or their representative as soon as possible afterwards (sometimes called **deferred consent**).

**Children.** For CTIMPs, a person with parental responsibility consents for children under 16, and the child's assent is sought. For other research, a Gillick-competent child may be able to consent, depending on the study and the REC's view. Research with children is necessary, because otherwise children would be treated with drugs tested only in adults.

::: example A trial in cardiac arrest {#ex-emergency}
The PARAMEDIC2 trial randomised over 8,000 adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in the UK to adrenaline or placebo, given by paramedics. Survival to 30 days was 3.2% with adrenaline and 2.4% with placebo, but there was no significant difference in survival with a favourable neurological outcome. How could such a trial be ethical when patients could not consent, and some families objected to placebo in resuscitation?
::: solution
**Value and equipoise:** adrenaline had been used for decades on the basis of animal data and observational studies, with genuine doubt about whether it improved meaningful survival. Only a placebo-controlled trial could answer the question, and the answer matters to millions of patients worldwide.

**Consent:** consent before enrolment was impossible. The trial used the emergency research provisions of the clinical trials regulations: it was approved in advance by a REC, patients were enrolled without prior consent, and survivors or their relatives were informed and asked for consent to continued participation and data use as soon as possible.

**Public involvement:** the investigators publicised the trial, consulted patients and the public, and allowed people to opt out in advance by requesting a bracelet indicating that they did not wish to take part.

**Risk–benefit:** because the benefit of adrenaline was uncertain, a participant receiving placebo was not knowingly deprived of effective treatment. The results then informed guidelines and families' understanding of resuscitation.
:::
:::

## Oversight and regulation in the UK {#oversight}

In England, the **Health Research Authority** (HRA), established on a statutory basis by the Care Act 2014, oversees research ethics and approves NHS research. **Research ethics committees** review protocols, information sheets and consent arrangements; they include lay members as well as experts. The **Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency** (MHRA) authorises clinical trials of medicines and devices. Each study needs a **sponsor**, an organisation that takes responsibility for its management, and the research must follow **Good Clinical Practice** standards (the ICH guideline, updated as E6(R3) in 2025).

Reformed clinical trials regulations (the Medicines for Human Use (Clinical Trials) (Amendment) Regulations 2025) came into force across the UK on 28 April 2026. They combine MHRA and ethics review, and make **transparency** a legal requirement for trials of medicines: trials must be registered in a public registry, a summary of results must be published, and findings should be shared with participants.

Transparency matters because **publication bias** distorts the evidence. Trials with positive results are more likely to be published, and quickly, than trials with negative results. Unregistered or unpublished trials betray participants who accepted risk to produce knowledge, and can lead to patients being given ineffective or harmful treatments. Since 2005, major medical journals have required trials to be registered before the first participant is enrolled.

::: definition Research misconduct {#def-misconduct}
**Research misconduct** includes **fabrication** (making up data or results), **falsification** (manipulating research materials, procedures or data so that the research record is not accurate), and **plagiarism** (presenting others' work as one's own). Other serious failures include failure to obtain ethics approval or consent, undisclosed conflicts of interest, and mistreatment of participants. Honest error and differences of opinion are not misconduct.
:::

The most damaging recent example in the UK was Andrew Wakefield's 1998 paper in *The Lancet* suggesting a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. Investigations found that children had been subjected to invasive procedures without appropriate ethical approval and that the data had been misrepresented. The paper was retracted in 2010, and Wakefield was struck off the medical register the same year. Vaccine confidence suffered for years afterwards ([[public-health/vaccination-programmes]]).

::: example An unregistered trial {#ex-unregistered}
A consultant runs a small randomised study comparing two dressings for leg ulcers in her clinic, without registration or ethics approval, because "both dressings are standard". The results favour one dressing and she submits them to a journal. What are the problems?
::: solution
**It is research:** randomising patients between treatments to produce generalisable knowledge is research, not audit, even if both treatments are standard. It needed HRA and REC approval, a sponsor, and informed consent from participants about being randomised.

**Consequences:** patients were entered into an experiment without knowing it, which breaches their autonomy. Without prospective registration, readers cannot check whether the outcomes reported were those planned, and most journals will not publish an unregistered trial.

**What to do now:** she should seek advice from her research and development office; the data may not be publishable; the matter may need to be reported through research governance. The right approach would have been to design a proper trial with approval and registration, which, for a question of genuine uncertainty between two standard dressings, would have been straightforward.
:::
:::

## Data, tissue and global research {#data-tissue}

Much modern research uses data and samples collected for other purposes. **Biobanks** such as UK Biobank, which recruited 500,000 adults between 2006 and 2010, store samples and data for future research that cannot be specified in advance. Participants give **broad consent** to future research within a governance framework, rather than specific consent to each study. The 2024 Declaration of Helsinki accepts this kind of consent for data and samples, under conditions including ethics committee oversight. Under the Human Tissue Act 2004, consent is needed to store and use tissue from the living for research, except where the tissue is anonymised and the research has REC approval. Research using confidential patient data without consent needs a legal basis, such as section 251 support ([[medical-ethics-law/confidentiality]]).

**Global research** raises questions of justice. In 1997 a controversy arose over placebo-controlled trials, in low-income countries, of shorter zidovudine regimens to prevent mother-to-child HIV transmission, when a longer regimen was already known to work and was standard in high-income countries. Critics called it a double standard; defenders said the long regimen was unaffordable locally and the question was what would work there. The debate led to stronger requirements in international guidelines (the CIOMS guidelines, last revised in 2016) for research to respond to local health needs, involve communities, and plan **post-trial access** to interventions shown to be effective.

::: example Using anonymised scans {#ex-scans}
A radiology department wants to share 50,000 anonymised chest radiographs with a technology company to train an artificial intelligence system to detect lung cancer. The company will own the resulting product. Is consent needed, and what other ethical issues arise?
::: solution
**Law:** truly anonymised data is not personal data, so data protection law and the duty of confidentiality do not apply in the same way. But images may contain identifiers (in the image or its metadata), and rare findings could allow identification, so anonymisation must be robust. Sharing identifiable or pseudonymised data would need a legal basis and, for research, approval.

**Beyond the law:** public trust in NHS data depends on transparency and fair terms. Patients may object to their data being used to create a commercial product, even if they cannot be identified. The Trust should be open about the arrangement, consider whether it returns benefit to the NHS (fair value, access to the product), use a trusted research environment where possible, and involve patients and the public in the decision.

**Justice and validity:** the dataset should represent the patients on whom the tool will be used, including different ages, sexes and ethnic groups, to avoid a product that works less well for some groups ([[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]]).
:::
:::

::: history
Some of the earliest ethical reflection on human experimentation came from physicians. In 1865 Claude Bernard wrote that a physician should never perform on a human being an experiment that might be harmful to him in any extent, even if the result might benefit science. Yet research abuses continued well into the 20th century in many countries.

The Nuremberg Code (1947) put voluntary consent first. The World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964) brought research ethics into the profession's own codes and has been revised many times, most recently in 2024. Henry Beecher's paper (1966) and the exposure of the Tuskegee study (1972) led to the US National Research Act (1974), the Belmont Report (1979) and institutional review boards. In the UK, local research ethics committees became expected from the late 1960s and were formally required in the NHS from 1991. The TGN1412 disaster (2006) changed first-in-human trial design, and the 2026 UK trial regulations made registration and results reporting a legal duty.
:::

::: summary
- Research aims at generalisable knowledge, so participants bear risks partly for others; this requires special safeguards. Distinguish research from audit and service evaluation.
- The codes grew from abuses: Nazi experiments (Nuremberg Code, 1947), Tuskegee, Willowbrook, Beecher's examples, UK organ retention (Human Tissue Act 2004). The Declaration of Helsinki was last revised in 2024; the Belmont Report set out respect for persons, beneficence and justice.
- Ethical research needs social value, scientific validity, fair selection, a favourable risk–benefit ratio, independent review, informed consent and respect for participants.
- Randomisation requires **clinical equipoise**; placebo is acceptable when no proven treatment exists or, with compelling reasons, when no serious or irreversible harm will result. Underpowered and unpublished trials are ethical failures.
- Consent to research is written and specific, with free withdrawal and no undue inducement. Adults lacking capacity can be included only under MCA ss30–34 or, for drug trials, the clinical trials regulations; emergency research allows deferred consent.
- In the UK, the HRA and RECs review research, the MHRA regulates trials of medicines, and from 28 April 2026 registration and results publication are legal requirements for clinical trials of medicines.
- Biobanks use broad consent; global research must respond to local needs and plan post-trial access.
:::

Classify each project: (a) comparing your unit's antibiotic prescribing with the local guideline; (b) testing whether a text-message reminder increases attendance by randomising patients to receive one or not; (c) describing how many patients use the new walk-in clinic and why.
::: solution
(a) Audit: measuring practice against a standard. (b) Research: randomisation to generate generalisable knowledge; it needs REC approval. (c) Service evaluation: describing a current service without reference to a standard.

::: exercise The Belmont principles {level=1}
For each Belmont principle, give its main practical application in research.
::: solution
Respect for persons: informed consent (and protection of people with diminished autonomy). Beneficence: systematic assessment of risks and benefits, minimising risk. Justice: fair selection of participants, so that burdens and benefits of research are fairly shared.

::: exercise Number needed to treat {level=1 check="125"}
In PARAMEDIC2, 30-day survival was 3.2% with adrenaline and 2.4% with placebo. How many patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest would need to receive adrenaline for one additional patient to survive to 30 days?
::: solution
Absolute difference $= 3.2\% - 2.4\% = 0.8\% = 0.008$. NNT $= 1/0.008 = 125$. Because there was no significant difference in survival with a favourable neurological outcome, many of the extra survivors had severe brain injury, which is why the result prompted debate about what outcomes matter to patients.
:::
:::

::: exercise Sample size and ethics {level=2 check="63"}
A trial will compare a new treatment with standard care for a continuous outcome. Using the approximation $n = 2(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 / d^2$ per group, with $z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96$, $z_\beta = 0.84$ (80% power) and a standardised effect size $d = 0.5$, how many participants are needed in each group? Round up to the next whole number.
::: solution
$(1.96 + 0.84)^2 = 2.8^2 = 7.84$. $n = 2 \times 7.84 / 0.25 = 62.72$, rounded up to $63$ per group. A trial with, say, 20 per group would have low power and might well miss a real effect; it would expose participants to the burdens of research without a good chance of answering the question.
:::
:::

::: exercise Payment {level=2}
A phase 1 unit offers £4,000 to healthy volunteers for a 3-week residential first-in-human study. A student in debt says the money is the only reason she would do it. Is this undue inducement?
::: solution
Payment for time, inconvenience and discomfort in phase 1 studies is accepted, and most volunteers take part partly for money. Payment becomes undue inducement if it leads people to accept risks they would otherwise judge unreasonable, to ignore information, or to conceal medical conditions that would exclude them. The safeguards are a REC's review of the amount relative to the time and burden, careful screening, a clear explanation of risks (including, since TGN1412, staggered dosing and the reasons for it), and a right to withdraw with payment pro rata. The student's motive does not by itself invalidate consent, but she should understand the risks and should not hide anything that would make the study unsafe for her.
:::
:::

::: exercise Research and capacity {level=2}
A researcher wants to study a new memory assessment tool in people with moderate dementia who lack capacity to consent. The study involves a 30-minute assessment and no change to treatment. Under the Mental Capacity Act, what conditions must be met?
::: solution
Under ss30–34: approval by a REC as the appropriate body; the research must be connected with the impairing condition (dementia); it must not be possible to do it as effectively with people who have capacity (a tool for moderate dementia must be tested in that group); and since it is unlikely to benefit participants directly, it must aim to provide knowledge about the condition, with negligible risk and no significant interference with their freedom or privacy (a 30-minute assessment probably meets this). The researcher must consult a personal consultee (such as a family member) or a nominated consultee about whether the person should take part and what their wishes would be, and must withdraw anyone who appears to object or become distressed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Global trial design {level=3}
A research group plans a trial in a low-income country comparing a cheap, short treatment for a common infection against placebo, although a more expensive, longer treatment is standard in high-income countries. Analyse whether the trial could be ethical.
::: solution
**Equipoise and comparator:** under Helsinki, the comparator should be the best proven intervention. If the long treatment is proven and could be provided in the trial, a placebo arm is hard to justify; comparing the short treatment with the long one (a non-inferiority design) would be more ethical. If neither treatment is available locally, some argue that placebo reflects the local standard of care, but this argument is controversial and was strongly criticised in the 1997 HIV debate.

**Responsiveness:** the research should address a health need of the host population, and the treatment, if effective, should be made reasonably available there afterwards, with post-trial access planned before the trial starts.

**Community engagement and review:** local researchers, a local ethics committee as well as the sponsor's, community advisory boards, and consent processes appropriate to local language and literacy.

**Fairness of burdens:** participants should not bear risks that would be unacceptable in the sponsor's own country unless there is a strong justification linked to local benefit.
:::
:::

::: exercise Outcome switching {level=3}
A published trial reports a statistically significant improvement in a secondary outcome (quality of life at 6 months). The trial registry shows that the primary outcome was mortality at 12 months, which is mentioned briefly as "not significantly different". Discuss the ethical and scientific issues.
::: solution
**Scientific:** emphasising a secondary outcome after the primary outcome failed is a form of outcome switching. With multiple outcomes, some will be "significant" by chance, so the finding is hypothesis-generating at best ([[epidemiology-ebm/randomised-trials]]).

**Ethical:** participants accepted risks to answer the registered question; reporting should be honest and complete about that question. Selective reporting misleads clinicians and patients, may lead to use of ineffective treatments, and wastes the contribution of participants. It may amount to research misconduct if deliberate.

**Remedies:** journals and readers should compare publications with registries and protocols; CONSORT reporting requires clear statement of primary outcomes and any changes; the UK's 2026 trial regulations require results summaries to be published, so that results for the registered outcomes are available whatever the paper emphasises.
:::
:::
