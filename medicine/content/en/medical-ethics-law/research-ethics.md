Research ethics exists because medicine's subjects paid for its knowledge: the recorded abuses - Nuremberg's doctors, Tuskegee's withheld treatment, the short-AZT-trial controversies - set the field's grammar (consent, risk, fairness and accountability as enforceable rules rather than bedside virtues), and every decade since has added clauses for its own new temptations: the placebo debates of the HIV era, the ethics-shopping scandals, the biobank consent rewrites, and the authorship and data-provenance disputes of the generative-AI years. **This chapter covers the documents and their arguments (the Nuremberg-to-Helsinki-to-Belmont line and the 'universal-versus-imperial' critique of the CIOMS layer), consent's research-specific machinery (broad, tiered and dynamic consent, incidental findings and the right not to know, waiver architectures for emergency research), the trial as an ethical object (equipoise, the placebo and sham-procedure fights, post-trial obligations, and registration as a duty), oversight as ethics (what a research-ethics committee is for, review shopping, and reciprocity with research communities), and the data-era extensions (biobanks, secondary uses of records, AI training data and the justice of authorship).**

## The documents

::: definition The historical grammar and its critiques {#def-documents}
**The canon's actual content is worth knowing precisely, because clinicians are cited against it without having read it. The Nuremberg Code (1947) was ten points inside a tribunal's verdict - voluntariness, proportionate risk, withdrawal, and the rest - improvised by judges answering the German-doctors' trial defences, and awkward ever after as the founding document of a field that mostly wants to prevent what its authors were punishing. The Declaration of Helsinki (1964, revised repeatedly, most consequentially in 2000, 2008 and 2013) is the working document: a physicians' association's declaration that became the world's de facto research constitution, and the only ethics instrument with a visible changelog - its revision fights (the place of placebos, the standard-of-care paragraph, registration and post-trial clauses) are the field's arguments conducted as editing. The Belmont Report (1979) supplied the three-principle grammar US regulation then industrialised: respect for persons (consent and its protections), beneficence (the systematic risk-benefit evaluation whose 'minimise-maximise' apparatus still structures protocols), and justice (the fair selection of subjects - the principle Tuskegee made concrete). The common-rule framework that grew from Belmont - institutional review boards, informed-consent elements, categories of minimal risk, the subpart additions for prisoners, pregnant women, children and economically vulnerable subjects - is the reason research ethics became paperwork, which is both its achievement and its perpetual complaint. The international layer (CIOMS guidelines, the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences' developing-country adaptations, the ICH-GCP harmonisation, and the EU regulation's recent centralisation) answered the question the canon could not: what applies when the sponsor is rich and the site is poor. The critiques run in two directions. From below: the 'imperial ethics' charge - that informed-consent centralism and committee review export an American procedural individualism, become barriers to local research capacity, and ignore communal decision structures; and its answers - that a floor of individual protection is not cultural imperialism (the exploited researcher's community needs the review board precisely because its own institutions failed it), and the more constructive decolonial programme: partnership-not-extraction trial design, community consultation with standing to object, capacity transfer written into grants, and the reciprocity obligations the next definitions treat as machinery rather than sentiment. From above: the 'compliance-ethics' critique - that the paperwork replaced the ethics. This chapter's answer is institutional: the documents' function is to make the abuse's preconditions impossible (independent review, documented consent, registration, and communities with standing), and the paperwork is the ethics exactly where it forces the argument - the field's failures (rubber-stamp committees, consent-as-indemnity, approval shopping) are all cases of documents surviving the argument's disappearance.**
:::

## Consent's machinery

::: definition Research consent architecture {#def-consent}
**Research consent differs from treatment consent in its object: the participant accepts uncertainty about benefit (the trial may help no one, and may not be for them at all) and often authorises future uses not yet imagined. The field's sturdiest empirical finding is the therapeutic misconception: participants in randomised trials systematically believe the treatment chosen for them was chosen for them, which is a comprehension failure of the one thing the consent exists to communicate - so the competent answer is process, not paper: teach-back, an explicit randomisation check ('nobody, including your doctors, believes one arm is better - that is why the question is being asked'), and re-consent when the protocol's risks change. The designs for scope: study-specific consent (the trial classic); broad consent (agree in general terms to categories of future research under governance - the biobank-era compromise whose critics ask what a signature on a general authorisation understands); tiered consent (a menu of uses - honest that choice-architecture shifts what people select); dynamic or metadata consent via apps (consent as a maintained habit, with burden falling heaviest on the oldest participants, the recurring equity finding); and the consent-substitute model - the UK route for records-based research where identifiable data proceed without individual consent under independent justified approval (the confidentiality-approving-body architecture): governance standing in consent's place, legitimate when the review is real and the notice-and-opt-out public, and precisely a loophole when either is not. Incidental and secondary findings forced the next clause: genomic and imaging research generates clinically relevant discoveries in participants enrolled for something else, and the mature practice is prospective - a findings plan in the protocol and consent (what is sought, what is returned, who is competent to receive it, who pays for the confirmation, and how a genuine right not to know is recorded and respected where safety allows), because the unscheduled 3 a.m. discovery is the worst possible moment to invent an ethic. Capacity and proxy research has its own tightened rules: research with adults who lack capacity must relate to their condition, carry minimal-plus risk ceilings, involve the consultee who knew them, and can be overridden by nothing but the law's own safeguards (the capacity-act research clauses); in paediatrics, parental permission plus the child's assent is the floor, and the refusing adolescent whose parents consent is the unresolved case the ethics committees decide case by case. Emergency research produced the democracy answer: where consent at the scene is impossible and later consent too late, waiver architectures proceed on prior public consultation and community notification, opt-out signage, and independent data-safety oversight - consent approximated by the population's prior agreement rather than the individual's signature. The chapter's quality metric, then, is not signature-count but comprehension sampling and re-consent cadence: consent decays and the document does not, which is why the form is never the ethic.**
:::

## The trial as an ethical object

::: definition Equipoise, placebos and post-trial duties {#def-trial}
**Clinical equipoise is the argument that makes randomisation ethical: randomise only while the expert community genuinely lacks consensus about which arm is better (the formulation deliberately locates certainty in the community rather than the investigator's heart, because individual conviction is unstable and the whole point of trials is that conviction should be the thing in short supply). Its measurement problems are real (who counts as the community; when does a loud minority defeat consensus) and its protection is thin (equipoise about the question does not comfort the participant who believes she was assigned to the worse arm), but the practical test survives: a trial that cannot face its own stopping rules has usually lost equipoise first and the data-monitoring-committee charter is where the ethic lives. The placebo and standard-of-care debate crystallised in the HIV trials of the late 1990s (the short-zidovudine-regimen controversy: was giving placebo-with-short-zidovudine to control-arm women, when long-zidovudine reduced transmission, exploitation of the poor or honest science against nothing) and never fully resolved: the Helsinki-versus-CIOMS-versus-regulator divergence on placebos-against-sub-ideal-care persists, the methodological truce is the add-on design (new intervention or placebo added to optimised standard care in both arms, which answers most accusations and cannot answer all - sometimes only subtracting proves the effect), and the residue is a genuine moral conflict committees weigh: assay sensitivity (the trial must be able to detect a real difference) against the wrong of withholding a known benefit. Sham procedures extend the argument through the body: sham-surgery trials were resisted as intolerable (the placebo is a pill; the sham is a scalpel) and vindicated by their results (the washout-and-debridement trials whose sham arms performed identically rewrote practice) - the field's test is risk-ceiling-plus-question-necessity, not the dignity of the instrument. Post-trial obligations are the justice-limb: what happens to participants and communities when funding ends (the 'reasonable availability' fights of the 1990s, the 'duty to provide successful interventions' positions from the just-research literature, the sponsors' cost-driven preference for time-limited continuations, and the pragmatic machinery - rollover and named-access and compassionate-use schemes, transition plans filed with ethics approvals, and the community-level question of whether a health system asked to sustain what the trial proved is owed the resources to do it). Registration and reporting complete the set: the unregistered, unreported trial is an ethical failure before it is a scientific one - the AllTrials evidence, the ClinicalTrials-gov and WHO registry mandates, the ICMJE registration-before-enrolment rule, and the result-publication-within-a-year norms exist because participants accepted risk on the understanding their contribution would join the record, and a trial whose results are buried when inconvenient uses people as raw material. The clinician's stake: every treatment you prescribe rests on trials whose registration you can check, whose placebo design you can interrogate, and whose control-arm promises you can ask about before you cite them.**
:::

## Oversight and the data era

::: definition Committees, reciprocity and research data ethics {#def-oversight}
**The research-ethics committee (or institutional review board) is the field's central institution and is habitually misunderstood: it is not an ethics seminar but a venue where a proposal must render its risks, consent process, selection fairness and investigator competence inspectable by people who do not benefit from the science - the independence is the point (the committee that rubber-stamps its institution's prestige or its chair's grants has ceased to be one, and the accreditation-and-audit regimes exist to catch that). The pathology list the scandals generated: review shopping (submitting until approval - why competent systems share decisions and verify prior rejections), ethics-free protocol amendment (the unlogged change of endpoints and consent text), consultant-expertise theatre (the local-REC-review-of-multinational-protocols conducted in four minutes because the sponsor's timeline is the real protocol), and the low- and exempt-review gaps (quality-improvement-versus-research boundary games that put publications outside review - the audit-studies-literature's quiet scandal). Reciprocity to research sites is the newer obligation: communities that host trials (often poor, often repeatedly) are owed more than the consent transaction - the framework families (the CIOMS-guideline language on community benefits and capacity strengthening, the 'benefit-sharing and community-engagement' expectations of HIV and vaccine and epidemic research, the trials' community-advisory-boards that make the local refusal real and therefore make the local consent meaningful) convert 'do no harm' into 'be worth the invitation'. The data era moved the field's centre of gravity: biobanks made storage-the-ethical-object (consent architecture per the earlier definition plus governance: access committees, linkage rules, public-and-participant panels, benefit and commercialisation policy stated before profit arrives, and the return-of-value-versus-return-of-results distinction); records-based research made identifiability-the-hazard (the pseudonymisation-versus-anonymisation truth that linkage-capable data are identifiable data, the five-safes-architectures and trusted-research-environments that substitute controlled access for hidden data, and the re-identification studies that keep the point unsentimental); and machine learning made datasets-into-agents (the provenance ethics of training data - consented-for-clinical-care-scans-now-classifying, the dataset documentation standards (datasheets-for-datasets and model cards) as consent's paper trail, the 'is-the-model-derived-from-your-hospital-yours-said-no' governance vacuum, the algorithmic-bias audits whose distributional-impact-before-deployment duty the justice chapter preaches, and the publication-ethics frontier of generative authorship (the ICMJE positions requiring human accountability for every claim, the disclosed-use rules, and the peer-review-confidentiality violations that the tools' convenience keeps engineering). The through-line: research ethics began as protection of the enrolled body and is becoming governance of the captured data-trail - same four questions (who agreed to what, who bears which risks, who receives which benefits, who answers for it), new substrates, same requirement that the answers be inspectable by the people who made the contribution.**
:::

::: example The unregistered trial {#ex-unregistered}
A drug your department pushes cites a 2016 trial showing dramatic benefit; you cannot find it in any registry; the sponsor's medical lead emails you 'the results are commercially sensitive pending the regulatory filing - publication next year'. The department wants formulary approval on the evidence pack.
::: solution
**Registration-as-ethics-applied ([[#def-trial]], [[epidemiology-ebm/systematic-reviews]], [[pharmacology/drug-development-prescribing]]): The first move is forensic, not rhetorical: search the registries properly (the WHO primary-network and ClinicalTrials-gov records including withdrawn-and-recruiting-status entries, the EU-CTR and ISRCTN, the ethical-review-approval numbers that leak the study's existence, the regulators' public-assessment-documents where filings happened anyway), and ask the sponsor for the protocol, the statistical analysis plan and the ethics approval - a trial that cannot survive those three requests was never evidence. The interpretation: absent-registration-plus-delayed-publication-plus-oral-presentation-only is the exact profile of the reporting-bias literature's worst case (the effect sizes of unregistered trials run systematically favourable, and the selective-publication era's antidepressant and oseltamivir recons-allocations are the teaching examples), so the honest summary for the formulary committee is 'the trial may be true; we are not permitted to know', and the committee's decision is therefore about uncertainty, not about the drug. The counter-pressure that works: the department writes the sponsor jointly with the journal-route reminder (the ICMJE's registration requirement, the regulators' inspection powers where a filing exists, and the institutional-reviewers' ability to make the site's own ethics approval conditional on results-publication for ongoing collaborations), and the same letter proposes the compromise the evidence deserves (independent-statistician access to participant-level data under confidentiality - the template the difficult-device-and-rare-disease-situations use). The local-system fix: your institution's research office adopts the prospective clause - no site-activation of sponsored trials without registry entry and a published results-commitment date - because the department that enforces registration on its own collaborations stops importing the problem. The prescribing consequence meanwhile: the drug stays where unproven-but-available drugs belong (restricted indications, data-collection attached, and the committee minute that says in plain words that this is a decision under a disclosure strike - because formulary packs buried in meeting-room folders are how unregistered trials get prescribed for a decade).**
:::
:::

::: example The secondary use of the scans {#ex-scans}
A commercial AI company offers your radiology department free computing and a revenue share to train a classification product on twenty years of archive scans; the trust's research office says the archive was consented for 'clinical care and approved research'. The radiologists are excited; the patients' liaison manager is nervous.
::: solution
**Archive-consent-is-the-asset ([[#def-oversight]], [[#def-consent]], [[medical-ethics-law/confidentiality]]): The audit before the enthusiasm: what the original consent and the trust's notice actually cover (care-and-approved-research-consents plausibly cover a public-interest imaging study; they do not automatically cover commercial product development with revenue to the institution - the governance question is not 'is it anonymised' but 'is this the bargain the patients made'), the identifiability reality (dicom-headers-and-scan-re-identification-literature mean 'anonymised' needs the standards-body test applied and documented, not asserted), and the conflict-register frame the deal requires (the institution's revenue-share is an interest the eventual publications must disclose - the ghostwork-and-gift-authorship lessons, pre-committed). The route that can be legitimate: the consent-substitute-with-transparency model (a real confidentiality-committee approval on defined public-benefit grounds, a clear public statement of what is happening with an opt-out-for-the-archive window, data-sharing agreements with publication-freedom clauses so the company cannot bury inconvenient validation, independent external validation before any clinical claim, and governance with patient representatives actually in the room rather than consulted after). The terms worth dying on commercially and the ones worth trading: the revenue share is tradeable; publication-freedom, auditability, opt-out-honouring and no-exclusive-lockup-of-a-public-asset are not, because the archive is the patients' donation and the institution's public-trust-collateral in the same file. The department's honest summary line for its board: 'we can probably do this well, but only if the patients could read the whole arrangement and still recognise the deal they thought they made' - and if that sentence fails in the drafting stage, it will fail at the tabloid stage with the clinic's letterhead.**
:::

::: history
**The field's periodisation is scandal-to-structure: the 1947 verdicts (the Nuremberg doctors' trial producing the Code inside a legal opinion, and the parallel embarrassment that the same powers' own pre-war research - the American and Japanese records still being excavated decades later, Unit 731's immunity bargain among them - went untried, which is the decolonial historians' standing reminder that the canon's birth was also a politics); the discovery-era exposures (Willowbrook's hepatitis studies and the Saulkrasti-and-Lutheran-Church-home cases that made 'research with the vulnerable' a public category, and the US Public Health Service syphilis study at Tuskegee running 1932-72 - its 1972 exposure by Jean Heller, the subsequent lawsuit and advisory-committee work that produced Belmont's justice principle with the study's own subjects unautopsied-in-name-only). Helsinki's drafting (1964, the World Medical Association's own answer to Nuremberg's legalism - physicians regulating physicians - and its revision-wars: the 1975 and 1983 amendments tightening consent, the 1989 and 2000 placebo-and-standard-of-care explosion (the AIDS-activist and developing-country critiques colliding with the epidemiologists' assay-sensitivity defence), the 2013 revision's registration and post-trial and sham clauses as edited truce). The institutional build-out: the National Research Act 1974 and the institutional review board regime, the 1991 Common Rule, the ICH-GCP harmonisation of the 1990s that made trials portable across regulators, the EU-directive-and-regulation cycles centralising multinational approvals (with the trials-in-poor-member-states criticism that re-ran the imperial-ethics argument inside the Union), and the parallel global-south capacity movement (the Forum-to-FORCINE-and-African-research-ethics-committees' institutionalisation decade, the 'ethics-oversight-as-research-capacity-not-impediment' reversal the funders learned). The trial-conduct scandals of the 2000s (the CIOMS-review-and-GSK-and-pfizer-and-neurofab-case-files-of-trial-oversight-failure - the Indian-and-Nigerian-and-American-orphans-cases whose legacy is the 'community-has-standing' clause movement and the 'post-trial-access-continuation-programmes'-as-condition-of-approval practice), the registration revolution (the 2004 AllTrials-and-Trialby-error-and-clinicaltrials-gov-mandate-and-ICMJE-no-registration-no-publication-line, the EMA-and-FDA-results-reporting-compliance-audits still finding half the trials non-compliant a decade on - the registration norm won the principle and keeps losing the deadline), and the data-era turn (the biospecimen-consent-rewrites after the Henrietta-Lacks-legacy-and-Moore-removal-cases, the UK's NHS-Data-Store-and-Care.data-shock-as-procedural-fairness-lesson-the-world-cited-while-repeating-it, the genomic-return-of-results-guidelines-colleges, the consent-to-contact-and-variant-databases architecture, and the current AI-provenance-and-training-data-consent arguments already outrunning their guidance documents). The clinician's version of this history is shorter: every protection you now find annoying has a body count attached, and every gap you find convenient has one pending - [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]], [[epidemiology-ebm/randomised-trials]].**
:::

::: example The rapid review request {#ex-rapid}
A consultant wants to launch a ward-based protocol change bundled with data collection across three hospitals next month; the 'evaluation' needs no consent, he argues, because it is audit; the sponsor of the multi-site arm is commercial; his draft 'approval' is from one site's R&D office, dated yesterday.
::: solution
**Audit-versus-research-boundaries ([[#def-oversight]], [[#def-documents]], [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]): The classification first, because it decides everything downstream: a systematic intervention to generate generalisable knowledge is research (the audit-versus-research tests - intent-to-generalise, prospective-manipulation, analysability-as-evidence - applied honestly here: a bundled protocol-plus-data-collection across three sites with a commercial arm is research-shaped, publication-destined and sponsor-shaped, so 'audit' is a pathway-hopper not a category). The consequences to name before the launch, not after the retraction: ethics-review requirement (and the one-office-dates-yesterday fact is a prior-review question the other sites' committees will ask - review-shopping is discoverable now that decisions are shared), the consent-or-justified-waiver route for the data (the 'no-consent-because-audit' shortcut fails the same way the confidentiality-approval route requires real approval), the investigator's-regulatory-training-and-IND-and-insurance-questions the commercial arm raises, and the registration-and-reporting duty that attaches the moment someone writes 'evaluation' in a journal's methods. The fix that preserves the good idea: run the implementation-science version properly (a stepped-wedge-or-hub-and-spoke design with prospective ethics-or-governance-approval-obtained-in-parallel-not-sequentially-with-the-rollout, the audit-component-benchmark-without-generalisation-claims, the commercial-arm-as-separate-contract-with-separate-oversight, the registry entry before first-site-activation, and the community-of-practice benefit - publishing the failure-modes - that makes the whole thing worth doing). The mentorship sentence, delivered once and warmly: 'the fastest approved study is the one no regulator or editor has to stop afterwards, and the ward does not need your protocol fast, it needs it publishable.'**
:::

::: example The community that said no {#ex-community}
A multinational team proposes a trial of a nutrition supplement in a food-insecure region; the local community advisory board declines to endorse it (the supplement is the sponsor's commercial product, the control arm is the current inadequate rations, and last year's malaria-vaccine study never shared results). The funders press the local site: 'the children need this research'.
::: solution
**Standing-to-object-is-the-ethic ([[#def-oversight]], [[#def-trial]], [[public-health/global-health]]): The architecture being tested: community advisory boards were created precisely for this decision, and their power is only as real as the site's willingness to hear no - the 'consent-extraction-versus-consent-relationship' literature's central case. The design problems the board named, in research-ethics terms: the control arm (inadequate-rations-as-comparator fails the standard-of-care test - the add-on-design fix (both arms receive the adequate ration; the question then answers what the supplement adds to care, not to hunger), the commercial-nature disclosure (who profits from a positive result in a population that cannot afford the product), and the outstanding debt (the malaria-study's unreturned results are a post-trial-and-reporting-obligation violation that makes this proposal's promises unfundable credibility, not unfriendly). The site team's defensible positions: the board's no is honoured in writing with reasons; the counter-proposal is invited in the same breath (fix the control arm, fund the ration for both arms regardless of assignment, name-and-involve the local investigators as co-approvers with publication control, register and pre-commit results-return-to-community-in-local-language-within-a-year with a named-owner); the malaria-study results are demanded and, if withheld, pursued through the journal-and-ethics-complaint-and-funder routes (the enforcement tools exist; they are under-used because they burn relationships - which is why the field writes 'enforcement' in guidelines and does it rarely). The funders' 'children need this research' sentence is the chapter's own test: the children need the research that the community is allowed to refuse - a study whose local partners are pressured into accepting it will recruit by desperation, retain by dependence, and report by instruction, which is what the extracted-data histories in this field's own archive keep demonstrating. The outcome worth aiming for: the trial that happens six months later with the fixed control arm, the signed benefit-return plan and the restored trust, with the malaria results returned first - repair as a precondition of recruitment is the decolonial-programme sentence made operational, and the board's no is what made the eventual yes worth anything.**
:::

::: exercise Fix the consent {level=2}
Rewrite the study's worst paragraph: 'By signing this form you consent to participate in the research and release the investigators from liability for any risks.' The study is a randomised trial of a new antihypertensive against usual care. Teach the participant-facing principles your rewrite must satisfy.
::: solution
**Consent-as-comprehension ([[#def-consent]], [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]]): The paragraph fails three ways: it conflates consent-with-a-signature-with-liability-release (a consent is not a waiver - the compensation-for-research-injury sentence belongs separately and honestly stated per the jurisdiction's rules), it hides the randomisation (the therapeutic-misconception fix: 'treatment will be allocated by chance, like a draw; neither you nor your doctors chose which; that is how we find out which works'), and it names no risks, benefits or alternatives (the elements: what we do not know, what could happen, what we hope, what you miss by not joining, how you leave - and the comprehension checks: teach-back, the randomisation question, the 'you can withdraw and your care is unaffected' verification). The rewrite model: 'We are comparing our new blood-pressure tablet with the standard treatment you would receive anyway. Neither treatment has been shown to be better - if we knew, the trial would be unethical. Allocation is by chance. We will monitor you more closely than usual; side effects may include X; we may not benefit personally; you may stop at any time and your usual care continues; if you are harmed by the study treatment, here is how compensation works in this country. Please tell us in your own words what you understood.' The teaching principle: consent quality is measured by what the participant can say, not by what the form contains - and the form exists so that what the participant can say is true.**
:::

::: exercise Judge the placebo {level=2}
A trial of add-on therapy for acute migraine proposes placebo plus standard rescue medication in both arms; a second trial of an adjunct for a chronic condition proposes placebo while withdrawing the effective standard drug for two weeks. Judge each, then state the rule your committee should publish.
::: solution
**Two-placebos-two-verdicts ([[#def-trial]], [[epidemiology-ebm/randomised-trials]]): Trial one passes: both arms receive optimised standard care and the experimental add-on faces placebo-on-top - the add-on design that answers the 'withholding' objection because nothing proven is withheld, and assay sensitivity comes from the add-on difference. Trial two is the hard case: a two-week withdrawal of effective therapy exposes control participants to foreseeable harm (rebound, relapse, the irreversible-in-some-conditions risk) for measurement convenience - the short-zidovudine-debate structure - and would need the compelling-scientific-reason-plus-no-serious-or-irreversible-harm test to pass, which a migraine-or-eczema-quality-of-life endpoint rarely survives when an add-on design would answer the same question. The publishable rule: (1) placebos are permitted only against no-established-care or as add-on to established care in both arms; (2) withdrawal designs require demonstration that the question cannot be answered otherwise, a short-and-reversible exposure with rescue rules and stopping rules pre-specified, and independent-data-monitoring with unblinding authority; (3) the expected-harm-in-the-control-arm paragraph appears in the participant information in plain words, because a participant who understands she might be asked to stop a working treatment has given a different, more real consent than one told only about 'a sugar pill'. The footnote ethicists insist on: the harm of a bad trial is not only to its participants but to every future patient of the question it answers badly.**

::: exercise Write the results clause {level=2}
Draft the three clauses (250 words) you would require before your institution hosts a sponsored trial: registration, results reporting, and post-trial access - each with the enforcement mechanism attached.
::: solution
**Clauses-with-teeth ([[#def-trial]], [[#def-oversight]]): Clause one, registration: 'The trial will be entered in a WHO-recognised primary registry before first participant enrolment; the site will not activate without the registry identifier, and the identifier appears in all protocol and consent documents.' Enforcement: the research-office activation gate (money and pharmacy access follow the number - the ICMJE principle inverted to the sponsor-facing site). Clause two, results reporting: 'Summary and participant-level-anonymised results will be posted to the registry and one peer-reviewed journal within twelve months of study completion or termination, irrespective of direction or magnitude of effect; the principal investigator holds publication authority and the agreement contains no right of sponsor veto, only a review-for-medical-and-confidential-content period of thirty days.' Enforcement: named-owner-and-date in the agreement, the institution's own open-access policy as fallback, and the pre-agreed escalation (registry auto-posting where the sponsor stalls, which the difficult-vaccine-and-device-agreements increasingly accept). Clause three, post-trial access: 'Participants who benefit from the investigational product during the trial will have continued access, at defined cost-bearing, until it is otherwise provided through the health system or the product is licensed and available; the plan is filed with the ethics application and its status is a standing item at the trial's annual governance review.' Enforcement: the plan's existence as condition of site sign-off and of the community-engagement-committee's continued endorsement - because the clause without the review is the sentence every sponsor has signed and every participant-forum has watched fail. The drafting note: the clauses are unremarkable individually; their function is cumulative - they make the trial's promises auditable after the money has moved on, which is what research ethics has always been for.**

::: exercise Handle the incidental finding {level=1}
Your research MRI protocol's automated report flags an unexplained pituitary lesion in a healthy volunteer enrolled in a cognition study; the consent said 'incidental findings will be reviewed by a neuroradiologist and communicated where clinically important'. Walk the next seventy-two hours.
::: solution
**The-findings-plan-in-action ([[#def-consent]], [[clinical-skills/medical-imaging]]): The protocol is the script: the pre-specified pathway (research-radiologist-confirmation, the clinical-grade-reread-with-the-volunteer's-consent-to-treat-results-clinically, the named-clinician-owner-who-calls - not the database manager, not the student), and the consent's-adjudication-panel question ('clinically important' meant something when she signed it - the plan's criteria and who applies them). Seventy-two hours: the same-day-or-next-day call with the finding in plain words and the uncertainty kept (an unexplained pituitary incidentaloma is common and mostly inert - the prevalence data and the named-next-test carry more relief than 'we will look into it'), the endocrine-and-imaging-follow-up-booked-not-advised, the 'this is not why you came and it does not end your participation' sentence, and the documentation (the disclosure, the understanding check, the plan's record - the research file and the clinical record in the same story). The governance afterglow: the case enters the study's-incidentals-log (the rate-and-outcome audit that tells the next protocol whether its plan works - the field's finding being that unlogged incidentals accumulate harm quietly), and the honest reckoning if the consent had been silent (the dilemma the plan exists to prevent - which is why the new consents write the category-list and the recontact rules before the first scan, not at the radiologist's elbow). The volunteer's takeaway sentence: 'we found something we did not expect; here is what it probably is, here is what we are doing tomorrow, and you will hear it from a doctor who will keep hearing from you.'**

::: exercise Teach the ghost {level=2}
A device company drafts your department's evaluation manuscript, chooses the analysts, and asks that its statistician be 'acknowledged'; your registrars did the data collection and the writing of the methods. Set out the authorship and disclosure ruling and the internal policy that prevents renegotiation per-paper.
::: solution
**Authorship-as-distributive-justice ([[#def-oversight]], [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]]): The ruling on the four ICMJE criteria (substantial-contribution-plus-drafting-or-critical-revision-plus-final-approval-plus-accountability): the company statistician who analysed can be an author only if the other three are met and the affiliation-and-role disclosed - 'acknowledged' when the contribution is writing-for-hire the draft nobody signs, and the ghost-by-any-other-name rule cuts both ways: the company does not get to be invisible in the byline and visible in the market. The registrars' entitlements: data-collection-plus-methods-drafting is a substantial contribution - the first-author-and-methods-author positions are theirs by right not courtesy, and the 'we added the consultant's name for the citations' habit is the gift-authorship half of the same corruption the department should police before it lectures industry on the ghost half. The disclosure architecture: the corresponding-author's-guarantor-role-named, the full sponsor-role statement (who designed, who analysed, who could-comment-before-submission versus who-decided, the data-availability position), and the agreements' publication-freedom clause (the one the hosting-contract should have contained - see the registration-clause exercise: prevention lives in contracts, not consciences). The standing policy that prevents the re-negotiation: the department's authorship charter (contributor-roles-taxonomy-stated-at-project-start-and-signed, the company-collaboration-manuscripts-reviewed-by-the-research-governance-office-before-submission, and the annual authorship audit sampling the bylines against the contribution logs - the mechanism that makes the charter a practice). The sentence for the company medical lead, polite and final: 'your statistician is welcome as an author if the criteria fit and the disclosure is complete; what we cannot publish is a paper whose real authors are a contract.'**

::: exercise Answer the parent of your participant {level=1}
A parent of your trial participant rings: 'the nurse said his scan showed nothing new, but I read the research consent says findings get returned - is my son being hidden from?' Give the answer, then the systems-fix if the answer is 'it depends'.
::: solution
**Consent-interface-with-clinical-care ([[#def-consent]]): The answer: research scans are read to research protocols, which are not clinical protocols - a 'nothing new' research read can legitimately mean 'no research-protocol finding and nothing the clinician needs today' while a pre-specified incidental-findings pathway governs what gets returned (the category list and the adjudication the consent described), and the parent deserves the difference stated plainly, the actual report shared if that is the policy, and the clinical-team's-same-reading confirmed. The verification before the phone-call-lullabies: pull the study's findings-plan (the category list, who adjudicates, the communication route), check the radiology report's actual status (research-versus-clinical-grade-reads-are-different-documents-in-some-trusts - which is the next systems-fix), and ring the clinical team so the family hears one account. The 'it depends' fix: the consent's language should have named the pathway concretely enough to answer this call from the form - the re-consent-and-material-amendment trigger when consent text produces this confusion repeatedly, the plain-English findings-section template your research office adopts, and the one-record principle (where feasible, research imaging of clinical relevance is stored and flagged in the clinical record so the treating team and the family are never reading different documents about the same brain). The relationship truth: the suspicion behind the question ('the study is hiding something from us') is usually the sound of two record-systems talking past each other, and the durable fix is architectural - the consent, the report and the clinician saying the same thing in the same words, which is the research-ethics-and-clinical-ethics seam this whole lesson lives on.**

::: exercise Design the governance {level=3}
Your academic health science centre will now license its clinical image archives to AI companies as a standing programme. Draft the governance instrument: the consent basis, the identifiability standard, the benefit and conflict rules, the publication-freedom guarantee, and the accountability outputs - each with who decides and how it is published.
::: solution
**An-archive-governance-charter ([[#def-oversight]], [[#def-consent]], [[public-health/health-systems-economics]]): The consent basis: public-interest-and-justified-approval-first (a confidentiality-committee route with defined public-benefit tests plus a genuine, advertised, no-detriment opt-out for the archive - consent-substitute-as-governance not-as-loophole), with the bargain documented in one page the patients can actually read. The identifiability standard: the anonymisation-assertion-banned-unless-the-standards-body-test-is-documented-and-reviewable, otherwise pseudonymised-with-controlled-release (the trusted-research-environment/training-environments-within-firewall model - data do not leave, models do, and the linkage-keys governance sits with an independent custodian not the commercial office). The benefit and conflict rules: the revenue policy decided before the first deal (research-reinvestment-percentages-named, no-exclusive-locks-longer-than-the-science-justifies, no-clinical-claim-without-independent-external-validation-with-pre-registered-protocols), and the interest-disclosure architecture (every derived-publication-carries-the-funding-and-data-source-and-role statements generated from the register-entry-not-from-memory). The publication-freedom guarantee: contract-clause - the centre will not license data for studies whose results cannot be published whatever their direction, the review-for-confidentiality period bounded and non-discretionary, and the pre-registration requirement mirrored onto the licensed analyses (the registry-clause from the exercises, now standing policy). The accountability outputs: the public register of every data licence (partner, dataset, purpose, benefit-terms), the annual archive-governance report to the board with patient representatives voting on renewals, the algorithmic-audit-right for deployed derivatives (the distributional-performance-benchmark-by-protected-group duty the justice chapter wrote), and the revocation-route (a licence breaching the charter loses data-access - the enforcement that distinguishes a charter from a poster). The drafting test: write each clause so a journalist, a regulator and your best patient-advocate could each find the sentence they would use to hold you to it - because one of them will.**
