End-of-life ethics is where the chapter on principles finally pays its tuition: the killing-letting-die distinction tested in practice, proportionality written as infusion rates, and the law's line between pain relief and euthanasia maintained by the doctrine of double effect's most-scrutinised paragraph. **This chapter works through refusing and withdrawing treatment (the equivalence doctrine and its contested edges, artificial nutrition and hydration's jurisprudence, advance decisions and surrogate judgement at life's end), symptom management's ethics (titration, the double effect and its critics, palliative sedation's boundary work), the assisted-dying debate as it now stands internationally (the referenda-and-statutes wave, the safeguard architectures and their empirical critics, and the disability-rights objection's force), and the afterword questions (organ donation after circulatory death's ethics, donation-transfusion conflicts, death determination's law-and-ritual interface, and the bereavement duties that finish the case).**

## Refusing and withdrawing

::: definition Withholding, withdrawing and the equivalence doctrine {#def-withdraw}
**The law's central move in the English-and-commonwealth tradition (the Bland-and-parallel-nutrition-case law, the ventilator-withdrawal litigations) is equivalence: withholding a treatment not owed and withdrawing one begun are the same act judged by the same reasons, so 'we started it, we must continue' is a feeling, not a doctrine - a settlement that follows from the analysis that the intervention is medicine's means, and if the means no longer serve the patient's ends the licence to use it lapses. The resistance is not only sentimental: the psychological and narrative differences are real (the 'withdrawal-as-killing-phenomenology' studies of staff and family distress, the ritual asymmetry that makes stopping feel like an act when starting felt like a failure), and the ethical literature splits accordingly (the 'symbolic-acts-and-commitments' defence of a weak withdrawal/withholding difference versus the consistency argument that patients should not die differently depending on when a machine arrived). Artificial nutrition and hydration carried the doctrine's growth-pains (the Quinlan-and-Cruzan-era ventilator cases, the Schiavo-continuation-litigation's family-war showcase, and the 'is-a-feeding-tube-a-medical-treatment-or-basic-care' argument whose resolution varies by jurisdiction - the American state-law 'food-and-water-unless-imminent-death' clauses versus the commonwealth totality-of-best-interests approach - with persistent clinical consensus that tube-feeding advanced dementia does not prevent aspiration or prolong meaningful survival, the trials evidence that settled the medical question while the values question kept moving). Advance decisions and substitutes at life's end (the 'refusal-of-life-sustaining-treatment-as-binding-when-valid-and-applicable' common-law rule, the proxy-judgement literature's finding that surrogates systematically overestimate patients' willingness to endure states they themselves would refuse - the 'surrogate-prediction-bias' studies that justify the goals-conversation while the patient can still give it, and the 'life-is-always-worth-living' best-interests presumption that the courts apply with increasing sensitivity to burdens and person-specific meanings, the 'consent-not-needed-to-forgo-treatment-but-cannot-consent-to-killing' asymmetry that structures every dying-Parkinson's-and-ALS-refusal case). The futility/coverage question at the ventilator (the 'non-beneficial-treatment' policies' second-version-era institutional procedures - the transfer-offer mechanism, the day-count notice, and the 'disagreement-without-abandonment' framing) plus the 'potentially-unacceptable-treatment' distinction (the 'treatment-may-be-offered-but-not-demanded-not-the-same-as-treatment-is-harmful' clarification that keeps the semantics out of the courtroom), and the 'trial-of-limitation' compromise whose honest function is time-for-adjustment for families, with its own documented burdens. The chapter's through-line: the questions are never only 'may we stop' but 'who decides, on what evidence about the person, with what support for the dying and the staff as the decision's meaning is lived'.**
:::

## Symptom and sedation

::: definition Proportionate treatment, the double effect, and palliative sedation {#def-symptom}
**The analgesia-at-life's-end structure: intent, foresight, proportionality (the doctrine-of-double-effect apparatus - relieving refractory suffering is the intended effect, respiratory depression and possible life-shortening at high doses foreseen but not intended, and the proportionality between the good and the foreseen harm judged by symptom-severity; the empirical substructure the palliative literature provides: the 'opioids-at-palliative-doses-do-not-shorten-survival' cohort-and-audit studies that make the doctrine's hardest application mostly a non-issue clinically, while the terminal-titration case remains the paradigm the doctrine exists for). The critics' best forms: the intent-foresight-distinction-is-psychological (the 'we-know-the-deaths-will-come-and-dose-for-it' analysis that calls the doctrine bad faith) answered by the clinical observation that intent-is-real-and-observable-in-practice (the 'we-document-our-reasoning-and-our-monitoring' response: the doctrine's function is less metaphysical than accountability-forcing - it makes the clinician state which thing they are doing); the 'letting-die-versus-killing-collapses-at-high-enough-dose' argument answered at the boundary by palliative sedation's own protocol discipline. Palliative (terminal) sedation's boundary work: the indication (refractory symptom-and-terminal-agitation definitions - the 'sedation-for-existential-suffering' sub-debate where symptom-refractoriness itself is contested), the proportionality architecture (minimum-depth-and-duration-for-relief, the 'proportionate-versus-deliberate-deep' distinction, time-limited-trials with reassessment), the hydration question (the sedated-patient-with-withdrawn-artificial-fluids controversy - the 'is-hydration-comfort-or-treatment-at-this-point' split, and the thromboembolism-and-secretions trade-offs the family must be told), and the death-timing statistics (the 'sedation-to-death-interval' audit literature - median days, the minority of prolonged cases that generate the ethical friction), plus the law's position (the sedation-as-treatment-of-symptom status everywhere, with the 'intended-to-hasten-death' cases' prosecution-boundary being the line the protocol-ethics exists to keep uncontested). The sedation case-law's cousin - continuous deep sedation until death-with-concurrent-withdrawal-of-life-sustaining-treatment (the Dutch-and-Belgian-practice-adjacent data the supreme-court-reviews examined, the 'is-this-euthanasia-in-prolonged-sleep' accusation versus the 'symptom-management-and-allowing-natural-death-in-combination' defence, and the empirical finding that well-governed services' audits show the distinction is practised honestly in the overwhelming majority - an ethics-of-institutions point as much as a philosophical one). The chapter's clinical-ethics payload: every sedation or titration order should answer four questions in the record - what symptom is refractory and how we know, what depth and duration proportionality requires, what we are not doing and why, and what the plan is for the people watching (the family's presence-and-understanding duty, because the double effect is a doctrine about minds and the only evidence of ours is our documentation and our company).**
:::

## Assisted dying

::: definition The assisted-dying argument and its safeguards {#def-assisted}
**The terminology first (physician-assisted suicide - the patient self-administers; voluntary euthanasia - the clinician administers; the distinction's moral weight varies by jurisdiction and by hand, and the empirical outcome-data suggest patients' reasons and the safeguards matter more than the last hand); the case for: the autonomy-and-compassion combination (the 'bodily-integrity-and-the-right-shape-of-dying' argument from the death-quality literature, and the 'the-Practice-already-exists-in-terminal-sedation-and-withdrawal-without-consistency-or-choice' honest observation), and the terminal-sedation-versus-assisted-dying symmetry argument that dominates current parliaments; the case against: the sanctity-and-inviolability tradition (the 'equal-protection-for-the-disabled-and-dependent' argument with its sharpest form - that a lethal-option menu changes what others' care costs and means), the slippery-slope evidence's honest state (the 'Oregon-versus-Belgium-and-Netherlands-comparison' literature: the 'strict-safeguards-have-not-yet-become-slope' camp versus the 'criteria-have-drifted-and-psychiatric-and-disability-cases-arrived' camp, and the methodological stalemate about whether case-count-growth-is-slope-or-normalisation), and the disability-movement's standing objection (the 'who-would-feel-expected-to-die' testimony and the justice-limb of the argument that the autonomy defences under-weight). The safeguard architectures as actually built (the Oregon physician-series' eligibility core - capacity, terminal-diagnosis-within-six-months-or-twelve-months-variants, two-physician-and-request-process-including-a-written-request-and-witnesses-and-a-psychiatric-or-specialist-consult-trigger, the self-administration requirement, the reporting-with-independent-review, the 'conscience-and-no-institutional-participation' clauses, and the 'end-of-life-options' counselling-with-palliative-referral-mandated design), and the Canadian-and-Belgian-and-Dutch-models' differences (mature-decision-capacity-versus-advance-request-coverage, the disability-and-mental-illness-eligibility controversies with their 'is-this-the-slope-arrived' readings, the 'death-as-continuation-of-care-with-clinician-review-committees' governance and its 'review-committees-normalising' critics). The ballot-box-and-parliament-era data the clinician should carry: referenda-and-legislative-votes'-patterns (the 'elite-versus-public-tempo' finding that the profession lags the electorate), the 'implementation-studies' emerging findings (the 'conscientious-objection-and-access-in-rural-Australia' report, the 'the-Oregon-participation-rates-stay-low-but-reassurance-effects-are-broad' observation that touches the 'palliative-care-substitution' argument on both sides), and the current legislative-and-judicial process's unresolved design question: whether the conscientious-objection-and-effective-access-architecture (this chapter's neighbouring definition) can be built at scale for a service with death inside it. The clinical-competence position regardless of conviction: know your jurisdiction's law and process exactly, know your service's conscience-and-referral architecture before the first request, document the request-and-the-exploration-underneath-it (the 'request-as-communication' literature: the wish for death usually speaks about exhaustion, loss-of-role, burden-fears and treatable-symptoms, and the palliative-medicine response to a request - exploring, optimising, not merely deferring nor complying - is now evidenceed practice), and know that most requests are not final (the 'ambivalence-is-the-modal-state' data, which makes the ethics of the conversation identical in every jurisdiction: presence, honesty about prognosis, and the un-abandonable relationship).**
:::

::: example The tube in dementia {#ex-tube}
A nursing-home resident with advanced Alzheimer's, non-verbal at baseline, aspirates repeatedly; the family splits - half 'he would never want this', half 'you are starving him'. The medical officer proposes 'no further antibiotics, hand-feeding only'; the daughter is a nurse and threatens legal action.
::: solution
**Care-versus-treatment-in-advanced-dementia ([[#def-withdraw]], [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]]): The evidence to state plainly: the tube-feeding literature in advanced dementia (no reduction in aspiration, no survival-or-quality gain, the restraints-and-agitation-and-oral-care-superiority findings) makes hand-feeding-with-aggressive-mouth-care the medicine, not the abandonment; antibiotics-for-each-recurrent-aspiration-versus-a-ceiling-decision is a separate proportionality question with its own honest answer (time-limited-trials-with-review, comfort-first-framing, and the 'we-will-treat-each-episode's-burden-and-benefit' position). The substituted-judgement work: the 'he would never want this' claim is testable - the prior-values interview (the old man's own statements, his role as a carer, his church and his will's silences), and the daughter-nurse's 'starving' frame answered with the distinction the family needs: we are stopping a treatment that does not work, not a feeding that does, and here is what feeding a dying person means now (the comfort-feeding protocols and the swallowing-risk conversations, with dietitians and the hospice team in the room). The process that survives litigation: the best-interests meeting with the whole family and the advocate, the minutes that record both daughters' positions and the reasoning, the second-opinion offer, the time-limited-trial structure so nothing irreversible is decided in fear, and the court-of-protection route used when the deadlock is about power rather than evidence - the referral being the system working, not the family failing. The long game: the home's advance-care-planning-with-when-to-transfer-and-when-not-to protocols, and the staff support (the 'feeding-the-dying-is-the-moral-heart-of-nursing' literature's warning that staff carry these decisions for years), because the daughter's next complaint will be about how it was said more than what was decided.**
:::

::: example The sedation transfer {#ex-sedation}
A 44-year-old with glioblastoma, chemorefractory vomiting and terminal agitation, is being sedated proportionately at a hospice; he has deteriorated, is not rousable, and his parents ask the acute hospital 'to stop the sedation and try again'. The hospice and family call you, the palliative-medicine consultant on call.
::: solution
**Sedation-boundaries-under-pressure ([[#def-symptom]], [[nervous-system/motor-systems]], [[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]]): The clinical audit first: refractoriness re-checked (the vomiting-and-agitation causes re-screened - raised-intracranial-pressure-pattern, steroid-optimisation-and-antiemetic-ladder completeness, the delirium-workup-within-the-dying-days-realism) because the family's request is sometimes the best symptom-monitor you get, and the sedation-depth-and-plan restated (proportionate-to-symptom-not-to-survival, the time-limited-review cadence documented). The conversation with the parents: the 'stopping-to-try-again' request answered with the double truth (we can lighten and see what returns - consciousness, and the vomiting and the agitation; and the previous attempt's record is the map), the 'is-he-starving/is-he-suffocating' questions answered with the examination findings and the medication plan's intent, and the presence-and-ritual support that converts watching into caring (the 'sitting-with-the-sedated' guidance for families, the music-and-touch-and-goodbye-logistics that the bereavement literature says they need permission to do). The governance when a family escalates: the records shared without defensiveness (the titration-charts-and-reviews-and-consent-conversations, the sedation-protocol-and-indication stated in the transfer letter if the acute team asks), the second-senior-palliative-review offered before any external one is imposed, and the MDT-with-family meeting that includes the hospice nurses whose continuity the parents are really contesting. The ethical register for the trainee note: palliative sedation's legitimacy rests on proportionality plus refractoriness plus transparency - a sedation that can explain itself (to the family, to the inspector, to the coroner's file afterwards) is the ethic, and the family's anger is usually grief with a question in it; answer the question, keep the comfort, and do not let the escalation drag the team into either stopping a needed sedation or hardening a protocol against the people who matter.**

## Where this leads

The clinical symptom work in [[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]] and [[geriatrics-palliative/palliative-care]] and [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]], the dementia applications in [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]], the withdrawing-and-futility procedures in [[emergency-critical-care/critical-care]], consent machinery in [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]], allocation and justice in [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]], and death certification and donation in [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]].

::: summary
- Withholding and withdrawing are the same judgment; the differences that matter are psychological and procedural, and the ethics's job is consistency plus support - for patients and for the staff who carry the act's meaning.
- Nutrition and hydration at life's end split into treatment and basic care differently across laws; the dementia-tube-feeding evidence is clear, the values dispute is not, and the process (substituted judgement done properly, time-limited trials, courts when necessary) is the honest container.
- Symptom titration and palliative sedation are governed by refractoriness, proportionality and transparency; the double effect is less metaphysics than accountability discipline, and its practice-form is the documented reasoning and the company kept.
- Assisted dying's international wave makes law-and-safeguard literacy a clinical competence: the models' eligibility cores, the drift-debate's honest stalemate, and the request-as-communication response that is the same wherever the law sits.
- Organ donation after circulatory death, death determination's public trust, and bereavement's duties extend the chapter past the death: end-of-life ethics finishes in the coroner's court, the transplant theatre and the funeral, and the clinician's obligations do not stop at the last obs.
- The chapter's clinical summary: decisions at life's end are legitimate when they are about the patient's ends, made through process the doubters can inspect, and accompanied by loyalty nobody can mistake for abandonment.
:::

::: example The time-limited trial {#ex-trial}
A 78-year-old with end-stage COPD and pneumonia is intubated 'as a trial'; day three, the family reads the improvement in saturations as recovery and rejects any ceiling talk; the registrar wants to 'give the family time' and the consultant wants to set the review meeting now.
::: solution
**Trials-need-definitions([[#def-withdraw]], [[emergency-critical-care/critical-care]], [[respiratory/respiratory-failure]]): The registrar's instinct is right and incomplete: the time-limited trial is the field's best conflict technology, but only if it was defined at intubation (the 'what-would-have-to-improve, by when, judged how' triple documented at the decision-moment - the literature's finding that undefined 'trials' become permanent defaults is exactly this family's trajectory), and the saturations improvement is the misreading to pre-empt (the 'the-monitor-is-not-the-patient' conversation: what improved, what did not, and what she could not do yesterday she cannot do today). The consultant's move is the procedure: the named-review-date-with-the-same-people-who-made-the-original-agreement, the 'we-are-still-in-the-trial-and-here-is-what-day-seven-will-decide' framing that keeps the family inside the plan rather than opposing it, and the 'what-would-make-you-change-your-mind-and-what-would-make-us' question that tests whether anyone's position is falsifiable. When day seven says no: the ceiling stated with the same specificity as the trial (the 'we-will-not-re-intubate-and-we-will-treat-every-infection-that-will-not-prolong-suffering' sentence), the extubation-as-hospice-transfer-not-withdrawal choreography (the symptom-first preparation, the family's presence-options including their fear of being asked to leave, and the 'we-are-not-stopping-caring-we-are-changing-what-cure-means' line that the staff also need said to themselves), and the second-opinion-and-ethics-committee-offer-before-the-family-has-to-demand-it. The teaching point: the trial's ethical power is epistemic and relational at once - it buys time for prognosis to resolve and for the family to move, and the failure mode this ward shows is running the clock without the definitions that make the end of the trial a decision rather than a fight.**
:::

::: example The certificate and the family {#ex-certificate}
A death in your palliative unit: the family want 'heart failure' on the certificate because 'his job's pension needs the wording for the children'; the dying was from the underlying cancer, the certification rules are strict, and the widow is crying.
::: solution
**Candour-to-the-dead-and-the-living([[#def-withdraw]], [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]]): The law first: the certificate states the causal chain to the best of knowledge (the underlying-cause rule, not the mechanism-of-death wish-list), and false certification is the kind of lie that outlives the mourner - the coroner-or-medical-examiner-referral-and-the-medical-director-and-bereaved-family-were-then-unravelled-when-insurance-reads-the-notes literature exists. The humanity in the same breath: the pension-and-benefits question usually has a truthful answer (the 'what-the-policy-actually-needs-is-the-death-certificate-and-its-sequence-not-the-clinicians-opinion' signposting, and the route walked with her: the employer-and-union-schemes, the funeral-fund-and-benefits-paperwork, the bereavement organisations, and a supporting letter explaining the timeline if the correct certificate is administratively awkward). The craft: the 'I cannot write what did not cause his death, and I will move heaven and earth with you on what his death leaves owed to you' sentence - the combination that keeps truth and loyalty in one room, and the unit's practice that the conversation happens after the body is cared-for-and-the-family-have-had-tea-and-a-day. The teaching add-on: the after-death visit or call (the bereavement-outcome literature's 'the-team-who-calls-in-the-dark-month' finding), the coroner-and-prosecutor-and-registrar-navigation offered before the family's second week, and the staff debrief on the request itself, because 'make-the-paperwork-work-for-her' is grief speaking and teams that hear it well get asked by the next family and the next.**

## Exercises

::: exercise Explain the doctrine {level=1}
A medical student asks how you can 'increase the morphine knowing it might stop his breathing' but not 'give an overdose to end it'. Give the ninety-second answer a student will still believe at their first death.
::: solution
**Intent-is-a-fact([[#def-symptom]], [[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]]): The ninety seconds: 'they are different acts and we can prove we mean it. In the first, the goal is the breathlessness and we give the smallest dose that reliably settles it, we watch what we do, we can reverse or adjust it, and if he dies sooner than we hoped, that was not the plan and was not how the medicine worked. In the second, death is the mechanism of relief - the only way that dose helps is by killing - and no amount of good motive changes which thing we did. Medicine has always accepted foreseeable deaths in service of goals the patient shares - anaesthesia, chemotherapy, the sedation we use every week. What it has never accepted is making the death the treatment. And here is the part the ward teaches: we keep the difference honest by writing down which one we are doing, and by the family hearing the same sentence from us, and if you ever cannot tell which you are doing, that is not a philosophy problem, it is a page-the-senior-and-slow-down problem.' The documentation add-on (the titration-chart-and-rationale and the monitored-and-reviewable pattern) makes the doctrine operational rather than decorative, and the student who has seen both kinds of order written side by side never forgets the difference.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Spot the drift {level=2}
Your palliative service's audit finds: deep sedation orders without documented refractoriness in a quarter of cases; the median sedation-to-death interval falling; families asked 'would you like us to keep him comfortable all the way' in handover language; and one nurse's exit interview mentioning 'the shortcut talk'. Investigate and respond, distinguishing process failures from boundary failures.
::: solution
**Audit-as-ethics-instrument([[#def-symptom]], [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]): The investigation design: the record review (the refractoriness-criteria-evidence-what-exists-versus-what-was-tried - the 'documented-refractoriness' definition operationalised before the reviewers look), the interval-trend analysis (case-mix-versus-practice decomposition - are the patients dying sooner anyway, a population question the sedation-clinician-interviews literature separates), the language audit (the 'comfortable-all-the-way' phrase's semantic drift from comfort-care-to-sedation-depth-stated - the handover-phrase study method), and the confidential interviews (the 'shortcut talk' item handled as a moral-distress-and-normalisation signal with the safe-reporting route the safety-science literature provides). The likely pattern: process erosion first (the indication-documentation habit lapsing under workload, the protocol becoming a ritual, the review-cadence skipped when families are grateful) with a minority of genuine boundary-blurring (the language audit's evidence usually the leading indicator). The response by layer: the protocol re-launch (the refractoriness-criteria-and-time-limited-review-and-documentation-standards taught as clinical-medicine-not-compliance), the double-signature-or-second-review-for-prolonged-deep-sedation (the Dutch-and-Belgian-reporting-architecture's-clinical-governance-transfer), the language-fix (the 'what-we-are-doing-and-why' script for family conversations, the same words in every handover), the staff-support-and-speaks-up-system (the exit-interview-as-safety-signal channel made routine), and the mortality-and-sedation-audit brought to the mortality-and-morbidity-meeting-with-a-name-on-it-because-the-whistle-only-works-if-someone-keeps-the-score. The line to hold publicly: a well-governed sedation service is the reason a jurisdiction can resist legalising something else, and protecting that reputation is itself a patient-safety argument.**
:::

::: exercise Design the service policy {level=2}
Write the assisted-dying-request-response policy for a palliative-care service in a jurisdiction where assisted dying is legal: the request's handling, the conscientious-objector staff architecture, the 'request-as-communication' clinical protocol, and the data you will publish annually.
::: solution
**A-policy-for-a-legal-landscape([[#def-assisted]], [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]]): The request-handling core: every request answered within-hours-not-weeks with the same three commitments (you will not be abandoned; we will explore what the request is about - the exhaustion-and-burden-and-fear-and-treatable-symptoms assessment; and the legal-options-are-part-of-your-care-information-whatever-we-personally-believe), the 'two-voice rule' (the objecting clinician's duties: handover-with-warmth, full-information-availability, no-obstruction, no-preaching), and the service-level guarantee that the lawful request reaches its lawful destination regardless of individual conscience (the 'conscience-at-the-clinician-access-at-the-service' split the UK-trial-bodies-and-Kaiser-Study-of-Oregon-physicians-reports converge on). The staff architecture: the declaration-of-conscience-recorded-at-hire-without-stigma, the rota-design-that-does-not-quietly-assign-the-work-to-the-willing-while-the-objectors-cover-wards (the 'conscience-must-not-become-career-structure' rule the nursing-regulators' guidance makes explicit), the team's-debrief-and-moral-distress-provision for all positions, and the 'the-patient-never-meets-an-objector-who-cannot-hide-it-badly' professionalism check. The clinical protocol (the request-as-communication evidence: the structured-exploration-interview, the depression-and-delirium-and-existential-distress-screening-with-psychiatric-consultation-routes, the symptom-optimisation-trial-offer-with-its-documentation, the family-meeting-with-the-patient's-lead, and the 'ambivalence-is-normal-and-time-is-medicine' rebooking rule). The annual data: requests-received, exploration-outcomes, symptom-and-psychiatric-interventions-delivered, referrals-and-their-completion-times, sedation-and-death-interval-audits, staff-conscience-declarations-count-not-names, and the equity-table (who-asked-by-age-sex-ethnicity-deprivation-diagnosis-because-the-'who-feels-expected-to-die' question must be monitored with local numbers or the justice-debate stays hypothetical). The publication honesty clause: the service reports its own outcome distribution even when uncomfortable, because in this field the services that stop publishing are the ones the critics are right about.**

::: exercise Teach the family meeting {level=3}
Design the 45-minute simulation for senior trainees: one family, one patient-trajectory, three trainees rotating the lead role, with the assessment rubric's five behaviours. Include the two scripted family responses that separate the technically-correct from the trustworthy.
::: solution
**A-sim-with-teeth([[#def-withdraw]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]): The case: the day-seven-COPD-trial-from-the-earlier-example, reused deliberately - the trainee discovers the definitions at minute three, which is the lesson. The rubric's five behaviours: (1) establishes-where-they-are ('what have you been told since Tuesday?' before any new information - the heart-attack-rate of bad meetings is the un-cleared misunderstanding), (2) names-the-uncertainty-and-the-hopes-separately ('he may live weeks with the machine; he will not come home; both are true'), (3) recommends-instead-of-surveying (the 'family-meeting-recommendation-versus-menu' trials' finding that a values-anchored recommendation shortens suffering and distress without reducing satisfaction), (4) holds-the-silence-and-the-next-question (the 'ask-permit-state-check' loop with the timing measured, and (5) closes-with-the-plan-and-the-person ('this is what we will do tonight, this is when we meet again, I am Dr-X and this is how you reach me'). The two scripted responses: the 'so you are saying you want him to die' bait (the correct move: refuse the frame, re-anchor the goal - 'no - we are saying the machine cannot give him the thing he told us he wanted; here is what we can'), and the 'my mother says we must not give up' triangulation (the correct move: honour the absent family member, widen the room - 'tell me what your mother hopes for; let us talk to her together tomorrow'), because the trustworthy clinician is the one who converts the family's war into the family's meeting. The debrief: the family-actor's-video-playback-with-the-trainees-own-words-returned-because-habitual-phrases-are-only-audible-from-outside, and the 'write-one-sentence-you-will-use-on-a-real-Thursday-night' close - which is what simulation is for.**
