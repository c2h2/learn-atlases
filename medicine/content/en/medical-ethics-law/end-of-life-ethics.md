In April 1989, 17-year-old Anthony Bland was crushed in the Hillsborough football stadium disaster. He suffered severe brain damage and remained in a persistent vegetative state, alive and breathing on his own but with no awareness, fed through a tube. After three years, his doctors and family agreed that tube feeding should stop. The hospital asked the courts whether this would be lawful. In 1993 the House of Lords said that it would: tube feeding was medical treatment, and continuing it was no longer in his best interests. He died in March 1993, after it was withdrawn.

Most deaths in the United Kingdom now follow a decision, explicit or not, about what treatment to give and what to stop. These decisions are among the most common and the most consequential in medicine. This chapter covers withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, decisions about cardiopulmonary resuscitation, symptom relief and sedation at the end of life, the law and arguments about assisted dying, and some legal duties after death.

## Withholding and withdrawing treatment {#withdrawing}

::: definition Life-sustaining treatment and its withdrawal {#def-withdraw}
**Life-sustaining treatment** is any treatment that has the potential to postpone death, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, antibiotics in some circumstances, and **clinically assisted nutrition and hydration** (CANH), meaning nutrition or fluids given by tube or drip.

**Withholding** a treatment means not starting it; **withdrawing** means stopping a treatment already started. In English law and in GMC guidance there is no legal or ethical difference in principle between them: both are decisions not to provide a treatment, and both must be justified by the same considerations.
:::

Many clinicians feel that stopping a treatment is harder than not starting it. The feeling is understandable but can lead to bad decisions. If withdrawing seemed forbidden, clinicians might hesitate to start treatments that could help, for fear of being unable to stop them. The equivalence principle supports offering **time-limited trials**: starting treatment to see whether it helps, with an agreement to stop if it does not.

::: theorem The limits of the duty to provide treatment {#thm-limits}
In England and Wales:

1. An adult with capacity may **refuse** any treatment, including life-sustaining treatment, and the refusal must be respected.
2. A patient cannot **demand** a treatment that the clinician judges not to be clinically appropriate. Doctors are not obliged to provide treatment they believe would not be of overall benefit, although they should arrange a second opinion if the patient wishes.
3. For an adult who lacks capacity, life-sustaining treatment should be given if, and only if, it is in their **best interests**. The question is not whether it is in their best interests to die, but whether it is in their best interests to receive the treatment.
4. Withdrawing treatment that is no longer in a patient's best interests is an **omission**, not an act of killing, and is lawful when done in good faith in accordance with the law.
:::

::: proof
Point 1 follows from the law of consent: treatment without consent is a battery, and English law protects a refusal by an adult with capacity even when it will lead to death (*Re B (Adult: Refusal of Medical Treatment)*, 2002, where a woman paralysed from the neck down was entitled to have her ventilator switched off).

Point 2 reflects the fact that a right to refuse does not create a right to demand. In *R (Burke) v GMC* (2005), the Court of Appeal held that a patient cannot require a doctor to provide treatment the doctor considers inappropriate, although it accepted that when a patient with capacity wants CANH to keep them alive, withdrawing it would normally be unlawful.

Point 3 was set out by the Supreme Court in *Aintree University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust v James* (2013): the focus is on whether the treatment would benefit the patient, judged widely and from the patient's point of view, not on whether death is in their interests. There is a strong presumption in favour of preserving life, but it can be displaced.

Point 4 comes from *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* (1993). The Lords distinguished between ending life by an act, which is unlawful even with a compassionate motive, and not continuing treatment that is no longer in the patient's best interests, which is lawful because there is then no duty to continue it. Lord Browne-Wilkinson acknowledged that the distinction can seem morally uncomfortable, but held that it reflects the law.
:::

Since *An NHS Trust v Y* [2018] UKSC 46, there is no legal requirement to ask a court before withdrawing CANH from a patient in a prolonged disorder of consciousness, provided the Mental Capacity Act has been followed, relevant guidance observed, and there is agreement on the patient's best interests. If there is disagreement or doubt, an application to the Court of Protection should still be made. National guidance from the British Medical Association and the Royal College of Physicians, endorsed by the GMC, describes the process.

The GMC's guidance *Treatment and care towards the end of life* (2010, updated in 2022 and 2024) applies these principles to practice. It emphasises good communication, early discussion of the patient's wishes, involving those close to the patient, and providing excellent care whatever decisions are made about treatment.

::: warning Withdrawing treatment is not withdrawing care
When a life-sustaining treatment is stopped, the patient still needs and is entitled to the best possible care: relief of pain, breathlessness, thirst and anxiety; mouth care; dignity; time and privacy with family; spiritual support. Phrases such as "withdrawal of care" or "there is nothing more we can do" are inaccurate and hurtful. There is always something to do. Planning for the period after withdrawal, including anticipatory medicines and the involvement of palliative care, is part of the decision ([[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]]).
:::

::: example A time-limited trial {#ex-trial}
A 79-year-old man with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and heart failure is admitted with pneumonia and type 2 respiratory failure. He has never discussed his wishes. He is drowsy but can answer simple questions. The registrar is unsure whether to offer non-invasive ventilation (NIV) and whether to refer to intensive care. How should the team decide?
::: solution
**Medical facts:** NIV may reverse his respiratory acidosis; intubation and ventilation would carry a high risk of not weaning, given his background. Find out his baseline: exercise tolerance, oxygen use, previous admissions, frailty.

**His wishes:** he can still communicate. Ask, simply, what matters to him, and whether he would want breathing support with a mask. Ask his family what he has said before.

**Plan:** offer NIV as a **time-limited trial** with explicit criteria: for example, if his pH and alertness are not improving after 4–6 hours, or he cannot tolerate the mask, NIV will be stopped and care will focus on comfort. Decide in advance whether intubation would be appropriate; for many patients in his position it would not, and a decision not to escalate should be discussed with him and recorded in a treatment escalation plan, along with his resuscitation status.

**Review:** if NIV is stopped because it is not helping, this is a withdrawal of treatment that is not working, and the plan moves to comfort, with opioids for breathlessness and family support.
:::
:::

::: example CANH in advanced dementia {#ex-tube}
A 84-year-old woman with advanced Alzheimer disease in a nursing home has stopped eating and is aspirating. The speech and language therapist finds her swallow unsafe. Her son asks for a feeding tube (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, PEG) because "we cannot let her starve". She has no advance decision or LPA. How should this be decided?
::: solution
**Capacity:** she lacks capacity for the decision, so a best-interests decision is required under the Mental Capacity Act, made by the senior clinician responsible, after consulting her son and the care home staff.

**Evidence:** in advanced dementia, tube feeding has not been shown to prolong life, prevent aspiration pneumonia, heal pressure ulcers or improve comfort, and the procedure carries risks. Reduced eating is often part of the dying process in advanced dementia.

**Her wishes and values:** what would she have wanted? What did she say about illness or about relatives in similar situations? What gives her comfort now?

**Options:** careful hand feeding for comfort ("risk feeding"), with modified textures and acceptance of the risk of aspiration, is usually more appropriate than a PEG.

**Communication:** her son's fear that she will "starve" needs a compassionate answer: she will be offered food and drink she enjoys for as long as she wants it, and her mouth will be kept moist; her decline is caused by the disease, not by lack of food. If agreement cannot be reached, a second opinion and, if necessary, the Court of Protection are available.
:::
:::

## Decisions about cardiopulmonary resuscitation {#cpr}

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is attempted by default when a person's heart stops, unless a decision has been made in advance not to attempt it. In UK hospitals, roughly one in five adults who have CPR after an in-hospital cardiac arrest survive to leave hospital, and far fewer in those with advanced, irreversible illness. CPR can cause rib fractures and other injuries and may leave survivors with brain damage ([[emergency-critical-care/resuscitation]]).

A **do not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation** (DNACPR) decision applies only to CPR. It does not mean that other treatment should be withheld. Joint guidance from the British Medical Association, the Resuscitation Council UK and the Royal College of Nursing, and the GMC's guidance, require that:

- if CPR would not restart the heart and breathing, or would not provide a benefit the patient would value, it need not be offered, but the decision should normally still be **discussed** with the patient;
- if CPR might succeed, the decision depends on whether the patient wants it, weighing the chance of benefit against the burdens;
- for patients who lack capacity, the decision is made in their best interests, after consulting those close to them.

Two cases shaped the duty to discuss. In *R (Tracey) v Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust* [2014] EWCA Civ 822, the Court of Appeal held that Article 8 of the European Convention requires a patient to be involved in a DNACPR decision unless the clinician thinks that discussion would be likely to cause physical or psychological **harm**; distress alone is not a reason to exclude them. In *Winspear v City Hospitals Sunderland* [2015], the High Court held that, for a patient who lacks capacity, the family or others close to them must be consulted where practicable.

Many areas now use the **ReSPECT** process (Recommended Summary Plan for Emergency Care and Treatment), which records a person's priorities and agreed recommendations about emergency care, including CPR, as part of a wider conversation. During the COVID-19 pandemic there were reports of DNACPR decisions applied to groups, such as care home residents or people with learning disabilities, without individual assessment; regulators made clear that such blanket decisions are unacceptable.

::: example A DNACPR conversation {#ex-dnacpr}
A 71-year-old woman with metastatic pancreatic cancer is admitted with sepsis from a blocked biliary stent. She has capacity. The consultant believes that if her heart stopped, CPR would not succeed. The junior doctor thinks it would be "too upsetting" to raise it with her. What does the law require, and how might the conversation go?
::: solution
**The law:** under *Tracey*, she should be told that a DNACPR decision is being made and involved in it, unless there is a real risk of physical or psychological harm. Possible distress is not enough.

**The conversation:** it is usually best framed within a discussion of her overall goals: "Can I talk to you about the treatment we're giving, and about what we would do if you became much more unwell?" Explain the plan to treat the infection. Then: "If your heart were to stop, we would not try to restart it with chest compressions and electric shocks, because with your illness this would not work and could make your last moments distressing. That doesn't change anything else: we will keep treating the infection and keep you comfortable."

**Her response:** she may agree, ask questions, or disagree. If she wants CPR even though the team believes it will not work, she should be offered a second opinion. The doctor is not obliged to provide CPR that will not work, but the decision should be explained, documented and reviewed.

**Record and share:** the form, the discussion and who was present; communicate the decision to her GP and community teams.
:::
:::

## Symptom relief and sedation {#symptom-relief}

Good palliative care controls pain, breathlessness, nausea, agitation and other distress at the end of life with appropriate doses of opioids, antiemetics, sedatives and other drugs, given in response to symptoms ([[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]]). This is lawful and ethical even though the patient is dying. The doctrine of double effect ([[medical-ethics-law/ethical-principles]]) explains why intention matters: treatment intended and titrated to relieve symptoms is different from treatment intended to end life.

The contrast is shown by two cases. In *R v Adams* (1957), the court accepted that a doctor may give treatment necessary to relieve pain even if it incidentally shortens life. In *R v Cox* (1992), Dr Nigel Cox was convicted of attempted murder after injecting potassium chloride into a patient with severe rheumatoid arthritis who had begged him to end her life. Potassium chloride has no pain-relieving effect, so its only purpose could be to cause death.

::: definition Palliative sedation {#def-sedation}
**Palliative sedation** is the monitored use of sedative medication to relieve **refractory** distress (suffering that cannot be controlled by other means) in a patient who is dying, by reducing their awareness. It is proportionate: the lowest level of sedation that relieves the distress is used, and it can be intermittent or continuous. **Continuous deep sedation until death** is reserved for the last days of life. Palliative sedation aims to relieve suffering, not to end life; it differs from euthanasia in intention, in method (titration to symptoms) and in outcome (death results from the underlying illness).
:::

The potential for misuse of end-of-life medication is real. The Gosport Independent Panel (2018) found that, between 1989 and 2000, the lives of over 450 patients at Gosport War Memorial Hospital were shortened by the routine use of opioids without clinical justification. The lesson is not that opioids are dangerous in palliative care, but that their use must be clinically indicated, documented and open to review.

## Assisted dying {#assisted-dying}

::: definition Euthanasia and assisted dying {#def-assisted}
- **Euthanasia** is the intentional ending of a person's life by another person, for the benefit of the person who dies. It is **voluntary** if requested by a person with capacity, **non-voluntary** if the person cannot express a wish (for example, an infant), and **involuntary** if against their wishes.
- **Assisted suicide** is intentionally helping a person to end their own life, for example by providing lethal medication that they take themselves.
- **Assisted dying** is usually used for laws that allow adults who are terminally ill, and who have capacity, to be given lethal medication to take themselves, under safeguards.
- Withdrawing or withholding treatment that is not in a patient's best interests, refusing treatment, and palliative sedation are **not** euthanasia or assisted suicide.
:::

**The law in England and Wales.** Euthanasia is murder, whatever the motive and even with consent. Under s2 of the Suicide Act 1961, it is an offence to do an act capable of encouraging or assisting the suicide or attempted suicide of another person, intending to do so, with a maximum sentence of 14 years. Prosecution requires the consent of the Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP).

Several cases have challenged this. In *Pretty v United Kingdom* (2002), the European Court of Human Rights held that the ban on assisted suicide was a proportionate interference with Diane Pretty's private life. In *R (Purdy) v DPP* (2009), the House of Lords required the DPP to publish a policy on when prosecution would be brought. The **DPP's policy** (2010, updated 2014) lists factors: prosecution is less likely where the person had a clear, settled and informed wish to die and the suspect was wholly motivated by compassion, and more likely where the suspect was acting as a doctor, nurse or other health professional in the course of their care. In *R (Nicklinson) v Ministry of Justice* (2014) and *R (Conway) v Secretary of State for Justice* (2018), the courts held that whether to change the law was a matter for Parliament.

**Parliamentary activity.** The **Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill**, a private member's bill, passed its third reading in the House of Commons in June 2025 but ran out of time in the House of Lords in April 2026. A new bill with the same title was introduced in the Commons in June 2026 and, as of October 2026, is at an early stage. In Scotland, the Scottish Parliament rejected an assisted dying bill at its final stage in March 2026. The law could change; check the current position.

**Other jurisdictions** permit different practices: the Netherlands and Belgium have permitted euthanasia under conditions since 2002; Switzerland does not punish assisting suicide unless the motive is selfish; Oregon's Death with Dignity Act (in force since 1997) and later laws in other US states allow assisted dying for terminally ill adults; Canada has allowed medical assistance in dying since 2016; Australian states (from Victoria in 2019) and New Zealand (2021) have assisted dying laws.

### The arguments {#arguments}

Arguments **for** permitting assisted dying include:

- **Autonomy:** people with capacity should be able to decide the timing and manner of their death.
- **Relief of suffering:** some suffering cannot be fully relieved even with excellent palliative care.
- **Consistency:** the law already permits refusal of life-sustaining treatment and withdrawal of treatment with death as the foreseen result.
- **Current practice:** some people travel abroad to die, often earlier than they would otherwise, or end their lives alone.

Arguments **against** include:

- **Protection of vulnerable people:** people who are elderly, disabled, poor or depressed might feel pressure to choose death to avoid being a burden, and safeguards might not detect this.
- **The slippery slope:** in some jurisdictions eligibility has widened over time, for example from terminal illness to chronic conditions or mental illness.
- **The role of doctors:** helping patients to die may change the doctor–patient relationship and trust.
- **Palliative care:** the priority should be to make excellent palliative care available to everyone.
- **Sanctity of life:** for many religious and secular thinkers, intentionally ending an innocent human life is always wrong.

Professional bodies differ. The BMA adopted a neutral position on assisted dying in 2021; others, including some royal colleges, have been neutral or opposed. Under current law, the GMC's guidance is clear: doctors must not encourage or assist suicide, but they may provide objective information about the law, continue to care for the patient, and respect their right to access their own records.

::: example A request about Switzerland {#ex-dignitas}
A 62-year-old man with motor neurone disease asks his GP for a medical report confirming his diagnosis and prognosis, so that he can apply to a Swiss organisation that provides assisted suicide. What should the GP do?
::: solution
**Listen and explore.** The request deserves a serious, compassionate conversation. What is he most afraid of: choking, dependence, being a burden, loss of control? Many of these fears can be addressed: anticipatory medicines for breathlessness, the option of non-invasive ventilation and of stopping it, advance decisions to refuse treatment, and specialist palliative care. Screen for depression, which is treatable.

**The legal and professional position.** Writing a report specifically to enable him to obtain assisted suicide could amount to assisting suicide, and the DPP's policy treats health professionals acting in that capacity as a factor in favour of prosecution. GMC guidance advises doctors not to provide such reports. However, the patient has a right to access his own records under data protection law, and the GP should not obstruct that.

**Continue care.** Whatever he decides, the GP continues to care for him without judgement, and makes sure he knows he can talk about his wishes openly. The GP should also seek advice from a medical defence organisation.
:::
:::

## After death {#after-death}

Several legal processes follow a death. A doctor or other trained professional **verifies** that death has occurred. In England and Wales, since September 2024, almost all deaths not referred to a coroner are scrutinised by an independent **medical examiner** before the attending doctor completes the Medical Certificate of Cause of Death. Deaths must be referred to the **coroner** when the cause is unknown or may be unnatural, violent or related to medical treatment, or the person died in state detention.

Where death is diagnosed by neurological criteria (brain-stem death), the Academy of Medical Royal Colleges' code of practice sets out the tests. **Organ donation** in Wales (since 2015), England (since 2020) and Scotland (since 2021) uses an opt-out system for most adults: consent is deemed unless the person opted out or falls into an excluded group, but families are always consulted, and specialist nurses approach them.

::: quiz
A 45-year-old woman with capacity, who is ventilator-dependent after a high spinal cord injury, asks for her ventilator to be switched off. She understands that she will die. Which statement is correct in English law?
- [ ] Switching off the ventilator would be assisted suicide.
- [ ] The doctors may refuse because the treatment is effective.
- [x] She is entitled to refuse continued ventilation; it should be withdrawn with sedation and symptom relief as needed, and doctors who object personally should transfer her care.
- [ ] A court order is required before the ventilator can be stopped.
::: solution
This is *Re B* (2002). An adult with capacity may refuse life-sustaining treatment, including continued ventilation. Withdrawing treatment at her request is not assisted suicide or euthanasia; continuing it against her wishes would be unlawful. No court order is needed when capacity is clear. Her care should include planning for the withdrawal, with sedation and opioids to prevent distress. Clinicians who feel unable to take part should hand over to colleagues, without delaying her wishes.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1976 the New Jersey Supreme Court allowed the parents of Karen Ann Quinlan, in a persistent vegetative state, to have her ventilator removed; the case launched modern debates about withdrawing treatment and about hospital ethics committees. In England, *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* (1993) established that tube feeding was medical treatment and could be withdrawn when not in the patient's best interests. *R v Cox* (1992) marked the line between relieving suffering and killing.

Diane Pretty, who had motor neurone disease, lost her case for a guarantee that her husband would not be prosecuted for helping her die (2002). Debbie Purdy, who had multiple sclerosis, won the right to know the DPP's prosecuting policy (2009), and Tony Nicklinson, who had locked-in syndrome, lost his challenge in the Supreme Court (2014). Meanwhile, Cicely Saunders founded St Christopher's Hospice in London in 1967, beginning the modern hospice movement and the argument that good palliative care changes what people ask for at the end of life. Parliament rejected an assisted dying bill in 2015, and in 2025 the House of Commons voted for one for the first time.
:::

::: summary
- Withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment are ethically and legally equivalent in principle; this supports **time-limited trials**.
- Adults with capacity may refuse any treatment (*Re B*); patients cannot demand inappropriate treatment (*Burke*); for adults lacking capacity, the question is whether the treatment is in their **best interests** (*Aintree*). CANH is medical treatment (*Bland*); court approval to withdraw it is needed only where there is disagreement or doubt (*Y*, 2018).
- Withdrawing treatment is never withdrawing care.
- DNACPR decisions concern CPR only; patients must be involved unless discussion would cause harm (*Tracey*), and families consulted when the patient lacks capacity (*Winspear*). Blanket decisions are unacceptable.
- Symptom relief titrated to need is lawful (double effect, *Adams*); acts whose only purpose is to end life are not (*Cox*). Palliative sedation is proportionate relief of refractory distress.
- In England and Wales euthanasia is murder and assisting suicide is an offence (Suicide Act 1961, s2), with prosecution governed by the DPP's policy. As of October 2026, assisted dying legislation has not been enacted in Great Britain, but bills continue to be debated.
- Deaths are scrutinised by medical examiners (since 2024) or referred to the coroner; organ donation uses deemed consent in England, Wales and Scotland.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Act or omission? {level=1}
Classify each as lawful or unlawful in England and Wales, and explain: (a) stopping dialysis at the request of a patient with capacity; (b) giving a lethal injection at the request of a patient with capacity; (c) not starting antibiotics for pneumonia in a dying patient for whom they would not provide overall benefit; (d) increasing morphine to control breathlessness in a dying patient.
::: solution
(a) Lawful: refusal of treatment by an adult with capacity. (b) Unlawful: euthanasia is murder regardless of consent. (c) Lawful: treatment is not in the patient's best interests, so there is no duty to give it. (d) Lawful: symptom relief titrated to need, with the intention of relieving suffering.
:::

::: exercise What DNACPR means {level=1}
A nurse asks whether a patient with a DNACPR form should still receive intravenous antibiotics and be referred to the outreach team if they deteriorate. Answer her.
::: solution
Yes, unless separate decisions have been made about those treatments. A DNACPR decision applies only to CPR. Other treatments, including antibiotics, fluids, oxygen, outreach review and even intensive care, should be considered on their own merits. Confusing DNACPR with "do not treat" is a well-recognised cause of harm. A treatment escalation plan such as ReSPECT makes the wider plan explicit.
:::
:::

::: exercise Survival with a good outcome {level=1 check="0.16"}
In a hospital, 20% of adults who receive CPR after in-hospital cardiac arrest survive to discharge, and 80% of survivors have a good neurological outcome. What proportion of patients receiving CPR survive to discharge with a good neurological outcome? Give the answer as a decimal.
::: solution
$0.20 \times 0.80 = 0.16$. About 16 in 100 patients, or roughly 1 in 6. For patients with advanced, irreversible illness the proportion is much lower, which is why individual assessment matters. Numbers like these should be part of a CPR discussion.
:::
:::

::: exercise Validating an advance refusal {level=2}
A man with severe COPD has a signed and witnessed advance decision stating: "I refuse intubation and ventilation in all circumstances, even if my life is at risk." He is admitted unconscious after a road traffic collision with a head injury and needs intubation for a CT scan and surgery. Is the ADRT applicable?
::: solution
It may not be. The ADRT appears valid (written, signed, witnessed, with the life-at-risk statement). But under s25(4)(c) of the Mental Capacity Act it is not applicable if there are reasonable grounds to believe that circumstances now exist which he did not anticipate and which would have affected his decision. He probably wrote it with an exacerbation of COPD in mind, where ventilation might mean a slow death on a machine. A head injury requiring short-term ventilation for surgery, after which he might return to his previous life, may be a circumstance he did not anticipate, although the words "in all circumstances" weigh the other way. The team should seek any evidence of what he meant (family, GP, the context in which he wrote it). If there is genuine doubt, life-sustaining treatment can be given while the doubt is resolved, if necessary by the Court of Protection.
:::
:::

::: exercise The family's request {level=2}
A woman is dying of heart failure. She lacks capacity. Her daughter asks the team to "give her something to end it now; she wouldn't want to go on like this". How should the team respond?
::: solution
With compassion, recognising the daughter's distress and love. The team should explain clearly that they cannot do anything with the intention of ending her mother's life, because that would be unlawful and wrong. But they should also explain what they can do: ensure her mother is comfortable, with medication titrated to any pain, breathlessness or agitation; stop treatments that are not helping, such as blood tests and observations that disturb her; and support the family to be with her. If the daughter believes her mother is suffering, the team should reassess her symptoms carefully. Palliative care input should be sought.
:::
:::

::: exercise Applying the DPP policy {level=2}
A woman accompanies her husband, who has advanced multiple sclerosis, to Switzerland, where he ends his life with the help of an organisation there. She booked the flights and pushed his wheelchair. Using the DPP's policy, list the factors likely to be relevant to whether she is prosecuted.
::: solution
Her actions are capable of assisting suicide, so an offence may have been committed. Factors **against** prosecution in the policy include: the victim had reached a voluntary, clear, settled and informed decision to commit suicide; the suspect was wholly motivated by compassion; the actions were of only minor encouragement or assistance; the suspect reported the death to the police and cooperated fully. Factors **in favour** would include: the victim was under 18 or lacked capacity, the suspect stood to gain financially in a way that suggested a motive other than compassion, pressure was applied, or the suspect was acting as a health professional caring for him. In most such cases involving family members, the DPP has decided not to prosecute, but each case is considered individually.
:::
:::

::: exercise Arguments about assisted dying {level=3}
Choose one argument for and one argument against legalising assisted dying for terminally ill adults with capacity. For each, state the strongest objection and how a defender might reply.
::: solution
**For: autonomy.** *Objection:* autonomy is exercised in a social context; a request may reflect fear of being a burden or inadequate care, so a "free" choice may be compromised. *Reply:* the same is true of refusals of life-sustaining treatment, which the law respects; the answer is assessment for capacity, coercion and depression, and good palliative care, not denying the choice to everyone.

**Against: protection of the vulnerable.** *Objection:* the evidence from some jurisdictions with long experience does not clearly show disproportionate use by disadvantaged groups; safeguards can be designed. *Reply:* pressure is hard to detect, eligibility has expanded in some countries, and once the option exists, it may change how vulnerable people see their own lives; the harm to a few people wrongly helped to die may outweigh the benefit to others.

A good answer treats both sides fairly and recognises that reasonable people, including clinicians, disagree.
:::
:::

::: exercise A CANH decision without agreement {level=3}
A 38-year-old man has been in a prolonged disorder of consciousness for 14 months after a cardiac arrest, receiving CANH via a gastrostomy. Expert assessments confirm a vegetative state with no realistic prospect of recovery. His parents believe he would not want to live like this. His sister believes that life should always be preserved and that he would want CANH continued. What process should be followed?
::: solution
**Best interests under the MCA:** the question is whether continued CANH is in his best interests, not whether death is. The decision-maker (his consultant) must consider his past and present wishes, feelings, beliefs and values, consulting his family and anyone else interested in his welfare, and the clinical evidence.

**Process:** follow the BMA/RCP guidance on CANH: confirm the diagnosis and prognosis with expert assessment, obtain a second opinion from a senior clinician outside the treating team, hold best-interests meetings with all family members, and record the reasoning.

**Disagreement:** after *An NHS Trust v Y* (2018), a court application is not required where there is agreement, but here there is disagreement within the family. Mediation may help. If it cannot be resolved, the Trust should apply to the Court of Protection, and CANH continues until the court decides.

**Care throughout:** whatever the outcome, his care, comfort and dignity are maintained, and if CANH is withdrawn, it is done with palliative care support and attention to the family.
:::
:::
