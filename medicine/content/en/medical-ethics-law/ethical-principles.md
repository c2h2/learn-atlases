Medical ethics is not a decorative module between biochemistry and clinical medicine: it is the discipline of the residual questions that technical competence cannot answer, and every clinical chapter in this library quietly depends on its four-question apparatus. **This opening chapter works through the principalist framework (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) that dominates anglophone bioethics, its German and continental cousins (dignity, solidarity), the rival theories clinicians actually reason with (casuistry, narrative ethics, care ethics, virtue accounts, contractualism's two versions), the framework's critics (its abstraction, its principlism-vs-principles ambiguity, its neglect of relations and institutions), and its laboratory applications - the four principles as they behave in consent, refusal, uncertainty, and the ethics of asking.**

## The framework

::: definition The four principles and their logic {#def-principles}
**The Beauchamp-and-Childress synthesis (the 'principles-biomedical-ethics' lineage from 1979's edition-one through its court-citation era) organises clinical ethics into four pro tempore commitments. Respect for autonomy: the acknowledgment of the person's considered authority over their own life - operationalised in informed consent and refusal, grounded in the Millian harm-liberty argument and Kant's end-in-himself formula, and interpreted through its own development from the paternalist 'physician-decides' era through the rights-based consent law of the 1960s-90s to today's relational-autonomy accounts (autonomy exercised in families and illness, not by island-selves - the critique that softened the framework without discarding it). Beneficence: the positive duty to confer benefit, which clinical medicine inflates into its whole professional self-image and ethics keeps humble (benefit is not whatever the clinician values; it is what the patient's own account of their good makes choiceworthy - the thin-vs-thick-good debate). Non-maleficence: the primum-non-sequere tradition (do no harm's Hippocratic-adjacent provenance is more complicated than the slogan - the 'primum-non-nocere' emerged in scholastic commentary), which does the analytic heavy lifting in three distinctions the clinical chapters lean on: doing-versus-allowing (the withdrawing-forgoing equivalence that end-of-life law settled), killing-versus-letting-die (the doctrine's contested coherence at the sedation and nutrition borders), and ordinary-versus-extraordinary (proportionate-versus-disproportionate means' modern restatement that powers futility disputes). Justice: the distribution questions the other three leave to the ward door (the allocation chapters own these) plus the procedural demands - consistency, transparency, appealability - that Rawlsian and Daniels-style accountabilities formalise. The principles are 'specifiable' not self-applying (the principism-versus-principlism quarrels: the framework gives weights to reasons, not an algorithm), they conflict by design (the real work is the justification of lexical orderings case by case), and their joint output is the four-question clinical review - what does the patient want, what does medicine offer, what must not be done, and who decides when the answers exceed this patient's claim - which is why the chapter order of this course puts ethics before neither consent nor death.**
:::

## The rivals

::: definition Rival theories and their clinical uses {#def-rivals}
**Casuistry (the casuistic revival from Jonsen and Toulmin's 1988 reappraisal of Jesuit case-method) reasons by paradigm case and analogy rather than principle-application: its clinical virtue is familiarity with the case's morphology (the refusal-with-family-pressure template, the ambiguous-capacity-infection template), and its hazard is consensus-drift (paradigms encode their era's prejudices - the asylum-era cases that taught 'benevolent' deception). Narrative ethics (the Frank-and-Gilligan-adjacent schools) treats the patient's story as moral data: who is telling what to whom, which identities the illness threatens, what the telling asks to be recognised - the apparatus behind narrative-medicine programmes' documented effects on empathy-measure and clinical outcomes' soft endpoints. Care ethics (the Gilligan-of-the-moral-genesology-tradition as systematised by Tronto and Held) relocates the moral scene from two strangers choosing to the ongoing web of dependencies (carers, children, the under-resourced teams) that the ward actually consists of, generating the relational-autonomy reinterpretation of consent (family-involved-consent models' legitimate pluralism against their abuse-risk caveats) and the moral-residue literature (the wrong that persists despite the right decision - the reason clinician-grief studies now appear in ethics journals). Virtue ethics' medical return (the 'medicine-as-moral-practice' tradition from Pellegrin through the character-focused faculty development) asks not what to do but who the actor is becoming: the candour-and-humility literature, the professional-vices taxonomy for the mistreatment studies, and the empirical turn (character is situationally fragile - the behavioural-ethics findings that justify systems approaches over hero-worship). Contractualist ethics (Scanlon's 'no-one-could-reasonably-reject' formulation) supplies the research-ethics and public-health-justification grammar (why coercion needs reasons that even the coerced could not reasonably reject - the Siracusa-Nuffield-adjacent proportionality tests). Consequentialism in its refined forms (rule-versus-act, prioritarian and sufficientarian variants) dominates health economics' ethics substrate (the QALY's egalitarian-or-else debate) where utilitarian shorthand once ruled. Dignity-based frameworks (the continental-and-postwar-rights-tradition from Kant through the Grundgesetz-influenced bioethics conventions) do work that principlism cannot: the inviolability intuitions behind torture-and-treatment prohibitions and the 'dignity-in-dying' jurisprudence's rival readings (dignity-as-inherent-worth versus dignity-as-quality - the argument that structures the assisted-dying debates on both sides). The appraisal habit this chapter teaches: none of these theories is correct in the way a theorem is correct; they are instruments, and the competent clinician knows which instrument each clinical moment needs - consent forms are principlist, refusal conversations are narrative, staffing rot is care-ethics territory, and triage is consequentialism under scrutiny.**
:::

## The applications

::: definition Consent, refusal, uncertainty and asking {#def-applications}
**Consent is the framework's first laboratory: the elements (disclosure of material risks and benefits and alternatives, comprehension in the patient's language, voluntariness free of inducement and pressure, competence, authorisation) are settled in law and miserable in practice (the 'signature-not-process' culture the consent-evidence literature documents, the disclosure-threshold arguments between reasonable-patient and professional standards that Montgomery-era judgments rebalanced toward the patient's materiality). Refusal divides the easy from the hard: the competent adult's right to refuse life-saving treatment (the decision-as-authority doctrine, settled in the common law's transfusion-and-ventilator cases, and unsettled nowhere else - Jehovah's-Witness-advance-decision architecture as the working model of refusal-with-foresight) versus the three hard classes: capacity-impaired refusals (the assessment and the sliding-scale-of-seriousness literature - the 'mind-capacity-law-adjacent' conditional thresholds), substituted judgement and best-interests regimes (the advance-directive-versus-surmates-debate spectrum across jurisdictions), and refusals under duress or untreated psychiatric illness (the 'assisted-suicide-versus-refusal-suicide' jurisprudence and the sectioning-versus-treatment debates the psychiatry chapters inherit). Uncertainty is ethics' default condition, and its instruments: proportionality review, precaution's graduated forms, the 'best-interests-as-totality' weighing with patient-values-priors written in (the Mental-Capacity-Act-adjacent frameworks), the futility-versus-non-beneficial-therapy vocabulary migration (the policy consensus that 'futility' claims need institutional process, not fiat - the secondary-review-and-transfer-offer procedures), and moral-residue and moral-distress accounting for teams (the 'moral-distress-as-workforce-harm' literature that turns an ethics concept into a retention variable). And asking - the clinical skill the whole chapter serves: the goals-of-care conversation architecture (the 'serious-illness-conversation' templates' evidence base), the elicit-offer-support recommendation sequence from the communication chapters, the values-first history ('what would make the next month worth living' as an instrument with validated-adjacent pedigrees), and the humility clause: ethics consultation's own outcome data (the 'ethics-consult-lowers-limits-of-care-not-conflict-scores' findings) teach that the discipline's real product is process that people can live with afterwards. The chapter closes where clinical ethics lives: not solving dilemmas so much as making the remaining ones visible, shared and documented.**
:::

::: example The refusal on the ward {#ex-refusal}
A 61-year-old with diabetic sepsis of a necrotic leg refuses amputation, repeatedly, with apparent understanding; the surgical registrar is certain the refusal is 'the depression talking' (treated for eight years) and asks you, the consultant on call, to 'have a serious talk'.
::: solution
**Refusal-appraisal-not-persuasion ([[#def-principles]], [[#def-rivals]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]): The sequence: assess capacity properly (the four-ability framework - understanding, retention, weighing, communicating - done by someone trained, documented as reasoning not vibes; depression does not equal incapacity, and the 'is-the-refusal-the-depression-symptom-or-the-patient-value' question is answered by the weighing-ability's quality, not by its agreement with you), involve psychiatry to treat and to assess, not to overrule (the dual-role trap the ethics-consultation literature warns about), test the refusal's stability and information base (is she refusing this, today, knowing the pain-and-sepsis trajectory and the alternatives - antibiotics-alone-and-palliative-pathway options she may not have been offered), and open the values conversation the refusal is an answer to ('what are you more afraid of - dying or living as an amputee' is a medical history question of the highest rank, and the family-as-context-not-jury in the relational model done right). The escalation only if the assessment supports it (the second-capacity-opinion and, in extremis, the court-of-protection-adjacent declaratory route - the process that treats the patient as a rights-holder even while doubting her, which is why it is slow and should be rarer than it is). The team-ethics work: the registrar's certainty is moral distress wearing epistemic clothes (the 'moral-distress-predicts-moral-overreach' findings), the nursing team's split deserves an ethics-consult-page (the process that converts the split into an agenda), and the honest documentation that protects everyone: capacity assessment attached, alternatives offered and refused recorded, and the 'we will keep treating the sepsis and revisiting this' paragraph that keeps the relationship open when the surgeon's job is to save a life and the patient's job is to decide whose it is.**
:::

::: example The one dose left {#ex-alloc}
One dose of a scarce antivenom, two envenomed patients in the district hospital: a 9-year-old child and the 34-year-old teacher who brought her in; both will lose the limb, one may die; the teacher's family has been in your casualty department for three days helping staff.
::: solution
**Allocation-at-the-bedside ([[#def-principles]], [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]], [[emergency-critical-care/acute-assessment]]): The framework before the feeling: the justification question is 'which decision could each lose without reasonably rejecting it' (the contractualist test), and candidate answers array: save-the-child (the fair-innings and years-of-life-priority intuitions, strongest when prognosis differs), save-the-prognosis-better (consequentialism's life-years-weighted clinical realism), first-come (the queue norm - weak in extremis and it favours whoever travels better), and save-the-helper-injured-at-your-hand (the special-obligation channel for healthcare workers and, by extension of her act, the teacher's own sacrifice - an argument the ward will make and the ethicist must weigh honestly, not suppress). The correct procedure given the facts: clinical-urgency-first (mortality-risk differential decides if it is large - ethics begins where medicine's ranking is silent), then the pre-agreed tie-breakers the hospital's pandemic-era frameworks settled on (fair-innings child-priority, with no bonus for social worth beyond the special-obligation exceptions applied consistently and in writing), and the decision made by the clinician-in-charge-with-documentation-not-by-the-family-in-the-corridor (the accountability and consistency requirement - the 'consistency-as-justice' rule that makes the same case at 3am receive the same answer). The residue management: the losing family's debrief and the team's (the 'after-allocation' moral-injury protocols the surge-era literature generated), the transparent policy the hospital should have had before the decision (the 'framework-first-not-limb-later' lesson repeated since the 1940s iron-lung committees), and the teacher's recognition in the record - gratitude is not a currency, and saying so out loud is what keeps the special-obligation exception from becoming a class system.**
:::

## Where this leads

Consent and capacity law and practice in [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]] and [[clinical-skills/records-handover]], confidentiality in [[medical-ethics-law/confidentiality]], the death-and-dying applications in [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]] and [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]], allocation in [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]] and [[public-health/health-systems-economics]], research in [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]], professional duties in [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]], and the communication machinery in [[clinical-skills/communication]].

::: history
**Medical ethics is older than medicine's writing (the Hippocratic corpus' oath and its 'do-good-and-do-no-harm-plus-loyalty-structure' of duties) and younger than its modern form deserves: the eighteenth-century Edinburgh and Leiden lectures (the Gregorium-and-Feeling-school 'medical ethics' as a named subject), Gregory's and Underwood's treatises, and the 'medical-ethics-professorships-1800s' that made duty a syllabus. The twentieth century forced the modern machinery: the Nuremberg Code (1947) and the declaration-of-Helsinki lineage after the Nazi trials (the research-ethics chapters' foundation), the civil-rights-era patient-autonomy cases (the Salgo-and-Katz consent-coining 1957, the Schloendorff self-determination formulation's long reach), the Tuskegee-and-Willowbrook-and-HeLa scandals that institutionalised review boards and the Belmont Report's principle-inventory (1979's year of the framework's first edition too), the right-to-die jurisprudence's first wave (the Quinlan-and-Cruzan-and-Bland-adjacent ventilator-and-nutrition-and-volume cases moving authority from medicine to courts to patients), and the British-rights-incorporation-era Mental-Health-and-Mental-Capacity-Acts-architecture (the 'best-interests-and-capacity-presumption' statutory ethics that exported widely). The principlist decade (1979-2000s) and its critics ran in parallel: the Georgetown-man-centre's consensus machinery and its textbook editions, the Kennedy Institute's encyclopaedic turn, the bioethics-commissions' era (the Warnock-and-BBC-adjacent public bioethics that made the field a newspaper), the American-encounters-with-the-patient's-revolt literature, and the critics' ledger - the feminist care-ethics challenge from its Gilligan genealogy, the medical-humanities narrative turn, the postcolonial-and-global-health-justice charges that principlism universalises a Western dyad while the world's ethics is collective and resource-besieged, and the sociological studies of ethics-committees-as-ritual. What the century's end left standing: an institutionalised field (clinical-ethics-consultation services, accreditation requirements, and the law's absorption of the framework's vocabulary), a permanent argument about its foundations, and the pandemic's brutal demonstration (triage protocols, palliative-care-in-carparks, vaccine apportionment) that the residual questions principlism names are settled by process when philosophy is silent - which is both the framework's vindication and its indictment.** - [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]], [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]].**
:::

::: summary
- The four principles are a reasons-inventory, not an algorithm: they name what counts (the patient's authorship, medicine's benefits, harm's priority, distribution's claims) and leave the weighting to justified judgement - which is why the same principles support good ward decisions and bad op-eds.
- Rival theories are instruments: casuistry for case-morphology, narrative for identity and recognition, care ethics for dependency webs, virtue ethics for the actor's formation, contractualism for coercion's justification, and dignity language for the inviolability intuitions principlism underplays.
- Consent is a process with legally settled elements and practically unsettled depths; refusal is its proof, and competent refusal binds even against medicine's statistics - capacity assessment is the only license to doubt a refusal, and it must be earned by training and documentation.
- Uncertainty is the default: proportionality, process, ethics consultation and the residue-management literature exist because the right answer often leaves a wrong behind, and teams need accounting for moral distress as a workforce variable.
- Allocation decisions need frameworks written before emergencies (fair-innings, tie-breakers, special-obligation exceptions applied consistently), because bedside improvisation is how ethics-talk becomes trust-destroying ad-hocracy.
- The endpoint is not dilemmas-solved but decisions made visible, shared, documented and liveable - for the patient, the family, and the team that has to do the next case.
:::

::: exercise Defend the framework {level=3}
A philosophy faculty colleague argues principlism is 'applied common sense with citations' and that ethics belongs to moral philosophy, not checklists. Give the strongest version of the critique, then the framework's defence on its actual aims, then the synthesis you would teach.
::: solution
**Principlism's trial ([[#def-principles]], [[#def-rivals]]): The strongest critique: the four principles are underdetermining (any verdict can be argued - the 'specification-weighing' moves are unformalised, so the framework's application is the clinician's prior intuitions in a lab coat); they are theoretically borrowed without their foundations (Kantian and Millian machinery whose disagreements the list flattens - the 'principles-are-phenomenology-not-theory' charge); their unit of moral analysis is the dyad-and-decision, obscuring the structures (workforce, markets, inequity) that produce most clinical wrongs; and their institutionalisation (consent forms, ethics-consult templates) converts moral reasoning into risk-management liturgy - the 'ethics-washing' accusation with documented cases. The defence on actual aims: principlism was designed as working consensus for plural societies and multidisciplinary teams (the 'who-decides-at-2am-by-people-who-disagree-about-God' problem), as an audit-language that makes decisions contestable (you can argue with a named principle; you cannot argue with vibes), and as a floor, not a ceiling - the framework's own authors' four-specifications-of-application programme explicitly subordinates it to justification, and the empirical literature (the guideline- and consult-documentation studies) shows structured reasons change decisions, not merely decorate them. The synthesis taught: principles as the reasons-taxonomy, theories as the reason-generators, process as the disagreement-settler, and virtue as the motivation-keeper - the four layers of a functioning clinical ethics (know what counts, know why it counts, know how to settle when counts conflict, and be the sort of person who does it when nobody is testing you) - and the honest admission the philosophy colleague extracts: without the second layer, the first is a clipboard, which is the failure mode to teach against, not the framework itself.**
:::

::: example The placebo in the prescription {#ex-placebo}
A GP is asked by a worried daughter to 'give her father something' for the early dementia he denies having; he is distressed by his memory slips. The GP considers an unlabelled placebo. Appraise using the chapter's instruments.
::: solution
**Placebos-between-kindness-and-deception ([[#def-principles]], [[#def-rivals]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]): The principles in alignment: beneficence's real target here is the distress, which has honest treatments (the memory-clinic pathway, the structured disclosure conversation, the carer-support that the distress usually relocates to); autonomy's claim is what the unlabelled route spends (the patient's ability to interpret his own symptoms while taking a 'memory tablet', the family's precedent for future unlabelled interventions - the deception-debt argument), and the open-label placebos literature (the 'placebos-with-honest-labels-work' trial programme, the rituals-and-meaning mechanisms) supplies the alternative that preserves both: an active-free, transparently symptomatic treatment framed as helping the distress rather than the disease. The care-ethics reading reframes the request: the daughter is asking to be relieved of helpless witnessing (the information-and-support conversation with her is the intervention with the largest effect), and the narrative reading notes what the father's denial protects (the identity whose threatened-loss the memory clinic must be briefed to handle). The policy layer most clinicians never reach: the professional-conduct rules on deception, the consent implications of covert administration, and the research-ethics double standard that makes placebos investigational-taboo and practice-taboo-in-public-but-practiced-in-private - the honesty of which is a justice question about who gets the truth: the articulate get explanations, the denied get sugar. The verdict: treat the distress openly, treat the daughter's burden directly, and log why the shortcut was declined, because the next clinician will be tempted by the same kindness.**
:::

::: example The student's line {#ex-student}
A medical student tells you she 'froze' during a resuscitation and the consultant berated her in front of the family; she is considering withdrawal; the consultant insists the feedback was 'needed and honest'.

The withdrawal question: name it early, because the attrition literature shows the exit decision is made in weeks of unaddressed shame, and the system's cost for losing her is the specialty's - the virtue-ethics point finally generalised: cultures are the aggregate of who stays, and who stays is decided in moments exactly like this one.**

::: solution
**Therapeutic-veil-and-power ([[#def-applications]], [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]]): The elements violated in structure if not intent: voluntariness (the patient's dependence on this clinician's continuing goodwill makes refusal costed - the 'therapeutic-illusion-plus-power-gradient' compound), comprehension (the same-encounter invitation blends trial and care, which is the therapeutic-veil mechanism the consent-comprehension studies document), and authorship (countersignature-in-the-clinic converts a process into paperwork). The design fixes: separation (a research nurse or investigator uninvolved in the day-to-day decision invites, after the treatment plan is settled and documented, with the explicit 'your care is unchanged either way' script), the cooling-off interval between invitation and signature, the 'care-team-will-not-know-unless-you-tell-them' option in sensitive settings, and the re-consent culture for protocol changes (the 'ongoing-consent-as-relationship' models now recommended in paediatric and longitudinal research). The registrar's education, delivered without accusation because the structure, not the person, is the fault: consent is a decision the patient makes when they are least able to be politely refused; the design must protect the refusal as carefully as the signature.**

::: exercise Argue the two sides {level=2}
Take a position on this and defend it in one paragraph, then steelman the opposite: mandatory universal newborn screening for a panel including conditions of uncertain childhood-onset, on the parental-autonomy-versus-population-benefit question.
::: solution
**Screening-and-the-child's-open-future ([[#def-principles]], [[public-health/screening]], [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]]): The pro-universal paragraph: newborns cannot consent and the state's interest in their future autonomy is exactly what warrants default enrolment; the panel's benefits (the metabolic conditions where delay costs brains, the equity gain when opt-out correlates with deprivation) accrue to the same children whose parents' autonomy is invoked; defaults are not coercion - the opt-out route preserved, unhurried and free keeps the choice where it belongs while the architecture does its work, which is the libertarian-paternalism the behavioural literature permits. The steelman: universal-with-opt-out collapses into mandate in practice (the 'how-do-you-refuse-the-blood-that-is-already-being-drawn' objection), and the panel's new members are the problem: conditions of uncertain onset convert the newborn screen from a treatment-launcher into a risk-classification-service whose results exceed the original justification (the 'the-responsibility-to-know-versus-the-responsibility-that-follows-knowing' argument the childhood-onset-ethics literature makes), the population benefit is carried by the follow-up infrastructure that defunded health systems do not have (making the programme an informed-consent fiction at the population level), and the child's open future is narrowed not by treatment but by a childhood filed under a probability. The synthesis the ward needs: the ethics of screening panels is the ethics of what happens after the result, and programmes earn their defaults one treatable-condition-at-a-time, in public, with the follow-up funded before the blood is taken.**
:::

::: exercise Teach the four questions {level=1}
Design the five-minute ward-teaching that leaves a new intake with the chapter's four-question clinical review (wants, offers, must-nots, who-decides), using one example they will meet this month. Include the two questions you would forbid them from asking in that order and why.
::: solution
**Four-questions-on-a-stroke-ward ([[#def-applications]], [[clinical-skills/history-taking]]): The example: the 84-year-old with a large stroke, a witnessed conversation from three months ago ('not the feeding tube'), and a daughter who arrived from abroad and wants 'everything done'. The four-question run: what does the patient want (the witnessed-account and prior-values evidence, the advance-statement's informality assessed, the substituted-judgement standard explained to the daughter as 'what would he say, not what would help him'), what does medicine offer (the feeding-tube statistics for this stroke pattern - the intervention's honest probability of delivering what he objected to), what must not be done (the harm of the tube-versus-the-harm-of-the-battle, the proportionality framing), who decides (the decision-tree: patient-authority absent, documented-wishes next, substituted judgement next, best-interests next, and the team's disagreement resolved by ethics-review-not-hierarchy). The forbidden questions: 'what do we do?' (the decision-delegation reflex that makes the nurse ask the doctor to own the family's conflict) before 'what is she asking and why?', and 'does the family understand?' (the comprehension-blame reflex) before 'have we explained in a way they could answer?' - the two question-orders that decide whether the encounter becomes an ethics case or a shared decision, which is the entire teaching.**
