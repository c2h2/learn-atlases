Mr Okafor is 74 and has a chest infection. His oxygen saturation is low and the emergency doctor recommends admission. He says no: his wife has dementia, he is her only carer, and he will not leave her alone overnight. The doctor knows that admission would probably be safer. She also knows that Mr Okafor is not confused, understands what he is being told, and has his own reasons. What should she do?

Nothing in pharmacology or physiology answers that question. It is a question about what *ought* to be done, and it involves the patient's right to decide, the doctor's duty to help, the risk of harm and the needs of another person. Questions like this arise every day, mostly in ordinary wards and surgeries rather than in famous court cases. **Medical ethics** is the study of how to answer them well: how to identify what matters morally in a clinical situation, how to weigh competing considerations, and how to reach a decision that can be explained and defended.

This chapter introduces the main tools: the four principles of biomedical ethics, the moral theories behind them, some distinctions that recur across the course, the relationship between ethics and law in England and Wales, and a structured way of working through a case. Later chapters apply these tools to consent, confidentiality, the beginning and end of life, resource allocation, research and professional regulation.

## Why ethics needs a method {#method}

Moral intuitions matter: a sense that something is wrong is often the first sign that it is. But intuitions differ between people, shift with how a question is framed, and can carry prejudice. A clinician who decides by gut feeling alone cannot explain the decision or check whether the feeling is reliable.

An ethical method does three things. It helps you **notice** the morally relevant features of a situation, including ones that are easy to miss, such as the interests of a carer or the effect of a decision on trust. It gives you a **vocabulary** for discussing them with people who see things differently. And it requires you to give **reasons**, so that a decision can be examined, challenged and improved.

A method does not produce answers mechanically. Two thoughtful clinicians using the same framework can reach different conclusions in a hard case. What the method excludes is the decision that cannot be justified at all: the one made for convenience, out of fear of complaint, or because "we always do it this way".

## The four principles {#four-principles}

The most widely used framework in clinical ethics was set out by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress in *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, first published in 1979 and now in its eighth edition (2019). It is often called **principlism**.

::: definition The four principles of biomedical ethics {#def-principles}
- **Respect for autonomy**: respecting the right of a person with capacity to make decisions about their own life and body, according to their own values, including decisions others think unwise. It includes positive duties: to give people the information they need, and to support them in deciding.
- **Beneficence**: acting for the benefit of the patient, by preventing or removing harm and promoting their health and welfare, judged by the patient's own goals where these are known.
- **Non-maleficence**: avoiding causing harm. Because almost every intervention carries some risk, in practice this means not causing harm that is disproportionate to the expected benefit.
- **Justice**: treating people fairly. This includes fair distribution of benefits, risks and costs (distributive justice), respect for people's rights, and respect for morally acceptable laws.
:::

The principles are deliberately general. Each is accepted, in some form, by people who hold very different religious and philosophical views. Beauchamp and Childress call this the **common morality**: the shared core of moral norms that thoughtful people across cultures recognise. The principles are a way of organising that shared core, not a new moral theory.

Autonomy is often treated as the first principle in modern Western medicine, but the framework does not rank the principles. The authors describe them as **prima facie** duties: each is binding unless it conflicts with another duty that is stronger in the circumstances.

::: theorem Principles are prima facie, and conflicts are resolved by specification and balancing {#thm-prima-facie}
Each principle states a genuine obligation, but none is absolute. When principles conflict in a particular case, the conflict is resolved in two ways:

1. **Specification**: making a principle more precise, by stating where, when, why, how and by whom it applies, so that the apparent conflict disappears or narrows.
2. **Balancing**: judging which obligation carries more weight in this case, with reasons. An infringed principle still leaves a residue: the clinician should choose the option that infringes it least, and should acknowledge and, where possible, remedy the loss.
:::

::: proof
Suppose the four principles were absolute. Then any case in which they pointed different ways would have no permissible action at all. Such cases are common: almost every effective treatment risks some harm, so beneficence and non-maleficence pull against each other whenever a patient is treated. A set of absolute duties that regularly cannot all be satisfied cannot guide action. Treating them as prima facie keeps each one morally serious while allowing a decision.

Specification does most of the everyday work. "Respect autonomy" is too vague to settle anything; "respect the informed refusal of an adult who has capacity for that decision, even when the refusal will lead to serious harm" gives a clear answer, and English law contains a near-identical rule ([[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]]). Where specification runs out, balancing is needed, and the requirement to give reasons and choose the least infringing option stops it becoming an excuse for doing what one already wanted.
:::

For Mr Okafor, autonomy supports his refusal, beneficence and non-maleficence point towards admission, and justice draws attention to his wife. Respect for autonomy applies with full force only if he has capacity for this decision, so that is checked first. If he has, balancing does not mean overriding him; it means finding an option that serves his goals and reduces the risk.

::: example A refusal of admission {#ex-refusal}
Continue the case. Mr Okafor has a respiratory rate of 24/min, oxygen saturation 92% on air, and a clear understanding of the situation. He can explain that he might get worse overnight and could die, and that he has decided the risk is worth taking because his wife cannot be left alone. How should the emergency doctor proceed?
::: solution
**Step 1: clarify the facts.** How high is the risk (a severity score such as CURB-65 helps)? Could he be treated safely at home with oral antibiotics and early review?

**Step 2: check capacity for this decision.** He understands, retains and weighs the information (he has explicitly traded his own risk against his wife's safety) and communicates a choice. Capacity is presumed and here confirmed; his decision is not invalid because the doctor would decide differently.

**Step 3: identify the principles and the people.** Autonomy supports his choice. Beneficence and non-maleficence ask the doctor to reduce the risk. His wife's welfare is a legitimate concern that he is entitled to weigh.

**Step 4: look for options that satisfy more principles at once.** Can social services or a relative arrange emergency care for his wife, so that admission becomes acceptable to him? If not, can he be managed at home with antibiotics, a pulse oximeter, safety-netting advice (who to call, which symptoms mean return), and a review by the hospital-at-home team or GP the next day?

**Step 5: decide, document and keep the door open.** If he still declines admission, the doctor respects this, provides the best alternative care, and records his capacity assessment, the risks discussed and the plan. She tells him that he can change his mind at any time. Respecting a refusal is not abandoning a patient.
:::
:::

### Criticisms of principlism {#criticisms}

The criticisms show what the framework can miss.

- **Too thin.** The principles do not say how to weigh them, so they can be used to support opposite conclusions.
- **Too individualistic.** Many patients decide with their families, and some prefer to defer to them or to their doctor. Respecting autonomy includes respecting a choice to involve others or to delegate.
- **Missing elements.** The framework says little directly about relationships, character, trust or dignity.

Raanan Gillon, who did much to spread the approach in the United Kingdom, added the idea of **scope**: we must also ask *to whom* each duty is owed. We owe beneficence to our patients in a strong form, but not to everyone in the world in the same way; we owe non-maleficence to everyone.

## The moral theories behind the principles {#theories}

The four principles draw on older moral theories. Knowing these helps explain why the principles conflict and what each one protects.

::: definition Main ethical theories {#def-theories}
- **Consequentialism** judges actions by their outcomes. The best-known form, **utilitarianism** (Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill), says the right action produces the greatest overall well-being. It underlies cost-effectiveness analysis and public health reasoning.
- **Deontology** judges actions by whether they conform to duties and respect rights, regardless of some consequences. Immanuel Kant argued that people must always be treated as ends in themselves, never merely as means. It underlies consent, truth-telling and the prohibition on using people in research without their agreement.
- **Virtue ethics**, which goes back to Aristotle, asks what a good person, or a good doctor, would do. It focuses on character traits such as honesty, compassion, courage and practical wisdom.
- **Care ethics**, developed by Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings and others, emphasises relationships, dependency and responsiveness to particular people rather than abstract rules.
- **Casuistry** reasons from cases. It compares a new case with clear "paradigm" cases where the answer is agreed, and asks which it most resembles (Albert Jonsen and Stephen Toulmin, *The Abuse of Casuistry*, 1988).
- **Narrative ethics** pays attention to the patient's story: how the illness fits into their life, and what a decision would mean within it.
:::

None of these theories has won the philosophical argument, and each has well-known problems. Pure utilitarianism can justify sacrificing one person to save several. Strict deontology can forbid a lie that would save a life. Virtue ethics can be vague about what to do. Clinical ethics therefore tends to use them as **lenses**: each draws attention to features of a case that the others may miss.

::: example One intensive care bed {#ex-alloc}
At 3 a.m. a district hospital has one intensive care bed and two patients who need it. A 46-year-old woman with severe pneumonia has a good chance of recovery with ventilation. An 82-year-old man with advanced heart failure and a new stroke has a much lower chance of surviving to discharge. Transfer to another hospital is possible for one of them but will take two hours. Use the theories as lenses.
::: solution
**Consequentialist lens:** which allocation gives the greatest expected benefit? The woman has a higher probability of surviving and more expected years of life. This favours giving her the local bed and arranging transfer for the man if transfer is clinically appropriate for him.

**Deontological lens:** both patients have an equal claim to be treated with respect, and neither may be dismissed because of age alone. Decisions must rest on clinical factors that predict benefit, not on worth or status. The man and his family are owed an honest explanation.

**Virtue and care lenses:** what would a compassionate and fair clinician do? Speak to both families, avoid a hurried decision made only by the most junior doctor, involve the consultant, and make sure that whoever does not get the bed still receives good care, including palliative care if escalation is not in his interests.

**Casuistry:** few people think "first come, first served" fair if one patient would almost certainly not benefit; most think it unfair to refuse a bed for age alone if the older patient would benefit as much.

**Conclusion:** the reasoning points to allocating by expected clinical benefit, using an assessment of each patient rather than age as a proxy, while ensuring the other patient receives the best available alternative. Whether intensive care would actually benefit the 82-year-old should be decided on its own merits, ideally with him or those close to him. This is the logic behind formal critical care triage guidance, discussed in [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]].
:::
:::

## Recurring distinctions {#distinctions}

Several distinctions appear throughout medical ethics. They are tools for describing cases accurately, not answers in themselves.

### Acts and omissions

Is there a moral difference between causing a harm and allowing it to happen? Many people think so: killing seems worse than letting die. English law draws a sharp line here at the end of life. A doctor may lawfully withhold or withdraw life-sustaining treatment when continuing it is not in the patient's best interests, but may not act to end life, as the House of Lords explained in *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* (1993). Some philosophers argue that the distinction carries no moral weight in itself, and that what matters is the intention and the outcome. This debate returns in [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]].

### The doctrine of double effect

::: definition The doctrine of double effect {#def-double-effect}
The **doctrine of double effect** holds that an action with a good effect and a foreseen bad effect may be permissible if all four conditions are met:

1. the action itself is not wrong;
2. the agent intends only the good effect; the bad effect is foreseen but not intended;
3. the bad effect is not the means by which the good effect is achieved;
4. there is a proportionate reason for accepting the bad effect.
:::

The classic medical example is giving opioids or sedatives to relieve severe distress at the end of life. In *R v Adams* (1957), Devlin J told the jury that a doctor is entitled to do all that is proper and necessary to relieve pain and suffering, even if the measures he takes may incidentally shorten life. In modern palliative care, opioids titrated to symptoms are rarely shown to shorten life, so double effect is needed less often than people assume. The doctrine still matters because it explains why intention matters: the same drug given in doses titrated to relieve symptoms is lawful, while a dose chosen in order to end life is not.

### Proportionate treatment

The old distinction between "ordinary" treatment, which must always be given, and "extraordinary" treatment has given way to asking whether a treatment is **proportionate**: whether its expected benefits to this patient outweigh its burdens, by the patient's own values. The same ventilator can be proportionate for one patient and not for another.

### Conscience

Doctors are moral agents too. A **conscientious objection** is a refusal to take part in a lawful procedure on grounds of conscience. Statute protects it for abortion (Abortion Act 1967, s4) and certain fertility treatment (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990, s38); elsewhere the GMC permits it only if patients can still get the care promptly (see the exercise "Conscience and access").

::: warning Ethics is not the same as law, and neither is the same as a doctor's opinion
Three different questions are often run together: *What does the law require?*, *What does professional guidance require?* and *What is ethically best?* The answers usually agree, but not always. Something can be lawful but unethical (being needlessly unkind while obtaining valid consent) or, in principle, ethical but unlawful (some argue this about assisted dying). And "best interests", in law and ethics, does not mean "what the doctor would choose". It means what is best for *this* patient, taking into account their own wishes, beliefs and values. Students who write "it is in the patient's best interests" to mean "it is medically recommended" are making a serious and common error.
:::

## Ethics and law in England and Wales {#ethics-law}

This course follows the law of **England and Wales** first, because it is the setting of most UK medical schools and of the GMC's guidance. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate legal systems, and some of their law differs significantly, for example on capacity (Adults with Incapacity (Scotland) Act 2000) and on abortion. Laws elsewhere in the world differ more widely still. Where this course describes the law, it does so for teaching, not as legal advice.

Medical law in England and Wales comes from four main sources:

- **Statute**: Acts of Parliament, such as the Mental Capacity Act 2005, the Human Tissue Act 2004 and the Abortion Act 1967.
- **Common law**: principles developed by judges in decided cases, such as the law of negligence and the law on consent. Decisions of higher courts bind lower ones.
- **The European Convention on Human Rights**, given effect in UK law by the Human Rights Act 1998. Public authorities, including NHS bodies, must act compatibly with Convention rights. The most relevant are Article 2 (right to life), Article 3 (freedom from inhuman or degrading treatment), Article 5 (liberty), Article 8 (respect for private and family life), Article 9 (freedom of thought, conscience and religion) and Article 14 (non-discrimination).
- **Professional regulation**: the GMC's guidance, starting with *Good medical practice* (2024), is not law in itself, but serious or persistent failure to follow it can put a doctor's registration at risk, and courts take it into account ([[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]]).

**Criminal law** punishes wrongs against society: treating without consent may be battery, and gross negligence causing death can be manslaughter. **Civil law** lets individuals claim compensation, most often in the tort of negligence.

::: definition Clinical negligence {#def-negligence}
To succeed in a claim of **clinical negligence**, a claimant must prove, on the balance of probabilities:

1. **Duty of care**: the clinician owed the patient a duty. This is almost always the case once a professional relationship exists.
2. **Breach**: the clinician fell below the standard of care. Under *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* (1957), a doctor is not negligent if they acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion. *Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority* (1997) added that the opinion must be capable of withstanding logical analysis. For the duty to inform patients about risks, *Bolam* has been replaced by the test in *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* (2015).
3. **Causation**: the breach caused the harm, usually judged by asking whether the harm would have happened "but for" the breach.
4. **Damage**: the patient suffered harm of a kind the law recognises.
:::

The law sets a floor, not a ceiling: the GMC expects doctors to practise well above the minimum needed to avoid liability.

## Working through a case {#structured-approach}

A useful structure for clinical cases is the **four-topics method** of Albert Jonsen, Mark Siegler and William Winslade (*Clinical Ethics*, 9th edition, 2022). It organises facts before principles, which prevents the common mistake of arguing about values when the real disagreement is about the facts.

::: definition The four topics of a clinical ethics case {#def-four-topics}
1. **Medical indications**: the diagnosis and prognosis, the goals of treatment, the options and their probabilities of success and harm. (Beneficence and non-maleficence.)
2. **Patient preferences**: does the patient have capacity? What do they want, and what have they said before? If they lack capacity, who can speak about their wishes, and is there an advance decision? (Respect for autonomy.)
3. **Quality of life**: what will the patient's life be like with and without treatment, judged from their perspective? Are there biases in the clinicians' assessment? (Beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy.)
4. **Contextual features**: family, carers, religion and culture, resources, law, confidentiality, conflicts of interest, research. (Justice and loyalty.)
:::

Then list the realistic options, test each against the principles, choose the one that can best be justified, and plan who should be involved and how the decision will be recorded and reviewed. Many hospitals have a **clinical ethics committee** whose advice is not binding. Serious unresolved disagreement can be taken to court: the Court of Protection for adults who lack capacity, the High Court for children.

Much of beneficence is a judgement under uncertainty: whether the expected benefit of treatment outweighs its burdens. A simple model makes the structure clear. Suppose a treatment gives a benefit $B$ if it works, which happens with probability $p$, and imposes a burden $H$ on everyone who receives it (side effects, time in hospital, cost to the person). Its expected net benefit is

$$E = pB - H.$$

Treatment is worth offering, on this simple model, when $E > 0$, that is when $p$ exceeds the **threshold probability** $p^* = H/B$. Real decisions involve more than one number, and the values of $B$ and $H$ depend on what matters to the patient. But the model shows why two patients with the same diagnosis can reasonably make different choices: if one person values the possible benefit more, or minds the burden less, their threshold is lower.

::: example A medical student and an examination under anaesthesia {#ex-student}
A fourth-year medical student in a gynaecology theatre is told by a registrar to perform a vaginal examination on an anaesthetised patient "for learning". The student does not know whether the patient agreed to this. What should the student do?
::: solution
**The facts:** an intimate examination of an anaesthetised patient for teaching purposes is lawful only with the patient's specific consent, obtained before the anaesthetic. Without it, the examination would be a battery and a serious breach of trust. GMC guidance on intimate examinations requires consent for any examination under anaesthesia that is not part of the planned procedure, including examinations by students.

**The principles:** respect for autonomy and bodily integrity is the decisive consideration. The educational benefit to the student, and future patients, cannot justify using a patient's body without permission: in Kantian terms, it would treat her merely as a means.

**The action:** the student should politely ask whether the patient consented to an examination by a student. If the answer is no or unclear, the student should decline. This takes courage, because there is a power imbalance between a student and a registrar. A clear, calm phrase helps: "I'm sorry, I'm not comfortable examining her without knowing she has consented to it." The student should later raise the matter with a tutor or through the medical school's concerns procedure. A good outcome would be a change in theatre practice so that consent for teaching is always discussed in advance, as is now standard. Raising concerns is a professional duty, discussed in [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]].
:::
:::

::: example A placebo prescription {#ex-placebo}
A GP has seen a 35-year-old woman many times with persistent fatigue. Investigations are normal. She asks for "something to help". The GP considers prescribing a vitamin supplement that she believes has no specific effect, telling the patient "this often helps people like you". Is this ethical?
::: solution
**Facts and preferences:** placebo effects on subjective symptoms are real but variable, and the supplement is unlikely to cause physical harm. But she has not agreed to be given an inactive treatment on misleading grounds; her autonomy depends on accurate information.

**Analysis:** "this often helps people like you" is deliberately misleading, even if technically true of placebo responses. Deception damages trust, can make her feel dismissed if discovered, and may divert attention from the real causes of her fatigue. GMC guidance requires doctors not to mislead patients.

**Better options:** take the symptoms seriously, explain honestly what the investigations show and what is known about fatigue, offer evidence-based approaches such as graded return to activity and sleep advice, screen for depression, and keep reviewing. Some studies have found benefit from "open-label" placebos, where the patient is told the tablet is inactive. Whether that has a place in routine practice is debated, but it at least avoids deception.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 30-year-old man with capacity refuses a blood transfusion for severe bleeding because he is a Jehovah's Witness. The surgeon argues that beneficence must override autonomy because he will die without blood. Which response best reflects the four-principles approach as applied in England and Wales?
- [ ] Beneficence overrides autonomy when life is at risk, so the transfusion should be given.
- [x] The principles are prima facie; specified for this case, respect for the informed refusal of an adult with capacity prevails, and beneficence is served by every acceptable alternative to blood.
- [ ] The doctor should transfuse if the family agrees.
- [ ] The surgeon may decline to operate at all, since the patient has refused standard care.
::: solution
In England and Wales an adult with capacity may refuse any treatment, even if the refusal will lead to death (*Re T*, 1992; *Re B*, 2002), and the family has no power to consent for him. The principles are not ranked, but specified for this situation (an informed refusal by an adult with capacity), autonomy is decisive. Beneficence does not disappear: it requires the team to use every alternative the patient accepts, such as cell salvage if acceptable to him, tranexamic acid, meticulous haemostasis, and treating anaemia, and to check privately that the refusal is his own and not the result of pressure. Declining to operate would abandon him to a worse outcome.
:::
:::

::: history
The Hippocratic Oath, probably written in the 5th or 4th century BCE, bound physicians to help the sick, avoid harm and keep confidences. For two thousand years medical ethics was largely about the doctor's character and etiquette; Thomas Percival's *Medical Ethics* (1803) shaped professional codes in Britain and the United States.

Modern bioethics emerged after the Second World War. The Nuremberg Code (1947), written after the trial of Nazi doctors, put voluntary consent at the centre of research ethics. In 1966 Henry Beecher, an anaesthetist at Harvard, published 22 examples of unethical research in mainstream American journals, showing that abuse was not confined to dictatorships. The new technologies of the 1960s and 1970s, including dialysis, ventilators and transplantation, raised questions about who should be treated and when treatment should stop. Beauchamp and Childress published *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* in 1979, the same year as the Belmont Report on research ethics. In the UK, Raanan Gillon's writing in the 1980s and 1990s made the four principles the standard teaching framework, and medical ethics and law became a core part of the medical curriculum.
:::

::: summary
- Medical ethics asks what ought to be done in clinical care and requires decisions to be justified with reasons.
- The four principles are **respect for autonomy**, **beneficence**, **non-maleficence** and **justice**. They are prima facie duties, not ranked; conflicts are resolved by **specification** and **balancing**.
- Principlism draws on consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, care ethics, casuistry and narrative ethics, which are best used as complementary lenses.
- Recurring tools include the acts–omissions distinction, the **doctrine of double effect** and the test of **proportionate** treatment.
- Law, professional guidance and ethics overlap but are not identical. In England and Wales medical law comes from statute, common law, the Human Rights Act 1998 and professional regulation.
- Negligence requires duty, breach (*Bolam*, *Bolitho*; *Montgomery* for information about risks), causation and damage.
- Work through hard cases with the four topics: medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life and contextual features. Get the facts right before arguing about values.
- "Best interests" means best for this patient, by their values, not what the doctor would choose.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Name the principle {level=1}
For each statement, name the principle most directly involved: (a) "I explained the three options and their risks, and she chose the one I would not have picked." (b) "The new drug costs £80,000 a year; funding it would mean fewer hip replacements." (c) "We stopped the chemotherapy because its side effects were greater than its likely benefit." (d) "I arranged physiotherapy so that he could get home sooner."
::: hint
Ask whose interest or right each statement protects, and whether it concerns helping, avoiding harm, choosing or fairness.
:::
::: solution
(a) Respect for autonomy: providing information and respecting the patient's choice. (b) Justice, specifically distributive justice: fair allocation of limited resources. (c) Non-maleficence, balanced against beneficence: the harm of treatment had become disproportionate. (d) Beneficence: positively promoting the patient's welfare.
:::
:::

::: exercise Theories as lenses {level=1}
Briefly state how a utilitarian, a Kantian and a virtue ethicist would each approach the question of whether to tell a patient about a minor drug error that caused no harm.
::: solution
A **utilitarian** compares consequences: disclosure may cause some anxiety but preserves trust and lets the system learn, while concealment risks greater harm if discovered; most would disclose. A **Kantian** holds that deceiving the patient, even by silence about their own body, fails to respect them as a rational agent, so disclosure is required. A **virtue ethicist** asks what an honest and compassionate doctor would do, and would disclose, with an apology. In the UK, the professional duty of candour requires disclosure in any case ([[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Double effect {level=1}
A patient dying of lung cancer is breathless and frightened. The palliative care doctor increases the morphine dose stepwise until the distress is controlled. Apply the four conditions of the doctrine of double effect.
::: solution
(1) The act, giving morphine to relieve breathlessness and distress, is not wrong in itself. (2) The doctor intends relief of symptoms; any shortening of life, if it occurred, would be foreseen as possible but not intended. Titrating in steps against symptoms is evidence of that intention. (3) The relief is achieved by the drug's effect on breathlessness, not by the patient's death. (4) Severe distress in the last days of life is a proportionate reason. The action is permissible. In practice, careful titration of opioids for symptoms is rarely shown to shorten life.
:::
:::

::: exercise A treatment threshold {level=2 check="0.25"}
A patient considering an operation says that if it works it would be worth the equivalent of 12 months of good-quality life to her. Recovery from the operation, which everyone who has it must go through, she values as a loss equivalent to 3 months. Using the model $E = pB - H$, what is the minimum probability of success at which the operation has positive expected value for her? Give the answer as a decimal.
::: hint
Set $E = 0$ and solve for $p$.
:::
::: solution
$p^* = H/B = 3/12 = 0.25$. If the chance of success is above 25%, the operation has positive expected value for her. A patient who valued success at 6 months would need $p > 0.5$, which is why the same probability can lead reasonable people to different choices.
:::
:::

::: exercise Specify the principle {level=2}
"Respect for autonomy" seems to conflict with "beneficence" when a 19-year-old with type 1 diabetes repeatedly declines to attend clinic. Specify the autonomy principle so that it guides action, and describe what beneficence still requires.
::: solution
A useful specification: "Respect the informed decisions of a young adult with capacity about his own diabetes care, including not attending, while offering care in forms he is likely to accept." The clinic cannot compel attendance, but beneficence still requires effort: find out why he does not attend (work, travel costs, burnout, depression, disordered eating); offer flexible or remote appointments; make sure he knows the warning signs of ketoacidosis; and keep the door open. Discharging him for non-attendance without exploring the reasons would fail beneficence.
:::
:::

::: exercise Negligence elements {level=2}
A junior doctor fails to check a patient's allergy status and prescribes penicillin. The patient has a documented penicillin allergy but, by chance, suffers no reaction. Is this negligence in law? Is it an ethical failure?
::: solution
A negligence claim would fail: there was a duty and probably a breach, but no damage. Ethically it is still a failure, because it exposed the patient to a serious avoidable risk. It should be disclosed to the patient, reported through the incident system so that the system can learn, and reflected on. Absence of legal liability does not make an act acceptable.
:::

::: exercise Four topics {level=3}
An 88-year-old woman with advanced dementia, living in a care home, develops pneumonia for the third time this year. She cannot swallow safely. Her daughter insists that "everything must be done", including intensive care. Organise the case using the four topics and outline a reasonable approach.
::: solution
**Medical indications:** recurrent aspiration pneumonia in advanced dementia carries a poor prognosis; antibiotics may treat this episode but not the cause, and intensive care is unlikely to return her to her previous state and would be very burdensome.

**Patient preferences:** she lacks capacity for this decision. Is there an advance decision, a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare, or any record of her previously expressed wishes, beliefs and values? The daughter's view is important evidence about these but is not decisive unless she holds a relevant power of attorney, and even then she must act in her mother's best interests.

**Quality of life:** is she comfortable; does she enjoy food, music, company? Do not assume that life with dementia has no value.

**Contextual features:** the daughter's distress, the care home's ability to provide end-of-life care, cultural or religious beliefs, the Mental Capacity Act framework.

**Approach:** a best-interests meeting with the daughter, care home staff and the clinical team: explore what her mother would have wanted, explain honestly what intensive care could and could not achieve, and discuss a plan focused on comfort with treatment of reversible problems where it helps, recorded in a plan such as ReSPECT. If agreement cannot be reached, a second opinion, ethics consultation or ultimately the Court of Protection are available.
:::

::: exercise Conscience and access {level=3}
A GP has a sincere religious objection to prescribing emergency hormonal contraception. A 17-year-old asks for it on a Saturday morning. Analyse the GP's obligations.
::: solution
Emergency contraception is not covered by the statutory conscience clause in the Abortion Act 1967, because it acts before implantation and is not legally abortion. The GMC allows doctors to opt out of providing a particular treatment for reasons of conscience, subject to strict conditions. The GP must: tell the patient without delay that she can see another practitioner; make sure she has enough information to do so promptly, in time for the treatment to be effective (levonorgestrel is most effective the sooner it is taken, and an intrauterine device is the most effective option); avoid any expression of disapproval or moral judgement; and not obstruct her access. If no other practitioner is available in time and delay would cause harm, the GP's duty to the patient comes first. The 17-year-old is entitled to confidentiality like any other patient. A GP whose objection would regularly prevent patients from getting timely care should arrange the practice so that this does not happen.
