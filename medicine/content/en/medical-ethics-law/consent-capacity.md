Consent is the law's and ethics' shared organ for turning a touching into a treatment and a treatment into a collaboration; capacity is the single variable that decides who may use it. **This chapter works through consent's elements and their legal architecture (disclosure standards, materiality, comprehension, voluntariness, authorisation), capacity as a decision-specific ability with its presumption-and-assessment machinery (the four-ability frameworks, supported decision-making, the sliding scale of doubt), the hard applications (advance decisions, proxy decision-making, children and Gillick-style evolving capacity, emergencies and necessities), and research consent's stricter species (the therapeutic illusion, the coercion gradient of incentives, and consent as an ongoing relationship rather than a signature).**

## Consent

::: definition The elements and their law {#def-elements}
**Consent's classic anatomy has five living parts. Disclosure: the material risks, benefits and reasonable alternatives must be offered, and the materiality standard migrated across the common law from the professional-practice convention (what doctors customarily tell) to the patient-centred test (what this patient would attach significance to - the Montgomery-line judgments that re-centred the tort and made the risk conversation bespoke rather than ritual); the disclosure ecosystem includes the guideline- and regulator-mandated lists, the patient-information leaflets' comprehension problems, and the honest framing of uncertainty (numbers as natural frequencies with denominators, absolute risks, and the uncertainty that the leaflet's reassuring prose routinely launders out). Comprehension: the tested-understanding reality (the consent-comprehension literature's durable findings: recall of risks is poor, the therapeutic misestimation inflates expected benefit, and the 'did-you-understand-yes' checkbox measures nothing) making the teach-back the only instrument with evidence behind it. Voluntariness: the freedom from inducement, undue pressure and the power gradient (the physician-patient-relationship-coercion that research ethics formalises and clinical practice mostly denies, the family-conference dynamics that make 'she agreed' a sociological claim, and the financial-toxicity question of whether agreement to unaffordable care is voluntary in any useful sense). Competence/capacity: the chapter's second definition. Authorisation: the signature's true function as evidence of a process, the statute-decided list of written-consent occasions (surgery, research, some treatments), the telephone-and-electronic-consent adaptations, and the treatment-versus-research-consent distinction with its separate legal bases (the contract-versus-tort-versus-human-rights layers that vary by jurisdiction but share the elements' logic). The two standing exceptions carry their own case-law densities: the emergency (the 'reasonable-person-and-implied-consent' rescue doctrine and its abuse as a lazy universal - the 'was-it-really-instant-and-serious' audit), and the statutory-treatment regimes (mental-health-and-capacity-acts' substituted decision frameworks, whose compatibility with the UN-Convention-on-the-Rights-of-Persons-with-Disabilities' supported-decision-making Article-12 interpretation is this decade's live jurisprudence).**
:::

## Capacity

::: definition Capacity: presumption, assessment, support, substitution {#def-capacity}
**Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific: the ability to understand, retain, weigh and communicate (the four-abilities framework that the Mental-Capacity-Act-style statutes codify and that functional testing operationalises), operating under two commitments: a presumption of capacity (rebutted by evidence of impairment-of-mind-or-brain affecting this decision now) and the maximal-support principle (every practicable help before a finding - the simplified-language and pictorial aids, the timing-to-medication-and-sleep, the decision-aids and trusted-person presence, all counted as the assessment's required groundwork not courtesies). The assessment's craft: the diagnosis-and-function distinction (a dementia or delirium diagnosis is not incapacity; the involuntary-admission-to-a-bed-and-delirium-untreated class of incapacity findings is iatrogenic), the sliding-scale of doubt (seriousness-and-irreversibility raise the evidential bar the assessor requires - the common-law 'approach-with-caution' tradition made operational), the appreciation-and-reasoning registers (why the patient's reasons need not be good, only theirs - the 'bad-reasons-are-protected' rule that separates eccentric from incapable), and the documentation as reasoning (the assessment that survives review records the abilities tested with the answers, not the conclusion asserted). Fluctuating capacity gets its own protocols (the delirium-and-bipolar-and-dawn-testing practices, the 'restore-capacity-first' rule that makes sleep-and-glycaemia-and-oxygen part of the assessment). Supported decision-making (the Article-12 reform programme and its Canadian-and-Irish-assisted-decision-making statutes) introduces appointable supporters and, in its pure versions, replaces substituted judgement entirely - the live debate being whether any supported model survives the hardest cases or the guardian remains unavoidable, with the critique that the UK-style best-interests tribunals are justice by calendar appointment. Substituted frameworks when capacity fails: the advance-decision layer (below), the lasting-power-of-attorney-and-welfare-attorney architecture (scope, registration, and the attorney's duties that make the role more office than inheritance), the court-of-protection-adjacent declaratory machinery for serious or contested matters (the 'best-interests-as-substituted-judgment-with-objective-parameters' hybrid the case law converges on), and the deputy-and-independant-advocate safeguards whose under-resourcing the review studies keep documenting. Paediatric capacity is its own jurisprudence: the evolving-competence doctrine (Gillick's 'sufficient-maturity-and-understanding' formulation and its case-by-case operation), the parental-responsibility-versus-young-person's-veto asymmetries (the 'courts-rarely-overrule-refusing-16-and-17s-but-do-order-treatment-for-15-year-olds' comparative mess), the mature-minor-blood-and-contraception-and-abortions-and-body-piercing applications, and the 'best-interests-of-the-child-with-the-welfare-paramount' standard that parents themselves cannot waive - the three-tier structure (child-competent-consents, parental-responsibility, court-ultimate) hiding in plain sight in every paediatric chapter of this library.**
:::

## The hard cases

::: definition Advance decisions, proxies, uncertainty and research {#def-hard}
**Advance decisions are autonomy projected into a future the author cannot observe: the instruments' legal teeth vary (the refusals-treatment-binding-versus-request-treatment-advisory asymmetry of the English statute, the living-will-movement's document ecology, the physician-orders-for-scope-of-treatment-and-relinquishing-documents' back-door through the chart), and their ethics is interpretation: applying words written for imagined scenarios to the actual one (the 'unchosen-circumstances-and-specification-of-wishes' problem), the authenticity test (does this still express the author's values, given the dementia-paradox literature on changed preferences), and the clinical-preservation reflex that makes valid refusals honoured unevenly by condition and by patient. Proxy decision-making (surrogates and attorneys) runs on the hierarchy that ethicists argue (substituted judgement - what this patient would say; best interests - what we would owe a patient; and the hybrid the case law actually uses) and that empirically fails in predictable ways (the optimism-and-projection bias, the family-hierarchy dynamics that sideline the person who actually knew, and the 'surrogates-choose-worse-than-patients-predict-for-themselves' studies that motivate the values-history conversation while the patient can still give it - the 'what-would-make-life-worth-enduring' interview that belongs to the communication chapter's serious-illness script). Consent under uncertainty (prognostic and diagnostic) is where the elements strain hardest: the risk-communication instruments (absolute numbers, framing counter-balances, the 'we-don't-know-and-here-is-what-we-can-say' register), the therapeutic-illusion management in trials (the 'consent-forms-that-read-like-trials-read-like-therapy' problem and the comprehension-check designs), and the deferred-and-reconsent practice (consent as a relationship revisited at each phase and each material change, the 'process-consent' evidence in dementia research). Research consent's stricter species adds its own doctrines: the incentive-coercion gradient (payment ethics between undue influence and just compensation, the 'research-subjects-with-scientific-pregnancy-and-placebo-exposure' special cases), the broad-consent-and-future-use-and-data-governance-councils of the biobank era (the 'one-time-versus-per-project-versus-metadata-of-use' consent models and their trust data), the emergency-research waivers (the 'consent-in-the-unconscious-patient-and-community-consultation'-pathway the resuscitation-science literature built), and the post-enrolment duties (returning results, re-contacting, the withdrawal-of-participation-versus-withdrawal-of-data distinction that the genomic databases made a household legal question). Across all of it the chapter's single practical sentence: consent is what the patient can be relied upon to have understood and chosen when it becomes expensive - and every element above exists to make that sentence testable.**
:::

::: example The son with the folder {#ex-advance}
A 79-year-old is brought in drowsy with sepsis; his son produces a printed 'advance decision' refusing 'tube-feeds and ventilators', unsigned and undated, and demands 'no intensive care'. The notes hold a nursing-home care-plan review, six months newer, recording the man's wish for 'full treatment short of resuscitation'.
::: solution
**Documents-interpret-not-instruct ([[#def-capacity]], [[#def-hard]], [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]]): The legal-engineering read: a valid and applicable advance decision to refuse life-prolonging treatment must meet the formal tests (the statute's specificity-and-voluntariness-and-applicability requirements; an unsigned undated general statement rarely binds for life-prolonging treatment), and the newer care-plan entry is the better evidence of current wishes anyway - the hierarchy being: the patient's contemporaneous best-communicated will first, then valid advance refusals, then the totality of documented values, then surrogate judgement, then best interests. The clinical moment: treat the sepsis now (antibiotics, fluids, ward-level care), because the document's scope is refusal of specified interventions, not of treatment-in-general, and the drowsiness is potentially reversible - the 'capacity-fluctuates-and-so-does-the-ethics' reflex that prevents an old paper from doing irreversible work. The conversation with the son: the folder's arrival is a gift, not an ambush (he is trying to honour his father; the team's job is to show which pieces are honoured automatically - the CPR status, the ceiling of care discussed and documented today - and which the law cannot take at face value), the family-meeting-with-geriatrician-and-palliative-colleagues sets the ceiling properly (the 'maximum-treatment-at-appropriate-level' framing that most such documents, honestly read, are reaching for), and the whole episode is written back into the care plan with a properly made advance decision offered (the right conversation on the right day). The systems lesson: the fragmented-document-ecology (nursing-home forms, printed internet wills, resuscitation mandates in different systems) is why every serious care-plan review should produce one canonical, witnessed, accessible decision-support document, and why the ambulance and admission paperwork should point to where it lives.**
:::

::: example The vaccine card and the mature fifteen {#ex-gillick}
A 15-year-old with a needle-phobia history asks for the school HPV vaccine 'because everyone else is having it'; the parents object 'until we discuss family decisions'. The school nurse holds the consent form.
::: solution
**Evolving-competence-in-practice ([[#def-capacity]], [[paediatrics/immunisation]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]): The doctrine applied: competence is decision-specific and this is a decision a well-informed fifteen-year-old may be capable of (the 'Gillick-competence' line's home territory - the nurse's structured assessment of understanding, retention, weighing and stable choice, documented), and the parental veto-versus-consent asymmetries (a competent minor's consent generally stands even against parents for routine-prophylaxis-level decisions; a competent minor's refusal is treated with more deference by the case law's pattern than by its rules - the 'courts-rarely-compel-treatment-of-adolescents-refusing' reality) put the presumption with the young person when competence is found. The practice layer that actually decides the day: the family conversation first (the parents' objection is about process and protection - both answerable: the nurse's information offer, the chance to withdraw after, the 'she can change her mind at any point including after' script), the privacy-and-confidentiality obligations if she discloses why she wants it (the safeguarding screen that belongs to every adolescent-clinic encounter - the 'why-today-and-who-else-knows' questions done well), and the chaperone-and-comfort measures that honour the needle phobia she volunteered as an inconvenience (the applied-literature techniques - the lying-position-and-tense-and-distraction protocols - that turn consent into a tolerable experience, which is the next vaccination's best advocate). The system's honesty check: school-immunisation programmes' consent-architecture inconsistencies (self-consent practice against parental-notification policy vary more than their legal theory admits), and the fair answer is the published, pre-agreed route: assess, inform the parents unless the young person's safety says otherwise, proceed when competence is documented, and never let the 'everyone else is having it' pressure substitute for the nurse's own conversation, because herd logic is her reason, not hers.**
:::

## Where this leads

Capacity's psychiatric applications in [[nervous-system/dementia-neurodegeneration]] and [[psychiatry/psychiatric-assessment]], the mental-health-law balance in [[psychiatry/treatments-mental-health-law]], children in [[paediatrics/safeguarding]] and [[obstetrics-gynaecology/contraception-fertility]], consent culture in [[clinical-skills/communication]] and [[surgery/perioperative-care]], research consent in [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]], and the ethical frame in [[medical-ethics-law/ethical-principles]].

::: history
**Consent's genealogy runs from battery to partnership: the early-twentieth-century American cases (Schloendorff's 'every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body' - the sentence that made self-determination a legal object, though its author's later practice with it was uglier), the therapeutic-privilege era when disclosure was what doctors judged patients could bear, Salgo's 1957 coining of 'informed consent' in a steroid-paralysis case, the Canterbury-versus-Nathanson-and-Ungar-line judgments' 1972 shift toward the reasonable patient, and the Montgomery-generation judgments completing the patient-materiality migration across the common law. Capacity's counter-history: the incompetency-probate traditions and their institutional abuses, the deinstitutionalisation and patient-rights litigation of the 1960s-70s (the Wyatt-and-Rutherford-line right-to-refuse-treatment cases, the consent-to-psychosurgery-commissions), the President's-Commission-era 'competency-is-task-specific' reformulation that gave the law its modern grammar, the mental-capacity statutes of the 2000s (the English 2005 Act's presumption-and-best-interests architecture, the Irish and Scottish variants, and the disability-convention's Article-12 challenge that the 2014 General-Comment interpretation turned into a reform programme against substituted judgement itself - a debate the jurisdictions are settling at different speeds and in different directions). Advance directives grew from the 1969-living-will-coinage and the 1976-Lanier-and-Karen-Ann-Quinlan-era cases, through the 1990 Patient-Self-Determination-Act's hospital-policy requirements, to the portable-orders-and-out-of-hospital-DNR apparatus whose mismatch-with-documents the emergency literature keeps counting; the dementia-paradox and authenticity debates arrived with the diseases' epidemiology. Paediatric consent's modern line runs from the parental-rights-primacy era through the child-personhood cases and Gillick's 1985 House-of-Lords judgement (the competence-of-the-under-16 formulation that exported through the common-law world under its colloquial name), the 'mature-minor-and-refusal' asymmetries the appellate courts never fully resolved, and the adolescent-confidentiality statutes with their public-health carve-outs. Research consent's arc belongs to the next chapter's history; the clinical chapter's closes with the signature's quiet reversal of status: from the document that ended an operation's legality question to one artefact in a relationship whose moral core is that the patient remains the author - a shift the form's growth in words ironically measures.** - [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]], [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]].**
:::

::: summary
- Consent is a process with testable elements - material disclosure, comprehension (taught back), voluntariness (protected from the power gradient), capacity, and authorisation - and its signature only evidences the smallest of them.
- Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific under a presumption: assessed on understanding, retention, weighing and communication, with support supplied first and the evidential bar rising with the decision's seriousness and irreversibility.
- Diagnoses do not lack capacity; people do, at moments: the delirium-and-sleep-and-glycaemia groundwork belongs to the assessment, and protected eccentric reasons differ from absent weighing - the distinction courts and wards still confuse.
- Advance decisions and proxies translate authorship across incapacity but interpret rather than instruct: the document hierarchy, the authenticity question and the newer-care-plan principle decide real cases, and the canonically-made single document beats the folder.
- Paediatric practice runs the three-tier structure (competent-young-person, parental-responsibility, court-ultimate) with the competence assessment done properly in both directions and the family conversation preceding rather than replacing it.
- Research consent polices the clinical ones stricter on voluntariness and comprehension because the purpose differs: the therapeutic illusion must be dispelled, incentives audited, and consent treated as a continuing relationship revisited at every material change.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Document the assessment {level=1}
Write the capacity-assessment paragraph (as it should appear in the notes) for a 68-year-old with mild cognitive impairment refusing an urgent MRI for suspected cord compression, having had delirium screened and corrected.
::: solution
**Notes-that-survive-review ([[#def-capacity]], [[clinical-skills/records-handover]]): Model text: 'Capacity assessment for the MRI decision, 14:20, after UTIC-and-delirium-screen negative, glucose-and-sodium-and-oxygen normal, usual medication given at 09:00 and no sedative given. Understanding: restated in her own words the cord-compression concern and that the scan decides treatment. Retention: recalled the discussion's elements at 10 minutes unprompted. Weighing: engaged the trade-offs spontaneously ('I know I could end up in a wheelchair, but I will not go back to that hospital after Dad died there'); the aversion is emotionally continuous with her history - her reasons are her own, though not good ones to me. Communicating: consistent choice across two conversations four hours apart, and with her daughter present when asked alone. Doubt-and-support record: information sheet left, decision-aid numbers given (absolute benefit range stated), daughter-available-as-supporter-not-decider, GP-and-usual-clinician-concurrence sought, reconsideration-offered-and-documented. Conclusion: capacity present for this decision at this time; refusal stands; safety-net plan agreed and written (re-contact-criteria, next-morning GP review, ambulance advice given and demonstrated understood).' The teaching annotation: every clause maps to a framework element or a support step, the conclusion states the decision and time, and the paragraph is written for the reader who was not there - the only audience that matters when capacity is later disputed.**
:::

::: exercise Handle the surrogate conflict {level=2}
An incapacitated stroke patient's court-appointed attorney and his long-term partner of twenty years agree on the goal ('comfort, not machines') but disagree on place (home-with-hospice versus the nursing facility the attorney controls); the patient's old notes say 'home or nowhere'. Advise the clinical team.
::: solution
**Goal-confluence-place-conflict ([[#def-capacity]], [[#def-hard]], [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]]): The frame: the disagreement is administrative dressed as moral - the substituted-judgement standard the attorney must apply is the patient's own expressed values ('home or nowhere' is direct evidence and the attorney's duty is to it, not to convenience or liability), and the partner is the values-witness whose testimony the law undervalues by default (the 'who-knew-him' evidence rule that clinical teams can strengthen by documenting the goals conversation and its sources now). The clinical team's moves: hold the meeting both-halves-first (confirm agreement on the goal explicitly in the record - most place-conflicts deflate when the goal is said aloud), translate the patient's sentence into requirements (what 'home or nowhere' meant - his bed, his cat, not dying in a corridor; which of those a hospice-at-home package versus an excellent-facility-room can meet), and pressure the legal machinery appropriately (the attorney's statutory duties include the patient's expressed wishes and, if the partner's evidence is material and unrepresented, the teams' route is the court-declaration-not-ward-attrition - the process that settles this in days with reasons). The equity and justice note: the partner's standing varies by jurisdiction (the next-of-kin-hierarchy statutes that exclude chosen family - the reform critiques and why the clinical documentation of 'the person who matters' is advocacy the team can do while anyone can still be asked). The bedside truth: the patient said his piece years ago, and the honouring apparatus - attorney, records, law - is the thing currently failing; the clinicians' task is to aim every instrument at his words, not at each other.**
:::

::: exercise Fix the consent culture {level=2}
Your trust's audit finds: consent forms signed a mean of 9 minutes before theatre, comprehension of key risks near-zero for day-case procedures, interpreters booked but not used 'because the family is here', and a 'surgery-on-the-cancellation-list' pressure culture. Write the improvement plan with evidence labels.
::: solution
**Consent-as-system-design ([[#def-elements]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]): The instruments by strength of evidence: decision-aids and structured pre-op education (the Cochrane-graded improvements in knowledge and realistic expectation, modest on anxiety), the teach-back requirement as a checked item not a checkbox (the comprehension-literature's only reliable repair), the 'cooling-off-by-design' defaults for non-urgent surgery (consent conversations at a separate visit or day - the '9-minutes-before' practice's remedy is scheduling, not exhortation), interpreter policy with teeth (the 'family-interpreting' prohibition except emergency or patient choice, with the use-audited-by-default because the booked-but-not-used finding is a known failure mode and the equity data on interpreter refusal's outcome effects keep getting buried in guideline appendices), and the cancellation-pressure countermeasure (the consent-quality protected-time rule - the surgeon's anaesthetist-and-list-management economics must not compete with the patient's understanding, which means consent conversations are staffed as clinical work with their own capacity line). The governance: the consent-comprehension audit repeated as a trust dashboard (sampled teach-backs, not signature completeness), the 'surgery-can-wait-until-understanding' norm led by the anaesthetists (whose pre-assessment is the last defensible interception point), and the training that works (the communication-skills deliberate-practice-with-feedback format, not the e-module). The closing data point the board paper needs: the consent-litigation environment's shift to patient-materiality standards means the 9-minute form is now the legal liability it was always an ethical failure.**

::: exercise Advise on the trial payment {level=3}
A phase-1 trial in healthy volunteers offers 4,000 pounds for five days' admission; recruitment skews to debt-holders and students. The sponsor says the payment is 'compensation for time' and the ethics committee approves. Advise the committee on payment ethics with the literature's frameworks, and name the point at which compensation becomes salience.
::: solution
**Payment-coercion-and-justice ([[#def-hard]], [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]]): The frameworks: the undue-influence standard (payment that 'subverts the voluntariness the subject's will would otherwise exercise' - the classic formulation) versus the just-compensation standard (payment for time and burden at a fair rate, with the 'wage-equivalence' proposals - minimum-wage-times-effort benchmarks) versus the structural-justice frame (the recruitment-skew-to-the-desperate finding means the payment's ethics is an inequality question: identical money is different temptation at different bank balances). The committee's defensible position with conditions: rate benchmarked to wage-equivalent work (not to the risk nor to the market's urgency), the 'break-even-by-attrition' structure removed (payments pro-rated for withdrawal-at-any-stage - the withdrawal-penalty-by-accounting finding that coercion literature's concrete fix), the risk-to-payment decoupling stated (payment is for time, not for assuming risks - the sentence that makes the consent form honest), the cooling-off interval between payment-revealed and consent-signed, and the recruitment-source audit as a monitored indicator (the 'debt-advice-leaflets-in-the-screening-pack' disclosure duty: if you are recruiting the financially vulnerable, say what you think you are doing and why it is still permissible). The salience threshold named: when the payment's weight in the enrolment decision exceeds the study's own risk-communication clarity - operationalised as the screened-cohort's 'why-are-you-here' answers, where payment-dominant response proportions above a pre-set level trigger redesign. The honesty sentence: phase-1 economics (the supply-market geography of trial sites) is the upstream variable, and a committee that optimises its subjects' welfare against the sponsor's calendar is the one that must at least price its own compromise aloud.**
:::

::: example The interpreter who wasn't {#ex-interpreter}
A Romanian builder's wife signs the consent for his hernia repair; she translates his history of 'a previous reaction to the anaesthetic' as 'no problems before'. The anaesthetist's pre-assessment notes 'family interpreting, seemed fine'.
::: solution
**Family-interpreting-is-a-safety-event ([[#def-elements]], [[clinical-skills/communication]], [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]): The double failure documented: the interpreter was booked (the finding's own punchline - booked-not-used is the audit class) and the family channel corrupted the two safety-critical data points (the drug-reaction history and the consent's comprehension) in the direction that gets the operation done - the 'family-as-interpreter' literature's known hazards (omission, summarisation, protective edits, and the power inversion of a spouse safeguarding or shielding). The immediate duties: the surgery paused and the history retaken through a professional interpreter (the drug-reaction question is a 'cannot-skip-under-any-list-pressure' item - the malignant-hyperthermia-and-suxamethonium-apnoea histories that arrive through exactly this gap), consent re-done with the same channel with teach-back, and the interpreter-availability economics faced honestly (the video-and-telephone-interpretation services that make the correct route cheaper than the litigation and the near-miss). The policy with the evidence behind it: family-interpreting prohibited except patient-declared-and-documented-choice in low-stakes settings or genuine emergency; the 'booked-and-used' audited as a safety indicator (the same dashboard position as the WHO-surgical-checklist items, because it belongs to the same harm class); the training that 'the appointment is with the patient, not the pair' for reception and ward staff (the 'they-understand-each-other-anyway' excuse's documented survival); and the equity frame stated plainly: interpreter refusal concentrates on the least powerful patients and its costs are theirs, which is why the guideline bodies now file it under discrimination as well as safety. The consent document's retrospective value: zero - the signature captured the wrong man's understanding.**
:::

::: example The fluctuating night {#ex-fluctuation}
A 74-year-old three days post-hip replacement refuses transfer to rehabilitation at 22:00, angrily, on a background of familiar evening confusion; the morning team found him 'clear and agreeing'. The night registrar must decide tonight.
::: solution
**Fluctuating-capacity-protocol ([[#def-capacity]], [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]]): The clinical read first: evening-predominant confusion in a hospitalised older adult is delirium until proven otherwise (the 'sundowning'-as-diagnosis failure), and the search for its drivers (opioids, anticholinergics, retention, constipation, infection, hypoxia, the sleep-and-glasses-and-hearing-aids-and-food-off-the-bedside-wardrobe) is the capacity intervention as much as the ethics one - the 'restore-capacity-first' rule with a treatment list. The ethics machinery: capacity is time-specific, so the 22:00 refusal does not overturn the morning consent; the correct move is the deferred decision (reassess at his best hour with his usual supporters and the simplified re-explanation, not a nocturnal argument), the minimal-treatment-tonight (what the day genuinely requires: analgesia, mobilisation-with-physio-in-the-morning, safety checks - not coercion to a transfer he can consent to at breakfast), and the documentation of the fluctuation pattern itself (the capacity-note's time-stamped pair - morning-agreement, night-refusal-with-delirium-features - which is the record that protects whichever decision eventually stands). The escalation only if morning brings a fixed refusal with clear sensorium (then the full assessment with the usual support and the second opinion, and the sliding-scale evidential bar applied to a refusal whose stakes - deconditioning, falls, home-loss - deserve the seriousness). The ward-system lesson: the delirium-friendly protocols (the help-in-the-room orientation aids, the sleep-protection bundle, the family-presence policies) are ethics infrastructure, because the 22:00 crisis is mostly the ward's own iatrogenic environment reflected back as a capacity problem.**
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Choose the standard {level=1}
State which decision-making standard applies (substituted judgement, best interests, patient-authority, or the specific legal mechanism) to each: (a) the demented patient's longstanding 'I'd never live like that' remark versus current contentment; (b) the trauma patient with no known wishes; (c) the competent dialysis patient's refusal of transfusion documented from a year ago, now unconscious.
::: solution
**Standards-matched-to-facts ([[#def-capacity]], [[#def-hard]]): (a) The dementia-paradox crucible: substituted judgement toward the prior statement versus best interests weighted by current experienced welfare - the law's hybrid (the 'past-values-as-strong-evidence-but-not-binding-absent-formal-directive' position, tempered by the authenticity reading that the remark expressed a value not a prediction), and the clinical duty to make the choice explicit and documented (the values-history interview before the paradox arrives). (b) Emergency best interests: the immediate-treatment doctrine with the reasonable-person standard, and the 'what-would-any-vigorous-account-of-his-values-suggest' search that never becomes a family-veto. (c) A valid and applicable advance refusal (the statute's formality-and-specificity tests applied to the document, the 'she-refused-transfusion-again-last-admission' consistency evidence) - patient-authority in absentia, which binds and which the 'clinical-preservation-reflex' literature shows is honoured unevenly by condition; the document's scope (transfusion, not all treatment) then reads the night's plan. The exercise's meta-point: each standard answers a different question (what would he say, what would anyone say, what does he say), and mislabeling which is operative is how most ward conflicts become unwinnable.**

::: exercise Teach capacity to a nursing shift {level=1}
Give the five-sentence version a charge nurse should carry: presumption, decision-specificity, support-first, the weighing-element's role, and the documentation rule. Then the one-sentence version for a busy ward.
::: solution
**Capacity-in-five-plus-one ([[#def-capacity]]): The five: every adult is presumed to decide for themselves until this decision, now, shows an impairment of the mind or brain getting in the way; capacity is about the decision (the same patient can consent to bloods and lack capacity for the amputation, and vice versa across the day); before any finding of incapacity, maximise (the glasses, the hearing aid, the quiet room, the morning-not-night, the trusted person, the pictorial aid - support is the assessment's prerequisite, not its footnote); the pivotal ability is usually weighing (patients may choose for reasons we hate; they must be engaging with the reasons - the line between eccentric and incapable); and if you write 'lacks capacity', write why in the four abilities' language, because the note may be read by a judge who was never in the room. The one sentence: ask what the brain is doing about this decision today, help it as far as it goes, and write what you found - which is the entire craft before any of the statutes.**

::: exercise Audit the minors policy {level=2}
Your region's adolescent-clinic guidance says: under-16s need parental consent; 16-17-year-olds can consent to treatment but refusals 'can be overridden'; courts are 'the final say' for serious refusals. Identify the doctrine each sentence compresses and the two places the compressions mislead practice.
::: solution
**Doctrines-under-the-sentences ([[#def-capacity]], [[paediatrics/safeguarding]]): Sentence one compresses Gillick-competence (under-16s with sufficient maturity and understanding may consent for themselves - the 'need parental consent' blanket is legally wrong and practically fatal: it deters the contraception-and-abortion-and-mental-health-and-substance presentations whose access depends on the exception); sentence two compresses the mature-minor asymmetry (the case law's pattern: courts rarely compel treatment on refusing adolescents even while nominally retaining the power - the 'refusals-of-16-and-17s-rarely-overridden-in-practice' reality that the sentence hides and that clinics must navigate with the safeguarding-not-stripping reflex); sentence three is true with a footnote: courts are the final say for serious contested cases, which means the team's job is early-ethics-consultation-and-declaration-seeking rather than midnight-coercion that the review will undo. The two misleading compressions named: the under-16 blanket (the confidentiality-and-competence expectations every adolescent-medicine curriculum contradicts, and the safeguarding cost of sending competent minors home to parents who might be part of the problem) and the override threat (the 'we-can-make-you' posture that converts a treatable refusal into a relapse - the psychiatric-adolescent literature's therapeutic-alliance findings). The audit's output: a rewritten one-pager with the competence-assessment template, the confidentiality rules with their public-health and safeguarding exceptions listed, the 'who-must-we-tell' section for the serious-refusal pathway, and the young-person's leaflet version - which is the document that actually travels.**

::: exercise Write the policy on withdrawal of consent {level=2}
Draft the trial-policy paragraph on a participant's mid-study withdrawal: the data question, the samples question, the therapeutic relationship question, and the relationship the sponsor will not accept but the ethics require.
::: solution
**Withdrawal-as-a-right-with-consequences-answered ([[#def-hard]], [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]]): Draft: 'A participant may withdraw from a trial at any time without reason and without prejudice to their clinical care. Withdrawal of further participation is distinguished in the consent conversation from withdrawal of already-collected data: data analysed in aggregate cannot practically be recalled once pooled, and the policy states what will be done with the participant's individual records up to withdrawal (retained-and-analysed-as-a-de-identified-record versus destroyed-on-request, mapped to the data-protection erasure right and its research-exemption conditions, and stated per-jurisdiction honestly). Biological samples follow their own consent tier (the 'samples-may-be-destroyed-on-request-unless-already-anonymised-or-in-published-analysis' rule written in the original consent, because it cannot be added later). The clinical relationship is explicitly firewalled: the treating team learns of withdrawal only if the participant agrees, and trial-withdrawal never affects access to the study drug where open-label extension or compassionate provisions exist. The paragraph the sponsors dislike: withdrawal rates and their reasons are a study outcome reported in the publication (the 'participants-are-not-noise' accountability rule that the consent-'withdraw-any-time'-promise is worthless without - a right whose exercise is hidden is a right that embarrasses, and the literature on under-reported withdrawal in psychotherapy-and-devices-trials shows how much retention bias the reporting duty corrects). The operational appendix: the withdrawal conversation's script (thank, ask-if-tellable-why-only-if-willing, confirm-data-preference, schedule any safety follow-up the participant accepts), because the moment a participant feels guilty withdrawing, the consent has been retroactively converted into an obligation.**
