Nadine Montgomery was a pregnant woman with insulin-dependent diabetes and of small stature. Babies of mothers with diabetes tend to be large, and her obstetrician knew that the risk of shoulder dystocia, where the baby's shoulder becomes stuck after the head is delivered, was about 9–10% in women like her. She did not tell Mrs Montgomery, because in her view, if she mentioned it, most women would ask for a caesarean section. Mrs Montgomery did ask whether the baby's size was a problem. During labour, shoulder dystocia occurred, and her son was born with cerebral palsy. In 2015 the UK Supreme Court held that she should have been told, and that the law on what patients must be told about risk had changed.

Consent is where respect for autonomy meets everyday practice. Almost every examination, test and treatment needs it. This chapter explains what makes consent valid in England and Wales, what information patients must be given, how to assess whether a person has the **capacity** to decide, how decisions are made for adults who lack capacity under the Mental Capacity Act 2005, and how the rules apply to children and young people.

## What makes consent valid {#valid-consent}

Touching a person without lawful justification is a **battery**, both a civil wrong and potentially a crime. Consent is what makes a clinical intervention lawful. In 1914 an American judge, Benjamin Cardozo, put the principle in words that are still quoted: "Every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body."

::: definition Valid consent {#def-valid-consent}
Consent to examination, treatment or care is **valid** when:

1. the person has the **capacity** to make the decision;
2. they have been given sufficient **information**, in a way they can understand, about the nature and purpose of the intervention, its material risks and the reasonable alternatives, including doing nothing;
3. the decision is **voluntary**: free from coercion or undue influence.

Consent may be **express** (spoken or written) or **implied** by conduct, such as holding out an arm for a blood test. A signature on a form is evidence that a discussion took place; it is not itself the consent and does not make consent valid if the discussion was inadequate.
:::

Consent is a **process**, not an event: the GMC's *Decision making and consent* (2020) describes a dialogue in which doctor and patient decide together. Consent can be withdrawn at any time, even part-way through a procedure, unless stopping would itself put the patient at serious risk.

Consent should be sought by the person doing the procedure or a suitably trained delegate who can answer the patient's questions.

English law distinguishes two kinds of failure. If a patient was not told **in broad terms** what the procedure was, any consent is not real and the clinician may be liable for battery (*Chatterton v Gerson*, 1981). If the patient knew the nature of the procedure but was not told about a risk they should have been told about, the claim is in **negligence**, and the patient must show that the failure caused them harm.

## Information: from Bolam to Montgomery {#information}

For decades the question "what must a patient be told?" was answered in the same way as other questions about the standard of care: by asking what a responsible body of doctors would do. That was the **Bolam** test, applied to information about risk by the House of Lords in *Sidaway v Bethlem Royal Hospital* (1985). Under it, if many doctors would not mention a risk, failing to mention it was not negligent.

*Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11 replaced that test for information disclosure. The Supreme Court said that patients are now treated as adults capable of understanding risk and making decisions, and that the law should reflect this.

::: theorem The Montgomery duty of disclosure {#thm-montgomery}
A doctor must take reasonable care to ensure that the patient is aware of any **material risks** involved in any recommended treatment, and of any **reasonable alternative or variant treatments**.

A risk is **material** if, in the circumstances of the particular case,

- a reasonable person in the patient's position would be likely to attach significance to it, **or**
- the doctor is or should reasonably be aware that this particular patient would be likely to attach significance to it.

Materiality cannot be reduced to percentages. It depends on the nature of the risk, its effect on the patient's life, the importance to the patient of the benefits, the alternatives and their risks. The information must be given in a way the patient can understand, through dialogue, not by "bombarding the patient with technical information". There is a narrow **therapeutic exception** where disclosure would seriously harm the patient's health, but it cannot be used to withhold information because the doctor fears the patient will make a choice the doctor disagrees with.
:::

::: proof
Why did the court abandon *Bolam* here? *Bolam* asks a question that doctors are experts in: was the treatment skilfully chosen and performed? Whether a patient would want to know about a risk is a different kind of question. It depends on the patient's values and circumstances, on which the patient, not the profession, is the authority. A test based on what doctors customarily say lets the profession set the limits of the patient's right to decide.

The "reasonable person" limb protects patients who ask no questions; the "particular patient" limb protects those for whom a risk matters especially, such as a violinist facing hand surgery. Mrs Montgomery satisfied both: most women would want to know of a 9–10% risk of shoulder dystocia, and she had asked about her baby's size.

The obstetrician's reason for not mentioning the risk, that the patient would choose caesarean section, is exactly what the test excludes. Choosing a different option because of information about risk is not a harm; it is the purpose of informing.
:::

Two later developments are important. In *McCulloch v Forth Valley Health Board* [2023] UKSC 26, the Supreme Court held that whether an alternative treatment is "reasonable", and so must be discussed, is decided by the professional practice test: a doctor need not discuss an alternative that they, supported by a responsible body of medical opinion, judge not to be reasonable. The doctor must still discuss all the reasonable alternatives, not only the one they prefer.

On causation, in *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41 a surgeon failed to warn of a 1–2% risk of cauda equina syndrome. The patient could show only that she would have delayed surgery to seek advice, yet a majority of the House of Lords let her recover, because otherwise the duty to warn would be empty.

### Communicating risk {#risk-communication}

Meeting the Montgomery duty depends on communicating risk well. Research on risk communication gives consistent advice:

- Use **absolute** risks and **natural frequencies** ("about 2 in every 100 people") rather than relative risks ("doubles the risk") alone, with the same denominator for every option.
- Describe both the chance of harm and the chance of no harm; use icon arrays where helpful.
- Check understanding with **teach-back**: ask the patient to explain in their own words.
- Use a professional interpreter, not a family member, when there is a language barrier.

::: example Consent for a laparoscopic cholecystectomy {#ex-montgomery}
A 52-year-old woman who works as a professional singer is listed for laparoscopic cholecystectomy for recurrent biliary colic. The surgeon's consent form lists bleeding, infection, bile leak, bile duct injury and conversion to open surgery. During the discussion she mentions an important concert tour in six weeks. What else should the consent discussion include?
::: solution
**Material risks for a reasonable person:** the common and serious risks, with numbers where known (bile duct injury is rare, a few in 1,000 operations, but serious), and recovery time.

**Material risks for this patient:** she has told the surgeon what matters to her. The effect of general anaesthesia on her voice, including the small risk of laryngeal or vocal cord injury from intubation, and the time needed to recover enough to sing on tour, are material for her even if they would not be for most patients. The surgeon should discuss these, perhaps with the anaesthetist, who may consider airway techniques that reduce risk to the larynx.

**Reasonable alternatives:** watchful waiting with dietary advice, or delaying surgery until after the tour, with the risks of further attacks or complications such as cholecystitis or pancreatitis in the meantime.

**Process:** give her time and written information, and record the specific risks discussed and her priorities.
:::
:::

## Voluntariness {#voluntariness}

Consent must be the person's own decision. Persuasion by reasoned argument is legitimate, and doctors can and should recommend what they think is best. **Coercion** (threats) and **undue influence** (pressure that overbears the person's will) invalidate consent.

In *Re T (Adult: Refusal of Treatment)* (1992), a 20-year-old woman injured in a car crash refused a blood transfusion after spending time alone with her mother, a Jehovah's Witness, although she was not a Witness herself. The Court of Appeal held that her refusal was not valid, partly because of her mother's influence and partly because she had not understood that her life might depend on transfusion. The case also restated the central rule: an adult with capacity may refuse treatment for any reason, rational or irrational, or for no reason at all.

Pressure can also come from clinicians and institutions. Seeing a patient alone for part of the discussion, offering time, and making clear that refusal will not mean abandonment all protect voluntariness.

## Capacity and the Mental Capacity Act 2005 {#capacity}

Consent is valid only if the person has capacity to give it. In England and Wales the **Mental Capacity Act 2005** (MCA), in force since 2007, sets out the test and how decisions are made for people aged 16 and over who lack capacity. Its accompanying *Code of Practice* (2007) gives detailed guidance; a revised Code has been consulted on but, as of 2026, not issued.

::: definition The five principles of the Mental Capacity Act (s1) {#def-mca-principles}
1. A person must be **assumed to have capacity** unless it is established that they lack it.
2. A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision unless **all practicable steps** to help them do so have been taken without success.
3. A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision merely because they make an **unwise decision**.
4. An act done or decision made for a person who lacks capacity must be done or made in their **best interests**.
5. Before acting, consider whether the purpose can be achieved in a way that is **less restrictive** of the person's rights and freedom of action.
:::

::: definition Lack of capacity (ss2–3) {#def-capacity-test}
A person lacks capacity in relation to a matter if, at the material time, they are **unable to make a decision for themselves** in relation to the matter **because of** an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain (s2). The impairment may be permanent or temporary.

A person is unable to make a decision (s3) if they cannot:

1. **understand** the information relevant to the decision;
2. **retain** that information (long enough to make the decision);
3. **use or weigh** that information as part of the process of making the decision; or
4. **communicate** the decision by any means.

The relevant information includes the reasonably foreseeable consequences of deciding one way or another, or of not deciding. The question is decided on the **balance of probabilities**.
:::

Capacity is **decision-specific** and **time-specific**: a person may be able to choose what to eat but not manage complex finances, or lack capacity during delirium and regain it after treatment. Diagnosis never settles the question; s2(3) forbids establishing incapacity merely by reference to age, appearance, a condition or behaviour.

In *A Local Authority v JB* [2021] UKSC 52, the Supreme Court said that the test is best applied in a particular order. First, ask the **functional** question: is the person unable to make the decision, by reference to the four abilities? Only then ask whether that inability is **because of** an impairment or disturbance of the mind or brain. This order prevents a diagnosis from colouring the assessment of ability, and makes clear that there must be a causal link.

"Use or weigh" is often the hardest element. It does not mean the person must give each factor the weight the clinician would. It means they must be able to engage with the information in reaching a decision. A person with severe anorexia nervosa may understand that they are dangerously underweight but be unable to weigh that information because the illness makes the fear of weight gain overwhelm everything else. A person who simply values things differently, such as a Jehovah's Witness who understands the risk of refusing blood and chooses to follow their faith, is weighing the information, even if the clinician would choose otherwise.

::: warning An unwise decision is not proof of incapacity
Clinicians tend to question capacity only when patients refuse what is recommended, and to accept agreement without question. Both habits are mistakes. A person who agrees to everything may lack capacity, and a person who refuses may have it. An unusual or risky decision is a reason to look carefully at capacity, especially if it is out of character, but it is never enough to conclude that capacity is absent. Equally, capacity is not "all or nothing": record which decision was assessed, when, how the person was helped, and the evidence for each of the four abilities.
:::

::: example A night of delirium {#ex-delirium}
An 81-year-old man with a urinary tract infection is admitted at 2 a.m. He is agitated, inattentive and believes he is at a railway station. He pulls out his cannula and says he wants to go home. At 10 a.m., after antibiotics and fluids, he is calm and oriented. How should capacity and treatment be approached over this period?
::: solution
**At 2 a.m.:** he appears to have delirium, a disturbance in the functioning of the brain. Ask the functional question first: can he understand that he has an infection that needs treatment, retain it, weigh the risk of leaving, and communicate a decision? If after all practicable steps (calm explanation, glasses and hearing aids, a quiet space, a familiar person on the phone) he cannot, he lacks capacity for the decision about treatment now. The team may then act in his best interests under s5 of the MCA: continue antibiotics, perhaps orally if he will not tolerate a cannula, and keep him safe using the least restrictive approach, such as one-to-one nursing rather than sedation. Any restraint must be necessary to prevent harm to him and proportionate to the likelihood and seriousness of that harm (s6).

**Can it wait?** Decisions that can wait until he is likely to regain capacity, such as a change of accommodation, should wait.

**At 10 a.m.:** reassess. If he now has capacity, he decides, after a full discussion of the risks. Document each assessment separately, with the time, the decision and the evidence.
:::
:::

## Decisions for adults who lack capacity {#best-interests}

If a person aged 16 or over lacks capacity for a decision, the MCA allows others to act for them, within limits.

::: definition Best interests (s4) {#def-best-interests}
Whoever makes a decision for a person who lacks capacity must act in that person's **best interests**. The decision-maker must:

- not decide on the basis of the person's age, appearance, condition or behaviour alone;
- consider whether and when the person is likely to regain capacity;
- permit and encourage the person to participate as fully as possible;
- where the decision concerns life-sustaining treatment, not be motivated by a desire to bring about the person's death;
- consider, so far as reasonably ascertainable, the person's **past and present wishes and feelings** (especially any written statement), the **beliefs and values** that would be likely to influence their decision, and other factors they would consider;
- take into account the views of anyone named by the person, anyone caring for them or interested in their welfare, any attorney and any deputy.
:::

Best interests is not the same as "medical best interests". In *Aintree University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust v James* [2013] UKSC 67, Lady Hale explained that decision-makers must look at the person's welfare in the widest sense, medical, social and psychological, and try to put themselves in the place of the patient to ask what their attitude to the treatment is or would be likely to be. The test is still best interests, not "what the person would have chosen", but the person's wishes and values carry great weight.

The MCA provides several instruments:

- **Advance decision to refuse treatment (ADRT)** (ss24–26). An adult with capacity can refuse specified treatment in specified circumstances in advance; a valid and applicable ADRT has the same effect as a contemporaneous refusal. Refusal of **life-sustaining treatment** must be in writing, signed, witnessed, and state that it applies even if life is at risk. An ADRT is invalid if withdrawn, overtaken by a later LPA covering the same treatment, or contradicted by clearly inconsistent conduct; it is inapplicable if the treatment or circumstances differ from those specified, or if unanticipated circumstances would have changed the decision. It cannot demand treatment.
- **Advance statements** of wishes and preferences are not binding but must be considered in best interests.
- **Lasting power of attorney (LPA)** (s9). A person aged 18 or over can appoint attorneys for **property and financial affairs** and/or **health and welfare**. The LPA must be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian. A health and welfare attorney can make decisions only when the person lacks capacity, must act in their best interests, and can refuse life-sustaining treatment only if the LPA expressly gives that power.
- **Deputies** may be appointed by the **Court of Protection**, which also decides disputed or serious cases.
- An **Independent Mental Capacity Advocate (IMCA)** must be instructed when an NHS body proposes serious medical treatment for a person who lacks capacity and has nobody other than paid carers to consult.

Relatives without an LPA cannot consent or refuse on behalf of an adult. Their role is to inform the best-interests decision. Telling a family that they must "sign the consent form" for an adult is a common error.

**Deprivation of liberty.** Article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights requires safeguards when care arrangements deprive a person of liberty. In hospitals and care homes in England and Wales these are the **Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards** (DoLS, since 2009); their planned replacement, the Liberty Protection Safeguards (legislated in 2019), has not been implemented as of 2026. In June 2026 the Supreme Court overruled the 2014 *Cheshire West* "acid test" for deprivation of liberty ([2026] UKSC 16), and guidance is being revised.

::: example The son with the folder {#ex-advance}
A 79-year-old woman with motor neurone disease is admitted with respiratory failure and is too drowsy to communicate. Her son brings a folder containing a document, written two years ago, that says: "I do not want to be put on a breathing machine, including a mask, if my illness makes me unable to breathe for myself, even if this means I will die." It is signed by her and by a witness. The respiratory team think non-invasive ventilation (NIV) could relieve her symptoms and prolong her life by months. What should they do?
::: solution
**Is it an ADRT?** It refuses a specified treatment (ventilation, including mask ventilation) in specified circumstances (when her illness makes her unable to breathe for herself).

**Is it valid?** Made when she was an adult and, so far as is known, had capacity. It is in writing, signed and witnessed, and states that it applies even if she will die, so it meets the formal requirements for refusing life-sustaining treatment. The team should check that she has not withdrawn it, has not since made a health and welfare LPA covering ventilation, and has not acted inconsistently with it (for example by recently agreeing to start NIV).

**Is it applicable?** Respiratory failure from MND is exactly the situation she anticipated, and nothing suggests unforeseen circumstances that would have changed her mind.

**Conclusion:** the ADRT binds the team as if she were refusing now, so they must not start NIV, but they must relieve her breathlessness with other means such as opioids and palliative care. Genuine doubt would allow life-sustaining treatment while the Court of Protection decides (s26(5)); doubt must not be manufactured.
:::
:::

**Emergencies.** When a patient cannot consent and immediate treatment is needed to save life or prevent serious deterioration, clinicians may give treatment that is necessary and in the person's best interests (the MCA, and before it the common law doctrine of necessity). Only treatment that cannot reasonably wait may be given. A known, valid refusal, including a valid ADRT, must still be respected.

## Children and young people {#children}

**Young people aged 16 and 17** are presumed to have capacity to consent to their own medical treatment (Family Law Reform Act 1969, s8), and most of the MCA applies to them. Only adults can make an ADRT or LPA.

**Children under 16** may consent if they are **Gillick competent**. In *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech Area Health Authority* (1985), the House of Lords held that a child under 16 can consent to treatment if they have sufficient understanding and intelligence to understand fully what is proposed. Competence depends on the child and the decision: a 12-year-old may be competent to consent to treatment for a fracture but not to a complex operation.

For contraception and sexual health advice to under-16s, Lord Fraser set out guidelines. The clinician should be satisfied that the young person understands the advice; cannot be persuaded to tell their parents or allow the clinician to do so; is likely to have sex with or without contraception; that their physical or mental health is likely to suffer without it; and that it is in their best interests to provide it without parental consent. These **Fraser guidelines** are specific to contraception and sexual health; **Gillick competence** is the general test. Any sexual activity involving a child under 13 is a safeguarding concern requiring action ([[paediatrics/safeguarding]]).

A child who lacks competence is treated with the consent of a person with **parental responsibility**, who must act in the child's best interests. When parents and clinicians disagree about serious treatment, the courts decide, applying the child's best interests.

**Refusal by young people.** English law is asymmetrical. In *Re R* (1991) and *Re W* (1992), the Court of Appeal held that a refusal by a competent child, or by a 16- or 17-year-old, can be overridden by a person with parental responsibility or by the court, in the young person's best interests. Courts have used this to authorise life-saving treatment, such as transfusions refused by teenage Jehovah's Witnesses. In practice, serious disputes go to court rather than relying on parental consent.

::: example A 15-year-old asks for contraception {#ex-gillick}
A 15-year-old girl attends her GP alone and asks for contraception. She has a 16-year-old boyfriend and says they have been having sex for two months. She does not want her parents to know. How should the GP respond?
::: solution
**Competence:** assess her understanding of the options, their benefits and risks, and what the decision involves. A 15-year-old who can discuss these is likely to be Gillick competent.

**Fraser guidelines:** encourage her to involve a parent or another trusted adult, and respect her decision if she will not. Is she likely to continue having sex? She says so. Would her health suffer without contraception (risk of pregnancy and infection)? Is it in her best interests? If all are satisfied, the GP may prescribe without parental consent.

**Safeguarding:** the age gap is small and the relationship sounds consensual, but the GP should ask sensitively about coercion, exploitation, the partner's age and behaviour, alcohol or drugs, and whether she feels safe. Any sign of exploitation or abuse changes the picture and triggers safeguarding procedures.

**Confidentiality and advice:** she is owed confidentiality like an adult, with the usual limits ([[medical-ethics-law/confidentiality]]). Offer advice on condoms and STI testing as well as contraception ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/contraception-fertility]]), and document competence and the Fraser criteria.
:::
:::

**Mental health law.** The Mental Health Act 1983 (substantially amended by the Mental Health Act 2025) allows treatment for **mental disorder** without consent in defined circumstances ([[psychiatry/treatments-mental-health-law]]). It does not authorise treatment of unrelated physical illness. In *Re C* (1994), a man detained in Broadmoor with schizophrenia was held to have capacity to refuse amputation of a gangrenous leg, because he understood, believed and weighed the information. He survived without amputation.

::: quiz
A 45-year-old man with a moderate learning disability needs a dental extraction under general anaesthesia. He is anxious and says "no" when shown the anaesthetic mask. His sister, who has no power of attorney, says she consents on his behalf. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] His sister's consent is sufficient because she is his next of kin.
- [ ] He lacks capacity because he has a learning disability.
- [x] His capacity for this decision must be assessed after all practicable steps to help him understand; if he lacks it, the decision is made in his best interests, with his sister consulted, using the least restrictive option.
- [ ] Because he said "no", the procedure can never go ahead.
::: solution
A learning disability does not establish incapacity, and a relative cannot consent for an adult without an LPA or deputyship. The team should help him understand (easy-read information, a visit, time, people who know him), then assess capacity for this decision. If he lacks it, the clinicians make a best-interests decision, consulting his sister and carers and considering his wishes and feelings, including his fear. Less restrictive options, such as desensitisation or oral premedication, come before any restraint, which must be necessary and proportionate. His "no" is important evidence but does not by itself prevent treatment in his best interests.
:::
:::

::: history
The common law of consent grew from cases about battery, such as *Schloendorff* (1914). The term "informed consent" appeared in the American case *Salgo* (1957), the same year McNair J set out the *Bolam* test in England. *Sidaway* (1985) left disclosure to medical opinion, over a dissent by Lord Scarman, who gave the leading speech in *Gillick* the same year. Other common-law countries moved first to patient-centred disclosure: *Canterbury v Spence* in the United States (1972), *Reibl v Hughes* in Canada (1980) and *Rogers v Whitaker* in Australia (1992). The Law Commission's work on incapacity in the 1990s led to the Mental Capacity Act 2005, which put the functional test already applied in *Re C* (1994) on a statutory footing. *Montgomery* (2015) finally adopted Lord Scarman's view, thirty years on.
:::

::: summary
- Valid consent requires **capacity**, sufficient **information** and **voluntariness**. A signature is evidence of consent, not consent itself; consent is a continuing process and can be withdrawn.
- Under *Montgomery* (2015), doctors must ensure patients know of **material risks** (those a reasonable person in the patient's position, or this particular patient, would consider significant) and **reasonable alternatives**. *McCulloch* (2023) applies the professional practice test to which alternatives are reasonable.
- Communicate risk in absolute terms and natural frequencies, and check understanding with teach-back.
- The **MCA 2005** presumes capacity, requires all practicable help, and protects unwise decisions. Incapacity means inability to understand, retain, use or weigh, or communicate, **because of** an impairment or disturbance of mind or brain. Capacity is decision- and time-specific.
- For adults lacking capacity, decisions are made in their **best interests**, giving weight to their wishes, feelings, beliefs and values, and choosing the least restrictive option. Relatives without an LPA cannot consent.
- A valid and applicable **ADRT** binds clinicians; refusals of life-sustaining treatment must be written, signed, witnessed and state that they apply even if life is at risk.
- 16–17-year-olds are presumed able to consent; under-16s may consent if **Gillick competent**; Fraser guidelines apply to contraception. Refusals by minors can be overridden by the court.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Elements of valid consent {level=1}
A patient signs a consent form for a colonoscopy after a nurse explains only the bowel preparation. The doctor performing the procedure does not speak to her. Is her consent valid? What should happen?
::: solution
Probably not. She knows the nature of the procedure in broad terms, so this is not battery, but she has not been told about material risks (such as perforation and bleeding after polypectomy), the benefits or the alternatives (such as CT colonography). The signature does not cure this. Before sedation, the endoscopist or a suitably trained delegate should discuss these with her, check understanding, answer questions and confirm she still wishes to proceed.
:::
:::

::: exercise The four abilities {level=1}
State the four abilities in the functional test of capacity under s3 of the MCA, and give one example of a practicable step to support each.
::: solution
**Understand** the relevant information: use simple language, pictures, an interpreter or easy-read material. **Retain** it long enough to decide: written notes, repeat the discussion, choose the time of day when the person is most alert. **Use or weigh** it: talk through the options and consequences with the person, involve someone they trust. **Communicate** a decision by any means: speech and language therapist support, communication aids, yes/no signals by blinking or hand squeeze.
:::
:::

::: exercise Natural frequencies {level=1 check="3"}
A procedure carries a 0.3% risk of a serious complication. Express this as a natural frequency: how many people out of 1,000 having the procedure would be expected to have the complication?
::: solution
$0.3\% \times 1{,}000 = 0.003 \times 1{,}000 = 3$. About 3 in every 1,000 people. Saying also that 997 in 1,000 do not have it gives a balanced picture.
:::
:::

::: exercise Relative and absolute risk {level=2 check="100"}
A preventive drug reduces the five-year risk of a stroke from 4% to 3%. A leaflet says it "cuts the risk of stroke by 25%". Calculate the absolute risk reduction and the number of people who need to take the drug for five years to prevent one stroke (the number needed to treat). Give the NNT as a whole number.
::: hint
The absolute risk reduction is the difference between the two risks; the NNT is its reciprocal.
:::
::: solution
Relative risk reduction $= (4 - 3)/4 = 25\%$, which is what the leaflet quotes. Absolute risk reduction $= 4\% - 3\% = 1\% = 0.01$. NNT $= 1/0.01 = 100$. In natural frequencies: of 100 people taking the drug for five years, 1 avoids a stroke, 3 still have one, and 96 would not have had a stroke anyway. Presenting only the 25% overstates the benefit for most patients, and Montgomery-compliant discussion should include the absolute figures.
:::
:::

::: exercise A refusal and capacity {level=2}
A 34-year-old woman at 39 weeks of pregnancy is told that her baby is showing signs of distress and that an emergency caesarean section is recommended. She refuses, saying she wants a natural birth. The midwife says, "She must lack capacity: no mother would risk her baby." Analyse.
::: solution
Pregnancy does not reduce a woman's right to refuse treatment; the fetus has no legal personality separate from the mother until birth (*St George's Healthcare NHS Trust v S*, 1998). The midwife infers incapacity from an unwise decision, the classic error. The team should give her the relevant information, support her understanding, and assess capacity properly. Panic or needle phobia can temporarily remove capacity (*Re MB*, 1997), but only if the functional test shows it. If she has capacity, her refusal must be respected and the offer kept open; genuine doubt about capacity calls for urgent legal advice.
:::
:::

::: exercise The LPA and the ADRT {level=2}
A man made a valid ADRT refusing antibiotics for pneumonia if he developed severe dementia. Two years later, with capacity, he made a health and welfare LPA appointing his daughter, with authority over life-sustaining treatment. He now has severe dementia and pneumonia. His daughter asks for antibiotics. Who decides?
::: solution
Under s25(2)(b) of the MCA, an ADRT is not valid if the person later created an LPA giving the attorney authority to consent or refuse the same treatment. Here the LPA, made after the ADRT, covers life-sustaining treatment, so the ADRT is not valid. The daughter, as attorney, makes the decision, but she must act in her father's best interests, which includes considering his past wishes, such as those expressed in the ADRT, as well as his present wishes and feelings. If the clinicians believe she is not acting in his best interests, they can seek the Court of Protection's view.

::: exercise A Gillick-competent refusal {level=3}
A 15-year-old boy with leukaemia, judged competent by his team, refuses a third cycle of chemotherapy after a severe infection in the second. Without treatment his chance of cure falls sharply. His parents want treatment to continue. Discuss the ethical and legal position.
::: solution
**Law:** under *Re R* and *Re W*, a competent minor's refusal can be overridden by a person with parental responsibility or by the court, in his best interests, and courts have generally authorised life-saving treatment. However, relying on parental consent to treat a competent, refusing adolescent is ethically fraught and legally risky; for a decision of this gravity, an application to the High Court is the appropriate route if agreement cannot be reached.

**Ethics:** respect for his developing autonomy weighs heavily, since his refusal follows real experience of the treatment's burdens; beneficence weighs heavily the other way. The team should explore his reasons (fear of infection, pain, loss of school and friends, depression), adjust the plan where possible, and seek a decision he can accept. If court is needed, his views must be represented.
:::
:::

::: exercise A consent audit {level=3}
You audit 40 consent forms for elective hip replacement. 38 list "infection, bleeding, DVT/PE, dislocation, leg length difference". Only 6 record any numerical risk, 3 record a discussion of alternatives, and none record what mattered to the patient. Interpret these findings in the light of Montgomery and propose improvements.
::: solution
**Interpretation:** a standard list may address the "reasonable patient" limb, but there is almost no evidence of tailoring to the particular patient, of numbers, or of alternatives (physiotherapy, analgesia, weight loss, delay). Montgomery requires material risks for *this* patient and reasonable alternatives, through dialogue. Absence of documentation does not prove absence of discussion, but makes it impossible to show.

**Improvements:** written information with natural frequencies before clinic; a decision aid comparing surgery with non-surgical options; form prompts for "what matters to you" and "alternatives discussed"; consent by the operating surgeon or trained delegate, not on the day of surgery; teach-back; re-audit.
:::
