Between 1984 and 1995, more babies died after heart surgery at the Bristol Royal Infirmary than would have been expected in a unit performing well. An anaesthetist, Stephen Bolsin, collected data and raised concerns for several years before they were acted on. The public inquiry, chaired by Professor Ian Kennedy and reporting in 2001, found no single villain. It described a "club culture", poor teamwork, no agreed way of monitoring outcomes, and a system in which concerns were not heard. Its recommendations reshaped how the NHS monitors quality and how doctors are regulated.

Patients trust doctors with their bodies, their secrets and their lives. Professionalism is what justifies that trust, and regulation is how society checks that it is deserved. This chapter covers what professionalism means, how doctors are regulated in the UK, the duty of candour when things go wrong, raising concerns, probity and conflicts of interest, boundaries, doctors' own health, and the criminal law as it applies to clinical practice.

## What professionalism means {#professionalism}

The Royal College of Physicians defined medical professionalism in 2005 as "a set of values, behaviours and relationships that underpins the trust the public has in doctors". The definition focuses on trust: a profession is granted privileges, such as self-regulation, exclusive rights to certain work and a high degree of autonomy, in return for a commitment to put patients' interests first and to maintain high standards.

In the UK, the standards are set out by the **General Medical Council** (GMC) in *Good medical practice*. The current version came into effect on 30 January 2024 and applies to all doctors registered with the GMC, and to physician associates and anaesthesia associates, whom the GMC has regulated since December 2024.

::: definition Good medical practice (2024) {#def-gmp}
*Good medical practice* sets out standards in four domains:

1. **Knowledge, skills and development**: providing a good standard of care, keeping up to date, recognising the limits of one's competence, keeping clear records, prescribing safely.
2. **Patients, partnership and communication**: treating patients with kindness and respect, supporting shared decision-making, respecting confidentiality, treating people fairly without discrimination, and maintaining professional boundaries.
3. **Colleagues, culture and safety**: contributing to a positive working environment, raising and acting on concerns, being open when things go wrong, and acting on bullying, harassment and discrimination, including sexual harassment.
4. **Trust and professionalism**: acting with honesty and integrity, managing conflicts of interest, cooperating with investigations, and looking after one's own health.

The GMC's more detailed guidance, on topics such as consent, confidentiality and end-of-life care, explains how these standards apply in specific situations.
:::

## How doctors are regulated {#regulation}

The GMC was established by the Medical Act 1858; the current legislation is the Medical Act 1983, as amended. Its functions are to keep the medical register, set standards for practice and for medical education, and act when a doctor's fitness to practise is in question. Since 2009 doctors also need a **licence to practise**. Since December 2012, licensed doctors must **revalidate** every five years, based on annual appraisal, supporting information (such as patient and colleague feedback, significant events and quality improvement work) and a recommendation from a **responsible officer**, usually the medical director of their organisation.

Fitness to practise hearings are held by the **Medical Practitioners Tribunal Service** (MPTS), which has operated separately from the GMC's investigation function since 2012. The **Professional Standards Authority** oversees the GMC and the other health professional regulators. In 2026 the government consulted on reforming the GMC's legislative framework, including protecting the titles of physician associates, who following the Leng review (2025) are to be renamed physician assistants.

::: theorem The overarching objective of regulation {#thm-objective}
In exercising its functions, the GMC's overarching objective, set out in the Medical Act 1983 since 2015, is the **protection of the public**, which involves:

1. protecting, promoting and maintaining the health, safety and well-being of the public;
2. promoting and maintaining **public confidence** in the medical profession;
3. promoting and maintaining **proper professional standards and conduct** for members of the profession.

Fitness to practise proceedings are therefore not a punishment for past conduct. They ask whether a doctor's fitness to practise is **currently impaired**, and what action is needed to protect the public.
:::

::: proof
The purpose of regulation explains how tribunals reason. Because the aim is protection, a tribunal looks forward: has the doctor shown insight into what went wrong, remedied it, and is it likely to be repeated? A doctor who made a serious clinical error, accepted it, retrained and has practised safely since may not be impaired at all, while a doctor who made a smaller error but denies it and repeats it may be.

The second and third limbs explain why some conduct leads to sanctions even when patients are not directly at risk. Dishonesty, sexual misconduct or serious criminal convictions can undermine public confidence in doctors generally. A doctor who fabricated research results may be technically competent, but if the profession tolerated such conduct, the public would reasonably lose trust in all doctors. This is why dishonesty often leads to erasure even when clinical work was sound.
:::

A doctor's fitness to practise may be impaired by reason of **misconduct**, **deficient professional performance**, a **criminal conviction or caution**, **adverse physical or mental health**, insufficient knowledge of English, or a determination by another regulator. Possible outcomes include no action, a warning, agreed **undertakings**, **conditions** on practice (up to three years at a time), **suspension** (up to 12 months at a time) and **erasure** from the register. Interim orders can restrict practice during an investigation if necessary to protect the public. The GMC can appeal to the High Court against a tribunal decision it considers insufficient to protect the public.

Most complaints to the GMC do not lead to a hearing. Many are resolved locally, and health concerns are usually managed by agreed undertakings and support rather than sanctions.

::: example A colleague who smells of alcohol {#ex-health}
A foundation doctor notices that her registrar has smelled of alcohol on two morning shifts this week and that his hands tremble. Today he is about to perform a lumbar puncture. What should she do?
::: solution
**Immediate safety:** patient safety comes first. She should not let the procedure go ahead if she believes he is unfit. She might say privately, "Are you all right? I'm worried you're not well enough to do this now," and, if necessary, contact the consultant immediately. If he insists, she should escalate without delay.

**Raising the concern:** she should inform the consultant or clinical lead, describing what she observed factually (smell of alcohol on two occasions, tremor), not as a diagnosis. Her duty under *Good medical practice* is to act when she believes a colleague's health or conduct may put patients at risk.

**Support for the registrar:** alcohol dependence is an illness. Doctors with addiction problems usually return to safe practice with treatment. He should be supported to seek help, for example from occupational health and NHS Practitioner Health, a confidential service for doctors. The GMC is likely to become involved only if there is a serious risk to patients or he does not engage with treatment; health cases are usually handled by undertakings rather than sanctions.

**Her own support:** raising concerns about a senior colleague is difficult. She can seek advice from her educational supervisor, the Freedom to Speak Up Guardian or a defence body, and should record what she observed and what she did.
:::
:::

## Candour: when things go wrong {#candour}

Errors in health care are common, and most are the result of system problems rather than individual carelessness ([[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]). How clinicians respond to them matters greatly to patients, to learning, and to trust.

::: definition The duty of candour {#def-candour}
**Professional duty of candour.** Every health professional must be open and honest with patients when something goes wrong with their treatment or care that causes, or has the potential to cause, harm or distress. They must tell the patient (or, where appropriate, those close to them), **apologise**, offer an appropriate remedy or support, and explain fully the short- and long-term effects. This is set out in joint guidance from the GMC and the Nursing and Midwifery Council (2015).

**Statutory duty of candour.** In England, providers registered with the Care Quality Commission must, under Regulation 20 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, notify the patient as soon as reasonably practicable after a **notifiable safety incident** (one that has caused, or could result in, moderate or greater harm or death), give a true account, apologise, and follow up in writing. Wales introduced an organisational duty of candour in 2023.
:::

An apology is not an admission of legal liability. Under the Compensation Act 2006 (s2), "an apology, an offer of treatment or other redress, shall not of itself amount to an admission of negligence or breach of statutory duty". Fear of litigation is therefore not a reason to avoid saying sorry. Patients who receive an open explanation and apology are often less, not more, likely to pursue complaints.

The NHS in England has replaced its older serious incident framework with the **Patient Safety Incident Response Framework** (PSIRF), which emphasises learning and system improvement over blame. **Martha's Rule**, introduced in English hospitals from 2024 after the death of Martha Mills, gives patients and families the right to request an urgent review if they are worried that a patient's condition is deteriorating and not being taken seriously.

::: example A medication error {#ex-candour}
A 67-year-old woman on the cardiology ward was given a double dose of her anticoagulant overnight because of a transcription error on her drug chart. She has no bleeding, but her coagulation results are prolonged. The FY2 doctor who wrote the chart discovers the error in the morning. What should happen?
::: solution
**Patient safety:** assess her immediately for bleeding, seek senior and pharmacy advice on monitoring and whether any reversal is needed, and adjust the plan.

**Candour:** she should be told promptly, by a senior clinician with the FY2 present if possible: what happened, what it means for her, what is being done, and a sincere apology ("I am sorry that this happened"). The explanation should be in plain language, and she should be invited to ask questions. Potential harm, not only actual harm, triggers the professional duty. If the incident meets the threshold for a notifiable safety incident, the statutory process, including written follow-up, applies.

**Recording and reporting:** document the error, the conversation and the plan in the notes; report it through the incident reporting system.

**Learning:** the review should look at why the error happened (handwritten transcription, workload at night, the design of the chart, checking processes) rather than simply blaming the FY2. The FY2 should reflect on the incident with their supervisor and include it in their portfolio. A culture where doctors can admit mistakes without fear is safer for everyone.
:::
:::

## Raising concerns {#raising-concerns}

The inquiries into Bristol, and later into Mid Staffordshire NHS Foundation Trust, showed the cost of not listening. Between 2005 and 2009 patients at Stafford Hospital suffered appalling care: left in soiled sheets, without food or water within reach, and with avoidable deaths. Robert Francis QC's public inquiry report in 2013 made 290 recommendations and called for a culture of openness, transparency and candour. His *Freedom to Speak Up* review (2015) led to **Freedom to Speak Up Guardians** in every NHS trust and a National Guardian's Office.

GMC guidance requires doctors to raise concerns where patient safety or care may be compromised, by the practice of colleagues, by systems or by the environment. The duty overrides personal and professional loyalties. Doctors who raise concerns honestly are protected: the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 protects workers who make "protected disclosures" from detriment and unfair dismissal.

The steps are usually:

1. Raise the concern with your manager, supervisor or the person responsible, unless you believe they are part of the problem.
2. If that does not work, or is inappropriate, use the organisation's procedures: the clinical director, medical director, Freedom to Speak Up Guardian or responsible officer.
3. If the concern is not addressed, or is urgent and serious, raise it with a regulator, such as the GMC or the Care Quality Commission.
4. Making a concern public, for example through the media, is justified only in exceptional circumstances, after other routes have failed or are clearly inappropriate, and without disclosing identifiable patient information.

Throughout, keep records of the concern and the response, and seek advice (from a defence organisation, the BMA or the GMC's confidential helpline) if unsure.

Concerns about clinical outcomes need care: some variation is due to chance. When outcomes are compared, statistical tools such as funnel plots show whether a unit or surgeon is outside the range expected by chance.

::: example Four deaths in a year {#ex-outlier}
A consultant surgeon has had 4 deaths after 60 elective operations of a type whose national 30-day mortality is 3%. A colleague says the surgeon "is obviously dangerous" and should be stopped from operating. How should the department respond?
::: solution
**Statistics:** the expected number of deaths is $60 \times 0.03 = 1.8$. The probability of 4 or more by chance, if the surgeon's true risk were 3%, is about 0.11. A higher count is a signal to investigate, not proof.

**Case mix:** were the patients higher risk than average (age, comorbidities, urgency)? Risk-adjusted outcomes are needed, and national audit data allow comparison.

**Case review:** an independent, structured review of each death can show whether care problems contributed and whether they had any common pattern.

**Fairness and safety:** the investigation should be prompt, confidential and fair to the surgeon, with support. If the review finds problems, actions can range from mentoring and changes to the team or case selection to restriction of practice. If patients are at immediate risk, interim restriction may be justified while the review takes place.

**Openness:** if care problems are found, the families should be told under the duty of candour. A surgeon's outcomes should never be dismissed because "it's chance", nor condemned on small numbers alone.
:::
:::

## Probity, conflicts of interest and boundaries {#probity}

**Probity** means being honest and trustworthy. *Good medical practice* requires doctors to be honest in all their professional activities, including clinical records, research, CVs, references, financial dealings and statements to courts and investigations. Dishonesty is one of the commonest reasons for erasure from the register, even when it is not connected with patient care.

**Conflicts of interest** arise when a doctor's judgement could be influenced, or be seen to be influenced, by personal interests, such as financial interests, gifts, relationships with industry or ownership of services. Doctors must not let such interests affect patient care, must declare them openly, and must not accept gifts or hospitality that could affect, or be seen to affect, their judgement. In the UK, the pharmaceutical industry's code of practice controls interactions with health professionals, and payments to doctors are published on the **Disclosure UK** database. Offering or accepting a bribe is a criminal offence under the Bribery Act 2010.

Doctors should not prescribe for themselves or for people with whom they have a close personal relationship, except in an emergency or where no alternative is available, and never for controlled drugs in such circumstances unless absolutely necessary to save life or avoid serious deterioration.

**Professional boundaries** protect patients. Doctors must not use their professional position to pursue a sexual or improper emotional relationship with a patient or with someone close to a patient. This applies to former patients too, if the relationship involves abuse of trust from the earlier professional relationship. Intimate examinations require an explanation, consent and the offer of a chaperone. *Good medical practice* (2024) makes explicit that doctors must not act in a sexual way towards colleagues, including making unwelcome comments, and must act if they witness sexual harassment.

::: warning The slippery slope of small gifts
Most doctors believe they are not influenced by small gifts, while believing that other doctors are. Research on industry interactions suggests that even small gifts and sponsored meals are associated with changes in prescribing. The question to ask is not "will this change my decisions?" (everyone says no) but "would I be comfortable if my patients knew?" and "would this look like influence to a reasonable observer?" Declare interests, decline gifts that could be seen as inducements, and base prescribing on independent evidence.
:::

::: example A conference invitation {#ex-conf}
A pharmaceutical company invites a GP to speak about her experience with its new diabetes drug at an international conference, offering business-class flights, three nights in a luxury hotel and a fee of £2,000. What should she consider?
::: solution
**The arrangement:** paying for genuine services (speaking, consultancy) at fair market value is allowed under the industry code, and hospitality must be reasonable and secondary to the purpose of the meeting. Business-class travel and luxury accommodation for a short talk may be excessive.

**Conflict of interest:** accepting would create a financial relationship with a company whose drug she prescribes. She must declare it (the payment will be published on Disclosure UK), make sure her prescribing remains based on evidence and guidance, and present balanced information rather than promotional material.

**Her employer's rules:** NHS organisations require declarations of interests and may prohibit some arrangements.

**The test:** would her patients and colleagues think her independence was compromised? If in doubt, she could decline, or accept only reasonable expenses and a modest fee, present the evidence including the drug's limitations, and declare the relationship to her practice and on any guideline group she sits on.
:::
:::

## Doctors' own health {#wellbeing}

Doctors have high rates of burnout, depression and substance misuse, and are often reluctant to seek help. *Good medical practice* requires doctors to be registered with a general practitioner, not to treat themselves, and to seek advice if their health may affect their judgement or performance. Reports such as *Caring for doctors, caring for patients* (2019) have argued that working conditions, including workload, lack of control and poor team support, are major causes, and that looking after the workforce is a patient safety issue. Confidential services such as NHS Practitioner Health support doctors with mental health and addiction problems.

## Doctors and the criminal law {#criminal}

Most clinical errors are dealt with through the civil law, complaints and professional regulation. In rare cases, a death caused by grossly negligent care leads to a prosecution for **gross negligence manslaughter**. The test, from *R v Adomako* (1994), in which an anaesthetist failed to notice for several minutes that a patient's breathing tube had become disconnected, requires: a duty of care; a breach of that duty; that the breach caused death; and that the negligence was so bad, judged against all the circumstances, that it should be judged criminal. Later cases have described the required standard as "truly exceptionally bad".

The case of Dr Hadiza Bawa-Garba brought these issues to wide attention. In 2011, six-year-old Jack Adcock died of sepsis at Leicester Royal Infirmary, where Dr Bawa-Garba, a paediatric trainee, was working under severe pressure with systems failures, including an IT failure and staff shortages. She was convicted of gross negligence manslaughter in 2015. A tribunal suspended her; on the GMC's appeal, the High Court ordered her erasure in 2018, but the Court of Appeal later that year restored the suspension, emphasising the tribunal's assessment of the systemic context and her low risk of repetition. The case led to reviews of gross negligence manslaughter in health care, and to strong concern among doctors about the effect of criminalising error on openness and learning.

The crimes of Harold Shipman, a GP convicted in 2000 of murdering 15 patients and found by the Shipman Inquiry to have killed about 250, showed how a doctor could exploit trust and weak systems. The inquiry's recommendations contributed to revalidation, controls on controlled drugs and, eventually, the medical examiner system for scrutinising deaths ([[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]]).

::: quiz
A doctor is convicted of drink-driving, with no patients involved and no evidence of a health problem. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] The conviction is irrelevant to the GMC because it is unconnected with clinical practice.
- [x] The doctor must inform the GMC without delay; the GMC will consider whether, in light of all the circumstances, fitness to practise is impaired, considering public confidence as well as patient safety.
- [ ] The doctor will automatically be erased from the register.
- [ ] The doctor needs to inform only their employer.
::: solution
*Good medical practice* requires doctors to inform the GMC without delay if they have accepted a caution or been charged with or convicted of a criminal offence anywhere in the world. A criminal conviction is a ground on which fitness to practise may be impaired, but the outcome depends on the circumstances, the doctor's insight and any underlying health problem. A single drink-driving conviction, with insight, is usually dealt with without restrictions, though the GMC will look for evidence of an alcohol problem. Erasure is not automatic.
:::
:::

::: history
The Medical Act 1858 created the General Medical Council and the medical register, allowing the public to distinguish qualified practitioners. For over a century the GMC dealt mainly with "infamous conduct in a professional respect", such as adultery with patients or advertising, rather than clinical competence.

That changed after a series of scandals. The Bristol inquiry (2001) found failures in monitoring outcomes. Harold Shipman's crimes and the Shipman Inquiry (2002–2005) exposed weaknesses in death certification and in checking doctors' ongoing fitness to practise. The Alder Hey organ retention scandal led to the Human Tissue Act 2004. Lay members were added to the GMC, and in 2012 the MPTS was separated from investigation and revalidation began. The Mid Staffordshire inquiry (2013) led to the statutory duty of candour and Freedom to Speak Up Guardians. A new edition of *Good medical practice*, in 2024, put more emphasis on workplace culture, kindness and the prevention of harassment.
:::

::: summary
- Professionalism is "a set of values, behaviours and relationships that underpins the trust the public has in doctors". In the UK its standards are set in the GMC's *Good medical practice* (2024), in four domains.
- The GMC keeps the register, sets standards, oversees education and revalidation (since 2012), and acts on fitness to practise. Hearings are held by the MPTS. The overarching objective is **protection of the public**, including public confidence and professional standards.
- Fitness to practise asks whether a doctor is **currently impaired**; insight and remediation matter. Outcomes range from no action to erasure.
- The **professional duty of candour** requires honesty and an apology when things go wrong; organisations have a **statutory duty of candour**. An apology is not an admission of liability.
- Doctors must **raise concerns** about patient safety, through local routes first, then regulators; whistleblowers are protected by the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998.
- Outcome data must be interpreted with chance and case mix in mind.
- Probity, management of conflicts of interest, and professional boundaries are core duties; dishonesty and sexual misconduct often lead to erasure.
- Doctors should look after their own health and seek help. Gross negligence manslaughter requires negligence so bad as to be criminal (*Adomako*); the Bawa-Garba case highlighted the role of systems.
:::

Using the binomial distribution with $n = 60$ and $p = 0.03$, the probability of 0, 1, 2 and 3 deaths is about 0.161, 0.298, 0.272 and 0.163. What is the probability of 4 or more deaths? Give the answer to 2 decimal places.

Subtract the sum of the given probabilities from 1.

::: solution
$1 - (0.161 + 0.298 + 0.272 + 0.163) = 1 - 0.894 = 0.106$, which is $0.11$ to 2 decimal places. A surgeon with an average true risk would have 4 or more deaths in 60 operations about 1 time in 10, so a single such result needs investigation, not judgement.

::: exercise An apology {level=2}
A consultant tells a junior doctor, "Don't say sorry to the family about the delayed diagnosis; it will be used against us in court." How should the junior doctor respond?
::: solution
The advice is wrong. The professional duty of candour requires an apology and an honest explanation when something has gone wrong that has caused or could cause harm, and the statutory duty of candour may require the organisation to do so formally. The Compensation Act 2006 (s2) states that an apology does not of itself amount to an admission of negligence. Openness is associated with fewer complaints and supports learning. The junior doctor should explain this politely, refer to GMC guidance, and if necessary seek advice from the clinical lead or patient safety team. Failing to be open could itself be a fitness to practise matter.
:::
:::

::: exercise A friend asks for a prescription {level=2}
A close friend, who is not your patient, asks you to prescribe a short course of zopiclone because she cannot sleep before an important interview. What should you do?
::: solution
Decline, kindly. GMC guidance says doctors should avoid prescribing for people with whom they have a close personal relationship, except in an emergency or where no other person with the authority to prescribe is available and the prescription is necessary to avoid serious deterioration. This is neither. Zopiclone is a controlled drug, and prescribing it for a friend would be particularly inappropriate. You lack her full medical history and objectivity, and there is no record in her GP notes. Suggest she sees her own GP, and offer non-drug advice on sleep and interview anxiety as a friend.
:::
:::

::: exercise A pattern of concerns {level=3}
Over six months, three nurses tell an FY2 that a senior colleague is rude and dismissive to patients, sometimes refuses to examine patients who complain, and once shouted at a patient with a learning disability. The FY2 has seen one incident herself. She is worried about her own training if she raises it. What should she do, and what protections exist?
::: solution
**Duty:** the behaviour described could compromise patient safety and dignity, and *Good medical practice* requires her to act on concerns. Her own observation gives her direct evidence; she should encourage the nurses to raise their own concerns too.

**Route:** record specific observations (dates, what happened, who was present), then raise the concern with her clinical or educational supervisor, the clinical director or the Freedom to Speak Up Guardian. If the senior colleague is her supervisor, go to someone else. If local routes fail, she can contact the GMC or CQC.

**Protection:** the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 protects workers who raise concerns in good faith from detriment; NHS trusts must not victimise those who speak up; postgraduate deans and the GMC can be told if training is affected. She should seek advice from her defence organisation or the BMA.

**Fairness:** the concern should be investigated fairly, with the colleague given a chance to respond; there may be health or workload factors that need support as well as action.
:::
:::

::: exercise Erasure or not? {level=3}
A doctor in her fifties, with an unblemished 25-year record, is found to have falsified her CPD records to appear compliant for revalidation, claiming attendance at courses she did not attend. There are no concerns about her clinical care. She admits it at an early stage, says she was overwhelmed by caring for a dying parent, and has since completed the requirements. Discuss how a tribunal might approach the sanction.
::: solution
**Impairment:** dishonesty in records used for regulatory purposes strikes at the integrity of revalidation and at public confidence in the profession. Even without clinical concerns, the tribunal is likely to find her fitness to practise impaired on the grounds of misconduct, because the second and third limbs of the overarching objective (public confidence and professional standards) are engaged.

**Sanction:** the tribunal considers the least restrictive sanction that protects the public. Factors in her favour: early admission, genuine insight, the personal context, remediation, an unblemished record, and a low risk of repetition. Factors against: the dishonesty was deliberate, repeated across several claims, and related to the regulatory process itself. Erasure is commonly imposed for serious dishonesty, but it is not automatic; a period of suspension may be judged sufficient to mark the seriousness and maintain confidence if insight is strong.

**Point:** this case illustrates that regulation is not only about clinical safety. The GMC could appeal if it thought a sanction insufficient to protect the public.
:::
:::
