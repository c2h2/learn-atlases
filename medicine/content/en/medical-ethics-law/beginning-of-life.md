Amira is 36 and 13 weeks pregnant. Her first-trimester screening result shows a higher chance that the fetus has Down syndrome, and she is offered a further blood test, then perhaps an invasive diagnostic test. She and her husband have not thought about what they would do with the result. Her midwife must explain the options without steering them, knowing that some people regard screening as a way of preventing suffering, and others as a statement that lives like their cousin's are less worth living.

Ethics at the beginning of life involves deep and lasting disagreement. People who share a commitment to the four principles can still disagree about the moral status of an embryo or fetus, and therefore about abortion, embryo research and prenatal testing. Clinicians need to understand the main arguments, know the law, and be able to care for patients whose views differ from their own. This chapter covers moral status, the law on abortion in England and Wales, prenatal screening, assisted conception and the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act, surrogacy, and decisions about newborn infants.

## Moral status {#moral-status}

Many of the hardest questions in reproductive ethics depend on one prior question: what is owed to an embryo or fetus? A being has **moral status** if its interests matter morally for its own sake, so that it can be wronged.

::: definition Views of the moral status of the embryo and fetus {#def-moral-status}
- **Conception view:** a human embryo has full moral status from fertilisation, because it is a living human organism with the potential to develop into an adult. On this view, ending a pregnancy is morally comparable to killing a born person.
- **Personhood view:** full moral status depends on capacities such as consciousness, self-awareness and the ability to value one's own future. Embryos and fetuses lack these, so they lack full moral status, although they may have some value.
- **Gradualist view:** moral status increases as the embryo and fetus develop, with morally relevant stages including the appearance of the primitive streak (around 14 days, after which twinning is no longer possible), the capacity for sentience, and viability.
- **Relational view:** status depends partly on relationships, such as being wanted and valued by the pregnant woman and others.
:::

Each view has well-known difficulties. The conception view must explain why the high rate of early embryo loss in natural conception is not treated as a tragedy on the scale of child deaths, and why IVF, which routinely creates embryos that are not implanted, is widely accepted. The personhood view seems to imply that newborn infants, who also lack self-awareness, have no greater status than late fetuses, a conclusion most people reject. The gradualist view is widely held and fits the structure of much law, but it must explain why particular lines are drawn where they are.

A separate argument, made famous by Judith Jarvis Thomson in 1971, asks whether it matters if the fetus is a person. Even if it were, she argued, nobody is obliged to let another person use their body to survive. On this view the central question is **bodily autonomy**, not only moral status.

The law in England and Wales takes a clear position for its own purposes. The fetus is not a legal person. It acquires legal personality on being born alive. A father cannot prevent an abortion (*Paton v British Pregnancy Advisory Service*, 1979). A court cannot make a fetus a ward of court to control the pregnant woman's conduct (*Re F (in utero)*, 1988). A woman with capacity may refuse a caesarean section even if her fetus will die (*St George's Healthcare NHS Trust v S*, 1998). The European Court of Human Rights has left the question of when the right to life begins to each state (*Vo v France*, 2004).

## Abortion law in Great Britain {#abortion-law}

Abortion remains a criminal offence in England and Wales under the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 (ss58–59) and, after viability, the Infant Life (Preservation) Act 1929. The **Abortion Act 1967**, which applies in England, Wales and Scotland, provides a defence when an abortion is carried out within its terms.

::: definition The grounds for abortion under the Abortion Act 1967 (as amended) {#def-abortion-grounds}
A pregnancy may be lawfully terminated by a registered medical practitioner when **two registered medical practitioners** are of the opinion, formed in good faith, that:

- **(a)** the pregnancy has not exceeded its **24th week** and continuing it would involve risk, greater than if it were terminated, of injury to the physical or mental health of the pregnant woman or any existing children of her family; or
- **(b)** termination is necessary to prevent grave permanent injury to the physical or mental health of the pregnant woman; or
- **(c)** continuing the pregnancy would involve risk to the life of the pregnant woman greater than if it were terminated; or
- **(d)** there is a substantial risk that if the child were born it would suffer from such physical or mental abnormalities as to be seriously handicapped.

Grounds (b), (c) and (d) have **no time limit**. In determining risk to health, account may be taken of the woman's actual or reasonably foreseeable environment. In an emergency, one practitioner may act to save the woman's life or prevent grave permanent injury.
:::

In practice the great majority of abortions in England and Wales take place under ground (a), before 10 weeks, and most are early medical abortions using mifepristone followed by misoprostol. Since 30 August 2022, in England, both drugs for an early medical abortion may be taken at home after a remote consultation, up to 9 weeks and 6 days of pregnancy; Wales made the same arrangement permanent. Abortions must otherwise take place in an NHS hospital or approved place, and every abortion must be notified to the Chief Medical Officer.

Two important reforms are recent. In **Northern Ireland**, abortion was decriminalised by Westminster legislation in 2019, and regulations since 2020 allow abortion on request up to 12 weeks, with other grounds later. In **England and Wales**, the **Crime and Policing Act 2026**, which received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026, removed women from the criminal law relating to ending their own pregnancy. The grounds and time limits in the Abortion Act are unchanged, and a person who carries out or assists an abortion outside the Act's terms can still commit an offence.

**Conscientious objection.** Section 4 of the 1967 Act provides that nobody is under a duty to participate in treatment authorised by the Act to which they have a conscientious objection, except where treatment is necessary to save the woman's life or prevent grave permanent injury. In *Greater Glasgow Health Board v Doogan* [2014] UKSC 68, the Supreme Court held that "participate" means taking part in a hands-on capacity, not managerial or supervisory tasks such as allocating staff. The GMC requires doctors who object to tell patients they can see another doctor and ensure they have enough information to do so without delay, and never to obstruct access or express disapproval.

::: example A request at 11 weeks {#ex-request}
A 17-year-old student tells her GP that she is 11 weeks pregnant and wants an abortion. She has not told her parents and is worried about her exams. The GP personally believes abortion is wrong. What are the GP's obligations?
::: solution
**Her rights:** at 17 she is presumed to have capacity to consent to treatment (Family Law Reform Act 1969, s8), and she is owed confidentiality. Ground (a) of the Abortion Act is likely to be met: two doctors can reasonably form the opinion, in good faith, that continuing the pregnancy would involve greater risk to her mental health than ending it, especially as the risks of early abortion are lower than those of continuing to term.

**The GP's conscience:** the GP may decline to participate directly, for example in signing the certificate, under s4. But the GP must tell her promptly that she can see another doctor or refer herself to an abortion provider (many accept self-referral), give her the information she needs to do so without delay, and not express disapproval or try to dissuade her. Time matters: delay narrows the options and increases risk.

**Good care:** offer to discuss her options (continuing the pregnancy with support, adoption, abortion) if she wishes, without pressure; encourage her to involve a trusted adult; consider safeguarding, such as the age and behaviour of the partner and whether she is being coerced; and offer contraception and sexual health advice afterwards.
:::
:::

## Prenatal screening and testing {#prenatal}

In England, all pregnant women are offered screening through the NHS Fetal Anomaly Screening Programme. The **combined test** (a nuchal translucency scan and blood tests at around 11–14 weeks) estimates the chance of trisomies 21, 18 and 13. Women with a higher-chance result, defined as 1 in 150 or greater, are offered a choice of further screening by **non-invasive prenatal testing** (NIPT, which analyses cell-free DNA in the mother's blood, much of it from the placenta), a diagnostic test (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, with a small risk of miscarriage), or no further testing. A mid-pregnancy anomaly scan is offered at 18–20+6 weeks ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]).

Screening is not diagnosis. Even a very accurate test produces false positives, and when the condition is uncommon in the group tested, a substantial share of positive results can be wrong.

Prenatal testing raises particular ethical issues.

- **Informed choice.** Screening is offered, not recommended. The purpose is to give people information on which to make their own decisions, including the decision not to be screened. Some women decline screening because they would not end a pregnancy, or because they do not want the anxiety.
- **Non-directive counselling.** Professionals should present information accurately, including about life with the condition, without steering parents towards any outcome.
- **The disability critique.** Some disability rights advocates argue that screening programmes aimed at detecting conditions such as Down syndrome express a negative judgement about the lives of people who have them (the **expressivist objection**), and may reduce support for those people. Others respond that an individual's choice need not express any judgement about others, and that informed choice respects reproductive autonomy.
- **Sex and non-medical traits.** The sex of a fetus is not a lawful ground for abortion in itself; ground (d) concerns serious abnormality. Wider genomic testing of fetuses raises questions about uncertain findings and about testing for adult-onset conditions, which would remove the child's own future choice about whether to know.

::: example A positive NIPT result {#ex-nipt}
Amira, from the start of the chapter, chooses NIPT. It reports a high chance of trisomy 21. She asks whether she should book a termination. How should the fetal medicine team respond?
::: solution
**Explain the result:** NIPT is a screening test. In her situation most positive results are correct, but a significant minority are not, often because the DNA tested comes largely from the placenta, which can carry a chromosome difference the fetus does not have. Professional guidance is that a diagnostic test (amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling) should be offered to confirm the result before any decision to end the pregnancy.

**Explain the diagnostic test:** its purpose, timing, the small risk of miscarriage, and how long the results take.

**Provide balanced information:** about Down syndrome itself (the range of abilities and health, support available, and the experience of families) and about all the options, including continuing the pregnancy, adoption and ending it. Offer contact with support organisations if she wishes.

**Respect her decision:** whatever she decides, including declining further testing, is her decision. Abortion under ground (d) has no time limit, so there is time to make it carefully; but later abortion is a more difficult procedure, and she should know the practical timelines.
:::
:::

## Assisted conception and embryo research {#assisted-conception}

The **Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990**, substantially amended in 2008, regulates in vitro fertilisation (IVF), donor conception, the storage of gametes and embryos, preimplantation genetic testing and embryo research. The **Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority** (HFEA) licenses and inspects clinics and research. The Act followed the Warnock Report (1984), written after the birth of Louise Brown, the first IVF baby, in 1978.

::: definition Key rules of the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act {#def-hfe}
- **14-day rule:** embryos may not be kept or used in research beyond 14 days after fertilisation or the appearance of the primitive streak, whichever is earlier.
- **Permitted embryos:** only embryos whose nuclear and mitochondrial DNA has not been altered may be placed in a woman, except where regulations permit **mitochondrial donation** to prevent serious mitochondrial disease (allowed since 2015). Germline genome editing for reproduction is prohibited.
- **Welfare of the child:** before providing treatment, a clinic must take account of the welfare of any child who may be born, including the child's **need for supportive parenting**.
- **Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT):** embryos may be tested for a serious genetic condition, for chromosomal abnormalities, or for tissue compatibility with a sibling who needs treatment, under HFEA authorisation. Sex selection is allowed only to avoid a serious sex-linked condition.
- **Donor identity:** people conceived with gametes donated after 1 April 2005 can learn their donor's identity from age 18.
- **Legal parenthood:** the woman who gives birth is the legal mother; rules determine who is the second legal parent.
:::

In 2025, the Newcastle team that pioneered mitochondrial donation reported in the *New England Journal of Medicine* that eight children had been born after pronuclear transfer, with low or undetectable levels of the mother's affected mitochondrial DNA. By contrast, in 2018 the Chinese scientist He Jiankui announced the birth of twins whose embryos he had genome-edited; he was widely condemned and imprisoned, and his work strengthened international calls to prohibit heritable genome editing for the time being.

::: theorem The ethical tests applied to new reproductive technologies {#thm-reproductive-tests}
UK regulation of a new reproductive technology has generally asked four questions:

1. **Safety:** is the procedure safe enough for the woman and the future child, given the evidence?
2. **Purpose:** does it serve a serious medical purpose, such as avoiding a serious inherited condition?
3. **Welfare:** has the welfare of the future child, including their identity and relationships, been considered?
4. **Public legitimacy:** has there been public consultation and explicit parliamentary approval?
:::

::: proof
These questions combine the principles with features specific to reproduction. Non-maleficence and beneficence apply to two people: the woman and a future child who cannot consent. Because the child's existence depends on the choice, the usual comparison ("better or worse off than otherwise") is hard to apply, which is why welfare and safety receive explicit attention. Justice and respect for pluralism require public legitimacy, because the decisions affect shared values on which citizens deeply disagree. The UK's approach to mitochondrial donation followed this pattern: years of scientific review and public consultation before Parliament approved regulations in 2015, then case-by-case licensing.
:::

::: example A saviour sibling {#ex-saviour}
A couple's 4-year-old son has beta thalassaemia major and needs regular transfusions. A haematopoietic stem cell transplant from a matched sibling could cure him, but no family member is a match. Both parents are carriers. They ask for IVF with preimplantation testing to select an embryo that is unaffected and a tissue match. Discuss.
::: solution
**Law:** the HFE Act permits testing embryos for tissue compatibility where a sibling has a serious condition that could be treated with umbilical cord blood stem cells, bone marrow or other tissue, under HFEA authorisation. The House of Lords upheld the HFEA's power to authorise this in *R (Quintavalle) v HFEA* (2005).

**Arguments for:** the parents would value the new child for themselves; the procedure could save their son's life; and the selected child would also be free of the condition.

**Arguments against:** the child is created partly as a means; the child may later be asked to donate bone marrow before being able to consent; and the sibling's expectations may affect the family.

**Response:** the concerns are real, but they can be managed. The HFEA permits the procedure only for serious conditions, and any later donation by the child is subject to separate scrutiny of the child's best interests. Families should be counselled about the low chance of success per cycle. With two carrier parents, each embryo has a 3 in 4 chance of being unaffected and an independent 1 in 4 chance of being an HLA match, so only $3/16$ of embryos will be suitable (see the exercise "A saviour sibling's chances").
:::
:::

**Surrogacy.** Under the Surrogacy Arrangements Act 1985, surrogacy is lawful in the UK but commercial arrangements and advertising are prohibited, and surrogacy agreements are not enforceable. The surrogate is the legal mother at birth. The intended parents can then apply for a **parental order** to become the child's legal parents. The Law Commission of England and Wales and the Scottish Law Commission recommended reform in 2023, including allowing intended parents to be legal parents from birth in some cases.

## Decisions about newborn infants {#newborn}

Babies born extremely preterm, or with severe conditions, raise questions about when intensive care is in the child's best interests. Parents are the usual decision-makers, but they must act in the child's best interests, and if parents and clinicians cannot agree, the courts decide.

For extremely preterm birth, the British Association of Perinatal Medicine's framework (2019; check for later revisions) recommends that decisions be based on an assessment of risk that includes gestational age, estimated fetal weight, sex, plurality and whether antenatal steroids were given, rather than gestational age alone. Survival-focused care may be considered from 22 weeks and 0 days. Babies are grouped by the estimated risk of death or severe impairment: where it is **extremely high** (over 90%), comfort-focused care is recommended; where it is **high** (50–90%), either survival-focused or comfort-focused care may be in the baby's best interests and the parents' wishes guide the choice; where it is **moderate** (under 50%), survival-focused care is normally recommended.

When treatment is not in a child's best interests, it may be withheld or withdrawn and the focus moved to palliative care. The Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health's framework (2015) describes circumstances in which this may be appropriate, such as when treatment cannot prolong life meaningfully or when the burdens of treatment outweigh its benefits.

Several cases have tested these principles. In *Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins)* (2000), the Court of Appeal authorised surgery to separate conjoined twins although it would inevitably cause the death of the weaker twin, because without it both would die. In the cases of Charlie Gard (2017) and Alfie Evans (2018), the courts held that continuing life-sustaining treatment, or moving the child abroad for experimental treatment, was not in the child's best interests, against the wishes of the parents. Both cases provoked intense public debate about the role of parents, the courts and the state.

::: warning Do not let a single number make the decision
Outcome statistics for extremely preterm babies, or for babies with a given diagnosis, describe groups. They are often based on babies born some years ago, in units with different practices, and individual risk varies with many factors. Use the best available numbers to inform parents honestly, describe uncertainty in both directions, and re-assess as the baby's condition evolves. Decisions are about this baby, with these parents.
:::

::: example A birth at 22 weeks {#ex-preterm}
A woman is admitted in preterm labour at 22 weeks and 4 days. Her baby is singleton, estimated fetal weight 520 g, and she received antenatal corticosteroids 24 hours ago. She and her partner ask the neonatal team what will happen when the baby is born.
::: solution
**Assess risk:** using the BAPM framework, a singleton baby at 22+4 with antenatal steroids and a reasonable weight is likely to fall into the high-risk rather than the extremely high-risk group. Without steroids, or at a lower weight, the risk would be higher.

**Discuss with the parents:** the neonatologist should explain, in plain language, the chances of survival and of survival without severe impairment, with honest uncertainty; what survival-focused care involves (resuscitation, intensive care, weeks to months in hospital, and the possibility that treatment may later be withdrawn if it fails); and what comfort-focused care involves (holding their baby, warmth, symptom relief, time together).

**Shared decision:** in the high-risk group, either survival-focused or comfort-focused care can be in the baby's best interests, and the parents' wishes should guide the decision. The plan should be documented and reviewed at birth, since the baby's condition then may change it.
:::
:::

::: history
Abortion was first made a statutory offence in England by Lord Ellenborough's Act of 1803; the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 set the offences that still exist. In *R v Bourne* (1938), the gynaecologist Aleck Bourne was acquitted after openly ending the pregnancy of a 14-year-old girl who had been raped, the judge directing the jury that preserving the life of the mother could include preventing her becoming "a physical or mental wreck". The Abortion Act 1967, introduced by David Steel, followed public concern about deaths from illegal abortion.

Louise Brown, the first child conceived by IVF, was born in Oldham in 1978 after the work of Robert Edwards and Patrick Steptoe. The Warnock Committee reported in 1984, proposing the 14-day limit and a statutory regulator, and the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 created the HFEA. In 2015 Parliament approved mitochondrial donation. In 2019 abortion was decriminalised in Northern Ireland, and in 2026 the Crime and Policing Act removed women in England and Wales from criminal liability for ending their own pregnancies.
:::

At 26 weeks, a fetus is found to have a severe brain malformation that is incompatible with independent life beyond infancy. On which ground of the Abortion Act could a termination be lawful, and what is required?
::: solution
Ground (d): a substantial risk that the child would be seriously handicapped. It has no time limit, so it applies at 26 weeks. Two registered medical practitioners must form the opinion in good faith, and the abortion must be carried out by a registered medical practitioner in an NHS hospital or approved place. After about 21–22 weeks, feticide is usually performed before a medical termination, according to professional guidance. Parents should also be offered the option of continuing the pregnancy with perinatal palliative care.

A screening test for trisomy 21 has sensitivity 99% and specificity 99.9%. In a group in which 4 in 1,000 pregnancies are affected, what proportion of positive results are true positives? Give the answer to 1 decimal place.
::: hint
Out of 100,000 pregnancies, count true and false positives.

::: solution
Out of 100,000: 400 affected, of which $0.99 \times 400 = 396$ test positive. 99,600 unaffected, of which $0.001 \times 99{,}600 = 99.6$ test positive. PPV $= 396/(396 + 99.6) \approx 0.799$, about $0.8$. Around 1 in 5 positive results is false, which is why diagnostic confirmation is offered.

::: exercise A saviour sibling's chances {level=2 check="0.1875"}
Both parents are carriers of an autosomal recessive condition. Assuming that whether an embryo is affected and whether it is an HLA match for the sick sibling are independent, and that the chance of an HLA match is 1/4, what fraction of embryos would be both unaffected and a match? Give the answer as a decimal.
::: solution
$P(\text{unaffected}) = 3/4$ (unaffected includes carriers). $P(\text{match}) = 1/4$. Assuming independence, $P(\text{both}) = 3/4 \times 1/4 = 3/16 = 0.1875$. Fewer than 1 embryo in 5 is suitable, and not all suitable embryos implant, so several IVF cycles may be needed. (In reality HLA genes and the disease gene can be linked, which changes the calculation for a particular family.)

::: exercise Conscience in the team {level=2}
A nurse on a gynaecology ward says she objects to caring for women who have had abortions and will not take their observations or give them analgesia. A doctor says he will not prescribe the post-procedure antibiotics. How do *Doogan* and s4 of the Abortion Act apply?
::: solution
Section 4 protects refusal to participate in treatment authorised by the Act, and *Doogan* interpreted participation as hands-on involvement in the course of treatment to terminate the pregnancy. Care after the abortion, such as observations, pain relief and antibiotics for a woman who has already had the procedure, is not participation in the termination. Neither the nurse nor the doctor can refuse it on the basis of s4, and refusing would breach their professional duties to provide care without discrimination. Both should be supported to discuss their views, but patients must be cared for.
:::

::: exercise Non-directive counselling {level=2}
Write two short statements a midwife might make when explaining a higher-chance combined screening result: one that is directive and one that is non-directive.
::: solution
**Directive:** "The result is high risk, so you'll want to have the amniocentesis so you can decide about ending the pregnancy." This assumes both the test and the decision.

**Non-directive:** "Your result means there is a higher chance than usual, about 1 in 80, that the baby has Down syndrome. That also means 79 in 80 pregnancies with this result are not affected. You can choose further screening with a blood test, a diagnostic test that gives a definite answer but has a small risk of miscarriage, or no further tests. We can talk about what each would mean for you, and about Down syndrome itself, whatever you decide."

::: exercise Should heritable genome editing ever be allowed? {level=3}
Construct the strongest argument for and against permitting heritable genome editing to prevent a serious single-gene disease, and say what conditions a regulator would need to see before allowing it.
::: solution
**For:** for a few couples (for example, where one partner has two copies of a dominant disease variant, or both partners are homozygous for a recessive condition), PGT cannot produce an unaffected embryo, and editing could allow a genetically related child free of serious disease. Preventing serious disease is a widely accepted goal, and mitochondrial donation provides a precedent for carefully regulated germline change.

**Against:** the safety of editing (off-target changes, mosaicism) is not yet established; harms would pass to future generations who cannot consent; alternatives (donor gametes, adoption, PGT in most cases) usually exist; there is a risk of moving from treatment to enhancement; and global consensus does not exist. He Jiankui's experiment shows how quickly things can go wrong.

**Conditions:** robust preclinical evidence of safety and accuracy; a serious condition with no reasonable alternative; long-term follow-up of children; independent licensing case by case; public consultation and explicit parliamentary approval, as with mitochondrial donation; and international coordination.
:::
:::

::: exercise A disagreement over a newborn {level=3}
A baby born at term has severe hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy. After two weeks of intensive care, MRI and clinical findings indicate very severe brain injury; the baby is unlikely to breathe independently and, if she survives, will have profound impairment. The team believes continued ventilation is not in her best interests. The parents want everything to continue. How should this be approached?
::: solution
**Communication first:** several meetings with the senior team, with time and support (chaplaincy, interpreters, psychological support), explaining the findings honestly and listening to the parents' hopes, beliefs and fears. Seek a second opinion from an independent specialist, which the parents may help choose.

**Best interests:** the decision rests on the baby's best interests, considered widely: the burdens of continued treatment, her capacity for comfort and pleasure, the prospects of meaningful recovery, and the parents' views, which are important but not decisive.

**Options:** a time-limited continuation with agreed review points; mediation; involvement of the clinical ethics committee.

**If agreement cannot be reached:** the Trust should apply to the High Court for a declaration, as in the Charlie Gard and Alfie Evans cases. Until the court decides, treatment generally continues. Throughout, the family should be treated with compassion and should not feel they are in a battle with the team. Palliative care can be introduced alongside intensive care.
:::
