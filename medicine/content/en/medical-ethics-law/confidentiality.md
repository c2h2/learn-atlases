A 48-year-old bus driver has his first tonic–clonic seizure. After investigation, epilepsy is diagnosed. His neurologist tells him that he must stop driving and inform the Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency (DVLA). Three weeks later the neurologist sees him arrive at clinic driving his bus. He says he cannot afford to lose his job and asks her to keep this between them.

Patients tell doctors things they tell nobody else, and allow them to examine their bodies and read their records. They do this because they trust that the information will be kept in confidence. That trust is the reason for confidentiality, and it is also why breaking it requires a strong justification. This chapter explains the duty of confidentiality in England and Wales, the situations in which information may or must be shared, how to make disclosure decisions, and how data protection law governs the use of health records, including for research and planning.

## Why confidentiality matters {#why}

There are three main arguments for medical confidentiality.

- **Consequences.** If patients feared that what they said might be passed on, many would withhold information or avoid care altogether. This harms them and, with infectious disease or risk to others, harms the public too. People with sexually transmitted infections, mental illness or drug use are especially likely to stay away from services they do not trust.
- **Autonomy and privacy.** Information about a person's body and health belongs to that person in an important sense. Respecting autonomy includes letting people control who knows about them.
- **Promise and trust.** Doctors have publicly undertaken to keep confidences since the Hippocratic Oath. Patients rely on this implied promise when they speak.

These arguments explain both the strength of the duty and its limits. Because the main justification is consequential, there are situations where keeping a secret would itself cause serious harm, and then the same reasoning can justify disclosure.

::: definition The duty of confidentiality {#def-duty}
Doctors owe patients a **duty of confidentiality**: personal information learned in a professional capacity must not be disclosed without a lawful justification. In England and Wales the duty comes from several sources:

- the **common law** of confidence: information given in circumstances where it is reasonable to expect it to be kept confidential is protected;
- **Article 8** of the European Convention on Human Rights (respect for private and family life), through the Human Rights Act 1998;
- **data protection law**: the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) and the Data Protection Act 2018;
- **professional regulation**: the GMC's guidance *Confidentiality: good practice in handling patient information* (2017) and *Good medical practice* (2024);
- contracts of employment and NHS policies.

The duty covers all identifiable information about a patient, including the fact that they are a patient, and it continues after the patient's death.
:::

## When information may be disclosed {#lawful-disclosure}

The GMC's guidance organises the circumstances in which confidential information may be disclosed into a small number of categories.

::: theorem The lawful bases for disclosure {#thm-disclosure}
Identifiable patient information may be disclosed when, and only when:

1. the patient **consents**, which may be **implied** for sharing within the team providing their direct care, or **express** for other purposes;
2. disclosure is **required by law**, such as by statute or a court order;
3. disclosure is justified in the **public interest**, because the benefits to an individual or society outweigh both the patient's interest and the public interest in keeping the information confidential;
4. for patients who lack capacity, disclosure is in their **best interests**, or is otherwise permitted by law.

In every case, disclose only the **minimum necessary** information, to the right person, for a clear purpose, and use anonymised information wherever that will serve the purpose.
:::

::: proof
The structure follows from the reasons for confidentiality. Where the patient consents, their autonomy is respected and their trust is not betrayed, so the duty is not breached. Where the law requires disclosure, Parliament or a court has already balanced the private and public interests for that class of case; the doctor's role is to comply, while disclosing no more than required. Where neither applies, disclosure must be justified case by case, and the test is the same consequential reasoning that grounds the duty in the first place: is the harm that disclosure would prevent serious enough to outweigh both the harm to this patient and the wider harm to trust in medical confidentiality?

The minimum-necessary rule follows too. A disclosure that is justified for one purpose is not a licence to share everything. A doctor who informs the DVLA about a patient's seizures has no reason to mention his alcohol history unless it is relevant to fitness to drive.
:::

### Sharing for direct care {#direct-care}

Most sharing of information is routine and necessary: a GP writing a referral, a ward team discussing a patient, a pharmacist checking a prescription. Patients generally expect this, and their consent is **implied** as long as they have been told how information is shared (for example by leaflets and notices) and have not objected. A patient with capacity can object to particular information being shared, and the objection should be respected, though the clinician should explain if this could affect their care.

Withholding information from people who need it to treat a patient can itself be harmful. The Caldicott principles, which govern the use of patient information in health and social care in England, include this explicitly.

::: definition The Caldicott principles (2020) {#def-caldicott}
1. Justify the purpose(s) for using confidential information.
2. Use confidential information only when it is necessary.
3. Use the minimum necessary confidential information.
4. Access to confidential information should be on a strict need-to-know basis.
5. Everyone with access to confidential information should be aware of their responsibilities.
6. Comply with the law.
7. The duty to share information for individual care is as important as the duty to protect patient confidentiality.
8. Inform patients and service users about how their confidential information is used.
:::

Every NHS organisation has a **Caldicott Guardian**, a senior person responsible for protecting confidentiality and advising on disclosure decisions. The **National Data Guardian** for Health and Social Care, a statutory role since 2018, advises across England.

## Disclosures required by law {#legal-requirement}

Some disclosures are required by law. The doctor should disclose what is required, no more, and should usually tell the patient.

- **Notifiable diseases.** Registered medical practitioners in England must notify the local health protection team (UK Health Security Agency) of suspected cases of specified infectious diseases, such as meningococcal disease, measles and tuberculosis, under the Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010 ([[public-health/communicable-disease-control]]). Wales has equivalent regulations. HIV is not a notifiable disease under these regulations.
- **Female genital mutilation (FGM).** Since 2015, regulated health and social care professionals and teachers in England and Wales must report to the police "known" cases of FGM in girls under 18: where the girl tells them, or where they observe physical signs that appear to show FGM.
- **Court orders.** A judge can order disclosure of records or require a doctor to give evidence. A doctor may object to the court about disclosing irrelevant information but must comply with the final order. Requests from police or solicitors, without a court order, are not legal requirements.
- **Road traffic.** Under the Road Traffic Act 1988 (s172), anyone, including a doctor, must give the police information that may identify a driver alleged to have committed certain offences, if asked. This extends only to identifying information, not clinical details.
- **Terrorism.** Under the Terrorism Act 2000 it is an offence not to disclose information that may help prevent an act of terrorism or apprehend a terrorist.
- **Other statutory duties** include notifying births and deaths, reporting certain deaths to the coroner, notifying abortions to the Chief Medical Officer, and responding to the regulators' statutory requests.

## Disclosure in the public interest {#public-interest}

The hardest decisions arise when no law requires disclosure, the patient refuses consent, and the doctor believes others are at risk. The common law and the GMC permit disclosure in the **public interest** where failure to disclose may expose others to a risk of **death or serious harm**, and the benefits of disclosure outweigh the harms.

The leading English case is *W v Egdell* (1990). W was detained in a secure hospital after killing five people. He applied to a tribunal for transfer, and his solicitors commissioned a report from an independent psychiatrist, Dr Egdell. The report revealed a long-standing interest in firearms and explosives that had not been known to the hospital, and concluded that W remained dangerous. W's lawyers withdrew the application and did not submit the report. Dr Egdell sent a copy to the hospital and the Home Office. The Court of Appeal held that disclosure was justified: there was a real risk of danger to the public, and the authorities responsible for W needed the information to make decisions about his release.

In the United States, the California Supreme Court held in *Tarasoff v Regents of the University of California* (1976) that a therapist whose patient had threatened to kill an identifiable woman had a duty to take reasonable steps to protect her. English law has not adopted a general legal **duty** to warn third parties; it gives doctors a **power** to disclose in the public interest, with professional guidance on when to use it.

::: definition Making a public interest disclosure {#def-public-interest}
Before disclosing without consent in the public interest, a doctor should consider:

1. **Is consent possible?** Seek it unless doing so is unsafe or impracticable, or would undermine the purpose (for example, by alerting someone to a police investigation).
2. **How serious and likely is the harm?** Disclosure is usually justified only where there is a risk of death or serious harm to others, or to prevent or detect serious crime.
3. **Is disclosure necessary and proportionate?** Would disclosure actually reduce the risk? Is there a less intrusive way?
4. **To whom, and what?** Disclose only to an appropriate person, only the minimum necessary information.
5. **Inform the patient** of the disclosure, unless that would put someone at risk.
6. **Document** the reasons, and seek advice from a senior colleague, the Caldicott Guardian or a defence body when time allows.
:::

Some common situations have specific GMC guidance:

- **Fitness to drive.** Patients are legally responsible for informing the DVLA of conditions that may affect safe driving. If a patient continues to drive when unfit and cannot be persuaded to stop, the doctor should inform the DVLA's medical adviser, telling the patient before and after.
- **Gunshot and knife wounds.** The police should usually be told when a person arrives with a gunshot wound or a wound from an attack with a knife or other blade, initially without identifying details. Identifying information is then disclosed only if consent is given or the public interest test is met.
- **Serious communicable disease.** If a patient with a serious sexually transmitted infection refuses to tell a sexual partner who is at risk, a doctor may disclose to that partner, without naming the patient where possible, after trying to persuade the patient to do so themselves. Partner notification through sexual health services is the usual route.
- **Genetic information.** A patient's genetic test result may show that relatives are at risk.

The genetics case is unusual because the information is about the relatives too. In *ABC v St George's Healthcare NHS Trust* [2020] EWHC 455 (QB), a man who had killed his wife was found, while detained, to have Huntington disease. He refused to allow his daughter to be told. She was pregnant and later found that she had inherited the condition. The High Court held that the clinicians owed her a legal duty to **balance** her interest in being informed against his interest in confidentiality, and to act on the result of that balancing; on the facts the duty had not been breached. The judgment was explicitly limited to close relationships where the clinicians know the relative and the risk.

::: example A bus driver with epilepsy {#ex-dvla}
Return to the bus driver at the start of the chapter. He is continuing to drive a bus after a diagnosis of epilepsy and asks the neurologist to keep it confidential. What should she do?
::: solution
**Step 1: understand and persuade.** The neurologist explains again, clearly and with sympathy, that his condition means he is not legally fit to drive, especially a bus, for which the DVLA's standards are much stricter than for a car, and that he is legally obliged to tell the DVLA. She explores his fears about losing his job and points him to help: his employer may have alternative duties, and advice on employment rights is available. She records the conversation.

**Step 2: assess the risk.** A seizure at the wheel of a bus could kill him, his passengers and other road users. This is a clear risk of death or serious harm to others.

**Step 3: decide.** If he will not stop driving, or she learns he has continued, the GMC's guidance says she should disclose the relevant medical information to the DVLA's medical adviser, in confidence. She should tell him beforehand that she intends to do so, and write to him afterwards to confirm.

**Step 4: minimum necessary.** She discloses the diagnosis and relevant clinical facts to the DVLA, not to his employer. Informing the employer is a separate question; it might be justified if there were an immediate risk the DVLA route could not address, but she should seek advice first.

**Step 5: maintain the relationship.** She continues to treat him and makes clear that the disclosure is about safety, not punishment.
:::
:::

::: example A stab wound at 2 a.m. {#ex-knife}
A 19-year-old man arrives with a stab wound to the thigh. He says he was attacked outside a club, refuses to give details and asks staff not to involve the police. What should the emergency team do?
::: solution
Treat the wound first. Following GMC guidance, the team should inform the police that a person has arrived with a knife wound from an assault, without giving his name at this stage, so that the police can assess the risk to the public (an armed attacker may still be at large). Whether to disclose his identity is a separate decision: it requires his consent or a public interest justification, such as a serious risk to others. The team should tell him what has been reported and why, record the decision, and consider safeguarding needs, since young people injured by knives are often at continuing risk.
:::
:::

Public interest decisions often depend on predicting what a person will do, and predictions are uncertain. In particular, when the outcome to be prevented is rare, even a reasonably accurate risk assessment will flag many people who would never have caused harm. This does not mean that risk should be ignored. It means that disclosure decisions should rest on specific, serious and well-evidenced concerns rather than on a category or a score, and that clinicians should understand how many false alarms a rule will produce.

::: example The father's genetic result {#ex-genetic}
A 52-year-old man is diagnosed with hereditary colorectal cancer caused by a pathogenic variant in a mismatch repair gene (Lynch syndrome). His two adult daughters could each have a 50% chance of carrying the variant; if they do, regular colonoscopy and other measures could greatly reduce their risk of dying from cancer. He refuses to let them be told, because he has not spoken to them for years. What should the genetics team do?
::: solution
**Explore and support.** Most patients who initially refuse later agree with time and support. The team should find out why he refuses, explain how much the information could help his daughters, and offer ways to tell them that do not require him to speak to them, such as a letter from the genetics service.

**Weigh the interests.** The risk to the daughters is serious (a high lifetime risk of cancer) and the benefit of knowing is large (effective surveillance and prevention). The harm to him from disclosure is real but more limited, especially if done without detailed information about him. Under *ABC*, where clinicians know of a specific relative at serious risk, they have a duty to weigh these interests.

**Disclose minimally if justified.** If he still refuses and the balance favours disclosure, the team might contact the daughters to say that a condition in the family may affect them and that genetic counselling is available, without revealing his diagnosis or details beyond what is necessary. He should be told about this decision. Professional guidance from the Royal College of Physicians, Royal College of Pathologists and British Society for Genetic Medicine supports this approach.

**Document and seek advice** from senior colleagues and the Caldicott Guardian.
:::
:::

## Children, safeguarding and adults at risk {#safeguarding}

Young people are owed confidentiality as adults are, and a competent young person's information should not be shared with parents without their agreement except where a lawful basis exists. The fear that a doctor will tell their parents is one of the main reasons young people avoid sexual health and mental health services.

The exception is **safeguarding**. Where a child or young person is at risk of significant harm, such as abuse, sexual exploitation or neglect, information should be shared with the appropriate agency (usually children's social care, sometimes the police), even without consent. The statutory guidance *Working Together to Safeguard Children* (2023) makes clear that data protection law is not a barrier to sharing information needed to keep a child safe. The child should be told what will be shared and why, unless that would increase the risk ([[paediatrics/safeguarding]]).

For **adults**, the position is different. An adult with capacity who is the victim of domestic abuse generally decides whether to involve the police or social care. Disclosure without consent may be justified if others, especially children, are at risk, if a serious crime is involved, if the adult is being coerced, or if they lack capacity for the decision. Local authorities have a duty to make enquiries when an adult with care and support needs is at risk of abuse or neglect (Care Act 2014, s42).

::: example A safeguarding dilemma {#ex-safeguarding}
A 26-year-old woman attends the emergency department with a fractured wrist. She is 20 weeks pregnant and has a 3-year-old son at home. She says she fell, but the injury pattern and bruises of different ages on her arms suggest assault. Alone with the doctor, she admits her partner hit her but begs the doctor not to tell anyone, because "it will make things worse".
::: solution
**Respect and safety first.** The doctor thanks her for disclosing, treats her injury, documents the injuries and her account accurately, and asks about her immediate safety: is it safe to go home tonight, are there weapons, has the violence escalated, has she been strangled? These questions identify high risk.

**Capacity and consent.** She appears to have capacity. For her own safety, her wishes carry great weight, and the doctor should offer support rather than override her: referral to an independent domestic violence advocate (IDVA), helpline numbers, and a safety plan.

**Others at risk.** Her son lives in the home, and she is pregnant. Domestic abuse in a household with a child is a safeguarding concern for the child. The doctor should discuss this with her and with the hospital safeguarding team, and a referral to children's social care is likely to be required, ideally with her knowledge and agreement, but without it if necessary.

**High risk.** If a structured risk assessment indicates high risk, a referral to a Multi-Agency Risk Assessment Conference (MARAC) may be made, which can be done without consent where justified by the risk.

**Explain.** She should be told honestly what will be shared, with whom and why, and that the aim is to support her and protect her children, not to punish her.
:::
:::

## Records, communication and data {#records}

Confidentiality is breached far more often by carelessness than by deliberate disclosure: a conversation in a lift, a handover list left in a café, a message to the wrong patient, a curious look at a celebrity's record. Accessing a record without a legitimate reason is a breach even if nothing is passed on, and NHS systems audit who looks at what. Social media posts that describe identifiable patients, or details that allow someone to identify them, are a breach and a frequent cause of GMC complaints.

Patients have a right to see their own records under data protection law (a **subject access request**), with limited exceptions, such as information likely to cause serious harm to the patient or another person, or information about a third party. After death, the duty of confidentiality continues; the Access to Health Records Act 1990 gives the deceased person's personal representative, and anyone with a claim arising from the death, a right of access to relevant records. Families can be told information that helps them understand the death, unless the patient asked for it to be kept from them.

::: definition Data protection and secondary use {#def-data}
Under the UK GDPR, health data is **special category data**. Processing it requires a lawful basis under Article 6 and a condition under Article 9, such as the provision of health care or public health. The law requires processing to be lawful, fair and transparent, limited to its purpose, minimised, accurate, secure, and kept no longer than necessary.

Uses of health data beyond direct care, such as research, audit, service planning and commissioning, are **secondary uses**. In England they normally require one of:

- the patient's **consent**;
- **anonymised** data, from which individuals cannot reasonably be identified;
- approval under **section 251** of the NHS Act 2006 and the Health Service (Control of Patient Information) Regulations 2002, which allow the common law duty to be set aside for specified purposes on the advice of the Confidentiality Advisory Group, when consent is impracticable and anonymised data would not do.

Patients in England can register a **national data opt-out** to stop their confidential information being used for research and planning, with some exceptions.
:::

::: warning "Anonymised" data may not be anonymous
Removing names and NHS numbers does not make data anonymous. A record containing a rare diagnosis, a full date of birth, a postcode and an admission date may identify a person to anyone who knows them. Small numbers in tables ("1 case of X in this practice") can do the same. Data that has had identifiers replaced by a code is **pseudonymised**, and in law it is still personal data. Before sharing any data outside the care team, ask whether someone could work out who it is about, and apply the minimum necessary rule.
:::

::: quiz
A police officer arrives on the ward and asks a junior doctor whether a named patient was admitted last night, and what injuries he has. There is no court order. The officer says the man is a suspect in a burglary. What is the most appropriate response?
- [ ] Give the information, because the police have a legal right to it.
- [ ] Refuse to speak to the police under any circumstances.
- [x] Explain that the information cannot be disclosed without the patient's consent or a lawful basis; ask the patient whether he consents; and if he refuses, seek senior advice on whether the public interest test is met, which is unlikely for a non-violent offence.
- [ ] Confirm he is a patient but do not describe his injuries.
::: solution
The police have no general right to confidential medical information without a court order or a specific statutory power. Even confirming that someone is a patient is a disclosure. The doctor should ask the patient whether he consents. If he does not, disclosure would need to be justified in the public interest, usually reserved for preventing or detecting serious crime, such as crimes involving death or serious harm. Burglary alone would not normally meet that threshold. The doctor should involve a senior colleague and the Caldicott Guardian, document the request and the decision, and remain courteous to the officer.
:::
:::

::: history
The Hippocratic Oath includes a promise to keep secret whatever the physician sees or hears in the course of treatment. In English law, however, medical confidentiality has never been an absolute privilege. In the trial of the Duchess of Kingston for bigamy in 1776, the surgeon Caesar Hawkins was required to give evidence about what he had learned as her medical attendant; doctors, unlike lawyers, have no legal privilege in court.

In the 20th century the courts developed a general law of confidence with public interest exceptions (*W v Egdell*, 1990). The growth of computerised records prompted the Caldicott Report (1997), commissioned after concern about the flow of patient data in the NHS; Dame Fiona Caldicott's six principles became seven in 2013 and eight in 2020. The Data Protection Act 1998, the GDPR (2018) and the statutory National Data Guardian (2018) followed. Debates about national data programmes, such as care.data, abandoned in 2016 after public concern, showed that public trust in data use depends on transparency and a meaningful choice to opt out.
:::

::: summary
- Confidentiality protects trust, privacy and the willingness of patients to seek care. The duty comes from the common law, Article 8, data protection law and GMC guidance, and survives death.
- Information may be disclosed with **consent** (implied for direct care), when **required by law**, in the **public interest**, or in the **best interests** of a patient who lacks capacity. Always disclose the **minimum necessary**.
- Legal requirements include notifiable diseases, FGM in under-18s, court orders, Road Traffic Act requests to identify drivers, and terrorism information. Police requests without a court order are not legal requirements.
- Public interest disclosure requires a risk of death or serious harm to others or serious crime, a proportionate disclosure to an appropriate person, and normally telling the patient. *W v Egdell* (1990) is the leading case; *ABC v St George's* (2020) requires clinicians to balance the interests of identified relatives at genetic risk.
- Safeguarding concerns about children justify sharing without consent; for adults with capacity, consent is generally needed unless others are at risk or serious crime is involved.
- Secondary uses of data need consent, effective anonymisation, or section 251 approval; patients can register a national data opt-out. Pseudonymised data is still personal data.
- The Caldicott principles include the duty to share for individual care as well as the duty to protect.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Which basis? {level=1}
For each disclosure, state the lawful basis: (a) a GP refers a patient to a cardiologist with a summary of her history; (b) a doctor notifies the health protection team of suspected measles; (c) a psychiatrist tells the police that a patient has described a credible plan to kill a named neighbour; (d) a hospital provides records to a court under an order.
::: solution
(a) Implied consent for direct care. (b) Legal requirement (Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010). (c) Public interest: risk of death or serious harm to an identifiable person. (d) Legal requirement: court order.
:::

::: exercise The relative on the phone {level=1}
A man phones the ward asking how his adult sister is after her operation. The nurse cannot verify who he is, and the patient has not said who may be told. What should the nurse say?
::: solution
Without knowing the caller's identity or the patient's wishes, the nurse should not give clinical information, or even confirm details beyond what the patient has agreed. She can take his number and say she will ask the patient whether she would like him to be updated, or ask the patient to call him. Many units agree a password or named contact with the patient on admission to make this easier.
:::
:::

::: exercise Positive predictive value {level=1 check="0.14"}
A risk assessment flags people predicted to commit serious violence. In a population where 2% would do so, the tool has sensitivity 80% and specificity 90%. What proportion of people flagged would actually go on to commit serious violence? Give the answer to 2 decimal places.
::: hint
Use natural frequencies: out of 1,000 people, how many true positives and false positives?
:::
::: solution
Out of 1,000 people, 20 would be violent; the tool flags $0.8 \times 20 = 16$ of them. Of the 980 who would not, $0.1 \times 980 = 98$ are wrongly flagged. Positive predictive value $= 16/(16 + 98) = 16/114 \approx 0.14$. About six in seven of those flagged would not have been violent.
:::
:::

::: exercise False alarms {level=2 check="98"}
Using the same figures, in a clinic population of 1,000 people, how many people would be flagged as high risk who would not in fact go on to commit serious violence?
::: solution
Of the 980 people who would not be violent, $10\%$ are falsely flagged: $0.1 \times 980 = 98$ people. If every flag led to disclosure without consent, 98 people's confidentiality would be broken unnecessarily in order to identify 16 true cases, while 4 true cases would be missed. This is a strong argument for basing disclosure decisions on specific evidence about an individual.
:::
:::

::: exercise HIV and a partner {level=2}
A man diagnosed with HIV tells his GP that he has not told his wife and does not intend to, and that they do not use condoms. His viral load is not yet suppressed. Outline the GP's approach.
::: solution
The GP should explain the risk to his wife and the benefits to her of knowing (testing, treatment, prevention), and how treatment that suppresses his viral load would prevent sexual transmission. Offer help to tell her, including partner notification through the sexual health clinic, which can contact partners without naming the patient. Explore his reasons, such as fear of violence or rejection. If he still refuses and there is a serious risk to an identifiable person, GMC guidance permits disclosure to the partner so that she can protect herself, after telling him of the intention to do so. The GP should seek advice, disclose only what is necessary, and record the reasoning. If the wife is also the GP's patient, the GP has a duty of care to her as well, which strengthens the case for ensuring she is informed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Research data {level=2}
A registrar wants to study outcomes of all patients admitted with diabetic ketoacidosis to her hospital over five years by reviewing their records, and to publish the results. What approvals and safeguards does she need?
::: solution
If the work is research (not audit of the hospital's own care against a standard), it needs Health Research Authority and research ethics committee approval ([[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]]). Reviewing identifiable records without consent requires a lawful basis: either the records are reviewed by the direct care team, who extract only anonymised data for the research, or she obtains section 251 support via the Confidentiality Advisory Group, since contacting hundreds of past patients for consent may be impracticable. National data opt-outs must be applied where required. She must extract the minimum necessary data, pseudonymise it, store it securely, and present results so that no individual can be identified, for example by suppressing small numbers.
:::
:::

::: exercise A social media post {level=3}
A foundation doctor posts on a closed social media group for doctors: "Amazing night shift in Leeds A&E: 19-year-old who swallowed a battery for a bet, now doing fine!" No name is given. Analyse whether this breaches confidentiality and what the risks are.
::: solution
It is likely to be a breach. The details (place, approximate time, age, unusual injury) could allow the patient, their family or friends to recognise the case, and closed groups are neither secure nor private: screenshots travel. The patient did not consent to their story being shared. GMC guidance says doctors must not share identifiable information about patients on social media, and should remember that a combination of details can identify someone. The tone, treating a patient's harm as entertainment, also risks undermining public trust. The doctor should remove the post. If the case is educationally interesting, it can be presented with consent, or anonymised properly (altering non-essential details), in an appropriate forum.
:::
:::

::: exercise A conflict of duties {level=3}
A 16-year-old with type 1 diabetes tells her consultant that she has been omitting insulin to lose weight, and that she has had several episodes of vomiting she managed at home. She asks him not to tell her parents. Discuss.
::: solution
At 16 she is presumed to have capacity, and she is owed confidentiality. The consultant must first consider her safety: deliberate insulin omission is associated with recurrent diabetic ketoacidosis, which can be life-threatening, and with eating disorders. Her vomiting episodes may have been ketoacidosis.

He should explore what is happening and her reasons for not wanting her parents told; offer support, including referral to the diabetes psychology service and assessment for an eating disorder; agree a safety plan, including when to seek emergency help; and encourage her to involve her parents, perhaps with his help.

If she has capacity and the risk, although real, is not immediate, he should respect her wishes while working closely with her. If the risk becomes serious (repeated ketoacidosis, very low weight), or if her eating disorder impairs her capacity to make decisions about her treatment, disclosure to her parents in her best interests or to protect her life may be justified, and safeguarding procedures may apply. He should tell her what he will share and why, and document his reasoning.
:::
:::
