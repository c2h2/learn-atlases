Confidentiality is the clinical instrument nobody built: the promise that makes patients tell the truth, threatened daily by the systems that exist to record what they are told. **This chapter covers the duty and its justifications (the fiduciary and instrumental and privacy-rights arguments, and Hippocratic-ancient provenance), the exceptions architecture (consent, public-interest disclosure, the statutory duties, and the minimum-necessary principle), the data-era transformations (electronic records and the audit trail, secondary use of health data and its governance, genomics' familial information problem, and international data law), and the professional collisions (HIV and communicable-disease disclosure, genetic risk to relatives, safeguarding's reporting duties, and the employer-and-insurer frontier).**

## The duty

::: definition Why confidentiality binds and to whom it belongs {#def-duty}
**The duty's three legs each do different work: the fiduciary argument (the clinical relationship's internal norm - the Hippocratic 'what I see I will not speak' provenance, and the argument that a disclosure-permitting practice self-destructs because the next patient withholds the symptom that matters - the instrumental justification whose evidence is the real-world suppression data: the sexually-transmitted-infection and mental-health and substance-use care-avoidance studies that quantify confidentiality's public-health value precisely where its exceptions are most demanded); the rights argument (privacy as the control of personal information - the Warren-and-Brandeis-to-Westin-to-the-data-protection-regimes lineage, with the autonomy reading that disclosure harms dignity whether or not it harms utility); and the virtue leg (fidelity and the professional-identity account - the General-Medical-Council-style oaths and the 'trust-as-the-physician's-asset' economics). The duty's boundaries are as clinical as its core: it belongs to the patient (survivors' and minors' and deceased-patients'-varied-legal-status - the 'duty-survives-death' professional codes versus jurisdictions' evidentiary rules, an unlovely patchwork), it covers identifiable information in all forms (the 'already-known-to-family' and 'the-patient-told-the-whole-street' arguments that the courts mostly reject - control-not-secrecy being the operative theory), and it runs to everyone in the room (the receptionist-and-cleaner-and-medical-student-and-interpreter-duties that the professional codes extend and the training departments forget). The exceptions architecture, developed next, is where the duty's real shape shows: a rule with exceptions everyone knows and procedures nobody memorises, which is why the practical clinician's risk is not the dramatic disclosure but the undocumented, unplanned, disproportionate one - the 'we-had-to-tell-someone' conversation with the family that the patient had expressly closed, the worst-error class the complaints data document.**
:::

## The exceptions

::: definition Disclosure without consent: the justification ladder {#def-exceptions}
**Disclosures divide by their source of authority. Consent (express or implied - the implied-by-the-treatment-context web that everyday care runs on: the referral letter, the shared prescriptions, the MDT discussion - whose outer boundary, the 'necessary-for-your-care-versus-useful-for-the-service' line, the data-minimisation rules draw tighter every revision). Statutory duties (the notification-of-infectious-disease-and-death-and-birth regimes, the female-genital-mutilation-reporting-mandates' contested public-health-evidence-and-trust-cost debate, the road-traffic-and-firearms-injury-disclosures, and the 'shall-report-versus-may-report' vocabularies that determine whether clinical judgment survives - the confidentiality-scholarship's worry about duty-stacking is a legislative design flaw with measured care-seeking effects). Court compulsion (the summons-and-warrant layers, the privilege-claims' weak-medical-variety, the practitioner's route of notify-patient-object-on-principles-appear-only-what-ordered - the process most clinicians have never run and every clinician should have read once). And public-interest (the widest and most regulated: the harm-prevention justification with its Siracusa-adjacent proportionality tests - the 'serious-preventable-harm-to-an-identifiable-person-or-the-public', the attempt-to-obtain-consent-where-possible, the minimum-necessary-disclosure-to-the-right-body, and the documentation-of-the-reasoning that makes the disclosure reviewable - the Tarasoff-line duty-to-warn jurisprudence with its continuing argument about predicted dangerousness's base rates and its racial-distributional consequences). The minimum-necessary principle is the working discipline across all categories (the 'the-employer-needs-fit-note-not-diagnosis', the 'the-police-need-whdrove-not-his-HIV-status' applications, and the 'information-governance-published-not-improvised' rule), and its companion, informing-the-patient-afterwards-when-safe, restores the relationship the exception had to breach. The chapter's hardest jurisprudence sits where exception-meets-population: the HIV-and-communicable-disease-disclosure cases (the professional-code 'exceptional-circumstances' routes whose letters-to-contacts programmes work better-with-patient-partnership - the public-health-ethics literature's assisted-notification design that converts a disclosure-exception into a consent-model), and the safeguarding duties whose mandatory-reporting-evidence (the under-reporting-versus-family-disruption trade documented in the child-protection research, the adolescent-confidentiality-with-exceptions architecture) keeps the balancing honest.**
:::

## The data era

::: definition Records, secondary use, and genomics {#def-data}
**Electronic records changed confidentiality's physics from secrecy to access-control: the audit-trail revolution (every read logged - the tool that deters, detects and, in the post-snooping-scandal-era reforms, becomes the patient-facing accountability instrument: the 'who-read-my-record' logs now discussed as rights and built as features), the breaking-down silos tension (interoperability's clinical benefit against the re-identification surface it widens - the linked-data literature's privacy-utility frontier, the pseudonymisation-versus-anonymisation standards with their 'k-anonymity-and-differential-privacy' technical answers whose clinical relevance is governance-committee language most clinicians must learn to interrogate), and the insider-class harms (the celebrity-and-neighbour-and-colleague-lookup studies, the 'snooping-is-a-sackable-offence' policies and their under-audited enforcement, and the clinician-as-data-subject problem when the staff health record meets the employment record). Secondary use is the era's new social contract: the 'consent-for-every-project-versus-broad-consent-versus-opt-out-versus-public-interest-with-independent-authorisation' spectrum (the English Care-Quality-and-Section-251-and-Scottish-National-Data-Opt-Out-adjacent architectures, the GDPR-research-exception-and-its-safeguards, and the 'data-donors'-versus-'data-citizens' framing of the participation debates), the trust-crises that shaped it (the Care.data walkout and its successor-governance-lesson: transparency-and-naming-a-purpose beat legal-sufficiency, and the 'anonymised-then-released' incidents like the freedom-of-information-era GP-practice datasets that proved context-release deanonymises), the commercial frontier (the health-app-and-wearable-data whose 'not-a-clinical-record-therefore-not-covered' gap the regulators are closing unevenly - the advertising-identifier-and-period-tracker-and-fertility-app scandals that made 'it-is-not-a-medical-record' a consumer-protection issue), and the research-commons model (the controlled-access-collaboratories like the UK Biobank's researcher-approval regime and the 'data-use-agreements-and-publication-gatekeeping' apparatus that other countries' biobanks and the global-health data-sharing systems copied and criticised for access-inequity). Genomics breaks the individual unit last: the familial-information problem (the 'your-sequence-is-half-theirs' fact that makes the genetic-duty-to-warn-relative debates - the 'P-adjacent-case-law-and-ACMG-and-ESE-guidance-on-disharmony' between jurisdictions - unavoidable, with the practical mitigations: cascade-testing-friendly-consents-written-at-first-contact, the 'share-with-relatives-through-the-clinic-not-directly' architecture, and incidental-and-secondary-findings policies as pre-agreed disclosure contracts), the direct-to-consumer re-disclosure loop (the raw-data-downloads-and-genealogy-database-matching that dissolved the company's promises and made 'confidentiality' a setting on an app), and the population-genomics-governance (the national-programme consent models, the indigenous-data-sovereignty principles that moved from theory to infrastructure, and the 'benefit-sharing-not-just-non-discrimination' frame the global genetics-justice literature now argues).**
:::

::: example The brother on the ward {#ex-brother}
A patient admitted with a new HIV diagnosis asks the team not to tell anyone 'including my brother - he works in this hospital and will ask the nurses'. Two days later the brother, a porter, appears at the desk 'just checking how my brother's getting on'.
::: solution
**Boundaries-under-friction ([[#def-duty]], [[#def-exceptions]], [[infectious-diseases/hiv]]): The immediate craft: the ward's answer is nothing ('he is in a bed and stable, that is all I can confirm, and I cannot discuss patients with staff relatives any differently - you would not want me to'), and the system behind that sentence (the record-access flags-not-shields-with-the-audit-log-deterrent, the 'staff-as-family-are-users-not-insiders' policy the trust should have posted, and the same-day conversation with the patient about what he fears and what the plan is). The clinical offer: the assisted-notification pathway for his partners (his own agency, the HIV-link-worker service that does contacts-without-naming-without-breach - the model that converts disclosure-ethics into public-health practice), his brother's status as a person-not-a-risk acknowledged but kept out of the disclosure decision, and the capacity-and-distress check the diagnosis-week routinely hides (the 'who-minds-most-and-who-is-falling-through' review). The longer architecture: the record-access-breach response if the audit shows the ward's casualty was real (the breach-notification duties that apply to health-staff-snooping exactly as to hackers, and the restorative conversation with the porter about why the boundary protects him too), and the hospital's family-of-staff policy that most trusts have as an email and should have as an access-control rule. The ethical register: the duty's point in this encounter is the patient's ability to keep a secret inside a building full of people who love him - which is precisely when confidentiality stops being polite and starts being the thing that makes testing and treatment possible at all.**
:::
:::

::: example The father's result {#ex-genetic}
A 40-year-old man declines genetic testing for his family's known LMNA variant ('I have made my peace'); his younger sibling, a 33-year-old with palpitations, asks you - the family's long-term GP - 'surely you can tell me if Dad carries it'.
::: solution
**Familial-information-navigation ([[#def-data]], [[#def-duty]], [[cardiovascular/valve-congenital-disease]]): The hard line first: the 40-year-old's testing decision and any results are his; the GP cannot confirm carrier status, cannot confirm refusal, and the sibling cannot be given information about another adult's health on request - the duty's core, not its exception. The legitimate moves, in order: the offer to the sibling as their own patient (cascade-education: 'here is what the family variant means, here is what a test would tell you, and the pathway is this - the genetics service can see you as an at-relative'), the offer to re-approach the 40-year-old about consenting to information-sharing-in-general (the 'family-communication-agreements' the genetics guidance increasingly formalises - consent designed for this conversation before it is needed), and the 'if-I-could-tell-you-anything-the-first-thing-I-would-tell-you-is-that-you-should-be-tested' honesty about the reason for silence. The residual-ethics layer the guidelines leave ragged: the professional-codes' 'exceptional-disclosure-to-prevent-serious-harm' routes applied to genetic information (the 'duty-to-warn-foreseeable-relatives' debates - the Tarasoff-to-genomics transplants with their proportionality tests: serious, preventable, imminent-ish in an inherited-cardiac-condition-with-known-surveillance-solution sense, and disclosure-via-the-clinic-not-by-airmail), the genetics profession's practical answer (the non-identifying-risk-information-that-relatives-receive-through-the-proband's-own-sharing, and the 'family-history-is-already-a-disclosure' realism), and the documentation of whichever path is chosen (the reasoning-recorded, the patient-notified-when-a-disclosure-is-contemplated - the process that makes an exception defensible or indefensible). The clinical endgame the case actually allows: the 33-year-old gets assessed on their own symptoms (palpitations are a consultation reason with their own workup), the family clinic re-offer stays open to the 40-year-old forever, and the GP's answer to 'surely you can' is the sentence that keeps both brothers trustable: 'I can tell you everything about your risk, and nothing about his - because next year it might be you asking me to protect yours.'**
:::

## Where this leads

Communicable-disease notification in [[public-health/communicable-disease-control]] and [[microbiology/infection-control]], HIV disclosure practice in [[infectious-diseases/hiv]], genetics services in [[genetics/genetic-testing]], safeguarding duties in [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], data systems in [[clinical-skills/records-handover]] and [[clinical-skills/medical-imaging]], staff duties in [[medical-ethics-law/professionalism-regulation]], and the ethical frame in [[medical-ethics-law/ethical-principles]].

::: history
**Confidentiality's rules are older than its exceptions (the Hippocratic 'at either sight or hearing... let me keep silent') and its modern form is an accident of professional architecture: the eighteenth-century hospital's case-notes culture made private knowledge institutional (the 'secrecy-when-everyone-in-the-therapeutic-familial-relationship-knew' analysis of the pre-anonymous-ward), and the professional codes' nineteenth-century codification (the Association-of-Medical-Editors'-and-then-AMA-and-BMA-canons) made silence a duty enforceable by the profession rather than by courts. The legal convergence came through the privacy and data revolution rather than the ethics one: Warren and Brandeis's 1890 argument, the post-Watergate-and-Stasi-commissions-era information-regulators, the health-insurance-portability-and-lifecycle-of-data-codes-adjacent American rules, and the EU-data-protection-directive-to-GDPR arc that made the patient a data-subject with access-and-erasure-rights - a conceptual reversal whose clinical reception ('my record is mine in what sense, exactly?') the informatics literature still measures. The HIV epidemic was the discipline's stress test (the 'duty-to-protect-third-parties' jurisprudence beginning with the Tarasoff-line cases and the Tarasoff's-own-clinical-origins-in-a-murder, the partner-notification-wars that the CDC-assisted-notification model resolved by consent-first design, the 'HIV-status-in-the-chart-to-whom-and-when' policy battles won by the record-access-and-need-to-know architecture), and the genetic era restarted the argument at the family's scale (the 'P-vs-tort-duty-to-warn' academic literature, the 'genetic-information-nondiscrimination-acts'-with-their-GINA-adjacent-employment-and-insurance-limbo, and the cascade-testing-consent designs the services settled on). The data breaches wrote the recent chapters: the-losing-a-disc-drive-generation-of-incidents (the 2005-15 hospital-and-insurer loss tallies) that forced encryption-and-breach-notification norms, the Care.data collapse (the 2014 opt-out storm's governance lesson - that consent-architecture is trust-infrastructure and legal-sufficiency is not public-legitimacy), the snooping-prosecutions-and-sackings (the 'who-read-my-record' transparency proposals), the app-store's 'not-a-medical-record' scandals (the period-and-fertility-and-prayer-app disclosure cases that moved the FTC and EU regulators to the wearables' shore), and the pandemic's emergency data-sharing (the test-and-trace-data-retention-and-function-creep arguments, the 'temporary-surveillance-is-the-ask-and-permanent-is-the-history' scepticism from the civil-liberties literature, and the contact-tracing apps' data-deletion promises whose keeping the field now audits). The through-line: every technology that moves information quietly moves the exception boundaries with it, and the profession's task stays the one the oath named - keeping the patient's story on his side of the line, now with a log, a purpose-limitation and an information-governance clause attached.** - [[clinical-skills/records-handover]], [[genetics/genetic-testing]].**
:::

::: summary
- Confidentiality is a clinical instrument before an ethical ornament: the care-avoidance and symptom-suppression data quantify what disclosure-risk costs, and it is costed highest in exactly the populations the exceptions chase.
- The duty belongs to the patient, covers identifiable information in any form, binds everyone who touches it, and survives the encounter - its variation at death and across borders is law's patchwork, ethics' constant.
- Exceptions have authority sources - consent (express or implied), statute, court order, and public interest with proportionality tests - and the working disciplines are minimum-necessary and inform-when-safe, which convert breach into justified disclosure.
- Electronic records traded secrecy for access-control: audit trails, breach duties and patient-facing 'who-read-me' logs are the new machinery, and the insider-lookup problem is the profession's own hygiene test.
- Secondary data use and genomics dissolved the individual container: broad-consent architectures, controlled-access research commons and the familial-information problem are governance questions every clinician will meet as patient questions.
- The practical competence: know the ladder, document the rung, and never let 'the family wanted to know' become the institution's confession.
:::

::: exercise Name the authority {level=1}
Identify the legal/ethical authority for each disclosure and the discipline it requires: (a) notification of a confirmed measles case; (b) releasing notes to a solicitor for a claim the patient brought; (c) telling a patient's daughter (a nurse) her father's diagnosis because 'she is the carer'; (d) disclosure to the DVLA of untreated syncope where permitted.
::: solution
**Authority-and-discipline-drill ([[#def-exceptions]]): (a) Statutory duty (the notification regulations): the discipline is completeness-and-speed plus the public-health conversation with the patient it entails (the notification is the start of an assistance process, not a betrayal - the contact-tracing partnership model). (b) Consent (express, and the claimant's implied waiver of privilege in the claim's scope): the discipline is scope (the pleadings define relevance; the solicitor's blanket request is not the authority - the 'releasewhat-is-asked-and-relevant-not-all-there-is' rule) and the patient's continuing right to know what left. (c) No authority whatsoever: the 'she is family and a nurse' argument is the classic - the fix is the carer-included-by-patient-permission conversation (and the 'what-if-the-patient-lacks-capacity-and-she-is-the-deputy-or-attorney-with-scope-for-this-decision' correct route that makes the request answerable). (d) The statutory-permission-plus-public-interest route with its procedures: tell-the-patient-first-and-aim-for-consent, disclose-only-the-medical-facts-the-regulator-requires, record-the-reasoning, and the clinical duty that actually prevents the disclosure (managing the syncope so the question goes away - the 'driving-advice-appointment-keeping' follow-up is the confidentiality-preserving intervention). The exercise's rule: every disclosure needs its named page (the act, the order, the consent form, the code paragraph), and the clinician who can cite it has moved from breach to governance.**
:::

::: exercise Write the policy sentence {level=1}
Draft the one-paragraph 'staff as family members' policy for your hospital's handbook, covering record-access, verbal information, escalation and audit, in language a porter can recognise.
::: solution
**APolicyParagraphForHumans ([[#def-duty]], [[#def-data]]): Draft: 'If someone you know becomes a patient here, they have exactly the same confidentiality as any patient - including from you. That means: do not look at their record (every access is logged, read by information-governance, and unexplained access to a known person's record is a dismissable offence even when your reason was love); do not confirm, deny or discuss their presence or care with anyone, including their family, their managers or the nurse on their ward - the ward will tell you nothing, and that is the policy working, not rudeness; if you need to pass information, ask the team to give it to the person you would normally tell, through them, with the patient's agreement; and if someone asks you about a patient you suspect is connected to you, tell the ward clerk to contact the nurse-in-charge and step away. Families of staff get the same letter and the same explanation: our silence protects your relatives too.' The gloss for the drafter: the paragraph names the four behaviours (access, confirm, relay, deflect), the sanction and its rationale, the escalation path, and the reciprocity argument that makes acceptance rational - the 'audit-log-deterrent-plus-normalisation' design the snooping-prevention reviews recommend over the threats-only version.**

::: exercise Handle the coroner's request {level=2}
A coroner requests the full GP record of a patient who died; the family instructs you to oppose it 'because he told us things about the children'. Advise: the legal position, the confidentiality analysis, and the humane middle.
::: solution
**Compelled-disclosure-practice ([[#def-exceptions]], [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]]): The law: coronial requests in a death-investigation are statutory and the duty of confidentiality yields to them (the 'deceased-patients'-records-and-the-coroner' position is the clearest case in the jurisdiction; refusal is not an option the clinician holds, though the registrar's-rules-objections about relevance and third-party-identifiers are the professional's voice). The analysis to state aloud for the family: the investigation's purpose (a death inquired into is the law reading the record for the living's protection), the confidentiality's surviving parts (the record goes to the coroner's office, not the public; irrelevant third parties' identities can be flagged; the disclosure is documented and bounded), and the honest answer to 'things about the children' (if the disclosure concerns living children at risk, a separate safeguarding duty may already have been owed and the coroner is not the only reader - the duty-stack review before the conversation, so the family hears one coherent account). The humane middle: the offer to ask the coroner's officer whether full-records-or-relevant-extracts suffices (in practice they say records-are-standard-and-narrowing-helps-nobody, but the question models advocacy), the family's own legal-aid signpost (the 'interested-persons'-representation-at-inquest' right they didn't know they had), and the practice's record-of-the-conversation so the next clinician does not re-litigate. The teaching close: the duty's shape in this case is its most reassuring - it yields only to law, never to curiosity, and the clinician's job in compelled disclosure is to be a lawyer's courteous counterpart and a family's honest translator at the same time.**
:::

::: exercise Design the secondary-use governance {level=2}
Your academic health centre wants a research data platform re-using routine records for nested trials and observational studies. Design the governance package: lawful basis, consent position, transparency artefacts, access controls, and the community-voice structure whose absence felled Care.data.
::: solution
**Governance-as-architecture ([[#def-data]], [[public-health/health-systems-economics]], [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]]): Lawful basis: the statutory-health-purpose-and-public-task-plus-research-exemption analysis (the Section-251-adjacent CAG approvals where consent is impracticable, the GDPR Article-9 processing conditions and its safeguards, and the 'no-detriment-to-care' commitment written in), stated per-project rather than once. Consent position: the honest 'not-per-patient-consent-and-here-is-why' public argument (the consent-only model's selection-bias critique - that consent-only research systematically misrepresents the sick - paired with the opt-out-route's actual function: a choice signal, not a permission engine, so its design (ease, visibility, non-punitive withdrawal) carries the legitimacy the legal analysis cannot). Transparency artefacts: the plain-English purpose register (one page per study, no acronym soup, published before data-flow), the 'who-applied-what-was-approved-what-was-rejected' log, the breach-and-incident disclosure norm, and the annual 'what-the-platform-found-and-who-it-helped' report - the story-return duty whose absence the data-donation literature keeps finding. Access controls: the controlled-access-tiered model (de-identified-open-for-replication, pseudonymised-for-approved-projects-in-secure-environments with output-disclosure review, identifiable-exception-only-and-logged), the researcher-training-and-attestation requirement, and the differential-privacy-and-synthetic-data-frontier where technically honest. Community voice: the standing public-panel-with-power (the 'lay-siders-who-can-block-projects-not-just-mince-words' design the post-Care.data evaluations recommend), the clinician-and-patient-co-design of the transparency artefacts, the equity-of-benefit clause (the platform must publish what it does for the deprived cohorts whose data it pools), and the sunset-and-review rhythm that keeps the architecture argued rather than inherited. The one-sentence design principle the whole package: assume the public is reasonable about benefit and savage about surprise, and engineer for the surprise.**

::: example The app that knew {#ex-app}
A patient with epilepsy asks whether she should delete a seizure-tracking app that 'seems to know everything' - it logs events, sleep, location history and syncs to a US cloud; she also wants its data in her NHS record.
::: solution
**Consumer-data-between-clinic-and-advertiser ([[#def-data]], [[clinical-skills/records-handover]], [[nervous-system/epilepsy]]): The appraisal for the patient: the app's terms are the medical device's privacy sheet inverted (the 'not-a-health-record-therefore-GDPR-light-and-HIPAA-absent' analysis, the sale-or-share-with-advertisers-SDK findings the period-tracker-and-fertility-app scandals made household knowledge, the location-history-as-seizure-diary inference problem - a seizure log plus location is a diagnosis-plus-movement profile no clinician could lawfully keep). The clinical answer with two hands: the data is genuinely useful (the seizure-frequency-and-trigger evidence the apps generate now feeds epilepsy-clinic decisions - the 'patient-generated-data-in-consultation' literature), so the move is the import-not-endorse: ask for the export (CSV or PDF of the event log), review it as a history, and file the clinically-relevant extract in the record (minimum-necessary applied in the patient's favour), while the raw feed and its cloud stay outside the NHS's boundaries. The documentation: the app-name-and-version in the notes (the 'device-as-therapeutic-adjacent-record' question the regulators are still settling), the patient's own consent-to-share-with-the-clinic recorded, and the advice she deserves stated plainly: 'this app is a diary with a business model; keep the diary's value, control the sharing, and here are the two apps whose privacy documents a pharmacist could defend.' The service-level asks: the patient-platform-interop-standards route (the FHIR-and-consent-manager architectures that would let her share app-data-by-choice with her record - the 'data-carrying-rights-as-clinical-infrastructure' argument), and the equity note (the app-recommendation-inequality: the digital-therapeutics-eligible are the digitally-served unless prescribing includes the privacy-and-cost appraisal). The closing line she repeats at her next appointment: 'the app knows my seizures; my clinic is the only place that has to keep them secret.'**

::: example The safeguarding dilemma {#ex-safeguarding}
A 15-year-old asthmatic admits during a consultation that her stepfather 'checks her phone and won't let her be alone with doctors' and asks you not to tell her mother; her growth chart and attendance pattern flag concern.
::: solution
**Confidentiality-where-protection-lives ([[#def-exceptions]], [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], [[clinical-skills/communication]]): The frame: adolescent confidentiality is a treatment-enabling trust with named exceptions (the safeguarding duties are the largest), and this presentation - controlling surveillance of a child, disclosure-avoidance behaviour, growth-and-attendance flags - is not a confidentiality puzzle but a safeguarding assessment wearing one. The craft sequence: the conversation's own handling (validate-then-explain-the-limits early - the 'there are three things I may have to share, and why' pre-disclosure script that is the ethically-defensible version of the promise), the information-gathering that respects the danger (the abuse-at-home-disclosures-escalate-risk-if-clumsily-handled literature; the 'who-is-safe-and-what-could-you-tolerate' questions; the school-nurse-and-teacher-views that need no breach because they are the same team's duty), the threshold decision (her account plus the indicators meet the 'child-in-need-versus-significant-harm' assessment line in most frameworks, and the consultation-with-the-designated-safeguarding-lead is the process that moves the decision off one GP's shoulders), and the disclosure's conduct when it comes (tell-her-what-will-happen-when-and-to-whom, offer-her-presence-at-the-strategy-meeting-adjacent-conversation, and the police-and-social-care routes' timing planned around her safety rather than the clinician's relief). The mother-question: she may be a protective ally or a managed-deception's host; the assessment decides, and 'tell the mother because she is the mother' is not the rule the duty actually contains. The record: contemporaneous, verbatim-where-possible, safeguarding-vocabulary-precise-because-a-court-may-read-it-in-a-year, and the confidentiality-decision's-reasoning-documented-so-the-successor-clinician-inherits-the-why. The teaching closing: the adolescent's trust and the child's safety are the same interest counted at different times, and the clinician's skill is making the systems notice without becoming the surveillance the girl named.**

::: exercise Answer the employer {level=1}
An employer calls a occupational-health clinic asking whether their night-shift driver 'is on any medication affecting driving - he consented to the medical'. Give the answer, the doctrine, and the record entry.
::: solution
**Fit-note-not-file ([[#def-exceptions]], [[public-health/environmental-occupational]]): The answer: 'we confirm he attended and we give fitness opinions; diagnoses and prescriptions are not disclosed - if you need function, ask for function: fit / fit-with-restrictions / temporarily-unfit, with the restrictions stated.' The doctrine: consented-disclosure-scope (consent-to-a-medical-is-consent-to-its-purpose - the fitness determination - not to the employer reading the clinical record; the 'consent-must-be-informed-about-what-leaves' rule the occupational codes make explicit), the minimum-necessary principle applied to the fit-note genre, and the driver-and-vehicle-authority-style separate channel for the licensing-relevant conditions (the 'regulator-not-employer-decides-licensing' architecture that protects both). The record entry: 'occupational medical performed; fitness opinion issued to employer dated; no clinical information disclosed; patient informed of these boundaries at outset' - the sentence that answers the inevitable later dispute about what the employer was told. The adjacent trap: the employee's own consent to 'the medical' signed in HR's onboarding bundle (a contract term is not clinical consent to disclosure - the voluntariness analysis from the consent chapter applied to the power gradient), and the GP-for-a-job-letter market's corruption problem the professional regulators keep fining.**

::: exercise Judge the research breach {level=2}
A researcher finds, in a linked hospital dataset she is auditing, that a former patient of hers - who asked years ago that 'psychiatric history never leave the trust' - appears in a multi-site collaboration's extract. The study is approved and the data pseudonymised. Advise her and the governance.
::: solution
**One-flag-versus-a-approval ([[#def-data]], [[#def-exceptions]], [[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]]): The immediate analysis: the disclosure was the dataset's (the approval-and-CAG-style-authorisation covered processing under stated conditions; pseudonymised data lawfully shared is a lawful basis question answered before she saw it), and her discovery changes nothing legally for the extract - the data is not 'hers', the project is not a series of private conversations, and unilateral deletion of one row corrupts a research dataset (the 'integrity-of-approved-research-data' rule - ad-hoc clinical mercy is not a data-processing lawful basis either). The things she must do: report the conflict and the flag's existence to the Data-Protection-Officer-and-CALIG-adjacent governance (the special-category-and-sensitive-flag question - the project's lawful basis should have accounted for mental-health data with enhanced safeguards, and the audit trail answer to 'could this row be linked back' is the governance item), recuse herself from analyses touching that cohort if her clinical relationship could bias decisions (the dual-role management the research-ethics frameworks prescribe), and ensure the patient's original request is honoured where it can be (the 'sensitive-records-flags-with-access-restriction' features that modern record systems offer - the technical control that makes such promises keepable rather than hopeful). The system finding to raise at the next committee: the trust's export-configuration should carry the patient's access-restriction flags through pseudonymisation (the 'preferences-as-data-that-travels-the-pipeline' design gap the national evaluations keep documenting), and the research community's honest sentence: pseudonymisation is risk-management, not anonymity, and promises made to patients should be tested against the linkage surface before being made. The personal note: she is not the breach; she is the audit that found the architecture's gap, and the governance response should thank the clinician without exempting the system.**

::: exercise Teach the first year {level=1}
Give the two-minute version of confidentiality for new medical students, including: why it exists, the three-sentence practical rule, and the example you will test them with next week.
::: solution
**Two-minutes-that-stick ([[#def-duty]], [[#def-exceptions]]): The why: 'patients only tell the truth where the telling is safe, and the things they hide are the things that kill them - confidentiality is why you get the drug history, the sexual history and the symptom they never mentioned to anyone else; break it once in a ward's earshot and you spend the profession's credit you did not earn.' The three-sentence rule: 'what the patient tells you stays with the team for the patient's care and goes nowhere else unless a consent, a court or a statute says so; when you must tell, tell the minimum to the right person and tell the patient you are doing it unless that makes things worse; and when in doubt at two in the morning, the answer is always the same sentence: I will find out who can authorise this before I say anything.' The test example for next week: the taxi-driver patient who asks you not to tell his employer he has insulin-treated diabetes and wants the prescription posted to work - the answer combining the posting-risk conversation (the 'prescription-to-the-workplace-is-a-disclosure' insight), the driving-licensing channel question, and the 'what-are-you-actually-afraid-of' clinical-history-behind-the-request move; they should arrive with a plan that keeps the duty, keeps the licence question honest, and keeps the patient.**

::: exercise Draft the genomics consent {level=2}
Draft the consent-form paragraphs for a clinical-genome programme that will: return actionable secondary findings, store sequence for reanalysis, and encourage cascade testing of relatives. Each paragraph must satisfy a skeptical genetic counsellor and a nervous lawyer.
::: solution
**Consent-as-relationship-terms ([[#def-data]], [[genetics/genetic-testing]]): Paragraph one, secondary findings: 'Some results about conditions not causing this referral may be found. We look for and will tell you only the list you can read here (treatable, childhood-onset-eligible conditions agreed by professional bodies), unless you decline this in section 4. You may choose: tell-me-the-list / tell-me-nothing / discuss-what-you-find-first. Changing your mind later is allowed and has no effect on your care.' Paragraph two, storage and reanalysis: 'Your DNA sample and a coded version of your results are stored in the laboratory for reanalysis as knowledge improves; we will not contact you about new findings unless reanalysis was part of your original diagnosis-care and your consent allows re-contact (options: yes / for-your-family-only / no / decide-when-you-are-older). Your stored sample will never be used for: insurance-or-employment purposes, direct-to-consumer-databases, or research outside approved projects (the approval-and-access-governance sentence the lawyer requires).' Paragraph three, relatives: 'Genetic results concern biological relatives. We will not contact them; we will support you to tell them (the written-information-pack and the clinic's help with letters and pathways), and we will record what you wish shared. If a result concerns a child who cannot yet decide, we keep the information available so they can choose for themselves when able.' The counsellor's-margin notes the form's design must honour: the list-not-all secondary-findings convention (the ACMG-and-UK-GSL-adjacent lists with their review cadence), the re-contact asymmetry's equity data (who actually calls back), and the cascade-support offer as a named service not a suggestion - because the literature's finding is that families transmit genetic information badly when the clinic hands them an unsupportable message, and a consent form that recruits patients as postal carriers has made confidentiality's exception problem everyone's kitchen-table problem. (The final form deletes this parenthetical; the committee keeps it.)**
