Urine leaves the kidney through the renal pelvis, travels down the ureters by peristalsis, is stored in the bladder and is expelled through the urethra. Most problems of this drainage system come from three sources: infection climbing up from the outside, stones and other blockages obstructing flow, and tumours growing from the lining. Each threatens the kidneys behind them, and each has a characteristic way of presenting: burning and frequency, colicky pain, difficulty passing urine, or blood.

This lesson covers urinary tract infection, kidney stones, obstruction and the prostate, polycystic kidney disease and the main urological cancers. It concentrates on the mechanisms and the reasoning behind investigation and management; the surgical procedures are described in [[surgery/vascular-urological-surgery]]. It builds on the anatomy of the pelvis in [[anatomy/pelvis-perineum]] and on bacterial pathogenesis in [[microbiology/bacterial-pathogenesis]]. The UK guidance referred to is from NICE (NG109 on lower urinary tract infection, NG111 on pyelonephritis, NG224 on infection in under-16s, NG118 on renal and ureteric stones and NG12 on suspected cancer).

## Urinary tract infection

### How infection reaches the urinary tract

Almost all urinary tract infections (UTIs) are **ascending**. Bacteria from the bowel colonise the perineum, enter the urethra and multiply in the bladder. From there some ascend the ureters to the kidney. Spread through the blood to the kidney is rare and suggests *Staphylococcus aureus* bacteraemia.

The body has several defences: regular complete emptying of the bladder, which washes bacteria out; the length of the male urethra; the acidity and high urea content of urine; uromodulin (Tamm–Horsfall protein), which binds bacteria; the ureterovesical junction, which acts as a valve; and the shedding of infected bladder lining cells.

Factors that defeat these defences predict who gets UTIs: the short female urethra and sexual intercourse; pregnancy (ureteric dilation from progesterone and pressure from the uterus); incomplete emptying (prostatic enlargement, neurogenic bladder, prolapse); foreign bodies (catheters, stones); structural abnormalities (vesicoureteric reflux in children); diabetes; and reduced oestrogen after the menopause, which changes the vaginal flora.

Bacterial factors matter too. **Uropathogenic *Escherichia coli*** carries fimbriae (P fimbriae and type 1 fimbriae) that attach to the urothelium. ***Proteus*** species make **urease**, which splits urea into ammonia, making the urine alkaline and promoting struvite stones.

::: definition Urinary tract infection {#def-uti}
**Urinary tract infection** is infection anywhere in the urinary tract. It is classified by site and by host:

- **lower UTI (cystitis)**: infection of the bladder, causing dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, new nocturia and cloudy urine;
- **upper UTI (acute pyelonephritis)**: infection of the kidney, causing fever, rigors, loin pain and tenderness, often with nausea and vomiting, with or without lower urinary symptoms;
- **uncomplicated** UTI occurs in a non-pregnant woman with a normal urinary tract; **complicated** UTI occurs in men, pregnancy, children, structural or functional abnormalities, catheters, stones, immunosuppression or kidney disease;
- **catheter-associated UTI** occurs in people with a urinary catheter;
- **urosepsis** is sepsis originating in the urinary tract;
- **asymptomatic bacteriuria** is bacteria in the urine without symptoms of infection.

*E. coli* causes most uncomplicated UTIs. Others include *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* (young sexually active women), *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterococcus* and, in complicated and catheter-associated infection, *Pseudomonas* and resistant organisms.
:::

### Diagnosis

In a woman under 65 with typical symptoms, the diagnosis is often clinical. UK diagnostic guidance (from the UK Health Security Agency) uses three key symptoms: dysuria, new nocturia and cloudy urine. When two or more are present, UTI is likely; with one, a urine dipstick helps. A positive **nitrite** test strongly supports bacterial infection, because nitrate-reducing bacteria such as *E. coli* convert dietary nitrate in the urine to nitrite. **Leucocyte esterase** indicates white cells, which accompany infection but also other inflammation.

Dipsticks are not used to diagnose UTI in people over 65 or in those with catheters, because asymptomatic bacteriuria is so common in these groups that a positive dipstick does not distinguish infection from colonisation. Urine **culture** is sent in men, pregnancy, children, people with sepsis, treatment failure, recurrent infection, catheters and those at risk of resistance.

The value of a dipstick depends on the probability of infection before the test.

### Management principles

The following summarises NICE antimicrobial prescribing guidance as of 2026; agents, doses and durations are chosen for each person from current local guidance, which reflects local resistance patterns.

- **Non-pregnant women with lower UTI**: consider self-care or a back-up prescription for mild symptoms, as many infections resolve; otherwise a **3-day course** of nitrofurantoin (if eGFR is 45 or more) or trimethoprim (if the risk of resistance is low).
- **Pregnant women**: treat with a **7-day course** (nitrofurantoin is first-line, avoided near term), send a culture, and treat **asymptomatic bacteriuria** detected on screening, because it increases the risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth.
- **Men**: a **7-day course**, with culture. Men with recurrent infection or features of prostatitis or pyelonephritis need further assessment.
- **Acute pyelonephritis**: a longer course (typically 7–10 days, depending on the agent) of an antibiotic that achieves good kidney tissue levels, such as cefalexin or co-amoxiclav (guided by culture) or a quinolone when appropriate; intravenous treatment and admission if the person is septic, vomiting or pregnant.
- **Children**: NICE NG224 sets out when to test and image; infants under 3 months with suspected UTI are referred urgently to paediatric care.

Nitrofurantoin is concentrated in the urine and is effective for cystitis, but does not reach adequate levels in the kidney or blood, so it is not used for pyelonephritis or sepsis.

::: example An obstructed, infected kidney {#ex-uro-sepsis}
A 54-year-old woman has 24 hours of right loin pain, fever of 39.4 °C and rigors. Her heart rate is 118/min, blood pressure 88/50 mmHg and lactate 4.1 mmol/L. Urine dipstick shows nitrite and leucocytes. CT shows a 7 mm stone in the right upper ureter with hydronephrosis. What is the diagnosis and the immediate priority?
::: solution
**Septic shock from an infected, obstructed kidney** (obstructive pyelonephritis). Pus under pressure behind the stone cannot drain, and antibiotics alone penetrate poorly.

**Priorities**, following the sepsis pathway: blood and urine cultures, intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour, fluid resuscitation and escalation to critical care. The key urological action is **urgent decompression** of the kidney, by percutaneous **nephrostomy** or retrograde **ureteric stent**, as an emergency. The stone itself is treated later, once infection has resolved. Failing to decompress an infected obstructed kidney is a well-recognised cause of avoidable death.
:::
:::

::: example A smelly urine in the care home {#ex-asb}
Staff in a care home send a urine sample from an 86-year-old woman with dementia because her urine smells strong and looks dark. She has no fever, no new confusion beyond her baseline, no dysuria and no loin pain. The dipstick is positive for leucocytes and nitrite and the culture grows *E. coli*. Should she receive antibiotics?
::: solution
No. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is very common in older women (and in almost everyone with a long-term catheter). Smelly or cloudy urine alone is not a sign of infection, and dark urine usually means she is drinking too little. Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria in older adults does not improve outcomes and causes harm: adverse drug effects, *Clostridioides difficile* infection and antimicrobial resistance.

The right response is to encourage fluids, observe for genuine signs of infection (new dysuria, fever, loin pain, new or worsening confusion with no other cause) and not to send urine for dipstick testing in this age group without specific symptoms. Pregnancy and planned urological procedures are the main situations in which asymptomatic bacteriuria is treated ([[infectious-diseases/stewardship-outbreaks]]).
:::
:::

## Kidney stones

::: definition Urolithiasis {#def-stones}
**Urolithiasis** is the formation of stones in the urinary tract. Stones form when urine is **supersaturated** with a stone-forming salt and inhibitors of crystallisation (such as citrate and magnesium) are insufficient. The main types are:

- **calcium oxalate** (the large majority) and **calcium phosphate**: associated with low urine volume, hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria (including after bowel resection or bariatric surgery), hypocitraturia, primary hyperparathyroidism and distal renal tubular acidosis;
- **uric acid**: associated with acidic urine, gout, metabolic syndrome and high cell turnover; not visible on plain radiographs but seen on CT; can be dissolved by alkalinising the urine;
- **struvite** (magnesium ammonium phosphate): formed in alkaline urine during infection with urease-producing organisms such as *Proteus*; may grow into large "staghorn" stones filling the renal pelvis;
- **cystine**: in the inherited disorder cystinuria; recurrent from childhood.
:::

Stones are common, affecting roughly one person in ten during their life in many high-income countries, and they often recur. A stone in the kidney may be silent. When it moves into the ureter and obstructs it, it causes **renal colic**: sudden, severe, colicky pain in the loin radiating to the groin or genitals, with restlessness, nausea and vomiting, and haematuria (often non-visible). The stone tends to lodge where the ureter narrows: the pelviureteric junction, where it crosses the iliac vessels, and the vesicoureteric junction.

**Investigation and management** (NICE NG118):
- **low-dose non-contrast CT** of the kidneys, ureters and bladder within 24 hours of presentation in adults (ultrasound in pregnancy and usually in children);
- **NSAIDs** as first-line analgesia by any route, with intravenous paracetamol and then opioids if needed;
- **alpha-blockers** considered for distal ureteric stones under 10 mm, to help passage;
- most small stones (under about 5 mm) pass spontaneously; larger or proximal stones, those that do not pass, and those causing uncontrolled pain need treatment: **ureteroscopy**, **shockwave lithotripsy** or, for large kidney stones, **percutaneous nephrolithotomy**;
- **any stone with obstruction and infection** needs urgent decompression, as in [[#ex-uro-sepsis]].

::: example Twin colics and prevention {#ex-stone-prevention}
A 38-year-old man who works outdoors as a roofer has his second episode of renal colic in two years. Both stones were calcium oxalate. He drinks about 1 L of fluid a day, mostly cola, and eats a lot of salty processed food. Serum calcium and urate are normal. What advice and investigations reduce his risk of recurrence?
::: solution
His main risk factor is **low urine volume**, worsened by sweating at work. The single most effective measure is to drink enough to produce **2.5–3 L of urine a day**, with water preferred to sugary or carbonated drinks; adding fresh lemon juice increases urinary citrate, an inhibitor of crystallisation.

Other dietary measures for calcium oxalate stones: **reduce salt** (sodium increases urinary calcium), keep a **normal calcium intake** (dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut; restricting calcium increases oxalate absorption and stone risk), moderate animal protein, and avoid very high oxalate intake.

Because he has recurrent stones, he should have a **metabolic assessment**: serum calcium (done), urate, and analysis of 24-hour urine for volume, calcium, oxalate, citrate, urate and sodium, which guides specific treatment such as a thiazide for hypercalciuria or potassium citrate for hypocitraturia.
:::
:::

## Obstruction and the prostate

::: definition Urinary tract obstruction {#def-prostate}
**Obstructive uropathy** is blockage of urine flow; the resulting dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces is **hydronephrosis**. Obstruction may be **upper tract** (stones, pelviureteric junction obstruction, tumours, retroperitoneal fibrosis, pregnancy) or **lower tract** (prostatic enlargement, urethral stricture, bladder tumour, neurogenic bladder, drugs and constipation causing retention).

**Benign prostatic enlargement** from benign prostatic hyperplasia is the commonest cause of lower tract obstruction in older men. It causes **lower urinary tract symptoms**: voiding symptoms (hesitancy, weak stream, straining, incomplete emptying, terminal dribbling) and storage symptoms (frequency, urgency, nocturia).

**Acute urinary retention** is a painful inability to pass urine with a distended bladder. **Chronic retention** is painless, with a large residual volume; when bladder pressure is high it can cause bilateral hydronephrosis and CKD.
:::

**Management of lower urinary tract symptoms** in men (NICE CG97) includes lifestyle measures (reducing evening fluids, caffeine and alcohol), **alpha-blockers** (such as tamsulosin) for moderate to severe voiding symptoms, which relax prostatic smooth muscle and work within days, and **5-alpha reductase inhibitors** (such as finasteride) for enlarged prostates or men at high risk of progression, which shrink the gland over 6–12 months. Surgery (transurethral resection and newer techniques) is offered for severe symptoms, retention or complications.

**Acute retention** is relieved by catheterisation. An alpha-blocker is usually started before a trial without catheter. Precipitants such as constipation, anticholinergic drugs, opioids, alcohol and infection are treated or removed.

::: example A painless large bladder {#ex-retention}
A 74-year-old man has been "wetting himself" at night for months and has a weak stream. He has no pain. His abdomen shows a dull, non-tender mass up to the umbilicus. Creatinine is 260 µmol/L (no previous result), and ultrasound shows bilateral hydronephrosis with a residual bladder volume of 1600 mL. Explain the findings and what to expect after catheterisation.
::: solution
**High-pressure chronic urinary retention**, most likely from prostatic obstruction. The bladder has slowly stretched until it holds a large volume at high pressure. The night-time wetting is **overflow incontinence**, not a storage problem. High bladder pressure is transmitted to both kidneys, causing **bilateral hydronephrosis** and kidney impairment.

After **catheterisation**, kidney function usually improves over days to weeks, though some loss may be permanent. A **post-obstructive diuresis** may follow, with urine output of several litres a day; fluid balance, weight and electrolytes are monitored and fluid is replaced if he becomes volume depleted. Some clinicians monitor blood pressure carefully during decompression of very large volumes. Long-term options are surgery to relieve obstruction or a long-term catheter, depending on his fitness and wishes. A trial without catheter is unlikely to succeed because his bladder muscle has been stretched for a long time.
:::
:::

## Cystic disease and cancers

::: definition Polycystic kidney disease and urological cancers {#def-cancers}
**Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease** (ADPKD) is the commonest inherited kidney disease, caused mainly by variants in *PKD1* or *PKD2*. Cysts grow progressively in both kidneys, enlarging them and destroying normal tissue, so that many affected people reach kidney failure in middle age (later with *PKD2*). Features include hypertension, loin pain, haematuria, cyst infection, stones, liver cysts and, in a minority, intracranial aneurysms. **Tolvaptan**, a vasopressin V2 receptor antagonist, slows cyst growth in selected adults with rapidly progressing disease. Simple kidney cysts, which are common with age, are a different and usually harmless finding.

**Bladder cancer** is usually a **urothelial carcinoma**. Its main risk factor is **smoking**; occupational exposure to aromatic amines (dye and rubber industries) and, worldwide, schistosomiasis (associated with squamous cell carcinoma) are others. It typically presents with **painless visible haematuria**.

**Renal cell carcinoma** arises from tubular epithelium; the clear cell type is the commonest and is linked to loss of the *VHL* gene. Risk factors include smoking, obesity and hypertension. Most are now found incidentally on imaging; the classic triad of haematuria, loin pain and a mass is a late presentation. It can cause paraneoplastic effects (polycythaemia, hypercalcaemia).

**Prostate cancer** is the commonest cancer in men in the UK. Early disease is usually asymptomatic. Assessment of a raised **prostate-specific antigen** (PSA) now uses multiparametric **MRI** before biopsy. PSA is not specific: it is also raised by benign enlargement, infection, recent ejaculation and catheterisation.
:::

::: example Painless red urine {#ex-haematuria}
A 63-year-old woman who has smoked 20 cigarettes a day for 40 years passes bright red urine with small clots on three occasions over a week. She has no pain and no urinary symptoms; dipstick for nitrite and leucocytes is negative. What is the most important diagnosis to exclude, and how?
::: solution
**Painless visible haematuria in an older smoker is bladder cancer until proven otherwise.** Kidney and upper tract cancers are also possible. NICE NG12 recommends referral on a suspected cancer pathway for people aged 45 and over with unexplained visible haematuria without urinary tract infection, or with visible haematuria that persists or recurs after successful treatment of a UTI.

Assessment in a haematuria clinic includes **flexible cystoscopy** to inspect the bladder, **imaging of the upper tract** (CT urography or ultrasound), kidney function and blood pressure. If a bladder tumour is found, it is resected endoscopically (transurethral resection of bladder tumour), which provides tissue for staging. She should be offered support to stop smoking, which lowers the risk of recurrence.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not attribute haematuria to the obvious {#warn-haematuria}
Haematuria in a person taking an anticoagulant, or with a urinary infection, is often blamed on the anticoagulant or the infection. Anticoagulants unmask bleeding from an underlying lesion more often than they cause it, so visible haematuria on anticoagulants is investigated in the same way as in anyone else. Haematuria with a UTI should be rechecked after treatment; if it persists, it needs investigation. Similarly, red urine is not always blood: beetroot, rifampicin and some other drugs colour the urine, and a dipstick that is positive for blood without red cells on microscopy suggests myoglobin or free haemoglobin ([[renal/filtration]]).
:::

::: quiz
A 26-year-old woman, not pregnant, has had dysuria and frequency for one day. She has no fever or loin pain and has had no UTIs in the last year. Which management is most consistent with NICE guidance?
- [ ] A 14-day course of ciprofloxacin
- [ ] Urine culture and no treatment until the result is available
- [x] A 3-day course of an appropriate antibiotic such as nitrofurantoin, or a back-up prescription with self-care advice if symptoms are mild
- [ ] Ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder
::: solution
This is uncomplicated lower UTI in a non-pregnant woman. NICE recommends a 3-day course of nitrofurantoin (if kidney function allows) or trimethoprim (if resistance risk is low), or for mild symptoms a back-up prescription with self-care advice, because many infections resolve without antibiotics. Culture is not routinely needed. Quinolones are reserved for specific situations because of adverse effects and resistance, and imaging is not needed for a single uncomplicated infection.

::: history
For most of history kidney stones were removed by open surgery, and "cutting for the stone" was one of the oldest and most dangerous operations. Shockwave lithotripsy changed this. Engineers at the German aerospace company Dornier noticed that shockwaves could damage materials, and with urologists at the University of Munich they developed a machine that focused shockwaves on a stone from outside the body. In February 1980 Christian Chaussy and colleagues treated the first patient, who sat in a water bath while the stone was broken into fragments small enough to pass. Within a decade lithotripsy and endoscopic techniques had made open stone surgery rare.
:::

::: summary
- Most UTIs are ascending; *E. coli* with adhesive fimbriae causes most. Risk factors include female anatomy, pregnancy, incomplete emptying, catheters, stones and structural abnormalities.
- Diagnose lower UTI in women under 65 from symptoms with or without a dipstick; do not use dipsticks in people over 65 or with catheters. Culture in men, pregnancy, children and complicated infection.
- NICE: 3 days of treatment for uncomplicated lower UTI in non-pregnant women, 7 days in men and pregnancy; treat asymptomatic bacteriuria only in pregnancy and before urological procedures.
- An infected obstructed kidney is a urological emergency requiring decompression.
- Most stones are calcium oxalate. Renal colic: CT within 24 hours, NSAIDs first-line, alpha-blockers for distal stones under 10 mm. Prevent recurrence with high fluid intake, less salt and normal dietary calcium.
- Benign prostatic enlargement causes voiding and storage symptoms, acute and chronic retention; high-pressure chronic retention causes bilateral hydronephrosis and post-obstructive diuresis after catheterisation.
- Painless visible haematuria in adults needs investigation for urological cancer; ADPKD is the commonest inherited cause of kidney failure.
:::

(a) Lower, uncomplicated. (b) Lower UTI in a man is complicated by definition; fever raises the possibility of prostatitis or pyelonephritis. (c) Upper (pyelonephritis), complicated by pregnancy; she needs admission and intravenous treatment. (d) Catheter-associated UTI, possibly with urosepsis, complicated; the catheter is usually changed and cultures taken from the new catheter before antibiotics.

::: exercise Why nitrite? {level=1}
Explain the biochemical basis of the nitrite dipstick test and give two reasons why it can be negative in a genuine UTI.
::: solution
Many Gram-negative uropathogens, including *E. coli*, reduce nitrate (from the diet) in the urine to nitrite, which the dipstick detects. It can be negative when (1) the organism does not reduce nitrate, such as *Enterococcus* or *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*; (2) the urine has not stayed in the bladder long enough (about four hours) for conversion, as with frequent voiding in cystitis; or (3) dietary nitrate is low or the urine is very dilute.
:::
:::

::: exercise Choose the stone treatment {level=1}
For each, state the most appropriate initial approach: (a) a 4 mm stone at the vesicoureteric junction, pain controlled; (b) an 8 mm stone in the proximal ureter with uncontrolled pain after 48 hours; (c) a 6 mm ureteric stone with hydronephrosis, fever and hypotension.
::: solution
(a) Watchful waiting with analgesia (NSAID), with or without an alpha-blocker for a distal stone; most stones this size pass. (b) Active treatment, such as ureteroscopy or shockwave lithotripsy; spontaneous passage is unlikely and pain is uncontrolled. (c) Emergency decompression (nephrostomy or stent) with antibiotics and resuscitation; definitive stone treatment later.
:::

::: exercise Predictive value of a dipstick {level=2 check="round(0.3*0.5/(0.3*0.5+0.7*0.05)*100)/100"}
In a group of women with urinary symptoms, the prevalence of UTI is 30%. A nitrite test has an illustrative sensitivity of 50% and specificity of 95%. Calculate the positive predictive value of a positive nitrite, as a decimal rounded to two places.
::: hint
Out of 1000 women, count true positives and false positives.
:::
::: solution
Out of 1000 women: 300 have UTI, 150 of whom test positive (true positives). Of the 700 without UTI, 5% test positive: 35 false positives. PPV $= 150/(150 + 35) = 0.81$. A positive nitrite makes UTI likely. But half of the women with UTI test negative, so a negative nitrite, in the face of typical symptoms, does not exclude infection.
:::

::: exercise Interpret the hydronephrosis {level=2}
A 62-year-old woman treated with radiotherapy for cervical cancer five years ago has a creatinine that has risen over six months from 80 to 190 µmol/L. Ultrasound shows bilateral hydronephrosis with an empty bladder. What does the empty bladder tell you, and what are the likely causes?
::: solution
Bilateral hydronephrosis with an empty bladder places the obstruction **above the bladder**, affecting both ureters. Likely causes are **recurrent pelvic malignancy** compressing the ureters, **radiation-induced ureteric stricture or fibrosis**, or retroperitoneal fibrosis. CT is needed to define the cause. Relief of obstruction with nephrostomies or ureteric stents may preserve kidney function, and decisions are made with her, considering the underlying diagnosis and her goals of care.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpret a PSA {level=2}
A 66-year-old man asks for a PSA test after a friend's diagnosis. His PSA is 6.2 ng/mL. Two weeks earlier he had a UTI. Discuss the interpretation and the next steps.
::: solution
PSA can be raised by prostate cancer, benign enlargement, infection or inflammation, recent ejaculation, vigorous exercise such as cycling, and instrumentation. A recent UTI is a common cause of a transiently raised PSA, so the test should be repeated after the infection has resolved (often after about six weeks). If PSA remains above the age-specific referral threshold, he is referred to urology, where **multiparametric MRI** is used to decide whether a biopsy is needed. PSA testing in men without symptoms involves a balance of possible benefit (earlier detection of significant cancer) and harm (false positives, overdiagnosis of slow-growing cancers and treatment side effects), which should be discussed with him before testing ([[public-health/screening]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Recurrent UTI in a child {level=3}
A 2-year-old girl has her second febrile UTI in six months; the first grew an atypical organism and she was slow to respond to treatment. Explain why children with UTI are investigated differently from adults, and the purpose of the imaging tests used.
::: solution
In young children, febrile UTI more often involves the kidney, and recurrent pyelonephritis can cause **renal scarring**, which can lead later to hypertension and CKD. UTI in a young child may also be the first sign of a **structural abnormality**, especially **vesicoureteric reflux** (urine flowing back up the ureter during voiding) or obstruction. Following NICE NG224, atypical or recurrent infection in a young child prompts imaging: **ultrasound** during or soon after the infection to look for obstruction and structural abnormalities; a **DMSA scan** some months later to detect scarring; and, in selected young children, a **micturating cystourethrogram** to show reflux. Management includes prompt treatment of future infections, attention to constipation and voiding habits, and specialist follow-up. Older children and adults with a single uncomplicated infection do not need this.
:::
:::

::: exercise Polycystic kidney disease in the family {level=3}
A 38-year-old man with hypertension is found to have enlarged kidneys with many cysts on ultrasound. His father started dialysis at 54. Discuss the diagnosis, the implications for him and his family, and the principles of management.
::: solution
**Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease**, most likely caused by a *PKD1* variant given the early kidney failure in his father. Each of his children has a 50% chance of inheriting it.

**For him**: control of blood pressure (an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker), healthy weight, a moderate salt intake and generous water intake; monitoring of kidney function; assessment for rapid progression (using kidney volume on imaging and the rate of eGFR decline) to decide whether **tolvaptan** is appropriate, with liver monitoring; management of pain, cyst bleeding and infection; and screening for **intracranial aneurysm** if there is a family history of aneurysm or subarachnoid haemorrhage. Planning for kidney failure, including transplantation, starts early.

**For the family**: genetic counselling. Testing of adult relatives, including children when they become adults, is a personal decision with implications for insurance and for living kidney donation: a relative considering donating to him must be shown not to have ADPKD ([[genetics/genetic-testing]]).
:::
