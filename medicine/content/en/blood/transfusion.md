Transfusion medicine is immunology practised at the bedside at maximum tempo: two antigens (ABO and Rh) and a score of others (Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNS, Lewis) arranged in Landsteiner's law, a vascular bed that reveals mismatch in minutes, and a 1901–1940s discovery sequence (the groups, the antigen–antibody reaction, citrate storage, the wartime blood bank) that underwrites every operation, every obstetric haemorrhage and every leukaemia induction. Modern practice adds a second half to the art: **blood is a scarce, risky, donated resource, and the discipline is as much about not transfusing as about transfusing safely** — patient blood management being the chapter's evidence-based second theme.

This chapter covers blood groups and antibody mechanics, component therapy and compatibility testing, transfusion reactions, haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, and the safety and stewardship systems.

## Groups and antibodies

::: definition The ABO and Rh systems {#def-abo}
**ABO** is the clinically dominant system because its antibodies are **naturally occurring**: the **Landsteiner rule** — individuals make IgM (and IgG) against the A and B carbohydrate antigens (the H-antigen precursor modified by A/B glycosyltransferases, the Bombay phenotype's anti-H being the rare exception that teaches the rule) **they lack**, without visible infection, presumably stimulated by cross-reactive microbial and food antigens: group O has **anti-A and anti-B**, group A has anti-B, group B anti-A, group AB neither — which is why the **major crossmatch** (donor reds against recipient plasma) is the life-critical orientation and why **group O red cells are the emergency universal donor** while **AB plasma (lacking both antibodies) is the plasma universal donor** — the mirror-image rule that makes a single unit of group-O whole blood unsuitable for an AB recipient. **Rh(D)** and the rest of the systems (Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNS, Lewis, Lutheran) generally require **immune (IgG) antibodies** from exposure (transfusion or pregnancy — the anamnestic, delayed, extravascular-haemolysis pattern of Kidd's anti-Jkᵃ and the transfusion-practice reason the **antibody screen is repeated on samples ≤3 days old** in potentially exposed patients). The clinical architecture that follows: **group and antibody screen** (the screen cell panel), **identification** (the panel and enzyme and thiol studies), **crossmatch or electronic issue** (where the screen is negative and the computer checks identity and history), the **direct antiglobulin test** (the Coombs that reads antibody or complement already on the cell — [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]] and [[blood/lymphoma-myeloma]]'s shared tool), and the emergency protocol (**O-negative red cells and AB plasma while the laboratory works, with the group-specific switch as soon as typing is safe**) — the whole system staffed by the one fact that **ABO-incompatible red cells cause immediate intravascular haemolysis** while IgG-coated cells are **splenic macrophage victims hours to days later** (extravascular — the delayed haemolytic reaction with its unexplained falling count and mild jaundice).
:::

## Components and the physiology of storage

::: definition What actually gets transfused {#def-components}
Whole blood separates into therapies with different shelf-biologies: **red cells** (additive-solution, 42 days at 4 °C; the **storage lesion** — ATP and 2,3-BPG fall (the dissociation curve's left shift that resolves over hours post-transfusion), membrane vesiculation and potassium leak (the reason rapid massive neonatal transfusion uses fresh cells and why hyperkalaic cardiac-surgery patients' red cells are washed)); **platelets** (apheresis units — one donor's therapeutic dose — stored **room temperature with continuous agitation for 5–7 days**: the bacterial-growth risk that makes platelets the most common transfusion-transmitted infection and drives pathogen-reduction technology and the storage-versus-sepsis trade); **plasma** (FFP frozen at ≤72 hours preserving labile factors V and VIII, thawed at 37 °C — the volume and citrate load of the [[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]-reversal question; and **cryoprecipitate** — the fibrinogen, von Willebrand, factor VIII and factor XIII fraction, the fibrinogen-replacement workhorse of [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s obstetric DIC); **granulocytes** (rare, niche, with the lung reactions of their lymphocyte content); and the **special-processings** that are physiology-as-prescription: **leucodepletion** (universal in developed banks — HLA alloimmunisation, febrile reactions and leucocyte-tropic virus (CMV) transmission reduced to "CMV-safe"), **irradiation** (against **transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease** — donor lymphocytes engrafting in the immunocompromised or HLA-homozygous-relative transfusion, near-always fatal, prevented by 25 Gy: the indication list — intrauterine and neonatal, purine-analogue and stem-cell patients, directed donations from relatives, HLA-matched products), **washing** (removes plasma protein — the anaphylaxis-prone IgA-deficient patient's route), and **volume-reduced/washed** for the IgA-deficient and severe-allergy histories — each processing a direct answer to a reaction in [[#def-reactions]].
:::

## Reactions and their mechanisms

::: definition The transfusion reaction differential {#def-reactions}
Sorted by the hour and the mechanism: **acute haemolytic transfusion reaction** (minutes — usually **ABO incompatibility from a clerical/bedside error**: fever, flank pain, hypotension, haemoglobinuria, DIC and the rare early death — the management algorithm that begins **stop the transfusion, keep the line with saline, check the tags and sample, direct antiglobulin test, and support the circulation and urine output**, the "most common serious error in hospital medicine" and the reason every barcode-verification step exists); **febrile non-haemolytic reaction** (cytokine accumulation in the stored product and recipient antibodies to donor leucocytes — the diagnosis of exclusion leucodepletion made rarer; paracetamol, and the work-up that must still exclude haemolysis and bacteria); **allergic and anaphylactic** (uriticaria to plasma protein; **anaphylaxis in the IgA-deficient patient with anti-IgA** — washed cells and minimal-plasma products, the adrenaline and airway response); **TRALI** (donor anti-HLA/HNA antibodies — often from multiparous donors, hence the male-only plasma policies — neutrophil priming in the recipient lung and **non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema within 6 hours with hypotension and fever**: supportive, no diuretic benefit, the diagnosis that must be separated from its mimic); **TACO** (the hydrostatic counterpart — volume and rate, hypertension, natriuretic peptide, radiographic congestion: **diuretics and slower rates**, the harm of the two diagnoses being opposite, which is why the algorithm demands BNP/echo assessment; the elderly cardiac and renal patients and the ICU bolus practice are its epidemiology); **bacterial contamination/sepsis** (platelets above all — rigors, shock, the gram stain of the bag and the urgent culture of patient and unit); **delayed haemolytic and serological reactions** (days — anamnestic IgG, unexplained post-transfusion anaemia and jaundice, the Kidd and Duffy systems' specialty, and the haemoglobinopathy patient's allo- plus auto-antibody minefield); **transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease** (days–weeks, rash, diarrhoea, pancytopenia, >90% fatal — the irradiation indication of [[#def-components]]); and the **long-tail hazards** — iron overload in the chronic transfused ([[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]'s chelation), transfusion-transmitted infection (HIV, HBV, HCV and syphilis by donor selection plus serology and **nucleic-acid testing**, malaria and babesiosis travel deferrals, the theoretical vCJD — each layer the history of one 1980s scandal), and the **immunomodulation** literature (the older "fewer infections with leucodepleted/autologous blood" observations now largely explained by the restrictive-transfusion physiology below). The unifying bedside rule: **any new sign during or just after any blood product — fever, hypoxia, hypotension, dark urine, rash — is the transfusion until the reactions are excluded.**
:::

::: example The dark urine on the table {#ex-ahtr}
Twenty minutes into the second unit of red cells at laparotomy, the anaesthetist notes oozing from the wounds, the catheter urine is cola-coloured, the patient becomes hypotensive and the monitor shows rising peak airway pressures; the surgeon asks what to do.
::: solution
**Acute haemolytic transfusion reaction until proven otherwise** — under general anaesthesia the classical triad is hidden and these are its surrogates: **unexplained hypotension, bronchospasm/rising airway pressure, diffuse bleeding (the consumptive coagulopathy of thromboplastin-like granule release) and dark urine (haemoglobinuria)**. The algorithm, memorised: **stop the transfusion immediately** (the rest of the unit and the giving set go back to the laboratory — the bag and the patient's sample are the medicolegal and clinical evidence); **maintain IV access and give saline/volume to support the circulation and urine output (fluid boluses, then low-dose catecholamine support as far as the surgery allows)**; **check the clerical chain (tag, wristband, sample) and send the post-reaction sample for group repeat, antibody screen, direct antiglobulin test, free haemoglobin, haptoglobin, fibrinogen, platelets, creatinine and potassium**; **communicate** — the blood bank freezes other components for the same patient and starts the lookback; and **treat the two effectors**: DIC (products per [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s targets) and the haemoglobinuric kidney (volume, avoid the nephrotoxic hit — the anaesthetic corollary of maintaining blood pressure over accepting a lower one during the case). The most common cause is not the laboratory but the bed-side: **wrong blood into the patient** — the barcode and two-identifier verification policies, and the "never transfuse a unit that does not match the wristband, and never ignore the stop" being the transfusion-safety equivalent of the surgical checklist ([[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]).
:::
:::

::: example The count that fell a week later {#ex-delayed}
Ten days after an uncomplicated transfusion for post-partum anaemia, a woman returns jaundiced and breathless; Hb has fallen below its post-transfusion peak, reticulocytes are rising, LDH 900, haptoglobin absent, DAT now positive with anti-Jkᵇ identified; the first transfusion work-up had been antibody-screen-negative.
::: solution
**Delayed haemolytic transfusion reaction** — the anamnestic response of [[#def-abo]] in textbook form: prior pregnancy (or transfusion) immunised her to Kidd (Jkᵇ); the titre decayed **below the screen's detection limit**, the re-exposure of transfusion drove an IgG rise over days, and antibody-coated donor (and, in the Kidd world, sometimes her own cross-reacting? — her own cells are Jkᵇ-negative — donor-cell destruction alone) cells were cleared by splenic macrophages — hence the jaundice, LDH and dropped count at day 5–14. The Kidd system's two notorious properties are the case: ** titres that fade to undetectability (the negative screen was truthful, not useless) and fixation of complement on the red cell (the potential for brisk — even intravascular — haemolysis and the rare acute-kidney picture)**. Management: **stop further electives, identify any still-circulating incompatible units' effect, support, transfuse Jkᵇ-negative when needed, and document and counsel** — the antibody is now lifelong, the identification goes on the record and the card, every future laboratory must find it in advance, and the **antenatal implication** for the next pregnancy (the [[#def-hdfn]] surveillance now includes Kidd as a genuine cause of HDFN, less severe than D but real). The epidemiology to quote at the morbidity meeting: most serious delayed reactions follow **clinically indicated but marginal** transfusions — the stewardship argument of [[#def-pbm]] written in one patient's jaundice.
:::

::: quiz
A group O-negative trauma patient receives emergency uncrossmatched blood. The correct product choice and reasoning:
- [ ] Group O positive red cells — Rh status does not matter in shock
- [x] Group O negative (or O positive in males and non-childbearing patients per some adult protocols) red cells — no A, B or D antigens to be attacked by preformed recipient antibody
- [ ] Group AB red cells — universal donor
- [ ] Group O plasma — contains the protective antibodies
::: solution
Group **O red cells carry no A or B antigen**, and the **O negative (D-negative)** choice removes the dominant immunising D antigen — the emergency-universal red cell ([[#def-abo]]); note the modern policy debate: **O positive red cells are acceptable for adult males and non-childbearing patients in some services to conserve O negative stocks for childbearing-age women and intrauterine/neonatal use**, since primary D immunisation's consequence is a future pregnancy. **AB plasma** (no anti-A/anti-B) is the universal plasma, the mirror rule; group O whole blood (the military and major-haemorrhage re-supply option) is a deliberate compromise — its plasma carries anti-A and anti-B — acceptable in exsanguinating adults with the group known only as unknown, tracked by the major-incident protocols of [[emergency-critical-care/acute-assessment]].
:::

::: example The first dose that closed the throat {#ex-anaphylaxis}
Seconds after the first millilitres of plasma a patient develops stridor, wheeze, diarrhoea and cardiovascular collapse with minimal fever; she has had two prior transfusions, both previously uneventful, and a history of recurrent chest infections.
::: solution
**Anaphylaxis in the IgA-deficient patient with anti-IgA antibody** — the classical mechanism of [[#def-reactions]]: selective IgA deficiency (the commonest immunodeficiency, often silent apart from sinobronchial infections and autoimmunity, [[immunology/immunodeficiency]]'s epidemiology) exposes **anti-IgA IgG/IgE** to the plasma protein in ordinary products; prior transfusions sensitised her, and re-exposure triggers mast-cell and basophil degranulation — **urticaria may be absent, collapse arrives with the first millilitres, and the reaction is to plasma protein, not red cells** (hence its appearance with plasma and platelet products as much as red cells). Immediate management is anaphylaxis, not "transfusion was paused": **stop the product, intramuscular adrenaline, airway and oxygen, fluid and supine positioning, antihistamine and steroid as adjuncts**, with the laboratory work-up (serum tryptase timing, **anti-IgA measurement and IgA level**) and — once stabilised — the transfusion plan that prevents recurrence: **washed red cells and platelets (the washing removes the plasma protein — [[#def-components]]'s processing indication), plasma and plasma-containing products only with IgA-deficient donor source or not at all (the FFP question is why frozen/deglycerolised washed-cell strategies are specified in advance), an allergy and immunology referral, the documented card and blood-bank flag, and the premedication-plus-slow-rate protocol when no alternative exists.** The differential the team must still exclude in the same minutes: bacterial contamination (the rigors and shock of the platelet unit), TRALI (hypoxia-predominant, slower), and the anaphylactoid complement-activating product reactions of haptoglobin-deficient patients' plasma — but the IgA story is the one that changes every future prescription.
:::

## Haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn

::: definition The alloimmune pregnancy {#def-hdfn}
The mechanism in one line: **maternal IgG alloantibody (formed against a fetal red-cell antigen inherited from the father — RhD most famously, but anti-c, Kell, Duffy and the rest increasingly in the D-prophylaxis era) crosses the placenta and haemolyses fetal red cells**, producing **anaemia with extramedullary erythropoiesis, hyperbilirubinaemia, and in the severe form hydrops** (the high-output failure, ascites and placentomegaly of [[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]'s hydrops physics in immune rather than globin guise); the neonatal face is the **rising, unconjugated, albumin-threatening jaundice of the first 24 hours** (unlike physiological jaundice's day-2–3 onset) with the **positive cord direct antiglobulin test** and the anaemia that may present hours after birth or at weeks (the Kell-positive "late anaemia" whose reticulocyte response is suppressed — Kell's target antigen on erythroid precursors, an aplastic as well as haemolytic lesion). Prevention is the chapter's greatest public-health win: **anti-D immunoglobulin to the D-negative mother at 28 weeks (or 30/34 by national schedule) and within 72 hours of a sensitising event**, its efficacy (the consensus trials and the population-level fall in HDFN) resting on clearing fetal cells before maternal immunisation; **the dose scales with fetomaternal haemorrhage size — the Kleihauer (acid-elution) or flow-cytometric quantification** (the arithmetic — worked in the example below) ⇒ additional anti-D. Surveillance in the already-alloimmunised: **antibody titres (the critical titre threshold), middle-cerebral-artery peak-systolic-velocity Doppler (the anaemia's viscosity physics — elevated velocity predicts fetal anaemia), and **intrauterine transfusion** (the ultrasound-guided cord tap with packed O-negative, CMV-negative, irradiated, leucodepleted, haematocrit-matched blood — the therapy that turned hydrops from a sentence into a management plan), delivery timing balanced against prematurity, then neonatal phototherapy, IVIG (the Fc-blockade rationale) and exchange transfusion for the hyperbilirubinaemic emergency — the kernicterus thresholds belonging to [[paediatrics/newborn]].
:::

::: example The arithmetic of anti-D {#ex-anti-d}
A D-negative woman delivers a D-positive baby at 38 weeks; 90 minutes later a screening test reports 4% fetal cells among the maternal circulation, and her total red-cell volume is estimated at 2.5 L of blood with 4.5 × 10¹² maternal cells/L.
::: solution
Fetomaternal haemorrhage volume: 4% of roughly (2.5 L × 4.5 × 10¹² cells/L ⇒ fetal cells ≈ 0.04 of that number). Converting counts to volume is imprecise — the laboratory reports the **fetal-cell percentage times maternal red-cell volume**: 0.04 × ~2,500 mL ≈ **100 mL of fetal whole blood** (≈50–60 mL of fetal red cells). The standard dose covers **4 mL of fetal red cells per 125–1500 IU depending on the national convention (UK: 125 IU (25 µg) per 2 mL fetal red cells; a 1,500 IU (300 µg) minidose pattern for larger bleeds)** ⇒ for ~60 mL of fetal red cells: roughly **3,750–4,000 IU, given as 1,500 IU repeated or by protocol-weighted dosing** (the exact figure is laboratory- and guideline-driven — the point is the **proportionality**: larger bleeds need more, and the test must be done on every D-negative delivery). The surrounding rules: **routine 28-week prophylaxis** (the routine antenatal anti-D prophylaxis schedule) covers silent third-trimester bleeds; **within 72 hours** of delivery or sensitising event (the schedule's grace to 7–10 days attenuates but does not abolish benefit); **anti-D is not indicated if the baby is D-negative or if the mother is already alloimmunised** (the antibody screen decides — and the false-positive "anti-D" of recent prophylaxis must be documented against the given dose); **the cell-free-fetal-RhD test has made routine antenatal prophylaxis unnecessary for the D-positive-fetus-negative mothers who do not exist**, targeted prophylaxis now replacing blanket schedules in several national programmes; and the **D-positive transfusion policy for the D-negative mother's own emergency** (transfuse her D-positive blood rather than deplete O negative stocks, accepting the immunisation that prophylaxis — if given early enough — may still blunt: the obstetric-haemorrhage guideline's nuance). The case is Landsteiner's 1901 discovery completing its arc: a century from the group system to the dose calculation that prevents a disease of second pregnancies.
:::
:::

## Stewardship: when (not) to transfuse

::: definition Patient blood management {#def-pbm}
The evidence era (TRICC's restrictive-versus-liberal ICU thresholds, the FOCUS and Villar trials in cardiac and orthopaedic surgery, the SAQRI and TRISS obstetric and trauma series) settles the central question: **a haemoglobin of 70–80 g/L triggers transfusion no more reliably than one of 90–100 in the stable patient — the trigger is physiology (myocardial ischaemia, hypovolaemic shock, ongoing haemorrhage, symptomatic anaemia), not a number** — restrictive strategies reduce allogeneic exposure without harm (and the "liberal is safe" conclusion of a few trials rests on transfusion actually being given, not on a higher set point being beneficial). The programme built on this: **pre-operative** (identify and treat iron deficiency — the [[cell-biochemistry/nutrition]] and [[blood/anaemia]] pathways, the oral-versus-IV decision; hold anticoagulants with bridging logic from [[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]; EPO in selected patients), **intra-operative** (normothermia and acidosis and calcium control — the lethal-triad physiology, cell salvage for the predicted blood loss, tranexamic acid — the CRASH-2 and WOMAN evidence revisited in [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]], surgical technique and haemostatic devices, and the **restrictive-to-physiology anaesthetic plan**), **post-operative and medical** (phlebotomy minimisation — the iatrogenic-blood-loss audit of every icteric inpatient chart, oral-iron and EPO-in-CKD strategies, the transfusion-appropriate-care documentation that made "indications written" a clinical-standard indicator, and the **single-unit-then-reassess rule** for the stable ward patient). The blood-conservation endpoints are safety in both directions: the un-transfused bleeder dies of shock, the over-transfused of TACO, TRALI, reaction and cost — with the **religious-objector programme** (bloodless-surgery pathways, albumin and factor alternatives, the cell-salvage and EPO acceptance discussions) the chapter's ethics-clinic appendix. The system layer: **haemovigilance** reporting (the serious-hazard-of-transfusion and haemovigilance-bureau schemes — the incident-rate dashboards that drove bedside barcode verification), **donor selection and testing** (the questionnaires and pathogen algorithms, NAT minipool testing, platelet pathogen reduction, the deferral-policy debates), **inventory** (outdating, emergency release and the major-incident panel), and the **traceability from vein to vein** that makes the lookback and the adverse-event investigation possible: the whole infrastructure behind the chapter's simplest clinical sentence — **transfuse for the patient, not the number; and treat every reaction as the transfusion until proven otherwise.**
:::

::: history
**Karl Landsteiner's** 1901 discovery of the human blood groups (the Nobel of 1930, with Decastello and Sturli's AB of 1902), **Ottenberg's** 1907 first transfusion and **Hustin and Brinkman's** typing-era practice, **Robert Robertson's** 1918 citrate and cold-storage of stored blood, **Norman Bethune's** 1932–36 mobile blood-bus service and the wartime plasma and whole-blood systems (Chatelain's lyophilised plasma, the North Africa landings), **Landsteiner and Wiener's** 1940 monkey-antiserum definition of the Rh factor (the Nobel-honoured work of Levine and Rosenfeld's haemolytic-disease mechanism), and **Clarke, Finn, Mourant, Race and Taylor's** 1940s–60s Liverpool–Bristol–London programme that took anti-D prophylaxis from the volunteer trials to national policy — the intervention that ended Rh disease as a common cause of infant death. The reaction nosology followed: **Mollison's** red-cell storage and compatibility textbook (the "Blood Transfusion in Clinical Medicine" generations), the 1970s TRALI recognition (Popovsky and Sachs's antibody studies following the Britton, Engelfriet and Korst series), the **Slichter and Aster** platelet studies and the apheresis era, and the modern restrictive-transfusion trials and haemovigilance systems completing the arc from Landsteiner's test-tube to the safety dashboard.
:::

## Where this leads

The immune mechanisms in [[immunology/antigen-recognition]] and [[immunology/hypersensitivity]]; the haemoglobinopathies' transfusion medicine in [[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]; obstetric haemorrhage management in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]] and [[emergency-critical-care/acute-assessment]]; the perioperative programmes in the anaesthesia and surgical chapters; iron and EPO therapies in [[blood/anaemia]] and [[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]].

::: summary
- ABO works because its antibodies are naturally occurring IgM against microbial-templated carbohydrates — Landsteiner's rule fixes the donor–recipient compatibility matrix (O red cells and AB plasma universal, and the mirror logic of emergency release); the other systems cause disease through immune IgG (delayed, extravascular, pregnancy-driven).
- Components are storage-physiology products — red cells at 4 °C with a storage lesion, platelets at room temperature with a bacterial risk, FFP and cryoprecipitate for factor and fibrinogen replacement — and the special processings (leucodepletion, irradiation, washing) each prevent one named reaction.
- Acute reactions are mechanical-clerical (wrong blood), immune (haemolysis, TRALI, allergy/anaphylaxis), circulatory (TACO), infective or immunologic-late (TA-GvHD, iron, alloimmunisation); the bedside algorithm is stop, verify, support, and report.
- Haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn is maternal IgG against fetal antigens — preventable by anti-D dosed to the measured fetomaternal haemorrhage, and manageable by Doppler surveillance and intrauterine transfusion in the alloimmunised.
- Restrictive, physiology-triggered transfusion is the evidence default, and patient blood management — pre-, intra- and post-operative — is now half of the discipline, supported by haemovigilance and donor-testing systems that make the other half safe.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Grouping logic {level=1}
Cells agglutinate with anti-A and anti-B; serum agglutinates A and B cells but not O cells. Resolve the apparent conflict and name the phenomenon to test for.
::: solution
Forward type = group **AB**; serum should contain **no anti-A or anti-B** — agglutination of both reagent cells suggests either **extra antibodies (anti-H, cold autoagglutinin, rouleaux or a positive control problem)** or — classically — **an acquired-B phenotype** (group A deacetylation by gut bacterial enzymes in sepsis and colorectal disease mimicking B) or, in the reverse direction, a **missing/weak reaction suggesting the Bombay (anti-H) or hypogammaglobulinaemic state**. Resolution: repeat with washed cells, examine the saline control, test saliva for ABH substances and check anti-H; the exam version: **forward/reverse discrepancies demand explanation before issue, never a transfusion on the "likely" group.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Why three days {level=1}
Why must the group-and-screen sample for a potentially pregnant or multiply-transfused patient be no older than 72 hours, and why may the trauma laboratory issue without any sample at all?
::: solution
The sample's job is to prove **current** absence of alloantibody: within days of transfusion or pregnancy a **anamnestic IgG rise can be below detection while the memory exists** — the delayed-haemolysis setup of [[#def-abo]] (and the reason Kidd antibodies' reputation: titres fall to undetectable then explode on re-exposure). Hence the 72-hour rule for the exposed population and electronic-issue rules requiring a **documented negative screen in the recent past**. The trauma exception is a risk calculus, not an immunology: **uncrossmatched O (D-negative or protocol-selected) red cells and AB plasma carry no recipient-antibody risk** (no ABO-compatible donor antigens present to react) — the risk accepted instead is the **D immunisation** and the wasted group-O stock, the policy trade-offs written into major-incident guidance.
:::
:::

::: exercise TRALI versus TACO {level=2}
One hour after two units of FFP a patient has SpO₂ 84%, bilateral infiltrates, temperature 38.5 °C, BP 85/50, JVP not raised, BNP mildly raised; another patient on the same night has the same hypoxaemia and infiltrates with BP 170/95, JVP to the angle of the jaw and BNP 1,400. Manage each and justify from the mechanisms.
::: solution
**First = TRALI** (fever, **hypotension**, no volume overload, donor-antibody neutrophil priming in the pulmonary microvasculature — non-cardiogenic permeability oedema): **stop transfusion, sit up, oxygen, ventilation as needed (the Lung-protective strategy of ARDS physiology — [[respiratory/respiratory-failure]]'s medicine), fluids judiciously for the hypotension, pressors, and no diuretics (the patient is not wet — the harm of the wrong label)**, with the laboratory reporting the unit for donor antibody testing (the implicated donor is then withdrawn — multiparous-woman plasma policies exist because of this case). **Second = TACO** (hypertension, distended neck, high BNP, hydrostatic): **stop or slow, upright posture, IV furosemide, oxygen ± non-invasive ventilation, and rate-and-volume recalibration for subsequent products** (smaller units, diuretic cover between, the elderly-cardiac and renal patients being the epidemiology). The discriminator at the bedside is **blood pressure and JVP more than the radiograph** — both can show oedema and both can have pleural effusions — and the reminder that both cases require the **transfusion to be stopped and the episode reported**: the haemovigilance denominator only fills if the nurses and doctors ring the blood bank.
:::
:::

::: exercise The difficult crossmatch {level=2}
A warm-autoimmune-haemolytic-anaemia patient (Hb 45, angina, DAT strongly IgG-positive, pan-reactive antibody screen) needs urgent surgery. Explain "least incompatible", the laboratory's work, and the clinical decisions.
::: solution
The autoantibody reacts with **every** donor cell (the Rh-core common determinant — warm AIHA antibodies are usually pan-reactive), so **a truly compatible crossmatch does not exist**; the laboratory **removes the autoantibody by absorption (elution/differential) to unmask any underlying alloantibody (10–30% coexist — the Kell, Duffy, Kidd ones that will cause real reactions)**, types around the coating (genotyping or pre-treatment elution), and issues **"least incompatible" units** — units whose crossmatch is no worse than the auto-baseline. Clinically: **transfuse anyway if the patient is ischaemic or bleeding (the fear of giving blood does not lower the reaction risk it warns about — withholding oxygen-carrying capacity kills faster than alloimmunisation)**, do it slowly with monitoring and the senior haematologist and laboratory director informed, add **steroids (and rituximab for the disease-modifying line — [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s AIHA ladder)** so the transfused cells survive longer, and document that the antiglobulin test will remain positive and that **post-transfusion "compatibility assessment" is serologically uninterpretable** — the same interpretive discipline applies to the recently transfused and to the multiply-alloimmunised sickle-cell patient's extended-matched units ([[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]'s programme).
:::
:::

::: exercise Who needs irradiated blood {level=2}
List the indications for irradiated components and the mechanism each prevents, and identify the one transfusion scenario in which even full-dose irradiation is judged unnecessary despite theoretical risk.
::: solution
**Irradiation (25 Gy) prevents donor-lymphocyte engraftment — transfusion-associated GVHD**, near-always-fatal (rash, diarrhoea, liver injury, marrow aplasia 4–30 days after transfusion), which occurs when donor T cells see no foreign HLA in the recipient: the indications — **intrauterine and previous intrauterine-transfusion infants, prematurity and exchange transfusion, congenital cellular-immunity defects and thymic transplant (DiGeorge), allogeneic and some autologous HSCT recipients and purine-analogue therapy (fludarabine, cladribine, clofarabine — the classic board list), HLA-matched and directed donations from blood relatives** (the shared-haplotype logic: even an immunocompetent recipient cannot reject a half-matched donor lymphocyte population). Cells irradiated last up to 28 days' storage (or until original expiry) and platelets are irradiated as routine in many neonatal banks. The "unnecessary despite theory" case: **component-type risk of non-lymphocyte products — plasma, cryoprecipitate and (per most guidelines) washed/frozen-thawed red cells are not irradiated, and fresh whole blood in the military/mass-casualty setting is the acknowledged residual-risk exception handled by donor selection (male-only, never-received-transfusion) rather than irradiation**, the operational compromise when 25 Gy is unavailable in the field.
:::

::: exercise Platelet refractoriness {level=2}
Two hours after each of two pools a myelodysplasia patient's count rises by 2 × 10⁹/L instead of 40; she has had thirty transfusions, is febrile, on amphotericin, with an enlarged spleen. Separate the mechanisms and give the work-up and options.
::: solution
**Refractoriness divides into consumption/sequestration (the common causes — fever and sepsis, amphotericin B, splenomegaly, DIC, active bleeding — all present in this stem) and immune alloantibody (HLA class I — HLA antibodies from pregnancy and prior transfusion, less often platelet-specific antigens like HPAs)**: the **corrected count increment or PRA at 10 minutes to 1 hour** (a poor 10-minute increment points to immune; a good 10-minute increment with a poor 24-hour one to consumption) and the **HLA antibody screen (solid-phase assay)**. The work-up and management: treat the sepsis and drug causes first (they are the majority); if immune — **HLA/HPA antibody identification and antigen-negative or crossmatch-selected platelets (the referral-laboratory service), ABO-matched products and avoidance of unnecessary transfusion; and the clinical-bleeding threshold rather than a prophylactic number when the patient is refractory** (the practical floor of the case), remembering that prophylactic platelet thresholds (10, higher with fever/anticoagulation/procedures) exist to prevent spontaneous bleeds and cannot be met in a fully consumption-driven patient — the haematologist's conversation with such patients is about bleeding risk, not the count, which is [[geriatrics-palliative]] and haematology-ethics material as much as transfusion-medicine technique.
:::

::: exercise The religious objector's laparotomy {level=2}
A Jehovah's Witness patient with a bowel cancer declines allogeneic blood. Write the perioperative blood-management plan across the three phases and the document that makes it enforceable.
::: solution
**Pre-operative**: the detailed, documented discussion (what she will and will not accept — most Witnesses accept **fractions (albumin, immunoglobulin, clotting factors; cryoprecipitate is a personal decision) and refuse the primary components (whole blood, red cells, platelets, plasma); cell salvage is a personal conscience decision (many accept it with the continuous-circuit modification)**), iron studies and repletion (oral or IV iron — the [[blood/anaemia]] arithmetic — with EPO where the oncology plan allows), vitamin K and folate/B₁₂ review, the **advance decision/lasting power of attorney and the hospital's Witness-liaison/chaplaincy involvement**, and anaesthesia and surgical planning (the senior surgeon, the day's position in theatre, arterial-line and point-of-care testing availability). **Intra-operative**: meticulous haemostasis (energy devices, topical haemostats, the normothermia–acidosis–calcium discipline of [[#def-pbm]]), **acute normovolaemic haemodilution where acceptable, cell salvage with the anticoagulated continuous circuit, low-CVP technique for hepatic and spinal work, tranexamic acid**, and restrictive ventilatory and fluid targets (the crystalloid-sparing, factor-and-fibrinogen-viscoelastic guidance of [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]). **Post-operative**: phlebotomy minimisation with paediatric tubes and point-of-care, aggressive iron/EPO, oxygen delivery optimisation (cardiac output, haemoglobin's oxygen carriage, the mixed-venous monitoring), avoidance of antiplatelet/anticoagulant unless essential, and the HDU-level thresholds for re-intervention. The **legally valid advance decision refusing transfusion (clear, specific, voluntary, applicable — the Mental Capacity Act's documentation standard, or jurisdictional equivalent)**, countersigned and carried in the notes and shared with the on-call team before the patient is anaesthetised, is the enforceable document; the corollary ethical rules (capacity assessment at the time of refusal, the pregnant patient's complex jurisprudence, the child's overriding best-interests doctrine) belong to [[medical-ethics-law]].
:::

::: exercise Design the massive-transfusion protocol {level=3}
Specify a hospital massive-transfusion protocol for major obstetric haemorrhage: trigger, product ratios and targets, laboratory support, adjuncts and audit measures — each element justified by the physiology in this chapter and in [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]].
::: solution
**Trigger**: clinical — major bleeding with haemodynamic compromise not responding to initial resuscitation, activation by the obstetric haemorrhage cart/bell and the **two-clinician call-in**, the laboratory notified in the first minutes (the group and screen from admission antenatally is the [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]] policy that makes the first minute possible). **Products**: 1:1:1-era fixed-ratio (red cells plus FFP plus platelets — the 6:1:1 comparison's earlier haemostasis and PROPP-ART-era survival trend, with the fibrinogen-first correction: **cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate to >1.5–2 g/L, the obstetric population's fastest fibrinogen fall** — the pregnancy's supraphysiological fibrinogen baseline falling into hypofibrinogenaemia as the earliest [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]] abnormality), **platelet targets >50 (some obstetric protocols >75 with ongoing bleeding)**, **TXA within three hours (the WOMAN evidence)**, **calcium monitored and replaced (the citrate-load hypocalcaemia the anaesthetist must not mistake for failure to resuscitate)**, normothermia (fluid warmers, forced-air, the lethal-triad physiology), and **viscoelastic-guided escalation after the empirical first round** (the ROTEM/TEG-guided obstetric algorithms' red-cell-and-product reduction in the trial series). **Laboratory**: the major-incident sample set (FBC, coagulation and fibrinogen, biochemistry with calcium and lactate, gas, cell count for the Kleihauer where relevant), **repeat at protocol-defined unit intervals**, **post-protocol iron and antibody follow-up**, and for the D-negative mother the anti-D arithmetic of [[#ex-anti-d]]. **Audit measures**: time to first product, ratio delivered, fibrinogen on arrival at ICU, calcium and temperature, hysterectomy and ICU rates, red-cell units per case, reactions and transfusion-appropriate-care documentation, and the **simulation programme** (the Motherhood-trial-era evidence that the protocol's value is rehearsal as much as ratio) — the complete example of transfusion physiology becoming systems design.
:::
