Anaemia — a reduction in red-cell mass below the level needed for oxygen delivery, operationally a haemoglobin below the range of the healthy population (13–17 g/dL in men, 12–15 in women, less in children and pregnancy) — is not a diagnosis but a finding with a mechanism, and the mechanism is usually visible in three numbers: the **mean corpuscular volume (MCV)**, the **reticulocyte count** and the **blood film**. Add iron studies, B₁₂ and folate, and the haemolysis screen, and the differential of the world's commonest haematological problem organises itself into a flowchart that begins in the phlebotomy chair and rarely needs more.

This chapter covers red-cell production and its measurement, the classification of anaemia, iron physiology, folate and B₁₂, the haemolytic anaemias, and the anaemias of disease.

## Classification and the first tests

::: definition The physiological axes of classification {#def-class}
Anaemia arises when production falls, destruction or loss rises, or both; the laboratory axes: **MCV** (normocytic 80–100 fL; microcytic — the haemoglobinisation problems: iron deficiency, thalassaemia, the anaemia of inflammation, and rare sideroblastic and copper-deficient states; macrocytic — the megaloblastic DNA-synthesis problems, B₁₂ and folate deficiency, and the large list of non-megaloblastic causes: alcohol, liver disease, reticulocytosis itself, hydroxycarbamide, myelodysplasia), **reticulocyte count** (the production audit — corrected for haematocrit and maturation time as the **reticulocyte production index**: < 2 inappropriately low (production problem), > 3 hyperproliferative (haemolysis or bleeding)), and the **film** (the red-cell morphology that localises the molecular defect: hypochromia and pencil cells in iron deficiency, macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils in megaloblastic change, spherocytes, schistocytes, sickle forms, bite cells — each a diagnosis announcing itself). The **haemoglobin** itself is a poor guide to acuity: a marathon of physiology's themes — plasma volume expansion dilutes (pregnancy's "physiological anaemia" at 11 g/dL is not disease), acute haemorrhage is invisible for 24 hours, and the chronic patient walks on 6 g/dL that would resuscitate at an emergency door.
:::

## Iron: the physiology of a scarce metal

::: definition Iron balance, absorption and hepcidin {#def-iron}
The adult body holds 3–5 g of iron, **two thirds in haemoglobin**, a third as storage ferritin and haemosiderin, a minute transport pool (transferrin-bound, ~3 mg) turning over hourly — and there is **no physiological excretion route**: men lose ~1 mg/day (gut, skin), women 1.5–2 mg/day menstruation-dependent, and iron balance is therefore governed wholly at **absorption**, by the duodenal enterocyte. Fe³⁺ is reduced at the brush border by **duodenal cytochrome b (DcyTB)** and taken up as Fe²⁺ by **DMT1**, stored as ferritin or exported across the basolateral membrane by **ferroportin** — the cell's only iron outlet — and the whole system is commanded by **hepcidin**, the hepatocyte peptide hormone whose message is one line: **hepcidin binds and degrades ferroportin**, so high hepcidin (iron replete, inflammation — IL-6-driven) locks iron inside enterocytes and macrophages, and low hepcidin (iron depleted, hypoxia, erythropoietic drive — via BMP6/SMAD and hemojuvelin signalling) opens the outlet. This one loop explains the era's central clinical facts: **iron deficiency in inflammation** (the anaemia of chronic disease — hepcidin-high, iron sequestered in macrophages, low serum iron with high ferritin, erythropoietin partially resistant because the erythroblast's iron door is shut); **haemochromatosis** (HFE mutations misreading body iron as high, hepcidin inappropriately low, unlimited absorption — the commonest inherited disease of northern Europeans, [[genetics/common-disease-genetics]]'s penetrance lesson); the oral iron supplement's **enterocyte block** (a dose given before the previous one's ferritin has cleared is partly wasted — alternate-day dosing works better in the hepcidin era); and the macrophage's dual life as recycler of senescent red-cell iron and hepcidin's captive, which is why transfused iron (each unit ~200 mg, no excretory escape) accumulates first in reticuloendothelial macrophages and later in parenchymal organs in the chronically transfused thalassaemic — chelation's terrain ([[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]).
:::

::: example The tired donor {#ex-irondef}
A 46-year-old woman donates blood twice yearly, feels breathless at stairs; Hb 104, MCV 74, ferritin 8, transferrin saturation 9%, platelets 480.
::: solution
**Iron-deficiency anaemia, dietary-demand mismatch in a menstruating blood donor** — the arithmetic of a system with no exit: repeated phlebotomy (each unit ~200 mg of iron she cannot re-absorb quickly enough) has emptied the stores (ferritin 8 — the earliest abnormality; <15 μg/L is diagnostic of absent stores, and a ferritin that "looks normal" in inflammation can be a raised false-negative, the acute-phase trap), the transport pool is low (saturation 9% — the marrow's supply line), and the anaemia is **microcytic, hypochromic (the pencil cells) with reactive thrombocytosis** (the commonest raised platelet count in practice — megakaryocytic cross-talk with iron deficiency), with the MCV falling last of the four numbers. The causes must still be named — in her case menstrual loss and donation; in a 70-year-old man or any post-menopausal woman iron deficiency is ** gastrointestinal blood loss until endoscopy says otherwise** (the right colon cancer presenting as fatigue and an MCV of 70 is one of medicine's most consistently missed diagnoses, and the coeliac, *H. pylori*, bariatric-surgery and PPI malabsorption list belongs on the same form). Treatment: oral iron (a dose, with vitamin C, on an empty stomach or alternate morning by the hepcidin physiology — 40–65 mg elemental daily or every other day), reticulocytosis in a week, haemoglobin correction in two months, **then three months of repletion of stores** (ferritin is the endpoint, not the haemoglobin), and intravenous iron (the modern carbohydrate-shell formulations, anaphylaxis now rare) for intolerance, malabsorption or the inflamed gut of IBD — plus the correction of the cause, because iron given back into a bleeding lesion is only a slower diagnosis.
:::
:::
::: example The arthritis patient's haemoglobin {#ex-acd}
A 60-year-old with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis on methotrexate has Hb 100, MCV 92, ferritin 220, serum iron 8, TIBC low-normal, saturation 14%, reticulocytes low, and no bleeding.
::: solution
This is the **anaemia of inflammation** ("anaemia of chronic disease"): IL-6 raises hepatocyte **hepcidin**, ferroportin is degraded on enterocytes and macrophages alike, iron is locked away (low serum iron with **high ferritin** — the opposite pairing to deficiency, with the TIBC that fails to rise as the discriminator), erythropoiesis is iron-restricted and the marrow mildly resistant to EPO, and the anaemia is normo- or microcytic, mild-to-moderate, and reticulocytopenic. Two confounders are present by name: **methotrexate** (a folate antagonist — check for macrocytosis beyond expectation and B₁₂/folate status; the MCV of 92 on methotrexate may be the drug, not "normal") and the possibility of coexistent GI blood loss from NSAIDs (faecal occult testing and the iron-versus-inflammation arithmetic of the exercise table). Management is the mechanism in order: **control the inflammation** (the anaemia improves as the disease does — the erythropoietic effect of successful biologics), correct true iron deficiency if coexists (the hepcidin-high state often needs **intravenous** iron to bypass the enterocyte block — functional iron deficiency is exactly why IV iron and HIF-PH inhibitors were developed for inflammatory anaemias), ESAs only for the symptomatic, CKD-overlap or chemotherapy settings, and the transfusion threshold unchanged by sympathy. The lesson generalises to every chronic inflammatory, infectious and malignant state the next chapters list: **the anaemia of inflammation is not marrow failure but iron quarantine — hepcidin's deliberate, defensive, and sometimes overtreated physiology.**
:::
:::

## Folate and B₁₂: the methylation vitamins

::: definition Megaloblastic erythropoiesis {#def-megaloblastic}
B₁₂ (cobalamin) and folate are both required for **thymidylate synthesis**: without methyl-B₁₂ (as the methionine synthase cofactor), folate is trapped as N5-methyl-THF — the "methyl-folate trap" — and DNA precursor pools collapse while **cytoplasmic protein synthesis continues**, giving the pathognomonic asynchrony: **megaloblastic maturation** — giant metamyelocytes in the marrow, macro-ovalocytes in the blood and **hypersegmented neutrophils** (six lobes — the earliest film sign), with ineffective erythropoiesis (intramedullary apoptosis: the raised LDH and mild indirect bilirubinaemia that can mimic haemolysis) and the same block in every rapidly turning-over epithelium (glossitis, angular stomatitis, gut malabsorption). **B₁₂ handling is the more complex and the more tested physiology**: food-bound cobalamin is released by pepsin, transferred to **R-binder (haptocorrin)**, which is digested by pancreatic protease in the duodenum, freeing B₁₂ onto **intrinsic factor (IF, gastric parietal cells)**; the IF–B₁₂ complex is absorbed in the **terminal ileum** (the resection and Crohn's rule), transported bound to **transcobalamin II** (the delivery one — its congenital absence causes infantile megaloblastic change with normal serum B₁₂, the assay trap), and the body's stores (liver, 2–5 mg, years' worth) explain the **latency** of vegan and gastrectomy deficiency (years) versus **folate** (body store months, dietary deficiency in weeks — the alcoholic, the haemolytic patient whose marrow is consuming folate at ten times normal, the pregnancy non-supplementer). B₁₂'s **neurological syndrome — subacute combined degeneration** (demyelination of dorsal and lateral columns: paraesthesiae, ataxia, spasticity, plus optic atrophy and cognitive change) — reflects the separate methylmalonyl-CoA and methylation arms (serum methylmalonate and homocysteine rise in B₁₂ deficiency, homocysteine only in folate), and the therapeutic rule that follows: **folate given alone to a B₁₂-deficient patient corrects the anaemia and lets the cord disease progress silently**. Diagnostic order (serum B₁₂, then methylmalonate/homocysteine, then IF and parietal-cell antibodies, then — rarely — trial of treatment) and the pernicious-anaemia mechanism (autoimmune atrophic gastritis with achlorhydria, IF antibodies ~50–60% sensitive, the associated thyroid autoimmunity and gastric-cancer surveillance question) belong to the clinical chapters; the physiology is the map they are all drawn on.
:::

::: example Two macrocytoses {#ex-macrocytic}
(a) A 78-year-old vegan-for-decades nursing-home resident: Hb 84, MCV 118, neutrophils with eight lobes, mild jaundice, LDH 1,200, symmetric numb feet and a positive Romberg. (b) A 34-year-old with coeliac disease: Hb 90, MCV 112, similar film, no neurological signs, normal methylmalonate.
::: solution
Both are **megaloblastic** (hypersegmentation, ineffective erythropoiesis with the LDH-and-bilirubin "pseudo-haemolysis" picture — the intramedullary death of nucleated red cells), and the branches are the methylmalonate and the history. **(a) B₁₂ deficiency** (the vegan decades, the dorsal-column signs — subacute combined degeneration): confirm with low B₁₂ (or the functional markers — raised MMA and homocysteine — when the level is borderline), then **lifelong intramuscular hydroxocobalamin** (1 mg loading then three-monthly; the modern oral high-dose 1–2 mg evidence covers compliant non-neurological patients), with **hypokalaemia monitoring in the first treatment week** (the rebuilding marrow's potassium uptake can be abrupt — the classic inpatient risk), and a search for pernicious anaemia (IF antibodies, gastritis) because that diagnosis carries the gastric-cancer and autoimmune-coincidence implications. **(b) Folate deficiency** (coeliac malabsorption of proximal small bowel, the MMA normal): treat with **oral folate 5 mg** (after excluding B₁₂ deficiency — the rule above — or with B₁₂ cover given empirically), correct the coeliac (the gluten-free diet restores proximal absorption), and remember the increased-demand list (haemolysis — every haemolytic patient needs folate; methotrexate's antifolate; pregnancy's neural-tube prophylaxis at 400 µg, 5 mg only on the specialist list). Two anaemias, one marrow picture, two molecules, and the neurological exam plus one metabolite separate them completely.
:::
:::

## Haemolysis: when the problem is destruction

::: definition The haemolysis signature and its compartments {#def-haemolysis}
Accelerated red-cell destruction speaks a fixed laboratory dialect: **reticulocytosis** (unless the marrow cannot respond — the aplastic **crisis** in a haemolytic patient is parvovirus B19's arrest of the erythroid progenitors, [[infectious-diseases]]-adjacent and worth remembering in any sudden anaemia of a chronic haemolytic), **indirect (unconjugated) bilirubinaemia** (the haemoglobin load exceeding hepatic conjugation — the jaundice of the sicker patients and the pigment stones of lifelong haemolysis), **raised LDH**, **low or absent haptoglobin** (free haemoglobin bound and cleared — the most sensitive marker, and an acute-phase reactant, so a "normal" during infection does not exclude), and **haemoglobinuria/haemosiderinuria** when the injury is **intravascular** (mechanical valves, march haemoglobinuria, complement attack, PNH) rather than the usual **extravascular** route (splenic macrophage removal — the spherocytosis, the immune and the sequestration syndromes). Causes sort inside and outside the red cell: **corpuscular** (membrane — hereditary spherocytosis and elliptocytosis, the spectrin and ankyrin defects of [[blood/haematopoiesis]]'s membrane paragraph; enzyme — G6PD and pyruvate kinase; haemoglobin — the haemoglobinopathies of the next chapter) and **corpuscular-extrinsic** (immune — warm AIHA (IgG, extravascular, spherocytes, DAT-positive), cold (IgM, complement, intravascular-ish, acrocyanosis and the Mycoplasma and EBV associations), drug-induced (the hapten, the autoantibody, the immune-complex mechanisms), haemolytic uraemic syndrome and TTP, DIC and mechanical injury (the **schistocyte**, helmet cells — the microangiopathic family), hypersplenism, infections (malaria — the world's leading cause), and **paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria** as the special case — the acquired **PIG-A** clonal deficiency of GPI anchors, so CD55 and CD59 cannot sit on the membrane and **complement** lyses the cell intravascularly (the classical night-time haemoglobinuria — in truth usually continuous; the modern picture: Coombs-negative haemolysis **plus thrombosis** (the abdominal and cerebral veins — complement-driven platelet activation), plus marrow failure — flow cytometry for absent FLAER-binding proteins is now the test, and eculizumab (anti-C5) turned the thrombotic mortality around.
:::

::: example The patient with dark urine {#ex-pnh}
A 32-year-old has had three weeks of back pain, fatigue and cola-coloured morning urine; Hb 78, reticulocytes 12%, LDH 2,400, haptoglobin absent, DAT negative; platelets 70; a leg ultrasound shows a femoral vein thrombosis.
::: solution
**Intravascular haemolysis with thrombocytopenia and unusual-site thrombosis in a young patient = PNH until flow cytometry says otherwise.** The mechanism is the [[#def-haemolysis]] clone: loss of the GPI anchor strips **CD59 (MAC inhibitor)** and CD55 from every haematopoietic lineage, so spontaneous complement activation is unopposed — chronic intravascular lysis (haemosiderinuria from filtered iron reabsorption, hence iron deficiency coexisting with haemolysis; the smooth-muscle dystonia — the back pain, abdominal cramp, dysphagia and erectile dysfunction from scavenged nitric oxide) and **thrombophilia of an unusual pattern** (hepatic, portal, cerebral and dermal veins; the classic Budd–Chiari in a young "cirrhotic"), on the aplastic background that links PNH to the immune marrow failure of [[blood/haematopoiesis]]. Diagnosis: flow cytometry on granulocytes for CD55/CD59 and FLAER (fluorescent aerolysin binds the GPI anchor itself), reported as clone size — small clones in aplasia need no complement blockade, large clinical clones do. Treatment by severity: **eculizumab/ravulizumab (anti-C5)** abolishes intravascular haemolysis, halves thrombosis risk and transforms survival (but with **meningococcal vaccination mandatory before first dose** — the drug's complement blockade and the organism's encapsulation meet at the same protein); **allo-HSCT** remains the only cure, reserved for the marrow-failure-dominant or poorly controlled; anticoagulation individualised with C5 blockade covering the thrombotic risk; and **avoid iron-loading transfusion practice** as for all chronic haemolysics. The night-time urine colour — a myth's kernel of truth (sleep's mild respiratory acidosis enhancing C9 assembly) — belongs with the rest of the chapter's theme: the history's oddity usually encodes real physiology.
:::

::: quiz
The anaemia of chronic kidney disease is chiefly due to:
- [ ] Shortened red-cell survival from uraemic toxins
- [x] Inadequate erythropoietin response for the degree of anaemia, plus iron-restricted erythropoiesis
- [ ] Occult gastrointestinal blood loss alone
- [ ] Hyperkalaemia suppressing the marrow

The peritubular EPO factory is destroyed or maldirected ([[blood/haematopoiesis]]'s HIF module), so plasma EPO fails to rise appropriately for the haematocrit — the controller is broken — and superimposed **iron restriction** (hepcidin raised by uraemic inflammation, ferroportin locked: functional iron deficiency with adequate stores) blunts even given EPO. Shortened survival, blood loss, hyperparathyroid marrow fibrosis and folate loss contribute, but the two-term answer (EPO deficiency + hepcidin) is what the treatments exploit: **recombinant EPO (or HIF-prolyl-hydroxylase inhibitors) plus iron repletion** ([[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]]'s anaemia protocol), with the targets kept sub-normal (the haemoglobin-above-normal trials' thrombosis signal — the same overdose logic as athletes' EPO abuse).

::: history
The cellular era began with **Leeuwenhoek's** first red-cell drawing (1695) and the anaemias' clinical taxonomy — **White of Warrington's** 1749 case of "chlorosis" (the green sickness of menstruating girls — among the oldest described diseases), Addison's and **Biermer's** 1858 mega-blastic pernicious anaemia, **Thomas Hodgkin's** and **Murchison's** splenic-anaemia descriptions, and **Paul Ehrlich's** methylene-blue film (1878 — the differential count, with supravital reticulocyte staining following in the 1900s to make the marrow's output visible). The therapeutic century turned on liver: **George Whipple, William Murphy and George Richards** proved the raw-liver cure of pernicious anaemia on decerebrate dogs (the Nobel of 1934, awarded to Murphy and Whipple), **William Castle's** 1929 demonstration that a heat-labile food factor and a heat-stable gastric factor together generated the anti-pernicious-anaemia principle supplied the intrinsic/extrinsic-factor doctrine and the naming of intrinsic factor itself, and **Minot and Murphy's** 1926 Nobel had already rewarded the liver treatment's clinical discovery. Iron biology's molecular era opened with the transferrin and ferritin work of the 1960s–70s, the cloning of the transferrin and ferritin genes and the HFE gene in 1993–94, and the discovery of **hepcidin in 2000–2001** — mouse and human genetics (Hamp, HJV, BMP6 and HFE) fusing within a few years into the single most consequential physiology of the iron field. The haemolysis side belongs to the spleen surgeons, to **Coombs's** 1939 antiglobulin test (the antibody-coated red cell made visible), and to the citrate transfusion services of the 1940s that turned all of this from physiology into routine practice.
:::

## Where this leads

Iron and vitamin biochemistry in [[cell-biochemistry/nutrition]]; the haemoglobinopathies in [[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]; marrow failure in [[blood/haematopoiesis]]; transfusion thresholds in [[blood/transfusion]]; the anaemias of kidney disease in [[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]] and of pregnancy in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]; malaria in [[infectious-diseases]].

::: summary
- Anaemia is a mechanism finding: MCV, reticulocyte count (corrected as the production index) and the film sort it into production failure versus destruction/loss before any expensive test.
- Iron balance has no excretion arm; hepcidin's control of ferroportin unifies iron deficiency, the anaemia of inflammation (hepcidin-high sequestration) and haemochromatosis (hepcidin-low overload), and dictates modern oral-iron and intravenous-iron dosing.
- B₁₂ and folate deficiency arrest thymidylate synthesis and produce megaloblastic, ineffective erythropoiesis; B₁₂'s absorption cascade (R-binder, intrinsic factor, terminal ileum, transcobalamin II) predicts its deficiency causes, and its neurological syndrome is the reason folate must never be given blind.
- Haemolysis is reticulocytosis, unconjugated bilirubin, LDH and low haptoglobin, split into intravascular and extravascular and into corpuscular and extrinsic causes; PNH, the GPI-anchor clone, is the syndrome that combines haemolysis, thrombosis and marrow failure.
- The anaemias of inflammation and kidney disease share the same final pathways — hepcidin and EPO — and are treated by reversing or replacing them, with iron management as the rate-limiting co-factor.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise The reticulocyte decision {level=1}
Hb 65, reticulocytes 8%, haematocrit 22%. Compute the corrected reticulocyte count and the production index (maturation time ~2 days at this haematocrit), and classify the anaemia.
::: solution
Corrected = 8 × (22/45) ≈ **3.9%**; RPI = 3.9/2 ≈ **1.9**. Interpretation: an untrained eye calls 8% brisk; corrected for anaemia and maturation time it is **borderline/inappropriately low-ish**: the expected response to an acute Hb of 65 would be an RPI > 3. So either an **evolving haemolysis/haemorrhage with inadequate marrow response** (check folate, iron, renal function) or a **hypoproliferative process with some reticulocyte leak** — the next tests are the film, the haemolysis screen and the iron/B₁₂/folate panel, and the message of the arithmetic: **a reticulocyte count is only meaningful after correction** — the RPI is [[#def-class]]'s production audit in one number.
:::
:::

::: exercise Iron studies, three ways {level=1}
Tabulate serum iron, TIBC/transferrin saturation and ferritin for: iron deficiency, anaemia of inflammation, and haemochromatosis; and state the one number that is diagnostic in each.
::: solution
| | Iron | TIBC | Saturation | Ferritin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iron deficiency | ↓ | ↑ | ↓↓ | **↓ (diagnostic <15)** |
| Inflammation | ↓ | **↓/normal (the "TIBC does not rise")** | ↓ | **normal/↑ (acute phase — the trap)** |
| Haemochromatosis | ↑ | ↓/normal | **↑↑ (>45–50, earliest)** | ↑ |
The soluble transferrin receptor (raised in tissue iron deficiency, unaffected by inflammation) and the transferrin-receptor–ferritin index separate the mixed states; the diagnosis of iron deficiency in inflammation still rewards the "TIBC low-normal with low saturation plus history" pattern, and **soluble transferrin receptor, bone-marrow iron staining (the old gold standard) or a therapeutic trial** when the non-invasive numbers tie.
:::

::: exercise The trait or the deficiency {level=2}
Hb 112, MCV 68, RBC 5.9 × 10¹²/L, ferritin 40, HbA₂ 4.8%. Name the condition, the rule that flagged it, and what you would not do.
::: solution
**β-thalassaemia trait** (microcytosis out of proportion to the anaemia, **raised red-cell count** — the Mentzer index MCV/RBC = 68/5.9 ≈ 11.5, <13 favouring trait versus iron deficiency — with **normal ferritin** and the diagnostic **raised HbA₂ (>3.5%)** on haemoglobin HPLC/electrophoresis, the compensatory α₂δ₂). What you would not do: **prescribe iron** (the mislabelled "refractory iron deficiency" who is given iron for years — the store-loading risk), and what you would do: counsel (the partner screen and the reproductive-risk arithmetic that [[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]] and [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]] develop, since two β-trait parents face 25% β-thalassaemia major per pregnancy), and look for the co-existing iron deficiency that can **normalise a spuriously low HbA₂** (the same HbA₂ reading needs iron-replete confirmation before excluding β-trait).
:::
:::

::: exercise The DAT-positive patient {level=2}
A 29-year-old with new SLE: Hb 68, reticulocytes 15%, spherocytes present, DAT positive for IgG, negative for C3d. Explain the mechanism, the spleen's role, and the treatment ladder.
::: solution
**Warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia**: IgG autoantibody (often Rh-family specificity in lupus) coats red cells at body temperature; **splenic macrophages bite off membrane** (the Fc-receptor partial phagocytosis) producing spherocytes and, when severe, complete erythrophagocytosis — **extravascular haemolysis with a DAT reporting the IgG** (the direct Coombs: patient's cells + antiglobulin reagent → agglutination; the eluate identifies the antibody). The spleen is the theatre, hence the ladder: **high-dose corticosteroid** first (response in days; the reticulocyte and bilirubin track), **rituximab** as the accepted second line (the modern shift), **splenectomy** third (effective, with the lifelong encapsulated-organism vaccination and penicillin-prophylaxis discipline of [[blood/haematopoiesis]]'s asplenia rules), then the steroid-sparing and salvage agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, IVIG's modest role), with **transfusion "as needed and with the blood bank forewarned"** (the pan-reactive autoantibody makes cross-matching difficult — "least incompatible" units are given safely when needed; the fear of transfusing is more dangerous than the haemolysis in the hypoxic patient), and the lupus itself treated.
:::
:::

::: exercise The bite cell {level=2}
A Maltese-Cypriot medical student takes primaquine for malaria prophylaxis and passes dark urine two days later; Hb falls 30 g/L and the film shows bite cells and blister forms, then normalises by week three. Explain, including the film's morphology and the self-limited course.
::: solution
**G6PD deficiency, class II/III variant, oxidant-stressed**: an **X-linked** enzyme ([[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]'s criss-cross inheritance) supplies all the cell's NADPH, hence reduced glutathione; primaquine's oxidant metabolites denature haemoglobin into **Heinz bodies** (precipitated, membrane-bound), which splenic passage **bites** out (bite cells, blister forms) with intra- and extravascular haemolysis of the most enzyme-poor cells. The **self-limited course is the physiology's own rescue**: old cells (enzyme-poor) die, the reticulocytosis that follows is **enzyme-rich and resistant**, so haemolysis abates despite continuing the drug — the reason the diagnosis must be sought **after** recovery (a normal test during the crisis reflects the young cells' false negative) and the reason favism and infection-triggered crises are milder in adults than the neonatal jaundice of the deficient-but-untransferrable first weeks (the severe Mediterranean variants carry the highest risk of kernicterus). The management list: **drug avoidance taught with the modern safe/unsafe table** (primaquine/tafenoquine with quantified dosing, dapsone, nitrofurantoin, methylene blue, high-dose vitamin C; fava beans; and the reassuring majority of everyday drugs), neonatal-screening and the **X-linked population geography** (the Mediterranean, African and Asian prevalence and its malaria-selection explanation — [[genetics/common-disease-genetics]]), and transfusion only for the severe fall.
:::

::: exercise Haemolysis versus blood loss versus marrow failure {level=2}
Give the reticulocyte count, bilirubin, LDH and expected marrow appearance in: (a) three days after acute haemorrhage, (b) autoimmune haemolysis on presentation, (c) early aplastic anaemia — and explain each.
::: solution
**(a)** Reticulocytes rising (peak days 5–7; EPO-driven erythroid hyperplasia — the marrow's transit time shortened), bilirubin mildly raised (the reabsorbed haem from extravasated blood — the resorption jaundice of a large haematoma is the same load), LDH near normal (no intracellular release beyond the lost cells), marrow **erythroid hyperplasia with stainable iron falling** (the stores funding recovery). **(b)** Reticulocytes high (unless marrow-failure-haemolysis overlap — the PNH, or the aplastic crisis of parvovirus in a chronic haemolytic), **bilirubin and LDH high** (destruction plus, in megaloblastic-adjacent states, the ineffective-erythropoiesis mimic), haptoglobin absent in the intravascular varieties, marrow **erythroid hyperplasia with iron and haemosiderin** (recycling overwhelmed). **(c)** Reticulocytes **low (the key abnormality — the anaemia without response)**, bilirubin and LDH normal, marrow **hypocellular, fatty, with haematopoietic islands and no haemosiderin** (the diagnostic trephine). The trio — response (reticulocyte), destruction markers (bilirubin/LDH/haptoglobin), cellularity (marrow) — is the same flowchart read at three different breaks, and the reticulocyte count remains the first branch on every path.
:::

::: exercise Anaemia in the older adult {level=2}
An 82-year-old with CKD stage 4, moderate frailty and no symptoms has Hb 102, MCV 88, ferritin 180, transferrin saturation 22%, reticulocytes low-normal, B₁₂ and folate normal, and no bleeding identified. Work through the mechanism hierarchy and the treatment threshold argument.
::: solution
The profile is **multifactorial anaemia of ageing**: the **kidney's blunted EPO response** (peritubular mass and HIF signalling decline — the [[blood/haematopoiesis]] controller with a lower gain), **inflammation-type iron restriction** (ferritin 180 with saturation 22% — hepcidin-mediated functional deficiency on a background of inflamm-aging), possibly **iron-poor intake and absorption** (atrophic gastritis, PPIs — the [[cell-biochemistry/nutrition]] malabsorption list) and marrow responsiveness decline; occult GI blood loss must still be excluded if the indices or history lean that way (the ferritin of 180 can hide a coexisting loss — the dual-raiser states). The treatment argument follows the physiology: **correct iron if truly deficient or functionally restricted (a trial of IV iron is reasonable in the inflammation pattern before committing to erythropoiesis-stimulating agents)**, B₁₂/folate if low-normal with neurological or psychiatric ambiguity, and **ESAs only under programme rules** (the CKD guidelines: symptomatic patients, targets ~100–115 g/L, never normal haemoglobin — the thrombosis and stroke signals of the normalising trials, the same lesson as athletes' abuse, and the cancer-mortality caution in the non-CKD tumour patient), with transfusion reserved for symptoms — the frail older adult whose anaemia is being "treated to a number" is being treated against the evidence, and the correct intervention may be deprescribing (ACE inhibitors? the mild effect; the PPI's iron and B₁₂ malabsorption), nutrition and exercise capacity as the outcome that matters.
:::

::: exercise Design a screening programme {level=3}
Iron deficiency is the world's commonest nutritional deficiency. Design a population strategy for preschool children in a low-income setting: state the physiology-based justification, the intervention options with their trial evidence, the harm to avoid, and the reason universal iron supplementation has been abandoned in some settings.
::: solution
**Physiology first**: infants' requirements are enormous (the 3–5 g body iron and haemoglobin mass doubling of the first year must come from diet after the ~4–6 months' store is spent — [[cell-biochemistry/nutrition]]'s requirement curve), and the hepcidin logic matters twice: iron-deficient children **absorb more** (low hepcidin opens duodenal ferroportin), so supplementation works, but iron-replete children do not (and unabsorbed iron feeds enteric pathogens and perturbs the microbiome). **Interventions ranked by mechanism and evidence**: food **fortification** (flour, double-fortified salt/soy sauce — reaches everyone including the replete, cheap per capita, the classic public-health lever with documented haemoglobin gains), **intermittent supplementation** (the WHO weekly iron–folic-acid micronutrient powder for 3-monthly courses in anaemic populations — the hepcidin-friendly once-weekly dosing), **targeted treatment of anaemic children** (therapeutic dosing, deworming where soil-transmitted helminths cause the blood-loss arithmetic), and the cause layer (malaria control, hooks, diet diversity, and the hemoglobinopathy trait-avoidance counselling that changes the population's anaemia prevalence without any iron at all). **Harms**: the malaria signal — the **Pemba trial** (oral iron and folic acid increased severe anaemia/mortality in a **non-screened, malaria-transmission** coastal setting), which restructured WHO guidance toward **screen-and-treat plus malaria control where transmission is intense**, and the microbiome and (iron-overload-risk) genetics arguments (the HFE-haemochromatosis regions — [[genetics/common-disease-genetics]]'s advice against universal iron in northern-European descent populations). **The abandonment argument completed**: in settings with adequate malaria and infection cover, iron reduces anaemia; in settings without, the intervention redistributes risk; the programme design therefore is **enteric-infection control, then fortification, then targeted treatment** — physiology, then epidemiology, then ethics, in that order.
