Haemostasis is the compromise the circulation makes with itself: keep blood fluid enough to perfuse capillaries while sealed tight enough that a paper cut is trivial. The solution is spatial and temporal — platelets and von Willebrand factor plug the hole in seconds (primary haemostasis), the plasma protein cascade reinforces it with a fibrin mesh in minutes (secondary), and the fibrinolytic system and natural anticoagulants stand ready to reverse everything once the vessel is repaired. Bleeding and clotting disorders are read from this design almost mechanically: the **history says platelet or plasma, the laboratory says which factor, and the mixing study says deficiency or inhibitor.**

This chapter covers platelet and vessel-wall physiology, the coagulation cascade in its cell-based reality, fibrinolysis, the laboratory approach, and the common inherited and acquired bleeding disorders.

## Primary haemostasis

::: definition The platelet plug {#def-platelet}
Arterial injury exposes subendothelial **collagen**, to which platelets bind directly (GPVI and α2β1) and, at arterial shear, indirectly through **von Willebrand factor (VWF)** — the endothelial (Weibel–Palade body) and megakaryocyte glycoprotein whose **A1 domain unfurls under high shear** to link platelet **GPIb-IX-V** to the matrix: the reason VWF is the platelet adhesive of arterioles (and why its congenital deficiency, von Willebrand disease, is a mucocutaneous bleeding problem, and its extreme deficiency — aortic stenosis's Heyde syndrome, cardiopulmonary bypass's shear — produces angiodysplasia bleeding: **the shear-sensing molecule explains both**). Adhesion activates: shape change, thromboxane A₂ and ADP release (the aspirin and thienopyridine targets — COX-1 and P2Y12), **GPIIb-IIIa (αIIbβ3) activation** for fibrinogen-bridged aggregation (its deficiency is Glanzmann thrombasthenia; its drug blockade is abciximab and the oral agents' bleeding cost), and procoagulant surface exposure (phosphatidylserine — the tenase and prothrombinase platform of [[#def-cascade]]). The platelet count thresholds are memorised surgery: **>50 × 10⁹/L for most haemostasis, 80–100 for neuro- and ocular procedures, spontaneous petechial bleeding below ~10–20**, with **qualitative** defects (uraemia, aspirin, cardiopulmonary bypass) bleeding at counts that pass these numbers — which is why the bleeding time's job is done by history and, when needed, PFA-100 closure times or aggregometry.
:::

## Secondary haemostasis: the cascade, cell-based

::: definition The enzyme cascade and its regulation {#def-cascade}
The teaching diagram (intrinsic/extrinsic pathways meeting at factor X) is a **laboratory artefact with clinical uses**; the physiological picture is **cell-based**: **tissue factor (TF, factor III)** on perivascular cells meets circulating **factor VII**, and the **TF–VIIa complex** generates a thrombin burst small enough to be checked by **antithrombin (heparin cofactor — the drug's cofactor), TFPI and thrombomodulin-activated protein C (the thrombin "switch" from procoagulant to anticoagulant signal, its failure — protein C or S deficiency — a venous-thrombosis inheritance, warfarin skin necrosis's mechanism)**, while amplification on the activated-platelet surface runs the **tenase (FIXa–FVIIIa)** and **prothrombinase (FXa–FVa)** complexes, FVIII and FV being the **cofactors released from VWF and inactivated by protein C** (the haemophilia A and factor V Leiden axis). Fibrinogen → **fibrin**, cross-linked by **factor XIII** (the clot's setting; its deficiency's delayed bleeding and poor wound healing), and the cross-link's consequence — resistance to urea — is the old clot-solubility test. The two clinical screens report segments of this machine: **PT/INR** the extrinsic and common arms (VII first to fall — shortest half-life — then X, V, II, fibrinogen: the warfarin and liver-synthetic monitor), **APTT** the intrinsic and common (VIII, IX, XI, XII — the heparin and haemophilia monitor), the **mixing study** (1:1 normal plasma corrects a deficiency, fails to correct an inhibitor — with the lupus anticoagulant's characteristic **paradox**: a laboratory prolongation that predicts **thrombosis**, not bleeding — [[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]'s problem), and fibrinogen antigen/activity plus D-dimer (fibrin turnover — raised in the fibrinolytic response to clot, pregnancy, inflammation, cancer: the **high-sensitivity/low-specificity** marker whose clinical value is its very high **negative predictive value** for venous thromboembolism — [[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]] again).
:::

::: definition Fibrinolysis {#def-fibrinolysis}
**Plasminogen** is incorporated into the clot through the kringle domains that bind fibrin's lysine sites, and is cleaved to **plasmin** by endothelial **tissue-type plasminogen activator (t-PA)** (and urokinase), a reaction **co-localised to fibrin** (the basis of streptokinase's and the modern thrombolytics' fibrin selectivity and their intracranial-bleed risk); **α₂-antiplasmin** neutralises stray plasmin and **PAI-1** (the endothelial inhibitor, raised in the metabolic syndrome — the proposed morning-MI myth and a real prothrombotic tilt) ends the system, while **tranexamic acid** (a lysine-analogue blocking plasminogen's fibrin binding — the CRASH-2 trauma and WOMAN post-partum-haemorrhage evidence, and the reason it is now first-line in those two bleeds) and **aprotinin** (withdrawn, then reappeared for cardiac surgery in some systems) are its pharmacological brakes.
:::

::: example Joint bleeding in a toddler {#ex-haemophilia}
An 18-month-old boy is brought for a swollen, immobile right knee after a trivial knock; he bruises easily; his maternal uncle "had something with injections"; PT normal, APTT 78 s, platelets 240.
::: solution
**Haemophilia A until the assay says otherwise** — the pattern is the chapter's whole first paragraph: **deep-tissue bleeding (haemarthrosis, muscle haematoma) = plasma problem; the normal PT/prolonged APTT = intrinsic factor deficiency (VIII, IX, XI, XII); a boy with an affected maternal uncle = X-linked ([[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]'s classic pedigree)** — factor XII deficiency prolongs the APTT without bleeding at all, so the clinical picture is half the diagnosis. Confirm with factor assays (VIII <1% severe: the spontaneous-joint-and-muscle phenotype; 1–5% moderate: bleeding after minor trauma; >5% mild: the surgical and post-partum discovery). Immediate management: **factor replacement first (recombinant VIII, or emicizumab — the bispecific "mimic antibody" that has redefined severe haemophilia prophylaxis — in the prophylaxis-treated child, bypassing agents if inhibitors exist)**, analgesia, rest and early physiotherapy, and **never aspirate a fresh haemophilic joint after factor cover is running except for diagnostic doubt**. The long arc is the reason this diagnosis still gets said quietly: **inhibitor development** (alloantibody to the infused protein — the historical and current major risk, monitored with titres and managed by immune tolerance or bypassing agents), the **hepatitis and HIV haemophilia cohort** (the untreated-factor disasters of 1970s–85 — treated now, cured now, and a [[medical-ethics-law]] case study in iatrogenic justice), **prophylaxis** (factor or emicizumab from childhood converting a joint-destroying disease into a near-normal one, at prices that are their own ethical problem) and genetic counselling of the maternal line (carrier testing, prenatal options, the one-third of cases arising without a family history — [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]'s discussion of the at-risk pregnancy).
:::
:::

::: example The mouth that will not stop bleeding {#ex-vwd}
A 21-year-old woman has heavy periods since menarche, epistaxis twice monthly, and bled after a dental extraction; her mother carries the same history; platelets 260, PT normal, APTT 41 s (mildly long), factor VIII 28%, VWF antigen 22%, ristocetin cofactor 20%.
::: solution
**von Willebrand disease, type 1** — mucocutaneous bleeding pattern (menstrual, nasal, dental — the platelet-type history), **low VWF antigen and function with the factor VIII dragged down** (VWF is VIII's carrier protein — hence the APTT's modest lengthening and the haemophilia-A mimic that delays diagnosis for years in women), autosomal dominant transmission (both sexes, family history) with the **type-1 (quantitative, most common, mild-to-moderate)** versus **type 3 (undetectable VWF, very low VIII — the severe recessive)** distinction, and **type 2's qualitative defects** (the 2B variant's gain-of-function GPIb binding that causes a **thrombocytopenia that desmopressin worsens** — the single most dangerous trap in the field). Work-up: the panels above repeated (VWF is an **acute-phase reactant** — exercise, stress, oestrogen and inflammation normalise it falsely; test twice, off the pill, not during bleeding), with multimer analysis and RIPA for type-2 subtyping. Management by severity: **desmopressin** (releases endothelial VWF stores — the trial dose with sodium and fluid instructions, tachyphylaxis after repeats, contraindicated in type 2B and caution in the elderly's hyponatraemia risk), **VWF concentrate** (plasma-derived with or without rVIII) for the desmopressin-refractory and type-3 patient, **antifibrinolytic adjuncts** (tranexamic acid for dental and menstrual bleeding; hormonal contraception for menorrhagia), and **the emergency card and pre-procedure plan** — the disease that was called "pseudohaemophilia" and missed in half its population before the assays existed.
:::

::: quiz
A patient on warfarin has a prolonged PT but a normal APTT early in treatment. The best explanation:
- [ ] Laboratory error
- [x] Factor VII (extrinsic arm) has the shortest half-life, so the PT rises before the intrinsic-arm factors fall
- [ ] Heparin contamination affects only the APTT
- [ ] Warfarin inhibits only factor VII synthesis
::: solution
Warfarin blocks **γ-carboxylation of II, VII, IX and X (and protein C and S)** — all four — but **factor VII's ~6-hour half-life** depletes first, and the PT (VII-sensitive) therefore prolongs within a day or two while the APTT (X, II, IX-sensitive; II's half-life ~60 hours) lags: the **antithrombotic effect arrives with the therapeutic INR days later** (prothrombin itself must fall), which is the physiological justification for **heparin bridging** in acute thrombosis ([[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]) and for the **transient early hypercoagulability** (protein C's short half-life falling before X and II — the warfarin-necrosis and early-recurrence phenomena). A useful mnemonic with a real basis: the INR measures the drug's first casualty; the antithrombotic effect waits for its slowest.
:::

## Acquired bleeding disorders

::: example The low platelets of a well child {#ex-itp}
A 5-year-old appears well with three days of petechiae and widespread bruising after a viral rash; Hb and white count normal, platelets 8 × 10⁹/L, blood film otherwise normal, no splenomegaly.
::: solution
**Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)** — isolated thrombocytopenia in an otherwise well child (the definition and the diagnosis: the film, the normal other lines, the absent spleen — if the spleen is big or the film odd, the diagnosis changes to leukaemia, hypersplenism or thrombotic microangiopathy until excluded). Mechanism: **anti-GPIIb-IIIa/IB auto-IgG** (often post-viral, cross-reactivity with platelet glycoproteins) opsonises platelets for splenic macrophage removal — **destruction, not underproduction**, hence the large platelets on film and the normal-to-hypercellular megakaryocytes if anyone bothers to look (they should not — the bone marrow is not part of the work-up of a typical case). The treatment rule that surprises: **treat the bleeding, not the number** — most childhood ITP (two-thirds) resolves spontaneously within weeks regardless, so observation covers the child with skin-only disease; **corticosteroid (or, for significant mucosal bleeding or a procedure), IVIG for the fastest rise (its Fc-receptor saturation in the spleen — the mechanism explains the rapidity), and anti-D only in the Rh-positive non-splenectomised population with its haemolysis caveat**; the relapsing/persistent/chronic categories move to thrombopoietin-receptor agonists (the [[blood/haematopoiesis]] TPO physiology used as drug) and rituximab, with splenectomy last (and its lifelong infection rules). Adults follow the same physiology with a different natural history (chronic course, treatment thresholds — platelets <20–30 or bleeding, and the pre-operative numbers of [[#def-platelet]]). The dangerous differentials the well-appearing child forces you to exclude in one look at the film and the count: **TTP, DIC, leukaemia, aplastic anaemia and hypersplenism** — the chapter's next example runs the first.
:::
:::
::: example The placenta that triggers the clotting system {#ex-dic}
Thirty minutes after a placental abruption with a stillbirth, a woman's uterus bleeds dark and incoagulable from every margin; the IV sites ooze; fibrinogen 0.6 g/L, INR 2.4, platelets 55, D-dimer sky-high.
::: solution
**Disseminated intravascular coagulation** — obstetric DIC is the archetype because tissue factor (thromboplastin-rich decidua and myometrium entering the circulation in abruption, amniotic-fluid embolism, eclampsia, retained dead-fetus syndrome) is the most potent systemic activator of [[#def-cascade]] the body possesses: thrombin generated everywhere lays fibrin in the microcirculation (the kidney and cortex at risk), and the secondary **hyperfibrinolysis** (plus consumption of fibrinogen, platelets and factors II/V/VIII — note the liver-synthesised cofactors V and VIII fall in DIC, unlike in liver disease where VIII, an endothelial protein, is preserved — a classic discriminator) leaves blood that cannot clot at the wound. Management is the mechanism in order: **remove the trigger** (delivery, evacuation, hysterectomy if needed — no product works while the thromboplastin tap runs), **large-component resuscitation guided by targets** (fibrinogen first — cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate to >1.5–2 g/L, platelets to >50, FFP proportionally, the massive-transfusion ratios of [[blood/transfusion]]), **tranexamic acid early** (the WOMAN trial's mortality signal in traumatic and obstetric haemorrhage within three hours — the [[#def-fibrinolysis]] drug at its evidence best), heparin only for the rare thrombosis-dominant picture (purpura fulminans, retained-fetus chronic low-grade DIC), and the calcium, temperature and pH that let what's left of the cascade work (the hypothermic-acidotic-trauma literature's "lethal triad" applies in the obstetric theatre too). The laboratory snapshot — low fibrinogen, prolonged both times, low platelets, very high D-dimer with schistocytes — is consumption and fibrinolysis together, and that pairing is the signature separating DIC from every single-factor lesion in this chapter.
:::
:::

::: history
The plasma theory of clotting was established by **Alexander Schmidt** and **Paul Morawitz**, whose 1903 account of the thrombin–fibrin cycle (with calcium and prothrombin) still opens every textbook chapter, after a decade of argument with the rival cellular theories. The century's pharmacology wrote the biochemistry into practice: **Henry Dale's** discovery that a lab contaminant lowered blood pressure led to **the isolation of heparin** (the 1916 Johns Hopkins student McLean, the 1936 Toronto clinical release) and the Quick prothrombin time (1935), while dicoumarol — from the spoiled-clover cattle-loss mystery at Wisconsin — became warfarin within thirty years of its identification. The platelet side ran from **Bizzozero's** 1868 description of the blood plates and their plug, through the release-reaction physiology of the 1960s, to the prostaglandin era — Hamberg and Samuelsson's thromboxane A₂ and Vane's prostacyclin (with Moncada) — that made the platelet's pharmacology the druggable cascade it is. **Eric von Willebrand's** 1926–33 accounts of the Åland islanders' "pseudohaemophilia" — a dominant, female-including bleeding disease that would not fit the X-linked dogma — were forgotten until the factor assays of the 1950s recovered it, with multimer analysis (and, in the 1990s, Moake's and Zimmerman's ultralarge-vWF and ADAMTS13 work) completing the mechanism a lifetime later. The fibrinolytic system followed the same arc from fibrin plates to purified activators, and **emicizumab's** 2014–2018 clinical arc closed a century in which the cascade's proteins became, in turn, measures, drugs and antibody mimics.
:::

## Where this leads

Thrombotic disorders, anticoagulants and the perioperative plan in [[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]; platelet production in [[blood/haematopoiesis]]; uraemic and liver bleeding in [[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]] and [[gastrointestinal/liver-disease]]; transfusion support and the massive-transfusion coagulopathy in [[blood/transfusion]]; DIC's triggers in [[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]] and [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]].

::: summary
- Primary haemostasis (platelets, VWF's shear-sensing adhesion, GPIIb-IIIa aggregation) produces the platelet plug and the mucocutaneous bleeding phenotype when it fails; secondary haemostasis (the TF-initiated, platelet-surface-amplified thrombin burst and fibrin) produces deep-tissue bleeding when it fails — the history is the first localising test.
- The cascade is a cell-based enzyme amplifier with three named brakes (antithrombin, TFPI, protein C/S); screen tests report segments of it (PT — extrinsic/common, APTT — intrinsic/common), and the mixing study splits deficiency from inhibitor.
- Warfarin's factor kinetics (VII first, II last), heparin's antithrombin dependence, and the DOACs' single-target blocks each read off the cascade design — and each dictates its own monitoring and reversal.
- Fibrinolysis is fibrin-localised plasmin generation; its inhibitors and drugs (tranexamic acid) are now first-line in trauma and post-partum haemorrhage.
- The commonest bleeding disorders are immune (ITP — treat bleeding not numbers), hereditary (VWD — the missed diagnosis of menstruating women; haemophilia — X-linked deep bleeds with an inhibitor- and ethics-laden therapy) and acquired (liver, vitamin K, DIC — the coagulopathy of thrombin run wild).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Screen, mix, assay {level=1}
PT 12 s (normal), APTT 90 s (normal 25–37), platelets normal, mixing study corrects partially then prolongs on incubation. Name the likely disorder and the two tests that confirm it.
::: solution
Isolated APTT prolongation that **corrects then re-prolongs with incubation** is the fingerprint of **factor VIII autoantibody (acquired haemophilia)** — the time-dependent, incubation-dependent inhibitor (versus immediate correction = simple deficiency; no correction at time zero = lupus anticoagulant or a fast-acting inhibitor). Confirm with a **Bethesda assay** (inhibitor titre) and factor VIII level (usually very low), and treat both the bleeding (bypassing agents — rFVIIa or FEIBA — not more VIII) and the antibody (steroid, ciclosporin or rituximab eradication). The clinical face — the elderly or post-partum patient with sudden large ecchymoses and muscle haematomas and no bleeding history — is the clue that turns a laboratory oddity into a diagnosis worth hours, not days.
:::

::: exercise Vitamin K's clock {level=1}
Why does vitamin K deficiency prolong the PT before the APTT, why is the newborn at special risk, and why does the bleeding present as the haemorrhagic disease of the newborn's intracranial form in some breastfed infants?
::: solution
Vitamin K is required for γ-carboxylation of **II, VII, IX, X** (plus C and S); **VII's short half-life** means the PT (VII-sensitive) moves first (the same arithmetic as the quiz above for warfarin). The newborn: **placental transfer of vitamin K is poor, the gut is sterile (no bacterial vitamin K₂ until colonisation), and breast milk is K-lean** — hence the routine **intramuscular vitamin K** at birth (the oral regimens require adherence and miss the late form). The **late haemorrhagic disease (2–12 weeks, classically the exclusively breastfed, unsupplemented, cholestatic infant — check the conjugated bilirubin for the occult biliary atresia)** presents with **intracranial bleeding** because the deep intraparenchymal veins of the developing brain meet the II/IX/X-depleted, carboxylation-poor plasma at their weakest — the presentation that made universal prophylaxis a public-health measure, and the case that keeps paediatrics writing about a "nutritional deficiency" in rich countries.
:::

::: exercise The pre-operative platelet count {level=2}
For each of: platelet count 12, 45, 60, 90 — state whether the patient may receive (a) aspirin, (b) dental extraction, (c) total hip arthroplasty, (d) spinal anaesthesia, and give the reasoning including the qualitative caveat.
::: solution
The working thresholds (plus the caveat that **uraemia, aspirin/antiplatelets, cardiopulmonary bypass and monoclonal therapy can make any count qualitative-deficient** — ask the drug history and the bleeding history): **12 × 10⁹/L**: no aspirin, no neuraxial, no major surgery — spontaneous petechial risk territory, transfuse only for bleeding or planned procedure (and in ITP transfused platelets are consumed in minutes — give with IVIG/steroid cover and near the incision). **45**: dental extraction and most general surgery acceptable (the >50 rule is comfortably met at 45 for minor work with local measures; some dental guidelines accept lower with tranexamic mouthwash), **hip arthroplasty borderline — most anaesthetists decline spinal near this number** (the neuraxial threshold ~70–80, institutional 50–80 debate: quote your unit's policy), **aspirin acceptable if indicated, count monitored**. **60**: all listed procedures except spinal at some units' cut-offs — acceptable at most (the SRA guidance sits near 70–80; below that the haematoma-versus-benefit discussion). **90**: everything including spinal. The general pattern: **haemostatic competence is count × function × site × procedure**, with the epidural-haematoma disaster defining the strictest threshold — [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]'s checklist logic.
:::

::: exercise DIC versus TTP versus heparin-induced thrombocytopenia {level=2}
Three thrombocytopenic, unwell patients. Give the discriminating tests and the mechanistic reason each test works.
::: solution
The trio and its triage: **DIC** (sepsis, obstetric catastrophe, cancer, trauma — **consumption coagulopathy with fibrinolysis**): PT and APTT **prolonged**, fibrinogen **low**, platelets low, **D-dimer very high**, film with some schistocytes — the mechanism read-out is that thrombin ran unopposed (factor consumption) and plasmin cleared the resulting fibrin (the D-dimer); treatment is the cause (plus blood products for bleeding — FFP, platelets, cryoprecipitate for the low fibrinogen; the prophylactic-normalising-trials logic belongs to [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]). **TTP** (the classical pentad is rarely complete: thrombocytopenia, **neurological signs, relatively renal-sparing microangiopathic haemolysis with many schistocytes and raised LDH**, PT/APTT **normal**): **ADAMTS13 activity <10% with inhibitor**, the microangiopathic haemolysis arising from uncleaved ultralarge VWF multimers shearing red cells across platelet-rich arteriolar thrombi — **plasma exchange (remove antibody, supply enzyme) plus caplacizumab (anti-VWF-A1) is the emergency; platelet transfusion is relatively contraindicated (new platelets feeding the platelet-rich microthrombi — the exacerbations behind the warning)**. **Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia** (day 5–10 of heparin, falling count, **thrombosis more than bleeding**): **anti-PF4–heparin IgG with serotonin-release assay confirmation** — the antibody activating platelets via FcγRIIA (the procoagulant side of [[#def-platelet]]'s machinery) — stop all heparin, non-heparin anticoagulant, the detailed physiology in [[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]. Three low-platelet syndromes: consumption of factors, consumption plus shearing, and immune activation — the PT/APTT and the film do most of the separating.
:::

::: exercise The liver patient's blood {level=2}
A cirrhotic patient has INR 1.8, platelets 70, fibrinogen 1.4 g/L, and stable haemoglobin on diuretics. Explain why "the INR is his bleeding risk" is wrong, and give the repletion strategy for an elective paracentesis.
::: solution
The cirrhotic is a **rebalanced haemostatic system**: the liver makes too little procoagulant proteins (VII, X, II, fibrinogen — the INR rises) **and** too little anticoagulants (protein C, S, antithrombin) plus plasminogen, while vWF is **high** (endothelial release, reduced ADAMTS13 clearance), partly compensating the thrombocytopenia — so the INR measures the procoagulant deficit without the anticoagulant one: the ratio, not each arm, decides bleeding and clotting, and cirrhotics clot (portal vein thrombosis) and bleed in equal proportion to what the number would predict for any other disease. Practical rules: **prolonged INR is not a contraindication to low-bleeding-risk procedures** (paracentesis, dental, most endoscopy) — the AASLD/EASL evidence shows FFP "correction" fails (the volume-overloaded patient, the INR barely moves, the half-life mismatch) while **platelets and fibrinogen (cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen concentrate) target the true deficits**: for paracentesis no repletion for a count >50 (virtually all patients qualify); for high-risk surgery use **viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM)** to guide component therapy and consider thrombopoietin agonists pre-procedure for refractory low counts, with vitamin K given early in case deficiency coexists (the malnourished alcoholic) and the liver's **hyperfibrinolytic** phenotype recognised when it shows. The general lesson: **the INR is a screening artefact in liver disease, and the thrombin-generation assays are the research-era replacement.**
:::

::: exercise Interpret the lupus anticoagulant {level=2}
A 34-year-old's pre-operative APTT is 58 s; mixing fails to correct; dilute Russell viper venom time is positive; she has never bled and has had two early pregnancy losses. Explain the paradox, the thrombotic mechanism, and the obstetric management.
::: solution
The **lupus anticoagulant is an antibody against phospholipid-binding proteins (β2-glycoprotein I, prothrombin) that lengthens phospholipid-dependent clotting tests in the tube while promoting coagulation in the body** — in vitro anticoagulant, in vivo procoagulant: the mixing study and the DRVVT (a phospholipid-poor test that un-masks the antibody's interference) identify it, and the **platelet-surface, endothelial-tissue-factor, complement and protein-C-pathway activation** explains the venous and arterial thrombosis and the **obstetric syndrome** (placental thrombosis and complement-mediated loss — the catastrophic spectrum of [[medical-ethics-law]]'s antiphospholipid case discussions). Management: **low-dose aspirin plus prophylactic-dose LMWH in pregnancy** (the standard obstetric antiphospholipid protocol, with the loss-history risk stratification — thrombotic-history patients need therapeutic anticoagulation), warfarin for thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome (with the DOACs' adverse-trial signal in triple-positive disease — the exception that must not be assumed), lifelong review after arterial events, and the surgical note **never to give FFP to "correct" the APTT** (it is not a deficiency; bleeding risk is normal unless the rare hypoprothrombinaemia syndrome coexists). The pre-operative caller's question resolves: this patient needs **no factor product — she needs VTE prophylaxis and a plan for the pregnancy she may want.**
:::

::: exercise Design the reversal {level=2}
Match the bleeding situation to the specific reversal: warfarin intracranial bleed, apixaban gastrointestinal bleed, dabigatran with renal failure, thrombolytic-related retroperitoneal bleed, and the platelet-inhibited (clopidogrel) patient needing emergency surgery.
::: solution
Each reversal is the cascade's design read backwards. **Warfarin + intracranial bleed**: **prothrombin complex concentrate (4-factor PCC — the fastest INR correction and the volume-sparing superiority over FFP) plus intravenous vitamin K** (the sustained synthesis fix; PCC without K simply delays the rebound). **Apixaban/edoxaban (factor Xa inhibitors)**: **andexanet alfa (the decoy protein that binds Xa inhibitors)** where available, else **high-dose PCC**; dabigatran's own reversal is **idarucizumab (the antibody fragment — the direct reversal; dialysis removes the drug too, the only DOAC that is dialysable — the renal-failure hint in the question)**. **Thrombolytic-related bleeding**: stop the infusion, **tranexamic acid or EACA (the lysine-analogue of [[#def-fibrinolysis]]), cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen for the consumed fibrinogen, platelets if dysfunctional**, and supportive surgery — the lytic state is a pharmacological fibrinolysis and gets its antidotal brake plus substrate. **Clopidogrel emergency surgery**: **platelet transfusion is against recent evidence in spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage (the PATCH trial's harm signal) but remains standard for surgical bleeding on the drug**, with desmopressin as adjunct and **neuroaxinal/spinal timing rules** (hold clopidogrel 5–7 days for elective neuraxial work — the P2Y12 platelet's irreversible inhibition means function returns only with new platelets ([[blood/haematopoiesis]]'s 10-day turnover) — the pharmacology of aspirin's COX acetylation and clopidogrel's active-metabolite adduct is the same story in [[pharmacology/drug-receptor]]). Reversal, in every case, is the same three-part audit: which molecule is blocked, which cofactor supplies its bypass, and what the half-life and the kidney do about clearance.
:::

::: exercise The surgical patient with haemophilia {level=3}
A 26-year-old with severe haemophilia A on emicizumab prophylaxis presents for appendicectomy. Plan the perioperative haemostatic management and explain the two laboratory traps his prophylaxis creates.
::: solution
**The plan**: emicizumab (the bispecific antibody bridging FIXa and FX — a **factor VIII mimetic** with a 4-week half-life) provides continuous haemostatic cover equivalent to low-normal VIII activity and is **continued throughout**; the surgical add-on is **recombinant factor VIII to peak levels (80–100%) for major surgery with daily dosing to a graded trough for 10–14 days** (guided by factor levels), tranexamic acid adjunct, and the multidisciplinary haemophilia-centre protocol (the guideline answer to the question every trainee asks: yes, you can operate, and the patient's prophylaxis is why it is safe). **Trap 1 — the APTT is useless**: emicizumab (like heparin, and like a lupus anticoagulant) **shortens the APTT at baseline**, so the APTT no longer tracks any factor deficiency and cannot be used to monitor; use **chromogenic factor X assays** — and **trap 2 — the bovine-reagent problem**: older one-stage chromogenic bovine VIII assays read falsely with emicizumab (its human FIX/FX epitopes), so the **human-reagent chromogenic VIII or explicit emicizumab-aware assays** must be ordered. The historical mirror: the same bispecific-era protocols now run through delivery suites (the obstetric haemophilia carrier plans of [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]] — fetal status determining the delivery mode) and through the emergency department, where the **bypassing-agent-plus-emicizumab thrombotic signal (the TMA events of the early trials with concurrent aPCC overuse)** means "if in doubt, give factor VIII, not more bypassing agent, and call the centre". The case is the chapter's theme in one patient: modern haemophilia care is pharmacology, laboratory vigilance and planning, with the bleeding phenotype largely a memory.
:::
