An adult's litre of blood contains fifty thousand million red cells, a few thousand white and two hundred thousand platelets — and every one of them was made, and is being replaced, at four hundred billion cells a day, by an organ that weighs more than the liver and fits inside the bones. Haematopoiesis is the body's best-characterised stem-cell system: a branching hierarchy from a single self-renewing stem cell to the terminal machines of the circulation, governed by the same growth factors whose recombinant forms now fill the haematology cupboard. The blood film is a printout of this factory's output, and the language of blood counts — the differential, the indices, the reticulocyte — is the first dialect of medicine.

This chapter covers the stem-cell hierarchy and its niches, the growth factors and lineages, the sites and lifespan of blood cells, and the clinical syndromes of marrow failure.

## The hierarchy

::: definition The haematopoietic stem cell {#def-hsc}
All blood cells descend from the **haematopoietic stem cell (HSC)** — defined functionally, not by appearance: **self-renewal** (daughter stem cells restore the pool) plus **multipotency** (reconstitution of every lineage on transplantation), properties first proved in the mouse by Till and McCulloch's spleen-colony assay and later by serial transplantation and genetic barcoding. The hierarchy runs HSC → **multipotent progenitor** (lineage-biased; the old strict "myeloid vs lymphoid split" is now known to leak — lymphoid-primed multipotent progenitors retain myeloid potential) → committed progenitors (**CFU-GEMM** and the lineage-restricted **CFU-G, CFU-E, BFU-E, CFU-Meg, CFU-GM/Eo/Baso**) → morphologically recognisable precursors (**proerythroblast, myeloblast, promyelocyte, myelocyte** — the maturation pool) → mature cell, with the marrow holding a ~5-day transit amplification at each progenitor step (a single stem cell's daily output of ~10⁶ progeny is amplification, not division of the stem itself — the true stem stays mostly **quiescent (G0)**, which is why it survives decades of cytotoxic chemotherapy that kills the cycling progenitors, and why it is the target of the transplant and the CAR-T era's gene therapies). The **niche** that keeps it quiescent and reversible: endosteal osteoblastic and perivascular stromal cells (**CXCL12 (SDF-1)-expressing CAR cells**, megakaryocyte–sinusoidal units), signalling through **SCF–c-KIT, Thpo–c-Mpl, CXCL12–CXCR4 and NOTCH/angiocrine cues**; the HSC traffics to spleen and liver when the CXCR4–CXCL12 tether is pharmacologically cut — **the whole mechanism of peripheral-blood stem-cell mobilisation in one receptor pair** (see the example).
:::

::: definition Sites and seasons of blood-making {#def-sites}
Haematopoiesis migrates during life: **yolk sac** (primitive, nucleated, wave one of the embryo — [[anatomy/embryology]]'s mesoderm), **fetal liver** (the main second-trimester factory, with seeding of the marrow from ~28 weeks), **spleen** (transiently), and finally the **bone marrow** — axial skeleton first (vertebrae, ribs, pelvis, sternum), with the appendicular marrow converting from red (haematopoietic) to yellow (fatty) centripetally from the fingers inward, so adult aspiration is a tibial decision in the infant and an iliac-crest one in the adult, and **the femoral and humeral shafts are marrow failure's first casualties** in infiltration. **Extramedullary haematopoiesis** — liver, spleen and paravertebral patches reverting to fetal function — occurs when the marrow is fibrosed, infiltrated or consumed (myelofibrosis, thalassaemia major, severe chronic haemolysis), and produces the **leuko-erythroblastic film** (nucleated reds and early myeloid cells pushed out through stressed marrow sinuses) with splenomegaly: that two-word film description is a differential of marrow invasion until proven otherwise.
:::

::: example The drug that frees the stem cells {#ex-mobilise}
A donor for a sister's transplant is given G-CSF for four days and then "stem cells" are harvested from an arm vein, not a marrow puncture.
::: solution
Healthy HSCs cling to marrow stroma through the **CXCR4–CXCL12** pair and VLA-4–VCAM-1 adhesions, in the perivascular niche of [[#def-hsc]]. **G-CSF** (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor — the neutrophil-lineage growth factor of [[#def-factors]]) remodels that niche within days: proteases (neutrophil elastase and MMP-9) cleave adhesion ligands, osteoblast support withdraws, and sympathetic signalling through the niche changes, so **HSCs detether and enter the blood**, where the apheresis machine collects CD34-positive cells (CD34 — the HSC-adjacent surface marker; the transplant dose is counted in CD34⁺ cells per kilogram). The clinical arithmetic follows: peripheral-blood grafts engraft faster (more stem cells collected, more mature T cells — the trade being a higher chronic **graft-versus-host** rate in unrelated donations, the reason bone-marrow harvest still wins in some protocols), and the alternative mobiliser **plerixafor** makes the mechanism explicit — a **CXCR4 antagonist** that releases cells within hours, used when G-CSF alone mobilises poorly (the older or heavily treated donor). The same axis organises ordinary traffic: the CXCR4–CXCL12 pair keeps lymphocytes and plasma cells homing to marrow and lymph nodes, which is why its blockade demobilises not only stem cells but lymphocytes (and why HIV's co-receptor politics — CXCR4-tropic virus, maraviroc's CCR5 counterpart — sit on this physiology's shelf).
:::
:::

## The growth factors

::: definition Lineage factors and their receptors {#def-factors}
| Factor | Lineage | Source | Clinical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Erythropoietin (EPO) | Erythroid (rescues CFU-E from apoptosis) | Peritubular renal fibroblast-like cells (85%; liver the rest) | CKD anaemia and the recombinant era's sporting abuse |
| Thrombopoietin (TPO) | Megakaryocyte/platelet (c-Mpl; made constitutively by liver, consumed by platelets) | Hepatocyte | Romiplostim/eltrombopag — the receptor agonists for immune thrombocytopenia (the hormone itself proved too antigenic) |
| G-CSF | Neutrophil | Endothelium, macrophage, fibroblast — induced by IL-1 and TNF | Post-chemotherapy neutropenia, mobilisation, chronic neutropenia |
| GM-CSF | Granulocyte-monocyte; also dendritic | T cells, macrophages | Rarely; sargramostim niches |
| SCF (kit ligand) | Early/mast/eosinophil priming | Stromal | Ex vivo expansion; the W locus's coat-colour mutations |
| IL-3, IL-5, IL-7 | Multi / eosinophil / lymphoid | T cells, stroma | Research and trial tools |
| Thrombopoietin's flip: platelet mass regulates its own signal — free TPO is mopped up by platelet c-Mpl, so **thrombocytopenia raises plasma TPO** (the megakaryocyte mass feedback of [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s reactive counts), whereas EPO rises with **hypoxia sensed by renal HIF-prolyl-hydroxylase** — the oxygen-sensing module conserved from the kidney's fibroblasts to the carotid body, and the target of the new **HIF-prolyl-hydroxylase inhibitors** (vadadustat class) that trick the cell into thinking it is hypoxic and treat the anaemia of kidney disease from a pill | | |
:::

The general design is the nervous system's inverted: **each lineage is rescued from programmed death by its own survival factor** — CFU-E without EPO undergoes apoptosis within hours, neutrophil precursors without G-CSF likewise — so "growth factors" are mostly **anti-apoptotic permissive signals**, and count regulation is regulation of factor level, not of cell division: infections raise neutrophils by raising G-CSF (and by **margin release** — the marrow's post-mitotic store empties within hours, the reason the white count jumps before any new cell is made, and the **left shift** of band forms follows when the store runs short); hypoxia raises red cells by raising EPO (plasma levels double within minutes of altitude exposure, and the haematocrit of the chronically hypoxaemic — the cyanotic congenital heart patient, the sleep-apnoeic — is the EPO–haematocrit axis read across a population).

::: definition The mature cells and their dates {#def-cells}
**Red cells**: 120 days in man, the membrane a spectrin–ankyrin cytoskeleton (its defects the hereditary spherocytosis and elliptocytosis of [[blood/anaemia]]), the metabolism anaerobic (no mitochondria — energy entirely glycolytic, the ATP that runs the cation pumps, the pentose-phosphate NADPH that keeps glutathione reduced and the methaemoglobin-reductase pathway running — deficiencies of G6PD and pyruvate kinase are therefore **red-cell-restricted haemolytic diseases**, and the erythrocyte, which cannot make protein, cannot repair membrane oxidation: the senescence that macrophages read as phosphatidylserine exposure and remove in the spleen's cords). **White cells**: granulocytes ~hours in blood (6–16 hours margin and circulating, then tissue; neutrophil emigration and death — the pus of a healing wound is the spent cells), monocytes days in blood before becoming tissue **macrophages** (the reticuloendothelial system that also recycles the iron of every senescent red cell — the iron cycle belongs to [[blood/anaemia]]), lymphocytes **years** (memory) or days (naïve recirculating through the nodes of [[immunology/adaptive-responses]]). **Platelets**: ~10 days, fragments shed from megakaryocyte proplatelets that penetrate the sinus, cleared by desialylation-readers in the liver. The **spleen** filters all three: congestion (hypersplenism — portal hypertension's cytopenias that never reach transfusion levels), and the post-splenectomy film (Howell-Jolly bodies, target cells, thrombocytosis, and the encapsulated-organism sepsis risk of [[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]] — the asplenic patient's vaccine rules belong to the hyposplenism box).
:::

::: example Traffic: why steroids raise the white count and why some children never make pus {#ex-traffic}
A patient's neutrophil count doubles four hours into hydrocortisone therapy for asthma; and a two-year-old presents with a necrotic umbilical stump, omphalitis, and a neutrophil count of 60 × 10⁹/L with no pus anywhere.
::: solution
Both are the emigration step of [[#def-cells]]. Circulating neutrophils are held loosely on endothelium (the **marginated** pool, roughly equal to the circulating pool, released by adrenaline and catecholamine stress within minutes — the "demargination" that a β-agonist infusion or a frightened child's count displays); glucocorticoids then **down-regulate adhesion molecules (L-selectin shedding changes, integrin and selectin expression altered) and delay apoptosis**, so neutrophils leave the vessel more slowly and live longer in blood — a benign, predictable, interpretation-critical leukocytosis (the steroid patient's count of 20 is not an infection; the left shift and the bands decide). The toddler's problem is the same door welded shut: **leukocyte adhesion deficiency type 1** — absent **CD18**, the common β₂-integrin subunit (LFA-1, Mac-1) without which firm adhesion and transmigration are impossible — gives the triad of **delayed cord separation, recurrent bacterial infection without pus, and marked resting neutrophilia** (the marrow makes and holds neutrophils that cannot exit), with the flow cytometry (CD11/CD18 staining) as the diagnosis and infection control, then haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation as the cure — a gene therapy trial for the corrected ITGB2 being the modern version ([[genetics/genomic-medicine]]). Between the two extremes sits the daily clinic: the β-agonist and steroid-treated asthmatic whose leukocytosis must not trigger a septic work-up, and the child with infections and no pus whose count should never be called "a reassuring leucocytosis".
:::

::: quiz
A chemotherapy patient's neutrophil count falls to zero three days after a dose, but platelets and haemoglobin are near normal. The best explanation:
- [ ] The drug targets dividing stem cells only
- [x] Different lineages have different transit times, reserve pools and lifespans, so nadirs fall at different days
- [ ] G-CSF was given too late
- [ ] The marrow is aplastic
::: solution
Nadirs follow each lineage's cell kinetics: the **neutrophil** pool is small and its tissue demand constant, so counts fall within days of any proliferative block; **platelets** (10-day lifespan, large reserve) fall later and recover later; **red cells** (120 days, huge reserve, no consumption) barely move in a short cycle — which is why the chemotherapy patient is neutropenic on day 7 and still not anaemic, and why the chronic marrow infiltrate first presents with the neutrophil and platelet lines. Aplasia would hit all three (with the fatty, empty biopsy to prove it), and stem-cell kill would spare no lineage but spare the patient (the quiescent HSC survives) — the kinetics of [[#def-hsc]] and [[#def-cells]] read as a blood-count calendar.
:::
:::

## Marrow failure and its faces

::: example Three cytopenias, three biopsies {#ex-failure}
(a) A 19-year-old with bruising, mouth ulcers and recurrent infections: Hb 62, neutrophils 0.3, platelets 12, reticulocytes 0.2%. (b) A 68-year-old with Hb 88, macrocytosis, neutrophils 1.2 and platelets 480: the film shows pseudo-Pelger–Huët granulocytes and dimorphic red cells. (c) A 55-year-old with Hb 90, teardrop red cells, nucleated reds and myelocytes on the film and a spleen to the umbilicus.
::: solution
All three are "low counts", and the films and ages separate three pathologies of the factory. **(a) Aplastic anaemia** — **pancytopenia with a reticulocyte of zero and no abnormal cells**: the stem-cell pool destroyed (immune, T-cell-mediated in most cases — the association with hepatitis, drugs, toxins and rare inherited telomere and Fanconi forms that must be excluded at this age). The marrow is **empty and fatty** (the trephine, not the film, is the diagnosis), and treatment is either immunosuppression (antithymocyte globulin plus ciclosporin — the immune proof that removing T cells restores output, and the same therapy's documented overlap with paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria — see [[blood/anaemia]]) or **HSC transplantation** (the young, matched-donor route — curative and now the field's everyday procedure thanks to [[#ex-mobilise]]'s harvest). **(b) Myelodysplastic syndrome** — cytopenias with a **cellular, hypercellular marrow**: ineffective haematopoiesis, the precursor cells dying intramedullary (the apoptosis of bad clones), giving macrocytic anaemia, dysplastic neutrophils (hypolobated Pelger-like forms, absent granules) and the leak-prone counts of clonal stem-cell disease with its 5q−, −7 and complex karyotypes and its variable leukaemic risk — the WHO/ICC classification and IPSS-R scoring that decide observation, lenalidomide (the 5q− specific), hypomethylation or transplant. **(c) Myelofibrosis** — the **leuko-erythroblastosis and teardrop cells (dacrocytes squeezed through fibrotic sinuses) and the massive spleen** of extramedullary haematopoiesis: a clonal megakaryocyte neoplasm (JAK2/CALR/MPL — the myeloproliferative family of [[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]'s neighbours) that fibres the marrow with dumped PDGF and TGF-β, the reverse of [[#def-sites]]'s rule that when the marrow fails the spleen tries. Three biopsies — empty, crowded-and-dying, fibrotic — three mechanisms — stem-cell loss, clonal inefficiency, stromal destruction — and the complete blood count plus film already knows which is which.
:::
:::
::: example The spleen that eats three lines {#ex-spleen}
A cirrhotic patient has Hb 95, platelets 70 and neutrophils 2.5 with a spleen 18 cm on ultrasound, a normal reticulocyte count and a normal marrow biopsy.
::: solution
This is **hypersplenism** — pooled rather than under-produced: the congested spleen sequesters up to 90% of the platelet mass (normal one third) and a third of the granulocyte pool, so counts fall with **compensatory marrow hyperplasia** (the reticulocyte and myelocyte reserves are working; the biopsy is hypercellular — the opposite of [[#ex-failure]]a). The rule that separates pooling from failure is the **severity ceiling**: hypersplenism alone rarely drops platelets below ~40–50 or neutrophils below ~1.5, because the expanded pools compensate; counts lower than that mean a second process (folate deficiency, alcohol's marrow toxicity, hepatitis-related aplasia, haemoglobin dilution of the cirrhotic state, or the ever-present possibility of a coincident immune cytopenia). No treatment is aimed at the numbers (transfused platelets are sequestered in minutes; the surgical and radiological options — partial embolisation, TIPS for the portal pressure, splenectomy with its lifelong infectious price) are aimed at the cause and at symptomatic patients, the general principle that **cytopenia with a working marrow is a distribution problem, and distribution problems are not solved by adding cells** — the chapter's compartment physiology again deciding the therapy.
:::
:::

::: history
The hierarchy was inferred before it was seen: Ehrlich's triangle staining (1877) and Pappenheim's nomenclature gave the cells names; Maximow's **unitarian** doctrine (one stem cell for all blood, versus the competing family trees that held until the 1960s) was vindicated functionally by **Till and McCulloch** — the mouse spleen-colony assay (1961), serial transplantation (1963), and the demonstration that a single injected cell could reconstitute a lethally irradiated mouse — the same logic that carried **Donnall Thomas** from dog experiments to the first human marrow transplant (1957, the Nobel of 1990) and to today's worldwide cord, haploidentical and mobilised-peripheral graft programmes. The growth-factor era ran from the erythropoietin purification of the 1970s and its gene clone (1985, the first molecularly defined haemopoietin — hence the recombinant EPO that converted dialysis anaemia, and the doping industry it created) to the molecular cloning of G-CSF and GM-CSF in the later 1980s and of thrombopoietin in 1994; and the telomere-biology (dyskeratosis congenita) and Fanconi anaemia genetics supplied inherited models of stem-cell ageing and repair failure, and the modern period is **HSC single-cell transcriptomics** (the continuum replacing the tree) and gene-corrected autologous HSC for thalassaemia and sickle cell disease ([[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]'s newest chapter) — the therapy of [[genetics/genomic-medicine]]'s promises, delivered first in the blood.
:::

::: summary
- Blood is a self-renewing organ built from one quiescent, niche-guarded stem cell through branching, lineage-biased progenitors with large amplifying transit pools — the architecture that lets a small stem pool serve enormous daily outputs.
- Lineage survival factors (EPO, TPO, G-CSF) act as anti-apoptotic permissive signals; counts are regulated by factor level (oxygen and HIF for EPO, platelet consumption for TPO, inflammation for G-CSF), and the recombinant factors and receptor agonists are among medicine's biggest drug classes.
- Haematopoiesis migrates through life (yolk sac → liver → marrow) and reverts when the marrow fails — extramedullary haematopoiesis, splenomegaly and the leuko-erythroblastic film are one phenomenon.
- Cell lifespans and reserve pools differ, so each lineage fails and recovers on its own calendar; the differential of cytopenias is read from which lines, which indices and which film.
- Marrow failure has three faces — aplastic (empty), dysplastic (crowded and dying), and infiltrative/fibrotic (invaded) — and each has a defining biopsy, prognosis and therapy.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Count the reserves {level=1}
Give approximate lifespans and marrow-reserve logic for neutrophils, platelets and red cells, and explain why acute bleeding normalises haemoglobin slowly while the reticulocyte rises in days.
::: solution
**Neutrophils**: hours in blood (marrow store releasable within hours — the stress neutrophilia); **platelets**: ~10 days (large marrow reserve — thrombocytopenia implies increased consumption, failed production or hypersplenism, not underproduction alone); **red cells**: 120 days with a huge post-mitotic marrow reserve (one to two days' output releasable). After acute blood loss the **haemoglobin is initially normal** (whole blood lost; dilution over 24–48 hours reveals it), while EPO is already climbing, and the **reticulocytosis of days 3–5** — the released reserve and then the EPO-amplified production — proves the marrow works; a bleeding patient whose reticulocytes never rise has marrow failure or nutrient restriction unmasked by the challenge ([[blood/anaemia]]'s "reticulocyte is the first branch point").
:::
:::

::: exercise Factor prescriptions {level=1}
Match the drug to the physiology and the indication: epoetin alfa, darbepoetin, filgrastim, plerixafor, romiplostim, eltrombopag.
::: solution
**Epoetin alfa** — recombinant EPO, three times weekly, the anaemia of chronic kidney disease (the controller failure of [[#def-factors]]) and some chemotherapy anaemias, with iron repletion as mandatory co-treatment (the new red cells need the substrate: [[cell-biochemistry/nutrition]]); **darbepoetin** — sialylation-extended, weekly, same physiology, same black-box caution against haemoglobin targets above normal (the trial-era thrombosis signal); **filgrastim** — G-CSF for chemotherapy neutropenia prophylaxis and mobilisation (bone pain from the expanding marrow is the mechanism heard as a side effect); **plerixafor** — CXCR4 antagonist, rapid stem-cell mobilisation in poor mobilisers ([[#ex-mobilise]]); **romiplostim/eltrombopag** — TPO-receptor agonists (peptibody and small molecule) raising platelet counts in chronic immune thrombocytopenia — the physiology reversed to therapy, and the caution (thrombosis, reticulin) written in the same pathway ([[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]).
:::

::: exercise Read the film {level=2}
A leuko-erythroblastic blood film with teardrop poikilocytes; list four causes and the single next test that begins the work-up.
::: solution
The film means **marrow infiltration or stress severe enough to expel immature cells and distort red-cell shape through fibrosis**: myelofibrosis (primary — the JAK2/CALR biology of [[#ex-failure]]c), marrow metastases (carcinoma — breast, prostate, lung, gastric: the film that should prompt the search for a primary), haematological infiltration (leukaemia, lymphoma, myeloma), and the reactive marrow-stress states (severe sepsis, burns, hypoxia, marked haemolysis with erythroid hyperplasia), plus the granulomatous and storage infiltrations (TB, sarcoid, Gaucher). Next single test: the **trephine biopsy** (the aspirate alone runs dry — a "tap" in myelofibrosis — and the reticulin/collagen stain and the immunohistochemistry for metastatic keratins decide which disease the film named), with JAK2/molecular studies and cross-sectional imaging for a primary as the trail develops.
:::
:::

::: exercise Haematopoiesis and the spleen {level=2}
Explain each of the following post-splenectomy findings from this chapter's physiology: thrombocytosis, target cells, Howell-Jolly bodies, and the vaccine schedule.
::: solution
The spleen's cords are the platelet **pool** (~a third of the mass sequestered there), the membrane **remodeller** (removal of inclusions and redundant membrane, so its loss leaves the thin-membrane **target cell** and unremoved nuclear remnants — **Howell-Jolly bodies**, the film's sign of hyposplenism — and pitted red cells that regenerate), and the **marginal-zone immune organ** where encapsulated organisms (pneumococcus, Haemophilus, meningococcus) are trapped and presented to the antibody machinery of [[immunology/adaptive-responses]]. Hence: platelets rebound upward for weeks after splenectomy (transfusion-level thrombocytosis is rare and reactive — treat the patient, not the number); the film is diagnostic; and the asplenic patient needs the **three-organism vaccine programme with boosters, antibiotic standby after fever and the medicalert discipline** — because the spleen's first-pass filtration is the innate defence whose loss turns bacteraemia into the fulminant **overwhelming post-splenectomy infection** of [[infectious-diseases/fever-sepsis]]'s emergencies.
:::
:::

::: exercise The hypoxia sensor {level=2}
A high-altitude dweller, a cyanotic congenital heart patient and a patient with a high-oxygen-affinity haemoglobin: three states that raise EPO by three different routes of the same sensing module. Explain, then say why renal failure abolishes the compensation.
::: solution
The **HIF prolyl-hydroxylase (PHD–VHL–HIF)** module — oxygen-dependent hydroxylation marks HIF-α for VHL-mediated destruction, so falling oxygen tension (or falling delivery) stabilises HIF, which transactivates **EPO**, the iron-transport genes (TFRC, hepcidin/ferroportin regulation — [[cell-biochemistry/nutrition]]'s iron branch) and the glycolytic programme — is one sensor with three triggers: **altitude** (low inspired PO₂ → low arterial content), **right-to-left shunt** (desaturated arterial blood — the cyanotic child's erythrocytosis, in which the modern teaching is to treat iron deficiency and reserve phlebotomy for symptomatic hyperviscosity only, and **high-affinity haemoglobins and BPG deficiency** (a left-shifted dissociation curve that under-delivers at the tissue and turns on the same HIF output at normal PO₂). Renal failure abolishes the response because the **peritubular interstitial cell is the EPO factory** — parenchymal loss destroys the sensor-effector cell itself (so the anaemia is hypoproliferative with inappropriately low EPO: the diagnosis of exclusion of every other cause first — iron, B₁₂, inflammation, haemolysis, myeloma — then recombinant hormone), the one anaemia whose treatment is the missing hormone itself.
:::

::: exercise Design the transplant product {level=2}
For an HLA-matched sibling transplant, compare bone-marrow harvest versus G-CSF-mobilised peripheral blood on: stem-cell dose, T-cell content, engraftment speed, chronic graft-versus-host disease, and the donor's experience — and say which the modern choice usually is for a myeloid disease.
::: solution
**Peripheral-blood grafts** deliver **more CD34⁺ cells per collection** (typically double the marrow harvest), **engraft faster** (neutrophil ~11 versus ~16 days — the difference that matters when the patient is already septic), but carry **~10× more mature T cells** — with the documented price of **more chronic graft-versus-host disease** (the trial-era meta-analyses: faster engraftment, equivalent survival, and that cost). **Marrow harvest** (under general anaesthesia, iliac crests, the product filtered) hurts the donor's back for a week and gives the lower-GvHD product that wins in **aplastic anaemia** (where the goal is immune replacement with minimal alloreactivity and the host is not myeloablated the same way) and in many **paediatric and unrelated-donor** protocols; G-CSF mobilisation (apheresis via two antecubital lines over 1–2 days, the day before the bone-pain) wins where **fast neutrophil recovery dominates** — the myeloid leukaemias and the MDS of [[blood/leukaemia]] with conditioning regimens. Choice of product is therefore disease- and protocol-specific — the same stem cells, two harvests, one axis ([[#ex-mobilise]]) and one trade-off, and the graft-versus-leukaemia benefit that makes the T-cell content a two-edged quantity in [[blood/transfusion]]'s transfusion-medicine echo (the irradiation that prevents transfusion-associated GvHD is the same immunology).
:::

::: exercise Numbers and niches {level=2}
A transplant centre reports that its CD34⁺ cell dose correlates with neutrophil and platelet engraftment but not with long-term myeloid reconstitution at five years. Reconcile this with the hierarchy of [[#def-hsc]].
::: solution
The product's **short-term and long-term repopulating activities live in different cells**: the bulk CD34⁺ population contains transit amplifiers and committed progenitors that home, differentiate and restore the blood within weeks (hence the engraftment correlation — the "progenitor" dose), while the rare true long-term HSC (CD34⁺CD38⁻CD45RA⁻CD90⁺ Thy1⁺ phenotype; the Lin⁻CD34⁺CD38⁻ side-population with the highest serial-repopulating titre) is a small fraction of what is counted, whose function depends on **niche entry and quiescence**, not merely number. The reconciliation: early counts track the progenitor mass; five-year myeloid output (and clonal haematopoiesis's diversity) track the rare stem pool and its engraftment biology — which is why dose thresholds were set empirically (CD34 × 10⁶/kg), why cord-blood units are dosed by total nucleated and CD34 content with slower engraftment (a smaller stem pool per unit, more niche-limited), and why the field's current assays (limiting-dilution xenograft, barcoding) are built to count the long-term fraction directly — the medicine of this chapter's first definition still running ahead of the biology's measurement.
:::

::: exercise Ageing, clonal haematopoiesis and the clinic {level=3}
Define clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), its commonest genes, and the three clinical arenas it has colonised — haematological, cardiovascular and oncological — with the mechanistic reasoning for each.
::: solution
**CHIP**: an **expanded somatically mutated blood clone (variant allele fraction ≥2%) without criteria for a blood cancer**, from the normal process of stem-cell competition and ageing — the marrow's ~50,000 functional HSCs progressively dominated by whichever clone's mutation confers fitness (DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1 the commonest, then the DNA-damage-response genes PPM1D/TP53, whose carriers expand fastest under chemotherapy and are the reason "therapy-related" myeloid neoplasms have a clonal origin story of their own). **Haematological**: the pipeline to MDS/AML (risk scaled by gene, clone size — DNMT3A's small clones are near-benign, TP53's multi-hit are malignant destiny) and the donor-side question of **donor-derived CHIP** (an older donor's clone that can engraft, expand and rarely cause donor-cell leukaemia or graft failure: the transplant registry's new variable). **Cardiovascular**: the paradigm shift — TET2 and others **reprogramme macrophage inflammation** (NLRP3/IL-1β/IL-6 signalling — the CANTOS trial's interleukin hypothesis in genetic form), raising myocardial infarction and stroke several-fold, so CHIP is now a causal atherosclerosis risk factor, not an incidental genotype (the field's urgent question: does it deserve a clinic?). **Oncological**: cell-free DNA assays pick up CHIP mutations, and a rising or high-VAF clone in "liquid biopsy" for another disease can confound the result (the sequencing pipeline's need for matched white-cell controls — a pre-analytical rule now in every cfDNA protocol). CHIP completes the chapter's arc: the stem-cell hierarchy is not a steady-state machine but an **evolving population**, and ageing's clonal ecology turns out to be a disease of the whole organism, seen first in the blood count.
:::
