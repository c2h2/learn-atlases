Haemoglobin is the course's clearest example of genotype becoming phenotype through biophysics: a tetramer of two α-like and two β-like chains, each holding a haem that binds oxygen by the ferrous iron its physiology was built around, with five developmental switches (embryonic, fetal, adult — and the stress haematopoiesis that reverses them) written into the gene clusters on chromosomes 16 and 11. Mutations do one of two things to this molecule — **change its behaviour** (the structural variants: polymerisation, instability, altered oxygen affinity) or **change its amount** (the thalassaemias: chain imbalance) — and the entire clinical picture of sickle cell disease, thalassaemia major and the affinity variants follows from which of those two errors occurred.

This chapter covers the genetics and development of the haemoglobins, the structural variants with sickle cell disease as their type, and the thalassaemias with their transfusion and gene-therapy era.

## The molecules and their genes

::: definition Globin genetics and development {#def-globins}
Two clusters, two pairs of paralogous families: the **α-like cluster on 16** (ζ → α2, α1; each red cell two α genes per chromosome, four total) and the **β-like cluster on 11** (ε → Gγ, Aγ → δ → β), with **pairwise locus-control-region enhancer competition** (the HS sites looping to one promoter at a time) explaining the developmental switches: embryonic (ζ2ε2 — Gower, and Portland) → **fetal HbF (α2γ2)** — high oxygen affinity because γ chains bind 2,3-BPG weakly, the transplacental oxygen-stealing molecule, with the **γ→β switch (BCL11A and the MYB/HBS1L regulators)** falling over the first post-natal months (the anaemia of infancy at 8–12 weeks — physiological nadir plus switch — and the reason sickle pain never appears before ~6 months: **HbF protects the neonate**). Adult **HbA (α2β2)** with HbA₂ (α2δ2, 2–3%) and residual HbF (<1% adults; up to 20%+ in hereditary persistence of fetal haemoglobin — the naturally occurring disease modifiers). Post-natal **stress erythropoiesis** partially reverses the switches (HbF rise, and in β-thalassaemia the ineffective δ/γ attempts visible on electrophoresis), and the therapeutic exploitation of the same plasticity — **hydroxyurea and the newer γ-repressor-targeting drugs raising HbF**, and **lentiviral or CRISPR (BCL11A enhancer) gene addition/editing in autologous HSCs** now licensed in the US and UK for severe disease — closes the chapter's circle: the same gene regulation that made the fetus sickle-proof is being re-engineered to make the adult.
:::

::: definition The structural variants worth knowing {#def-structural}
Over a thousand Hb variants exist; the clinically meaningful ones share four mechanisms: **polymerisation** (HbS — β6 Glu→Val, the glutamate→valine that creates a hydrophobic knob docking into a hydrophobic pocket (β85 Phe, β88 Leu) on a neighbouring deoxy-HbS tetramer, nucleating fibre assembly that **distorts the cell into the sickle shape** — delay time set by the ~15th power of intracellular HbS concentration, which is why MCHC (cell hydration) matters as much as concentration of the gene); **altered affinity** (Hb Chesapeake and the high-affinity variants' familial erythrocytosis, and low-affinity variants' cyanosis-with-normal-gas — the oxygen-dissociation curve as a pedigree trait); **instability** (the unstable haemoglobins — Heinz bodies, splenic bite-cell haemolysis, drug-aggravated) and **M-haemoglobins** (Fe³⁺-stabilised — the chocolate cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen, the methaemoglobinaemia differential with the acquired oxidant causes: dapsone, benzocaine sprays, well water). Electrophoresis/HPLC position follows the charge change (HbS migrates slower than A; HbC (β6 Glu→Lys) faster, and HbC's crystal formation gives the target-cell-rich, mild-course counterpart disease), and the **interaction genetics** (S/β-thal, SC, S-C, S/beta-zero) recombine these mechanisms with chain imbalance — the compound heterozygotes whose severity tracks their HbF content and their partner allele.
:::

## Sickle cell disease

::: theorem The crisis pathophysiology and its modifiers {#thm-sickle}
Polymerised HbS damages the red cell (calcium leak, K⁺-Cl⁻ efflux → **dehydration raising its own HbS concentration** — the vicious cycle) and turns the membrane into an adhesivity and procoagulant platform (VCAM/VLA-4 and α4β1-mediated adhesion to inflamed endothelium, phosphatidylserine exposure, microparticles), while free plasma Hb from intravascular haemolysis **scavenges nitric oxide** — the vasculopathy of the chronic phase (pulmonary hypertension, priapism, renal concentrating and acidification defects, leg ulcers). Superimposed **ischaemia–reperfusion inflammation** (NF-κB, P-selectin, the neutrophil and monocyte swarms) produces the **vaso-occlusive crisis** — pain, fever, and the syndromes: **acute chest syndrome** (new infiltrate with fever/respiratory symptoms — infection, fat emboli from marrow, local sickling and hypoventilation splinting — the leading infectious-mode death), **splenic sequestration** (the pooling crisis of the infant spleen before autoinfarction), **aplastic crisis** (parvovirus B19 arresting the erythroid factory — the reticulocyte of zero in a haemolytic patient), **stroke** (large-vessel moyamoya in children; silent infarcts on screening MRI), and priapism and multi-organ sickle nephropathy. **Fetal haemoglobin is the master modifier** (HbF below ~20% inside the cell excludes HbS from the polymer — the reason hereditary persistence of fetal Hb and the X-linked and Senegal/Benin haplotypes run mild, the target of every disease-modifying drug), and **the spleen's early loss** (functional asplenia by mid-childhood in HbSS — Howell-Jolly bodies) dictates the infection prophylaxis package: **penicillin from two months, pneumococcal and meningococcal and H. influenzae vaccination, fevers-as-emergency rules** — the single most successful primary-care protocol in haematology.
:::

::: example The feving child in pain {#ex-vaso}
A four-year-old with known HbSS arrives with fever of 39.2 °C, painful swelling of both feet, Hb 58 (baseline 75), reticulocytes 12%, CXR clear.
::: solution
Two rules fire simultaneously. **Fever in a functionally asplenic child is a medical emergency**: blood culture, **broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics within the hour** (the encapsulated-organism sepsis that the penicillin prophylaxis and vaccines were designed to prevent — breakthrough occurs, and the protocol exists because death from pneumococcus in HbSS is hours, not days), malaria testing where endemic, full observation overnight for the respiratory syndrome that declares itself on days two–three. **The pain is a vaso-occlusive crisis**: rapid analgesia (parenteral opioid on a weight protocol plus paracetamol and an NSAID if renal function allows; the baseline opioid-tolerant child needs their home dose plus more), **heat and fluids with sodium-aware rehydration** (the sickling arithmetic: hypotonic fluid lowers plasma sodium and worsens cell swelling dynamics — use isotonic-containing fluids with glucose monitoring), transfusion **only to specified indications** (the Hb of 58 with a normal reticulocyte is this child's compensated haemolysis — transfuse for the fall from baseline, the aplastic crisis, acute chest, stroke, sequestration; simple top-up to ~100 g/L, exchange transfusion for the severe organ syndromes — the aim is to dilute HbS below ~30%, not to normalise the haemoglobin). Then the maintenance audit: hydroxyurea eligibility and dose (the HbF and neutrophil endpoints), the transfusion programme if stroke risk (the transcranial-Doppler screening protocol — elevated velocities lead to chronic transfusion, the STOP trials' logic), vaccination and penicillin status, and the family's genetic counselling (the sibling screen and carrier questions — [[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]'s recessive arithmetic).
:::

::: example The endurance athlete's haematocrit {#ex-affinity}
A 24-year-old distance runner is found to have Hb 195 g/L and haematocrit 58% after a viral illness; her father and paternal aunt have "thick blood", all phlebotomised occasionally; EPO level is low, saturations normal, BCR-ABL negative, and the oxygen-dissociation analysis reports a left-shifted curve.
::: solution
**Familial erythrocytosis from a high-oxygen-affinity haemoglobin variant** (the Hb Chesapeake and Kemp Town class — α- and β-chain interface substitutions, classically de novo or dominant): the variant's stabilised **R-state holds oxygen too tightly** (low P50 — the left shift of [[#def-structural]]), tissues read relative hypoxia, the renal HIF module ([[blood/haematopoiesis]]) raises EPO **transiently**, and red-cell mass expands until delivery matches — the new steady state has a high haemoglobin with a **suppressed-to-normal EPO** (the chronic state's feedback reset), normal saturations (excluding the lung and shunt causes) and no JAK2 clone (excluding polycythaemia vera, whose EPO is also low but whose neutrophils and platelets rise and whose symptoms and thrombosis risk are different: the differential the chapter's affinity axis adds to the erythrocytosis work-up, completed by the P50 measurement and globin sequencing). The clinical point is restraint: **these patients are adapted, not hyperviscous by rule** — phlebotomy is for symptoms (the athletes' caution: reducing the mass below its adapted level leaves oxygen delivery worse, not better), the thrombosis risk of polycythaemia vera is not assumed, and the genetic (dominant) counselling and the avoidance of pre-operative "correction" complete the management — a benign haemoglobin phenotype reached through the same dissociation-curve biophysics that makes HbF protective and Bart's lethal: **oxygen affinity is a goldilocks property, and the variants show both edges at once.**
:::
:::

## The thalassaemias

::: definition Chain imbalance as disease {#def-thal}
The **α-thalassaemias** are nearly always **deletional** (−α, −−; four-gene dosage: silent carrier, α-trait with two-gene deletion cis (the Southeast Asian −−/αα at risk of **Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis** — no α chains at all, γ4 tetramers so oxygen-avid they refuse to unload — the fatal-in-utero-with-maternal-toxaemia state now managed with intrauterine transfusions), **HbH disease** (−−/−α: β4 tetramers, moderate unstable-erythropoiesis haemolysis with the 'golf-ball' supravital stain), and the **Hb Constant Spring** non-deletional accentuator), while the **β-thalassaemias** are hundreds of **point mutations** (promoter, splice — the IVS variants — and nonsense; population-specific founder alleles — the Mediterranean β⁰ IVS-I-110 and IVS-I-6, the Asian codon 41/42 frameshift, the Indian IVS-I-5) whose **β⁺/β⁰ output grades the phenotype**: trait (mild microcytic anaemia with raised HbA₂ — the diagnosis the iron-prescribers miss), **intermedia** (transfusion-independent-ish, still splenectomising and extramedullary-haematopoiesis deformed), and **major (Cooley's anaemia)** — β⁰/β⁰ with **ineffective erythropoiesis plus peripheral haemolysis of the γ-chain-excess, unstable inclusions-loaded red cells**, presenting at 6–24 months as the HbF→HbA switch fails: failure to thrive, jaundice, marrow expansion (the chipmunk facies and hair-on-end skull, the extramedullary masses), and the **iron loading of ineffective erythropoiesis itself** — hepcidin suppressed by erythroferrone, absorption maximal, so even untransfused patients accumulate iron. Modern management is **regular transfusion to ~90–105 g/L (suppressing the drive and the absorption) plus chelation (deferasirox/deferoxamine/deferiprone, cardiac and hepatic T2* MRI monitoring)** — a 1980s-to-2000s regimen that converted a fatal childhood disease into an adulthood with endocrine, cardiac and hepatic sequelae, and then into the **gene-therapy era** (lentiviral β-globin addition — the betibeglogen autotemcel class — and CRISPR editing approaches, [[genetics/genomic-medicine]]'s flagship in practice) and and the **maturation agent luspetercept (an activin-B ligand trap that corrects ineffective erythropoiesis)**. Population screening (Cyprus, Sardinia, Thailand — carrier detection and genetic counselling halved birth rates: the model public-health genetics programme, [[epidemiology-ebm]]'s screening criteria honoured) sits alongside the malaria-selection explanation that made the clusters where they are ([[genetics/common-disease-genetics]] again).
:::

::: example The pale toddler with a big spleen {#ex-thalmajor}
A 14-month-old Cypriot infant, breast-fed without weaning iron, is pale, not thriving, jaundiced; Hb 52, MCV 58, target cells, nucleated reds, spleen 6 cm; father has "β-trait".
::: solution
**β-thalassaemia major** until proven otherwise — the age (the γ→β switch has just failed, unmasking the absent β output), the count (the most severe microcytic anaemia with the most dramatic film — nucleated reds and target cells from the membrane-damaged, globin-poor red cells), the family (both parents carriers — mother's "trait" undiagnosed; the 1-in-4 recurrence the counselling must state). Confirm with **Hb electrophoresis/HPLC** (no HbA, HbF 80–95% — the diagnostic picture, versus intermedia's higher HbF-with-some-A) and the **genetic test** that serves the family's future. Acute management: **slow transfusion** (24-hour volume-slowed packed red cells with a diuretic or exchange for the cardiac-failure anaemia — this child's heart is working at a haemoglobin of 52), folate, and the **transfusion programme decision** (regular to 90–105 — the growth, facies and cardiac outcome depend on it), plus the iron-chelation start once iron-loaded (typically after 1–2 years of transfusion or ferritin >1,000 µg/L), the **splenectomy question** (only for symptomatic hypersplenism driving transfusion needs — with the asplenic vaccine and antibiotic code of [[blood/haematopoiesis]]), the endocrine surveillance the transfusion era created (diabetes, thyroid, gonadal failure from iron), and the referral conversation: **HSCT from an HLA-matched sibling is curative and the current standard of care where available; gene therapy extends cure to the sibling-less**, the decision tree that [[genetics/genomic-medicine]]'s ethics columns wrote before the clinics ran.
:::
:::

::: example The athlete who should not have collapsed {#ex-trait}
A 19-year-old African-American recruit collapses during initial intense conditioning with extreme muscle cramps and cola urine; he was told at college entry that his "sickle test was positive but he has nothing — just the trait".
::: solution
**Sickle cell trait (HbAS) exertional collapse with rhabdomyolysis** — the rare, real, and medicolegally loud complication. Trait is not benign in exactly the settings physiology predicts: **extreme exertion with heat, dehydration and acidosis** (the polymer-delay-time arithmetic of [[#def-structural]]: even ~40% HbS polymerises when the cell dehydrates and acidifies — hence the exertional-rhabdo and sudden-death clusters in military and fire-fighting recruits, the classic case of the first week of camp), **high altitude and hypobaric environments** (the renal medulla's physiologic hypoxia and osmotic stress are why **painless haematuria and the rare renal medullary carcinoma** belong to trait too), **hypoxia and acidosis of anaesthesia and critical illness**, and the **splenic and exertional heat-stroke syndromes**. The management consequences: trait screening and counselling (the reproductive counselling is its main medical purpose — the partner screen for HbS, HbC, HbE and β-thal alleles — the compound-heterozygote risk that determines whether offspring have disease), the protective exercise rules (graduated conditioning, hydration, cessation on pain/cramping, no timed maximal runs in heat) written into military and NCAA policy after the deaths, and the pre-operative and obstetric vigilance for hypoxia and acidosis. Trait is, precisely, **a normal life with a handful of extreme-state caveats** — and the athlete's case is the cautionary tale of never saying "nothing".
:::

::: quiz
Sickle-linked pain is virtually absent before six months of age because:
- [ ] Newborns' acidosis inhibits polymerisation
- [x] High cellular HbF dilutes HbS below the polymerisation threshold and falls only over the first year
- [ ] Infant spleens clear the sickled cells efficiently
- [ ] Fetal red cells lack the Band-3 adhesion targets
::: solution
The protective molecule is **HbF (α2γ2)**: the γ chain excludes HbS from the polymer (no β6,85,88-complementary interface and reduced copolymerisation), so cells with high F-fraction — which the neonate has — do not sickle whatever the stimulus, and the crisis onset tracks the **γ→β switch's completion** (and the reason hydroxyurea, HPFH and S/β⁺ genotypes modify severity in the same direction). The corollaries are clinical: newborn screening catches the disease before the first crisis; penicillin prophylaxis starts at two months (the infection risk precedes the pain); and a sickle infant with pain or fever under six months still deserves the full emergency protocol — HbF lowers probability, not consequence.
:::

::: history
The molecules were clinical first: **Herrick's** 1910 Chicago case of the "peculiar elongated and target-shaped bodies" in the blood of a student with recurrent pain (the paper written after a search for "instrument cases" in the records of the patient's prior herniotomy), **Hahn and Gillespie's** 1927 proof that the sickling was intracellular and reversible, **Pauling, Itano, Singer and Wells's** 1949 electrophoresis showing HbS as a disease of a **molecular abnormality** — the paper that coined "molecular disease" (the Nobel chemistry of Pauling, the discovery of the sickle-sickle-cell heterozygote's malaria protection running through **Allison's** 1954 East African work), **Ingram's** 1956 amino-acid assignment of the single glutamate→valine (the first one-mutation-one-difference proof, done with fingerprinting on paper), and **Weatherall and Crichton's** thalassaemia-biochemistry and **Kan's** prenatal-diagnosis work for α-thalassaemia in the 1970s, which made the haemoglobinopathies the first routine prenatal molecular applications; the **Cypriot and Sardinian** screening programmes Cao and his colleagues championed (the population-medicine version), **Thomas's** early transplants grown into the curative standard, and the **2023 licensing of the first CRISPR therapy for a monogenic disease — for sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent β-thalassaemia** — closed in one year the gap between the 1956 gel and the gene.
:::

## Where this leads

The inheritance arithmetic in [[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]; population genetics and malaria selection in [[genetics/common-disease-genetics]]; prenatal and newborn screening in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]] and [[paediatrics/newborn]]; iron chelation pharmacology in [[pharmacology/adverse-reactions]]; gene therapy in [[genetics/genomic-medicine]].

::: summary
- Haemoglobin disease splits into qualitative variants (polymerisation, instability, altered affinity) and quantitative thalassaemias (chain imbalance); both read from the same gene clusters and the same developmental switches.
- Sickle cell disease is a polymerisation biophysics with an inflammatory and nitric-oxide-scavenging clinical face; HbF is the master modifier, which is why hydroxyurea, gene addition and BCL11A editing all work by raising it.
- The asplenic child's infection protocol — penicillin, vaccines, fevers-as-emergency — and the transfusion/exchange criteria are the interventions that turned the disease's epidemiology around.
- The thalassaemias are the imbalance diseases: deletional for α, mutational for β, with ineffective erythropoiesis and hepcidin-suppressed iron overload central to even the untransfused patient's therapy.
- Sickle trait is a normal life with extreme-state caveats — heat, exertion, altitude, hypoxia — and a reproductive message, not a diagnosis of health.
- Screening, counselling, transfusion, chelation and now gene therapy make the haemoglobinopathies the model of monogenic-disease medicine from gel electrophoresis to edited stem cells.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Electrophoresis in the nursery {level=1}
Newborn screening shows FSA pattern; at six months the HbF has fallen and HbS is 68% with HbA 0 and HbF 30%. Give the genotype reasoning and why HbA of zero excludes the benign possibilities.
::: solution
At birth HbF dominates, so the newborn screen's **F and S/A bands are sensitive for presence but uninformative for severity** (screening detects total HbS); by six months the γ→β switch has run and the composition reads: **HbS majority with no HbA** means either **HbSS** or **S/β⁰-thalassaemia** (S/beta-plus would make some A; S-C and S/trait would show the partner band or only S with normal counts), while **HbA of zero excludes sickle trait** (which makes A) and excludes S/β⁺. The differentiating data: the red-cell indices (microcytosis and raised A₂ in S/β⁰) and the family studies or DNA. The clinical point of the exercise: the **newborn screen's job is to start penicillin prophylaxis and parental education before nine months**, when the first infections and the first pain arrive — the reason all US states, the UK NHS programme and the WHO African framework screen.
:::
:::

::: exercise The four-gene arithmetic {level=1}
Write the four genotype classes of α-thalassaemia with their deletions and their clinical pictures, and state which matings produce hydrops.
::: solution
**1 deletion (−α/αα)** silent carrier; **2 deletions** either **cis (--/αα)** (Asian/Mediterranean — at risk) or **trans (−α/−α)** (African — benign trait), both microcytic with Hb Bart's traces at birth; **3 deletions (−−/−α) = HbH disease** — β4 in adults, golf-ball inclusions, moderate haemolytic anaemia with splenomegaly, transfusion intermittently; **4 deletions (−−/−−) = Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis** — γ4 tetramers, oxygen affinity so high that nothing unloads at the tissues: fetal anaemia, heart failure, maternal mirror toxaemia; survivors now require exchange-transfusion-at-birth-then-chronic-programme and HSCT cure is reported. **Hydrops requires cis (−−) carriers in both parents** — each transmitting the double-deleted chromosome 16; the trans configuration never gives the −−/−− zygote, which is why the at-risk populations (Southeast Asia) differ from the carrier-frequency map alone. The counselling arithmetic: cis × cis → 25% hydrops per pregnancy — the reason antenatal screening of at-risk pregnancies and DNA-based carrier testing are mandatory in those communities ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Raise the fetal haemoglobin {level=2}
List three ways to increase HbF in a sickle patient — one physiological, one pharmacological, one genetic — and explain why each works by the polymer-exclusion mechanism.
::: solution
**Physiological**: the **F-cell asymmetry and stress erythropoiesis** — HbF-containing cells (F-cells) are unevenly distributed and expand under erythropoietic stress (pregnancy, aplasia recovery, hydroxyurea-independent erythropoietin drive); the mechanism of protection is polymer exclusion — a cell with HbF above the ~20–30% threshold has its HbS concentration and/or the miscibility gap pushed below polymerisation. **Pharmacological**: **hydroxyurea** (ribonucleotide-reductase inhibition at cytotoxic dose; at clinical dose the signalling routes — nitric oxide and cGMP among them — converge on **recruitment of F-cell progenitors and γ-gene activation**, plus the neutrophil and adhesion-molecule effects); the HbF rise over months correlates with fewer crises and the mortality signals; the newer agents attack the repressor machinery itself — **BCL11A-downregulating lentiviral vectors (co-expressing a BCL11A-targeting microRNA alongside the β-gene) and the CRISPR enhancer edit (licensed exa-cel)** — and the oral γ-inducer classes (hypomethylating agents and the butyrate-pathway compounds). **Genetic**: **hereditary persistence of fetal haemoglobin** (deletional and non-deletional HPFH, the African-American and Greek chromosomes, and the δβ-thal with pan-cellular F — the naturally occurring proof of concept: S/HPFH is clinically near-normal). All three work along the same biophysics: the γ chain has no polymer-competent interface and dilutes the S out of the fibre — the therapy is the fetus, reintroduced.
:::

::: exercise The transfusion programme {level=2}
Two sickle patients: one with an abnormal transcranial Doppler, one with a first ischaemic stroke. State the guideline transfusion strategy for each and the iron-risk management, and say why "normalise the haemoglobin" is not the aim.
::: solution
**Abnormal TCD (conditional→abnormal velocities, 200+ cm/s)**: **chronic simple transfusion to keep HbS <30%** (the STOP trial's primary-prevention-of-stroke result; now with the option of **hydroxyurea in the low-resource or non-eligible setting** — the TWiTCH de-escalation evidence) — months-to-years of programme, ferritin and MRI-T2* monitoring and chelation as iron accrues (each unit ~200–250 mg of iron; the transfusion-iron route bypasses the blocked excretion of [[blood/anaemia]]'s physiology). **First stroke**: **exchange transfusion acutely** (automated erythrocytapheresis — rapid HbS reduction without volume or viscosity cost), then **chronic exchange or simple programme**, with the surgical question of **STA-MCA bypass for moyamoya** in selected failures. The aim is **HbS dilution, not haemoglobin normalisation**: the goal is 30% HbS with total Hb kept ~100–110 — pushing the Hb higher increases viscosity (the sickle microcirculation is a compliance-and-flow problem; hyperviscosity begets vaso-occlusion), and the trials of aggressive normalisation delivered harm, not benefit. The same arithmetic governs acute chest and pre-operative exchange thresholds — the chapter's biophysics again: you treat the polymer fraction, never the number on the bottle.
:::

::: exercise The jaundiced newborn of α-trait parents {level=2}
A term Southeast-Asian infant is hydropic at birth with hepatosplenomegaly, 10% red-cell nucleated fragments and Hb 40; electrophoresis shows a large Hb Bart's peak (85%) with Hb Portland and no HbA. Explain, manage the mother first, then say the diagnosis's antenatal signature.
::: solution
The picture is **Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis (−−/−−)**: no α chains at all, so the embryo substitutes the **γ4 (Bart's) and ζ4 (Portland)** combinations — Bart's (Hb of four γ chains) has essentially **no Bohr-effect unloading and oxygen affinity far above the maternal–fetal transfer gradient**, so the tissues are chronically hypoxic despite a red-cell mass trying to compensate: high-output cardiac failure, hepatosplenomegaly (the extramedullary erythropoiesis), the nucleated-cell chaos on film. **The mother comes first**: mirror syndrome (the maternal oedema, hypertension and toxaemia-like picture that resolves with delivery of the hydropic placenta — the reason expectant management is not passive), caesarean judgement for the giant placenta and the preterm infant, and the **neonatal rescue protocol** (immediate exchange transfusion to remove Bart's and support oxygenation, then a chronic transfusion programme; survivors are now a published series, most cured by **HSCT**, the option the families must be told exists). The **antenatal signature**: microcytic, hypochromic fetus with Hb Bart's on cord or fetal blood sampling (raised **MCH <24** with nucleated cells and the placentomegaly on ultrasound are earlier clues), **parental cis (--/αα) carrier status**, and targeted fetal DNA testing (CVS or cell-free) as the modern route, with preimplantation and prenatal options that the at-risk couple should have met **before** the second trimester.
:::
:::

::: exercise Unstable variants and the drug history {level=2}
A 30-year-old with "G6PD-normal" Mediterranean ancestry develops acute haemolysis with Heinz bodies and bite cells after taking nitrofurantoin for a UTI; the family history includes splenectomy for "congenital haemolytic anaemia". Explain the two diagnoses that fit, and the tests that separate them.
::: solution
Both possibilities are **oxidant/unstable-haemoglobin haemolysis**, and the differential is corpuscular versus enzyme: **(1) an unstable haemoglobin variant** (the β-chain mutations — Hb Köln and kin — that denature on oxidant or heat challenge, giving Heinz bodies, the splenic bite-cell picture, **family history of lifelong jaundice and splenectomy**, and a normal G6PD) or **(2) a G6PD variant the assay called normal** (class I/II variants during reticulocytosis, or the female heterozygote with enough mosaic enzyme — the assay's classic false negatives). Separating tests: the **heat-precipitation and isopropanol instability tests**, **chromatography/electrophoresis and globin sequencing** for the structural variant; **repeat quantitative G6PD after recovery** plus, where available, genotyping for the deficiency. The unifying physiology is the haem's and the chain's shared vulnerability — oxidative stress converts Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ (methaemoglobin) and cross-links the globin (hemichromes, Heinz bodies) unless NADPH keeps glutathione reduced — the same axis explains why **sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, dapsone, primaquine, naphthalene and high-dose vitamin C** are the shared "avoid" list, and why the unstable-haemoglobin patient may present as **drug-provoked haemolysis** with baseline near-compensation; the negative G6PD does not close the case, and the family history is the next test.
:::

::: exercise The β-thal intermedia question {level=2}
A 12-year-old with documented β⁺/β⁰ compound thalassaemia has Hb 85 without ever having been transfused, splenomegaly, gallstones, and ferritin 850 with a normal cardiac T2*. Explain how ineffective erythropoiesis causes iron overload without transfusion, and give the current treatment options by severity.
::: solution
The mechanism runs through **erythroferrone**: expanded, erythropoietin-driven erythroid precursors secrete ERFE, which **suppresses hepcidin** whatever the body's iron, so duodenal ferroportin stays maximally open and **absorption climbs toward multiples of normal** — the transfusion-free patient loads iron through the gut, and the reticuloendothelial and (later, once the reticuloendothelial cap is exceeded) parenchymal pattern follows. Treatment graded: **observation with surveillance** (annual ferritin and T2* MRI; LFT and endocrine screening) for the milder, **splenectomy** for symptomatic hypersplenism or transfusion-driving consumption (with the asplenic infection code), **transfusion support when needed** (the intermedia's crises and pregnancy), **chelation when iron-loaded** (deferasirox first — the modern oral agent), **hydroxyurea** to raise HbF in the γ-competent genotypes (the β⁺ intermedia's best pharmacological lever), the **maturation/inhibitor-class agents (luspetercept — the activin-receptor ligand trap that suppresses Smad2/5 and lets the erythroid precursors mature)** now approved in several settings, and **HSCT (increasingly accepted for intermedia in young patients) or gene therapy** for the progressing case. The physiology — imbalance, ineffective erythropoiesis, hepcidin suppression — dictates the monitoring, because in intermedia the **iron risk is as real as the transfusion need and far quieter.**
:::

::: exercise Design the population programme {level=3}
Design a haemoglobinopathy prevention-and-care programme for a country with 8% β-thal carrier frequency and scarce specialist resources. Justify each element by evidence from the classical programmes (Cyprus, Sardinia, Iran, Thailand).
::: solution
**Prevention (premarital/pre-conception carrier screening with reproductive counselling)**: Cyprus and Sardinia showed that **voluntary, free, couple-based screening with prenatal diagnosis available and non-directive counselling** reduces affected births by >90% within two decades — the elements that worked: school-level education before marriage-age screening, partner testing triggered by a positive carrier result, and a functioning prenatal diagnostic service (CVS at 11 weeks with the founder-allele panel — cheap, since a few mutations account for most alleles in a founder population; the population-specific multiplex PCR or Luminex panel, not full sequencing). Iran's university pre-marital programme extended the model at national scale; the pitfalls (stigma, sex-selection controversies, the need for the counselling to be genuinely non-directive) are the ethics components [[medical-ethics-law]] treats. **Care**: register the patients, **standardise transfusion and chelation protocols** (the single most cost-effective clinical lever: the Thalassemia International Federation guidelines; ferritin monitoring via ELISA, T2* MRI where referral is possible), **train district haematology nurses**, secure a safe blood supply (the transfusion-transmitted-infection arithmetic: thalassaemic cohorts were decimated by HIV and hepatitis before screening — a patient-identification number and blood-bank audit is a core indicator), **deliver HSCT for matched-sibling cases** (the Iranian and Indian experience shows cure at feasible cost in high-prevalence settings), and prepare for gene therapy's pricing question. **Surveillance**: birth-incidence registry (the denominator that proves the programme works), plus the newborn screening for sickle in the African-prevalence counterpart. The unifying evidence: haemoglobinopathies are the paradigm where **physiology, public health and health economics close the loop** — this chapter's full arc as policy.
