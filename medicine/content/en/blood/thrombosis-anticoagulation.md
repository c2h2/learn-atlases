Thrombosis is haemostasis in the wrong place: Virchow's triad — stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability — remains the clinical grammar of why clots form where they do (venous stasis and fibrin-rich red thrombi in legs and atria; arterial shear, platelet white thrombi and plaque rupture in coronaries and carotids), and anticoagulant pharmacology is nothing more than the coagulation cascade of the last chapter drawn with its drug targets ringed. The clinical syndromes — deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, arterial thrombotic disease, and the iatrogenic hazards of preventing them — are among the commonest and most protocolised encounters in medicine, which makes their physiology worth owning completely.

This chapter covers the pathophysiology and epidemiology of venous and arterial thrombosis, the anticoagulant drugs as cascade physiology, and the thrombotic microangiopathies and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.

## Why clots form where they do

::: definition Virchow's triad, applied {#def-virchow}
**Venous thromboembolism (VTE)** — deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, incidence ~1–2/1,000/year rising steeply with age — runs on **stasis plus hypercoagulability**: calf venous stasis (immobility, flight, heart failure, the paretic limb), the post-surgical and post-trauma inflammatory thromboplastin and platelet-activating milieu (the acquired-risk list of surgery, malignancy — the Trousseau phenomenon of mucin adenocarcinomas, oestrogen and pregnancy's rebalanced haemostasis of [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]), and the inherited thrombophilias — **factor V Leiden** (the R506Q that deletes protein C's first cleavage site: activated protein C resistance, the most common European inherited risk, ~5% heterozygotes, venous-not-arterial), **prothrombin G20210A** (raised prothrombin output), **protein C, protein S and antithrombin deficiency** (the classical mendelian-looking families whose antithrombin deficiency heparin cannot fully anticoagulate — the drug's cofactor is the lesion), the **dysfibrinogenaemias** and the acquired **antiphospholipid syndrome** ([[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s in-vitro-artefact-in-vitro-menace). The clot's consequences: post-thrombotic limb (the valve destruction and the compression-stockings industry), pulmonary embolism, and the paradoxes of its management below. **Arterial thrombosis** — myocardial infarction, stroke, acute limb ischaemia — runs on **endothelial disease and shear**: atheroma rupture with lipid-core tissue factor and platelet adhesion under arterial shear (the VWF and GPVI and thromboxane biology of [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s platelet section), giving the **antiplatelet** drug class its domain, and atrial fibrillation's stasis-with-endothelial-abnormality atrial thrombi (the anticoagulant, not antiplatelet, answer). The distinction determines the drug: **red thrombus → anticoagulant; white thrombus → antiplatelet** — the sentence behind every secondary-prevention prescription.
:::

::: example The swollen calf and its rules {#ex-dvt}
A 52-year-old nurse has a four-day painful left calf after a long-haul flight; Wells score moderate; D-dimer high; compression ultrasound shows a non-compressible popliteal vein.
::: solution
The diagnostic physiology is exact: the **Wells model** pre-tests probability from the pathophysiology (cancer, immobility, unilateral swelling, superficial vein deviation — the clinical prediction rule of [[epidemiology-ebm]] in its most-used form), the **D-dimer's high negative predictive value** excludes in the unlikely-probability patient (fibrin turnover is near-baseline in health — the test's power is in what it rules out, not in what it rules in, since surgery, pregnancy, tumour and age all raise it), and **compression ultrasonography** — non-compressibility is thrombus, physically — confirms. Management follows the cascade: **anticoagulate now** (a DOAC — apixaban or rivaroxaban loading then maintenance, or LMWH lead-in — the targets below), treat 3–6 months for a provoked episode (the flight) and decide on extended therapy by recurrence and bleeding risk in the unprovoked (the sex, the residual-vein-thrombosis and D-dimer-off-therapy questions), **compressive stockings** for symptom control (their post-thrombotic-syndrome prevention having disappointed the trials — the honest teaching), and the safety-netting: **the first days' risk of embolisation is the reason we treat, not the risk of death from an isolated calf clot** (the distal-DVT surveillance-vs-treat debate). Then the mandatory hunt the case hides: **an unprovoked VTE in any adult deserves age-appropriate malignancy awareness (not blind CT scanning — the trials of extensive screening show harm-free-cost balance), and thrombophilia testing only when it changes management** (it mostly does not: heterozygous factor V Leiden with a first provoked clot changes nothing — the over-testing epidemic this sentence should end, except where oestrogen, pregnancy planning or warfarin skin necrosis make the answer matter).
:::
:::

## The anticoagulants as cascade physiology

::: definition Heparins {#def-heparin}
**Unfractionated heparin** is a sulfated glycosaminoglycan whose pentasaccharide binds **antithrombin**, inducing the conformational change that accelerates its inhibition of thrombin (IIa) **one hundred-thousand-fold** — but only the long chains (>13 sugars) bridge antithrombin to **both** Xa and IIa, which is why UFH prolongs the APTT (its thrombin inhibition), is reversible with protamine (the basic peptide's salt-precipitation — and the reason protamine also neutralises LMWH only partially), and can be titrated in minutes (its short half-life and the neutrophil and platelet clearing of the polymer: the pharmacology of [[pharmacology/dosing-principles]]). **Low-molecular-weight heparins** (enoxaparin, tinzaparin — the β-elimination or nitrous-deamination fragments) keep the anti-Xa arm mostly (anti-Xa to anti-IIa activity ~2–4:1), giving predictable pharmacokinetics (subcutaneous, weight-dosed, no routine monitoring — anti-Xa levels only in the extremes of weight, renal failure and pregnancy where the kidney's clearance matters), the lower **heparin-induced thrombocytopenia** risk (chain-length-dependent PF4 complexing) and the cancer-thrombosis and obstetric niches. **Fondaparinux** — the synthetic pentasaccharide alone — is pure anti-Xa via antithrombin (the HIT-proof option its trials made, the renal-clearance caveat unchanged). The APTT/INR asymmetry of the quiz in [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]] returns here therapeutically: **heparins act on the intrinsic/common APTT-visible arm, warfarin and the Xa inhibitors show first on the PT/INR-visible arm**, and only UFH's thrombin-side effect moves the APTT at all — the laboratory fingerprints the ward uses as proof of infusion.
:::

::: definition Warfarin and the vitamin-K cycle {#def-warfarin}
Warfarin inhibits **vitamin-K-epoxide reductase (VKORC1)**, draining the reduced-quinone cofactor for the **γ-glutamyl carboxylase** that makes functional II, VII, IX and X and proteins C and S: the drug is a **dietary- and antibiotic-dependent vitamin K antagonist** (the gut flora's K₂ contribution, the leafy-vegetant's consistency advice, the CYP2C9 and VKORC1 polymorphisms that set dose requirements two- to four-fold apart across populations — the first pharmacogenetic label the FDA carried, [[pharmacology/patient-variation]]). The kinetics of the four targets run the protocol (VII's short half-life → the INR rises in days while **antithrombotic coverage awaits factor II's three-day fall** — hence heparin bridging; protein C's fall explains the **early hypercoagulable window and warfarin skin necrosis** in the occult protein-C-deficient). Monitoring is **INR** (the ISI-referenced ratio standardising thromboplastin variation — the WHO arithmetic of a number every nurse knows), its targets 2.0–3.0 (most indications) and 2.5–3.5 (mechanical mitral valves and antiphospholipid recurrence), and its reversal ladder (withhold ± oral vitamin K for a high INR without bleeding; **PCC plus intravenous vitamin K for major bleeding** — the [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]] rule), all of which the DOACs were designed to escape.
:::

::: definition The direct oral anticoagulants {#def-doac}
**Dabigatran** (a direct thrombin inhibitor prodrug — the argatroban/bivalirudin parenteral family given an oral future), **rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban** (direct Xa inhibitors) hit single cascade enzymes with predictable kinetics (fixed dosing, no monitoring in ordinary practice — the anti-Xa or dilute thrombin time reserved for the extremes, renal clearance 35–80% the dose-adjustment axis, and the GI-bleed trade-off: dabigatran's higher and apixaban's lower, the half-life 5–17 hours the reason a missed dose is less forgiving than warfarin's). Their trials (RE-MODEL to Hokusai — non-inferior-to-superior efficacy with **half the intracranial haemorrhage**, the class-defining safety fact) made them first-line for VTE and atrial fibrillation (the stroke-prevention CHA₂DS₂-VASc and HAS-BLED arithmetic of [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]]), with three niches where warfarin or LMWH still rule: **mechanical valves and antiphospholipid syndrome** (the DOAC trials' harm signals — the thromboplastin-pathway reasoning of a disease that is not single-enzyme), **severe renal failure** (the dialysis-patient PK data that guidelines still hedge), and **pregnancy** (the DOACs' placental transfer and animal toxicity: LMWH stays the obstetric anticoagulant, [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]). Reversal is [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s exercise answered: idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet alfa or PCC for the Xa inhibitors, with the half-life and renal clearance the final arbiters.
:::

::: example The falling platelets on day seven {#ex-hit}
Six days after an emergency laparotomy and enoxaparin prophylaxis, a patient's platelets fall from 280 to 95; the leg swells anew with fresh femoral thrombus despite therapeutic anticoagulation.
::: solution
**Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, type II** — the chapter's most physiological adverse drug reaction. Day 5–10 (the IgG class-response timing — earlier on re-exposure), a **>50% platelet fall** (absolute counts rarely reach bleeding levels), and **thrombosis, not bleeding, is the signature**: IgG against **platelet factor 4–heparin complexes** activates platelets through **FcγRIIA** (the procoagulant activation of [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s platelet section — the microparticle and thrombin-generation storm), giving venous and arterial clot risk of tens of percent untreated (the 4T score — timing, thrombocytopenia degree, thrombosis, other causes — pre-tests; **immunoassay for anti-PF4/heparin, then the functional serotonin-release or heparin-induced platelet-activation assay** confirms). The management is three commands and a reason: **stop every heparin flush and coating** (the arterial lines and the LMWH cross-reaction), **start a non-heparin anticoagulant** (argatroban — the direct thrombin inhibitor with the hepatic-clearance APTT titration, or bivalirudin or fondaparinux or a DOAC in stable patients — the prophylactic dose is not enough, the thrombotic load demands therapy), and **never give platelets** (the theoretical feeding of the fire, observed harm in the arterial cases) nor start warfarin until platelets recover (the protein-C-collapse venous-limb-gangrene of early warfarin in HIT — the same mechanism as its skin necrosis). The old distinction earns its place: **type I HIT** (days 1–2, mild, non-immune, continue heparin) versus this type II; and the day-7 rule explains why post-surgical patients on prophylaxis are the classic case: exposure, then the immune window, then the clot.
:::
:::

::: example The nurse's painful thigh {#ex-necrosis}
Six days into warfarin (bridged from heparin) for a DVT, a 38-year-old develops exquisitely tender, well-demarcated purpuric patches on her breasts and thighs with a rising INR; the platelets have fallen to 90.
::: solution
Two of this chapter's entities share a mechanism here — **warfarin-induced skin necrosis**, the textbook being the **protein-C-deficient** patient: warfarin's early fall in protein C (half-life ~8 hours, the same kinetics as [[#def-warfarin]]'s procoagulant window) against still-circulating prothrombin creates a **transient hypercoagulable state in the microvasculature of the protein-C-rich fatty tissues** (breast, thigh, buttock), with platelet consumption in the local thromboses — hence the falling count and the distribution. The **differential is HIT** (the heparin exposure, the day-5–10 timing, the 4Ts and the anti-PF4 assay — the two can coexist and both demand stopping the offending drug), and the management: **stop warfarin, give vitamin K** (despite the necrosis fear of "rebound" — the pathology is the low protein C, not the INR), **full anticoagulation with a non-heparin or LMWH/argatroban agent per the HIT answer**, **protein concentrate or FFP to restore protein C** (and the specific concentrate where available), wound care and plastics early, and **the work-up for the inherited deficiency after recovery** (chronic warfarin, if ever restarted, with overlapping cover and slow initiation — the recurrence risk in re-challenge is high). The exam-pair footnote: warfarin necrosis without protein C deficiency occurs in the HIT and antiphospholipid settings for the same arithmetic reason — the drug's first casualty is the body's own anticoagulant.
:::

::: quiz
The reason anticoagulated patients with atrial fibrillation are protected from stroke but patients with carotid stenosis are given antiplatelets is:
- [ ] Atrial thrombi form on damaged endothelium, carotid thrombi in low shear
- [x] Stasis-driven fibrin-rich ("red") thrombi respond to anticoagulation; shear-driven platelet-rich ("white") thrombi respond to antiplatelet agents
- [ ] Warfarin reaches the cerebral arterial wall better than the veins
- [ ] Antiplatelet drugs lower blood pressure enough at the carotid
::: solution
The haemodynamic-environment rule of [[#def-virchow]]: low-shear venous and atrial stasis generates thrombin-driven fibrin clot (the anticoagulant's target — and AF's fibrillating appendage is a stasis organ), while high-shear arterial disease generates platelet-driven white clot at ruptured or eroded plaque (aspirin's COX-1 and the P2Y12 and GP IIb/IIIa drugs' territory, with anticoagulation added only in the specific high-embolic settings — the ACS short dual-plus-anticoagulant windows and their bleeding arithmetic, and the failed PAD "COMPASS-type" low-dose-rivaroxaban-plus-aspirin experiments' carefully selected risk–benefit). The same rule decides prosthetic valves (both mechanisms — hence warfarin), LV thrombus (stasis — anticoagulant) and carotid endarterectomy's aspirin.
:::
:::
::: example The migratory clot that found the pancreas {#ex-trousseau}
A 67-year-old man has had two superficial thrombophlebitides in different legs and a calf DVT over three months, with back pain and 8 kg of weight loss; the D-dimer is sky-high and the platelets slowly falling.
::: solution
**Trousseau's syndrome** — cancer-associated thrombosis presenting as **migratory, unprovoked and unusual-site thrombosis** (superficial thrombophlebitis in atypical sites, splanchnic and cerebral vein clot, non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis with embolic stroke): mucin-secreting adenocarcinomas (pancreas, stomach, ovary, lung) drive coagulation by **tissue-factor and cancer-procoagulant microparticles, platelet and monocyte activation and NET webs** — a hypercoagulability of continuous low-grade thrombin generation, reflected in the very high D-dimer and the consumption's slow platelet drift. The teaching points: **unprovoked or migratory venous clot at any age should raise the malignancy question** (though the blind full-body-CT screening trials showed fewer deaths and more anxiety — the age- and sex-appropriate evaluation plus symptom-led investigation is the evidence answer, [[epidemiology-ebm]]'s over-investigation balance again), **the treatment is LMWH or a site-selected DOAC** (the mucosal-bleed caveat of the earlier exercise), and the syndrome's reverse reading — a falling platelet count with rising INR and fibrin degradation products in a cancer patient is the **chronic compensated DIC** end of the same biology. The back pain and weight loss in the stem are the tumour's own symptoms — the case's purpose is to teach that the clotting system can be a cancer's first witness.
:::
:::

## Microangiopathies and purpuras

::: definition The thrombotic microangiopathies {#def-tma}
Where thrombosis is forced into the microcirculation the triad appears together — **microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes, raised LDH, low haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia and organ injury** — and the differential is a physiology seminar: **TTP** (ADAMTS13 deficiency — immune or the Upshaw–Schulman constitutional form — leaving ultralarge VWF multimers to shear red cells across platelet microthrombi: the neurological-fluctuating, renal-sparing picture, plasma exchange and caplacizumab [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]), **haemolytic uraemic syndrome** (Shiga-toxic *E. coli* — the bloody-diarrhoea prodrome and the complement- and coagulation-injured glomerulus; **atypical HUS** — the alternative-pathway complement-regulator mutations (CFH, CFI, MCP, C3, anti-FH antibodies) now treated with **eculizumab/ravulizumab**, the same terminal-complement block that works in PNH ([[blood/anaemia]])), **DIC** ([[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s consumption picture), the malignant-hypertension and pre-eclampsia/HELLP microangiopathies ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]), **scleroderma renal crisis, radiation nephritis, drug toxicity (quinine, gemcitabine, the VEGF inhibitors), and transplant-associated TMA**. The unifying lesion is **endothelial injury or shear with platelet–VWF–complement activation** — Virchow's triad compressed into an arteriole — and the working order (film for fragments, ADAMTS13, stool Shiga PCR and complement studies) reads the same machinery from its ends.
:::

::: history
**Virchow's** 1856 thrombosis triad (and his and Langer's and Wegner's cellular thrombosis-era histology), the pulmonary-embolism autopsy series of the 1860s Vienna school (the "commonest hidden killer" of hospital medicine, still quoted from the necropsy rooms), and the 1930s–40s when **Géza de Takats's** postoperative-embolism surgery met **McLean's and Scott's** heparin and the dicoumarol-to-warfarin translation of the 1940s (de Takats's own dicoumarol prophylaxis experiments in post-surgical dogs) to open the anticoagulation century whose INR plateau is now half-replaced by the direct inhibitors: **hirudin's** centuries (the leech's gland, then recombinant desirudin and lepirudin, dabigatran's small-molecule descendant), the factor-Xa-inhibitor programme from tick antistatin biology, and the HIT saga — **White and Bedford's and Shapiro's** 1973 thrombocytopaenia reports and the 1990s Warkentin and Kelton dissection of the PF4–heparin immune complex into the 4Ts, the assays and the modern day-7 rule.
:::

## Where this leads

The cascade and its bleeding mirror in [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]; the atrial-fibrillation and acute-coronary applications in [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]] and [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]; the pregnancy and postpartum risk arithmetic in [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]; cancer-associated thrombosis in [[pathology/cancer-biology]]; the hospital and post-surgical prophylaxis policies in [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]; the pharmacogenetics of dosing in [[pharmacology/patient-variation]].

::: summary
- Venous and arterial thrombosis obey the same triad in different environments: stasis and plasma-driven fibrin clot (anticoagulants) versus shear and platelet-driven white clot (antiplatelets).
- The inherited thrombophilias (factor V Leiden, prothrombin variant, protein C/S and antithrombin deficiency) and the acquired ones (antiphospholipid syndrome, malignancy, pregnancy, oestrogen) shift venous risk and mostly not arterial — and testing is only for the answers that change management.
- Heparins exploit antithrombin's thrombin and Xa acceleration; warfarin drains the vitamin-K cycle's factor carboxylation; the DOACs strike single enzymes — each drug's monitoring, reversal and failure modes follow from which node it blocks.
- Bridging, INR targets, extended therapy and perioperative rules are kinetics questions: what falls first, what lasts, and what the kidney clears.
- HIT is immune platelet activation (thrombosis, not bleeding, with a day-5–10 clock), and the thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP, HUS and their mimics) are the same lesion read in the arteriole: schistocytes, low platelets, organ injury, each with a named mechanism and a specific therapy.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Preschool the Wells, order the tests {level=1}
Why is the D-dimer useful only in low-and-intermediate pre-test probability, and why does a negative Doppler ultrasound not exclude a clinically suspected calf DVT in the same patient?
::: solution
D-dimer's likelihood ratios work **only around the threshold that sets post-test probability below the imaging trigger** — in high pre-test probability a negative D-dimer cannot pull disease probability low enough to stop imaging (the pre-valence arithmetic of [[epidemiology-ebm]]), and in the hospitalised, post-operative, pregnant or elderly the **specificity collapses** (fibrin turnover rises with the conditions of life) so the test runs positive and informs nothing. The calf Doppler: distal and non-occlusive thrombi are technically harder to see (compressibility, operator dependence, the isometric-vein anatomy), so a single negative study in a probable case warrants a **repeat scan at 5–7 days or earlier imaging** — the same pre/post-test logic as the D-dimer in reverse: an imperfect sensitivity test must be repeated or escalated when the clinical suspicion survives it.
:::
:::

::: exercise Choose the anticoagulant {level=1}
For each patient choose and justify: (a) 34-year-old with unprovoked PE, normal kidneys, no cancer; (b) 70-year-old with mechanical mitral valve; (c) 45-year-old with pancreatic cancer and a femoral DVT; (d) 29-year-old at 20 weeks' gestation with a saddle PE.
::: solution
**(a) DOAC** (apixaban or rivaroxaban monotherapy from the start, or LMWH lead-in dabigatran/edoxaban) — non-inferior efficacy, half the intracranial bleeding, no monitoring; stop at 3–6 months with an extended-therapy reassessment. **(b) Warfarin, target 2.5–3.5** — mechanical valves and antiphospholipid disease are the DOAC trials' harm zones (the matrix-endothelium-driven thromboplastin biology of [[#def-virchow]] needs the broad block); LMWH bridging per valve and bleeding risk. **(c) LMWH or a DOAC per current cancer-thrombosis guidance** (the CLIP and the SELECT-D and CARAVAGG comparisons make DOACs acceptable in most tumours except luminal gastrointestinal and genitourinary cancers, where their mucosal-bleed signal favours LMWH — the nuance is the tumour site and the intact mucosa). **(d) LMWH throughout pregnancy** (the DOACs' placental transfer and the warfarin embryopathy window — 6–12 weeks and the fetal-bleeding and CNS risks later — both excluded; therapeutic weight-dosed LMWH with anti-Xa monitoring at the extremes is the obstetric standard, conversion near labour, and the postpartum plan — warfarin is breastfeeding-compatible, DOACs not established, [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]).
:::

::: exercise Peri-warfarin arithmetic {level=2}
A warfarin patient (INR 2.6, mechanical aortic valve) needs a dental extraction and, separately, develops an INR of 8.2 with no bleeding after a course of co-trimoxazole. Manage each, and explain the interaction's mechanism.
::: solution
**Dental extraction**: for minor oral surgery on warfarin with INR ≤3–4, **do not stop warfarin** (the valve- and rebound-thrombosis risk and the low extraction-bleed risk) — check the INR within 24–72 hours, use local measures (pressure, sutures, tranexamic-acid mouthwash — the [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]] antifibrinolytic), review with the dentist and anticoagulation clinic, and never "cover with heparin" a well-controlled warfarin patient for a minor procedure (the bridging trials: more bleeding, no fewer events — the BRIDGE trial's logic applied). **INR 8.2, no bleed**: **withhold warfarin, give oral vitamin K 1–2.5 mg, recheck within 24 hours** (the intravenous route and PCC are for bleeding; the subcutaneous route is erratic and no longer recommended), resume at a reduced maintenance once the INR falls, and read the cause: **co-trimoxazole inhibits CYP2C9 (the S-warfarin route — [[pharmacology/metabolism-excretion]]) and displaces protein binding and reduces the vitamin-K-producing flora** — the classic interaction, with azoles, amiodarone and metronidazole in the same list; the antibiotic course is the reason every clinic has an INR-check-with-antibiotics standing order ([[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Read a mixing study {level=2}
PT 22 s, APTT 70 s in a bleeding patient: 1:1 mix gives PT 14, APTT 36 immediately; after 2 hours' incubation the APTT rises to 64. Give the pattern's name, the likely target and the confirmatory assays.
::: solution
The immediate correction proves a **deficiency** (both pathways' shared factors are present in the normal plasma), and the **post-incubation prolongation of the APTT** unmasks a **time-dependent inhibitor against an intrinsic-pathway factor — classically factor VIII autoantibody (acquired haemophilia A)**: the autoantibody's slow, temperature-dependent neutralisation hides at time zero ([[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s exercise twin). Confirm with **factor VIII activity (low), the Bethesda titre (quantifies the inhibitor)** and the exclusion of the mimics (lupus anticoagulant — which corrects poorly at time zero but does not cause bleeding; heparin — check the thrombin time/reagent neutralisation). Treatment is [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]'s emergency: bypassing agents for bleeding (rFVIIa, FEIBA) and immunosuppression for eradication (steroid ± ciclosporin/rituximab/cyclophosphamide) — the clinical lesson being that the same mixing curve can mean haemorrhagic emergency or laboratory curiosity, and only the history separates them.
:::

::: exercise Extended therapy decision {level=2}
A 46-year-old man has a second unprovoked proximal DVT. Explain the recurrence-risk reasoning that decides indefinite anticoagulation, the choice of dose for the long term, and the bleeding-risk mitigations you would install.
::: solution
Recurrence risk after unprovoked VTE is ~10% at one year and ~30% at five off therapy (higher in men, higher with a positive post-cessation D-dimer and with residual vein thrombosis and the near-normal-vs-abnormal **PADIS/CURE-style reasoning**), and a **second unprovoked event puts recurrence probability above the threshold at which continued anticoagulation's prevented deaths exceed its bleeding deaths in almost every model** — hence **indefinite therapy with annual reassessment**. The dose question: **full-dose versus reduced DOAC for extension** (the apixaban 2.5 mg twice-daily and rivaroxaban 10 mg once-daily programmes — the AMPLIFY-EXT and EINSTEIN-CHOICE trials halved recurrence further at little added bleeding — the modern default for many), warfarin if DOAC-unusable (valve, antiphospholipid, cost), and the bleeding mitigations that make "indefinite" tolerable: **avoid concomitant NSAIDs and antiplatelets unless separately indicated, PPI cover in the GI-risk patient, alcohol review, fall- and occupational-risk discussion, renal and liver surveillance, the on/off DOAC interaction list ([[pharmacology/metabolism-excretion]])** and the written plan (the annual stop/no-stop note that prevents both indefinite drift and premature cessation). The physiology summary: you are balancing a plasma-thrombosis-prone circulation against a mucocutaneous and intracranial bleed budget that ages faster than the thrombosis risk does.
:::

::: exercise The green-limb question {level=2}
A patient on day 4 of warfarin for HIT (started before the diagnosis) develops a painful blue, swollen leg with intact pulses and a falling platelet count. Name the syndrome and the three-step mechanism.
::: solution
**Venous limb gangrene / warfarin-associated venous limb ischaemia in HIT** — the syndrome that turned a pharmacology footnote into a boxed warning. Mechanism: (1) **HIT's thrombin generation is running** (the FcγRIIA platelet activation and microparticle storm of [[#ex-hit]]); (2) **warfarin collapses protein C first** (its short half-life, the same kinetics as [[#def-warfarin]]'s early procoagulant window) while factors II and X are still functional and the platelet-driven thrombin output is rising — the **protein C–to–procoagulant disproportion** measured as a steep fall in the protein C level relative to factor VII; and (3) the **microvascular and venous thromboses that follow** in a limb whose outflow is already occluded by fresh DVT — gangrene with palpable pulses (the arterial inflow intact, the venous outflow and microcirculation destroyed), and the disseminated intravascular coagulation-like picture in severe cases. The three rules: **never start warfarin in acute HIT** (until platelets recover to ≥150), **reverse any warfarin already running with vitamin K**, and **anticoagulate on a non-heparin agent** (argatroban et al.) — with the skin-necrosis-of-warfarin (the protein-C-deficient analogue of the same arithmetic, [[#def-warfarin]]) as the exam pair.
:::

::: exercise The stroke with an open foramen {level=2}
A 32-year-old long-haul cyclist has a left MCA-territory stroke; leg ultrasound shows a popliteal DVT; the echocardiogram shows a patent foramen ovale with a right-to-left shunt on Valsalva. Explain the pathway, and the closure-versus-medication debate.
::: solution
A **paradoxical embolism**: venous thrombus (the post-flight popliteal clot — stasis again) crosses to the arterial circulation through the **PFO** when right atrial pressure transiently exceeds left (coughing, straining, the Valsalva release phase — the same pressure physics as the right-heart filling sections of [[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]]), and the embolus — often fragmented, the "venous-to-arterial" transit explained — lands in the brain (or coronary, renal, limb). Estimating attributable causality (the **RoPE score** — age, cortical imaging, lack of vascular risks, clot availability) selects the patients whose PFO is culprit rather than innocent bystander (present in ~25% of adults), and the trials (CLOSE, REDUCE, DEFENSE-PFO) support **percutaneous closure plus antiplatelet therapy over medical therapy alone for recurrent-stroke reduction in carefully selected under-60s with imaged shunt-related infarcts**, against a background of device risk (atrial fibrillation, residual shunt, the rare erosion). Anticoagulation rather than antiplatelet therapy is preferred when the **venous clot is the active problem** (treat the DVT on its own indications — the DOAC rules of [[#def-doac]]), and the secondary prevention returns to the provoked-clot logic: a flight-proximal DVT gets months of anticoagulation regardless of the foramen's future.
:::

::: exercise Design the prophylaxis policy {level=3}
Write the VTE prophylaxis policy for a general surgical unit: risk model, the mechanical-versus-pharmacological decision, special populations, and the audit indicators — and justify each element from this chapter's physiology.
::: solution
**Risk model**: every admitted surgical patient scored on a validated tool (the Caprini model, tested in the RAM trial — age, malignancy, immobility, prior VTE, thrombophilia, operation type and duration), because the **stasis-plus-inflammatory-thromboplastin arithmetic of [[#def-virchow]] is not evenly distributed** and a single policy over-treats the low-risk (their bleed budget is not free). **Mechanical methods** (graded compression stockings — with the correctly sized stock and skin-check discipline, and intermittent pneumatic compression — the calf-pump physiology that reverses stasis directly) for the **bleeding-risk-high or pharmacologically contraindicated**, and combined with drugs for the highest-risk (trauma, major cancer, spinal-surgery) patients, the trial evidence favouring IPC-first combinations over either alone in high-risk groups. **Pharmacological default**: LMWH (or UFH in renal failure) started pre- or early-post-operatively and continued **to discharge and beyond in major abdominal–pelvic cancer surgery (the 28–35-day extended-LMWH data)**; the DOACs' emerging surgical roles (the elective-arthroplasty oral-agent trials — accepted in some guidelines, still LMWH-anchored in most general-surgical policy). **Special populations**: pregnancy (LMWH, the obstetric dosing and delivery timing of [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]), renal failure (UFH or dose-adjusted LMWH with anti-Xa), neuraxial catheters (the interval rules between last dose and needle — applied pharmacokinetics), prior HIT (fondaparinux or a DTI plan), and active cancer (the DOAC/LMWH rules of the previous exercise). **Audit indicators**: prophylaxis rate within 24 hours of admission, contraindication documentation, extended prophylaxis in cancer surgery, post-mortem- and radiologically-confirmed hospital-acquired VTE, bleeding complications and readmissions — the policy's proof that **stasis is preventable, thromboplastin is temporary, and the iatrogenic bleed is the price you manage, not ignore.**
