Bacteria are single-celled organisms without a nucleus. Most of them are harmless, many are useful, and a small number cause most of the bacterial disease seen in hospitals. To reason about those few, a clinician needs a handful of facts about each organism: the shape of the cell, how it takes up the Gram stain, whether it needs oxygen, whether it forms spores, and how it exchanges genes with other bacteria.

These facts are not trivia. The Gram reaction tells you which antibiotics can reach the organism. The oxygen requirement tells you where it lives in the body and which specimen will grow it. Spore formation explains why some infections survive alcohol hand rub, and gene transfer explains why resistance spreads so quickly between species.

This chapter covers the structure of the bacterial cell, the Gram stain and the main types of cell envelope, bacterial growth and spores, bacterial genetics, and a practical classification of the bacteria that cause human disease.

## The bacterial cell {#structure}

A bacterium is a **prokaryote**: its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm rather than inside a nuclear membrane. Bacteria are much smaller than human cells. A typical rod is about 1 µm wide and 2–3 µm long, roughly the size of a human platelet.

::: definition The bacterial cell {#def-structure}
Every bacterium has the same core parts:

- a **cytoplasmic membrane**, a phospholipid bilayer without sterols (*Mycoplasma* is the exception);
- a **nucleoid**: a single circular chromosome, not enclosed by a membrane;
- **70S ribosomes**, made of a 30S and a 50S subunit, which differ from human 80S ribosomes;
- in almost all species, a **cell wall** of **peptidoglycan**, a mesh of sugar chains cross-linked by short peptides.

Many bacteria also have optional structures:

- **plasmids**: small circles of DNA that replicate on their own and often carry resistance or toxin genes;
- a **capsule**: a loose outer layer, usually polysaccharide, that resists phagocytosis;
- **flagella** for swimming, and **pili** (fimbriae) for attaching to surfaces or to other bacteria;
- **endospores**: dormant, highly resistant forms made by a few genera.
:::

Each of these parts is a target or an obstacle. The 70S ribosome is why aminoglycosides, macrolides and tetracyclines can block bacterial protein synthesis with limited effect on human cells. Peptidoglycan exists only in bacteria, so drugs that block its synthesis, such as penicillins, are among the safest antibiotics we have. The capsule is why people without a working spleen are at risk from encapsulated organisms such as *Streptococcus pneumoniae*.

### Two kinds of envelope {#envelopes}

The layers outside the cytoplasmic membrane are called the **cell envelope**. Medically important bacteria have one of two designs, and the Gram stain distinguishes them.

::: definition Gram-positive and Gram-negative envelopes {#def-envelope}
A **Gram-positive** envelope has a cytoplasmic membrane covered by a thick layer of peptidoglycan, roughly 20–80 nm deep. Threaded through it are **teichoic acids** and **lipoteichoic acids**. There is no outer membrane.

A **Gram-negative** envelope has a cytoplasmic membrane, a thin layer of peptidoglycan only a few nanometres thick, and then a second, **outer membrane**. The space between the two membranes is the **periplasm**. The outer leaflet of the outer membrane is made of **lipopolysaccharide** (LPS), and small molecules cross it through protein channels called **porins**.
:::

Lipopolysaccharide has three parts. **Lipid A** anchors it in the membrane and is the toxic part, often called **endotoxin**. A core oligosaccharide links lipid A to the **O antigen**, a long, variable sugar chain used to type strains (for example *Escherichia coli* O157).

The outer membrane matters clinically in three ways. It is a barrier: large molecules such as vancomycin cannot pass through it, which is why vancomycin has no useful activity against Gram-negative bacteria. The periplasm holds enzymes such as β-lactamases right where incoming β-lactam antibiotics arrive. And lipid A released from Gram-negative bacteria is a powerful trigger of inflammation and septic shock, as [[microbiology/bacterial-pathogenesis]] explains.

### Why the Gram stain works {#gram}

::: theorem The Gram stain separates two envelope types {#thm-gram}
In the Gram stain, cells are stained with **crystal violet**, treated with **iodine**, washed with **alcohol or acetone**, and counterstained with **safranin** or neutral red. Bacteria with a thick peptidoglycan wall keep the purple dye and are called **Gram-positive**. Bacteria with a thin wall and an outer membrane lose the purple dye and take up the pink counterstain; they are called **Gram-negative**.
:::

::: proof
Crystal violet enters every cell. Iodine binds to it and forms a large dye–iodine complex inside the cell.

The decolouriser is the decisive step. In a Gram-positive cell, alcohol dehydrates the thick peptidoglycan mesh, which shrinks and traps the large complex inside. In a Gram-negative cell, alcohol dissolves much of the lipid-rich outer membrane, and the thin peptidoglycan layer cannot hold the complex, so the purple washes out.

The counterstain then colours every cell pink, but in Gram-positive cells the pink is hidden by the deeper purple. The result is two colours that report one structural difference.
:::

The stain fails in predictable ways. Over-decolourising makes Gram-positive bacteria look Gram-negative. Old cultures and bacteria damaged by antibiotics may stain unevenly. Some bacteria barely stain at all, and those exceptions are worth learning as a group.

::: warning Bacteria that the Gram stain cannot show
A Gram stain that shows no organisms does not rule out bacterial infection. Several important bacteria stain poorly or not at all:

- **mycobacteria**, whose waxy wall rich in **mycolic acids** resists the dyes; they need an acid-fast stain (Ziehl–Neelsen or auramine);
- **Mycoplasma**, which has no cell wall at all;
- **Chlamydia** and **Rickettsia**, which live inside human cells;
- **Legionella**, which stains faintly;
- **spirochaetes** such as *Treponema pallidum*, which are too thin to see by light microscopy.

When one of these is suspected, the request must say so, because the laboratory will use a different stain, culture method, antigen test or PCR.
:::

### Shape, arrangement and oxygen {#classification}

After the Gram reaction, the next things a microbiologist reports are **shape** and **arrangement**. Round cells are **cocci**, rod-shaped cells are **bacilli**, and curved or spiral forms include vibrios and spirochaetes. Cocci that divide in one plane form pairs or **chains** (streptococci); cocci that divide in several planes form **clusters** (staphylococci).

::: definition Oxygen requirements {#def-oxygen}
Bacteria are grouped by how they use oxygen:

- **obligate aerobes** need oxygen (*Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*);
- **facultative anaerobes** grow with or without oxygen (*E. coli*, *Staphylococcus aureus*); most common pathogens are in this group;
- **obligate anaerobes** are harmed by oxygen and grow only without it (*Bacteroides fragilis*, *Clostridium* species);
- **microaerophiles** need oxygen at low concentration (*Campylobacter*, *Helicobacter pylori*).
:::

Oxygen preference predicts where organisms live and cause disease. Obligate anaerobes dominate the colon and the gaps between teeth and gums. They cause infection when tissue loses its blood supply or when bowel contents leak, so they are typical in abscesses, bowel perforation, aspiration pneumonia and necrotic wounds. Anaerobic infections often smell foul, and specimens for anaerobes must be sent as pus or tissue rather than dry swabs.

## Growth and spores {#growth}

Bacteria reproduce by **binary fission**: one cell copies its chromosome, grows, and divides into two identical daughters. The time taken for a population to double is the **generation time** or doubling time.

::: definition Generation time and the growth curve {#def-growth}
If a population starts with $N_0$ cells and doubles every $g$ minutes, then after $t$ minutes it contains

$$
N = N_0 \cdot 2^{t/g}
$$ {#eq-growth}

The number of generations is $n = t/g = \log_2(N/N_0)$.

In a closed culture, growth follows four phases:

1. **lag phase**, while cells adapt to the new medium and do not yet divide;
2. **exponential (log) phase**, when growth follows [[#eq-growth]];
3. **stationary phase**, when nutrients run out and waste accumulates, so division balances death;
4. **death phase**, when viable numbers fall.
:::

Generation times differ widely. *E. coli* can double in about 20 minutes in rich broth. *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* takes roughly 15–20 hours, and *Mycobacterium leprae* about two weeks. Generation time explains much of clinical practice. A blood culture with *E. coli* usually becomes positive within a day. Mycobacterial cultures are held for six weeks or more before being reported as negative. Slow-growing organisms are also hard to kill: tuberculosis treatment lasts months, partly because antibiotics act best on dividing cells.

Generation times in the body are longer than in broth, because nutrients are limited and the immune system kills bacteria as they grow. The equation is still useful for estimating how quickly an untreated infection can expand, and why an hour's delay in treating sepsis matters.

::: example How many cells after three hours? {#ex-growth}
A urine sample containing $10^3$ *E. coli* per mL is left on a warm laboratory bench for three hours before it is refrigerated. Assume a generation time of 20 minutes and no lag phase. How many bacteria per mL will be present, and why does this matter?
::: solution
The number of generations is $n = 180/20 = 9$.

$$
N = 10^3 \times 2^9 = 10^3 \times 512 = 5.12 \times 10^5 \text{ per mL}
$$

A sample from a person with no infection now looks like one with a heavy infection, because counts above $10^5$ per mL have traditionally been used to suggest urinary infection. This is why urine must reach the laboratory quickly or be refrigerated, or be collected into a container with a preservative such as boric acid. A wrong result here is a pre-analytical error: the laboratory measured accurately, but the specimen had changed before it arrived.
:::
:::

### Endospores {#spores}

::: definition Endospore {#def-spore}
An **endospore** is a dormant cell made inside a bacterium when nutrients run short. It has a dehydrated core, a thick protective cortex and a tough protein coat. Spores have no measurable metabolism and can survive for years.

Two medically important groups form spores:

- **aerobic** spore formers: *Bacillus* (*B. anthracis*, *B. cereus*);
- **anaerobic** spore formers: *Clostridium* (*C. tetani*, *C. botulinum*, *C. perfringens*) and *Clostridioides difficile*.
:::

Spores resist heat, drying, radiation and many disinfectants, including alcohol. When conditions improve they **germinate** back into growing cells. These properties have direct clinical consequences:

- *C. difficile* spores survive on ward surfaces and on hands cleaned only with alcohol rub, so soap-and-water hand washing and chlorine-based cleaning are used during outbreaks;
- *C. tetani* spores in soil enter wounds and germinate where tissue is dead and oxygen is low;
- *C. botulinum* spores survive inadequate heating of preserved food;
- *B. cereus* spores in cooked rice survive boiling, then germinate and make toxin if rice is kept warm.

Sterilisation methods, such as steam under pressure in an autoclave, are designed and validated to kill spores. They are the benchmark for medical sterilisation, as [[microbiology/infection-control]] describes.

::: example Paralysis after a home-preserved meal {#ex-botulism}
A 46-year-old woman develops double vision, drooping eyelids and difficulty swallowing 24 hours after eating vegetables she had bottled at home. Over the next day the weakness spreads to her arms. She is alert, has no fever and her sensation is normal. Her husband, who ate the same meal, develops similar symptoms. Explain the microbiology.
::: solution
The picture is **foodborne botulism**: a symmetrical, descending flaccid paralysis starting in the cranial nerves, with normal consciousness and sensation, in two people who shared a meal.

The chain of events depends on spores. *C. botulinum* spores from soil contaminated the vegetables. Home bottling of low-acid food heated it enough to kill competing organisms, but not enough to destroy the spores. The sealed jar was then anaerobic, so the spores germinated and the bacteria produced **botulinum neurotoxin**. The toxin, not infection, causes the disease: it blocks release of acetylcholine at nerve terminals, and the toxin was already present in the food when it was eaten.

Management follows national guidance. Botulism is a public health emergency, so suspected cases are notified at once to find the food source and other people at risk. Treatment is supportive care, close monitoring of breathing because respiratory failure can develop, and **antitoxin** as early as possible. Antitoxin neutralises circulating toxin but cannot reverse toxin already bound to nerves, which is why early treatment matters. Recovery requires new nerve terminals to grow and can take weeks to months.
:::
:::

## Bacterial genetics {#genetics}

Bacteria are haploid and divide quickly, so a new mutation can spread through a population within hours if it gives a growth advantage. Mutation alone explains some resistance, such as the step-wise resistance of *M. tuberculosis* to individual drugs. But much of bacterial evolution happens by acquiring genes that already exist in other bacteria.

::: definition Horizontal gene transfer {#def-hgt}
**Horizontal gene transfer** is the movement of DNA between bacteria other than from parent to daughter cell. There are three mechanisms:

- **Transformation**: a bacterium takes up free DNA from its surroundings, released by dead cells. *S. pneumoniae* and *Neisseria* do this naturally.
- **Transduction**: a **bacteriophage** (a virus that infects bacteria) carries bacterial DNA from one cell to another.
- **Conjugation**: two bacteria make contact through a pilus, and one passes a copy of a plasmid to the other. Conjugative plasmids can move between different species.

Mobile genetic elements make these transfers efficient. **Transposons** can jump between chromosome and plasmid. **Integrons** capture resistance genes as cassettes and line them up for expression.
:::

Transfer by phage explains why some bacteria are dangerous only when they carry a particular virus. The gene for **diphtheria toxin** is carried by a phage; a strain of *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* without the phage does not make toxin. The genes for **Shiga toxin** in *E. coli* O157 and for **cholera toxin** in *Vibrio cholerae* are also phage-borne. A phage that integrates into the bacterial chromosome and changes the host's properties in this way causes **lysogenic conversion**.

Conjugation explains the speed of modern antibiotic resistance. A single plasmid can carry genes for resistance to several antibiotic classes at once. Using any one of those antibiotics then selects for the whole plasmid. Plasmids carrying extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) or carbapenemase genes can pass from *E. coli* to *Klebsiella pneumoniae* in the gut of one patient, as [[microbiology/antimicrobials]] describes.

::: example A resistance plasmid on a surgical ward {#ex-plasmid}
Over three months, a surgical ward isolates carbapenem-resistant bacteria from five patients. Two have *K. pneumoniae*, two have *E. coli* and one has *Enterobacter cloacae*. Typing shows the bacteria are unrelated strains, but all carry the same carbapenemase gene on the same plasmid. What is happening, and why does it change the investigation?
::: solution
Different species and unrelated strains carrying an identical plasmid suggest a **plasmid outbreak**: the resistance gene is spreading by conjugation between bacteria, not as a single clone passing from patient to patient.

This changes where the infection control team looks. A clonal outbreak points to transmission of one organism between patients, often on hands or equipment. A plasmid outbreak can also involve a shared environmental reservoir, such as sink drains, where many species meet in biofilm and exchange plasmids. The team would screen contacts, review hand hygiene and cleaning, and sample sinks and drains. Plasmid typing, now usually by whole-genome sequencing, is the tool that reveals this pattern; species identification alone would have missed it.
:::
:::

## The bacteria that cause disease {#pathogens}

A practical classification starts with the Gram stain and shape, then adds oxygen requirement and a few simple tests. In modern laboratories the final identification is usually by **MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry**, which recognises a species from the pattern of its proteins, but the traditional groups still organise clinical thinking. The tables below list the organisms that a medical student should recognise.

::: definition Gram-positive cocci {#def-cocci}
**Staphylococci** form clusters and are **catalase-positive**.

- *Staphylococcus aureus* is **coagulase-positive**. It causes skin and soft-tissue infection, abscesses, bacteraemia, endocarditis, bone and joint infection, and toxin diseases (food poisoning, toxic shock syndrome, scalded skin syndrome). Meticillin-resistant strains are **MRSA**.
- **Coagulase-negative staphylococci**, such as *S. epidermidis*, live on skin. They infect prosthetic material and intravascular lines, and are the commonest contaminants of blood cultures. *S. saprophyticus* causes urinary infection in young women.

**Streptococci** form pairs or chains and are **catalase-negative**. They are grouped by haemolysis on blood agar and by Lancefield surface antigens.

- **β-haemolytic** (complete haemolysis): *S. pyogenes* (group A) causes pharyngitis, cellulitis, necrotising fasciitis and scarlet fever, with rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis as later immune complications; *S. agalactiae* (group B) causes neonatal sepsis and meningitis.
- **α-haemolytic** (partial, green haemolysis): *S. pneumoniae*, seen as lancet-shaped diplococci, causes pneumonia, otitis media, sinusitis and meningitis; the **viridans streptococci** of the mouth cause endocarditis.
- **Enterococci** (*E. faecalis*, *E. faecium*) live in the gut and cause urinary, biliary and line infections. They are intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins.
:::

::: definition Gram-positive rods {#def-gprods}
- **Spore-forming anaerobes**: *Clostridium tetani* (tetanus), *C. botulinum* (botulism), *C. perfringens* (gas gangrene and food poisoning) and *Clostridioides difficile* (antibiotic-associated colitis).
- **Spore-forming aerobes**: *Bacillus anthracis* (anthrax) and *B. cereus* (food poisoning).
- *Listeria monocytogenes*: a foodborne intracellular organism that causes sepsis and meningitis in pregnancy, newborns, older adults and people with impaired cell-mediated immunity. Like enterococci, it is intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins.
- *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*: diphtheria, through a phage-encoded toxin.
- **Branching rods**: *Nocardia* (lung, brain and skin infection in immunocompromised people) and *Actinomyces* (chronic abscesses with sinus tracts).
:::

::: definition Gram-negative bacteria {#def-gneg}
**Cocci**: *Neisseria meningitidis* (meningitis and septicaemia), *N. gonorrhoeae* (gonorrhoea) and *Moraxella catarrhalis* (otitis media, exacerbations of COPD). *Neisseria* appear as kidney-shaped diplococci.

**Rods** fall into several groups:

- **Enterobacterales**, facultative anaerobes of the gut: *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterobacter*, *Proteus*, *Serratia*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella* and *Yersinia*. They cause urinary infection, intra-abdominal infection, bacteraemia and gastroenteritis, and are the main carriers of ESBL and carbapenemase genes.
- **Non-fermenters**, aerobes found in water and hospital environments: *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Acinetobacter baumannii*. They are intrinsically resistant to many antibiotics.
- **Fastidious respiratory rods**: *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Bordetella pertussis* (whooping cough) and *Legionella pneumophila* (Legionnaires' disease).
- **Curved rods**: *Campylobacter* (the commonest bacterial cause of gastroenteritis in the UK), *Helicobacter pylori* (gastritis and peptic ulcer) and *Vibrio cholerae*.
- **Anaerobes**: *Bacteroides fragilis*, the main anaerobe of intra-abdominal abscesses.
:::

::: definition Bacteria outside the Gram scheme {#def-odd}
- **Mycobacteria**: acid-fast rods with mycolic acid walls. *M. tuberculosis* causes tuberculosis, *M. leprae* causes leprosy, and **non-tuberculous mycobacteria** cause lung disease in people with structural lung disease and infections in advanced HIV.
- **Mycoplasma**: the smallest free-living bacteria, with no cell wall. *M. pneumoniae* causes atypical pneumonia and is unaffected by β-lactams.
- **Obligate intracellular bacteria**: *Chlamydia trachomatis* (genital infection and trachoma), *Chlamydia psittaci*, *Rickettsia* (typhus and spotted fevers) and *Coxiella burnetii* (Q fever).
- **Spirochaetes**: thin, spiral, motile bacteria. *Treponema pallidum* causes syphilis, *Borrelia burgdorferi* causes Lyme disease and *Leptospira* causes leptospirosis.
:::

::: example Gram-positive cocci in a blood culture {#ex-staph-bacteraemia}
A 63-year-old man on haemodialysis through a tunnelled central line develops fever and rigors. Two sets of blood cultures are taken. Fourteen hours later both sets flag positive, and the Gram stain shows **Gram-positive cocci in clusters**. What does this tell you, and what happens next?
::: solution
Gram-positive cocci in clusters are **staphylococci**. The two possibilities are *S. aureus* and a coagulase-negative staphylococcus.

Three features point to a real infection rather than contamination: both sets are positive, they flagged quickly, and there is an obvious source in the central line. Until the species is known, the team treats this as possible *S. aureus* bacteraemia and follows local guidance for empirical therapy.

The laboratory then identifies the organism, usually by MALDI-TOF within hours, and tests susceptibility, including whether it is MRSA. If it is *S. aureus*, the management principles are source control (removing the line), repeat blood cultures to show clearance, echocardiography to look for endocarditis, a search for metastatic infection, and specialist infection advice. *S. aureus* bacteraemia carries a high mortality, so it is managed by protocol in most hospitals.

If a coagulase-negative staphylococcus grew in only one of several sets, it would more likely be a skin contaminant. The Gram stain gives the first clue; the number of positive sets and the clinical picture decide what it means.
:::
:::

::: example Gram-negative diplococci in cerebrospinal fluid {#ex-meningococcus}
A 19-year-old student is admitted with fever, headache, neck stiffness and a non-blanching purpuric rash. Antibiotics are given immediately. A lumbar puncture, done later when it is safe, shows many neutrophils, and the Gram stain shows **Gram-negative diplococci**. Interpret the result and explain the public health response.
::: solution
Gram-negative diplococci in cerebrospinal fluid, in a young adult with a purpuric rash, are almost certainly ***Neisseria meningitidis***.

The microbiology explains the clinical course. The meningococcus has a polysaccharide **capsule** that resists phagocytosis, and its outer membrane releases large amounts of endotoxin, which drives septic shock and the rash caused by damaged blood vessels. Because antibiotics were given before the lumbar puncture, culture may be negative; **PCR** of blood or cerebrospinal fluid can still confirm the diagnosis and identify the capsular group.

Meningococcal disease is notifiable in the UK. Public health teams identify close contacts, mainly household members and others with similar close contact in the previous seven days, and offer them antibiotic prophylaxis to clear carriage. Where the capsular group is one covered by a vaccine, contacts may also be offered vaccination. Antibiotic choice follows national guidance.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 22-year-old woman has a dry cough, low-grade fever and patchy changes on her chest X-ray. Her cough does not improve after a week of amoxicillin. Which organism best explains why the β-lactam failed?
- [ ] *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, because its capsule blocks penicillin
- [x] *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, because it has no cell wall
- [ ] *Haemophilus influenzae*, because it is Gram-negative
- [ ] *Staphylococcus aureus*, because it forms clusters
::: solution
β-lactams act by blocking peptidoglycan synthesis. *Mycoplasma* has no cell wall, so they have no target in it. It is a common cause of atypical pneumonia in young adults and is treated with antibiotics that act on protein synthesis or DNA, chosen according to local guidance. The capsule of *S. pneumoniae* resists phagocytosis but not penicillin, and many *H. influenzae* and *S. aureus* strains are resistant only when they make a β-lactamase.
:::
:::

::: history
Hans Christian Gram, a Danish physician working in Berlin, published his staining method in 1884. He was trying to make bacteria stand out in sections of lung from people who had died of pneumonia, and noted modestly that some bacteria did not keep the stain. That observation became the most widely used test in bacteriology.

The study of bacterial genes came later. In 1928 Frederick Griffith showed that a harmless strain of pneumococcus could be "transformed" into a lethal, encapsulated one by material from dead virulent bacteria. In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty identified that material as DNA. Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum described conjugation in 1946, and Norton Zinder and Lederberg described transduction in 1952.

In 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox compared ribosomal RNA sequences and showed that prokaryotes form two very different groups, now called bacteria and archaea. Sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene became a standard way to classify bacteria and to identify organisms that cannot be grown in culture.
:::

How bacteria attach, invade and damage tissue is the subject of [[microbiology/bacterial-pathogenesis]]. The normal bacterial flora is covered in [[microbiology/microbiota]], and the laboratory methods mentioned here, from the Gram stain to MALDI-TOF and sequencing, are explained in [[microbiology/laboratory-diagnosis]]. Antibiotic targets and resistance follow in [[microbiology/antimicrobials]]. ::: summary
- Bacteria are prokaryotes with 70S ribosomes and, in almost all species, a peptidoglycan cell wall; both are antibiotic targets that human cells lack.
- Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan wall and no outer membrane. Gram-negative bacteria have a thin wall and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide, whose lipid A is endotoxin.
- The Gram stain works because the thick wall traps the crystal violet–iodine complex during decolourisation. Mycobacteria, *Mycoplasma*, intracellular bacteria and spirochaetes are not reliably seen.
- Shape, arrangement and oxygen requirement narrow the possibilities: clusters suggest staphylococci, chains suggest streptococci, and foul-smelling pus suggests anaerobes.
- In exponential growth $N = N_0 \cdot 2^{t/g}$. Generation time explains why some cultures take hours and others weeks.
- Endospores of *Bacillus*, *Clostridium* and *C. difficile* survive heat, drying and alcohol, which shapes hand hygiene, cleaning and food safety.
- Transformation, transduction and conjugation move genes between bacteria. Phages carry several major toxin genes, and plasmids spread resistance between species.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Read the Gram stain {level=1}
A urethral swab from a man with purulent discharge shows many neutrophils containing Gram-negative diplococci. What is the most likely organism, and why is this finding so useful?
::: hint
Which Gram-negative cocci form pairs, and which of them is sexually transmitted?
:::
::: solution
The most likely organism is ***Neisseria gonorrhoeae***. Gram-negative diplococci inside neutrophils in a urethral specimen from a man with symptoms are highly specific for gonorrhoea, so microscopy allows treatment to start on the same day. Culture is still needed for susceptibility testing, because gonococcal resistance is common, and nucleic acid amplification tests are used for diagnosis in most settings and for sites such as the throat and rectum.
:::

::: exercise Count the generations {level=1 check="3"}
A culture grows from 200 to 1,600 cells per mL during exponential growth. How many generations have passed?
::: hint
Use $n = \log_2(N/N_0)$.
:::
::: solution
$N/N_0 = 1600/200 = 8$, and $\log_2 8 = 3$. Three generations have passed.
:::

::: exercise Spores and hand hygiene {level=1}
Explain why staff on a ward with several cases of *Clostridioides difficile* infection are asked to wash their hands with soap and water rather than use alcohol hand rub.
::: hint
Think about what alcohol does to spores, and what washing does instead.
:::
::: solution
*C. difficile* is spread by **spores**, which are resistant to alcohol. Alcohol rub kills many vegetative bacteria and enveloped viruses, but it leaves spores alive on the hands. Soap and running water work mechanically: friction and rinsing remove spores from the skin even though they do not kill them. For the same reason, environmental cleaning during an outbreak uses chlorine-releasing agents or other sporicidal products.
:::
:::

::: exercise Estimate a doubling time {level=2 check="30"}
During exponential growth a culture rises from $10^4$ to $10^7$ cells per mL in 5 hours. Estimate the generation time in minutes, rounded to the nearest minute.
::: hint
First find the number of generations from the thousandfold rise, then divide the elapsed time by it.
:::
::: solution
The rise is a factor of 1,000, so the number of generations is $n = \log_2 1000 \approx 9.97$. The elapsed time is 300 minutes, so

$$
g = \frac{300}{9.97} \approx 30.1 \text{ minutes}
$$

The generation time is about **30 minutes**. A handy shortcut is that ten doublings give a factor of about 1,000.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why vancomycin and β-lactams differ {level=2}
Vancomycin is active against *S. aureus* but not against *E. coli*. Amoxicillin is active against many strains of both. Explain the difference using the structure of the cell envelope.
::: hint
Compare the size of the two drugs and the route each must take to reach peptidoglycan.
:::
::: solution
Both drugs act on peptidoglycan synthesis, which takes place on the outer face of the cytoplasmic membrane. In Gram-positive bacteria such as *S. aureus* the peptidoglycan is exposed on the surface, so both drugs can reach it.

In Gram-negative bacteria such as *E. coli*, the peptidoglycan lies beneath an **outer membrane**. Small hydrophilic molecules such as amoxicillin can pass through **porins** into the periplasm. Vancomycin is a large glycopeptide that cannot pass through porins, so it never reaches its target. Gram-negative resistance to amoxicillin usually comes from β-lactamases in the periplasm, which destroy the drug after it has crossed the outer membrane.
:::

::: exercise A strain that gained a toxin {level=2}
A non-toxigenic strain of *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* is isolated from a throat swab. A colleague says it can be ignored because "it can never cause diphtheria". Is this correct?
::: hint
Where is the diphtheria toxin gene carried?
:::
::: solution
Not entirely. The **diphtheria toxin gene** is carried by a bacteriophage. A non-toxigenic strain can become toxigenic if it is infected by the phage and the phage integrates into its chromosome, which is **lysogenic conversion**. Non-toxigenic strains can still cause sore throat and, occasionally, invasive infection, but they do not cause the toxin-mediated disease. This is why reference laboratories test isolates for the toxin gene by PCR and confirm toxin production, and why the public health response depends on whether the isolate is toxigenic, following national guidance.
:::
:::

::: exercise How long does a tuberculosis culture take? {level=3 check="17"}
Assume that a liquid culture system signals a positive result once it holds about $10^7$ bacteria, and that a sputum sample introduces 10 viable *M. tuberculosis* bacilli. With a generation time of 20 hours and no lag phase, how many days will it take to flag positive? Give your answer to the nearest whole day, then comment on what this means for the clinician.
::: hint
The rise is a millionfold. Find the number of generations, multiply by 20 hours, and convert to days.
:::
::: solution
The rise is $10^7/10 = 10^6$, so the number of generations is $n = \log_2 10^6 \approx 19.9$.

$$
t = 19.9 \times 20 \approx 399 \text{ hours} \approx 16.6 \text{ days}
$$

To the nearest day, about **17 days**. The model is simplified: real cultures have a lag phase and many bacilli in a specimen may not be viable, so the observed time is often longer. In practice smear-positive samples may flag within a couple of weeks, while samples with few bacilli can take much longer, and cultures are held for about six weeks before being reported negative.

For the clinician, waiting for culture is too slow to guide initial decisions. Treatment is often started on the basis of the clinical picture, microscopy and rapid molecular tests, which detect *M. tuberculosis* DNA and key resistance mutations within hours. Culture is still essential for full susceptibility testing.
:::

::: exercise Gram stains in neonatal meningitis {level=3}
A 10-day-old baby has fever and a bulging fontanelle. The cerebrospinal fluid has a high neutrophil count. List the three classic bacterial causes of meningitis in this age group, the Gram-stain appearance of each, and explain why one of them affects the choice of empirical antibiotic.
::: hint
Think about organisms acquired from the maternal genital and gut flora during birth, and about intrinsic resistance to cephalosporins.
:::
::: solution
The three classic causes in the first weeks of life are:

- **group B streptococcus** (*Streptococcus agalactiae*): Gram-positive cocci in pairs and chains;
- ***Escherichia coli***, especially strains with the K1 capsule: Gram-negative rods;
- ***Listeria monocytogenes***: short Gram-positive rods, sometimes mistaken for diphtheroids or cocci.

*Listeria* is intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins. A cephalosporin alone would cover group B streptococcus and most *E. coli* but miss *Listeria*, so empirical regimens for meningitis in young infants add a drug such as amoxicillin, following national and local guidance. The Gram stain, if it shows organisms, can then narrow treatment while culture and susceptibility results are awaited.
:::
