The autonomic nervous system controls the heart, blood vessels, airways, gut, bladder, eyes and glands, mostly without our awareness. It uses only a few chemical transmitters and a handful of receptor types, and drugs that mimic or block those transmitters are among the most widely used in medicine: β-blockers, salbutamol, adrenaline, atropine, ipratropium, tamsulosin, the drugs that paralyse muscles during anaesthesia and the drugs that reverse them.

Because the system is so orderly, its pharmacology can be predicted from its physiology. If you know which receptor a tissue uses and what that receptor does, you can work out the main effects and adverse effects of any drug acting there. This chapter sets out that map and then works through cholinergic drugs, neuromuscular blocking drugs and adrenergic drugs. The physiology is covered in more depth in [[physiology/autonomic-nervous-system]], and the receptor principles in [[pharmacology/drug-receptor]].

## The autonomic map {#sec-map}

Both divisions of the autonomic system use a two-neuron chain from the central nervous system to the target organ. The **preganglionic** neuron runs from the brainstem or spinal cord to a ganglion; the **postganglionic** neuron runs from the ganglion to the effector.

::: definition Transmitters and receptors of the autonomic and somatic motor systems {#def-ans}
- In **all autonomic ganglia**, sympathetic and parasympathetic, preganglionic neurons release **acetylcholine** (ACh), which acts on **nicotinic ($\mathrm{N_N}$) receptors**, ligand-gated ion channels.
- **Parasympathetic** postganglionic neurons release ACh onto **muscarinic receptors** (M₁–M₃ are the clinically important subtypes), which are G protein-coupled.
- Most **sympathetic** postganglionic neurons release **noradrenaline** onto **adrenoceptors** (α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, β₃), also G protein-coupled. The exception is the sympathetic supply to most sweat glands, which releases ACh onto muscarinic receptors.
- The **adrenal medulla** is a modified sympathetic ganglion. Its chromaffin cells, stimulated by preganglionic fibres, release mainly **adrenaline** (with some noradrenaline) into the blood.
- **Somatic motor neurons** release ACh onto **nicotinic ($\mathrm{N_M}$) receptors** at the neuromuscular junction of skeletal muscle.

Transmitter action is ended differently. ACh is hydrolysed within milliseconds by **acetylcholinesterase** in the synaptic cleft. Noradrenaline is mainly taken back into the nerve terminal by the **noradrenaline transporter** (NET, "uptake 1"), then recycled into vesicles or broken down by **monoamine oxidase** (MAO); circulating catecholamines are also broken down by **catechol-O-methyltransferase** (COMT).
:::

The receptors that matter most for drug effects are summarised below. A drug's effects follow from which receptors it acts on, and in which tissues.

| Receptor | Main locations | Effect of stimulation |
|---|---|---|
| M₁ | CNS, gastric parietal cells (indirectly) | Cognition; gastric acid secretion |
| M₂ | Heart (sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes) | Slower heart rate and AV conduction |
| M₃ | Smooth muscle of airway, gut and bladder; glands; iris sphincter; vascular endothelium | Bronchoconstriction, gut motility, bladder contraction, secretions, pupil constriction, vasodilation through nitric oxide |
| α₁ | Vascular smooth muscle; iris dilator; bladder neck and prostate | Vasoconstriction, pupil dilation, contraction of bladder outlet |
| α₂ | Presynaptic nerve terminals; brainstem | Reduced noradrenaline release; reduced sympathetic outflow |
| β₁ | Heart; juxtaglomerular cells | Faster rate, greater contractility and conduction; renin release |
| β₂ | Airway smooth muscle; arterioles of skeletal muscle; uterus; liver; skeletal muscle | Bronchodilation, vasodilation, uterine relaxation, glycogenolysis, potassium uptake into cells, tremor |
| β₃ | Bladder detrusor; adipose tissue | Detrusor relaxation; lipolysis |

Most organs receive both divisions, which usually oppose each other. The heart rate at rest is dominated by vagal (parasympathetic) tone, so blocking muscarinic receptors with atropine raises it. Blood vessels, by contrast, are innervated almost only by the sympathetic system, so their tone is controlled by changing sympathetic activity, not by a parasympathetic brake.

## Cholinergic drugs {#sec-cholinergic}

Drugs that increase cholinergic activity are called **cholinomimetics** or parasympathomimetics. Those that block muscarinic receptors are **antimuscarinics** (often called anticholinergics).

::: definition Muscarinic agonists, anticholinesterases and antimuscarinics {#def-muscarinic}
**Direct muscarinic agonists** act on muscarinic receptors. Pilocarpine constricts the pupil and increases drainage of aqueous humour; it is used in some forms of glaucoma and, orally, for dry mouth after radiotherapy.

**Anticholinesterases** inhibit acetylcholinesterase, so ACh accumulates and acts at *all* cholinergic synapses, muscarinic and nicotinic. Reversible inhibitors include neostigmine and pyridostigmine, which do not cross the blood–brain barrier and are used to reverse neuromuscular block and to treat myasthenia gravis; and donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine, which enter the brain and are used in Alzheimer's disease ([[nervous-system/dementia-neurodegeneration]]). **Organophosphates**, used as insecticides and as chemical weapons, inhibit the enzyme effectively irreversibly.

**Antimuscarinics** are competitive antagonists at muscarinic receptors. Examples and uses:

- atropine: symptomatic bradycardia, reducing secretions, organophosphate poisoning;
- glycopyrronium: reducing secretions, and given with neostigmine to block its muscarinic effects; a quaternary amine that does not enter the brain;
- hyoscine hydrobromide (crosses into the brain; motion sickness, secretions at the end of life) and hyoscine butylbromide (does not; gut and bladder spasm);
- ipratropium (short-acting) and tiotropium (long-acting), inhaled bronchodilators for COPD;
- oxybutynin, solifenacin and others for overactive bladder;
- tropicamide and cyclopentolate eye drops to dilate the pupil for examination.
:::

Blocking muscarinic receptors throughout the body produces a predictable pattern: dry mouth and skin, blurred vision from paralysis of accommodation, dilated pupils, tachycardia, constipation, urinary retention, flushing, raised temperature from loss of sweating, and, when the drug enters the brain, confusion and agitation. The traditional mnemonic is "dry as a bone, red as a beet, blind as a bat, hot as a hare, mad as a hatter". Two complications are worth knowing: antimuscarinics can precipitate acute angle-closure glaucoma in eyes with narrow angles, and urinary retention in men with prostatic enlargement.

Many drugs not thought of as anticholinergics also block muscarinic receptors: tricyclic antidepressants, first-generation antihistamines such as chlorphenamine, some antipsychotics, and drugs for bladder instability. In older people the total **anticholinergic burden** is associated with confusion, falls and cognitive decline. Scales such as the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale score each drug 1 to 3 and add the scores; a high total is a prompt to review the medicines ([[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]]).

::: example The confused man with a full bladder {#ex-anticholinergic}
An 84-year-old man is brought to the emergency department with three days of increasing confusion. He has a tender, palpable bladder, a temperature of 37.8 °C, a pulse of 104/min, dry skin and dilated pupils. His medicines include oxybutynin for urinary frequency, amitriptyline for neuropathic pain (recently increased), chlorphenamine for itch, and furosemide. Explain the presentation and how the medicines would be reviewed.
::: solution
His findings form an antimuscarinic (anticholinergic) syndrome: confusion, urinary retention, tachycardia, dry skin, mild hyperthermia from loss of sweating, and mydriasis. Three of his medicines are strongly antimuscarinic. On the ACB scale oxybutynin, amitriptyline and chlorphenamine each score 3 and furosemide 1, a total of 10; a score of 3 or more is generally considered clinically relevant. Older people are particularly vulnerable because cholinergic reserve in the brain falls with age, and the recent rise in amitriptyline increased his exposure. Urinary retention itself can worsen confusion, and the oxybutynin intended for his bladder has contributed to the retention.

Management principles: catheterise to relieve the retention, look for other causes of delirium (infection, dehydration, constipation, electrolytes) and stop or reduce the antimuscarinic drugs where possible, with alternatives considered for each indication. The review should be done with the person and his carers, because some of these drugs were started for real symptoms. Delirium care follows [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]].
:::
:::

### Organophosphate poisoning

Organophosphate pesticide poisoning is a major cause of death from self-poisoning worldwide, particularly in rural Asia, and also follows occupational exposure. Inhibition of acetylcholinesterase causes ACh to accumulate at every cholinergic synapse. **Muscarinic** effects include profuse secretions (salivation, sweating, lacrimation, bronchorrhoea), bronchoconstriction, vomiting, diarrhoea, urinary incontinence, bradycardia and small pupils. **Nicotinic** effects at the neuromuscular junction include muscle fasciculation followed by weakness and paralysis, including of the respiratory muscles. **Central** effects include confusion, seizures and coma. Death usually results from respiratory failure, from secretions, bronchospasm, muscle weakness and central depression together.

The principles of management are resuscitation with attention to the airway and oxygenation, decontamination with protection of staff, and **atropine** given in escalating doses until the chest is clear of secretions, the heart rate is adequate and the blood pressure maintained. Atropine blocks muscarinic effects but does nothing for the nicotinic weakness, which may need ventilatory support. Oximes such as pralidoxime can reactivate the enzyme if given before it "ages" into a permanently inactive form, but trials have not shown a consistent benefit, and their use follows local toxicology advice ([[emergency-critical-care/poisoning]]).

## Drugs at the neuromuscular junction {#sec-nmj}

::: definition Neuromuscular blocking drugs {#def-neuromuscular}
**Neuromuscular blocking drugs** produce skeletal muscle relaxation for tracheal intubation and surgery by acting at the nicotinic $\mathrm{N_M}$ receptor. They have no effect on consciousness or pain, so they are only ever given with adequate anaesthesia.

- **Non-depolarising** blockers, such as rocuronium, atracurium and vecuronium, are competitive antagonists at the nicotinic receptor. Their block can be reversed by raising ACh at the junction with an anticholinesterase (neostigmine), given with glycopyrronium or atropine to prevent the muscarinic effects of the extra ACh, especially bradycardia. Rocuronium and vecuronium can also be reversed by **sugammadex**, a cyclodextrin that encapsulates the drug molecule and removes it from the receptor.
- The **depolarising** blocker **suxamethonium** is an agonist that opens the channel and depolarises the end-plate, producing brief muscle fasciculation, and then keeps it depolarised so that the muscle cannot respond. It has a very fast onset and short action because it is hydrolysed by plasma **butyrylcholinesterase**.
:::

Suxamethonium has several hazards that follow from its mechanism. Depolarisation of muscle releases potassium; the rise is small in healthy people but can be dangerous where receptors have proliferated outside the end-plate, as after burns, crush injury or denervation. It is a trigger of **malignant hyperthermia** in susceptible people. And in people with inherited butyrylcholinesterase deficiency, its action can last hours instead of minutes ("suxamethonium apnoea"), requiring continued ventilation and sedation until it wears off ([[pharmacology/patient-variation]]).

## Adrenergic drugs {#sec-adrenergic}

Drugs that stimulate adrenoceptors are **sympathomimetics**. They act directly on receptors, or indirectly by releasing noradrenaline or blocking its reuptake. Drugs that block adrenoceptors are α-blockers and β-blockers.

::: definition Adrenergic agonists and antagonists {#def-adrenergic}
**Direct agonists** and their main clinical uses:

- **adrenaline** (α and β): anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest, and with local anaesthetics to slow their absorption;
- **noradrenaline** (mainly α₁, some β₁): vasopressor in septic and other forms of distributive shock;
- **dobutamine** (mainly β₁): inotrope in cardiogenic shock;
- **isoprenaline** (β₁ and β₂, no α): bradycardia and heart block awaiting pacing;
- **salbutamol** and **terbutaline** (β₂, short-acting) and **salmeterol** and **formoterol** (β₂, long-acting): asthma and COPD;
- **phenylephrine** and **metaraminol** (α₁): hypotension during anaesthesia; phenylephrine also as a mydriatic and nasal decongestant;
- **clonidine** (α₂, central): reduces sympathetic outflow; used in some pain and withdrawal states;
- **mirabegron** (β₃): overactive bladder.

**Indirect sympathomimetics** release noradrenaline from nerve terminals (amphetamines, ephedrine, pseudoephedrine) or block its reuptake (cocaine, and to a lesser extent some antidepressants).

**α-blockers**: selective α₁ antagonists such as doxazosin (hypertension) and tamsulosin (benign prostatic enlargement); phenoxybenzamine, an irreversible non-selective α antagonist, used before surgery for phaeochromocytoma.

**β-blockers**: β₁-selective ("cardioselective") drugs such as bisoprolol, atenolol, metoprolol and nebivolol; non-selective drugs such as propranolol and timolol; and drugs with additional α₁ blockade, carvedilol and labetalol.
:::

### Adrenaline in anaphylaxis

Adrenaline is the first treatment for anaphylaxis because it reverses each life-threatening component through a different receptor: α₁ stimulation constricts blood vessels and reduces mucosal oedema; β₁ stimulation increases cardiac output; β₂ stimulation relaxes bronchial smooth muscle and reduces further mediator release from mast cells. As of the Resuscitation Council UK guidance (2021), the adult dose is 500 micrograms intramuscularly (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL adrenaline), into the anterolateral thigh, repeated after 5 minutes if there is no improvement, with smaller doses for children. Intramuscular injection is preferred to subcutaneous injection because absorption from muscle is faster and more reliable. Intravenous adrenaline is reserved for specialist use with monitoring, because rapid intravenous doses can cause arrhythmias and severe hypertension ([[emergency-critical-care/anaphylaxis]]).

People taking β-blockers may respond poorly to adrenaline in anaphylaxis, because its β effects are competitively blocked, and the unopposed α effect can raise blood pressure while the bronchospasm persists. Glucagon, which raises cyclic AMP through its own receptor, is sometimes used in this situation on specialist advice.

### β-blockers in practice

β-blockers reduce heart rate, contractility and renin release. Their uses include angina, secondary prevention after myocardial infarction, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (bisoprolol, carvedilol, nebivolol and modified-release metoprolol, started at low dose and increased slowly), rate control in atrial fibrillation, hypertension (not a first-line choice in NICE guidance, but used where there are other indications), symptomatic treatment of thyrotoxicosis, essential tremor, migraine prophylaxis, anxiety with prominent physical symptoms, prevention of variceal bleeding in portal hypertension, and, as eye drops, glaucoma.

Their adverse effects follow from the receptor map:

- **bronchospasm** from β₂ blockade, which is why β-blockers are generally avoided in asthma; cardioselective drugs are used with care in COPD, where they are usually well tolerated;
- **bradycardia and heart block** from β₁ blockade, made worse by verapamil or diltiazem; intravenous verapamil with a β-blocker can cause asystole;
- **cold peripheries** and worsening of Raynaud's phenomenon, from loss of β₂ vasodilation;
- **fatigue**, and with lipophilic drugs such as propranolol, **sleep disturbance and nightmares**;
- **reduced awareness of hypoglycaemia**, because tremor and palpitations are β-mediated, and slower recovery from it in people treated with insulin;
- **rebound** angina or arrhythmia if long-term treatment is stopped abruptly, from receptor up-regulation;
- acute worsening of heart failure if a β-blocker is started at too high a dose in decompensated heart failure.

::: example The glaucoma drop that caused wheeze {#ex-timolol-wheeze}
A 70-year-old woman of Black Caribbean heritage with mild asthma, well controlled for years on a low-dose inhaled corticosteroid, is started on timolol eye drops for open-angle glaucoma. Over the following weeks she becomes breathless and wheezy, with a peak flow 30% below her usual value and a resting pulse of 52/min. How can eye drops cause this?
::: solution
Timolol is a non-selective β-blocker. Each drop that is not absorbed by the eye drains through the nasolacrimal duct into the nose, where it is absorbed across the nasal mucosa directly into the systemic circulation, avoiding first-pass metabolism in the liver ([[pharmacology/absorption-distribution]]). Systemic concentrations can be high enough to block β₂ receptors in the airways, causing bronchoconstriction in someone with asthma, and β₁ receptors in the heart, causing bradycardia.

Principles: the drops should be reviewed with the ophthalmology team and an alternative chosen, for example a prostaglandin analogue, which does not affect the airway. Teaching nasolacrimal occlusion (pressing over the inner corner of the eye for a minute after instilling the drop) reduces systemic absorption of any eye drop. Topical β-blockers are generally avoided in people with asthma or a history of bronchospasm, as the BNF advises. Her asthma treatment should be reviewed and stepped up if needed while the effect wears off.
:::
:::

::: example Alpha before beta {#ex-phaeo}
A 41-year-old man is found to have a phaeochromocytoma, a catecholamine-secreting tumour of the adrenal medulla, after episodes of headache, sweating and palpitations with severe hypertension. Before surgery he is started on phenoxybenzamine and, a few days later, propranolol. Explain the order, and what could happen if a β-blocker were given first.
::: solution
The tumour releases large amounts of noradrenaline and adrenaline. Their α₁ effects cause intense vasoconstriction and hypertension; β₁ effects cause tachycardia; β₂ effects in skeletal muscle arterioles cause some vasodilation that partly offsets the α constriction.

Phenoxybenzamine blocks α-receptors irreversibly, so the block cannot be overcome by surges of catecholamine from the tumour, and it lasts until new receptors are made. As vasoconstriction is relieved, the contracted plasma volume re-expands (fluid and salt intake are encouraged). Once α-blockade is established, a β-blocker can be added to control the reflex tachycardia that α-blockade and catecholamines produce.

If a β-blocker were given first, it would remove the β₂-mediated vasodilation while leaving α₁ vasoconstriction **unopposed**. Peripheral resistance would rise further, and a hypertensive crisis, with risks of stroke, myocardial infarction and acute heart failure, could follow. The rule "alpha before beta" follows directly from the receptor map.
:::
:::

### Competitive β-blockade, quantified

β-blockers are competitive antagonists, so their effect on the response to an agonist follows the dose ratio $DR = 1 + [B]/K_B$ ([[pharmacology/drug-receptor]]). The figure shows heart rate against the dose of the β-agonist isoprenaline, with and without a β-blocker.

::: widget plot

x: -2, 4
y: 50, 190
sliders: P=10:0:300:1
labels: \text{isoprenaline alone}; \text{isoprenaline + propranolol}
caption: Illustrative heart rate (beats/min) against log dose of isoprenaline, with dose expressed in multiples of the dose giving half the maximal rise ($x = 0$). $P$ is the propranolol concentration as a multiple of its $K_B$. The curve shifts right by $\log_{10}(1 + P)$ with the same maximum, so a large enough dose of agonist overcomes the block. At high $P$ the dose needed may be impractical, which is why other drugs are used in β-blocker overdose.

::: example How much more agonist? {#ex-iso-dose-ratio}
In an illustrative experiment in volunteers, the free plasma concentration of propranolol is 20 nmol/L, and its $K_B$ at cardiac β₁ receptors is taken as 2 nmol/L. By what factor must the dose of isoprenaline be increased to produce the same rise in heart rate as before propranolol?
::: solution
$DR = 1 + 20/2 = 11$. The dose of isoprenaline must be increased 11-fold, a shift of $\log_{10} 11 \approx 1.04$ log units. Because the maximum is unchanged, the full response is still available at a high enough agonist dose; this is the surmountable block of [[pharmacology/drug-receptor]].
:::
:::

::: warning Selectivity is relative
"Cardioselective" β-blockers block β₂ receptors too at higher doses and in susceptible people. Tamsulosin is "uroselective" for α₁A receptors in the prostate, yet it causes postural hypotension, and it is linked to floppy iris syndrome during cataract surgery, so surgeons need to know about it. Glycopyrronium does not cross the blood–brain barrier, but it still causes dry mouth, urinary retention and tachycardia. When predicting effects, assume that a drug may act on every receptor of its type that it can reach, and that selectivity narrows as the dose rises.
:::

::: quiz
A 25-year-old woman with asthma is wheezy and is given repeated nebulised salbutamol. An hour later she has a tremor and a pulse of 120/min, and her serum potassium has fallen from 4.2 to 3.1 mmol/L. Which receptor explains all three effects?
- [ ] α₁
- [ ] β₁ only
- [x] β₂ (with some β₁ contribution to the tachycardia)
- [ ] M₃
::: solution
Salbutamol is a β₂ agonist. β₂ receptors in skeletal muscle mediate tremor; β₂ stimulation drives potassium into cells through the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, lowering serum potassium; and tachycardia results from β₂-mediated vasodilation with reflex sympathetic activation, from direct stimulation of β₂ receptors in the heart, and, at high doses, from loss of selectivity with some β₁ stimulation. Potassium should be monitored during intensive bronchodilator treatment, especially with corticosteroids, diuretics or theophylline.
:::
:::

::: history
The chemical nature of nerve transmission was demonstrated by Otto Loewi in 1921. He stimulated the vagus nerve of an isolated frog heart, slowing it, and transferred the fluid bathing it to a second heart, which also slowed: a "Vagusstoff", later shown to be acetylcholine, had been released. Henry Dale had already characterised the muscarinic and nicotinic actions of acetylcholine, and Dale and Loewi shared the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Ulf von Euler identified noradrenaline as the transmitter of sympathetic nerves in 1946 and shared the 1970 Nobel Prize. In 1948 Raymond Ahlquist proposed α- and β-adrenoceptors from the different orders of potency of six catecholamines on different tissues, an idea that was largely ignored until James Black used it to design β-blockers. Black's propranolol entered clinical use in the mid-1960s, and he shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the principles of drug design that led to it and to the H₂ antagonist cimetidine.
:::

::: summary
- Preganglionic neurons of both divisions release ACh onto nicotinic receptors; parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release ACh onto muscarinic receptors; most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release noradrenaline onto adrenoceptors; the adrenal medulla releases adrenaline into the blood.
- The receptor map (M₂ heart, M₃ smooth muscle and glands, α₁ vessels, α₂ presynaptic, β₁ heart, β₂ airways and muscle vessels, β₃ bladder) predicts drug effects and adverse effects.
- Anticholinesterases raise ACh at all cholinergic synapses; organophosphate poisoning produces muscarinic, nicotinic and central effects, and atropine treats the muscarinic ones.
- Antimuscarinic effects add up across drugs; the anticholinergic burden in older people causes confusion, retention and falls.
- Non-depolarising neuromuscular blockers are competitive and reversible with neostigmine (plus glycopyrronium) or, for rocuronium and vecuronium, sugammadex; suxamethonium depolarises and has specific hazards.
- Adrenaline treats anaphylaxis through α₁, β₁ and β₂ receptors; the adult IM dose in UK guidance is 500 micrograms. β-blockers have broad uses and predictable adverse effects; give α-blockade before β-blockade in phaeochromocytoma.
:::

::: exercise Predict the effects of atropine {level=1}
A healthy adult is given an intravenous dose of atropine sufficient to block muscarinic receptors throughout the body. Predict the effect on (a) heart rate, (b) pupils and near vision, (c) salivation, (d) bladder function, (e) body temperature on a warm day.
::: solution
(a) Heart rate rises, because resting vagal tone (M₂) is removed. (b) Pupils dilate (loss of M₃-mediated sphincter contraction) and near vision blurs (paralysis of the ciliary muscle). (c) Salivation falls, giving a dry mouth. (d) Bladder contraction is reduced, so urinary retention may occur. (e) Temperature may rise because sweating, which is mediated by muscarinic receptors, is reduced.
:::

::: exercise An anticholinergic burden score {level=1 check="7"}
An 80-year-old woman takes solifenacin (ACB score 3), paroxetine (3) and an older antihistamine, chlorphenamine, at night (3). Her GP stops the chlorphenamine and adds a medicine with an ACB score of 1. What is her new total ACB score?
::: solution
Original total $= 3 + 3 + 3 = 9$. Stopping chlorphenamine removes 3 and the new medicine adds 1: $9 - 3 + 1 = 7$. A total of 3 or more is generally considered clinically relevant, so further review of the solifenacin and paroxetine would be reasonable.

::: exercise A dose ratio for β-blockade {level=2 check="16"}
A patient's free concentration of a competitive β₁ antagonist is 30 nmol/L, and the drug's $K_B$ is 2 nmol/L. By what factor must the concentration of a β₁ agonist be increased to give the same response as in the absence of the antagonist?
::: solution
$DR = 1 + [B]/K_B = 1 + 30/2 = 16$. The agonist concentration must be increased 16-fold.
:::

::: exercise Reversing neuromuscular block {level=2}
At the end of an operation, a patient who received rocuronium still has residual neuromuscular block. Explain (a) how neostigmine reverses the block, (b) why glycopyrronium is given with it, and (c) how sugammadex works differently, including one practical advantage.
::: solution
(a) Rocuronium is a competitive antagonist at nicotinic $\mathrm{N_M}$ receptors. Neostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase, so ACh accumulates in the synaptic cleft and outcompetes rocuronium for the receptors.

(b) Neostigmine also raises ACh at muscarinic synapses, causing bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, salivation and gut hypermotility. Glycopyrronium blocks these muscarinic effects; it does not cross the blood–brain barrier, so it does not cause central effects.

(c) Sugammadex is a modified cyclodextrin that encapsulates rocuronium (and vecuronium) molecules in plasma, lowering the free concentration; drug then moves away from the junction down its concentration gradient. It does not act on cholinesterase, so no antimuscarinic drug is needed, and it can reverse even deep block, which neostigmine cannot do because enzyme inhibition has a ceiling.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why alpha before beta? {level=2}
Explain, in terms of receptors, why a non-selective β-blocker given alone to a person with a phaeochromocytoma, or to a person intoxicated with cocaine, may make hypertension worse.
::: solution
In both conditions circulating or synaptic catecholamines are very high. They act on α₁ receptors to constrict arterioles and on β₂ receptors in skeletal muscle arterioles to dilate them, partly offsetting the constriction. A non-selective β-blocker removes the β₂ vasodilation and leaves α₁ vasoconstriction unopposed, so peripheral resistance and blood pressure can rise further, with risk of stroke, myocardial ischaemia and heart failure. In phaeochromocytoma, α-blockade is therefore established first. In cocaine toxicity, β-blockers are generally avoided acutely, and benzodiazepines and vasodilators are used according to toxicology advice.
:::
:::

::: exercise A farm worker with small pupils {level=3}
A 34-year-old agricultural worker in South Asia is brought to hospital an hour after a pesticide spill. He has pinpoint pupils, profuse sweating and salivation, crackles throughout both lungs, vomiting, diarrhoea, a pulse of 48/min and widespread muscle twitching, and his respiratory effort is weakening. (a) Group these findings by receptor type. (b) Explain why atropine improves some findings but not others. (c) What do you expect the red cell acetylcholinesterase activity to show, and what is the significance of "ageing"?
::: hint
Separate muscarinic, nicotinic and central effects.
:::
::: solution
(a) Muscarinic: miosis, sweating, salivation, bronchorrhoea (crackles) and bronchospasm, vomiting, diarrhoea, bradycardia. Nicotinic (neuromuscular junction): fasciculation, then weakness of skeletal and respiratory muscles. Nicotinic effects at autonomic ganglia can also cause tachycardia and hypertension in some patients, so the heart rate is variable. Central: any confusion, seizures or reduced consciousness.

(b) Atropine is a muscarinic antagonist. It dries secretions, relieves bronchospasm and restores the heart rate, which is why doses are titrated to a clear chest, adequate heart rate and blood pressure. It has no action at nicotinic receptors, so it does not reverse fasciculation or muscle weakness; respiratory failure from weakness needs ventilatory support.

(c) Red cell acetylcholinesterase activity would be markedly reduced, confirming exposure. "Ageing" is a chemical change in the phosphorylated enzyme, occurring over hours depending on the compound, after which it cannot be reactivated by oximes. That is the theoretical reason for giving oximes early, although clinical trials of pralidoxime have not shown consistent benefit; management follows toxicology advice.

::: exercise Anaphylaxis in a person taking a β-blocker {level=3}
A 62-year-old man taking bisoprolol for heart failure develops anaphylaxis after an intravenous contrast injection. He receives two doses of intramuscular adrenaline, 500 micrograms each, five minutes apart, with intravenous fluids, but remains hypotensive and wheezy, with a heart rate of 58/min. Explain, using receptor pharmacology, why his response may be poor, and how drugs acting through non-adrenergic receptors could help.
::: solution
Adrenaline's benefits in anaphylaxis depend on β₁ receptors (heart rate and contractility) and β₂ receptors (bronchodilation, mast cell stabilisation) as well as α₁ (vasoconstriction). Bisoprolol is a competitive β₁ antagonist, with some β₂ blockade at higher concentrations, so the dose ratio for adrenaline at β receptors is increased: the same dose produces less cardiac and bronchial effect. α₁ effects are not blocked, so vasoconstriction can occur without the cardiac output and bronchodilation that normally accompany it. Anaphylaxis itself may also cause relative bradycardia.

Glucagon acts through its own $G_s$-coupled receptor on cardiac myocytes to raise cyclic AMP, increasing heart rate and contractility without needing β-receptors: a physiological antagonism that bypasses the block. In refractory anaphylaxis, intravenous adrenaline infusion under specialist supervision and other vasopressors are used in line with Resuscitation Council UK guidance, alongside airway management and fluids. Whether to continue the β-blocker long term is a separate decision, weighing its benefit in heart failure against the risk of future reactions.
