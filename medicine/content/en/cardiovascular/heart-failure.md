Heart failure is where many cardiovascular diseases end. Myocardial infarction, long-standing hypertension, valve disease, cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia can all leave a heart that cannot pump enough blood at normal filling pressures. About one to two in every hundred adults in high-income countries live with it, rising to one in ten over the age of 70, and it is one of the commonest reasons for emergency admission in older people. Its outlook was once compared with that of many cancers. It has improved greatly because of one idea: that the body's own responses to a failing heart, which are helpful in the short term, become harmful in the long term, and that blocking them prolongs life.

This chapter covers the definition and classification of heart failure, its causes, the neurohormonal model of its progression, diagnosis, the treatments that change its course, acute heart failure and the care of people with advanced disease. UK guidance (NICE guideline NG106, *Chronic heart failure in adults*, updated in 2025) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 2021 guideline with its 2023 focused update are followed; management in practice follows current local guidance.

## What heart failure is

::: definition Heart failure {#def-hf}
**Heart failure** is a clinical syndrome of symptoms (such as breathlessness, fatigue and ankle swelling) and signs (such as raised jugular venous pressure, pulmonary crackles and peripheral oedema) caused by a structural or functional abnormality of the heart that results in raised intracardiac pressures or inadequate cardiac output, at rest or on exertion.

It is classified by the **left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)**:
- **heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)**: LVEF 40% or less;
- **heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF)**: LVEF 41–49%;
- **heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)**: LVEF 50% or more, with evidence of structural or functional abnormality and raised filling pressures (for example a raised natriuretic peptide).

Heart failure in someone whose LVEF was 40% or less and has since improved to above 40% is sometimes called **heart failure with improved ejection fraction**.
:::

Heart failure is also described by its time course and severity. **Acute heart failure** is the rapid onset or worsening of symptoms needing urgent treatment, either new (de novo) or a decompensation of chronic heart failure. Symptom severity is graded by the **New York Heart Association (NYHA) class**:

| NYHA class | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| I | No limitation of ordinary physical activity |
| II | Slight limitation: comfortable at rest, ordinary activity causes symptoms |
| III | Marked limitation: less than ordinary activity causes symptoms |
| IV | Symptoms at rest, worse with any activity |

"Left" and "right" heart failure describe which side's congestion dominates. Left-sided failure raises pulmonary venous pressure and causes breathlessness, orthopnoea and pulmonary oedema. Right-sided failure raises systemic venous pressure and causes a raised JVP, peripheral oedema, liver congestion and ascites. The commonest cause of right heart failure is left heart failure; isolated right heart failure suggests lung disease (cor pulmonale), pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension or right ventricular infarction.

## Causes

- **Ischaemic heart disease**: the commonest cause of HFrEF in the UK, through infarction and chronic ischaemia ([[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]).
- **Hypertension**: causes left ventricular hypertrophy and stiffness (a major cause of HFpEF) and, later, dilatation ([[cardiovascular/hypertension]]).
- **Valve disease**: aortic stenosis, mitral and aortic regurgitation ([[cardiovascular/valve-congenital-disease]]).
- **Cardiomyopathies**: dilated (genetic, after myocarditis, alcohol, chemotherapy such as anthracyclines and trastuzumab, peripartum), hypertrophic, restrictive (including amyloidosis, an under-recognised cause of HFpEF in older people) and arrhythmogenic.
- **Arrhythmias**: persistently fast rhythms, such as poorly controlled atrial fibrillation, can cause a **tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy** that recovers when the rate is controlled.
- **High-output states**: severe anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, large arteriovenous fistulas, Paget's disease of bone and beriberi (thiamine deficiency), in which the heart cannot meet an abnormally high demand.
- **Pericardial disease**: constrictive pericarditis, which restricts filling.

In people with known heart failure, a sudden worsening usually has a **precipitant**: myocardial ischaemia or infarction, arrhythmia (especially new atrial fibrillation), infection, uncontrolled hypertension, not taking medicines, excess salt or fluid, kidney injury, anaemia, thyroid disease, or drugs that retain fluid or depress the heart (NSAIDs, some calcium channel blockers, thiazolidinediones, some chemotherapy).

## The neurohormonal model

::: theorem The neurohormonal model of heart failure {#thm-neurohormonal}
When cardiac output falls, the body responds as it would to blood loss: it activates the **sympathetic nervous system**, the **renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system** and **antidiuretic hormone**. These responses support blood pressure and cardiac output in the short term, but sustained activation damages the heart and circulation and drives the progression of heart failure. Drugs that block these systems slow progression and prolong life.
:::

::: proof
Consider the sequence after a large anterior myocardial infarction.

1. **Stroke volume falls.** Baroreceptors sense the reduced arterial pressure, and the kidney senses reduced perfusion.
2. **Sympathetic activation** increases heart rate and contractility and constricts arterioles and veins. Arterial pressure is supported and venous return increases.
3. **Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation** constricts arterioles and makes the kidney retain sodium and water. The rise in blood volume increases end-diastolic volume, so the Frank–Starling mechanism raises stroke volume ([[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle#thm-starling]]).
4. **Antidiuretic hormone** retains water and constricts vessels.

Each response is helpful briefly. Sustained, they become harmful:
- vasoconstriction raises **afterload**, which a failing ventricle cannot overcome, so stroke volume falls further;
- salt and water retention raise **filling pressures** beyond the flat top of the failing heart's Starling curve, producing congestion (pulmonary and peripheral oedema) with little gain in output;
- noradrenaline and angiotensin II are directly toxic to cardiac muscle, causing **hypertrophy, apoptosis and fibrosis**; aldosterone promotes fibrosis of heart and vessels;
- the ventricle dilates and becomes more spherical (**remodelling**), which increases wall tension and oxygen demand (law of Laplace) and stretches the mitral ring, causing **functional mitral regurgitation**;
- tachycardia and catecholamines predispose to **arrhythmias** and sudden death.

The result is a vicious circle in which the compensation itself worsens the failure. The **natriuretic peptides**, released from stretched myocardium, oppose these systems by promoting sodium excretion and vasodilatation, but in heart failure they are overwhelmed.

The model explains a striking pattern in trials. Drugs that increase contractility directly (such as long-term oral inotropes) improved haemodynamics but increased mortality. Drugs that block neurohormones (ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and the combination of an angiotensin receptor blocker with a neprilysin inhibitor) improved survival, even though beta-blockers reduce contractility in the short term.
:::

The figure shows Frank–Starling curves of a normal and a failing ventricle. On the failing curve, a large increase in filling (and therefore congestion) buys very little extra stroke volume. Treatments move the patient in different directions: diuretics move left along the same curve (less congestion, little change in output), while vasodilators and drugs that reverse remodelling move the patient towards a higher curve.

## Symptoms, signs and diagnosis

The typical **symptoms** are breathlessness on exertion, **orthopnoea** (breathlessness lying flat, as blood redistributes from the legs to the chest), **paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea** (waking breathless after an hour or two of sleep), fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance and ankle swelling. Older people may present with confusion, falls, weight loss or nocturnal cough.

The **signs** include tachycardia, raised JVP, a displaced apex beat (in dilated ventricles), a third heart sound, murmurs (of a cause or of functional mitral regurgitation), basal crackles, pleural effusions, hepatomegaly and pitting oedema of the legs or sacrum. None of these is sensitive on its own: many people with heart failure have few signs, and ankle oedema has many other causes.

::: definition Diagnosing heart failure (NICE NG106) {#def-diagnosis}
In a person with suspected heart failure, measure **N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP)**:
- **above 2000 ng/L**: refer urgently for specialist assessment and transthoracic echocardiography within **2 weeks**;
- **400–2000 ng/L**: refer for specialist assessment and echocardiography within **6 weeks**;
- **below 400 ng/L** in an untreated person: heart failure is less likely, though not excluded.

Natriuretic peptide levels are **raised** by age over 70, atrial fibrillation, kidney disease, pulmonary embolism, sepsis and other causes of cardiac strain, and **lowered** by obesity and by treatment with diuretics, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, beta-blockers and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. **Echocardiography** confirms the diagnosis, measures ejection fraction and identifies the cause (wall-motion abnormalities, valve disease, hypertrophy).
:::

Other baseline tests include an ECG (a completely normal ECG makes HFrEF unlikely), chest X-ray (cardiomegaly, pulmonary venous congestion, effusions; and to exclude lung disease), full blood count (anaemia), iron studies (iron deficiency is common and treatable), renal function and electrolytes, liver and thyroid function, HbA1c and lipids. Further tests for the cause include coronary imaging, cardiac MRI (for cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, infiltration and scar) and, where suspected, tests for amyloidosis.

::: example Breathless with a normal ejection fraction {#ex-hfpef}
A 79-year-old woman with long-standing hypertension, type 2 diabetes and obesity (BMI 36) has had a year of increasing breathlessness on exertion and ankle swelling. She has paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. Her NT-proBNP is 1150 ng/L. Echocardiography shows a left ventricular ejection fraction of 62%, concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, a dilated left atrium and raised estimated filling pressures. Is this heart failure, and how is it managed?

::: solution
She has symptoms and signs of heart failure, a raised NT-proBNP (and her obesity lowers natriuretic peptide levels, so the true signal is if anything stronger), and structural evidence of raised filling pressure (hypertrophy, dilated left atrium) with an ejection fraction of 62%. This is **heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)**. With an NT-proBNP of 400–2000 ng/L she should have been referred for specialist assessment within 6 weeks.

The mechanism is a **stiff left ventricle**: hypertrophy, fibrosis and age-related changes reduce relaxation and compliance, so the ventricle needs a high filling pressure to reach a normal volume. That pressure is transmitted to the left atrium and lungs, especially during exercise, when a shorter diastole leaves less time to fill. Atrial fibrillation worsens filling by removing the atrial contribution and shortening some cycles.

Management:
- **Diuretics** for congestion.
- An **SGLT2 inhibitor** (dapagliflozin or empagliflozin), which reduced hospitalisation for heart failure in HFpEF and HFmrEF in the EMPEROR-Preserved and DELIVER trials; the mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone reduced worsening heart failure events in the FINEARTS-HF trial (2024).
- **Treatment of comorbidities**: blood pressure, **atrial fibrillation** (rate or rhythm control, and anticoagulation), diabetes, **obesity** (weight loss improves symptoms), and screening for causes such as cardiac amyloidosis, which is common in older people with HFpEF and has specific treatments.
- **Exercise rehabilitation**.

Unlike HFrEF, there is no evidence that ACE inhibitors or beta-blockers improve survival in HFpEF, though they may be needed for other conditions.
:::
:::

## Treatment of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

Treatment aims to relieve symptoms, prevent admissions and prolong life. The evidence comes from a series of landmark trials, each adding a drug class to the previous standard.

::: definition The core medicines for HFrEF {#def-treatment}
- **ACE inhibitor** (or **sacubitril–valsartan**, an angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor, ARNI). ACE inhibitors reduced mortality in severe heart failure in the CONSENSUS trial (1987) and in later trials. Sacubitril–valsartan, which adds inhibition of neprilysin (the enzyme that breaks down natriuretic peptides) to angiotensin receptor blockade, reduced cardiovascular death and heart failure hospitalisation compared with enalapril in PARADIGM-HF (2014). An ARB is used if ACE inhibitors are not tolerated.
- **Beta-blocker** licensed for heart failure (bisoprolol, carvedilol, or nebivolol in older people). They reduce mortality, including sudden death, despite reducing contractility at first. They are started at a low dose when the patient is stable and increased slowly.
- **Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA)**: spironolactone or eplerenone. Spironolactone reduced mortality in severe HFrEF in the RALES trial (1999).
- **SGLT2 inhibitor**: dapagliflozin or empagliflozin. In DAPA-HF (2019) and EMPEROR-Reduced (2020) they reduced cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure, with or without diabetes.

As of the 2025 update of NICE NG106, people with HFrEF are offered **all four** classes (ACE inhibitor, beta-blocker, MRA and SGLT2 inhibitor), unless contraindicated or not tolerated, titrated to the maximum tolerated doses. Sacubitril–valsartan is used in place of the ACE inhibitor when ACE inhibitors are not tolerated (other than because of angioedema), and switching to it is considered if symptoms persist; the ESC guideline lists sacubitril–valsartan as an alternative first choice to an ACE inhibitor.
:::

Each drug has characteristic problems that guide monitoring:

- ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI and MRAs can cause **hyperkalaemia** and a **rise in creatinine**; renal function and potassium are checked before starting and after each dose change. ACE inhibitors must be stopped at least 36 hours before starting sacubitril–valsartan, because the combination greatly increases the risk of angioedema.
- Beta-blockers can cause bradycardia, fatigue and temporary worsening of congestion during titration.
- SGLT2 inhibitors cause a small early fall in eGFR, genital fungal infections and, rarely, **ketoacidosis with normal or only modestly raised glucose**, particularly during acute illness or fasting.

**Loop diuretics** (furosemide, bumetanide) relieve congestion and are essential for symptoms, but have not been shown to prolong life. The dose is the lowest that keeps the patient free of congestion; daily weighing helps detect fluid retention early.

Further options, usually started by a specialist, include:
- **ivabradine** for people in sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 75 beats/min or more despite a beta-blocker;
- **hydralazine with isosorbide dinitrate**, particularly for people of African or Caribbean family origin with moderate to severe HFrEF;
- **digoxin** for symptoms, or for rate control in atrial fibrillation;
- **intravenous iron** for iron deficiency, which improves symptoms and exercise capacity and reduces admissions.

::: example Nausea and yellow vision {#ex-digoxin}
An 84-year-old woman with HFrEF and atrial fibrillation takes digoxin, bisoprolol, ramipril, spironolactone, dapagliflozin and furosemide. After a week of diarrhoea she has nausea, loss of appetite, confusion and says that lights look yellow. Her pulse is 44 beats/min and regular. Creatinine has risen from 105 to 190 µmol/L and potassium is 5.9 mmol/L. Explain what has happened.

::: solution
The picture suggests **digoxin toxicity** precipitated by **acute kidney injury**.

- Digoxin is cleared mainly by the kidneys. Dehydration from diarrhoea, on top of a diuretic and drugs that reduce glomerular filtration (ramipril, spironolactone, the early effect of dapagliflozin), reduced its clearance, and its concentration rose.
- Typical features of toxicity are gastrointestinal (nausea, anorexia), neurological (confusion) and visual (yellow-green tinting, blurring), with arrhythmias. A **slow, regular pulse in atrial fibrillation** suggests complete heart block with a junctional escape rhythm, a classic digoxin effect.
- **Hyperkalaemia** follows from kidney injury and the potassium-retaining drugs. In acute digoxin poisoning, hyperkalaemia also results from inhibition of the sodium–potassium pump; conversely, low potassium increases digoxin's toxicity.

Management follows local protocols: stop digoxin, check a digoxin level (taken at least 6 hours after the last dose), ECG monitoring, rehydration, correction of electrolytes, and temporarily withholding the drugs that impair renal function or raise potassium. **Digoxin-specific antibody fragments** are used for life-threatening arrhythmias, severe hyperkalaemia or haemodynamic compromise. The case illustrates why "sick day rules" and careful review of polypharmacy matter in older people with heart failure ([[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]]).
:::
:::

**Devices** treat electrical problems. An **implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD)** prevents sudden arrhythmic death in selected people with LVEF of 35% or less. **Cardiac resynchronisation therapy (CRT)**, which paces both ventricles together, improves symptoms and survival in people with a broad QRS complex (particularly left bundle branch block with QRS 150 ms or more) and LVEF of 35% or less.

::: warning Do not stop the life-prolonging drugs at the first sign of a creatinine rise
A small rise in creatinine (up to about 30%) after starting or increasing an ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI or SGLT2 inhibitor is expected, reflecting a change in glomerular pressure rather than kidney damage. Stopping these drugs because of such a rise deprives people of their survival benefit. Larger rises, hyperkalaemia, or deterioration during intercurrent illness need review, and temporary withholding during dehydrating illness ("sick day rules") is often advised. Equally, do not withdraw beta-blockers abruptly during a mild decompensation in a stable patient; reduce the dose only if there is shock or severe bradycardia.
:::

::: example A young man with a dilated heart {#ex-dcm}
A 38-year-old man presents with 3 months of increasing breathlessness and orthopnoea. He drinks about 70 units of alcohol a week. Echocardiography shows a dilated left ventricle with global hypokinesia and an ejection fraction of 25%; coronary angiography is normal. His blood pressure is 108/70 mmHg, pulse 96 beats/min in sinus rhythm, potassium 4.4 mmol/L and eGFR 85. Outline the principles of management.

::: solution
He has **HFrEF due to dilated cardiomyopathy**, probably alcohol-related, though a genetic cause, myocarditis and other causes should be considered (cardiac MRI helps, and family screening may be offered if a genetic cause is suspected).

Principles:
- **Stop alcohol completely**, with support to do so safely (assessment for alcohol withdrawal). Alcoholic cardiomyopathy can improve substantially with abstinence.
- **Diuretic** for congestion.
- **The four core medicines**: ACE inhibitor (or ARNI), beta-blocker, MRA and SGLT2 inhibitor, started at low doses and increased as tolerated, with checks of blood pressure, heart rate, potassium and renal function. His blood pressure is modest, so the order and speed of titration are individualised.
- **Reassess LVEF after about 3 months of optimised treatment.** If it remains 35% or less, an ICD (and CRT if he has a broad QRS) is considered.
- Vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus, exercise rehabilitation, and education about symptoms, daily weights and salt.

His ejection fraction may recover. Even then, guideline treatment is usually continued, because stopping it can lead to relapse.
:::
:::

## Acute heart failure

Acute heart failure is a medical emergency. Initial assessment looks for the **cause or precipitant** (acute coronary syndrome, hypertensive emergency, arrhythmia, acute valve problem, pulmonary embolism, infection, tamponade) and the patient's **haemodynamic profile**, judged at the bedside:

- **"Wet" or "dry"**: signs of congestion (raised JVP, crackles, oedema, orthopnoea) or not.
- **"Warm" or "cold"**: adequate peripheral perfusion, or signs of low output (cool peripheries, confusion, low urine output, narrow pulse pressure, rising lactate).

Most patients are **warm and wet**: well perfused but congested. Treatment centres on **intravenous loop diuretics**, oxygen if hypoxic, non-invasive ventilation (CPAP) for respiratory distress or hypoxia that does not respond to oxygen, and intravenous nitrates when blood pressure is high. Opiates are no longer given routinely. A smaller group is **cold and wet**, with **cardiogenic shock**: they need intensive care, treatment of the cause (such as urgent coronary revascularisation), and may need inotropes, vasopressors or mechanical circulatory support.

::: example Wet and cold {#ex-acute}
A 72-year-old man with known ischaemic HFrEF (ejection fraction 25%) is admitted with severe breathlessness. He is confused. Blood pressure 82/58 mmHg, pulse 118 beats/min, cool mottled peripheries, JVP raised to the earlobe, crackles to the mid-zones, SpO₂ 88% on air. Lactate 4.6 mmol/L, urine output 10 mL/h. Describe his haemodynamic profile and explain why treatment differs from a warm, wet patient.

::: solution
He is **cold and wet**: congested (raised JVP, pulmonary oedema, hypoxia) and poorly perfused (hypotension, cool mottled skin, confusion, high lactate, oliguria). This is **cardiogenic shock**.

In a warm and wet patient, high-dose diuretic and nitrates relieve congestion while perfusion is maintained. Here, cardiac output is too low to perfuse the organs. Nitrates would drop the blood pressure further; diuretics alone may not work because renal perfusion is poor. Management includes:
- **intensive care** and invasive monitoring;
- oxygen and ventilatory support for hypoxia;
- **treating the precipitant**: ECG and troponin to look for an acute coronary syndrome needing urgent revascularisation, and a search for arrhythmia, infection or mechanical complications;
- **inotropic support** (for example dobutamine) to raise cardiac output, sometimes with a vasopressor (noradrenaline) to maintain perfusion pressure;
- diuretics once perfusion is improved;
- consideration of **mechanical circulatory support** in selected patients, and early discussion of goals of care, because mortality is high.

Beta-blockers may need to be reduced or stopped temporarily in shock.
:::
:::

## Advanced heart failure and the end of life

When symptoms persist despite optimal treatment, the options are **heart transplantation** for selected, usually younger, patients; a **left ventricular assist device (LVAD)**, a mechanical pump either as a bridge to transplantation or as long-term therapy; and **palliative care**. The course of heart failure is often unpredictable, with repeated admissions and a risk of sudden death, so conversations about goals and preferences should start early and be revisited. Palliative care focuses on breathlessness, fatigue, oedema, pain, anxiety and low mood, and on planning where the person wishes to be cared for. Deactivating the shock function of an ICD at the end of life is discussed with the person and family in advance ([[geriatrics-palliative/palliative-care]]).

::: solution
**Loop diuretics** relieve congestion and are needed for symptoms, but trials have not shown that they prolong life. Beta-blockers, MRAs and SGLT2 inhibitors, together with ACE inhibitors or sacubitril–valsartan, reduce mortality and hospitalisation in HFrEF.

::: history
William Withering, a physician in Birmingham, published *An Account of the Foxglove* in 1785, describing how a family remedy containing foxglove relieved "dropsy"; digitalis became the main heart failure drug for nearly two centuries. Mercurial and then thiazide and loop diuretics, introduced in the 1950s and 1960s, transformed the relief of congestion. The CONSENSUS trial (1987) showed that an ACE inhibitor, enalapril, reduced mortality in severe heart failure, the first clear evidence that a drug could prolong life in the condition, and set the pattern for the neurohormonal era. Beta-blockers, once considered contraindicated, were shown to reduce mortality in the late 1990s, followed by spironolactone (RALES, 1999), sacubitril–valsartan (PARADIGM-HF, 2014) and SGLT2 inhibitors (DAPA-HF, 2019). Christiaan Barnard performed the first human heart transplant in Cape Town in 1967.
:::

::: exercise Constriction or failure? {level=2}
A 61-year-old man who had radiotherapy for lymphoma in his twenties has raised JVP that rises on inspiration, ascites, leg oedema and clear lung fields. Echocardiography shows normal ventricular size and ejection fraction. What diagnosis should be considered, and why is it important not to miss it?
::: solution
**Constrictive pericarditis**. A thickened, rigid pericardium (here probably from mediastinal radiotherapy; other causes include tuberculosis, previous cardiac surgery and viral pericarditis) prevents the ventricles from filling beyond a fixed volume. Venous pressure is high, the JVP rises on inspiration (**Kussmaul's sign**) and has a steep y descent, and right-sided congestion dominates, while the ventricles themselves contract normally. It can be mistaken for liver disease (ascites) or restrictive cardiomyopathy.

It matters because it is **potentially curable by pericardiectomy**, whereas drugs for heart failure help little. Diagnosis uses echocardiography with respiratory variation of flows, cardiac MRI or CT (pericardial thickening) and, if needed, simultaneous left and right heart catheterisation.
:::

::: exercise Which device? {level=2}
For each patient with HFrEF on optimal medical treatment for 3 months, state whether an ICD, CRT, both or neither is likely to be considered: (a) LVEF 30%, sinus rhythm, QRS 90 ms, NYHA II, ischaemic cause; (b) LVEF 28%, sinus rhythm, left bundle branch block with QRS 168 ms, NYHA III; (c) LVEF 33%, terminal cancer with a prognosis of weeks.
::: solution
(a) **ICD** may be considered to prevent sudden arrhythmic death; CRT would not help because the QRS is narrow.

(b) **CRT** is indicated because of the broad left bundle branch block; usually as a combined **CRT-defibrillator**, subject to individual assessment.

(c) **Neither**: the expected benefit of preventing sudden death is outweighed by the limited prognosis. A device would not be implanted, and an existing one's shock function might be deactivated after discussion.
:::

::: exercise Heart failure in pregnancy {level=3}
A 31-year-old woman develops increasing breathlessness, orthopnoea and leg swelling three weeks after the birth of her second child. Her NT-proBNP is 3400 ng/L and echocardiography shows LVEF 30% with a dilated left ventricle. Discuss the differential diagnosis, the likely diagnosis, and how the usual heart failure treatments must be adapted.
::: hint
Think about which drugs are teratogenic or pass into breast milk, and which complication of a dilated ventricle is particularly relevant in the peripartum state.
:::
::: solution
**Differential diagnosis**: peripartum cardiomyopathy; pre-existing dilated cardiomyopathy unmasked by the haemodynamic load of pregnancy (including genetic forms); myocarditis; myocardial infarction (including spontaneous coronary artery dissection); pulmonary embolism (a common cause of postpartum breathlessness); pre-eclampsia with pulmonary oedema, which can occur after delivery; and amniotic fluid or other rare causes.

The likely diagnosis is **peripartum cardiomyopathy**: heart failure with LVEF below 45% developing towards the end of pregnancy or in the months after delivery, without another cause.

Adaptations:
- **After delivery**, standard HFrEF treatment can be used, with attention to **breastfeeding**: some ACE inhibitors (such as enalapril) and beta-blockers are generally considered compatible, while ARNI, MRAs and SGLT2 inhibitors are usually avoided while breastfeeding, according to specialist and pharmacy advice. **During pregnancy**, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI and MRAs are contraindicated because of fetal harm.
- **Anticoagulation** is considered because of the high risk of left ventricular thrombus and the hypercoagulable peripartum state.
- **Bromocriptine** is used in some centres, on the hypothesis that a cleaved fragment of prolactin is toxic to the heart; it is given with anticoagulation and under specialist care.
- Care is shared between cardiology and obstetric teams. About half of women recover ventricular function, but a further pregnancy carries a risk of relapse, so counselling about **contraception and future pregnancy** is essential ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/postnatal-period]]).
:::
