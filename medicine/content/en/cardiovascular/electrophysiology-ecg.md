The electrocardiogram is the extracellular shadow of the action potentials in this book's physiology chapters: a vector sum of millions of myocyte potentials, recorded at nine electrodes, read as a sequence of clocks and directions. The clinical art has two halves — recognising the patterns that decide minutes of a patient's life (the occlusion myocardial infarction, the hyperkalaemia, the pre-excited atrial fibrillation, the long QT) and reading the chronic signatures (hypertrophy, block, chamber enlargement) that map onto the mechanics of the last chapter — and both halves rest on knowing which cellular event each deflection is.

This chapter covers cardiac electrophysiology, the biophysics of the ECG, and the systematic read with its emergencies.

## From ion channel to ECG deflection

::: definition The action potential families {#def-aps}
Working myocardium (atrial and ventricular myocytes, Purkinje fibres) runs the **fast-response action potential**: upstroke from the **all-or-none sodium current (I_Na)** — phase 0, the ECG's QRS; the **plateau (phase 2)** from L-type calcium current balanced by delayed-rectifier potassium currents — the ST segment; and repolarisation (phases 3–4) — the T wave, whose normal outward-from-endocardium-to-epicardium sequence (epicardial cells repolarise first, short action potential) gives the upright T in most leads. **Nodal tissue** (sinoatrial and atrioventricular node) runs the **slow response**: no stable resting potential, **diastolic depolarisation** from the **funny current I_f (HCN channels — the pacemaker current, accelerated by noradrenaline via cAMP-shifted voltage dependence and braked by acetylcholine and ivabradine)**, threshold reached by T- then L-type calcium channels — hence calcium-channel-blocker bradycardia, and the property that the AV node, not the His-Purkinje system, is the **physiological delay** (decremental, rate-adaptive, vagally controlled — the gate that protects the ventricle from atrial arrhythmias and makes PR prolongation benign at first degree and dangerous when the block sits lower). **Refractoriness** — the effective (no propagated response) and relative (requiring stronger or later stimuli) periods set by sodium-channel recovery — is the tissue property behind the strength–interval curve, the antiarrhythmic drug target, and the reason re-entry needs an excitable gap to sustain itself: the mechanism of most clinical tachycardias (the AV-nodal and accessory-pathway re-entry circuits below, atrial flutter's macro-re-entry, scar-related ventricular tachycardia of [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]).
:::

::: definition Vectors, leads and normals {#def-leads}
Einthoven's triangle and the augmented and precordial leads sample the heart's electrical vector from fixed directions: **the mean QRS axis** (about +60° in adults — determined by which limb leads are positive: left axis deviation the left anterior fascicle and aortic stenosis's left ventricular hypertrophy territory, right axis the child, the dextrocardium's mirror and right ventricular hypertrophy), and **the precordial sequence** (V1–V6 sampling right septum to lateral wall — the anatomical basis of the infarct localisation table below). Normal arithmetic: **rate 60–100** (the 300/large-squares shortcut and its limits in irregular rhythms), **PR 120–200 ms, QRS <120 ms, QTc** (the Bazett- and Fridericia-corrected values against the rate — the 450/460 ms thresholds and the 500 ms torsades cliff), **Q waves** (pathological when ≥40 ms or >25% of the R in two contiguous leads — the completed infarct's window), **ST segments** (the J-point deviations measured to the millimetre that decides reperfusion), and the **P wave** (the left atrial terminal force of mitral disease and hypertensive diastolic failure). The reading discipline is a fixed sequence — **rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, hypertrophy, then ischaemia against the clinical story** — because missed findings cluster in the steps skipped (the STEMI in a patient read as "fast atrial fibrillation", the long QT in a syncope charted as "normal sinus").
:::
::: example The sawtooth at 150 {#ex-flutter}
A 70-year-old with hypertension and breathlessness has a regular ventricular rate of 150; the ECG shows no sinus P waves, and the inferior leads a sawtooth baseline at 300 per minute; after slow intravenous beta-blockade the ventricular rate falls to 75 and the diagram below the trace doubles in clarity.
::: solution
**Atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction unmasked by rate-slowing to 4:1** — the mechanism is a **macro-re-entrant right atrial circuit (cavotricuspid isthmus–dependent, the typical counterclockwise loop)** firing the atrium at ~300/min; the AV node's decremental physiology ([[#def-aps]]) blocks every other beat, and the ventricle sits at 150 — the classic "regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150 in an older adult is flutter until the inferior leads say otherwise". Slowing conduction increases the block ratio and reveals the sawtooth (the flutter waves best seen inferiorly and in V1), the diagnostic manoeuvre that adenosine also performs (transiently, with the caveat that it is a diagnostic, not therapeutic, tool here). Management: **rate control (beta- or non-dihydropyridine calcium-blocker — beware the combination's block), the fact that flutter is harder to rate-control than fibrillation, anticoagulation by the same stroke-risk rules as fibrillation, and the definitive answer that is also this chapter's most successful ablation: cavotricuspid isthmus linear block (>90% cure), with the note that a proportion of these patients later declare atrial fibrillation and need the [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]] long-term plan** ([[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]] takes the pharmacology; the physiology point here is that a macro-re-entry with a fixed rate and a protected isthmus is the ideal catheter target — the circuit's anatomy is the prescription).
:::
:::

::: example The trace that ends the argument {#ex-stemi}
A 58-year-old man, 40 minutes of crushing central pain, sweats: ECG shows 4 mm ST elevation in II, III and aVF with reciprocal depression in I and aVL, and a tall R with ST elevation in V2–V3 at the level of the S-T junction.
::: solution
**Inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction with posterior extension — activation of the catheter laboratory, not a "repeat the ECG in 20 minutes" conversation.** The localisation is anatomical arithmetic: **II/aVF and III = inferior wall, the right coronary territory (dominant) — occlusion proximal to the acute marginal and, with posterior V2–V3 depression-and-tall-R (posterior leads V7–V9 confirm), the right or left circumflex posterior descending depending on dominance; reciprocal change (high lateral depression) is the strongest single ECG clue to true occlusion versus pericarditis and early-repolarisation mimic**. The associated traps this ECG carries: **right ventricular involvement** (inferior MIs: take a V4R — the elevated RV ST segment predicts the preload-dependent hypotension that must not be given nitrates and needs fluid — the [[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]] Guyton argument in the coronary unit), **bradyarrhythmias and AV block** (the right coronary supplies the AV node in most hearts — nodal ischaemia plus the Bezold–Jarisch vagal surge: atropine, temporary pacing standby — [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]]), and the **mechanical complications clock** (papillary muscle rupture, free-wall rupture, septal rupture — days 2–5, each a new murmur and shock, [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]). Management: **primary percutaneous coronary intervention within the network's minute budget (door-to-balloon), aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor and anticoagulation loading, the thrombolytic alternative when the catheter laboratory is beyond the transfer window (the symptom-onset and ECG-evolution arithmetic of [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]])** — and the reading lesson: **occlusion patterns beat time windows; a convincing trace with ongoing pain is occlusion until the angiogram says otherwise**, including the equivalents (posterior, right ventricular, the de novo left bundle-branch and the hyperacute-T occlusion syndromes).
:::

## The blocks, the shortcuts and the dark channels

::: definition AV block and pacing logic {#def-block}
**First-degree** (PR >200 ms with every P conducted) is a conduction-delay marker, benign when nodal (vagal, athletes, beta-blocker) and worth follow-up when infra-His (the wide-QRS first-degree that preannounces higher block). **Second-degree Mobitz type I (Wenckebach)** — progressive PR lengthening then a dropped QRS — is usually **nodal**, vagal or drug-related or inferior-ischaemic, atropine-responsive and temporary. **Mobitz type II and 2:1 and higher-grade block with a wide QRS** — block **infranodal** (His–Purkinje, fibrotic or anterior-infarct), sudden, unreliable atropine, the **permanent-pacemaker indication** whose physiology is the escape hierarchy: junctional 40–60 or ventricular 20–40, fragile below the block. **Third-degree (complete)** — AV dissociation (the cannon a waves of [[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]]'s exercise, the regular bradycardia, the risk of asystolic pauses and the broad-complex escape): **atropine only for nodal block, temporary then permanent pacing for the infra-His, and the modern physiological-pacing refinements (left-bundle-area and His-bundle pacing) that avoid the dyssynchrony of chronic right-ventricular apical pacing** — the same dyssynchrony that makes cardiac resynchronisation therapy the block patient's and heart-failure patient's joint answer. The indications document (the ACC/AHA and NICE pacing guidance) is refractory-physiology law: **symptoms attributable to block, high-grade block with pauses, infranodal block with wide QRS, and the neuromuscular diseases whose conduction disease outpaces their myopathy.**
:::

::: example The palpitations of a fit 24-year-old {#ex-wpw}
A healthy 24-year-old has abrupt-onset regular palpitations at 190; ECG in sinus rhythm after termination shows short PR (90 ms) and a slurred upstroke (delta wave) with secondary T inversion; during the episode the QRS is narrow and regular.
::: solution
**Wolff–Parkinson–White pattern with orthodromic atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia** — the accessory pathway (an embryological muscle bridge surviving the annular fibrosis — [[anatomy/embryology]]'s insulation failure) conducts **anterogradely down the node (narrow QRS, the usual orthodromic circuit) and retrogradely up the pathway**, the re-entry of [[#def-aps]] completed by the pathway's unidirectional block. Management splits by the circuit's direction and the atrium's behaviour: **vagal first, adenosine (the AV-node blocker that terminates the node-dependent circuit — given early, in wide-complex tachycardia, only when the diagnosis is confident and the patient monitored, because the pre-excited atrial fibrillation it can leave unmasked is another, dangerous, story — the irregularly irregular broad-complex rhythm where AV-node blockade is contraindicated (adenosine, beta-blocker, digoxin and verapamil all accelerate the ventricle down the pathway); procainamide or ibutilide or cardioversion there)**. Definitive: **electrophysiology study and catheter ablation of the pathway (the young patient with symptomatic accessory pathway, and the discussion about the high-risk occupation or the pathway's refractory-period profile found incidentally)**, with **sudden-death-cause statistics** the counselling counterweight (the rare first-manifestation ventricular fibrillation during pre-excited fibrillation — the risk-stratification programme's purpose).
:::
:::

::: example The dialysis patient's wide complexes {#ex-hyperk}
A 61-year-old anuric dialysis patient misses two sessions; ECG shows bradycardia 40, loss of P waves, broad bizarre QRS and peaked, narrow-tailed T waves; potassium 8.1.
::: solution
**Hyperkalaemia — the membrane-potential chapter written on the ECG**: partial depolarisation (the raised extracellular potassium shifting E_K — [[physiology/membrane-potentials]]'s Nernst arithmetic) **inactivates sodium channels (the QRS broadening — the "sine wave" is the terminal merging), suppresses atrial and nodal electrical activity (the vanished P, the junctional or idioventricular escape) and hastens repolarisation early (the tented T)** — the progression to arrest is continuous, not stepwise, and **the ECG correlates imperfectly with the number: treat the trace, and treat hard when the trace is this far along.** The order is the physiology: **calcium (gluconate or chloride — the membrane stabiliser that raises threshold excitability within minutes, the direct antagonist at the channel, not a potassium-lowering drug), then insulin–dextrose (and salbutamol nebulised; both intracellular shifts via Na/K-ATPase and its insulin-driven insertion — [[physiology/homeostasis-body-fluids]]'s potassium machinery), then the removal that must eventually happen (dialysis — the anuric patient's only true exit; gastrointestinal binders the slow adjunct; treat the acidosis and stop the drivers — the ACE, the potassium, the missed session), with pacing cautious (hyperkalaemia raises capture thresholds — the strength–duration curve of the earlier widget made clinical) and atropine often useless below a nodal blockade that is myocyte-depolarisation, not vagal.** The differential the case hides — the broad-complex bradycardia of end-stage sodium-channel-blocker poisoning (tricyclic, flecainide) treated with hypertonic sodium instead — completes the lesson: wide QRS plus slow rate is a membrane-current problem, and the question is which current.
:::

::: quiz
The PR interval's normal 120–200 ms exists chiefly because:
- [ ] Atrial muscle conducts slowly
- [x] The AV node imposes a physiological delay that protects the ventricle from atrial rates it cannot follow
- [ ] The Bundle of His delays conduction to time the QRS
- [ ] Vagal tone sets a fixed 150 ms everywhere
::: solution
The **AV node's slow, decremental, calcium-dependent conduction** is the PR interval's bulk — a protective gate (the reason atrial flutter usually conducts 2:1 and fibrillation never drives the ventricle at 350 without an accessory pathway — [[#ex-wpw]]'s exception that proves it), rate-adaptive (vagally lengthened at rest, shortened by exercise and catecholamines — the same HCN and calcium physiology as [[physiology/autonomic-nervous-system]]'s chronotropic story), and clinically the level at which first-degree prolongation is often benign and below which (His–Purkinje) the same milliseconds become a pacing question ([[#def-block]]).

::: history
**Augustus Waller's** 1887 thoracic-galvanometer tracing on himself (the cardigram before the electrocardiogram), **Willem Einthoven's** string galvanometer, nomenclature (the P–Q–R–S–T letters inherited from Lippmann's capillary electrometry) and 1906 Nobel; **Lewis's** mechanism-era work (the flutter and fibrillation circuits in the 1920s, the paroxysmal tachycardias, the "factor X" of the conducting system), **Aschoff and Tawara's** node discoveries (the pacemaker and the atrioventricular bundle, the His of His's bundle), **Rosenblueth and Cannon's** autonomic coding; **Goldberger's** augmented limb leads (the 1940s — the 12-lead standard), **Wilson's** precordial and unipolar system and the vectorial revolution that made localisation possible, **Master's** two-step exercise test and the Master–Person tables of normality; **Sodi-Pallares's and the mid-century MI localisation atlases (the 1940s–60s; the "de Winter" and Wellens patterns later recovered from the older literature), **Moe, Kaufman and Otani's** and **Scherf's** AV-nodal physiology, **the accessory-pathway era of the 1910s–1930s (the "short PR" of the Wolf–Parkinson–White 1930 paper)** and **Durrer's and Wellens's** mapping era, then **Scheinman's and Josephson's** 1980s ablation programme that turned the chapter's circuits into catheter cures; and the **Moss's, Vincent's and Keating's molecular QT era** (the LQTS genes and channel-subtype pharmacology — the genetics of [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]] presenting as syncope) that made the T wave a genotype question.
:::

## Where this leads

The membrane biophysics in [[physiology/membrane-potentials]] and [[physiology/synapses]]; autonomic modulation in [[physiology/autonomic-nervous-system]]; the mechanical consequences of every rhythm here in [[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]]; the arrhythmia management in [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]]; the ischaemic syndromes in [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]; electrolyte physiology in [[renal/potassium-calcium-phosphate]]; the channelopathies' genetics in [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]].

::: summary
- Two action-potential families — fast sodium (working myocardium, Purkinje) and slow calcium/pacemaker (nodes) — generate the ECG's waves and the two clinically distinct conduction diseases; refractoriness and re-entry explain most tachycardias.
- The ECG is vectors sampled from fixed angles: axis localises, precordial leads regionalise, and intervals quantify conduction; a fixed reading sequence prevents the missed emergency.
- The occlusion patterns (ST elevation, reciprocal change, posterior and right-ventricular extensions) are activation decisions, and the AV-block patterns are anatomy decisions (nodal versus infra-His) with opposite urgency.
- Accessory pathways turn the node's protective delay into a re-entry limb — the narrow complex is the common circuit, the irregular broad complex the dangerous one, and AV-node blockade's contraindication the most examinable sentence in the field.
- Electrolytes and drug-channel block (potassium, sodium-channel poisonings, the QT drugs) rewrite the trace through the same membrane mechanisms — read them as biophysics, treat them as emergencies.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Rate and axis quick-read {level=1}
An ECG has R–R intervals of 4.5 large squares; lead I positive, aVF negative, lead II positive. Give the rate and the axis category.
::: solution
Rate = 300/4.5 ≈ **67/min**. Lead I positive with aVF negative = left-axis direction; lead II positive keeps it within the normal–left border (axis ~0 to −30° is normal-left; true left axis deviation is I positive, II negative). So: **normal to leftward axis** — with the clinical meaning: pathological left axis (below −45°) implies left anterior fascicular block or inferior-infarct loss, and the next step is QRS width and the morphology of II, III, aVF (the small q and shallow R of the infarct versus the rS of the fascicle).
:::
:::

::: exercise Measure the QT {level=1}
QT 520 ms at a rate of 100. Compute QTc by Bazett and judge; list four drug classes and two electrolyte lesions that do this.
::: solution
Bazett: QTc = 520/√0.60 ≈ **671 ms** (RR = 0.60 s at 100/min) — markedly prolonged, the >500 ms torsades-risk band (Bazett over-corrects at high rates — Fridericia or a rate-matched confirmation is the careful clinician's second arithmetic). Causes: **class Ia and III antiarrhythmics (quinidine, sotalol, dofetilide, amiodarone — though its torsades rate is low), macrolides, azoles, fluoroquinolones, antipsychotics (haloperidol, ziprasidone, quetiapone) and methadone, ondansetron and the other antiemetics, and the tricyclics**; electrolytes: **hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia (also hypocalcaemia — the U-wave and the neuromuscular signatures of [[renal/potassium-calcium-phosphate]])**. Management: stop the drug, correct potassium to high-normal and magnesium, monitor, and the congenital-question (the beta-blocker therapy and the family screening of [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]]'s long-QT story).
:::
:::

::: exercise Read the irregular broad complex {level=2}
A pre-excited atrial fibrillation and a ventricular tachycardia can both look "irregular and wide". Give three discriminators (clinical or ECG) and the management rule that survives uncertainty.
::: solution
**Pre-excited AF**: the history of young age, palpitations, the short-PR delta in sinus afterward, and the ECG's **extremely rapid (often >250/min), irregularly irregular, varying-morphology broad complexes** ("polymorphic-looking", the fusion and the shortest pre-excited RR <250 ms the risk markers); **VT with irregularity** (AF with aberrancy or VT with variable capture) favours age, structural disease, prior infarct and the regular-run patterns (fusion, capture, AV dissociation, concordance). **The management rule: an unstable irregular-wide-complex tachycardia is DC cardioversion regardless of mechanism; a stable one is procainamide or ibutilide (if pre-excitation suspected) or the VT protocol after expert consultation — and never adenosine, verapamil, diltiazem or digoxin in an undifferentiated irregular wide-complex rhythm, because if it is pre-excited AF, each accelerates the ventricle toward fibrillation ([[#ex-wpw]]'s contraindication)** ([[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]] and [[emergency-critical-care/acute-assessment]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise The pace-maker trace {level=2}
A dual-chamber pacemaker ECG shows P-wave tracking with a long AV delay and occasional non-conducted atrial extrasystoles with ventricular pacing at the upper rate limit in a pattern that alternates. Explain the device behaviour in electrophysiological language and the one symptom the patient reports.
::: solution
The device is **tracking the atrium (atrial-sensed → ventricular-paced at the programmed AV interval — the PR the programmer sets, the Wenckebach or 2:1 behaviour at the upper tracking rate when atrial ectopy or sinus acceleration exceeds it — the pacemaker-mediated tachycardia circuit when retrograde VA conduction completes the loop, recognised by the ventricular-paced rhythm at the upper rate with a P after every QRS)**. Management is the same physiology: **lengthen the AV delay (the alternating conduction pattern is the upper-rate behaviour, and a long AV delay also reduces unnecessary right-ventricular apical pacing — the dyssynchrony argument of [[#def-block]]), initiate the PVC/upper-rate-behaviour modes, and terminate pacemaker-mediated tachycardia with magnet-application or a programming extension (the refractory manoeuvre that blocks the retrograde limb)**. The patient's symptom is the **neck pounding of atrial contraction against a closed tricuspid when AV timing is wrong (the cannon waves of [[cardiovascular/cardiac-cycle]] in device form) or the breathlessness of the lost atrial kick when the mode has quietly switched the patient into pacing-only ventricular life — "pacemaker syndrome", the haemodynamic cost of bad electrical sequencing, the whole chapter in one complaint.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Pericarditis versus occlusion {level=2}
Give the four ECG discriminators (and one clinical and one laboratory) separating acute pericarditis from early repolarisation and from STEMI in a 25-year-old with positional chest pain.
::: solution
ECG: (1) **distribution** — pericarditis: widespread concave ST elevation with PR depression (the atrial-injury current) in the inferolateral leads and **PR elevation in aVR**; occlusion: a coronary territory with reciprocal depression (the [[#ex-stemi]] rule); early repolarisation: anterior concave elevation without PR change and a prominent notch. (2) **Concavity** — pericarditis' upward-concave "saddle" versus occlusion's convex/tombstoning. (3) **Evolution** — pericarditis' diffuse stage sequence (days) versus territory-specific minutes-to-hours QRS/T changes. (4) **The Spodick sign (down-sloping TP) and the absence of Q waves** favour pericarditis; PR depression is its near-specific marker. Clinical: **the positional, pleuritic, forward-leaning pain, the friction rub, the viral prodrome and the fever**; laboratory: **troponin (modest rise occurs in myopericarditis — the number's pattern, not its presence, discriminates), CRP, and the echocardiographic effusion and the cardiac magnetic resonance's inflammation** — the imaging chapter's version of the same physiology, with the management fork (NSAID-plus-colchicine versus catheter laboratory) decided by the trace that begins the encounter; the chest-pain pathway itself is in [[emergency-critical-care/acute-assessment]].
:::

::: exercise The Brugada and the fever {level=2}
A 30-year-old Southeast-Asian man is resuscitated from nocturnal arrest; the post-arrest ECG shows coved ST elevation in V1–V2 with a right bundle-branch-like pattern; fever preceded the night. Explain the channelopathy, the sodium-potential mechanism of the fever link, and the management.
::: solution
**Brugada syndrome** — the commonest inherited arrhythmia syndrome of young men in parts of Asia (the "sudden unexplained nocturnal death" of regional folklore — its channelopathy basis a modern electrocardiographic discovery). Pathophysiology: **loss of sodium-current (SCN5A and relatives; and calcium-current and trafficking partners) disproportionately affecting the right ventricular epicardium, where the transient-outward-potassium current (I_to) is densest — the resulting voltage gradient gives the V1–V2 ST elevation and phase-2 re-entry's ventricular fibrillation**. **Fever and sodium-blocker drugs (flecainide; the ajmaline or flecainide provocation test; and the tricyclic and cocaine sodium blockade) unmask it because warming further inactivates the mutant channels and speeds their gating — the same [[physiology/membrane-potentials]] biophysics of temperature and threshold**: hence the fever-aggressive treatment rule, the drug-avoidance list (the brugadadrugs.org discipline) and the ECG's dynamic, diagnostic-plus-provocable nature (the spontaneous type 1 pattern the risk marker). Management: **ICD for survivors (secondary prevention — this patient) and the selected symptomatic family members; quinidine (the I_to blocker — the channel-logic antidote) and ablation (the epicardial substrate) for electrical storms; family screening with ECG and genetics (an autosomal-dominant-with-reduced-penetrance pedigree — [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]]'s lesson), and the reassurance track for the incidental type-2/3 pattern that fever and drugs convert** — the chapter's closing demonstration that an ECG pattern is a membrane current written in millimetres ([[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Localise the bundle branch {level=2}
QRS 140 ms with a broad monophasic R in V5–V6 and small q, and an rS with wide S in V1. Name the block, its usual causes in the young and the old, and its significance when new with chest pain.
::: solution
**Left bundle-branch block** (the ventricle activates muscle-cell-to-myocyte from the septum's leftward-to-rightward then the delayed left wall — the QRS≥120 with the loss of the normal septal q in I/V6 and the wide S in V1; right bundle-branch block is its mirror: rSR′ in V1, slurred S in I/V6). In the young an isolated right bundle-branch block may be normal variant or atrial-septal-defect territory (the ostium secundum's surgical signature — [[cardiovascular/valve-congenital-disease]]); left bundle-branch block in the old means conducting-system fibrosis (Lenegre/Lev disease), hypertensive or ischaemic or valvar heart — it is never "normal". **New-onset left bundle-branch block with chest pain is an occlusion until proven otherwise** (historically the "STEMI-equivalent" — today the Sgarbossa and modified-Sgarbossa criteria and serial comparison with the old ECG do the sorting, since primary-closure trials showed many such traces are old and non-ischaemic); and its presence destroys the ischaemia reading (the secondary ST-T changes), the exercise-ECG option (→ imaging stress instead, [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]) and the axis arithmetic, while its iatrogenic twin — the pacing-induced left-bundle pattern — carries the dyssynchrony cost of [[#def-block]]'s right-apical pacing.
:::

::: exercise Design the triage of the abnormal ECG {level=3}
A district hospital introduces AI-assisted ECG interpretation. Design the clinical governance: the three findings that must bypass all delays, the three that most often over-called, and the human-factor safeguards — each argued from this chapter's physiology.
::: solution
**Bypass-everything findings (minutes, not queues)**: (1) **ST-elevation and occlusion-equivalent patterns** (the [[#ex-stemi]] anatomy: territory elevation, posterior and right-ventricular extensions, de Winter and hyperacute T, the Sgarbossa-positive left bundle-branch) — because the lesion is a closing artery and the trace beats the troponin; (2) **the malignant-urgent rhythms and intervals** — complete and infranodal block with syncope ([[#def-block]]), pre-excited AF and regular broad-complex tachycardia ([[#ex-wpw]]), QTc in the torsades band with a culprit drug ([[#ex-hyperk]]'s neighbours), and the Brugada and early-repolarisation and ARVC signatures (epsilon waves, terminal activation delay) in arrest survivors; (3) **the electrolyte and poison traces** (the sine-wave potassium, the sodium-blocker QRS, the digoxin's bidirectional and scooped-with-block picture) — membrane-current emergencies. **Over-called findings** (the false-positive tax): early repolarisation read as STEMI in the young and athletic (the concave saddle and the notch), atrial and body habitus normals — the left-axis and low-voltage mimics of infarct and hypertrophy, sinus arrhythmia and wandering pacemaker read as heart block, and **the long-QT algorithm's rate-dependent over-correction at high rates** (the Bazett artefact — the human review of the corrected value). **Human-factor safeguards**: every alert reaches a named clinician with a telephone escalation (the interrupted-override problem of decision support), **the trace and the story must be reconcilable** (pericarditis versus occlusion by history and [[#ex-stemi]]'s reciprocal logic; the algorithm's "anterior infarct" in a normal-variant pectus), serial comparison as a first-class feature (the old ECG the most powerful artificial intelligence), audit of activation times and false-negative harm with physiology-weighted review (missed occlusions, not missed normals, the metric), **and the teaching mode** — the system that explains its activations keeps the human judgment this chapter's emergencies still need on the night shift ([[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]).
