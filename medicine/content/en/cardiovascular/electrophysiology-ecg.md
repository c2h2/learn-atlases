Every heartbeat starts as an electrical event. A small group of cells in the right atrium fires spontaneously, the impulse spreads through the atria, is delayed in the atrioventricular node, and then races through specialised conducting fibres to activate the ventricles almost all at once. The electrocardiogram (ECG) records the sum of this activity from the skin. It is cheap, quick and safe, and it is often the investigation that changes management fastest: an ECG taken in the ambulance can send a patient with a blocked coronary artery straight to the catheter laboratory.

This chapter explains the cardiac action potentials and the ion currents behind them, the conduction system, how the ECG is recorded and why each lead sees what it sees, and a systematic method for reading a 12-lead ECG, with the common abnormal patterns. Rhythm disorders are developed further in [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]].

## Cardiac action potentials

The resting cell membrane is charged, negative inside, because potassium leaks out through open channels more readily than other ions move in. The general principles of resting and action potentials are in [[physiology/membrane-potentials]]. The heart has two quite different kinds of action potential.

::: definition The ventricular (fast-response) action potential {#def-aps}
Working atrial and ventricular myocytes and Purkinje fibres have a stable resting potential of about −90 mV and a fast upstroke. The action potential has five phases:

- **Phase 0, rapid depolarisation.** Voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium rushes in, taking the membrane to about +20 mV within a millisecond or two.
- **Phase 1, early repolarisation.** Sodium channels inactivate and a transient outward potassium current produces a small notch.
- **Phase 2, plateau.** Calcium enters through L-type calcium channels, balanced by outward potassium current, so the membrane stays depolarised for 200 ms or more. The calcium entering triggers calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and so contraction.
- **Phase 3, repolarisation.** Calcium channels close and delayed-rectifier potassium currents (the rapid component $I_{Kr}$ and slow component $I_{Ks}$) return the membrane to rest.
- **Phase 4, rest.** The inward-rectifier potassium current $I_{K1}$ holds the membrane near −90 mV.
:::

The long plateau gives cardiac muscle a long **refractory period**. While sodium channels remain inactivated, the cell cannot fire again, so a second impulse cannot arrive until contraction is nearly over. This prevents tetanus, which would be fatal in a pump that must relax to fill. Many drugs act on these currents. Class I antiarrhythmics block sodium channels and slow phase 0; class III drugs such as amiodarone and sotalol block potassium channels and prolong phase 3. Many non-cardiac drugs also block $I_{Kr}$ (the channel encoded by *hERG*, or *KCNH2*) and prolong the QT interval, which is why drug-induced long QT is common.

::: definition The pacemaker (slow-response) action potential {#def-pacemaker}
Cells of the **sinoatrial (SA) node** and **atrioventricular (AV) node** have no stable resting potential. During phase 4 they depolarise slowly from about −60 mV, driven by the **funny current** $I_f$ (an inward sodium–potassium current through HCN channels that is activated by hyperpolarisation) and by T-type calcium channels. When the threshold of about −40 mV is reached, L-type calcium channels produce a slow upstroke. There is no fast sodium current, so conduction through nodal tissue is slow. The cell then repolarises through potassium channels and the cycle repeats.
:::

The rate of the SA node is set by the slope of phase 4. Sympathetic stimulation, acting through β1-adrenoceptors and cyclic AMP, increases $I_f$ and calcium current, steepens the slope and speeds the heart. Vagal stimulation releases acetylcholine, which opens acetylcholine-activated potassium channels, hyperpolarises the cell and flattens the slope, slowing the heart. Without any autonomic input the SA node fires at about 100 beats/min; the resting rate of 60–80 reflects predominant vagal tone. Ivabradine slows the heart by blocking $I_f$ without affecting contractility. Because AV nodal conduction depends on calcium channels, the AV node is slowed by calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem), beta-blockers, digoxin and adenosine. These are the drugs used to control the ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation or to terminate re-entry that involves the node.

## The conduction system

The impulse follows a fixed route:

1. The **SA node**, at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium, fires first.
2. The impulse spreads through both atria, producing the P wave.
3. It reaches the **AV node** in the lower interatrial septum. Conduction here is slow, which delays ventricular activation by about 0.1 s and lets atrial contraction finish filling the ventricles. The AV node also protects the ventricles from very fast atrial rhythms, because it cannot conduct every impulse.
4. The **bundle of His** carries the impulse through the fibrous skeleton that otherwise insulates atria from ventricles.
5. The **right and left bundle branches** (the left dividing into anterior and posterior fascicles) run down either side of the septum.
6. The **Purkinje fibres** spread the impulse rapidly across the endocardium, so the ventricles are activated in about 0.08–0.1 s. The septum is activated first, from left to right.

Each level of the system can act as a pacemaker if the one above fails, but at a progressively slower rate: AV junctional rhythms at about 40–60 beats/min, ventricular escape rhythms at about 20–40. This hierarchy explains why complete heart block with a ventricular escape produces a slow pulse and can cause syncope.

## Recording the ECG

The ECG records the potential difference between electrodes on the skin. When a wave of depolarisation travels towards a positive electrode, the trace goes up; when it travels away, the trace goes down; when it travels at right angles, the deflection is small or equally positive and negative (**isoelectric** or **biphasic**). Repolarisation travels the other way and normally produces a T wave in the same direction as the QRS, because in the ventricles the last areas to depolarise (the epicardium) are the first to repolarise.

::: definition Leads and what they see {#def-leads}
A standard 12-lead ECG uses ten electrodes and produces twelve views.

- **Limb leads** look at the heart in the frontal plane. The **standard leads** I, II and III (Einthoven) measure between pairs of limbs; the **augmented leads** aVR, aVL and aVF compare one limb with the average of the other two.
- **Chest (precordial) leads** V1–V6 look at the heart in the horizontal plane, from the right of the septum (V1) round to the left lateral wall (V6).

Leads are grouped by the territory they face:

| Territory | Leads | Usual coronary artery |
|---|---|---|
| Inferior | II, III, aVF | Right coronary (or circumflex) |
| Septal | V1, V2 | Left anterior descending |
| Anterior | V3, V4 | Left anterior descending |
| Lateral | I, aVL, V5, V6 | Circumflex or diagonal branches |

Lead aVR looks into the cavity of the heart from the right shoulder, so in sinus rhythm its P, QRS and T are normally all negative.
:::

The standard recording speed in the UK is 25 mm/s and the calibration 10 mm per mV. On standard paper, **one small square is 1 mm, which is 0.04 s**, and **one large square is 5 mm, which is 0.2 s**. Five large squares make one second.

## Waves and intervals

::: definition The normal ECG {#def-normal}
- **P wave**: atrial depolarisation. Normally upright in II and less than 0.12 s wide and 2.5 mm tall.
- **PR interval**: from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS complex; time for conduction through atria, AV node and His–Purkinje system. Normal 0.12–0.20 s (3–5 small squares).
- **QRS complex**: ventricular depolarisation. Normally less than 0.12 s (3 small squares). A Q wave is any initial negative deflection, R the first positive deflection and S the negative deflection after R.
- **ST segment**: from the end of QRS (the **J point**) to the start of the T wave; normally at the baseline, when the whole ventricle is depolarised.
- **T wave**: ventricular repolarisation. Normally upright in most leads except aVR and often V1.
- **QT interval**: from the start of QRS to the end of T, the total duration of ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation. It shortens as the heart rate rises, so it is corrected for rate (QTc).
:::

::: theorem Heart rate and QT correction {#thm-rate}
For a regular rhythm on standard paper:
$$
\text{rate (beats/min)} = \frac{300}{\text{number of large squares between R waves}} = \frac{1500}{\text{number of small squares between R waves}}.
$$
For an irregular rhythm, count the QRS complexes on a 10-second rhythm strip and multiply by 6.

**Bazett's formula** corrects the QT interval for heart rate:
$$
QT_c = \frac{QT}{\sqrt{RR}}
$$ {#eq-bazett}
with QT in ms (or s) and the RR interval in seconds. A QTc above about 450 ms in men or 470 ms in women is prolonged; above 500 ms the risk of torsades de pointes rises considerably.
:::

Bazett's formula over-corrects at high heart rates and under-corrects at low ones. Other formulas (Fridericia, which uses the cube root of RR, and Framingham) behave better at extremes; many machines report more than one. The figure shows how a fixed measured QT translates into a corrected value as the RR interval changes.

::: example Rate, rhythm and QTc {#ex-qtc}
A 70-year-old woman taking several new medicines has an ECG with regular narrow complexes. The R waves are 4 large squares apart. The QT interval measures 11 small squares. Calculate the heart rate, the RR interval, the QT interval and the QTc by Bazett's formula.

::: solution
Rate $= 300/4 = 75$ beats/min.

RR interval $= 4 \times 0.2 = 0.8$ s.

QT $= 11 \times 0.04 = 0.44$ s $= 440$ ms.

$QT_c = 440/\sqrt{0.8} = 440/0.894 \approx 492$ ms.

This is prolonged for a woman (above 470 ms) and close to 500 ms. The next steps are to review her medicines for QT-prolonging drugs (for example some macrolide and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, antipsychotics, methadone, ondansetron, citalopram, and class Ia and III antiarrhythmics), check potassium, magnesium and calcium, and repeat the ECG. Low potassium and magnesium both prolong repolarisation and add to the risk.
:::
:::

## The cardiac axis

The **mean QRS axis** is the average direction of ventricular depolarisation in the frontal plane, measured in degrees with lead I at 0° and aVF at +90°. The normal range is about −30° to +90°.

A quick method uses leads I and II:

- **QRS mostly positive in both I and II**: normal axis.
- **Positive in I, negative in II**: left axis deviation (more negative than −30°). Causes include left anterior fascicular block, inferior myocardial infarction and some cases of left ventricular hypertrophy.
- **Negative in I, positive in II (or aVF)**: right axis deviation (beyond +90°). Causes include right ventricular hypertrophy, pulmonary embolism, left posterior fascicular block, and it can be normal in tall, thin young people.

For a closer estimate, find the limb lead in which the QRS is most nearly isoelectric; the axis lies at right angles to that lead.

::: example Working out the axis {#ex-axis}
In a 32-year-old man's ECG, lead I shows a QRS that is mainly negative (small r, deep S), lead II is mainly positive and aVF is strongly positive. Lead aVR is close to isoelectric. Estimate the axis and suggest possible causes.

::: solution
Lead I negative with aVF positive places the axis between +90° and +180°: **right axis deviation**. The isoelectric lead is aVR, which lies at −150°. Axes at right angles to it are +120° and −60°; since aVF is positive, the axis is about **+120°**.

Possible causes include right ventricular hypertrophy (for example from pulmonary hypertension or pulmonic stenosis), an acute or chronic pulmonary embolism, left posterior fascicular block, dextrocardia or limb-lead reversal (check that lead I's P wave is upright: in left–right arm reversal it is inverted). In a tall, thin young adult with no symptoms, mild right axis deviation can be normal. The finding is interpreted with the clinical picture and the rest of the ECG.
:::
:::

## A systematic reading

Read every ECG in the same order so that nothing is missed.

1. **Details**: patient's name, date and time, any symptoms at the time, and the calibration and paper speed.
2. **Rate**.
3. **Rhythm**: is it regular? Is there a P wave before every QRS and a QRS after every P? Is the P wave upright in II (suggesting it comes from the sinus node)?
4. **Axis**.
5. **P waves**: shape and size.
6. **PR interval**: short, normal or long; constant or changing.
7. **QRS**: width, height, Q waves, bundle branch block patterns.
8. **ST segment**: elevation or depression, and its shape.
9. **T waves**: inversion, peaking, flattening.
10. **QT interval** and any U waves.
11. **Compare** with previous ECGs. A change matters more than any single abnormality.

## Common abnormal patterns

### Ventricular hypertrophy and bundle branch block

**Left ventricular hypertrophy** produces tall QRS voltages over the left ventricle and deep S waves on the right. One widely used criterion (Sokolow–Lyon) is the S wave in V1 plus the larger R wave in V5 or V6 exceeding 35 mm. ECG voltage criteria are specific but insensitive, and voltages are often high in thin young adults; echocardiography is a better test of left ventricular mass. A "strain" pattern of ST depression and T-wave inversion in the lateral leads suggests more severe hypertrophy.

**Bundle branch block** widens the QRS to 0.12 s or more, because one ventricle is activated slowly through muscle rather than through the conducting system.

- **Right bundle branch block (RBBB)**: an RSR′ pattern in V1 (a second R wave) and a wide slurred S wave in I and V6. It can occur in healthy people, and also with atrial septal defect, pulmonary embolism and ischaemic disease.
- **Left bundle branch block (LBBB)**: a broad, often notched R wave in I, aVL, V5 and V6, and a deep broad S wave in V1. LBBB is nearly always a sign of underlying heart disease (ischaemic, hypertensive, cardiomyopathy or valve disease). It distorts the ST segments and makes ischaemia hard to read.

A useful memory aid is that in RBBB the V1 complex is mainly positive, and in LBBB it is mainly negative.

### Ischaemia and infarction

Ischaemia affects repolarisation first. **ST depression** and **T-wave inversion** suggest subendocardial ischaemia. **ST elevation** suggests transmural injury, usually from complete occlusion of an epicardial coronary artery.

::: definition ST elevation of acute myocardial infarction {#def-stemi}
In the absence of left ventricular hypertrophy or left bundle branch block, the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction (2018) defines ischaemic ST elevation as new ST elevation at the J point in at least two contiguous leads of:

- **≥ 1 mm** in all leads other than V2–V3;
- in **V2–V3**: ≥ 2 mm in men aged 40 or over, ≥ 2.5 mm in men under 40, and ≥ 1.5 mm in women of any age.

**Reciprocal ST depression** in the leads facing the opposite wall supports the diagnosis. New LBBB, a paced rhythm, posterior infarction (ST depression in V1–V3 with tall R waves) and certain other patterns can also indicate an acute coronary occlusion and are judged by specialist criteria.
:::

The evolution of a full-thickness infarct follows a typical sequence: hyperacute, tall T waves within minutes; ST elevation over hours; development of pathological Q waves (from hours to days) as the infarcted muscle becomes electrically silent and the electrode "sees through" it to the opposite wall; and T-wave inversion over days. With prompt reperfusion the sequence may be interrupted. Q waves are the electrical scar of an old infarct.

::: example Chest pain with an abnormal trace {#ex-stemi}
A 61-year-old man has had 40 minutes of central crushing chest pain with sweating. His ECG shows ST elevation of 3 mm in II, III and aVF, ST depression of 2 mm in I and aVL, and sinus rhythm at 52 beats/min. Interpret the ECG and state the immediate implication.

::: solution
**Acute inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).** ST elevation in II, III and aVF (contiguous inferior leads) well exceeds the 1 mm threshold, and the ST depression in I and aVL is reciprocal, which strengthens the diagnosis. The culprit is most often the right coronary artery.

The slow heart rate fits: the right coronary artery usually supplies the SA and AV nodes, and inferior infarction also increases vagal tone. Right-sided leads (V4R) should be recorded, because right ventricular involvement makes the patient sensitive to nitrates and dependent on adequate filling.

The immediate implication is **emergency reperfusion**. Under UK (NICE) and European guidance, primary percutaneous coronary intervention is the preferred strategy when it can be delivered within 120 minutes of the time fibrinolysis could have been given, for presentations within 12 hours of symptom onset; otherwise fibrinolysis is used. The details are in [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]].
:::
:::

**Pericarditis** also causes ST elevation, but it is widespread (not confined to one coronary territory), concave ("saddle-shaped"), without reciprocal depression (except in aVR and V1), and often with PR-segment depression. **Benign early repolarisation**, common in young men, causes modest concave ST elevation with a notched J point, mainly in the chest leads, and is stable over time.

### Electrolytes and drugs

Potassium has the most dramatic effects:

- **Hyperkalaemia**: tall, peaked T waves; then flattening and loss of P waves and lengthening PR; then broadening of the QRS; finally a sine-wave pattern, ventricular fibrillation or asystole. The ECG changes do not correlate closely with the potassium level, so a normal ECG does not exclude dangerous hyperkalaemia.
- **Hypokalaemia**: flattened T waves, ST depression and prominent U waves (a small wave after the T), with an apparently long QT.
- **Hypercalcaemia** shortens the QT; **hypocalcaemia** prolongs it.
- **Digoxin** causes a sagging ("reverse tick") ST depression at therapeutic levels; in toxicity it can cause almost any arrhythmia, classically atrial tachycardia with AV block.

::: example Broad complexes in a dialysis patient {#ex-hyperk}
A 58-year-old woman on haemodialysis has missed two sessions. She feels weak. Her ECG shows absent P waves, QRS duration 0.16 s and tall peaked T waves; the heart rate is 48 beats/min. Interpret the ECG and outline the immediate principles of management.

::: solution
The pattern of peaked T waves, loss of P waves and a broad QRS with bradycardia indicates **severe hyperkalaemia** with a high risk of imminent cardiac arrest. Missed dialysis is the likely cause.

Management follows local and Resuscitation Council UK guidance and has three aims:

1. **Protect the heart**: intravenous calcium (gluconate or chloride) stabilises the myocardial membrane within minutes. It does not lower potassium.
2. **Shift potassium into cells**: intravenous insulin with glucose, often with nebulised salbutamol; both act within about 15–30 minutes and need glucose monitoring.
3. **Remove potassium from the body**: in a person on dialysis, urgent dialysis is the definitive treatment; potassium binders have a slower, supporting role.

Continuous cardiac monitoring is essential. The ECG is repeated after calcium; persistence of the changes may need further doses.
:::
:::

### Pre-excitation and inherited channel disorders

In **Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) syndrome** an extra conducting pathway (an accessory pathway) connects atrium and ventricle, bypassing the AV node. Part of the ventricle is activated early, producing a **short PR interval** (less than 0.12 s) and a slurred upstroke of the QRS, the **delta wave**, which widens the QRS. The pathway allows re-entrant tachycardias. If atrial fibrillation develops, the pathway can conduct very rapidly to the ventricles and cause ventricular fibrillation, which is why AV-nodal blocking drugs are avoided in pre-excited atrial fibrillation. Catheter ablation of the pathway is usually curative.

Several inherited conditions affect the ion channels themselves. **Congenital long QT syndromes** (most commonly from variants in *KCNQ1*, *KCNH2* or *SCN5A*) prolong repolarisation and predispose to torsades de pointes; typical triggers differ by type (exercise and swimming, sudden noise or emotion, sleep). **Brugada syndrome**, often due to reduced sodium current, produces coved ST elevation in V1–V2 and a risk of ventricular fibrillation, typically at rest or during fever. Both are reasons why unexplained syncope or sudden death in a young person prompts ECG screening of relatives ([[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]).

::: warning Read the patient as well as the trace
Two errors are common. The first is trusting the machine's printed interpretation. Automated reports are useful but miss subtle ST elevation, misread artefact as arrhythmia, and over-call minor abnormalities; every ECG needs a human reading in clinical context. The second is assuming that a normal ECG rules out an acute coronary syndrome. A first ECG is normal or non-diagnostic in a substantial proportion of people later shown to have myocardial infarction, especially with circumflex occlusion, whose territory is poorly seen by the standard leads. Repeat ECGs, troponin testing and the history are needed.
:::

::: quiz
On a standard ECG recorded at 25 mm/s, the R waves of a regular rhythm are 15 small squares apart. What is the heart rate?
- [ ] 60 beats/min
- [ ] 75 beats/min
- [x] 100 beats/min
- [ ] 150 beats/min
::: solution
Rate $= 1500/15 = 100$ beats/min. Equivalently, 15 small squares is 3 large squares, and $300/3 = 100$. Each small square is 0.04 s, so the RR interval is 0.6 s and $60/0.6 = 100$.
:::
:::

::: history
Augustus Waller recorded the first human electrocardiogram in London in 1887 using a capillary electrometer, but the trace was small and hard to interpret. Willem Einthoven in Leiden built a far more sensitive string galvanometer and by 1903 was recording clear tracings; he named the P, Q, R, S and T waves, defined the three standard limb leads and described the relationship between them that bears his name. He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1924. Frank Wilson introduced unipolar chest leads in the 1930s, and Emanuel Goldberger the augmented limb leads in 1942, completing the 12-lead ECG used today. Thomas Lewis in London, working with Einthoven's instruments, used the ECG to explain atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias in the early twentieth century.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Heart rate from the trace {level=1 check="round(300/3.5)"}
A regular rhythm has R waves 3.5 large squares apart on paper running at 25 mm/s. Calculate the heart rate, rounded to the nearest whole beat per minute.
::: solution
Rate $= 300/3.5 = 85.7$, which rounds to **86 beats/min**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Corrected QT {level=1 check="500"}
The measured QT interval is 400 ms and the RR interval is 0.64 s. Calculate the QTc by Bazett's formula in ms (exact value).
::: solution
$\sqrt{0.64} = 0.8$, so $QT_c = 400/0.8 =$ **500 ms**. This is markedly prolonged; it would prompt a review of drugs and electrolytes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which territory? {level=1}
Name the territory and the most likely culprit artery for ST elevation in: (a) V1–V4; (b) II, III and aVF; (c) I, aVL, V5 and V6.
::: solution
(a) **Anteroseptal**, left anterior descending artery.

(b) **Inferior**, usually the right coronary artery (sometimes a dominant circumflex).

(c) **Lateral**, circumflex artery or a diagonal branch of the left anterior descending.
:::
:::

::: exercise Left or right bundle {level=2}
Two ECGs both show a QRS duration of 0.14 s. In the first, V1 shows an rSR′ complex and lead I a broad S wave. In the second, V1 shows a deep, broad QS complex and V6 a broad, notched R wave with no Q wave. Identify each pattern and explain the significance of each.
::: solution
The first is **right bundle branch block**. The right ventricle is activated late, producing the second positive deflection (R′) in V1 and the late, broad S wave in the left-sided leads. It can be a normal finding, but may accompany atrial septal defect, pulmonary embolism or ischaemic disease.

The second is **left bundle branch block**. Septal activation runs right to left (so there is no septal q wave in V6) and the left ventricle is activated slowly. LBBB almost always indicates structural heart disease and should prompt echocardiography; it also makes ST segments hard to interpret for ischaemia.
:::
:::

::: exercise Pericarditis or occlusion? {level=2}
A 23-year-old man has sharp chest pain worse on lying flat and eased by sitting forward, after a viral illness. His ECG shows 1.5 mm concave ST elevation in I, II, aVL, aVF and V2–V6, PR depression in II, and ST depression only in aVR. List the features that favour pericarditis over an acute coronary occlusion.
::: solution
- ST elevation is **widespread**, crossing several coronary territories, rather than confined to one.
- The ST segments are **concave** ("saddle-shaped").
- There is **no reciprocal ST depression** except in aVR (and sometimes V1), which is expected in pericarditis.
- **PR-segment depression** reflects atrial injury from the inflamed pericardium.
- The **pain** is positional and pleuritic, after a viral illness, in a young man.

Q waves and the evolution of an infarct are absent. Troponin may still be mildly raised if there is accompanying myocarditis, and echocardiography looks for an effusion. If doubt remains, serial ECGs and specialist review are needed, because missing an occlusion is the more harmful error.
:::
:::

::: exercise The potassium sequence {level=2}
Put the following ECG changes in the order they usually appear as serum potassium rises: broad QRS; peaked T waves; sine-wave pattern; loss of P waves and lengthening PR. Explain why calcium is given first in treatment.
::: solution
**Peaked T waves → loss of P waves and lengthening PR → broad QRS → sine-wave pattern** (then ventricular fibrillation or asystole). The order is a typical progression, but changes do not correlate closely with the measured potassium and may skip steps.

Calcium is given first because it acts within minutes to stabilise the cardiac membrane, raising the threshold for excitation and reducing the risk of arrhythmia, while the slower treatments that shift potassium into cells or remove it from the body take effect. Calcium does not lower the potassium.
:::
:::

::: exercise Short PR and a slurred upstroke {level=2}
A 19-year-old student has episodes of rapid regular palpitation. Between episodes her ECG shows a PR interval of 0.10 s and a QRS of 0.13 s with a slurred upstroke. Explain these findings and why digoxin or verapamil would be dangerous if she developed atrial fibrillation.
::: solution
These are the features of **Wolff–Parkinson–White pattern**. An accessory pathway conducts from atrium to ventricle without the AV nodal delay, so part of the ventricle is activated early: the PR is short and the early, slow activation through ordinary muscle produces a delta wave and a wide QRS. Her palpitations are most likely an atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia using the pathway.

In atrial fibrillation, the AV node normally limits how many of the very rapid atrial impulses reach the ventricles. The accessory pathway has no such limit. Drugs that block the AV node (digoxin, verapamil, diltiazem, and also adenosine) remove the node's competition and can increase conduction through the pathway, producing extremely fast ventricular rates that can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation. She should be referred for electrophysiology study and catheter ablation.
:::
:::

::: exercise A trace to interpret {level=3}
An 81-year-old man is admitted after a fall in which he briefly lost consciousness. His ECG shows sinus rhythm at 66 beats/min, PR interval 0.28 s, QRS 0.14 s with an rSR′ in V1 and a broad S wave in I, and the QRS in lead I is mainly positive while lead II is mainly negative. Interpret the ECG fully and discuss what it implies about the cause of his fall.
::: hint
Work through rate, rhythm, axis, PR, QRS. How many of the conducting fascicles show evidence of disease?
:::
::: solution
- **Rate and rhythm**: sinus rhythm, 66 beats/min.
- **PR interval** 0.28 s: **first-degree AV block** (above 0.20 s).
- **QRS** 0.14 s with rSR′ in V1 and broad S in I: **right bundle branch block**.
- **Axis**: positive in I, negative in II: **left axis deviation**, which in this context indicates **left anterior fascicular block**.

Right bundle branch block plus left anterior fascicular block is **bifascicular block**: only the left posterior fascicle is conducting normally. Together with a prolonged PR interval, this suggests widespread conduction disease (sometimes loosely called "trifascicular" disease, although the PR delay may lie in the AV node).

His fall with loss of consciousness could therefore be due to **intermittent complete heart block** (Stokes–Adams attack), which would explain sudden collapse without warning and rapid recovery. The differential includes orthostatic hypotension, carotid sinus hypersensitivity, ventricular tachycardia (if there is structural heart disease) and non-cardiac causes such as seizure or a mechanical fall. He needs cardiac monitoring, review of drugs that slow conduction, an echocardiogram and specialist assessment; documented or strongly suspected intermittent high-grade block is an indication for a permanent pacemaker ([[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]]).
:::
:::
