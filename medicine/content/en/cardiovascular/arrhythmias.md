An arrhythmia is any rhythm other than normal sinus rhythm. Some are harmless, such as the occasional ectopic beat that almost everyone has. Some cause distressing palpitations but little danger. Some cause fainting by slowing the heart too much. A few, ventricular fibrillation above all, kill within minutes unless treated. And one, atrial fibrillation, is dangerous less for the rhythm itself than for the clots that form in the fibrillating atrium and travel to the brain.

This chapter explains the three mechanisms of arrhythmia, a practical approach to slow and fast rhythms, the bradycardias and heart block, the supraventricular tachycardias, atrial fibrillation and its stroke risk, ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, and the drugs and devices used to treat them. It builds on [[cardiovascular/electrophysiology-ecg]]. UK guidance (NICE NG196 for atrial fibrillation and the Resuscitation Council UK algorithms) is followed, with European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidance noted; management in practice follows current local guidance.

## Mechanisms: three ways to misfire

::: definition Mechanisms of arrhythmia {#def-mechanisms}
- **Abnormal automaticity**: cells outside the sinus node depolarise spontaneously and fire, or the sinus node itself fires too fast or too slowly. Examples: sinus tachycardia, some atrial tachycardias, accelerated idioventricular rhythm after reperfusion.
- **Triggered activity**: after-depolarisations, small oscillations of membrane potential that follow an action potential, reach threshold and trigger extra beats. **Early after-depolarisations** occur during a prolonged action potential (long QT) and trigger torsades de pointes. **Delayed after-depolarisations** occur after repolarisation when the cell is overloaded with calcium, as in digoxin toxicity and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia.
- **Re-entry**: an impulse travels round a circuit and re-excites tissue it has already activated. It is the mechanism of most sustained tachycardias, including atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia, atrial flutter, tachycardias using accessory pathways and ventricular tachycardia around a scar.
:::

::: theorem Conditions for re-entry {#thm-reentry}
Re-entry requires:
1. two pathways with different conduction or refractory properties, joined at both ends to form a circuit;
2. **unidirectional block** in one pathway;
3. **slow enough conduction** round the circuit that, by the time the impulse returns to its starting point, the tissue there has recovered.

The circuit can sustain itself if its length is greater than the **wavelength**, the product of conduction velocity and refractory period.
:::

::: proof
Imagine an impulse arriving at a fork with pathways A and B. Pathway B is still refractory from the previous beat, so the impulse travels only down A (unidirectional block). At the far end, it enters B from below and travels back up. If conduction is slow enough, B has recovered by now, so the impulse conducts back to the start, finds A recovered too, and goes round again. A premature beat is the usual trigger, because it arrives when one pathway is still refractory.

The wavelength rule follows: the head of the circulating wave must not catch up with its own refractory tail. Slow conduction (from ischaemia or scar) or short refractory periods (from atrial stretch or vagal tone) shorten the wavelength and make re-entry easier. Treatments act on these terms: **antiarrhythmic drugs** slow conduction further until block occurs, or prolong refractoriness to lengthen the wavelength; **catheter ablation** destroys a critical part of the circuit; and **electrical cardioversion** depolarises all the tissue at once, leaving no excitable gap for the wave to enter.
:::

## A practical approach

The first question about any arrhythmia is whether the patient is **stable**. The Resuscitation Council UK algorithms identify four **adverse features**: **shock** (hypotension, pallor, sweating, cold extremities, confusion), **syncope**, **myocardial ischaemia** (chest pain, ischaemic ECG changes) and **severe heart failure**. A patient with a tachyarrhythmia and adverse features needs **synchronised electrical cardioversion** (under sedation or anaesthesia); a patient with a bradyarrhythmia and adverse features needs **atropine** and, if that fails, **pacing**. A patient without a pulse is in cardiac arrest.

In a stable patient, the 12-lead ECG answers three questions:
- **Rate**: slow (below 60) or fast (above 100)?
- **QRS width**: narrow (below 0.12 s), meaning the ventricles are activated through the normal conduction system and the rhythm arises above the ventricles; or broad, meaning ventricular origin or abnormal conduction?
- **Regularity**: regular or irregular?

## Bradycardias and heart block

**Sinus bradycardia** is normal in fit people and during sleep. It also results from drugs (beta-blockers, rate-limiting calcium channel blockers, digoxin, amiodarone), hypothyroidism, hypothermia, raised intracranial pressure and inferior myocardial infarction. **Sinus node dysfunction** (sick sinus syndrome), usually from degenerative fibrosis in older people, causes inappropriate bradycardia, sinus pauses and, often, alternating bradycardia and atrial tachyarrhythmias (tachy–brady syndrome).

::: definition Atrioventricular block {#def-block}
- **First-degree AV block**: PR interval longer than 0.20 s; every P wave conducts. Usually benign.
- **Second-degree AV block, Mobitz type I (Wenckebach)**: the PR interval lengthens progressively until a P wave fails to conduct; then the cycle restarts. The block is usually in the AV node. It is common in sleep and in athletes and is usually benign.
- **Second-degree AV block, Mobitz type II**: the PR interval is constant, and P waves intermittently fail to conduct without warning. The block is usually below the AV node, in the His–Purkinje system, and can progress suddenly to complete block.
- **2:1 AV block**: every second P wave conducts; it can be type I or II and is judged by the QRS width and context.
- **Third-degree (complete) AV block**: no P waves conduct. Atria and ventricles beat independently; the ventricles are driven by an escape rhythm, junctional (narrow QRS, 40–60 beats/min) or ventricular (broad QRS, 20–40 beats/min).
:::

Causes of AV block include degenerative fibrosis of the conducting system (the commonest cause in older people), ischaemic heart disease, drugs, hyperkalaemia, infiltrative disease (sarcoidosis, amyloidosis), infections (Lyme disease, endocarditis with aortic root abscess) and damage during cardiac surgery or transcatheter aortic valve implantation.

A **permanent pacemaker** is generally indicated for Mobitz II, complete heart block and advanced block (two or more consecutive non-conducted P waves), even without symptoms, and for symptomatic sinus node dysfunction, once reversible causes such as drugs or hyperkalaemia have been excluded.

::: example Fainting while shaving {#ex-carpark}
A 79-year-old man has had three episodes of sudden loss of consciousness without warning, one while shaving, each lasting less than a minute with full recovery. He takes no rate-slowing drugs. His ECG shows sinus rhythm at 72 beats/min, PR 0.22 s and right bundle branch block with left axis deviation. Ambulatory monitoring records an episode of dizziness during which P waves continue at 80/min but there are no QRS complexes for 6 seconds, followed by a broad-complex escape rhythm at 30 beats/min. What is the diagnosis and the treatment?

::: solution
The monitor shows **paroxysmal complete heart block**: P waves fail to conduct, the ventricles stop, and a slow ventricular escape takes over. Sudden loss of consciousness without warning and with rapid recovery, caused by a period of asystole or extreme bradycardia, is called a **Stokes–Adams attack**.

His baseline ECG predicted it. Right bundle branch block with left axis deviation (left anterior fascicular block) is **bifascicular block**; with a long PR interval, conduction depends on the remaining fascicle, which can fail intermittently.

The treatment is a **permanent pacemaker**. Episodes of syncope while shaving can raise the possibility of **carotid sinus hypersensitivity** (pressure on the neck triggering bradycardia or vasodilatation), but here the documented heart block during symptoms answers the question. He should be advised not to drive until treated, according to current licensing rules.
:::
:::

## Narrow-complex tachycardias

The common regular narrow-complex tachycardias are:

- **Sinus tachycardia**: a normal response to fever, pain, anxiety, hypovolaemia, anaemia, hypoxia, thyrotoxicosis or pulmonary embolism. It is treated by treating the cause, not the rate.
- **Atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT)**: re-entry within the AV node using a fast and a slow pathway. The commonest paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), especially in women. Rate typically 140–250 beats/min; P waves are usually hidden in or just after the QRS.
- **Atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT)**: re-entry using an accessory pathway, as in Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome.
- **Atrial tachycardia**: a focus in the atrium firing rapidly.
- **Atrial flutter**: a large re-entry circuit, usually round the tricuspid annulus in the right atrium. The atria beat at about 300/min with "sawtooth" flutter waves best seen in II, III and aVF; with 2:1 conduction through the AV node, the ventricular rate is about **150 beats/min**. Any regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150 should prompt a search for flutter waves.

**Atrial fibrillation** is the common **irregular** narrow-complex tachycardia.

For a stable regular narrow-complex tachycardia, the steps are:

1. **Vagal manoeuvres**. The **modified Valsalva manoeuvre** (blowing into a syringe for 15 seconds while semi-recumbent, then lying flat with the legs raised) terminated SVT much more often than a standard Valsalva in the REVERT trial (2015). Carotid sinus massage is avoided in older people because of the risk of stroke.
2. **Adenosine**, given as a rapid intravenous bolus with ECG recording. It blocks the AV node for a few seconds, terminating re-entry that uses the node (AVNRT, AVRT) or, in flutter or atrial tachycardia, briefly slowing the ventricular rate to reveal the atrial activity. It causes a brief, unpleasant sense of doom, flushing and chest tightness, and is used with caution in asthma.
3. If adenosine fails or is contraindicated, a **beta-blocker** or **verapamil** (not in pre-excited tachycardia or with a beta-blocker), or **synchronised cardioversion**.

**Catheter ablation** of the slow pathway in AVNRT, or of an accessory pathway, is curative in most patients and is offered to people with recurrent episodes.

::: example Palpitations at a concert {#ex-svt}
A 26-year-old woman develops sudden rapid regular palpitations at a concert. In the emergency department her heart rate is 186 beats/min, blood pressure 118/76 mmHg, and she is alert with no chest pain. The ECG shows a regular narrow-complex tachycardia with no visible P waves. She has had similar shorter episodes that stopped suddenly. Outline the diagnosis and management.

::: solution
A **regular narrow-complex tachycardia** with abrupt onset and offset in a young woman, with no visible P waves, is most likely **AVNRT**. She has **no adverse features**, so drug or vagal treatment is appropriate.

1. **Modified Valsalva manoeuvre**, with ECG monitoring.
2. If it fails, **adenosine** as a rapid intravenous bolus into a large vein followed by a saline flush, with continuous ECG recording; further doses if needed according to protocol. She should be warned about the brief, intense unpleasant sensation.
3. After termination, record a 12-lead ECG in sinus rhythm to look for **pre-excitation** (short PR, delta wave).
4. Check electrolytes and thyroid function.

For the future, she can be taught the modified Valsalva manoeuvre. With recurrent episodes, she should be referred for **electrophysiology study and catheter ablation**, which is usually curative; long-term drug treatment is an alternative.
:::
:::

## Atrial fibrillation

::: definition Atrial fibrillation {#def-af}
**Atrial fibrillation (AF)** is chaotic atrial electrical activity with no coordinated atrial contraction. The ECG shows **no P waves**, an irregular baseline and an **irregularly irregular** ventricular rhythm. AF is classified by its time course:
- **paroxysmal**: terminates spontaneously or with intervention within 7 days;
- **persistent**: lasts more than 7 days;
- **long-standing persistent**: continuous for more than 12 months when rhythm control is still being pursued;
- **permanent**: the patient and clinician have accepted the rhythm and no further attempts at rhythm control are planned.
:::

AF is the commonest sustained arrhythmia. Its prevalence rises steeply with age, and it affects a substantial minority of people over 80. Most AF arises from rapid firing of muscle sleeves in the **pulmonary veins**, sustained by an atrium remodelled by stretch, fibrosis and inflammation. Risk factors include age, hypertension, heart failure, valve disease (especially mitral), coronary disease, obesity, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnoea, alcohol, thyrotoxicosis and endurance exercise. Acute triggers include infection, surgery, pulmonary embolism and alcohol binges.

AF causes harm in three ways: **stroke and systemic embolism** (blood stagnates in the left atrial appendage and clots); **symptoms** (palpitations, breathlessness, fatigue, reduced exercise capacity); and **heart failure**, both by losing atrial contraction and by fast ventricular rates. Management therefore has three parts: prevent stroke, control the rate or rhythm, and treat the underlying conditions and risk factors.

### Stroke prevention

The risk of stroke in AF is estimated with the **CHA₂DS₂-VASc** score:

| Factor | Points |
|---|---|
| **C**ongestive heart failure (or left ventricular dysfunction) | 1 |
| **H**ypertension | 1 |
| **A**ge 75 or over | 2 |
| **D**iabetes | 1 |
| Previous **S**troke, transient ischaemic attack or thromboembolism | 2 |
| **V**ascular disease (previous myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, aortic plaque) | 1 |
| **A**ge 65–74 | 1 |
| **S**ex **c**ategory female | 1 |

Under **NICE NG196** (2021), anticoagulation is **offered** to people with a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of **2 or more**, and **considered** for men with a score of 1, taking bleeding risk into account. The **ESC 2024** guideline uses **CHA₂DS₂-VA**, which omits female sex, because female sex increases risk mainly in the presence of other factors; it recommends anticoagulation at a score of 2 or more and suggests considering it at a score of 1.

A **direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC)**, such as apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban or dabigatran, is preferred to warfarin for most people. **Warfarin** remains the anticoagulant for people with **mechanical heart valves** or **moderate to severe mitral stenosis**, in whom DOACs are not used. Bleeding risk is assessed (NICE recommends the ORBIT score) mainly to identify and correct modifiable factors, such as uncontrolled hypertension, excess alcohol, unnecessary antiplatelet drugs or NSAIDs, and anaemia, rather than as a reason to withhold anticoagulation. **Aspirin is not used for stroke prevention in AF**: it is much less effective than anticoagulation and still causes bleeding. **Left atrial appendage occlusion** is an option for selected people who cannot take anticoagulants.

::: example An irregular pulse at 80 {#ex-af-old}
An 82-year-old woman with hypertension and type 2 diabetes is found to have an irregularly irregular pulse of 112 beats/min at a routine review. She has mild breathlessness on exertion. ECG confirms atrial fibrillation. Blood pressure 146/84 mmHg, eGFR 58, haemoglobin normal. She takes amlodipine, metformin and over-the-counter ibuprofen for knee pain. Calculate her CHA₂DS₂-VASc score and outline management.

::: solution
**CHA₂DS₂-VASc**: hypertension 1, age 75 or over 2, diabetes 1, female sex 1 = **5**. Her stroke risk is high, and **anticoagulation should be offered**, after a discussion of benefits and risks. A **DOAC** is usual, with dose based on the product criteria (age, weight, renal function) and local formulary.

**Bleeding risk**: modifiable factors are addressed rather than used to deny treatment: **stop ibuprofen** (NSAIDs increase bleeding with anticoagulants and worsen hypertension and renal function), control blood pressure, and check alcohol intake.

**Rate control**: for most people with AF, NICE recommends rate control first, with a **beta-blocker** or a rate-limiting calcium channel blocker (diltiazem or verapamil). She already takes amlodipine, a dihydropyridine that does not slow the heart; switching to a beta-blocker would control rate and treat hypertension. Digoxin is an option for sedentary people.

**Further steps**: echocardiography (structure, valve disease, left ventricular function), thyroid function, and assessment of whether symptoms persist once the rate is controlled. If symptoms continue, **rhythm control** (cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drugs or catheter ablation) is considered. Risk-factor control (blood pressure, weight, alcohol, sleep apnoea) reduces AF burden.
:::
:::

### Rate or rhythm?

Rate control alone is reasonable for many older people with few symptoms. **Rhythm control** aims to restore and maintain sinus rhythm and is preferred when AF has a reversible cause, causes heart failure, or causes persistent symptoms despite rate control. The EAST-AFNET 4 trial (2020) found that starting rhythm control early, within a year of diagnosis, reduced cardiovascular death, stroke and hospitalisation compared with usual care, which has encouraged earlier consideration of rhythm control.

- **Cardioversion** (electrical, or pharmacological with flecainide in people without structural heart disease, or amiodarone) restores sinus rhythm. Because clot can form in the left atrium within about 48 hours of onset and a newly restored atrial contraction can dislodge it, cardioversion is done only if AF has definitely lasted less than 48 hours, or after at least 3 weeks of therapeutic anticoagulation, or after transoesophageal echocardiography shows no clot. Anticoagulation is continued for at least 4 weeks afterwards, and long term if the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score indicates it.
- **Antiarrhythmic drugs** maintain sinus rhythm: beta-blockers, flecainide (with an AV-nodal blocker, and only without structural or ischaemic heart disease), dronedarone, sotalol and amiodarone (the most effective, with substantial long-term toxicity).
- **Catheter ablation** by **pulmonary vein isolation** is more effective than drugs at maintaining sinus rhythm and is offered when drugs fail or are not tolerated, and increasingly earlier.

Restoring sinus rhythm does not remove the need for anticoagulation in people at high stroke risk, because AF often recurs silently.

## Ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death

**Ventricular ectopic beats** are common and usually benign; very frequent ectopics can, rarely, cause a cardiomyopathy.

**Ventricular tachycardia (VT)** is a broad-complex tachycardia arising in the ventricles, usually from re-entry around scar after myocardial infarction or in cardiomyopathy. **Monomorphic** VT has uniform complexes; **polymorphic** VT varies. **Torsades de pointes** is a polymorphic VT whose axis twists around the baseline, associated with a long QT interval; it is treated with intravenous **magnesium**, correction of potassium and removal of QT-prolonging drugs. **Ventricular fibrillation (VF)** is chaotic ventricular activity with no cardiac output.

::: definition Broad-complex tachycardia {#def-vf}
A regular **broad-complex tachycardia** should be treated as **ventricular tachycardia** unless there is clear evidence otherwise. Alternatives are SVT with bundle branch block (aberrancy) and pre-excited tachycardia. Features favouring VT include structural heart disease (especially previous infarction), **atrioventricular dissociation** (P waves marching through independently), fusion and capture beats, very broad QRS, extreme axis deviation, and concordance of the chest leads (all positive or all negative).
- **Pulseless** VT or VF: cardiac arrest; defibrillation and advanced life support.
- VT **with adverse features**: synchronised cardioversion.
- **Stable** VT: specialist advice; intravenous amiodarone is commonly used; cardioversion if it fails.
:::

In **cardiac arrest** with VF or pulseless VT (shockable rhythms), the Resuscitation Council UK algorithm (2021, reaffirmed in the 2025 update) gives a shock as soon as possible, followed by 2 minutes of CPR before the rhythm is reassessed, with **adrenaline and amiodarone after the third shock** and adrenaline every 3–5 minutes thereafter. Reversible causes (hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo- or hyperkalaemia and other metabolic disorders, hypothermia, thrombosis, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins) are sought throughout ([[emergency-critical-care/resuscitation]]).

**Sudden cardiac death** in older adults is mostly due to VF from coronary disease. In people under 35, inherited conditions predominate: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, the long QT and Brugada syndromes, and catecholaminergic polymorphic VT. An **implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD)** is offered to survivors of VF or sustained VT without a reversible cause (secondary prevention) and to selected people at high risk (primary prevention), such as those with severely reduced ejection fraction ([[cardiovascular/heart-failure]]).

::: example The footballer who collapsed {#ex-arvc}
A 19-year-old footballer collapses during a match and is found in VF; bystander CPR and a public-access defibrillator restore sinus rhythm. His ECG afterwards shows T-wave inversion in V1–V3 and occasional ventricular ectopics with a left bundle branch block shape. Echocardiography and cardiac MRI show a dilated, poorly contracting right ventricle with fatty and fibrous replacement. What is the likely diagnosis and what follows?

::: solution
The likely diagnosis is **arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy** (arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy): an inherited disease, usually of desmosomal proteins, in which heart muscle (mainly right ventricular) is replaced by fat and fibrous tissue that forms the substrate for re-entrant VT. T-wave inversion in V1–V3 beyond adolescence, ectopics from the right ventricle (LBBB morphology) and imaging findings support the diagnosis; it is made with formal task-force criteria.

What follows:
- an **ICD** for secondary prevention, because he survived VF;
- **avoidance of competitive and high-intensity endurance sport**, because exercise accelerates the disease and triggers arrhythmias;
- beta-blockers, and sometimes catheter ablation for recurrent VT;
- **genetic testing** and **cascade screening** of first-degree relatives with ECG and imaging, as for other inherited cardiac conditions ([[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]).

His survival depended on immediate bystander CPR and early defibrillation, the strongest links in the chain of survival.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not give verapamil to a broad-complex tachycardia
Verapamil (and other AV-nodal blockers) can cause profound hypotension and cardiac arrest if the rhythm is actually VT, because it depresses contractility and vasodilates in a heart that is already failing. In pre-excited AF it can accelerate conduction through the accessory pathway. Unless the diagnosis of SVT with aberrancy is certain, treat a broad-complex tachycardia as VT.
:::

## Antiarrhythmic drugs

The **Vaughan Williams classification** groups antiarrhythmic drugs by their main action, though many drugs have several:

| Class | Action | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| I | Sodium channel blockade (slows phase 0, conduction) | Ia disopyramide; Ib lidocaine; Ic flecainide |
| II | Beta-adrenoceptor blockade | bisoprolol, metoprolol |
| III | Potassium channel blockade (prolongs repolarisation and refractoriness) | amiodarone, sotalol |
| IV | L-type calcium channel blockade (slows AV node) | verapamil, diltiazem |
| Others | | adenosine, digoxin, magnesium, ivabradine |

All antiarrhythmic drugs can also **cause** arrhythmias (proarrhythmia). The **CAST trial** (1989) showed this decisively: flecainide and encainide suppressed ventricular ectopics after myocardial infarction but increased mortality, because slowing conduction in scarred myocardium promoted re-entry. This is why flecainide is avoided in structural and ischaemic heart disease, and why suppressing ectopic beats is not in itself a goal. Amiodarone is effective and safe in heart failure but has many adverse effects: thyroid dysfunction (both over- and underactivity), pulmonary fibrosis, liver injury, photosensitivity and skin discolouration, corneal deposits, optic neuropathy, and interactions that raise warfarin and digoxin levels.

::: quiz
An ECG shows a regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150 beats/min in a 70-year-old man. Which diagnosis should be specifically considered because of the rate?
- [ ] Sinus tachycardia from pain
- [x] Atrial flutter with 2:1 block
- [ ] Ventricular tachycardia
- [ ] Atrial fibrillation
::: solution
**Atrial flutter** usually has an atrial rate of about 300/min; with 2:1 AV conduction the ventricular rate is about **150**. Flutter waves can be hidden in the T waves, so look in the inferior leads, or slow the ventricular rate briefly with vagal manoeuvres or adenosine to reveal them. AF is irregular, and VT is broad-complex.
:::
:::

::: history
James Mackenzie recorded arterial and venous pulses together with his polygraph around 1900 and described a persistently irregular pulse in which the atrial wave disappeared from the venous trace; Thomas Lewis identified the rhythm as atrial fibrillation on the electrocardiogram in 1909. Paul Zoll paced the human heart through the chest wall in 1952. In 1958 Åke Senning and Rune Elmqvist in Stockholm implanted the first fully implantable pacemaker, in Arne Larsson, who lived until 2001. Bernard Lown developed direct-current cardioversion synchronised to the QRS complex in the early 1960s, and Michel Mirowski's implantable defibrillator was first used in a patient in 1980. In 1998 Michel Haïssaguerre and colleagues in Bordeaux showed that most paroxysmal AF is triggered from the pulmonary veins, the basis of ablation by pulmonary vein isolation.
:::

::: summary
- Arrhythmias arise from abnormal automaticity, triggered activity or re-entry; re-entry needs two pathways, unidirectional block and slow conduction.
- Check for adverse features (shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia, severe heart failure): tachyarrhythmias then need synchronised cardioversion, bradyarrhythmias atropine and pacing.
- Mobitz II, complete and advanced AV block usually need a permanent pacemaker; first-degree and Mobitz I block are usually benign.
- Regular narrow-complex tachycardia: vagal manoeuvres (modified Valsalva), adenosine, then other drugs or cardioversion; ablation cures most AVNRT and AVRT. A rate of 150 suggests flutter.
- AF: anticoagulate by CHA₂DS₂-VASc (NICE: offer at 2 or more, consider in men at 1; ESC uses CHA₂DS₂-VA); DOACs preferred except with mechanical valves or moderate–severe mitral stenosis; not aspirin. Address bleeding risks rather than withholding.
- Rate control with a beta-blocker or rate-limiting calcium channel blocker; rhythm control by cardioversion (after less than 48 hours of AF, 3 weeks' anticoagulation or TOE), drugs or ablation.
- Treat broad-complex tachycardia as VT; torsades responds to magnesium; in arrest, shock early, with adrenaline and amiodarone after the third shock.
- All antiarrhythmic drugs can be proarrhythmic (CAST); ICDs prevent sudden death in selected patients.
:::

::: exercise Amiodarone monitoring {level=2}
A patient is started on long-term amiodarone. List the organs and tests to monitor, and two important drug interactions.
::: solution
- **Thyroid**: thyroid function before starting and every 6 months (hypo- and hyperthyroidism, because of iodine content and direct effects).
- **Liver**: liver function tests before and during treatment.
- **Lungs**: baseline chest X-ray and, if symptoms develop, assessment for pulmonary fibrosis or pneumonitis.
- **Eyes**: corneal microdeposits are common; optic neuropathy is rare but serious; report visual change.
- **Skin**: photosensitivity (sun protection) and slate-grey discolouration.
- **Heart**: ECG (QT prolongation, bradycardia); potassium and magnesium.

**Interactions**: amiodarone raises **warfarin** effect (INR) and **digoxin** levels, so doses are usually reduced; it also interacts with some statins (simvastatin dose limits) and other QT-prolonging drugs. Its very long half-life means effects persist for months after stopping.
:::

::: exercise Cardioversion and the clock {level=2}
A 58-year-old man without structural heart disease has palpitations from AF that began 3 days ago. He is stable. Can he be cardioverted today? Explain the options.
::: solution
Because AF has lasted **more than 48 hours**, a clot may have formed in the left atrial appendage, and cardioversion could cause a stroke. The options are:
- **anticoagulate for at least 3 weeks**, control the rate meanwhile, and then cardiovert electively; or
- perform **transoesophageal echocardiography** to exclude left atrial thrombus, then cardiovert under anticoagulation.

In either case, anticoagulation continues for at least 4 weeks after cardioversion, and long term according to his CHA₂DS₂-VASc score. If he became unstable, emergency cardioversion would proceed despite the risk.
:::

::: exercise The syncope clinic {level=2}
Classify each of these causes of transient loss of consciousness as high or low risk for a cardiac cause, and give the reason: (a) a 22-year-old who faints after standing for an hour in a hot church, with prodromal sweating and nausea; (b) a 64-year-old who collapses during exertion, with a systolic murmur; (c) a 34-year-old whose brother died suddenly at 30, with collapse on hearing an alarm clock; (d) a 75-year-old with collapse without warning and bifascicular block.
::: solution
(a) **Low risk**: the classic setting and prodrome of **vasovagal syncope**.

(b) **High risk**: exertional syncope with a murmur suggests **aortic stenosis** or **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy**; needs urgent echocardiography.

(c) **High risk**: sudden auditory trigger and family history of sudden death suggest **long QT syndrome** (type 2 is classically triggered by sudden noise); needs ECG, QTc measurement and specialist review.

(d) **High risk**: syncope without warning with conduction disease suggests **intermittent complete heart block**.

Features that increase cardiac risk include exertional or supine syncope, absence of prodrome, palpitations before collapse, structural heart disease, abnormal ECG and family history of sudden death.
:::
:::

::: exercise A broad-complex tachycardia {level=3}
A 67-year-old man who had an anterior myocardial infarction 4 years ago presents with palpitations and light-headedness. He is alert; BP 104/68 mmHg. The ECG shows a regular broad-complex tachycardia at 172 beats/min, QRS 0.16 s, with occasional P waves visible that bear no relation to the QRS complexes, and one narrower complex that appears earlier than expected. A junior doctor suggests verapamil for "SVT with bundle branch block". Interpret the ECG, discuss the differential diagnosis, and outline management.
::: hint
What do independent P waves and an early, narrower beat tell you about where the rhythm comes from?
:::
::: solution
**Interpretation**: a regular broad-complex tachycardia with **AV dissociation** (P waves unrelated to QRS) and a **capture or fusion beat** (an early narrower complex when a sinus impulse briefly activates the ventricles normally). These features are diagnostic of **ventricular tachycardia**. The history of **myocardial infarction** makes scar-related re-entrant VT the leading diagnosis even without them.

**Differential diagnosis**: SVT with pre-existing or rate-related bundle branch block; pre-excited tachycardia (AVRT with antegrade conduction over an accessory pathway); paced rhythms; hyperkalaemia or sodium-channel-blocker toxicity producing broad complexes.

**Management**:
- **Do not give verapamil**: if the rhythm is VT it may cause collapse.
- He has no adverse features at the moment (alert, BP 104/68, no chest pain or heart failure), but must be monitored closely with a defibrillator available; if adverse features develop, **synchronised cardioversion** under sedation.
- In stable VT, specialist advice; **intravenous amiodarone** is commonly used; correct potassium and magnesium.
- Look for ischaemia (troponin, ECG after termination) and assess left ventricular function.
- After recovery, an **ICD** is likely to be offered, given sustained VT with previous infarction, and catheter ablation may be considered.
:::
:::
