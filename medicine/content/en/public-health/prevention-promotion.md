Prevention is medicine's oldest claim and newest industry: from the sanitation revolutions that bought most of the extra life expectancy of the twentieth century, through the vaccine and tobacco-control campaigns that showed organised persuasion working, to today's argument that the biggest remaining gains hide in commodity prices, early childhood and the built environment rather than in the clinic. **This chapter covers the classic taxonomy (primordial, primary, secondary and tertiary prevention with their screening and risk-factor inheritances), the evidence for population and high-risk strategies (Rose's theorem that shifting the whole distribution saves more lives than treating the tails, and its equity caveats), the behavioural science of changing what people do (the COM-B and stages-of-change models, nudges and defaults, and the individual-versus-environment debate), the named policy instruments with their measured effects (tobacco's textbook campaign, alcohol pricing and marketing, food reformulation and labelling, activity infrastructure), and prevention's own error history (the J-curve controversies, the supplement and hormone reversals, and the overdiagnosis debt that prevention's success now generates).**

## The map

::: definition Levels of prevention and who they serve {#def-levels}
**The taxonomy in clinical order: primordial prevention (keeping the causes of the causes from emerging - the food-environment and industry-code designs), primary (before disease: vaccination, safety regulation, tobacco, alcohol and diet population action, and the occupational and environmental protections), secondary (earlier detection of asymptomatic disease - the screening armature the screening chapter polices), and tertiary (after diagnosis: the secondary-prevention pharmacology of statins and antihypertensives, the cardiac, stroke and pulmonary rehabilitation programmes, and the multifactorial diabetes-risk programmes whose polypill trials showed large effect sizes from organised prescribing) - with quaternary prevention (protecting patients from over-prevention) now its own literature. The strategy frame is Geoffrey Rose's: the population approach shifts everyone's risk slightly, and because most disease arises from the large middle of the risk distribution rather than the high-risk tail, it prevents more cases than the high-risk approach of screening and treating the top percentiles; the high-risk approach is clinically satisfying and individually ethical but has its own paradox when only treated individuals are measured. The equity caveat is the field's central modern problem: population interventions widen gaps when uptake tracks advantage (the inverse-prevention-law literature across dietary and activity campaigns), so modern programmes specify distributional impact as a design requirement - proportionate universalism, universal services with intensity scaled to need - rather than discovering it in evaluation. Who does prevention changed as much as what: the sanitary engineers and vaccine campaigners, the infant-welfare and immunisation services, the health-promotion movement born at the Ottawa Charter (1986) with its 'enabling and mediating' rather than 'telling' language, and today's arrangement of local public-health teams, national agencies and guideline bodies that appraise community interventions with economic models attached. The clinician's contact-based menu is codified and auditable (the immunisation offer, the 'very brief advice' for smoking and its payment-history evidence, blood-pressure and cholesterol checks with their overdiagnosis debates, the alcohol short-conversation trials, falls and frailty signposting, the 'making every contact count' doctrine) - and the honest evidence is bimodal: a few contact interventions have large effects and many have effect sizes in percentage points, which is why the field keeps saying the consultation is the smaller lever and the shop and the street are the larger ones.**
:::

## The levers

::: definition Behaviour, structure and the policy instruments {#def-levers}
**Behaviour-change science reaches three honest conclusions. Individual capability and motivation matter but sit downstream of opportunity: the COM-B model (capability, opportunity, motivation interacting with behaviour) and the stages-of-change lineages describe rather than conjure, and 'education-only' interventions keep finding small effects without structural support - the knowledge-attitude-practice gap every public-health student rediscovers. Choice architecture works within its reach: defaults (the opt-out organ-donation logic), friction removals (reminder, transport and scheduling systems with trial-grade effects), salience tools (labelling and calorie-information studies - modest alone, reformulation-forcing at scale), and social-norm and commitment devices; the 'nudge' critique (architecture exonerates industry) is answered by the field's own rule that nudges never substitute for taxes, regulation and supply. Most health behaviour is collectively produced, and the smoking history is the template: price increases, advertising bans, smoke-free legislation, standardised packs, cessation services and cultural denormalisation stacked in sequence produced the tobacco-control miracle - prevalence in affluent countries falling from about half of adults to about one in seven across sixty years - and the 'endgame' proposals (nicotine reduction, smoke-free generations) now argue the next act. The alcohol, food and gambling industries run the same opposition playbook the tobacco archive documented (doubt, denigration, deflection), which is why modern prevention policy carries conflicts-of-interest clauses excluding commercial parties from the tables they profit from. The structural instruments and their measured effects: fiscal (tobacco price elasticity and the 'taxes save lives' framework; the sugar-levy evaluations from Mexico and the UK showing purchase shifts and reformulation; Scotland's alcohol minimum-pricing results), regulatory (the advertising watershed and sponsorship bans and the documented failures of self-regulation, the marketing-to-children restrictions, the food-restriction rules around schools, occupational safety law after asbestos, and the speed, seat-belt and helmet enforcement literature - the laws save more lives than the leaflets), supply and environment (the active-travel before-and-after studies, 20-mph and traffic-calming effects, green-space access, housing warmth programmes, school and workplace food standards), and clinical-delivery instruments (immunisation coverage as the success metric, pharmacy delivery, and the outreach that closes the gaps universal design opens). The economics make these survive treasuries: the cost-effectiveness literature on tobacco and alcohol pricing and salt reduction and activity interventions (the WHO 'best buys'), the wider appraisal frameworks that value health beyond the health budget, and the honest limit that prevention's economics are diffuse, lagged and politically inconvenient - the benefits arrive after the election is the field's truest quip.**
:::

## The honesty

::: definition Overreach, backlash and the frontiers {#def-honesty}
**Prevention's errors are instructive. The J-curve mirage: the moderate-drinking-is-protective literature largely dissolved under better design (the sick-quitter and reference-group artefacts, the Mendelian-randomisation studies), and the alcohol-advocacy residue still clouds guidelines - a case study in commercial capture of an evidence base. The nutrient-and-biomarker fallacy: the antioxidant-supplement and hormone-replacement reversals (the beta-carotene lung-cancer signal and the Women's Health Initiative halt) established that biological plausibility plus observational association plus randomised harm equals humility about correcting natural levels - a lesson the vitamin-D cycle is re-teaching as its trials disappoint. The screening-and-overdiagnosis inheritances (PSA, thyroid, melanoma, and now the low-dose-CT arguments) belong to the screening chapter but are prevention's epistemic debt: earlier and more detection must pay for its own false positives. The framing critiques matter: the 'personal responsibility' politics that moved blame from industry to individuals across decades of deliberate strategy (now documented in the litigation-published internal documents), and the disability and ageing communities' objection to 'prevention' framing (whose deaths count as preventable, and who decides) are legitimate bioethics agenda. The frontiers: the commercial-determinants-of-health framework and its conflicts-of-interest management tools; the planetary-health overlap (sustainable-diet co-benefits, land use, the climate-security agenda the global-health chapter meets); early-years investment economics; and the digital-and-AI prevention promises - app and wearable trials real but small, personalisation hype against population-effectiveness reality, and the data-governance questions the nudge-to-app era imports. The '80 percent' arithmetic clinicians should quote: most premature death traces to behaviours and exposures, most behaviours are shaped by price, promotion and place - therefore most preventable death is addressed by policy, which is medicine with delayed, distributed, unfashionable pharmacology. Its side effects are documented too: the nanny-state backlash and its sequencing triggers (support before restriction outperforms restriction alone), the infodemic-era trust deficits (with the presumptive-recommendation communication evidence still the strongest clinical tool), and the accountability gap - prevention programmes rarely receive the mortality audit a surgical unit endures. The clinical implication: deliver the proven contact-based interventions at every visit, and describe the rest accurately: the advice works or fails within an environment, and fixing the environment is the same science with a different letterhead.**
:::

::: example The smoking that took forty years {#ex-tobacco}
A 60-year-old lorry driver, 45-pack-year history, quits for the fourth time after your ten-minute conversation and varenicline plus stop-smoking-service referral; he asks why nobody did 'this much' at his first quit attempt in 1995.
::: solution
**The tobacco compound ([[#def-levers]], [[#def-levels]], [[respiratory/asthma-copd]]): the honest answer - the system knew less and charged less: the 1990s pre-price-hike era, cheap roll-ups and smoking-at-work cultures, evidence that brief advice works but was neither delivered nor incentivised, and cessation pharmacotherapy that matured later. The stack making this quit stick: pharmacotherapy plus behavioural support plus the price-and-norms environment (tax, smoke-free law, standardised packs, advertising restriction) that made quitting the default rather than the heroic act, plus the cessation-services' expansion into maternity, mental-health and emergency-department settings - the 'every contact quits' trials' point that opportunism works. The population arithmetic he is part of: quit attempts per smoker per year are historically stable, so prevalence falls when the environment raises quit success and lowers initiation (the youth price-elasticity and the smoke-free-law cardiac-admission findings) - his success is less about willpower than about instruments. And the frontier answering his next question ('will I get lung cancer anyway'): the low-dose-CT lung-screening mortality benefit for his age and pack history, the COPD case-finding pathways, and the 'quitting at 60 buys years' numbers said out loud, because the 'too late' belief is a barrier the cessation literature names.**
:::

::: example The sugar tax and the reformulation {#ex-sugartax}
Three years after a sugar levy's introduction: sales-weighted sugar in beverages down 35 percent, small manufacturers' reformulation near-total, large brands' price pass-through partial, and obesity trends unchanged. The tabloids say 'the tax failed'.
::: solution
**Grading a policy with its own marking scheme ([[#def-levers]], [[#def-honesty]]): the levy was designed as a reformulation lever (the Mexico and UK purchase-data evaluations: volume-weighted sugar down substantially within two years, reformulation where the threshold bites), not an obesity cure; obesity is the wrong outcome at year three, because energy-balance lags and substitution make any single food-policy weight effect small and slow. The findings graded: reformulation - a bullseye, the at-scale result no voluntary pact achieved; uneven pass-through - the design lesson (threshold structures reward reformulation; premium brands absorbing the levy tells you about brand strategy, not failure); volume falling less than sugar - exactly what a sugar-specific instrument should do. The equity check: low-income households' purchase responses (the tobacco precedent: higher price responsiveness means progressive health gains even where spending looks regressive) and substitution to untaxed categories. The board's recommendations: close the milk-based and children's-drink loopholes, pair the levy with the school, sport and marketing package (single levers plateau), publish the distributional analysis, and measure the decade - the children's dental-extraction data (already moving in the taxed-generation studies) is the mid-term metric the tax was sold on; the obesity trend is the metric it never promised.**
:::

## Where this leads

Screening as secondary prevention is governed in [[public-health/screening]], immunisation programmes in [[public-health/vaccination-programmes]], the substance-policy clinical partners in [[psychiatry/substance-use]], the determinants frame in [[public-health/determinants-inequalities]], the planetary and global dimension in [[public-health/global-health]], the appraisal tools in [[public-health/health-systems-economics]], and overdiagnosis statistics in [[epidemiology-ebm/measuring-disease]].

::: history
**Prevention's history is longer and more consequential than medicine's. The first era is infrastructure: the sanitation revolutions - the London sewerage and filtration works following the cholera inquiries and John Snow's 1854 pump handle, the 'sanitary miracle' that demographers credit with most of the nineteenth century's mortality decline - then pasteurisation, milk safety, housing and factory acts, and the occupational protections codified after the phossy-jaw, radium-dial and asbestos lessons. The second era is the vaccine: Jenner's 1796 line from variolation to cowpox, Pasteur's germ-era programme, the mass diphtheria and tetanus campaigns, the smallpox eradication certified in 1980 - prevention's greatest single achievement and the template for every targeted campaign since - and the Salk and Sabin polio trials whose marches-of-dimes defined mass-immunisation politics. The third era is the risk factor: Framingham from 1948 inventing the concept, Keys and the diet-heart and seven-countries studies anchoring population cholesterol, and the Doll-Hill and Wynder-MacMahon smoking-causation proof (1950-1964, concluding under political fire with the Surgeon General's report). The fourth era is health promotion and its self-critique: the 1974 Lalonde report's 'health field concept' moving prevention beyond health care, the Ottawa Charter of 1986, the evidence-based-public-health turn of the 1990s, and the 'making every contact count' era's mixed trials. The fifth era is the policy instrument's golden age and its enemies: tobacco control built trial by trial (price studies, secondhand-smoke evidence, the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control of 2003 - the first international public-health treaty - and the plain-packaging litigation Australia and Britain survived); the alcohol minimum-pricing and marketing fights; the sugar-levy and reformulation wave; and the industry-framing research that read the tobacco archive aloud and found the same scripts in use. The over-reach chapters are taught too: the hormone-replacement and antioxidant reversals of 2002 and before, the vitamin-D and multivitamin deflations, and the overdiagnosis reckoning from PSA onward. The 2020s add the planetary-health commissions, the commercial-determinants agenda, and the infodemic-era communication science of trust. The honest arc: medicine learned to prevent in stages - first the water, then the microbes, then the risk numbers, then the laws - and each stage met the same objection, that adults should be left alone, answered in the same currency: deaths averted by people who never knew they had been saved.** - [[public-health/screening]], [[public-health/vaccination-programmes]], [[public-health/global-health]].**
:::

::: summary
- Prevention's levels (primordial, primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary) each have instruments, and Rose's theorem governs strategy: shifting the whole population's risk prevents more cases than treating tails - if the shift is designed to land on the deprived too.
- Behaviour change is mostly environmental: education alone underperforms; defaults and friction-removals help modestly; price, marketing regulation, product standards and infrastructure carry the measured effects.
- The tobacco playbook - price, help, denormalisation, advertising bans, packaging - is the template for commodity campaigns, and the commercial-determinants framework generalises both its lessons and its opposition tactics.
- Prevention overreaches memorably (HRT, antioxidants, the alcohol J-curve, overdiagnosis); the corrections are its best rules: randomise the claim, measure the harms, pay for false positives, and audit commercial influence on evidence.
- Contact-based clinical prevention worth doing is short-listed (vaccinate, brief smoking advice, blood-pressure and cholesterol pathways with explicit overdiagnosis policy, alcohol conversations, falls signposting) and auditable at every encounter.
- The 'best buys' economics is what survives treasuries; prevention's benefits are delayed and distributed against concentrated costs - which is why advocacy is now an ethical duty, not an extra.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Sort the interventions {level=1}
Classify as primordial, primary, secondary, tertiary or quaternary: (a) smoke-free legislation; (b) the population health check; (c) cardiac rehabilitation; (d) stopping PSA screening above 80; (e) the school-meals standard; (f) low-dose CT for smokers.
::: solution
**Taxonomy with reasons ([[#def-levels]]): (a) primary, primordial in intent - it changes the exposure environment and denormalises the behaviour; (b) secondary with preventive intent - it finds risk rather than disease, a hybrid guidelines call risk-based assessment, carrying an uptake-equity audit duty; (c) tertiary - post-event morbidity and mortality reduction; (d) quaternary - protecting from over-prevention's harms; (e) primordial-primary - shaping the food environment before preferences form, with school-setting delivery evidence; (f) secondary - screening under Wilson-Jungner criteria updated with overdiagnosis and shared-decision requirements. The labels predict who funds, audits and markets each intervention - and the mislabelling (celebrating (b) while defunding (a) and (e)) is where policy dishonesty begins.**
:::

::: exercise Write the brief intervention {level=2}
A 39-year-old drinks 14 units weekly 'just to switch off' after stressful shifts. Deliver the evidence-based brief intervention in six moves and name the two classic errors.
::: solution
**The short conversation ([[#def-levers]], [[#def-honesty]], [[psychiatry/substance-use]]): 1) ask normally and quantifiably (AUDIT-C-style units and pattern, not diagnosis-hunting); 2) feedback against guidance using his own numbers ('you're at the boundary of the low-risk guidance; here is what "low risk" actually means'); 3) elicit reasons both ways - the drinking's genuine function (switching off after trauma shifts) is not denial, it is the target; 4) menu not lecture (the 28-day pause experiment, sleep and activity substitutes, tracking, the partner conversation); 5) if not ready, plant the follow-up; 6) document the offer so the next contact counts. The two errors: scare-tactics (evidence-free and resistance-hardening - the argument the motivational-interviewing literature exists to prevent) and 'come back when you have a problem' (dismissing the exact window this conversation serves). The honest limit: a few-percentage-point population instrument whose partner is the pricing and marketing environment - the second prescription is policy.**
:::

::: exercise Cost the levy {level=2}
Model a 20-pence-per-litre sugar levy and present it to a health committee: expected effects, distributional impacts, predicted industry responses, and the two outcomes you would audit at two years.
::: solution
**A levy brief ([[#def-levers]], [[#def-honesty]], [[public-health/health-systems-economics]]): mechanism - a sugar-density (or volume) levy on the modelled consumption base; the threshold structure is the point: industry reformulates where avoiding the tax is cheaper than paying it (the UK data), passes through prices where it is not, and shifts brand strategy at the premium end. Expected effects: purchase-weighted sugar down double digits, sales shifting to water and low-sugar lines, weight effects small in year one (energy-balance lag). Distributional: larger absolute health gains in low-income groups (the tobacco elasticity pattern) even where spending looks regressive - report incidence both ways honestly. Revenue: a declining asset by design; never budget permanent services with it. Predicted responses: the jobs-and-small-manufacturer framing campaign, threshold gaming just below the line, product-mix migration to untaxed categories - so design the base wide and the review clause live. Audit at two years: sales-weighted sugar content (the instrument's own target) and children's dental and weight indicators, with 'obesity unchanged' pre-declared as expected at that horizon so the audit cannot be weaponised by either side.**
:::

::: exercise Design the campaign {level=3}
Design a nicotine-vaping prevention campaign for 14-17-year-olds where youth vaping is 20 percent and adult smoking 12 percent. Include the evidence constraints that make this prevention's hardest case.
::: solution
**The vaping dilemma ([[#def-levers]], [[#def-honesty]], [[respiratory/asthma-copd]]): the constraints stated plainly - vaping is far less harmful than smoking for adults (the harm-reduction logic that helped adult smoking fall, with its contested evidence base), genuinely harmful to adolescent lungs and developing nicotine-dependent brains, a gateway for a susceptible minority (the gateway-versus-common-liability debate), and the tobacco industry's growth strategy (the acquisition-forensics literature). Overstate the risk and you undermine adult cessation trust; understate it and you recruit the next generation. The design that survives: 1) age-gating and retail-access enforcement first (compliance-buy data: messaging without supply control fails); 2) flavour, disposable and marketing regulation - the actual intervention the campaign announces; 3) the message: no lies, the truth framed for the audience ('it is nicotine; nicotine rewires a 15-year-old brain; adults swap, teenagers don't start either') inside the 'big tobacco owns this' denormalisation frame that youth panels rate highest; 4) the adult channel unchanged and strengthened (quit-and-then-quit-the-vape pathways); 5) measurement: youth-vaping and youth-smoking series reported together, monthly, never one without the other; and the governance clause - content co-designed with teenagers, and the funder of the campaign must never be the seller of either product. That firewall is the campaign's credibility in one line.**
:::

::: exercise Make the economic case {level=1}
The treasury rejects a smoke-free-legislation proposal as 'a cost to hospitality'. Build the four-line rebuttal using prevention economics.
::: solution
**Four-lines-that-pay ([[#def-levers]], [[public-health/health-systems-economics]]): 1) the health effect is measured, not modelled: the secondhand-smoke evidence and the natural experiments of smoke-free laws showing falls in coronary events (and the smaller, contested stroke and respiratory findings) - the 'laws save lives' literature; 2) the economic effect is measured too: the hospitality-impact studies showing no sustained output or employment loss (with the substitution and compliance-cost nuances acknowledged), so the 'cost to hospitality' claim is a forecast, not a finding; 3) the avoided costs are real money: the acute-and-long-term-care of the averted cardiac and respiratory events and the passive-smoking-at-work productivity effects, valued at the usual social-discount-rate conventions; and 4) the distributional line: the largest protection goes to the youngest and least-protected workers (the 'workers' air-quality-injury-claim' point) - so the policy is progressive in health terms even where it is neutral in fiscal ones. The honest tail: none of this survives if the proposal is 'bans without support'; the sequencing and enforcement costs belong in the same paper, because prevention that ignores its politics reliably fails on them.**
