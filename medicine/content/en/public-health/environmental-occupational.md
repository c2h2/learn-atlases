In December 2020 a coroner in London concluded that air pollution had made a material contribution to the death of Ella Adoo-Kissi-Debrah, a nine-year-old girl with severe asthma who lived close to the South Circular Road. It was the first time in the UK that air pollution had been recorded as a cause of a person's death. In the same month, in Rochdale, two-year-old Awaab Ishak died from a respiratory condition caused by prolonged exposure to mould in his family's social housing flat; the law that followed, Awaab's Law, requires social landlords in England to investigate and fix serious damp and mould hazards within fixed time limits. Both deaths came from exposures that were known, measurable and preventable.

Environmental and occupational health deal with the effects on health of the physical, chemical and biological conditions in which people live and work. This chapter covers how environmental hazards are assessed, the main environmental exposures in the UK and globally (air pollution, climate change and heat, housing, chemicals and radiation), and occupational disease: its recognition, its legal framework and its prevention. The clinician's role is central: occupational and environmental causes are often missed unless someone asks.

## Hazard, exposure and risk {#hazard-risk}

::: definition Hazard, exposure and risk {#def-hazard}
- A **hazard** is anything with the potential to cause harm: a chemical, a physical agent (noise, radiation, heat), a biological agent or a situation.
- **Exposure** is contact between a person and a hazard. It is described by its route (inhalation, ingestion, skin contact), concentration or intensity, duration and frequency. The **dose** is the amount that enters the body or reaches the target organ.
- **Risk** is the probability that harm will occur, and its severity, given a particular exposure.

A hazard without exposure carries no risk: asbestos sealed in an undisturbed ceiling is a hazard, but becomes a risk when someone drills into it.
:::

**Environmental health risk assessment** has four steps: **hazard identification** (can the agent cause harm, from toxicology and epidemiology?); **exposure–response assessment** (how does the risk change with the dose?); **exposure assessment** (who is exposed, how much, for how long?); and **risk characterisation** (combining these to estimate the burden in the population, with its uncertainty). The result informs **risk management**: standards, limits and interventions, which also weigh costs, feasibility and public values.

Two kinds of exposure–response relationship matter for setting limits. For many toxic effects there is a **threshold** below which no harm is expected, and a limit can be set below it with a safety margin. For genotoxic carcinogens and for some pollutants such as fine particles and lead, there is no identifiable threshold: risk falls with exposure but never reaches zero. Limits for these are set by judging what risk is acceptable and achievable, and exposure should be kept **as low as reasonably practicable**.

## Air pollution {#air-pollution}

The main outdoor pollutants of health concern are:

- **Particulate matter.** PM₁₀ (particles under 10 µm in diameter) and PM₂.₅ (under 2.5 µm). Fine particles penetrate deep into the lungs and some reach the circulation. Sources include road traffic (exhaust and brake and tyre wear), domestic wood and coal burning, industry, agriculture (ammonia forms secondary particles) and particles carried from abroad.
- **Nitrogen dioxide** (NO₂), mainly from diesel vehicles.
- **Ozone** (O₃), formed in sunlight from other pollutants, highest in summer and in rural areas downwind of cities.
- **Sulphur dioxide**, now low in the UK since coal burning declined.

Long-term exposure to PM₂.₅ increases mortality from cardiovascular and respiratory disease and lung cancer, and is associated with type 2 diabetes, low birthweight and dementia. Short-term peaks increase hospital admissions for asthma, COPD and cardiovascular events. Children, older people, people with heart or lung disease and people in deprived areas near busy roads are most affected.

The WHO's 2021 **Air Quality Guidelines** set an annual mean guideline for PM₂.₅ of **5 µg/m³** (halved from 10 µg/m³ in 2005) and for NO₂ of 10 µg/m³, reflecting evidence of harm at very low concentrations. The legal limit for annual mean PM₂.₅ in the UK is 20 µg/m³, and England has a target of 10 µg/m³ by 2040. The Committee on the Medical Effects of Air Pollutants (COMEAP) has estimated that long-term exposure to human-made air pollution in the UK has an annual effect on mortality equivalent to 28,000 to 36,000 deaths.

::: theorem Exposure–response for long-term PM₂.₅ exposure {#thm-pm-response}
Cohort studies show that long-term mortality risk rises approximately log-linearly with the annual average concentration of PM₂.₅, with no threshold. For a coefficient expressed as a relative risk $RR_{10}$ per 10 µg/m³, a change in concentration of $\Delta c$ gives

$$
RR(\Delta c) = RR_{10}^{\,\Delta c/10}.
$$ {#eq-pm-rr}

COMEAP has used $RR_{10} = 1.06$ for all-cause mortality in adults aged 30 and over in UK burden calculations. The fraction of deaths attributable to an excess exposure $\Delta c$ in the whole population is $1 - 1/RR(\Delta c)$.
:::

::: proof
If each increment of concentration multiplies the hazard by the same factor, the hazard is an exponential function of concentration, so the relative risk for a change $\Delta c$ is $RR_{10}$ raised to the number of 10 µg/m³ steps in $\Delta c$. When the whole population is exposed to the excess, the attributable fraction is $(RR - 1)/RR = 1 - 1/RR$, which is the population attributable fraction with an exposure prevalence of 1. Because the relationship has no threshold, every reduction in concentration, even well below current limits, is expected to reduce mortality.
:::

::: example Benefit of cleaner air {#ex-air}
A city has 9,000 deaths a year among adults aged 30 and over. A clean air zone and a ban on burning wet wood are expected to reduce the population-weighted annual mean PM₂.₅ from 12 to 10 µg/m³. Using $RR_{10} = 1.06$, estimate the number of deaths a year that the policy would prevent in the long term.
::: solution
$\Delta c = 2$ µg/m³. $RR = 1.06^{0.2} = 1.0117$.

Attributable fraction $= 1 - 1/1.0117 = 0.0116$, or 1.16%.

Deaths prevented $\approx 9{,}000 \times 0.0116 = 104$ a year.

The number seems modest for a city, but the gains accumulate over many years and are accompanied by fewer hospital admissions and better lung growth in children. The benefits arrive gradually because the effects of long-term exposure take years to reverse. Such estimates also carry uncertainty: the coefficient has a confidence interval, and the composition of particles varies between places.
:::
:::

**Indoor air.** People in the UK spend most of their time indoors. Indoor hazards include damp and mould (asthma, respiratory infection, and in rare cases fatal disease, as in Awaab Ishak's death), carbon monoxide from faulty appliances (headache, dizziness and nausea in several members of a household, which improve away from home), second-hand smoke, and particles from wood burning and cooking. Globally, **household air pollution** from cooking with solid fuels and kerosene is a major cause of death among women and young children in low-income countries.

Clinicians contribute by asking about home and work exposures, giving advice on reducing exposure (for example on high-pollution days for people with lung disease), documenting environmental contributions to illness, and supporting patients' cases with landlords and local authorities.

## Climate change and health {#climate}

Climate change affects health directly, through heatwaves, floods, storms and wildfires, and indirectly, through effects on food and water security, air quality, the range of vectors such as *Aedes* mosquitoes and ticks, displacement and conflict, and mental health. It amplifies existing inequalities, because the poorest people and countries are most exposed and least able to adapt, while having contributed least to emissions.

**Heat.** Mortality rises steeply above a threshold temperature, especially among people aged over 75, those with cardiovascular, respiratory or renal disease, dementia or severe mental illness, people taking certain medicines (diuretics, anticholinergics, some antipsychotics), people living alone, and residents of top-floor flats and care homes. In England, UKHSA operates a **heat–health alert** system with yellow, amber and red alerts based on forecast impacts; it estimated about 3,000 heat-associated deaths in the summer of 2022, when temperatures above 40 °C were recorded in the UK for the first time. Simple measures save lives: identifying vulnerable people in advance, checking on them, keeping homes cool, adjusting medication where appropriate and ensuring hydration. Longer-term adaptation includes building design, urban green space and care home standards.

**Mitigation co-benefits.** Many measures that reduce greenhouse gas emissions also improve health directly: active travel (more physical activity, less air pollution), diets with less red and processed meat, and warmer, better-insulated homes. The NHS in England has committed to net zero for the emissions it controls directly by 2040, and for those it can influence (including the supply chain) by 2045. Health care contributes about 4–5% of national emissions; inhalers containing propellant gases, anaesthetic gases and medicines are significant sources.

## Chemicals, radiation and other hazards {#chemicals}

- **Lead** causes neurodevelopmental harm in children with no known safe threshold. Sources include old lead paint, lead water pipes, some imported cosmetics and remedies, contaminated soil and certain hobbies and occupations. In England, the **public health intervention concentration** for blood lead in children was lowered in 2021 to **0.24 µmol/L (5 µg/dL)**, at or above which a case is notified and the source investigated.
- **Radon** is a radioactive gas from uranium in rocks and soil that accumulates in buildings in some areas (for example parts of Cornwall, Devon, Derbyshire and Northamptonshire). It is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the UK after smoking, and its risk is multiplied in smokers. The UK action level for homes is 200 Bq/m³; remediation (sumps, ventilation) is effective.
- **Carbon monoxide** binds haemoglobin and causes headache, nausea, confusion and death; it should be suspected when several people or pets in one building are ill, and confirmed by carboxyhaemoglobin measurement. Alarms and appliance servicing prevent it.
- **Water.** Safe drinking water depends on protection of sources, treatment and distribution. Outbreaks still occur (for example *Cryptosporidium* in public supplies).
- **Chemical incidents** (fires, spills, deliberate releases) are managed by emergency services with UKHSA advice on exposure, decontamination and health effects; see [[emergency-critical-care/major-incidents]].
- **Environmental noise** from roads, railways and aircraft disturbs sleep and is associated with hypertension and cardiovascular disease.

::: example A child with a raised blood lead {#ex-lead}
A 3-year-old girl is investigated for iron-deficiency anaemia and constipation. Her parents mention that she often puts flakes of paint in her mouth. The family recently moved into a Victorian terraced house that is being renovated, with old paint being sanded off the window frames. Her blood lead concentration is 0.48 µmol/L. Interpret the result and outline the public health response.
::: solution
**Interpretation.** 0.48 µmol/L (about 10 µg/dL) is twice the English public health intervention concentration of 0.24 µmol/L. Iron deficiency increases lead absorption, and pica (eating non-food substances) is a classic route of exposure. Lead paint was widely used in houses built before the 1960s, and dry sanding of old paint creates fine lead dust that settles on floors, windowsills and toys.

**Notification and investigation.** The result is notified to the local UKHSA health protection team. Environmental health officers visit the home to identify sources (paint, dust, water pipes, soil, cosmetics or traditional remedies, parental occupations or hobbies that bring lead home on clothes) and advise on safe remediation.

**Reducing exposure.** Stop dry sanding; work on old paint should be done by trained contractors using wet methods and containment, with the child away from the house; clean with damp cloths and mops; wash hands and toys often. Siblings and other children in the home should be tested, and pregnant household members advised, because lead crosses the placenta.

**Clinical care.** Treat the iron deficiency, which also reduces absorption; repeat the blood lead to confirm that it is falling; and refer to a paediatrician, who decides with specialist toxicology advice whether chelation is needed (rarely at this level). Developmental follow-up is appropriate, because there is no known safe level of lead for the developing brain.
:::
:::

## Occupational health {#occupational}

Work can harm health, and health can affect the ability to work. Good work is also one of the strongest determinants of good health: unemployment is associated with worse physical and mental health and higher mortality.

::: definition Occupational disease and work-related ill health {#def-occupational}
An **occupational disease** is a disease caused mainly or entirely by an exposure at work, such as mesothelioma from asbestos, silicosis from crystalline silica, or noise-induced hearing loss. **Work-related ill health** is broader: illness caused or made worse by work, including musculoskeletal disorders and stress, depression or anxiety, which in Great Britain together account for most cases of self-reported work-related ill health.

The **hierarchy of control** ranks measures to protect workers, from most to least effective:

1. **Elimination**: remove the hazard (stop using asbestos).
2. **Substitution**: replace it with something less hazardous (water-based instead of solvent-based paints).
3. **Engineering controls**: isolate people from the hazard (enclosure, local exhaust ventilation, guarding, noise barriers).
4. **Administrative controls**: change the way people work (job rotation, limiting exposure time, training, signage).
5. **Personal protective equipment** (PPE): respirators, gloves, hearing protection.

PPE is the last line of defence, because it depends on correct selection, fit, use and maintenance by every worker every time, and protects only the wearer.
:::

### The legal framework in Great Britain {#law}

The **Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974** places a general duty on employers to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health, safety and welfare of employees and others affected by their work. The **Health and Safety Executive** (HSE) and local authorities enforce it. Specific regulations include:

- the **Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002** (COSHH), which require assessment and control of exposure to hazardous substances, and health surveillance where appropriate. **Workplace exposure limits** for many substances are published by the HSE (EH40);
- the **Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012**;
- the **Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005**, which set a lower exposure action value of 80 dB(A) (employers must provide information and make hearing protection available), an upper exposure action value of 85 dB(A) (hearing protection compulsory, a programme of noise control and health surveillance required), and an exposure limit value of 87 dB(A), taking account of hearing protection, which must not be exceeded;
- the **Control of Vibration at Work Regulations 2005** (hand–arm vibration syndrome);
- the **Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013** (RIDDOR). Employers must report certain diagnosed occupational diseases (including carpal tunnel syndrome, severe cramp of the hand or forearm, occupational dermatitis, hand–arm vibration syndrome, occupational asthma, tendonitis, occupational cancers and diseases due to biological agents) when they receive a written diagnosis from a doctor linking the disease to the work.

People with some **prescribed diseases** caused by work can claim Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit, without having to prove employer negligence.

### Important occupational diseases {#diseases}

- **Asbestos-related disease.** Inhaled asbestos fibres cause mesothelioma, lung cancer, asbestosis and pleural plaques, decades after exposure (typically 30–40 years or more for mesothelioma). Use of asbestos was banned in the UK in 1999, but asbestos remains in many buildings built before 2000, and people who disturb it (builders, plumbers, electricians) are still exposed. Mesothelioma kills over 2,000 people a year in Great Britain, mostly men exposed in shipbuilding, construction and industry decades ago. Almost all mesothelioma is caused by asbestos, so every case should prompt a detailed occupational history.
- **Silicosis** from respirable crystalline silica in quarrying, stonemasonry, construction and, recently, the cutting of engineered stone kitchen worktops, which has caused severe, rapidly progressive silicosis in young workers; Australia banned engineered stone in 2024.
- **Occupational asthma**, caused by sensitisers such as isocyanates (spray painting), flour and grain dust (bakers), wood dust, latex, glutaraldehyde and laboratory animals, or by irritants. Early diagnosis and removal from exposure give the best chance of recovery.
- **Occupational dermatitis**, irritant or allergic, common in hairdressers, health care workers (wet work and gloves), cleaners, cooks and construction workers (cement).
- **Noise-induced hearing loss**, with a characteristic notch at around 4 kHz on the audiogram, and tinnitus.
- **Hand–arm vibration syndrome**, with vascular (white finger), sensory and musculoskeletal components, in users of vibrating tools.
- **Occupational cancers**, including bladder cancer from aromatic amines (historically in dye and rubber workers), and the scrotal cancer of chimney sweeps that Percivall Pott described in 1775.
- **Musculoskeletal disorders** and **work-related stress, depression and anxiety**, the commonest causes of working days lost.
- **Infections**: needlestick injuries in health care (hepatitis B and C, HIV), zoonoses in farm workers and vets (Q fever, leptospirosis), and tuberculosis.

::: example A baker with asthma {#ex-baker}
A 34-year-old man who has worked for three years in a supermarket bakery presents with wheeze and breathlessness that started a year ago. He has rhinitis at work and his symptoms are better on holiday. What is the likely diagnosis, how should it be confirmed, and what are the implications?
::: solution
**Likely diagnosis.** Occupational asthma caused by a sensitiser, probably flour dust or fungal alpha-amylase (an enzyme added to flour). Rhinitis often precedes the asthma, and improvement away from work is a key clue.

**Confirmation.** Confirm asthma (spirometry with reversibility, FeNO). Serial peak expiratory flow recording at least four times a day for several weeks, including periods at and away from work, analysed with a validated method, gives objective evidence of a work-related pattern. Specific IgE or skin prick tests to flour and amylase support sensitisation. Refer early to a specialist in occupational lung disease, because the diagnosis has major consequences for his job and should be made objectively.

**Management.** The aim is to stop exposure to the sensitiser as early as possible: continued exposure worsens the prognosis, and asthma can persist after exposure ends, especially if diagnosis is delayed. Respiratory protection alone is usually not sufficient. With his consent, the employer should be informed so that exposure can be reduced for him and his colleagues (engineering controls such as dust extraction, avoiding dusty tasks such as tipping flour), and the employer must report the diagnosis under RIDDOR. He may be eligible for Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit. Health surveillance of colleagues may find other cases. Advice on employment rights and retraining is important, because occupational asthma frequently leads to job loss.
:::
:::

### Noise exposure {#noise}

Sound level is measured on a logarithmic decibel scale, A-weighted (dB(A)) to reflect the sensitivity of human hearing. Every increase of 3 dB doubles the sound energy, so 88 dB(A) for 4 hours delivers the same noise dose as 85 dB(A) for 8 hours. The **daily personal noise exposure** $L_{EP,d}$ normalises exposure to an 8-hour day:

$$
L_{EP,d} = L_{A} + 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{T}{8}\right),
$$ {#eq-lepd}

where $L_A$ is the average sound level during the exposure and $T$ the exposure time in hours. Exposures in different periods are combined by adding their energies, not their decibels.

::: widget plot
f: L + 10*log10(x/8)
x: 0.25, 8
y: 70, 100
sliders: L=94:80:105:1
hlines: 80; 85; 87
labels: L_{EP,d}\ \text{(dB(A))}
caption: Daily personal noise exposure for a worker exposed to a noise level L dB(A) for x hours, with the rest of the day quiet. The horizontal lines are the lower and upper action values (80 and 85 dB(A)) and the exposure limit value (87 dB(A)). At 94 dB(A), about one hour is enough to reach the upper action value; each 3 dB increase halves the permitted time.
:::

::: example A worker's noise exposure {#ex-noise}
A joiner uses a circular saw producing an average of 92 dB(A) at his ear for 2 hours a day, and is otherwise in quiet surroundings. (a) What is his $L_{EP,d}$? (b) What are his employer's duties? (c) If he also spends 4 hours a day at 85 dB(A) in a noisy workshop, what is his total $L_{EP,d}$?
::: solution
(a) $L_{EP,d} = 92 + 10\log_{10}(2/8) = 92 - 6.0 = 86.0$ dB(A).

(b) This exceeds the upper exposure action value (85 dB(A)) but not the limit value (87 dB(A), taking account of hearing protection). The employer must reduce exposure as far as reasonably practicable by a programme of technical and organisational measures (quieter equipment, enclosures, limiting time), designate hearing protection zones and make hearing protection compulsory there, and provide health surveillance (regular audiometry), information and training.

(c) Add energies: $10\log_{10}\!\left[\dfrac{2 \times 10^{9.2} + 4 \times 10^{8.5}}{8}\right] = 10\log_{10}(3.96 \times 10^{8} + 1.58 \times 10^{8}) = 10\log_{10}(5.54 \times 10^{8}) = 87.4$ dB(A).

His total daily exposure is about 87.4 dB(A). Without hearing protection, this would exceed the exposure limit value of 87 dB(A), so effective noise control and properly fitted hearing protection are essential.
:::
:::

### The clinician's role {#clinician}

Occupational and environmental causes of disease are frequently missed. Every clinician should be able to take an **occupational history**: What is your job? What do you actually do? What are you exposed to (dusts, fumes, chemicals, noise, vibration, infections, stress)? Do others at work have similar symptoms? Are the symptoms better at weekends or on holiday? What jobs have you done in the past, especially in industry, construction or shipbuilding? For diseases with long latency, such as mesothelioma, the relevant exposure may have been 40 years ago.

Clinicians also issue **fit notes**, which since 2022 can be issued by registered nurses, occupational therapists, pharmacists and physiotherapists as well as doctors, and can advise on phased return, altered hours, amended duties or workplace adaptations. Occupational health services advise employers on fitness for work, adjustments and health surveillance. The Equality Act 2010 requires employers to make reasonable adjustments for disabled employees.

::: warning PPE is not the first answer
When a workplace exposure is found, the instinctive response is to recommend a mask, gloves or ear defenders. These are the least effective controls. Respirators protect only if they are the right type, fit-tested to the individual's face, worn throughout exposure and maintained; facial hair breaks the seal; hearing protection is often removed because it is uncomfortable or prevents communication. The law requires employers to work down the hierarchy of control: elimination, substitution and engineering controls first. A clinician's letter or report that recommends only PPE may leave the worker and colleagues exposed.
:::

::: quiz
A 68-year-old retired plumber presents with breathlessness and a unilateral pleural effusion. Pleural biopsy shows mesothelioma. Which of the following is correct?
- [ ] An occupational history is not relevant because he retired several years ago.
- [x] His exposure to asbestos may have occurred 30–40 or more years ago, and he and his family may be entitled to compensation.
- [ ] Mesothelioma is mainly caused by smoking.
- [ ] The diagnosis is reportable by his former employer under RIDDOR only if he is still employed.
::: solution
Almost all mesothelioma is caused by asbestos, with a latency typically of 30–40 years or more, so retirement is irrelevant. A detailed occupational history (and history of household exposure, for example from washing a relative's work clothes) should be taken. He may be entitled to Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit, lump-sum payments under statutory schemes and civil compensation; specialist advice should be offered early. Deaths from mesothelioma are referred to the coroner because they are usually due to industrial disease. Smoking does not cause mesothelioma, although it multiplies the lung cancer risk from asbestos.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1700 Bernardino Ramazzini published *De Morbis Artificum Diatriba* (Diseases of Workers), which described the diseases of more than 50 occupations and urged physicians to ask their patients what work they did. In 1775 Percivall Pott described scrotal cancer in chimney sweeps who had been sent up chimneys as children, the first occupational cancer. The Factory Acts of the 19th century began to regulate working conditions in Britain. In the United States, Alice Hamilton's investigations of lead and other industrial poisons in the early 20th century founded industrial medicine. In December 1952 a smog in London caused around 4,000 excess deaths in the following weeks, with later estimates much higher; it led to the Clean Air Act 1956. In 1955 Richard Doll showed that asbestos workers had a greatly increased risk of lung cancer, and in 1960 Christopher Wagner and colleagues linked mesothelioma to crocidolite mining in South Africa. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 created the modern British system of workplace regulation.
:::

::: exercise Hierarchy of control {level=1}
A furniture workshop uses a solvent-based glue that causes headaches, and workers sand hardwood with hand-held sanders, producing visible dust. Propose controls for each hazard, in order of the hierarchy of control.
::: solution
**Solvent glue.** Elimination: a process that needs no glue, if feasible. Substitution: a water-based adhesive with lower volatility and toxicity. Engineering: local exhaust ventilation at the gluing bench, enclosed application. Administrative: limit the quantity in use, close containers, training, rotation. PPE: suitable gloves, and respiratory protection only if exposure cannot otherwise be controlled.

**Hardwood dust** (a respiratory sensitiser and, for some hardwoods, a cause of nasal cancer). Elimination or substitution: pre-finished components, or processes that make less dust. Engineering: sanders with integrated on-tool extraction connected to a suitable extraction unit, and downdraught benches; vacuum cleaning rather than sweeping or compressed air. Administrative: maintenance and testing of extraction, training, health surveillance (respiratory questionnaires). PPE: fit-tested respirators for residual exposure.
:::
:::

A region has 20,000 deaths a year among adults aged 30 and over. Policies are expected to reduce population-weighted PM₂.₅ by 5 µg/m³. Using $RR_{10} = 1.06$, estimate how many deaths a year would be prevented in the long term. Give a whole number.
::: solution
$RR = 1.06^{0.5} = 1.0296$. Attributable fraction $= 1 - 1/1.0296 = 0.0287$. Deaths prevented $\approx 20{,}000 \times 0.0287 = 575$ a year.

::: exercise Combining noise exposures {level=2 check="round(10*10*log10((4*10^9 + 4*10^8.5)/8))/10"}
A worker spends 4 hours at 90 dB(A) and 4 hours at 85 dB(A). Calculate the $L_{EP,d}$ to 1 decimal place.
::: solution
$L_{EP,d} = 10\log_{10}\!\left[\dfrac{4 \times 10^{9.0} + 4 \times 10^{8.5}}{8}\right] = 10\log_{10}(5 \times 10^8 + 1.58 \times 10^8) = 10\log_{10}(6.58 \times 10^8) = 88.2$ dB(A). The 85 dB(A) period contributes little; the louder period dominates.

::: exercise A household with headaches {level=2}
In January, a woman brings her two children to the GP with headaches, nausea and tiredness for a week; she has similar symptoms, and they all feel better at school and work. Their dog is lethargic. What should be suspected and done?
::: solution
**Carbon monoxide poisoning** should be suspected: several members of a household (and a pet) with non-specific symptoms that improve away from home, in winter when heating is used. Measure carboxyhaemoglobin (venous or arterial blood, or breath CO; pulse oximetry does not distinguish it), bearing in mind that levels fall after leaving the source. If confirmed or strongly suspected, refer urgently for assessment and high-flow oxygen; severe cases (loss of consciousness, neurological signs, pregnancy, cardiac ischaemia) need hospital care. The family should not return home until the appliance has been checked: advise them to contact the gas emergency service and leave the house if the source may still be present. Report to the local health protection team, which can advise and arrange investigation, and recommend an audible CO alarm and regular servicing of appliances. Other households in the building may be affected.

::: exercise Planning for a heatwave {level=3}
A GP practice serves many older people, including two care homes. UKHSA issues an amber heat–health alert for the coming week. Describe what the practice and its partners should do, and explain why some medicines matter.
::: solution
**Identify those at risk** using the practice register: people aged over 75, living alone, with cardiovascular, respiratory or renal disease, diabetes, dementia, severe mental illness, Parkinson's disease, or reduced mobility, and residents of the care homes.

**Act.** Contact the most vulnerable people (or arrange for family, carers or community services to check on them daily). Advise on keeping homes cool (closing curtains on sunny windows, opening windows when it is cooler outside), staying out of the heat in the middle of the day, drinking enough fluids and recognising heat exhaustion and heatstroke. Work with the care homes to monitor room temperatures, provide cool areas, encourage fluids and watch for deterioration.

**Medicines.** Diuretics and SGLT2 inhibitors increase fluid loss; ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers can worsen kidney function in dehydration; anticholinergics and some antipsychotics impair sweating and thermoregulation; lithium levels can rise with dehydration. Review high-risk patients, give advice on what to do if they become unwell ("sick day" guidance where relevant), and do not stop medicines routinely without individual assessment.

**Afterwards.** Review what happened, including deaths and admissions, and update the practice's heat plan. Longer-term measures include building adaptation, green space and care home standards.
