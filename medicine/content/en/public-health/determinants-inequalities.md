Health determinants are the reasons patients arrive at clinics with different bodies, and inequalities are the measured, unjust, avoidable differences in who arrives first, sicker, and less prepared. **This chapter covers the evidence architecture of the social determinants (the gradient itself - not just poverty but rank - with the Whitehall studies as its signature, the mechanisms from weathering to wealth, and the fundamental-cause framework), the life-course and early-development evidence (fetal programming, adverse childhood experiences, and the schooling-and-earnings returns of early intervention), the named risk factors of modern mortality (poverty, housing, education, employment, income, food, tobacco, alcohol, drugs, obesity inactivity, and social connection, each with its epidemiology and its policy lever), the measurement and surveillance of inequality (how gaps are quantified, the ratio-versus-rate debates, the area-deprivation indices and their individual-vs-ecological traps), and the closing of gaps: the Health-in-All-Policies and proportionate-universalism programmes, the cost-of-illness-versus-cost-of-investment arithmetic, and the honest finding that most health inequalities are a distribution problem wearing a medical coat.**

## The gradient

::: definition The social determinants and their mechanisms {#def-determinants}
**The central empirical fact of medical geography is the gradient: within every society, health and lifespan improve stepwise with income, education, occupation and rank, so that the middle classes die worse than the rich and better than the poor, and the gradient runs through the whole distribution rather than stopping at a poverty line - the finding of the Whitehall studies of London civil servants (the original cohort and the follow-up showing social grade predicting mortality from almost every cause, with classic risk factors, fitness and even brain volume explaining only part of it) and reproduced in every dataset since, including the recent US analyses showing the US gradient steepening while absolute levels lag peer nations. The mechanisms are cumulative and partly known: material (income, housing quality and cold, food security, the ability to heat and eat and fix), behavioural-but-not-free-choice (the cost and marketing and availability asymmetries that stratify smoking, alcohol, diet and activity), psychosocial (status, control and demand - the stress physiology of low control and high demand, the 'weathering' literature on allostatic load and accelerated biological ageing in discriminated and deprived populations, and the telomere-and-cortisol-era biology of hardship), and developmental (below). The life-course evidence adds timing: the developmental-origins literature (the Barker hypothesis and the Dutch-famine-and-British-1948-birth-cohort lines) showing fetal and infant nutrition programming blood pressure, glucose metabolism and organ structure; the adverse-childhood-experiences epidemiology (the dose-response associations of childhood adversity with adult heart disease, mental illness, addiction and early death, with the mediating pathways now partly reversible - the intervention trials on parenting and the mental-health returns); education as the second determinant with the largest evidence behind it (the schooling-mortality associations and the natural experiments of compulsory-schooling and reduced-class-size reforms whose earnings-and-health payoffs were measured decades later); and neighbourhood and place (the postcode effect quantified - the two-decade life-expectancy gaps between neighbouring districts of British and American cities, the walkability-and-food-environment-and-air-quality-and-green-space literatures, and the neighbourhood-effects trials - the housing-relocation and Moving-to-Opportunity-style studies whose mixed but suggestive results (mental-health and mortality gains where children moved young) show that places get under the skin over years). The theoretical frames clinicians should carry: the fundamental-causes theory (that when a disease's causes are identified and fixed, advantaged groups capture the fix first, so inequalities regenerate by new routes - the smoking-and-HIV-and-statins-and-cancer-screening-uptake history repeating), the causes-of-the-causes distinction (why risk-factor prevalence itself is patterned by wealth, marketing, and the built environment), and the equity-vs-equality-ethics the bioethics course develops (the difference between unequal treatment and unfair difference, with the Marmot-Strand-Strategic-Review-style frameworks that turn the fact into obligation). The moral arithmetic is public health's strongest claim and its most attacked: within countries these gaps are unnecessary, avoidable and unjust by any reasonable standard, they cost the economies tens of billions in lost work, health care and benefits, and they narrow only with deliberate distributional policy - which is the next section's problem.**
:::

## The measurement

::: definition Measuring and surveilling inequality {#def-measurement}
**Gaps are measured four ways and the choice is political before it is statistical: absolute differences in rates or life expectancy (the deaths per 100,000 subtracted; the life-expectancy gap in years), relative ratios (the highest-over-lowest quotient that rises even when everyone improves, if the top improves faster - the 'inequality increased while health improved' paradox that headlines misread constantly), population-attributable fractions (what share of all disease would vanish if the gap closed, which converts inequality into a disease-burden number finance understands - the tens-of-percent-of-cardiovascular-disease-in-each-country estimates), and social-gradient summaries (the index-of-regression-based measure and concentration index that capture whether improvements landed rich-first or poor-first across a whole programme). The instruments of routine surveillance: area-deprivation indices (the multiple-deprivation-index-style composites of income, employment, education, health, crime, barriers and environment - powerful, used for resource allocation and research targeting, and correctly criticised for the ecological fallacy risk - an affluent person in a deprived ward is not deprived and the small-area estimate should never replace the person's own circumstances at the bedside), linked census-mortality and birth datasets (the gold standard for national gradients, including the ethnic and disability inequalities the census-linked studies made undeniable), health-service data equity analyses (the 'inequality in the use of health care' literature: the inverse-care law - that availability inversely matches need - tested on everything from general-practice lists to hip replacements to CPR documentation, and the digital-uptake gradients now that access increasingly means an app), and surveys with their missing populations (the sampling frames that omit prisoners, the homeless, migrants and the very sick - the epidemiology of 'underbelly' populations that only special studies reach - and the mortality-blindness of surveys of survivors). The classic interpretation traps: small-numbers volatility in tiny areas (why smoothed maps and pooled years are honesty, not cosmetics), period-versus-cohort effects (the educated-cohort mortality rises in middle-aged white Americans - the Case-Deaton 'deaths of despair' analysis of drugs, alcohol and suicide - that is a generational story no cross-section shows), the ratio-versus-rate confusion, and attribution overreach (association is not mechanism; the alcohol-harm gap between deprived drinkers is about pattern and context as well as volume, and the obesity gradient is about food economics before will). The clinician's role in measurement is smaller but real: recording occupation, housing and income-adequacy questions where validated, asking the social-history questions that screening and self-management programmes miss (the 'can you afford the inhaler' audit), and interpreting the clinic's own data by deprivation quintile and ethnicity - because every service's inequality audit is the local version of the national one, and the finding 'our patients are all the same once they reach us' is a request to look at who never reached us.**
:::

## The closure

::: definition Closing the gap: policy, proportionate universalism and the clinic's share {#def-closure}
**What actually narrows gaps: the evidence base of the interventions is strongest where it changes the distribution of money, place and start-in-life rather than information alone. The income-and-material levers: cash-transfer and minimum-wage and benefit-floor studies (the child-benefit and earned-income-credit evaluations showing mental-health, hospitalisation and schooling effects of income itself - the 'cash is a health intervention' literature), housing-first and quality-retrofit trials (the warm-home and mould-removal studies' asthma and mental-health outcomes), and the debt-and-benefit-navigation services now embedded in some clinics. The early-life levers: the parenting and early-education trials (the Perry-Preschool-and-SYC-and-Extra-Hands cohorts whose 30-year follow-ups quantify the cardiovascular, mental-health and earnings returns), perinatal support programmes for the stressed-and-deprived-pregnancies, and the school-meals-and-healthy-start-style-provision evaluations. The commodity levers where gradients invert fastest when price moves: tobacco taxation and smoke-free policies (the deprivation-gap narrowing when prices rise because lower-income smokers respond more to price - the most-consistently-found result in the field), alcohol pricing and availability, and the sugar-tax evaluations (the product-reform sales data and the modest-but-real low-income purchase responses). The services levers: outreach and navigation that meet people (the invited screening with language support that closed uptake gaps, the 'opt-out' and community-setting designs, the consultation-and-interpreter-reforms), differentiated and universal-adjacent access (the same-day-and-working-hours-and-pharmacy-based services that pull the unmet need), and proportionate universalism - Marmot's formulation that the population-wide services must be universal with intensity scaled to need (because targeted-only programmes get underfunded and stigmatised while universal-only leave gaps widening). The governance frames: health-in-all-policies (the fiscal, transport, housing, education and Justice portfolios' quantified health effects, the health-impact-assessment requirement, and the WHO's health-in-all-policies implementation reports), the 'making every health-care contact count' equity mandate (the NHS-Long-Term-Plan-style fairness commitments with their statutory inequality-reduce duties and their repeated audit finding: duties without data, budget and accountability lapse), and the political economy nobody teaches (why the effective interventions - early years, housing, tobacco price, income floors - lose to concentrated costs and diffuse benefits, and how the inquiry-reports'- 'cross-government strategy' recommendation dies). The clinic's share: the right-hand-side work of asking and acting on the social history (the 'food is a vital sign' and housing-as-comorbidity practice programmes and the social-prescribing link workers with their mixed-but-promising evaluations), the equity-of-access audits of every local programme, the 'cost-of-living crisis response' clinics and warm spaces the British hospitals ran, and the advocacy duty the professional bodies now write into ethics (the 'speaking publicly on the determinants' licence and its limits); and the honest summary a clinician should be able to give a patient or a parliament: medicine treats the person the conditions harmed, and public health - meaning government, markets and communities, not doctors alone - decides how many people it harms next year, which is why the gap narrows with budgets and buildings and prices, and only afterwards with prescriptions.**
:::

## Where this leads

The epidemiological tools for measuring these gaps are in [[epidemiology-ebm/measuring-disease]] and [[epidemiology-ebm/observational-studies]], the screening-equity problem in [[public-health/screening]], the tobacco, alcohol and food policy detail in [[public-health/prevention-promotion]] and [[psychiatry/substance-use]], the housing-and-environment detail in [[public-health/environmental-occupational]], and the global replication of these patterns in [[public-health/global-health]].

::: history
**The doctrine that where you live, work and learn determine your health is older than germ theory - the 'air, water and places' of the Hippocratic treatise, the sixteenth-century civic medicine of John Graunt's bills of mortality, and above all the statistical revolution begun by William Farr, whose annual letters from the Registrar General's office in the 1840s-80s tabulated deaths by district, occupation and dwelling, correlated cholera with water supply and social grade, and invented mortality statistics as a science of society. Edwin Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population, Simon's medical-officer reports, and the slum-clearance and public-health acts of the 1848-75 made the connection policy before it was mechanism; Virchow's 1848 Silesian-typhus commission ('medicine is a social science, and politics is nothing but medicine on a larger scale') and his pathological insistence that poverty bred epidemic disease remain the field's founding quotation. The Nobel-era bacteriology then complicated the story twice: specificity of cause pushed official attention from conditions to germs, while the epidemiologic transition theory (not 'transition' of the mind but of mortality - the factorial-cause studies of the 1930s and the 'one factor at a time' medicine of the same decades) turned chronic disease into the object. The modern determinants science begins with the gradient's discovery: O'Connor's and the Scottish registering-clinician-era notices of class differentials, then the Black Reports of 1980-82, whose committee of British medical-officers quantified the persistence of class health inequalities despite universal health care and concluded that most of the answer lay outside medicine. The Whitehall Studies (Morris and Sherry's 1958-66 first cohort, then Marmot's Whitehall II from 1985) made the gradient a mechanism-seeking science rather than a curiosity, and the same years produced the life-course school (Barker's malnutrition-and-mortality reanalysis of wartime cohorts in 1986 and the developmental-origins programme), the fundamental-causes theory (Link and Phelan 1995), and the psychosocial-workplace model (Karasek and Theorell's demand-control literature from 1979). The measurement of inequity matured with Whitehead's and Dahlgren's 1991 concepts of 'inequities in health' as the ethical object, the Peters and SocBioHealth-adjacent gradient indices, and the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health (2005-2008, Michael Marmot chairing), whose closing report distilled the evidence and named 'the causal pathway of the social gradient itself' as the global target, echoing the 1978 Alma-Ata primary-health-care promise and its social determinants chapter. The UK's Marmot Review (Fair Society, Healthy Lives, 2010) translated it into national policy (the six strategic recommendations and the proportionate-universalism formula) and the subsequent Marmot-Index and 'Health in All Policies' and 'inclusion of health equity in all policies' era gave the idea a dashboard. The counter-evidence era keeps it honest: the OECD reviews and UK analyses showing inequalities widening in life expectancy after 2010 (the austerity-debate literature using the 'Natural Experiment' framework after the 2012 Welfare-Reform-Act changes, whose findings on welfare-rollout-and-mental-health-and-destitution-effects made the causal claim uncomfortably easy - and its critics' methodological counterpoints equally cited), the US-exceptionalism analyses (the Stokes and the 'unequal-treatment' studies and the Commonwealth-Fund comparisons, with the 'deaths of despair' cohort finding of Case and Deaton in 2015), and the universal-health-coverage literature showing that coverage without equity-audits reproduces the gradient inside the clinic. The current chapter is the pandemic's inequality data - the mortality-by-deprivation-and-ethnicity tables that made the 'conditions' argument in a week - and the 'levelling up' and 'health equity' programme politics that followed. Its one-line history lesson: Farr knew, Chadwick legislated, the Black Report documented, the commissions re-documented; the knowledge has never been the bottleneck.** - [[public-health/prevention-promotion]], [[epidemiology-ebm/observational-studies]], [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]].**
:::

::: summary
- Within every society health follows the social gradient: rank predicts mortality from nearly every cause; classic risk factors, behaviour and even biology explain only part of it, and the gradient is produced by income, housing, work, education, early life, place and stress physiology acting together over decades.
- The life-course evidence (fetal programming, adverse childhood experiences, education and earnings returns, neighbourhood relocation studies) shows timing matters as much as level, and that early intervention has measurable adult-disease returns.
- Inequalities are measured as absolute gaps, relative ratios, attributable fractions and gradient indices; deprivation indices and linked census data are the surveillance tools; the ecological fallacy, ratio-versus-rate effects and missing populations are the traps.
- Fundamental causes mean gaps regenerate: whenever a fix arrives, advantage captures it first (smoking, screening, statins, now digital care), so equity must be deliberately distributed, audited and funded.
- Effective gap-closing is distributional: income floors and housing quality, early-years programmes, price levers on tobacco and alcohol, and proportionate universalism - universal services with intensity scaled to need.
- Clinicians contribute the social history, the equity audits of their own services, make-every-contact-count and navigation work, anchor-institution power, and the public advocacy that the determinants evidence licenses.
:::

A unit's birth-mode data: women from the two most-deprived quintiles are four times likelier to deliver by emergency caesarean and book antenatal care a month later than the least deprived; the consultants say 'these are lifestyle differences'.

**'Lifestyle'-is-a-diagnosis-of-the-data ([[#def-determinants]], [[#def-measurement]], [[obstetrics-gynaecology/labour-birth]]): the audit before the excuse - 'later booking is itself the outcome to explain (the knowledge-and-transport-and-work-and-previous-bad-experience-and-language-and-visa-and-no-fixed-address factors, each auditable), and emergency-pathway differences decompose into: who books early (the risk-stratification and birth-plan conversations happen in the booked visits), who can raise concerns and be heard (the consultation-length-and-shared-decision-making gradient and the documented ethnic-disparities-in-reports-of-not-being-listened-to literature), and who arrives in labour unmonitored (the anomaly-detection and IUGR-detection gradient that later booking mechanically worsens); the 'lifestyle' claim tested - 'smoking-and-obesity-and-age do partly explain it, and each is itself gradient-produced (the fundamental-causes pattern), so 'lifestyle' names the mechanism one step downstream of the council and the employer, and statistically explains less than the booking interval once you adjust for it; the interventions with evidence - 'the personalised-and-community-midwife continuity models (the Birthplace-and-Group-Health-adjacent literature and the ethnic-minority-and-deprived-cohort trials of community-based continuity showing preterm-birth-and-satisfaction gains), the 'bring-forward-the-book' outreach (the first-trimester-in-the-community and pharmacy-and-employer-site booking), the interpreter-and-'listening-visit' quality work, and the feedback loop back to the unit's own equity dashboard; and the leadership sentence - 'the disparities are the unit's quality signal, not the population's character flaw - which is the same sentence this whole chapter keeps needing re-auditing.'**

::: exercise Explain the paradox {level=1}
A tabloid headline: 'Cancer survival at record high!' Your public-health colleague: 'And the richest-poor gap widened again.' Explain how both are true in five sentences using this chapter's vocabulary.
::: solution
**Both-true-at-once ([[#def-measurement]], [[#def-determinants]]): survival statistics are averages, and averages can rise while the distribution splits; the new treatments and screening arrive first in advantaged groups (the fundamental-cause mechanism: those with money, education and flexible jobs capture each innovation - the stage-at-diagnosis and trial-participation and digital-booking gradients are the capture routes), so the top-quintile survival curve lifts faster than the bottom's; relative gaps widen during exactly the period when absolute outcomes improve for everyone - which is why 'we are all healthier' and 'fairness worsened' coexist; the policy implication is that every clinical advance needs an equity-delivery plan attached at launch (the targeted-catch-up-and-cost-removal-and-navigation work, not a later apology), because 'first for some, later for all' is not a delivery timetable but a widened-gap generator; and the vocabulary the headline needs is the one the Black-Report-era researchers fought for: inequality is a distribution property, and distributions, not averages, are where fairness lives.'**
:::

::: exercise Write the two-minute consultation that changes a prescription {level=2}

::: exercise Run the health-impact assessment {level=2}
The council is deciding a road scheme that removes a bus lane, closes a children's crossing and demolishes a recreation ground for housing. Write the health-impact assessment one-pager the director of public health asks for: pathways, distribution, evidence, and the two recommendations that survive cost-benefit.
::: solution
**HIA-in-one-page ([[#def-determinants]], [[#def-closure]], [[public-health/environmental-occupational]]): the pathways - 'transport (the mode-shift arithmetic: bus-lane removal lengthens the working-class commute and the active-travel and air-quality effects go the wrong way - the walking-and-cycling-infrastructure trials' physical-activity and mental-health effects), safety (the children's-crossing closure against the road-danger-counts and the equity-of-injury gradient - child-pedestrian harm tracks deprivation), space (the recreation-ground loss and the green-space evidence: the mental-health-and-physical-activity-and-mortality associations and the 'green-gentrification' counter-argument answered by 'retrofit the existing green, do not delete it'), housing (the genuine gain, which is why the honest HIA concedes it and then conditions it - the quality-and-tenure-and-affordability of the housing, not the fact of it, decides its health value), and community (the demolition-of-a-meeting-place effect on social connection, the 'social capital' pathway with its mortality evidence); the distribution - 'each pathway hit hardest falls on the car-less, the young, the outdoor-exercise-dependent and the renters - the equity audit that the scheme's own equality-impact-assessment must reconcile with this one; the evidence-citations kept to the strongest (the active-travel-before-and-after studies, the green-space cohort meta-analyses, the road-safety-infrastructure Cochrane-level findings); and the two recommendations that usually survive the treasurer - '1) keep the crossing and replace the recreation ground on-site as part of the scheme's open-space requirement (the 'net-gain' condition that converts a loss into a lever), and 2) the bus-and-cycle provision is reinstated or the scheme goes back with a health-cost line in the treasury report (the 'health impact assessment means costing the avoided harm, not just the road' sentence that is the whole point of the instrument).'**

::: exercise Testify at the inquiry {level=3}
You are called to a public inquiry into a health-inequality failure (say, the excess deaths in a deprived district traced to a cluster of welfare, housing and service cuts). Draft your testimony's spine: the evidence you would present, the causal language you would and would not use, and the recommendations you would tie to names and dates.
::: solution
**Expert-testimony-architecture ([[#def-determinants]], [[#def-measurement]], [[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]]): the evidence you present - 'the descriptive gradient (the district's life-expectancy and morbidity lines against national and comparator areas, with the ratio-and-rate pair stated honestly), the exposure evidence (the specific policy changes' implementation dates, the coverage-and-income-and-destitution data series, the housing-quality-and-winter-mortality records), the mechanism literature (stress-physiology and weathering and material-deprivation pathways, cited at their actual certainty), and the natural-experiment studies (the welfare-reform and austerity evaluations with their designs named and their limitations recited before opposing counsel does), including your own service's data if you have it - the demand-and-deprivation correlation the clinic watched in real time;
