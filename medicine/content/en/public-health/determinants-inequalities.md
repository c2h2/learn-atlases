Two babies are born on the same day in the same English city, a few miles apart. One is born in a neighbourhood in the most deprived tenth of the country, the other in the least deprived tenth. On current figures, the second baby can expect to live several years longer, and to live almost twenty more of those years in good health. Neither child has yet met a doctor. The difference is already written into the places they will grow up in, the money their families have, the schools they will attend and the work they will do.

Public health starts from this observation. Medical care matters, but most of the variation in health between groups of people comes from the conditions in which they are born, grow, live, work and age. This chapter explains what those conditions are, how they get "under the skin", how inequalities in health are measured, and what kinds of action reduce them. The measures are the same ones met in [[epidemiology-ebm/measuring-disease]], applied to comparisons between social groups.

## What determines health {#determinants}

::: definition Health and its determinants {#def-determinants}
The constitution of the World Health Organization (1948) defines **health** as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity". The definition is often criticised as unattainable, but it makes the point that health is more than the absence of diagnoses.

The **determinants of health** are the factors that influence the health of individuals and populations. They are usually grouped into:

- fixed individual factors: age, sex and genes;
- individual behaviours: smoking, diet, physical activity, alcohol and drug use, sexual behaviour;
- social and community networks: family, friends, community support, social isolation;
- living and working conditions: income, education, employment and working conditions, housing, food, water and sanitation, transport, and access to health and social care;
- general socioeconomic, cultural and environmental conditions: the economy, taxes and benefits, laws, culture and the physical environment.

The **social determinants of health** are the non-medical factors in the last three groups, together with the distribution of power, money and resources that shapes them.
:::

The best-known picture of these layers is the "rainbow" model published by Göran Dahlgren and Margaret Whitehead in 1991. The individual sits at the centre with fixed factors such as age and sex. Around them are layers of lifestyle, social networks, living and working conditions, and finally the wider socioeconomic, cultural and environmental conditions. The model is useful because it shows that behaviour, the layer clinicians talk about most, is surrounded by and shaped by the outer layers. People smoke, eat and drink within the constraints of their income, neighbourhood, work and culture.

How much of health is due to each layer? Several analyses have tried to apportion it, and they often conclude that medical care accounts for only a minority, perhaps 10–20%, of variation in health outcomes, with behaviour and social and economic factors accounting for most of the rest. These numbers are rough, depend on how the question is asked and should not be quoted as precise. The robust point is qualitative: improving health care alone cannot close the large differences in health between groups.

### How social conditions get under the skin {#pathways}

Saying that income "causes" heart disease needs a mechanism. Four kinds of pathway are usually described, and they act together.

- **Material pathways.** Low income limits access to good housing, healthy food, heating, safe neighbourhoods and transport. Cold, damp housing worsens respiratory and cardiovascular disease. Crowding spreads infection. Hazardous jobs cause injury and occupational disease.
- **Psychosocial pathways.** Insecure work, debt, low control over one's life and discrimination cause chronic stress. Repeated activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system raises blood pressure, worsens insulin resistance and promotes inflammation. The cumulative "wear and tear" of chronic stress is called **allostatic load**.
- **Behavioural pathways.** Smoking, poor diet, physical inactivity and harmful drinking are more common in deprived groups, partly because they are cheaper, more heavily marketed in poorer areas, or used to cope with stress. Behaviour is therefore partly a consequence of social position, not simply a free choice.
- **Life-course pathways.** Disadvantage accumulates. Low birthweight, poor nutrition and adverse childhood experiences have effects that persist into adult life, and disadvantage in one period makes disadvantage in the next more likely.

Access to health care is a fifth route, but in a system free at the point of use it explains only part of the gradient.

## The social gradient {#gradient}

::: theorem The social gradient in health {#thm-gradient}
Health is graded across the whole of the social hierarchy, not divided into a poor-health minority and a healthy majority. At each step up in income, education, occupational grade or neighbourhood deprivation, average health is better and life expectancy longer. The gradient holds for most causes of death and in almost every country studied.
:::

::: proof
The clearest evidence comes from the Whitehall studies of British civil servants. All participants were in stable office employment with access to the NHS, so absolute poverty and lack of health care cannot explain differences between them. In the first Whitehall study, begun in 1967, men in the lowest employment grades had about three times the mortality of men in the highest grades over the following decade, and every grade had higher mortality than the one above it. Classical risk factors (smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol) explained only part of the difference in coronary deaths.

The Whitehall II study, begun in 1985 under Michael Marmot, added women and measured the psychosocial work environment. Low control at work predicted coronary heart disease independently of grade and of conventional risk factors. Together the studies show that relative position, and the control and security that go with it, matter for health, in addition to material deprivation.
:::

The gradient has an important consequence for policy. If only the poorest group had worse health, policy could target that group alone. Because the gradient runs through everyone, most of the excess burden of disease falls on the large middle of the distribution, and policies must act across the whole population with an intensity that increases with disadvantage. Michael Marmot's 2010 review for England called this **proportionate universalism**.

## Inequality, inequity and how they are measured {#measuring}

::: definition Health inequality and health inequity {#def-inequity}
A **health inequality** is any measurable difference in health between groups: by age, sex, area, income, ethnicity or another characteristic. Some inequalities are unavoidable: older people have more disease than younger people.

A **health inequity** is a difference in health that is avoidable, unnecessary and unjust (the definition given by Margaret Whitehead in 1990). Differences that arise from social and economic conditions are inequities because they could be changed.

In UK usage, "health inequalities" is often used to mean the unfair differences, so the reader must check which sense is meant.
:::

Health inequalities are usually described along several axes at once. In England, the NHS and public health bodies use socioeconomic deprivation (measured by area), protected characteristics such as ethnicity, sex, disability and sexual orientation, geography (for example coastal towns), and **inclusion health** groups: people experiencing homelessness, Gypsy, Roma and Traveller communities, sex workers, people in contact with the justice system, vulnerable migrants and people with drug or alcohol dependence. Inclusion health groups often have mortality many times higher than the general population and are poorly captured by routine data.

### Measuring deprivation {#imd}

Deprivation is usually measured for small areas rather than individuals, because routine health data rarely record income. In England the **Index of Multiple Deprivation** (IMD) combines seven domains: income, employment, education and skills, health and disability, crime, barriers to housing and services, and living environment. Each small area (a Lower-layer Super Output Area of about 1,500 people) is ranked from most to least deprived, and the ranks are grouped into fifths (quintiles) or tenths (deciles). Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own indices.

Area measures are practical but imperfect. Not everyone in a deprived area is deprived, and many deprived people live in mixed areas. Assigning an area's characteristic to an individual risks the **ecological fallacy**.

### Simple measures: gaps and ratios {#gap-ratio}

The simplest comparisons use two groups, usually the most and least deprived.

- The **rate difference** (absolute gap) is the rate in the disadvantaged group minus the rate in the advantaged group.
- The **rate ratio** (relative gap) is the rate in the disadvantaged group divided by the rate in the advantaged group.

The two measures can move in opposite directions. If mortality falls in both groups by the same proportion, the ratio is unchanged but the absolute gap narrows. If both fall by the same absolute amount, the gap is unchanged but the ratio widens. Reports should give both.

::: example Absolute and relative gaps {#ex-gaps}
In 2010, age-standardised mortality from cardiovascular disease in the most deprived fifth of a region was 300 per 100,000 per year, and in the least deprived fifth it was 150 per 100,000. By 2020 the rates were 200 and 90 per 100,000. Did inequality narrow or widen?
::: solution
In 2010: rate difference $= 300 - 150 = 150$ per 100,000; rate ratio $= 300/150 = 2.0$.

In 2020: rate difference $= 200 - 90 = 110$ per 100,000; rate ratio $= 200/90 = 2.22$.

The absolute gap narrowed, by 40 deaths per 100,000 per year. The relative gap widened, because the least deprived group improved proportionally more (a 40% fall against 33%). Both statements are true. The absolute gap counts the excess deaths that could be prevented; the relative gap shows that the benefits of progress were shared unequally. A report that quoted only one of them would mislead.
:::
:::

### Measures that use the whole gradient {#sii}

Comparing only the two extreme groups ignores everyone in between and depends on how big those groups are. Measures based on the whole gradient avoid this.

::: definition Slope and relative index of inequality {#def-sii}
Rank the population from most to least deprived and give each group the midpoint of its cumulative share of the population, so that the **relative rank** runs from 0 (most deprived) to 1 (least deprived). For quintiles of equal size the midpoints are 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9.

Fit a straight line by regression of the health measure on relative rank (weighting each group by its population if the groups are unequal).

- The **slope index of inequality** (SII) is the difference between the fitted values at the two ends of the scale, rank 0 and rank 1. It is an absolute measure, in the units of the health measure.
- The **relative index of inequality** (RII) is a relative measure. In UK practice it is often the ratio of the fitted values at the two ends; some authors instead divide the SII by the population mean. The method should always be stated.
:::

The ONS reports life expectancy and healthy life expectancy inequality in England using the SII. In its release covering 2022 to 2024, healthy life expectancy at birth was 49.8 years for males in the most deprived areas and 69.2 years for males in the least deprived areas; for females the figures were 48.2 and 68.5 years. The SII for healthy life expectancy was about 19 years for males and 20 years for females. The gap in total life expectancy is smaller, roughly 8 to 10 years in recent estimates. People in the most deprived areas therefore have shorter lives and spend a larger share of them in poor health.

::: example Calculating the slope index {#ex-sii}
Age-standardised all-cause mortality rates in the five deprivation quintiles of a city are, from most to least deprived: 1,300, 1,100, 1,000, 900 and 800 per 100,000 per year. The quintiles have equal populations. Calculate the SII and the RII (as the ratio of the fitted values at the two ends).
::: solution
Relative ranks: $x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9$, mean $\bar x = 0.5$. Mean rate $\bar y = 5{,}100/5 = 1{,}020$.

Deviations: $x - \bar x = -0.4, -0.2, 0, 0.2, 0.4$; $y - \bar y = 280, 80, -20, -120, -220$.

$$
b = \frac{\sum (x-\bar x)(y - \bar y)}{\sum (x - \bar x)^2} = \frac{-112 - 16 + 0 - 24 - 88}{0.16+0.04+0+0.04+0.16} = \frac{-240}{0.4} = -600.
$$

The intercept is $a = \bar y - b\bar x = 1{,}020 + 300 = 1{,}320$. The fitted rate at rank 0 (most deprived) is 1,320, and at rank 1 (least deprived) is $1{,}320 - 600 = 720$ per 100,000.

SII $= 1{,}320 - 720 = 600$ deaths per 100,000 per year. RII $= 1{,}320/720 = 1.83$.

The SII is larger than the simple gap between the extreme quintiles ($1{,}300 - 800 = 500$), because it estimates the difference between the very top and very bottom of the hierarchy, not between the averages of two fifths.
:::
:::

### How many deaths are due to inequality? {#excess}

A third way to express inequality asks how many deaths, or cases, would not occur if every group had the health of the most advantaged group. This is the logic of the **population attributable fraction** (PAF), applied to social position instead of a single risk factor.

::: example Excess deaths {#ex-excess}
In the city of [[#ex-sii]], each quintile contains 200,000 people. How many deaths a year would be avoided if every quintile had the mortality rate of the least deprived quintile (800 per 100,000)? What fraction of all deaths is that?
::: solution
Deaths in each quintile $=$ rate $\times 200{,}000/100{,}000 =$ rate $\times 2$: 2,600, 2,200, 2,000, 1,800 and 1,600, a total of 10,200.

If every quintile had a rate of 800, each would have 1,600 deaths, a total of 8,000.

Excess deaths $= 10{,}200 - 8{,}000 = 2{,}200$ a year, which is $2{,}200/10{,}200 = 21.6\%$ of all deaths.

Notice where the excess falls: 1,000 in the most deprived quintile, but 1,200 in the three middle quintiles combined. This is the gradient in action, and it is why policies aimed only at the poorest fifth cannot remove most of the excess.
:::
:::

::: warning Rates must be age-standardised before groups are compared
Deprived areas in England often have younger populations than affluent areas, and rural affluent areas often have older ones. Crude death rates would then *understate* the excess mortality in deprived areas, because younger populations have fewer deaths. Comparisons of mortality or disease between areas or groups should use age-standardised rates, or life expectancy, which is independent of age structure. The same caution applies to comparisons by ethnicity, because ethnic groups in the UK have very different age structures.
:::

## Inequalities in the UK: the main patterns {#uk-patterns}

A few patterns recur across the data.

- **Deprivation.** Life expectancy and healthy life expectancy follow a steep gradient by area deprivation, as above. Infant mortality, smoking, obesity in children, cardiovascular and respiratory deaths, lung cancer, suicide and drug-related deaths are all more common in more deprived areas. Since 2010, improvement in life expectancy in England has slowed markedly, and in the most deprived areas, particularly for women, it stalled or fell for some years. The 2020 follow-up of Marmot's review described this as a decade of lost progress.
- **Ethnicity.** Patterns differ by condition and cannot be summarised as "worse" or "better". In England, people of South Asian ethnicity have higher rates of type 2 diabetes and coronary heart disease, and Black people higher rates of hypertension, stroke and some cancers, while some ethnic minority groups have lower overall mortality than the White British group. Maternal mortality in the UK has been substantially higher among Black women than White women in successive MBRRACE-UK reports. Covid-19 mortality in 2020–21 was higher in several minority ethnic groups, largely through occupational exposure, household crowding and deprivation. Racism, both personal and structural, acts through each of the pathways above.
- **Geography.** Coastal towns and former industrial areas in the north of England have some of the worst outcomes, linked to economic decline, poor housing and the movement of healthy younger people away.
- **Severe mental illness and learning disability.** People with severe mental illness die on average 15 to 20 years earlier than the general population, mostly from physical diseases such as cardiovascular and respiratory disease. People with a learning disability also die much younger, and the national mortality review programme (LeDeR) has repeatedly found avoidable deaths.

### The inverse care law {#inverse-care}

In 1971 Julian Tudor Hart, a general practitioner in a Welsh mining village, wrote in *The Lancet* that "the availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need for it in the population served". He called this the **inverse care law**. Practices in deprived areas have more patients with multiple long-term conditions, but have historically received funding and staffing that did not match that extra need, and consultations there are often shorter relative to the complexity of the problems. The inverse care law also operates through uptake: screening, vaccination and NHS Health Checks are taken up less often in deprived areas and in some minority ethnic groups, as later chapters show.

::: example A consultation shaped by the determinants {#ex-case}
A 54-year-old woman, a care worker on a zero-hours contract, attends her GP with breathlessness. She has COPD, smokes 15 cigarettes a day and was admitted twice last winter with exacerbations. She lives in a privately rented flat with black mould in the bedroom and says she cannot afford to heat it. She has missed two pulmonary rehabilitation appointments because she could not take unpaid time off. How could the consultation address the determinants of her health, not only her lungs?
::: solution
**Clinical care first.** Review the COPD diagnosis and inhaler technique, check her vaccinations (influenza, pneumococcal, and COVID-19 if eligible), and make a personalised management plan according to current NICE guidance. Offer evidence-based stop-smoking support with behavioural support and medication or a vaping-based approach.

**Housing.** Cold and damp housing directly worsens COPD. With her consent, the practice can refer to the local authority's environmental health service, which can require a private landlord to deal with serious hazards such as damp and mould, and to schemes that help with energy efficiency and heating. A letter documenting the health impact of the housing can support her case.

**Income.** Ask whether she has had advice about benefits she may be entitled to and refer to a welfare rights or money advice service. Many practices now host these advisers; the evidence that income maximisation improves health is modest but the financial gains can be substantial.

**Work.** A fit note can recommend adjustments. Rehabilitation sessions at a time or place she can manage, or a home-based programme, address the reason she missed them.

**Connection.** A social prescribing link worker can coordinate these referrals and community support.

The point is not that the GP must solve her housing and employment. It is that ignoring them makes clinical care less effective, and that the health service can act as a gateway to the agencies that can help. Asking about money, housing and work is part of a good history.
:::
:::

## What reduces health inequalities? {#action}

Interventions differ in how far "upstream" they act. **Downstream** interventions treat disease or change individual behaviour; **midstream** interventions change living and working conditions; **upstream** interventions change the economic and social structures that produce those conditions.

::: theorem Structural interventions reduce inequalities more reliably than individual ones {#thm-structural}
Interventions that change the environment in which people make choices, or that redistribute resources, tend to reduce health inequalities, because they work without relying on the individual's time, money, education or motivation. Interventions that depend on individuals noticing, understanding and acting on information tend to be taken up more by advantaged groups, and so can widen inequalities even while improving average health.
:::

::: proof
Consider two ways of reducing sugar intake. A leaflet campaign about the dangers of sugar works only for people who read it, believe it, have the money and time to buy and prepare alternatives, and are not under stress that makes change harder. Each of those conditions is more often met in advantaged groups. A tax that leads manufacturers to reformulate drinks reduces the sugar in everyone's drinks without any effort from consumers; it may even benefit lower-income groups more, because they consume more of the affected products.

Systematic reviews of "intervention-generated inequalities" find this pattern repeatedly. Media campaigns and individually targeted advice tend to favour advantaged groups; price measures (tobacco and alcohol taxes, minimum unit pricing), legislation (smoke-free laws), reformulation and structural workplace or housing measures tend to be neutral or to favour disadvantaged groups. The same logic explains why tobacco taxes reduce smoking most among people on low incomes, who are most sensitive to price.
:::

This does not mean that individual interventions are useless. Stop-smoking services, for example, help many people in deprived areas and can be designed to reach them. The principle is about the expected effect on the *distribution* of health, and it means that every programme should check who benefits.

### Marmot's six policy objectives {#marmot}

The 2010 Marmot Review, *Fair Society, Healthy Lives*, set six policy objectives for England, which have since been adopted by many local authorities ("Marmot places"):

1. Give every child the best start in life.
2. Enable all children, young people and adults to maximise their capabilities and have control over their lives.
3. Create fair employment and good work for all.
4. Ensure a healthy standard of living for all.
5. Create and develop healthy and sustainable places and communities.
6. Strengthen the role and impact of ill-health prevention.

The 2020 follow-up added two more: tackle racism, discrimination and their outcomes, and pursue environmental sustainability and health equity together. Only the sixth objective concerns the health service directly. Early childhood comes first because investment there has the largest and longest-lasting effects.

### The role of the health service {#nhs-role}

Health services cannot close the gap alone, but they can avoid widening it and can contribute in several ways.

- **Equitable access.** Funding formulas weighted for need; outreach to groups with low uptake; interpreters; flexible appointments; accessible information.
- **Proportionate universalism in services.** Universal offers such as health visiting, with extra support for families with greater need.
- **Targeted programmes.** NHS England's **Core20PLUS5** approach (introduced in 2021) focuses on the most deprived 20% of the population, locally defined PLUS groups, and five clinical areas for adults: maternity, severe mental illness, chronic respiratory disease, early cancer diagnosis and hypertension (with lipid management). A children's version has its own five areas.
- **Anchor institutions.** NHS organisations are large employers and purchasers. Recruiting locally, paying a living wage and buying from local suppliers act on the determinants directly.
- **Data.** Recording ethnicity, deprivation and disability accurately makes inequalities visible.

### The legal framework in England {#law}

Several laws require public bodies to consider inequalities. The **Equality Act 2010** requires public authorities to have due regard to eliminating discrimination and advancing equality of opportunity for people with protected characteristics (the public sector equality duty). The **Health and Social Care Act 2012** placed duties on the Secretary of State and NHS bodies to have regard to the need to reduce health inequalities in access and outcomes, and the **Health and Care Act 2022** extended similar duties to integrated care boards. Since 2013 local authorities, led by their Directors of Public Health, have been responsible for most public health services in England.

### Inequalities between countries {#global-inequalities}

The same patterns exist between countries. Life expectancy at birth differs by more than 20 years between the countries with the highest and lowest values. The WHO **Commission on Social Determinants of Health**, chaired by Michael Marmot, reported in 2008 under the title *Closing the Gap in a Generation*. Its three overarching recommendations were to improve daily living conditions; to tackle the inequitable distribution of power, money and resources; and to measure and understand the problem and assess the impact of action. The international dimension is taken up in [[public-health/global-health]].

::: quiz
A local authority runs a weight-management campaign based on leaflets, a website and a free smartphone app. After two years, average body mass index among adults has fallen slightly, but the gap in obesity prevalence between the most and least deprived wards has widened. Which explanation is most likely?
- [ ] The campaign was harmful to people in deprived wards.
- [x] The campaign was taken up and acted on more by people in less deprived wards.
- [ ] Obesity is mainly genetic, so the campaign could not affect the gap.
- [ ] The widening must be a chance finding because the average improved.
::: solution
This is the typical pattern of an intervention-generated inequality. Information-based campaigns that rely on individual time, money, digital access and motivation are taken up more by advantaged groups, so they improve average health while widening the gap. The campaign did not make anyone worse off; the gap widened because the benefit was unequal. Structural measures (food environment, pricing, reformulation, active travel) and proportionately targeted support would be expected to do better on equity.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1826 the French physician Louis-René Villermé showed that mortality in the arrondissements of Paris varied with the wealth of their residents, measured by the proportion of untaxed rents. In 1842 Edwin Chadwick's *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain* documented that the average age at death of labourers in Liverpool was far below that of the gentry, and led to the Public Health Act of 1848. In the same year Rudolf Virchow, sent to investigate a typhus epidemic in Upper Silesia, concluded that its causes were poverty and political neglect, and that "medicine is a social science".

In the twentieth century, Britain's Registrar General's social classes allowed mortality to be tracked by occupation. The working group chaired by Sir Douglas Black, set up in 1977, reported in 1980 that class differences in mortality had not narrowed under the NHS and that material conditions were the main explanation. The government of the day published the Black Report with little publicity, but it became the foundation of modern research on inequalities. Margaret Whitehead's *The Health Divide* (1987) updated it. The Whitehall studies of civil servants, from 1967, established the gradient, and the Acheson inquiry (1998), the Marmot Review (2010) and its ten-year follow-up (2020) set the policy agenda in England.
:::

::: summary
- Health is shaped mainly by the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age: the **social determinants of health**. Medical care accounts for a minority of the differences between groups.
- Social conditions affect health through material, psychosocial, behavioural and life-course pathways.
- Health follows a **social gradient** across the whole hierarchy, as the Whitehall studies showed; most excess deaths occur in the middle of the distribution, not only among the poorest.
- An **inequity** is an avoidable, unjust difference in health. Inequalities are measured with absolute gaps, relative gaps, the **slope** and **relative indices of inequality**, and excess deaths. Absolute and relative measures can move in opposite directions; report both and use age-standardised rates.
- In England healthy life expectancy differs by about 19–20 years between the most and least deprived areas (ONS, 2022–24).
- The **inverse care law**: good care tends to be least available where it is most needed.
- Structural interventions (price, regulation, reformulation, housing, income) tend to reduce inequalities; information-based individual interventions can widen them. **Proportionate universalism** combines universal action with intensity matched to need.
- Clinicians contribute by asking about money, housing and work, referring to services that can help, ensuring equitable access, and recording data that make inequalities visible.
:::

::: exercise Rate ratio {level=1 check="1.6"}
Infant mortality is 4.8 per 1,000 live births in the most deprived quintile of a country and 3.0 per 1,000 in the least deprived quintile. Calculate the rate ratio.
::: solution
Rate ratio $= 4.8/3.0 = 1.6$. The rate difference is $4.8 - 3.0 = 1.8$ per 1,000 live births.

::: exercise Healthy life expectancy gap {level=1 check="19.4"}
According to the ONS, healthy life expectancy at birth for males in England in 2022–2024 was 49.8 years in the most deprived areas and 69.2 years in the least deprived areas. What is the difference between the two, in years?
::: solution
$69.2 - 49.8 = 19.4$ years. A man born in one of the least deprived areas can expect almost twenty more years of good health than one born in the most deprived areas. (The ONS's slope index, which uses all deciles, is slightly smaller, at about 19.3 years.)
:::

::: exercise Inequality or inequity? {level=1}
Classify each difference as an inequality that is not an inequity, or as an inequity, and explain briefly. (a) People aged 80 have a higher incidence of dementia than people aged 40. (b) Children in the most deprived areas are more likely to have dental decay than children in the least deprived areas. (c) Men have higher rates of haemophilia A than women. (d) People with severe mental illness die 15–20 years earlier than the general population.
::: solution
(a) Not an inequity: the difference is due mainly to age and is not avoidable in itself. (b) An inequity: dental decay is preventable (fluoride, diet, access to dentistry) and its social patterning arises from avoidable conditions. (c) Not an inequity: the difference arises from X-linked inheritance. (d) An inequity: most of the excess is due to preventable physical disease, smoking, side effects of medication and poorer access to physical health care, all of which can be changed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Gaps moving in opposite directions {level=2 check="round(100*(20/6 - 30/12))/100"}
Smoking prevalence was 30% in the most deprived quintile and 12% in the least deprived quintile in one year. Ten years later it was 20% and 6%. (a) Calculate the absolute gap at both times, in percentage points. (b) Calculate the relative gap (ratio) at both times. Give the change in the ratio (later minus earlier), to 2 decimal places, as your answer.
::: solution
(a) Absolute gap: $30 - 12 = 18$ points earlier; $20 - 6 = 14$ points later. The absolute gap narrowed.

(b) Ratio: $30/12 = 2.50$ earlier; $20/6 = 3.33$ later. The change is $3.33 - 2.50 = 0.83$: the relative gap widened.

Smoking fell in both groups, by more percentage points in the most deprived group but by a smaller proportion (33% against 50%). Whether inequality "improved" depends on the measure, so both should be reported.
:::
:::

::: exercise Slope index from three groups {level=2 check="30"}
A town is divided into three equal-sized deprivation groups (thirds). The percentage of adults reporting poor health is 30% in the most deprived third, 20% in the middle third and 10% in the least deprived third. Using relative ranks of 1/6, 1/2 and 5/6, calculate the slope index of inequality in percentage points.
::: hint
The three points lie exactly on a straight line, so the slope can be read from any two of them.
:::
::: solution
Between ranks 1/6 and 5/6 (a distance of 2/3) the rate falls by $30 - 10 = 20$ points. The slope is $-20/(2/3) = -30$ points per unit of rank. The fitted value at rank 0 is $30 + 30 \times 1/6 = 35\%$; at rank 1 it is $35 - 30 = 5\%$.

SII $= 35 - 5 = 30$ percentage points, larger than the 20-point gap between the extreme thirds, because the SII extrapolates to the very ends of the hierarchy.
:::
:::

::: exercise Population attributable fraction {level=2 check="round(10000*0.4/1.4)/10000"}
In a population, 20% of adults smoke, and smokers have three times the risk of a particular disease compared with non-smokers. Calculate the population attributable fraction, $PAF = \dfrac{p(RR-1)}{1 + p(RR-1)}$, to 4 decimal places.
::: solution
$p(RR - 1) = 0.2 \times 2 = 0.4$. $PAF = 0.4/1.4 = 0.2857$. About 29% of cases of the disease in this population are attributable to smoking and would not occur if no one smoked (assuming the relationship is causal and unconfounded). If smoking prevalence is higher in deprived groups, the PAF is higher there too, and smoking explains part of the gradient in that disease.
:::

::: exercise Age structure and crude rates {level=3}
Area A (deprived) has a young population; area B (affluent) has an old population. Crude all-cause mortality is 900 per 100,000 in A and 1,100 per 100,000 in B. A local councillor argues that B has the bigger health problem. Explain why this conclusion may be wrong, what measure you would calculate instead, and what you would expect it to show.
::: solution
Mortality rises steeply with age, so an older population has more deaths even if its age-specific rates are low. Crude rates mix differences in age structure with differences in risk. Direct age standardisation applies each area's age-specific rates to a common standard population (for example the European Standard Population 2013) to give rates that can be compared; life expectancy is another age-independent measure. Given the strong deprivation gradient in England, A's age-specific rates are likely to be higher at every age, so its age-standardised rate will probably exceed B's, and its life expectancy will be lower. The councillor has confused the number of deaths, which matters for service planning, with the risk of death, which measures health.
:::
:::

::: exercise Designing for equity {level=3}
An integrated care board wants to increase uptake of the NHS Health Check, which is currently 55% in the least deprived quintile and 35% in the most deprived quintile. Propose a plan that is likely to raise uptake overall without widening the gap, and explain how you would evaluate it.
::: solution
**Principles.** Proportionate universalism: keep the universal invitation but direct more effort to groups with low uptake. Reduce the costs of attending (time, travel, lost pay, digital access, language) rather than relying on information alone.

**Actions.** Use practice data to identify non-attenders in deprived areas and in groups with low uptake. Offer checks outside working hours and in community venues (workplaces, places of worship, food banks, community centres, mobile units), delivered by trusted community health workers. Use telephone invitations and text reminders in the languages people use, and pre-booked appointment times rather than "please call to book". Make sure that people found to have high risk are actually followed up with treatment and support, because a check without follow-up has no benefit. Work with local employers to allow paid time off.

**Evaluation.** Measure uptake, and follow-up of those found at high risk, by deprivation quintile, ethnicity and sex before and after the change, with an absolute and a relative measure of the gap (difference and ratio, or SII and RII). Compare with similar areas that did not change their approach, to separate the effect of the plan from secular trends. Check costs and, longer-term, changes in blood pressure control and cardiovascular events by quintile.
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a slope {level=2 check="round(10000*1100/600)/10000"}
In the city of [[#ex-sii]], a later survey gives fitted mortality of 1,100 per 100,000 at relative rank 0 and 600 per 100,000 at rank 1. The earlier values were 1,320 and 720. Calculate the new RII (ratio of the two fitted values) to 4 decimal places and say whether relative inequality changed.
::: solution
New RII $= 1{,}100/600 = 1.8333$; the earlier RII was $1{,}320/720 = 1.8333$. The relative inequality is unchanged: both ends improved by the same proportion (about 17%). The absolute SII fell from 600 to 500 per 100,000, so absolute inequality narrowed.
