Orthopaedics is the surgery of bones, joints, ligaments, tendons and the spine. It deals with injuries, from a broken wrist to a mangled leg, and with chronic conditions such as arthritis, deformity and back pain. A large part of every emergency department's work is musculoskeletal, and the commonest serious fracture in the UK, the fractured hip in an older person, is as much a medical and social emergency as a surgical one.

This chapter covers fracture healing and the principles of fracture management, the emergencies (open fractures, compartment syndrome, dislocations, cauda equina syndrome and septic arthritis), the hip fracture pathway, common fractures and injuries, bone and joint infection, children's orthopaedics, bone tumours and joint replacement. Guidance followed is UK-first (NICE, including CG124 on hip fracture, and the British Orthopaedic Association Standards for Trauma, BOASTs) as of 2026. Management of individual patients follows local protocols. Fracture biology and the medical aspects of bone disease are covered further in [[musculoskeletal/fractures]] and [[musculoskeletal/bones-joints]].

## How fractures heal

::: definition Fracture healing {#def-fracture}
A **fracture** is a break in the continuity of a bone. Healing restores bone, not scar tissue, and passes through overlapping stages:

1. **Haematoma and inflammation** (days): bleeding from the bone ends forms a haematoma; inflammatory cells and growth factors arrive.
2. **Soft callus** (weeks 1–3 or so): fibroblasts and chondroblasts form fibrous tissue and cartilage that bridge the gap, and new blood vessels grow in.
3. **Hard callus** (weeks to months): the cartilage is replaced by woven bone through endochondral ossification. The fracture is now clinically united: not tender and not moving.
4. **Remodelling** (months to years): woven bone is replaced by lamellar bone, shaped by the loads it carries (Wolff's law). In children, remodelling can correct a large amount of angulation.

This route, through callus, is **secondary (indirect) bone healing**, and it happens when the fracture is held with some movement (**relative stability**), as in a cast, an intramedullary nail or a bridging plate. When the fragments are compressed together with no movement (**absolute stability**), as with a compression plate or lag screw, bone heals by **primary (direct) bone healing**, with osteons crossing the fracture directly, without visible callus.
:::

Fractures heal faster in children, in cancellous bone (metaphysis) and in bones with good blood supply. Healing is impaired by smoking, diabetes, poor nutrition, NSAIDs at high doses for long periods (the evidence is mixed), infection, excessive movement at the fracture, too rigid fixation with a gap, and poor blood supply. Some sites are vulnerable to **avascular necrosis** because their blood supply enters from one end: the **scaphoid** (proximal pole), the **femoral head** (after an intracapsular neck fracture) and the **talus**.

A fracture that heals in a poor position is a **malunion**. Failure to heal in the expected time is **delayed union**, and failure to heal at all is **non-union**, which may be due to poor biology (atrophic non-union) or too much movement (hypertrophic non-union, with abundant callus but no bridging).

## Principles of management

Fracture management is often summarised as **resuscitate, reduce, hold, rehabilitate**.

- **Resuscitate:** treat the patient before the fracture. A long-bone or pelvic fracture can cause major blood loss, and life-threatening injuries elsewhere take priority ([[surgery/trauma]]).
- **Reduce:** restore the alignment of the fragments if displacement is unacceptable, by closed manipulation or by open surgery. Not all fractures need reduction: some displacement is acceptable depending on the bone, the patient's age and function.
- **Hold:** keep the fragments in position while they heal: a sling, splint or cast, traction, or surgical fixation with plates and screws, intramedullary nails, wires or external fixators.
- **Rehabilitate:** restore movement, strength and function, with physiotherapy from early on; prolonged immobilisation causes joint stiffness, muscle wasting and thrombosis.

Every fracture is **described** systematically so that it can be communicated by phone: the patient, the bone, the side, the site (diaphysis, metaphysis, epiphysis, intra-articular), the fracture pattern (transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted, segmental, avulsion), the **displacement** (translation, angulation, rotation, shortening, described by the position of the distal fragment) and whether it is **open or closed**. The neurovascular status of the limb is examined and documented before and after any manipulation.

**Indications for surgical fixation** include fractures that cannot be held in an acceptable position without surgery, displaced intra-articular fractures (to restore a smooth joint surface and reduce later arthritis), open fractures, fractures with vascular injury, multiple injuries (to allow early mobilisation), pathological fractures and fractures where early mobility is vital, such as hip fractures in older people.

### Complications of fractures

| Early (hours to days) | Late (weeks to years) |
|---|---|
| Haemorrhage and shock | Delayed union, non-union, malunion |
| Compartment syndrome | Avascular necrosis |
| Nerve and vessel injury | Post-traumatic osteoarthritis |
| Infection (especially open fractures) | Joint stiffness |
| Fat embolism syndrome | Complex regional pain syndrome |
| Venous thromboembolism | Growth disturbance (physeal injuries in children) |

**Fat embolism syndrome**, typically 24–72 hours after a long-bone (especially femoral) fracture, causes respiratory distress, confusion and a petechial rash over the upper body and conjunctivae. Early fixation of femoral fractures reduces it.

## Orthopaedic emergencies

### Open fractures

An **open fracture** communicates with the outside through a wound, so the fracture and its haematoma are contaminated. The **Gustilo–Anderson classification**, finalised at debridement, grades the injury: type I (wound under 1 cm, clean), type II (1–10 cm without extensive soft-tissue damage), type IIIA (extensive soft-tissue damage but bone can be covered), type IIIB (periosteal stripping, needs a flap for cover) and type IIIC (arterial injury needing repair).

The BOA standard for open fractures sets out the timings:

- **intravenous antibiotics as soon as possible, ideally within 1 hour of injury**, and tetanus prophylaxis as indicated;
- photograph the wound, remove gross contamination, cover with a saline-soaked dressing and splint the limb; document neurovascular status;
- **debridement in theatre** by consultant orthopaedic and plastic surgeons working together: **immediately** for highly contaminated wounds (agricultural, aquatic or sewage) or vascular compromise; within **12 hours** for other high-energy injuries; within **24 hours** for other open fractures;
- **definitive soft-tissue cover within 72 hours** of injury, ideally at the same time as definitive fixation.

Complex open fractures of the lower limb are treated in specialist centres with combined orthoplastic teams.

::: example An open tibial fracture {#ex-open}
A 31-year-old cyclist is hit by a car. His right lower leg is deformed, with a 6 cm wound over the shin through which bone is visible, and soil in the wound. His foot is warm with palpable pulses and normal sensation. What are the immediate steps and timings?
::: solution
**Assessment:** after the primary survey, this is an **open tibial fracture**, high-energy, with a large wound (at least Gustilo II, probably type III; the final grade is made at debridement).

**Immediate steps:**

1. **Intravenous antibiotics** within 1 hour of injury, according to local guidance (typically a broad-spectrum agent such as co-amoxiclav or a cephalosporin), and tetanus prophylaxis according to his immunisation status.
2. Analgesia; **photograph** the wound for the record; remove gross contamination; cover with a saline-soaked dressing and an impermeable film; do not repeatedly inspect the wound.
3. Realign and **splint** the limb; recheck and document pulses, capillary refill and sensation (including the deep peroneal and tibial nerves).
4. Monitor for **compartment syndrome**, which can occur even with an open fracture.

**Timing:** soil contamination makes this a highly contaminated wound, so debridement should be **immediate**, otherwise within 12 hours for a high-energy injury, by orthopaedic and plastic surgeons together. Temporary stabilisation (often an external fixator) may be used, with definitive fixation and soft-tissue cover (possibly a flap) within 72 hours.
:::
:::

### Compartment syndrome

::: theorem Compartment syndrome reduces perfusion pressure {#thm-compartment}
Muscles of the limbs lie in compartments enclosed by inelastic fascia. If the pressure within a compartment rises, for example from bleeding or swelling after a fracture or reperfusion, it compresses the veins and capillaries. Tissue perfusion depends on the difference between the arterial pressure and the compartment pressure. When this **perfusion pressure** falls too low, the muscles and nerves become ischaemic, which causes further swelling, a vicious circle that leads to muscle necrosis within hours unless the compartment is opened.
:::

::: proof
Blood flows through capillaries only if the pressure driving it exceeds the pressure of the surrounding tissue. As compartment pressure rises, the low-pressure veins collapse first, so outflow is obstructed while arterial inflow continues; capillary pressure rises, more fluid leaks into the tissue, and compartment pressure rises further. Because the fascia does not stretch, small increases in volume produce large increases in pressure. Distal pulses usually remain palpable, because the large arteries have much higher pressure than the compartment; this is why a palpable pulse does not exclude compartment syndrome. The clinically useful measure is the **delta pressure**: diastolic blood pressure minus compartment pressure. A delta pressure **below 30 mmHg** indicates inadequate perfusion and is an indication for fasciotomy.
:::

The cardinal symptom is **pain out of proportion to the injury**, especially **pain on passive stretching** of the muscles in the compartment (for example, passively extending the toes stretches the deep posterior compartment of the leg). Paraesthesia, tense swelling and, late, weakness follow. Pallor and pulselessness are **late** signs; waiting for them means waiting for muscle death. Common settings are tibial fractures, forearm fractures, crush injuries, reperfusion after vascular repair, tight casts and prolonged limb compression in people who have been immobile for a long time.

Management is to remove all **circumferential dressings and split the cast** down to skin, keep the limb at heart level (elevation reduces arterial inflow), restore blood pressure if the patient is hypotensive, and, if symptoms do not resolve rapidly, perform an urgent **fasciotomy**, opening all the compartments (four in the lower leg). Compartment pressure can be measured when clinical assessment is unreliable, for example in an unconscious or sedated patient.

::: example A forearm after a crush {#ex-compartment}
A 40-year-old man's forearm was trapped under machinery for 2 hours. He has severe forearm pain that is worse when his fingers are passively extended. His blood pressure is 110/60 mmHg, and the measured flexor compartment pressure is 42 mmHg. Is fasciotomy indicated?
::: solution
**Delta pressure:** $60 - 42 = 18$ mmHg, well below 30 mmHg.

**Clinical picture:** pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch after a crush injury are typical of compartment syndrome; a palpable radial pulse would not change this.

**Decision:** yes, urgent **fasciotomy** of the forearm compartments. Note that his relatively low diastolic pressure lowers the threshold at which perfusion fails; restoring his blood pressure is part of treatment but must not delay surgery. After a crush injury, also check creatine kinase, potassium and kidney function for rhabdomyolysis.
:::
:::

### Dislocations and nerve injuries

A **dislocation** is complete loss of contact between joint surfaces. It needs prompt reduction, after X-ray and documentation of the neurovascular status:

- **Shoulder:** most are **anterior**, after a fall onto an outstretched, abducted and externally rotated arm. The **axillary nerve** (sensation over the regimental badge area, deltoid function) is at risk. Posterior dislocations follow seizures and electric shocks and are easily missed.
- **Hip:** posterior dislocation, usually from a dashboard injury in a car crash, with the leg shortened, flexed, adducted and internally rotated; the **sciatic nerve** is at risk, and reduction is urgent because of the risk of femoral head avascular necrosis. Dislocation of a hip replacement is more common in everyday practice.
- **Knee:** dislocation of the tibiofemoral joint (not the patella) is a limb-threatening injury because the **popliteal artery** is often damaged; vascular assessment (ABPI, imaging) is needed even after reduction.

### Cauda equina syndrome

**Cauda equina syndrome** is compression of the lumbosacral nerve roots below the end of the spinal cord, most often by a large central lumbar disc prolapse. Symptoms include back pain and sciatica (often bilateral), **saddle anaesthesia**, **bladder dysfunction** (reduced sensation, difficulty starting, retention) and bowel or sexual dysfunction. Once painless urinary retention with overflow has developed, the prognosis for recovery of bladder function is poorer. Any patient with suspected cauda equina syndrome needs **emergency MRI** and referral to the spinal surgical team for decompression. Safety-netting advice for people with back pain should include these symptoms ([[musculoskeletal/back-pain]]).

## The hip fracture pathway

Around 70,000 people break a hip each year in the UK, mostly frail older people after a fall from standing. Around a quarter or more die within a year, and many do not return to their previous level of independence. Hip fractures are therefore managed on a coordinated pathway.

::: definition Hip fracture types and their surgery {#def-joint}
Hip fractures are divided by their relation to the hip joint capsule:

- **Intracapsular** (femoral neck) fractures can disrupt the blood supply to the femoral head, which comes mainly from retinacular vessels running up the neck. The **Garden classification** describes them: I incomplete, II complete but undisplaced, III complete and partially displaced, IV completely displaced. Undisplaced fractures (Garden I–II) are usually fixed with screws or a sliding hip screw. **Displaced** fractures (Garden III–IV) carry a high risk of avascular necrosis and non-union, so the head is replaced: a **hemiarthroplasty**, or a **total hip replacement** for people who were walking independently outdoors with no more than a stick, are not cognitively impaired and are medically fit (NICE CG124).
- **Extracapsular** fractures (intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric) do not threaten the blood supply to the head, so they are fixed: a **sliding hip screw** for most trochanteric fractures and an **intramedullary nail** for subtrochanteric fractures.
:::

The patient typically presents after a fall, unable to bear weight, with a **shortened and externally rotated leg**. X-rays (AP pelvis and lateral hip) usually show the fracture; if they are normal but the history and examination are suggestive, **MRI** (or CT if MRI is not possible) is used to find an occult fracture.

NICE CG124 recommends:

- **surgery on the day of, or the day after, admission**;
- analgesia immediately on arrival, including a **nerve block** (fascia iliaca or femoral) and regular paracetamol, with care with opioids;
- **orthogeriatric** care from admission, with rapid medical optimisation in parallel with preparation for surgery, not as a series of delays;
- surgery that allows the patient to **bear full weight** immediately afterwards;
- **mobilisation** by physiotherapy on the day after surgery;
- assessment of **bone health** and **falls risk** to prevent the next fracture.

The **National Hip Fracture Database** audits these standards for every hospital in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The medical aspects of the case are in [[surgery/perioperative-care]].

::: example Hip fracture on an anticoagulant {#ex-warfarinhip}
An 86-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation on warfarin falls at her care home and has a displaced intracapsular fracture of the left femoral neck (Garden IV). Her INR is 3.1. Before the fall she walked around the home with a frame and has moderate dementia. What operation is appropriate, and how should her anticoagulation be managed?
::: solution
**Operation:** a displaced intracapsular fracture is treated by replacing the femoral head. She does not meet the criteria for total hip replacement (she does not walk independently outdoors and has cognitive impairment), so a **cemented hemiarthroplasty** is appropriate, allowing full weight-bearing straight away.

**Anticoagulation:** warfarin should not delay surgery beyond what is needed. Her INR of 3.1 is too high for surgery and spinal anaesthesia; it is reversed with **intravenous vitamin K**, which typically lowers the INR enough within 6–12 hours, so she can still have surgery on the day of or after admission. Prothrombin complex concentrate is reserved for urgent reversal, such as bleeding. VTE prophylaxis is given after surgery, and warfarin restarted when the bleeding risk allows, with her stroke risk discussed.

**Pathway:** fascia iliaca block on arrival, orthogeriatric assessment, delirium prevention (her dementia increases the risk), early mobilisation, and assessment of bone health and falls. Her care home should be involved in planning her return.
:::
:::

## Common fractures and injuries

- **Distal radius fractures** are common at both ends of life. A **Colles' fracture** (fall onto an outstretched hand) has dorsal displacement and angulation of the distal fragment ("dinner fork" deformity); a **Smith's fracture** is displaced volarly. Many are treated with manipulation and a cast; unstable or intra-articular fractures are fixed with a volar plate. Median nerve symptoms must be looked for. A distal radius fracture in an older person is a **fragility fracture** that should trigger assessment for osteoporosis.
- **Scaphoid fractures** follow a fall onto an outstretched hand in young adults, causing tenderness in the **anatomical snuffbox** and pain on compressing the thumb along its axis. Initial X-rays may be normal. Suspected fractures are immobilised and imaged early with MRI. The proximal pole gets its blood supply from distal to proximal, so proximal fractures risk **avascular necrosis** and non-union.
- **Ankle injuries:** the **Ottawa ankle rules** limit X-rays to patients with bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus, or who cannot bear weight for four steps both immediately after the injury and in the emergency department (with similar rules for the midfoot: navicular and base of fifth metatarsal). Stable fractures are treated in a boot; unstable fractures (involving both sides of the joint, or with talar shift) usually need fixation.
- **Soft-tissue knee injuries:** an **anterior cruciate ligament** rupture (a twisting injury with a "pop", rapid swelling from haemarthrosis and later instability), **meniscal tears** (locking, joint-line tenderness) and collateral ligament injuries; MRI confirms them.
- **Achilles tendon rupture** follows sudden push-off; squeezing the calf does not plantar-flex the foot (**Simmonds' test**). It is treated with functional bracing or repair.

::: quiz
A 22-year-old falls onto his outstretched hand. He has tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox, but scaphoid X-rays are normal. What is the most appropriate management?

- [x] Immobilise the wrist and arrange early MRI or review, because scaphoid fractures can be invisible on initial X-rays
- [ ] Arrange immediate surgical fixation
- [ ] Give a steroid injection into the wrist
::: solution
Scaphoid fractures are often not visible on initial X-rays. Missing one risks non-union and avascular necrosis of the proximal pole, causing long-term disability. A clinically suspected fracture is immobilised and investigated, usually with MRI, according to local pathway.
:::

## Bone and joint infection

**Septic arthritis** is bacterial infection of a joint. It destroys cartilage within days, so it is an emergency. *Staphylococcus aureus* is the commonest cause; streptococci, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (in young sexually active adults), Gram-negative organisms (older and immunosuppressed patients) and *Kingella kingae* (young children) also occur. It presents as a hot, swollen, painful joint with a reduced range of movement; the patient may be febrile. Risk factors include existing joint disease (rheumatoid arthritis), a prosthetic joint, diabetes, immunosuppression and injecting drug use.

The key investigation is **joint aspiration before antibiotics** (unless the patient is septic and antibiotics cannot wait): Gram stain, culture, white cell count and crystals. Crystals do not exclude infection, as gout and sepsis can coexist. Blood cultures are taken. Treatment is intravenous antibiotics according to local guidance, followed by a prolonged course, and **washout** of the joint (arthroscopic or open) for most native joint infections.

In children, distinguishing septic arthritis of the hip from **transient synovitis** (a common, self-limiting inflammation after a viral illness) is difficult. **Kocher's criteria** give one point each for: inability to bear weight, temperature above 38.5 °C, ESR above 40 mm/h and white cell count above $12 \times 10^9$/L. The probability of septic arthritis rises steeply with the number of criteria present, from very low with none to very high with all four in the original study; many centres also use CRP above 20 mg/L. A child with suspected septic arthritis needs ultrasound, aspiration and urgent orthopaedic review.

**Osteomyelitis** is infection of bone. In children it is usually **haematogenous**, affecting the metaphysis of long bones. In adults it more often follows open fractures, surgery or diabetic foot ulcers, and vertebral osteomyelitis follows bacteraemia. MRI is the most sensitive imaging; bone biopsy identifies the organism. Treatment combines prolonged antibiotics and surgical removal of dead bone (sequestrum) when present.

**Periprosthetic joint infection** affects a small percentage of joint replacements. Early infection (within weeks of surgery) may be treated by debridement, antibiotics and retention of the implant; chronic infection usually requires removal of the implant and revision in one or two stages. A painful, warm joint replacement, a wound that keeps leaking, or a sinus track should be assessed by the arthroplasty team; antibiotics should not be started "blind" before aspiration, because this makes the organism harder to identify.

::: example The limping child {#ex-kocher}
A 6-year-old girl has refused to walk on her right leg since yesterday. She had a cold last week. Her temperature is 38.9 °C; she holds the hip flexed and resists movement. Her white cell count is $16.1 \times 10^9$/L, ESR 52 mm/h and CRP 64 mg/L. Ultrasound shows a hip effusion. What do Kocher's criteria suggest, and what should be done?
::: solution
**Kocher's criteria:** non-weight-bearing (1), temperature above 38.5 °C (1), ESR above 40 mm/h (1), white cell count above $12 \times 10^9$/L (1): **4 out of 4**. Her CRP is also above 20 mg/L.

**Interpretation:** with all four criteria present, septic arthritis of the hip is highly likely, much more likely than transient synovitis, despite the recent viral illness.

**Action:** urgent orthopaedic review; **aspiration** of the hip (under ultrasound guidance or in theatre) for microscopy and culture; blood cultures; then intravenous antibiotics according to local paediatric guidance and **surgical washout**. Delay risks destruction of the femoral head and growth plate.

**Note:** criteria such as these support clinical judgement; a child with fewer criteria but a worrying clinical picture still needs aspiration.
:::
:::

## Children's orthopaedics

Children's bones are different: the periosteum is thick, bones bend before they break (**greenstick** and **buckle** fractures), and injuries can involve the **growth plate (physis)**, classified by the **Salter–Harris** system (I to V; higher types involve the joint surface or crush the physis and carry more risk of growth disturbance). Remodelling corrects much angulation near the growth plate in the plane of joint movement, but not rotation.

The common causes of a limp vary with age:

- **Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH):** screened at the newborn and 6–8-week checks using the Barlow and Ortolani manoeuvres, with ultrasound for babies with abnormal findings or risk factors (breech presentation, family history). Early treatment is with a Pavlik harness.
- **Transient synovitis** (ages about 3–10): the commonest cause of an acute limp, after a viral illness, in a well child; a diagnosis of exclusion after septic arthritis is ruled out.
- **Perthes disease** (ages about 4–8, more common in boys): avascular necrosis of the femoral head, presenting with a limp and hip or knee pain.
- **Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SUFE)** (early adolescence, often overweight): the femoral head slips off the neck through the growth plate. It may present with **knee pain** (referred pain via the obturator nerve), a limp and loss of internal rotation of the hip. It is treated by surgical pinning; missed cases lead to avascular necrosis and early arthritis.

Non-accidental injury must be considered in young children with fractures that do not fit the history ([[paediatrics/safeguarding]]).

## Bone tumours and metastases

Primary bone tumours are rare; the commonest malignant ones in young people are **osteosarcoma** (around the knee, in adolescents) and **Ewing's sarcoma**. Persistent bone pain, especially at night, a mass, or a pathological fracture in a young person needs urgent investigation and referral to a bone tumour centre, where the biopsy should be done.

In adults, the commonest bone tumour by far is **metastasis**, from cancers of the **breast, lung, prostate, kidney and thyroid**, and myeloma. Metastases weaken bone and cause **pathological fractures**, hypercalcaemia and spinal cord compression. Prophylactic fixation before a fracture occurs gives better function. **Mirels' score** estimates the risk of fracture through a lytic metastasis in a long bone, scoring four features from 1 to 3 each: site (upper limb 1, lower limb 2, around the trochanter 3), pain (mild 1, moderate 2, functional, meaning pain on use, 3), lesion type (blastic 1, mixed 2, lytic 3) and size as a proportion of the bone's diameter (under a third 1, a third to two-thirds 2, over two-thirds 3). A total of **9 or more** suggests prophylactic fixation; 8 is borderline.

## Joint replacement

**Total hip and knee replacements** are among the most successful operations in medicine for end-stage osteoarthritis, relieving pain and restoring function for most patients for 15–20 years or more. They are offered when symptoms substantially affect quality of life despite non-surgical treatment (exercise, weight loss, analgesia), not on the basis of X-ray appearances or age alone ([[musculoskeletal/osteoarthritis]]). Complications include infection, dislocation (hip), VTE (prophylaxis follows NICE NG89), leg length difference, nerve injury, periprosthetic fracture, persistent pain (more common after knee than hip replacement) and, in the long term, loosening and wear needing revision. The National Joint Registry records every joint replacement in the UK and identifies implants that fail early.

::: warning
**A palpable pulse does not exclude compartment syndrome, and analgesia requirements are a warning sign.** In a patient with a tibial or forearm fracture, increasing pain that does not respond to strong analgesia, or a patient who needs more and more opioid, should prompt immediate assessment for compartment syndrome, not a further dose. Regional nerve blocks and patient-controlled analgesia can mask the pain; patients receiving them after high-risk injuries need regular, documented assessment, and compartment pressure monitoring when pain cannot be assessed.
:::

::: history
X-rays, discovered by Wilhelm Röntgen in 1895, were used to image fractures within weeks. Hugh Owen Thomas, a bonesetter's son practising in Liverpool, designed the Thomas splint for femoral fractures; his nephew Robert Jones introduced it on the Western Front during the First World War, where it is reported to have reduced mortality from compound femoral fractures dramatically. In 1958 the Swiss AO group (Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen) began developing the principles and implants of internal fixation that are still taught today. At Wrightington Hospital in Lancashire, John Charnley developed the low-friction total hip replacement in the early 1960s, using a small metal head articulating with high-density polyethylene and fixed with acrylic bone cement, and introduced clean-air enclosures to reduce infection.
:::

::: solution
(a) Internal fixation (screws or sliding hip screw): the blood supply to the head is likely to be preserved in an undisplaced fracture (though some surgeons consider arthroplasty in older patients). (b) **Total hip replacement**: she walks independently outdoors, is not cognitively impaired and is likely to be fit, and THR gives better function than hemiarthroplasty in such patients. (c) **Hemiarthroplasty** (cemented): the head must be replaced because displacement threatens its blood supply, but THR is not indicated given her mobility and cognition. (d) **Sliding hip screw**: an extracapsular fracture with a healthy femoral head blood supply; an intramedullary nail is used for subtrochanteric or reverse oblique patterns.
:::

::: exercise The swollen knee {level=2}
A 58-year-old man with rheumatoid arthritis on methotrexate has had a hot, swollen, painful left knee for 2 days and a temperature of 38.2 °C. He has gout in his history. Outline your management. If the aspirate shows monosodium urate crystals, does that settle the diagnosis?
::: solution
**Management:** treat as **septic arthritis** until proven otherwise: immunosuppression and existing joint disease are risk factors. Aspirate the joint before antibiotics (unless he is septic and antibiotics cannot wait) and send fluid for Gram stain, culture, white cell count and crystals; take blood cultures, full blood count, CRP and kidney function; then start intravenous antibiotics according to local guidance and refer to orthopaedics for washout if infection is likely or confirmed. Discuss his methotrexate with rheumatology. **Crystals:** finding urate crystals does **not** exclude infection, because gout and septic arthritis can coexist. Antibiotics continue until the culture result and clinical course allow infection to be excluded.
:::
:::

::: exercise Knee pain in a teenager {level=2}
A 12-year-old boy who is overweight has had pain in his left knee for 4 weeks and has started limping. Examination of the knee is normal. What diagnosis must not be missed, how should it be investigated, and what is the treatment?
::: solution
**Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SUFE).** Hip pathology often presents with knee pain because of shared innervation (obturator nerve), so the **hip must be examined** in any child with knee pain; in SUFE, internal rotation is reduced and the hip may rotate externally as it is flexed. **Investigation:** AP and **frog-leg lateral** X-rays of both hips (the slip may be bilateral, and it is easier to see on the lateral view). **Treatment:** urgent orthopaedic referral, non-weight-bearing, and surgical fixation with a screw across the growth plate (in situ pinning); prophylactic pinning of the other hip is considered. Delayed diagnosis increases the risk of a severe slip, avascular necrosis and early osteoarthritis.
:::
:::

::: exercise The disappearing pulse after reduction {level=3}
A 25-year-old man has a knee dislocation from a motorcycle crash, which is reduced in the emergency department. Afterwards, his foot is warm and the dorsalis pedis pulse is weakly palpable. The junior doctor plans to admit him for elevation and review in the morning. Explain why this is unsafe and what should be done.
::: solution
Knee dislocation is frequently associated with **popliteal artery injury** (intimal tears and thrombosis), because the artery is tethered above and below the joint. A palpable pulse does not exclude injury: an intimal flap can thrombose over hours, and collateral flow can maintain a weak pulse at first. Missed popliteal injury leads to ischaemia, compartment syndrome and amputation. **What should be done:** measure the **ABPI** (an ABPI below 0.9 indicates arterial injury); arrange urgent **CT angiography** or vascular assessment if abnormal or if there is any doubt; repeat neurovascular checks frequently (the common peroneal nerve is also often injured); involve the vascular surgeons early; and monitor for compartment syndrome. Revascularisation, if needed, should be done within hours, often with fasciotomy. Ligament reconstruction is planned later.
:::
:::

::: exercise A cast that is too tight {level=3}
Twelve hours after manipulation and casting of a forearm fracture, a 9-year-old girl is crying with pain despite paracetamol and ibuprofen. Her fingers are swollen and she cries when her fingers are passively extended. Her radial pulse is palpable. Outline your immediate actions, the likely diagnoses and why the pulse is not reassuring.
::: solution
**Immediate actions:** elevate the arm to heart level (not above); **split the cast and all padding down to skin** along its whole length, and spread it; reassess within minutes. If the pain and pain on passive stretch do not settle promptly, call the orthopaedic registrar for urgent assessment for **forearm compartment syndrome** and fasciotomy. **Likely diagnoses:** constriction by the cast as the limb swells, or established compartment syndrome; less likely, displacement of the fracture or nerve injury. Increasing analgesic requirements and pain on passive finger extension are the key warning signs, and in children the "three As" (anxiety, agitation and increasing analgesia requirement) can be the first clue. **Why the pulse is not reassuring:** compartment pressure that is high enough to stop capillary perfusion of muscle is far below the arterial pressure needed to stop a pulse in the radial artery, so the pulse remains until very late, by which time muscle may be dead.
:::
