Breast and endocrine surgery share a way of working. Most patients arrive with a lump or an abnormal test result rather than an emergency, and the first job is to decide, with as little intervention as possible, whether the problem is benign or malignant. Decisions are made by multidisciplinary teams that include surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, oncologists, endocrinologists and specialist nurses. And the operations, though rarely large, take place close to structures whose injury would permanently change a patient's life: the nerves of the larynx, the parathyroid glands, the nerves of the axilla.

This chapter covers the assessment of breast symptoms, breast cancer and its surgical management, inherited risk and screening, the thyroid nodule and thyroid surgery, hyperparathyroidism and the adrenal gland. Guidance followed is UK-first (NICE guidelines NG101 on early and locally advanced breast cancer, CG164 on familial breast cancer, NG145 on thyroid disease and NG132 on hyperparathyroidism, and the British Thyroid Association) as of 2026. Management of individual patients follows local multidisciplinary protocols.

## Breast symptoms and triple assessment

Most breast symptoms are caused by benign conditions, but breast cancer is the commonest cancer in women in the UK, so every new breast symptom deserves a clear answer. In the UK, people with suspicious breast symptoms are seen in a **one-stop breast clinic**, where examination, imaging and biopsy can be done in a single visit.

::: definition Triple assessment {#def-breast}
**Triple assessment** is the standard evaluation of a breast lump or other suspicious breast symptom. It has three parts:

1. **Clinical assessment:** history (duration, change with the menstrual cycle, nipple discharge, skin changes, family history, hormone use) and examination of both breasts and the axillae and supraclavicular fossae.
2. **Imaging:** **ultrasound** for women under about 40, whose dense breast tissue makes mammography less useful, and **mammography** plus ultrasound for older women.
3. **Tissue sampling:** **core needle biopsy**, usually guided by ultrasound or by stereotactic mammography, which gives a histological diagnosis and allows receptor testing; fine-needle aspiration is used mainly for cysts and axillary nodes.

Each part is scored on a five-point scale (for example 1 normal, 2 benign, 3 indeterminate, 4 suspicious, 5 malignant). The results must agree. If any part is suspicious, the lesion is treated as potentially malignant until proven otherwise, even if the others are reassuring.
:::

Common benign conditions include:

- **Fibroadenoma:** a smooth, mobile, rubbery lump ("breast mouse") in a young woman, made of overgrown lobular tissue. Once confirmed, it is usually left alone unless large or growing.
- **Cysts:** fluid-filled, often in women in their 40s and around menopause; they may appear suddenly and be tender. Ultrasound confirms them; aspiration is done for symptoms.
- **Fibrocystic change** (benign breast change): cyclical lumpiness and pain.
- **Mastitis and breast abscess:** usually in breastfeeding women; treated with antibiotics, continued feeding or expressing, and ultrasound-guided aspiration of any abscess. In women who are not breastfeeding, periductal mastitis is associated with smoking, and inflammatory breast cancer must be excluded.
- **Nipple discharge:** usually benign if it is from multiple ducts and not bloodstained; a single-duct, bloodstained discharge may come from an intraduct papilloma or, less often, cancer.
- **Gynaecomastia:** benign enlargement of male breast tissue, caused by physiological changes (puberty, older age), drugs (spironolactone, anti-androgens, some antipsychotics, anabolic steroids, cannabis), liver disease, hypogonadism and rarely tumours that secrete hormones. Men can develop breast cancer, so a hard, eccentric lump is assessed in the same way as in women.

::: example A lump in a young woman with dense breasts {#ex-dense}
A 34-year-old woman notices a lump in her left breast. On examination there is a 2 cm firm, irregular lump in the upper outer quadrant. Ultrasound reports a "probably benign" lesion (score 3, indeterminate). Her mammogram, done because the examination was suspicious, shows dense breast tissue and no clear abnormality. What should happen next?
::: solution
**Principle:** the three parts of triple assessment must agree. Here, examination is suspicious (firm and irregular) and imaging is indeterminate. A normal mammogram in a woman with dense breasts does not exclude cancer, because dense glandular tissue and a tumour can look alike on mammography.

**Next step:** an **ultrasound-guided core biopsy**, which is required whenever any part of the assessment is not clearly benign. If the biopsy is benign but the clinical findings remain suspicious, the case is discussed in the multidisciplinary meeting, and further biopsy, MRI or excision is considered.

**Context:** breast cancer in young women is less common but often more aggressive, and delayed diagnosis is a recognised source of harm. A young age must not make the team less careful.
:::
:::

## Breast cancer

Most breast cancers are **invasive ductal carcinoma** (also called invasive carcinoma of no special type), followed by **invasive lobular carcinoma**, which tends to spread diffusely and can be hard to see on imaging. **Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)** is a proliferation of malignant cells confined within the ducts, often found on screening as microcalcifications; it does not metastasise but can progress to invasive cancer.

Treatment depends on three sets of information:

- **Stage:** the size of the tumour, the involvement of axillary lymph nodes and distant spread (bone, liver, lung, brain), using the TNM system.
- **Grade:** how abnormal the cells look (grade 1 to 3).
- **Receptor status:** **oestrogen receptor (ER)** and progesterone receptor, which predict benefit from **endocrine therapy** (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors), and **HER2**, which predicts benefit from HER2-targeted drugs such as trastuzumab. Cancers that lack all three are called **triple-negative**; they are more common in younger women and in carriers of *BRCA1* variants, and they are treated with chemotherapy and, increasingly, immunotherapy.

### Surgery of the breast

There are two main options:

- **Breast-conserving surgery** (wide local excision, also called lumpectomy) removes the tumour with a margin of normal tissue, followed by **radiotherapy** to the remaining breast. Long-term trials in the 1970s and 1980s showed that survival after breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy is the same as after mastectomy for suitable tumours. Impalpable lesions are localised before surgery with a wire or marker placed under imaging guidance.
- **Mastectomy** removes the whole breast. It is used for large tumours relative to the breast, multiple tumours in different quadrants, when radiotherapy cannot be given, and when the patient prefers it. **Reconstruction**, immediate or delayed, using implants or the patient's own tissue (for example a flap from the abdomen), is offered to suitable women.

Large tumours may first be treated with **neoadjuvant** (preoperative) chemotherapy or endocrine therapy to shrink them, allowing breast conservation. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is also standard for many HER2-positive and triple-negative cancers, because the response predicts outcome and guides later treatment.

### Surgery of the axilla

::: theorem The sentinel node principle {#thm-sentinel}
Lymph from a tumour drains first to one or a few lymph nodes, the **sentinel nodes**. If the sentinel nodes are free of cancer, the remaining nodes in that basin are very unlikely to be involved. Removing and examining only the sentinel nodes therefore stages the axilla accurately while avoiding the complications of removing all the nodes.
:::

::: proof
Lymphatic spread from a tumour follows the anatomical path of lymph drainage, which runs in an orderly way from the primary site to the first nodes in the basin and only then to further nodes. Injecting a radioactive tracer (technetium-99m) and/or a blue dye near the tumour or under the areola marks the lymphatic path; the surgeon finds the sentinel nodes with a gamma probe and by their colour. Large validation studies, in which sentinel node biopsy was followed by full axillary clearance, showed that the sentinel nodes correctly predicted the status of the axilla in the great majority of women, with a low false-negative rate when both tracers are used. Because full **axillary node clearance** causes lymphoedema of the arm, shoulder stiffness and numbness in a substantial proportion of women, while sentinel node biopsy rarely does, the principle allows most women with clinically node-negative cancer to avoid clearance altogether.
:::

Before surgery, the axilla is assessed with **ultrasound**, and any abnormal node is biopsied. If ultrasound is normal, **sentinel lymph node biopsy** is done at the time of breast surgery. If the nodes are proven to contain cancer, the axilla is treated with axillary clearance or radiotherapy. Trials have shown that some women with limited sentinel node involvement (for example one or two nodes) who are having breast-conserving surgery and radiotherapy can safely avoid further axillary surgery; the decision is made by the multidisciplinary team.

### Adjuvant treatment and prognosis

After surgery, **adjuvant** treatments reduce the risk of recurrence: radiotherapy, endocrine therapy for ER-positive cancers (usually for at least 5 years), chemotherapy for higher-risk cancers, and HER2-targeted therapy for HER2-positive cancers. Online tools such as **NHS Predict** estimate the benefit of each treatment for an individual. The **Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI)** is a simple, long-established prognostic score:

$$
\text{NPI} = 0.2 \times \text{tumour size (cm)} + \text{node score} + \text{grade}
$$ {#eq-npi}

where the node score is 1 if no nodes are involved, 2 for 1–3 nodes and 3 for 4 or more, and the grade is 1, 2 or 3. Lower scores mean better prognosis: a commonly used grouping is good (up to 3.4), moderate (3.41–5.4) and poor (above 5.4).

::: example Calculating the NPI {#ex-npi}
A 58-year-old woman has a wide local excision and sentinel node biopsy for a 2.5 cm grade 2 invasive ductal carcinoma, ER-positive and HER2-negative. One of her two sentinel nodes contains a metastasis. Calculate her NPI and interpret it.
::: solution
**Size term:** $0.2 \times 2.5 = 0.5$.

**Node score:** 1–3 nodes involved, so 2.

**Grade:** 2.

**NPI:** $0.5 + 2 + 2 = 4.5$.

**Interpretation:** an NPI of 4.5 falls in the moderate prognostic group. In practice, her treatment plan will be based on a fuller assessment: the multidisciplinary team will consider radiotherapy to the breast (and possibly to the axilla), endocrine therapy because her tumour is ER-positive, and whether chemotherapy adds benefit, often using NHS Predict and, in some cases, a genomic test of the tumour. The NPI predates receptor testing and targeted therapies, so it is a starting point rather than the final word.
:::
:::

## Risk, screening and inherited breast cancer

::: definition Breast cancer screening and familial risk {#def-risk}
**Screening.** The NHS Breast Screening Programme invites women from age 50 up to their 71st birthday for **mammography every 3 years**; women over 71 can self-refer. Screening finds cancers earlier and reduces breast cancer mortality, but it also causes **overdiagnosis**: detection of cancers (and DCIS) that would never have caused harm, which are then treated. The 2012 Independent UK Panel on Breast Cancer Screening estimated that for every breast cancer death prevented, about three women are overdiagnosed.

**Familial risk.** About 5–10% of breast cancers are due to inherited high-risk variants, most often in **BRCA1** and **BRCA2**, and less often in *PALB2*, *TP53* and others. Features suggesting inherited risk include several close relatives with breast or ovarian cancer, young age at diagnosis, bilateral cancer, male breast cancer, triple-negative cancer at a young age and Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry. NICE CG164 sets out who should be referred to genetics services, and enhanced surveillance (including annual MRI for younger women at high risk) for those at increased risk.
:::

Women who carry a high-risk variant can choose **risk-reducing surgery**: bilateral mastectomy, which reduces breast cancer risk by about 90% or more, and, for *BRCA* carriers, **risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy** after childbearing, which reduces ovarian cancer risk and, in some women, breast cancer risk. These are personal decisions made after genetic counselling ([[genetics/cancer-genetics]]).

::: warning
**A negative mammogram does not exclude cancer in a woman with a lump.** Mammography misses a proportion of cancers, especially in dense breasts and lobular cancers. A screening result describes a population test in women without symptoms; a symptomatic woman needs full triple assessment, whatever her last screening mammogram showed. Likewise, a reassuring ultrasound does not override a suspicious clinical examination: when the three parts disagree, biopsy.
:::

## The thyroid

::: definition The thyroid nodule and thyroid surgery {#def-endocrine-neck}
**Thyroid nodules** are very common, and most are benign. The assessment aims to identify the few cancers without operating on the many benign nodules:

1. **History and examination:** rapid growth, a hard or fixed nodule, hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement), stridor, cervical lymphadenopathy, previous neck irradiation and family history of thyroid cancer or MEN2 suggest malignancy.
2. **Thyroid function tests:** a low TSH suggests a hyperfunctioning ("hot") nodule, which is rarely malignant and is assessed with a radioisotope scan.
3. **Ultrasound**, graded by the British Thyroid Association **U1–U5** system (U1 normal, U2 benign, U3 indeterminate, U4 suspicious, U5 malignant). Features of concern include a solid hypoechoic nodule, irregular margins, microcalcification and abnormal lymph nodes.
4. **Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC)** for U3–U5 nodules, reported using the **Thy1–Thy5** categories (Thy1 non-diagnostic, Thy2 non-neoplastic, Thy3 indeterminate, with Thy3a atypia and Thy3f possible follicular neoplasm, Thy4 suspicious of malignancy, Thy5 malignant).

**Surgery** is either **hemithyroidectomy** (lobectomy), used for diagnosis of indeterminate nodules and treatment of small low-risk cancers, or **total thyroidectomy**, used for larger or higher-risk cancers, Graves' disease and large multinodular goitres causing compression.
:::

### Thyroid cancer

- **Papillary carcinoma** is the commonest type (around four in five). It spreads to cervical lymph nodes and has an excellent prognosis in most patients.
- **Follicular carcinoma** spreads by the blood to bone and lung. It cannot be distinguished from a benign follicular adenoma on cytology, because the diagnosis requires seeing capsular or vascular invasion on histology. This is why a Thy3f result usually leads to diagnostic hemithyroidectomy.
- **Medullary carcinoma** arises from the calcitonin-secreting parafollicular C cells. About a quarter are hereditary, caused by *RET* mutations (MEN2). Calcitonin is used as a tumour marker.
- **Anaplastic carcinoma** occurs in older people, grows rapidly and has a very poor prognosis.
- **Lymphoma** of the thyroid is associated with Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

Treatment of differentiated (papillary and follicular) cancer is surgery, followed in selected higher-risk patients by **radioactive iodine** ablation and TSH suppression with levothyroxine. Thyroglobulin is used as a marker in follow-up after total thyroidectomy.

### Complications of thyroid surgery

The thyroid lies close to the **recurrent laryngeal nerves**, which run in the tracheo-oesophageal groove and supply all the intrinsic muscles of the larynx except cricothyroid, the **external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve**, which supplies cricothyroid and controls pitch, and the **parathyroid glands**, usually four, on the back of the thyroid.

- **Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury** causes a hoarse, weak voice if unilateral, and stridor and airway obstruction if bilateral. Temporary palsy occurs in a few per cent and permanent palsy in about 1% or less in experienced hands. The vocal cords are checked before and after surgery; many surgeons use intraoperative nerve monitoring.
- **Hypocalcaemia** follows bruising or removal of the parathyroid glands after total thyroidectomy. It is common and usually temporary, but sometimes permanent. Symptoms are perioral tingling, paraesthesia of the fingers, cramps and, if severe, tetany, seizures and arrhythmias. Calcium (or parathyroid hormone) is measured after total thyroidectomy and replaced as needed.
- **Neck haematoma** after thyroid surgery can compress the airway within minutes, mainly through venous and lymphatic congestion and laryngeal oedema. It is an emergency that ward staff must be trained to recognise and treat.
- **Thyroid storm** (a severe exacerbation of thyrotoxicosis) is prevented by making patients with hyperthyroidism euthyroid before surgery.

::: example Stridor after thyroidectomy {#ex-haematoma}
Four hours after a total thyroidectomy for a large goitre, a 56-year-old woman becomes distressed, says her neck feels tight and has noisy breathing. Her neck is swollen and the dressing is tense. Her oxygen saturation is 93%. What is happening and what should be done?
::: solution
**Diagnosis:** a **neck haematoma** compressing the airway. The combination of neck swelling, a sensation of tightness, stridor and falling saturations soon after thyroid surgery is diagnostic until proven otherwise.

**Immediate action:** call for help (surgeon and anaesthetist urgently), sit her up, give high-flow oxygen, and **open the wound at the bedside** without waiting for theatre if her airway is compromised. UK guidance (the "SCOOP" approach, endorsed by the Difficult Airway Society and the British Association of Endocrine and Thyroid Surgeons) summarises the steps: **S**kin exposure, **C**ut sutures, **O**pen skin, **O**pen muscles, **P**ack the wound. Releasing the clot relieves the pressure on the airway. She then goes to theatre to control the bleeding point, with an anaesthetist prepared for a difficult airway.

**Prevention of harm:** stitch cutters or a suture removal kit are kept at the bedside of every patient after neck surgery, and nursing staff are trained to recognise and act on early signs.
:::
:::

## The parathyroid glands

**Primary hyperparathyroidism** is excessive secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH) by the parathyroid glands, causing hypercalcaemia. It is caused by a **single adenoma** in about 80–85% of patients, with multigland hyperplasia (seen in MEN1 and MEN2A) and rarely carcinoma accounting for the rest. Most cases are found on routine blood tests. Symptoms, when present, include fatigue, thirst, polyuria, constipation, low mood, kidney stones, and bone pain or fractures from osteoporosis ("bones, stones, abdominal groans and psychiatric moans").

The diagnosis is a raised **albumin-adjusted calcium** with a PTH that is raised or inappropriately normal (in other causes of hypercalcaemia, such as malignancy, PTH is suppressed). Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia, a benign inherited condition, is excluded by measuring urinary calcium excretion. Because about 40% of serum calcium is bound to albumin, total calcium is adjusted for the albumin concentration:

$$
\text{adjusted calcium (mmol/L)} = \text{total calcium} + 0.02 \times (40 - \text{albumin in g/L})
$$ {#eq-calcium}

Local laboratories may use slightly different formulas. The biochemistry of calcium is covered in [[endocrine/bone-calcium]].

NICE NG132 (2019) recommends referral for surgery for people with primary hyperparathyroidism who have symptoms, end-organ disease (kidney stones, fragility fractures or osteoporosis) or an albumin-adjusted serum calcium of **2.85 mmol/L or above**. Before surgery, the abnormal gland is **localised** with neck ultrasound and a sestamibi scan or other imaging. A single adenoma can then be removed through a small incision (**focused parathyroidectomy**), with intraoperative PTH measurement in some units to confirm a fall. If imaging is negative or multigland disease is suspected, a **bilateral neck exploration** examines all four glands. After successful surgery, calcium may fall sharply ("hungry bone syndrome" in patients with significant bone disease) and is monitored.

::: example Calcium and the scan report {#ex-ca}
A 61-year-old woman's routine blood tests show a total calcium of 2.72 mmol/L with an albumin of 32 g/L. Her PTH is 9.8 pmol/L (reference range about 1.6–6.9). She has had a kidney stone. Calculate her adjusted calcium and decide whether she should be referred for surgery.
::: solution
**Adjusted calcium:** $2.72 + 0.02 \times (40 - 32) = 2.72 + 0.16 = 2.88$ mmol/L.

**Diagnosis:** a raised adjusted calcium with a raised PTH is consistent with **primary hyperparathyroidism**. Urinary calcium excretion should be measured to exclude familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia, and vitamin D checked.

**Surgery:** she meets the NICE criteria on two counts: her adjusted calcium is above 2.85 mmol/L, and she has end-organ disease (a kidney stone). She should be referred to an endocrine surgeon. Imaging (ultrasound and sestamibi) localises the adenoma before surgery; it is not used to make the diagnosis, which is biochemical. Bone density should also be assessed.
:::
:::

## The adrenal gland

The adrenal glands are increasingly operated on for tumours found by chance. An **adrenal incidentaloma** is a mass discovered on imaging done for another reason. Every incidentaloma is assessed for two questions:

- **Is it secreting hormones?** Tests include an overnight dexamethasone suppression test for cortisol excess, plasma or urine **metanephrines** for phaeochromocytoma, and, in patients with hypertension or hypokalaemia, the aldosterone-to-renin ratio for primary aldosteronism.
- **Could it be malignant?** Size (larger lesions, especially over about 4 cm), a high density on unenhanced CT and growth on follow-up raise concern for adrenocortical carcinoma or metastasis. Most small lesions with a low density on unenhanced CT (lipid-rich) are benign adenomas.

**Phaeochromocytoma** is a catecholamine-secreting tumour of the adrenal medulla, causing episodic headache, sweating, palpitations and hypertension. Before surgery, patients receive **alpha-blockade** (for example phenoxybenzamine or doxazosin) for some weeks, with fluid and salt loading, and **beta-blockade only after adequate alpha-blockade**. Beta-blockers given first leave alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed and can cause a dangerous rise in blood pressure. The operation itself, usually laparoscopic adrenalectomy, requires careful anaesthetic management of blood pressure swings when the tumour is handled and after its veins are ligated. The endocrine background is in [[endocrine/adrenal]].

**Multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN)** syndromes link these organs. **MEN1** (parathyroid, pituitary and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours) is caused by *MEN1* mutations; **MEN2A** (medullary thyroid cancer, phaeochromocytoma and hyperparathyroidism) and **MEN2B** (medullary thyroid cancer, phaeochromocytoma, mucosal neuromas and a marfanoid body shape) by *RET* mutations. Children who carry *RET* mutations are offered prophylactic thyroidectomy at an age that depends on the specific mutation. A patient with medullary thyroid cancer must have phaeochromocytoma excluded before thyroid surgery.

::: quiz
A 42-year-old man with episodic headaches, sweating and hypertension has raised plasma metanephrines and a 3 cm adrenal mass. Before his adrenalectomy, which plan is correct?
- [ ] Start a beta-blocker to control his heart rate, then add an alpha-blocker
- [x] Start an alpha-blocker, with fluid and salt loading, and add a beta-blocker only after adequate alpha-blockade if tachycardia persists
- [ ] Operate immediately without preparation to remove the source of catecholamines
- [ ] Use a diuretic to control his blood pressure
::: solution
Alpha-blockade comes first to control vasoconstriction and allow plasma volume to expand. A beta-blocker given first leaves alpha-receptor vasoconstriction unopposed and can precipitate a hypertensive crisis. Operating without preparation risks severe blood pressure swings. Diuretics worsen the volume depletion that these patients already have.
:::
:::

::: example A neck lump in a 30-year-old {#ex-necklump}
A 30-year-old man has a painless 2.5 cm lump in the right lobe of his thyroid, noticed by his partner. His thyroid function is normal. Ultrasound grades it U4 (solid, hypoechoic, with microcalcifications) and shows a slightly enlarged lymph node in the right side of the neck. FNAC of the nodule is reported Thy5. What is the likely diagnosis and how is it managed?
::: solution
**Likely diagnosis:** **papillary thyroid carcinoma** with possible lymph node spread. Papillary cancer is the commonest type, typically in young adults, and microcalcifications on ultrasound reflect psammoma bodies.

**Further assessment:** FNAC of the abnormal lymph node (with thyroglobulin measured in the needle washout), vocal cord check before surgery, and calcium. Cross-sectional imaging is used if there is concern about extensive local spread.

**Treatment:** discussed at the thyroid cancer multidisciplinary meeting. If the lymph node is confirmed to contain cancer, the usual plan is **total thyroidectomy with neck dissection** of the involved lymph node compartments, followed by consideration of **radioactive iodine** and TSH suppression. Smaller cancers without nodal spread may be treated with hemithyroidectomy alone.

**Counselling:** the prognosis for a young adult with papillary cancer is excellent. The risks of surgery (voice change, low calcium, need for lifelong levothyroxine after total thyroidectomy) are discussed.
:::
:::

::: history
Theodor Kocher, professor of surgery in Bern from 1872, reduced the mortality of thyroidectomy from a level that had led some to call the operation butchery to well under 1% through meticulous technique and careful attention to anatomy. He also noticed that patients who had their whole thyroid removed developed a condition he called "cachexia strumipriva", which turned out to be hypothyroidism; the observation helped to reveal the function of the thyroid. He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1909. In breast surgery, William Halsted's radical mastectomy (1894), removing the breast, both pectoral muscles and the axillary contents, remained the standard for 80 years, until trials led by Bernard Fisher in the United States and Umberto Veronesi in Milan showed that far smaller operations combined with radiotherapy gave the same survival.
:::

::: summary
- Breast symptoms are assessed by **triple assessment**: clinical examination, imaging (ultrasound under about 40, mammography and ultrasound above) and core biopsy. If any part is suspicious, the lesion is treated as possibly malignant.
- Breast cancer treatment depends on **stage, grade and receptors (ER, PR, HER2)**. Breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy gives the same survival as mastectomy for suitable tumours.
- **Sentinel node biopsy** stages a clinically node-negative axilla and spares most women axillary clearance and lymphoedema.
- NPI = 0.2 × size (cm) + node score + grade.
- The NHS screens women aged 50–71 every 3 years; screening saves lives but causes overdiagnosis. *BRCA1/2* carriers may choose enhanced surveillance or risk-reducing surgery.
- Thyroid nodules are assessed with TFTs, ultrasound (**U1–U5**) and FNAC (**Thy1–Thy5**). Papillary cancer is commonest; follicular cancer needs histology; medullary cancer is linked to *RET* and MEN2.
- Thyroidectomy risks: recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, hypocalcaemia and **airway-threatening haematoma** (SCOOP at the bedside).
- Primary hyperparathyroidism: raised adjusted calcium with unsuppressed PTH; surgery for symptoms, end-organ disease or calcium ≥ 2.85 mmol/L; localise before focused parathyroidectomy.
- Adrenal incidentalomas are tested for hormone excess and malignancy; phaeochromocytoma needs **alpha-blockade before beta-blockade**.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Triple assessment in practice {level=1}
A 45-year-old woman has a breast lump scored 2 (benign) on examination, 2 on imaging and 4 (suspicious) on core biopsy. What is the conclusion? What if the scores were 4, 2 and 2?
::: solution
In both cases the assessment is **discordant**, so the lesion cannot be called benign. In the first, the biopsy is suspicious, so she needs further tissue sampling or excision. In the second, the clinical examination is suspicious despite benign imaging and biopsy; the case is discussed at the multidisciplinary meeting and usually leads to repeat biopsy or excision, because a sampling error is possible. The rule is that all three parts must agree before reassurance is given.
:::

A patient has a total calcium of 2.52 mmol/L and an albumin of 28 g/L. Calculate the albumin-adjusted calcium in mmol/L using the formula in this chapter. Give the exact value.
::: solution
$2.52 + 0.02 \times (40 - 28) = 2.52 + 0.24 = 2.76$ mmol/L. Although the total calcium is near the upper limit of normal, the adjusted value is raised. In a patient with a low albumin, an unadjusted calcium can hide hypercalcaemia.

::: exercise Interpreting cytology {level=2}
Explain why a Thy3f result (possible follicular neoplasm) on FNAC of a thyroid nodule usually leads to a diagnostic hemithyroidectomy, while a Thy2 result usually leads to reassurance or follow-up.
::: solution
Follicular carcinoma and follicular adenoma have the same appearance on cytology: both consist of follicular cells. The difference is whether the tumour invades its capsule or blood vessels, which can be seen only on histological examination of the whole nodule. A Thy3f result therefore has a substantial chance of malignancy that cannot be resolved without removing the lobe. A Thy2 result is non-neoplastic (for example a colloid nodule or thyroiditis); combined with a benign ultrasound appearance, it does not need surgery unless the nodule causes pressure symptoms, grows or has worrying features, and some units repeat FNAC to confirm.
:::
:::

::: exercise Who needs a genetics referral? {level=2}
For each woman, decide whether referral to a genetics service for possible inherited breast cancer is likely to be appropriate, and why: (a) a 62-year-old woman whose mother had breast cancer at 75; (b) a 35-year-old woman with triple-negative breast cancer; (c) a 50-year-old woman of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry whose sister had ovarian cancer; (d) a man with breast cancer.
::: solution
(a) Probably not: a single relative diagnosed at an older age modestly increases risk but does not usually suggest a high-risk variant. Local criteria apply. (b) Yes: triple-negative cancer at a young age is associated with *BRCA1*, and testing affects treatment and family members. (c) Yes: ovarian cancer in a close relative and Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, in which founder *BRCA* variants are relatively common, both raise the likelihood of an inherited variant. (d) Yes: male breast cancer is associated with *BRCA2*. Genetic testing criteria are set nationally and change as testing becomes more available.
:::

::: exercise Hoarse after surgery {level=2}
A 47-year-old teacher is hoarse and her voice tires easily the day after a right hemithyroidectomy. What is the likely cause, how is it assessed, and what is the prognosis? Why would bilateral injury be more dangerous?
::: solution
**Likely cause:** injury (often neuropraxia from traction or bruising) of the **right recurrent laryngeal nerve**, causing a paralysed right vocal cord. **Assessment:** laryngoscopy by an ENT surgeon to see vocal cord movement. **Prognosis:** most nerve injuries in which the nerve was seen to be intact recover within weeks to months; permanent palsy is uncommon. Speech and language therapy helps, and procedures to move the paralysed cord to the midline are available if it does not recover. Her occupation as a teacher makes the voice particularly important, which should have been part of her consent discussion. **Bilateral injury** can leave both cords near the midline, causing stridor and airway obstruction after extubation, and may require reintubation or a tracheostomy.

::: exercise Hypercalcaemia that is not the parathyroid {level=3}
A 70-year-old man has an adjusted calcium of 3.15 mmol/L, a PTH of 0.6 pmol/L (suppressed), weight loss and back pain. He is drowsy and dehydrated. Explain why surgery for hyperparathyroidism is not the answer, list the likely causes, and outline the immediate treatment.
::: solution
**Why not hyperparathyroidism:** in primary hyperparathyroidism PTH is raised or inappropriately normal. A **suppressed PTH** shows that the parathyroid glands are responding normally to high calcium, so the cause lies elsewhere. **Likely causes:** **malignancy**, through bone metastases (for example from lung, breast, prostate or kidney cancer) or **multiple myeloma**, or secretion of PTH-related peptide by a tumour (for example squamous cell lung cancer); less commonly vitamin D excess, sarcoidosis, thiazides, immobilisation. Weight loss and back pain point to malignancy, possibly myeloma. **Immediate treatment:** intravenous 0.9% sodium chloride to correct dehydration and promote renal calcium excretion, with monitoring of fluid balance; stop drugs that raise calcium (thiazides, calcium and vitamin D supplements); then an intravenous **bisphosphonate** (or denosumab in some situations) once rehydrated, according to local guidance. Investigations: myeloma screen (serum and urine protein electrophoresis, free light chains), PTH-related peptide, chest X-ray or CT and a search for a primary tumour. See [[endocrine/endocrine-emergencies]].
:::

::: exercise Planning a screening invitation {level=3}
A local campaign wants the breast screening invitation letter to say only "Screening saves lives — book now". Using what you know about the benefits and harms of mammographic screening, explain why this is not adequate and what a balanced invitation should include.
::: solution
**Problem:** the message gives only the benefit and implies that participation is simply the right thing to do. Informed choice requires knowing both benefits and harms. **Balanced content:** that screening reduces the risk of dying from breast cancer; that it can find cancers that would never have caused harm (**overdiagnosis**), so some women are treated with surgery, radiotherapy and drugs they did not need (the UK Independent Panel estimated about three overdiagnosed women for each death prevented); that some women are recalled for further tests and biopsies and turn out not to have cancer (false positives), which causes anxiety; that mammography misses some cancers, so new symptoms between screens should be reported; and that the choice whether to attend is the woman's. The NHS leaflet for breast screening has been revised along these lines. The ethics and evaluation of screening are covered in [[public-health/screening]].
:::
