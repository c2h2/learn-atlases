Vascular and urological surgery share an alphabet of pipes: occlusion, aneurysm, stone and obstruction. **Vascular surgery is the discipline of flow and pressure - the claudicant's arteries, the aneurysm's wall, the diabetic foot's run-off, the dialysis patient's access and the venous leg's reflux - practised increasingly through needle-sized gates. Urology is the surgery of low-pressure systems that present with bleeding and obstruction - the prostate, the stone, the haematuria, the testis and the upper tract - where a scope, a stent and a scan do most of the work and a radical nephrectomy still sometimes does the rest.** Both teach that timing beats technique and that the imaging report is a hypothesis.

::: summary
- Vascular decisions balance anatomy, disability and durability: supervised exercise and best medical therapy are operations, and reconstruction is judged on limb salvage and quality of life, not pictures.
- Ruptured AAA and infected obstructed uropathy are minutes-and-hours emergencies where protocol, named activation and honest ceilings save more than technique.
- Chronic limb threat lives or dies on multidisciplinary foot care, timely revascularisation and amputation only when it serves function and dignity.
- Urological cancer care is surveillance-heavy: haematuria gets cystoscopy and imaging, a testicular lump gets inguinal orchidectomy and markers, and every stent needs a written exit plan.
- Endovascular and device eras trade early morbidity for late reintervention and lifelong imaging follow-up - counselling must state both sides.
- Renal function is the shared long-term stake of stones, obstruction, cancer surgery and contrast; someone must own it by name.
