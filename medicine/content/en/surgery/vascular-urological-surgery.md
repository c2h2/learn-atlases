Vascular surgery and urology are separate specialties, but they share a ward round in many hospitals and several features. Both treat common conditions of older people, often smokers with heart disease and diabetes. Both have emergencies in which minutes or hours decide whether an organ survives: a leg without blood flow, a twisted testis, a ruptured aorta, an infected obstructed kidney. And both increasingly treat disease through catheters and wires rather than open incisions.

This chapter covers peripheral arterial disease and limb ischaemia, aortic aneurysm, carotid disease, venous disease and the diabetic foot, then the main urological conditions: stones and the obstructed kidney, urinary retention and benign prostatic enlargement, haematuria and urological cancers, and the scrotal and penile emergencies. Guidance followed is UK-first (NICE, the NHS Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Screening Programme, the European Society for Vascular Surgery and the UK National Screening Committee) as of 2026. Management of individual patients follows local protocols.

## Peripheral arterial disease

::: definition Peripheral arterial disease {#def-vascular}
**Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)** is narrowing or occlusion of the arteries to the limbs, almost always by **atherosclerosis**. Its main risk factors are smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, chronic kidney disease and age. It presents in stages:

- **asymptomatic**, found by absent pulses or a low ankle pressure;
- **intermittent claudication**: cramping pain in the calf, thigh or buttock brought on by walking a predictable distance and relieved within minutes by rest;
- **chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI)**: pain at rest (typically in the forefoot at night, relieved by hanging the leg over the bed), or tissue loss (ulcers or gangrene), for more than two weeks.

PAD is a marker of widespread atherosclerosis: people with claudication are at much greater risk of myocardial infarction and stroke than of losing their leg.
:::

The key bedside test is the **ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI)**: the highest systolic pressure at the ankle (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial artery, measured with a hand-held Doppler) divided by the highest brachial systolic pressure.

$$
\text{ABPI} = \frac{\text{ankle systolic pressure}}{\text{highest brachial systolic pressure}}
$$ {#eq-abpi}

A normal ABPI is about 1.0–1.4. Values below 0.9 indicate PAD; values below about 0.5 suggest severe disease. In people with diabetes or chronic kidney disease, calcified arteries cannot be compressed, so the ABPI may be falsely normal or above 1.4; toe pressures and arterial waveforms are then used instead.

NICE guideline CG147 sets out management. Everyone with PAD receives **cardiovascular risk reduction**: smoking cessation (the most important single measure), an antiplatelet drug, a high-intensity statin, and control of blood pressure and diabetes ([[cardiovascular/vascular-disease]]). For claudication, a **supervised exercise programme** is the first treatment and improves walking distance substantially. Revascularisation (angioplasty, stenting or bypass) is considered when exercise has not helped enough and symptoms limit life, and is needed for CLTI to save the limb. Imaging with duplex ultrasound, then CT or MR angiography, plans the procedure.

::: example Measuring the ABPI {#ex-abpi}
A 68-year-old man who smokes has pain in his right calf after walking about 200 metres, relieved by resting for 2 minutes. Doppler pressures: right arm 146 mmHg, left arm 138 mmHg; right ankle 88 mmHg (posterior tibial, higher of the two ankle arteries); left ankle 140 mmHg. Calculate his ABPIs and interpret them.
::: solution
**Denominator:** the higher brachial pressure, 146 mmHg.

**Right ABPI:** $88 / 146 \approx 0.60$.

**Left ABPI:** $140 / 146 \approx 0.96$.

**Interpretation:** the right ABPI of 0.60 confirms moderate PAD on the right, consistent with his calf claudication (likely superficial femoral artery disease). The left is normal.

**Management:** this is claudication, not limb-threatening ischaemia. He needs smoking cessation support, an antiplatelet drug, a high-intensity statin, blood pressure and glucose assessment, and referral to a supervised exercise programme. Revascularisation is considered later if his symptoms remain disabling despite exercise. He should be told that his greatest risk is a heart attack or stroke, which is why the medical treatment matters most.
:::
:::

## Acute limb ischaemia

**Acute limb ischaemia** is a sudden decrease in limb perfusion that threatens the limb's viability. It is caused by **embolism** (often from the heart in atrial fibrillation or after myocardial infarction), **thrombosis** of an artery already narrowed by atherosclerosis or of a bypass graft, trauma, or dissection.

The classical features are the **six Ps**: **pain**, **pallor**, **pulselessness**, **perishingly cold**, **paraesthesia** and **paralysis**. Sensory loss and muscle weakness are the signs that the limb is **threatened**: nerves and muscle are the tissues most sensitive to ischaemia, and irreversible damage can begin within about 6 hours. Fixed mottling that does not blanch, a hard and tender calf and complete paralysis indicate an irreversibly damaged limb.

Embolic and thrombotic occlusion are distinguished by history: embolism causes a sudden onset in a patient with a source (atrial fibrillation) and no previous claudication, often with normal pulses in the other leg; thrombosis occurs in a patient with known PAD, often with a less abrupt onset because collateral vessels have developed.

Management follows the **European Society for Vascular Surgery** guidance (2020): immediate **heparin** to stop the clot propagating, analgesia, urgent vascular surgical assessment, and revascularisation by **embolectomy** (with a balloon catheter), **bypass**, or **catheter-directed thrombolysis** and endovascular treatment, depending on the cause and the severity. A threatened limb needs revascularisation within hours, often without time for detailed imaging. After restoring blood flow, the limb swells (reperfusion injury), and **compartment syndrome** may need fasciotomy ([[surgery/orthopaedics]]). Reperfusion can also release potassium, acid and myoglobin, causing hyperkalaemia, arrhythmias and acute kidney injury.

::: example A cold white leg {#ex-ali}
A 79-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation, not anticoagulated, has had sudden severe pain in her left leg for 3 hours. The leg is white and cold below the knee, with no pulses below the femoral. She cannot feel light touch on her foot and has weak toe movements. Her right leg pulses are normal. What is the diagnosis and the priority?
::: solution
**Diagnosis:** acute limb ischaemia, most likely from an **embolus** (sudden onset, atrial fibrillation, normal pulses on the other side, no history of claudication). The occlusion is probably at the popliteal or femoral bifurcation.

**Severity:** sensory loss and weakness mean the limb is **immediately threatened**. Without revascularisation within hours, she will lose muscle and nerve function and may need an amputation.

**Priority:** give intravenous **heparin** and analgesia, call the vascular team immediately, take bloods (including group and save, kidney function and creatine kinase) and an ECG, and keep her nil by mouth. She is likely to need an urgent **embolectomy**, possibly with on-table angiography, and the team must be prepared for fasciotomy and for reperfusion effects. Long-term anticoagulation for her atrial fibrillation will be needed to prevent further emboli.
:::
:::

## Aortic aneurysm

An **abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)** is a permanent dilatation of the abdominal aorta to a diameter of **3.0 cm or more**. Most are infrarenal. Risk factors are age, male sex, smoking (the strongest modifiable factor), family history, hypertension and atherosclerosis. Most AAAs are asymptomatic until they rupture, and rupture is often fatal before the person reaches hospital.

::: theorem Laplace's law and aneurysm growth {#thm-laplace}
For a cylindrical vessel, the tension in the wall is proportional to the product of the pressure inside and the radius: $T \propto P \times r$. A wider aorta therefore experiences greater wall tension at the same blood pressure, which causes it to dilate further, and both a larger diameter and higher blood pressure increase the risk of rupture.
:::

::: proof
Consider a length of vessel cut along its axis. The pressure $P$ acting on the inside pushes the two halves apart with a force proportional to $P$ times the projected area, which is proportional to the diameter. This is opposed by the wall tension acting along the two cut edges. Balancing the forces gives wall tension proportional to $P \times r$; the stress within the wall material is this tension divided by the wall thickness, so a thinner wall makes it worse. In an aneurysm, the wall is weakened by degradation of elastin and collagen and becomes thinner as it stretches, so the stress rises faster than the diameter. This is a positive feedback loop: dilatation increases stress, which causes further dilatation. It explains why small aneurysms grow slowly and large ones grow faster, why rupture risk rises steeply with diameter, and why blood pressure control and smoking cessation (which slows growth) are part of surveillance.
:::

**Screening.** The **NHS AAA Screening Programme** invites men for a single abdominal ultrasound in the year they turn 65; older men can self-refer. Results determine follow-up:

| Aortic diameter | Classification | Action |
|---|---|---|
| < 3.0 cm | normal | discharged |
| 3.0–4.4 cm | small AAA | annual ultrasound surveillance |
| 4.5–5.4 cm | medium AAA | ultrasound every 3 months |
| ≥ 5.5 cm | large AAA | referral to a vascular surgeon to be seen within 2 weeks |

Women are not routinely screened because AAA is much less common in women, but aneurysms in women rupture at smaller diameters.

**Repair.** Repair is considered for aneurysms of **5.5 cm or more**, for those growing faster than about 1 cm a year (once over 4.0 cm), and for **symptomatic** aneurysms (tender, or causing back or abdominal pain). Below this size, the risk of rupture is lower than the risk of operation. There are two methods: **open repair**, replacing the diseased segment with a synthetic graft through a laparotomy, and **endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR)**, placing a stent-graft through the femoral arteries. EVAR has lower early mortality but needs lifelong imaging surveillance for **endoleaks** (continued flow into the aneurysm sac) and more reinterventions; open repair is more durable. The choice depends on the anatomy, the patient's fitness and their preference, following NICE guideline NG156 (2020).

**Rupture** presents with abdominal or back pain, collapse and a pulsatile mass, though the mass may be hard to feel. It may be mistaken for renal colic, diverticulitis or a myocardial infarction. Management is **permissive hypotension** (enough blood pressure to keep the patient conscious; aggressive fluids increase bleeding), blood products, and immediate transfer to theatre for repair, by EVAR if anatomy allows and a CT can be obtained safely, otherwise open repair.

::: example The painful pulsatile mass {#ex-rupture}
A 74-year-old man collapses at home with sudden severe back pain. In the emergency department his heart rate is 118/min, blood pressure 82/50 mmHg, and he is pale and sweaty but talking. There is a tender pulsatile mass above the umbilicus. What is the management?
::: solution
**Diagnosis:** ruptured AAA until proven otherwise.

**Management:**

1. Call the vascular surgeon, anaesthetist and theatre team immediately; activate the **major haemorrhage protocol**.
2. Two large-bore cannulae; blood for crossmatch, full blood count, clotting, kidney function.
3. **Permissive hypotension:** because he is conscious with a systolic pressure of 82 mmHg, avoid large fluid boluses; give blood products if he deteriorates. Raising the blood pressure towards normal can increase bleeding from the rupture.
4. If he is stable enough, a **CT angiogram** shows the anatomy and whether EVAR is possible. If he is unstable, he goes straight to theatre.
5. Discuss with him (and his family) what is happening, the very high risk, and his wishes, because some patients, particularly with severe comorbidity, would choose comfort care.

Mortality of a ruptured AAA is high even with prompt surgery, which is why screening and elective repair of large aneurysms matter.
:::
:::

### Carotid disease

Atherosclerosis at the carotid bifurcation causes stroke and transient ischaemic attack (TIA) by embolism. For people with a TIA or non-disabling stroke and a **symptomatic carotid stenosis** of 50–99% (by the NASCET method), **carotid endarterectomy** reduces the risk of further stroke, and the benefit is greatest when surgery is done early: NICE (NG128) recommends assessment and referral within a week of symptom onset and surgery within a maximum of two weeks. Asymptomatic stenosis is usually managed with best medical therapy. Stroke medicine is covered in [[nervous-system/stroke]].

## Venous disease

**Varicose veins** are dilated, tortuous superficial veins caused by incompetent valves, usually in the great or small saphenous system. Many people have them without symptoms. NICE guideline CG168 recommends referral to a vascular service for people with symptomatic varicose veins, skin changes (pigmentation, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis), superficial vein thrombosis with incompetence, a venous leg ulcer or a healed ulcer. Treatment, after duplex ultrasound, is usually by **endothermal ablation** (radiofrequency or laser) of the incompetent trunk, with foam sclerotherapy or surgery as alternatives.

**Venous leg ulcers** occur around the medial malleolus ("gaiter area") in legs with chronic venous hypertension. They are shallow, with sloping edges, and surrounded by skin changes. The mainstay of treatment is **compression**, which is safe only after arterial disease has been excluded with an ABPI. Treating the underlying superficial venous reflux early speeds healing and reduces recurrence.

**Deep vein thrombosis** is diagnosed with a Wells score, D-dimer and compression ultrasound, and treated with anticoagulation ([[blood/thrombosis-anticoagulation]]). Massive iliofemoral DVT can cause a swollen, blue, painful leg (**phlegmasia cerulea dolens**) that threatens limb viability and may need catheter-directed thrombolysis.

### The diabetic foot

Diabetic foot disease combines **neuropathy**, **ischaemia** and **infection**. Neuropathy removes pain and causes deformity, so ulcers form at pressure points without warning; ischaemia prevents healing; and infection spreads quickly in the tissue planes of the foot. NICE guideline NG19 recommends a multidisciplinary foot service, regular foot checks for everyone with diabetes, and immediate referral for limb-threatening problems. Amputation rates fall when these services work well ([[endocrine/diabetes-complications]], [[surgery/wounds-surgical-infection]]).

## Urology

::: definition The main urological emergencies {#def-urology}
- **Obstructed, infected kidney:** a stone (or other cause) blocks the ureter and the urine above it becomes infected. This is a surgical emergency requiring **urgent drainage** by nephrostomy or ureteric stent, in addition to antibiotics.
- **Acute urinary retention:** painful inability to pass urine; treated by catheterisation.
- **Testicular torsion:** twisting of the testis on its spermatic cord, cutting off its blood supply; treated by **immediate surgical exploration**.
- **Fournier's gangrene:** necrotising infection of the perineum and genitalia; treated by resuscitation, antibiotics and urgent debridement.
- **Ischaemic priapism:** a painful erection lasting more than 4 hours, with no arterial inflow; treated by aspiration of blood from the corpora and injection of a vasoconstrictor.
- **Paraphimosis:** a retracted foreskin that cannot be returned over the glans, which swells; treated by reduction.
:::

### Kidney and ureteric stones

**Renal colic** is severe, colicky loin pain radiating to the groin (and to the testis or labium as the stone descends), often with nausea and microscopic haematuria. The patient cannot keep still. Stones form from calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate, uric acid, struvite (with urease-producing infection) or cystine; risk factors include dehydration, diet, hypercalciuria, gout, previous stones and a family history.

NICE guideline NG118 (2019) recommends **NSAIDs** as first-line analgesia (unless contraindicated), and **low-dose non-contrast CT** of the kidneys, ureters and bladder within 24 hours of presentation; ultrasound is used for children and pregnant women. Many small stones pass spontaneously; an **alpha-blocker** may be offered for distal ureteric stones under 10 mm. Stones that do not pass, cause uncontrolled pain or are large are treated by **shockwave lithotripsy**, **ureteroscopy** or, for large kidney stones, **percutaneous nephrolithotomy**.

::: example Fever and a stone {#ex-pyo}
A 44-year-old woman with known right renal colic is brought back to the emergency department 2 days later with rigors, a temperature of 39.4 °C, heart rate 124/min and blood pressure 90/56 mmHg. Her right loin is tender. CT shows a 7 mm stone in the right mid-ureter with hydronephrosis. What is the diagnosis and treatment?
::: solution
**Diagnosis:** an **obstructed, infected kidney** with sepsis. Pus is trapped above the obstruction under pressure, and bacteria enter the bloodstream.

**Treatment:** follow sepsis guidance (blood and urine cultures, lactate, intravenous fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics according to local guidance), and arrange **urgent decompression** of the kidney, by a percutaneous **nephrostomy** placed by an interventional radiologist or a retrograde **ureteric stent** placed by a urologist. Antibiotics alone cannot clear infection from an obstructed system.

**Do not:** attempt definitive stone treatment (such as ureteroscopy) in the infected kidney; the stone is dealt with later, once infection has cleared. A septic patient with a stone is never discharged with analgesia alone.
:::
:::

### Urinary retention and the prostate

**Acute urinary retention** presents with a painful, palpable bladder and inability to void. In men it is most often caused by **benign prostatic enlargement**, often precipitated by constipation, anticholinergic or opioid drugs, alcohol, infection or surgery. Other causes include urethral stricture, clot retention, neurological disease (including cauda equina syndrome) and, in women, pelvic masses. Management is **catheterisation**, recording the residual volume, and treatment of the precipitating cause. Kidney function is checked, and **post-obstructive diuresis** may follow relief of chronic retention. Men usually start an **alpha-blocker** (for example tamsulosin) before a **trial without catheter**.

**Chronic retention** develops painlessly, with a large residual volume. High-pressure chronic retention can cause hydronephrosis and kidney failure.

**Benign prostatic enlargement** causes lower urinary tract symptoms: weak stream, hesitancy, frequency, urgency and nocturia. Treatment ranges from lifestyle advice to alpha-blockers (relax prostatic smooth muscle), 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (shrink the prostate over months) and surgery, such as **transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP)** or laser procedures.

### Haematuria and urological cancers

**Visible haematuria** without infection is a red flag for bladder or kidney cancer. Under NICE NG12, adults aged 45 and over with unexplained visible haematuria without urinary tract infection (or persisting after treatment of infection) are referred on a suspected cancer pathway; people aged 60 and over with unexplained non-visible haematuria and either dysuria or a raised white cell count are also referred. Investigation is usually **flexible cystoscopy** and imaging of the upper tract (CT urography or ultrasound).

- **Bladder cancer** (mostly urothelial carcinoma) is associated with **smoking** and occupational exposure to aromatic amines. Non-muscle-invasive tumours are resected endoscopically and followed with surveillance cystoscopy; muscle-invasive tumours are treated by radical cystectomy or radiotherapy.
- **Kidney cancer** (renal cell carcinoma) is often found incidentally on imaging. Treatment is partial or radical nephrectomy for localised disease.
- **Prostate cancer** is the commonest cancer in men in the UK. Diagnosis follows a raised **PSA** or an abnormal digital rectal examination, then **multiparametric MRI** of the prostate and targeted biopsy, as recommended by NICE NG131. Management ranges from **active surveillance** for low-risk disease to radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy, and hormone therapy for advanced disease. PSA is not specific for cancer: it rises with benign enlargement, infection and instrumentation. In May 2026 the UK National Screening Committee recommended against population PSA screening and recommended targeted screening every 2 years for men aged 45 to 61 with a confirmed *BRCA2* variant and a relevant family history; at the time of writing the UK governments had not announced their decisions. Men who ask for a PSA test can have one after a discussion of its benefits and harms.
- **Testicular cancer** is the commonest solid cancer in young men. It presents as a painless, hard lump in the testis. Ultrasound confirms it; tumour markers (**AFP, β-hCG, LDH**) are measured; and treatment begins with **radical inguinal orchidectomy** (not through the scrotum, to avoid altering lymphatic drainage). Cure rates are very high, even with metastatic disease.

### The acute scrotum

::: warning
**Testicular torsion is a clinical diagnosis, and imaging must not delay surgery.** A boy or young man with sudden severe testicular pain, often with nausea and vomiting and sometimes presenting with lower abdominal pain, has torsion until proven otherwise. The testis may lie high with a horizontal lie, and the cremasteric reflex is often absent. Salvage rates are high if the testis is untwisted within about 6 hours and fall steeply after that. Doppler ultrasound can be falsely reassuring. The correct action is urgent scrotal exploration, untwisting and fixation of both testes (because the anatomical predisposition is usually bilateral). Always examine the testes in a boy with abdominal pain.
:::

Other causes of the acute scrotum include **torsion of a testicular appendage** (pain localised to the upper pole, sometimes with a "blue dot" sign), **epididymo-orchitis** (more gradual onset, fever, urinary symptoms; caused by sexually transmitted organisms in younger men and urinary organisms in older men), trauma, an incarcerated inguinal hernia and, rarely, a tumour presenting after minor injury.

::: example A boy with scrotal pain {#ex-testis}
A 14-year-old boy woke at 05:00 with severe pain in his left testis and has vomited twice. He arrives at 07:30. The left testis is very tender and lies higher than the right, and the left cremasteric reflex is absent. His mother asks whether an ultrasound can be done first. What should happen?
::: solution
**Diagnosis:** testicular torsion until proven otherwise: sudden severe pain, vomiting, a high-riding testis and an absent cremasteric reflex in an adolescent.

**Action:** urgent **scrotal exploration** under general anaesthetic, without waiting for an ultrasound. He has had symptoms for 2.5 hours; the chance of saving the testis is good if he reaches theatre promptly. Keep him nil by mouth, give analgesia, and obtain consent from his mother (with his agreement), explaining that if the testis is not viable it will be removed (orchidectomy), and that the other testis will be fixed to prevent future torsion.

**If exploration shows torsion of a testicular appendage** instead, it is removed and no harm has been done. Exploring a testis that turns out not to be twisted is accepted as the price of not missing torsion.
:::
:::

**Fournier's gangrene** is necrotising fasciitis of the perineum, scrotum and penis ([[surgery/wounds-surgical-infection]]). It is more common in men with diabetes, obesity or alcohol misuse. Pain and systemic illness exceed the visible skin changes; crepitus may be felt. Treatment is resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics and immediate radical debridement, often repeated.

::: quiz
A 70-year-old man is brought in unable to pass urine for 12 hours, with suprapubic pain. He started codeine 3 days ago for back pain and is constipated. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] An ultrasound of the kidneys must be done before he is catheterised
- [x] He should be catheterised, the residual volume recorded, and the codeine and constipation addressed
- [ ] He should be started on an alpha-blocker and sent home without a catheter
- [ ] Back pain with retention is always due to prostatic enlargement
::: solution
Acute urinary retention is relieved first by catheterisation, and the residual volume is recorded. Opioids and constipation are common precipitants and should be treated; an alpha-blocker is usually started before a later trial without catheter. Back pain with retention should also prompt a check for **cauda equina syndrome** (saddle anaesthesia, leg weakness, bowel symptoms), which needs urgent MRI.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1912 the French surgeon Alexis Carrel received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his techniques of suturing blood vessels, developed with Charles Guthrie, which made vascular reconstruction and organ transplantation possible. In 1951 Charles Dubost in Paris performed the first resection of an abdominal aortic aneurysm with replacement by a homograft. In 1964 Charles Dotter described transluminal angioplasty, dilating narrowed arteries with catheters, and in 1990 Juan Parodi in Buenos Aires reported the first endovascular repair of an abdominal aortic aneurysm. In urology, the cystoscope developed by Maximilian Nitze in 1879 allowed the bladder to be seen from inside, and in 1980 Christian Chaussy and colleagues in Munich reported the first treatment of kidney stones by extracorporeal shockwave lithotripsy.
:::

- **Acute limb ischaemia** (six Ps): sensory loss and weakness mean a threatened limb; heparin and revascularisation within hours; watch for reperfusion injury and compartment syndrome.

- **Testicular torsion**: explore without delay; examine the testes in boys with abdominal pain.

Under the NHS AAA Screening Programme, what happens to a 65-year-old man whose aorta measures (a) 2.4 cm, (b) 3.8 cm, (c) 5.0 cm, (d) 6.1 cm?
::: solution
(a) Normal; discharged from the programme. (b) Small AAA; annual ultrasound surveillance, with smoking cessation and cardiovascular risk management. (c) Medium AAA; ultrasound every 3 months. (d) Large AAA; referral to a vascular surgeon to be seen within 2 weeks, for assessment for repair. In all cases with an aneurysm, he is advised to stop smoking and his blood pressure and lipids are treated.

::: exercise Laplace in numbers {level=2 check="(6/2)*(150/120)"}
Using the simplified relation $T \propto P \times r$, how many times greater is the wall tension in a 6 cm aneurysm at a systolic pressure of 150 mmHg than in a normal 2 cm aorta at 120 mmHg? Give the exact value.
::: hint
The ratio is the product of the diameter ratio and the pressure ratio.
:::
::: solution
Diameter ratio: $6/2 = 3$. Pressure ratio: $150/120 = 1.25$. Tension ratio: $3 \times 1.25 = 3.75$. The wall of the aneurysm also becomes thinner, so the actual stress in the wall rises by even more. This is why both diameter and blood pressure control matter.
:::

::: exercise A stone in pregnancy {level=2}
A woman who is 24 weeks pregnant has right loin pain radiating to the groin and microscopic haematuria. How is her investigation and management different from that of a non-pregnant woman?
::: solution
**Imaging:** ultrasound is the first-line test, to avoid ionising radiation; physiological hydronephrosis of pregnancy (more common on the right) can make interpretation difficult, so MRI without contrast or, rarely, low-dose CT may be used after specialist discussion. **Analgesia:** NSAIDs are avoided in pregnancy, particularly in the third trimester (risk to the fetal ductus arteriosus and kidneys) and are generally not used; paracetamol and opioids are used according to obstetric guidance. **Differential:** appendicitis (displaced upwards in pregnancy), pyelonephritis, placental abruption and preterm labour. **Treatment:** most stones pass; if there is obstruction with infection or uncontrollable pain, the kidney is drained with a stent or nephrostomy, and definitive treatment is usually deferred until after delivery. The obstetric team is involved.
:::
:::

::: exercise A painless lump {level=2}
A 29-year-old man notices a painless, hard lump in his right testis. Outline the investigations and first treatment, and explain why the testis is removed through the groin.
::: solution
**Investigations:** scrotal **ultrasound** (confirms an intratesticular solid mass); **tumour markers** AFP, β-hCG and LDH before orchidectomy; CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis for staging. Offer **sperm banking** before treatment. **First treatment:** **radical inguinal orchidectomy**, removing the testis and spermatic cord through an incision in the groin, with histology to distinguish seminoma from non-seminomatous germ cell tumours; subsequent treatment (surveillance, chemotherapy or radiotherapy) depends on histology, markers and stage. **Why inguinal:** the testis drains via lymphatics that run with the spermatic cord to the para-aortic nodes. A scrotal incision could seed tumour into the scrotal skin and open a new lymphatic route to the inguinal nodes, complicating staging and treatment.
:::
:::

::: exercise Haematuria triage {level=3}
For each patient, decide whether a suspected cancer referral is indicated under NICE NG12 and what else should be done: (a) a 52-year-old man who smokes with a single episode of painless visible haematuria and a negative urine culture; (b) a 30-year-old woman with dysuria, frequency, visible haematuria and a positive culture, whose symptoms resolve with antibiotics; (c) a 65-year-old woman with non-visible haematuria on two dipsticks and a raised white cell count, with no infection found.

(a) Yes: age 45 or over with unexplained visible haematuria and no infection. A single episode is enough. He needs cystoscopy and upper tract imaging, and smoking cessation advice. ::: exercise A leg after embolectomy {level=3}
Six hours after a successful femoral embolectomy for an acutely ischaemic leg (ischaemic time about 8 hours), a 72-year-old man's calf becomes tense and very painful on passive stretching of the toes. His potassium is 6.4 mmol/L, his urine is dark and his creatine kinase is 48,000 U/L. Explain what is happening and outline the management.
::: solution
**What is happening:** **reperfusion injury**. Restoring blood flow to ischaemic muscle generates free radicals and increases capillary permeability, so the muscle swells within its fascial compartments, causing **compartment syndrome** (pain on passive stretch is an early sign; pulses may still be present). Damaged muscle releases **potassium**, acid and **myoglobin** (rhabdomyolysis), causing hyperkalaemia and dark urine, and myoglobin can cause **acute kidney injury**.

**Management:** urgent **four-compartment fasciotomy** of the lower leg to relieve compartment pressure; treat **hyperkalaemia** immediately (ECG monitoring, intravenous calcium to protect the heart, insulin with glucose, and further measures according to the hyperkalaemia guideline); give generous intravenous fluids to maintain a high urine output and protect the kidneys from myoglobin; monitor kidney function, potassium, CK and fluid balance closely, with renal replacement therapy if needed; continue anticoagulation as appropriate. Reperfusion syndrome is anticipated after long ischaemic times, so fasciotomy is often performed at the time of revascularisation in these patients.
