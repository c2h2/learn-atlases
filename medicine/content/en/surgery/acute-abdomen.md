The **acute abdomen** is abdominal pain of recent onset, usually less than a week, that may need urgent treatment, often surgery. It is one of the commonest reasons for emergency admission. Most patients have conditions that settle with simple care, but among them are people with a perforated bowel, a strangulated hernia, an ischaemic gut or a leaking aneurysm, for whom hours matter. The skill is to recognise those patients early, resuscitate them and get them to the right treatment quickly.

This chapter explains how abdominal pain arises and where it is felt, how to assess the patient, the four basic surgical processes (inflammation, obstruction, perforation and ischaemia), the common conditions, the non-surgical mimics, and the standards of care for emergency laparotomy. Guidance followed is UK-first (NICE, the National Emergency Laparotomy Audit and specialist societies) as of 2026. Drug choices are given as principles; individual prescribing follows local guidance.

## Why abdominal pain is felt where it is

Two kinds of nerve supply carry pain from the abdomen, and they behave differently.

**Visceral pain** comes from the gut wall and the visceral peritoneum. It is carried by autonomic afferent fibres that enter the spinal cord at several levels on both sides, so it is **poorly localised**, felt in the midline and described as dull, crampy or colicky. It is triggered by stretch, distension and ischaemia, not by cutting. Where it is felt depends on the embryological origin of the organ:

- **foregut** (oesophagus to the second part of the duodenum, liver, gallbladder, pancreas): **epigastrium**;
- **midgut** (duodenum to the proximal two-thirds of the transverse colon, including the appendix): **periumbilical region**;
- **hindgut** (distal transverse colon to the upper anal canal): **suprapubic region**.

**Somatic (parietal) pain** comes from the parietal peritoneum, which is supplied by the same segmental spinal nerves as the overlying abdominal wall. It is **sharp and well localised**, made worse by movement and coughing, and causes local tenderness and guarding.

::: theorem Pain migrates as inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum {#thm-migration}
When an inflamed organ first causes pain, the pain is visceral and felt in the midline according to the organ's embryological origin. When the inflammation spreads to the overlying parietal peritoneum, the pain becomes somatic and moves to the organ's actual position, where it is sharp and accompanied by tenderness.
:::

::: proof
The visceral afferents from the appendix travel with sympathetic fibres to the T10 segment of the spinal cord, which also receives input from the skin around the umbilicus; the brain therefore interprets the signal as periumbilical. As the appendix becomes more inflamed, the inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum of the right iliac fossa, supplied by somatic nerves from the local abdominal wall segments, so the pain is now felt precisely over the appendix, and pressure or movement of the peritoneum there hurts. The same logic explains why cholecystitis starts with epigastric pain and moves to the right upper quadrant, and why **referred pain** occurs: irritation of the diaphragm (by blood or pus) is felt at the shoulder tip, because the phrenic nerve (C3–C5) shares segments with the skin of the shoulder. The anatomy is covered in [[anatomy/abdomen]].
:::

## Assessment

::: definition Assessment of the acute abdomen {#def-assessment}
Assessment of a patient with acute abdominal pain has four parts, done together:

1. **Physiology first:** check airway, breathing and circulation and look for sepsis or shock (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, urine output, lactate). Resuscitate before investigating further.
2. **History:** site, onset (sudden, gradual), character (constant or colicky), radiation, severity and timing of the pain; vomiting, bowel habit and flatus; urinary and gynaecological symptoms; previous operations; medicines (especially anticoagulants, NSAIDs, steroids and immunosuppressants); and comorbidities.
3. **Examination:** general appearance (lying still suggests peritonitis; writhing suggests colic), signs of shock, jaundice, abdominal distension, scars, hernial orifices (always examine the groins), tenderness, guarding, rigidity and bowel sounds; and digital rectal examination where relevant.
4. **Investigations** chosen to answer specific questions (see below), including a **pregnancy test in anyone who could be pregnant**.
:::

**Peritonitis** is inflammation of the peritoneum. **Localised peritonitis** causes tenderness and guarding over one area. **Generalised peritonitis**, from widespread contamination (for example a perforated ulcer), causes a rigid, board-like abdomen, a patient lying very still, pain on any movement and often shock. Generalised peritonitis usually needs urgent surgery.

### Investigations

- **Blood tests:** full blood count, urea and electrolytes, liver function tests, **amylase or lipase**, C-reactive protein, a venous or arterial blood gas with **lactate**, clotting, group and save, and blood cultures if septic. Note that a normal white cell count or CRP does not exclude serious disease, especially early or in older and immunosuppressed patients.
- **Urine:** dipstick and a **pregnancy test** (urine or serum hCG).
- **Erect chest X-ray:** free gas under the diaphragm suggests a perforated viscus. It is quick but less sensitive than CT.
- **Ultrasound:** the first test for suspected gallbladder disease, and for suspected appendicitis in children and pregnant women; also for gynaecological causes.
- **CT of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast:** the most useful single test in adults with an uncertain diagnosis. It shows free gas and fluid, obstruction and its cause, inflammation, collections and many vascular emergencies. **CT angiography** is used when mesenteric ischaemia or aneurysm rupture is suspected.

## The four surgical processes

::: definition Inflammation, obstruction, perforation and ischaemia {#def-conditions}
Most surgical causes of acute abdominal pain fall into four categories, which may overlap:

- **Inflammation** of an organ: appendicitis, cholecystitis, diverticulitis, pancreatitis. Pain is constant, gradually worsening and localises as the parietal peritoneum becomes involved; fever and raised inflammatory markers are common.
- **Obstruction** of a tube: small or large bowel obstruction, biliary colic, ureteric colic. Pain is **colicky** (waves as smooth muscle contracts against the blockage), with vomiting and distension in bowel obstruction.
- **Perforation** of a hollow organ: perforated peptic ulcer, perforated diverticulum, perforated appendix. Pain is sudden and severe, and generalised peritonitis follows.
- **Ischaemia** of an organ: acute mesenteric ischaemia, strangulated hernia, volvulus. Pain is severe and constant, often **out of proportion** to the examination findings early on, with a rising lactate.

A patient may move from one category to another: an obstructed loop may become ischaemic and then perforate.
:::

## Common conditions

### Appendicitis

Appendicitis is the commonest surgical emergency, most frequent in the second and third decades. Obstruction of the appendix lumen (by a faecolith or lymphoid hyperplasia) leads to distension, bacterial overgrowth, inflammation and, if untreated, gangrene and perforation.

The classical history is **periumbilical pain moving to the right iliac fossa**, with anorexia, nausea and a low-grade fever. On examination there is tenderness and guarding at **McBurney's point** (two-thirds of the way from the umbilicus to the anterior superior iliac spine), pain in the right iliac fossa when the left is pressed (Rovsing's sign) and sometimes pain on extending the right hip (psoas sign, suggesting a retrocaecal appendix). Many patients do not have the classical picture: a pelvic appendix can cause diarrhoea, urinary symptoms and little abdominal tenderness, and presentations in young children, older adults and pregnancy are often atypical.

Clinical scores help to estimate the probability. The **Alvarado score** gives points for migration of pain (1), anorexia (1), nausea or vomiting (1), right iliac fossa tenderness (2), rebound tenderness (1), temperature of 37.3 °C or more (1), leucocytosis (2) and neutrophilia ("left shift", 1), for a maximum of 10. Low scores make appendicitis unlikely; high scores make it likely. The Appendicitis Inflammatory Response (AIR) score and the Adult Appendicitis Score perform similarly or better. Scores guide whether to image, observe or operate; they do not replace judgement.

Treatment is usually **laparoscopic appendicectomy** with antibiotics. In selected patients with uncomplicated appendicitis on CT, **antibiotics alone** are an option: in the American CODA trial (2020), antibiotics were not inferior to appendicectomy for general health status at 30 days, but about 3 in 10 patients treated with antibiotics had an appendicectomy within 90 days, more so if an **appendicolith** was present. The choice is discussed with the patient.

::: example An Alvarado score {#ex-alvarado}
A 19-year-old man has had pain for 18 hours, starting around the umbilicus and now in the right iliac fossa. He has no appetite and has vomited once. He is tender in the right iliac fossa with rebound tenderness. His temperature is 37.8 °C, white cell count $13.5 \times 10^9$/L with a neutrophilia. Calculate his Alvarado score and outline the next steps.
::: solution
**Score:** migration (1) + anorexia (1) + vomiting (1) + RIF tenderness (2) + rebound (1) + temperature ≥ 37.3 °C (1) + leucocytosis (2) + left shift (1) = **10**.

**Interpretation:** a score of 9–10 means appendicitis is very likely. In a young man with this picture, many surgeons proceed to laparoscopy without CT; others obtain CT to confirm the diagnosis and look for complications, particularly if antibiotic treatment is being considered.

**Next steps:** analgesia (pain relief does not mask signs), intravenous fluids, antibiotics according to local guidance once the decision is made, keep nil by mouth, consent, and arrange laparoscopic appendicectomy. Discuss the option of antibiotic treatment, its success rate and the risk of recurrence.
:::
:::

### Bowel obstruction

**Small bowel obstruction** is most often caused by **adhesions** from previous surgery, followed by **hernias** (always examine the groins and scars) and less commonly by tumours, Crohn's strictures and gallstone ileus. It causes colicky central pain, early and frequent vomiting (bilious, later faeculent), distension and absolute constipation (no stool or flatus), which may come later in high obstructions.

**Large bowel obstruction** is most often caused by **colorectal cancer**, followed by **volvulus** (twisting of the sigmoid colon or caecum on its mesentery) and diverticular strictures. It causes gradual distension, constipation, and vomiting later. If the ileocaecal valve is competent, the colon becomes a **closed loop** between the valve and the obstruction, and the caecum may distend until it perforates.

The most important question in obstruction is whether the bowel is **strangulated**: its blood supply cut off, as in a tight hernia, a closed loop or a volvulus. Signs suggesting strangulation are constant (rather than colicky) pain, tenderness, fever, tachycardia, peritonism, a raised lactate and CT features of ischaemia. Strangulation requires urgent surgery.

Adhesional small bowel obstruction without strangulation is usually managed non-operatively at first ("**drip and suck**"): intravenous fluids with electrolyte replacement, a nasogastric tube to decompress the stomach, analgesia, and monitoring. A **water-soluble contrast** agent (for example Gastrografin) given through the tube is both diagnostic and possibly therapeutic: if it reaches the colon on an X-ray within about 24 hours, the obstruction is very likely to resolve without surgery. Surgery is needed if the obstruction does not resolve or if strangulation develops.

Sigmoid volvulus is usually decompressed with a flexible sigmoidoscopy and a flatus tube, followed by consideration of elective resection because it recurs. Caecal volvulus usually needs surgery. Obstructing colorectal cancer may be treated by resection, a defunctioning stoma or sometimes a colonic stent as a bridge to surgery.

### Perforation

**Perforated peptic ulcer** causes sudden severe epigastric pain that becomes generalised, with a rigid abdomen. NSAIDs, *Helicobacter pylori*, smoking and steroids are risk factors. An erect chest X-ray or CT shows free gas. Treatment is resuscitation, antibiotics, a proton pump inhibitor and usually laparoscopic or open repair with an omental patch. Some sealed perforations in unfit patients are treated non-operatively.

### Acute mesenteric ischaemia

Acute mesenteric ischaemia is caused by **embolism** to the superior mesenteric artery (often from atrial fibrillation), **thrombosis** of an atherosclerotic artery, **venous thrombosis**, or **non-occlusive** ischaemia in low-flow states. The classical feature is severe pain with surprisingly few abdominal signs. Lactate may be normal early. The diagnosis is made with **CT angiography**, and delay is lethal because infarcted bowel perforates and causes overwhelming sepsis. Treatment combines anticoagulation, revascularisation (endovascular or open) and resection of dead bowel, often with a planned second-look operation.

::: example Pain out of proportion in an older woman {#ex-ami}
A 78-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation, who stopped her anticoagulant a month ago because of nosebleeds, presents with 4 hours of severe, constant central abdominal pain. She is distressed and has vomited. Her abdomen is soft and only mildly tender. Heart rate 110/min, irregular; blood pressure 140/80 mmHg. Lactate 3.8 mmol/L. What should the team suspect and do?
::: solution
**Suspicion:** acute mesenteric ischaemia from an **embolus** to the superior mesenteric artery. The key features are severe pain with few signs (pain out of proportion), atrial fibrillation without anticoagulation, and a raised lactate.

**Actions:** resuscitate with oxygen, intravenous fluids and analgesia; start anticoagulation with heparin unless contraindicated; give broad-spectrum antibiotics; request an urgent **CT angiogram** of the abdomen; and involve the surgical, vascular and interventional radiology teams immediately. If the CT confirms an occlusion, she needs revascularisation (embolectomy or endovascular treatment) and laparotomy to assess and resect any dead bowel.

**The trap:** a soft abdomen is falsely reassuring. Waiting for peritonitis to develop means waiting for the bowel to die. A normal lactate early on would not have excluded the diagnosis either.
:::
:::

### Biliary emergencies

Gallstones cause a spectrum of conditions ([[gastrointestinal/pancreas-biliary]]):

- **Biliary colic:** a stone temporarily blocks the cystic duct, causing right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, typically after meals, lasting minutes to hours and then settling. No fever, normal inflammatory markers.
- **Acute cholecystitis:** persistent obstruction causes inflammation of the gallbladder. Pain lasts longer, with fever, raised inflammatory markers and **Murphy's sign** (pain and arrested inspiration when the right upper quadrant is palpated). Ultrasound shows a thick-walled gallbladder with stones and fluid around it. Treatment is antibiotics and, as NICE recommends, **laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 1 week of diagnosis** where possible.
- **Acute cholangitis:** infection of an obstructed common bile duct. **Charcot's triad**: fever (often with rigors), jaundice and right upper quadrant pain. Adding confusion and hypotension gives **Reynolds' pentad**, indicating severe sepsis. Treatment is resuscitation, antibiotics and urgent drainage of the bile duct, usually by **ERCP**. The Tokyo Guidelines (2018) define severity.
- **Gallstone pancreatitis**, below.

### Acute pancreatitis

::: definition Acute pancreatitis (revised Atlanta classification) {#def-pancreatitis}
**Acute pancreatitis** is diagnosed when two of three criteria are met: (1) typical abdominal pain (acute, severe, epigastric, often radiating to the back); (2) serum lipase or amylase at least **three times** the upper limit of normal; (3) characteristic findings on imaging. Severity is graded by the revised Atlanta classification (2012):

- **mild:** no organ failure and no local or systemic complications;
- **moderately severe:** transient organ failure (resolving within 48 hours) or local complications;
- **severe:** **persistent organ failure** lasting more than 48 hours.
:::

The commonest causes in the UK are **gallstones** and **alcohol**. Others include hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercalcaemia, drugs, ERCP and trauma. Scoring systems such as the modified Glasgow (Imrie) score, which gives a point each for PaO₂ below 8 kPa, age over 55, white cell count above 15, calcium below 2 mmol/L, urea above 16 mmol/L, raised LDH or AST, albumin below 32 g/L and glucose above 10 mmol/L in the first 48 hours, identify patients likely to have severe disease; a score of 3 or more predicts severe pancreatitis. Treatment is supportive: careful fluid resuscitation (avoiding excessive fluids), analgesia, early enteral feeding as tolerated, and organ support. Antibiotics are not given routinely, only for proven or strongly suspected infection. Gallstone pancreatitis is followed by cholecystectomy, ideally in the same admission for mild disease, to prevent recurrence; urgent ERCP is reserved for coexisting cholangitis or persistent bile duct obstruction. Complications include fluid collections, necrosis (which may become infected) and pseudocysts.

### Diverticulitis

**Diverticular disease** is common in older adults in Western countries, mostly in the sigmoid colon. **Diverticulitis** (inflammation of a diverticulum) causes left iliac fossa pain, fever and raised inflammatory markers. Complicated diverticulitis includes abscess, perforation, fistula (to the bladder or vagina) and stricture. The **Hinchey classification** grades perforated disease: I, pericolic abscess; II, pelvic or distant abscess; III, purulent peritonitis; IV, faecal peritonitis.

NICE guideline NG147 (2019) recommends CT for suspected complicated diverticulitis, and suggests that people who are systemically well with uncomplicated diverticulitis may be managed without antibiotics, with simple analgesia and review. Abscesses are drained radiologically when large enough. Purulent or faecal peritonitis needs surgery, often a **Hartmann's procedure** (resection of the sigmoid colon with an end colostomy), or resection with anastomosis in selected patients.

## Mimics: medical, gynaecological and vascular

Many conditions outside the gut cause abdominal pain, and some are emergencies in their own right. A careful history and a few tests catch them.

- **Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm:** an older patient (often a man who smokes) with abdominal, back or flank pain and collapse. It can be mistaken for renal colic. See [[surgery/vascular-urological-surgery]].
- **Gynaecological:** **ectopic pregnancy** (always perform a pregnancy test), ovarian torsion, ruptured ovarian cyst, pelvic inflammatory disease ([[obstetrics-gynaecology/early-pregnancy]]).
- **Urological:** renal colic, pyelonephritis, urinary retention, testicular torsion (presenting with lower abdominal pain in boys).
- **Medical:** myocardial infarction (epigastric pain), lower lobe pneumonia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hypercalcaemia, sickle cell crisis, Addisonian crisis, herpes zoster before the rash, and acute porphyria.

::: example Right iliac fossa pain in a young woman {#ex-pregnancy}
A 24-year-old woman presents with 12 hours of right iliac fossa pain. Her last period was 7 weeks ago and she has had some spotting. Heart rate 104/min, blood pressure 108/70 mmHg. She is tender in the right iliac fossa. What test must be done first, and why?
::: solution
**First test:** a **pregnancy test** (urine hCG, followed by serum hCG). A positive result in a woman with pain, a delayed period and vaginal spotting means **ectopic pregnancy** until proven otherwise. Her mild tachycardia could be early haemorrhage from a rupturing ectopic.

**If positive:** involve the gynaecology team urgently, gain intravenous access, send group and save or crossmatch, and arrange transvaginal ultrasound. A ruptured ectopic pregnancy is a surgical emergency.

**If negative:** the differential includes appendicitis, ovarian cyst accident or torsion, pelvic inflammatory disease and urinary tract infection. Ultrasound of the pelvis and appendix is a sensible first image in a young woman.

**General rule:** a pregnancy test is mandatory for anyone who could be pregnant who presents with abdominal pain, before imaging involving radiation and before surgery.
:::
:::

## Diagnostic tests and probability

An imaging test does not make a diagnosis certain; it changes its probability. The same ultrasound result means something different in a child whose clinical score makes appendicitis likely than in one whose score makes it unlikely. ::: warning
**Do not withhold analgesia until a surgeon has examined the patient.** Randomised trials and systematic reviews have shown that giving opioid analgesia to patients with acute abdominal pain does not reduce the accuracy of diagnosis or delay treatment. Pain relief is humane and makes examination easier. Equally, do not be falsely reassured by a soft abdomen in an older, immunosuppressed or steroid-treated patient, or by a normal white count or CRP early in the illness.
:::

## Emergency laparotomy

Emergency laparotomy carries a much higher risk than most elective surgery: in the UK, mortality has been around 9% in recent reports of the **National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA)**, and much higher in older and frailer patients. NELA has driven a set of standards:

- assess and document the **risk of death** before surgery, using a validated tool such as the NELA risk calculator;
- give **antibiotics within 1 hour** to patients with sepsis;
- **CT reported** before surgery where the patient is stable enough, with consultant review of the images where possible;
- arrival in theatre within a time appropriate to the urgency;
- **consultant surgeon and consultant anaesthetist present** in theatre for high-risk patients;
- **postoperative critical care** for high-risk patients;
- assessment by a geriatrician for older patients, especially those who are frail.

::: example The older patient who "just has constipation" {#ex-elderly}
An 84-year-old woman with dementia is sent from her care home with 3 days of constipation and vomiting. Her abdomen is distended and mildly tender. Heart rate 112/min, blood pressure 102/60 mmHg, temperature 37.9 °C. Lactate 3.1 mmol/L, creatinine 168 µmol/L (baseline 85). She has a small, tender, irreducible lump in the right groin below and lateral to the pubic tubercle. What is the likely diagnosis and plan?
::: solution
**Likely diagnosis:** small bowel obstruction from an incarcerated **femoral hernia**, probably strangulated. Femoral hernias are common in older women, lie below and lateral to the pubic tubercle and have a narrow, rigid neck, so they strangulate easily. Tachycardia, fever, tenderness and a raised lactate suggest ischaemic bowel. "Constipation" in this context is absolute constipation from obstruction.

**Plan:** resuscitate (intravenous fluids, catheter, nasogastric tube, analgesia, antibiotics); treat her acute kidney injury; calculate her risk with a tool such as NELA; involve the senior surgeon, anaesthetist and a geriatrician; and discuss with her family or attorney the risks and benefits of surgery, which in this case is likely to be the best option, because a strangulated hernia will kill her without an operation and the repair can often be done through a groin incision. CT is helpful if there is doubt but must not delay surgery for a clear strangulated hernia.

**Lesson:** always examine the hernial orifices, especially in older women with obstruction; femoral hernias are small and easily missed.
:::
:::

::: history
The appendix was long blamed on the caecum: inflammation in the right iliac fossa was called "perityphlitis". In 1886 the Boston pathologist Reginald Fitz analysed several hundred cases and showed that the appendix was usually the source, coined the term "appendicitis" and argued for early removal. In 1889 Charles McBurney described the point of maximum tenderness that bears his name, and in 1894 a muscle-splitting incision. Appendicectomy became widely known after Frederick Treves drained an appendix abscess in King Edward VII in 1902, two days before his planned coronation. Laparoscopic appendicectomy was first performed by the German gynaecologist Kurt Semm in 1980.
:::

::: summary
- Visceral pain is poorly localised to the midline by embryological origin (foregut epigastric, midgut periumbilical, hindgut suprapubic); somatic pain is sharp and localised. Pain **migrates** when inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum.
- Assess physiology first and resuscitate; take a full history; examine the hernial orifices; test for pregnancy in anyone who could be pregnant.
- The four processes are **inflammation, obstruction, perforation and ischaemia**; they may overlap.
- Appendicitis: migratory pain, RIF tenderness; scores such as Alvarado guide imaging; laparoscopic appendicectomy or, in selected cases, antibiotics.
- Bowel obstruction: adhesions and hernias in the small bowel, cancer and volvulus in the large bowel; look for **strangulation**.
- Mesenteric ischaemia: pain out of proportion, AF, raised lactate; CT angiography without delay.
- Biliary: colic, cholecystitis (cholecystectomy within a week), cholangitis (Charcot's triad, urgent drainage).
- Pancreatitis: 2 of 3 criteria; severity by persistent organ failure; supportive care.
- Analgesia does not impair diagnosis. NELA standards (risk assessment, early antibiotics, CT, consultant presence, critical care) improve outcomes.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Where is the pain felt? {level=1}
State where early visceral pain is typically felt for: (a) acute cholecystitis; (b) appendicitis; (c) sigmoid diverticulitis; (d) small bowel obstruction. Explain the principle.
::: solution
(a) Epigastrium (foregut). (b) Periumbilical (midgut). (c) Suprapubic or lower abdomen (hindgut), though diverticulitis often presents with left iliac fossa pain once the parietal peritoneum is involved. (d) Periumbilical, colicky (midgut). Visceral afferents enter the spinal cord at levels determined by the embryological origin of the organ, and the brain refers the pain to the skin of the same segments, in the midline because the gut develops as a midline structure.
:::
:::

::: exercise Adding the score {level=1 check="6"}
A 30-year-old woman has right iliac fossa tenderness, nausea, a temperature of 37.5 °C and a white cell count of $12 \times 10^9$/L without a neutrophilia. Her pain did not migrate, she has her usual appetite and there is no rebound tenderness. Calculate her Alvarado score.
::: solution
RIF tenderness (2) + nausea (1) + temperature ≥ 37.3 °C (1) + leucocytosis (2) = **6**. Migration, anorexia, rebound and left shift score 0. A score of 5–6 is intermediate: appendicitis is possible, and imaging (ultrasound first in a young woman, as gynaecological causes are also possible) and a pregnancy test are indicated.
:::
:::

::: exercise Charcot or not? {level=1}
A 70-year-old man has right upper quadrant pain, a temperature of 39 °C with rigors, and yellow sclerae. His blood pressure is 85/50 mmHg and he is confused. Name the diagnosis, the eponymous signs and the definitive treatment.
::: solution
**Acute cholangitis** with severe sepsis: fever, jaundice and right upper quadrant pain form **Charcot's triad**; adding hypotension and confusion gives **Reynolds' pentad**. Management is resuscitation, blood cultures and intravenous antibiotics, followed by **urgent biliary drainage**, usually by ERCP with stone extraction or stenting, or percutaneous drainage if ERCP is not possible.
:::

::: exercise Predictive value of a scan {level=2 check="round(100*0.3*0.85/(0.3*0.85+0.7*0.1))"}
In a group of children with suspected appendicitis, the pre-test probability is 30%. An ultrasound has sensitivity 85% and specificity 90%. What is the probability (as a percentage, rounded to the nearest whole number) that a child with a positive scan has appendicitis?
::: hint
Imagine 1000 children: how many have appendicitis and test positive, and how many do not have it but test positive?
:::
::: solution
Of 1000 children, 300 have appendicitis, of whom $0.85 \times 300 = 255$ test positive. Of the 700 without appendicitis, $0.10 \times 700 = 70$ test positive. Positive predictive value: $255/(255 + 70) = 255/325 \approx 0.785$, which rounds to **78%** (to the nearest whole number, 78). A negative scan leaves $45/(45 + 630) \approx 6.7\%$ probability, so a child with persisting symptoms still needs review.
:::
:::

::: exercise Severity of pancreatitis {level=2 check="4"}
A 64-year-old woman with gallstone pancreatitis has, at 48 hours: PaO₂ 7.6 kPa, white cell count $17 \times 10^9$/L, calcium 2.15 mmol/L, urea 12 mmol/L, AST 150 U/L, LDH 450 U/L, albumin 30 g/L, glucose 8 mmol/L. What is her modified Glasgow score? Is severe pancreatitis predicted?
::: solution
PaO₂ < 8 kPa (1); age > 55 (1); white cell count > 15 (1); calcium < 2 (0); urea > 16 (0); LDH > 600 or AST > 200 (0); albumin < 32 (1); glucose > 10 (0). Total **4**. A score of 3 or more predicts severe pancreatitis, so she should be managed in a high-dependency setting with careful monitoring for organ failure. Whether her disease is actually severe is decided by the revised Atlanta criteria: persistent organ failure over 48 hours.
:::
:::

::: exercise Obstruction on the ward {level=2}
A 58-year-old man who had a laparotomy 10 years ago presents with 24 hours of colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, distension and no flatus. His groins are normal. He is not tender and his lactate is 1.4 mmol/L. CT shows small bowel obstruction with a transition point and no signs of ischaemia. Outline the management.
::: solution
This is probably **adhesional small bowel obstruction** without strangulation. Management: intravenous fluids with electrolyte replacement (vomiting causes hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis); a nasogastric tube on free drainage; analgesia and antiemetics; fluid balance monitoring with a catheter if needed; VTE prophylaxis; and **water-soluble contrast** through the nasogastric tube with an abdominal X-ray after several hours to see whether contrast reaches the colon (predicting resolution). Review regularly: development of constant pain, tenderness, fever, tachycardia or rising lactate suggests strangulation and needs surgery. Failure to resolve after a few days (local practice varies, often around 72 hours) is also an indication for surgery.
:::
:::

::: exercise Collapse with "renal colic" {level=3}
A 71-year-old man who smokes presents with sudden left flank pain radiating to the groin. He felt faint at home. Heart rate 105/min, blood pressure 98/60 mmHg. His urine dipstick shows a trace of blood. The admitting doctor plans analgesia and a CT KUB for renal colic. What else must be considered, and how should the assessment change?
::: solution
**Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm** must be considered first. A first presentation of "renal colic" in a man over 60, especially a smoker, with faintness, tachycardia and relative hypotension, is a ruptured AAA until proven otherwise. Microscopic haematuria does not distinguish them. **Change in approach:** examine the abdomen for a pulsatile, expansile mass; obtain large-bore access, send crossmatch; perform bedside ultrasound of the aorta if a trained operator is available; call the vascular team immediately. Use **permissive hypotension** (avoid driving blood pressure up with large fluid volumes). If he is stable enough, CT angiography confirms the diagnosis and shows suitability for endovascular repair. The pain of renal colic is not usually associated with hypotension; shock with flank pain should always prompt this thought.
:::

::: exercise A risk conversation {level=3}
An 81-year-old man with heart failure and chronic kidney disease has a perforated sigmoid diverticulum with faecal peritonitis. His NELA-predicted mortality is 32%. He has capacity. Using the NELA standards, outline how his care should be organised and how the decision should be discussed with him.
::: solution
**Organisation:** resuscitate and give antibiotics within an hour; obtain CT (already done) and consultant review; calculate and document the risk; because the risk is high, the **consultant surgeon and consultant anaesthetist** should be present in theatre and a **critical care bed** arranged; involve a geriatrician for perioperative care; aim to reach theatre quickly, as faecal peritonitis is time-critical.

**Discussion:** explain clearly what is happening, that without surgery faecal peritonitis is very likely to be fatal, and that surgery itself carries about a one-in-three risk of death and a high chance of complications, a stoma (likely a Hartmann's procedure), a stay in intensive care and a long recovery with a possible loss of independence. Ask what matters most to him. Discuss alternatives, including treatment focused on comfort. Respect his decision, whichever option he chooses, and document the discussion, his understanding and his decision. If he declines surgery, ensure good palliative care rather than withdrawal of care.
:::
