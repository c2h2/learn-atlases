Injury is a leading cause of death in children and young adults worldwide, and of years of life lost. Many trauma deaths happen within minutes of injury, from injuries nobody could survive. Many others happen in the first hours, from bleeding, airway obstruction or brain compression, and these are the deaths that a well-organised trauma system can prevent.

This chapter describes how injured patients are assessed and resuscitated, how major bleeding is controlled, and how the common life-threatening injuries of the head, chest, abdomen, pelvis and spine are recognised. It ends with burns and with the special features of injury in children, older adults and pregnancy. Guidance followed is UK-first (NICE guidelines NG39 on major trauma, NG40 on major trauma services, NG41 on spinal injury and NG232 on head injury) with the American College of Surgeons' Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) course, now in its 11th edition (2025), as of 2026. Doses are given only as principles; management follows local trauma protocols.

## Trauma systems

Badly injured patients do better when they are taken to a hospital that can treat all their injuries at once. In England, since 2012, regional **trauma networks** link **major trauma centres** (with 24-hour consultant-led trauma teams, interventional radiology, neurosurgery and other specialties on site) to **trauma units** in other hospitals. Ambulance crews use triage tools to take the most seriously injured patients directly to the major trauma centre, even if it is further away. National audit by the Trauma Audit and Research Network (TARN) followed the introduction of networks and reported improving survival.

Within the hospital, a **trauma team** with a designated leader receives the patient. The team is alerted before arrival using a structured pre-alert from the ambulance crew, for example in the **ATMIST** format: Age, Time of injury, Mechanism, Injuries found, Signs (vital signs) and Treatment given. Roles are allocated in advance, so that assessment and treatment happen in parallel rather than one after another.

## The primary survey

::: definition The primary survey {#def-survey}
The **primary survey** is a rapid, structured assessment that finds and treats immediately life-threatening problems in the order in which they kill. In current UK and ATLS practice it is written **<C>ABCDE** or **xABCDE**:

- **<C> or x — catastrophic (exsanguinating) external haemorrhage:** stop it immediately with direct pressure, haemostatic dressings or a tourniquet.
- **A — airway with cervical spine protection:** look for obstruction; open and secure the airway; restrict cervical spine movement in patients at risk of spinal injury.
- **B — breathing:** look for tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive haemothorax and flail chest; give oxygen.
- **C — circulation with haemorrhage control:** look for shock and its source; obtain vascular access; take blood; start blood-based resuscitation.
- **D — disability:** level of consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale), pupils, blood glucose, limb movement.
- **E — exposure and environment:** remove clothing to examine the whole body (including the back, with a log roll or minimal-movement assessment), then prevent hypothermia.

Each problem is treated as it is found, before moving on. The patient is reassessed whenever their condition changes.
:::

After the primary survey and initial resuscitation comes the **secondary survey**: a head-to-toe examination and full history, often summarised as **AMPLE** (Allergies, Medicines, Past history and pregnancy, Last meal, Events and environment). It looks for all remaining injuries and may be delayed until after life-saving surgery. A **tertiary survey**, repeated within 24 hours, finds injuries that were missed when attention was elsewhere, such as small fractures of the hand or foot.

Imaging is part of the assessment. NICE NG39 recommends **whole-body CT** (from the vertex to mid-thigh) for adults with blunt major trauma and suspected multiple injuries. A chest X-ray and a pelvic X-ray may be done in the resuscitation room. **FAST** (focused assessment with sonography for trauma) looks for free fluid around the heart and in the abdomen; it is useful in an unstable patient to identify a source of bleeding, but a negative scan does not exclude injury.

## Haemorrhage and shock

Bleeding is the leading cause of preventable death after injury. **Shock** is inadequate tissue perfusion; after trauma it is assumed to be **haemorrhagic** until proven otherwise, although tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade and spinal cord injury (neurogenic shock) can also cause it ([[emergency-critical-care/shock-fluids]]).

The normal adult blood volume is about **70 mL/kg** (about 80 mL/kg in children). The body compensates for blood loss by vasoconstriction and tachycardia, so blood pressure is maintained until a large volume has been lost. ATLS has long described four classes of haemorrhage:

| Class | Blood loss | Heart rate | Blood pressure | Other features |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | up to 15% | normal | normal | minimal |
| II | 15–30% | slightly raised | normal | narrowed pulse pressure, anxious |
| III | 30–40% | raised (often > 120/min) | falls | confused, reduced urine output |
| IV | > 40% | markedly raised | low | drowsy, minimal urine output |

The table is a teaching approximation. Real patients do not fit it neatly: young adults and athletes compensate well and then deteriorate suddenly; older patients and those taking beta-blockers may not become tachycardic; and pain or fear raises the heart rate on its own. A falling blood pressure in a trauma patient is a late sign. A **base deficit** or raised **lactate** on a blood gas often reveals hypoperfusion earlier.

::: example Estimating blood loss {#ex-blood-loss}
An 80 kg man has been stabbed in the abdomen. His heart rate is 128/min, blood pressure 92/60 mmHg, respiratory rate 28/min, and he is anxious and confused. Using the ATLS classes, roughly how much blood has he lost?
::: solution
**Blood volume:** $70\ \text{mL/kg} \times 80\ \text{kg} = 5600$ mL.

**Class:** tachycardia above 120/min, a falling blood pressure and confusion fit **class III**, a loss of about 30–40%.

**Volume lost:** $0.30 \times 5600 = 1680$ mL to $0.40 \times 5600 = 2240$ mL, so roughly 1.7–2.2 litres.

**What follows.** The estimate matters less than the conclusion: he has lost a large volume and is still bleeding, and he needs blood products and urgent surgical control of the source, not large volumes of crystalloid. The trauma team activates the major haemorrhage protocol and he goes to theatre.
:::
:::

### Damage control resuscitation

::: definition Damage control resuscitation and surgery {#def-damage-control}
**Damage control resuscitation** is the strategy for a patient with major traumatic haemorrhage. Its elements are:

- **rapid control of bleeding**, by surgery or interventional radiology (embolisation);
- **permissive hypotension** until bleeding is controlled: accepting a lower-than-normal blood pressure (for example a palpable radial pulse or a systolic pressure of about 80–90 mmHg) to avoid dislodging clots, except in traumatic brain injury, where adequate pressure is needed to perfuse the brain;
- **blood-based resuscitation**: red cells, plasma and platelets given early in a balanced ratio through a **major haemorrhage protocol**, with minimal crystalloid;
- **tranexamic acid** as early as possible and within 3 hours of injury;
- prevention and correction of **hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy**, and of low ionised calcium.

**Damage control surgery** is an abbreviated operation for a patient who is too physiologically deranged to survive a long definitive procedure: stop the bleeding, control contamination (for example by stapling off injured bowel), pack and temporarily close the abdomen, then correct physiology in intensive care and return to theatre for definitive repair 24–48 hours later.
:::

::: theorem The lethal triad {#thm-triad}
**Hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy** reinforce one another in a bleeding trauma patient, so that each makes the other two worse. Once established, the cycle can make bleeding uncontrollable even after the surgical source has been dealt with. Preventing all three from the start, rather than correcting them after they appear, is the basis of damage control.
:::

::: proof
Blood loss reduces oxygen delivery, so tissues switch to anaerobic metabolism and produce lactic acid. Exposure, cold fluids and an open body cavity cause hypothermia; shock reduces heat production. Clotting factors are enzymes, and both hypothermia and acidosis slow their activity and impair platelet function. Major injury also causes **acute traumatic coagulopathy**, driven by tissue damage and shock, before any fluid is given; diluting the blood with crystalloid makes it worse. Poor clotting causes more bleeding, which deepens shock, acidosis and cooling. Damage control breaks the cycle at every point: blood products instead of crystalloid, warmed fluids and active warming, early tranexamic acid, and short operations.
:::

The evidence base includes two large trials. **CRASH-2** (2010), in over 20,000 bleeding trauma patients, found that tranexamic acid reduced all-cause mortality (14.5% against 16.0%) and death from bleeding. Later analysis showed the benefit depended on early treatment: given within 3 hours of injury it helped, and after 3 hours it appeared harmful. **PROPPR** (2015) compared plasma, platelets and red cells in a 1:1:1 ratio against 1:1:2 in 680 patients. It found no significant difference in mortality at 24 hours or 30 days, but more patients in the 1:1:1 group achieved haemostasis and fewer died from exsanguination in the first 24 hours. These findings support early, balanced blood product resuscitation ([[blood/transfusion]]).

::: warning
**Two litres of saline is not a resuscitation strategy for haemorrhage.** Large volumes of crystalloid dilute clotting factors and platelets, cool the patient, worsen acidosis (0.9% saline adds chloride) and raise blood pressure enough to dislodge fragile clots. In a bleeding trauma patient, the priorities are to stop the bleeding and give blood products. Crystalloid is limited to small boluses while blood is being obtained.
:::

## Injuries of the chest

Chest injuries cause a quarter or more of trauma deaths, but most can be treated without a thoracotomy: chest drains and good analgesia manage the majority. Several are immediately life-threatening and must be found in the primary survey.

- **Tension pneumothorax:** air enters the pleural space through a one-way valve and cannot escape. Pressure rises, the lung collapses, the mediastinum is pushed to the other side and venous return to the heart falls. Signs are respiratory distress, hypoxia, tachycardia, hypotension, reduced breath sounds and hyper-resonance on the affected side, tracheal deviation away from it (a late sign) and distended neck veins (absent if the patient is hypovolaemic). It is a **clinical diagnosis**, treated immediately by decompression (needle or finger thoracostomy, usually in the 4th or 5th intercostal space just anterior to the mid-axillary line in adults) followed by a chest drain.
- **Open pneumothorax:** a chest wall wound through which air is sucked. It is covered with a vented dressing and a chest drain is placed.
- **Massive haemothorax:** a large amount of blood in the pleural space (for example more than 1500 mL on drain insertion, or ongoing loss of about 200 mL/h). It causes both hypovolaemia and respiratory compromise and often needs thoracotomy.
- **Flail chest:** a segment of chest wall fractured in two or more places on two or more adjacent ribs moves paradoxically. The underlying lung contusion is what causes hypoxia. Treatment is analgesia (often regional), oxygen and ventilatory support; surgical rib fixation is used in selected patients.
- **Cardiac tamponade:** blood in the pericardium restricts filling of the heart, usually after penetrating injury. Signs are hypotension, distended neck veins and muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad), though these are often absent. Ultrasound shows the effusion. Treatment is surgical: a **resuscitative thoracotomy** for a patient in or near cardiac arrest after penetrating chest injury.

Rib fractures in older people deserve special mention. A few broken ribs that a young adult tolerates can cause pneumonia, respiratory failure and death in an 80-year-old. These patients need early and effective analgesia, physiotherapy and close monitoring.

## Injuries of the abdomen and pelvis

Abdominal injury is caused by **blunt** trauma (road collisions, falls, assaults) or **penetrating** trauma (stab and gunshot wounds). In blunt trauma the solid organs, the **spleen and liver**, are most often injured and bleed. Hollow organs (bowel, bladder) are injured less often but may cause peritonitis that develops over hours.

Assessment depends on stability:

- **Unstable** patient with signs of abdominal bleeding (positive FAST or obvious peritonism): straight to theatre for **laparotomy**, or interventional radiology in selected cases.
- **Stable** patient: **CT with intravenous contrast** defines the injuries. Many solid organ injuries in stable patients are now managed **non-operatively**, with observation, serial examination and embolisation if there is active contrast extravasation.

A **pelvic ring fracture** can cause massive bleeding, mainly from veins and fractured bone surfaces in the pelvis. The pelvis should not be "sprung" (compressed by hand) to test for instability, because this can disturb clots. In a patient with a suspected pelvic fracture and shock, a **pelvic binder** is applied at the level of the greater trochanters to close and stabilise the ring. Bleeding is then controlled by angioembolisation, pre-peritoneal packing or external fixation. Urethral injury is suspected with blood at the meatus; a catheter is not forced, and urology advice is sought.

::: example A fall from height {#ex-fall}
A 28-year-old man falls 6 metres from scaffolding and lands on his side. On arrival he has a patent airway, respiratory rate 26/min, SpO₂ 94% on oxygen, heart rate 132/min, blood pressure 84/50 mmHg and GCS 14. His left lower ribs are tender, and his pelvis is painful. Paramedics have applied a pelvic binder. Describe the primary survey priorities and likely sources of bleeding.
::: solution
**<C>:** no catastrophic external bleeding.

**A:** patent; cervical spine movement is restricted because of the mechanism.

**B:** tachypnoeic with lower rib tenderness; look for pneumothorax and haemothorax (chest examination, ultrasound or chest X-ray) and decompress or drain if needed.

**C:** he is in shock (tachycardia, hypotension). Likely sources: **spleen** (left lower rib injury), **pelvis** (painful pelvis after a fall from height), chest and long bones. Activate the major haemorrhage protocol; give blood products via large-bore access; give tranexamic acid; check the binder is correctly placed over the trochanters. FAST may show free fluid in the abdomen.

**D:** GCS 14 may reflect shock or head injury; check pupils and glucose.

**E:** expose, examine the back with minimal movement, and keep him warm.

**Decision.** If he responds only transiently to blood, he needs immediate haemorrhage control: laparotomy for splenic bleeding if FAST is positive, and pelvic angioembolisation or packing if the pelvis is the source. A hybrid theatre allows both. If he stabilises, whole-body CT will define the injuries and guide treatment, which might include splenic embolisation.
:::
:::

## Head injury

Traumatic brain injury is the commonest cause of death after major trauma in young people. The **primary injury** happens at the moment of impact. **Secondary injury** follows over hours to days, from hypoxia, hypotension, raised intracranial pressure, seizures and hypo- or hyperglycaemia. Emergency care cannot reverse the primary injury; its purpose is to prevent secondary injury.

The **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** measures consciousness by the best response in three areas:

| Eye opening (E) | Verbal response (V) | Motor response (M) |
|---|---|---|
| 4 spontaneous | 5 orientated | 6 obeys commands |
| 3 to sound | 4 confused | 5 localises to pain |
| 2 to pressure | 3 words | 4 normal flexion (withdrawal) |
| 1 none | 2 sounds | 3 abnormal flexion |
| | 1 none | 2 extension |
| | | 1 none |

The total ranges from 3 to 15 and should always be reported with its components (for example E2 V3 M5 = 10). A GCS of 8 or less usually means the patient cannot protect the airway and needs intubation. Head injury is graded as **mild** (GCS 13–15), **moderate** (9–12) or **severe** (3–8).

NICE guideline NG232 (2023) sets out which patients need CT of the head. Indications for CT **within 1 hour** in adults include a GCS below 13 on initial assessment in the emergency department, a GCS below 15 two hours after the injury, suspected open or depressed skull fracture, signs of a basal skull fracture (haemotympanum, "panda eyes", cerebrospinal fluid leak from the ear or nose, Battle's sign), a post-traumatic seizure, a focal neurological deficit, and more than one episode of vomiting. People taking anticoagulants who have had a head injury, without these features, should have a CT within 8 hours.

An **extradural haematoma**, usually from a tear of the middle meningeal artery under a temporal bone fracture, classically causes a brief loss of consciousness followed by a **lucid interval** and then rapid deterioration. An **acute subdural haematoma**, from torn bridging veins, is more common, especially in older people and those on anticoagulants, and is often associated with underlying brain injury. Both may need urgent neurosurgical evacuation. Signs of raised intracranial pressure and herniation include a falling GCS, a unilateral dilated pupil and Cushing's response (hypertension with bradycardia).

## Spinal injury

Spinal injury must be suspected in any patient with a dangerous mechanism, neck or back pain, or neurological symptoms. Movement of the spine is restricted until injury has been excluded, but rigid collars are no longer applied routinely in every patient: current practice emphasises **manual in-line stabilisation**, careful handling and minimising time on hard boards, which cause pressure injuries. NICE NG41 and clinical decision rules such as the Canadian C-spine rule guide who needs imaging; CT is the first-line imaging for the cervical spine in adults, with MRI for neurological deficit.

**Neurogenic shock** follows cord injury above about T6: loss of sympathetic outflow causes hypotension with **bradycardia** and warm peripheries. It must be distinguished from haemorrhagic shock, which it can mask.

## Burns

A burn is assessed by its **extent**, as a percentage of total body surface area (%TBSA), and its **depth**. Superficial (epidermal) burns, which are red and painful without blisters, like sunburn, are **not** included in the %TBSA. The **rule of nines** gives a quick adult estimate: head 9%, each arm 9%, front of trunk 18%, back of trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%. The **Lund and Browder chart** is more accurate and allows for the larger head and smaller legs of children. The patient's palm with fingers is about 1% of their body surface area.

Depth determines healing. Superficial partial-thickness burns are pink, blistered and painful and heal within two weeks. Deep partial-thickness and full-thickness burns are paler or white, leathery and less painful (nerve endings are destroyed) and usually need excision and grafting.

Large burns cause massive fluid loss from leaky capillaries. In the UK, formal fluid resuscitation is usually started for burns of more than about 15% TBSA in adults and 10% in children. The traditional **Parkland formula** gives the crystalloid volume (usually Hartmann's solution) for the first 24 hours from the time of injury:

$$
V = 4\ \text{mL} \times \text{weight (kg)} \times \%\text{TBSA}
$$ {#eq-parkland}

Half is given in the first 8 hours **from the time of the burn** (not from arrival) and the rest over the next 16 hours. The formula is only a starting point. Many protocols, including ATLS, now start adults at 2 mL/kg/%TBSA to reduce over-resuscitation ("fluid creep"), which causes oedema, compartment syndromes and respiratory failure. The fluid rate is adjusted hourly to urine output, typically aiming for about 0.5 mL/kg/h in adults and 1 mL/kg/h in children.

Other priorities in burns include early recognition of **airway burns** (burns in an enclosed space, facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in the mouth, hoarseness or stridor), which may need early intubation before swelling makes it impossible; **carbon monoxide** and **cyanide** poisoning after smoke inhalation; **escharotomy** for circumferential full-thickness burns of the chest or limbs that restrict breathing or blood flow; analgesia; and referral to a specialist burns service.

::: example A burn fluid calculation {#ex-parkland}
A 70 kg woman sustains 30% TBSA deep partial-thickness and full-thickness burns in a house fire at 10:00. She arrives in the emergency department at 12:00. Using the traditional Parkland formula, how much fluid should she receive in the first 24 hours, and at what rate between 12:00 and 18:00?
::: solution
**Total:** $4 \times 70 \times 30 = 8400$ mL in the first 24 hours after the burn.

**First 8 hours (10:00 to 18:00):** half the total, 4200 mL.

**Already given:** suppose paramedics gave 500 mL before arrival. Then $4200 - 500 = 3700$ mL remains to be given between 12:00 and 18:00.

**Rate:** $3700\ \text{mL} \div 6\ \text{h} \approx 617$ mL/h.

**Then:** the remaining 4200 mL over the next 16 hours (about 263 mL/h).

**Adjust** the rate hourly to her urine output (target about $0.5 \times 70 = 35$ mL/h). Using the lower starting value of 2 mL/kg/%TBSA would give 4200 mL in 24 hours; whichever formula is used, the response is what matters. She also needs assessment for airway burns and carbon monoxide poisoning, given the house fire.
:::
:::

## Special groups

**Children** have a larger head relative to body, a more anterior and floppy airway, and greater physiological reserve: they maintain blood pressure until late, then deteriorate suddenly. Doses, fluid volumes and equipment sizes are based on weight. Injuries inconsistent with the history, or delays in presentation, should raise concern about **non-accidental injury**, and safeguarding procedures are followed ([[paediatrics/safeguarding]]).

**Older adults** are often seriously injured by low-energy mechanisms such as a fall from standing. They may not mount a tachycardia (beta-blockers, reduced reserve), their "normal" blood pressure may be relative hypotension, and anticoagulants increase bleeding, especially intracranial. Undertriage of older patients is a recognised problem; NICE and trauma networks now include age and anticoagulant use in triage.

In **pregnancy**, the physiology changes: blood volume rises, so signs of shock appear late, and the fetus may be compromised before the mother shows signs. After about 20 weeks, the uterus compresses the inferior vena cava when the woman lies flat, so she is positioned with **manual uterine displacement** to the left or a left lateral tilt. The best treatment for the fetus is effective resuscitation of the mother. Rhesus-negative women receive anti-D immunoglobulin after abdominal trauma. In maternal cardiac arrest after about 20 weeks, a **resuscitative hysterotomy** (perimortem Caesarean section) within about 5 minutes of arrest can improve maternal resuscitation.

::: quiz
A 75-year-old man on a beta-blocker and apixaban falls down stairs. His heart rate is 72/min and blood pressure 118/70 mmHg (his usual is 160/90). He is drowsy. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] His normal heart rate excludes significant haemorrhage
- [x] He may be in shock, and his anticoagulation raises the risk of intracranial bleeding
- [ ] He can be managed as a minor injury because the mechanism was low-energy
- [ ] Head CT is not needed if his GCS improves to 15
::: solution
Beta-blockers blunt the tachycardic response, and a blood pressure of 118 is well below his usual 160, so he may already be hypotensive for him. Falls in older adults cause major injuries, and anticoagulation increases the risk of intracranial haemorrhage. He needs full trauma assessment and head CT according to NICE NG232, and anticoagulant reversal if bleeding is found.
:::
:::

::: example A child with a femoral fracture {#ex-child}
A 2-year-old boy is brought in with a swollen thigh. His parents say he fell from a sofa yesterday. X-ray shows a spiral fracture of the femur. He also has bruises of different ages on his back and ears. How should this be approached?
::: solution
**Clinical care first:** analgesia, splinting and orthopaedic management of the fracture.

**Concerns:** femoral fractures in children who are not yet walking independently, delay in seeking help, bruising on the ears and back (areas not usually injured in falls), and bruises of different ages are all features that raise concern about **non-accidental injury**. A fall from a sofa would not usually cause this fracture in a toddler.

**Actions:** document the history and findings carefully and objectively; inform the senior doctor; follow the local safeguarding procedure, which includes discussion with the paediatric safeguarding team and children's social care; and arrange further investigations, usually including a skeletal survey and, depending on age and findings, brain imaging and eye examination. The aim is to protect the child while assessing the explanation fairly; the parents are told about the concerns and the process honestly.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1966 the US National Academy of Sciences' National Research Council published *Accidental Death and Disability: The Neglected Disease of Modern Society*, which showed that injured people were often treated by untrained staff in poorly equipped hospitals. It led to emergency medical services and trauma systems. In 1976 the orthopaedic surgeon James Styner crashed his light aircraft in rural Nebraska; his wife was killed and his children were badly injured, and the care they received in a small local hospital was disorganised. He said, "When I can provide better care in the field with limited resources than what my children and I received at the primary care facility, there is something wrong with the system." With colleagues he developed the first Advanced Trauma Life Support course in 1978, which the American College of Surgeons adopted in 1980 and which is now taught worldwide.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Order the primary survey {level=1}
A motorcyclist has a deformed thigh with pulsatile bleeding that is soaking through the dressings, noisy breathing with blood in the mouth, a respiratory rate of 30/min and a GCS of 11. In what order are the problems dealt with, and what is done for each?
::: solution
1. **<C> Catastrophic haemorrhage:** pulsatile bleeding from the thigh is controlled first, with direct pressure and a tourniquet if needed. 2. **Airway:** suction blood, use airway manoeuvres and adjuncts, while restricting cervical spine movement; a definitive airway may be needed, especially with GCS 11 and blood in the airway. 3. **Breathing:** assess the cause of tachypnoea (pneumothorax, haemothorax, contusion), give oxygen. 4. **Circulation:** assess for shock, gain access, give blood products, splint the femur to reduce bleeding. 5. **Disability:** GCS components, pupils, glucose. 6. **Exposure:** full examination and warming. In a team, several of these happen at the same time, but the leader keeps the order of priority.
:::
:::

After a head injury, a man opens his eyes only when his trapezius is squeezed, says inappropriate words, and moves his hand towards the painful stimulus to push it away. What is his total GCS?

Eyes open to pressure: E2. Inappropriate words: V3. Localises to pain: M5. Total $2 + 3 + 5 = 10$, reported as E2 V3 M5 = 10, a moderate head injury. He needs CT within 1 hour under NICE NG232 (GCS below 13 on initial assessment) and close observation of his airway.

A 70 kg man has 30% TBSA burns. Using 4 mL/kg/%TBSA, half the total volume is to be given in the first 8 hours after the burn. He arrives 2 hours after the burn and has received no fluid. At what rate in mL/h should fluid run for the next 6 hours to stay on schedule? Give the exact value.

The first-8-hour volume must now be given in the remaining 6 hours.

Total: $4 \times 70 \times 30 = 8400$ mL; first-8-hour volume: 4200 mL. Remaining time in the first 8 hours: 6 hours. Rate: $4200 \div 6 = 700$ mL/h. The rate is then adjusted to urine output (target about 35 mL/h for 70 kg).

::: exercise The shocked patient with a quiet chest {level=2}
A 40-year-old woman has a stab wound to the left chest at the 5th intercostal space just lateral to the sternum. Her heart rate is 130/min, blood pressure 75/50 mmHg, neck veins are distended and breath sounds are equal on both sides. What is the most likely diagnosis, how would you confirm it, and what is the treatment?
::: solution
**Most likely:** cardiac tamponade. A stab wound over the precordium with hypotension and distended neck veins, and equal breath sounds (making tension pneumothorax less likely), fits. **Confirm:** with bedside ultrasound (FAST, pericardial view), which shows pericardial fluid; do not delay treatment for formal imaging if she is deteriorating. **Treatment:** immediate surgical decompression and repair of the cardiac wound, by sternotomy in theatre if she is stable enough to get there, or a **resuscitative thoracotomy** in the emergency department if she arrests or is peri-arrest. Needle pericardiocentesis is only a temporising measure when surgery is not immediately available. Give blood while preparing.
:::

::: exercise Anticoagulated head injury {level=2}
An 82-year-old woman taking apixaban for atrial fibrillation hits her head in a fall. She has no loss of consciousness, no vomiting, GCS 15 and no focal signs. Does she need a CT head, and when? What would happen if it showed an acute subdural haematoma?
::: solution
Under NICE NG232, a person taking anticoagulants who has had a head injury, even without other indications, should have a CT head within 8 hours of the injury. If it showed an acute subdural haematoma, her anticoagulation would be reversed urgently (for apixaban, with a specific reversal agent such as andexanet alfa or with prothrombin complex concentrate, according to local protocol), and she would be discussed with the neurosurgical team. Treatment depends on the size of the haematoma, midline shift and her neurological state; her overall frailty and wishes are part of the decision. She also needs regular neurological observations.
:::

::: exercise Reading the pelvic X-ray {level=3}
A 35-year-old driver in a head-on collision has a heart rate of 140/min and blood pressure 70/40 mmHg after 2 units of red cells. Chest X-ray is normal, FAST shows no free fluid, and the pelvic X-ray shows wide diastasis of the pubic symphysis and widening of the right sacroiliac joint ("open-book" fracture). There is blood at the urethral meatus. Explain the source of shock and the management, including two things that should not be done.
::: solution
**Source:** the pelvic ring fracture. An open-book injury increases pelvic volume and tears the venous plexuses and fractured bone surfaces, and sometimes arteries. The normal chest X-ray and negative FAST make the chest and peritoneal cavity less likely sources (although FAST does not exclude retroperitoneal bleeding). **Management:** check the pelvic binder is applied correctly over the greater trochanters; continue the major haemorrhage protocol with balanced blood products and tranexamic acid; warm the patient; and arrange urgent haemorrhage control by **angioembolisation**, **pre-peritoneal packing** or **external fixation**, according to local resources. **Do not:** repeatedly spring the pelvis to test stability, which disturbs clots; or pass a urethral catheter blindly, because blood at the meatus suggests urethral injury — a urology opinion and retrograde urethrography or suprapubic catheter are needed.
:::

::: exercise A system that failed {level=3}
A 19-year-old pedestrian hit by a car at night is taken to the nearest small hospital without a CT scanner or on-site surgeons out of hours. He has a GCS of 9 and an unstable pelvic fracture. Transfer to the major trauma centre takes 3 hours to arrange, and he dies of haemorrhage during the transfer. Analyse what went wrong and how a trauma network should prevent this.
::: solution
**What went wrong:** the triage decision at the scene took him to a hospital that could not treat his injuries, so time was lost in two places: the time spent at the first hospital and the time to arrange transfer. In haemorrhage, the time to definitive control is critical. Possible contributing factors include a triage tool that was not applied, lack of a pre-alert, absence of a clear transfer protocol, and no mechanism to start blood products and tranexamic acid early.

**Prevention:** a regional network with a clear **pre-hospital triage tool** that sends patients with signs of major injury (low GCS, unstable pelvis, abnormal physiology) directly to the major trauma centre, accepting a longer journey; access to pre-hospital critical care teams who can give blood products and tranexamic acid; a **"no refusal" policy** at the major trauma centre and a single phone call for urgent transfer; and trauma units staffed and equipped to stabilise and transfer quickly if a patient does arrive. Audit of every death through TARN-style data and morbidity and mortality review identifies failures, and the case should be reported and reviewed openly.
