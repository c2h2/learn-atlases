General surgery is largely the surgery of the digestive tract and the abdominal wall. Its everyday work ranges from repairing hernias and removing gallbladders, among the commonest operations in any hospital, to major cancer resections that take a whole day and involve a team of specialists. This chapter covers the conditions that a junior doctor on a surgical ward will meet most often and the principles that link them: how a hernia forms and when it needs repair, the surgery of gallstones, the upper and lower gastrointestinal cancer pathways, inflammatory bowel disease, anorectal problems, stomas and anastomoses, and the surgery of obesity.

Emergency presentations of the same organs (appendicitis, obstruction, perforation, cholecystitis) are covered in [[surgery/acute-abdomen]], and the medical side of these diseases in [[gastrointestinal/oesophagus-stomach]], [[gastrointestinal/bowel-disease]] and [[gastrointestinal/gi-bleeding-cancer]]. Guidance followed is UK-first (NICE) as of 2026; treatment of individual patients follows local multidisciplinary protocols.

## Hernias

::: definition Hernia {#def-hernia}
A **hernia** is the protrusion of a viscus, or part of a viscus, through a defect in the wall of the cavity that normally contains it. An abdominal wall hernia has a **sac** (peritoneum), a **neck** (the defect) and **contents** (often omentum or small bowel). A hernia is:

- **reducible** if the contents can be pushed back into the abdomen;
- **irreducible** (incarcerated) if they cannot;
- **obstructed** if bowel within it is obstructed;
- **strangulated** if the blood supply to its contents is cut off, which leads to ischaemia, necrosis and perforation.
:::

The risk of strangulation depends mostly on the width of the neck: a **narrow, rigid neck** traps contents more easily. This is why femoral hernias, with their narrow ring, strangulate far more often than direct inguinal hernias, with their wide neck.

**Inguinal hernias** are the commonest type and are much more common in men. An **indirect** inguinal hernia passes through the deep inguinal ring, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, along a patent processus vaginalis, and may descend into the scrotum. A **direct** hernia pushes through the posterior wall of the inguinal canal (Hesselbach's triangle), medial to those vessels, through acquired weakness. Clinically, both appear above and medial to the pubic tubercle; the distinction is often made only at operation. The anatomy is explained in [[anatomy/abdomen]].

**Femoral hernias** pass through the femoral canal, below the inguinal ligament, and appear **below and lateral to the pubic tubercle**. They are more common in women (though inguinal hernias are still more common in women overall) and often present as an emergency with obstruction. Every femoral hernia should be repaired promptly.

Other hernias include **umbilical and paraumbilical** hernias, **epigastric** hernias in the linea alba, **incisional** hernias through surgical scars ([[surgery/wounds-surgical-infection]]), and **hiatus** hernias of the stomach through the diaphragm.

**Management.** Symptomatic inguinal hernias are usually repaired. In men with minimal symptoms, **watchful waiting** is a reasonable option, because the risk of strangulation is low, though many eventually choose surgery as symptoms increase. Repair uses **mesh** to reinforce the posterior wall of the canal without tension, either by an open approach (the **Lichtenstein** repair) or laparoscopically from behind (**TAPP**, transabdominal preperitoneal, or **TEP**, totally extraperitoneal). Laparoscopic repair is often preferred for bilateral and recurrent hernias. Chronic groin pain after repair, from nerve irritation, is the most important long-term complication to discuss when consenting.

## Gallstone disease

Gallstones are common: most are **cholesterol stones**, formed when bile is supersaturated with cholesterol, often in combination with gallbladder stasis. **Pigment stones** occur in haemolytic disease and infection. Most people with gallstones have no symptoms and need no treatment.

Symptomatic gallstones present as biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, cholangitis, obstructive jaundice from stones in the common bile duct (choledocholithiasis) or pancreatitis ([[surgery/acute-abdomen]]). The investigation of choice is **abdominal ultrasound**. If the bile duct is dilated or liver tests are obstructive, **MRCP** (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography) looks for duct stones, which are removed by **ERCP** before or at the time of surgery, or by bile duct exploration during the operation.

NICE guideline CG188 recommends offering laparoscopic cholecystectomy to people with symptomatic gallstones, and for acute cholecystitis, performing it within a week of diagnosis.

::: theorem The critical view of safety {#thm-cvs}
**Bile duct injury** is the most serious complication of cholecystectomy. It happens mainly when the common bile duct is mistaken for the cystic duct and divided. To prevent this, the surgeon should not clip or divide any structure until the **critical view of safety** has been achieved: (1) the hepatocystic triangle has been cleared of fat and fibrous tissue; (2) the lower third of the gallbladder has been separated from the liver bed; and (3) **two, and only two**, structures are seen entering the gallbladder: the cystic duct and the cystic artery.
:::

::: proof
Bile duct injuries arise from a misidentification error rather than a lack of technical skill: in an inflamed gallbladder, the common bile duct can be pulled up into a position where it looks like the cystic duct, and once a surgeon believes the wrong structure is the cystic duct, everything seen afterwards tends to be interpreted to fit that belief. The critical view of safety, described by Steven Strasberg in 1995, replaces recognition of a single tubular structure with a positive test that only the correct anatomy can pass: if exactly two structures enter the gallbladder and the gallbladder has been separated from its bed, neither can be the common bile duct. When the view cannot be achieved, because inflammation is too severe, the safe options are to convert, to perform a **subtotal cholecystectomy**, or to stop and drain, rather than continue dissecting in a dangerous area.
:::

::: example A difficult gallbladder {#ex-cvs}
During a laparoscopic cholecystectomy for acute cholecystitis 10 days after the onset of symptoms, the surgeon finds dense inflammation around the hepatocystic triangle. After 45 minutes the critical view of safety still cannot be achieved. What are the options, and which should not be taken?
::: solution
**Options:** (1) call for a second, senior opinion; (2) perform a **subtotal cholecystectomy**, removing most of the gallbladder and leaving or closing the part near the cystic duct, which avoids dissection in the dangerous area; (3) convert to an open operation if this would help, recognising that the same inflammation is present when open; (4) place a drain or a **cholecystostomy** tube and plan a later operation once inflammation settles.

**What not to do:** clip and divide a structure believed to be the cystic duct without the critical view. This is the classic route to a bile duct injury, which may require major reconstructive surgery and permanently affect the patient's life.

**Afterwards:** the operation note documents the findings and decision; the patient is told about the subtotal procedure and its possible consequences (a bile leak that usually settles, and a small risk of later stones in the remnant).
:::
:::

## Upper gastrointestinal surgery

::: definition The upper gastrointestinal cancer pathway {#def-upper}
**Oesophageal cancer** is either **adenocarcinoma**, now the commoner type in the UK and associated with reflux and Barrett's oesophagus and usually in the lower third, or **squamous cell carcinoma**, associated with smoking and alcohol and usually in the upper and middle thirds. **Gastric cancer** is mostly adenocarcinoma, associated with *Helicobacter pylori*, smoking and diet. Both present late, often with **dysphagia** (progressive, first to solids) or weight loss, anaemia and dyspepsia.

The pathway is: urgent endoscopy with biopsy (NICE guideline NG12 recommends an urgent direct-access upper GI endoscopy for people with dysphagia, and for people aged 55 and over with weight loss and upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia); **staging** with CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis, PET-CT for oesophageal cancer, and sometimes endoscopic ultrasound and staging laparoscopy; and discussion in a **multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting**. Curative treatment for fit patients with localised disease usually combines chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy with **oesophagectomy** or **gastrectomy**. Many patients present with advanced disease and receive palliative treatment, such as a stent for dysphagia.
:::

Oesophagectomy and gastrectomy are major operations with substantial risks, including anastomotic leak, pneumonia and long-term nutritional problems. In England, these operations are concentrated in specialist centres, because outcomes are better in high-volume units.

**Peptic ulcer disease** is now treated mainly by medicine (eradication of *H. pylori*, stopping NSAIDs, proton pump inhibitors), but its complications still involve surgeons: **perforation** (repair, see [[surgery/acute-abdomen]]), **bleeding** that endoscopy and interventional radiology cannot control, and, rarely, gastric outlet obstruction.

## Colorectal surgery

::: definition Colorectal cancer {#def-lower}
**Colorectal cancer** is one of the commonest cancers in the UK. Most arise from **adenomatous polyps** over years (the adenoma–carcinoma sequence), which is why screening that removes polyps prevents cancer. Risk factors include age, family history and inherited syndromes (Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis), inflammatory bowel disease, red and processed meat, obesity, smoking and alcohol.

Presentation depends on site. **Right-sided** cancers often present with iron deficiency anaemia, weight loss or a mass. **Left-sided** and rectal cancers present with a change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, tenesmus or obstruction.

Staging uses the **TNM** system: depth of invasion through the bowel wall (T), lymph node involvement (N) and distant metastases (M), most often to the liver and lungs.
:::

**Screening and referral.** In England the NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme sends a home **faecal immunochemical test (FIT)** every two years to people aged 50 to 74; a result above the programme threshold leads to colonoscopy. For people with symptoms, NICE recommends FIT in primary care to guide referral: an urgent suspected cancer referral is made for adults with a FIT result of at least **10 µg haemoglobin per gram of faeces**, and also when there is a rectal or abdominal mass or other strong clinical concern.

**Investigation.** Colonoscopy with biopsy is the key diagnostic test; CT colonography is used when colonoscopy is not suitable. Staging is with CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis, and **MRI of the pelvis** for rectal cancer, which shows the relationship of the tumour to the **mesorectal fascia**, the boundary of the planned resection. Every colorectal cancer is tested for mismatch repair deficiency to detect Lynch syndrome ([[genetics/cancer-genetics]]).

**Surgery.** The operation removes the affected segment with its blood supply and lymph nodes:

- caecum and ascending colon: **right hemicolectomy**;
- descending colon: **left hemicolectomy**;
- sigmoid colon: **sigmoid colectomy**;
- upper and middle rectum: **anterior resection** with **total mesorectal excision (TME)**, removing the rectum in its intact envelope of fat and lymph nodes;
- low rectum involving the sphincters: **abdominoperineal resection**, with a permanent colostomy.

Rectal cancer is often treated first with **radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy** to shrink the tumour and reduce local recurrence. Some patients have a complete clinical response and may be managed by close surveillance ("watch and wait") in specialist units. Liver and lung metastases can sometimes be removed with curative intent.

::: example Anaemia in an older man {#ex-iron}
A 72-year-old man is found to have a haemoglobin of 92 g/L with a low ferritin and microcytosis. He has no bowel symptoms, has lost 3 kg and feels tired. His GP's FIT result is 64 µg/g. What does this suggest, and what is the next step?
::: solution
**Interpretation:** iron deficiency anaemia in a man (or a postmenopausal woman) is due to gastrointestinal blood loss until proven otherwise. Right-sided colon cancers often bleed slowly without changing bowel habit, so the absence of symptoms is not reassuring. His FIT of 64 µg/g is well above the referral threshold of 10 µg/g.

**Next step:** an urgent suspected cancer referral for lower gastrointestinal investigation, usually **colonoscopy**, and, because both the upper and lower gut can cause iron deficiency, an **upper GI endoscopy** too (with duodenal biopsies to exclude coeliac disease). Start iron replacement while awaiting investigation.

**If a caecal cancer is found:** CT staging, MDT discussion and, if localised, a laparoscopic right hemicolectomy. Correcting the anaemia with intravenous iron before surgery reduces the need for transfusion ([[surgery/perioperative-care]]).
:::

### Diverticular disease and inflammatory bowel disease

Elective surgery for **diverticular disease** is considered for recurrent complicated attacks, fistulas (for example to the bladder, causing pneumaturia and recurrent urinary infection) and strictures, rather than simply after a number of uncomplicated episodes.

In **ulcerative colitis**, surgery is needed for **acute severe colitis** not responding to medical treatment, for dysplasia or cancer, and for chronic disease that medicine cannot control. In acute severe colitis, patients on intravenous steroids are reassessed around day 3; if they are not responding, the options are rescue medical therapy or **colectomy**. Delayed surgery in an unresponsive patient increases the risk of perforation and death. Because ulcerative colitis affects only the colon and rectum, **proctocolectomy** cures it; many patients later have an **ileal pouch–anal anastomosis**, which avoids a permanent stoma.

**Crohn's disease** can affect any part of the gut, and surgery does not cure it; recurrence at the anastomosis is common. Surgery is used for complications such as strictures, fistulas and abscesses, and conserves as much bowel as possible (limited resection, or **strictureplasty** that widens a stricture without removing bowel). Perianal Crohn's disease is managed jointly by surgeons and gastroenterologists.

### Anorectal conditions

- **Haemorrhoids** are enlarged vascular cushions of the anal canal. They cause painless bright red bleeding on the paper or in the pan and prolapse. Treatment begins with fibre and fluids; **rubber band ligation** is used for persistent symptoms, and surgery (haemorrhoidectomy) for large or recurrent haemorrhoids. A thrombosed external haemorrhoid causes acute pain.
- An **anal fissure** is a tear in the anoderm, usually in the posterior midline, causing severe pain on defecation and bright red bleeding. Internal sphincter spasm reduces blood flow and prevents healing. Treatment is stool softeners, topical **diltiazem** or **glyceryl trinitrate** to relax the sphincter, then botulinum toxin or lateral internal sphincterotomy if these fail. A fissure away from the midline raises the possibility of Crohn's disease, infection or malignancy.
- A **perianal abscess** arises from infection of an anal gland. It is treated by **incision and drainage**. About a third or more are associated with, or later develop, an **anal fistula**, a track between the anal canal and the perianal skin. Fistulas are treated by laying open (fistulotomy) when little sphincter is involved, or with a **seton** and sphincter-preserving procedures when the track crosses a lot of sphincter, to protect continence.
- **Pilonidal disease** is chronic infection of hair-containing sinuses in the natal cleft, common in young men. Abscesses are drained; chronic disease is treated by excision, often with an off-midline closure.

Rectal bleeding should never be attributed to haemorrhoids without considering cancer, particularly in people over 50, with a change in bowel habit, or with dark blood mixed with stool.

## Anastomoses and stomas

::: definition Anastomoses and stomas {#def-anastomosis}
An **anastomosis** is a surgical connection between two hollow structures, for example two ends of bowel after a resection. It can be hand-sewn or stapled. Healing depends on a good **blood supply**, **no tension**, healthy tissue and a well-nourished patient.

A **stoma** is an opening of a hollow organ onto the skin surface. In bowel surgery:

- an **ileostomy** is usually sited in the right iliac fossa and is **spouted** (it protrudes 2–3 cm) because small bowel effluent is liquid and irritates the skin;
- a **colostomy** is usually in the left iliac fossa and is **flush** with the skin because its output is formed;
- an **end** stoma has a single opening (the bowel beyond is removed or closed);
- a **loop** stoma brings a loop of bowel to the surface with two openings, usually to **defunction** (divert faeces away from) a downstream anastomosis, and is reversed later.
:::

An **anastomotic leak** is the most feared complication of bowel surgery. Rates are higher for low rectal anastomoses than for colonic ones, and higher in men, smokers, malnourished patients and after radiotherapy. A leak usually declares itself between days 3 and 7 with tachycardia, fever, abdominal pain, ileus, rising inflammatory markers or new organ dysfunction ([[surgery/perioperative-care]]). CT confirms it. Small, contained leaks may be drained radiologically; larger leaks with peritonitis need return to theatre, often with taking down the anastomosis and forming a stoma. This is why a loop ileostomy is often formed to protect a low rectal anastomosis: it does not prevent a leak, but makes its consequences less severe.

**Stoma complications** include skin irritation, retraction, stenosis, prolapse, **parastomal hernia** and ischaemia (a dusky or black stoma in the early postoperative period). A **high-output stoma** (often defined as more than 1.5–2 litres per day from an ileostomy) causes dehydration, sodium and magnesium depletion and acute kidney injury. Management includes measuring output, treating causes (infection, intra-abdominal sepsis, drugs, partial obstruction), restricting **hypotonic** oral fluids (which increase sodium loss), giving an **oral rehydration solution** with a high sodium content, and drugs that slow transit (loperamide, codeine) and reduce secretion (proton pump inhibitors). Patients are taught to recognise dehydration, and a stoma nurse is central to their care.

::: example A stoma that will not slow down {#ex-high-output}
A 45-year-old man is 6 days after a subtotal colectomy with an end ileostomy for acute severe ulcerative colitis. His stoma output has been 2.8 litres a day for 3 days. He is thirsty and drinks lots of water and squash. His sodium is 131 mmol/L, magnesium 0.55 mmol/L, creatinine 145 µmol/L (baseline 70). What is happening and how should it be managed?
::: solution
**Interpretation:** he has a **high-output ileostomy** with dehydration, hyponatraemia, hypomagnesaemia and acute kidney injury. Drinking large volumes of water and squash makes it worse: hypotonic fluid draws sodium into the gut lumen and increases output.

**Management:**

1. Correct the deficit with **intravenous** sodium chloride and magnesium, and monitor kidney function and electrolytes daily.
2. Look for a cause: intra-abdominal sepsis, *C. difficile* or other infection, partial obstruction, and drugs (for example stopping steroids abruptly, or prokinetics).
3. Restrict hypotonic oral fluids (for example to about 1 litre a day) and give a **glucose–saline oral rehydration solution** with a sodium concentration of about 90 mmol/L or more for the remainder.
4. Give **loperamide** (taken before meals, sometimes at higher than usual doses on specialist advice, opened capsules if transit is fast) and possibly codeine; a **proton pump inhibitor** reduces gastric secretions.
5. Involve the stoma nurse and dietitian; teach him to measure output and recognise dehydration; adjust as output falls.
:::
:::

## Bariatric surgery

Obesity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obstructive sleep apnoea, fatty liver disease, osteoarthritis and several cancers. **Bariatric (metabolic) surgery** produces greater and more sustained weight loss than any other treatment, and often brings remission of type 2 diabetes.

NICE guidance on overweight and obesity management (NG246, 2025) recommends referral for assessment for bariatric surgery for adults with a **BMI of 40 kg/m² or more**, or **35–39.9 kg/m²** with a significant health condition that weight loss could improve, provided they agree to long-term follow-up. Lower thresholds (by 2.5 kg/m²) are considered for people of South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean family background, who develop obesity-related disease at a lower BMI. An expedited assessment is offered to people with recent-onset type 2 diabetes and a BMI of 35 or more, and considered for those with a BMI of 30–34.9.

The two commonest operations are:

- **sleeve gastrectomy**, which removes about three-quarters of the stomach along the greater curvature, leaving a narrow tube;
- **Roux-en-Y gastric bypass**, which creates a small stomach pouch joined to a limb of jejunum, bypassing most of the stomach and the duodenum.

Both work partly by restriction but mainly through changes in gut hormones (such as GLP-1 and ghrelin) that reduce appetite and improve insulin sensitivity. Complications include leaks, bleeding, strictures, reflux (especially after sleeve gastrectomy), **internal hernia** after bypass (which can cause bowel ischaemia years later, presenting with intermittent abdominal pain), gallstones, dumping syndrome and **nutritional deficiencies** (iron, vitamin B12, folate, calcium, vitamin D). Patients need lifelong supplements and annual monitoring. Medicines for obesity (GLP-1 receptor agonists and related drugs) are an increasingly important alternative or addition.

::: example Applying the BMI thresholds {#ex-bmi}
A 47-year-old man of Pakistani family background weighs 101 kg and is 1.70 m tall. He has obstructive sleep apnoea treated with CPAP and hypertension. Does he meet the NICE criteria for referral for bariatric assessment?
::: solution
**BMI:** $101 / 1.70^2 = 101 / 2.89 \approx 34.9$ kg/m².

**Standard thresholds:** 40 or more, or 35–39.9 with a significant condition. At 34.9 he is just below the standard comorbidity threshold of 35.

**Adjusted threshold:** for people of South Asian family background, NICE recommends considering thresholds 2.5 kg/m² lower: 37.5, or 32.5 with a significant condition. His BMI of 34.9 with sleep apnoea and hypertension is above 32.5, so referral for specialist assessment should be considered.

**Point:** BMI underestimates metabolic risk in some ethnic groups, which develop type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at lower BMIs, and the adjusted thresholds aim to correct this inequity. The final decision rests on specialist assessment and his own wishes.
:::
:::

::: warning
**Abdominal pain after gastric bypass is an internal hernia until proven otherwise.** After Roux-en-Y bypass, small bowel can herniate through defects in the mesentery, often after substantial weight loss. The pain may be intermittent, the abdomen may be soft and blood tests may be normal, and CT can look nearly normal. Delay leads to strangulation of a long length of small bowel. Patients with persistent or recurrent pain after bypass should be discussed early with a bariatric surgeon, and a diagnostic laparoscopy may be needed even if imaging is unclear.
:::

::: history
In 1881 Theodor Billroth in Vienna performed the first successful resection of the distal stomach for cancer, joining the remaining stomach to the duodenum (the Billroth I operation). In 1908 William Ernest Miles described abdominoperineal excision of the rectum, based on his study of how rectal cancer spreads to lymph nodes. In 1982 the British surgeon Bill Heald described **total mesorectal excision**, removing the rectum in its intact fatty envelope; it reduced local recurrence of rectal cancer dramatically and is now the standard of care. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy was first performed by Erich Mühe in Germany in 1985 and was popularised from 1987 by Philippe Mouret in Lyon and François Dubois in Paris, starting the era of minimally invasive general surgery.
:::

::: summary
- A hernia can be reducible, irreducible, obstructed or **strangulated**; narrow necks (femoral) strangulate. Inguinal hernias are repaired with mesh, open or laparoscopic; watchful waiting suits some men with few symptoms.
- Symptomatic gallstones: laparoscopic cholecystectomy, within a week for acute cholecystitis. The **critical view of safety** prevents bile duct injury; subtotal cholecystectomy is a safe bailout.
- Oesophageal and gastric cancer present late; urgent endoscopy for dysphagia; staging and MDT; major resections in specialist centres.
- Colorectal cancer arises from adenomas; England screens with FIT every two years from 50 to 74; symptomatic FIT ≥ 10 µg/g prompts urgent referral. Surgery removes the segment with its lymphatic drainage; rectal cancer uses **TME**, often after (chemo)radiotherapy.
- UC is cured by proctocolectomy; Crohn's surgery is conservative and treats complications.
- Anorectal conditions: banding for haemorrhoids, sphincter relaxation for fissures, drainage for abscesses, sphincter-preserving treatment for complex fistulas.
- Anastomoses need blood supply and no tension. Leaks present days 3–7. Ileostomies are spouted, colostomies flush; high-output stomas need sodium-rich fluids and hypotonic restriction.
- Bariatric surgery (sleeve, bypass) is offered at BMI ≥ 40, or ≥ 35 with comorbidity (2.5 lower for some ethnic groups); internal hernia after bypass is a dangerous late complication.
:::

::: exercise Is it safe to wait? {level=2}
A 66-year-old man has had a small, easily reducible right inguinal hernia for 2 years. It causes mild discomfort only after long walks. He asks whether he must have an operation. What do you tell him?
::: solution
For men with minimal symptoms from an inguinal hernia, **watchful waiting** is a reasonable option: the risk of the hernia becoming strangulated is low. However, many men with inguinal hernias eventually develop more symptoms and choose repair. The decision depends on his preferences, his fitness and how the hernia affects his life. He should be taught the warning signs of incarceration and strangulation: a lump that becomes painful, tense and irreducible, especially with vomiting or abdominal distension, needs urgent assessment. If he chooses surgery, the options (open mesh repair or laparoscopic repair) and the risk of chronic groin pain and recurrence are discussed.

::: exercise A failing colitis {level=2}
A 28-year-old woman with acute severe ulcerative colitis has been on intravenous corticosteroids for 3 days. She still has 9 bloody stools a day, CRP 65 mg/L and a heart rate of 104/min. What are the options, who should be involved, and what is the danger of waiting?
::: solution
She is not responding to intravenous steroids by day 3 (frequent bloody stools and raised CRP predict failure). The options are **rescue medical therapy** (infliximab or ciclosporin) or **colectomy** (usually subtotal colectomy with an end ileostomy, preserving the rectum for a later pouch if she wishes). The decision is made jointly by gastroenterologists and colorectal surgeons with her, and she should meet the stoma nurse early. Daily review continues, with abdominal X-rays to look for colonic dilatation. **Danger of waiting:** prolonged unsuccessful medical treatment increases the risk of toxic megacolon, perforation and death, and makes her a weaker candidate for surgery. If rescue therapy fails within a few days, surgery should not be delayed further.
:::
:::

::: exercise A perianal problem {level=2}
A 34-year-old man has a 3-day history of throbbing pain next to his anus, worse on sitting, and a temperature of 38.1 °C. There is a tender, red, fluctuant swelling 3 cm from the anal verge. What is the diagnosis and treatment, and what should he be told about the future?
::: solution
**Diagnosis:** perianal abscess. **Treatment:** incision and drainage, usually under general anaesthesia in an emergency list, with examination of the anal canal; antibiotics are not a substitute for drainage, though they may be added for cellulitis, diabetes or immunosuppression. **Future:** a significant proportion of patients (around a third or more) later develop, or are found to have, an **anal fistula**, which presents as persistent discharge from the drainage site or recurrent abscesses. He should be told to return if this happens. Recurrent or complex disease prompts consideration of Crohn's disease and imaging with MRI.
:::

::: solution
Maintenance: $25 \times 60 = 1500$ mL. Excess stoma loss: $2200 - 1000 = 1200$ mL. Total: $1500 + 1200 = 2700$ mL.

Ileostomy fluid is rich in sodium (roughly 90–100 mmol/L or more), so the replacement part should be a **sodium-containing** crystalloid such as 0.9% sodium chloride with potassium as needed, guided by her electrolytes, rather than glucose. Magnesium is checked and replaced. At the same time the team looks for a cause of the high output, starts loperamide, restricts hypotonic oral drinks in favour of an oral rehydration solution once she can drink, and involves the stoma nurse and dietitian. Daily weights, fluid balance and kidney function guide the plan.

::: exercise Pain after a bypass {level=3}
A 38-year-old woman had a laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass 2 years ago and has lost 45 kg. She presents with her third episode in a month of severe central abdominal pain after eating, lasting a few hours. Today the pain is constant. Her abdomen is soft with mild central tenderness, her white cell count and lactate are normal, and CT shows a "swirl" of the mesenteric vessels. Give a differential diagnosis, the most likely diagnosis and the management.
::: solution
**Differential:** internal hernia; adhesional obstruction; gallstone disease (gallstones are common after rapid weight loss); marginal ulcer at the gastrojejunal anastomosis (especially in smokers and NSAID users); intussusception; and non-surgical causes such as pancreatitis.

**Most likely:** an **internal hernia** through a mesenteric defect. Recurrent postprandial pain after major weight loss and a mesenteric swirl on CT are typical. Normal blood tests and a soft abdomen do not exclude it, and constant pain suggests the bowel may now be compromised.

**Management:** keep her nil by mouth, give fluids and analgesia, and discuss urgently with the bariatric surgical team. She needs **urgent laparoscopy** to reduce the hernia and close the mesenteric defects, with resection of any non-viable bowel. Waiting for peritonitis or a rising lactate risks losing a long segment of small bowel. If she has been taken to a non-bariatric hospital, the general surgeon should operate if she is deteriorating rather than delay for transfer.
