Two of every five adults admitted to a general medical ward in a high-income country are at risk of malnutrition on screening, and in most hospitals the diet is addressed later, or not at all. At the other end of the same spectrum, more than a billion people live with obesity, and micronutrient deficiency remains, globally, the commonest nutritional disorder of all — because it occurs in people who are not thin.

Nutrition is therefore not the soft part of medicine. It determines whether a wound heals, whether a ventilated patient can be weaned, whether a child with diarrhoea survives refeeding, whether a vegan toddler gets rickets and whether a person who has had a gastric bypass comes back confused. This lesson covers energy, the macronutrients, the vitamins and trace elements, and the two clinical states — malnutrition and obesity — in which all of it matters at once.

## Energy: what is required and what it costs

Energy requirement is estimated from body weight and stress (roughly 25–30 kcal/kg/day for most inpatients, more with fever, sepsis, trauma, burns or major surgery, and measured directly by indirect calorimetry where that is available), and it is worth knowing the components: resting metabolic expenditure is 60–75% of the total in a sedentary adult, the thermic effect of food about 10%, and physical activity the most variable part. Resting expenditure per kilogram is highest in infants and falls with age and with loss of lean mass, which is why the same prescription that refeeds an elderly patient too fast will underfeed a young one.

The arithmetic of storage is unforgiving and worth teaching patients: 1 kg of body fat is of the order of 7700–8000 kcal, so a sustained excess of 100 kcal a day — two biscuits, a glass of juice — predicts about 4–5 kg a year if nothing else changes. In practice, weight change blunts as expenditure rises with the mass gained, and maintenance is harder than loss for reasons of appetite signalling rather than willpower.

## Protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre, fluid

**Protein** should provide about 0.75 g/kg/day for a healthy adult (the UK reference nutrient intake), and more — commonly 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day, sometimes higher — in critical illness, major trauma, burns, healing pressure injuries, pregnancy and lactation, and in older adults with acute disease, where anabolic resistance means the same intake yields less muscle. Nitrogen balance and a plain urea trend are crude monitors; a rising urea with adequate fluid and renal function usually means too little protein or too much catabolism, not the reverse.

**Fat** should not be driven below roughly 20% of energy, both because it carries the fat-soluble vitamins and essential fatty acids and because very low fat intakes worsen satiety and adherence. **Carbohydrate** quality matters more than the total: the evidence supports dietary patterns (Mediterranean-style, higher in whole grains, legumes, nuts, fruit and vegetables, lower in refined carbohydrate and sugar-sweetened drinks) rather than single-nutrient targets. **Fibre**, at 25–35 g/day, improves stool weight and a lipid profile, feeds the colonic microbiota whose short-chain fatty acids fuel the colonocyte, and helps glycaemic control. **Fluid** requirements for a normal adult are around 30–35 mL/kg/day, more with fever, diarrhoea, high output stomas, or a diuresis such as the post-obstructive diuresis, and less in renal and cardiac failure.

## Vitamins: functions, deficiency, toxicity

| Vitamin | Main roles | Deficiency | Notes and toxicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (retinol) | Vision, epithelial differentiation, immunity | Night blindness, xerosis and keratomalacia, Bitot spots, measles mortality | Teratogenic in excess; hepatotoxic; avoid high doses in pregnancy and with retinoids |
| D (cholecalciferol) | Calcium and phosphate absorption, bone | Rickets in children, osteomalacia, proximal myopathy, raised falls risk | Toxicity from over-supplementation causes hypercalcaemia; needs renal 1α-hydroxylation, so standard vitamin D does not correct the metabolic bone disease of advanced kidney disease |
| E (tocopherol) | Membrane antioxidant | Haemolysis and posterior column/cerebellar signs in fat malabsorption and abetalipoproteinaemia | Fat-soluble; deficiency is slow and usually reflects a lipid disorder |
| K (phyllo/menaquinone) | γ-carboxylation of clotting factors, osteocalcin | Bleeding with raised INR, corrected by vitamin K | Newborns are given prophylaxis; gut bacteria contribute; warfarin antagonises the cycle |
| B₁ (thiamine) | Pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, transketolase | Beriberi (wet and dry), Wernicke–Korsakoff | Depleted in 2–3 weeks; haemolytic anaemia in transketolase-dependent haemolytic syndromes; give before glucose in at-risk patients |
| B₂ (riboflavin) | FAD/FMN | Angular stomatitis, cheilosis, glossitis, seborrhoeic dermatitis | Often part of a multiple deficiency |
| B₃ (niacin) | NAD/NADP | Pellagra: dermatitis in sun-exposed sites, diarrhoea, dementia | Made from tryptophan, so maize-based diets and carcinoid syndrome deplete it; high-dose nicotinamide is used therapeutically |
| B₆ (pyridoxine) | Transamination, decarboxylation, ALA synthesis | Sideroblastic anaemia, peripheral neuropathy, seizures, glossitis | Isoniazid and penicillamine cause deficiency; excess pyridoxine itself causes neuropathy |
| B₇ (biotin) | Carboxylases | Dermatitis, alopecia, lactic acidosis | Raw egg white (avidin) and prolonged parenteral nutrition without supplementation |
| B₉ (folate) | One-carbon transfer, DNA synthesis | Megaloblastic anaemia, glossitis, neural tube defects, raised homocysteine | Depleted in weeks; corrects the anaemia of B₁₂ deficiency while neurological damage progresses |
| B₁₂ (cobalamin) | Methionine synthase, methylmalonyl-CoA mutase | Megaloblastic anaemia, subacute combined cord degeneration, neuropathy, glossitis, optic atrophy, psychiatric features | Stores last years; vegans, gastrectomy, ileal disease or resection, prolonged proton-pump inhibition, metformin, nitrous oxide |
| C (ascorbate) | Hydroxylation of proline and lysine, antioxidant, iron absorption | Scurvy: perifollicular haemorrhage, corkscrew hairs, swollen bleeding gums, poor wound healing, anaemia | Depleted in weeks; tea-and-toast diets, severe mental illness, extreme food selectivity in autism |

Two principles organise the table. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are absorbed with dietary fat and are lost in cholestasis, small-bowel disease, pancreatic insufficiency and orlistat therapy, and they accumulate to toxicity. Water-soluble vitamins are rarely toxic, are lost in dialysis, and are depleted over weeks rather than years — except B₁₂, whose several-year store makes its deficiency a disease of malabsorption and of strict avoidance rather than of poor intake.

## Minerals and trace elements

- **Iron.** Still the commonest nutritional deficiency worldwide. Look for blood loss (menstruation, gastrointestinal), increased demand (pregnancy, erythropoietic drive), malabsorption (coeliac disease, atrophic gastritis, bariatric surgery, proton-pump inhibitors) and inflammation. Ferritin is the best single test but is an acute-phase protein: a ferritin of 40 µg/L in a patient with a CRP of 120 does not exclude iron deficiency, and transferrin saturation, a reticulocyte response to a trial of iron, and a search for the source all matter. Oral iron fails in some patients through hepcidin, intravenous iron corrects faster and is preferred when absorption is uncertain, losses are ongoing, or tolerance fails.
- **Calcium, phosphate, magnesium, vitamin D and parathyroid hormone** are treated as a system in [[renal/potassium-calcium-phosphate]]; from a nutritional angle, remember that phosphate binders must be taken with food, that magnesium deficiency causes refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia, and that vitamin D requirements rise with body weight.
- **Iodine.** Required for thyroxine; deficiency causes goitre and, in severe fetal and infant deficiency, preventable learning difficulty. Iodisation of salt has largely corrected this in many countries, but re-emergence has been documented as iodine fortification fell — an example of a public-health measure that must be maintained.
- **Zinc.** Wound healing, taste, immunity, skin. Deficiency causes a scaly, erosive dermatitis in acral and periorificial distribution (acrodermatitis enteropathica when congenital), alopecia, diarrhoea, poor wound healing and hypogonadism; risk rises with high stoma output, parenteral nutrition without supplementation, and chronic diarrhoea. Excess zinc induces copper deficiency.
- **Copper.** Deficiency causes anaemia with neutropenia and a myeloneuropathy resembling B₁₂ deficiency, after gastric surgery, with excess zinc, or in severe malnutrition; Menkes disease is a failure of copper absorption with kinked hair and neurodegeneration, and Wilson disease a failure of biliary copper excretion with liver, basal ganglia and psychiatric disease and Kayser–Fleischer rings.
- **Selenium.** Deficiency causes cardiomyopathy (Keshan disease) and muscle pain; it is seen in long-term parenteral nutrition without trace element supplementation. Glutathione peroxidases are selenoenzymes.
- **Chromium and manganese** are needed in trace amounts; chromium contamination has caused rare poisoning, and manganese accumulates in cholestasis and in parenteral nutrition, causing parkinsonism.

::: theorem Use the gut {#thm-enteral}
Whenever the gastrointestinal tract can be used safely, enteral nutrition is preferable to parenteral nutrition: it preserves mucosal and immune function, carries less infection and metabolic risk, and is several times cheaper.
:::

::: proof
The mechanisms are separate and each has been demonstrated clinically. First, the intestinal mucosa is maintained largely by the nutrient contents of the lumen — glutamine and short-chain fatty acids for the colonocyte, direct contact for villous height — so starvation of the gut causes mucosal atrophy and loss of barrier function, with bacterial translocation. Second, feeding through the gut preserves gut-associated lymphoid tissue and secretory IgA, and in trials of critical illness and of pancreatitis, enteral feeding reduces infective complications relative to parenteral feeding. Third, a central venous catheter for parenteral nutrition is a route for bloodstream infection, and the formulation itself carries hyperglycaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia, liver steatosis and cholestasis, electrolyte shifts and refeeding risk, plus the need for monitoring of trace elements and micronutrients. Fourth, cost: the drug costs alone differ by roughly an order of magnitude before staff time is counted. The exceptions define the rule — bowel obstruction, ischaemia, high-output fistula, uncontrolled bleeding, severe ileus or intolerance, and short bowel in its early phase — and they are reasons to *supplement* enterally rather than replace it whenever any part of the tract works, which is why distal feeding with a proximal decompressing tube and trophic feeding at 10–20 mL/hour are used in practice.
:::

## Malnutrition: identifying it, then not making it worse

::: definition Protein-energy malnutrition {#def-pem}
**Undernutrition** is insufficient intake of energy and protein; **protein-energy malnutrition** describes its two extremes. **Marasmus** is overall calorie deficiency: wasted, loss of subcutaneous fat and muscle, low subcutaneous stores, bradycardia, hypothermia, and normal-ish biochemistry. **Kwashiorkor** is associated with oedema, skin and hair changes (patchy depigmentation, "flaky paint" dermatosis), hepatomegaly with steatosis, apathy, and often hypoalbuminaemia; the modern view treats the oedema as a disorder of capillary permeability and inflammation rather than low albumin alone, and kwashiorkor as what severe undernutrition looks like when infection and micronutrient deficiency are added. Severe acute malnutrition in children is defined by weight-for-height below −3 standard deviations, mid-upper-arm circumference below 115 mm, or nutritional oedema.
:::

Screening on admission (for example with the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool, or a validated local tool) is the intervention that changes outcomes, because the commonest nutritional error in hospitals is not the prescription but the omission: patients kept nil by mouth for procedures that never happen, documented "poor intake" for three days, and no dietitian review. The consequences are not abstract — impaired wound and anastomotic healing, pressure injuries, respiratory muscle weakness and failed weaning, immune paresis and infection, longer stay, more readmission, worse drug tolerance.

Management is the sequence **identify, correct the cause, refeed safely, supplement, monitor, support** (see the refeeding discussion in [[cell-biochemistry/metabolic-integration]]). The order matters because in severe and prolonged undernutrition the danger is not the deficit but the insulin that comes with feeding. Treat the micronutrient deficiencies with it: thiamine, folate, B₁₂, vitamin D, copper, selenium, zinc and phosphate, and in children with severe acute malnutrition the World Health Organization protocol deliberately starts with a low-energy, low-sodium, micronutrient-rich feed before catch-up growth is attempted.

::: example Bleeding gums and a diet of tea and toast {#ex-scurvy}
A 71-year-old who lives alone, has a bad back and rarely cooks, presents with bruising, painful swollen legs, "screaming pain" on minimal movement and bleeding gums. Haemoglobin 88 g/L, white cell count normal, platelet count normal, INR normal, alkaline phosphatase raised, and the legs show perifollicular petechiae with coiled hairs. Explain.
::: solution
This is scurvy. Ascorbate is required to hydroxylate proline and lysine residues in collagen, so deficiency produces structurally defective collagen: fragile capillaries (petechiae, ecchymoses, bleeding gums, haemarthrosis), impaired wound healing, abnormal hair shafts and bone pain from subperiosteal haemorrhage and disordered bone matrix, with a raised alkaline phosphatase. The anaemia is multifactorial — blood loss, reduced iron absorption because ascorbate aids non-haem iron uptake, and frequent coexistent folate deficiency in the same diet. The clues are the distribution (perifollicular, on the legs), the normal platelet count and INR in a patient who is bleeding into skin, and the diet. Confirm with a plasma ascorbate, but treat immediately: vitamin C, a proper diet with social support and a dental review, with pain improving in days and bruising in weeks. The differential — vasculitis, thrombocytopenia, non-accidental injury, amyloid purpura — is worth knowing because scurvy is missed in exactly the patients nobody weighs.
:::
:::

::: example Anaemia with a normal mean cell volume {#ex-b12}
A 68-year-old vegetarian of ten years on metformin 2 g daily has fatigue, Haemoglobin 96 g/L, MCV 89 fL, platelets 100 × 10⁹/L, bilirubin 34 µmol/L, low haptoglobin, hypersegmented neutrophils, and reduced vibration sense at both ankles with an unsteady gait. Explain the normal MCV, and why giving folate alone would be wrong.
::: solution
The picture is ineffective haematopoiesis with intramedullary destruction of cells (raised bilirubin and LDH with a low haptoglobin), which can mimic haemolysis, plus a posterior column neuropathy — a B₁₂ deficiency until disproved. The MCV is normal because two opposing influences are present: macrocytosis from disordered DNA synthesis and microcytosis from coexistent iron deficiency (dietary, and common in this age group from menstrual or bowel blood loss); the red cell distribution width and a low platelet count are often the first clue. Both metformin (which impairs ileal absorption) and long-standing strict vegetarianism without supplementation are risk factors, so this is a plausible double hit. Folate alone will correct the anaemia and can worsen or unmask subacute combined degeneration of the cord; the correct sequence is to measure B₁₂ (and methylmalonic acid and homocysteine if borderline), check for pernicious anaemia antibodies and coeliac disease, and treat with intramuscular hydroxocobalamin, with potassium monitored during the first week because a brisk reticulocytosis can cause hypokalaemia. A low or borderline B₁₂ with neurological signs is treated, not re-tested later.
:::
:::

::: warning "She is overweight, so she cannot be malnourished"
Body mass index measures mass, not nutrition. A person with obesity can have iron deficiency (often from heavy menstruation), vitamin D sequestration in adipose with low plasma 25-hydroxyvitamin D and secondary hyperparathyroidism, B₁₂ deficiency, folate deficiency, and protein deficiency when intake is restricted abruptly; and obesity with sarcopenia — low muscle mass in a heavy body — is common and predicts poor surgical and medical outcomes. Conversely, a "normal" BMI can accompany severe recent weight loss, as in atypical anorexia nervosa, or occult malabsorption. Assess nutrition by trajectory, intake, function, muscle and micronutrients, not by the number on the scale.
:::

## Obesity

::: definition Obesity {#def-obesity}
**Obesity** is adiposity that impairs health. The anthropometric shortcut is BMI (weight in kg divided by height in m²): 25–29.9 overweight, 30–39.9 obese, ≥40 morbidly obese, with lower thresholds used in South Asian and some other populations because risk begins at lower BMI. Waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio capture central adiposity and cardiovascular risk more usefully than BMI in an individual, and body composition (sarcopenic obesity, fluid overload) explains most of the discrepancies.
:::

The causes are overwhelmingly an energy imbalance sustained by an environment of cheap, palatable, energy-dense food and reduced expenditure, on a background of polygenic susceptibility to weight gain and to defending a higher weight; single-gene obesity (leptin and leptin receptor, pro-opiomelanocortin, melanocortin-4 receptor) is rare but important because some forms are treatable. Secondary causes worth excluding are Cushing syndrome, hypothyroidism (modest effect on weight), and drugs — corticosteroids, atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine and clozapine most), valproate, mirtazapine, tricyclics, insulin and sulfonylurels, some beta-blockers, and antiretroviral regimens including older integrase inhibitors. Weight affects every system: type 2 diabetes, obstructive sleep apnoea and obesity hypoventilation, osteoarthritis and back pain, gastro-oesophageal reflux and steatotic liver disease, polycystic ovary syndrome and subfertility, depression and stigma, venous disease, anaesthesia and surgical difficulty, and raised risk of at least thirteen cancers, with a dose–response relationship to BMI.

Management is staged and needs maintenance in its design: a moderate energy deficit with a diet the patient can sustain, plus behavioural support and activity that is not weight-dependent for benefit; pharmacotherapy (orlistat, GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists, which produce weight loss of the order of 15–22% in trials) as an adjunct, with the expectation of relapse when it stops; and metabolic and bariatric surgery for appropriate patients — offered on the basis of BMI with comorbidity or with very high BMI alone, after multidisciplinary assessment, with the understanding that it is the most effective treatment of type 2 diabetes available and that its risks (leak, internal hernia, marginal ulcer, gallstones, dumping, iron, B₁₂, folate, calcium, vitamin D and thiamine deficiency, and a raised suicide risk) require lifelong follow-up and supplementation. Post-operative vomiting is a medical emergency for the thiamine in it: Wernicke encephalopathy after banding or bypass has blinded and killed young patients whose "just vomiting" was never investigated.

::: example Vomiting eight weeks after a bypass {#ex-bariatric}
A 33-year-old woman, eight weeks from a laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, has had three weeks of vomiting and now has nystagmus, an ataxic gait and confusion. Sodium 131 mmol/L, glucose 4.2 mmol/L. A computed tomography head scan is normal. What do you give, and why does the order matter?
::: solution
Give high-dose intravenous thiamine now. This is Wernicke encephalopathy from thiamine depletion after bariatric surgery — the diagnosis is clinical, and the classic triad is present in only a minority, but she has two of its three features with a compatible history. A normal CT does not exclude it; magnetic resonance imaging of the mammillary bodies and periaqueductal grey matter supports the diagnosis when obtained but must never delay treatment. The order matters because carbohydrate oxidation requires thiamine-dependent enzymes, so a glucose-containing infusion in a depleted patient can precipitate or worsen the encephalopathy; magnesium is the cofactor for thiamine-dependent enzymes and its deficiency makes thiamine ineffective, so replace both, then correct sodium cautiously and investigate the cause of the vomiting (stricture, sleeve twist, obstruction) once she is stabilised. Long term, bariatric patients need a defined supplementation regimen, monitoring of iron, B₁₂, folate, vitamin D, calcium, parathyroid hormone and thiamine where vomiting recurs, and reliable access to parenteral nutrients if they cannot keep anything down.
:::
:::

::: example Vomiting in pregnancy, then double vision {#ex-hyperemesis}
A 26-year-old at 15 weeks' gestation has had seven weeks of vomiting, has lost 12 kg, and now has diplopia, nystagmus, a wide-based gait and a slow, confused speech. She has received 3 L of 5% dextrose in saline over the past day in an outside unit. What is the diagnosis, what happened, and what is the management?
::: solution
This is Wernicke encephalopathy due to thiamine depletion in hyperemesis gravidarum — one of the commonest settings for the condition in a high-income country, and the reason that prolonged vomiting in pregnancy is an indication for thiamine before dextrose. Thiamine-dependent dehydrogenases fail, lactate rises, and the mammillary bodies, medial thalami and periaqueductal grey matter are damaged; the ocular signs and ataxia may improve within minutes to days of treatment, but the memory disorder of Korsakov syndrome — anterograde amnesia with confabulation — may not. Giving glucose first accelerates the consumption of remaining thiamine, so a dextrose infusion in an untreated patient can precipitate the full picture, as here. Management is immediate high-dose intravenous thiamine (given as a preparation with magnesium, since magnesium is required for the thiamine-dependent enzymes and is often low after vomiting), then glucose with electrolytes, antiemetic control of the vomiting, thiamine continued orally once able to tolerate, and monitoring for other deficiencies (folate, B₁₂, potassium, phosphate, vitamin D) and for the psychological morbidity of hyperemesis. Documentation and follow-up matter: a partially treated case with residual amnesia has lifelong consequences and a preventable cause.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A patient has taken nothing by mouth for 24 days due to an ileus and is now being fed. Which nutrient store is most likely to already be depleted to a clinically dangerous level?
- [ ] Vitamin B₁₂.
- [ ] Vitamin A.
- [x] Thiamine.
- [ ] Body protein.
::: solution
Thiamine stores are exhausted in roughly two to three weeks without intake, and depletion becomes symptomatic when carbohydrate metabolism is restarted — which is why the refeeding protocol pairs feeding with thiamine and electrolyte replacement. Vitamin B₁₂ and vitamin A stores last years, and body protein is depleted but is not an acute biochemical danger in itself; the anaemia of B₁₂ deficiency, if it were to develop, would take far longer than 24 days.
:::

::: history
Nutrition as a science begins with controlled comparisons: James Lind's citrus trial at sea in 1747, and James Cook's practical prevention of scurvy on long voyages decades before the causative molecule was known. Christiaan Eijkman showed in 1897 that polyneuritis in hens fed polished rice was prevented by the husk, and Gowland Hopkins demonstrated in 1906–1912 that animals needed trace "accessory food factors" beyond protein, fat, carbohydrate and salts, for which he shared the 1929 Nobel Prize; Casimir Funk coined "vitamine" in 1912. Joseph Goldberger proved in 1914–1915 that pellagra was a dietary rather than an infectious disease, and Conrad Elvehjem's identification of nicotinic acid as the antipellagra factor in 1937 closed the loop. Albert Szent-Györgyi isolated the antiscorbutic factor as hexuronic acid in 1928 and Walter Haworth established its structure and synthesis with Edmund Hirst shortly afterwards; George Whipple, George Minot and William Murphy showed that liver cured the anaemia of pernicious anaemia (Nobel Prize, 1934), and in 1948 an active liver fraction was concentrated for clinical use, with the structure of cyanocobalamin established by Dorothy Hodgkin's crystallography — a feat that earned her the 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry and remains one of the finest achievements of the method. In the middle of the Second World War, Ancel Keys' Minnesota experiment quantified human adaptation to semi-starvation — bradycardia, hypothermia, apathy, oedema and the psychology of food — and Elsie Widdowson and Robert McCance's composition tables made rationing and therapeutic diets possible; both remain the reason we can prescribe a diet as a dose.
:::

::: summary
- Energy requirements are weight- and stress-dependent, and 1 kg of fat is about 7700 kcal: small sustained imbalances produce large long-term changes.
- Fat-soluble vitamins depend on fat absorption and accumulate to toxicity; water-soluble vitamins deplete in weeks, except B₁₂, whose store lasts years.
- Thiamine, folate and vitamin C run out fast enough to matter in ordinary hospital practice; give thiamine before glucose in any patient at risk.
- Iron deficiency is commonest worldwide; ferritin must be interpreted with inflammation, and a normal MCV does not exclude B₁₂ or folate deficiency.
- Malnutrition should be screened for on admission and treated by finding the cause, then refeding slowly with electrolyte and micronutrient cover; the refeeding danger is insulin, not hunger.
- Marasmus and kwashiorkor are the same deficit with different inflammation; obesity does not exclude malnutrition and is a disease of adiposity with polygenic, environmental and occasionally endocrine and drug causes.
- Bariatric surgery is effective and requires lifelong supplementation and follow-up, with a low threshold for thiamine in anyone vomiting after upper gastrointestinal surgery.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Estimate a requirement {level=1 check="1800"}
Estimate the daily energy requirement of a 60-kg woman with no acute illness, using 30 kcal/kg/day.
::: solution
$60 \times 30 = 1800$ kcal/day. Note the assumptions: no fever, no sepsis, no surgery, no pregnancy or lactation, and no correction for obesity (in obesity the calculation is usually done on adjusted or ideal body weight to avoid overfeeding). A measured value from indirect calorimetry is better in critical illness.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which deficiency? {level=1}
Match: (a) angular stomatitis, glossitis and a normocytic anaemia in an alcoholic; (b) dermatitis in a sun-exposed V-neck, diarrhoea and confusion; (c) petechiae and corkscrew hairs; (d) ataxia, areflexia and haemolytic anaemia in cholestasis; (e) rash, alopecia and diarrhoea with a high stoma output.
::: solution
(a) Riboflavin (B₂), often with folate and other B vitamins; the anaemia may be mixed. (b) Niacin (pellagra), which can be produced by isoniazid or by carcinoid syndrome consuming tryptophan. (c) Vitamin C (scurvy). (d) Vitamin E, which requires bile and fat absorption and whose deficiency gives a spinocerebellar syndrome with haemolysis. (e) Zinc, with acrodermatitis-like rash in the acral and periorificial distribution; check the stoma output and the trace-element content of parenteral nutrition.
:::
:::

::: exercise Iron indices {level=1 check="16"}
Transferrin saturation is calculated as serum iron ÷ total iron-binding capacity × 100%. With serum iron 8 µmol/L and TIBC 50 µmol/L, calculate the saturation and say what it means with a ferritin of 28 µg/L and a CRP of 5 mg/L.
::: solution
$8/50 \times 100 = 16\%$. A low transferrin saturation with a low-normal ferritin and a normal CRP is iron deficiency without inflammation; ferritin below 30 µg/L is generally diagnostic in that setting, and below 15 µg/L is close to specific. The next step is not a further iron study but a search for the cause — menstrual history, diet, coeliac serology, and gastrointestinal investigation in men and postmenopausal women.
:::
:::

::: exercise Vitamin D and kidney disease {level=2}
A patient with stage 5 chronic kidney disease has a 25-hydroxyvitamin D of 42 nmol/L, calcium 2.1 mmol/L, phosphate 1.8 mmol/L and parathyroid hormone 1100 pg/mL. Explain why nutritional vitamin D alone is unlikely to control the bone disease, and what else is needed.
::: solution
The hydroxylation of vitamin D to 25-hydroxyvitamin D occurs in liver and is reflected by the measured level; the regulated step to the active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D is renal 1α-hydroxylation, which fails as functioning nephron mass and klotho-dependent fibroblast growth factor 23 signalling change. So a mildly low 25-hydroxy level should be corrected, but secondary hyperparathyroidism in this setting is driven by phosphate retention, calcitriol deficiency and skeletal resistance to parathyroid hormone. Management is phosphate lowering (dietary phosphate, binders taken with meals), calcium and vitamin D balance, a vitamin D receptor agonist or calcimimetic in chosen patients, treatment of metabolic acidosis, and dialysis adequacy; refractory disease may need parathyroidectomy. Note the safety point: giving active vitamin D without controlling phosphate risks hypercalcaemia and vascular calcification.
:::
:::

::: exercise The nil-by-mouth order {level=2}
A frail 84-year-old is admitted with a fracture of the femur. She lives alone, lives on toast and tea, has lost 6 kg over two months, and is made nil by mouth at 19:00 for surgery "the following morning", which slips to the afternoon, then a further NBM order overnight for a postponed scan. Write the nutritional plan.
::: solution
She is already at high risk on any screening tool (unintentional weight loss over 5% in 3–6 months plus poor intake plus age), and the biggest single improvement is to stop unnecessary fasting: modern pre-operative fasting allows clear fluids up to 2 hours before induction, and carbohydrate-loading drinks are used in enhanced recovery; the scan indication and the need for true fasting must be checked, and "nil by mouth" written as a timed order with an end date and a review. Then: dietitian referral on day 1, oral nutritional supplements if intake is inadequate, protein at the higher end (1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for fracture healing), vitamin D and calcium, thiamine, folate and B₁₂ given the diet, phosphate and potassium watched during refeeding, mobilisation and physiotherapy, and a written plan for feeding after surgery with speech and language review if delirium or reduced consciousness delays oral intake. Documentation of the planned total fasting time in the handover prevents the commonest cause of hospital malnutrition, which is nobody's decision.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reading a feeding chart {level=2}
A patient on long-term home parenteral nutrition develops a parkinsonian syndrome and a microcytic anaemia with neutropenia. What two trace-element problems should be considered, and how do you confirm them?
::: solution
Manganese accumulation and copper deficiency, in opposite directions. Manganese is excreted in bile and accumulates in cholestasis and in some parenteral nutrition regimens, causing a parkinsonism with characteristic basal ganglia signal on MRI, and levels are measured in whole blood; management is to reduce or remove manganese from the feed and to treat cholestasis. Copper deficiency produces an anaemia (often with neutropenia and, in severe cases, a myeloneuropathy mimicking B₁₂ deficiency), and is measured by serum copper and ceruloplasmin with allowance for ceruloplasmin's behaviour as an acute-phase reactant; check zinc, since excess zinc is a common cause of copper deficiency. Both are reasons that home parenteral nutrition requires scheduled trace-element and liver monitoring rather than only calories and electrolytes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Obesity clinic reasoning {level=3}
A 41-year-old man with BMI 43, type 2 diabetes for 4 years, obstructive sleep apnoea on continuous positive airway pressure and hypertension asks about gastric bypass after a YouTube video. What would you establish before referring, and what would you tell him about the trade-offs?
::: solution
Establish the disease burden and the reversibility of the comorbidity: HbA1c and duration of diabetes (longer duration and insulin treatment predict less diabetes remission), whether the sleep apnoea improves with weight loss, blood pressure control, liver disease and steatotic liver fibrosis, joint and reflux symptoms (bypass helps reflux; sleeve can worsen it), reproductive plans, and — most important — his own goals and the psychological and social context, including previous treatments, relationship with food, mood, trauma history and suicide risk. Confirm assessment by the multidisciplinary team, smoking cessation, and the practical commitment to lifelong supplementation, follow-up and bloods. Then the honest trade-off: metabolic surgery is the most effective long-term weight and diabetes intervention available, with reductions in mortality and in microvascular outcomes, but it is not curative — relapse occurs, diabetes can recur, weight regain is common, and complications (internal hernia, marginal ulcer, strictures, gallstones, dumping, deficiencies of iron, B₁₂, folate, calcium, vitamin D and thiamine) and a need for reoperation are real. If he is not ready for surgery, the alternatives are now substantial: intensive lifestyle intervention with maintenance support, and drug treatments that produce 15% or more weight loss in a proportion of patients, with cardiometabolic benefit; and drug and surgical therapy can be combined and sequenced. Whatever is chosen, the plan has to survive long after the initial decision.
:::

::: exercise Global nutrition {level=3}
A 14-month-old in a displacement camp has weight-for-height −3.4 SD, oedema of both feet, a "flaky paint" rash, sparse reddish hair, and diarrhoea. Write the first 48 hours of management using the World Health Organization approach, and say why the obvious intervention would be harmful.
::: solution
The obvious intervention — high-energy feeding at once — is harmful, because the starved child's insulin response drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium intracellularly, and because excess sodium and protein load worsen cardiac and renal function; the classic cause of death in the first days of treatment is refeeding, and the answer is a staged protocol. First 48 hours: treat and prevent hypoglycaemia with frequent feeds (starting formula F-75, low in sodium and protein, given every two to three hours around the clock, including at night), prevent hypothermia by skin-to-skin contact and covering, treat dehydration with a low-sodium rehydration solution given frequently in small volumes, correct micronutrient deficits with a therapeutic supplement providing vitamin A, zinc, copper and B vitamins, give broad-spectrum antibiotics because clinical infection is often absent despite fever being absent too, treat and prevent malaria and measles where relevant, and do not give oral iron during stabilisation. Monitor weight daily, watch for signs of refeeding failure (increasing oedema, rising pulse and respiratory rate, abdominal distension, lethargy), and if the child cannot feed or is vomiting, or has shock or severe complication, escalate to the inpatient protocol. Once appetite returns and oedema resolves, transition to F-100 or ready-to-use therapeutic food with catch-up growth, then to a follow-up diet and caregiver education. The programme-level lesson is that this protocol is one of the most cost-effective interventions in medicine, and that its success depends on staff who can recognise oedematous malnutrition, which is the presentation most often missed.
