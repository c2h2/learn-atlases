Fat is the body's fuel reserve and its water store at the same time: one kilogram of triglyceride carries about 38 MJ, whereas the same energy as glycogen would drag several kilograms of bound water with it. Nitrogen is the reverse problem — the body can store carbon and dispose of it at leisure, but it must dispose of amino-nitrogen continuously, and the machinery for doing so fails in a way that is dramatic within hours.

This lesson covers the two halves of that asymmetry: the metabolism of lipids, from a fatty meal to a lipoprotein particle and from an adipocyte to a ketone body; and the metabolism of amino acids, from transamination to urea, with the inborn errors and the acquired diseases that make the pathways clinically visible.

## Fats in and fats out

Triacylglycerol is three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone. The fatty acids vary in chain length and in the number and position of double bonds, and the differences matter: humans cannot introduce a double bond beyond carbon 9, so **linoleic (n-6) and α-linolenic (n-3) acids are essential**, needed for membrane phospholipids and as precursors of arachidionate, prostaglandins, docosahexaenoic acid and the resolving mediators. A diet without them causes a scaly dermatitis, poor wound healing and, in infants, failure to thrive.

Digestion requires emulsification. Bile salts made from cholesterol in the liver, stored in the gall bladder and returned by enterohepatic circulation, break fat into droplets; pancreatic lipase with colipase releases 2-monoglyceride and free fatty acids; bile salts, monoglyceride, fatty acids, cholesterol and fat-soluble vitamins assemble into **micelles** that deliver them to the enterocyte. Inside, fatty acids are re-esterified and packaged as **chylomicrons**, which enter lymph — not portal blood — and so bypass the liver. That single anatomical fact explains three things: why medium-chain triglycerides (which go directly to the liver in portal blood) are used when fat absorption is impaired, why a lymphatic leak gives chylous ascites and loss of lymphocytes, and why an oral fat load raises triglycerides for hours while the liver's own product (VLDL) peaks later.

::: definition β-oxidation and the carnitine shuttle {#def-box}
**β-oxidation** in the mitochondrial matrix removes two-carbon units as acetyl-CoA from any fatty acyl chain, in a cycle of dehydrogenation (FAD), hydration, dehydrogenation (NAD⁺) and thiolysis. Long-chain fatty acyl-CoA cannot cross the inner membrane; it is transferred to carnitine by **carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1)** on the outer membrane, exchanged across the membrane, and converted back by CPT2. **Malonyl-CoA**, the first product of fatty-acid synthesis, inhibits CPT1 — so insulin, which raises malonyl-CoA, switches fat oxidation off.
:::

::: example Sixteen carbons, and what they are worth {#ex-palmitate}
Calculate the net ATP yield from complete oxidation of one molecule of palmitate, using 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH₂, and allowing for the cost of activation.
::: solution
Activation to palmitoyl-CoA costs the equivalent of 2 ATP (ATP → AMP + PPi). Seven cycles of β-oxidation cleave eight acetyl-CoA and produce 7 NADH and 7 FADH₂: $7 \times 2.5 + 7 \times 1.5 = 28$ ATP. The eight acetyl-CoA give $8 \times 10 = 80$ ATP through the citric acid cycle. Net $= 28 + 80 - 2 = 106$ ATP — about five and a half times what one glucose yields, which is why fat is the storage form and why a patient starving for a week is not protein-depleted to the same extent as one starved for two days. The arithmetic also explains the value of the "fat-burning" switch: at rest, most of the ATP of cardiac and resting skeletal muscle comes from fatty acids, and free fatty acids compete with glucose (the Randle effect), which is one reason insulin resistance in obesity is partly a lipid phenomenon.
:::
:::

Two accessory systems matter clinically. **Peroxisomes** shorten very-long-chain and branched-chain fatty acids; their failure (Zellweger spectrum) or the failure of their import of these lipids into mitochondria (X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy, in which very-long-chain fatty acids accumulate and damage white matter and adrenal cortex) gives accumulation measurable in plasma. **Odd-chain** fatty acids yield propionyl-CoA, converted to methylmalonyl-CoA by a biotin carboxylase and then to succinyl-CoA by a **vitamin B₁₂**-dependent mutase; this is the only fatty carbon that can become glucose, and the reason B₁₂ deficiency causes methylmalonic aciduria.

::: definition Ketone bodies {#def-ketone}
Ketone bodies — **acetoacetate**, **β-hydroxybutyrate** and the breakdown product **acetone** — are made in liver mitochondria from acetyl-CoA when oxaloacetate is scarce and acetyl-CoA is plentiful: prolonged fasting, a very low carbohydrate intake, prolonged exercise, uncontrolled type 1 diabetes and, in ruminants, lactation. HMG-CoA synthase is the regulated step. Extrahepatic tissues reconvert acetoacetate to acetyl-CoA; **the liver cannot use ketone bodies**, because it lacks the thiophorase (succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase).
:::

Ketosis is therefore a physiological adaptation that supplies water-soluble fuel to brain, heart and muscle, sparing glucose and muscle protein; after days of starvation, ketones provide much of the brain's energy and this is why a starved person stays lucid while an hypoglycaemic one does not. **Ketoacidosis** is the same chemistry without control: in insulin deficiency there is no brake on lipolysis (hormone-sensitive lipase is disinhibited) and no oxaloacetate for gluconeogenesis to spare, so ketone production outruns buffering and the pH falls. Alcoholic ketoacidosis and the starvation ketoacidosis of pregnancy occur in the same way, and both may present with a near-normal glucose.

::: warning The urine ketone stick may say the patient is getting worse while she is getting better
Nitroprusside reagents detect acetoacetate (and acetone) but not β-hydroxybutyrate. In severe ketoacidosis the raised NADH/NAD ratio pushes the equilibrium towards β-hydroxybutyrate, so urine ketones can look modest early; with treatment, as the redox state normalises, β-hydroxybutyrate is converted back to acetoacetate and the stick reads more strongly. Judging response by urine ketones, or by a "rising" ketone result, misleads. Follow the ketone body concentration in blood (β-hydroxybutyrate), the pH or bicarbonate, the anion gap and the clinical state.
:::

## Fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis, and lipoproteins

Fatty-acid synthesis is the mirror image, run in the cytosol on the fatty-acid synthase multifunctional protein with acyl-carrier protein, NADPH from the pentose phosphate pathway and citrate lyase exporting acetyl units from the mitochondrion. Its committed step, **acetyl-CoA carboxylase**, is a useful example of metabolic logic: activated by citrate (plenty of mitochondrial fuel) and by insulin through dephosphorylation, inhibited by AMP-activated protein kinase phosphorylation and hence by energy stress. Its product malonyl-CoA both feeds synthesis and blocks oxidation through CPT1, which is why a cell does not synthesise and burn fat at the same time.

Cholesterol is made from acetyl-CoA through mevalonate, and the pathway's regulated enzyme, **HMG-CoA reductase**, is the target of statins and the reason the pathway is worth knowing:

- Nearly every cell synthesises cholesterol; the liver also makes it for export as VLDL and secretes it in bile, and only the liver (and bacteria in the gut) can degrade the ring.
- Intracellular cholesterol controls its own supply through **SREBP**: when sterol levels fall, the transcription factor is cleaved and induces HMG-CoA reductase and the **LDL receptor**. Statins lower intracellular cholesterol, which up-regulates the LDL receptor, and that is how they clear LDL from plasma — the reason their effect depends on receptor function, and why a patient with no LDL receptors (homozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia) responds poorly.
- **PCSK9** binds the LDL receptor and directs it to lysosomal degradation; antibodies and small interfering RNA against PCSK9 lower LDL dramatically by preserving receptors.

::: theorem The lipoprotein logic {#thm-lipoprotein}
Lipoproteins are defined by what they carry and where they are going. Triglyceride-rich particles (chylomicrons from gut, VLDL from liver) deliver fat to adipose and muscle through lipoprotein lipase, which requires apoC-II and is activated by insulin; the remnants are taken up by the liver. Cholesterol-rich particles run the other way: LDL delivers hepatic cholesterol to tissues through the LDL receptor, and HDL collects cholesterol from peripheral cells through ABCA1 and returns it to the liver, where it is secreted in bile.
:::

::: proof
The direction of each flux follows from the enzymes each particle carries or meets, not from the particle "wanting" to travel. Chylomicrons and VLDL encounter lipoprotein lipase on capillary endothelium in muscle and adipose; apoC-II is its obligatory cofactor, so the rare apoC-II deficiency presents with chylomicronaemia and pancreatitis. As triglyceride is removed the particle shrinks, returns its cholesterol esters via CETP and is cleared by apoE-mediated hepatic uptake. LDL is what remains of VLDL after most triglyceride is gone, is the main cholesterol carrier in plasma, and is removed by receptor-mediated endocytosis — a process whose failure raises LDL from birth. HDL is assembled from lipid-poor apoA-I taking up cholesterol through ABCA1, esterified by LCAT so the core can fill, and gives its cholesterol to the liver selectively through SR-BI. Clinical lipid patterns fall out of this: raised triglycerides mean trouble clearing triglyceride-rich particles (uncontrolled diabetes and alcohol are the common causes, hypothyroidism and renal disease the classic secondary ones), while isolated raised LDL means impaired receptor-mediated clearance or overproduction. Atheroma follows exposure of the artery to LDL particles over time, which is why **lifetime exposure**, not a single measurement, drives risk — the argument behind treating familial hypercholesterolaemia early.
:::

::: example Tendon lumps and a heart attack at 34 {#ex-fh}
A 29-year-old, fit and lean, is found to have a cholesterol of 11.4 mmol/L with normal triglycerides and an HDL of 1.2 mmol/L. His father had a myocardial infarction at 44 and died at 51; his grandmother lost two sons "young". Examination shows firm swellings of both Achilles tendons and small yellowish nodules over the knuckles. Calculate his LDL if a measured value is unavailable when triglycerides are 1.1 mmol/L, and outline why statins alone may not suffice.
::: solution
Friedewald's equation in conventional units gives LDL cholesterol = total − HDL − (triglyceride ÷ 2.2 in mmol/L), i.e. $11.4 - 1.2 - 1.1/2.2 = 9.7$ mmol/L. Tendon xanthomas with an LDL this high, a family history of premature disease in two generations and no secondary cause are clinical diagnostic criteria for **heterozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia**, usually a loss-of-function allele of *LDLR*, sometimes of *APOB* (the ligand) or gain-of-function in *PCSK9*. Management is high-intensity statin plus ezetimibe, and LDL apheresis or PCSK9-directed therapy when targets are not met; cascade screening of relatives is the most valuable act, because the condition is common (about 1 in 250) and largely asymptomatic until the coronary event. Statins work partly by up-regulating the LDL receptor, so a patient with little residual receptor activity responds less well; homozygotes may need lomitapide or inclisiran, and liver transplantation historically.
:::
:::

## Nitrogen: transamination, glutamate and urea

Amino acids are not stored. Their carbon skeletons are used or burnt; their nitrogen is transferred, and eventually excreted. Transamination by **pyridoxal phosphate**-dependent aminotransferases moves an amino group onto α-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate; glutamate either gives up its nitrogen to urea in the liver or exports it as **glutamine**, the non-toxic transport form that carries two nitrogens.

::: definition The urea cycle {#def-urea}
In the hepatocyte, one free ammonia (as carbamoyl phosphate) and one nitrogen from aspartate are condensed to **urea**, which is excreted, and fumarate, which returns to the cycle. The five enzymes are carbamoyl phosphate synthetase **I** (mitochondrial, requires **N-acetylglutamate** as its obligatory activator), ornithine transcarbamylase, argininosuccinate synthetase, argininosuccinate lyase and arginase. Four high-energy phosphate bonds are consumed per urea, and the whole cycle is up-regulated with a high protein intake and in catabolism.
:::

::: example A boy with vomiting after a high-protein meal {#ex-otc}
A three-year-old boy is admitted with vomiting, ataxia and confusion after a week of eating more protein than usual at a family event. Ammonia is 480 µmol/L, respiratory alkalosis on blood gas, normal anion gap, normal glucose, normal liver enzymes, plasma glutamine high and citrulline low; urinary orotic acid is markedly increased. His maternal uncle died of an unexplained "metabolic" illness in infancy. What is the enzyme, why is the orotic acid raised, and why is the respiratory alkalosis a clue?
::: solution
This is **ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency**, the commonest urea cycle disorder and the only X-linked one, which fits a boy with an affected maternal uncle. Ammonia cannot enter the cycle, so it accumulates; glutamine and alanine rise as the body tries to store nitrogen, and glutamine in astrocytes is osmotically active — the mechanism of the cerebral oedema of hyperammonaemia and of the encephalopathy of liver failure. Carbamoyl phosphate made in mitochondria cannot be used, leaks to the cytosol and feeds pyrimidine synthesis, so **orotic acid** in urine distinguishes an OTC block from carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I or N-acetylglutamate synthase deficiency, where orotic acid is low. Ammonia stimulates the respiratory centre directly, so the child arrives with a respiratory alkalosis rather than the acidosis that shock or a metabolic error would suggest: **hyperammonaemia with a normal anion gap and a respiratory alkalosis is a urea cycle defect until disproved**. Treatment is emergency ammonia removal (hemodialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy if severe), stopping protein briefly while providing enough calorie as glucose and lipid to stop catabolism, arginine intravenously (to supply citrulline and keep the rest of the cycle moving and to make urea from the retained nitrogen), and nitrogen scavengers such as sodium benzoate and sodium phenylbutyrate/phenylacetate; long term, protein restriction, essential amino acid supplements, oral arginine or citrulline, liver transplantation for severe phenotypes, and emergency regimens written out for the family before they need them.
:::
:::

Chronic hyperammonaemia from liver failure produces the fluctuating confusion, disturbed sleep, asterixis and fetor of hepatic encephalopathy, treated by identifying the precipitant (constipation, infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, sedatives, large protein load, dehydration) and by lactulose to acidify the gut lumen and trap ammonia as ammonium, with rifaximin as an add-on. The biochemistry also shows why a low-protein diet is no longer recommended routinely in cirrhosis: these patients are already muscle-wasted, and muscle is a major site of nitrogen disposal.

## Individual amino acids worth their clinical consequences

- **Phenylalanine → tyrosine** by phenylalanine hydroxylase with tetrahydrobiopterin. Deficiency of the enzyme gives **phenylketonuria**, now detected by newborn screening; untreated, it causes learning difficulties, seizures, eczema, a musty odour and fair hair and skin (tyrosine being the precursor of melanin). Treatment is a restricted diet with tyrosine supplemented, monitored by plasma phenylalanine; the mother's diet matters in pregnancy, because maternal PKU damages the fetal brain. Deficiency of the pterin cofactor causes a form with neurological disease that diet alone does not correct, which is why a low phenylalanine with normal enzyme needs cofactor testing.
- **Tyrosine** continues to DOPA and the catecholamines, to thyroxine, to melanin (tyrosinase, deficient in albinism) and to fumarate and acetoacetate. Blocks along this last route cause tyrosinaemia type I (liver failure, renal tubular dysfunction, porphyria-like neurotoxic crises; treated with nitisinone, which inhibits the pathway upstream of the toxic metabolites) and alkaptonuria (homogentisate oxidase; dark urine on standing, ochronotic arthropathy in adulthood).
- **Homocysteine** is remethylated to methionine using methylcobalamin and 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, or transsulphurated using pyridoxal phosphate. Cystathionine β-synthase deficiency causes **homocystinuria**: tall thin habitus with lens dislocation typically downwards and inwards (upwards in Marfan syndrome), osteoporosis, learning difficulties and — the dangerous part — thromboembolism at a young age; about half respond to pyridoxine. Regardless of cause, a raised homocysteine also accompanies folate or B₁₂ deficiency and is one mechanism linking B-vitamin status to neural tube defects, hence periconceptional folate.
- **Methylation** depends on the folate–methionine cycle, and this is why folate deficiency causes both megaloblastic anaemia and neural tube defects, and why giving folate alone to a B₁₂-deficient patient can correct the blood count while demyelination progresses.
- **Branched-chain amino acids** (leucine, isoleucine, valine) are transaminated in muscle and oxidised there and in brain, not in liver; their ketoacids are degraded by a thiamine-dependent complex related to pyruvate dehydrogenase. Its deficiency, **maple syrup urine disease**, presents in the first week with poor feeding, encephalopathy and sweet-smelling urine, with leucine driving the neurological damage; biotin and thiamine-responsive forms exist, and management is urgent dialysis, a special formula and restriction with careful monitoring, in a diet that must still supply enough for growth.
- **Glycine, glutamate, aspartate, alanine, proline, serine, tyrosine, cysteine, asparagine, arginine and (in the neonate) glutamine** are "non-essential" only conditionally: the list lengthens when intake or synthesis fails, as cysteine does in phenylketonuria and premature infants.

::: example Dark urine, back pain and a drug list {#ex-porphyria}
A 27-year-old woman has three days of severe colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, tachycardia, hyponatraemia of 126 mmol/L and now weakness of both arms; urine passed in hospital has turned port-wine coloured on standing. She was started on carbamazepine for trigeminal neuralgia six weeks ago and has been dieting. What is the diagnosis, why are the two new features relevant, and what is the specific treatment?
::: solution
This is an acute hepatic porphyria (acute intermittent porphyria, porphobilinogen deaminase deficiency, autosomal dominant with low penetrance). Precipitants are anything that increases the demand for haem synthesis in the liver: cytochrome P450-inducing drugs such as carbamazepine, barbiturates, sulfonamides and some progestogens; and reduced carbohydrate supply, as in dieting or infection, since glucose represses ALA synthase. The clinical triad is abdominal, autonomic (tachycardia, hypertension, constipation, urinary retention) and neurological (motor neuropathy that can cause respiratory failure, seizures, and hyponatraemia usually from inappropriate antidiuresis); the urine contains porphobilinogen and δ-aminolevulinic acid, which darken on standing — and, importantly, the abdomen is soft and imaging unhelpful, so the disease is often mistaken for a surgical emergency. Confirm with a random urine porphobilinogen measured in the dark, then treat with intravenous **haem arginate**, which feeds back on ALA synthase, plus glucose and removal of the precipitant; givosiran, which silences ALAS1 in the liver, has reduced attacks in frequent relapsers. Never give progesterone-heavy or enzyme-inducing drugs to these patients without checking a porphyria safety list.
:::

Which two nitrogen atoms of urea come from which sources?
- [ ] Both from free ammonia.
- [x] One from free ammonia (via carbamoyl phosphate) and one from aspartate.

Aspartate is regenerated from fumarate by transamination, so in practice nitrogen reaches urea from many amino acids through glutamate and the transaminases, but the immediate donors are these two.

::: history
Archibald Garrod's *Inborn Errors of Metabolism* (1908) proposed that a heritable block in a single chemical step could produce a disease — the idea behind every enzyme assay in this lesson. Six years earlier Knoop had mapped the β-oxidation spiral by feeding dogs phenyl-tagged fatty acids and looking at the urinary benzoates, and Krebs and Kurt Henseleit solved the urea cycle in 1932. Asbjørn Fölling described phenylketonuria in 1934, and Robert Guthrie's bacterial inhibition assay from 1961 made population screening of newborns a matter of a heel-prick and a filter paper. Konrad Bloch and Feodor Lynen worked out cholesterol biosynthesis and the activation of acetate (Nobel Prize, 1964); Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein then explained how cells sense and import cholesterol through the LDL receptor (Nobel Prize, 1985), which is why the statins work at all. Akira Endo isolated compactin from a Penicillium in 1976 while screening fungal metabolites for HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, and lovastatin followed — a drug class discovered by sifting mouldy rice metabolites for an enzyme assay.

:::

::: summary
- Fat is the concentrated store: bile and micelles are needed for absorption, chylomicrons take dietary fat through lymph, and β-oxidation in mitochondria (long chains via the carnitine shuttle) yields about 106 ATP per palmitate.
- Malonyl-CoA is the switch: it drives synthesis and blocks oxidation through CPT1, so fed and fasting states choose one route.
- Ketone bodies are made by the liver for others, and are physiological in fasting and pathological only when unregulated, as in insulin deficiency.
- Lipoproteins are classified by cargo and direction: triglyceride-rich outward, cholesterol-rich homeward, LDL by receptor clearance, HDL by reverse transport; the LDL receptor explains statins, familial hypercholesterolaemia and PCSK9.
- Nitrogen is moved as glutamine and excreted as urea at a cost of four phosphate bonds; hyperammonaemia causes astrocyte swelling and respiratory alkalosis, and urine orotic acid separates the urea cycle blocks.
- Individual amino-acid pathways explain PKU, homocystinuria, maple syrup urine disease, tyrosinaemia, alkaptonuria and the acute porphyrias, and most of them are now screened for at birth in some jurisdictions.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Essential or not {level=1}
Explain why linoleic acid is essential in the human diet whereas stearic acid is not, and name two derivatives of linoleic acid worth remembering.
::: solution
Humans have desaturases only at positions Δ9 or closer to the carboxyl end, so no double bond can be introduced between C9 and the methyl end of the chain; the n-6 and n-3 fatty acids therefore must be eaten. Stearic acid, by contrast, is made by synthase and desaturated to oleate by Δ9 desaturase. Linoleic acid gives arachidionate, the precursor of prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes and lipoxins, and the eicosanoid signalling that NSAIDs and lipoxygenase inhibitors interrupt; it is also required for the epidermal barrier, which is why essential fatty-acid deficiency causes dermatitis.
:::
:::

::: exercise Carnitine {level=1}
A patient on long-term parenteral nutrition with no carnitine develops weakness, raised creatine kinase and hypoketotic hypoglycaemia during intercurrent illness. Why might carnitine depletion reproduce a CPT2-like picture?
::: solution
Long-chain fatty acyl groups must be carried across the inner mitochondrial membrane on carnitine. If free carnitine is low — as after years of dialysis or in carnitine-transport defects — the shuttle cannot deliver long-chain fat for oxidation, so muscle and liver cannot use their main fuel: weakness and rhabdomyolysis appear, ketone production falls because acetyl-CoA supply is low, and glucose falls because the liver must both export glucose and has little ATP from fat. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids, which enter without carnitine, are spared, which is why medium-chain triglyceride oil helps some of these patients and why the biochemical pattern is "hypoketotic" rather than "ketotic" hypoglycaemia.
:::
:::

::: exercise Friedewald {level=1 check="6"}
Total cholesterol 8.0 mmol/L, HDL 1.0 mmol/L, triglycerides 2.2 mmol/L. Calculate the LDL cholesterol.
::: solution
$\text{LDL} = 8.0 - 1.0 - 2.2/2.2 = 6.0$ mmol/L. The equation fails when triglycerides exceed about 4.5 mmol/L (chylomicrons and very-large VLDL make the estimate invalid), in which case a direct LDL measurement or a preparative method is needed.
:::

::: exercise Where does the carbon go? {level=2}
Say whether each substrate can give net glucose in humans, and justify briefly: (a) palmitate; (b) glycerol; (c) leucine; (d) odd-chain fatty acid; (e) ethanol.
::: solution
(a) No: acetyl-CoA cannot be converted net to oxaloacetate, since the two decarboxylations of the cycle are irreversible; the ATP and reducing power it provides, however, let the liver run gluconeogenesis from other substrates. (b) Yes: glycerol is phosphorylated by glycerol kinase (abundant in liver) and enters at dihydroxyacetone phosphate. (c) No: leucine is purely ketogenic. (d) Partly: the propionyl-CoA from the terminal three carbons yields succinyl-CoA and thus glucose, so the odd end is glucogenic while the even part is not. (e) No in net terms: ethanol is oxidised to acetate and then to acetyl-CoA, and it also raises the NADH/NAD ratio, which diverts oxaloacetate to malate and favours ketogenesis and lactate accumulation — one reason alcoholic ketoacidosis develops.
:::
:::

::: exercise Urea cycle arithmetic {level=2 check="4"}
How many high-energy phosphate bonds are consumed per molecule of urea synthesised?
::: solution
Four: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I uses 2 ATP converted to ADP + Pi, and argininosuccinate synthetase converts ATP to AMP + PPi, equivalent to 2 more. The energy cost explains why the cycle is up-regulated in high protein intake and catabolism, and why severe liver failure, not merely reduced intake, is needed before ammonia rises.
:::

::: exercise Interpret the lipid profile {level=2}
Three patients: (A) triglycerides 24 mmol/L, milking plasma, dull abdominal pain, glucose 22 mmol/L; (B) LDL 6.4 mmol/L from age 20, normal triglycerides, Achilles tendon thickening; (C) triglycerides 4.0 mmol/L, HDL 0.7 mmol/L, waist 106 cm, blood pressure 148/92, glucose 6.8 mmol/L. Identify the pattern and the immediate risk in each.
::: solution
A: severe hypertriglyceridaemia with chylomicronaemia, uncontrolled diabetes being the commonest precipitant through insulin deficiency reducing lipoprotein lipase activity — the immediate risk is acute pancreatitis, so the targets are pain control, insulin and stopping fat, not a statin. B: the picture of heterozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia, where the risk is premature atherosclerosis and management is high-intensity lipid-lowering with family screening. C: the dyslipidaemia of metabolic syndrome (high triglycerides, low HDL, small dense LDL), where absolute cardiovascular risk drives treatment, weight and activity change is the most effective intervention, and a statin is chosen on calculated risk rather than on the LDL alone. Note that only one of these patients needs the LDL number most.
:::

::: exercise Why the liver makes ketones it cannot use {level=3}
Give the biochemical and the evolutionary reasoning for the liver's lack of succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase.
::: solution
Biochemically, ketolysis requires the transferase to activate acetoacetate to acetoacetyl-CoA using succinyl-CoA; the enzyme is absent from hepatocyte cytosol, so hepatic acetoacetate has nowhere to go but out. Its HMG-CoA synthase, in contrast, is mitochondrial and highly active, and its phosphatases release ketone bodies. Evolutionarily, a tissue that consumed its own export would defeat the purpose: ketone bodies exist to move fuel from liver to brain, heart and muscle when glucose is short, and if the liver were efficient at using them the whole pathway would be a futile cycle burning the very fat the animal is relying on. Reciprocal enzyme distribution is a common design for making a one-way flow — the same logic as the absence of hexokinase and presence of glucose-6-phosphatase in the liver, and of thiophorase in extrahepatic tissues.
:::

::: exercise Neonatal screening in practice {level=3}
A newborn screen at five days shows a phenylalanine of 320 µmol/L (normal < 120). The baby is well and breast feeding. Set out your reasoning and immediate actions, and explain the two most common pitfalls.
::: solution
Treat the result as significant until proven otherwise, but interpret it in context: the sample must be at least 24 hours old (earlier samples miss rising phenylalanine), and premature or growth-retarded babies, or those on parenteral nutrition or antibiotics, give both false positives and false negatives. Act by seeing the family the same day, arranging confirmatory quantitative plasma amino acids and urine pterins to distinguish classic phenylketonuria from a tetrahydrobiopterin metabolism defect, starting a low-phenylalanine formula with breastfeeding continued alongside it, and referring to the regional metabolic service. The two pitfalls that cause harm: (i) dismissing the result and rechecking in weeks, during which the child accumulates phenylalanine, the neurotoxicity of which is age-dependent; and (ii) treating a cofactor defect with diet alone, which corrects the blood level while the monoamine neurotransmitter synthesis stays deficient and the child develops progressive neurological disease despite a "normal" phenylalanine. Sapropterin responsiveness testing is used in some centres; either way, the target range, not the exclusion, is what protects the brain.
:::

::: exercise Starvation, from the first hour to day thirty {level=3}
Describe the changing fuel and the changing nitrogen balance from one hour after a meal to a month of total starvation, naming the hormones and pathways.
::: solution
The post-absorptive hours depend on hepatic glycogenolysis, driven by a falling insulin-to-glucagon ratio, with glucose spared for the brain and red cells while muscle takes up fatty acids and ketones begin to appear. By about a day glycogen is nearly exhausted, so gluconeogenesis from amino acids (muscle protein), glycerol and lactate becomes the main source of glucose, and ketone production rises as oxaloacetate is drawn into gluconeogenesis. Over days, brain adapts to oxidise ketone bodies for a substantial fraction of its requirement, which reduces the glucose demand and therefore the daily protein loss: nitrogen excretion falls from around 10–12 g/day to a new lower steady state, ketones reach 5–7 mmol/L without acidosis because insulin is not absent and bicarbonate regeneration keeps pace, and resting metabolic rate falls with the loss of lean tissue and of thyroid and sympathetic tone. By the third to fifth week, protein loss again becomes the constraint, adipose reserves are being readied for the last part of the journey, and refeeding becomes dangerous because insulin's return drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium intracellularly — see [[cell-biochemistry/nutrition]] and [[gastrointestinal/clinical-nutrition]].
:::
:::
