A patient's disease is very often a protein that does not work: missing, misshapen, mistargeted, unregulated, or blocked by a molecule designed to fit into its active site. Sickle cell disease is one amino acid in a chain. Scurvy is the failure of an enzyme to hydroxylate collagen because a cofactor is absent. Myeloma is a protein made by a clone and deposited in the kidney as amyloid. Methotrexate works because it fits dihydrofolate reductase better than the substrate does.

This lesson is therefore about two things: how a polypeptide chain becomes a working protein, and how enzymes behave once they exist. Both have equations, and both have a clinical examination attached to them.

## Amino acids: the alphabet

Proteins are polymers of twenty amino acids, joined head to tail by peptide bonds, each with the same backbone — amino group, α-carbon, carboxyl group — and a variable side chain. It is the side chains that make proteins chemically interesting, and that determine both the fold and the disease.

| Class | Side chains | What to remember clinically |
|---|---|---|
| Non-polar | Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Ile, Pro, Met, Phe, Trp | Buried in the core; glycine is needed wherever helices pack closely (its absence in collagen causes severe disease) |
| Polar, uncharged | Ser, Thr, Cys, Tyr, Asn, Gln | Sites of glycosylation, phosphorylation, disulfide bonds |
| Positively charged | Lys, Arg, His | DNA-binding; histidine buffers and sits in many active sites |
| Negatively charged | Asp, Glu | Bind calcium; often the mutated residue in laminin and fibrillin defects |
| Aromatic | Phe, Tyr, Trp | Absorb ultraviolet light at 280 nm (how protein is measured); precursors of catecholamines, thyroxine, melanin |

Two properties repay memorising. First, **proline** is an imine and imposes a kink; a run of them in a receptor's extracellular loop or in collagen is structurally decisive. Second, only **cysteine** carries a thiol with a pKa low enough to be reactive at physiological pH in a protein environment: it forms disulfide bonds, coordinates zinc, and is the target of alkylating drugs.

Proteins also carry chemistry added after translation: over 200 kinds of modification, of which the medically important ones are glycosylation (asparagine-linked in the endoplasmic reticulum, serine/threonine-linked in the Golgi), hydroxylation of proline and lysine in collagen (vitamin C–dependent), γ-carboxylation of glutamate residues in clotting factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C and S (vitamin K–dependent), phosphorylation in signalling, and ubiquitination in degradation. A modification is a switch, an address label, or a quality-control stamp — and its absence is a disease.

::: example A single residue, and what it does {#ex-hbs}
A child of West African ancestry has painful swelling of the hands and feet (dactylitis) at nine months, then recurrent painful crises; electrophoresis shows haemoglobin S. Explain the molecular lesion and why its consequences are so little to do with oxygen binding.
::: solution
The mutation is a Glu→Val substitution at position 6 of the β-globin chain: a charged, hydrophilic residue on the surface is replaced by a hydrophobic one. In deoxyhaemoglobin the new valine fits into a hydrophobic pocket on an adjacent tetramer, so deoxyhaemoglobin S polymerises into rods that distort the red cell into a sickle. The affinity for oxygen is close to normal; the disease comes from an *interaction surface* that should not exist — rigid cells that obstruct the microcirculation, haemolysis, functional asplenism, and the strokes, chest syndromes and renal medullary damage that follow. Note the two features that generalise: disease can arise from a new surface property rather than from loss of the active site; and the abnormality is expressed only in one conformational state of the protein (deoxygenation), which is why hypoxia, acidosis, dehydration and fever all precipitate crises.
:::
:::

## How a chain becomes a protein

::: definition Levels of protein structure {#def-structure}
**Primary structure** is the amino-acid sequence. **Secondary structure** is local, repeating conformation stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds: the α-helix and β-sheet, with turns and loops between them. **Tertiary structure** is the complete three-dimensional arrangement of one polypeptide. **Quaternary structure** is the arrangement of several polypeptide chains (subunits) in one functional protein, as in haemoglobin's α₂β₂.
:::

The forces holding a folded protein are individually weak and collectively specific: the hydrophobic effect (the main driving force, burying non-polar surface), hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, van der Waals contact in the tightly packed core, and disulfide bonds, which contribute stability rather than information and are mainly found in secreted and membrane-oxidising compartments.

::: theorem Anfinsen's dogma {#thm-anfinsen}
For a small globular protein in vitro, the amino-acid sequence contains sufficient information to specify the native conformation; the native state is the conformation of lowest free energy under the prevailing conditions.
:::

::: proof
Anfinsen reduced the disulfide bonds of ribonuclease and unfolded it in urea; on removing the denaturant and allowing re-oxidation, the enzyme refolded and regained activity, whereas if re-oxidation occurred while the chain was still unfolded, most product was inactive until a thiol-disulfide exchange shuffled it to the correct pairing. Sequence therefore determines fold, and the fold is thermodynamically selected. Two caveats make the clinical point. In the test tube the yield was only partial, because aggregation competes with folding; in the cell, molecular chaperones (Hsp70 on the nascent chain, the Hsp60/GroEL-like chaperonin barrel, calnexin and BiP in the endoplasmic reticulum) do not supply the information but prevent the wrong encounters while folding happens. And "lowest free energy" means the state is condition-dependent: change pH, temperature, redox state or concentration and a protein that was soluble may not be.
:::

Not all proteins are neat spheres. **Fibrous proteins** are structural and repeat in sequence: collagen (a Gly–X–Y repeat forming a triple helix, hydroxyproline-stabilised by hydrogen bonding, cross-linked after secretion by lysyl oxidase), keratins, elastin (with desmosine cross-links), and fibrin. Collagen alone is a quarter of body protein and the seat of a large part of inherited connective-tissue disease.

::: example Brittle bones and blue sclerae {#ex-oi}
A term infant has six fractures at birth, a compressible skull, blue sclerae and short limbs; the parents are well and unrelated. Collagen studies show a structurally normal α1 chain present in reduced amount, and a mutant α2 chain that migrates faster and slows helix formation. Which class of mutation is present, and why is it so damaging?
::: solution
This is osteogenesis imperfecta, most often due to a point substitution of the obligatory glycine in the Gly–X–Y repeat of *COL1A1* or *COL1A2*. Because the three chains wind with the glycines packed at the axis of the triple helix, any larger residue at that position blocks folding locally and, since chains assemble into heterotrimers, mutant chains poison the whole molecule — a **dominant-negative** ("structural") effect, so the phenotype can be severe in a child of unaffected parents through a new dominant mutation. By contrast, a *COL1A1* null allele simply halves the amount of normal collagen and gives mild disease (type I), because the collagen that is made is normal. The rule — null alleles are usually recessive, structural alleles dominant — recurs throughout matrix disease, including the Marfan and Ehlers–Danlos spectrum.
:::
:::

## When folding goes wrong

Cells have quality control: chaperones give a protein several attempts, misfolded molecules are retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and destroyed by endoplasmic-reticulum-associated degradation, and persistent misfolding triggers the unfolded protein response. Disease arises when this system is overwhelmed, when a mutation makes aggregation inevitable, or when a protein is unstable and degraded (a **loss-of-function conformational disease**: the most common severe cystic fibrosis allele, ΔF508, is largely a folding and trafficking failure, which is why "corrector" drugs aim at chaperone interactions rather than at the channel itself).

Aggregation diseases share a structural motif: a β-strand-rich amyloid fibril, in which the peptide backbone rather than the side chains does the bonding, so different proteins can form the same kind of fibril.

| Amyloid | Precursor protein | Clinical pattern |
|---|---|---|
| AL | Immunoglobulin light chain | Myeloma; nephrotic syndrome, cardiomyopathy, macroglossia |
| ATTR | Transthyretin (wild-type or variant) | Familial amyloid polyneuropathy; wild-type cardiac amyloidosis in older men |
| Aβ, tau | APP; tau | Alzheimer disease |
| α-synuclein | α-synuclein | Parkinson disease, multiple system atrophy |
| Islet amyloid polypeptide | IAPP | Islet amyloid in type 2 diabetes |
| PrP | Prion protein | Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, variant CJD, kuru |

**Prions** are the extreme case: a misfolded conformer of a normal host protein (PrP^C^, mostly α-helical, to PrP^Sc^, β-rich) that templated the same change in other molecules. With no nucleic acid involved, the "infection" carries a strain of conformation, is inherited when the gene is mutated, and is transmissible by tissue that contains the protein — hence the iatrogenic clusters from cadaveric dura and growth hormone, and the resistance of the aggregate to autoclaving, formalin and protease.

::: warning A protein being "abnormal on electrophoresis" is not a diagnosis of the mechanism
Haemoglobin S, an abnormal immunoglobulin band, a mutant transthyretin, a positive PrP immunohistochemistry: each tells you the protein is present and has different charge, size or conformation. None tells you whether the disease is due to loss of normal function, toxic gain of function, or both — and that distinction determines whether the treatment is replacement (factor VIII), stabilisation and correctors (CFTR), silencing (the antisense approaches in ATTR), or clearance of the precursor (the clone in AL amyloidosis). Ask which mechanism you are treating before accepting a test result as a plan.
:::

## Enzymes: what a catalyst may and may not do

An enzyme accelerates the approach to equilibrium. It cannot alter the equilibrium, because it stabilises the **transition state** equally in both directions and lowers the activation energy ΔG‡; the standard free-energy change ΔG°′ of the reaction is untouched. Rates are increased by factors of 10⁶–10¹², by a combination of proximity and orientation, strained substrate binding, acid–base catalysis by residues such as histidine, covalent catalysis (pyridoxal phosphate, biotin, thiamine pyrophosphate, lipoate), and the exclusion of water.

Vitamins are largely the raw material of these cofactors: thiamine → TPP, riboflavin → FAD/FMN, niacin → NAD⁺/NADP⁺, pantothenate → coenzyme A, pyridoxine → pyridoxal phosphate, biotin, folate, lipoate, and the metal ions Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺/³⁺, Cu²⁺, Se (as selenocysteine in glutathione peroxidase). This is why a deficiency produces a syndrome of several pathways at once, and why "which cofactor does this enzyme use?" is a predictable exam question and a good clinical one.

::: definition Michaelis–Menten kinetics {#def-mm}
For $E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \to E + P$, the initial velocity at substrate concentration $[S]$ is
$$
v_{0} = \frac{V_{\max}\,[S]}{K_{m} + [S]},
$$ {#eq-mm}
where $V_{\max} = k_{\text{cat}}[E]_{\text{T}}$ and $K_m = (k_{-1} + k_{2})/k_{1}$, the substrate concentration at half-maximal velocity. $K_m$ approximates the dissociation constant of ES when $k_2$ is small; $k_{\text{cat}}/K_m$ is the specificity constant, and is capped by the rate of diffusion.
:::

::: proof
Assume that after a brief transient the concentration of ES is steady, so that formation ($k_1[E][S]$) equals loss ($k_{-1}[ES] + k_2[ES]$):
$$
k_{1}[E][S] = (k_{-1} + k_{2})[ES]
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
[E] = \frac{(k_{-1}+k_{2})[ES]}{k_{1}[S]} = \frac{K_m[ES]}{[S]}.
$$
Total enzyme is $[E]_{\mathrm T} = [E] + [ES]$, so $[E]_{\mathrm T} = [ES]\left(1 + K_m/[S]\right)$ and
$$
[ES] = \frac{[E]_{\mathrm T}[S]}{K_{m} + [S]}.
$$
The rate of product formation is $v_{0} = k_{2}[ES] = V_{\max}[S]/(K_{m}+[S])$. Note what the derivation needed: $[S] \gg [E]_{\mathrm T}$, initial velocity so that little product has accumulated, and the steady-state assumption. When $[S] \ll K_m$ the equation is first order in $[S]$; when $[S] \gg K_m$ it is zero order, and the enzyme is working flat out.
:::

This single plot underlies most of clinical pharmacology's talk about "overcoming" an antagonist.
:::

::: definition Types of reversible inhibition {#def-inhibition}
**Competitive** inhibition: inhibitor binds the active site; apparent $K_m$ increases, $V_{\max}$ unchanged, surmountable by more substrate (statins at HMG-CoA reductase; methotrexate at dihydrofolate reductase; ethanol at alcohol dehydrogenase). The same algebra describes reversible receptor antagonism, as with naloxone and flumazenil, which is why "competitive" and "surmountable" appear in both pharmacology and biochemistry.
**Non-competitive** inhibition: inhibitor binds elsewhere and reduces the number of functional enzyme molecules; $V_{\max}$ falls, $K_m$ unchanged.
**Uncompetitive** inhibition: inhibitor binds only the ES complex, so apparent $V_{\max}$ and $K_m$ both fall by the same factor. The pattern is uncommon for simple one-substrate enzymes but frequent in two-substrate mechanisms, and is what a drug shows if it binds the enzyme–substrate complex rather than free enzyme.
**Irreversible** inhibition: covalent or permanent inactivation; recovery requires new protein synthesis, so the effect outlasts the plasma concentration and the dose matters more than the level (aspirin on cyclo-oxygenase, clopidogrel on P2Y12, organophosphates on acetylcholinesterase, omeprazole on the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase).
:::

::: example Two patients, two kinds of block {#ex-inh}
Patient A, on simvastatin 40 mg, has an LDL of 2.6 mmol/L; doubling the dose to 80 mg gives only a further 6% reduction. Patient B, on low-dose aspirin daily, has platelet thromboxane suppression that is complete and lasts the whole life of the platelet although aspirin's plasma half-life is about 20 minutes. Explain both in terms of enzyme kinetics.
::: solution
Statins compete with HMG-CoA, the substrate, for HMG-CoA reductase. Raising inhibitor concentration while substrate is unchanged shifts the apparent $K_m$ but cannot push $V_{\max}$ lower, and hepatic cholesterol feedback up-regulates the transporter and the enzyme; the dose–response is therefore shallow and roughly log-linear at therapeutic doses — the "rule of 6%", where doubling the statin dose buys about 6% more LDL reduction. Aspirin acetylates a serine in the cyclo-oxygenase channel irreversibly. A platelet has no nucleus and cannot synthesise new enzyme, so the affected platelets are silenced for their 8–10 day lifespan; recovery depends on new platelets, which is why bleeding time is prolonged for a week and why the drug is dosed once daily. The clinical corollaries are different too: A's response can be improved by removing substrate (ezetimibe, bile-acid sequestrants, PCSK9 antibodies lowering the demand side) rather than by more of the same drug, whereas B's effect cannot be undone by anything except transfusion, and other NSAIDs occupying the same site will blunt aspirin's irreversible acetylation if taken before it.
:::
:::

## Regulation: how the cell sets enzyme activity

A pathway is useful only if its flux can be changed. The mechanisms, in increasing order of how fast they act:

- **Substrate availability**: many enzymes operate below $K_m$ or at a step whose substrate concentration varies (rate-limited flux in the urea cycle; the mass action of glucose in glycolysis in pancreatic β-cells and hepatocytes, where glucokinase's high $K_m$ makes it the sensor).
- **Allosteric control**: binding of an effector at a site other than the active site changes conformation. Allosteric enzymes give **sigmoidal** rather than hyperbolic kinetics, described by the Hill coefficient $n_{\mathrm H}$, and are usually the committed, regulated step — phosphofructokinase-1 with ATP, citrate and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate; aspartate transcarbamoylase; haemoglobin with oxygen, protons, CO₂ and 2,3-BPG.
- **Covalent modification**: phosphorylation by kinases and removal by phosphatases, under hormonal control (glucagon and adrenaline via cyclic AMP; insulin via kinase cascades), plus adenylylation, uridylylation and redox changes. The amplification is the point: one hormone molecule moves many enzyme molecules.
- **Control of amount**: induction and repression of transcription (steroid and thyroid hormone receptors), and control of degradation via ubiquitin ligases — the mechanism by which cyclins, IκB and hypoxia-inducible factor are given their sharp time courses.
- **Compartmentation and sequestration**: glycolysis in cytosol, β-oxidation in mitochondria, fatty-acid synthesis in cytosol, with shuttle systems and transporters as regulated gates (carnitine palmitoyltransferase I inhibited by malonyl-CoA is a favourite exam answer and the reason for the muscle's fat-burning switches).
- **Zymogen activation**: irreversible proteolytic cleavage, as in digestive enzymes and coagulation, where activation must not be reversible and is locally inhibited (antithrombin, α₁-antitrypsin).

::: example Enzyme missing from the wrong place {#ex-g6pd}
A young man of Mediterranean ancestry takes primaquine for malaria prophylaxis and develops back pain, dark urine and a haemoglobin fall from 145 to 78 g/L with bite cells and Heinz bodies; his blood film six weeks later is normal. Explain the kinetics and the inheritance, and why the episode stops by itself.
::: solution
Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase is the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway and the only source of NADPH in the red cell. NADPH keeps glutathione reduced, and reduced glutathione removes hydrogen peroxide via glutathione peroxidase (a selenoenzyme) — the cell's protection against oxidant load. Without it, haemoglobin is oxidised and precipitates as Heinz bodies, which splenic macrophages bite out (bite cells) and the membrane damage causes intravascular and extravascular haemolysis. The deficiency is X-linked, so haemizygous males are affected and heterozygous females are variable because of X-inactivation. The attack self-limits because the enzyme is most active in young red cells: reticulocytes that the marrow releases have relatively more activity and survive the oxidant stress, so the haemoglobin stabilises even while the drug continues — a nice demonstration that an enzyme's clinical severity depends on the kinetics of the cell population that contains it. The same logic explains neonatal jaundice in the condition and the need to check for it before giving dapsone, rasburicase, primaquine or nitrofurantoin, and why fava beans can do the same.
:::
:::

::: quiz
An enzyme assay gives a $V_{\max}$ of 120 µmol/min and a $K_m$ of 2 mmol/L in the absence of a drug. In the presence of the drug, $V_{\max}$ is unchanged at 120 µmol/min and the apparent $K_m$ is 8 mmol/L. What has happened?
- [ ] The drug is a non-competitive inhibitor.
- [x] The drug is a competitive inhibitor and could be out-competed by raising substrate about four-fold.
- [ ] The drug is an irreversible inhibitor.
- [ ] The drug increases $k_{\text{cat}}$.
::: solution
Unchanged $V_{\max}$ with an increased apparent $K_m$ is the signature of competitive inhibition: at saturating substrate the inhibitor is displaced and the same maximum rate is reached. The apparent $K_m$ has risen 4-fold, so roughly 4-fold more substrate is needed for half-maximal velocity — hence the phrase "surmountable". An irreversible or non-competitive inhibitor would have lowered $V_{\max}$, and changing $k_{\text{cat}}$ would change $V_{\max}$ rather than $K_m$.
:::
:::

## Measuring enzymes in the clinic

Enzymes are measured in plasma not because their plasma activity matters but because their presence there means leaky cells. Three conventions matter: the **IU** (1 µmol of substrate converted per minute under stated conditions), the requirement to report the method and reference interval (they vary a lot between laboratories), and the fact that a fall in an enzyme can mean failure of the organ making it (a low alkaline phosphatase in a malnourished child, or low cholinesterase in liver failure or organophosphate poisoning).

Isoenzymes give some tissue specificity: creatine kinase MB and troponin in myocardium; alkaline phosphatase of bone, liver and placenta; acid phosphatase of prostate; LDH isoenzyme patterns (now largely replaced by imaging and specific tests). Because enzymes disappear from plasma with their own half-lives, the *time course* is diagnostic: creatine kinase rises within hours of a myocardial infarction, and troponin stays up for one to two weeks.

::: history
Wilhelm Kühne named the yeast-derived activity "enzyme" in 1877, and Eduard Buchner showed in 1897 that cell-free yeast juice ferments sugar. Emil Fischer's lock-and-key model of 1894 was widened by Daniel Koshland's induced fit in 1958. Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten published their kinetics of invertase in 1913, in Berlin; G. E. Briggs and J. B. S. Haldane put the treatment on the steady-state footing used in this lesson in 1925, and Linus Pauling explained catalysis by transition-state stabilisation in 1946. James Sumner's crystallisation of urease in 1926 and John Northrop's of pepsin established enzymes as proteins. Sickle cell disease became the first "molecular disease" — Linus Pauling's biochemical demonstration in 1949 that the haemoglobin of affected people moves differently, and Ingram's identification of the single amino-acid change in 1956 — and Christian Anfinsen's ribonuclease folding experiments (Nobel Prize, 1972) made protein folding an experimental science. Stanley Prusiner's prion concept, awarded the 1993 Nobel Prize, is the strangest offspring of the same idea: a phenotype carried by conformation alone.
:::

## Where this leads

How membranes and transporters obey the same kinetics is in [[cell-biochemistry/membranes-transport]]; how the enzymes described here are regulated by insulin and glucagon in [[cell-biochemistry/metabolic-integration]]; how mutations in collagen and fibrillin damage the aorta and skeleton in [[pathology/cell-injury]] and [[musculoskeletal/connective-tissue-vasculitis]]; how inhibitors become medicines in [[pharmacology/drug-receptor]].

::: summary
- Twenty amino acids, one backbone, and side chains that determine charge, solubility, reactivity (cysteine) and the ability to pack (glycine, proline).
- Structure has four levels; the hydrophobic effect drives folding, and Anfinsen's dogma says the sequence is sufficient — chaperones stop it aggregating on the way, and conditions can undo the fold.
- Collagen is the most abundant protein and its glycine repeats explain why single substitutions act dominantly-negative.
- Misfolding causes disease by loss of function (ΔF508), by toxic aggregation (amyloid), or by transmissible conformation (prion); the mechanism determines the treatment.
- Enzymes lower activation energy, never ΔG°′; Michaelis–Menten kinetics follow from the steady-state assumption, and $K_m$, $V_{\max}$ and $k_{\text{cat}}/K_m$ each mean something specific.
- Inhibitors are classified by what they do to $K_m$ and $V_{\max}$; irreversible inhibition makes duration depend on protein turnover, not plasma half-life.
- Flux is regulated by substrate, allosteric effectors, covalent modification, enzyme amount, compartmentation and zymogen activation.
- Plasma enzymes are markers of cell damage; isoenzymes and time course provide the localisation.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Charge and electrophoresis {level=1}
At pH 7.4, state the charge of the side chains of Asp, Lys, His and Tyr, and explain why a Glu→Val substitution makes haemoglobin migrate more slowly towards the anode.
::: solution
Asp is negative (pKa ~3.9), Lys positive (~10.5), His largely unprotonated and neutral at pH 7.4 although its pKa (~6.0) makes it an effective buffer and active-site participant near physiological pH, and Tyr is essentially unionised (pKa ~10) though the phenolate form of a few active-site tyrosines matters. Glu→Val replaces a negative charge with a neutral hydrophobic side chain, reducing the net negative charge of the β chain; in alkaline electrophoresis haemoglobin S therefore moves more slowly towards the anode than haemoglobin A, which is how the classic tests separate them.
:::
:::

::: exercise Four levels {level=1}
Give one example of a disease associated with each level of structure (primary, secondary/quaternary assembly, post-translational modification, degradation/sorting).
::: solution
Primary: sickle cell disease (single substitution) or β-thalassaemia nonsense mutation. Assembly: osteogenesis imperfecta or Marfan syndrome (mutant chains poison the multimer); also the quaternary lesions in pyruvate kinase deficiency. Post-translational: scurvy (uncross-linked, under-hydroxylated collagen), osteogenesis imperfecta type II, or bleeding from vitamin K deficiency (under-carboxylated factors). Degradation/sorting: ΔF508 cystic fibrosis (retained and degraded), I-cell disease (missing mannose-6-phosphate address), and the ER storage diseases.
:::

::: exercise Reading a plot {level=1}
An enzyme has $V_{\max}$ 200 U and $K_m$ 5 mM. What fraction of $V_{\max}$ is reached at 5 mM and at 15 mM? Sketch why a 2-fold increase in $K_m$ changes little at high substrate.
::: solution
At $[S] = K_m$, $v/V_{\max} = 0.5$ (100 U). At 15 mM, $v/V_{\max} = 15/20 = 0.75$ (150 U). At substrate well above $K_m$ the enzyme is nearly saturated, so changing $K_m$ by a factor of two moves the fraction bound only slightly ($15/(10+15) = 0.6$ vs 0.75 — a real but smaller effect than at low substrate). This is why competitive inhibition matters most when substrate concentration is near or below $K_m$, and why drug action at such enzymes depends on the physiological substrate level (as with sucrase inhibition and dietary carbohydrate).
:::
:::

::: exercise Enzyme units {level=2 check="4000"}
A 3 mL assay cuvette contains 30 µL of serum. Over 2 minutes the product increases by 0.24 µmol. Report the activity in the serum sample in U/L (1 U = 1 µmol/min).
::: solution
Rate in the cuvette $= 0.24/2 = 0.12\ \mu\text{mol} = 0.12$ U. This is the activity of the 30 µL of serum, so per litre: $0.12 / (30 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{L}) = 4000$ U/L. Note the two dilution factors that students lose marks on: sample volume and the reaction time; the cuvette's total volume does not enter.
:::

::: exercise Cooperativity and the Hill plot {level=2}
Haemoglobin has $n_{\mathrm H} \approx 2.8$ for oxygen binding and myoglobin a rectangular hyperbola. Explain the structural basis of the cooperativity, the physiological benefit, and what 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate does.
::: solution
Cooperativity arises because oxygen binding shifts the tense (T) quaternary state of the tetramer to the relaxed (R) state, in which the haem groups fit better and the salt bridges that resist oxygen binding are broken; the first binding event makes the next more likely, so $n_{\mathrm H} > 1$ and the curve is sigmoid. The benefit is a loading–unloading design: near-saturation in the lung where $p_{\mathrm O_2}$ is high, and large unloading for a small fall in $p_{\mathrm O_2}$ in tissues. Myoglobin, a monomer, must be hyperbolic and holds oxygen tightly at tissue $p_{\mathrm O_2}$ — a store, not a transporter. 2,3-BPG binds in the central cavity of the T state, stabilising it and shifting the curve right (lower affinity), which is why it rises in chronic hypoxia and in stored blood; the fetal haemoglobin γ-subunits bind 2,3-BPG less well, so fetal blood has higher affinity and takes oxygen across the placenta.
:::
:::

::: exercise Chaperone therapy {level=2}
Explain why a "corrector plus potentiator" regimen can improve ΔF508 cystic fibrosis but not a nonsense mutation such as G542X, and say which class of drug addresses the latter.
::: solution
ΔF508 deletes a phenylalanine in nucleotide-binding domain 1; the channel, if it reaches the membrane, works reasonably well, but folding and assembly fail and the protein is degraded by endoplasmic-reticulum quality control. Correctors bind hydrophobic patches and help the protein escape degradation and reach the surface; potentiators increase the open probability of whatever arrives. G542X truncates the protein, so there is no full-length molecule to rescue: readthrough agents (aminoglycoside-like and newer compounds) aim to make the ribosome ignore the stop codon, and antisense or gene-replacement approaches bypass it; the phenotype also depends on how much normal protein is made, which is why the same mutation can have variable expressivity. The general principle — treat the mechanism, not the gene — is the reason mutation-specific therapy has replaced a single "cystic fibrosis drug".
:::

::: exercise A fold that will not fold {level=3}
A patient with nephrotic syndrome and cardiomyopathy has Congo-red-positive, apple-green birefringent deposits that stain for lambda light chain. Another patient, aged 78, has heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction, bilateral carpal-tunnel history and a similar Congo-red stain that types as transthyretin. Compare the therapeutic logic.
::: solution
Both are amyloid, and both deposits are made of misfolded protein rich in β-strand — but the precursors, the production site and the reversibility differ. AL amyloid comes from a plasma-cell clone, so the treatment is haematological: suppress or destroy the clone (bortezomib-, daratumumab- or stem-cell-based regimens), accepting that existing deposits resolve slowly and that organ function may not recover; response is monitored by free light chain and by imaging. ATTR comes from a liver-made transport protein, so the options are to stabilise the tetramer (tafamilide, which reduces dissociation into amyloidogenic monomers), silence the hepatic mRNA (patisiran, inotersen/vutrisiran), edit the gene, or in selected cardiac cases transplant. In wild-type ATTR the "why now" is age and the male predominance, and the carpal tunnel years before heart failure is a diagnostic clue worth remembering. Neither patient is treated by removing fibrils, and neither by correcting a folding defect — the mechanism defines the target.
:::

::: exercise Enzyme kinetics in poisoning {level=3}
A patient is thought to have drunk methanol. Ethanol is sometimes given to block its metabolism. Use the concept of $K_m$ and of competitive inhibition to explain why this works, why the treatment must continue for many hours even though methanol levels fall slowly, and what the modern alternative is.
::: solution
Methanol itself is fairly harmless; toxicity is produced by alcohol dehydrogenase and then aldehyde dehydrogenase, giving formaldehyde and formate, whose accumulation causes the visual loss and metabolic acidosis. Ethanol is a competitive substrate for the same enzyme with much higher affinity than methanol, so occupying the active site blocks formation of the toxic metabolites; the parent methanol is then cleared slowly by the kidneys and lungs, with a half-life of hours, which is why the ethanol infusion must be continued until methanol is no longer detectable, and why haemodialysis is used to remove methanol (and formate) when levels are high or the patient is acidotic and visual symptoms present. Fomepizole is now the usual agent: a competitive alcohol-dehydrogenase inhibitor with far greater potency than ethanol, predictable kinetics and no sedation, so it does not require monitoring of a therapeutic alcohol concentration. Note the twist on the quiz above — here the "substrate" you want to keep off the enzyme is the poison, and the inhibitor you give is itself a competing substrate.

::: exercise Dominant-negative arithmetic {level=3}
If a structural collagen mutation produces mutant α2 chains that assemble at random with normal α1 chains into heterotrimers of composition α1(I)₂α2(I), what fraction of trimers contain at least one mutant chain? Predict why the phenotype of such a mutation is worse than 50% haploinsufficiency.
::: solution
With equal amounts of mutant and normal α2 chain, the probability that a given trimer contains two normal α2 chains is $(1/2)^{2} = 1/4$, so $3/4$ of trimers carry at least one mutant chain and are structurally abnormal, while the total amount of collagen may be near normal. That is worse than a null allele, which halves the quantity but leaves every molecule normal — hence the clinical gradient from mild brittle-bone disease (quantitative defect) to lethal perinatal disease (structural defect in the same genes). The arithmetic generalises to any multimeric protein in which subunits mix: haemoglobin, laminin, connexins and p53 all show dominant-negative behaviour, and the fraction affected depends on the stoichiometry.
