Delirium and dementia are the two commonest causes of confusion in older people, and they often occur together. Delirium is an acute brain failure, usually caused by a physical illness or a drug, and it is a medical emergency. Dementia is a chronic, progressive decline in cognition that changes how a person experiences illness, hospital and treatment. Up to a quarter of older people in hospital have delirium, and a similar proportion have dementia; many have both.

This chapter covers how delirium is recognised, why it happens, how it is prevented and managed, and how to care for people with dementia in general medical settings. It ends with the legal framework for decisions when a person lacks capacity. The diseases that cause dementia are covered in [[nervous-system/dementia-neurodegeneration]].

## Delirium {#sec-delirium}

::: definition Delirium {#def-delirium}
**Delirium** is an acute disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to days, tends to fluctuate during the day, and is accompanied by a further disturbance of cognition (such as memory, orientation, language or perception). There is evidence that it is a direct physiological consequence of a medical condition, a drug or drug withdrawal, or several causes together. (This summarises the DSM-5-TR criteria; ICD-11 is similar.)
:::

Inattention is the core feature. A delirious person cannot hold focus: she loses the thread of a conversation, cannot say the months of the year backwards, and is easily distracted. Other features include disorganised thinking, altered level of consciousness, disturbed sleep–wake cycle, hallucinations (usually visual), delusions, and emotional changes such as fear or irritability.

Delirium has three **motor subtypes**:

- **Hyperactive delirium**: agitation, restlessness, wandering, shouting, pulling at lines. It is noticed because it is disruptive.
- **Hypoactive delirium**: drowsiness, withdrawal, slowness, reduced eating and drinking. It is the commonest subtype in older people and the one most often missed, because the person seems "quiet" or "tired". It also carries the worst prognosis.
- **Mixed delirium**: fluctuating between the two.

Delirium matters because it is common and dangerous. It is associated with longer hospital stays, falls, pressure injuries, new admission to a care home, a faster decline in cognition in people with dementia, and higher mortality. Many people remember their delirium as frightening.

::: theorem Delirium arises from vulnerability and insult {#thm-model}
The risk of delirium depends on two sets of factors that interact: **predisposing factors** (the person's vulnerability) and **precipitating factors** (the insults during the illness). A highly vulnerable person can become delirious after a minor insult, such as a new sedative or a night in an unfamiliar ward; a robust person needs a major insult, such as severe sepsis or major surgery. (This model was described by Inouye and Charpentier in 1996.)
:::

::: proof
Predisposing factors include older age, dementia or other cognitive impairment, frailty, severe illness, visual and hearing impairment, and alcohol misuse. Each reduces the brain's reserve ([[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty#thm-threshold]]). The mechanisms by which precipitants then cause brain failure are not fully understood, but include an imbalance of neurotransmitters (cholinergic deficiency and relative dopamine excess, which explains why anticholinergic drugs cause delirium and dopamine antagonists can reduce agitation), inflammation spreading from the body to the brain, and impaired brain energy supply in hypoxia, hypoglycaemia and hypoperfusion. Because the threshold depends on reserve, the most vulnerable patients often have several small precipitants, each of which must be found and treated.
:::

### Causes

Delirium usually has more than one cause. The search should be systematic:

- **Infection**: chest, urinary, skin and soft tissue, and others.
- **Drugs**: anticholinergic drugs, opioids, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs, corticosteroids, antiparkinsonian drugs, and many others; also **withdrawal** from alcohol, benzodiazepines or antidepressants.
- **Metabolic**: hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hypo- and hyperglycaemia, dehydration, acute kidney injury, liver failure, thyroid disease.
- **Hypoxia and hypoperfusion**: pneumonia, heart failure, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, anaemia.
- **Brain**: stroke, subdural haematoma, seizures (including non-convulsive status epilepticus).
- **Retention and pain**: urinary retention, constipation, and untreated pain.
- **Environment**: unfamiliar surroundings, ward moves, sleep deprivation, lack of glasses or hearing aids, physical restraints and catheters.

### Detection

Delirium is missed in about half of cases when staff rely on informal impression. Routine screening of older people at admission, and whenever a change is noticed, improves detection. In the UK the **4AT** is widely used and is recommended by SIGN (guideline 157, 2019). It takes about two minutes and needs no special training.

::: definition The 4AT {#def-4at}
The **4AT** has four items:

1. **Alertness**: normal (0) or clearly abnormal, for example drowsy or agitated (4).
2. **AMT4**: age, date of birth, place, current year: no mistakes (0), one mistake (1), two or more mistakes or untestable (2).
3. **Attention**: months of the year backwards from December: seven or more correct (0), starts but scores fewer than seven or refuses (1), untestable because too unwell, drowsy or inattentive (2).
4. **Acute change or fluctuating course** in alertness, cognition or other mental function over the past two weeks and still present in the past 24 hours: no (0), yes (4).

A total of **4 or more** suggests possible delirium, with or without cognitive impairment. A score of 1–3 suggests possible cognitive impairment. A score of 0 makes delirium or severe cognitive impairment unlikely, although delirium is still possible if the information on item 4 is incomplete.
:::

In a meta-analysis of studies in older people (Tieges and colleagues, 2021), the 4AT had a pooled sensitivity and specificity of about 88% each. The **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)**, developed by Inouye in 1990, is an alternative that requires acute onset and fluctuation, inattention, and either disorganised thinking or altered consciousness. Screening tools are aids: a positive result calls for a clinical assessment, and an account from a relative or carer of the person's usual state is essential.

::: example The woman who was "just tired" {#ex-hypoactive}
Mrs Lin Zhou, 82, was admitted three days ago with a fractured pubic ramus after a fall. The nurses report that she is "no trouble": she sleeps most of the day and has eaten little. Her son says she was doing the crossword every morning before admission. She is drowsy but rousable, gets her age wrong but gives her date of birth, the year and the hospital correctly, and cannot say the months backwards beyond November. She is taking regular codeine. Score the 4AT and outline the next steps.
::: solution
**4AT.** Alertness: drowsy, so clearly abnormal (4). AMT4: one mistake (1). Months backwards: she starts but gets fewer than seven correct (1). Acute change: her son describes a clear change from her baseline (4). Total $4 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 10$, which indicates probable delirium. This is **hypoactive delirium**, easily mistaken for tiredness.

**Next steps.** Look for causes ([[#thm-model]]): codeine (constipation, urinary retention and direct sedation), dehydration and poor intake, pain from the fracture, infection, hyponatraemia and other metabolic disturbances, and hypoxia. Examine her, check bladder volume and bowels, and send blood tests including sodium, calcium, glucose and renal function. Replace codeine with better-tolerated analgesia (for example regular paracetamol, with a low-dose opioid only if needed, and a laxative). Encourage fluids and food, get her up with physiotherapy, make sure she has her glasses and hearing aids, involve her son, and reassess with the 4AT daily. Tell the family what delirium is and that it usually improves when the causes are treated, though recovery may take days to weeks.
:::
:::

### Prevention and management

About a third of delirium in hospital can be prevented. NICE guideline CG103 (last updated 2023) recommends assessing people at risk on admission and providing a **multicomponent intervention** tailored to their risk factors, delivered by a familiar team and avoiding unnecessary ward moves.

::: definition Multicomponent delirium prevention {#def-prevention}
**Multicomponent delirium prevention** addresses each modifiable risk factor: orientation (clocks, calendars, explaining where the person is and why) and cognitive stimulation; avoiding dehydration and constipation; assessing for hypoxia; looking for and treating infection; early mobilisation; assessing and treating pain; reviewing medicines; good nutrition, with dentures fitted; correcting sensory impairment with glasses and hearing aids; and promoting good sleep by reducing noise and avoiding procedures and medication rounds at night.
:::

The model for this approach is the **Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP)**. In a controlled trial of 852 older medical inpatients (Inouye and colleagues, 1999), delirium developed in 9.9% of the intervention group and 15.0% of the usual-care group.

The **management** of established delirium has three parts:

1. **Find and treat the causes.** This is the only treatment that addresses the problem.
2. **Supportive care.** Use the same measures as prevention; involve family; keep the person safe without restraint where possible; explain what is happening.
3. **Treat distress.** If a person is very distressed or at risk of harming themselves or others, and talking and other non-drug measures have failed, NICE suggests a short course (usually up to a week) of haloperidol at the lowest effective dose, titrated cautiously according to local guidance. Antipsychotics do not treat delirium itself; they only reduce severe agitation. They must be avoided or used only with specialist advice in people with Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies. Benzodiazepines can worsen delirium and are reserved for specific situations, such as alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, or when antipsychotics are contraindicated.

::: warning Sedation is not a diagnosis
A common error is to treat an agitated, confused older person with a sedative without looking for the cause. Sedation can hide hypoxia, urinary retention, pain or a full bladder, and may turn hyperactive delirium into hypoactive delirium with aspiration and falls. Ask first: what has changed in this person's body, drugs or surroundings?
:::

## Dementia in general care {#sec-dementia}

::: definition Dementia {#def-dementia}
**Dementia** (major neurocognitive disorder in DSM-5-TR) is an acquired, usually progressive decline in cognition, in one or more domains (memory, executive function, language, visuospatial ability, attention or social cognition), that is severe enough to interfere with independence in everyday activities and is not explained by delirium or another mental disorder.
:::

Close to a million people in the UK are estimated to live with dementia, and the number rises as the population ages. The commonest causes are Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, mixed Alzheimer's and vascular disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, and frontotemporal dementia ([[nervous-system/dementia-neurodegeneration]]).

### Delirium, dementia or depression?

The three are easily confused, and they often coexist.

| | Delirium | Dementia | Depression |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Acute (hours to days) | Gradual (months to years) | Weeks to months |
| Course | Fluctuating | Slowly progressive | Persistent low mood |
| Attention | Impaired: the core feature | Usually normal until late | Reduced concentration, but can attend with effort |
| Consciousness | Often altered | Normal | Normal |
| Typical answers to tests | Inattentive, distractible | Tries but cannot recall | "I don't know"; little effort |

Dementia is the strongest risk factor for delirium, and delirium speeds up the decline of dementia. A diagnosis of dementia should not be made during an episode of delirium.

### Treatment of dementia

The **acetylcholinesterase inhibitors** donepezil, galantamine and rivastigmine are recommended by NICE (NG97, 2018) for mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease, and for dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson's disease dementia. **Memantine**, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is used in moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease or when cholinesterase inhibitors are not tolerated. Their benefits on cognition and function are modest, and side effects include nausea, bradycardia and syncope (cholinesterase inhibitors), and dizziness and drowsiness (memantine).

The anti-amyloid antibodies lecanemab and donanemab slow decline modestly in early Alzheimer's disease confirmed by amyloid testing. They were licensed by the MHRA in 2024 but carry a risk of brain swelling and bleeding (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities) and need intensive monitoring. NICE did not recommend them for NHS use in 2025, on grounds of cost-effectiveness; after appeals were upheld in 2026, the appraisals were being reconsidered. Their status is changing and should be checked when reading.

### Behaviour and distress

People with dementia often show changes in behaviour, such as agitation, aggression, wandering, calling out, resisting care, apathy, or psychotic symptoms. These are often called **behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)**. A more useful way to think about them is as a communication of distress or an unmet need by someone who can no longer explain it.

::: definition Managing distressed behaviour in dementia {#def-dementia-care}
The management of distressed behaviour in dementia begins with a structured search for causes, then non-drug approaches, and only then drugs. Causes to look for include pain, constipation, urinary retention, infection, delirium, hunger, thirst, the need for the toilet, being too hot or cold, hearing or vision problems, fear, boredom, depression, side effects of medicines, and how care is delivered. Person-centred care uses what is known about the person (history, routines, preferences) to reduce distress. Antipsychotics are considered only for severe distress or risk of harm when other approaches have failed.
:::

Antipsychotics in people with dementia increase the risk of stroke and death (by roughly 1.6 to 1.7 times in pooled trial data, which led to regulatory warnings in the mid-2000s), and their benefit for agitation is small. In the UK, risperidone is the only antipsychotic licensed for this use: for short-term treatment (up to 6 weeks) of persistent aggression in moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease when there is a risk of harm. Any antipsychotic should be started at a low dose, its effect reviewed regularly, and stopped when no longer needed. People with dementia with Lewy bodies can have severe, sometimes fatal, sensitivity reactions to antipsychotics.

Hospitals can make care safer for people with dementia by recording personal information (for example the Alzheimer's Society "This is me" leaflet), welcoming carers to stay with the person (John's Campaign), using familiar staff, avoiding ward moves at night, and providing meaningful activity.

::: example The man with "agitation" and visual hallucinations {#ex-dlb}
Mr Desmond Hughes, 76, is admitted with pneumonia. His wife describes two years of fluctuating confusion, seeing children in the garden who are not there, falls, and acting out his dreams at night. On the second night he is agitated and is given haloperidol. The next morning he is rigid, drowsy and cannot swallow. What is the likely underlying diagnosis, and what went wrong?
::: solution
Fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder and falls (with parkinsonism now evident) are core features of **dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)**. People with DLB are very sensitive to antipsychotics: haloperidol can cause severe rigidity, sedation, falls and an illness resembling neuroleptic malignant syndrome, and has been associated with death.

What went wrong: the history of DLB was available from his wife but was not used, and an antipsychotic was used for agitation without first looking for causes (the pneumonia, hypoxia, pain, a full bladder or constipation, an unfamiliar ward at night). Now: stop haloperidol, give supportive care (fluids, swallowing assessment, nutrition, checking creatine kinase and temperature), treat the pneumonia, and get specialist advice. For future agitation, address causes and use non-drug measures; if a drug is unavoidable, specialist advice is needed. His record should carry a clear alert about antipsychotic sensitivity.
:::
:::

::: example The "monthly urinary infection" {#ex-recurrent-uti}
Mrs Bridget Murphy, 88, lives in a care home and has moderate Alzheimer's disease. She has had six courses of antibiotics in six months for "UTI", each started after an episode of increased agitation and a positive urine dipstick. She has had two episodes of *Clostridioides difficile* diarrhoea. How should her care be changed?
::: solution
In people over 65, and especially in care-home residents, bacteria in the urine without infection (**asymptomatic bacteriuria**) is common, so a positive dipstick does not show that the bladder is the cause of her behaviour. UK guidance advises against dipstick testing to diagnose urinary infection in people over 65; diagnosis rests on new urinary symptoms or signs such as dysuria, new frequency or urgency, new incontinence, suprapubic pain, fever, or rigors. Repeated unnecessary antibiotics have caused *C. difficile* infection and favour resistant organisms.

A better approach for each episode of agitation: assess for delirium (4AT), look for pain (for example with a structured pain tool for people who cannot report it), constipation, urinary retention, dehydration, other infections, and changes in her routine or care. Agree a care-home plan with staff and family that describes when urine testing and antibiotics are and are not appropriate. Review her medicines for anticholinergic load. Antibiotics are reserved for clear signs of infection.
:::
:::

::: example The discharge that became a readmission {#ex-dsd}
Mr Pradeep Shah, 84, with mild vascular dementia, was admitted with delirium caused by pneumonia. On day 5 he was medically stable but still muddled at night, and was discharged home alone at 7 pm with new tablets and no follow-up. Two days later he was readmitted after being found wandering in the street. Identify the failures and describe a safer discharge.
::: solution
Failures: delirium superimposed on dementia often takes days to weeks to resolve, and he was discharged while still delirious; a late-evening discharge to an empty house removed every safeguard; new tablets without explanation or a dosette box were likely to be taken incorrectly; there was no follow-up, and probably no communication with his GP or family.

A safer discharge: assess his function and cognition before discharge (an occupational therapist's assessment of whether he can manage at home); talk with him and his family about delirium and its expected recovery; consider a period of intermediate care or reablement at home; plan the medicines with a pharmacist (a minimum of changes, a clear list, help with administration); discharge in daytime with someone at home; send a discharge summary that names delirium as a diagnosis, explains that his cognition should be reassessed in a few weeks, and asks the GP to review it; and give the family a contact number. Delirium is a risk factor for later dementia progression, so follow-up of cognition is needed in its own right.
:::
:::

## Capacity and best interests {#sec-capacity}

Delirium and dementia often affect a person's ability to make decisions. In England and Wales the **Mental Capacity Act 2005** provides the framework; Scotland has the Adults with Incapacity (Scotland) Act 2000, and Northern Ireland the Mental Capacity Act (Northern Ireland) 2016.

::: definition Mental capacity (England and Wales) {#def-capacity}
Under the Mental Capacity Act 2005, a person lacks **capacity** to make a particular decision at a particular time if they are unable to make it because of an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain. A person is unable to make a decision if they cannot do one or more of the following: **understand** the information relevant to the decision; **retain** it long enough to decide; **use or weigh** it as part of the process of deciding; or **communicate** the decision by any means. Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific.
:::

The Act sets out five principles:

1. A person must be assumed to have capacity unless it is established that they lack it.
2. A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision unless all practicable steps to help them have been taken without success.
3. A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision merely because they make an unwise decision.
4. A decision made on behalf of a person who lacks capacity must be made in their **best interests**.
5. Before acting, consider whether the purpose can be achieved in a way that is less restrictive of the person's rights and freedom.

A best-interests decision considers the person's past and present wishes, feelings, beliefs and values, and the views of family, carers and anyone with a lasting power of attorney. A valid **lasting power of attorney** for health and welfare, or a valid and applicable **advance decision to refuse treatment**, must be followed within its scope. People who lack capacity and have no one appropriate to consult should be referred to an **Independent Mental Capacity Advocate** for serious medical decisions or changes of accommodation.

Restraint (including physical holding, bed rails, and sedation used to control) is lawful under the Act only if it is necessary to prevent harm to the person and is a proportionate response. If a person who lacks capacity to consent is under continuous supervision and control and is not free to leave a hospital or care home, this may amount to a **deprivation of liberty**, which must be authorised under the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (the planned replacement, the Liberty Protection Safeguards, has been postponed indefinitely since 2023).

In delirium, capacity often fluctuates. Decisions that can wait should wait until the person improves.

::: quiz
An 80-year-old man with delirium wants to leave the ward to go home. He has fluctuating attention. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] He lacks capacity because he has delirium
- [ ] He has capacity because he can state his wish clearly
- [x] His capacity to make this decision must be assessed using the functional test, after taking steps to help him decide
- [ ] Capacity only needs to be assessed if he is going to be restrained
::: solution
A diagnosis does not decide capacity. The functional test (can he understand, retain, use or weigh the relevant information and communicate a decision?) is applied to this decision at this time, after helping him (for example, choosing a moment when he is most alert, and explaining simply). If he lacks capacity, a best-interests decision is made, using the least restrictive option ([[#def-capacity]]).
:::
:::

## Where this leads

The diseases that cause dementia, their pathology and diagnosis are in [[nervous-system/dementia-neurodegeneration]]. Drugs that cause delirium are covered in [[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]]. Delirium near the end of life, which may be irreversible, is discussed in [[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]] and [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]]. The law on capacity and consent is developed in [[medical-ethics-law]].

::: history
The word delirium comes from the Latin *de lira*, "out of the furrow", and the clinical picture of acute confusion in fever was described in the Hippocratic writings. For most of the twentieth century it was known by many names (acute confusional state, toxic psychosis, ICU psychosis), which hindered research. In 1906 Alois Alzheimer presented the case of Auguste Deter, a woman in her fifties with progressive memory loss and behavioural change whose brain showed the plaques and tangles now named after him; his colleague Emil Kraepelin gave the disease its eponym in his textbook in 1910. In 1990 Sharon Inouye and colleagues published the Confusion Assessment Method, which made delirium measurable in routine practice, and her team's 1999 trial of the Hospital Elder Life Program showed that it could be prevented. In the UK, Alasdair MacLullich and colleagues developed the 4AT (validated in 2014), designed to be used quickly by any member of staff.
:::

::: summary
- Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness caused by illness, drugs or withdrawal. It is common, frequently missed (especially the hypoactive form), and dangerous.
- Risk depends on predisposing vulnerability and precipitating insults; vulnerable people need only a small precipitant, and there is usually more than one cause.
- Screen with the 4AT (4 or more suggests delirium), confirm clinically, and take a collateral history.
- About a third of hospital delirium can be prevented by multicomponent interventions.
- Treat the causes; use non-drug care; reserve short-term low-dose haloperidol for severe distress or risk, and avoid antipsychotics in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies.
- In dementia, distressed behaviour usually signals an unmet need: look for pain, constipation, delirium and environmental causes before considering drugs. Antipsychotics increase stroke and mortality.
- Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific. Use the functional test, help the person decide, and act in their best interests in the least restrictive way if they lack capacity.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Score the 4AT {level=1 check="6"}
An 86-year-old man is alert. He gives his age, his date of birth and the name of the hospital correctly, but says the year is 1998. He says "December, November, October, September" and stops. His daughter says he was well until yesterday. Calculate his 4AT score.
::: solution
Alertness normal: 0. AMT4: one mistake (the year), so 1. Months backwards: he starts but gets fewer than seven correct, so 1. Acute change: yes, 4. Total $0 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 6$, which is 4 or more and suggests possible delirium ([[#def-4at]]). The next step is a clinical assessment and a search for causes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Delirium, dementia or depression? {level=1}
For each person, state the most likely diagnosis: (a) an 80-year-old woman whose daughter reports two years of gradually worsening memory, getting lost while driving, and missed bills, with normal attention on testing; (b) a 78-year-old man who, over two days after starting a bladder medicine, has become drowsy, cannot follow a conversation and is picking at the bedclothes; (c) a 74-year-old widower who says he "can't be bothered", answers "I don't know" to most questions, and has lost weight since his wife died.
::: solution
(a) Dementia: gradual onset over years, affecting daily function, with preserved attention. (b) Delirium: acute onset, inattention, altered consciousness, probably caused by an anticholinergic drug (for example oxybutynin). (c) Depression: low mood, apathy, "don't know" answers and weight loss after a bereavement. Depression can mimic dementia and should be treated; cognitive testing is repeated once mood improves.
:::
:::

::: exercise Withdrawal delirium {level=1}
A 70-year-old man has an elective hip replacement. On the third day after surgery he becomes agitated, sweaty and tremulous, with a heart rate of 115 and visual hallucinations. What cause should be considered first, and what history would confirm it?
::: solution
Alcohol withdrawal, which typically causes delirium (delirium tremens) two to four days after the last drink, with tremor, sweating, tachycardia and hallucinations. Take a careful alcohol history, ideally confirmed with family or the GP record, and ask about benzodiazepine use, since sudden withdrawal of benzodiazepines causes a similar picture. Management follows local alcohol withdrawal protocols, which use a benzodiazepine and thiamine. Other causes (infection, hypoxia, pain, pulmonary embolism, urinary retention) must still be looked for.
:::
:::

::: exercise Predictive value of a positive 4AT {level=2 check="round(1000*0.2*0.88/(0.2*0.88+0.8*0.12))/10"}
On an acute medical ward, 20% of older patients have delirium. The 4AT has a sensitivity and a specificity of 88%. What proportion of patients with a positive 4AT actually have delirium? Give the percentage to one decimal place.
::: hint
Imagine 1,000 patients and count the true and false positives.
:::
::: solution
Of 1,000 patients, 200 have delirium and 800 do not. True positives: $200 \times 0.88 = 176$. False positives: $800 \times 0.12 = 96$. Positive predictive value $= 176 / (176 + 96) = 176/272 = 0.647$, so **64.7%**. About a third of positive screens are false positives (many are people with dementia but no delirium), so a positive 4AT must be followed by a clinical assessment. The negative predictive value is high (about 97%), so a negative screen is reassuring, provided the information on acute change was complete.
:::
:::

::: exercise Number needed to treat for delirium prevention {level=2 check="round(1/(0.150-0.099))"}
In the HELP trial, delirium developed in 15.0% of patients receiving usual care and 9.9% of those receiving the intervention. Calculate the absolute risk reduction and the number needed to treat to prevent one case of delirium, to the nearest whole number.
::: solution
Absolute risk reduction $= 0.150 - 0.099 = 0.051$ (5.1 percentage points). Number needed to treat $= 1/0.051 \approx 19.6$, so about **20**. The relative risk reduction is $0.051 / 0.150 = 34\%$. The intervention is mostly volunteer and staff time (orientation, mobilisation, sleep, hydration, glasses and hearing aids), with essentially no harms, so an NNT of 20 is very favourable.
:::
:::

::: exercise Review an antipsychotic in a care home {level=2}
Mrs Ellen Baxter, 89, with severe Alzheimer's disease, has taken risperidone for 14 months. It was started in hospital when she was delirious and hitting staff. She is now calm, sleeps much of the day, and has had two falls. What should be done?
::: solution
Risperidone is licensed only for short-term treatment (up to 6 weeks) of persistent aggression in Alzheimer's disease, and the original indication (agitation during delirium) has resolved. It increases her risk of stroke, death, falls and sedation. The plan: discuss with her family and the care home, then reduce and stop risperidone gradually, monitoring for return of distress. Set out a person-centred plan for any recurrence (look for pain, constipation, infection and environmental triggers; use non-drug approaches). Review her other medicines and her falls. Document the decision and the review date.
:::
:::

::: exercise Assess capacity for discharge {level=3}
Mrs Agnes Wright, 87, with moderate dementia, wants to go home after an admission for a fall. She lives alone and has fallen four times this year. The team thinks a care home would be safer. Describe how you would assess her capacity for this decision, and what happens if she has capacity, and if she does not.
::: solution
Capacity assessment: assume capacity at the start. Identify the relevant information: the options (home with support, home without support, care home), the main risks of each (falls, long lies, not managing medicines or meals), and the support available. Help her decide: a quiet room, at her best time of day, simple language, perhaps visiting her home with an occupational therapist so she can show how she manages. Then test whether she can understand, retain, use and weigh the information, and communicate a choice. "Using and weighing" is the part most often affected in dementia: does she recognise her falls and their consequences, and weigh them against what home means to her? Record the questions asked and her answers.

If she has capacity: her choice to go home must be respected even if the team thinks it unwise. The team's job is to reduce the risk: carers, a pendant alarm, home adaptations, a falls programme, medication review. If she lacks capacity: a best-interests decision considers her wishes and values (she wants to be at home), the views of family and any attorney, and the least restrictive option. A trial at home with a substantial care package is often the right first step. If there is no family or friend to consult, an Independent Mental Capacity Advocate must be involved in a decision about a change of accommodation.
:::
:::

::: exercise Plan ahead with a person who has early dementia {level=3}
Mr Joseph Mbeki, 72, has just been diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease. He has capacity and lives with his wife. Outline the advance planning conversations that should be offered over the next year and why timing matters.
::: solution
Timing matters because capacity for complex decisions will decline, and conversations held early let him decide for himself. Topics: (1) what matters most to him and how he wants to live, recorded in an advance statement; (2) lasting powers of attorney for property and financial affairs and for health and welfare, made while he can understand them; (3) whether there are treatments he would want to refuse in future (an advance decision to refuse treatment, which must be written, signed and witnessed, and state that it applies even if his life is at risk if it refuses life-sustaining treatment); (4) driving: he must inform the licensing authority and his insurer; (5) work, finances and a will; (6) future care preferences, including where he would want to be cared for and, later, his views on hospital admission, artificial feeding and resuscitation; (7) support for his wife as a carer, including a carer's assessment. Conversations should be revisited as things change, and documents shared with his GP so they are available in an emergency.
