About a third of people aged 65 and over living at home fall each year, and about half of those aged 80 and over. Most falls cause no serious injury, but falls are the commonest cause of injury-related death in older people, cause most hip fractures, and often mark the start of a decline into dependence. A fall is also a symptom: it is the visible end point of problems with the heart, the brain, the muscles, the eyes, the medicines and the home.

This chapter covers how falls are classified, why they happen, how to assess an older person who has fallen, and which interventions prevent further falls. It builds on the ideas of reserve and frailty in [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty]].

## What a fall is and why it happens {#sec-causes}

::: definition Fall {#def-fall}
A **fall** is an event in which a person comes to rest unintentionally on the ground, floor or another lower level. The definition says nothing about cause: falls caused by fainting, tripping or a seizure are all falls, and the assessment has to work out which.
:::

It helps to sort falls into three broad groups, because each leads to a different assessment.

- **Mechanical (accidental) falls**: a trip or slip in a person whose balance and strength would normally cope. A truly accidental fall in a fit person needs little more than treatment of the injury. But "mechanical fall" is overused: a healthy 30-year-old rarely falls when catching a toe on a rug, so an older person who did may have weak legs, poor vision or slow reactions.
- **Syncopal falls**: transient loss of consciousness from reduced blood flow to the brain, for example from orthostatic hypotension, an arrhythmia, carotid sinus hypersensitivity or aortic stenosis. Older people often do not remember losing consciousness, so syncope frequently presents as an "unexplained fall".
- **Multifactorial falls**: the commonest pattern in frail people, where several modest risk factors combine to cross the threshold for a fall.

::: definition Risk factors for falls {#def-causes}
**Intrinsic risk factors** belong to the person: previous falls, older age, muscle weakness, gait and balance disorders, visual impairment, cognitive impairment and dementia, Parkinson's disease, stroke, peripheral neuropathy, arthritis, foot problems, urinary urgency and incontinence, depression, postural hypotension, and fear of falling. **Extrinsic risk factors** belong to the environment and treatment: medicines (especially sedatives and drugs that lower blood pressure), poor lighting, loose rugs, stairs, unsuitable footwear, and bifocal or varifocal glasses on stairs. **Situational factors** describe what the person was doing: getting up at night to pass urine, rushing, or carrying something on the stairs.
:::

::: theorem Fall risk rises with the number of risk factors {#thm-multifactorial}
The risk of falling increases steeply with the number of risk factors a person has. In a classic cohort of community-dwelling people aged 75 and over (Tinetti and colleagues, 1988), the proportion who fell in a year rose from 8% of those with no risk factors to 78% of those with four or more.
:::

::: proof
Balance is a control system. The brain combines information from the eyes, the inner ear and the joints and skin (proprioception), and corrects posture through the muscles. Each risk factor degrades one part of this system: a cataract weakens vision, neuropathy weakens proprioception, a sedative slows the processing, weak quadriceps limit the correction. A healthy system has redundancy, so one weak component is compensated by the others. When several components are weak at once, there is no spare capacity left, and an ordinary disturbance (a turn, a trip, standing up quickly) cannot be corrected. This is the threshold principle of [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty#thm-threshold]] applied to balance, and it explains why removing even one or two risk factors can make a worthwhile difference.
:::

### Medicines that cause falls

Medicines are among the most modifiable causes. The main groups (often called **fall-risk-increasing drugs**) are:

- **Psychotropics**: benzodiazepines and "Z-drugs" (zopiclone, zolpidem), antidepressants, antipsychotics, and sedating antihistamines.
- **Drugs that lower blood pressure**: antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers used for prostate symptoms (tamsulosin, doxazosin), nitrates, and drugs for Parkinson's disease.
- **Drugs that affect the brain or balance**: opioids, gabapentinoids, anticholinergic drugs, and some antiepileptics.
- **Drugs that cause hypoglycaemia**: insulin and sulfonylureas.

Taking several medicines also increases risk independently of their type, which is one reason a falls assessment always includes a medication review ([[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]]).

### The consequences of a fall

Injuries range from bruising to fractures of the wrist, humerus, pelvis and hip, and to head injury. Older people taking anticoagulants are at particular risk of intracranial bleeding, including **chronic subdural haematoma**, which can present days or weeks after a minor head injury with confusion, headache, drowsiness or a gradual change in behaviour.

A **long lie** (usually defined as remaining on the floor for an hour or more) can cause pressure injury, dehydration, hypothermia, rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury, and it predicts a poor outcome. An inability to get up is itself a sign of weakness that needs attention.

**Fear of falling** is common after a fall, and sometimes before one. It leads people to restrict their activity, which causes further muscle loss, worse balance and more falls: the **post-fall syndrome**. Treating it (with exercise, confidence building and a plan for what to do if a fall happens) is part of falls care.

::: warning Don't stop at the injury
A common mistake is to treat the fracture or suture the laceration and discharge the patient without asking why she fell. Every fall in an older person needs two questions answered: what injury was caused, and what caused the fall. A fall is a strong predictor of future falls, so an assessment at the first presentation is the best chance to prevent the next one.
:::

## Assessment {#sec-assessment}

Falls are so common that not every person who has fallen can have a full specialist assessment. NICE guideline NG249 (2025), which replaced the 2013 guideline CG161, advises that people aged 65 and over (and people aged 50 to 64 at higher risk) should be asked about falls, and that a comprehensive multifactorial falls assessment should be offered to those who have fallen in the past year and who are living with frailty, were injured and needed medical treatment, lost consciousness, could not get up without help, or have fallen two or more times in the past year. People who have fallen without these features should have their gait and balance assessed and be offered a falls prevention exercise programme if these are impaired. NG249 also advises against using falls risk prediction tools to estimate an individual's risk: the history and assessment do that job better.

::: definition The multifactorial falls assessment {#def-assessment}
A **multifactorial falls assessment** looks for every treatable contributor to falls. Its main components are:

1. **History of the fall**: what the person was doing, any warning symptoms, loss of consciousness, how long she was on the floor, and an account from any witness.
2. **Previous falls and fear of falling.**
3. **Medication review**, focusing on fall-risk-increasing drugs.
4. **Cardiovascular assessment**: pulse, heart sounds (aortic stenosis), lying and standing blood pressure, and an ECG.
5. **Gait, balance, strength and mobility**, for example with the Timed Up and Go test or gait speed.
6. **Neurological examination**: signs of Parkinson's disease, stroke, neuropathy, and cerebellar disease.
7. **Vision**, cognition, mood, continence, feet and footwear.
8. **Bone health and fracture risk** (for example FRAX), because a fall without a fracture today may cause one tomorrow.
9. **Home hazards and activities**, usually by an occupational therapist.
:::

### The history

The history is the most useful part of the assessment. Ask what happened before, during and after the fall. Warning symptoms such as light-headedness on standing point to orthostatic hypotension; palpitations or no warning at all point to an arrhythmia; spinning points to a vestibular problem. A fall while turning the head or with a tight collar suggests carotid sinus hypersensitivity. Ask a witness whether the person went pale, lost consciousness, or jerked, and how quickly she recovered: prolonged confusion afterwards suggests a seizure, head injury or hypoglycaemia. Older people often under-report or rationalise falls ("I just tripped"), so an account from family or carers is valuable.

### Lying and standing blood pressure

::: definition Orthostatic hypotension {#def-oh}
**Orthostatic (postural) hypotension** is a sustained fall in systolic blood pressure of at least 20 mmHg, or in diastolic blood pressure of at least 10 mmHg, within 3 minutes of standing from lying (or of head-up tilt). It is measured after the person has lain down for at least 5 minutes, with readings taken at about 1 and 3 minutes after standing; symptoms during the test should be recorded.
:::

Causes include dehydration, blood-pressure-lowering drugs, diabetic and Parkinsonian autonomic failure, and prolonged bed rest. The pulse response helps: a large rise in heart rate on standing suggests volume depletion, while little or no rise suggests autonomic failure or beta-blockade. Some people with autonomic failure have high blood pressure when lying down (**supine hypertension**) and low blood pressure when standing; their treatment needs specialist care.

### Gait and balance tests

In the **Timed Up and Go (TUG)** test, the person rises from a standard chair, walks 3 metres at a normal pace, turns, walks back and sits down. Times above about 12–15 seconds suggest impaired mobility and higher falls risk; the World Falls Guidelines (2022) use a TUG over 15 seconds or a gait speed below 0.8 m/s to identify people who need a fuller assessment. Watching the test is as informative as timing it: pushing up with the arms suggests weak quadriceps, short shuffling steps suggest Parkinson's disease or cerebrovascular disease, and an unsteady turn suggests poor balance.

### Investigations

Investigations are guided by the history. An ECG is standard. Blood tests often include a full blood count, renal function and electrolytes, glucose, and, where relevant, vitamin B12 and thyroid function. After a long lie, check creatine kinase and renal function. If the falls are unexplained or suggest syncope, prolonged heart rhythm monitoring (ambulatory ECG, an external patch or an implantable loop recorder) may be needed, and carotid sinus massage can be performed under ECG and blood pressure monitoring by trained staff. Brain imaging is not routine: it is indicated for head injury according to head injury guidance, new focal neurological signs, or symptoms suggesting a subdural haematoma.

::: example Two "mechanical" falls and a heart block {#ex-syncopal}
Mrs Joyce Adeyemi, 79, has had three falls in five months, all without warning. Two emergency department notes record "mechanical fall". She takes amlodipine and indapamide for hypertension, and was started on tamsulosin last month for urinary frequency. Her ECG shows sinus rhythm with first-degree heart block (PR interval 260 ms). Lying blood pressure is 152/84 mmHg; at 3 minutes standing it is 118/68 mmHg, and she feels light-headed. Interpret the findings and outline a plan.
::: solution
**Interpretation.** Falls without warning in an older person should be treated as possible syncope until shown otherwise. Her blood pressure falls by $152 - 118 = 34$ mmHg systolic and $84 - 68 = 16$ mmHg diastolic with symptoms, which meets the definition of orthostatic hypotension ([[#def-oh]]). Three drugs contribute: a calcium-channel blocker, a thiazide-like diuretic and an alpha-blocker. Each was started for a reasonable reason, but together they lower her standing blood pressure. The first-degree heart block is not by itself a cause of falls, but in a person with unexplained falls without warning it raises the possibility of intermittent higher-degree block.

**Plan.**

1. Review the medicines. Tamsulosin is the most recent addition and the likeliest culprit; consider stopping it and reassessing her urinary frequency (the diuretic may be the cause of it). Review whether her blood pressure targets suit her, and reduce or stop antihypertensives if appropriate.
2. Arrange prolonged ECG monitoring, because an intermittent arrhythmia has not been excluded. If symptoms recur and monitoring is unrevealing, an implantable loop recorder may be considered.
3. Give advice on standing up slowly, fluids, and what to do if she feels faint.
4. Advise her about driving: she must follow the driving licensing authority's rules on unexplained loss of consciousness, and the advice should be documented.
5. Offer a strength and balance programme and assess bone health.
:::
:::

## Prevention and management {#sec-prevention}

::: definition Multifactorial falls prevention {#def-prevention}
**Falls prevention** combines interventions targeted at the person's identified risk factors. The interventions with the best evidence are: progressive strength and balance exercise; medication review with withdrawal or reduction of fall-risk-increasing drugs; home hazard assessment and modification by an occupational therapist for people at high risk; treatment of visual problems, especially cataract surgery; management of orthostatic hypotension; cardiac pacing for selected people with bradyarrhythmias or cardioinhibitory carotid sinus hypersensitivity; and treatment of osteoporosis to reduce the risk of fracture if a fall occurs.
:::

**Exercise** is the single most effective intervention. A 2019 Cochrane review of exercise in community-dwelling older people (Sherrington and colleagues) found that exercise reduced the rate of falls by about 23% (rate ratio 0.77). Programmes that mainly challenge balance and function, such as the Otago programme or the Falls Management Exercise (FaME) programme, work well, and programmes combining balance with resistance training seem to work best. To be effective, exercise must be progressive, continued for months, and delivered in a dose that challenges balance; a leaflet of exercises without follow-up is not enough. Tai chi also reduces falls. Walking alone does not.

**Medication review** reduces the burden of drugs that cause falls. Stopping or tapering psychotropic drugs is particularly worthwhile, but must be done gradually to avoid withdrawal effects (see [[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]]).

**Home hazard modification**, assessed by an occupational therapist who visits the home, reduces falls mainly in people at higher risk, such as those who have recently been in hospital or have poor vision. Typical changes are grab rails, better lighting, removal of trip hazards, and moving the bed closer to the toilet.

**Vision.** Expedited cataract surgery on the first eye reduces falls in people with cataract. People should be warned that new glasses, especially multifocal lenses, may increase falls risk while they adjust, and that single-vision distance glasses can be safer for walking outdoors and on stairs.

**Vitamin D.** Vitamin D supplements correct deficiency and are part of bone health treatment, but large trials show that they do not prevent falls in people who are not deficient. One trial of a very large annual dose (500,000 units given once a year) actually increased falls and fractures (Sanders and colleagues, 2010). Vitamin D should therefore be thought of as treatment for deficiency, not as a falls prevention drug.

**Hip protectors** (padded garments) may reduce hip fractures in care homes, where they are worn consistently, but have little effect in the community, mainly because people do not wear them.

**Alarms and telecare** do not prevent falls, but a pendant alarm can shorten a long lie, which is a worthwhile aim in itself for someone who lives alone.

::: example The anticoagulated head injury {#ex-subdural}
Mrs Irene Kowalczyk, 82, takes warfarin for atrial fibrillation. She falls in her bathroom and hits her head on the edge of the bath. She is not sure whether she lost consciousness. Her INR is 3.2. She feels well and declines admission. Two weeks later her daughter phones: her mother is forgetful, has a headache and is "talking oddly". What should have happened at the first presentation, and what should happen now?
::: solution
**At first presentation.** NICE head injury guidance (NG232, 2023) advises a CT head scan within 8 hours of a head injury for people taking anticoagulants, even without other risk factors. If the scan is normal, she and her family should receive clear verbal and written advice about warning signs (worsening headache, confusion, drowsiness, vomiting, weakness) and whom to contact, because a subdural haematoma can develop later. Her refusal of admission should be respected if she has capacity for that decision, with the capacity assessment, the information given and the safety-net plan documented.

**Now.** New confusion, headache and speech change two weeks after a head injury on warfarin strongly suggest a **chronic subdural haematoma**. She needs urgent assessment and a CT scan today. If a haematoma is found, warfarin is reversed urgently (prothrombin complex concentrate and intravenous vitamin K, following local protocols) and the neurosurgical team is consulted; symptomatic chronic subdurals are often drained through burr holes.

**Afterwards.** The decision about restarting anticoagulation balances her stroke risk against her bleeding risk and is made with her, her cardiologist or physician and the neurosurgeons. A full falls assessment should have happened after the first fall and must happen now: why did she fall in the bathroom, and what can be changed (grab rails, a shower seat, lighting, a review of her medicines)? Falls alone are not a reason to withhold anticoagulation from someone who benefits from it; the treatment of the falls is the falls assessment.
:::
:::

::: example "My legs just went" {#ex-dropattack}
Mrs Ada Lindqvist, 84, has had four episodes in a year in which "my legs just gave way", once fracturing her wrist. She has not noticed losing consciousness and has no dizziness. Neurological examination, including leg strength, is normal. She was told her knees were weak and given a walking frame. On detailed questioning, two episodes happened while she was turning her head to reverse the car and one while she was wearing a tight-collared blouse. What diagnosis should be considered, and how is it confirmed?
::: solution
Episodes triggered by turning the head or pressure on the neck suggest **carotid sinus syndrome**: an exaggerated reflex response to stimulation of the carotid sinus baroreceptors that causes bradycardia (the **cardioinhibitory** type), a fall in blood pressure (the **vasodepressor** type), or both. Older people often do not recall brief loss of consciousness, so "my legs went" can describe syncope. Normal leg strength argues against her knees as the cause.

Carotid sinus massage is performed by trained staff with continuous ECG and blood pressure monitoring, first lying and then upright (on a tilt table), on one side at a time, never both. It is avoided in people with a recent stroke or transient ischaemic attack, or a carotid bruit unless carotid disease has been excluded. A ventricular pause of more than 3 seconds or a fall in systolic blood pressure of more than 50 mmHg is abnormal (European Society of Cardiology syncope guideline, 2018); the diagnosis of carotid sinus syndrome needs the test to reproduce her symptoms in the context of a compatible history.

If the cardioinhibitory type is confirmed, dual-chamber pacing can reduce further episodes and should be discussed with a cardiologist. She should also be advised to avoid tight collars, to turn her whole body rather than just her head when possible, and to follow the licensing authority's rules on driving after syncope. Her bone health needs assessing after the wrist fracture.
:::
:::

::: example Seeing the stairs {#ex-cataract}
Mrs Patience Owusu, 71, has fallen repeatedly over two years. She has not completed three referrals to exercise classes. Her visual acuity is 6/24 in both eyes because of cataracts. She has twice declined referral to an eye clinic, saying "I manage fine at home; I know where everything is." How would you approach this?
::: solution
**The evidence.** First-eye cataract surgery reduces falls in people with cataract, and poor vision is a modifiable risk factor that people often under-report because they compensate in familiar surroundings. "I know where everything is" describes someone who has learned to cope without good sight; her falls occur when the environment is less predictable.

**The conversation.** Earlier offers may have been framed in terms of eyesight alone. Linking surgery to her own goals (fewer falls, staying independent, going out safely) and solving practical barriers (transport, someone to accompany her, what happens after the operation) can change her decision. It is her choice; if she still declines, that is respected and recorded.

**The rest of the plan.** Vision is only one contributor. Ask why the exercise referrals failed (transport, timing, pain, low mood) and offer an option she can attend, such as a home-based programme from a physiotherapist. Review her medicines and blood pressure, improve lighting and mark stair edges, and assess her bone health. Treating the cataract is likely to be the largest single gain, but a multifactorial plan gives the best overall result.
:::
:::

::: example Counting the falls prevented {#ex-rate}
A community falls service runs a strength and balance programme for 200 people whose average falls rate is 2.0 falls per person per year. Assuming the programme reduces the rate of falls by the 23% seen in the Cochrane review (rate ratio 0.77), how many falls would be prevented in a year?
::: solution
Without the programme: $200 \times 2.0 = 400$ falls per year. With it: $400 \times 0.77 = 308$ falls per year. Falls prevented: $400 - 308 = 92$ falls per year.

Two points of interpretation. First, a rate ratio applies to the number of falls, which is why the result is expressed in falls prevented, not people protected: some people fall many times, and the programme reduces repeated falls as well as first falls. Second, the trial effect applies only if the programme matches what the trials delivered (progressive, balance-challenging exercise over months) and people keep attending.
:::
:::

### Falls in hospital

Falls are among the commonest incidents reported in hospitals. Inpatients are at high risk because of acute illness, delirium, unfamiliar surroundings, new medicines and urgency to reach the toilet. Good practice includes a multifactorial assessment of every older inpatient (rather than a risk score), lying and standing blood pressure, a review of sedating drugs, delirium prevention, access to walking aids and the call bell, regular help to the toilet, and appropriate footwear. After any inpatient fall, the person should be checked for injury before being moved, using safe lifting equipment if a hip fracture or spinal injury is possible, and medically reviewed, with neurological observations if a head injury cannot be excluded. Bed rails and restraint can cause harm and are not a substitute for individual assessment.

Osteoporosis and fracture prevention are in [[endocrine/bone-calcium]]. Parkinson's disease and other movement disorders are in [[nervous-system/movement-disorders]]. ::: history
For most of the twentieth century, falls in older people were regarded as accidents or as an unavoidable part of ageing. In 1986, Bernard Isaacs and colleagues described the "get-up and go" test, a simple bedside observation of rising, walking and turning; in 1991 Diane Podsiadlo and Sandra Richardson added a stopwatch, creating the Timed Up and Go. In 1988, Mary Tinetti and colleagues followed 336 people aged 75 and over in New Haven, Connecticut, for a year and showed that falls were predictable from a small number of risk factors, and that risk rose steeply as they accumulated. Tinetti's later trial (1994) showed that a multifactorial intervention targeting those risk factors reduced falls. In New Zealand, A. John Campbell, M. Clare Robertson and colleagues developed and tested a home-based strength and balance programme in the late 1990s, which became known as the Otago Exercise Programme. Rose Anne Kenny and colleagues in Newcastle showed in 2001 that pacing reduced falls in older people with cardioinhibitory carotid sinus hypersensitivity, helping to establish that many "unexplained" falls are cardiovascular in origin.
:::

::: summary
- A fall is a symptom. Classify it as mechanical, syncopal or multifactorial, and remember that older people often do not recall losing consciousness.
- Risk rises steeply with the number of risk factors; removing even a few helps.
- The assessment is multifactorial: the history and a witness account, medication review, lying and standing blood pressure, ECG, gait and balance, neurology, vision, cognition, continence, feet, bone health and home.
- Orthostatic hypotension is a fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing.
- Exercise that challenges balance and strength, continued for months, is the most effective intervention. Medication review, cataract surgery, home hazard modification in high-risk people, and pacing for the right bradyarrhythmia add to it.
- Vitamin D treats deficiency but does not prevent falls in people who are not deficient.
- People on anticoagulants with a head injury need prompt CT scanning and a safety net for delayed subdural haematoma.
- A long lie and fear of falling are harms in their own right.
:::

::: exercise Interpret a lying and standing blood pressure {level=1}
A 76-year-old man's blood pressure is 138/76 mmHg after 5 minutes lying, 124/70 mmHg at 1 minute standing and 112/66 mmHg at 3 minutes standing; his pulse rises from 64 to 68 beats per minute. Does he have orthostatic hypotension? What does the pulse response suggest?
::: solution
At 3 minutes the systolic pressure has fallen by $138 - 112 = 26$ mmHg, which meets the definition (at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes). The diastolic fall is 10 mmHg, which also meets it. The heart rate has risen by only 4 beats per minute, a blunted response that suggests autonomic failure (for example from diabetes or Parkinson's disease) or a rate-limiting drug such as a beta-blocker, rather than simple volume depletion, which usually causes a larger rise.
:::

A care home has 50 residents with an average falls rate of 3 falls per resident per year. If an exercise programme reduces the rate of falls by 23%, how many falls would it prevent in a year?
::: solution
Baseline falls: $50 \times 3 = 150$ per year. Falls prevented: $150 \times 0.23 = 34.5$ falls per year, so about 34 or 35. Note that trials in care homes have shown less consistent results than trials in the community, so this estimate is optimistic.

Using the simple model in the figure, a woman falls at random at an average rate of 0.8 falls per year. Her probability of at least one fall in a year is $1 - e^{-0.8}$. Calculate her probability of at least one fall in a year if she completes an exercise programme with a rate ratio of 0.77. Give the answer as a percentage to one decimal place.

Multiply the rate by the rate ratio first, then use $1 - e^{-\text{rate}}$.

Without exercise: $1 - e^{-0.8} = 0.551$, or 55.1%. With exercise the rate becomes $0.8 \times 0.77 = 0.616$ falls per year, and the probability of at least one fall is $1 - e^{-0.616} = 0.4599$, so **46.0%**. The absolute reduction in the chance of falling at all (about 9 percentage points) is smaller than the 23% reduction in the number of falls, which is why trials report both outcomes and why the two numbers should not be confused.

::: exercise Medication review after a fall {level=2}
An 83-year-old woman who has fallen twice takes: amitriptyline 25 mg at night for "poor sleep", zopiclone 7.5 mg at night, ramipril, bendroflumethiazide, doxazosin, oxybutynin for urinary urgency, and paracetamol. Her standing blood pressure falls by 28 mmHg systolic. Identify the medicines that may be contributing and describe how you would change them.
::: solution
Contributing medicines: amitriptyline (sedative, anticholinergic, causes postural hypotension), zopiclone (sedative hypnotic), oxybutynin (anticholinergic; can worsen cognition), and three blood-pressure-lowering drugs (ramipril, bendroflumethiazide and doxazosin) in a woman with symptomatic orthostatic hypotension. Approach: discuss with her what matters and agree priorities. Stop or reduce doxazosin first (an alpha-blocker is a frequent cause of postural hypotension), and reassess the need for the others against her current blood pressure. Taper zopiclone and amitriptyline gradually, with sleep advice and attention to the cause of poor sleep (for example nocturia or pain). Consider non-drug management of urgency (bladder training, timed voiding) and stopping oxybutynin. Make one or two changes at a time, recheck lying and standing blood pressure, and arrange follow-up. Doses and taper schedules follow current guidance and the BNF.
:::

::: exercise After a fall on a DOAC with a normal scan {level=2}
An 80-year-old man taking apixaban for atrial fibrillation falls and bumps his head. A CT scan within 8 hours is normal and he is alert. His family asks whether he should stop his anticoagulant "because he keeps falling". How do you respond?
::: solution
Explain that the scan shows no bleeding now, and give written advice on symptoms of delayed bleeding (worsening headache, confusion, drowsiness, vomiting, weakness) and whom to contact. On the anticoagulant: in atrial fibrillation, the stroke risk prevented by anticoagulation usually outweighs the added risk of bleeding from falls, and analyses suggest a person would need to fall very many times a year for the balance to reverse. Falls alone are therefore not usually a reason to stop. The decision is individual: check the indication, renal function and dose, review any drugs that increase bleeding (for example NSAIDs or antiplatelets), and above all treat the falls with a multifactorial assessment. Record the discussion and his views.
:::
:::

::: exercise Should he have a pacemaker? {level=3}
A 77-year-old man has had five unexplained falls with facial injuries. Carotid sinus massage on the right, while tilted upright, produces a pause of 6 seconds and reproduces his light-headedness. His blood pressure falls by 30 mmHg. He has mild cognitive impairment and lives with his wife. Discuss the case for and against pacing, and what else is needed.
::: solution
For pacing: a reproduced symptom with a pause of more than 3 seconds, together with recurrent unexplained falls with injury, fits cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome, for which dual-chamber pacing can reduce syncope and falls. The injuries show that the episodes are dangerous. Against or cautions: the blood pressure fall of 30 mmHg suggests a vasodepressor component as well, which pacing will not treat, so falls may not stop completely; pacing has procedural risks (infection, lead problems); and the evidence that pacing prevents falls in older people is mixed. Other causes of falls must also be addressed. What else: discuss the options with him and his wife, including his own priorities, and assess his capacity to consent; review drugs that lower blood pressure or slow the heart; offer strength and balance exercise and home assessment; give driving advice; and assess bone health and fracture risk. The final decision is taken with a cardiologist.

::: exercise Design a falls pathway {level=3}
You are asked to design a falls pathway for a town of 60,000 people, of whom about 11,000 are aged 65 and over. Using the figure of one in three falling each year, estimate how many older people fall each year, and describe how the pathway should identify and help them.
::: solution
About $11{,}000 / 3 \approx 3{,}700$ older people fall each year, far more than a specialist clinic could see. The pathway therefore needs tiers. (1) Case finding: ask about falls at routine contacts (GP reviews, pharmacy, emergency department, ambulance call-outs, social care) and use ambulance and A&E data to identify people who fell but were not taken to hospital. (2) Triage using NICE NG249 criteria: people with frailty, injury, loss of consciousness, inability to get up, or two or more falls go to a multifactorial assessment by a falls service; others have a gait and balance check and are offered exercise. (3) Interventions with evidence: accessible strength and balance programmes (community classes and home-based options, with transport), medication review by a pharmacist or doctor, occupational therapy home visits for higher-risk people, rapid access to cataract surgery, cardiology links for syncope, and a fracture liaison service for bone health. (4) Equity: falls risk is higher in deprived areas, so services must reach people who are housebound, do not speak English, or have caring duties. (5) Measurement: falls, injurious falls, hip fractures, uptake and completion of exercise, and people's own goals.
