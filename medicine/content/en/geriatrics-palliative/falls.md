Falls are the geriatric syndrome par excellence: common, costly, mostly preventable, and routinely under-investigated because nobody brings a fall to hospital until it has broken something. **This chapter covers falls as a symptom with a differential (the mechanical slip, the syncopal blackout, the multifactorial collapse of accumulating margins), the assessment that actually finds causes (the story from whoever saw it, the orthostatic pressures, the medicine cabinet, the feet, the eyes and the timed walk), the consequences cascade (injury, the fracture, the head bleed on anticoagulation, the fear of falling that becomes housebound-deconditioning-falls itself), the treatments with real evidence (strength and balance exercise as the single best fall-prevention drug, targeted medication reduction, cataract surgery, pacemakers for the right rhythm, home hazard work in higher-risk homes), and the service that ties it together (the integrated falls pathway from ED or GP to a multifactorial falls clinic with a name on it). The chapter's thesis: 'she fell' is not a diagnosis, and 'old knees' is not a plan.**

## The symptom and its causes

::: definition Falls as a geriatric syndrome {#def-causes}
**The numbers first, because they justify the special interest: about one in three community-dwelling adults over 65 falls each year, half of them fall again; falls are the leading cause of injury death in older adults in many countries and the commonest reason for emergency attendance in the over-65s; hip fracture carries 20-30 percent one-year mortality; and the economic burden exceeds that of many diseases with far better marketing. The taxonomy that drives the workup: the mechanical fall (a genuine slip or trip in someone whose margins are normal-ish - these exist, but the 'I slipped' label deserves the same scrutiny as the unexplained collapse, because a healthy 30-year-old who slips on the same wet floor does not fall); the syncopal fall (transient cerebral hypoperfusion - the orthostatic, the cardiac arrhythmia, the carotid-sinus and cough and micturition syndromes, the aortic-st valve and the haemoglobin, with the blackout-versus-fall distinction of the syncope chapter applied in reverse: in the elderly, syncope often presents as an unexplained fall and the fall workup must ask about the seconds before it); and the multifactorial fall (the usual story, in which five partial causes - mild proximal weakness and a cataract and a small-vessel gait and two blood-pressure tablets and slippers - combine past a threshold). The intrinsic-risk catalogue: age-related (the gait and balance and vision and proprioception and reaction-time decline that is normal and modifiable-in-degree), disease-related (parkinsonism and stroke and dementia and peripheral neuropathy and arthropathy and incontinence-urgency and the delirium and infection that turn a chronic gait into an acute fall), and treatment-related (the psychotropic load - sedatives, antidepressants, antipsychotics; the antihypertensives especially at initiation and dose change; the polypharmacy-with-four-plus-drugs-as-a-falls-risk-factor-in-itself; which makes the falls clinic a deprescribing clinic wearing gym clothes). The extrinsic layer: the rug and the stairs and the night-time bladder journey and the footwear and ill-fitting glasses and the unheated bathroom, real and cheap to fix, but with the honest evidence footnote that home-hazard interventions work best in the people whose homes are actually hazardous and whose falls are actually mechanical-in-origin, which is what the trials showing 'no effect' and 'effect in high-risk subgroups' mean. The consequences to anticipate before asking about them: injury (the wrist, the humeral head, the pelvis, the hip, the chin-on-foresign of the head-strike), the intracranial haemorrhage with its anticoagulant-multiplier and its delayed-subdural-presentation and its 'I had a fall three weeks ago and now I am confused' sentential-urgency; the post-fall syndrome (the fear of falling that shrinks the world - activity stops, muscles go, more falls follow, and depression and care-home admission are often the actual outcome rather than any fracture); and the presentation-phenotype the GP knows as the spiral (the fall that stops being reported because she stopped being asked, until the fracture makes the history mandatory). The clinical rule that organises everything: any fall in an older adult gets a cause sought (what happened, not just what hurt), an injury hunt (including the examination she is embarrassed about), a medicines check, and a plan - and the plan must include strength, because the fall itself is now a risk factor for the next fall and time is muscle - [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty]], [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]], [[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]].**
:::

## The assessment

::: definition The falls assessment: story, systems, stride {#def-assessment}
**The history is the highest-yield test and is usually skipped: the five questions before the fall (what were you doing; did it happen in stages; any warning - the light-headedness that says orthostasis, the palpitation and the absence-of-warning that says arrhythmia, the room-spinning that says vestibular, the chest pain, the drop-without-blackout-that-is-a-drop-attack-not-syncope), the seconds of consciousness (witnessed if possible, because 'she went down like a tree' and 'she crumpled' are different sentences), the after (confusion duration, which says concussion or ictal or hypoglycaemia; and how long she lay there, because the lie is where the rhabdomyolysis and the AKI and the hypothermia hide, and the fact that nobody found her is itself a social emergency); and the collateral from the care worker or the neighbour who saw it, because older fallers minimise ('I just knelt down quickly to look at something') and witnesses exaggerate nothing accurately, so both histories get taken. The examination's physical set: the lying-standing blood pressures with a proper technique and a pulse response read (the drop that defines orthostatic hypotension - 20/10 with the autonomic-versus-drug-versus-dehydration interpretation and the parkinsonism-red-flag that the supine-hypertension-standing-hypotension pattern is autonomic failure and that this patient's high-lying-pressure must not be chased into the ground), the cardiac exam with the murmurs and the carotid-pulse-character, the nervous system with the parkinsonian signs and the upgoing toes and the vibration-and-proprioception and the tandem gait and the Romberg and the pull test (whose abnormality names the retropulsion that causes her backward falls and her wall-following habit), the feet (the nails, the deformity, the pulses, the insoles, the slippers with no heel counter), the vision (acuity and the cataract and the bifocal-for-stairs question and the new-glasses-adjustment period), and the cognition and mood because adherence to the exercise plan runs through them. The performance tests do the gait talking: the Timed Up and Go (over 12 seconds flags high fall risk, and it also measures her rehab progress), the 4-metre pace, the five-times-sit-to-stand, and the simple 'get up off the floor' observation which demonstrates the strategies and the fear. The targeted investigations, not the fishing: the ECG (the arrhythmia clues and the PR and QT and the paced rhythm and the ischaemia question), the falls-relevant bloods when indicated by history (the postural symptoms - FBC and ferritin and HbA1c and B12 and TFTs and calcium and glucose; the unexplained fall with prolonged lie - CK and renal and urine), the brain imaging only when the history says (the new focal signs, the head injury with the anticoagulant and the delayed-subdural-rule that every 'she has not been herself since the fall three weeks ago' needs a scan today, and the syncope-with-no-cause-that-CT-scans-well-rarely-help), and the ambulatory ECG-or-loop-recorder-for-the-unexplained-musician's-blackout-adjacent-arrhythmia-hunt with the pacing thresholds of the asystolic pauses - [[clinical-skills/history-taking]], [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]], [[nervous-system/sensory-systems]].**
:::

## Treatment and the pathway

::: definition Falls prevention and the integrated pathway {#def-prevention}
**What works, with its approximate strength: exercise is the drug of choice - the meta-analyses of the Otago-type and group-balance-and-strength-and-Tai-Chi-programmes show meaningful falls-rate reduction (roughly a fifth to a quarter) with effect concentrated in balance-and-functionally-challenging progressive programs delivered with sufficient dose, which is why 'I will use the stick' and 'she can do the exercises in the leaflet' are not the intervention and the physiotherapist-and-community-gym-and-dance-and-martial-arts-with-a-falls-credential-and-the-adherence-problem-is-the-intervention-is-the-service-design - plus the home-exercise-that-gets-followed-because-it-is-short-and-attached-to-a-tea-kettle. Medicines: the targeted deprescribing of the sedative-hypnotic-and-anticholinergic-and-orthostasis-producing-and-blood-pressure-over-treatment (the STOPP-and-Beers-adjacent lists and the falls-clinic evidence that medication review by a pharmacist or geriatrician with prescriber authority reduces falls in the many-medicated), the vitamin-D-for-the-deficient-and-nursing-home-adjacent-not-the-mega-annual-bolus (the trial literature that dethroned bolus dosing) and the honest note that vitamin D is a supplement for deficiency, not a falls drug for everyone. Senses and structure: cataract-surgery-reduces-falls-in-trial-terms (the second-eye effect), the new-prescription-and-bifocal caution, the podiatry and the footwear, and the hip protectors' evidence-in-institution-versus-world-reality. Cardiac fixes: the pacemaker for the documented cardioinhibitory carotid-sinus-syndrome and the arrhythmia-causes-caught-in-time (which is why the unexplained-fall-without-warning in a 78-year-old deserves rhythm capture, not just a hip protectors leaflet), the orthostatic-hypotension management ladder (slow position change and water and salt and compression where compatible and the fludrocortisone-and-droxidopa-and-midodrine-adjacent-prescribing-with-the-supine-hypertension-juggling-which-needs-a-clinician-who-does-this-often), and the over-treated-hypertension-in-the-frail (the SPRINT-older-adults-honesty-about-benefit-versus-falls-with-the-individualised-target-conversation and the deprescribing-after-a-fracture question). The environment: hazard work in higher-risk homes (the NICE-mandated multifactorial assessment including 'where did the last fall happen and what was within arm's reach'), the grab-rail-and-shower-and-lighting-and-removed-carpets done by an OT who can see the actual house, and the telecare-and-pendant-alarm-that-is-because-she-lay-for-nine-hours-not-because-it-prevents-falls (it prevents the consequences of the lie, which is a legitimate goal named as such). The service architecture: the integrated falls service (the NICE-recommended model - ED and GP and ward referral routes into one clinic with a medical, a physio, an OT and medicines review inside the same appointment-day where possible, with 8-plus falls-risk stratification and the reable and the exercise-on-referral and the fracture-liaison-attachment so the osteoporosis treatment she qualified for at the wrist actually arrives), the hospital side (the falls-risk-assessment-on-admission and the post-fall-checklist-and-cluster-analysis-which-is-how-a-ward-sees that its showers and its 2am anti-hypertensive round are the problem rather than declaring the patients unpreventable), and the culture piece - the honest enquiry after every inpatient fall without blame, because the alternative is hidden falls and a falsely safe unit. The last evidence footnote: multifactorial interventions beat single interventions in higher-risk people, and the whole chapter is that sentence extended: the clinic does not fix falls one cause at a time, it stacks small reductions across five domains until her risk curve goes back under her life** - [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty]], [[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]], [[endocrine/bone-calcium]].**
:::

::: example The 79-year-old who 'just went down' {#ex-syncopal}
Three falls in five months, all without warning, one with a chin wound and no fracture; 'mechanical' in two ED notes; on amlodipine and indapamide and now also tamsulosin started last month for urinary frequency; ECG in clinic shows first-degree AV block and the lying-standing drop is 34/16.
::: solution
**Two-causes-found-by-taking-the-falls-seriously ([[#def-causes]], [[#def-assessment]]): the read - 'fall-without-warning-is-syncope-until-proven-otherwise (the arrhythmia question is urgent in a way the hip question is not) but her story also names orthostasis (the three-drug combination that drops pressure on standing: the diuretic plus the calcium blocker plus the alpha-blocker-for-wee - each started sensibly, the sum indefensible) and the conduction clue (the PR abnormality with no-warning falls demands rhythm capture - a 24-hour tape may miss, the 7-14 day patch or the implantable recorder is the modern answer, and the carotid-sinus-massage-in-a-monitored-bed-for-the-neck-turn-triggered-blackout-is-the-oldest-test-she-should-have-had-in-her-sixties)'; the plan - 'the medication surgery first (the indapamide review, the tamsulosin's indication questioned - the frequency may be the diuretic's timing or the prolapse or the diabetes or the habit, so the bladder history replaces the alpha-blocker and not the other way round), the salt-and-water-realism-not-a-lecture, the compression-stockings-fit-properly-if-compatible-with-her-arteries, and the ambulatory-monitoring-booked-today-with-the-loop-recorder-if-the-patch-is-negative because 'she could have died on the pavement from a heart block and we called it clumsy'; and the safety net - 'the driving-and-DVLA-(or-equivalent)-licensing-conversation-about-recurrent-syncope held properly and documented because that is the consultation nobody enjoys and everybody must have, and the clear written plan she takes home (the warning signs, the sit-down-immediately rules, and who to ring when)'. The lesson she will quote to her friends: 'two doctors wrote "mechanical" before someone counted my tablets and took my pulse standing up'.**
:::

::: example The 82-year-old on warfarin with a knocked head {#ex-subdural}
An anticoagulated (warfarin for AF) 82-year-old strikes her head on the bathroom cabinet during a fall; no loss of consciousness known; INR 3.2; she seems fine and declines admission; two weeks later her daughter rings: 'she has been forgetful and headachy and now her speech is strange'.
::: solution
**The-delayed-subdural-and-the-anticoagulation-double-bind ([[#def-causes]], [[#def-prevention]], [[blood/haemostasis-bleeding]]): the rules - 'any head trauma on an anticoagulant gets same-day assessment and a low threshold for imaging, and the delayed-subdural-needs-a-safety-net-with-whom-to-call-and-what-signs-in-language-the-family-remembers because "she seemed fine" is the subdural's overture, and her speech now is an emergency CT today, not a GP review next week'; the acute management - 'the scan (the crescent that crosses suture lines) and the reversal decision with the stroke-and-neurosurgery teams (the prothrombin-complex-concentrate and vitamin K for the life-threatening bleed with the thrombotic-risk-honesty-she-is-now-not-protected-against-stroke-and-the-when-to-restart question that has no tidy answer and needs a documented MDT-style decision, and the repeat imaging and the neurosurgical route from bur-hole-to-craniotomy-for-the-organised-membrane-that-needs-it)'; the after - 'the falls-workup-that-should-have-happened-weeks-ago, the anticoagulation-later decision with her CHA2DS2-VASc and bleed-risk discussed in her own numbers (the left-atrial-appendage-occlusion-referral-is-now-a-real-option-in-this-exact-conversation), the warfarin-versus-DOAC-review-with-the-renal-arithmetic-and-the-adherence-reality, and the bathroom (the grab rail, the shower chair, the non-slip, the night light, and the OT who has been to her actual home because the cabinet that opened her skull has been there for twenty years)'. The system note: her declined-admission needed a documented capacity conversation, a named safety-net contact and a booked review - the 'she refused' that protects nobody is usually the note that says the words without the work.**

::: example The 84-year-old with 'drop attacks' {#ex-dropattack}
Four episodes over a year of 'my legs just give way', two on stairs, one causing a wrist fracture; no loss of consciousness ever, no dizziness; neurological exam normal including power; she has been told it is her knees and given a frame; on closer history the episodes last seconds, start after neck rotation (reversing the car, turning in bed), and once after a tight collar.
::: solution
**Carotid-sinus-hypersensitivity-and-the-legend-of-the-give-way-legs ([[#def-causes]], [[#def-assessment]]): the history that changes the diagnosis - 'positionally-triggered cerebral hypoperfusion with preserved awareness for seconds is the carotid-sinus-syndrome pattern (the head-turn and the collar and the reverse-into-the-drive), and "my legs gave way" is how many older people describe global cerebral hypoperfusion because drop-without-loss-of-consciousness-when-triggered-by-neck-mechanics-is-cerebral-not-spinal-and-the-knees-were-never-the-culprit'; the tests - 'the monitored carotid-sinus-massage (performed only with a clinician ready to pace-defibrillate and never bilaterally or with recent TIA-or-bruits-recent-caveats: the asystolic response over 3 seconds or the depressor drop of 50 mmHg names the syndrome) with the ECG-correlate documentation and the syncope-unit-referral when the bedside test is equivocal, and the tilt table's role if the picture instead points to the neurally mediated form'; the treatment - 'the pacing-for-the-cardioinhibitory-variant evidence (the Kenny-adjacent trials and the UK practice where the mixed-and-cardioinhibitory responses do best) as a real conversation with the cardiologist rather than the frame she was given, plus the education (the neck-turn awareness and the driving-licensing rules for recurrent syncope stated properly) and the collar-loosening and the reversal-lessons-which-sound-like-jokes-until-you-have-seen-the-fifth-fracture-from-them'; and the meta-lesson - 'the give-way-legs history is a syncope question in a disguise she cannot be expected to wear better: in older adults, recurrent unexplained falls without head-injury-pattern get the same workup as syncope because that is what many of them are, and "the knees" and "clumsiness" and "just old" are the three labels that precede most of the missed pacing cases in the audit'.**

::: example The 71-year-old whose cataract surgery halved her falls {#ex-cataract}
Recurrent falls for two years, 'multifactorial' label, three exercise referrals not completed, still falling; visual acuity 6/24 bilaterally with mature cataracts; she declined the eye clinic twice because 'I manage better at home, I know where things are'.
::: solution
**The-eye-that-is-a-falls-organ ([[#def-prevention]], [[nervous-system/sensory-systems]]): the evidence - 'cataract surgery reduces falls and fall-related injuries in trial terms (the Thompson/Fitchett-style data, with the second-eye effect and the biggest gains in the most impaired), and depth-contrast-and-visual-function-loss is a treatable intrinsic falls risk that patients under-report because they compensate at home exactly as she said - "I know where things are" is the definition of a vision-dependent person whose falls happen when the world moves'; the reframed-conversation - 'her two refusals were reasonable given how the offer was made: the ophthalmology conversation must be about falls and independence, not charts, and the drop-in-falls promise is the adherence intervention, plus the practicalities that decide everything (the lift, the daughter coming for both eyes, the glasses afterwards and the "no bifocals on stairs" advice which nobody gives in the pre-assessment clinic)'; the package-around-it - 'the exercise referral that failed gets redesigned (the class with a transport solution and a face she likes, or the home program the OT shortens to three moves she actually does), the contrast-lighting-and-stair-edge-painting which is visual-falls-medicine for people who will not or cannot have surgery, and the new-spectacle-and-bifocal-on-stairs caution for her husband who just upgraded his distance glasses'; and the honesty - 'fixing one domain rarely fixes a multifactorial faller: she probably still needs the strength work and the medication review - but after two years of falling, the cataracts are the largest removable single term in her equation, and "we will see what the eyes do first" is a legitimate, evidence-graded sequencing decision that also gives her a win she can attribute to herself - which is the psychological fuel for the rest of the plan'.**

::: exercise Weigh pacing for carotid sinus syndrome {level=3}
An 83-year-old with documented cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome (7-second asystole on gentle massage during a monitored tilt) and recurrent falls has a dual-chamber pacemaker offered. Her son asks why a wire is better than 'the tablets and the chair exercises we already do', and whether the device could be 'turned off if she declines'. Answer both and state the evidence's limits.
::: solution
**The-pacemaker-conversation ([[#def-prevention]], [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]], [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]]): why-a-wire - 'in the cardioinhibitory variant the mechanism is a pause: the brain briefly loses flow because the heart stops, and no tablet or thigh prevents asystole reliably, whereas pacing removes that mechanism; the trial evidence is modest in size (the Kenny-adjacent and TASK-adjacent pacing studies with their selection and crossover problems) but the effect in properly selected patients (the documented long pauses with symptom-rhythm correlation, as here) is convincing enough that guidelines endorse pacing for recurrent syndrome with cardioinhibitory response; the chair exercises still matter for everything the device does not fix, which is her orthostasis and her strength - the device and the programme treat different diseases and she needs both'; the turning-off - 'yes: a pacemaker is a treatment like any other and can be withheld or de-activated when she (or her best-interests process) decides it no longer serves her goals, de-activation is legally and ethically distinct from stopping a ventilator-in-place debates aside and widely accepted as withdrawing support, the conversation is held without panic and with the cardiology and palliative teams in the room, and the symptom consequences of de-activation (the pauses return; the falls return) are explained honestly - which in a frail, late-life-dementia patient is exactly the conversation this family may eventually need and should have now rather than under a trolley'; and the limits stated - 'the evidence base is smaller than the certainty of the clinics (selection matters, the mixed-type patients do less well, and the trials' crossovers and small numbers mean we quote improvement, not immortality); and the offer should be made with the same honesty about both: "this stops the pauses that are dropping you; it will not make you steady, and you can stop it when stopping makes more sense than carrying on".**

::: history
**Falls were medicine's least-studied killer until demography forced the question: the eighteenth-century 'declined and fell' of the gentle obituary, the nineteenth-century alienists' interest in the senile gait, and the early-twentieth-century recognition (the Marjory Warren-era work in Britain and the American gerontology of the 1940s-50s) that falls cluster in the institutionalised elderly and predict the fractures everyone was learning to count. The modern science begins with the epidemiological turn of the 1970s-80s: the Massachusetts and Auckland and Australian cohort studies that produced the one-in-three figure (a number so counterintuitive it was re-measured four times before it was believed), the Campbell and Robertson and Otago trial of 1997 which made exercise a falls intervention rather than a platitude, and the Tinetti and Guralnik and American-Brooklyn-Multirational-Study-era dismantling of the 'accident' concept into gait and balance and fear. The physiological mechanisms followed: the Lord and the Melbourne Falls Interest Group's reaction-time and low-limb-strength work, the Morris and the Ian-Campbell-adjacent-beta-blocker-and-falls-reassurances-and-the-Cummings-and-Nevitt-osteoporotic-fracture-epidemiology-which-named-falls-as-the-trigger-and-bone-strength-as-the-ammunition-of-the-hip-fracture (the 'fall is the event, osteoporosis is the disease' framing that the bone chapter inherits), and the sensory and vestibular-medicine work that explained why 'she gets dizzy standing up' is a diagnosis rather than an excuse. The blackouts literature supplied the internal-medicine half: the Wieling and Kenny and Sheldon and NASPE and ESC syncope-guidelines era which progressively moved unexplained falls into syncope workups (the Kenny-adjacent-cardiac-pacing-for-carotid-sinus-syndrome-trials-of-the-1990s-2000s and the loop-recorder revolution that kept finding the arrhythmias the old chest examination could not), with the embarrassing corollary that older people labelled 'drop attacks' and 'clumsy' for decades were having intermittent heart block. The service era consolidated: the NICE falls guideline and its 2013-update, the STOPP-and-Beers-medicines-review-evidence-crossing-over-from-prescribing-to-falls-outcomes, the Gill and the Ontario system-quality-indicator work on post-fall cascades, and the Gillespie-led Cochrane reviews that settled it: exercise works, hazards work mostly where hazards are, and multifactorial interventions work in high-risk populations - the evidence base most guidelines then diffuse into a 'do all these' sentence. The dark side-history is the care-home and hospital-falls-industry: the bed-alarm-and-wristband-and-lap-belt-and-bed-pushed-into-the-corner coercion era of the 1990s-2010s which reduced some falls and produced the restrained, alarmed, isolated resident literature and the dignity-and-liberty-damage the regulatory frameworks now name (the 'falls are not a patient safety problem, unprevented injurious falls and restrictive responses together are' shift and the restrictive-practice-reduction-programmes and the Swedish and Australian and British restriction-reduction literature and above all the 2010s-2020s movement away from bed alarms toward toileting-and-visibility-and-activity which is falls-medicine's behavioural-rotation-generality-lesson: the technology was never the intervention, the staff-time-and-judgment was). The current chapter is the integrated-pathway economics: the post-2011 NICE implementation variation and the 2020s integrated falls services' evaluation literature and the fracture-liaison-and-orthogeriatric-evidence-beside-it, and the equity frame: falls risk tracks deprivation and ethnicity and the people most likely to fall and least likely to be offered the exercise class are the same woman, so the pathway's real intervention is a bus ticket and a person who rings her back.** - [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty]], [[cardiovascular/arrhythmias]], [[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]].**
:::

::: summary
- A fall is a symptom: classify it as mechanical, syncopal or multifactorial; unexplained fall without warning is syncope until proven otherwise and needs rhythm and orthostatic assessment.
- The highest-yield tests are free: the witnessed story, the lying-standing blood pressures with pulse response, the medicine cabinet, the feet, the eyes, the Timed Up and Go.
- Exercise with adequate dose is the falls drug of first choice; targeted deprescribing, cataract surgery, and cardiac pacing for the right rhythm are the other big effect sizes.
- Anticoagulated head injury means same-day assessment and a delayed-subdural safety net the family can actually use; prolonged lies are medical emergencies (rhabdomyolysis, AKI, hypothermia).
- Fear of falling is a treatable outcome with its own morbidity: the plan must buy confidence, not just prevent the next impact.
- Multifactorial services beat scattered single interventions in higher-risk people; the pathway's success metric is a functioning person at home, not a completed checklist.
:::

::: exercise Audit the inpatient falls {level=2}

::: exercise Design the falls service {level=3}

::: solution
**Falls-system-design ([[#def-prevention]], [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty]]): the diagnosis-of-the-current-state - '3 000 uninvestigated "mechanical" labels is an unmatched-cohort of next falls and next fractures and this is the service's largest hidden morbidity: the ED label converts a symptom into a closed episode and the same woman re-presents in six months with a hip; the model - 'screened capture at every interface: the ED and the ambulance and the GP and the fracture-clinic all trigger the same single-question-plus-TUG-minimum assessment with an automatic referral-into-the-multifactorial-clinic-for-the-high-risk-and-a-protected-fast-route-for-the-red-flags (the no-warning syncope gets rhythm capture this month not this year);
