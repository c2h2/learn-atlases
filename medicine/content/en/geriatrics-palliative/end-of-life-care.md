How a person dies stays with their family for the rest of their lives. Care in the last days of life is something every doctor provides, in every specialty, and much of it is simple: recognising that the person is dying, talking honestly with them and their family, relieving symptoms, stopping what no longer helps, and caring for the body and the bereaved after death. When it goes wrong, it usually goes wrong through failures of recognition and communication rather than of technical skill.

This chapter covers recognising dying, care in the last days and hours, the legal and ethical framework for end-of-life decisions in the UK, what happens after death, and bereavement. It builds on [[geriatrics-palliative/palliative-care]] and [[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]].

## Recognising dying {#sec-recognition}

::: definition Recognising that a person may be dying {#def-recognition}
A person may be entering the last days of life when several of these changes develop together and are not explained by a reversible cause: increasing weakness and fatigue, so that the person spends most or all of the day in bed; reduced intake of food and fluids and difficulty swallowing; increasing drowsiness or reduced consciousness; agitation or confusion; changes in breathing pattern (including Cheyne–Stokes breathing and noisy respiratory secretions); and cool, mottled peripheries. NICE guideline NG31 (2015) advises that recognising dying is a judgement based on gathering and documenting such signs, considering whether they might be reversible, seeking advice from more experienced colleagues when uncertain, and reviewing the person at least every 24 hours.
:::

Uncertainty is normal. Some people who seem to be dying improve, especially when a reversible cause such as infection, hypercalcaemia, dehydration, opioid toxicity or hypoglycaemia is treated. The aim is not certainty but a plan that fits the most likely course and is revisited as things change. The uncertainty itself should be explained to the family ("I think he may be dying, and it could be in the next few days, but I can't be sure; we will look at him again every day").

### Lessons from the Liverpool Care Pathway

The Liverpool Care Pathway, an integrated care pathway for the dying, was used widely in UK hospitals in the 2000s. An independent review chaired by Baroness Julia Neuberger (*More Care, Less Pathway*, 2013) found that, although the pathway could support good care, it was often applied as a tick-box protocol: patients were placed on it without discussion, fluids were withdrawn without explanation, and families felt excluded. The pathway was phased out in 2014. In its place, the Leadership Alliance for the Care of Dying People (*One Chance to Get It Right*, 2014) set out five **priorities for care of the dying person**:

1. The possibility that the person may die within the coming days and hours is **recognised** and communicated clearly, decisions are made and actions taken in accordance with the person's needs and wishes, and these are regularly reviewed and revised.
2. Sensitive **communication** takes place between staff and the dying person, and those identified as important to them.
3. The dying person, and those identified as important to them, are **involved** in decisions about treatment and care to the extent that the dying person wants.
4. The needs of families and others identified as important to the dying person are actively explored, respected and met as far as possible (**support**).
5. An **individual plan** of care, which includes food and drink, symptom control and psychological, social and spiritual support, is agreed, coordinated and delivered with compassion.

::: example A last admission {#ex-lastdays}
Mr George Ellis, 89, with advanced dementia and heart failure, lives in a care home. Over five days he has stopped eating, takes only sips of fluid, sleeps most of the day, and is now admitted to hospital with low blood pressure and a chest infection. His daughter says he would not have wanted "to be kept going on machines". Describe the steps in his care.
::: solution
**Recognition.** His decline over several days, with poor intake, drowsiness and a probable infection on a background of advanced dementia and heart failure, suggests he may be dying. Consider reversible factors and whether treating them would match his wishes and be likely to help: an antibiotic for the chest infection may be reasonable if it is likely to relieve symptoms or might lead to recovery, but intensive treatment would not match his wishes.

**Communication and involvement.** Talk with his daughter (and any attorney): explain that he may be dying, that this is uncertain, what will be done, and what will not. Ask about his values, any advance care plan, ReSPECT form or advance decision, and spiritual or cultural needs.

**Individual plan** ([[#def-dying]]): stop non-essential medicines and routine blood tests and observations; continue symptom-relieving medicines by a route that works; prescribe anticipatory medicines; mouth care; offer sips if he can swallow safely; decide individually about clinically assisted hydration; record a DNACPR decision and treatment escalation plan; consider whether he could return to the care home, where he is known, if that is what his family believe he would want and it can be arranged quickly. Review at least daily.
:::
:::

## Care in the last days and hours {#sec-dying}

::: definition The individual plan of care for the last days of life {#def-dying}
The **individual plan of care** for a dying person includes: review and **stopping** of medicines and interventions that no longer benefit the person (for example statins, antihypertensives, routine blood tests and frequent observations), while continuing those that relieve symptoms; **anticipatory medicines** prescribed in advance for the common symptoms of the last days (pain, breathlessness, agitation, nausea and vomiting, and noisy respiratory secretions), usually by the subcutaneous route; individual decisions about **hydration and nutrition**, including support to drink if the person wishes and can do so safely, and frequent mouth care; regular assessment of symptoms and comfort; and attention to psychological, social, spiritual and cultural needs of the person and their family.
:::

### Anticipatory medicines

As people approach death they usually lose the ability to swallow, and symptoms may develop at night or at weekends. **Anticipatory prescribing** makes medicines available in the home, care home or ward before they are needed, so that a nurse can give them without delay. The usual groups are:

| Symptom | Typical drug class (examples) |
|---|---|
| Pain and breathlessness | Opioid (morphine; alternatives such as oxycodone or alfentanil in renal impairment) |
| Agitation, anxiety and distress | Benzodiazepine (midazolam) |
| Nausea and vomiting | Antiemetic (haloperidol, levomepromazine, or cyclizine) |
| Noisy respiratory secretions | Antimuscarinic (hyoscine butylbromide or glycopyrronium) |

Doses depend on the person's current medicines, renal function and frailty, and follow local guidelines. If symptoms recur and several doses are needed in 24 hours, a **syringe pump** (continuous subcutaneous infusion over 24 hours) is set up, with the 24-hour dose based on the doses used, and breakthrough doses remain available.

::: example Setting up a syringe pump {#ex-syringe}
Mrs Ruth Abrams, 76, with metastatic lung cancer, is dying at home. She took no regular opioid before this week. Over the past 24 hours the district nurses have given her five doses of subcutaneous morphine 2.5 mg for pain and breathlessness, and two doses of subcutaneous midazolam 2.5 mg for agitation, with good effect each time. Suggest the contents of a 24-hour syringe pump and the breakthrough doses.
::: solution
**Morphine.** Total used: $5 \times 2.5 = 12.5$ mg in 24 hours. A 24-hour infusion of about 10–15 mg of subcutaneous morphine would reflect her use; many clinicians would choose 10 mg in an opioid-naive, frail person and keep breakthrough doses available. The breakthrough dose remains 2.5 mg (about one-sixth of 15 mg, or one-quarter of 10 mg; in low doses the breakthrough dose is usually set at the smallest practical dose).

**Midazolam.** Total used: $2 \times 2.5 = 5$ mg; an infusion of 5–10 mg over 24 hours, with 2.5 mg breakthrough doses.

**Practical points.** Morphine and midazolam are compatible in a syringe pump with water for injection. Steady state takes several hours to reach, so if she is uncomfortable when the pump is started, a breakthrough dose is given at the same time (see the figure below). Review the doses daily and after any breakthrough doses. These figures illustrate the reasoning; doses follow local guidance.
:::

### Hydration and nutrition

Dying people usually drink and eat less, and families often worry that the person is "starving" or "dying of thirst". In the last days, reduced intake is part of dying rather than its cause. A dry mouth is the main source of thirst and is relieved by frequent mouth care and sips if the person can swallow. NICE NG31 advises supporting the person to drink if they wish and can, assessing hydration status daily, and discussing the risks and benefits of **clinically assisted hydration** (for example subcutaneous fluids) with the person and those important to them. A trial of assisted hydration may be appropriate in some situations, for example when thirst or delirium may be related to dehydration, with review at least every 12 hours; in others, fluids may cause fluid overload, oedema or secretions without benefit. Clinically assisted hydration and nutrition are medical treatments, and decisions about them are made in the person's best interests.

### Changes in the last hours

Families should be prepared for common changes:

- **Breathing** may become irregular, with pauses (apnoea) and then deeper breaths (Cheyne–Stokes breathing), or shallow, or with occasional gasps near death.
- **Noisy respiratory secretions** ("death rattle") occur when the person can no longer clear secretions from the throat. It probably does not distress the unconscious person, but can distress family. Repositioning helps; antimuscarinic drugs are often given, although evidence that they reduce the noise is limited. Suction is rarely helpful and can cause distress.
- **Skin** becomes cool and mottled, particularly the knees and feet.
- **Consciousness** fades; hearing may persist, and families can be encouraged to talk to the person.

::: warning Don't stop everything, and don't stop talking
Two opposite errors are common. One is to continue routine observations, blood tests and medicines that no longer help, which can cause discomfort and distract from what matters. The other is to "stop everything" without discussion, including mouth care, attention to symptoms, and conversation with the family. Recognising dying changes the goals of care, not the amount of care.
:::

### Ethics and law at the end of life

In UK law and professional guidance:

- **Withholding or withdrawing** life-prolonging treatment that is not in a person's best interests, or that a person with capacity refuses, is lawful (*Airedale NHS Trust v Bland*, 1993). Clinically assisted nutrition and hydration are treatments in this sense. Since *An NHS Trust v Y* (Supreme Court, 2018), court approval is not required to withdraw them in a person who lacks capacity, provided the Mental Capacity Act and professional guidance are followed and there is agreement about best interests.
- **Symptom relief** with appropriate doses of opioids and sedatives, titrated to relieve suffering, is lawful even if there is a theoretical risk of shortening life (the doctrine of double effect). In practice, studies suggest that opioids and sedatives used carefully in palliative care do not shorten survival.
- **Palliative sedation**, the proportionate use of sedatives to relieve refractory distress at the end of life, is lawful and ethical when the intention is to relieve suffering, the dose is proportionate, and the decision is discussed, documented and reviewed, ideally with specialist palliative care advice.
- **Euthanasia** (a doctor deliberately ending a patient's life) is unlawful; in England and Wales, assisting or encouraging suicide is an offence under the Suicide Act 1961.
- **Assisted dying legislation** has been debated in all parts of the UK. In England and Wales, a private member's bill (the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill) passed the House of Commons in 2025 but ran out of parliamentary time in the House of Lords in April 2026, and a similar bill was reintroduced in the Commons later in 2026; in Scotland, the Scottish Parliament rejected an assisted dying bill in March 2026. At the time of writing (late 2026), assisted dying is not lawful anywhere in the UK, and readers should check the current position.

When a patient asks a doctor to end their life, the request deserves a careful, compassionate response: exploring what lies behind it (uncontrolled symptoms, fear of what dying will be like, depression, feeling a burden, loss of control), addressing what can be addressed, and explaining clearly what the doctor can and cannot do.

## After death {#sec-after}

::: definition Verification and certification of death {#def-after}
**Verification of death** is confirmation that a person has died. Following the Academy of Medical Royal Colleges' code of practice (2008), after cardiorespiratory arrest the person is observed for a minimum of **five minutes** to establish that irreversible cardiorespiratory arrest has occurred: absence of a central pulse on palpation and of heart sounds on auscultation (supplemented in hospital, where available, by asystole on a continuous ECG, absent pulsatile flow on intra-arterial monitoring, or absent contractile activity on echocardiography). Any spontaneous return of cardiac or respiratory activity during this period restarts the observation. After five minutes, the absence of pupillary responses to light, of corneal reflexes and of any motor response to supraorbital pressure is confirmed. The time of death is recorded as the time at which these criteria are met. **Certification of death** is the completion of the Medical Certificate of Cause of Death (MCCD), which states the cause of death and allows the death to be registered.
:::

In many settings, verification may be carried out by appropriately trained registered nurses or paramedics as well as doctors, following local policy. Expected deaths at home are verified by community nurses or GPs.

**Certification in England and Wales.** Since 9 September 2024, every death in England and Wales is either reviewed by a **medical examiner** (an independent senior doctor) or investigated by a coroner before it can be registered. A registered medical practitioner who attended the deceased during their lifetime may complete the MCCD; the previous rule requiring the doctor to have seen the patient in the 28 days before death, or after death, no longer applies. The medical examiner's office also speaks with the bereaved family, who can raise any concerns about the care. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems.

**Referral to the coroner** (in Scotland, the procurator fiscal) is required for deaths that may be unnatural or whose cause is unknown, including deaths caused by violence, trauma or injury (including falls), poisoning, self-harm, neglect, an industrial disease or occupation, or medical treatment, and deaths in custody or state detention. Doctors should discuss any doubt with the medical examiner or the coroner's office.

**Care after death** follows the person's and family's wishes and cultural and religious practices, and national guidance on care after death. It includes laying the person flat, closing eyes and mouth, removing lines and devices unless the death is being referred to the coroner, identifying the person, and handling the body with dignity. Pacemakers and some other implanted devices must be removed before cremation. Families may wish to spend time with the person, and to take part in washing or dressing them.

::: example A death at 3 a.m. {#ex-nightdeath}
A nurse calls the junior doctor at 3 a.m.: Mrs Iris Bell, 92, whose death was expected and who had a DNACPR decision and an individual plan of care, has stopped breathing. Her son is with her. What should the doctor do?
::: solution
Go to the ward without delay and speak with the nurse and the son, expressing condolences. Verify death ([[#def-after]]): observe for at least five minutes for absence of a central pulse, heart sounds and breathing; then confirm absence of pupillary light reflexes, corneal reflexes and motor response to supraorbital pressure. Record the findings, the time of death (when the criteria were confirmed), and who was present. Check whether there is any reason for coroner referral (for example a recent fall or fracture, an injury, or a death related to a procedure); if so, lines and tubes are left in place. Ask the son whether there are cultural or religious wishes and whether other family members need to be contacted. Explain what happens next (the MCCD, the medical examiner's review, and how the bereavement office will contact him). Make sure the GP is informed in the morning, and give the son written information about practical steps and bereavement support.
:::
:::

::: example Washing the body and a rapid burial {#ex-ghusl}
Mr Yusuf Rahman, 67, dies in hospital from metastatic colon cancer. His family are Muslim and wish to wash his body (ghusl) and bury him within 24 hours. It is Saturday. How can the hospital help?
::: solution
Many faiths, including Islam and Judaism, prefer burial as soon as possible after death. The medical examiner system in England and Wales provides for **urgent release** of the body: the attending doctor completes the MCCD promptly, and the medical examiner's office and registrar can arrange out-of-hours scrutiny and registration where local arrangements exist. Contact the on-call medical examiner, the bereavement office and the mortuary as early as possible. If the death must be referred to the coroner, the coroner's office should be told of the request; coroners try to accommodate faith needs where possible.

For the washing: with the family's agreement, staff should handle the body minimally, keep it covered, and avoid procedures the family do not want, such as washing by staff if the family wish to do this themselves. Many hospitals have facilities for families to perform washing, or work with local mosques and funeral services. Infection control advice applies if the person had a notifiable infection. Recording and respecting these wishes before death, during advance care planning, makes the process easier.
:::
:::

## Bereavement {#sec-bereavement}

**Grief** is the normal response to loss. It is not a series of fixed stages, and it varies widely between people and cultures. Many bereaved people oscillate between focusing on the loss and on restoring everyday life (the dual-process model of Stroebe and Schut). Most people adapt over time with the support of family, friends and their communities.

Some develop **prolonged grief disorder**, in which intense longing or preoccupation with the deceased, with emotional pain, persists beyond what is expected in their culture, and impairs function. ICD-11 requires persistence for at least six months after the death; DSM-5-TR requires at least twelve months in adults. Risk factors include a sudden or traumatic death, a very close or dependent relationship, previous mental health problems, lack of social support, and difficult circumstances around the death. Bereavement is also associated with increased physical illness and mortality in the surviving partner.

Bereavement support in the UK is often organised in three tiers: information about grief and available support for all bereaved people; support from trained volunteers or community services for those who need more; and specialist psychological support for those with complex needs. Hospices provide much of this. Doctors can help by acknowledging the death, offering a follow-up conversation (for example to explain what happened or answer questions about the illness), and recognising when a bereaved patient needs more help.

::: example The daughter who was the carer {#ex-bereaved}
Ms Carol Simmons, 58, cared for her mother with dementia for seven years until her death eight months ago. She has given up her job and lives alone. She tells her GP she cannot stop thinking about her mother, still sets two places at the table, avoids friends, and feels her life has no purpose. She sleeps badly and has lost weight. How should her GP respond?
::: solution
Her grief is intense and persistent at eight months and is impairing her daily life. Possible prolonged grief disorder (by ICD-11 criteria, which require at least six months) and depression should both be considered: ask about mood, anhedonia, hopelessness and thoughts of suicide, and assess risk sensitively. Carers who lose the person they cared for often lose their daily role, income and social contacts at the same time, which increases risk.

Response: listen and acknowledge the loss and the years of caring; explain that intense grief can be helped. Offer referral to bereavement support (for example the local hospice or a bereavement charity) or psychological therapy, and treat depression if present. Practical help with finances, work and social connection (carers' organisations often support former carers) is part of care. Arrange follow-up. If she expresses suicidal thoughts, follow local safety and crisis guidance and give her information about support services, such as Samaritans (116 123 in the UK).
:::

Communication and advance care planning are in [[geriatrics-palliative/palliative-care]], and capacity in [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]].

::: history
In 1969 the Swiss-American psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross published *On Death and Dying*, based on interviews with dying patients in Chicago. Its five "stages" (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) were never intended as a fixed sequence and are not supported as such by later research, but the book persuaded doctors to talk with dying people rather than around them. In 1993 the House of Lords' judgment in *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* established in English law that withdrawing life-prolonging treatment, including artificial nutrition and hydration, could be lawful when it was not in the patient's best interests. In 1999 Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut published the dual-process model of grief. In the UK, the Liverpool Care Pathway, developed in the late 1990s by the Royal Liverpool University Hospital and the Marie Curie Hospice in Liverpool, spread widely before the Neuberger review of 2013 led to its withdrawal and its replacement by individualised care based on the five priorities for care of the dying person.
:::

::: summary
- Recognising dying is a clinical judgement based on several signs together, after considering reversible causes; uncertainty is normal and should be shared; review at least every 24 hours.
- The five priorities for care: recognise, communicate, involve, support, and an individual plan of care.
- Stop what no longer helps; prescribe anticipatory medicines for pain, breathlessness, agitation, nausea and secretions; use a syringe pump when several doses are needed.
- Mouth care relieves thirst; decisions about assisted hydration are individual and reviewed.
- Withdrawing non-beneficial treatment and proportionate symptom relief, including palliative sedation, are lawful; euthanasia and assisted suicide are unlawful in the UK at the time of writing.
- Verify death after at least five minutes' observation of cardiorespiratory arrest, then confirm absent pupillary, corneal and motor responses.
- In England and Wales, every death is reviewed by a medical examiner or a coroner; unnatural deaths (including those after falls and injuries) go to the coroner.
- Grief is normal; prolonged grief disorder and depression need recognition and support.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Write the anticipatory prescription {level=1}
List the five symptoms that anticipatory medicines usually cover, and the drug class used for each.
::: solution
Pain and breathlessness: an opioid. Agitation and anxiety: a benzodiazepine (midazolam). Nausea and vomiting: an antiemetic (for example haloperidol, levomepromazine or cyclizine). Noisy respiratory secretions: an antimuscarinic (hyoscine butylbromide or glycopyrronium). Doses depend on the person's current medicines, renal function and frailty and follow local guidance; the prescription also states the route (usually subcutaneous), the dose range, and the maximum frequency.
:::

::: exercise Verify a death {level=1}
List the steps in verifying death after cardiorespiratory arrest, according to the 2008 code of practice.
::: solution
Observe the person for a minimum of five minutes to establish irreversible cardiorespiratory arrest: no central pulse on palpation, no heart sounds on auscultation, and no breathing. Any spontaneous return of cardiac or respiratory activity restarts the five-minute observation. Then confirm absence of pupillary responses to light, absence of corneal reflexes, and absence of any motor response to supraorbital pressure. Record the time of death as the time these criteria are fulfilled, and document the findings.
:::

::: exercise Explain the noisy breathing {level=1}
A dying man's wife is distressed by his rattling breathing. What would you tell her, and what can be done?
::: solution
Explain gently that the noise is caused by saliva and secretions collecting in the throat because he is too weak to cough or swallow them, that it is common near death, and that he is probably not aware of it or distressed by it. Repositioning him on his side often reduces the noise. An antimuscarinic drug can be given to reduce new secretions, although it does not always help. Suction is rarely helpful and may distress him. Encourage her to sit with him, talk to him and hold his hand if she wishes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Morphine for a syringe pump {level=2 check="5*2.5"}
An opioid-naive woman who is dying has needed five subcutaneous doses of morphine 2.5 mg for pain in 24 hours. What total dose of subcutaneous morphine (in mg) did she receive?
::: solution
$5 \times 2.5 = 12.5$ mg in 24 hours. This total guides the dose for a 24-hour syringe pump (for example 10–15 mg, depending on her frailty and renal function and on local guidance), with breakthrough doses still available.
:::
:::

::: exercise From oral to subcutaneous morphine {level=2 check="80/2"}
A man takes modified-release morphine 40 mg twice daily and has needed no breakthrough doses. He can no longer swallow. Using an oral to subcutaneous ratio of 2:1, what 24-hour subcutaneous morphine dose (in mg) should the syringe pump contain?
::: solution
Oral TDD $= 40 \times 2 = 80$ mg. Subcutaneous dose $= 80 / 2 = 40$ mg over 24 hours. Breakthrough subcutaneous dose about $40/6 \approx 6.7$ mg, rounded to a practical dose according to local guidance. The pump is started when the next oral dose would have been due.
:::
:::

::: exercise Answer the hydration question {level=2}
The son of a woman dying of pancreatic cancer asks for an intravenous drip because "she's dehydrated and she'll die of thirst". She is drowsy, has a dry mouth and is taking sips. How would you respond and decide?
::: solution
Listen to his concern and explain that reduced drinking is a normal part of dying, and that a dry mouth, the main source of thirst, is relieved by frequent mouth care (moistening the mouth, lip balm), which he can help with. Then make an individual decision ([[#def-dying]]): if dehydration may be contributing to distress or delirium, and fluids are unlikely to cause harm (for example no significant oedema, ascites or respiratory secretions), a trial of subcutaneous fluids is reasonable, with clear review criteria at least every 12 hours. If fluids are likely to cause harm without benefit, explain why they are not recommended. Record the discussion and the decision, and revisit it if things change.
:::
:::

::: exercise A request to end life {level=3}
Mrs Gwen Pritchard, 79, with metastatic cancer and severe breathlessness, says to her doctor: "I want you to give me something to end it." How should the doctor respond?
::: solution
Respond with openness rather than shutting the conversation down: acknowledge the request and ask what has led her to it. Explore suffering in all its dimensions: uncontrolled breathlessness or other symptoms, fear of what dying will be like (for example fear of choking), depression, feeling a burden, loss of control or dignity, family or spiritual distress. Assess for depression and suicidal thoughts. Address what can be addressed: better management of breathlessness (fan, opioids, anxiety management), explaining that dying from her illness can usually be made comfortable, offering specialist palliative care, and reassuring her that she can refuse treatments she does not want and that distress at the end of life can be relieved, including with sedation if needed. Be honest that the doctor cannot lawfully end her life or help her to end it, since euthanasia and assisted suicide are unlawful in the UK at the time of writing. Document the conversation, involve the team, and continue to see her: the request is often a sign of suffering that responds to care.
:::
:::

::: exercise Medical examiner or coroner? {level=3}
Mr Frank Holloway, 86, fell at home, fractured his hip and had surgery. He developed pneumonia and died ten days later. The ward doctor proposes the cause of death "1a Pneumonia, 2 Dementia" and plans to complete the MCCD. Is this correct?
::: solution
No. The death followed an injury (a hip fracture from a fall), and the chain of events is: fall, fractured neck of femur, surgery, immobility and pneumonia, death. A death caused or contributed to by an injury is not a purely natural death and must be referred to the coroner. A more accurate cause of death would be along the lines of "1a Bronchopneumonia, 1b Immobility following surgery for fractured neck of femur, 1c Fall", with dementia and other conditions in part 2 if they contributed. The doctor should discuss the case with the medical examiner, who will advise on referral to the coroner; the coroner may decide that an inquest is required or may allow the doctor to issue the MCCD with the injury recorded. The family should be told why the referral is being made. Accurate certification matters for the family, for public health statistics and for learning from deaths.
:::
:::
