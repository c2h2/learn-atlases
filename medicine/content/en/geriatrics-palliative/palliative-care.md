Palliative care is the discipline that treats the person when curing is no longer the plan: total pain (Cicely Saunders' phrase, still the field's best diagnostic instrument), the multidisciplinary team that makes that phrase operational, and the timing argument that has reorganised it - that palliation belongs beside treatment from diagnosis, not after treatment ends. **This chapter covers the philosophy and its machinery (holistic assessment, the MDT, the settings and their funding), the communication skill-set that is the specialty's actual procedure (prognostic honesty under uncertainty, goals-of-care and advance-care planning, hope recalibration rather than hope removal), and the disease-specific practice of modern palliation: cancer, the organ-failure trajectories with their late-referral scandal, conservative kidney management, the neurology clinics, dementia and frailty at the end of the category system, and the outpatient-palliation model that now serves more people than the historic inpatient one. Its two governing sentences: 'total pain is real and treatable in all its components', and 'the prognosis question is never all-or-nothing; it is what happens next, and what matters to you, asked in the same conversation.'**

## The discipline and its machinery

::: definition Palliative care: philosophy, team and settings {#def-discipline}
**The foundation is a moral observation with clinical consequences: that dying people suffer in more than one dimension at once - Cicely Saunders' total pain (the physical and the psychological, social and spiritual arriving as one complaint: the bone pain that is also the fear of being a burden and the unfinished argument with the brother and the loss of the work that was the self), which her generation converted from a chaplain's insight into an assessment template; and St Christopher's and the modern hospice movement's second insight: that this assessment is a team sport and the team needs a building or a visiting model or both. The team's composition is its evidence: the medical and nursing staff trained in symptom science, the specialists who hold the hard conversations as their main work, the pharmacists (the anticipatory-prescribing and the injectable-pump and the communication-with-difficult-supplies literature), the physiotherapists and OTs (the energy-conservation-equipment-and-bathroom-geometry that determines whether a death at home is possible), the psychologists and counsellors, the chaplains and spiritual-care workers (the spiritual-history instruments - FICA and FOLEY and the 'where do you find strength' question that opens the conversation for the self-described atheist as often as the vicar), the social workers (the benefits-and-debts-and-housing-and-immigration-status medicine no one else prescribes), the therapists (the music and art and reminiscence work that is not entertainment), and the volunteers (the companionship and the carer-relief that the Kings Fund and NICE-end-of-life documents count as an intervention because the research does). The settings and their honest geography: the hospice inpatient unit (for the small proportion with truly unmanageable symptoms - the specialist symptom control that cannot be done elsewhere, and the respite that prevents carer collapse which is itself a mortality-prevention service for the caregiver), the home as first choice for most (the district nurse and hospice-at-home and the fast-track admission route and the out-of-hours medicines and the 'who do I telephone at 3am' that determines place of death more than any policy leaflet), the hospital liaison and the outpatient clinics (now the majority of contacts in most services - the early-palliative-integration model from the Temel and Bakitas and the Cochrane reviews of specialised palliative care in advanced cancer showing the symptom-burden and quality-of-life and sometimes survival signal, and the concurrent-care models that let palliation sit beside chemotherapy rather than after it), the nursing homes and the dementia trajectories (the palliative-care approach-not-setting debate which is the current professional literature's central argument that hospice philosophy travels with the patient into every bed), and the access inequities documented across countries: the non-cancer-disease referrals late or never, the ethnic-minority and rural and LGBTQ+ and homeless and learning-disability and prison cohorts under-served, the children-palliative-medicine-as-its-own-discipline, and the funding lottery that makes place of death a postcode statistic - which is why this chapter treats commissioning as a clinical topic. The timing evidence in one paragraph: the early-integration trials (the oncology-outpatient-arm-randomised studies showing improved quality of life and mood and, in several, survival signals, and the guidelines now recommending referral at advanced-diagnosis not at treatment-exhaustion); the practical timing tools (the Surprise Question - 'would I be surprised if this patient died in the next year' - a screening prompt not a verdict, the disease-trajectory recognition below, the 'trigger tools' used in hospitals to flag the last-year-of-life review conversation, and the frailty-index-and-fracture-and-organ-failure-prognostication-adjacent-medicine-which-is-whole-book-territory-reached-here-because-palliative-referral-decisions-are-prognosis-decisions-with-a-phone-number); and the funding-and-policy layer the clinician should know because it determines the plan (the continuing-health-care-and-PCT-funding-assessment-battlegrounds-and-the-direct-payments-adjacent-personal-budgets-and-the-differences-between-a-funding-review-and-a-care-plan-and-horse-racing-blinkers-the-family-should-not-have-to-learn-to-read-which-is-why-the-specialist-nurse-with-time-is-often-the-difference-between-a-cruel-few-weeks-and-a-dignified-one).**
:::

## The conversation

::: definition Goals, prognosis and advance care planning {#def-conversation}
**The communication procedure this specialty invented for general medicine: the serious-illness conversation with its learnable structure (the setting and the seating and the 'what do you already know' opening; the agenda-setting question - 'some people like details, some prefer the big picture, which are you' - which is consent as much as courtesy; the warning-shot-then-stop-then-answer-the-actual-question mechanics of bad-news delivery that the breaking-bad-news literature (Baile and SpiKES and Back and Arnold) formalised; the emotion-as-data response (the name-and-normalise and the silence that lets the tears finish their sentence before the pharmacology starts); and the close with a plan and a person's name on it - because 'call if worse' is the non-plan that produces the crisis-admission). Prognostication under honesty: the craft of the ranges and the trajectories (the 'we cannot tell you the date; we can tell you what usually happens and where you sit relative to that; I would not be surprised if...' as the calibrated sentence; the optimism-bias and the accuracy-audits and the fact that clinicians are wrong in both directions which is why the good prognostic statement names what would change the estimate), the prognostic tools' legitimate use (the renal-prognostication indices-and-the-glasgow-prognostic-score-in-palliative-medicine-and-the-surprise-question-as-screen-not-as-sentence), and the hardest application - telling someone they may be dying when you are 60 percent sure and they are 100 percent terrified (the 'I am worried this illness is moving faster than we hoped' sentence that is both true and hearable). Goals of care, done properly, is a document plus a relationship: the values conversation ('what does a good day look like now', 'what would you not want treatment for', 'what are you willing to go through for what chance'), the treatment-breadth ladder (the full-active-versus-limited-versus-comfort-only decisions made specifically - the CPR conversation with its honest disease-specific survival figures, the ICU question with its real numbers for this cancer at this stage rather than the leaflet's averages, the feeding-tube and dialysis and ventilation decisions each with their own trials-of-benefit-honesty below), and the artefacts (the advance decisions refusing specified treatments with their legal force and the ReSPECT-style emergency-care-and-treatment-planning record which is the recommendation-not-refusal half and the power of attorney which is the person-half and the 'document is only as good as the system that can find it' rule: the scanned, indexed, shared and registered version (the advance-decision registration services exist for exactly this)-and-the-cold-stroke-of-a-plan-sitting-in-a-handbag). The culture-and-truth-telling layer, because the model patient is not a London graduate: the family-conspiracy literature (the protective-disclosure patterns in South Asian, East Asian, African, Middle Eastern and some Southern European families and the clinical answer that is neither forced disclosure nor collusion - the negotiated 'how much does she want to know, asked with her family present, revisited' and the one-relative-as-translator-not-gatekeeper arrangement when it can be made), the hope-recalibration technique (never 'there is no hope' but 'I hope we can keep you out of pain; I hope for the good month; hope changes its object and the clinician names the new objects out loud'), the 'total pain' screen that runs through all of it (the spiritual distress and the practical terror - 'who pays the rent, who feeds the dog, will he stop breathing in front of the children' - all recorded as symptoms with treatments, because the social-work and chaplaincy and benefits referrals are palliative prescriptions and a plan that ignores them is what the conversation promised it would not be), and the clinician's own line: the moral distress and the 'difficult patient' who is usually an unmet total-pain presentation and the Balint-adjacent-support-systems-and- Schwartz-rounds-adjacent-fabric-of-this-workspace-because-a-specialty-built-on-seeing-clearly-cannot-run-on-staff-too-exhausted-to-look.**

::: example The 63-year-old with pancreatic cancer, three months in {#ex-early}
Newly diagnosed metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma; starting modified FOLFIRINOX; she asks the oncologist point-blank 'how long', and her husband writes things down in clinic but the family 'doesn't talk about it at home'.
::: solution
**Early-integration-done-right ([[#def-conversation]], [[#def-discipline]], [[gastrointestinal/gi-bleeding-cancer]]): the honest answer - 'median-survival-numbers-quoted-as-medians-are-cruel-if-unexplained-and-useless-if-hidden: the craft is the range plus the trajectory plus what-might-change-it ("most people live about a year; some longer; the scan after two cycles tells us more about your particular tumour; whatever the number, our plan treats the cancer and looks after you at the same time") with the question-underneath-named ("are you asking because of something specific - the children, the wedding, the business") which is where the actual planning starts'; the referral that same week - 'palliative-care-outpatient-with-the-chemotherapy-not-after-it-because-the-Temel-model-data-say-her-symptoms-her-mood-and-possibly-her-survival-improve-with-concurrent-specialist-palliation-and-the-referral-wording-matters ("you are getting both teams; this is not giving up, this is the standard of care") and the husband's-notebook-turned-into-a-shared-document-she-can-also-read; the plan's unglamorous-highest-yield-items - 'the anticipatory-medicines-list-written-in-advance-and-held-at-home (the pain-and-nausea-and-breathlessness-and-agitation-bundle that prevents the 2am call that ends in the A&E department), the preferred-place-of-care conversation logged when she can choose (with the district nurse and hospice-at-home introduced before they are needed because the system moves slower than pancreatic cancer), the benefits-and-work-and-driving-and-finance-referral (the specialist nurse who knows the fast-track-benefits-process is worth more than most of the pharmacology), and the family-communication-eliciting-the-conspiracy ("who in the family does not know what; does she want them to") which predicts a good death more reliably than any scan'.**

::: example The 80-year-old who declines dialysis {#ex-conservative-kidney}
An 80-year-old man with diabetic kidney failure, eGFR 7, frail, lives with his daughter, says firmly 'no machines, I have seen what it does to my brother-in-law'; the renal registrar offers 'the full range of options' in a meeting that lasted ten minutes and left the family researching dialysis survival statistics online.
::: solution
**Conservative kidney management offered as a real option ([[#def-trajectories]], [[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]]): the decision made properly - 'he has made his choice and it is a medically reasonable one: in frail patients over 75-80 starting dialysis for comorbid kidney failure, several good-quality studies (the Murata and Loughlin and Chandurkar matched-cohort studies) show survival comparable to dialysis in matched groups with better symptom scores and fewer hospital days, and the choice carries the risk of uraemic death which he must be told plainly; the wrong version of "full range of options" was the ten-minute meeting - the right version is a repeated conversation with time, the daughter present, and his brother-in-law's story taken seriously as evidence about his values'; the service build - 'the structured conservative-care programme: the renal symptom-protocol (the nausea and itch and cramp and sleep and pain plans with dose-adjusted-to-eGFR pharmacology and the palliative team's input written into it), the anaemia-and-fluid-and-blood-pressure-medicine-continuing-with-goal-shifted-targets, the advance-plan-recorded (the hospital-avoidance and antibiotics-for-the-last-pneumonia and resuscitation-position questions answered in his words with his GP and the out-of-hours service able to see them), the 'where when and with whom' preferred-place-of-care logged, and the shared care with the district nurse and the hospice with the named people in it'; and the family - 'the daughter's internet research answered explicitly: what dying of kidney failure usually looks like (the increasing sleepiness, the manageable most of the time, the crisis-medicines-held-at-home, and the honest "not everyone" caveat) because the uraemia myth of gasping and vomiting is the specific terror that keeps frail people onto dialysis machines, and the offer of her attending the next clinic because the bereavement support starts before the death in good services, which this chapter's last section is about'.**

A 72-year-old with end-stage ischaemic cardiomyopathy, four admissions this year, ICD shocks 'like a kick', ejection fraction 15 percent, breathless at the window; the cardiology team 'keeps referring to palliative care' and he keeps refusing 'because that means they are stopping'.

**The-bridge-service-he-doesn't-know-he's-asking-for ([[#def-trajectories]], [[#def-discipline]], [[cardiovascular/heart-failure]]): the mislabel that wasted a year - 'his refusal is rational given the sentence he heard: the modern palliative offer in advanced heart failure is concurrent (the symptom team, not the stopping team), and the reframe that got him in ("they are the ones who deal with the breathlessness and the shocks and the tablets that make you swollen - nothing about stopping your water pills") is the referral language every cardiology clinic should carry; the device conversation - 'the ICD-shock question in declining trajectory: deactivation of the shock function is a legitimate, reversible, non-euthanasia decision that many patients make once told it exists (the HRS-adjacent-ethical-consensus statements are explicit) and it is discussed in clinic with the electrophysiologist and the palliative team and the spouse in the room, with the pacing function usually kept, and the sentence that unlocks it ("the defibrillator protects you from sudden death; ::: example The hospice bed that was 'not available' {#ex-notavailable}

**The-admission-system-is-the-clinical-care ([[#def-discipline]], [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]): the honest review-answer - 'a hospice's first function is the 3am telephone and the bed that appears when the symptom crisis that the home cannot hold arrives; the metrics with meaning - 'the plan-and-preferred-place-availability-for-who-dies-this-year (the denominator audited against the death registrations), the place-of-death-trend-by-quintile-because-the-aggregate-improvement-can-hide-the-gap-widening, the 'did you get home' family-survey-item, the ambulance-calls-ending-in-unexpected-death review, and the service's own confession-metric: the number of people who needed specialist palliative care and never met it, reported quarterly, because this whole chapter began with one woman insisting that dying badly is a systems fact - and the programme that cannot count its failures has already made them'.**
:::

## Where this leads

Symptom pharmacology is the next chapter ([[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]]), the legal architecture of refusing and withholding is in [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]] and [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]], the dying-phase physiology is in [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]], and the disease-specific details link from [[gastrointestinal/gi-bleeding-cancer]], [[cardiovascular/heart-failure]] and [[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]].

::: history
**Palliative care's history is a biography before it is a discipline: the two Cicely Saunders (the nurse-social-worker-then-doctor who, working at St John's and St Joseph's hospices in the 1950s-60s, married the modern hospice idea - inpatient symptom control plus the family plus the volunteer plus the research - to the word 'total pain', which her correspondence and her clinical notebooks made operational, and who forced British medicine to notice that dying was a clinical specialty, ending at the 1967-opening Royal Marsden-adjacent St Christopher's Hospice she founded and the Mount-Sinai and Yale and the international-house-hospice-daughterhood that followed her lectures across five continents); the American-branch-history (the Mount Sinai home-care program that her 1963 lectures inspired, and Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's 'On Death and Dying' of 1969-which-wrongly-frozen-into-the-five-stages-that-everyone-in-the-field-now-teaches-as-a-warning-about-simplification-while-her-actual-lectures-were-about-listening, and the home-hospice-movement-of-the-1970s-and-the-Medicare-hospice-benefit-of-1983-which-made-the-American-field-funding-structured-and-eligibility-contested-in-one-legislative-blow). The second phase was scientific: the symptom-science era of the 1980s-90s - the opioid-rotation and receptor-pharmacology work, and the journals and textbooks of the National Hospice and Palliative Care Organization and the UK Association for Palliative Medicine that made symptom evidence citable, the quality-of-dying measurement era of the 1990s-2000s, the US Institute of Medicine reports that named much hospital and nursing-home dying as a system failure, and the Dartmouth Atlas geography showing that end-of-life intensity is a postcode phenomenon, not a patient-preference one, and the SUPPORT-study-of-1995-whose-name-is-still-a-byword (the 'wishes were usually known but rarely documented; do-not-resuscitate orders arrived late; pain undertreated; ICU deaths undignified' findings that triggered a generation of advance-care-planning policy and whose re-analysis in 2016 admitted the intervention fell short - the honest record of a field that studies its own failures unusually honestly). The early-integration turn is the current chapter: the Temel-2010-lung-cancer and the Bakitas-TPC and the oncology outpatient trials and the ASCO and NICE guidance moving specialist palliation from last resort to concurrent standard, the supportive-palliative-care-department-name-changes across academic centres signalling the shift, the conservative-kidney-management-randomisation-and-cohort-program-of-the-2010s-2020s proving that the 'dialysis or nothing' question was a false dichotomy, and the 'palliative care for dementia and frailty' literature that is still arguing about eligibility while the demography argues back. The dark thread, kept where it belongs: the map-of-the-missed-people (the 'out-of-sight out-of-mind' reports on the dying in care homes and prisons and institutions; the Macmillan and National-Audit-era findings that a third of people in some countries get no palliative contact before death; the reports of dying people caught in immigration-detention and 'hostile environment' policy, which put the access question into political language the specialty cannot outsource). The history's trajectory: from a house in South London to a medical discipline arguing with oncology and nephrology and cardiology about timeliness, still fighting for the same thing Saunders fought for - that dying badly is usually a systems failure and that naming it as one is a clinical act.** - [[medical-ethics-law/end-of-life-ethics]], [[public-health/health-systems-economics]], [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]].**
:::

::: summary
- Total pain - physical, psychological, social, spiritual - is the assessment template; the MDT (with pharmacy, therapy, spiritual care, social work and volunteers) is the treatment.
- Early integration with disease-directed care improves symptoms, mood and sometimes survival; late referral is the dominant failure across organ-failure, renal, dementia and frailty cohorts.
- Prognostic honesty is a craft of ranges, trajectories and named uncertainty; the values conversation and the plan belong together, with the artefacts (advance decisions, emergency care plans, attorneys) findable by the system that must honour them.
- Hope is recalibrated, not removed, and truth-telling is negotiated with families, not imposed on them; the 'difficult' patient is usually total pain.
- Disease-specific palliation is now a subdiscipline: cancer emergencies, the organ-failure crises, conservative kidney management, the MND clinic model and the dementia approach each have their own scripts and evidence.
- Place of death is a system output: anticipatory medicines, named 3am contacts, funding navigation and early preferred-place conversations determine it more than beds do.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Write the referral that works {level=1}
Compose (three sentences) a referral from the oncology ward to community palliative care for a 58-year-old with metastatic breast cancer, poorly controlled pain, at home with a husband who is 'the strong one', two teenagers, and a 'we don't say the word' family rule.
::: solution
**The-referral-as-a-clinical-document ([[#def-discipline]]): a version - 'Mrs X, 58, metastatic breast cancer with bone and liver involvement, currently on [regimen], poorly controlled pain despite [current analgesia] and admitted twice this month; your symptom expertise and anticipatory prescribing would help us keep her at home. Two teenagers at home and the family avoid discussion of prognosis, so your experience of family communication at this stage is as important as the medicines. Preferred place of care not yet discussed - she has said she wants home if possible; husband is the sole carer and will need respite planning.' The mechanics this gets right: the specific problem (pain, the carer, the unspoken), the ask (symptoms AND communication - services act on named requests), the family-rule-mentioned-early-because-'we-don't-say-the-word'-families-get-better-results-from-the-team-that-already-knows, and the no-jargon-timetable (the 'asap-or-routine' field completed honestly by the referrer's actual surprise-question judgment) - [[clinical-skills/communication]].**
:::
:::

::: exercise Handle the family conspiracy {level=2}
In a South Asian family, the adult children meet you in the corridor: 'Please don't tell our mother the cancer has come back; she will lose hope and die sooner.' She has asked you twice whether the scan was 'all clear'. What do you do?
::: solution
**Negotiated truth ([[#def-conversation]]): the first mistake avoided - 'agreeing to lie destroys the one thing all three parties need (her trust in you and their trust in you and your ability to document a real conversation), and refusing in the corridor converts a family in grief into an adversary; the literature and the case-law answer is the negotiated path'; the technique - 'the assessment interview with the family: what exactly do they fear, what has she been told so far, what is her own track record (the woman who has 'asked twice' is telling you she wants information - but confirm privately with her: "some people in your position like full details, some prefer the broad picture - which are you, and who should be involved?") done with respect and privacy and often an interpreter who is not the family'; then the contract - 'a staged, followed-the-patient's-pace plan agreed with the family (the honest-but-measured language in the joint meeting, which is what she will later describe as "they told me at my own speed"), and the family won over by the fact that the plan protects her hope rather than puncturing it'; and the record - 'the conversation documented, her stated information-preference recorded for the 2am doctors, and the truth that she can revoke or change - the plan is a process and the process is the palliation; and the meta-awareness: the 'she will lose hope' fear is not silly: prognostic disclosure does carry a real nocebo-and-distress risk in some patients which is exactly why pacing exists, and why the alternative (the secret the whole family keeps while she dies pretending) is the worse medicine'.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Manage the MND clinic visit {level=2}
A 57-year-old motor-neurone-disease patient, functioning dropping, non-invasive-ventilation being discussed, says 'when I can't breathe any more I want to die at home like my father, not strapped to a machine'. What does that sentence generate, by when, and in which documents?
::: solution
**The-proactive-advance-planning-consult ([[#def-trajectories]], [[#def-conversation]], [[nervous-system/neuromuscular]]): the answer's components - 'her sentence is a treatment plan asking to be written: (1) the ventilator conversation had now in the clinic not at the crisis (the NIV-benefits-and-burden-and-what-escalation-would-look-like-including-the-tracheostomy-question-in-MND-where-the-evidence-is-modest-and-the-choice-genuinely-divides-patients, the 'we can start and stop' rule that removes the trap of the machine-without-an-exit, and her answer recorded); (2) the anticipatory symptom bundle (the breathlessness-and-pain-and-secretion-and-anxiety medicines planned in advance with the injectable route and the syringe-driver familiarity and the hospice-at-home enrolment before the emergency); (3) the advance decision (the specific refusals she may want to make - intubation, tracheostomy, feeding tube at a stated stage - written with the capacity assessment current and the solicitor-and-second-doctor-witnessing-where-the-law-or-the-clinics-prefer, and the communication-access plan (the eye-gazer and the augmentative-communication board now because her ability to make her wishes known is a declining resource - which is this disease's cruelest interaction); (4) the practicals (the hospital-avoidance plan with the out-of-hours number and the medications-at-home and the ambulance-crew-aware-of-the-plan-registry and the children's-and-funeral-and-legacy-conversations-the-specialist-nurse-holds-while-she-can-join-in) - and the schedule: all of this is routine at this visit because the MND clinic model's defining virtue is that the conversations happen months before the crisis makes them impossible, which is the palliation chapter's whole timing argument in one disease.**
:::
:::

::: exercise Audit a service's equity {level=3}
Audit request: your hospice's palliative-care-eligible population reaches only 41 percent of decedents; non-cancer diseases, care-home residents, ethnic-minority and homeless patients are under-represented; the inpatient unit is always full; the waiting list for home nursing is real. Write the findings memo and the intervention set with owner and metric per line.
::: solution
**Equity-audit-as-clinical-work ([[#def-discipline]], [[public-health/screening]], [[clinical-skills/patient-safety]]): the findings frame - '41 percent with the skew toward exactly the cohorts the system has always known how to serve is a service design result, not a preference finding, and the census denominator proves which of the dying we see and which we miss; the interviews that must accompany the numbers (the nursing homes' 'we didn't know you came to homes', the GP surgeries' 'I assumed she was already known to you', the homeless-cohort 'nobody books a hospice from a night shelter', and the families' late-referral stories which all contain one of the same three sentences: the referral-route was unknown, the eligibility was assumed, or the trust was never built)'; the interventions with owners and metrics - 'the automatic-flag pathway (the ED and the inpatient wards flag the surprise-question-positive and the fracture-liaison and the renal and heart-failure clinics co-produce the referral triggers, owned by the liaison team; metric: referral timing from last-year-flag, by diagnosis group), the homes-and-streets model (the named-liaison-nurse-per-care-home-cluster with the antipsychotic-and-anticipatory-medicines-clinic-which-is-also-the-delirium-dementia-chapter's-ask and the homeless-team-and-drug-and-alcohol-service partnership with the same-documentation-flexibility-the-prison-and-immigration-detention-advocates-fight-for; metric: the care-home and rough-sleeper death-in-preferred-place rate), the language-and-trust-layer (the community-organisation-and-faith-group-partnerships-with-paid-community-navigators-not-flyers-in-six-languages-and-the-interpreter-booking-system-that-is-a-default-not-a-favour-and-the-workforce-training-on-family-disclosure-patterns; metric: the family-survey-equity-items-reported-disaggregated), the capacity re-balancing (the inpatient unit's always-full status audited for whether it is serving unmanageable-symptom needs or functioning as a front door it should not be; the home-first investment in the fast-track-admission-and-the-7-day-sitting-nursing-because-capacity-plans-write-the-place-of-death-curve-before-the-family-asks); and the accountability - 'the quarterly equity report with the decedent-cohort denominator and the non-cancer-representative-percentages published internally to the board and externally in the service's report, because the history of this specialty is a woman insisting that who dies badly is a systems fact, and the audit is how a modern service proves it has heard her'.**
:::
