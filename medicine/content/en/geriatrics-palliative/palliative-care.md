Everyone dies, and in high-income countries most people die after a period of illness rather than suddenly. Palliative care is the part of medicine concerned with living as well as possible with an illness that cannot be cured, and with dying well. It is not only for the last days, not only for cancer, and not the opposite of active treatment: it can run alongside chemotherapy, dialysis or heart failure treatment, and in some situations it improves survival as well as quality of life.

This chapter covers what palliative care is and who provides it, the different ways in which people approach the end of life, how to recognise that someone may be in the last year of life, and the conversations and advance care planning that help people get the care they want. Symptom control is covered in [[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]] and the last days of life in [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]].

## What palliative care is {#sec-discipline}

::: definition Palliative care {#def-discipline}
The World Health Organization (2002) defines **palliative care** as an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problems associated with life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual. Palliative care affirms life and regards dying as a normal process, intends neither to hasten nor to postpone death, offers a support system to help patients live as actively as possible until death and to help families cope during the illness and in bereavement, and is applicable early in the course of illness, in conjunction with other therapies intended to prolong life.
:::

Three points in this definition deserve emphasis. First, the unit of care is the patient **and the family**. Second, suffering has **several dimensions**: Cicely Saunders called this **total pain**, meaning that physical pain is shaped and worsened by psychological distress (fear, depression), social problems (money, family conflict, loss of role) and spiritual or existential distress (meaning, guilt, what happens after death). Third, palliative care applies **early** in the illness.

### Who provides it

Most palliative care is **generalist** care, given by the professionals who already look after the person: general practitioners, district nurses, hospital teams, care-home staff and the specialists in the person's disease. **Specialist palliative care** teams (palliative medicine consultants, clinical nurse specialists and allied professionals) support them and see people with complex needs, in hospitals, in the community, and in hospices, which offer inpatient beds, day services and hospice-at-home care.

In England, the national framework *Ambitions for Palliative and End of Life Care* (2021–2026) sets out six ambitions: each person is seen as an individual; each person gets fair access to care; maximising comfort and wellbeing; care is coordinated; all staff are prepared to care; and each community is prepared to help. Since the Health and Care Act 2022, integrated care boards in England have a legal duty to commission palliative care services. Most UK hospices are charities that receive part of their funding from the NHS.

::: theorem Early palliative care improves outcomes {#thm-early}
Integrating specialist palliative care early in the course of serious illness, alongside disease-directed treatment, improves quality of life and mood and reduces aggressive treatment at the end of life, without shortening survival. In a randomised trial of 151 patients with newly diagnosed metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer (Temel and colleagues, 2010), those offered early palliative care had better quality of life, fewer depressive symptoms and less aggressive end-of-life care than those receiving standard oncology care alone; median survival was also longer (11.6 versus 8.9 months).
:::

::: proof
Early palliative care changes several things at once. Symptoms are assessed and treated more thoroughly. Patients and families have repeated conversations about prognosis and goals, which help them understand their illness and make decisions about treatment that reflect their priorities. In the Temel trial, patients in the early palliative care group were less likely to receive chemotherapy in the last weeks of life and more likely to be referred to hospice earlier. The survival difference was a secondary finding, and its mechanism is uncertain; it may reflect fewer complications from late aggressive treatment and better management of depression and symptoms. Later trials across cancers and in some non-cancer conditions have supported benefits on quality of life and mood.
:::

::: example Referral at diagnosis {#ex-early}
Mr David Kim, 63, has just been diagnosed with pancreatic cancer with liver metastases. He is starting palliative chemotherapy. He has epigastric and back pain, has lost 8 kg, and his wife says he is not sleeping. The oncologist wonders whether a palliative care referral is "too early" because he is still having treatment. What is your view?
::: solution
It is not too early. Metastatic pancreatic cancer has a poor prognosis, and he already has pain, weight loss and distress. Early palliative care alongside chemotherapy is supported by trial evidence ([[#thm-early]]). The referral is framed as additional support, not as giving up on treatment.

What palliative care can offer now: assessment and treatment of pain (which in pancreatic cancer often has a neuropathic component from coeliac plexus involvement; a coeliac plexus block may help), assessment of weight loss (pancreatic exocrine insufficiency is common and treatable with pancreatic enzyme replacement), sleep and mood, and support for his wife. It also opens a space for gradual conversations about what matters to him, what he understands about his illness, and his wishes for the future, so that decisions later in the illness are informed by them.
:::
:::

## Trajectories of dying {#sec-trajectories}

::: definition Illness trajectories {#def-trajectories}
Research on how function changes in the last months of life (Lynn and Adamson, 2003; Murray and colleagues, 2005) describes three typical **illness trajectories**:

1. **Short period of evident decline**, typical of cancer: function is maintained for a long time, followed by a fairly predictable decline over weeks to a few months.
2. **Long-term limitations with intermittent serious episodes**, typical of organ failure (heart failure, COPD, chronic kidney disease, liver disease): gradual decline punctuated by acute exacerbations, any of which may be fatal, with partial recovery after each.
3. **Prolonged dwindling**, typical of frailty and dementia: low function from the start, declining slowly over years, with death often following a minor event such as a chest infection or a fracture.
:::

The trajectories matter because they shape the timing of conversations and the predictability of death. In cancer, the decline is usually recognisable. In organ failure, it is hard to know which exacerbation will be the last, and patients often die in hospital while receiving intensive treatment without ever having discussed their wishes. In frailty and dementia, the end of life may last years and is often not recognised as such at all.

People with non-cancer conditions have historically had less access to specialist palliative care, despite similar or greater symptom burden. Access is also unequal by deprivation, ethnicity and place of residence, which the Ambitions framework identifies as a priority.

### Recognising the last year of life

Clinicians are poor at estimating how long people will live. In a study of terminally ill patients referred to hospice programmes in Chicago (Christakis and Lamont, 2000), doctors' predictions were correct to within a third of the actual survival in only about 20% of cases, and they overestimated survival by a factor of about five on average. Because exact prediction is unreliable, practice focuses on identifying people who are **likely** to be in the last year of life, so that care can be planned.

Tools to prompt this include:

- **The surprise question**: "Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?" If the answer is no, a review of goals and needs is triggered. In a meta-analysis (Downar and colleagues, 2017), its pooled sensitivity was about 67% and specificity about 80% for death within 6 to 18 months; it is a prompt, not an accurate predictor.
- **General and disease-specific indicators**, such as those in the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT) and the Gold Standards Framework proactive identification guidance: declining performance status, unplanned admissions, weight loss, persistent symptoms despite optimal treatment, and disease-specific markers (for example, heart failure with symptoms at rest despite optimal treatment, or COPD with severe airflow obstruction and frequent exacerbations).
- **Performance status scales** such as the Palliative Performance Scale, which grade function and correlate with survival in groups.

Once identified, people can be placed on a primary care palliative care register, have their needs assessed, be offered advance care planning, and have their information shared with out-of-hours services.

::: warning Waiting for certainty
A common mistake is to delay palliative care conversations until death is certain, by which time the patient may be too unwell to take part. Uncertainty is the normal condition in organ failure and frailty. Conversations can acknowledge it ("I hope you'll be well for a long time, but I'm worried that you might get sicker, and I think we should plan for that too") and should be revisited as things change.
:::

::: example Heart failure: planning while treating {#ex-hf}
Mrs Margaret O'Brien, 81, has heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, chronic kidney disease and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD). She has had three admissions with fluid overload in six months, is breathless walking across a room, and has lost weight. She is taking all tolerated heart failure medicines. Her cardiologist asks you to discuss future care. What topics should be covered?
::: solution
She meets indicators for the last year of life: repeated admissions, symptoms at rest or on minimal exertion despite optimal therapy, weight loss and kidney disease. Topics:

1. **Her understanding and priorities**: what she knows about her heart condition and what matters most to her (for example, being at home, avoiding hospital, a family event).
2. **Continuing heart failure treatment**: diuretics and other medicines continue as long as they help her symptoms; this is palliative as well as disease treatment.
3. **The ICD**: an ICD may deliver painful shocks as she approaches death. Deactivation of the shock function, which does not cause death and does not affect pacing, should be discussed while she can decide. Guidance from the British Heart Foundation and the Resuscitation Council UK supports these conversations.
4. **Treatment escalation and resuscitation**: whether she would want further admissions, intensive treatment, or cardiopulmonary resuscitation, which is unlikely to succeed in advanced heart failure. Recording her preferences, for example on a ReSPECT form, helps emergency teams.
5. **Where she would prefer to be cared for**, and the support needed to make that possible (community heart failure nurses, district nurses, parenteral diuretics at home in some services).
6. **Symptom control**: breathlessness, fatigue, low mood.

These conversations are usually spread over several meetings, involve her family if she wishes, and are recorded and shared with her GP and out-of-hours services.
:::
:::

## Conversations and advance care planning {#sec-conversation}

::: definition Advance care planning {#def-conversation}
**Advance care planning (ACP)** is a voluntary process of discussion between a person, their care providers and often those close to them, about their values, goals and preferences for future care, so that care can be guided by those wishes if they later lose capacity or cannot communicate. Outputs may include an **advance statement** of wishes and preferences (not legally binding, but must be taken into account in best-interests decisions), an **advance decision to refuse treatment** (legally binding if valid and applicable), the appointment of a **lasting power of attorney** for health and welfare, and an emergency care and treatment plan such as **ReSPECT**.
:::

Under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (England and Wales), an **advance decision to refuse treatment** (ADRT) must be made by a person aged 18 or over with capacity, must specify the treatment refused, and, if it refuses life-sustaining treatment, must be in writing, signed and witnessed, and include a statement that it applies even if the person's life is at risk. A person cannot use an advance decision to demand a particular treatment.

**ReSPECT** (Recommended Summary Plan for Emergency Care and Treatment), introduced by the Resuscitation Council UK and partners in 2016, records a person's preferences and clinical recommendations for emergency care, including (but not limited to) cardiopulmonary resuscitation. A **do not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation (DNACPR)** decision is a clinical decision that CPR should not be attempted. In England and Wales, the Court of Appeal ruled in 2014 (the Tracey case) that there is a duty to involve the patient in such a decision unless doing so would cause physical or psychological harm; a disagreement does not oblige doctors to offer CPR that will not work, but the patient should be told and offered a second opinion.

### How to have the conversation

Several frameworks help structure difficult conversations. **SPIKES** (Baile and colleagues, 2000) describes six steps for sharing bad news:

1. **Setting**: privacy, sitting down, time, the right people present, no interruptions.
2. **Perception**: ask what the person already understands ("What have you been told about the scan?").
3. **Invitation**: ask how much information they want and how they want it.
4. **Knowledge**: give a warning that bad news is coming, then give information in small pieces, in plain language, checking understanding.
5. **Emotions**: respond to emotion with empathy; allow silence; name the emotion ("I can see this is a shock").
6. **Strategy and summary**: agree a plan and the next steps.

Other useful techniques are **ask–tell–ask** (find out what the person knows, tell them, then check what they have understood), and the approach of hoping for the best while preparing for the worst. When discussing prognosis, clinicians are encouraged to be honest about uncertainty, to give ranges ("weeks to months" rather than "three months"), and to explore what the person hopes for and fears.

Some patients do not want detailed information, and some families ask that information be withheld. The patient's own wishes about information take priority; the clinician can ask the patient directly how much they wish to know and whom they want involved, rather than assuming either way.

::: example Declining dialysis {#ex-conservative-kidney}
Mr Ernest Walker, 80, has chronic kidney disease stage 5 (eGFR 9 mL/min/1.73 m²), diabetes and heart failure, and is CFS 6. He has been offered haemodialysis but says he does not want "to spend my last years attached to a machine". His daughter wants him to have it. How should the team respond?
::: solution
**Capacity and information.** Assume he has capacity unless there is reason to doubt it; a person can refuse treatment even if others disagree. Make sure his decision is informed: explain what dialysis involves, the likely benefits and burdens for someone of his age and frailty (studies of older people with significant comorbidity suggest that the survival gain from dialysis may be small, while time spent on dialysis and in hospital is considerable), and what the alternative involves.

**Conservative kidney management** is an active treatment pathway, not "no treatment": it includes management of anaemia, fluid balance, potassium and acidosis, symptom control (itch, fatigue, breathlessness, nausea, restless legs), advance care planning and support for the family, delivered by renal and palliative teams together.

**The family.** Listen to his daughter's concerns; she may fear that her father is giving up or may not understand what conservative care offers. With his permission, include her in discussions. The decision remains his. Record the discussion, his decision, and the plan, and make clear that he can change his mind.
:::
:::

::: example The hospice bed that was "not available" {#ex-notavailable}
Mrs Fatima Hassan, 74, has metastatic breast cancer. She is in hospital with increasing weakness and is thought to be in the last weeks of life. She wants to die at home, but the discharge is delayed because the local hospice has no beds, and staff assume she must stay in hospital. How else could her wish be met?
::: solution
The hospice inpatient unit is only one of several routes. Options: (1) care at home with a package arranged through the NHS Continuing Healthcare **Fast Track Pathway**, which allows rapid funding of care for people with a rapidly deteriorating condition who may be entering a terminal phase; (2) hospice-at-home or community specialist palliative care nurses; (3) district nurses, GP and out-of-hours services informed through a shared care record; (4) equipment (hospital bed, pressure mattress, commode) delivered before discharge; (5) anticipatory medicines prescribed and dispensed to the home ([[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]]); (6) a completed ReSPECT or equivalent form so that ambulance crews know her wishes.

Before discharge, check what she and her family want and can manage, and whether her husband and children feel able to care for her with support. The barrier was an assumption, not a lack of options: in many areas a rapid discharge to home can be arranged within a day or two.
:::
:::

## Where this leads

Symptom control in advanced illness is covered in [[geriatrics-palliative/symptom-control]], and care in the last days and after death in [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]]. The law on capacity, consent and treatment decisions is developed in [[medical-ethics-law]]. Frailty as a trajectory is described in [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty]].

::: history
The modern hospice movement began with Cicely Saunders, who trained as a nurse, a medical social worker and then a doctor. Working at St Joseph's Hospice in Hackney in the late 1950s, she showed that regular, scheduled oral morphine controlled pain in dying patients far better than doses given only when pain returned, and she developed the concept of total pain. In 1967 she opened St Christopher's Hospice in Sydenham, south London, which combined clinical care, teaching and research. In 1974 Florence Wald founded the first US hospice in Connecticut, and the Canadian surgeon Balfour Mount coined the term "palliative care" for the service he opened at the Royal Victoria Hospital in Montreal in 1975, choosing a word without the religious associations "hospice" had in French-speaking Quebec. Palliative medicine was recognised as a medical specialty in the UK in 1987.
:::

::: summary
- Palliative care improves quality of life for people with life-threatening illness and their families by preventing and relieving suffering in all its dimensions (total pain).
- It applies early, alongside disease-directed treatment, and is for all life-limiting conditions, not only cancer. Early specialist palliative care improves quality of life and mood.
- Most palliative care is generalist; specialist teams support complex cases.
- Three trajectories (cancer, organ failure, frailty and dementia) shape when and how to plan.
- Clinicians overestimate survival; tools such as the surprise question and SPICT identify people likely to be in the last year of life so that care can be planned.
- Advance care planning records values and preferences; an ADRT refusing life-sustaining treatment must be written, signed, witnessed and state that it applies even if life is at risk.
- Structured communication (SPIKES, ask–tell–ask) helps share bad news and discuss uncertainty honestly.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Answer the "how long" question {level=1}
A man with advanced lung cancer asks you, "How long have I got?" Describe how you would respond.
::: solution
First explore the question: ask what has prompted it and what he already understands, and whether he wants a number or something else (for example, whether he will see a family event). If he wants an estimate, give an honest range in time units such as "weeks to months" rather than a single number, explain that you cannot be precise and that some people live longer and some shorter, and explain what signs might suggest that time is shorter. Respond to his emotion, allow silence, and ask what is most important to him in the time ahead. Offer to talk again and to include his family if he wishes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Identify palliative care needs {level=1}
List four indicators that a person with COPD may be in the last year of life, and two actions that should follow their recognition.
::: solution
Indicators (any of): severe airflow obstruction with breathlessness at rest or on minimal exertion; frequent exacerbations or admissions; need for long-term oxygen therapy; weight loss or low body mass index; declining performance status and increasing dependence; right heart failure; "no" to the surprise question. Actions: add the person to the primary care palliative care register and coordinate care; offer advance care planning, including preferences about hospital admission, ventilation and resuscitation; review symptom control (breathlessness, anxiety); share information with out-of-hours services.
:::
:::

::: exercise What makes an ADRT valid? {level=1}
A woman with motor neurone disease wants to refuse invasive ventilation in the future, even if she would die without it. What must her advance decision include to be valid and applicable in England and Wales?
::: solution
She must be 18 or over and have capacity when she makes it. The decision must specify the treatment refused (invasive ventilation), and may specify circumstances. Because it refuses life-sustaining treatment, it must be in writing, signed by her (or by another person in her presence and at her direction), signed by a witness, and include a statement that the decision applies even if her life is at risk. It is not valid if she later withdraws it, creates a lasting power of attorney that covers the same decision, or acts in a way clearly inconsistent with it, and not applicable if circumstances arise that she did not anticipate. She should share copies with her GP, neurologist and family.
:::
:::

::: exercise Predictive value of the surprise question {level=2 check="round(100*0.2*0.67/(0.2*0.67+0.8*(1-0.802)))"}
In a general medical clinic, 20% of the patients will die within 12 months. The surprise question has a sensitivity of 67% and a specificity of 80.2%. Among patients for whom the clinician answers "No, I would not be surprised", what percentage will die within 12 months? Give the answer as a whole-number percentage.
::: hint
Work with 1,000 patients: count those who die and are flagged, and those who survive but are flagged.
:::
::: solution
Of 1,000 patients, 200 die and 800 survive. Flagged and die: $200 \times 0.67 = 134$. Flagged and survive: $800 \times (1 - 0.802) = 800 \times 0.198 = 158.4$. Positive predictive value $= 134 / (134 + 158.4) = 134 / 292.4 = 0.458$, about **46%**. Fewer than half of flagged patients die within the year, and a third of those who die are not flagged. The question is useful to trigger planning, which is helpful for anyone with serious illness, but it must not be used to tell a person how long they will live.
:::
:::

::: exercise Respond to a family request to withhold information {level=2}
A son asks you not to tell his mother, who has capacity, that her cancer has spread: "It will kill her. In our culture we don't talk about it." How do you respond?
::: solution
Listen and acknowledge his love and concern; ask what he fears will happen if she is told and what he knows of her wishes. Explain that you will not force information on her, but that you cannot lie to her and that she has a right to know as much as she wants. Agree to ask her, sensitively, how much she wants to know and whom she wants involved in discussions about her illness: some patients prefer that information is shared with family, and this is a legitimate choice if it is hers. If she asks directly, answer honestly at the pace she sets. Cultural background informs how information is shared but does not override her own wishes. Document the conversations.
:::
:::

::: exercise Plan care in motor neurone disease {level=2}
Mr Neil Fraser, 58, has motor neurone disease with weakening bulbar and respiratory muscles. He can still communicate with some difficulty. List the decisions that should be discussed early, and why timing is important.
::: solution
Decisions: gastrostomy feeding (best placed before respiratory function declines too far, for safety of the procedure); non-invasive ventilation (which can improve symptoms and survival) and whether he would want it withdrawn in future, and under what circumstances; invasive ventilation via tracheostomy; preferences for hospital admission and resuscitation; preferred place of care and death; communication aids and voice banking (before speech is lost); a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare; and an advance decision if he wishes to refuse specific treatments. Timing matters because speech, swallowing and respiratory function may decline quickly, and the conversations need his own voice while he can give it. A specialist MND multidisciplinary team, including palliative care, coordinates this.
:::
:::

::: exercise Design the referral that works {level=3}
A hospital's specialist palliative care team receives most referrals in the last 72 hours of a patient's life, and few for people with organ failure. Propose changes that would make referrals earlier and fairer, and explain how you would measure the effect.
::: solution
Changes: (1) agreed referral triggers based on needs rather than diagnosis (uncontrolled symptoms, complex family or decision-making needs, repeated admissions, surprise question "no"); (2) joint clinics or ward rounds with cardiology, respiratory, renal and geriatric medicine teams; (3) education for generalist teams in recognising palliative needs and having conversations, so that generalists do more and refer the complex cases; (4) electronic flags for patients on the GP palliative care register, with shared ReSPECT forms; (5) outreach to groups under-represented in referrals (by deprivation and ethnicity), working with community and faith groups. Measures: time from referral to death (median and distribution); proportion of referrals with non-cancer diagnoses; proportion of deaths in hospital preceded by a documented advance care plan; symptom scores and patient and family experience; and referrals compared with the local population profile.
:::
:::

::: exercise Handle a disagreement about resuscitation {level=3}
Mr Leonard Gray, 88, with advanced dementia and recurrent aspiration pneumonia, is admitted again. The medical team believes cardiopulmonary resuscitation would not work and would cause harm. His wife insists that "everything must be done". He lacks capacity. How should the decision be made and communicated?
::: solution
A DNACPR decision is a clinical judgement when CPR would not be successful, but there is a duty to involve the patient (here, through those close to him, as he lacks capacity) unless this would cause harm, as established in the Tracey (2014) and Winspear (2015) cases. Steps: meet his wife in a private setting with a senior clinician; explore her understanding and her husband's values and any previously expressed wishes; explain honestly what CPR involves and why it is unlikely to restart his heart or to return him to his previous condition; make clear what will be done (treatment of the pneumonia if appropriate, symptom control, comfort, nutrition and mouth care) so that "not for CPR" is not heard as "not for care". If she disagrees, the team is not obliged to provide CPR that it judges will not work, but should offer a second opinion and document the discussion, the reasons and her views. If there is a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare, the attorney must be consulted; if the question is genuinely one of best interests (CPR might work but may not be wanted), the decision follows the best-interests process of the Mental Capacity Act.
:::
:::
