Medicines are among the most powerful tools in medicine, and older people take the most of them. In England, many people over 75 take five or more regular medicines, and a substantial minority take ten or more. Each drug may have been started for a good reason, by a different prescriber, following a guideline written for a single disease. Together they can cause falls, confusion, kidney injury, bleeding and hospital admission. A classic UK study of admissions to two Merseyside hospitals (Pirmohamed and colleagues, 2004) found that about 6.5% of admissions were related to an adverse drug reaction, and older people taking several drugs were the most affected.

This chapter explains why older people are more vulnerable to adverse effects, how harmful prescribing patterns arise, the tools used to review medicines, and how to stop medicines safely. The pharmacological background is in [[pharmacology/patient-variation]] and [[pharmacology/adverse-reactions]].

## Polypharmacy and why it matters {#sec-problem}

::: definition Polypharmacy {#def-problem}
**Polypharmacy** is the concurrent use of multiple medicines by one person; it is commonly defined as five or more regular medicines. Polypharmacy can be **appropriate**, when every medicine is indicated, effective and acceptable to the person and the combination has been optimised, or **problematic**, when medicines are prescribed inappropriately or their intended benefit is not realised, or when the overall burden of treatment outweighs the benefit to the person.
:::

The number itself is not the problem. A person with heart failure, diabetes and chronic kidney disease may rightly take eight medicines. The problem is unreviewed accumulation: drugs that are no longer needed, drugs whose risks have grown as the person has aged, and drugs that treat the side effects of other drugs.

### Why older people are more vulnerable

::: theorem Start low, go slow {#thm-startlow}
Because older people have smaller physiological reserve, slower drug elimination and greater sensitivity to many drug effects, a given dose produces a larger and longer effect than in younger adults. Drugs should therefore be started at low doses and increased slowly, with the effect reviewed at each step.
:::

::: proof
**Pharmacokinetic changes.** Renal clearance falls, so renally excreted drugs and active metabolites accumulate (for example digoxin, lithium, gabapentin, the direct oral anticoagulants and the metabolites of morphine). Body fat rises and body water falls, so fat-soluble drugs (such as diazepam) have a larger volume of distribution and a longer half-life, while water-soluble drugs reach higher concentrations. Hepatic blood flow and first-pass metabolism may fall. These changes are described in [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty#eq-halflife]].

**Pharmacodynamic changes.** The ageing brain is more sensitive to sedatives, opioids and anticholinergic drugs, so confusion and falls appear at lower doses. A slower baroreflex makes postural hypotension more likely with antihypertensives. Reduced thirst and renal concentrating ability make diuretics more likely to cause dehydration and hyponatraemia. Bleeding risk with anticoagulants and antiplatelets rises with age.

**Interactions.** The number of possible pairs of drugs rises faster than the number of drugs: with $n$ drugs there are $n(n-1)/2$ pairs. Five drugs give 10 possible pairs; ten drugs give 45. Not every pair interacts, but the opportunities for interaction multiply.
:::

### Prescribing cascades

::: definition Prescribing cascade {#def-cascade}
A **prescribing cascade** occurs when an adverse effect of a drug is misinterpreted as a new medical condition, and a second drug is prescribed to treat it. The second drug may cause further adverse effects, and the cascade can continue.
:::

Some common cascades:

| First drug | Adverse effect | Second drug |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium-channel blocker (amlodipine) | Ankle oedema | Loop diuretic |
| Cholinesterase inhibitor (donepezil) | Urinary incontinence | Bladder antimuscarinic (oxybutynin) |
| NSAID | Raised blood pressure | Antihypertensive |
| Metoclopramide or an antipsychotic | Parkinsonism | Levodopa |
| Thiazide diuretic | Gout | Allopurinol or colchicine |
| ACE inhibitor | Cough | Cough suppressant or antibiotic |

The cholinesterase inhibitor cascade is especially harmful: the anticholinergic bladder drug works against the dementia drug and can worsen cognition.

::: example The ankle, the water tablet and the fall {#ex-cascade}
Mrs Hannah Levi, 81, was started on amlodipine for hypertension. Two months later she developed ankle swelling, and furosemide was added. She now has urinary urgency and incontinence, has fallen twice rushing to the toilet, and her sodium is 131 mmol/L. Her blood pressure is 118/66 mmHg sitting and 100/60 mmHg standing. Explain what has happened and propose a plan.
::: solution
This is a prescribing cascade ([[#def-cascade]]). Amlodipine causes ankle oedema by dilating precapillary arterioles, which raises capillary pressure. This oedema is not caused by fluid overload, so a diuretic does little for it, but furosemide has caused urgency, falls while rushing to the toilet, mild hyponatraemia and postural hypotension. Her blood pressure is now low for her age and frailty.

Plan: stop furosemide (there is no other indication such as heart failure; check this). Reassess her blood pressure; if treatment is still needed, a lower dose of amlodipine or a different class (for example an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker, with renal function monitoring) may be used. Recheck sodium and lying and standing blood pressure after the change, review the falls, and record "ankle oedema with amlodipine" as an adverse reaction so the cascade is not repeated.
:::
:::

## Tools for medication review {#sec-tools}

::: definition Medication review and its tools {#def-tools}
A **structured medication review** is a planned, person-centred review of every medicine a person takes, with the aim of agreeing what each one is for, whether it still works and is still wanted, and whether it is causing harm. Explicit criteria support the review: **STOPP/START** (version 3, 2023) lists 133 potentially inappropriate prescribing situations (STOPP) and 57 potential prescribing omissions (START) in people aged 65 and over; the **American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria** (most recently updated in 2023) list medicines to avoid or use with caution in older adults; and **anticholinergic burden scales** score drugs by their anticholinergic activity.
:::

Explicit criteria are useful prompts, but a medicine flagged by STOPP may still be right for a particular person, and a START omission may be wrong for someone with a short life expectancy. The review must start from the person's goals.

### Anticholinergic burden

Many drugs have anticholinergic activity, including some that are not thought of as anticholinergic. Their effects add up: dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, tachycardia, confusion, delirium and falls. On the **Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale**, each drug scores 1 (possible anticholinergic effect) to 3 (definite and clinically relevant), and the scores are added. Higher totals have been associated with cognitive decline, falls and mortality in older people; a total of 3 or more is generally regarded as clinically relevant. Strongly anticholinergic drugs include tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline), bladder antimuscarinics (oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin), sedating antihistamines (chlorphenamine, promethazine), paroxetine, and some antipsychotics. Different published scales assign slightly different scores, so the scale used should be stated.

::: example Counting the anticholinergic load {#ex-acb}
Mr Victor Okoro, 79, with mild cognitive impairment, takes amitriptyline at night for neuropathic pain, oxybutynin for urinary urgency, chlorphenamine for itch, and furosemide for heart failure. Using these ACB scores: amitriptyline 3, oxybutynin 3, chlorphenamine 3, furosemide 1, calculate his total and suggest changes.
::: solution
Total ACB $= 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 10$, a very high load in a man with cognitive impairment. Changes, made one at a time with his agreement and with monitoring: stop chlorphenamine and treat the itch (emollients, look for the cause); for urgency, try bladder training and consider a drug with little anticholinergic effect (for example mirabegron, if his blood pressure allows) or none; for neuropathic pain, consider an alternative with less anticholinergic effect (for example duloxetine or a gabapentinoid, which have their own adverse effects and need renal dose adjustment). Furosemide is needed for heart failure and contributes little. Reassess his cognition after the load has been reduced.
:::
:::

### Time to benefit

Every preventive drug has a **time to benefit**: the delay between starting it and the point at which a person is likely to have gained from it. For example, statins for primary prevention and drugs for osteoporosis take months to years to prevent events. When life expectancy is shorter than the time to benefit, the person bears the burden and the risks of the drug with little chance of gaining from it. This is one of the strongest reasons to review preventive medicines in frail people or near the end of life.

## Deprescribing {#sec-deprescribing}

::: definition Deprescribing {#def-deprescribing}
**Deprescribing** is the planned and supervised process of dose reduction or withdrawal of a medicine that may be causing harm or is no longer providing benefit, with the goal of managing polypharmacy and improving outcomes. It is part of good prescribing, not a separate activity, and decisions are shared with the person.
:::

A typical deprescribing process has five steps:

1. **List all medicines**, including over-the-counter and herbal products, and find out how the person actually takes them (medicines reconciliation).
2. **Assess the overall risk** of drug harm: the number of drugs, high-risk drugs, frailty, cognition and kidney function.
3. **Assess each drug** for its indication, benefit and harm, and its place in the person's goals.
4. **Prioritise** drugs to stop: those with the most harm and least benefit, those easiest to stop, and those the person wants to stop.
5. **Implement and monitor**: stop one drug at a time where possible; taper drugs that cause withdrawal effects (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, antidepressants, opioids, gabapentinoids, corticosteroids, beta-blockers, proton pump inhibitors); agree what to watch for and when to review.

The Scottish Government's polypharmacy guidance (*Realistic Prescribing*, 3rd edition, 2018) organises a similar process into seven steps, starting with "what matters to the patient". NICE guidance on medicines optimisation (NG5, 2015) and multimorbidity (NG56, 2016) supports structured medication reviews for people with multimorbidity and polypharmacy, and in England structured medication reviews are offered in primary care to people at higher risk, often by clinical pharmacists.

::: warning Stopping can also cause harm
Deprescribing is not "stop everything". Abrupt withdrawal of some drugs is dangerous: benzodiazepines (rebound anxiety, insomnia, seizures), beta-blockers (rebound angina or tachycardia), corticosteroids (adrenal insufficiency), antiepileptics, antiparkinsonian drugs, and antidepressants (discontinuation symptoms). And some medicines are under-used in older people: anticoagulants in atrial fibrillation, bone protection after a fragility fracture, and adequate pain relief. A good review both stops and starts.
:::

::: example The fall and the refused anticoagulant {#ex-anticoag}
Mr Albert Novak, 84, has atrial fibrillation, hypertension and diabetes, and had a minor stroke two years ago. He has fallen twice in the past year without injury. His GP stopped his apixaban "because of the falls risk". Was this the right decision?
::: solution
Probably not. His previous stroke, age, hypertension and diabetes place him at high annual risk of a further stroke, which anticoagulation reduces substantially. Falls do increase the risk of bleeding, especially subdural haematoma, but modelling studies have suggested that a person on anticoagulation would need to fall very many times a year before the bleeding risk from falls outweighed the stroke prevention. Falls alone are therefore not usually a reason to stop anticoagulation.

A better approach: (1) restart anticoagulation after a shared discussion with him, checking the correct dose for his age, weight and renal function (Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance); (2) reduce bleeding risk by stopping unnecessary antiplatelets and NSAIDs and controlling blood pressure; (3) treat the falls with a multifactorial assessment ([[geriatrics-palliative/falls]]); (4) give advice on head injury and when to seek urgent help. If he still prefers not to take an anticoagulant after understanding the balance, his informed choice is respected and recorded.
:::
:::

::: example The night-time hypoglycaemia {#ex-insulin}
Mrs Grace Thompson, 86, lives in a care home, has moderate dementia and is CFS 7. She takes gliclazide twice daily and once-daily basal insulin. Her HbA1c is 48 mmol/mol (6.5%). Night staff have found her sweaty and confused twice, with capillary glucose of 2.8 and 3.1 mmol/L. How should her diabetes treatment change?
::: solution
In frail older people, the main aims of diabetes treatment are to prevent hypoglycaemia and symptomatic hyperglycaemia, not to achieve tight long-term control, which brings little benefit over a short life expectancy and causes hypoglycaemia, falls, confusion and cardiac events. An HbA1c of 48 mmol/mol with hypoglycaemia means she is over-treated. Guidance such as that of the American Diabetes Association recommends relaxed targets for people with complex health and avoiding reliance on HbA1c for those with very poor health; UK guidance similarly advises individualised, relaxed targets in frailty.

Plan: stop gliclazide first (a sulfonylurea with a high risk of prolonged hypoglycaemia in older people with reduced renal function). Review whether insulin is still needed: reduce the dose, and consider stopping it with monitoring if glucose stays acceptable. Agree a glucose range with the care home that avoids symptoms (for example no readings below about 6 mmol/L and none persistently high enough to cause thirst, polyuria or infections), a monitoring plan, and what to do about readings outside it. Review her renal function and other medicines.
:::
:::

::: example The "non-adherent" inhaler user {#ex-adherence}
Mr Samuel Wright, 80, with COPD, has been prescribed three different inhalers over two years because his symptoms "aren't controlled", and is labelled non-adherent because he orders them irregularly. He has arthritis of the hands and mild cognitive impairment. What should be assessed?
::: solution
**Non-adherence** is often unintentional. Assess his inhaler technique by watching him use each device: arthritic hands may not be able to press a metered-dose inhaler or coordinate breathing with it, and cognitive impairment can make several different devices confusing. Ask him what he believes each inhaler is for and whether he has noticed side effects or cost problems.

Solutions include a single device type, a spacer or a breath-actuated device, a once-daily regimen, written instructions, and help from a pharmacist or family member. Escalating treatment on the basis of "poor control" without checking technique is a common cause of polypharmacy. Multi-compartment compliance aids are not a solution for inhalers and are not recommended routinely for tablets either: they suit some people but can introduce errors and reduce flexibility, so their use should follow an individual assessment.
:::
:::

## The system around the prescription {#sec-system}

::: definition Medicines optimisation in the care system {#def-system}
**Medicines optimisation** is a person-centred approach to safe and effective medicines use, so that people obtain the best possible outcomes from their medicines. Key processes include **medicines reconciliation** (an accurate list of current medicines at every transfer of care, which NICE NG5 recommends completing within 24 hours of hospital admission), regular structured medication reviews, clear communication of changes and their reasons at discharge, monitoring of high-risk drugs, and reporting of suspected adverse reactions (in the UK, through the MHRA Yellow Card scheme).
:::

Transitions of care are the most dangerous moments. Drugs stopped in hospital may be restarted by mistake from an old repeat prescription; new drugs started for a temporary reason (for example a proton pump inhibitor or a sleeping tablet) may continue for years. A good discharge letter states what has changed and why, and what should be reviewed and when.

::: quiz
An 85-year-old woman with dementia takes donepezil. She develops new urinary incontinence. Which response is most appropriate?
- [ ] Add oxybutynin to treat the incontinence
- [x] Consider donepezil as a possible cause and assess her incontinence before adding any drug
- [ ] Stop donepezil immediately and permanently
- [ ] Insert a long-term urinary catheter
::: solution
Cholinesterase inhibitors can cause urinary incontinence. Adding an anticholinergic bladder drug would start a prescribing cascade that opposes the dementia treatment and can worsen confusion ([[#def-cascade]]). The first step is to assess the incontinence (infection, retention, constipation, mobility, access to the toilet) and to consider whether donepezil is responsible; any change is then discussed with her and her family.
:::
:::

## Where this leads

The general principles of pharmacokinetics and adverse reactions are in [[pharmacology/patient-variation]] and [[pharmacology/adverse-reactions]]. Medicines that cause falls and delirium are covered in [[geriatrics-palliative/falls]] and [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]]. Near the end of life, deprescribing of preventive drugs becomes part of planning for [[geriatrics-palliative/palliative-care]].

::: history
The term "prescribing cascade" was introduced by Paula Rochon and Jerry Gurwitz in a 1997 *BMJ* article, and in 2005 Sudeep Gill and colleagues showed in a large Canadian cohort that older people with dementia who started cholinesterase inhibitors were more likely to be prescribed bladder antimuscarinic drugs. Explicit criteria for inappropriate prescribing began with Mark Beers and colleagues in 1991, initially for nursing-home residents in the United States; the American Geriatrics Society now maintains them. In Ireland, Denis O'Mahony, Paul Gallagher and colleagues published the STOPP/START criteria in 2008, designed around European prescribing and including omissions as well as excesses. The word "deprescribing" came into common use in the 2000s, and a 2015 article by Ian Scott and colleagues in *JAMA Internal Medicine* set out a widely used stepwise protocol.
:::

::: summary
- Polypharmacy (five or more medicines) can be appropriate or problematic; the aim is the right medicines, not the fewest.
- Older people are more vulnerable because of slower clearance, altered distribution and greater sensitivity, especially of the brain and the circulation: start low, go slow.
- Prescribing cascades arise when an adverse effect is treated as a new disease; recognise and reverse them.
- STOPP/START, the Beers Criteria and anticholinergic burden scales are prompts for review, not rules; the person's goals come first.
- Preventive drugs have a time to benefit; when life expectancy is shorter, their value falls.
- Deprescribe one drug at a time, taper drugs with withdrawal effects, monitor and review. Some drugs are under-used and need starting.
- Medicines reconciliation and clear communication at transfers of care prevent many errors.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Identify the cascade {level=1}
For each sequence, name the original adverse effect and suggest the better action: (a) a man starts on ramipril, develops a dry cough, and is given two courses of antibiotics and a cough linctus; (b) a woman with nausea is given metoclopramide for several months, develops a tremor and slowness, and is referred for possible Parkinson's disease.
::: solution
(a) ACE-inhibitor cough. Stop the antibiotics and linctus; switch ramipril to an angiotensin receptor blocker if the indication remains. (b) Drug-induced parkinsonism from metoclopramide, a dopamine antagonist. Stop metoclopramide, look for the cause of the nausea, and reassess the tremor after a few weeks to months; drug-induced parkinsonism may take time to resolve. Starting levodopa would extend the cascade.
:::
:::

::: exercise Count the possible drug pairs {level=1 check="12*11/2"}
A woman takes 12 regular medicines. How many different pairs of medicines are there?
::: solution
$n(n-1)/2 = 12 \times 11 / 2 = 66$ pairs. Each pair is a possible interaction (most will not interact, but software checks and pharmacist reviews must consider them all).
:::
:::

::: exercise Anticholinergic burden {level=1 check="3+1+1"}
Using these ACB scores: oxybutynin 3, warfarin 1, digoxin 1, calculate the total ACB score for a man taking oxybutynin, warfarin, digoxin and paracetamol (score 0). Is the load clinically relevant?
::: solution
Total $= 3 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 5$. A total of 3 or more is generally regarded as clinically relevant. The largest contribution is oxybutynin, which is also the easiest to replace or stop.
:::
:::

::: exercise Apply STOPP and START {level=2}
Mrs Rita Sandhu, 78, has osteoporosis with a previous vertebral fracture, hypertension and gastro-oesophageal reflux. She takes amlodipine, omeprazole 40 mg daily for 6 years without review, zopiclone nightly for 3 years, and ibuprofen for back pain. She is not taking any bone protection. Identify likely STOPP and START issues.
::: solution
STOPP-type issues: long-term high-dose proton pump inhibitor without review (consider reducing to the lowest effective dose or stopping, as long-term use is associated with fractures, *C. difficile* and hypomagnesaemia); long-term hypnotic (zopiclone: falls, fractures, confusion; taper gradually with sleep advice); NSAID in an older person with hypertension (raised blood pressure, renal and gastrointestinal harm; use alternatives such as paracetamol, topical NSAIDs or physiotherapy). START-type omission: bone-protection treatment after a fragility fracture (for example a bisphosphonate, after checking renal function, calcium and vitamin D, and dental health). Changes are discussed with her and introduced one at a time.
:::
:::

::: exercise Taper a benzodiazepine {level=2}
A 79-year-old man has taken temazepam nightly for 10 years. He has had two falls and is willing to try stopping. Outline the principles of a safe taper.
::: solution
Principles: agree the plan and his goals; explain possible withdrawal symptoms (rebound insomnia, anxiety) and that they usually settle; reduce the dose gradually, for example by about 10–25% every two to four weeks, slowing if symptoms are troublesome (the actual schedule follows local guidance and the BNF, and long-term users may need several months); offer support and cognitive behavioural approaches for insomnia (sleep hygiene, stimulus control, a regular wake time); avoid substituting another hypnotic; and review regularly. Abrupt withdrawal after long-term use risks severe withdrawal symptoms, including seizures, and should be avoided.
:::
:::

::: exercise Convert an HbA1c target {level=2 check="round(10*(64/10.929+2.15))/10"}
HbA1c in mmol/mol (IFCC units) can be converted to the older percentage units (DCCT/NGSP) with the formula $\% = \text{mmol/mol} / 10.929 + 2.15$. A relaxed target of 64 mmol/mol is agreed for a frail older woman. What is this in percentage units, to one decimal place?
::: solution
$64 / 10.929 + 2.15 = 5.856 + 2.15 = 8.006$, so **8.0%**. For people with complex health problems, the ADA suggests targets around this level (below 8.0%, or 64 mmol/mol), and for people in very poor health it advises avoiding reliance on HbA1c at all and focusing on preventing hypoglycaemia and symptomatic hyperglycaemia.
:::
:::

::: exercise Time to benefit {level=3 check="round(1/(0.06-0.045))"}
In an illustrative primary prevention trial, a drug reduces the 5-year risk of a cardiovascular event from 6.0% to 4.5%, with little benefit seen in the first two years. Calculate the number needed to treat for 5 years to prevent one event, to the nearest whole number. Then discuss whether to continue the drug in an 89-year-old woman with CFS 7 whose estimated life expectancy is about 1 to 2 years.
::: solution
Absolute risk reduction $= 0.060 - 0.045 = 0.015$. Number needed to treat $= 1/0.015 \approx 66.7$, so about **67** people treated for 5 years to prevent one event. For the woman: her expected survival is shorter than the period over which most of the benefit accrues, so her chance of benefit is very small, while she bears the daily burden (tablets, monitoring) and the risk of adverse effects. Stopping the drug is reasonable, after discussion with her and her family, explaining that the change reflects the drug's limited value now, not a withdrawal of care. The numbers here are illustrative; for real drugs, time-to-benefit estimates come from trial data.
:::
:::

::: exercise "They're taking her off her tablets" {level=3}
After a medication review, Mrs Patricia Owen, 91, had her statin, aspirin (taken for primary prevention) and bisphosphonate (taken for 8 years) stopped. Her son is angry: "You're giving up on her." How would you respond, and what makes this deprescribing safe?
::: solution
Respond by listening, acknowledging his concern, and explaining the reasons in plain language: each medicine was reviewed for what it can still achieve for her. Aspirin for primary prevention gives little benefit and a real bleeding risk at her age. The statin's preventive benefit takes years to accrue and she was having muscle aches. After 5 to 10 years of bisphosphonate treatment, a review is standard, and a "drug holiday" is reasonable for many people because the drug persists in bone; her fracture risk will be reassessed. Emphasise what continues: treatment of her symptoms and conditions, and the plan to monitor her. What makes it safe: the decision was shared with her (or made in her best interests if she lacks capacity, with family involved), one change at a time, a documented reason for each stop, a plan for monitoring and review, and clear communication to her GP and care team. Offering him a follow-up conversation shows that the review is part of active care.
:::
:::
