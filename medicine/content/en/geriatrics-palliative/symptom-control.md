People with advanced illness commonly have several symptoms at once: pain, breathlessness, nausea, constipation, fatigue, poor appetite, low mood, anxiety and itch. Good symptom control does not depend on rare skills. It depends on careful assessment, an understanding of the mechanism behind each symptom, a clear plan explained to the patient, and frequent review. This chapter covers the principles of symptom control and the management of the commonest symptoms, including the arithmetic of opioid prescribing.

All drug information in this chapter describes principles and typical approaches in UK practice. Doses for an individual patient come from the current BNF, the *Palliative Care Formulary* and local palliative care guidelines, and specialist advice should be sought when symptoms are difficult to control.

## Principles {#sec-principles}

::: theorem The principles of symptom control {#thm-principles}
Effective symptom control follows five steps: (1) **assess** each symptom, its severity, its effect on the person and its likely cause and mechanism; (2) **explain** the cause and the plan to the patient and family; (3) **treat what is reversible**, where this is consistent with the person's goals and burden; (4) **manage** the symptom with non-drug and drug measures chosen for its mechanism; and (5) **review** the effect frequently and adjust.
:::

::: proof
Most symptoms in advanced illness have more than one cause, and the treatment depends on the cause. Nausea from constipation needs a laxative, not an antiemetic; breathlessness from a pleural effusion may respond to drainage; confusion from opioid toxicity needs a lower dose, not a sedative. Explanation reduces fear, which amplifies many symptoms (Saunders's "total pain"). Whether to treat a reversible cause depends on burden and benefit: draining an effusion is worthwhile if the person will live long enough to benefit, while a blood transfusion in the last days may not be. Symptoms in advanced illness change quickly, so a plan that worked last week may be wrong today: frequent review is part of the treatment.
:::

## Pain {#sec-pain}

::: definition Pain in advanced illness {#def-pain}
Pain is classified by mechanism. **Nociceptive pain** arises from tissue damage: **somatic** pain (bone, skin, muscle) is usually well localised and aching; **visceral** pain (organs, capsules, hollow viscera) is poorly localised, deep, and may be colicky or referred. **Neuropathic pain** arises from damage to the nervous system and is often burning, shooting or electric, with altered sensation (numbness, allodynia) in a nerve or dermatomal distribution. Many people with cancer have mixed pain. Assessment records the site, character, severity, timing, aggravating and relieving factors, the effect on function and sleep, the person's understanding and fears, and the response to previous treatment.
:::

### The analgesic ladder and its successors

The **WHO analgesic ladder**, published in 1986, introduced a simple stepwise approach to cancer pain: step 1, a non-opioid (paracetamol, NSAID); step 2, a weak opioid (codeine, tramadol) plus a non-opioid; step 3, a strong opioid (morphine and alternatives) plus a non-opioid; with **adjuvants** at any step. Its accompanying principles were "by the mouth, by the clock, by the ladder, for the individual, with attention to detail". The ladder made oral morphine available worldwide. WHO's 2018 cancer pain guideline moved to a more individualised framework, and in practice many patients with moderate to severe pain move straight to a low dose of a strong opioid, bypassing weak opioids, which add little and have unpredictable metabolism (codeine is a prodrug converted to morphine by CYP2D6, which varies between people).

### Strong opioids

NICE guidance on strong opioids in palliative care (CG140) recommends oral morphine as the first-line strong opioid for most patients with advanced disease. Key principles:

- **Starting**: in opioid-naive patients with normal renal function, a typical starting regimen is 20–30 mg of oral morphine a day, given as modified-release (twice daily) or immediate-release (four-hourly) preparations, with immediate-release doses available for breakthrough pain. In frail older people and those with renal impairment, lower doses are used.
- **Breakthrough doses**: a dose of immediate-release opioid for pain that "breaks through" regular analgesia. In UK practice the breakthrough dose is commonly one-tenth to one-sixth of the total daily dose of the same opioid.
- **Titration**: the regular dose is adjusted, usually daily, according to the total breakthrough doses needed and the side effects.
- **Prevent side effects**: prescribe a laxative for everyone on regular opioids; make an antiemetic available; explain that drowsiness and nausea often settle within days, but constipation does not.
- **Watch for toxicity**: drowsiness, confusion, hallucinations, myoclonic jerks, pinpoint pupils and, in severe cases, respiratory depression. Toxicity often follows a fall in renal function.

::: definition Total daily dose and breakthrough dose {#def-tdd}
The **total daily dose** (TDD) of an opioid is the sum of all regular and breakthrough doses taken in 24 hours. The **breakthrough dose** is a fraction of the TDD of the same opioid, commonly one-sixth:

$$
\text{breakthrough dose} \approx \frac{\text{TDD}}{6}
$$ {#eq-btp}

To compare or switch opioids, each is expressed as its **oral morphine equivalent** using conversion ratios. Commonly used approximate ratios in UK practice are: oral morphine to subcutaneous morphine, divide by 2; oral morphine to subcutaneous diamorphine, divide by 3; oral morphine to oral oxycodone, divide by 1.5 to 2 (tables differ); oral oxycodone to subcutaneous oxycodone, divide by about 2. All conversion ratios are approximate, and local guidance should be followed.
:::

**Renal impairment.** Morphine is metabolised to morphine-3-glucuronide and morphine-6-glucuronide, which are excreted by the kidneys and accumulate when renal function falls, causing drowsiness, confusion and myoclonus. In moderate to severe renal impairment, an opioid with less renal dependence is preferred: oxycodone at reduced dose in moderate impairment, and in severe impairment opioids such as alfentanil or fentanyl, with specialist advice.

**Transdermal patches** (fentanyl, buprenorphine) are useful when oral intake is difficult, but they take many hours to reach a steady effect and to wear off after removal, so they are suited to stable pain rather than to rapid titration.

**Opioid switching** (rotation) is used when side effects are intolerable or pain is uncontrolled despite dose increases. Because tolerance to one opioid is not complete for another (incomplete cross-tolerance), the calculated equivalent dose of the new opioid is often reduced by 25–50%, especially when switching because of toxicity.

::: example Opioid toxicity and a switch {#ex-rotation}
Mr Thomas Ward, 72, has prostate cancer with bone metastases. He takes modified-release morphine 60 mg twice daily and has needed four breakthrough doses of 10 mg immediate-release morphine each day. Over two days he has become drowsy, has jerking movements of his limbs, and sees insects on the wall. His eGFR has fallen from 55 to 25 mL/min/1.73 m² because of dehydration. Calculate his oral morphine TDD and outline a safe change, using an oral morphine to oral oxycodone ratio of 2:1 and a 25% dose reduction for the switch.
::: solution
**Assessment.** Drowsiness, myoclonus and hallucinations with a fall in renal function indicate **opioid toxicity** from accumulating morphine metabolites. Reversible factors: dehydration (offer fluids, consider subcutaneous or intravenous fluids if appropriate) and any other cause of kidney injury, such as an NSAID or urinary obstruction (a risk in prostate cancer).

**TDD.** Regular: $60 \times 2 = 120$ mg. Breakthrough: $4 \times 10 = 40$ mg. TDD $= 160$ mg oral morphine per day.

**Switch to oxycodone.** Equivalent: $160 / 2 = 80$ mg oral oxycodone per day. Reduce by 25% because of toxicity and incomplete cross-tolerance: $80 \times 0.75 = 60$ mg per day, for example modified-release oxycodone 30 mg twice daily. Breakthrough dose ([[#eq-btp]]): about $60 / 6 = 10$ mg immediate-release oxycodone when needed.

**Monitoring.** In the first 24 hours, the regular dose may be withheld until signs of toxicity improve, while ensuring pain is controlled. Review pain and toxicity at least daily, and recheck renal function. If renal function worsens further, specialist advice is needed on alternatives such as alfentanil. Doses here illustrate the arithmetic; actual prescribing follows local guidance.
:::
:::

### Adjuvant analgesics

Some pains respond poorly to opioids alone:

- **Neuropathic pain**: amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin (NICE CG173). Gabapentinoids need dose reduction in renal impairment and can cause drowsiness and dizziness; tricyclics have anticholinergic effects that limit their use in older people.
- **Bone pain**: NSAIDs (with gastroprotection, and caution in renal impairment and heart failure); **palliative radiotherapy**, often as a single fraction, which relieves pain from a bone metastasis in a majority of patients; bisphosphonates or denosumab to reduce skeletal events.
- **Nerve compression, liver capsule pain and raised intracranial pressure**: corticosteroids (dexamethasone) reduce oedema around tumours.
- **Colic from bowel obstruction**: an antispasmodic such as hyoscine butylbromide.
- **Procedures**: nerve blocks (for example coeliac plexus block for pancreatic pain), and in selected cases spinal analgesia.

::: example Bone pain that needed three treatments {#ex-bone}
Mrs Ngozi Eze, 67, has breast cancer with a metastasis in the right femur. She has constant aching pain in the thigh, worse on walking. Morphine has helped only partly and made her drowsy. What else should be done?
::: solution
**Assess the bone.** Pain on weight-bearing in a femoral metastasis raises the possibility of an impending pathological fracture. An X-ray and an orthopaedic opinion are needed: prophylactic fixation may be indicated, followed by radiotherapy.

**Treatments for bone pain**: palliative radiotherapy to the femur (single-fraction treatment is common and effective); an NSAID if renal function and other risks allow, with a proton pump inhibitor; continue a bone-targeted agent (bisphosphonate or denosumab) as advised by her oncologist; and adjust the opioid to the lowest effective dose to reduce drowsiness.

**Check for complications**: hypercalcaemia of malignancy (thirst, constipation, confusion, nausea, drowsiness; check adjusted calcium), which could explain the drowsiness, and metastatic spinal cord compression if there is back pain or neurological symptoms.

Bone pain often needs a combination of approaches; adding more opioid alone tends to increase side effects without controlling movement-related pain.
:::
:::

::: warning Metastatic spinal cord compression
New back pain in a person with cancer, especially thoracic pain, pain that is worse lying down, or pain with a band-like distribution, may be the first sign of **metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC)**. Do not wait for weakness, sensory loss or sphincter disturbance, by which time function is often lost permanently. NICE guidance (NG234, 2023) advises urgent assessment and whole-spine MRI (within 24 hours if there are neurological symptoms or signs; sooner in some circumstances), with corticosteroids for those with neurological deficits and definitive treatment with radiotherapy or surgery.
:::

## Breathlessness, cough and secretions {#sec-breathlessness}

::: definition Breathlessness in advanced illness {#def-dyspnoea}
**Breathlessness** is the subjective experience of uncomfortable breathing. It does not correlate closely with oxygen saturation or respiratory rate: a person may be very breathless with normal saturations. In advanced illness it arises from the disease (tumour, effusion, heart failure, COPD, neuromuscular weakness), from complications (infection, anaemia, pulmonary embolism) and from the anxiety and panic it provokes, which in turn worsen it (the breathing–thinking–functioning cycle).
:::

**Treat reversible causes** where appropriate: drain a pleural effusion, treat infection or heart failure, transfuse for symptomatic anaemia, consider a stent for airway obstruction by tumour.

**Non-drug measures** have the best evidence for chronic breathlessness: a **handheld fan** directed at the face (cool air on the face reduces the sensation of breathlessness), breathing techniques, positioning, pacing activities, pulmonary rehabilitation where possible, and a written plan for breathlessness crises. Specialist breathlessness services combine these.

**Drugs**:

- **Opioids** at low doses are widely used for breathlessness in advanced illness, particularly at rest and in the last weeks of life. The evidence is mixed: earlier small trials suggested benefit, but larger recent trials of regular low-dose sustained-release morphine for chronic breathlessness, including the BEAMS trial in COPD (Ekström and colleagues, 2022), did not show a benefit on their primary outcome. Use is therefore individual and reviewed.
- **Benzodiazepines** (for example lorazepam sublingually or midazolam) may help when anxiety and panic are prominent, but are not first-line for breathlessness itself.
- **Oxygen** helps people who are hypoxaemic. In people who are breathless but not hypoxaemic, a randomised trial (Abernethy and colleagues, 2010) found that oxygen was no better than medical air delivered by nasal cannula; a fan is simpler.

**Cough** may respond to treatment of the cause, simple linctus, or an opioid such as low-dose morphine or codeine. **Noisy respiratory secretions** at the end of life are covered in [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]].

## Nausea, constipation and other symptoms {#sec-other}

::: definition Mechanism-based antiemetic choice {#def-other}
Nausea and vomiting in advanced illness are managed by identifying the likely **mechanism** and choosing an antiemetic that acts on the corresponding receptors:

| Mechanism | Typical causes | First-line antiemetic (examples) |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical (chemoreceptor trigger zone) | Opioids, hypercalcaemia, renal failure, other drugs | Haloperidol (dopamine D2 antagonist) |
| Gastric stasis | Opioids, hepatomegaly, ascites, autonomic failure | Metoclopramide or domperidone (prokinetic) |
| Vestibular or movement-related | Motion, cerebellar or vestibular disease | Cyclizine (antihistamine) |
| Raised intracranial pressure | Brain tumour or metastases | Dexamethasone plus cyclizine |
| Bowel obstruction | Abdominal or pelvic cancer | See below; avoid prokinetics in complete obstruction with colic |
| Anxiety | Anticipatory nausea | Address fear; benzodiazepine if needed |

When the cause is unclear or several mechanisms are involved, a broad-spectrum antiemetic such as levomepromazine is often used. Reversible causes (constipation, hypercalcaemia, drugs, infection, raised intracranial pressure) are treated at the same time.
:::

Metoclopramide and haloperidol are dopamine antagonists and should be avoided in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies; domperidone, which crosses the blood–brain barrier poorly, is sometimes used instead, with attention to its cardiac risks.

**Constipation** is almost universal with opioids, because tolerance to this effect does not develop. A regular laxative is prescribed from the start: usually a stimulant (senna, bisacodyl) with or without a softener or osmotic laxative (docusate, macrogol). If constipation persists despite laxatives, a **peripherally acting mu-opioid receptor antagonist** (PAMORA) such as naloxegol (oral) or methylnaltrexone (subcutaneous) may be used, provided bowel obstruction has been excluded. Rectal examination and, if needed, suppositories or enemas treat faecal loading.

::: example Malignant bowel obstruction {#ex-mbo}
Mrs Julia Bennett, 69, has advanced ovarian cancer with peritoneal disease. She has colicky abdominal pain, distension, and vomits large volumes once or twice a day. She has not opened her bowels for five days. A CT scan shows multiple levels of small-bowel obstruction. Surgery is judged not to be possible. Outline her management.
::: solution
Malignant bowel obstruction with multiple levels of obstruction is rarely suitable for surgery. Management aims to relieve pain, nausea and vomiting while allowing her to drink and eat small amounts if she wishes.

- **Route**: oral absorption is unreliable, so drugs are given by **continuous subcutaneous infusion** via a syringe pump, with subcutaneous doses as needed.
- **Pain**: a subcutaneous opioid for continuous pain; **hyoscine butylbromide** (an antimuscarinic that does not cross the blood–brain barrier) for colic. It also reduces gastrointestinal secretions.
- **Nausea and vomiting**: haloperidol or levomepromazine. Avoid prokinetics such as metoclopramide when there is colic and complete obstruction, because they can worsen colic.
- **Secretions**: hyoscine butylbromide, and if vomiting remains high-volume, **octreotide**, which reduces secretions further.
- **Corticosteroids**: a trial of dexamethasone may reduce oedema around the obstruction and help resolution in some patients; stop if no benefit within about a week.
- **Venting**: a nasogastric tube may be used briefly; a venting gastrostomy is an option for persistent high-volume vomiting.
- **Fluids and nutrition**: mouth care is essential; small sips and food for pleasure if she wishes. Parenteral fluids or nutrition are discussed individually in the light of her prognosis and wishes.

Explain that vomiting once or twice a day may be an acceptable outcome if nausea is controlled, and agree goals with her.
:::
:::

### Other symptoms

- **Anorexia and cachexia**: weight loss from cancer cachexia is driven by inflammation and metabolic change and does not reverse with feeding alone. Management focuses on the person's and family's distress: small, frequent meals, food for pleasure, treating reversible causes (mouth problems, nausea, constipation, depression). Corticosteroids may stimulate appetite briefly.
- **Fatigue**: very common. Treat reversible contributors (anaemia, hypercalcaemia, depression, insomnia, drugs), pace activities, and consider a short trial of a corticosteroid near the end of life.
- **Itch**: depends on cause. Emollients for everyone. In **cholestatic itch**, relieve biliary obstruction if possible (a stent); drugs used include sertraline, rifampicin and naltrexone, with specialist advice. In **uraemic itch**, gabapentin at low renally adjusted doses is often used; difelikefalin is licensed for people on haemodialysis. Antihistamines help little in either.
- **Anxiety and depression**: common and treatable, with psychological support and, where appropriate, antidepressants (with attention to time to effect) or short-acting anxiolytics.
- **Mouth care**: a dry or sore mouth (from drugs, oxygen or candidiasis) causes considerable distress and is easily treated.
- **Hypercalcaemia of malignancy** (an emergency): rehydration with intravenous fluids and an intravenous bisphosphonate, after checking that treatment matches the person's goals.

::: example The itch that antihistamines did not help {#ex-pruritus}
Mr Ibrahim Saleh, 84, has chronic kidney disease stage 5 and has chosen conservative kidney management. He has generalised itch that keeps him awake. His skin is dry with scratch marks. He has been taking chlorphenamine, which makes him drowsy but does not help. What would you do?
::: solution
This is likely to be **uraemic (CKD-associated) pruritus**, which is not mediated mainly by histamine, so antihistamines help little. Chlorphenamine is also strongly anticholinergic and sedating, which increases the risk of falls and confusion in an older man: stop it.

Management: regular **emollients** applied liberally, avoiding hot baths and soap; keep nails short; check and correct contributing factors (dry skin, raised phosphate, iron deficiency, other drugs, skin disease such as scabies). If itch persists, a low dose of **gabapentin**, adjusted for his very low renal function, is commonly used, with monitoring for drowsiness and dizziness. Difelikefalin is licensed for people on haemodialysis and is therefore not an option for him. Review sleep and mood, which itch affects. The goal is comfort, consistent with the conservative pathway he has chosen.
:::
:::

## Where this leads

The pharmacology of opioids is covered in [[pharmacology/drug-receptor]] and [[pharmacology/dosing-principles]]. Care in the last days of life, including syringe pumps and anticipatory prescribing, is in [[geriatrics-palliative/end-of-life-care]]. Delirium is discussed in [[geriatrics-palliative/delirium-dementia-care]], and prescribing in renal impairment in [[geriatrics-palliative/polypharmacy]].

::: history
Morphine was isolated from opium by the German pharmacist Friedrich Sertürner in the early nineteenth century, but its use for the pain of dying people was long limited by fears of addiction and of shortening life. In the 1950s and 1960s Cicely Saunders and colleagues showed that regular oral morphine, given before the pain returned, controlled cancer pain without escalating doses or addiction in most patients; in the 1970s Robert Twycross studied oral morphine and diamorphine at St Christopher's Hospice and showed that, given orally, they were equally effective. In 1986 the World Health Organization published *Cancer Pain Relief*, which introduced the three-step analgesic ladder and helped to make oral morphine an essential medicine worldwide. Access remains very unequal: many low- and middle-income countries still have little access to oral morphine.
:::

::: summary
- Assess each symptom and its mechanism, explain, treat reversible causes where appropriate, manage with mechanism-based measures, and review frequently.
- Classify pain as nociceptive (somatic, visceral) or neuropathic; most cancer pain is mixed.
- Oral morphine is the usual first strong opioid. Prescribe regular doses with breakthrough doses (often one-sixth of the total daily dose), a laxative, and an antiemetic as needed.
- Morphine metabolites accumulate in renal impairment; watch for drowsiness, myoclonus and hallucinations, and switch to an opioid with less renal dependence.
- Conversion ratios are approximate: oral to subcutaneous morphine, divide by 2; to subcutaneous diamorphine, divide by 3; oral morphine to oxycodone, divide by 1.5–2. Reduce by 25–50% when switching for toxicity.
- Use adjuvants for neuropathic and bone pain; think of MSCC in new back pain and of hypercalcaemia in drowsiness.
- For breathlessness, a fan and non-drug measures come first; oxygen only for hypoxaemia; opioids are used individually, with mixed evidence.
- Choose antiemetics by mechanism; avoid dopamine antagonists in Parkinson's disease; avoid prokinetics in complete obstruction.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Total daily dose and breakthrough dose {level=1 check="90/6"}
A woman takes modified-release morphine 30 mg twice daily and has needed three breakthrough doses of immediate-release morphine 10 mg in the past 24 hours. Calculate her total daily dose and the new breakthrough dose using one-sixth of the TDD (in mg).
::: solution
TDD $= 30 \times 2 + 3 \times 10 = 60 + 30 = 90$ mg of oral morphine. Breakthrough dose $= 90 / 6 = 15$ mg. If her pain is controlled with these doses and she has no side effects, the regular dose may be increased to reflect her TDD, for example to 45 mg twice daily, according to local guidance.
:::
:::

::: exercise Oral to subcutaneous morphine {level=1 check="90/2"}
A man taking 90 mg of oral morphine a day can no longer swallow. Using a ratio of 2:1, what dose of subcutaneous morphine should run over 24 hours in a syringe pump?
::: solution
$90 / 2 = 45$ mg of subcutaneous morphine over 24 hours. A subcutaneous breakthrough dose is about one-sixth of this, $45 / 6 = 7.5$ mg. Local guidance and the available drug strengths determine the exact prescription.
:::
:::

::: exercise Choose the antiemetic {level=1}
Suggest a first-line antiemetic for each: (a) a man with hypercalcaemia and nausea while the calcium is being corrected; (b) a woman with liver metastases and early fullness who vomits undigested food after meals; (c) a man with brain metastases who has early-morning headache and vomiting.
::: solution
(a) Chemical cause: haloperidol, while treating the hypercalcaemia. (b) Gastric stasis (squashed stomach): a prokinetic such as metoclopramide, if there is no complete obstruction. (c) Raised intracranial pressure: dexamethasone to reduce cerebral oedema, with cyclizine.
:::
:::

::: exercise Oral morphine to subcutaneous diamorphine {level=2 check="120/3"}
A woman taking 120 mg of oral morphine a day is moved to a subcutaneous diamorphine infusion. Using an oral morphine to subcutaneous diamorphine ratio of 3:1, what is the 24-hour diamorphine dose (in mg)? What would a subcutaneous breakthrough dose be, using one-sixth?
::: solution
$120 / 3 = 40$ mg of diamorphine over 24 hours. The breakthrough dose is $40 / 6 \approx 6.7$ mg, which in practice is rounded to a convenient measurable dose according to local guidance. Diamorphine is very soluble, which makes it useful when large doses are needed in a small volume.
:::
:::

::: exercise Morphine to oxycodone {level=2 check="90/1.5"}
A man taking 90 mg of oral morphine a day has troublesome itch and nausea from morphine and is switched to oral oxycodone. Using a morphine to oxycodone ratio of 1.5:1 and no dose reduction (pain is controlled and he has no signs of toxicity), what is his total daily oxycodone dose in mg?
::: solution
$90 / 1.5 = 60$ mg of oral oxycodone a day, for example modified-release 30 mg twice daily, with an immediate-release breakthrough dose of about $60/6 = 10$ mg. Some guidance uses a 2:1 ratio, which would give 45 mg a day; the ratio used should follow local policy, and in a switch for toxicity a further reduction would be made.
:::
:::

::: exercise Manage breathlessness {level=2}
Mrs Dorothy Hill, 78, has advanced COPD. She is breathless at rest, frightened, and her oxygen saturation is 93% on air. She asks for oxygen. Outline your approach.
::: solution
Assess for reversible causes (exacerbation, infection, heart failure, anaemia, pneumothorax) and make sure inhaled therapy and technique are optimal. Her saturations do not suggest that oxygen will relieve breathlessness: oxygen helps hypoxaemic patients, and in non-hypoxaemic patients it was no better than air. Explain this, and offer a handheld fan, positioning, breathing techniques and a breathlessness plan for panic. Pulmonary rehabilitation or a breathlessness service may help. If breathlessness remains severe, a trial of a low-dose opioid can be considered with careful review, explaining the mixed evidence; a short-acting benzodiazepine may help panic. Discuss her goals and future care.
:::
:::

::: exercise Terminal agitation or something reversible? {level=3}
Mr Patrick Doyle, 70, with metastatic lung cancer, has been restless for 24 hours, calling out and trying to get out of bed. He is on a syringe pump with morphine 30 mg and midazolam 10 mg over 24 hours. His family asks for "something stronger". List what must be checked, and how decisions about sedation should be made.
::: solution
Check reversible causes before increasing sedation: urinary retention (palpate or scan the bladder), constipation or faecal impaction, pain (including pressure areas and positioning), opioid toxicity (myoclonus, renal function), hypercalcaemia, hypoxia, infection, drug or alcohol or nicotine withdrawal, and fear or spiritual distress. Check whether the syringe pump is running properly and the site is not inflamed. If he is dying and the restlessness is distressing despite treating causes, increasing midazolam or adding an antipsychotic (for example levomepromazine) is appropriate, aiming at the lowest dose that relieves distress. Explain to the family what is happening and what the aim of treatment is. Proportionate sedation to relieve refractory distress at the end of life is lawful and ethical; it is aimed at comfort, not at shortening life, and the decision, its rationale and its review should be documented, with specialist palliative care advice where needed.
:::
:::

::: exercise New back pain in a man with cancer {level=3}
Mr Michael Turner, 66, with prostate cancer and known bone metastases, reports a new pain in the middle of his back for a week, worse when lying flat and with a band-like spread around his chest. His legs feel "a bit heavy". His calcium is normal. What is the likely diagnosis, how urgent is it, and what should happen?
::: solution
New thoracic back pain, worse lying flat, with band-like radiation and subjective leg heaviness in a man with bone metastases suggests **metastatic spinal cord compression**, with possible early neurological involvement. This is an oncological emergency. Steps: immediate discussion with the local MSCC coordinator or acute oncology team; full neurological examination (power, sensation level, reflexes, bladder and bowel function); urgent whole-spine MRI (within 24 hours given neurological symptoms, sooner if deteriorating, according to NICE NG234); corticosteroids (dexamethasone) as directed by local protocol while awaiting definitive treatment; analgesia; and spinal precautions if there is mechanical instability. Definitive treatment is usually radiotherapy, or surgery in selected patients. The outcome depends mainly on how well he can walk at the time of treatment, which is why waiting for weakness to become obvious is a serious error.
:::
:::
