Cancer is a somatic genetic disease with germline consequences. A tumour arises when a cell acquires enough heritable changes to escape the controls that keep its lineage in check, and then evolves — its descendants competing for space, nutrients and, eventually, against therapy. Almost every common pathway in that process was discovered because a family, or a childhood tumour, or a leukaemia-specific chromosome, told someone where to look.

This chapter covers how somatic mutation produces cancer, how germline variants predispose to it, and how both are used in practice: which patient to investigate, what to order, what the result means for relatives, and how a tumour's sequence now directs treatment.

## From mutation to tumour

::: definition Drivers, passengers and clonal evolution {#def-drivers}
A **driver** mutation confers a growth or survival advantage and is selected during tumour development; a **passenger** is functionally neutral and merely carried along. A tumour is a **clone** and its subclones: one cell acquires the initial advantage, its descendants accumulate further drivers, and the tumour becomes a population — which is why a single biopsy may under-represent the variants that eventually cause resistance. **Tumour mutational burden** (mutations per megabase) measures how much a tumour has been mutated, and predicts response to immune-checkpoint blockade: mismatch-repair-deficient and polymerase-ε hypermutant tumours carry thousands of neoantigens.
:::

::: definition Oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes {#def-onc-tsg}
A **proto-oncogene** becomes an **oncogene** by a *gain* of function — point mutation, amplification, translocation, or a promoter rearrangement that places it under a strong enhancer. One altered allele suffices at the cellular level, so oncogene activation behaves dominantly. Typical examples: *KRAS* codon 12/13/61, *BRAF* V600E, *PIK3CA* H1047R, *ERBB2* (HER2) amplification, *MYCN* amplification, *BCR-ABL1* fusion, *PML-RARA*, *ALK* fusions, and TERT promoter mutation.
A **tumour suppressor gene** is inactivated by *loss* of function of both alleles — the **two hits** of Knudson — by mutation plus deletion, loss of heterozygosity, mitotic recombination, or epigenetic silencing of one allele with mutation of the other (the classic example being *MLH1* promoter methylation in sporadic microsatellite-unstable colorectal cancer versus a germline mismatch-repair mutation in Lynch syndrome). Some act by haploinsufficiency (*PTEN*, *RB1* partly), and some mutant proteins acquire new oncogenic properties (*TP53* gain-of-function mutants).
:::

The functional categories — the "hallmarks" — are the useful skeleton, because each contains druggable targets: sustained proliferative signalling (receptor tyrosine kinases, RAS–MAPK, PI3K–AKT–mTOR); evasion of growth suppression (RB–E2F, p16^INK4A^, cyclin D); resistance to cell death (BCL-2 in the t(14;18) of follicular lymphoma); loss of senescence and replicative limits (TERT, *CDKN2A*, and the alternative lengthening phenotype); invasion and metastasis (E-cadherin/*CDH1*, EMT programmes, chemokine receptors); tumour angiogenesis (VHL loss and HIF-driven VEGF); immune evasion (PD-L1 up-regulation, loss of β2-microglobulin and HLA, and the reason checkpoint inhibitors fail in some clones); genome instability and mutation (mismatch repair, homologous recombination, polymerase proofreading, mitotic checkpoints); deregulated metabolism; and tumour-promoting inflammation.

::: theorem Somatic mutation accumulates with age and with the number of rate-limiting steps {#thm-multistage}
Cancer incidence rises roughly as a power of age, and the exponent reflects the number of rate-limited events a cell must accumulate; therefore a tissue's cancer risk depends on its stem-cell division count and its exposure to mutagens, and a hereditary syndrome that supplies the first hit raises incidence without changing the shape of the age curve.
:::

::: proof
If malignancy requires $k$ sequential rate-limiting alterations, each occurring at a small constant probability per unit time in a stem-cell lineage, the hazard behaves as $t^{k-1}$ — the multistage model that explains why epithelial cancers become so much more common with age, and why retinoblastoma, needing one fewer step when the first is inherited, presents in infancy. Two consequences follow that are clinically load-bearing. First, tissues differ in risk partly because of the number of lifetime stem-cell divisions, which is why a commonly renewed epithelium (colon, skin, breast during parity history) is more often the site of cancer than the left ventricle — though mutagen exposure (tobacco, ultraviolet radiation, aflatoxin, aristolochic acid, chronic inflammation) can dominate the term, and this is why population differences in cancer incidence are mostly environmental, as migrant studies show. Second, a germline pathogenic variant in a tumour suppressor ("one hit in every cell") shifts the curve upwards and leftwards — earlier onset, more tumours, bilateral paired organs — which is the epidemiological signature to look for in a family history, and the reason early-onset disease is itself a referral criterion. The corollary is Peto's paradox: an elephant, with vastly more cells and divisions than a mouse, does not have vastly more cancer, and comparative genomics attributes this to extra copies of functional *TP53* and related duplications in elephants and to enhanced tumour-suppressor networks in other large and long-lived species.
:::

## Inherited cancer syndromes

Roughly 5–10% of cancers arise in people with a high- or moderate-penetrance germline variant; another band of familial clustering reflects shared environment plus polygenic background. The clinical trigger is never the diagnosis alone but the **pattern**: young age, multiple primaries, bilateral paired organs, a tumour typical of the syndrome, an unusual histology, and the same tumour type or a related one across generations.

::: example The 34-year-old with a caecal cancer {#ex-lynch}
A 34-year-old man has a stage II adenocarcinoma of the caecum. His mother had endometrial cancer at 48 and a maternal uncle colorectal cancer at 39. Tumour testing shows loss of MSH2 and MSH6 staining on immunohistochemistry and high-level microsatellite instability.
::: solution
The pattern is Lynch syndrome: right-sided early-onset colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer in a first-degree relative, loss of MSH2/MSH6 with secondary loss of MSH6 (which destabilises with MSH2), and microsatellite instability. The immunohistochemistry and instability results tell you the mechanism; only germline testing (sequencing plus deletion/duplication analysis of *MSH2*, considering *EPCAM* deletion, which silences *MSH2* in cis) tells the family. Confirm before acting, because sporadic *MLH1* hypermethylation with *BRAF* V600E is the commonest cause of mismatch-repair-deficient colorectal cancer and would be wrong to chase germinally. Once a germline variant is found: each first-degree relative has a 1 in 2 chance of carrying it, so cascade testing is the single most valuable act; the patient's management changes now (colonoscopy rather than segmental resection is a reasonable choice given metachronous risk, annual colonoscopy after diagnosis, gynaecological surveillance and discussion of risk-reducing hysterectomy in women once childbearing is complete, urinalysis and dermatological review, and consideration of aspirin chemoprevention as studied in Lynch); and immune checkpoint inhibition becomes a legitimate option for any future advanced disease because mismatch-repair deficiency predicts response. Note that his children's risk depends on his carrier status and partner's genetics, and that a negative predictive test in a relative is only informative for the familial variant.
:::
:::

::: example A germline result in a breast clinic {#ex-brca}
A 38-year-old woman with grade 3, triple-negative breast cancer and no family history has germline testing: pathogenic *BRCA1* variant, and her tumour shows loss of the wild-type allele. She asks what this means for her, her sister aged 42, and her 14-year-old son.
::: solution
For her, the variant predicts a substantially increased lifetime risk of breast cancer in the remaining breast and of ovarian, fallopian tube and peritoneal cancer, with risks in the ranges quoted by guidelines; it also predicts sensitivity to platinum chemotherapy and to PARP inhibition in the appropriate settings, both of which have moved into adjuvant use for high-risk hereditary disease. Management is a discussion of contralateral mastectomy versus intensive MRI surveillance, and of risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy — with the honest statement that ovarian cancer screening has not been shown to reduce mortality, so surgery offered after childbearing is the effective option, and its consequences include immediate surgical menopause, bone and cardiovascular considerations, and, where the uterus is removed or retained, individual decisions about hormone replacement. For her sister: predictive testing for the known familial variant is available, inexpensive, and should be offered with counselling rather than simply requested by letter — a negative result for *that* variant is genuinely reassuring, and a positive one changes management. For the 14-year-old son: **do not test** a healthy minor for an adult-onset condition; the reason to record the familial variant now is so that he can be offered testing in adulthood (and, importantly, so that his future children's reproductive options exist), with the same counselling pathway. Explain, once and clearly, that a positive result is not a diagnosis, that risk figures are population numbers applied to an individual with unknown modifier genes and life history, and that his aunt's course is not his obligation.
:::
:::

## Repair genes, synthetic lethality and therapy

Cells choose between homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining for double-strand breaks; homologous recombination needs an intact *BRCA1*–*BRCA2*–PALB2–*FANCD2*–*RAD51* axis. Loss of that route leaves a cell dependent on backup repair and on PARP-mediated single-strand-break repair. **PARP inhibitors** trap PARP at single-strand breaks, and in a homologous-recombination-deficient cell those breaks collapse replication forks — the tumour dies while homologous-recombination-proficient tissue recovers. This is **synthetic lethality** realised clinically, in ovarian, breast, pancreatic and prostate cancer with *BRCA1/2* or related alterations; the same logic explains the sensitivity of these tumours to platinum and to ionising radiation, and their resistance mechanisms: reversion mutation restoring the reading frame, loss of 53BP1 or SHLD1/2-dependent end protection, drug efflux, and restoration of the fork.

Three other therapy-resistance patterns belong in a genetics course because they are genetic: **on-target mutation** (*BCR-ABL1* T315I and the third-generation inhibitors, *EGFR* T790M then C797S, *PIK3CA* and pathway reactivation, secondary *KIT* mutations); **bypass signalling** (MET amplification, RAS–MAPK reactivation, lineage switch to small-cell phenotype in *RB1*/*TP53*-loss lung cancer treated with EGFR inhibition, and neuroendocrine transformation in prostate cancer); and **immune escape** (HLA or β2-microglobulin loss, and JAK1/2 mutations abolishing interferon signalling — which is why checkpoint blockade can fail after an initial response).

::: example A tumour panel that is not about the tumour {#ex-chip}
A 71-year-old with metastatic pancreatic cancer has a liquid biopsy reported as *TP53* mutation plus a *DNMT3A* variant at 24% variant allele frequency, and a germline panel reports a pathogenic *ATM* variant. The treating team asks whether the *DNMT3A* finding is a target and whether the family needs testing for *ATM*.
::: solution
The *DNMT3A* variant is almost certainly **clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential** (CHIP): leucocyte-derived DNA released into plasma carries somatic mutations in *DNMT3A*, *TET2*, *ASXL1*, *JAK2* and *PPM1D* at frequencies that rise steeply with age, and cell-free DNA assays cannot distinguish them from tumour-derived variants. That has three consequences: it is not a therapeutic target for the pancreatic cancer; it is a common cause of false-positive "tumour" calls on plasma assays and of spurious germline calls on blood-based tumour-only testing (paired normal tissue sequencing is what distinguishes them); and, with a high variant fraction, the finding predicts cardiovascular risk and treatment-related cytopenias, and is worth mentioning to haematology if counts are abnormal. The *ATM* result must be treated differently again: a single-allele *ATM* variant is a moderate-risk breast cancer gene and is relevant to the family's surveillance; homozygous or biallelic *ATM* loss causes ataxia-telangiectasia, so if any relative has neurological features, both alleles should be examined; and *ATM*-altered tumours may be more sensitive to DNA-damaging agents and PARP inhibition, an area of active trial evidence rather than routine practice. The general rule: a tumour or plasma result is interpreted with the normal sample, the variant allele fraction, and the patient's age — and a germline result is counselled as a family matter, not a tumour matter.
:::
:::

::: warning A negative tumour panel does not exclude an inherited predisposition, and a germline variant of uncertain significance explains nothing
Two mistakes recur. First, somatic panels test the genes and the alterations they were designed for, and a negative result says nothing about a germline syndrome — the majority of predisposition genes are not in tumour panels, and germline testing needs a paired normal sample and a laboratory that reports germline variants with appropriate classification. Second, a variant of uncertain significance in a predisposition gene must not drive management: risk-reducing surgery, or excluding a relative from testing, on the basis of a VUS is harm, and the correct action is to seek segregation and functional evidence, or to manage on personal and family history as if no variant had been found. Where the germline and somatic compartments are sequenced together, remember also the reverse trap: a "germline" variant found only in blood at 20% may be CHIP, and one found in both may be a benign polymorphism in a gene nobody has implicated.
:::

::: example Bilateral retinoblastoma in a nine-month-old {#ex-rb}
A nine-month-old presents with leukocoria; examination shows bilateral, multifocal retinal tumours. The parents are healthy and unrelated, and there is no family history. Genetic testing of blood and tumour shows a pathogenic *RB1* variant in blood together with loss of the second allele in tumour.
::: solution
A detectable *RB1* variant in blood means **heritable retinoblastoma**, even without a family history: about 40% of cases are heritable and most of those arise from a new germline variant, typically paternal in origin and often present at low level in a parent's blood (gonadal mosaicism). Because every cell carries the first hit, the second hit occurs in many retinal cells, which is why the disease is bilateral and multifocal and presents in infancy (see [[#thm-multistage]]). Three consequences follow, and they are the reason germline testing is done at diagnosis rather than later. **Second primaries:** survivors have a markedly increased lifetime risk of osteosarcoma, soft-tissue sarcoma, pineoblastoma (trilateral disease) and, later, melanoma, and the risk is amplified by radiotherapy — so treatment planning favours intra-arterial or intravitreal chemotherapy, focal therapies and enucleation over external-beam radiotherapy wherever feasible, and long-term follow-up includes surveillance imaging with attention to minimising radiation exposure. **Siblings:** a sibling's risk is not negligible even with negative parental blood testing, because of possible gonadal mosaicism; the practical answer is examination under anaesthesia in infancy and consideration of targeted testing for the familial variant. **The parents' future:** if the child is the first in the family, the parents' own risk is near background but their siblings' offspring risk depends on whether a parent is mosaic; and the child, if he survives, will have a 1 in 2 risk of transmitting the variant to each of his children, which is why the result and the familial variant are recorded in a form he can access as an adult. Note what a tumour-only panel would have missed: without a paired blood sample the germline first hit is invisible, and the family loses the single most actionable piece of information in the case.
:::

::: quiz
Which best explains why PARP inhibitors kill cells with biallelic *BRCA1* inactivation while sparing normal tissue?
- [ ] PARP is more abundant in tumour cells.
- [x] Loss of both *BRCA1* alleles abolishes homologous recombination, so the cell cannot repair the replication-associated breaks that PARP inhibition creates; a normal cell retains one functional allele and repairs them.
- [ ] PARP inhibitors are activated by tumour-specific kinases.
- [ ] *BRCA1*-deficient cells cannot perform non-homologous end joining at all.
::: solution
Synthetic lethality requires that neither lesion alone is lethal but both together are. A heterozygous normal cell retains homologous recombination and recovers; a biallelic tumour cell cannot, and retains trapped PARP and collapsed forks. This is also why reversion of the germline-type lesion, restoration of homologous recombination (for example by loss of 53BP1-dependent end protection or by a secondary *BRCA* reversion mutation), and efflux are the resistance mechanisms seen in relapsed disease.
:::

::: history
Theodor Boveri proposed in 1914 that aberrant chromosomes could cause a cell to divide without restraint and that cancer is cellular in origin — vindicated piece by piece over eighty years. Alfred Knudson's 1971 statistical analysis of retinoblastoma, using age distributions and bilaterality, inferred two hits and distinguished inherited from sporadic disease before any gene was known; the *RB1* cDNA was cloned by three groups in 1986 and the gene's product and its control of E2F worked out over the following decade. J. Michael Bishop and Harold Varmus's discovery that the Rous sarcoma virus oncogene *src* has a normal cellular counterpart (1970, explained 1976) established the proto-oncogene and earned the 1989 Nobel Prize. Bert Vogelstein's group defined the adenoma–carcinoma sequence and *APC* in 1987–1991; the mismatch-repair genes underlying Lynch syndrome followed in 1993, and *BRCA1* and *BRCA2* were cloned in 1994 and 1995 by collaborative efforts including the teams of Mary-Claire King and of the Icelandic and Icelandic-Australian groups. Chromosome translocations defined the haematological malignancies — the Philadelphia chromosome in 1960 and t(14;18) in 1976 — and targeted therapy followed: imatinib, designed to inhibit BCR-ABL1, was approved in 2001 after Brian Druker's group showed durable cytogenetic responses in chronic myeloid leukaemia. Hanahan and Weinberg's 2001 synthesis of the hallmarks gave the field its language; the 2005 demonstration that PARP inhibition is lethal to homologous-recombination-deficient cells turned a DNA-repair pathway into a drug class; ipilimumab's approval in 2011 opened checkpoint inhibition; and the sequencing era's incidental discovery of clonal haematopoiesis in 2014 has made interpretation of "a mutation in blood" a routine clinical problem.
:::

## Where this leads

Cell-cycle control, apoptosis and their checkpoints are in [[cell-biochemistry/signalling-cell-cycle]]; DNA repair pathways in [[cell-biochemistry/gene-expression]]; tumour pathology and staging in [[pathology/cancer-biology]]; risk-informed screening and treatment in each organ system's course; the ethics of predictive testing in [[genetics/genetic-testing]] and [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]].

::: summary
- Cancer is somatic evolution: driver mutations are selected, passengers accumulate, and the resulting subclonal structure explains mixed responses and inevitable resistance.
- Oncogenes are activated dominantly (mutation, amplification, fusion, enhancer hijacking); tumour suppressors are lost by two hits, including epigenetic silencing — the cellular mechanism that Knudson inferred statistically.
- Incidence rises as a power of age because several rate-limiting events are needed; a germline hit removes one step, producing earlier onset, bilaterality and multiplicity — the epidemiological signature of a hereditary syndrome.
- Recognise syndromes by pattern (age, multiplicity, bilateral paired organs, histology, pedigree), confirm with the right test (universal MMR testing of colorectal and endometrial tumours finds what family history misses), and manage with surveillance plus, where evidence supports it, risk-reducing surgery.
- DNA repair defects are therapeutic opportunities: homologous-recombination deficiency and mismatch-repair deficiency predict platinum, PARP-inhibitor and checkpoint-inhibitor sensitivity respectively, and their resistance mechanisms are genetic.
- Interpret tumour and plasma sequencing with a paired normal and with the variant allele fraction: clonal haematopoiesis generates false positives, and germline findings are family information requiring counselling.
- Do not test healthy minors for adult-onset predispositions; do offer cascade testing for a known familial variant; never act on a variant of uncertain significance.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Two hits in words {level=1}
Explain why an inherited *RB1* variant gives rise to tumours in infancy, whereas sporadic retinoblastoma requires two somatic events in the same cell and presents later.
::: solution
An inherited pathogenic allele means every retinal cell already carries one hit, so only a single somatic event — mutation, mitotic recombination or deletion — inactivates the remaining allele; with many target cells and a small per-cell probability, tumours appear within the first year or two, are frequently multifocal and bilateral, and the individual's other tissues are at risk throughout life. A sporadic tumour requires two independent somatic events in the same cell, an event whose probability rises steeply with time, giving unilateral later disease. The same logic explains why hereditary *TP53*, *VHL* or *APC* syndromes present earlier with more lesions, and why "the second hit" is what makes an autosomal dominant predisposition recessive at the cellular level.
:::
:::

::: exercise Classify the alteration {level=1}
For each finding, state oncogene or tumour suppressor and the mechanism: (a) ERBB2 copy number 12; (b) homozygous deletion of *CDKN2A*; (c) t(9;22); (d) biallelic somatic *VHL* mutation; (e) TERT promoter −124C>T.
::: solution
(a) Oncogene by amplification (ligand-independent HER2 signalling). (b) Tumour suppressor by deletion — loss of p16^INK4a^ releases CDK4/6 and hyperphosphorylates RB. (c) Oncogene by fusion: BCR-ABL1 gives constitutive tyrosine-kinase activity (and is the target of imatinib and its successors). (d) Tumour suppressor by two somatic hits, giving HIF stabilisation and VEGF/PDGF-driven angiogenesis. (e) Oncogene by promoter mutation creating an ETS binding site and reactivating telomerase — an early event in glioma, melanoma and bladder cancer.
:::

::: exercise Referral arithmetic {level=1}
Which of these patients warrant referral to clinical genetics for cancer predisposition assessment, and why: (a) a 46-year-old with breast cancer and a maternal aunt with breast cancer at 60; (b) a 35-year-old with breast cancer; (c) a 60-year-old with endometrial cancer whose tumour is MMR-deficient with isolated loss of MSH6 and no family history; (d) a 5-year-old with unilateral retinoblastoma?
::: solution
(a) Probably not for germline testing on these grounds alone — the pattern is compatible with polygenic and shared-environment familial clustering, though a full pedigree including ovarian, pancreatic and prostate cancers and male breast cancer may change the assessment. (b) Yes: age at diagnosis alone meets most services' criteria. (c) Yes: isolated MMR protein loss on tumour testing warrants germline evaluation even without family history, because Lynch syndrome can present with no informative pedigree, especially for the lower-penetrance *MSH6* and *PMS2* genes (and because a family history may be short by chance, adoption, or small family size). (d) Yes: any retinoblastoma, including unilateral, warrants assessment for a germline *RB1* variant because a proportion of unilateral cases are heritable, with implications for the child's second-tumour risk, for siblings, and for avoiding radiotherapy.

::: exercise Mechanism and management {level=2}
A 40-year-old woman has a pathogenic *BRCA2* variant found on a commercial panel. She has no personal or family history of cancer. What are her risks, and what should she be offered?
::: solution
A pathogenic *BRCA2* variant confers a substantially increased lifetime risk of breast cancer in women, with a risk that rises from the thirties, plus increased ovarian, fallopian tube and peritoneal, pancreatic, prostate (in male relatives) and melanoma risk. Offered: structured high-risk breast surveillance from the age recommended by the local programme, usually annual MRI from the late twenties or thirties with mammography added, and explicit discussion that surveillance detects rather than prevents; risk-reducing mastectomy as an option with a quantified benefit and its body-image and sexual-health consequences; risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy once childbearing is complete (typically in the early forties for *BRCA1* and slightly later for *BRCA2*), with discussion of surgical menopause, hormone replacement in selected women without breast cancer, and the fact that peritoneal cancer risk persists after surgery; lifestyle measures (alcohol, weight, physical activity) with honest numbers; and cascade testing of relatives. Also address the reproductive implications — a partner's carrier status matters for future children if the couple want that information — and record what was discussed so the plan can be revisited as she ages.
:::
:::

::: exercise Predict the treatment {level=2}
A tumour is reported as microsatellite-instable, mismatch-repair-deficient by immunohistochemistry with loss of MLH1 and PMS2, and *BRAF* V600E wild type. What are the two possible explanations, which test distinguishes them, and what treatment does the MSI status support if the disease is metastatic?
::: solution
Loss of MLH1 with secondary loss of PMS2 arises either from a germline *MLH1* variant (Lynch syndrome) or from somatic/epigenetic *MLH1* promoter hypermethylation, which in sporadic colorectal cancer is usually part of the CpG-island methylator phenotype and is associated with *BRAF* V600E. The distinguishing tests are *MLH1* promoter methylation on the tumour (positive strongly favours sporadic) and, if negative or borderline, germline sequencing with deletion/duplication analysis of *MLH1* and *EPCAM*; the wild-type *BRAF* here makes sporadic methylation less likely and raises the priority of germline testing. For metastatic disease, mismatch-repair deficiency is predictive of response to PD-1-directed immune checkpoint inhibition, which is a licensed tissue-agnostic indication and can produce durable remissions; a germline result additionally directs surveillance and cascade testing for the family, and would support discussion of aspirin chemoprevention and of the surgical options described in Lynch syndrome.
:::

::: exercise Why the family is not enough {level=3}
A 29-year-old man with bilateral acoustic neuromas and a new meningioma has a pathogenic germline *NF2* variant. His parents and two siblings test negative. His partner is pregnant. Explain the inheritance and what you tell the couple about the pregnancy and about his own oncological future.
::: solution
*NF2* is autosomal dominant with high penetrance for vestibular schwannomas; a negative test in both parents, with no relevant family history, means the variant arose *de novo* in him (or, rarely, in a parent's germ line, which would matter for his siblings only as a small residual risk they should be told about). Their pregnancy risk is therefore 1 in 2 in each child, based on his status rather than the family history — the recurrence figure they need. Options to discuss: no testing; invasive prenatal diagnosis for the known familial variant (available and reliable because the variant is known); non-invasive prenatal diagnosis for a known familial variant in a few centres; preimplantation genetic testing if they contemplate further pregnancies by IVF; and the option of not testing. His oncological future needs a specialist service: serial imaging with hearing preservation as a priority, medical therapy with bevacizumab in selected rapid cases, surgical or radiotherapy options with the risks to hearing and facial nerve function, plus surveillance for meningioma, ependymoma and peripheral nerve sheath tumours, ophthalmological review, and management of the psychosocial burden of a condition that threatens hearing and balance in early adult life.
:::
:::

::: exercise Design a surveillance plan {level=3}
A 26-year-old woman is found to carry a pathogenic *PTEN* variant (Cowden syndrome spectrum). Outline her surveillance and the reasoning behind each element, and say what to do about her 30-year-old brother.
::: solution
*PTEN* hamartoma tumour syndrome raises the risk of breast, thyroid (follicular-cell), endometrial and renal cancer, with macrocephaly, mucocutaneous lesions, gastrointestinal polyps and vascular malformations, and rare Lhermitte-Duclos disease. Surveillance follows those risks with the reasoning stated: annual breast surveillance from the mid-thirties or earlier according to local protocol, with MRI, because the lifetime risk approaches that of *BRCA*-associated disease; annual thyroid ultrasound, because thyroid cancer is common and often early, with surgery guided by cytology rather than by prophylactic thyroidectomy (which is not indicated, unlike *RET*); endometrial surveillance with a low threshold for sampling on abnormal bleeding, and discussion of hysterectomy once childbearing is complete; renal imaging periodically, since bilateral, early renal tumours are a major contributor to mortality; dermatological review and attention to gastrointestinal polyps. For her brother: offer cascade testing for the known familial variant now, since a negative result removes him from a lifelong programme and a positive one starts it; a negative result does not explain symptoms if he has macrocephaly and multiple trichilemmomas, in which case further testing for the second allele or for an alternative gene is appropriate. Document each discussion, and record the plan in a form the patient can carry, so that a change of hospital does not reset the programme.

::: exercise Ethics and evidence {level=3}
A healthy 22-year-old asymptomatic man's father died at 41 of a *TP53*-associated adrenocortical carcinoma within Li-Fraumeni syndrome. He asks to be tested. How do you proceed, and what would you do differently if he were 12?
::: solution
He is an adult with capacity and there is a surveillance benefit to knowing, so testing is appropriate: confirm the familial variant from the father's records or stored sample, counsel him beforehand about the possibility of a positive result (lifelong whole-body MRI surveillance, avoidance of radiotherapy where possible, reproductive implications, psychological impact, insurance and employment in jurisdictions without protection) and of an inconclusive one, obtain written consent, disclose in person, and arrange follow-up and support whichever result returns. At 12, the default is not to test a healthy child for an adult-onset predisposition when there is no benefit in childhood, because doing so removes his future autonomy, may affect insurability in some settings, and can burden family dynamics — but Li-Fraumeni is one of the few adult-onset syndromes where childhood surveillance is recommended, so the decision is genuinely contested and is made with the family and, for a Gillick-competent child, with his own view, with the possibility of deferring to adolescence; document the reasoning. Note the two errors to avoid: refusing testing to an adult who has capacity and a clear benefit, and reflexively testing a child because a parent wants certainty.
