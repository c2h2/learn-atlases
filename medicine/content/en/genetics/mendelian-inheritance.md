A pedigree is a piece of evidence with a logic of its own. Three generations of apparently healthy people with one affected child of each sex, parents from the same village, argues for a recessive mechanism; an affected man in every generation with no affected sons of his argues for X-linkage; a family with an adult-onset condition in which each generation "skips" nothing argues for dominant transmission with age-dependent penetrance. Reading that logic correctly changes what relatives are offered, what risk they are quoted, and what a negative test means.

This chapter sets out the single-gene patterns — autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked — the vocabulary of why they deviate from expectation, and the arithmetic used in a clinic.

## The vocabulary of a single gene

::: definition Basic terms {#def-terms}
**Genotype** is the pair of alleles at a locus; **phenotype** is the observable consequence. An individual with two identical pathogenic alleles is **homozygous**; with two different pathogenic alleles at the same locus, **compound heterozygous** — the common situation for recessive disease out of consanguineous unions, and the reason severity can vary with the pair of alleles. A male with a variant on the X (or a cell with a deletion) is **hemizygous**. A **carrier** has a pathogenic allele without disease — a term that, strictly, applies to recessive inheritance, and whose careless use in X-linked or dominant conditions misleads families. **Consanguinity** means shared ancestry: the **coefficient of inbreeding** $F$ is the probability that the two alleles at a locus in a child are identical by descent, $F = 1/16$ for the child of first cousins. A **phenocopy** is an environmentally caused phenotype identical to a genetic one (hearing loss from aminoglycosides or from a connexin variant; a prolonged QT from drugs or from *KCNQ1*).
:::

Three mechanisms produce a dominant phenotype from one mutant allele, and knowing which one operates explains the phenotype and the therapy: **haploinsufficiency** (50% of the product is not enough — *PAX6*, *TBX1*, *LMNA* partly); a **dominant-negative** effect (the mutant product spoils the multimer — collagen triple helix, p53, connexins); and **gain of function** (a new or unregulated activity — *FGFR3* in achondroplasia, *RET* in MEN2, the sodium channel *SCN4A* in hyperkalaemic periodic paralysis). Recessive disease, by contrast, is usually a loss of function with an enzyme or a transporter, where 50% activity exceeds the threshold — the threshold effect described in [[cell-biochemistry/gene-expression]].

## Autosomal dominant inheritance

::: definition Autosomal dominant {#def-ad}
The phenotype appears in the heterozygote. Typically: the trait appears in every generation (vertical transmission); males and females are equally affected and both transmit it; an affected heterozygous person transmits it to half their children, sons and daughters alike; and there may be no family history at all because of a *de novo* variant, reduced penetrance, mild disease gone unrecognised, or — historically — early death before reproduction.
:::

Examples chosen for what each illustrates: **marfan syndrome** (*FBN1*, variable expressivity: aortic root disease, lens dislocation upward and outward, skeletal features; aortic dissection risk drives the whole management plan); **neurofibromatosis type 1** (pleiotropy: café-au-lait macules, neurofibromas, optic glioma, bone and vascular disease, learning difficulties; the diagnosis is clinical, and the same variant can give a mild or severe course in siblings); **achondroplasia** (*FGFR3* gain of function; 80% arise from a new mutation, associated with paternal age; the homozygote is lethal); **adult polycystic kidney disease** (*PKD1*/*PKD2*; late onset, so young relatives need imaging or, better, predictive testing after counselling); **myotonic dystrophy type 1** (CTG expansion with anticipation, congenital form only through maternal transmission, and multisystem disease — see [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]]); **hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer / Lynch syndrome** (dominant inheritance of a *predisposition*, with recessive inactivation at the cellular level — Knudson's two hits); **familial hypercholesterolaemia** (dominant for the LDL phenotype, and a model for cascade screening).

Two features dominate counselling. **Late onset** means relatives are at risk before anyone is ill, which is why predictive testing is offered with a protocol — counselling before, an interval for reflection, a confirmed test on a known family variant, disclosure arrangements, and follow-up — and why a negative result can carry survivor guilt as well as relief. **Incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity** mean that an apparently unaffected parent may carry the variant, so the recurrence risk to a grandchild is not zero and a "healthy" family history does not exclude the diagnosis.

## Autosomal recessive inheritance

::: definition Autosomal recessive {#def-ar}
Two pathogenic alleles are required. Typically: the propositus is the only affected person, among sibs (horizontal pattern); males and females are equally affected; each child of two carrier parents has a 1 in 4 risk, a 1 in 2 chance of being a carrier and a 1 in 4 chance of being neither; the parents are usually healthy, and if the condition is rare, the parents may be related. An affected person's children are all carriers, and are affected only if the partner is also a carrier (**pseudodominance**, seen where a recessive condition is common in a population).
:::

Recessive conditions are disproportionately **enzymopathies and transporter diseases**, and their clinical patterns follow: cystic fibrosis, the haemoglobinopathies, phenylketonuria and the other amino acid disorders, the glycogen and lysosomal storage diseases, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Wilson disease, albinism, spinal muscular atrophy, and the sensorineural deafnesses. Two arithmetic consequences matter in clinic. First, an unaffected sib of an affected child has a 2 in 3 chance of being a carrier (1 in 2 × 1/2 among the non-affected: the conditional calculation students omit), and that person's offspring's risk is $2/3 \times 1/2 \times$ (the partner's population carrier frequency). Second, consanguinity raises the chance that a rare recessive allele is present on both sides: for the child of first cousins, $F = 1/16$, so a rare recessive condition has roughly $1/16$ times the excess risk implied by the couple's shared ancestry — empirically, the risk of a serious autosomal recessive or multifactorial disorder rises from a baseline of about 2–3% to about 4–6%, with a small additional rise in perinatal mortality, and the figure to quote is that individual couple's family history, not a generic one.

## X-linked inheritance

::: definition X-linked inheritance {#def-xl}
**X-linked recessive**: hemizygous males are affected; there is no male-to-male transmission (a father gives his X only to daughters); the variant enters the family through carrier females; the affected male's sisters have a 1 in 2 chance of being carriers and his daughters are obligate carriers. **X-linked dominant**: both sexes are affected, males sometimes more severely or prenatally lethally (e.g. incontinentia pigmenti, *IKBKG*), transmission from an affected father to all daughters and no sons — the pattern that distinguishes it from autosomal dominance. **Manifesting heterozygote**: a carrier female with features, because X-inactivation is skewed against the normal allele or because of a structural X abnormality or Turner mosaic.
:::

Carrier testing in X-linked disease is the classic place where probability must be explicit. If a woman's brother has Duchenne muscular dystrophy and her mother is an obligate carrier, her prior risk is 1 in 2; each unaffected son multiplies that by 1/2, and a normal creatine kinase or a normal dystrophin study shifts it further — the numbers must be combined, not replaced, and the result differs between families with the same pedigree drawing. Female carriers also need clinical attention, because cardiomyopathy occurs in a substantial minority of *DMD* and *DYSTROPHIN*-carrier women and requires surveillance whether or not they have weakness; haemophilia carriers may bleed, and that affects surgery, delivery and anaesthesia planning.

::: example Quantifying a carrier {#ex-carrier}
A woman has a brother with X-linked severe combined immunodeficiency; her parents are unaffected, and she has two unaffected sons. What is her risk of being a carrier, and her son's risk of being affected?
::: solution
Take the mother as an obligate or possible carrier depending on family structure; for the standard case in which the mother is a definite carrier, the woman's prior risk is $1/2$. Each unaffected son halves it, because a carrier's son is affected with probability $1/2$ while a non-carrier's son is unaffected: after two sons the posterior risk is $\dfrac{0.5 \times 0.5^{2}}{0.5 \times 0.5^{2} + 0.5} = 1/5$. Her son's risk of being affected is then $1/5 \times 1/2 \approx 1/10$ — not the 1 in 4 the family remember from the original consultation. If instead the mother's carrier status is itself uncertain, or if the proband's variant arose *de novo*, a new mutation rate must enter the prior, and the arithmetic changes accordingly; this is why a geneticist wants the actual variant, the actual relatives tested, and a pedigree drawn on the screen, not the words "we think it runs through the women".
:::
:::

## Why real pedigrees misbehave

| Phenomenon | What it means | What it does to the pedigree |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced penetrance | Some carriers never show disease | Apparent skipped generations; risk cannot be excluded clinically |
| Variable expressivity | Same variant, different severity | A "mild" parent with the same variant as a severely affected child |
| Anticipation | Earlier onset or greater severity in successive generations | Expansion of a repeat: myotonic dystrophy, Huntington disease, fragile X |
| Locus heterogeneity | Variants in different genes give the same phenotype | Negative testing for one gene does not exclude the disease (retinitis pigmentosa, non-syndromic deafness, hereditary breast cancer) |
| Allelic heterogeneity | Different variants in one gene give different phenotypes | Genotype–phenotype correlations; and population-specific founder alleles |
| Pleiotropy | One gene, many organ systems | Neurofibromatosis, Marfan, *PKD1* |
| Phenocopy | Environment mimics genotype | Drug-induced deafness or QT prolongation; a "sporadic" case in a high-penetrance family |
| Uniparental disomy, mosaicism, germline mosaicism | Non-Mendelian transmission | Recurrence after an apparently *de novo* diagnosis; recurrence risk above the quoted figure if gonadal mosaicism is present |
| Pseudodominance | Very common recessive allele | Affected person with an affected parent, mimicking dominance (thalassaemia in some populations) |

Two further points of language prevent most of the errors seen in clinic. **Recurrence risks are per pregnancy**: the fact that a couple already have two affected children does not change the 1 in 4 for the next, and the reassurance that "it must have skipped us" is the gambler's fallacy in clinical clothing. And **negative does not mean clear**: a negative test for a variant that has not been shown to exist in the family, or a negative panel in a gene with locus heterogeneity, or a normal enzyme assay in a tissue where the enzyme is not expressed, answers a different question from the one asked.

::: example The short father and the tall pregnancy {#ex-achondroplasia}
A woman at 26 weeks is referred for a short femur length. The father has achondroplasia; the mother is of average stature with no family history. Scan shows femoral shortening below the 3rd centile, and there is no polyhydramnios.
::: solution
Achondroplasia is autosomal dominant due to gain-of-function *FGFR3* variants, and the homozygote — which this couple cannot have, since the mother has no variant — is lethal in the perinatal period. The a priori risk is therefore 1 in 2, and the scan finding raises the posterior probability substantially, so the plan is to confirm rather than to assume: targeted testing for the paternal familial variant on amniocytes or chorion villus cells (fast, and it answers the actual question), a detailed anatomical survey including head shape and thoracic circumference, and counselling that the anticipated phenotype is that of the father — with the risks that follow for him and for the baby, namely foramen magnum stenosis and cervicomedullary compression in infancy, middle ear disease and hearing loss, thoracolumbar kyphosis, obstructive sleep apnoea, and the need for growth, neurological and orthopaedic follow-up and for specific anaesthetic precautions. If the fetus is average, the scan finding needs another explanation (constitutional, placental, chromosomal, or a skeletal dysplasia with a different inheritance), so growth surveillance continues and the couple's recurrence risk may not be the low one they were promised. Testing on the basis of "the scan looks short" without knowing the familial variant is how the wrong question gets answered.
:::
:::

::: example Consanguinity and a "negative" test {#ex-consanguinity}
A pregnant woman whose parents are first cousins has a nephew with spinal muscular atrophy. Her own SMN1 deletion test is reported as negative. She asks whether her baby is at risk.
::: solution
Two separate calculations. First, the risk that she is a carrier despite a negative test: 95–98% of patients with spinal muscular atrophy have a homozygous *SMN1* exon 7 deletion, so a deletion-based carrier test misses the 2–5% of carriers whose second allele is a point mutation, and a "negative" result leaves a residual risk of roughly one in thirty to one in fifty of that prior being exceeded — the correct statement is "your result makes it much less likely, and here is the number". Second, the family structure: her nephew's affected parent is her sib or her spouse's sib, and her partner may also share the same ancestor. The couple should have their own carrier testing done by a method that detects point mutations (*SMN1* sequencing plus copy number), and if both partners carry a pathogenic allele, the risk to the fetus is 1 in 4 regardless of the earlier negative. Meanwhile the baseline risk from first-cousin parentage still applies to the pregnancy — an extra 2–3% of serious recessive or multifactorial disease — and it should be mentioned once, in the context of the family history, and not repeated as though it were a diagnosis.
:::
:::

::: example Two babies, healthy parents: recurrence after a "new" dominant variant {#ex-mosaicism}
A couple's first child is stillborn with the skeletal changes of lethal (type II) osteogenesis imperfecta; both parents are of normal stature with no bone fragility and a negative family history, and testing of the parents' blood shows no *COL1A1* or *COL1A2* variant. Their second pregnancy is unaffected. At 10 weeks of the third pregnancy they ask what the risk is.
::: solution
A dominant-negative collagen mutation is expected to be lethal in the homozygous or, for structural alleles, heterozygous state, so most such cases are new mutations. But the risk to the couple's next pregnancy is **not** the population mutation rate: a variant present in a proportion of one parent's germ cells but not in their blood — gonadal or germline mosaicism — is not detected by a blood test, and empirically about 5–10% of couples with an affected first child and negative parental testing have a second affected child. The correct counselling is therefore a quoted range (of the order of a few per cent, with most services saying 5–10% for lethal osteogenesis imperfecta because recurrences are reported), a plan for early ultrasound and, in this pregnancy, targeted testing on chorion villus or amniotic cells for the familial variant identified in the affected child — which is why stored DNA from the first baby (or a skin fibroblast culture) is worth retrieving before any further pregnancy is contemplated. If the variant cannot be obtained, testing can still be informative only with caution, and the couple should be told that a "low risk" estimate carries the same gonadal-mosaicism caveat. The same logic — a negative parental test does not buy a zero risk — applies to achondroplasia, to X-linked disease when the mother may be mosaic, and to the many conditions in which a recurrence is reported after an apparently sporadic case.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 30-year-old man has autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease with a known *PKD1* variant. His parents are both alive, and his father, aged 62, has had no ultrasound. What is the best estimate of the father's carrier status?
- [ ] He is certainly unaffected, because the patient's grandfather had no kidney disease.
- [x] He may carry the variant with incomplete age-related penetrance; his own risk of having the condition is not zero and an ultrasound or genetic testing is the only way to tell.
- [ ] He must carry it, because *PKD1* is dominant.
- [ ] His status is irrelevant to his other children.
::: solution
Adult polycystic kidney disease has age-dependent penetrance: many carriers have no cysts detectable in their twenties, so absence of disease at 62 lowers but does not exclude the possibility, and a *de novo* variant in the patient is also possible. The distinction matters clinically, because if he carries it, his other children (the patient's uncles and aunts) each have a 1 in 2 risk and would benefit from screening and blood-pressure management, and because the patient's own sibling risks depend on which parent transmitted the variant.
:::

::: history
Mendel's 1866 paper supplied the ratios, but the medical patterns came from Garrod: his 1902 account of alkaptonuria argued explicitly that the disease was recessive, that consanguineous parents were over-represented, and that "chemical individuality" ran in families, and his 1908 Croonian lectures generalised the idea to a whole class of disease. William Bateson applied Mendelism to human medicine in 1905, Archibald Garrod and colleagues described the "inborn errors", and Frederick Hutchison, Harold Suter and others began to accumulate pedigrees of deafness, ataxia and blindness. J. B. S. Haldane's 1935 paper on the rate of mutation of X-linked lethal genes showed that the frequency of an X-linked disease reflects a balance between mutation and selection and derived the result that a third of cases of an X-linked lethal, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy, can be expected to arise from new mutations — the reason one-third of mothers in such families are not carriers. Charlotte Auerbach's work on mutation and, from the 1960s, Victor McKusick's *Medical Genetics: Conceptual Approaches and Clinical Medicine* and the compilation that became OMIM gave the specialty its textbook and its catalogue. Mary Lyon's 1961 hypothesis explained the carrier female, and Arno Motulsky, Charles Scriver and their contemporaries built the biochemical and counselling practice that made clinical genetics a specialty with its own ethic: information, non-directiveness, and the family rather than the patient as the unit of care.
:::

## Where this leads

Exceptions and complications — mitochondrial inheritance, imprinting, repeat expansion, mosaicism — are [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]]; how to test what a pedigree suggests is [[genetics/genetic-testing]]; chromosome-level patterns are in [[genetics/chromosomal-disorders]]; the polygenic arithmetic of common disease in [[genetics/common-disease-genetics]].

::: summary
- Autosomal dominant: vertical transmission, equal sexes, 1 in 2 to each child, and no family history compatible with the diagnosis because of new mutation, late onset or reduced penetrance.
- Autosomal recessive: horizontal, sibs, 1 in 4 per pregnancy, often enzymic; the unaffected sib's carrier risk is 2 in 3, and consanguinity raises risk through identity by descent ($F = 1/16$ for the child of first cousins).
- X-linked recessive: affected males, carrier females, no male-to-male transmission; carrier risk is a Bayesian quantity that changes with each tested relative, and carrier women need cardiac and bleeding surveillance.
- Dominance arises by haploinsufficiency, dominant-negative action or gain of function; recessive disease is a loss of function below a threshold — the mechanism predicts both the pedigree and the treatment.
- Pedigrees deviate through penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, heterogeneity, pleiotropy, phenocopy, mosaicism and uniparental disomy; the clinician's task is to identify which of these is in play before quoting a risk.
- Recurrence risks are per pregnancy, and a negative test means only what the test could see: family-variant-specific testing, and locus heterogeneity, are the two commonest sources of falsely reassuring results.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Inbreeding arithmetic {level=1 check="0.0625"}
What is the coefficient of inbreeding $F$ for the child of first cousins?
::: solution
$F = 1/16 = 0.0625$: the path of descent runs through two generations on each side and one meiotic step per generation, giving $(1/2)^{5}$ per ancestral path × 2 shared grandparents. The path runs from each of the two shared grandparents down through a parent to the child — five meioses in all, $(1/2)^{5}$ per grandparent, doubled for two grandparents — so 6.25% of loci are expected to be homozygous by descent, which is what raises the risk of rare recessive conditions.
:::
:::

::: exercise The 2 in 3 rule {level=1}
Both parents are carriers of a recessive condition. Their daughter is unaffected. What is the probability that she is a carrier, and what is the risk that her first child is affected if her partner is a carrier from the same population?
::: solution
Given that she is unaffected, the possibilities are carrier (probability $2/3$) or neither (1/3), so she is a carrier with probability $2/3$. If the partner *is* a carrier, the risk to her first child is $2/3 \times 1/4 = 1/6$; if the partner's own carrier probability is $p$, the risk is $2/3 \times p \times 1/4$. If the partner's carrier status is itself $p$, multiply: risk $= 2/3 \times p \times 1/4$. Keeping the two factors separate is what allows the number to be updated when the partner's test result arrives.
:::
:::

::: exercise Risk with incomplete penetrance {level=1 check="1/15"}
A man has a mother with a confirmed autosomal dominant condition with 80% penetrance; he is 25 and unaffected. What is the probability that his own child is affected? Give the answer as a fraction.
::: solution
His prior risk of carrying the variant is 1/2; being unaffected at 25 reduces it, by Bayes, to $\dfrac{0.5 \times 0.2}{0.5 \times 0.2 + 0.5 \times 1} = 1/6$. His child's risk of inheriting the variant is $1/6 \times 1/2 = 1/12$, and of being affected $1/12 \times 0.8 \approx 0.067$ — about 1 in 15. Note how the two questions ("does he carry it?", "would it show?") are answered separately, and how much of the calculation is simply not confusing the two.
:::
:::

::: exercise Pattern recognition {level=2}
For each pedigree description, name the most likely mode of inheritance: (a) affected males in a sibship, all their maternal uncles affected, no transmission through unaffected females to their sons; (b) every daughter of an affected man affected, none of his sons; (c) two affected children of each sex with healthy parents from a small community; (d) a mildly affected parent and a severely affected child, with an anticipation pattern and congenital disease only when transmitted by the mother.
::: solution
(a) X-linked recessive (males affected, transmission through carrier females, and her sons — the maternal uncles — affected). (b) X-linked dominant, since an affected father transmits his X to all daughters and none of his sons. (c) Autosomal recessive, with a probable founder allele. (d) Autosomal dominant due to a repeat expansion with anticipation and maternal bias for the severe congenital form — myotonic dystrophy type 1 is the model.
:::
:::

::: exercise New mutation or inherited? {level=2}
A boy with achondroplasia has two parents of average stature, and the couple are considering another pregnancy. What is the recurrence risk, and what reasoning supports it?
::: solution
About 80% of achondroplasia cases are new dominant mutations, most on the paternally derived allele and associated with paternal age; if the fetal *FGFR3* variant is confirmed as absent from both parents' blood, the recurrence risk is low but not negligible, because gonadal (germ-line) mosaicism occurs in a small proportion of such families, and because non-paternity is a consideration to be handled by the counselling service rather than by laboratory error. The risk to the couple's other children is therefore on the order of 1% or less, quoted as a range with the reason, and with the option of early ultrasound for femur length in future pregnancies. The mechanism is a gain-of-function variant whose homozygote is lethal, which matters for the family's questions about two affected parents.
:::
:::

::: exercise Carrier testing that goes wrong {level=3}
A laboratory reports a "negative" result on a targeted mutation panel for cystic fibrosis in a woman whose second cousin died of the disease. Her partner's panel is also negative. Explain, as you would to the couple, why their residual risk is not zero, giving three separate reasons.
::: solution
First, the panel is not the gene: a targeted panel detects the variants it contains (typically 70–90% of pathogenic alleles in the population it was designed for), so a negative result leaves the possibility of a rare or novel *CFTR* variant, and the residual carrier risk is a calculable number, usually a fraction of the population risk rather than zero. Second, ancestry matters: a panel built for northern European variants performs worse in people of African, Asian or Middle-Eastern ancestry, and the couple's ancestry should be documented before the result is quoted. Third, phenotype: *CFTR* variants span a spectrum from classic disease to isolated congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens or pancreatitis, so a "mild" variant found in a relative may not appear on the panel, and some genotypes do not raise sweat chloride. Then say what follows: full-gene analysis (or exome/genome sequencing) if they want the smallest residual risk, the availability of prenatal and preimplantation testing once both alleles are known, and the option of proceeding with the numbers as they stand.
:::
:::

::: exercise Manifesting carrier {level=3}
A 19-year-old woman has progressive proximal weakness and a creatine kinase of 1,800 U/L; her nephew has Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Genetic testing shows she carries the familial *DMD* deletion. Explain how a female carrier can be affected, and what else you would investigate.
::: solution
Because of the random X-inactivation described by Lyon, a carrier female is a mosaic; if inactivation is skewed so that the X carrying the normal allele is preferentially silenced in muscle — by chance, or because of a structural X abnormality, or selection against cells expressing the normal X — she expresses the phenotype, sometimes as cardiomyopathy alone and sometimes with limb-girdle weakness. Investigations: cardiac assessment (ECG, echocardiography or cardiac MRI) regardless of symptoms, since carrier-related dilated cardiomyopathy can occur without skeletal weakness; respiratory assessment if weakness is significant; CK, and myopathy work-up (MRI pattern, and biopsy with dystrophin staining if the diagnosis is in doubt); renal and other causes of a raised CK excluded; genetic counselling about her own children (1 in 2 risk of transmitting the variant, giving affected sons and carrier daughters) and about the rest of the family; and a clear flag in her record, so that obstetric, anaesthetic and surgical teams know before a procedure that she has a dystrophinopathy — respiratory and cardiac assessment are then required, and succinylcholine and volatile anaesthetics are best avoided.
:::
:::

::: exercise Design the counselling {level=3}
A 24-year-old asymptomatic man's father has just been diagnosed with Huntington disease, genetically confirmed. He presents asking for a test. Set out how you would proceed and why each step matters.
::: solution
Do not test at the visit. Take a full history (mood, alcohol and substance use, relationships, employment, children and pregnancy plans), explain that a positive result cannot be undone and carries risks of depression, survivor guilt, relationship change and insurance or occupational implications in jurisdictions without protection, and that a negative result also has psychological consequences. Confirm that the familial variant is documented in the affected relative, arrange a pre-test counselling session with the clinical genetics team and, ideally, with a support organisation, and ask who in the family will be told. Offer a cooling-off period and a second appointment; test only with written consent, in a laboratory experienced in predictive testing, with disclosure of the result in person and follow-up arranged, including mental-health support. Discuss the alternatives he may not have considered: prenatal diagnosis with exclusion testing (where the result does not reveal whether the at-risk grandparent carries the variant), preimplantation genetic testing with non-disclosure protocols, and reproductive planning without testing. Document capacity, voluntariness and the disclosure plan. If he proceeds and is positive, plan surveillance and support even though there is no preventive treatment, and address driving, firearms, insurance and family disclosure; if negative, release him from follow-up and address the residual uncertainty, if any, about the familial variant.
:::
:::
