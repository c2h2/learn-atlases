Mendel's rules describe nuclear alleles that segregate cleanly, behave identically whether they come from mother or father, and change length never. Real families break those rules in a small number of ways, and each exception has a mechanism, a test and a recurrence risk that follows from the mechanism rather than from the pedigree shape.

This chapter covers the exceptions that appear in practice: mitochondrial inheritance, genomic imprinting, repeat-expansion disease with anticipation, and mosaicism in its somatic and germ-line forms, together with uniparental disomy and the non-genetic reasons a pedigree misbehaves.

## Mitochondrial inheritance

::: definition Mitochondrial DNA and heteroplasmy {#def-mt}
Mitochondria carry their own circular DNA of 16,569 base pairs, encoding 13 respiratory-chain subunits, 2 ribosomal RNAs and 22 transfer RNAs; the other ~1,300 mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded. A cell contains hundreds of mitochondria and thousands of mtDNA copies, and a person may therefore carry a mixture of wild-type and mutant genomes — **heteroplasmy**. A disease appears when the mutant proportion in a tissue exceeds a **threshold**, which is why the same variant can be asymptomatic in one person and devastating in their sibling. Because mitochondria are transmitted through the oocyte, mtDNA variants show **maternal inheritance**: an affected man transmits nothing to his children.
:::

Two phenomena make the pedigree unpredictable. The **bottleneck** of mtDNA during oogenesis means a woman's oocytes receive samples from a greatly reduced mitochondrial pool, so her own mutant load in blood tells her little about any one child's load, which may be 5% or 80%. And **mitotic segregation** with selection means that a tissue's load changes with time and differs between tissues: mtDNA deletions are notoriously low in blood and higher in post-mitotic tissue, which is why a suspected single large-scale deletion syndrome (Kearns–Sayre, Pearson, chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia) is investigated on urine sediment or muscle, not on a blood sample.

::: theorem Threshold and tissue determine the phenotype {#thm-threshold-mt}
For a given mtDNA variant, disease manifests in a tissue when the mutant load in that tissue exceeds the load at which the tissue can meet its ATP and redox demands; because load varies between tissues and with age, an "asymptomatic" carrier may develop disease later and may have affected children whose loads differ from her own.
:::

::: proof
Consider the m.3243A>G transfer-RNA variant. At 20–40% load a person may have only migraine, or deafness, or nothing; at 60–80% in muscle and brain the same variant produces the stroke-like episodes, seizures, lactic acidosis and short stature of MELAS, and maternal transmission of that same variant explains a family in which one woman has diabetes and deafness, her son has a childhood "stroke" with normal vessels, her sister died of a cardiomyopathy and her brother is well. The load is not fixed: post-mitotic tissues accumulate or lose mutant genomes unevenly, and in blood, cells bearing a large deletion or a severe transfer-RNA variant are selected against during mitosis, so measured load falls with age. The consequence for counselling is a rule of practice as much as of biology: quote the observed family pattern and the tissue-specific loads rather than a single risk number, measure load in more than one tissue where it changes management, and remember that a mildly affected mother can have a severely affected child, and an apparently unaffected mother of an affected child may herself be a low-level carrier who will transmit.
:::

The clinical patterns to know: **LHON** (leber hereditary optic neuropathy, classically m.11778G>A, painless sequential visual loss in young adults with male preponderance whose cause is not X-linked transmission), **MELAS**, **MERRF** with myoclonus and lipomas, **Kearns–Sayre syndrome** (progressive external ophthalmoplegia, pigmentary retinopathy, cardiac conduction block before age 20 — a pacemaker may be needed years before the other features declare themselves), **Pearson syndrome** (sideroblastic anaemia with pancreatic insufficiency in infancy), Leigh syndrome (which may be mtDNA- or, more often, nuclear-encoded), and mtDNA depletion syndromes from nuclear genes affecting nucleotide pools. Because most paediatric mitochondrial disease is caused by **nuclear** genes, its inheritance is often autosomal recessive with a 1 in 4 recurrence risk — the single most important distinction to make before counselling a family about "mitochondrial disease".

Counselling about reproduction: prenatal diagnosis on villi or amniocytes is technically possible but generally not offered for heteroplasmic variants because the sampled tissue does not predict the child; preimplantation testing is limited for the same reason and by the small amount of DNA available. **Mitochondrial donation** — replacing the mother's faulty mitochondria at the pronuclear or spindle stage, using donor oocyte cytoplasm — has been licensed for clinical use in the United Kingdom since a 2015 regulatory decision, produces children with a tiny percentage of the mother's mtDNA remaining (reversion is reported), and raises germline and identity questions that are part of the consent discussion.

::: example A mother's question {#ex-mt-counsel}
A 29-year-old woman with the m.3243A>G variant (heteroplasmic, 38% in blood, migraine and sensorineural hearing loss; her mother died at 41 with MELAS) asks what the risk is to her future children and whether she can have a test in pregnancy.
::: solution
Her children will all inherit her mtDNA, since fathers do not transmit it, so none of them can be free of the variant by inheritance; what varies is the load, and it varies unpredictably because of the oocyte bottleneck. Her own mildly affected course gives no reliable information: her mother, with the same variant, died of the severe form. Prenatal testing is not recommended, because chorionic-villus or amniotic fluid heteroplasmy correlates poorly with any tissue in the child; likewise preimplantation testing, which can even select embryos on unreliable grounds. What can be offered: a measured baseline (load in a second tissue, e.g. urinary epithelium, for context rather than prediction), a reproductive discussion including oocyte donation and mitochondrial donation where legally available and with regulatory approval, pre-pregnancy assessment of her own diabetes, cardiac, audiological and neurological status (pregnancy can unmask mitochondrial disease), and early paediatric involvement for the infant. Say plainly that "we cannot give you a number", and give the reason; families usually accept uncertainty more easily than a number that later proves wrong.
:::
:::

## Imprinting: parent of origin matters

::: definition Genomic imprinting {#def-imprinting}
At an imprinted locus, one allele is expressed and the other silenced according to **which parent it came from**, through differential DNA methylation established in the gametes and maintained after fertilisation, usually over a domain containing several genes and a non-coding RNA. Consequences: uniparental disomy of an imprinted chromosome is not neutral; a deletion phenotype depends on the parent from which it is inherited; and imprinting disorders may be caused by deletion, by uniparental disomy, by an imprinting-centre defect, or by mutation in a trans-acting epigenetic gene (e.g. *ZFP57* in transient neonatal diabetes).
:::

The clinical archetypes are the reciprocal pair at 15q11-q13: loss of the **paternally** expressed transcripts (*SNRPN*, the SNORD116 cluster) causes **Prader-Willi syndrome**, whereas loss of the **maternally** expressed *UBE3A* in neurons causes **Angelman syndrome** — same interval, opposite parent, opposite disease. At 11p15, gain of the paternal growth-promoting pattern (IC1 gain of methylation, IC2 loss of methylation, or paternal uniparental disomy) causes **Beckwith-Wiedemann spectrum** with its embryonal tumour risk, while reduced *IGF2* activity (IC1 loss of methylation) causes **Silver-Russell syndrome** with growth failure; other imprinting disorders include **transient neonatal diabetes** ( paternal 6q24), **pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1a** (maternally inherited *GNAS* inactivation with Albright osteodystrophy, whereas the same mutation inherited from the father gives little more than hormone-resistant features), **Kagami-Ogata** and **Temple** syndromes (14q32), and **multi-locus imprinting disturbance** in children with more than one imprinting phenotype.

Imprinting is also a tumour mechanism — loss of the normal allele's parent-of-origin copy in retinoblastoma and Wilms tumour — and it explains why a "maternal-effect" family history can appear in the clinic without mitochondrial transmission.

::: example The methylation result that is not a diagnosis {#ex-pws}
A 5-day-old boy with poor feeding, hypotonia and cryptorchidism has an abnormal methylation test at 15q11-q13. His parents ask which "gene" is affected and what the chance is of another affected child.
::: solution
The methylation test shows loss of the paternal methylation pattern — consistent with Prader-Willi syndrome, and abnormal in all four mechanisms that cause it — so it establishes the disorder but not the mechanism, and the mechanism is what determines recurrence. Testing proceeds with a parental-deletion study (FISH or microarray, which identifies a paternal 15q11-q13 deletion in about 70% of cases), and with studies for maternal uniparental disomy (SNP-based) and, if both are negative, for an imprinting-centre defect. Recurrence risks: a paternal deletion carried by the father gives a 50% risk in each of his children, while a *de novo* deletion (most cases, arising on the paternal chromosome in his own conception) carries a low risk; uniparental disomy is nearly always a sporadic event with a negligible recurrence risk; an imprinting-centre microdeletion gives a 50% risk, and a trans-acting defect (*ZFP57*) is recessive with a 1 in 4 risk. Explain to the parents that "your son has Prader-Willi syndrome, and we need one more test to tell you about future pregnancies", and that the same methylation result in a girl with Angelman features means the opposite parental chromosome is involved.
:::
:::

## Repeat expansions and anticipation

More than forty neurological and developmental disorders are caused by the expansion of a short tandem repeat, with five main classes: trinucleotide CAG/CAA coding repeats producing polyglutamine proteins (the spinocerebellar ataxias, Huntington disease, spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy, dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy), non-coding repeats (CTG in the 3′UTR of *DMPK* in myotonic dystrophy, CGG in the 5′UTR of *FMR1* in fragile X, GAA in *FXN* intron 1 in Friedreich ataxia, GGGGCC in *C9orf72* in frontotemporal dementia and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis), and tetranucleotide and pentanucleotide forms.

::: definition Anticipation and instability {#def-anticipation}
**Anticipation** is earlier onset or greater severity in successive generations. Its mechanism is **meiotic instability** of the repeat: an intermediate or premutation-length allele tends to expand when transmitted, so the child's allele is longer than the parent's and the disease starts earlier. Instability is biased by the sex of the transmitting parent — expansions in Huntington disease occur chiefly in male transmission, whereas full mutations in fragile X arise almost exclusively in maternal transmission, and congenital myotonic dystrophy occurs almost only through the mother.
:::

Fragile X illustrates both the mechanism and the ethics of testing. Normal *FMR1* carries about 5–44 CGG repeats; 45–54 is an intermediate "grey zone"; 55–200 is a **premutation**, which is transcribed to excess mRNA and causes a different disease from the full mutation — **fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome** in older carriers (usually male) and **primary ovarian insufficiency** in about one in five carrier women; above 200 the promoter is methylated and the gene is silenced, giving **fragile X syndrome**: the commonest inherited cause of intellectual disability, with long face, prominent ears, macro-orchidism, anxiety and autism-spectrum features, and mitral valve prolapse. Because the phenotype depends on expansion size, one family may contain a grandfather with tremor, a granddaughter with learning difficulty, a carrier mother with early menopause and a woman with no symptoms whose repeat length is unknown. Testing therefore has to be sized and reported as a number with the interpretation attached, and cascade testing of extended family members is where the clinical value lies.

::: example Three generations, three different diseases {#ex-fxr}
A 7-year-old boy has learning difficulties, anxiety, hand-flapping, prominent ears and macro-orchidism. His 62-year-old maternal grandfather has a progressive action tremor, gait ataxia and memory decline, and his 34-year-old mother had a hysterectomy at 39 for "early menopause". Testing of the boy shows 420 CGG repeats with methylation at *FMR1*.
::: solution
The boy has **fragile X syndrome** from a fully methylated expansion that passed through the maternal line: his mother carries a premutation (by inference: her brother's or her own repeat needs measuring), and expansion to a full mutation occurs in maternal transmission, the risk of a full mutation rising steeply with the mother's repeat length (a maternal allele above about 90 repeats almost always expands to over 200). The grandfather's tremor and ataxia is **fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome**, caused by the premutation through RNA gain of function, and the mother's early ovarian failure is **premutation-associated primary ovarian insufficiency** — the same locus producing three mechanistically distinct diseases at three repeat sizes. Actions follow from the mechanism: measure the mother's repeat number and methylation, test her sibs and her mother (an intermediate or premutation allele in a grandmother identifies further at-risk pregnancies), offer the mother's daughters and their relatives counselling about ovarian reserve and about having affected children, discuss that a full-mutation male relative's sisters may be mildly affected carriers, and note that the boy's management needs educational support, behavioural treatment for anxiety and autism-spectrum features, and cardiac and orthopaedic review rather than a disease-modifying drug. Because testing has implications across three generations, results are disclosed with the family's agreement and with the parents' consent for the child, and the report should be written for the family as well as for the clinician.
:::

## Mosaicism

::: definition Mosaicism {#def-mosaicism}
**Mosaicism** is the presence of two or more genetically distinct cell lines within one individual. **Somatic mosaicism** arises from a post-zygotic mutation and its extent depends on the developmental stage and lineage at which it occurred; **germ-line (gonadal) mosaicism** is confined wholly or largely to germ cells, so a parent with two normal-looking genomes in blood can have several affected children; and **gonosomal mosaicism** involves both. A mosaic individual may have a phenotype restricted to the affected lineage: segmental skin disease following Blaschko's lines, unilateral cataract, isolated hemihyperplasia, or a brain malformation in one hemisphere.
:::

Three clinical forms matter. **Rasopathy and PIK3CA-related overgrowth**, and **McCune-Albright syndrome** (post-zygotic activating *GNAS* mutation, with polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait macules with jagged "coast of Maine" edges that respect the midline, precocious puberty and other endocrine overactivity) are mosaic by necessity, since the same mutation in the germline would be lethal. **Recurrent recurrence after an apparently de novo dominant variant** — the osteogenesis imperfecta and achondroplasia families with two affected children of unaffected parents — is germ-line mosaicism, and it is the reason a quoted "less than 1%" recurrence risk should be given as a range with the mechanism named. **Detection** depends on the tissue and the depth: a variant at 8% in skin fibroblasts may be absent in blood, so targeted deep sequencing (or, for copy number, a higher-density array or microscopy) of an affected tissue is a diagnostic step rather than a last resort, and a "negative" blood result should never be reported to a family as excluding mosaicism.

::: example A rash along a line {#ex-mosaic}
A 6-year-old has linear verrucous skin lesions following the lines of Blaschko on the left leg since infancy, and now has epilepsy with an MRI showing a focal cortical dysplasia in the left frontal lobe. Skin biopsy of normal-looking abdomen shows no variant on standard sequencing.
::: solution
Both lesions are consistent with post-zygotic activation of a growth-signalling gene — the pattern of epidermal nevus syndromes such as those caused by mosaic *PIK3CA*, *HRAS*, *AKT3* or *KRAS*, in which the same variant is present in brain and skin but at low allele fraction and never in blood. The negative standard test on distant skin does not exclude the diagnosis; the right sample is the affected tissue (lesional skin, or the resected brain if surgery is undertaken) and the right method is deep targeted sequencing able to detect a few per cent of reads. Management is symptomatic and anticipatory: seizure control and referral for epilepsy surgery if the dysplasia is resectable and concordant with the semiology, developmental support, dermatological and orthopaedic review, and surveillance for the associated features of the relevant syndrome (including tumour risks in some). Recurrence risk for the parents is negligible, and the child's own reproductive risk is low and lineage-dependent — an honest answer that requires the mechanism to be stated rather than the pedigree to be read.
:::
:::

::: warning A negative test in blood does not exclude mosaicism, uniparental disomy, or an imprinting defect
The three commonest reasons for a falsely reassuring "normal" result are all sampling problems. A mosaic variant may be present at 5% in skin and undetectable in leucocytes, and standard Sanger or exome pipelines discard low-fraction calls. Uniparental disomy leaves the copy number normal and is invisible to microarray unless the platform reports heterozygosity or the analysis looks for it; segmental UPD masquerades as loss of heterozygosity from a deletion. An imprinting defect can be missed when only methylation at one locus is tested, and methylation results can be partially normal in mosaic imprinting disorders. So the question before ordering is: which tissue, which mechanism, and can this assay see it at the level I need?
:::

::: quiz
A woman has a brother with myotonic dystrophy type 1 and a 45 CTG repeat expansion in the premutation range. She is asymptomatic at 35. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] Because she has no symptoms, her repeat cannot have expanded and her children are not at risk.
- [x] She may carry a normal or intermediate allele, or a premutation-length allele that could expand on maternal transmission to give a congenital or early-onset form in her child.
- [ ] Her risk is 1 in 2 of being affected but her children are not at risk.
- [ ] Anticipation in myotonic dystrophy occurs only through the father.
::: solution
Myotonic dystrophy is autosomal dominant with anticipation, and the congenital form arises almost exclusively through maternal transmission of a markedly expanded allele. An asymptomatic 35-year-old may have a repeat in the normal range, in the mutable intermediate range or, less commonly, a low-level expansion with no symptoms yet; a repeat-sizing test (not a panel or exome) answers this, and expansion risk in her oocytes must be discussed if she is a carrier. Testing her father's side matters too, since the expansion in her brother may have come from either parent and other relatives may be at risk.
:::
:::

::: history
The idea that an individual could be a genetic mosaic goes back to Theodor Boveri in the early 1900s. Mitochondrial inheritance entered medicine in 1988, when Ian Wallace and colleagues identified the m.11778G>A variant of Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and showed that a maternally transmitted mitochondrial DNA mutation causes human disease; the transfer-RNA syndromes MELAS and MERRF followed, and heteroplasmy and the oocyte bottleneck were worked out in the 1990s. Genomic imprinting was established in the 1980s and 1990s from mouse work by Marion Weiss, Azim Surani, Davor Solter and others, with the human counterpart seen in David Ledbetter's and Raphael Matalon's groups' reports of uniparental disomy and in the 1989–1993 demonstration that 15q11-q13 deletions cause Prader-Willi or Angelman syndrome depending on the parent of origin — an explanation of two clinical entities that had been described by Prader, Labhart and Willi in 1956 and by Harry Angelman in 1965. The repeat-expansion era opened with the fragile X CGG repeat in 1991 and myotonic dystrophy's CTG in 1992 and Huntington disease's CAG in 1993, and it explained anticipation, an observation made in families of affected people since the 1920s, as a molecular property of DNA polymerase slippage and repair.
:::

## Where this leads

The machinery behind the exceptions is in [[cell-biochemistry/gene-expression]] (replication slippage, DNA methylation, chromatin) and [[cell-biochemistry/signalling-cell-cycle]] (growth-pathway mosaics). Pedigree-based risk arithmetic is in [[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]; the laboratory routes to these diagnoses in [[genetics/genetic-testing]]; tumour consequences of UPD and imprinting in [[genetics/cancer-genetics]].

::: summary
- Mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited, heteroplasmic and threshold-dependent; the oocyte bottleneck makes load, and therefore severity, unpredictable between relatives, and blood load is a poor proxy for post-mitotic tissue.
- Most mitochondrial disease in children is nuclear-encoded, hence autosomal recessive with a 1 in 4 recurrence risk — establish which form a family has before counselling.
- Imprinted loci are expressed according to parent of origin, so uniparental disomy, deletion laterality and imprinting-centre defects all produce disease; Prader-Willi and Angelman are the same interval viewed from opposite parents.
- Repeat expansion causes anticipation, with parent-of-origin bias for the sex in which the repeat expands; the same locus produces different diseases at different repeat lengths (premutation, full mutation).
- Mosaicism explains "sporadic" dominant disease with recurrence, segmental skin and brain disease, and much of the gap between phenotype and blood testing; diagnose on the affected tissue with a deep assay.
- Anticipation, imprinting and mosaicism are not curiosities: each changes the recurrence risk quoted, the test ordered and the tissue sent.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Pedigree of a mitochondrial disease {level=1}
An affected man with the m.3243A>G variant has three children with an unaffected woman. What is the risk that his children or their children are affected, and why?
::: solution
None of his children inherits mtDNA from him, because mitochondria come from the oocyte; their children are therefore also not at risk through this line. His sisters, if they carry the variant, may transmit it, and his mother's family needs to be traced. This is the one situation in which the pedigree gives a clean answer, and it is worth saying to the family in those words.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why the mother's test failed {level=1}
A girl with clinical Kearns-Sayre syndrome has a normal blood mtDNA analysis. Explain, and say what to do.
::: solution
Single large-scale mtDNA deletions are typically present at low or undetectable level in leucocytes because cells carrying a large deletion are selected against during mitotic division, whereas post-mitotic tissue retains it. Test a urinary epithelial sample (a "buccal-urine" DNA pair is standard), and if still negative, muscle, for the deletion by long-range PCR or Southern blot; a muscle biopsy may also show ragged-red and COI-negative fibres. Most single deletions are sporadic, with a low recurrence risk for the mother's future children, so the answer also settles the reproductive question.
:::
:::

::: exercise Repeat size and risk {level=2}
A man has 41 CAG repeats in *HTT*; his father developed Huntington disease at 60. What is the significance of 41 repeats, and what is the risk to his children?
::: solution
Normal *HTT* alleles carry up to about 26 repeats, 27–35 are intermediate (not associated with disease but unstable in male transmission), 36–39 are reduced-penetrance and 40 or more are full penetrance. At 41 repeats he will, if he lives long enough, develop disease, with an expected onset in late adult life; each child has a 1 in 2 risk of inheriting the allele, and expansion is more likely during spermatogenesis, so a child inheriting his allele may have a longer repeat and an earlier onset than he did. Predictive testing of minors for an adult-onset condition is not offered; the discussion is with him, including reproductive options — prenatal diagnosis, preimplantation genetic testing with exclusion approaches when the at-risk parent does not want to know their own status, and the choice of no testing — with counselling and support for each.
:::
:::

::: exercise Mosaicism in practice {level=2}
A boy with a clinical diagnosis of neurofibromatosis type 1 has café-au-lait macules, freckling and a plexiform neurofibroma confined to one limb, and no family history; standard sequencing of blood is negative for *NF1* variants. What is the explanation, and how do you test and counsel?
::: solution
Segmental (type V) neurofibromatosis is caused by a post-zygotic somatic *NF1* mutation restricted to the affected lineage, so leucocyte DNA is negative and the diagnosis is made clinically, with molecular confirmation only if affected skin or a neurofibroma is sequenced deeply. Recurrence risk for his own children is not the 1 in 2 of classic NF1 but is not zero either, because gonadal involvement may coexist; the practical advice is a low single-figure risk, examination of his parents and siblings for the milder features that would indicate an inherited variant, and referral for the surveillance that segmental disease still partly requires (blood pressure, ophthalmology, and vigilance for the tumours of NF1 in the affected region).
:::
:::

::: exercise Two mechanisms, one result {level=3}
In a girl with Angelman syndrome the methylation test is abnormal, microarray shows no deletion, and testing of parental DNA shows two maternal alleles and no paternal allele at 15q11-q13. State the mechanism, the recurrence risk, and the two things you must still check.
::: solution
The mechanism is **maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15** (or segmental UPD): she has two maternal copies of the region, hence no paternal *UBE3A*-expressing chromosome and loss of the paternally expressed transcripts, giving Angelman syndrome. Recurrence risk after uniparental disomy is very low, since the mechanism is a sporadic trisomy-rescue or monosomy-rescue event; but a parental Robertsonian translocation or a mitotically active meiotic error must be excluded on the parents' karyotypes, and the mechanism confirmed on a second method (SNP-based copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity or microsatellite analysis), because a small deletion or an imprinting-centre defect can mimic UPD if only methylation is examined. The phenotype correlates with mechanism — deletions usually give the most severe course and UPD a somewhat milder one — which is why the answer to "how will she do?" depends on the mechanism you have established.
:::
:::

::: exercise Epigenetics and the family {level=3}
A child has transient neonatal diabetes mellitus with hypertriglyceridaemia and macroglossia; methylation testing shows loss of maternal methylation at the 6q24 PLAGL1/HYMAI imprinting control region. His mother has type 2 diabetes diagnosed at 30 and a history of large-for-gestational-age babies. Explain the family picture and the reproductive implications.
::: solution
Transient neonatal diabetes results from overexpression of the paternally expressed *PLAGL1*/*HYMAI* locus, and one mechanism is maternal hypomethylation of the imprinted control region, which allows paternal-pattern expression from the maternal allele. A maternal *ZFP57* variant — a transcription factor needed to maintain imprinting in the early embryo — can cause the same hypomethylation and, being recessive, recurs in sibs with a 1 in 4 risk; maternal *ZFP57* carriers also show a tendency to gestational diabetes and macrosomic babies, which is consistent with the mother's history, and *ZFP57*-related cases often show hypomethylation at more than one imprinted locus. So: test the imprinted loci genome-wide (multi-locus imprinting disturbance), sequence *ZFP57*, examine the father's methylation and, if the pattern fits, offer the parents carrier-informed counselling — the recurrence risk is driven by whether this is a *ZFP57* recessive effect, an imprinting-centre defect, or a sporadic epimutation.
:::
:::

::: exercise Design a study {level=3}
A 10-year-old with hemimegalencephaly and intractable epilepsy has a normal brain MRI-confirmed unilateral malformation; blood exome is non-diagnostic, and the surgical specimen is available. Describe the investigation and the interpretive limits.
::: solution
Sequence the resected tissue and, if available, skin fibroblasts from the affected side, at depth (targeted panel or whole-exome on the tissue) and compare allele fractions with blood to distinguish post-zygotic mosaicism from a germline variant; look also for copy-number change and for the mechanistic target pathways (mTOR-pathway genes, *PIK3CA*, *AKT3*, *PTEN*, *MTOR*, *DEPDC5*), with immunohistochemistry for pathway activation as supportive evidence where sequencing is negative. Interpretive limits: a variant at a few per cent may be missed in a low-purity sample and a variant at high fraction may reflect selection in the specimen rather than the causal event; a variant found in brain is causal only if it is a known or plausibly pathogenic gain-of-function change in a gene whose mosaic phenotype matches, which requires functional or database support and, ideally, demonstration in microdissected lesional tissue. And the clinical use of the result is real: mTOR-pathway mosaicism can change surgical and medical management (mTOR inhibitors are used in some tuberous-complex and related presentations), and it establishes that the recurrence risk for the family is negligible rather than 25%.
:::
:::

::: exercise Nuclear or mitochondrial? {level=2}
Two children in the same family, a brother and a sister, develop a Leigh-like syndrome with lactic acidosis and basal-ganglia change; their mother is well and their parents are first cousins. mtDNA sequencing of blood shows no pathogenic variant. What inheritance is most likely, what does it mean for the parents, and what testing is indicated?
::: solution
Two affected sibs of both sexes, healthy parents and consanguinity point to an **autosomal recessive nuclear** mitochondrial disorder, and the negative mtDNA sequencing is consistent rather than surprising, since most childhood mitochondrial disease is nuclear-encoded — complexes I, II, III, IV or V assembly and translation factors (e.g. *SURF1* in Leigh syndrome with cytochrome c oxidase deficiency), CoQ10 and lipoic acid pathways, mitochondrial DNA maintenance and depletion syndromes (*TK2*, *DGUOK*, *POLG*), and pyruvate dehydrogenase complex genes, of which *PDHA1* is X-linked and can look mitochondrial. Each future pregnancy of this couple carries a 1 in 4 risk, and the extended family needs the same counselling. Testing: respiratory-chain enzyme activities in muscle and fibroblasts, mtDNA copy number and deletion analysis, and nuclear sequencing — a targeted mitochondrial gene panel or, preferably, exome/genome sequencing with homozygosity mapping given the consanguinity — with muscle biopsy if the sequencing is unrevealing. Establishing the gene matters because a few of these have treatment (riboflavin-responsive complex I deficiency, CoQ10, biotin for holocarboxylase, thiamine for PDH deficiency with a ketogenic diet) and because liver or other transplantation has been done in selected depletion syndromes.
:::
:::
