Chromosome disorders are commoner than most students assume and stranger than the diagrams suggest. About 1 in 150 live births has a chromosome abnormality if the sex-chromosome aneuploidies are counted; roughly half of all first-trimester miscarriages carry one; and yet the vast majority of aneuploid conceptions never reach term. Between the zygote and the clinic there is therefore a filter of lethal dosage imbalance, which is why the surviving trisomies are the small ones, why an unbalanced translocation may present as recurrent miscarriage rather than as a child, and why a "high-risk" screening result in a low-risk woman is more likely to be wrong than right.

This chapter covers numerical and structural chromosome disorders: what they look like, how they arose, what the tests can and cannot say, and what follows for the patient and the family.

## Numbers first

::: definition Aneuploidy and polyploidy {#def-aneuploidy}
**Aneuploidy** is an abnormal number of individual chromosomes: **trisomy** (three copies, e.g. 47,XX,+21), **monosomy** (one copy, e.g. 45,X). **Polyploidy** is a whole extra set — triploidy 69,XXX/XXY/YYY and tetraploidy 92,XXXX — usually from two sperm entering one ovum or from a failed division; triploidy is common in miscarriage and, when a pregnancy continues, lethal or near-lethal, with a partial molar phenotype when the extra set is paternal.
:::

Approximate frequencies, worth knowing because they drive counselling: Down syndrome about 1 in 700 births at large (higher with advancing maternal age: roughly 1 in 1,000 at age 25, 1 in 350 at 35, 1 in 30 at 42); trisomy 18 about 1 in 5,000; trisomy 13 about 1 in 12,000; a sex-chromosome aneuploidy together about 1 in 500 births. Fewer than 1% of babies with trisomy 21 have a chromosome 21 abnormality visible on karyotype other than free trisomy — the rest are translocation or mosaic.

::: theorem Dosage decides survival {#thm-dosage}
Whether an aneuploidy reaches term depends on the number and content of the extra or missing genes, not on the identity of the chromosome: trisomy for a small, gene-poor chromosome is compatible with live birth, trisomy for a large gene-rich one is not, and sex-chromosome aneuploidy is the most tolerated of all.
:::

::: proof
Every chromosome carries genes whose products must be present in the ratio they have evolved to maintain; an extra copy raises expression by about half for each dose-sensitive gene on it, and many such products work in complexes, on membranes or as regulatory RNAs, where imbalance is itself deleterious (the "gene-balance" constraint: stoichiometry matters as much as quantity). Chromosome 21 is the smallest human autosome and comparatively gene-poor, and trisomy 13 and 18 are intermediate — which is why those three, and they alone among the autosomes, are seen at term with any regularity, whereas trisomy 16, the commonest trisomy in miscarriage, is lethal by the end of the first trimester. Monosomy is worse still, since no second copy buffers the loss; autosomal monosomy is essentially never seen in a liveborn child. The sex chromosomes escape most of this: an extra X is inactivated (a Barr body forms), leaving only the pseudoautosomal and escape genes overdosed, which is why 47,XXX, 47,XXY and 47,XYY have mild and variable phenotypes, and why the Turner phenotype depends on the *lack* of a second sex chromosome, that is, on haploinsufficiency for the genes that normally escape inactivation. The same logic explains why a partial trisomy may be compatible with life when the whole-chromosome trisomy is not, and why microdeletion and microduplication syndromes are, in essence, controlled experiments in dosage of a few genes.
:::

## Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome)

The phenotype is recognisable but variable: hypotonia and joint laxity in the newborn, flat facial profile with upslanting palpebral fissures, single palmar crease, sandal gap, Brushfield spots, short stature and brachycephaly. Congenital heart disease affects about half — complete atrioventricular septal defect being characteristic, with ventricular septal defect and Fallot tetralogy also common. Also: duodenal atresia ("double bubble", sometimes with annular pancreas), Hirschsprung disease, oesophageal and duodenal atresia, hypothyroidism and coeliac disease, cataracts and refractive errors, atlantoaxial instability (relevant before anaesthesia and sport), obstructive sleep apnoea, hearing loss, primary immunodeficiency with recurrent chest infection, and a substantially raised risk of leukaemia in childhood (acute megakaryoblastic leukaemia, preceded by transient abnormal myelopoiesis in the newborn) and of Alzheimer disease with amyloid angiopathy in middle age, the extra *APP* gene on chromosome 21 doing what the gene does.

Cognition spans a wide range, and the difference between children is larger than the difference between trisomy and mosaic trisomy; early intervention, hearing and vision correction, thyroid surveillance and cardiac repair change what the outcome is. Life expectancy in high-income countries now reaches the sixties, with the disabilities of ageing, particularly Alzheimer disease, becoming the dominant clinical problem.

The mechanism in 90–95% of cases is maternal meiotic nondisjunction, which is why the association with maternal age exists; 3–4% are paternal or post-zygotic, 3–4% arise from a Robertsonian translocation, and 1–2% are mosaic.

::: example A high-risk screening result in a 32-year-old {#ex-cri}
A woman at 12+2 weeks with no family history has an apparently normal ultrasound and a cell-free DNA test reported as "high risk for trisomy 21". She asks whether the baby has Down syndrome.
::: solution
No — and the answer is arithmetic rather than reassurance. Cell-free DNA testing has a sensitivity for trisomy 21 above 99% and a false-positive rate around 0.05–0.1%, but its **positive predictive value depends on prevalence**. With a prior risk at age 32 of about 1 in 300 (0.33%), a "high risk" result gives a posterior probability of roughly $0.99 \times 0.0033 / (0.99 \times 0.0033 + 0.001 \times 0.9967) \approx 77\%$; at age 40 with a similar prior, more than 99%. So the result moves the probability a great deal but does not establish the diagnosis, and the reasons it can be wrong are biological, not technical: confined placental mosaicism (the DNA is trophoblastic, not fetal), a vanishing or affected co-twin, maternal copy-number change or confined mosaicism in the mother, maternal neoplasia, and low fetal fraction causing failure rather than a false positive. The next step is a diagnostic test — chorion villus sampling from 11 weeks or amniocentesis after 15 weeks — with quantitative fluorescence or microarray on uncultured cells as well as culture, because rapid tests can detect the common aneuploidies while a low-level mosaic is only visible after counting cells, and only cytogenetics or microarray will identify a Robertsonian translocation, which is what the parents' own risk depends on. Never terminate on a screening result; and offer the same counselling in reverse, because a "low-risk" result on cell-free DNA is not a normal karyotype.
:::
:::

## Trisomy 18 and trisomy 13

Both are severe, and both are now encountered as continuing pregnancies because screening is optional and because intensive care decisions are made with parents rather than unilaterally.

**Trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome)**: growth restriction, prominent occiput, micrognathia, clenched hands with the index finger overlapping the fifth, rocker-bottom feet, camptodactyly, choroid plexus cysts, oesophageal and umbilical abnormalities, and complex cardiac defects in most. Median survival is days to weeks; about 5–10% survive beyond a year, with severe disability. **Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome)**: holoprosencephaly and its spectrum (from alobar holoprosencephaly to isolated hypotelorism), cleft lip and palate, microphthalmia and coloboma, postaxial polydactyly, scalp defects (aplasia cutis), renal anomalies, cardiac defects and ambiguous genitalia; survival is similarly limited.

The clinical implication is that the diagnosis changes management planning — place of birth, whether cardiac surgery is offered, palliative and neonatal pathways, feeding and respiratory support, and bereavement and genetic follow-up — and that these are decisions to make with parents in advance, on the specific findings (which organ, which defect, which diagnosis) rather than on the chromosome alone. Fetal akinesia, hydrops and severe cardiac disease may make early delivery appropriate; a normal head and heart in mosaic trisomy 18, by contrast, is compatible with longer survival, and mosaicism detected only on chorion villus sampling needs amniocentesis to interpret.

## Sex chromosome aneuploidy

These are the commonest chromosome anomalies at birth, and the most under-diagnosed in adults, because the phenotype is often subtle and the karyotype sometimes reported years later.

::: example The girl with no periods and a short stature {#ex-turner}
A 15-year-old, height on the 3rd centile since childhood, has had no menarche. Examination shows a broad chest with widely spaced nipples, low posterior hairline, cubitus valgus and bilateral non-pitting ankle oedema. Bone age is delayed; FSH and LH are raised and oestradiol low.
::: solution
This is Turner syndrome, from complete or mosaic monosomy X; the lymphoedema reflects abnormal development of lymphatics in fetal life, and the ovarian failure (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism) causes absent puberty and short stature, the latter largely through haploinsufficiency of *SHOX* on the pseudoautosomal region, which is why growth hormone is started early and why oestrogen–progestogen replacement is given for pubertal development and bone health. Investigate the associated lesions before they present: echocardiography and cardiac and aortic MRI for bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation (with aortic dissection risk in adulthood, so pregnancy carries substantial risk and needs specialist assessment), renal ultrasound for horseshoe kidney, thyroid function and coeliac serology, audiology, and ophthalmology. **Look for Y-chromosome material** by PCR or FISH if there is any virilisation, any Y-derived marker chromosome, or a 45,X/46,XY mosaicism: the risk of gonadoblastoma then justifies gonadectomy. Fertility may be possible with oocyte donation, and cartilage and hearing problems, diabetes and hypertension need lifelong attention. If a 45,X cell line is found prenatally, the difference between a fetal 45,X, mosaic 45,X/46,XX, and a male with 45,X/46,XY disorder of sex development is significant and cannot be resolved on chorion villus sampling alone.
:::
:::

**47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome)**, present in about 1 in 600 males, is frequently not diagnosed until fertility assessment: tall stature with long limbs, small firm testes, gynaecomastia, sparse body hair, reduced virilisation, azoospermia (with sperm retrievable by microdissection testicular sperm extraction in a majority, and intracytoplasmic injection possible), and raised risks of breast cancer, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, osteoporosis, venous thromboembolism, autoimmune disease and anxiety or language-based learning difficulties. Testosterone replacement improves virilisation, mood and bone density but not fertility, and the diagnosis matters because it changes surveillance and spares men years of unexplained fatigue and shame. **47,XYY** (about 1 in 1,000 males) and **47,XXX** (1 in 1,000 females) are often undiagnosed; tall stature, learning difficulties (particularly speech and language), hypotonia and increased risk of autism-spectrum and behavioural problems are described, and the outdated association of XYY with criminality was an artefact of ascertainment in institutions and should be named as such if raised. Prenatal diagnosis of sex-chromosome aneuploidy is the situation in which counselling most often outstrips the evidence, and the reason many services now advise that incidental sex-chromosome findings on cell-free DNA need not be reported unless requested.

## Structural disorders

::: definition Types of structural change {#def-structural}
**Deletion**: interstitial (within an arm) or terminal; if small, a **microdeletion** detectable only by microarray or targeted testing.
**Duplication**: often the reciprocal product of the same unequal meiotic exchange that made a sibling's deletion.
**Inversion**: paracentric (one arm) or pericentric (including the centromere); usually phenotypically balanced, but a carrier can produce gametes with deletions and duplications after a crossover inside the inverted segment, and one breakpoint can disrupt a gene.
**Translocation**: reciprocal (exchange between non-homologues) or Robertsonian (whole-arm fusion of two acrocentrics, e.g. 45,XX,der(14;21)).
**Ring chromosome**: breakage at both ends with fusion, unstable in mitosis, which is why a ring can give mosaicism and a phenotype worse than the deleted material predicts (ring 20 with refractory epilepsy is a familiar example).
**Isochromosome**: loss of one arm with duplication of the other, e.g. isochromosome Xq, a common cause of Turner features with short stature and gonadal failure.
**Marker or supernumerary chromosome**: an extra structurally abnormal chromosome; a bisatellited inv(15) marker causes the inv(15) duplication syndrome with hypotonia, epilepsy and autism.
:::

The **deletion syndromes** are recognisable because dosage of a few genes in a critical region is enough: 5p− (cri-du-chat: cat-like cry, microcephaly, severe learning difficulty); 4p− (Wolf–Hirschhorn: "Greek helmet" facies, clefts, seizures); 1p36 deletion (the commonest terminal deletion — severe delay, cardiomyopathy, hearing and vision loss); 7q11.23 (**Williams syndrome**, including *ELN*: supravalvular aortic and pulmonary stenosis, infantile hypercalcaemia, overfriendly personality with strong verbal ability against visuospatial difficulty, and connective-tissue features); 22q11.2 (**22q11.2 deletion syndrome**, see below); 11p13 (WAGR: *WT1*/*PAX6* with Wilms tumour risk, which is why the deletion, not just the gene, triggers surveillance); 15q24, 1q21.1, 16p12.1 and many other recurrent intervals with variable expressivity.

::: example The baby with a seizure and a low calcium {#ex-22q11}
A term baby, day two, has a generalised seizure; calcium is 1.5 mmol/L (ionised 0.68 mmol/L) with a low parathyroid hormone, and the newborn examination reveals a short conotruncal murmur. Later, feeding difficulty with nasal regurgitation is noted and a subtle palatal abnormality is found on endoscopy.
::: solution
Hypocalcaemia from parathyroid hypoplasia, a conotruncal cardiac defect and palatal dysfunction are the triad that should prompt testing for the 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge/velo-cardio-facial syndrome, *TBX1* among the critical genes) — by microarray or targeted FISH, since the deletion is below the resolution of a routine karyotype in most cases. The syndrome is the classic illustration of both variable expressivity and of what else to look for: thymic hypoplasia with T-cell lymphopenia (check subsets before live vaccines and before blood products that are not irradiated), renal anomalies, learning difficulties particularly in arithmetic, psychiatric disease in adolescence and adulthood (schizophrenia in about a quarter of adults), palatal surgery needs, speech and feeding support, and anaesthetic airway considerations. Inheritance is usually autosomal dominant with most cases *de novo*; if a parent is found to carry the deletion — and one in ten or so cases has an affected parent, often mildly so — the recurrence risk is 50%, so the parents need testing and any affected adult relative needs the cardiac, psychiatric and metabolic follow-up they may never have had.
:::
:::

**Unbalanced products of a parental translocation** typically produce a child with developmental delay plus focal structural change: microcephaly or macrocephaly, growth failure, dysmorphism, and cardiac, renal and brain malformations depending on the segments gained or lost. A partial trisomy of the same segment may show no structural abnormality but present as severe intellectual disability — the deletion phenotype is usually, but not always, more severe than the corresponding duplication because there is no second copy at all.

**Balanced rearrangements** in a carrier are usually harmless, with three exceptions worth remembering: a breakpoint that disrupts a gene (the classic examples of *DMD* and *RS1* disruptions by X-chromosome breakpoints, and unmasking of a recessive allele on the other chromosome); a position effect that moves a gene away from its regulatory environment; and the reproductive consequences of quadrivalent segregation described in [[genetics/genome-chromosomes]].

## Mechanism determines recurrence

| Finding | Usual mechanism | Recurrence risk after a de novo case |
|---|---|---|
| Free trisomy 21 | Maternal meiotic nondisjunction | About 1% (allowing germ-line mosaicism), adjusted for maternal age |
| Trisomy 21 from der(14;21) inherited from mother | Robertsonian carrier | ~10–15% (paternal carrier: a few per cent) |
| Trisomy 21 from t(21;21) | Carrier of 21q21q fusion | 100% of viable conceptions affected |
| 22q11.2, Williams, 1p36 deletion | Usually de novo NAHR or repair error | ~1% if truly de novo and germline tested; 50% if a parent carries it |
| Balanced reciprocal translocation | Familial or de novo | High for miscarriage and for an unbalanced child (a few to ~20%) |
| Mosaic trisomy in the child | Post-zygotic mitotic error | Low, but the phenotype is unpredictable |
| Sex-chromosome aneuploidy | Nondisjunction, often paternal meiosis I for XXY | Generally low |

::: example A "normal" karyotype with a microarray deletion {#ex-microarray}
A 4-year-old with global delay, behavioural problems, recurrent otitis media and a repaired ventricular septal defect has a normal karyotype. Microarray reports a 1.8 Mb deletion at 22q11.2, and both parents' studies are normal. What can you tell the family?
::: solution
The deletion is *de novo*, so the recurrence risk for the parents is low — around 1%, covering the possibility of germ-line mosaicism — but it is not zero, and that number is what makes the "can it happen again?" conversation honest rather than reassuring and false. It also explains the family's other questions: the phenotype is variable even within a family, so the older sister's mild speech difficulty and the parent's own subtle findings (if any) should be looked for deliberately rather than attributed to chance; the diagnosis carries surveillance obligations (cardiac and palatal assessment, immune subsets before live vaccines, thyroid and calcium, renal imaging, developmental and psychiatric follow-up into adolescence, and hearing); and it should not be used to predict this child's abilities. Explain, in words the family will still understand later, that a copy-number result is a finding whose meaning changes with time: reanalysis of the same data may upgrade or downgrade the interpretation, and that is the laboratory being careful rather than having made a mistake.
:::
:::

::: warning Cell-free DNA screening and the decisions it provokes
Cell-free DNA is a screening test with a high negative predictive value and a positive predictive value that depends on the patient's prior risk, and it is not a diagnostic test, not a test for the fetus when the placenta is mosaic, and not validated for every condition people hope it covers. No decision that cannot be reversed — and in particular no termination — should be taken on a screening result alone; the requirement is a diagnostic sample with cytogenetic or microarray study, and counselling that names the conditions that make a result discordant. The same discipline applies to the corollary: a low-risk cell-free DNA result does not exclude a chromosome abnormality, a microdeletion or a single-gene disorder, and should never be described to a patient as "the baby's chromosomes are normal".
:::

::: quiz
A girl has features of Turner syndrome; her karyotype is 46,X,i(Xq). What is the mechanism?
- [ ] Nondisjunction in maternal meiosis I.
- [x] Transverse misdivision of the centromere (or breakage and fusion), leaving two long arms of one X chromosome and loss of the short arm.
- [ ] A Robertsonian translocation.
- [ ] Post-zygotic mitotic nondisjunction.
::: solution
An isochromosome forms when the centromere divides horizontally rather than vertically, so the daughter cell inherits two copies of one arm and none of the other; in 46,X,i(Xq) there is haploinsufficiency for short-arm genes including *SHOX* (short stature, Turner habitus) and one copy of the long arm. Fertility is uncommon but pregnancy with donated oocytes may be possible, and the cardiovascular risk is the same as in Turner syndrome, which is why the same surveillance applies.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1959, three months after Tjio and Levan fixed the human chromosome number, Jérôme Lejeune, Paul Michel and Raymond Turpin reported trisomy 21 in Down syndrome and, almost simultaneously, Claire Jacobs and John Strong in Edinburgh reported an extra X chromosome in Klinefelter syndrome and a missing one in Turner syndrome; within two years trisomy 13 and 18 had been described, and the deletion syndromes followed as banding made chromosomes individually identifiable. DNA studies from the 1980s showed that in most 45,X conceptions the single X chromosome is maternal, the sex chromosome having been lost from the paternal gamete, and work on nondisjunction and uniparental disomy began to explain rare recessive disease in the child of one carrier parent. The microdeletion era opened with high-resolution banding in the 1980s and closed the gap between description and mechanism once fluorescence in-situ hybridisation and then comparative genomic hybridisation (described in 1992) and its microarray form made the "subtle" syndromes testable; in 2010 an expert consensus recommended chromosomal microarray as the first-tier test for unexplained developmental delay and multiple congenital anomalies. In 1997 Dennis Lo reported fetal DNA in maternal plasma, and by 2011 non-invasive prenatal testing for the common aneuploidies was in clinical use — an advance that has improved screening and, at the same time, made the interpretation of discordant results the central skill of prenatal genetic counselling.
:::

## Where this leads

The mechanisms behind these findings — nondisjunction, NAHR, UPD, mosaicism — are set out in [[genetics/genome-chromosomes]]; imprinting and repeat disorders in [[genetics/non-mendelian-inheritance]]; inheritance patterns and recurrence arithmetic in [[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]]; how the laboratory tests differ in [[genetics/genetic-testing]]; the syndromic faces in childhood in [[paediatrics/congenital-genetic]].

::: summary
- Aneuploidy is common at conception and mostly lethal: the surviving trisomies are those with the least gene content, and the filter is why prenatal risk is quoted as probability rather than certainty.
- Trisomy 21 has a recognizable, variable phenotype with cardiac, gastrointestinal, endocrine, haematological, immune, airway and, later, neurodegenerative complications; its management is surveillance, and its mechanism determines recurrence risk.
- Trisomy 18 and 13 are usually compatible only with short survival; the prenatal diagnosis changes the plan of care and must be discussed with parents, not decided for them.
- Sex-chromosome aneuploidies are the commonest and most often missed: Turner syndrome needs cardiac, renal, endocrine, hearing and gonadal surveillance and a search for Y material, and Klinefelter syndrome is a diagnosis worth making in adulthood.
- Structural disorders are deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations, rings, isochromosomes and markers; a balanced change in a parent explains recurrent miscarriage and unbalanced children.
- Recurrence risk depends on mechanism — free trisomy about 1%; a maternal Robertsonian carrier 10–15%; t(21;21) 100%; an inherited microdeletion 50% — so mechanism, not the label, drives counselling.
- Karyotype, microarray and sequencing have different resolutions; cell-free DNA is screening; and every result should be reported to the patient with its residual uncertainty attached.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Naming findings {level=1}
Interpret: (a) 47,XX,+18; (b) 46,XX,del(4p16.3); (c) 46,XX,der(14;21)(q10;q10),+21; (d) 46,XY,inv(9)(p12q13).
::: solution
(a) Trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome). (b) Deletion at the terminal region of 4p, i.e. Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome. (c) 46 chromosomes with a normal-looking karyotype but a derivative chromosome 14;21 replacing one chromosome 14, plus an extra chromosome 21: translocation Down syndrome. The derivative carries 14q and 21q fused at the centromere, so the child has three copies of 21q — the derivative itself acting as the third copy — and the parents must be tested, because the same fusion in a mother (45,XX,der(14;21)) is a balanced carrier state with a 10–15% recurrence risk. (d) Pericentric inversion of chromosome 9, a common polymorphic variant considered benign, and reported to avoid it being mistaken for a cause of the presenting problem — a useful reminder that a chromosome report must be read alongside the phenotype.
:::
:::

::: exercise Maternal age risk {level=1}
Explain why the combined first-trimester test (nuchal translucency plus biochemical markers) plus maternal age is a better screen than maternal age alone, and why cell-free DNA is better than both for trisomy 21.
::: solution
Maternal age alone gives about 30% detection for a 5% screen-positive rate: the risk gradient is real but weak per pregnancy. Nuchal translucency, free β-hCG and PAPP-A each carry independent information about the fetus, so combining them with the age-related prior gives roughly 85–90% detection at a 5% false-positive rate. Cell-free DNA uses fetal (placental) DNA sequences directly, giving >99% detection at a false-positive rate below 0.1% for trisomy 21 — hence its dramatically better likelihood ratio and its higher positive predictive value in a screened population — but it remains a screening test, is weaker for trisomies 13 and 18 and for the sex chromosomes, can fail for low fetal fraction, and is confounded by placental mosaicism, vanished twins and maternal copy-number change. It also does not detect the structural and single-gene conditions that nuchal translucency and ultrasound may pick up.
:::
:::

::: exercise Positive predictive value {level=1 check="0.5"}
A screening test has 99% sensitivity and 98% specificity. In a population in which the condition has a prevalence of 1 in 100, what proportion of positive results are true positives?
::: solution
Take 10,000 people: 100 affected, 99 detected; 9,900 unaffected, of whom 2% (198) test positive. Positive predictive value $= 99/(99 + 198) = 0.5$ — half of positives are false. The point of the exercise is that specificity, not sensitivity, dominates positive predictive value when prevalence is low, which is why a screening test used in a low-prevalence population needs much higher specificity than 98%, and why a patient's own prior risk must be included in the number quoted back to her.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which test first? {level=1}
Choose between karyotype, FISH/rapid PCR, chromosomal microarray and sequencing for: (a) a newborn with a suspected chromosome disorder and an obvious structural heart defect; (b) a 6-year-old with unexplained learning difficulty and autism; (c) a 16-week pregnancy with a nuchal translucency of 5 mm and normal cell-free DNA; (d) a child with recurrent infections and a conotruncal defect.
::: solution
(a) Karyotype (plus rapid aneuploidy testing if clinically urgent), because a numerical or large structural change is likely and a translocation must be recognised for the parents' sake. (b) Chromosomal microarray first-line, with Fragile X testing where indicated; exome/genome sequencing if the array is non-diagnostic. (c) Diagnostic sampling (villi or amniocytes) with microarray: increased nuchal translucency with normal cell-free DNA points to copy-number change, a single-gene or syndromic cause, and increasingly to sequencing panels, so sequencing should be considered if the array is normal. (d) Targeted 22q11.2 testing (microarray or FISH), because the clinical question is specific and the deletion is below routine karyotype resolution.
:::
:::

::: exercise Mosaicism in a prenatal sample {level=2}
Chorion villus sampling at 13 weeks for a high-risk cell-free DNA result shows mosaicism for trisomy 21 in direct (cytotrophoblast) preparation only, with a normal culture. What do you advise, and why?
::: solution
The two preparations sample different cell populations: the direct preparation reflects the cytotrophoblast (extravillous/inner cell mass derived) while cultured cells reflect the mesodermal core, so a mosaic result in one and not the other is exactly the pattern of **confined placental mosaicism** — commonest with chromosome 16 but well described for 21, where it also explains false-positive cell-free DNA results. Because the fetal chromosome complement cannot be determined from that sample, the advice is amniocentesis (amniocytes are of fetal origin), with cell counting adequate to exclude low-level mosaicism, and, if the finding persists, consideration of fetal blood sampling and of placental examination at delivery. Two counselling points matter as much as the technique: the reason for the original sampling was a screening result, so the possibility of a false-positive screen and a mosaic placenta should have been anticipated; and increased nuchal translucency or growth restriction with confined placental mosaicism type III carries a real risk of an affected fetus even when amniocytes are normal, so growth surveillance continues.
:::
:::

::: exercise Recurrence arithmetic {level=2}
A couple have a child with Down syndrome. For each of the following, state the recurrence risk and the reason: (a) the child has 47,XX,+21 and the mother is 34; (b) the child has 46,XX,+21,der(14;21)mat; (c) the mother is 45,XX,der(21;21); (d) the child has 46,XX/47,XX,+21 with 10% mosaic cells in blood.
::: solution
(a) About 1% above the age-related baseline: the trisomy arose by nondisjunction, usually maternal meiosis I, and germ-line mosaicism is the residual explanation for rare recurrence; offer prenatal diagnosis in any case. (b) A maternal Robertsonian carrier: empirically ~10–15% for a liveborn child with Down syndrome, with additional losses from unbalanced conceptions; if the father were the carrier, only a few per cent. (c) 100% of viable conceptions affected, since every viable conceptus must inherit the 21q21q derivative plus a normal 21; the options are donor gametes or preimplantation testing. (d) Low and unpredictable: a post-zygotic mitotic error is not repeated, but the phenotype in the child depends on which tissues carry the line and cannot be inferred from a 10% blood count.
:::
:::

::: exercise Imprinting enters the picture {level=2}
Two children both have paternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15 — one for the whole chromosome, one for the 15q11-q13 region only. Predict the phenotype of each, and explain why a maternal deletion of the same region gives a different disorder.
::: solution
Both children lack a maternal contribution at 15q11-q13 and have two active paternal copies, which causes **Prader-Willi syndrome** (neonatal hypotonia and feeding difficulty followed by hyperphagia and obesity, hypogonadism, learning difficulties, behavioural features); a maternal deletion of 15q11-q13 causes **Angelman syndrome** because that region contains genes expressed from the maternal allele (notably *UBE3A* in neurons) as well as paternally expressed ones (*SNRPN*, the SNORD116 cluster). The two disorders are therefore the same interval viewed from opposite parents — the reason testing starts with methylation studies (abnormal in both), and why the mechanism (deletion, uniparental disomy or an imprinting-centre defect) must then be established: only deletion carries a substantial recurrence risk, and only an imprinting defect raises the possibility of a trans-acting epigenetic machinery gene.
:::
:::

::: exercise A teenager with a marker chromosome {level=3}
A 12-year-old with hypotonia, epilepsy from age 3, autism and scoliosis has an extra small acrocentric-like chromosome in all cells, identified as a bisatellited marker from 15q11-q13, present on the paternal chromosome. Explain the phenotype, the mechanism, and the recurrence risk.
::: solution
This is the **isodicentric chromosome 15 / inv dup(15) syndrome**: a supernumerary marker carrying two copies of 15q11-q13 in addition to the two normal homologues, so the critical region is present in four or five copies. Dosage of that region, which includes the *UBE3A*-cluster and imprinted genes, drives the phenotype: infantile-onset epilepsy (often difficult to control), hypotonia, absent or minimal speech, autistic features and behavioural disturbance, with severity increasing with copy number and with parental origin — a paternal origin (as here) means the extra copies of the imprinted, expressed material are unopposed, whereas a maternal extra copy is partly silenced. The mechanism is usually unequal crossing over or breakage–fusion–bridge events in a parent who is typically unaffected, or arising de novo in the child; because most cases are de novo the recurrence risk for the parents is low, but if a parent carries a balanced 15q rearrangement (or the marker was inherited from a mosaic parent), the risk rises substantially, so parental karyotype and microarray are essential. Management is symptomatic and multidisciplinary — anti-seizure medication (note that some are reported to worsen behaviour), developmental support, scoliosis and sleep management — and the family should be told that the marker's size and content, not the phrase "extra chromosome", predicts outcome.

::: exercise Deciding with parents {level=3}
A couple at 21 weeks are told their fetus has trisomy 18 with a large atrioventricular septal defect and a horseshoe kidney. They ask what you advise. Structure your answer, and identify the decisions that will need to be made in the next weeks.
::: solution
Start by making sure the diagnosis is secure and complete: a diagnostic karyotype or microarray (and exome sequencing in selected cases, since a minority of fetuses with an apparently "trisomy 18" phenotype have additional findings that change the prognosis), a detailed anatomical survey and fetal echocardiography, and referral to a fetal medicine and neonatal/palliative care team with experience of the condition. Then give the statistics honestly, in the form of ranges rather than verdicts: most affected pregnancies miscarry or are stillborn; among liveborn infants, median survival is days to weeks, with a small minority living beyond a year, usually with severe multi-disability; cardiac disease, feeding and airway problems determine the course, and the specific defects found matter. Present the options without steering: continuing the pregnancy with a plan for comfort-focused neonatal care, continuing with a request for intensive management including cardiac surgery (which some units will provide, and which has produced long-term survivors, with substantial disability in most), and ending the pregnancy, all with time to talk and access to a perinatal palliative-care team and to other families. Decisions that must be made in advance and written in the notes include place and mode of delivery, resuscitation and intensive care at birth, feeding (including whether tube feeding is offered), the thresholds for surgery, and resuscitation status if the baby deteriorates; then antenatal investigations for the parents (parental karyotype if not already done, to look for a translocation), bereavement and sibling support, and postnatal genetic counselling about recurrence and about future pregnancies.
