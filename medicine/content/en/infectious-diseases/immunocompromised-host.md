Infection in the immunocompromised host is medicine's open-book exam: **the host determines the organism, the timeline determines the host, and the syndrome lies.** A pneumonia in a neutropenic patient on day 12 of induction chemotherapy, a headache in an HIV patient with a CD4 count of 40, and an abscess around a renal allograft on triple therapy are three different diseases that happen to share a chest or meninges, and each has a predictable cast list if you know which decade, which defect and which drugs are in play.

This chapter covers the defect-based approach, the timelines of transplant and chemotherapy, the classic HIV-related and iatrogenic syndromes, and the practical prescriptions - prophylaxis, screening before immunosuppression, and when to stop.

## Reading the syndrome when the host lies

A final clinical art: in these patients the syndrome you were taught rarely arrives intact. Pneumonia may be an incidental CT finding in an afebrile neutropenic man on broad antibiotics; meningitis may present as three days of vague unsteadiness and a headache that "is not bad"; cryptococcal pressure rises behind a normal funduscopy until it does not; disseminated tuberculosis can smoulder as fever of unknown origin with a normal chest image and a mildly raised alkaline phosphatase. The compensating disciplines are mechanical. Sample before antibiotics whenever sampling does not delay antibiotics, and never delay antibiotics for sampling. Image on suspicion rather than on signs, because signs require the immunity you are asking about. Reassess on clock, not on vibe: a defined hour at which someone decides again whether the empiric plan is working, whether prophylaxis has failed, and whether the immunosuppression itself has become the disease being treated.

Two negative-space lessons round this off. First, drug fever, engraftment syndrome, tumour flare, organ rejection and neutralising cytokine release all mimic infection in exactly these hosts, and the art is not choosing which story is true but sequencing the tests so that the treatable-in-hours possibilities are excluded first. Second, the social immune system matters: adherence to prophylaxis collapses with cost, transport, immigration anxiety and treatment fatigue, and the clinic that schedules a pharmacy call with the appointment keeps more patients alive than the clinic with the cleverer antifungal.

## Two habits that prevent most disasters

The first is the pre-immunosuppression consultation as a fixed ritual, whatever the drug: birthplace and travel, hepatitis and HIV and tuberculosis history, current household vaccines and the patient's own, the antibody panel whose results change prescriptions rather than satisfying forms, and the written plan for prophylaxis and for fever, countersigned by the person who will be on call at two in the morning when the plan is tested. The second is the fever instruction delivered in a way that survives: a card, a phone number, a named arriving time, and the repeated sentence that inflammation may be silent, so counting symptoms is less reliable than counting hours. Both habits cost minutes and both have prevented the case that ends a ward's quiet year.

One last framing for the modern ward: many of these patients are not "immunosuppressed" as a state but immunosuppressed as a schedule - the rituximab calendar, the steroid taper, the prophylaxis stop-date. Writing medicine against a schedule means writing forwards: what will this patient's immunity look like in six weeks, who will be responsible then, and has anyone told them.


None of this is exotic if you keep three index cards in your head: the defect, the timeline, and the drug list. A patient is not "immunosuppressed" in general; they are a neutrophil count on a Tuesday, a month number after an operation, and a prophylaxis table that somebody, ideally you, has actually read this week.


## A note on stopping

Equally often, the right question is when to stop. Antibiotic prophylaxis after transplant can usually end once immune maintenance is stable, and continuing it past that point buys colonisation, C difficile and resistant organisms rather than protection; the decision belongs to a dated protocol, not to whoever last changed the drug chart. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor given as treatment rather than prevention adds little once appropriate antibiotics are running and adds bone pain, fever confusion and cost. Empiric antifungal courses kept running "because she was neutropenic last week" obscure the day the neutrophils returned and with them the ordinary diagnostic logic. Corticosteroid tapers, the quiet engine of so much of this medicine, deserve written end-dates and review points because every risk table in this chapter moves the day the dose falls below the threshold, and the patient who knows that date can tell you things the calendar cannot.

Stopping prophylaxis also means starting something else: the vaccination rebuild. After haematopoietic stem-cell transplant the immune system is a child again, and the schedule that protects it is a paediatric one with adult appointments and adult hesitations; after splenectomy the vaccines are the prophylaxis; after rituximab the best timing is the weeks before the next dose rather than the months of absence between them. Whoever stops the tablets should be the person who writes the date the jabs begin, and both dates should face the patient, not the file. Prophylaxis is a prescription with an expiry date, and the expiry is a clinical decision as real as the first dose.

::: summary
- Ask which defence is broken before asking which organism: neutrophils, T cells, antibody, complement or barrier each carry a signature.
- Ask the timeline next: months from transplant, days from chemotherapy, years from HIV diagnosis; prophylaxis reshapes but does not erase the calendar.
- Immunocompromised patients under-signal: no pus, no infiltrate, no meningism; imaging, cultures and a low threshold to sample carry the diagnosis.
- Febrile neutropenia is a stopwatch disease: cultures without delay, broad antibiotics within the hour, then a structured day-2-to-5 escalation plan.
- Screen for HBV, TB, HIV, strongyloides and VZV before immunosuppression, vaccinate before elective therapy, and write the prophylaxis plan before the first dose.
- The transplant team owns the immunosuppression and the infection team owns the microbiology; the patient is served only while both talk.

## Taking it further

The infectious-diseases mindset in one paragraph: think in source, route, host and time. Identify the source before naming the syndrome; ask what the exposure history permits and what the local resistance map makes likely; adjust everything for the host standing in front of you; and treat the clock as a drug - the sepsis, endocarditis and meningitis outcomes are functions of hours. Then audit yourself against the two failure modes of the specialty: anchoring on the first plausible organism while the atypical presentation evolves, and de-escalating never - the narrow-spectrum switch that should happen on day three and does not. Both failures are curable by the habit of re-asking, at every review, what the story said yesterday that it no longer says today.
