"Tropical" infections are not confined to the tropics. Millions of people travel between continents every year, and many people living in Europe were born in, or regularly visit, countries where malaria, dengue and typhoid are common. A fever that would be a minor viral illness in someone who has stayed at home can be life-threatening malaria in someone who returned from West Africa two weeks ago. The single most useful question in a febrile patient is often: **"Where have you been?"**

The global burden is enormous. WHO's *World Malaria Report 2025* estimated 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 deaths in 2024, about 95% of the deaths in Africa and most of them in children under five. Dengue has spread across the tropics and subtropics, and neglected infections such as schistosomiasis and soil-transmitted worms affect hundreds of millions of the world's poorest people.

This chapter teaches the approach to the returning traveller with fever, then malaria in depth, then dengue and other arboviruses, enteric fever and other bacterial infections, emerging and high-consequence infections, and the helminths. UK practice follows the UK malaria treatment guidelines and UK Health Security Agency guidance; global practice follows WHO. Treatment is described as principles; regimens and doses come from current guidelines and specialist advice.

## The returning traveller with fever

::: definition Fever in the returning traveller {#def-fever}
**Fever in a returning traveller** is fever in a person who has travelled abroad, usually within the previous year, and especially to tropical or subtropical regions. Its assessment rests on four questions:

1. **Where** exactly did they go, including rural areas, stopovers and altitude?
2. **When** did they arrive and leave, and when did the fever start? The gap between exposure and illness is compared with the **incubation period** of each candidate infection.
3. **What** did they do: freshwater swimming, insect and animal bites, unsafe food and water, sexual contacts, healthcare, visiting friends and relatives, caring for sick people?
4. **What protection** did they have: vaccines, malaria chemoprophylaxis taken correctly, insect-bite avoidance?

The first rule is absolute: **anyone with fever who has been in a malaria-endemic area must be tested for malaria urgently**, whatever other diagnosis seems likely.
:::

Most fevers in returned travellers are caused by ordinary infections such as influenza, pneumonia and urinary infections, and it is important not to forget them. Among infections specific to travel, the commonest in UK practice are **malaria** (above all from sub-Saharan Africa), **dengue** (South and South-East Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean), **enteric fever** (South Asia) and **rickettsial infections** (southern Africa and Asia).

The incubation period narrows the list:

| Onset after exposure | Typical infections |
|---|---|
| Under 10 days | Dengue, chikungunya, rickettsial infections, influenza, travellers' diarrhoea; viral haemorrhagic fevers (up to 21 days) |
| 10–21 days | Falciparum malaria (at least 7 days), enteric fever, leptospirosis, acute HIV, viral haemorrhagic fevers |
| Over 21 days | Malaria (especially vivax and ovale, which can relapse months later), tuberculosis, hepatitis A, B and E, amoebic liver abscess, acute schistosomiasis, visceral leishmaniasis |

If fever starts more than 21 days after leaving an area, dengue, rickettsial infection and viral haemorrhagic fever are effectively excluded; malaria never is.

## Malaria

::: definition Malaria {#def-malaria}
**Malaria** is infection of red blood cells by protozoa of the genus ***Plasmodium***, transmitted by the bite of female ***Anopheles*** mosquitoes, which bite mainly between dusk and dawn. Five species commonly infect humans:

- ***P. falciparum***: most of the world's deaths, and the cause of severe malaria; predominant in sub-Saharan Africa.
- ***P. vivax*** and ***P. ovale***: form dormant liver stages (**hypnozoites**) that cause relapses months or years later.
- ***P. malariae***: low-grade, long-lasting infection; associated with nephrotic syndrome.
- ***P. knowlesi***: a monkey parasite in South-East Asia with a 24-hour cycle that can rapidly become severe.

**Life cycle.** Sporozoites injected by the mosquito travel to the liver, multiply in hepatocytes for one to two weeks (no symptoms), then release thousands of merozoites into the blood. Each merozoite invades a red cell, multiplies, and bursts it to release more merozoites: about every **48 hours** for falciparum, vivax and ovale, and 72 hours for *P. malariae*. The synchronised bursting of red cells causes the fever. Some parasites become **gametocytes**, which infect the next mosquito.

**Diagnosis.** Examination of **thick and thin blood films** by an experienced microscopist remains the standard: the thick film is sensitive, and the thin film identifies the species and gives the **parasitaemia** (percentage of red cells infected). **Rapid diagnostic tests** detecting parasite antigens are used alongside microscopy. One negative test does not exclude malaria: UK guidance is to repeat testing so that **three samples over 48–72 hours** are negative.
:::

::: proof
**Why *P. falciparum* kills.** Three features distinguish it from the other species:

1. **It can invade red cells of any age**, so parasitaemia can rise without limit; vivax and ovale prefer young reticulocytes, which keeps their parasitaemia low.
2. **Sequestration.** Infected red cells express a parasite protein, PfEMP1, on knobs on their surface. This binds receptors on the endothelium of small vessels (cytoadherence) and to uninfected red cells (rosetting). Mature parasites therefore disappear from the circulating blood and pack the microcirculation of the brain, kidneys, gut, lungs and placenta, obstructing flow and provoking inflammation. A peripheral film can underestimate the total parasite burden.
3. **Metabolic consequences.** Parasites consume glucose and produce lactate; obstructed microcirculation adds to the **lactic acidosis**. **Hypoglycaemia** results from parasite consumption, impaired gluconeogenesis in the liver and, in the past, quinine-induced insulin release. **Anaemia** results from destruction of infected and uninfected cells and suppressed marrow.

The clinical syndromes of severe malaria (coma, acidosis, kidney injury, respiratory distress, severe anaemia) follow from these mechanisms. They can develop within hours in a non-immune traveller, which is why falciparum malaria is treated as a medical emergency.
:::

::: definition Severe falciparum malaria {#def-severe}
**Severe malaria** is *P. falciparum* (or *P. knowlesi*, occasionally *P. vivax*) infection with one or more of the following features, adapted from WHO criteria for adults:

- **Impaired consciousness** (Glasgow Coma Scale below 11) or **prostration**; **repeated seizures** (more than two in 24 hours).
- **Acidosis** (base deficit above 8 mmol/L, bicarbonate below 15 mmol/L or lactate 5 mmol/L or more).
- **Hypoglycaemia** (glucose below 2.2 mmol/L).
- **Severe anaemia** (haemoglobin below 70 g/L in adults).
- **Acute kidney injury** (creatinine above 265 µmol/L).
- **Jaundice** (bilirubin above 50 µmol/L) with high parasitaemia.
- **Pulmonary oedema** or acute respiratory distress; **shock** (systolic pressure below 80 mmHg with poor perfusion); **significant bleeding**.
- **Hyperparasitaemia**: above 10% in WHO criteria. UK guidelines advise intravenous treatment for non-immune travellers with **more than 2%** of red cells parasitised, even without other features.
:::

**Treatment.** **Severe malaria** is treated with **intravenous artesunate**, which kills young parasites before they sequester. Two large trials established it: SEAQUAMAT (adults in Asia, 2005) found about a third fewer deaths than with quinine, and AQUAMAT (children in Africa, 2010) about a fifth fewer. Once the patient can take tablets, treatment is completed with a full course of an oral **artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)**. Patients need high-dependency or intensive care, frequent glucose checks, cautious fluids (fluid overload causes pulmonary oedema) and monitoring for **delayed haemolysis** in the weeks after artesunate. **Uncomplicated falciparum malaria** is treated with an oral ACT such as artemether–lumefantrine, with the first days often in hospital because a non-immune patient can deteriorate. **Vivax and ovale** malaria are treated with an ACT or, where it remains effective, chloroquine, followed by **primaquine** or **tafenoquine** to kill hypnozoites, after testing for **G6PD deficiency**, because these drugs cause haemolysis in G6PD-deficient people.

**Resistance.** Partial resistance to artemisinins, caused by mutations in the parasite *kelch13* gene, emerged in the Greater Mekong subregion and has now been confirmed in parts of East Africa, which threatens the drugs that underpin malaria control.

**Prevention.** Travellers are taught **ABCD**: **A**wareness of risk, **B**ite avoidance (repellents containing DEET, covering up, insecticide-treated nets), **C**hemoprophylaxis appropriate to the destination and taken as directed, and prompt **D**iagnosis of any fever. People who grew up in endemic areas and now live abroad lose their partial immunity within a few years, and those **visiting friends and relatives** account for most imported cases in the UK. In endemic countries, WHO has recommended two vaccines for children, **RTS,S/AS01** (2021) and **R21/Matrix-M** (2023), alongside insecticide-treated nets, indoor spraying and seasonal chemoprevention.

::: example A fever after visiting family {#ex-malaria-severe}
A 38-year-old man who has lived in London for 15 years returned 10 days ago from a month in Nigeria visiting family. He took no malaria prophylaxis. He has had fever and headache for four days and is now confused. Temperature 39.6 °C, pulse 118 per minute, blood pressure 104/60 mmHg, GCS 13, mild jaundice. Thin film: *P. falciparum*, 270 parasitised cells counted among 4,500 red cells. Glucose 3.1 mmol/L, creatinine 290 µmol/L, bilirubin 64 µmol/L, platelets 38 × 10⁹/L, lactate 4.2 mmol/L.
::: solution
**Parasitaemia**: $270/4500 = 0.06 = 6\%$.

**Severity**: he has **severe falciparum malaria** by several criteria: impaired consciousness, acute kidney injury (creatinine above 265 µmol/L), jaundice with high parasitaemia, and a parasitaemia above the UK threshold of 2%. Thrombocytopenia is almost universal in malaria and is not by itself a severity criterion; his glucose is low-normal and needs close watching.

**Management**:
1. **Intravenous artesunate** immediately; do not wait for an intensive care bed. If artesunate is not immediately available, intravenous quinine is started and switched.
2. Admission to intensive care or a high-dependency unit; frequent glucose monitoring and treatment of hypoglycaemia.
3. **Cautious fluid resuscitation**: severe malaria causes leaky lung capillaries, and over-filling precipitates pulmonary oedema. Renal replacement therapy if kidney injury worsens.
4. Blood cultures and consideration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, because bacterial co-infection is common in severe malaria.
5. Daily parasitaemia until falling, then a full oral ACT course; a haemoglobin check in the weeks after artesunate for delayed haemolysis.
6. Discussion with a specialist tropical medicine centre.

He is a typical imported case: a person who grew up in an endemic country, has lost his immunity, and did not take prophylaxis when visiting family.
:::
:::

::: warning
**One negative test and a history of prophylaxis do not exclude malaria.** A first blood film can be negative early in infection or when parasites are sequestered; repeat until three samples over 48–72 hours are negative. Prophylaxis reduces but does not abolish risk, especially if doses were missed or the drug was not suited to the region. And malaria can present with diarrhoea, jaundice or cough, so a "gastroenteritis" or "chest infection" label in a returned traveller must not stop malaria testing.
:::

## Dengue and other arboviruses

**Arboviruses** are viruses transmitted by arthropods. **Dengue** virus (four serotypes) is spread by *Aedes* mosquitoes, which bite by day and breed in small containers of water in towns and cities. After an incubation period of 3–14 days, most infections are mild or asymptomatic. Symptomatic dengue has three phases:

1. **Febrile phase** (2–7 days): sudden high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pains, nausea, often a flushed or blanching rash; leucopenia and falling platelets.
2. **Critical phase**, around the time the fever settles (typically days 3–7): in a minority, **plasma leakage** from capillaries causes a rising haematocrit, pleural effusions and ascites, and can progress to shock; bleeding and organ involvement may follow.
3. **Recovery phase**: leaked fluid is reabsorbed; a convalescent rash with "islands of white in a sea of red" may appear; over-hydration now causes fluid overload.

WHO classifies dengue as **dengue without warning signs**, **dengue with warning signs** (abdominal pain or tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation, mucosal bleeding, lethargy or restlessness, liver enlargement, and a rising haematocrit with a rapid fall in platelets) and **severe dengue** (severe plasma leakage with shock or respiratory distress, severe bleeding, or severe organ involvement). A second infection with a different serotype carries a higher risk of severe disease, partly through **antibody-dependent enhancement**.

Diagnosis is by **NS1 antigen** or **PCR** in the first five days, and IgM serology later. There is no antiviral treatment; care is supportive, with careful monitoring of haematocrit and fluid balance through the critical phase and avoidance of **aspirin and NSAIDs** because of bleeding risk. A vaccine (TAK-003) is licensed in the UK; UK advice restricts its use to people who have had dengue before.

Other arboviruses include **chikungunya** (fever and disabling, sometimes prolonged, joint pain), **Zika** (usually mild, but infection in pregnancy can cause congenital microcephaly) and **yellow fever** (hepatitis, bleeding and high mortality, preventable by vaccination).

::: example Day five in Bangkok {#ex-dengue}
A 25-year-old woman developed fever, severe headache and aching behind her eyes two days before flying home from three weeks in Thailand. On day 5 of illness her fever has settled, but she has abdominal pain and has vomited four times. Pulse 108 per minute, blood pressure 102/80 mmHg. Platelets have fallen from 110 to 40 × 10⁹/L in 24 hours, and haematocrit has risen from 0.38 to 0.46. Malaria tests are negative; NS1 antigen is positive.
::: solution
**Diagnosis**: **dengue with warning signs**, entering the **critical phase**. Defervescence is when danger peaks, not when it ends. The warning signs are abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, and a rising haematocrit with a rapidly falling platelet count, which together indicate **plasma leakage**. The haematocrit has risen by $(0.46-0.38)/0.38 \approx 21\%$, a degree of haemoconcentration that signals significant leakage. The narrowed pulse pressure (102/80) is an early sign of impending shock.

**Management**: admission; frequent observation of pulse, blood pressure, perfusion and urine output; serial haematocrit; and **careful intravenous crystalloid**, adjusted to the clinical state and haematocrit rather than given as fixed large volumes, because fluid needs fall rapidly once leakage stops after 24–48 hours. No aspirin or NSAIDs; paracetamol for fever and pain. Platelet transfusion is not given just for a low count; it is reserved for significant bleeding. She should be told that a future infection with a different serotype carries a higher risk of severe disease.
:::
:::

## Enteric fever and other bacterial infections

**Enteric fever** (typhoid and paratyphoid) is caused by ***Salmonella enterica* serovars Typhi and Paratyphi**, spread by food and water contaminated with human faeces; most UK cases are acquired in South Asia. After 1–3 weeks, an insidious fever rises over several days, with headache, malaise, abdominal discomfort and either constipation or diarrhoea. Signs include a relative bradycardia, faint pink "rose spots" on the trunk and an enlarged spleen. Untreated, complications in the third week include **intestinal bleeding and perforation**. Diagnosis is by **blood culture** (bone marrow culture is more sensitive); the old Widal serological test is unreliable. Antibiotic choice follows the place of acquisition: fluoroquinolone resistance is widespread in South Asia, and an **extensively drug-resistant** strain in Pakistan, recognised from 2016, is also resistant to third-generation cephalosporins, so azithromycin or a carbapenem is used. A few people become chronic carriers in the gallbladder. Typhoid conjugate vaccines are now used in endemic countries.

**Rickettsial infections** include **African tick-bite fever** (fever, headache and one or more black **eschars** at tick-bite sites, often after safari or rural travel in southern Africa) and **scrub typhus** in Asia. They respond quickly to **doxycycline**, which is started on clinical suspicion. **Leptospirosis**, acquired through freshwater exposure to rat urine (rafting, flooding, farming), causes fever, myalgia and conjunctival suffusion, and in severe cases jaundice, kidney injury and lung haemorrhage (Weil's disease).

## Emerging and high-consequence infections

::: definition Emerging and high-consequence infectious diseases {#def-emerging}
An **emerging infection** is one that has newly appeared in a population, or whose incidence or geographical range is rapidly increasing. Most emerging human infections are **zoonoses**, passing from animals to humans when people, animals and environments change: deforestation, farming, wildlife trade, travel and climate change. The "One Health" approach links human, animal and environmental surveillance.

In the UK, a **high-consequence infectious disease (HCID)** is an acute infection with high case fatality, no or limited treatment, the ability to spread in the community and in healthcare, and the need for a specialised response. Examples are the contact-transmitted **viral haemorrhagic fevers** (Ebola, Marburg, Lassa, Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever) and some airborne infections (MERS coronavirus, certain avian influenza strains). A returning traveller with fever within **21 days** of leaving an area where a viral haemorrhagic fever occurs, or who has had contact with a case, undergoes a structured **risk assessment** before routine tests are sent.
:::

High-risk patients are isolated, and malaria testing is still done urgently, in a high-containment laboratory if needed, because malaria remains by far the most likely diagnosis. Confirmed cases are transferred to specialist HCID units. The West African Ebola epidemic of 2014–2016 showed that the main determinants of outbreak size were the strength of local health systems, infection control and community trust, and it led to ring vaccination with an effective Ebola vaccine in later outbreaks. More recently, **mpox** spread internationally, and WHO declared public health emergencies of international concern in 2022 and again in 2024.

## Helminths and other parasites

::: definition Helminths {#def-helminths}
**Helminths** are parasitic worms: **nematodes** (roundworms), **trematodes** (flukes) and **cestodes** (tapeworms). With an important exception (*Strongyloides*), adult worms **do not multiply inside the human host**: the worm burden depends on how many infective stages are acquired, so heavy disease reflects repeated exposure. Larval migration through tissues causes **eosinophilia**, which is a useful clue in a returning traveller.

- **Soil-transmitted helminths** (*Ascaris*, hookworm, *Trichuris*) infect around 1.5 billion people, mainly children in poor communities, causing anaemia, malnutrition and impaired development. Control relies on periodic mass treatment with albendazole or mebendazole, sanitation and footwear.
- **Schistosomiasis** is acquired when larvae (cercariae) released by freshwater snails penetrate the skin during swimming or washing, for example in Lake Malawi. *S. haematobium* causes urinary and genital disease (haematuria, bladder fibrosis and bladder cancer, female genital schistosomiasis); *S. mansoni* causes intestinal and liver disease with periportal fibrosis and portal hypertension. Acute infection 2–8 weeks after exposure can cause **Katayama syndrome** (fever, urticaria, cough, eosinophilia). Treatment is with praziquantel, which kills adult worms but not immature ones, so treatment is timed or repeated accordingly.
- ***Strongyloides stercoralis*** can complete its life cycle inside the host (**autoinfection**), so infection persists for decades after leaving an endemic area.
- **Cysticercosis** follows ingestion of eggs of the pork tapeworm *Taenia solium* from a human carrier; larval cysts lodge in muscle and brain (**neurocysticercosis**), a leading cause of acquired epilepsy worldwide.
:::

::: example Sepsis after steroids {#ex-strongyloides}
A 68-year-old man who grew up in Jamaica and moved to the UK 45 years ago is treated with high-dose prednisolone for an exacerbation of COPD. Ten days later he develops abdominal pain, diarrhoea, worsening breathlessness with bilateral lung infiltrates, and then fever, confusion and neck stiffness. Blood cultures grow *Escherichia coli*; his eosinophil count is normal. On questioning he recalls years of an itchy, creeping rash on his buttocks and thighs.
::: solution
**Diagnosis**: ***Strongyloides* hyperinfection syndrome** with Gram-negative meningitis and bacteraemia. Chronic, low-grade *Strongyloides* infection acquired decades earlier was held in check by his immunity. High-dose corticosteroids allowed explosive autoinfection: vast numbers of larvae penetrate the gut wall and migrate through the lungs and other organs, carrying gut bacteria into the blood and meninges. The migrating rash he describes is **larva currens**, characteristic of chronic strongyloidiasis. Eosinophilia is often **absent** in hyperinfection, partly because of the steroids.

**Management**: reduce immunosuppression as far as possible; **ivermectin** (given for longer than in uncomplicated infection, with specialist advice); antibiotics for the Gram-negative meningitis; stool, sputum and other samples for larvae; and intensive care support. Mortality is high.

**Prevention** is the real lesson: people from endemic areas, even decades after migration, should be screened (by serology) or treated empirically before high-dose steroids, transplantation or other immunosuppression ([[infectious-diseases/immunocompromised-host]]).
:::
:::

::: example A first seizure {#ex-neurocysticercosis}
A 32-year-old woman who moved to the UK from Bolivia four years ago has a first generalised seizure. She has had occasional headaches. MRI brain shows a single 1.5 cm cystic lesion in the right frontal lobe with a small eccentric nodule inside it and surrounding oedema.
::: solution
**Diagnosis**: a cyst with an eccentric **scolex** (the tapeworm's head) is characteristic of **neurocysticercosis**, caused by the larval stage of *Taenia solium*, common in parts of Latin America, Asia and Africa. The seizure occurs when the dying cyst provokes inflammation and oedema. Serology supports the diagnosis. The differential for a single ring lesion includes tuberculoma, abscess, toxoplasmosis and tumour.

**Management**: antiseizure medication; for viable cysts, antiparasitic treatment with **albendazole** (with praziquantel added for multiple cysts) given together with **corticosteroids** to limit the inflammatory reaction as the cysts die; and follow-up imaging. Antiparasitic drugs are not given when there is raised intracranial pressure from many cysts until this is controlled. Because cysticercosis is acquired from eggs shed by a **human tapeworm carrier**, household members may be screened for intestinal *T. solium* infection.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1880 Alphonse Laveran, a French army doctor in Algeria, saw moving parasites in the blood of malaria patients. Patrick Manson had already shown in 1877–78 that mosquitoes transmit filarial worms. In 1897, working in India, Ronald Ross found malaria parasites in the gut of an *Anopheles* mosquito that had fed on an infected person, and in 1898 he described the full transmission cycle in bird malaria; in the same year Giovanni Battista Grassi and colleagues showed that *Anopheles* mosquitoes transmit human malaria. Ross (1902) and Laveran (1907) received Nobel prizes. In the 1970s a Chinese research programme led by Tu Youyou, inspired by a fourth-century text, extracted artemisinin from sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*); she received the Nobel Prize in 2015, and artemisinin derivatives now underpin malaria treatment worldwide.
:::

- [ ] Falciparum malaria
- [x] Dengue

- [ ] Acute schistosomiasis
::: solution
Dengue has an incubation period of 3–14 days, so fever beginning 30 days after the last possible exposure is not dengue. Falciparum malaria usually presents within a few months but can present later; amoebic liver abscess and acute schistosomiasis typically present weeks to months after exposure.

::: summary
- In any febrile returning traveller, ask where, when, what they did and what protection they had, and test urgently for malaria if they have been in an endemic area.
- Incubation periods narrow the differential: fever starting more than 21 days after leaving excludes dengue, rickettsial infection and viral haemorrhagic fever, but never malaria.
- *P. falciparum* kills because it invades red cells of all ages and sequesters in small vessels, causing coma, acidosis, hypoglycaemia, kidney injury and anaemia.
- Severe malaria (WHO criteria; in the UK also parasitaemia above 2%) is treated with intravenous artesunate followed by an oral ACT; vivax and ovale need primaquine or tafenoquine after G6PD testing.
- Dengue becomes dangerous around defervescence, when plasma leakage can cause shock; warning signs and a rising haematocrit guide careful fluid therapy, and NSAIDs are avoided.
- Enteric fever needs blood cultures and antibiotics chosen for local resistance; rickettsial infections respond to doxycycline.
- Helminths cause eosinophilia; *Strongyloides* persists for decades and can cause fatal hyperinfection after steroids, so people at risk are screened or treated before immunosuppression.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Counting parasites {level=1 check="3"}
On a thin film, 45 of 1,500 red cells contain *P. falciparum* parasites. What is the parasitaemia as a percentage?
::: solution
$45/1500 = 0.03 = 3\%$. This is above the UK threshold of 2% at which intravenous artesunate is advised for a non-immune traveller, even if there are no other features of severity.
:::

::: exercise Timing the bite {level=1}
A woman spent ten days in Goa, India, and develops fever 18 days after flying home. Which of dengue, falciparum malaria, vivax malaria and enteric fever remain possible? Explain.
::: solution
Exposure took place between 18 and 28 days before the fever began. **Dengue** (3–14 days) is very unlikely, although an infection acquired on the last day falls near the upper limit. **Vivax malaria**, common in India, is possible and can present months later. **Falciparum malaria**, less common in India but present, is possible. **Enteric fever** (usually 1–3 weeks) is entirely possible from South Asia. Malaria testing and blood cultures are both needed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Haemoconcentration {level=1 check="round(100*(0.46-0.38)/0.38)"}
In dengue, a patient's haematocrit rises from 0.38 to 0.46. By what percentage has it risen? Give the answer to the nearest whole number.
::: solution
$(0.46-0.38)/0.38 = 0.08/0.38 = 0.2105$, a rise of about **21%**. A rise of this size, together with falling platelets and clinical warning signs, indicates significant plasma leakage.
:::

::: exercise Relapse after a year {level=2}
A 30-year-old man had vivax malaria after a trip to Papua New Guinea and was treated with an ACT alone. Eleven months later, without further travel, he develops fever every 48 hours and his blood film shows *P. vivax* again. Explain why, and what should have been done.
::: solution
*P. vivax* forms dormant **hypnozoites** in the liver that are not killed by blood-stage drugs such as ACTs or chloroquine. They can reactivate weeks to months later, causing a **relapse** without new exposure. Treatment of vivax or ovale malaria should include a drug active against hypnozoites, **primaquine** or **tafenoquine**, after testing for **G6PD deficiency**, since these drugs can cause severe haemolysis in G6PD-deficient people. Pregnancy also contraindicates them; in that case treatment of hypnozoites is deferred, with suppressive chloroquine in the meantime. He now needs blood-stage treatment and radical cure.
:::
:::

::: exercise Fever after Lahore {level=2}
A 19-year-old student returns from three weeks in Lahore, Pakistan, and has a week of rising fever, headache, abdominal discomfort and constipation. Pulse is 84 per minute at 39.5 °C; the spleen is palpable. Malaria tests are negative. What is the likely diagnosis, how should it be confirmed, and what affects the choice of antibiotic?
::: solution
**Likely diagnosis**: **enteric fever** (typhoid), suggested by travel to South Asia, an insidious rising fever with headache and abdominal symptoms, **relative bradycardia** (a pulse of 84 at 39.5 °C) and splenomegaly. **Confirmation**: blood cultures (several sets; bone marrow culture is more sensitive if blood cultures are negative and suspicion is high); stool cultures; the Widal test should not be relied on. **Antibiotic choice**: Pakistan has an outbreak of **extensively drug-resistant typhoid**, resistant to fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins, so empirical treatment for a severely unwell patient from Pakistan would include a **carbapenem**, with azithromycin for milder illness, adjusted when susceptibility results are known. Enteric fever is notifiable, and public health follows up contacts and food handlers.
:::
:::

::: exercise Eosinophilia after Lake Malawi {level=2}
A group of eight students swam in Lake Malawi during a gap-year trip. Six weeks later, three develop fever, urticaria, a dry cough and an eosinophil count of 3.5 × 10⁹/L. What is the likely diagnosis, how is it confirmed, and how should the whole group be managed?
::: solution
**Likely diagnosis**: **acute schistosomiasis (Katayama syndrome)**, a hypersensitivity reaction to migrating schistosomula and early egg-laying, 2–8 weeks after freshwater exposure in an area where schistosomiasis is endemic. **Confirmation**: serology (which may take up to three months to become positive), and later urine and stool microscopy for eggs. **Management**: corticosteroids can relieve severe Katayama symptoms; **praziquantel** is given but is not active against immature worms, so it is given or repeated once the worms have matured (typically around 8–12 weeks after exposure). **The whole group** should be screened by serology at least 6–12 weeks after exposure and treated if positive, even without symptoms, because untreated infection can later cause bladder, genital, liver or spinal cord disease.
:::
:::

A 34-year-old nurse returned to the UK seven days ago after working in a treatment centre during an outbreak of Ebola virus disease. She now has a temperature of 38.4 °C, myalgia and diarrhoea. Discuss the immediate actions, the differential, and how testing should be organised.
::: hint
Think of the risk assessment, infection control, and the commonest serious cause of fever after travel to sub-Saharan Africa.
:::
::: solution
**Risk assessment**: fever within 21 days of leaving an outbreak area, with direct occupational contact with patients, makes her a **high possibility** of viral haemorrhagic fever in the UK risk-assessment algorithm. **Immediate actions**: isolate her in a single room (ideally with an anteroom), limit staff entry, use the specified personal protective equipment, and inform the local infection specialist, the health protection team and the national HCID network urgently. Ideally, people returning from such work are already under active monitoring and know to telephone before attending, so that they can be met and assessed safely.

**Differential**: Ebola virus disease; **malaria** (still the commonest dangerous cause of fever in returning travellers from sub-Saharan Africa, especially if prophylaxis was imperfect); typhoid; influenza, COVID-19 and other common viral infections; gastroenteritis; and bacterial sepsis.

**Testing**: urgent Ebola PCR at a designated high-containment laboratory; **malaria testing must not be delayed** and is done under appropriate containment; other routine tests are limited and handled according to protocol. If Ebola is confirmed she is transferred to a high-level isolation unit; if negative, care continues according to the alternative diagnosis, with repeat testing if the first sample was taken very early in illness.
:::
:::

::: exercise From percent to parasites {level=3 check="0.04*4.5e6"}
A patient with falciparum malaria has a parasitaemia of 4% and a red cell count of 4.5 × 10¹²/L. How many parasitised red cells are there per microlitre of blood? Comment on how this compares with WHO and UK severity thresholds and why the peripheral count may underestimate the total parasite burden.
::: hint
1 L = 10⁶ µL.
:::
::: solution
Red cells per microlitre $= 4.5 \times 10^{12}/10^{6} = 4.5 \times 10^{6}$. Parasitised cells $= 0.04 \times 4.5 \times 10^{6} = 1.8 \times 10^{5}$, or **180,000 per µL**.

A parasitaemia of 4% is below the WHO hyperparasitaemia threshold of 10% but above the UK threshold of 2% at which non-immune travellers receive intravenous artesunate. The peripheral film shows mainly young ring forms: mature falciparum parasites are **sequestered** in the microvasculature, so the total parasite biomass can be much larger than the count suggests, which is one reason the patient's clinical condition, not the count alone, defines severity.
:::
:::
