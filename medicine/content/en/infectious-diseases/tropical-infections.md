Tropical medicine in a connected world is mostly epidemiology performed at the bedside: **the traveller or migrant presents with a syndrome - fever, diarrhoea, rash, eosinophilia, hepatitis, neurology - and the diagnosis depends on asking where, when, how and against what, then choosing between diseases that kill in hours and diseases that smoulder for decades.** The first question is never "what parasite?"; it is "was malaria ever possible?" and the answer still saves lives.

This chapter covers the fever presentations, malaria, the emerging and re-emerging threats, helminths, and the prevention conversation.

## Whose diseases, whose medicine

One honest paragraph about the field's politics belongs in the clinical text, because it changes how care is delivered. The diseases in this chapter burden the world's poorest people and the travellers of its richest, and the pipeline that serves them - neglected-disease drug development, vaccine funding, vector control, migrant access to testing - is shaped by commerce and foreign policy as much as by science, which is why "one health" and "health security" are now the organising vocabularies of global communicable-disease strategy. For the individual clinician the practical consequence is twofold: never ration diagnostic seriousness by a patient's accent, occupation or housing status, and never let the logistics of funding an off-licence antiparasitic or a rabies immunoglobulin dose delay a time-critical treatment that has an agreed national route - knowing that route by telephone number is a clinical skill ([[medical-ethics-law/justice-resources]], [[public-health/global-health]], [[public-health/health-systems-economics]]).

## Two clinical habits worth installing

The first habit is temporal. Whenever a tropical infection is considered, write the dates down: arrival, departure, exposure window, symptom onset, and prophylaxis start and stop. Half of travel medicine is arithmetic against incubation tables, and a fever beginning five days after the first mosquito bite says dengue while a fever beginning eleven months after leaving Papua New Guinea says vivax relapse or something else entirely. The second habit is social: ask, without embarrassment and with interpreters when needed, about fresh-water bathing, animal and bat contact, bites, food and water practices, sexual exposures, traditional remedies, and injections or transfusions abroad, because those questions convert an "unexplained" illness into a short list, and patients answer them more readily than they volunteer them. Neither habit costs money; both change management; and both fail quietly when the patient is labelled "anxiety" or "irritable bowel" in a five-minute consultation, which is how chronic schistosomiasis, strongyloides carriage, and late malaria present in the second or third year after return.

A related habit belongs to the whole team: the traveller's check-up is a prevention visit, and prevention visits are where the primary-care physician earns the outcome. Checking routine immunisations, offering hepatitis A and B and typhoid where indicated, prescribing and actually starting chemoprophylaxis with a drug the patient will take, and naming the dangerous syndromes in writing - fever needs a malaria test within a day, a febrile child returning from an endemic area is seen the same day, animal bites get washed for fifteen minutes before anything else - does more per capita for tropical disease mortality than any clinic that only treats it afterwards, and it is the part of the work that scales.

::: summary
- Fever in a returning traveller is malaria until serially excluded; the destination, the dates and the prophylaxis history narrow everything else.
- Treat the kill-fast list first - falciparum malaria, meningococcus, typhoid, rickettsia, leptospira, viral haemorrhagic fever with its isolation reflex - then the smoulders.
- Eosinophilia is a map, not a diagnosis: tissue invasion versus benign trafficking, and remember that steroids and Strongyloides are a documented lethal pair.
- Several parasites are lifelong residents, not holiday souvenirs - vivax relapse, strongyloides latency, schistosomal fibrosis, echinococcal cysts - so travel history belongs in the problem list, not the discharge summary.
- Emerging infections are ecological stories with clinical endings; the clinician's biosecurity duties are suspicion, isolation, notification and honest risk communication.
- Prevention counselling works when it matches destination, behaviour, budget and timing; a travel clinic two weeks before departure is already late.

## Taking it further

The infectious-diseases mindset in one paragraph: think in source, route, host and time. Identify the source before naming the syndrome; ask what the exposure history permits and what the local resistance map makes likely; adjust everything for the host standing in front of you; and treat the clock as a drug - the sepsis, endocarditis and meningitis outcomes are functions of hours. Then audit yourself against the two failure modes of the specialty: anchoring on the first plausible organism while the atypical presentation evolves, and de-escalating never - the narrow-spectrum switch that should happen on day three and does not. Both failures are curable by the habit of re-asking, at every review, what the story said yesterday that it no longer says today.
