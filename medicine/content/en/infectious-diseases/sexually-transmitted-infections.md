Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are among the most common infections in the world. WHO estimates that more than a million curable STIs are acquired every day. Many cause no symptoms at all, yet they can lead to infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain, cancer, stillbirth and infections of the newborn, and they increase the risk of acquiring and transmitting HIV. In England, gonorrhoea diagnoses reached a record of about 85,000 in 2023, and syphilis diagnoses in 2024 were the highest since 1948.

Sexual health medicine has its own features. Diagnosis depends on a sensitive, non-judgemental history. Testing is directed at the sites of exposure, often in people without symptoms. Treatment of one person is incomplete without treatment of their partners. Confidentiality is central, and safeguarding concerns must be recognised, especially in young people. And one organism, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, has become a test case for antimicrobial resistance.

This chapter covers the sexual health consultation, the bacterial STIs, genital ulcers and syphilis, the viral STIs, and prevention. UK practice follows the British Association for Sexual Health and HIV (BASHH); treatments are described as principles, with individual regimens from current guidelines.

## The sexual health consultation

::: definition The sexual health assessment {#def-consultation}
A **sexual health assessment** combines a structured sexual history, examination where indicated, and testing directed by the history.

The **sexual history** covers: symptoms; the **partners** (number, gender, whether new or regular, and the date of last sex with each); **practices** (vaginal, anal or oral sex, and the sites exposed); **protection** (condoms, HIV PrEP, vaccination); **past STIs** and tests; **pregnancy** risk and contraception; drug and alcohol use around sex (including "chemsex"); and any concerns about coercion, exploitation or abuse.

**Testing** is by site of exposure. For chlamydia and gonorrhoea, **nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs)** are used on a **first-void urine** in men and a **self-taken vulvovaginal swab** in women, with rectal and pharyngeal swabs when those sites have been exposed. Blood is tested for **HIV**, **syphilis** and, according to risk, hepatitis B and C. A test taken soon after exposure may miss infection: the **window periods** are about two weeks for chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAATs, up to twelve weeks for syphilis serology, and 45 days for a fourth-generation HIV test.
:::

The consultation is confidential, and in the UK sexual health services are open access: people can attend without a referral, and records are kept separately from general medical records. Young people under 16 can receive confidential care if they are competent to consent, but their wellbeing must be assessed and possible sexual exploitation or abuse acted on.

**Partner notification** is the process of informing sexual partners that they may have been exposed, so that they can be tested and treated. It can be done by the patient, by the clinic (without revealing the index patient's identity) or by both. It covers partners within a **look-back period** that depends on the infection: for example, about two weeks for a man with symptomatic gonococcal urethritis, three months for gonorrhoea in other cases, four weeks for a man with symptomatic chlamydial urethritis, and six months for asymptomatic chlamydia.

## Bacterial STIs

::: definition Chlamydia, gonorrhoea and related infections {#def-bacterial}
***Chlamydia trachomatis*** is an obligate intracellular bacterium and the commonest bacterial STI. Infection is **asymptomatic** in most women and many men. Symptoms include urethritis and dysuria, vaginal discharge, intermenstrual or post-coital bleeding, and rectal symptoms. Complications include **pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)**, which can cause tubal infertility, ectopic pregnancy and chronic pelvic pain; epididymo-orchitis; sexually acquired reactive arthritis; and conjunctivitis and pneumonia in babies born to infected mothers. Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV), caused by invasive serovars, causes proctitis, mainly in men who have sex with men. Uncomplicated chlamydia is treated with a seven-day course of **doxycycline** in BASHH guidance, with alternatives in pregnancy.

***Neisseria gonorrhoeae*** is a Gram-negative diplococcus. In men it usually causes **urethritis with purulent discharge** a few days after exposure; in women, and at the pharynx and rectum in everyone, infection is often asymptomatic. Complications include PID, epididymitis and **disseminated gonococcal infection**. Because of resistance, samples are taken for **culture and susceptibility testing before treatment**. BASHH (2019) recommends a single intramuscular dose of **ceftriaxone** when susceptibility is unknown, and a **test of cure** for everyone treated.

***Mycoplasma genitalium*** causes urethritis, cervicitis and PID; resistance to macrolides and fluoroquinolones is common, so treatment is guided by resistance testing. ***Trichomonas vaginalis***, a protozoan, causes frothy vaginal discharge and vulvitis and is treated with metronidazole.
:::

Bacterial vaginosis and vulvovaginal candidiasis are common causes of vaginal discharge that are not sexually transmitted infections in the usual sense, and they are part of the differential diagnosis in every assessment.

::: warning
**Treat suspected pelvic inflammatory disease on clinical grounds.** PID is diagnosed clinically in a sexually active woman with lower abdominal pain and adnexal or cervical motion tenderness on bimanual examination, after excluding pregnancy, ectopic pregnancy and surgical emergencies. Negative chlamydia and gonorrhoea tests do **not** exclude it, because many cases involve other organisms or the infection is in the upper tract. Delaying treatment increases the risk of tubal damage and infertility, so broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment covering gonorrhoea, chlamydia and anaerobes is started straight away, and partners are notified.
:::

::: example A discharge after a weekend away {#ex-urethritis}
A 26-year-old man has had three days of dysuria and a yellow urethral discharge. Five days ago he had condomless vaginal sex with a woman he met on holiday, and he has a regular female partner at home. A Gram-stained urethral smear shows numerous polymorphs with **intracellular Gram-negative diplococci**.
::: solution
**Diagnosis**: **gonococcal urethritis**, confirmed presumptively by the Gram stain, which is highly sensitive and specific in symptomatic men.

**Before treatment**: take a urethral swab for **culture and susceptibility** testing, and NAATs for gonorrhoea and chlamydia; offer HIV and syphilis testing (and repeat tests after their window periods). Ask about oral and anal exposure, which would require pharyngeal or rectal samples.

**Treatment**: ceftriaxone as a single intramuscular dose, per current guidance. If chlamydia is also found, it is treated as well. He should avoid sex until he and his partners have been treated.

**Partners**: for a man with symptomatic urethritis, the look-back period for gonorrhoea is about **two weeks** before symptoms; both his holiday partner (if contactable) and his regular partner need testing and treatment. This conversation needs tact: the clinic can notify partners without naming him.

**Test of cure**: a NAAT about two weeks after treatment (or culture from three days), because ceftriaxone-resistant strains are now reported in England, often linked to travel to the Asia–Pacific region.
:::
:::

::: example Joint pains and pustules {#ex-dgi}
A 24-year-old woman has three days of fever, pain moving between her wrists, knees and ankles, and painful swelling along the tendons on the back of her left hand. There are six small pustules on a red base on her forearms and legs. She has a new male partner and is three days after her period.
::: solution
**Pattern**: fever, migratory polyarthralgia, **tenosynovitis** and scattered **pustular skin lesions** form the classic triad of **disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI)**, which follows blood-borne spread from a mucosal infection that is often asymptomatic. Menstruation, pregnancy and complement deficiency increase the risk. The main alternative is septic arthritis of a single joint, which is the other form of DGI; reactive arthritis, meningococcal disease and acute rheumatic conditions are in the differential.

**Investigations**: blood cultures; aspiration of any effusion for Gram stain and culture; NAATs and **cultures from cervix, urethra, rectum and pharynx**; HIV and syphilis tests; pregnancy test.

**Treatment**: admission for **intravenous ceftriaxone**, switched to oral therapy guided by susceptibility after clinical improvement, for a total of usually at least seven days. Chlamydia is tested for and treated. Partner notification and a test of cure follow. Recurrent neisserial infection would prompt testing for **complement deficiency**.
:::
:::

## Genital ulcers and syphilis

::: definition Genital ulcer disease {#def-ulcers}
A **genital ulcer** is a break in the skin or mucosa of the genitals or perianal area. In the UK the commonest infective cause is **genital herpes**, typically multiple, shallow and painful. **Syphilis** classically causes a single, painless, indurated ulcer (a **chancre**) with rubbery regional lymph nodes, though atypical and multiple chancres occur. Less common causes are **LGV**, and in travellers chancroid and donovanosis. Non-infective causes include trauma, fixed drug eruption, Behçet's disease and cancer. Every genital ulcer is tested for herpes (PCR) and for syphilis (PCR of the lesion where available, and serology), because the clinical appearance is unreliable.
:::

**Syphilis**, caused by the spirochaete ***Treponema pallidum***, progresses through stages if untreated:

- **Primary**: a chancre at the site of inoculation, typically about three weeks after exposure (range 9–90 days), healing on its own in a few weeks.
- **Secondary**: weeks to months later, a systemic illness with a widespread rash that often involves the **palms and soles**, mucous patches in the mouth, moist wart-like **condylomata lata**, lymphadenopathy, patchy hair loss, and sometimes hepatitis, kidney involvement or meningitis.
- **Latent**: no signs, only positive serology; **early latent** within the first two years (UK and WHO definitions), **late latent** after that.
- **Tertiary**, years later: gummas (destructive granulomas), cardiovascular syphilis (aortitis and aortic regurgitation) and late neurosyphilis (tabes dorsalis, general paresis).

**Neurosyphilis, ocular and otological syphilis** can occur at any stage, including early disease. Any person with syphilis who has visual symptoms, hearing loss or tinnitus, or neurological symptoms needs urgent assessment.

::: theorem Reading syphilis serology {#thm-serology}
Syphilis serology uses two kinds of test:

- **Treponemal tests** (enzyme immunoassay, TPPA) detect antibodies to *T. pallidum* antigens. They become positive first and **usually stay positive for life**, even after successful treatment, so they show that infection has occurred at some time but not whether it is active.
- **Non-treponemal tests** (RPR, VDRL) detect antibodies to lipid antigens released by damaged cells. Reported as a titre (1:2, 1:4, 1:8…), they **reflect disease activity**: they rise in active infection and fall after treatment.

Screening is done with a treponemal test; a positive result is confirmed with a second treponemal test and quantified with an RPR. Adequate treatment is shown by a **fourfold (two-dilution) fall** in the RPR titre, usually within 6–12 months; a fourfold rise indicates reinfection or relapse. A positive treponemal test with negative RPR usually means previously treated or very early or late infection, interpreted with the history.
:::

**Treatment** of early syphilis is a single intramuscular dose of **benzathine benzylpenicillin**, and of late latent syphilis three weekly doses; neurosyphilis needs higher drug levels in the cerebrospinal fluid and is treated with daily penicillin for about two weeks. Alternatives exist for penicillin allergy outside pregnancy. Within 24 hours of the first dose, many people with early syphilis develop the **Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction** (fever, headache, myalgia and worsening rash) caused by the release of treponemal products; it settles within a day, but in pregnancy it can trigger contractions.

**Congenital syphilis** results from transplacental infection and can cause miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth and a range of features in the infant (rash, hepatosplenomegaly, snuffles, bone lesions and, later, Hutchinson's teeth and deafness). It is prevented by screening every pregnancy and treating promptly with penicillin; the UK screens at the booking visit.

::: example A positive screen at booking {#ex-congenital}
A 27-year-old woman at 12 weeks of her first pregnancy has a positive syphilis screen at booking. Confirmatory treponemal testing is positive and RPR is 1:32. She has no symptoms and recalls no rash or ulcer. Her only partner for the last two years is her husband. Her screen in a previous year, before a termination of pregnancy, was negative.
::: solution
**Interpretation**: positive treponemal tests with a high RPR, after a negative screen within two years, indicate **early latent syphilis**: recent, active infection without current signs.

**Treatment**: benzathine benzylpenicillin by intramuscular injection, given urgently. **Penicillin** is the only treatment shown to reliably treat the fetus; if she were allergic, desensitisation would be considered rather than an alternative drug. She is warned about the **Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction**, which in pregnancy can cause contractions or fetal distress, and told to seek help if it occurs. She is tested for HIV, hepatitis B and other STIs, and examined for signs of secondary syphilis.

**Follow-up**: RPR is monitored through pregnancy to confirm a fourfold fall; a fetal ultrasound looks for signs of fetal infection later in pregnancy. At birth, the baby is examined and tested by a paediatrician, comparing the baby's RPR with the mother's.

**Partner**: her husband needs testing and treatment urgently, before they have sex again, or she will be re-infected. This needs a sensitive, confidential conversation, and the clinic can support partner notification.
:::
:::

## Viral STIs

::: definition Herpes, HPV and other viral STIs {#def-viral}
**Herpes simplex virus (HSV)** types 1 and 2 infect mucosal cells, then establish **lifelong latency** in sensory ganglia (sacral ganglia for genital infection) and can reactivate. Genital HSV-1, often acquired through oral sex, now causes a large share of first genital episodes in young adults. A **first episode** can be severe: multiple painful ulcers, dysuria, inguinal lymphadenopathy, fever, and sometimes urinary retention or aseptic meningitis. It is treated with an oral antiviral (aciclovir or valaciclovir) for about five days. **Recurrences** are milder and shorter, and are most frequent with HSV-2. The virus is also shed from normal-looking skin (**asymptomatic shedding**), which explains most transmission. Primary infection late in pregnancy risks **neonatal herpes**, which can be fatal.

**Human papillomavirus (HPV)**: low-risk types (mainly 6 and 11) cause **anogenital warts**; high-risk types (mainly 16 and 18) cause cervical, anal, penile, vulval, vaginal and oropharyngeal cancers. Infection is very common and usually clears within two years. The **HPV vaccine** prevents infection with the types it covers; in the UK it is offered to adolescents (a single dose since 2023) and to gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men up to age 45.

**Hepatitis B** is sexually transmissible and vaccine-preventable; vaccination is offered to people at higher sexual risk. **Hepatitis A** and **hepatitis C** can also be sexually transmitted, especially among men who have sex with men. **HIV** is covered in [[infectious-diseases/hiv]], and **mpox**, which spread through sexual networks from 2022, presents with painful anogenital lesions, proctitis and lymphadenopathy.
:::

::: example Herpes again {#ex-recurrent}
A 28-year-old woman was diagnosed with genital HSV-2 two years ago. She has had eight recurrences in the past year, each lasting four to five days, and they are affecting her relationship and her mood. She and her partner are planning a pregnancy.
::: solution
**Options**: with six or more recurrences a year, **suppressive antiviral therapy** (daily aciclovir or valaciclovir) reduces the frequency of recurrences substantially and reduces, but does not eliminate, asymptomatic shedding and transmission. Treatment is reviewed after about a year to see whether recurrences have become less frequent. Episodic treatment started at the first warning symptoms is an alternative for people with fewer recurrences.

**Transmission and disclosure**: transmission can occur between recurrences. Condoms and suppressive therapy each reduce the risk. If her partner has never had HSV-2, testing him for HSV-2 antibodies can clarify his risk. Disclosure is her decision, and supportive counselling helps.

**Pregnancy**: recurrent infection carries a low risk of neonatal herpes, because she has antibodies that cross the placenta. Suppressive aciclovir from about 36 weeks reduces recurrences at delivery and the need for caesarean section. The greatest danger is **primary** infection acquired in the third trimester, which is managed differently, usually with caesarean birth. Her mental health should also be addressed; recurrent herpes commonly causes distress.
:::
:::

## Prevention and the problem of resistance

STI control combines several approaches:

- **Testing and treatment** of people with symptoms and of people without symptoms at risk, with **partner notification** to break chains of transmission.
- **Condoms**, which greatly reduce transmission of most STIs, and **HIV PrEP**.
- **Vaccination** against HPV, hepatitis A and B, and mpox for those at risk. In August 2025 England started a programme offering the meningococcal B vaccine 4CMenB to gay and bisexual men at higher risk, because it gives partial cross-protection against gonorrhoea.
- **Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxyPEP)**, a single dose taken within 72 hours after sex, which reduces syphilis and chlamydia. BASHH guidance (2025) recommends it for gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men and trans women at elevated risk of syphilis, while noting concern about antimicrobial resistance and effects on the microbiome.

***N. gonorrhoeae*** **has acquired resistance to every antibiotic used to treat it**: sulphonamides, penicillin, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and more recently oral cephalosporins and azithromycin. Ceftriaxone is the last reliable empirical treatment, and resistant strains are being reported in the UK and elsewhere. Responses include culture before treatment, test of cure, national surveillance with genomic typing, rapid contact tracing for resistant cases, and new oral antibiotics such as zoliflodacin and gepotidacin, which have completed phase 3 trials. This links to the wider problem discussed in [[infectious-diseases/stewardship-outbreaks]].

::: history
Albert Neisser described the gonococcus in 1879. In 1905 Fritz Schaudinn and Erich Hoffmann identified *Treponema pallidum* in syphilitic lesions, and in 1906 August von Wassermann introduced the first blood test for syphilis. In 1910 Paul Ehrlich and Sahachiro Hata introduced arsphenamine (Salvarsan), the first synthetic antimicrobial drug, for syphilis. In 1943 John Mahoney and colleagues in the United States showed that penicillin cured early syphilis, and it remains the treatment of choice. The history of STIs also includes one of medicine's worst ethical failures: in the Tuskegee study (1932–1972), the US Public Health Service observed untreated syphilis in hundreds of Black men without informed consent, and withheld penicillin after it became standard treatment. Its exposure led to modern rules on research ethics and consent ([[medical-ethics-law/research-ethics]]).
:::

::: quiz
A 45-year-old man has a positive treponemal EIA and TPPA, with RPR negative. He was treated for syphilis eight years ago and his RPR fell appropriately. He has no symptoms and no new partners. What is the most likely interpretation?
- [ ] Active secondary syphilis
- [x] Previously treated syphilis with persistent treponemal antibodies
- [ ] A false-positive treponemal test
- [ ] Neurosyphilis
::: solution
Treponemal antibodies usually persist for life after treatment, while a negative RPR indicates no current disease activity. With documented adequate treatment and no new risk, this is a "serological scar". A new risk, symptoms, or a rising RPR would change the interpretation.
:::

::: summary
- STIs are common and often asymptomatic; testing is guided by a non-judgemental sexual history and directed to every site of exposure, with attention to window periods.
- Chlamydia, the commonest bacterial STI, is usually silent and can cause PID and infertility; it is treated with doxycycline.
- Gonorrhoea is cultured before treatment, treated with ceftriaxone, and followed by a test of cure; it has developed resistance to every antibiotic class used against it.
- PID is treated on clinical suspicion; negative tests do not exclude it.
- Genital ulcers are tested for both herpes and syphilis. Syphilis has primary, secondary, latent and tertiary stages, and can affect the eye, ear and nervous system at any stage.
- Treponemal tests stay positive for life; non-treponemal titres (RPR) track activity, and a fourfold fall shows adequate treatment. Penicillin treats syphilis and prevents congenital infection.
- Partner notification, condoms, PrEP, vaccines (HPV, hepatitis B, 4CMenB) and doxyPEP prevent transmission.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Counting dilutions {level=1 check="3"}
A man's RPR titre falls from 1:64 at diagnosis to 1:8 nine months after treatment. By how many twofold dilutions has it fallen?
::: solution
$64 \to 32 \to 16 \to 8$: **three** twofold dilutions, an eightfold fall. This exceeds the fourfold (two-dilution) fall that indicates adequate treatment.
:::
:::

::: exercise Too early to tell {level=1}
A woman attends five days after condomless sex with a new partner, worried about infection. She has no symptoms. What tests are useful now, and when should she be retested?
::: solution
Tests taken now will detect infections acquired earlier but may miss infection from this exposure. A chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAAT can be done now, but should be **repeated two weeks after the exposure**. HIV testing with a fourth-generation test should be repeated at **45 days**, and syphilis serology at about **twelve weeks**. Emergency contraception should be discussed (she is within the time window for an intrauterine device or oral options), and HIV PEP considered if the partner is known to have HIV or is from a group with high prevalence (PEP must start within 72 hours).
:::
:::

::: exercise Chlamydia screening and false positives {level=2 check="round(100*0.01*0.95/(0.01*0.95 + 0.99*0.005))/100"}
A chlamydia NAAT has sensitivity 95% and specificity 99.5%. In a population with a prevalence of 1%, what is the positive predictive value? Give the answer to two decimal places.
::: solution
$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{0.01 \times 0.95}{0.01 \times 0.95 + 0.99 \times 0.005} = \frac{0.0095}{0.01445} \approx 0.66
$$

About one positive result in three is a false positive at this prevalence. This matters for a diagnosis with relationship consequences, which is why low-prevalence settings and some sites (such as the pharynx for gonorrhoea) use confirmatory testing.
:::
:::

::: exercise Hepatitis B serology {level=2}
Three people attending a sexual health clinic have the following hepatitis B results. Interpret each. (a) HBsAg negative, anti-HBc negative, anti-HBs 150 IU/L. (b) HBsAg negative, anti-HBc positive, anti-HBs 80 IU/L. (c) HBsAg positive, anti-HBc positive (IgG), anti-HBs negative, on two occasions six months apart.
::: solution
(a) **Immune through vaccination**: anti-HBs alone, without anti-HBc (which only follows natural infection). (b) **Past infection that has cleared**, with natural immunity; this person can reactivate the virus under strong immunosuppression such as rituximab ([[infectious-diseases/immunocompromised-host]]). (c) **Chronic hepatitis B infection**: surface antigen persisting for more than six months. This person needs HBeAg, viral load, liver assessment, testing for hepatitis D, specialist referral, and vaccination and testing of partners and household contacts.
:::
:::

::: exercise Pelvic pain {level=2}
A 19-year-old woman has a week of lower abdominal pain, deep dyspareunia and intermenstrual bleeding. Temperature 37.8 °C. On examination she has cervical motion tenderness and bilateral adnexal tenderness. Pregnancy test is negative. Swabs are taken. What is the diagnosis, what must be excluded, and how is she managed?
::: solution
**Diagnosis**: clinical **pelvic inflammatory disease**. **Exclude**: ectopic pregnancy (the negative pregnancy test helps), appendicitis, ovarian torsion or cyst accident, and endometriosis; tubo-ovarian abscess if she is very unwell (ultrasound). **Management**: start antibiotics **immediately**, without waiting for results, with a regimen that covers gonorrhoea, chlamydia, *M. genitalium* where indicated, and anaerobes, as in current BASHH guidance; admit if she is severely unwell, pregnant, has an abscess or cannot take oral treatment. Review after 72 hours. Partner notification and treatment are essential to prevent reinfection. She should be told about the risks of future infertility and ectopic pregnancy, and that early treatment reduces them.
:::
:::

::: exercise A painful testis {level=2}
A 22-year-old man has a two-day history of right testicular pain and swelling, with dysuria. The epididymis is tender and the testis lies normally; the cremasteric reflex is present. What are the important differential diagnoses, and how does management differ from that of a 70-year-old man with the same symptoms?
::: solution
**Differential**: **testicular torsion** must be excluded first, because it needs surgery within hours; the gradual onset, dysuria, a normally lying testis and a preserved cremasteric reflex make torsion less likely, but any real doubt means urgent surgical exploration. The most likely diagnosis is **epididymo-orchitis**, which in a young sexually active man is usually caused by **chlamydia or gonorrhoea**. Testing: NAATs and urethral culture; treatment covers both organisms, with partner notification. In a **70-year-old**, epididymo-orchitis is usually caused by **enteric Gram-negative bacteria** related to urinary tract infection or bladder outflow obstruction, so a midstream urine culture is sent, an antibiotic for urinary pathogens is chosen, and the urinary tract is investigated.
:::
:::

::: exercise Treatment failure {level=3}
A 30-year-old man who has sex with men was treated with ceftriaxone for rectal gonorrhoea. His test of cure NAAT three weeks later is positive. He reports no sex since treatment. Discuss what may have happened, what should be done, and why this case matters beyond the individual.
::: hint
Consider reinfection, persistent nucleic acid, and true resistance; think about surveillance.
:::
::: solution
**Possible explanations**: (1) **reinfection**, if he has had sex (he says not, but the history should be explored sensitively, including with untreated regular partners); (2) a **false-positive or persisting nucleic acid**, less likely three weeks after treatment; (3) **treatment failure due to ceftriaxone resistance**, or inadequate treatment at the rectal site.

**Actions**: take **cultures from all exposed sites** before any further treatment, so that the organism's susceptibility can be measured; inform the local health protection team and send the isolate to the national reference laboratory for susceptibility testing and genomic typing; treat according to specialist advice, using an alternative regimen guided by susceptibility; repeat test of cure; and trace and test all partners urgently, including those abroad if possible.

**Wider importance**: ceftriaxone is the last reliable empirical treatment for gonorrhoea. Each resistant case is a potential start of spread in sexual networks, and national surveillance depends on detecting and reporting such cases quickly. The case illustrates why culture and test of cure are standard in UK guidance.
:::
:::

::: exercise Syphilis that affects the eyes {level=3}
A 38-year-old man with well-controlled HIV has a week of blurred vision in both eyes and floaters. Two months ago he had a rash on his palms that resolved. Ophthalmology finds bilateral uveitis. His RPR is 1:128 and the treponemal tests are positive. Discuss the diagnosis, the investigations and the management.
::: solution
**Diagnosis**: **ocular syphilis** complicating **secondary syphilis** (the palmar rash). Ocular, otological and neurological syphilis can occur at any stage and are more common, though not exclusively, in people with HIV. A high RPR confirms active infection.

**Investigations**: urgent ophthalmology assessment; full neurological examination and hearing assessment; **lumbar puncture** for cerebrospinal fluid cell count, protein and VDRL to look for neurosyphilis, according to local guidance (ocular syphilis is managed as neurosyphilis whatever the CSF shows); HIV viral load and CD4; tests for other STIs.

**Management**: treatment as for **neurosyphilis**, with high-dose intravenous benzylpenicillin (or an equivalent regimen giving treponemicidal levels in the central nervous system) for about two weeks, in liaison with ophthalmology, who may add corticosteroids for inflammation. The Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction can transiently worsen eye inflammation. Follow-up includes RPR, eye examination and repeat CSF where abnormal. Partner notification covers the previous six months or more, and doxyPEP and HPV and hepatitis vaccination can be discussed for the future.
:::
:::
