Tuberculosis is the world's leading infectious cause of death and, in most rich countries, the disease clinicians know least well: **one mycobacterium in one third of humanity, usually asleep, occasionally devastating, and diagnosable and curable - the failures are almost always failures of suspicion, of infection control, and of six-month treatment systems rather than of science.**

This chapter covers mycobacteriology and pathogenesis, latent and pulmonary tuberculosis, extranodal disease, drug regimens and resistance, and public health control.

## Control: the boring machinery that eliminates diseases

A word on BCG, because clinicians misread it constantly: neonatal vaccination protects mainly children against severe disseminated and meningeal tuberculosis and does little reliable work against adult pulmonary disease, which is why a vaccinated person still gets pulmonary TB, why the tuberculin test is confounded by the scar and the interferon test is not, and why national policies differ according to disease burden and childhood mortality rather than according to vaccine quality ([[public-health/vaccination-programmes]], [[#def-latent]], [[clinical-skills/laboratory-tests]]). A word too on the chest radiograph's double life - as an early triage instrument in symptomatic adults and as a screening gate before latent-TB therapy, where its normality lowers but never empties the probability of active disease in immunosuppression ([[clinical-skills/medical-imaging]], [[immunology/immunodeficiency]]).

Tuberculosis ends, where it ends, through an unglamorous chain: rapid diagnosis in symptomatic people, immediate airborne precautions, notification that actually triggers action, structured contact investigation with children prioritised, treatment regimens people can afford to complete, drug-susceptibility testing everywhere, rational use of latent-infection therapy in defined risk groups, and vaccine policy kept honest about what BCG does and does not do. Every link is a commissioning decision and a staffing rota, which is why elimination targets move with housing policy and health-system reach, not with guidelines. Routine laboratory and radiographic follow-up in uncomplicated, fully susceptible, improving pulmonary tuberculosis can be lighter than tradition suggests - symptom-led review, culture conversion at the defined milestone, and treatment-supervision contact are the load-bearing elements, while serial imaging and endless bloods mostly generate anxiety and cost - but monitoring must be heavier, not equal, in pregnancy, HIV, liver and kidney disease, old age, and every resistant regimen, and the follow-up schedule belongs in the shared-care document that the TB nurse, the GP and the chest clinic all read ([[pharmacology/dosing-principles]], [[public-health/health-systems-economics]], [[clinical-skills/records-handover]]). The clinician's role in that machinery is threefold and non-optional: never miss the diagnosis that is coughing in front of you, never break the chain you have started (notification, contact list, follow-up dates), and never prescribe a fluoroquinolone for a cough that might be tuberculosis without a thought about what that single choice does to diagnosis, to resistance and to the next patient who arrives later and sicker than this one would have.

::: summary
- One third of humanity carries latent tuberculosis; disease is what happens when containment fails, and every risk factor - HIV, TNF blockade, steroids, diabetes, age, undernutrition - is a containment failure waiting to be announced.
- Cough beyond three weeks with constitutional symptoms plus a risk context is tuberculosis until rapid testing says otherwise; respiratory isolation and notification come before confirmation.
- IGRA and tuberculin tests diagnose infection, never disease; exclude active disease before prophylaxis and always image and screen first.
- Drug-susceptible treatment is a six-month rifampicin-anchored architecture whose true difficulty is the interaction map, the hepatotoxicity surveillance and the adherence system, not the tablets.
- Extrapulmonary TB speaks every organ's language - nodes, pleura, peritoneum, urine, spine, meninges, pericardium, marrow - and the diagnosis is usually made by someone who asked the question.
- Resistance is manufactured by broken programmes and cured only by specialist centres, long all-oral regimens and social support that makes adherence possible.
:::

## Taking it further

The infectious-diseases mindset in one paragraph: think in source, route, host and time. Identify the source before naming the syndrome; ask what the exposure history permits and what the local resistance map makes likely; adjust everything for the host standing in front of you; and treat the clock as a drug - the sepsis, endocarditis and meningitis outcomes are functions of hours. Then audit yourself against the two failure modes of the specialty: anchoring on the first plausible organism while the atypical presentation evolves, and de-escalating never - the narrow-spectrum switch that should happen on day three and does not. Both failures are curable by the habit of re-asking, at every review, what the story said yesterday that it no longer says today.
