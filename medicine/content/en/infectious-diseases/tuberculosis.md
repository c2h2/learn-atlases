Tuberculosis (TB) is an ancient disease that is curable with cheap drugs, yet it remains the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. WHO's 2025 global report estimated that 10.7 million people fell ill with TB in 2024 and 1.23 million died. About a quarter of the world's population is thought to have been infected at some time. Most of them will never become ill; the bacteria stay contained for life. The challenge is to find and cure the people with active disease, who spread it, and to treat infection in people at high risk of progressing.

TB rewards slow, careful thinking. Its symptoms are vague and develop over weeks. It can affect almost any organ. Its treatment lasts months and needs several drugs together. Its management always has a public-health side: every case was caught from someone, and may have passed it on.

This chapter covers the organism and how infection becomes disease, pulmonary TB, treatment, TB infection without disease, extrapulmonary TB and drug resistance. UK practice follows NICE (NG33) and UK Health Security Agency guidance; international practice follows WHO. Regimens are described as principles; treatment is always supervised by a specialist TB service.

## The organism and the infection

::: definition *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* {#def-mtb}
***Mycobacterium tuberculosis*** is a slow-growing, strictly aerobic, rod-shaped bacterium. Together with *M. bovis* (from cattle) and *M. africanum* it forms the *M. tuberculosis* complex, all of which cause TB. Its cell wall is rich in long-chain **mycolic acids**, which make it waterproof, resistant to many disinfectants and drugs, and **acid-fast**: once stained with a dye such as carbol fuchsin (Ziehl–Neelsen stain) or auramine (fluorescence), it resists decolorisation by acid and alcohol. It divides only about once a day, so culture takes two to six weeks.

TB is spread by **airborne droplet nuclei**: tiny particles expelled by coughing, which stay suspended in the air for hours and are small enough to reach the alveoli. Only people with disease of the lungs or larynx are infectious; the risk is greatest from people whose sputum smears are positive and those with cavities, and it depends on closeness and duration of contact.
:::

::: proof
**From infection to disease.** Inhaled bacilli are engulfed by alveolar macrophages. Unlike most bacteria, *M. tuberculosis* prevents the phagosome from fusing with lysosomes and survives inside the macrophage. It multiplies slowly and spreads to the hilar lymph nodes and, in small numbers, through the blood to other organs.

After two to eight weeks, a cell-mediated immune response develops. CD4 T helper 1 cells release **interferon-gamma**, which activates macrophages to kill intracellular bacteria, and **tumour necrosis factor** helps to organise and maintain the **granuloma**: a ball of activated macrophages (epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells) surrounded by lymphocytes. Its centre often undergoes **caseous necrosis**, a cheese-like dead tissue. The initial lung lesion is the **Ghon focus**; with the involved hilar node it forms the **Ghon complex**, which may later calcify.

In most people the granulomas contain the infection: the bacilli survive in a slowly replicating state without causing symptoms. This is **TB infection** (traditionally "latent TB"). Disease develops when the balance tips: either soon after infection (**primary TB**, especially in young children and people with HIV) or years later (**reactivation**). Without HIV, about 5–10% of infected people develop disease in their lifetime, half of them within the first two years. The risk rises sharply with HIV, treatment with TNF inhibitors or other immunosuppressants, recent infection, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, silicosis, malnutrition, smoking, alcohol misuse and young age. In reactivation the bacteria multiply in the well-oxygenated **lung apices**; caseous centres liquefy and drain into a bronchus, leaving a **cavity** that may contain a billion bacilli and that spreads them in the sputum.
:::

## Pulmonary tuberculosis

::: definition Active pulmonary tuberculosis {#def-pulmonary}
**Active pulmonary TB** is disease of the lung caused by multiplying *M. tuberculosis*. Typical features develop over weeks to months: a **cough lasting more than two to three weeks**, sputum, sometimes haemoptysis, fever, **night sweats**, weight loss and fatigue. The chest radiograph classically shows upper-lobe consolidation with **cavitation**, but it can show hilar lymphadenopathy, a pleural effusion, a **miliary** pattern of tiny nodules (from blood-borne spread) or be nearly normal, especially in people with HIV.

**Diagnosis** rests on finding the organism in respiratory samples (sputum, induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage; at least two or three samples including an early-morning one):

- **Smear microscopy** for acid-fast bacilli: rapid and cheap, but needs about 5,000–10,000 bacilli per mL, so it misses many cases.
- **Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs)** such as Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra: detect *M. tuberculosis* DNA within hours and at the same time detect mutations in the *rpoB* gene that cause **rifampicin resistance**. WHO recommends a rapid molecular test as the initial diagnostic test.
- **Culture**, preferably in liquid media: the most sensitive test, and needed for full drug-susceptibility testing. In England, cultured isolates undergo **whole-genome sequencing**, which identifies the species, predicts resistance and shows whether cases are linked by transmission.
:::

TB is **notifiable** in the UK. Diagnosis triggers **contact tracing**: household and other close contacts are assessed for active disease and TB infection. A person with suspected infectious pulmonary TB in hospital is cared for in a single room with airborne precautions (a negative-pressure room if drug resistance is suspected); with drug-susceptible disease they are usually regarded as no longer infectious after two weeks of effective treatment.

Everyone diagnosed with TB is offered an **HIV test**, and tested for hepatitis B and C, diabetes and kidney and liver function before treatment.

::: example A cough that went on for months {#ex-cavitary}
A 45-year-old man who moved to the UK from India eight years ago has had a productive cough for three months, with night sweats and 7 kg of weight loss. He lives with his wife and two teenage children. The chest radiograph shows consolidation and a 3 cm cavity in the right upper lobe. Sputum smear is positive for acid-fast bacilli; Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra detects *M. tuberculosis* with **no rifampicin resistance**. HIV test negative; liver and kidney function normal.
::: solution
**Diagnosis**: smear-positive, cavitary pulmonary TB, presumed drug-susceptible pending culture and sequencing.

**Treatment**: the standard regimen for drug-susceptible pulmonary TB is a **two-month intensive phase** of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, then a **four-month continuation phase** of isoniazid and rifampicin (written **2HRZE/4HR**). Pyridoxine is given with isoniazid to prevent neuropathy. Ethambutol is stopped once susceptibility to isoniazid is confirmed. Before starting: baseline liver and kidney function, and visual acuity and colour vision because of ethambutol. Dosing is by body weight according to the formulary.

**Infection control and public health**: he is infectious. He should be isolated (at home if appropriate, avoiding new contacts) until he has had two weeks of treatment. The case is notified to the health protection team, and his wife and children are screened for active TB and TB infection.

**Adherence**: treatment fails mainly when it is not taken. A named case manager, fixed-dose combination tablets, and directly or video-observed therapy where needed are standard in the UK. Sputum is repeated at two months; a positive culture then needs review for adherence and resistance.
:::
:::

## Treatment of tuberculosis

::: theorem Principles of TB treatment {#thm-treatment}
1. **Use several drugs together.** A large bacillary population always contains drug-resistant mutants; combining drugs to which the strain is susceptible ensures that no mutant resistant to all of them survives.
2. **Treat for months.** Bacilli in caseous material and inside macrophages replicate slowly and are killed slowly. An **intensive phase** with several drugs reduces the bacterial load quickly; a **continuation phase** with fewer drugs sterilises the remaining "persisters" and prevents relapse.
3. **Make sure it is taken.** Incomplete treatment causes relapse and acquired resistance. Treatment is supported, and sometimes directly observed, for its whole length.
:::

::: proof
**Why monotherapy fails.** Spontaneous chromosomal mutations conferring resistance arise at characteristic frequencies: roughly one bacillus in $10^{6}$ is resistant to isoniazid and one in $10^{8}$ to rifampicin. A cavity may contain $10^{8}$–$10^{9}$ bacilli, so it already holds about $10^{2}$–$10^{3}$ isoniazid-resistant and $1$–$10$ rifampicin-resistant organisms before any treatment. Because the mutations are independent, the frequency of a bacillus resistant to both is about $10^{-6} \times 10^{-8} = 10^{-14}$, and no such organism is expected in a single patient. Treating with isoniazid alone kills the susceptible majority and leaves the resistant minority to grow; treating with both drugs kills all of them. This is the logic that the 1948 streptomycin trial exposed when resistance emerged within months of single-drug treatment.
:::

| Drug | Main adverse effects and points to know |
|---|---|
| Isoniazid (H) | Hepatitis; peripheral neuropathy (prevented by pyridoxine, especially in people with diabetes, alcohol dependence, malnutrition, kidney disease, HIV or pregnancy) |
| Rifampicin (R) | Hepatitis; orange-red urine, tears and sweat; **potent enzyme inducer** that reduces levels of hormonal contraceptives, warfarin, methadone, many antiretrovirals and other drugs; rarely thrombocytopenia and flu-like reactions |
| Pyrazinamide (Z) | Hepatitis (the most hepatotoxic of the four); raised urate and gout; rash |
| Ethambutol (E) | **Optic neuritis** with loss of visual acuity and red–green colour vision; dose reduced in kidney disease |

**Drug-induced liver injury** is the most common serious toxicity. A small rise in transaminases is common and harmless. Treatment is usually stopped if ALT rises above five times the upper limit of normal, above three times with symptoms, or if the bilirubin rises, and the drugs are then reintroduced one at a time under specialist supervision.

**Shorter regimens.** As of 2026, WHO also recommends a **four-month regimen** of rifapentine, isoniazid, moxifloxacin and pyrazinamide for people aged 12 and over with drug-susceptible pulmonary TB, and a four-month regimen for children with non-severe disease. **Longer treatment** is used for TB meningitis (usually 12 months) and sometimes for bone and joint TB. **Corticosteroids** are added for TB meningitis and pericarditis.

::: example How many resistant bacilli? {#ex-mutants}
A cavity contains about $10^{9}$ bacilli. Using resistance frequencies of $10^{-6}$ for isoniazid and $10^{-8}$ for rifampicin, estimate the expected number of bacilli resistant to (a) isoniazid, (b) rifampicin, (c) both.
::: solution
(a) $10^{9} \times 10^{-6} = 10^{3}$, about 1,000 isoniazid-resistant bacilli.

(b) $10^{9} \times 10^{-8} = 10$ rifampicin-resistant bacilli.

(c) $10^{9} \times 10^{-14} = 10^{-5}$: on average one in 100,000 such cavities would contain a single doubly resistant bacillus. Combination therapy therefore has an overwhelming chance of killing every organism, provided all drugs are taken. Multidrug resistance arises mainly when treatment is interrupted or effectively given as monotherapy, which allows resistance to be selected one drug at a time.
:::
:::

## TB infection without disease

::: definition TB infection (latent TB) {#def-latent}
**TB infection** (latent TB infection) is a state of persistent immune response to *M. tuberculosis* without clinical, radiological or microbiological evidence of active disease. The person has no symptoms, a normal chest radiograph (or only old calcified lesions), and is **not infectious**. It is detected indirectly:

- **Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs)** measure interferon-gamma released by the person's T cells when exposed to antigens (ESAT-6 and CFP-10) that are present in *M. tuberculosis* but absent from BCG and most environmental mycobacteria.
- The **tuberculin skin test (TST, Mantoux)** measures the skin induration 48–72 hours after intradermal tuberculin. It is cheaper but cross-reacts with BCG vaccination and environmental mycobacteria.

Neither test distinguishes infection from active disease, and both can be falsely negative when immunity is weak.
:::

Testing for TB infection makes sense only when a positive result would lead to treatment. In the UK it is offered to close contacts of people with pulmonary TB; to people aged 16–35 who have arrived within the last five years from countries with a high incidence of TB; to people with HIV; and before treatment with TNF inhibitors or other biological drugs, organ transplantation and dialysis. Treatment reduces the risk of later disease substantially. **NICE** recommends either **three months of isoniazid with rifampicin** or **six months of isoniazid** (each with pyridoxine). WHO also lists **three months of weekly rifapentine with isoniazid**, four months of daily rifampicin, and one month of daily rifapentine with isoniazid.

::: warning
**Exclude active TB before treating TB infection, and don't use a negative IGRA to rule out active TB.** A person with unrecognised active disease who is given isoniazid alone receives monotherapy, which can select isoniazid resistance. Everyone with a positive test therefore needs symptom review and a chest radiograph, and sputum tests if there is any doubt. Conversely, IGRAs are negative in a substantial minority of people with active TB, especially the very ill, children and people with HIV. When symptoms, imaging or histology suggest TB, send samples for microbiology whatever the IGRA shows.
:::

::: example Screening the household {#ex-contact}
The wife of the man in [[#ex-cavitary]] is 41, well, and takes a combined oral contraceptive pill. Her IGRA is positive and her chest radiograph is normal. Their 15-year-old son is well; his first IGRA, taken three weeks after his father's diagnosis, is negative. How should each be managed?
::: solution
**The wife** has no symptoms and a normal radiograph, so she has **TB infection** rather than active disease. Recent infection from a household case carries a high risk of progression, so treatment is offered: in NICE guidance, three months of isoniazid with rifampicin, or six months of isoniazid, each with pyridoxine. If rifampicin is used, it will make her **contraceptive pill unreliable**, and she needs an alternative method (such as an intrauterine device) during treatment and for four weeks afterwards. Liver function is checked at baseline, and she is told to stop and seek advice if she develops symptoms of hepatitis.

**The son** may have been infected too recently for the IGRA to become positive: it takes two to eight weeks for the immune response to develop. His test is **repeated about six weeks after his last exposure**. If it becomes positive, active TB is excluded and he is treated for TB infection. If he had symptoms at any point, he would be assessed for active disease straight away, whatever the IGRA showed.
:::
:::

## Extrapulmonary tuberculosis

::: definition Extrapulmonary tuberculosis {#def-extrapulmonary}
**Extrapulmonary TB** is disease outside the lung parenchyma, from blood-borne or lymphatic spread. It accounts for nearly half of UK cases and is more common in people with HIV, children, women and people of South Asian and African origin. Major forms:

- **Lymph node TB**, the commonest: painless, slowly enlarging, usually cervical nodes that may become matted, fluctuant or discharge (scrofula).
- **Pleural TB**: an exudative lymphocytic effusion, often with a raised adenosine deaminase; pleural biopsy has a higher yield than fluid culture.
- **TB meningitis**: subacute headache, fever and confusion, cranial nerve palsies, hydrocephalus and strokes.
- **Spinal TB (Pott's disease)**: destruction of adjacent vertebral bodies and the disc between them, usually in the thoracic spine, with paravertebral or psoas abscesses, kyphosis (gibbus) and risk of cord compression.
- **Abdominal TB**: ileocaecal disease mimicking Crohn's disease, and peritonitis with ascites.
- **Genitourinary TB**: sterile pyuria, haematuria, ureteric strictures, infertility.
- **Pericardial TB**: effusion and, later, constrictive pericarditis.
- **Miliary (disseminated) TB**: widespread blood-borne spread with tiny nodules in the lungs, liver, spleen, bone marrow and choroid of the eye.

Diagnosis usually needs **tissue or fluid** from the affected site for histology (caseating granulomas), NAAT and culture. Culture also allows drug-susceptibility testing, so it should always be attempted.
:::

::: example A slow headache {#ex-tb-meningitis}
A 26-year-old woman has had two weeks of headache and fever and is now drowsy and confused. She has double vision from a left sixth cranial nerve palsy. Sodium is 128 mmol/L. CT shows mild hydrocephalus. Cerebrospinal fluid: opening pressure raised, white cells 240/µL (85% lymphocytes), protein 2.1 g/L, glucose 1.6 mmol/L with blood glucose 6.0 mmol/L. Gram stain negative.
::: solution
**Interpretation**: a subacute meningitis with **lymphocytic** CSF, **very high protein** and **low glucose** (CSF:blood ratio $1.6/6.0 \approx 0.27$, below the normal of about 0.6), cranial nerve palsy, hydrocephalus and hyponatraemia is typical of **TB meningitis**. The differential includes cryptococcal meningitis (test CSF cryptococcal antigen and HIV), partially treated bacterial meningitis, listeria, viral encephalitis, neurosyphilis and malignant meningitis.

**Investigations**: large-volume CSF for acid-fast smear, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and culture; HIV test; chest radiograph (which may show miliary or pulmonary disease); MRI brain.

**Treatment must not wait for confirmation**: microbiological tests are often negative and delay costs lives. Treatment starts on clinical suspicion, with the four standard drugs for two months and isoniazid and rifampicin to complete **twelve months**, plus **dexamethasone**, which reduced mortality in HIV-negative adults in a Vietnamese trial; its benefit in people with HIV is uncertain. Hydrocephalus may need neurosurgical drainage. Outcome depends heavily on the stage at which treatment starts: this woman is already drowsy with focal signs, which carries a substantial risk of death or disability.
:::
:::

## Drug-resistant tuberculosis

::: definition Drug-resistant TB {#def-resistance}
WHO categories (2021 definitions):

- **Rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB)**: resistance to rifampicin, with or without resistance to other drugs.
- **Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB)**: resistance to at least **isoniazid and rifampicin**.
- **Pre-extensively drug-resistant TB (pre-XDR-TB)**: MDR/RR-TB plus resistance to any **fluoroquinolone**.
- **Extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB)**: pre-XDR-TB plus resistance to **bedaquiline or linezolid**.

Resistance is **acquired** when inadequate treatment selects resistant mutants, or **transmitted** when a person is infected with a resistant strain; most new MDR-TB today is transmitted. Risk factors include previous TB treatment, contact with a person with drug-resistant TB, and origin in a country with high rates of MDR-TB.
:::

WHO estimates that roughly 400,000 people develop MDR/RR-TB each year, and fewer than half are diagnosed and treated. Treatment has improved dramatically. Until about 2020, MDR-TB treatment lasted 18–24 months and included months of daily injections that frequently caused deafness. As of 2026, WHO recommends all-oral six-month regimens for most people with MDR/RR-TB, such as **BPaLM** (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid and moxifloxacin), with BPaL (omitting moxifloxacin) for fluoroquinolone resistance. Toxicity monitoring includes the full blood count, nerves and vision for linezolid, and the ECG QT interval for bedaquiline and the fluoroquinolones. In the UK all drug-resistant cases are managed with advice from a national clinical advisory service.

::: history
On 24 March 1882 Robert Koch presented his identification of the tubercle bacillus to the Physiological Society in Berlin, using new staining methods and culture on solidified serum; the date is now World TB Day. Albert Calmette and Camille Guérin developed the BCG vaccine from an attenuated strain of *M. bovis*, first given to a child in 1921; it protects young children against severe forms of TB such as meningitis but gives variable protection against adult lung disease. In 1943 Albert Schatz, working in Selman Waksman's laboratory, isolated streptomycin. The British Medical Research Council's streptomycin trial, published in 1948, was one of the first randomised controlled trials; it showed that the drug worked and that resistance emerged quickly when it was used alone. Combination therapy followed with para-aminosalicylic acid and isoniazid (1952), and rifampicin in the late 1960s. East African and British trials led by Wallace Fox in the 1970s established six-month short-course chemotherapy. In 1993 WHO declared TB a global emergency, and the first new TB drugs in decades, bedaquiline and pretomanid, were approved in 2012 and 2019.
:::

::: quiz
Which baseline test is required specifically because of **ethambutol**?
- [ ] Serum urate
- [x] Visual acuity and colour vision
- [ ] Audiometry
- [ ] Nerve conduction studies
::: solution
Ethambutol can cause optic neuritis, which first affects visual acuity and red–green colour vision. Baseline testing, and advice to report any visual change immediately, allows the drug to be stopped before permanent damage. Pyrazinamide raises urate; audiometry was needed for the injectable drugs once used for MDR-TB; isoniazid neuropathy is prevented with pyridoxine rather than monitored with nerve studies.
:::

::: summary
- *M. tuberculosis* is a slow-growing, acid-fast, airborne pathogen. Infection is contained in granulomas in most people; about 5–10% of people without HIV develop disease, a risk raised by HIV, TNF inhibitors, diabetes and other factors.
- Pulmonary TB presents with weeks of cough, night sweats and weight loss; diagnosis uses smear, rapid NAAT (which also detects rifampicin resistance) and culture with susceptibility testing.
- Drug-susceptible TB is treated with 2HRZE/4HR; combination therapy prevents resistance, and long treatment kills slowly dividing persisters. Supporting adherence is part of treatment.
- Key toxicities: hepatitis (H, R, Z), neuropathy (H, prevented by pyridoxine), enzyme induction (R) and optic neuritis (E).
- TB infection is detected by IGRA or TST and treated in people at high risk after active disease is excluded.
- Extrapulmonary TB needs tissue for diagnosis; TB meningitis is treated on suspicion, for 12 months, with corticosteroids.
- MDR-TB is resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin; all-oral six-month regimens such as BPaLM are now standard.
:::

Match each drug (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) with its most characteristic adverse effect or property: (a) gout; (b) red–green colour blindness; (c) peripheral neuropathy; (d) failure of the combined oral contraceptive pill.
::: solution
(a) Pyrazinamide raises serum urate and can precipitate gout. (b) Ethambutol causes optic neuritis. (c) Isoniazid causes peripheral neuropathy by depleting pyridoxine. (d) Rifampicin induces liver enzymes and reduces the effect of hormonal contraceptives, so a reliable alternative such as an intrauterine method is needed.

::: exercise Resistant mutants in a small lesion {level=1 check="100"}
A non-cavitary lesion contains about $10^{8}$ bacilli. If one bacillus in $10^{6}$ is resistant to isoniazid, how many isoniazid-resistant bacilli are expected?
::: solution
$10^{8} \times 10^{-6} = 10^{2} = 100$. Even a modest lesion contains resistant mutants, which is why a single drug is never used to treat active TB.

::: exercise Who is infectious? {level=1}
Which of the following people with TB are likely to be infectious to others: (a) a man with smear-positive cavitary lung disease; (b) a woman with cervical lymph node TB and a normal chest radiograph; (c) a child aged four with primary TB and hilar lymphadenopathy; (d) a man with laryngeal TB?
::: solution
(a) Yes, highly: cavities release large numbers of bacilli into the sputum. (b) No: lymph node TB without lung involvement is not spread by the airborne route (though a discharging sinus is handled with care). (c) Usually not: young children with primary TB have few bacilli and cough weakly; the priority is to find the adult who infected the child. (d) Yes: laryngeal TB is highly infectious.
:::

::: exercise A positive IGRA in a nurse {level=2 check="round(100*0.01*0.8/(0.01*0.8 + 0.99*0.02))/100"}
A UK-born nurse with no known exposure has a routine IGRA as part of occupational screening. Assume a prevalence of TB infection of 1% in this group, IGRA sensitivity 80% and specificity 98%. What is the probability that a positive result is a true positive? Give the answer to two decimal places, and comment.
::: hint
Work per 10,000 people screened.
:::
::: solution
Per 10,000 people: 100 are infected, of whom 80 test positive. Of 9,900 uninfected, $9{,}900 \times 0.02 = 198$ test falsely positive.

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{0.01 \times 0.8}{0.01 \times 0.8 + 0.99 \times 0.02} = \frac{0.008}{0.0278} \approx 0.29
$$

Fewer than a third of positive results are true positives. Screening low-risk groups produces many false positives, which leads to unnecessary chest radiographs and months of potentially toxic treatment. This is why testing is targeted to people with a real risk of infection and of progression, and why a borderline positive result in a low-risk person is often repeated.
:::

::: exercise Rising liver enzymes {level=2}
Three weeks into standard treatment for pulmonary TB, a 58-year-old woman develops nausea, vomiting and dark urine. ALT is 410 U/L (upper limit of normal 40), bilirubin 46 µmol/L; baseline tests were normal. What is the likely cause and what should be done?
::: solution
This is **drug-induced liver injury**, most likely from pyrazinamide, isoniazid or rifampicin. ALT above five times normal, symptoms and jaundice all mean the hepatotoxic drugs must be **stopped**. Other causes (viral hepatitis, alcohol, other drugs, biliary obstruction) are excluded. If she is very unwell or still infectious, non-hepatotoxic drugs (for example ethambutol with a fluoroquinolone and others on specialist advice) are given while the liver recovers. Once liver tests return to near normal, the drugs are **reintroduced one at a time** with monitoring, usually starting with rifampicin then isoniazid, with pyrazinamide often omitted; the regimen is then lengthened to compensate. All decisions are made with the specialist TB team.
:::
:::

::: exercise A lump in the neck {level=2}
A 27-year-old woman who moved from Pakistan five years ago has a painless left cervical lump that has grown over three months. She feels well apart from mild night sweats. The node is 3 cm, firm and slightly fluctuant, with a violaceous overlying skin. Chest radiograph is normal. Give the differential diagnosis and explain how to establish the diagnosis.
::: solution
**Differential**: **TB lymphadenitis** (the most likely, given her origin, the slow painless course and fluctuation with skin change), lymphoma, metastatic carcinoma (less likely at her age), non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection (mostly in young children), cat-scratch disease, toxoplasmosis and sarcoidosis.

**Diagnosis needs tissue**: ultrasound-guided core biopsy or fine-needle aspiration (or excision if these are non-diagnostic), sent **both** to histology (caseating granulomas; lymphoma excluded) **and** to microbiology for acid-fast smear, NAAT and culture. Placing the whole sample in formalin prevents culture and loses the chance of drug-susceptibility testing. An HIV test is offered. If TB is confirmed, she receives six months of standard treatment; nodes can enlarge paradoxically during treatment without meaning failure.
:::
:::

::: exercise Back pain that was not mechanical {level=3}
A 52-year-old man from Somalia has had five months of thoracic back pain, worse at night, with weight loss. Over the last week his legs have become weak and he has difficulty passing urine. MRI shows destruction of the T8 and T9 vertebral bodies and the intervening disc, with a paravertebral collection extending into the spinal canal. Discuss the differential, the investigations and the immediate management.
::: hint
Which part of the vertebra does each diagnosis tend to affect, and what does the neurological deterioration demand?
:::
::: solution
**Differential**: **spinal TB (Pott's disease)**, **pyogenic spondylodiscitis** (for example *S. aureus*, often with a more acute course and higher inflammatory markers), brucellosis (dietary history of unpasteurised dairy), and **metastatic malignancy** or myeloma. Involvement of two adjacent vertebrae and the disc between them, a large paravertebral abscess and a slow course favour infection, and TB in particular; tumours tend to spare the disc.

**Investigations**: blood cultures, inflammatory markers, HIV test, chest radiograph, and **CT-guided biopsy or surgical sampling** of the collection for histology, acid-fast smear, NAAT and culture with susceptibility testing.

**Immediate management**: progressive weakness and urinary retention indicate **spinal cord compression**, which is a surgical emergency. He needs urgent spinal surgical review for decompression and stabilisation; samples taken at surgery establish the diagnosis. Standard TB treatment is started as soon as samples are taken, and is usually continued for longer than for pulmonary disease. Rehabilitation and monitoring of neurological recovery follow.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rifampicin resistance detected {level=3}
A 35-year-old woman who was treated for TB in another country six years ago, and stopped after three months, presents with a cough and a new cavity. Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra detects *M. tuberculosis* with **rifampicin resistance detected**. Interpret the result and outline management, including public-health actions.
::: solution
**Interpretation**: previous incomplete treatment is a major risk factor for **acquired resistance**. Rifampicin resistance detected by a molecular test is treated as **RR-TB**, and in most cases isoniazid resistance also exists (MDR-TB). Standard first-line treatment would amount to monotherapy with isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol and would fail.

**Management**: isolation with airborne precautions in a negative-pressure room; urgent referral to the TB service and to the national drug-resistant TB advisory service; rapid molecular tests for fluoroquinolone and isoniazid resistance; culture with full phenotypic testing and whole-genome sequencing. While results are awaited, a WHO-recommended all-oral regimen such as **BPaLM** for six months is typical if fluoroquinolone resistance is excluded. Baseline ECG (QT interval), full blood count, liver function, visual testing and assessment for neuropathy are needed, and repeated during treatment. HIV testing, adherence support and directly observed therapy are standard.

**Public health**: notification, and contact tracing with priority given to household contacts and children. Contacts are screened for active disease; management of infection in contacts of MDR-TB is specialist, and they are followed up closely for at least two years.
:::
:::
