Cancer biology is cell biology with the safeguards removed: **a tumour is a somatic-evolution problem - mutated stem cells escaping growth control, recruiting vessels, evading immune destruction, tolerating metabolic chaos and eventually acquiring the motility and resilience to colonise foreign tissue.** The hallmarks framework organises the chaos, but the clinical payloads are specific: why carcinogens have latency, why inherited mutations shift tumour spectra, why targeted drugs work then fail, and why cancer remains a disease of ageing and chance.

This chapter covers the somatic mutation logic, oncogenes and tumour suppressors, hallmarks from angiogenesis to invasion, the tumour microenvironment and immune editing, and cancer genetics and epidemiology patterns.

::: summary
- Cancer is somatic evolution: driver mutations confer clonal advantage, passengers accumulate, and subclones branch - which is why single-biopsy snapshots and single-drug plans fail.
- Two gene classes with opposite genetics: oncogenes are dominant gain-of-function (one hit), tumour suppressors recessive loss-of-function (two hits, Knudson), with p53 and RB as the master examples.
- The hallmarks are acquired capabilities: sustained proliferative signalling, evasion of growth suppressors and death, replicative immortality, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming.
- Angiogenesis and hypoxia make tumour vessels abnormal, which is both a drug-target opportunity and the reason delivery is poor; anti-vascular drugs carry hypertension, thrombosis and wound hazards.
- Metastasis is inefficient but organised: EMT plasticity, circulation survival, chemokine-influenced homing and dormancy explain both late relapse and organ patterns.
- Immune editing predicts therapy: high mutational burden, mismatch repair loss and viral antigens mark checkpoint-responsive tumours; oncogene-driven tumours often escape by other routes.
:::
