Cell injury is the first chapter of every disease: **cells respond to stress with a small repertoire of fates - adaptation, reversible injury, death by apoptosis or necrosis, or the quiet accumulations of a cell that has outlived its logistics - and learning to read which fate is happening, in which tissue, from which insult, is the skill histopathology exists to teach.** This chapter covers the stimuli and signalling of injury, the morphologies of cell death, adaptations and accumulations, and the tissue-specific consequences that clinical medicine then inherits.

This chapter covers causes of cell injury, the injury cascade and its membranes and organelles, apoptosis versus necrosis and ferroptosis and pyroptosis, hypertrophy hyperplasia metaplasia dysplasia and accumulations, and the pathological anatomy of ischaemia-reperfusion, chemical and immune injury.

::: summary
- Cell injury follows a common arithmetic: insult severity and duration versus cell type, adaptive capacity and energy reserves; the same stress yields hypertrophy in one cell and death in its neighbour.
- Reversible injury is swelling and fatty change; commitment is membrane and mitochondrial failure; the two execution routes are quiet apoptosis and noisy necrosis, with necroptosis, ferroptosis and pyroptosis as the regulated middle ground.
- Necrosis subtypes (coagulative, liquefactive, caseous, fat, fibrinoid, gangrenous) are tissue-specific stories worth memorising because they read like a differential list.
- Adaptations are diagnoses: hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy, metaplasia and dysplasia each name an environment the tissue has been living in, and metaplasia-dysplasia is a road with exits.
- Accumulations map biochemistry to pigment and fat - steatosis, haemosiderin, lipofuscin, glycogen, amyloid, cholesterol and the lysosomal storage diseases.
- Time is the hidden variable: infarct histology, biomarker kinetics and complication windows all follow the ischaemic clock, and clinical protocols are those clocks made operational.
:::
