Every disease, whatever organ it affects, begins with cells that are under stress. A cell can respond in only a few ways. It can adapt, changing its size, number or type so that it copes with the new demand. It can be injured but recover. Or it can die, and the way it dies decides what the tissue looks like afterwards and what the body does next. This chapter teaches that small repertoire of responses, the mechanisms behind each one, and how a pathologist recognises them under the microscope.

The ideas here are used in every later chapter. Acute inflammation is the body's reaction to dead and damaged cells. Infarction is cell death caused by a blocked artery. Cancer begins in tissue that has passed through adaptation and dysplasia. A reader who understands why a heart muscle cell dies after twenty minutes without blood will understand much of clinical medicine.

## Causes of cell injury

Cells are damaged by a limited set of insults:

- **Oxygen deprivation.** *Hypoxia* means too little oxygen. *Ischaemia* means too little blood flow, so the tissue loses oxygen and nutrients and also cannot clear its waste. Ischaemia is the commonest cause of cell injury in clinical practice.
- **Physical agents**: trauma, heat and cold, electrical current and ionising radiation.
- **Chemicals and drugs**: poisons such as carbon monoxide, and ordinary medicines in excess, such as paracetamol in overdose. Alcohol is the commonest chemical cause of liver injury.
- **Infectious agents**: viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites, either by direct damage or through the host's immune response.
- **Immune reactions**: autoimmune disease and hypersensitivity reactions, where the body's own defences damage its tissues.
- **Genetic defects**: for example, a misfolded protein that the cell cannot clear.
- **Nutritional imbalance**: deficiency (protein, vitamins) and excess (obesity, which loads cells with lipid).

Whether a given insult causes adaptation, reversible injury or death depends on its **severity**, its **duration** and the **type of cell**. Neurons tolerate only a few minutes of complete ischaemia, heart muscle cells about twenty to thirty minutes, and skeletal muscle and fibroblasts several hours. A cell's state also matters: a hypertrophied heart muscle cell that already uses most of its oxygen supply is more vulnerable than a normal one.

## Cellular adaptations

When the stress is mild or slow, cells adjust their structure or function instead of being damaged. These adaptations are usually reversible once the stress is removed.

::: definition Cellular adaptations {#def-adaptation}
- **Hypertrophy** is an increase in the size of cells, and so of the organ, without an increase in cell number. It occurs in cells that cannot divide much, such as cardiac and skeletal muscle.
- **Hyperplasia** is an increase in the number of cells, from proliferation of differentiated cells or of stem cells. It occurs only in tissues whose cells can divide.
- **Atrophy** is a decrease in cell size, and often number, caused by loss of the substance of the cell.
- **Metaplasia** is the reversible replacement of one differentiated cell type by another differentiated cell type, better able to withstand the stress.
- **Dysplasia** is disordered growth, in which cells lose their uniform size, shape and orientation. It is not an adaptation in the strict sense: it is a precursor lesion that can progress to cancer.
:::

**Hypertrophy** may be physiological or pathological. The uterus enlarges in pregnancy through both hypertrophy and hyperplasia of smooth muscle, driven by oestrogen. A weightlifter's skeletal muscle hypertrophies. The heart of a person with long-standing hypertension develops left ventricular hypertrophy: each muscle cell makes more contractile proteins so that the wall can generate the extra pressure. Mechanical stretch and growth factors activate signalling pathways that switch on these genes. The adaptation has a limit. A hypertrophied heart has a thicker wall but no more capillaries per cell, so its cells are at greater risk of ischaemia, and eventually the muscle fails ([[cardiovascular/heart-failure]]).

**Hyperplasia** is often hormonal. The breast epithelium proliferates at puberty and in lactation. The liver regenerates by hyperplasia after part of it is removed. Pathological hyperplasia follows excessive stimulation. Endometrial hyperplasia caused by oestrogen that is not opposed by progesterone is an important example, because it raises the risk of endometrial carcinoma. Benign prostatic hyperplasia, driven by androgens, does not. Hyperplasia remains controlled growth: if the hormone is withdrawn, it regresses. That control is what separates it from cancer, but a tissue that is dividing more is a tissue in which mutations can accumulate.

**Atrophy** has many causes: disuse (a leg in plaster), loss of nerve supply, reduced blood supply (the brain in chronic ischaemia), poor nutrition, loss of hormonal stimulation (the endometrium after the menopause) and pressure (a tumour compressing nearby tissue). The cell breaks down its own proteins through the ubiquitin–proteasome system and digests its own organelles by **autophagy**, in which parts of the cytoplasm are enclosed in a vacuole and fused with a lysosome. Some undigested material remains as **lipofuscin**, a brown "wear and tear" pigment.

**Metaplasia** is a trade. In the bronchi of a person who smokes, the normal ciliated columnar epithelium is replaced by stratified squamous epithelium. The squamous cells resist the smoke better, but they do not secrete mucus or have cilia, so the lung loses part of its clearance mechanism. In the lower oesophagus of a person with chronic acid reflux, squamous epithelium is replaced by columnar epithelium of gastric or intestinal type: **Barrett's oesophagus**. Metaplasia happens by reprogramming of stem cells, not by one mature cell turning into another. Its importance is that metaplastic epithelium is the soil in which dysplasia and carcinoma often develop: squamous carcinoma of the bronchus, adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus.

**Dysplasia** describes cells that look abnormal: enlarged dark nuclei, a high ratio of nucleus to cytoplasm, mitoses above the basal layer, and loss of the orderly maturation from base to surface. It is graded as low or high. In the cervix it is called cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and graded 1 to 3. When dysplasia involves the full thickness of the epithelium but has not crossed the basement membrane, it is called **carcinoma in situ**. Dysplasia can regress, especially low-grade dysplasia, but high-grade dysplasia carries a substantial risk of invasive cancer, which is why it is treated or watched closely. The link between these lesions and cancer is developed in [[pathology/cancer-biology]].

::: example A man with long-standing reflux {#ex-barrett}
A 61-year-old man has had heartburn for many years. At endoscopy the lower 4 cm of his oesophagus is salmon pink instead of pale. Biopsies show columnar epithelium with goblet cells and no dysplasia. Explain the finding and its significance.
::: solution
1. Chronic acid and bile reflux injure the normal stratified squamous lining of the oesophagus.
2. The stem cells of the lining respond by producing columnar epithelium, which tolerates acid better. Goblet cells show that it is of intestinal type. This is **metaplasia**, and the condition is Barrett's oesophagus.
3. The new epithelium is a better barrier to acid, but it is unstable. A small proportion of people with Barrett's oesophagus develop dysplasia and then adenocarcinoma.
4. Absence of dysplasia means his annual risk of cancer is low. Management follows current guidance (in the UK, the British Society of Gastroenterology guideline): control of reflux and endoscopic surveillance at intervals that depend on the length of the segment and the presence of dysplasia.
:::
:::

## Reversible injury

If the insult is too strong for adaptation, the cell is injured. At first the damage is reversible: if the cause is removed, the cell recovers fully.

The central event in most reversible injury, and especially in hypoxia, is **failure of ATP production**. Without oxygen, oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria stops. The cell switches to anaerobic glycolysis, which makes far less ATP and produces lactic acid. Several consequences follow from the lack of ATP:

- The **sodium–potassium pump** (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) slows. Sodium accumulates inside the cell and water follows it by osmosis. The cell and its organelles swell.
- Lactic acid lowers the intracellular pH. The chromatin clumps.
- Ribosomes detach from the rough endoplasmic reticulum and protein synthesis falls.
- In cells that handle fat, such as hepatocytes, lipid accumulates as small vacuoles.

Under the light microscope, reversible injury shows two main changes. **Cellular swelling** (also called hydropic change) gives the cytoplasm a pale, vacuolated look. **Fatty change** (steatosis) is the accumulation of triglyceride droplets, seen most often in the liver in alcohol misuse, obesity and diabetes. Under the electron microscope there are blebs on the plasma membrane, loss of microvilli, swollen mitochondria and dilated endoplasmic reticulum.

::: example Swelling in numbers {#ex-swelling}
A cell that behaves as an ideal osmometer has a volume of 2.0 pL. Failure of the sodium pump allows its content of osmotically active solute to rise by 15%, while the extracellular osmolality stays the same. Estimate the new cell volume.
::: solution
1. Water moves until the osmolality inside equals the osmolality outside. Osmolality is solute divided by water volume.
2. If the solute content rises by a factor of 1.15 and the osmolality must return to its original value, the water volume must also rise by a factor of 1.15.
3. Treating the whole volume as osmotically active water: new volume $= 2.0 \times 1.15 = 2.3$ pL.
4. In reality part of the cell volume is solids, so the true increase is a little smaller. The point is that a modest gain of intracellular sodium produces visible swelling, which is why cellular swelling is the first change seen in injured cells. The physiology of water movement between compartments is in [[physiology/homeostasis-body-fluids]].
:::
:::

## Irreversible injury and the point of no return

If the insult continues, the cell passes a point beyond which it cannot recover, even if blood flow is restored. Two features mark this point consistently:

1. The **mitochondria cannot recover their function**, so ATP cannot be regenerated.
2. **Membranes are seriously damaged**: the plasma membrane leaks, and lysosomal membranes release digestive enzymes into the cytoplasm.

::: theorem The mechanisms of irreversible cell injury {#thm-injury}
Four interlinked mechanisms carry a cell from reversible injury to death:

1. **Mitochondrial damage**, with opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, loss of the membrane potential and failure of ATP synthesis.
2. **Influx of calcium** into the cytosol, from outside the cell and from intracellular stores.
3. **Accumulation of reactive oxygen species** (free radicals), which damage lipids, proteins and DNA.
4. **Membrane damage**, of the plasma membrane and of lysosomes.

Each mechanism worsens the others, so that injury, once past a threshold, accelerates.
:::

::: proof
Calcium is normally kept about ten thousand times lower in the cytosol than outside the cell, by ATP-dependent pumps. When ATP falls, calcium enters and is released from the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. Cytosolic calcium activates enzymes that damage the cell: phospholipases attack membranes, proteases break down the cytoskeleton, endonucleases fragment DNA, and ATPases consume what ATP remains. Calcium also opens the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, which collapses the mitochondrial membrane potential, so the mitochondria can no longer make ATP even if oxygen returns.

Damaged mitochondria leak electrons from the respiratory chain to oxygen, forming **reactive oxygen species**: superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and the very reactive hydroxyl radical, generated from hydrogen peroxide by free iron (the Fenton reaction). These species oxidise the lipids of membranes, cross-link proteins and break DNA. The cell's defences, such as superoxide dismutase, catalase and glutathione peroxidase, depend on reducing power that the injured cell is losing.

Membrane damage completes the cycle. A leaky plasma membrane lets more calcium in and lets cell contents out. Leaky lysosomes release acid hydrolases that digest the cell. At this stage the cell cannot restore its ion gradients or its energy supply, and death follows.
:::

The leak of cell contents into the blood has a practical use. Proteins that belong inside particular cells appear in the plasma when those cells die. **Cardiac troponin** in myocardial infarction, alanine aminotransferase in liver injury and creatine kinase in muscle injury are all measured for this reason. Their rise is evidence of cell death, not merely of reversible stress.

### Ischaemia–reperfusion injury

Restoring blood flow to ischaemic tissue is the only way to save it, yet reperfusion can itself add injury. When oxygen returns to cells with damaged mitochondria, a burst of reactive oxygen species is produced. Calcium floods into cells whose pumps are weak. Neutrophils and complement are delivered with the blood and attack damaged cells. This **reperfusion injury** contributes to damage after opening a blocked coronary artery, after organ transplantation and after cardiac arrest. It does not mean reperfusion should be withheld. It explains why outcomes depend on how long the tissue was ischaemic before flow returned.

## Necrosis

Two main pathways of cell death are recognised. **Necrosis** is death after severe injury, in which the cell swells, its membranes break and its contents leak out, provoking inflammation. **Apoptosis** is a regulated programme in which the cell dismantles itself in an orderly way without leaking its contents.

::: definition Necrosis and its morphological patterns {#def-necrosis}
**Necrosis** is the set of changes that follow cell death in living tissue, caused by enzymatic digestion of the cell and denaturation of its proteins. The nucleus shrinks and becomes dense (**pyknosis**), breaks into fragments (**karyorrhexis**) and fades away (**karyolysis**). The cytoplasm becomes more eosinophilic (pinker) on haematoxylin and eosin staining. The pattern seen in the tissue depends on the cause and the organ:

- **Coagulative necrosis**: the outline of dead cells is preserved for days because proteins are denatured, including the enzymes that would digest them. Typical of infarcts in solid organs except the brain.
- **Liquefactive necrosis**: dead tissue is digested into a liquid mass. Seen in bacterial and fungal infection (pus) and in brain infarcts.
- **Caseous necrosis**: a cheese-like, white, crumbly mass with no cell outlines, surrounded by granulomatous inflammation. Typical of tuberculosis.
- **Fat necrosis**: destruction of fat by lipases released from the injured pancreas, or after trauma to fatty tissue such as the breast. Released fatty acids combine with calcium, forming chalky white deposits.
- **Fibrinoid necrosis**: deposits of immune complexes and fibrin in the walls of blood vessels, giving a bright pink smudgy appearance. Seen in vasculitis and malignant hypertension.
- **Gangrene** is a clinical term for necrosis of a limb or bowel. *Dry gangrene* is mainly coagulative. *Wet gangrene* is necrosis with bacterial infection and liquefaction.
:::

Why does the brain liquefy when other organs coagulate? Brain tissue has little supporting connective tissue and a high content of lipids, and its own lysosomal enzymes and those of incoming microglia digest it rapidly. A brain infarct becomes a fluid-filled cavity over weeks. A kidney or heart infarct, in contrast, remains a firm pale area whose dead cells keep their outlines until inflammatory cells remove them and scar tissue replaces them.

::: example The timeline of a myocardial infarct {#ex-infarct}
A 70-year-old woman dies five days after the onset of crushing chest pain. At autopsy, the anterior wall of the left ventricle contains a yellow, soft area with a red rim. What will the microscope show, and why did her cardiac troponin rise during life?
::: solution
1. Occlusion of the left anterior descending coronary artery caused ischaemia of the anterior wall. After about 20 to 30 minutes the muscle cells passed the point of no return.
2. The dead cells show **coagulative necrosis**: their outlines persist, the cytoplasm is intensely eosinophilic and the nuclei are lost.
3. Dead cells attract neutrophils, which peak at about one to three days. By three to seven days macrophages are removing the dead tissue, which is why the infarct is soft and yellow at five days. The red rim is early granulation tissue at the edge. Scar replaces the infarct over the following weeks.
4. Troponin is a structural protein of heart muscle. When the plasma membranes of the dying cells failed, troponin leaked into the blood. A rising troponin is therefore evidence of myocardial cell death.
5. The softened wall at three to seven days is mechanically weak, which is when rupture of the ventricular wall is most likely. The clinical aspects are covered in [[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]].
:::
:::

## Apoptosis

**Apoptosis** is programmed cell death: the cell activates enzymes that degrade its own DNA and proteins in an orderly way. It occurs in health, to remove cells that are no longer needed, and in disease, to remove cells that are damaged beyond repair.

Physiological examples include the loss of cells between the fingers during embryonic development, the shedding of the endometrium, the deletion of self-reactive lymphocytes in the thymus, and the death of neutrophils at the end of an inflammatory response. Pathological examples include cells with irreparable DNA damage, cells infected by viruses (killed by cytotoxic T cells), and the atrophy of an organ whose duct is blocked.

::: definition Apoptosis and its two pathways {#def-apoptosis}
**Apoptosis** is a regulated form of cell death in which caspases (cysteine proteases) dismantle the cell. The cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses at the nuclear membrane, the nucleus fragments, and the cell breaks into membrane-bound **apoptotic bodies** that are eaten by macrophages. The plasma membrane stays intact, so there is no inflammation.

- The **intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway** is triggered by internal stress such as DNA damage, misfolded proteins or loss of growth factors. Sensor proteins of the BCL-2 family (BH3-only proteins) activate **BAX** and **BAK**, which make pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane. Cytochrome *c* escapes into the cytosol, binds APAF-1 to form the apoptosome and activates **caspase-9**. Anti-apoptotic proteins **BCL-2** and BCL-XL oppose this step.
- The **extrinsic (death receptor) pathway** is triggered when a ligand binds a death receptor on the cell surface: Fas ligand on a cytotoxic T cell binding Fas, or TNF binding TNF receptor 1. The receptor recruits adaptor proteins and activates **caspase-8**.
- Both pathways converge on the **executioner caspases**, caspase-3 and caspase-7.
:::

Apoptotic cells expose phosphatidylserine on the outer leaflet of their membrane. This acts as an "eat me" signal, so macrophages remove the cell before its contents can leak. This is why apoptosis is immunologically quiet, and why tissue can lose many cells by apoptosis with little visible reaction.

The balance between survival and death is a target for both disease and treatment. Many cancers overexpress BCL-2 or lose p53, the protein that senses DNA damage and triggers the intrinsic pathway; these cells survive damage that should kill them ([[pathology/cancer-biology]]). Follicular lymphoma is driven by a translocation that places *BCL2* under the control of an immunoglobulin gene. A drug class that inhibits BCL-2 is now used in some leukaemias. Excess apoptosis contributes to neurodegenerative diseases and to the loss of CD4 T cells in HIV infection.

::: warning Apoptosis is not a gentle necrosis
A common error is to treat apoptosis as a milder form of necrosis. They differ in kind. In necrosis the cell swells, the membrane ruptures, contents leak out and inflammation follows; it always indicates pathology. In apoptosis the cell shrinks, the membrane stays intact, fragments are eaten without inflammation, and the process may be entirely normal. One stimulus can cause either: a mild insult may trigger apoptosis, while a severe insult to the same cell causes necrosis because there is no ATP left to run the apoptotic programme.
:::

| Feature | Necrosis | Apoptosis |
|---|---|---|
| Cell size | Enlarged (swelling) | Reduced (shrinkage) |
| Nucleus | Pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis | Condensation, fragmentation into neat pieces |
| Plasma membrane | Disrupted | Intact |
| Cell contents | Leak out, enzymatic digestion | Retained in apoptotic bodies |
| Inflammation | Usually present | Absent |
| Role | Always pathological | Often physiological; can be pathological |

### Other forms of regulated cell death

Research over the last twenty years has shown that some deaths that look like necrosis are in fact controlled by specific pathways. **Necroptosis** is triggered, for example, by TNF when caspase-8 is blocked; the kinases RIPK1 and RIPK3 activate the protein MLKL, which punctures the plasma membrane. **Pyroptosis** occurs in macrophages infected by microbes: the inflammasome activates caspase-1, which cleaves gasdermin D to form membrane pores and releases interleukin-1β. **Ferroptosis** is iron-dependent death caused by lipid peroxidation when the enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 fails. These forms all end in membrane rupture and inflammation, and they matter in infection, ischaemia and drug development, but in diagnostic histology they look like necrosis. The inflammatory consequences are taken up in [[pathology/acute-inflammation]].

::: quiz
A biopsy of a lymph node shows scattered single shrunken cells with dense fragmented nuclei, each inside a clear halo, with no surrounding neutrophils. Which process is most likely?
- [ ] Coagulative necrosis
- [x] Apoptosis
- [ ] Liquefactive necrosis
- [ ] Fat necrosis
::: solution
Single cells dying in isolation, shrunken, with condensed and fragmented chromatin and no inflammation, are the signature of apoptosis. Necrosis affects groups of cells, causes swelling and provokes an inflammatory response. Germinal centres of lymph nodes normally contain many apoptotic B cells, which macrophages engulf.
:::
:::

## Intracellular accumulations

Cells may accumulate substances when they make too much of them, cannot break them down, or take up material they cannot process. Accumulations can be harmless markers or the direct cause of disease.

- **Lipid.** Triglyceride accumulates in **steatosis**, most often in the liver. Causes include alcohol, obesity and insulin resistance (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease), and some drugs. Cholesterol accumulates in macrophages in the wall of arteries in atherosclerosis, where these lipid-laden cells are called foam cells.
- **Protein.** Excess protein appears as pink droplets. In the kidney, proximal tubular cells reabsorb protein that leaks through damaged glomeruli. Plasma cells making large amounts of immunoglobulin may contain round inclusions called Russell bodies. Misfolded proteins can kill cells, as in alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, where the abnormal protein accumulates in hepatocytes.
- **Glycogen** accumulates in the glycogen storage diseases and in the renal tubules in poorly controlled diabetes.
- **Pigments.** *Lipofuscin* is the brown residue of digested membranes in ageing and atrophic cells; it does no harm. *Haemosiderin* is a golden-brown iron-storage pigment that appears where red cells have broken down (an old bruise) or when the body is overloaded with iron (haemochromatosis, repeated transfusion). Perls' Prussian blue stain shows it as blue. *Melanin* is made by melanocytes. *Carbon* from polluted air or smoke is taken up by alveolar macrophages and carried to lymph nodes, which turn black (anthracosis).

::: example A transfused patient's liver {#ex-iron}
A 24-year-old woman with beta-thalassaemia major has received red cell transfusions every few weeks since childhood. A liver biopsy shows golden-brown granules in hepatocytes and Kupffer cells, which stain blue with Perls' stain, together with fibrosis. Explain the findings.
::: solution
1. Each unit of red cells contains iron, and the body has no regulated route for excreting iron. Repeated transfusion therefore causes **iron overload**.
2. Excess iron is stored as ferritin and its aggregated, insoluble form **haemosiderin**, which is the golden-brown pigment. Perls' stain detects ferric iron and turns it blue.
3. Free iron catalyses the production of hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction. The resulting oxidative injury damages hepatocytes, and repeated injury and repair lead to fibrosis and eventually cirrhosis. The heart and endocrine glands are also damaged.
4. This is why people receiving regular transfusions are given iron chelation therapy and monitored for iron load, following specialist guidance ([[blood/haemoglobin-disorders]]).
:::
:::

## Pathological calcification

Calcium salts may be deposited in tissues abnormally. There are two kinds, and the distinction rests on the blood calcium.

::: definition Dystrophic and metastatic calcification {#def-calcification}
- **Dystrophic calcification** is deposition of calcium salts in dead or degenerating tissue while the serum calcium and phosphate are **normal**. Examples are atherosclerotic plaques, damaged and ageing heart valves (calcific aortic stenosis), caseous necrosis in old tuberculosis, and the concentric **psammoma bodies** seen in some tumours such as papillary thyroid carcinoma.
- **Metastatic calcification** is deposition of calcium salts in **normal** tissue as a result of a raised serum calcium or phosphate. Causes include primary hyperparathyroidism, chronic kidney disease with phosphate retention and secondary hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D toxicity, and destruction of bone by tumour. It affects tissues that lose acid and so have an alkaline internal environment: the lungs, the kidneys (nephrocalcinosis), the gastric mucosa and the walls of arteries.
:::

The word *metastatic* here has nothing to do with cancer: it means that the calcium has been moved from its normal place (bone) to other tissues.

::: example Calcium and phosphate in chronic kidney disease {#ex-caxp}
A 55-year-old man with stage 5 chronic kidney disease has a serum calcium of 2.40 mmol/L and a serum phosphate of 2.10 mmol/L. Calculate the calcium–phosphate product and explain why his arteries are at risk.
::: solution
1. Product $= 2.40 \times 2.10 = 5.04$ mmol²/L².
2. A traditional rule of thumb held that the risk of calcium phosphate precipitating in soft tissue rises when the product exceeds about 4.4 mmol²/L² (55 mg²/dL² in conventional units). His value is above this.
3. The kidneys cannot excrete phosphate, so phosphate rises. Vascular smooth muscle cells exposed to high phosphate change their behaviour and deposit mineral actively, so calcification of the arterial media is common in advanced kidney disease.
4. Current guidelines (KDIGO) advise managing calcium and phosphate individually rather than relying on the product, because the product hides which component is abnormal. The calculation still shows why phosphate control matters ([[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]]).
:::
:::

## Cellular ageing

Cells accumulate damage over time. Telomeres, the repetitive sequences at the ends of chromosomes, shorten with each division in most somatic cells; when they become critically short the cell stops dividing (**replicative senescence**). DNA damage accumulates, misfolded proteins are cleared less efficiently, and mitochondria produce more reactive oxygen species. Senescent cells secrete inflammatory cytokines and proteases, a feature that links ageing to low-grade chronic inflammation ([[pathology/chronic-inflammation-repair]]). Ageing at the level of the whole person is discussed in [[geriatrics-palliative/ageing-frailty]].

::: history
Rudolf Virchow's *Die Cellularpathologie* (1858) argued that disease is the disturbed life of cells, and that every cell comes from a pre-existing cell. This replaced the old idea that disease arose from imbalances of bodily fluids and made the cell the unit of pathology. The term **apoptosis** was introduced in 1972 by John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie, who described a form of cell death with a distinct morphology and argued that it was a controlled process complementary to cell division. The genetic basis of programmed cell death was worked out in the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, in which exactly 131 cells die during development; Sydney Brenner, H. Robert Horvitz and John Sulston shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2002 for this work.
:::

## Where this leads

Cell death sets off the body's response. Necrotic tissue releases signals that recruit neutrophils and start **acute inflammation**, the subject of [[pathology/acute-inflammation]]. Dead tissue is then removed and replaced by regeneration or scar ([[pathology/chronic-inflammation-repair]]). Ischaemic cell death in a whole region of tissue is **infarction**, covered in [[pathology/haemodynamics]]. The progression from metaplasia to dysplasia to carcinoma is the subject of [[pathology/cancer-biology]].

::: summary
- Cells respond to stress by adapting, by reversible injury or by death. The outcome depends on the severity and duration of the insult and on the type and state of the cell.
- Adaptations: hypertrophy (bigger cells), hyperplasia (more cells), atrophy (smaller cells), metaplasia (a different mature cell type). Dysplasia is disordered growth and a precursor of cancer.
- Reversible injury is driven mainly by ATP depletion: pump failure, swelling and fatty change.
- Irreversible injury is marked by mitochondrial failure and membrane damage, driven by calcium influx and reactive oxygen species. Leakage of cell proteins (troponin, transaminases) is the clinical marker of cell death.
- Necrosis patterns: coagulative (infarcts), liquefactive (abscesses, brain), caseous (tuberculosis), fat, fibrinoid; gangrene is the clinical term.
- Apoptosis is regulated, caspase-mediated, non-inflammatory death through the intrinsic (BCL-2 family, cytochrome *c*, caspase-9) or extrinsic (death receptor, caspase-8) pathway.
- Dystrophic calcification occurs in damaged tissue with normal serum calcium; metastatic calcification occurs in normal tissue when calcium or phosphate is high.
:::

In the ATP model of this chapter, $A(t) = e^{-kt}$ with $t$ in minutes. A tissue has $k = 0.1$ per minute. How many minutes until ATP reaches 25% of normal? Round to one decimal place.
::: hint
Solve $e^{-kt} = 0.25$.
:::
::: solution
$t = \ln 4 / 0.1 = 13.86\ldots$, which rounds to 13.9 minutes. A faster rate of energy use shortens tolerance.
:::

::: exercise Explain the calcification {level=2}
A 78-year-old man with normal serum calcium has a heavily calcified, narrowed aortic valve. A 45-year-old woman with primary hyperparathyroidism has calcium deposits in both kidneys. Classify each, and explain the difference in mechanism.
::: solution
The man has **dystrophic** calcification: decades of mechanical stress damage the valve cusps, and calcium is deposited in the damaged tissue while serum calcium is normal. The woman has **metastatic** calcification (nephrocalcinosis): excess parathyroid hormone raises serum calcium, and calcium salts precipitate in normal tissue, favouring the alkaline interstitium of the kidney. The first is driven by local tissue damage, the second by abnormal blood chemistry.
:::

::: exercise A death in two speeds {level=3}
Hepatocytes exposed to a low concentration of a toxin die singly, with shrinkage and no inflammation. At a high concentration, sheets of hepatocytes die with swelling, and neutrophils gather. Explain how one toxin can produce both pictures, and what blood test result would differ.
::: hint
The apoptotic programme needs energy.
:::
::: solution
At low concentration, the cells are damaged but retain enough ATP to run the apoptotic programme. DNA or mitochondrial damage activates the intrinsic pathway, caspases dismantle each cell, and the fragments are removed by macrophages without inflammation. At high concentration, ATP collapses, calcium floods in and membranes rupture before the apoptotic programme can run, so the cells undergo necrosis. Their contents leak and recruit neutrophils. Serum alanine aminotransferase would rise much more in the second case, because necrosis releases cell contents into the blood. (Apoptotic cells also release some enzymes, but far less.)
:::

::: exercise A breathless man with a big heart {level=3}
A 66-year-old man with poorly controlled hypertension for twenty years presents with breathlessness. Echocardiography shows a thick-walled left ventricle with reduced contraction. Explain, at the cellular level, how an adaptation became a disease. Include why his heart is more vulnerable to ischaemia.
::: solution
Pressure overload stimulated the heart muscle cells through stretch and growth factor signalling to make more contractile protein, so they enlarged (hypertrophy) and the wall thickened. This maintained output at first. However, the number of capillaries did not increase in step, so the distance oxygen must diffuse rose and each enlarged cell received less blood per unit mass. The thicker wall also raised oxygen demand. Repeated episodes of relative ischaemia caused cell loss by apoptosis and necrosis, and lost cells were replaced by fibrous tissue, making the ventricle stiff. The surviving cells switched to less efficient fetal-type gene expression. The adaptation reached its limit and became heart failure. Lowering blood pressure can partly reverse the hypertrophy, which is one reason hypertension is treated.
:::
