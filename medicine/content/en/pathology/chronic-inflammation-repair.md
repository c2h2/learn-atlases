Acute inflammation is designed to be brief. It removes the cause of injury and then switches itself off. When the cause cannot be removed, or the switch fails, inflammation continues for weeks, months or years. The cells change, the tissue is damaged by the response that was meant to protect it, and repair runs alongside the destruction. This is **chronic inflammation**, and it underlies many of the major diseases of adult life: tuberculosis, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, cirrhosis and, in a quieter form, atherosclerosis.

The second half of this chapter is about **repair**: how tissue is restored after injury, either by regeneration of the original cells or by a scar of connective tissue. The two topics belong together, because the cells that drive chronic inflammation also drive scarring, and because excessive repair (fibrosis) is itself a major cause of organ failure.

## What chronic inflammation is

::: definition Chronic inflammation {#def-chronic}
**Chronic inflammation** is inflammation of prolonged duration (weeks to years) in which active inflammation, tissue destruction and attempts at repair proceed at the same time. It is characterised by:

- infiltration by **mononuclear cells**: macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells;
- **tissue destruction**, caused largely by the products of these cells;
- **attempts at healing**, by new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis) and fibrosis.
:::

Chronic inflammation may follow acute inflammation that fails to resolve, as when an abscess is not drained. More often it begins insidiously, with no acute phase. The main causes are:

- **Persistent infections** by organisms that resist killing: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, *Treponema pallidum*, some viruses, fungi and parasites. These often provoke a T cell response called delayed-type hypersensitivity.
- **Prolonged exposure to toxic agents**, either from outside (inhaled silica, which cannot be digested) or inside (lipids in the wall of arteries).
- **Immune-mediated inflammatory diseases**: autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, where the immune system reacts against the body's own tissues, and allergic diseases such as asthma.

## The cells of chronic inflammation

### Macrophages

The **macrophage** is the central cell of chronic inflammation. Blood monocytes enter the tissue, as described for acute inflammation, and mature into macrophages, which can live for months. Many organs also contain resident macrophages that settled there before birth: Kupffer cells in the liver, microglia in the brain, alveolar macrophages in the lung.

Macrophages respond to their surroundings and can be activated in two broad ways. This two-way classification is a simplification of a continuous spectrum, but it is useful.

- **Classical activation** (sometimes called M1) is induced by microbial products and by interferon-γ (IFN-γ) from T cells. These macrophages kill microbes efficiently, producing reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide and lysosomal enzymes, and secrete inflammatory cytokines such as TNF, IL-1 and IL-12. They also damage tissue.
- **Alternative activation** (sometimes called M2) is induced by IL-4 and IL-13. These macrophages are poor at killing microbes but promote repair: they secrete growth factors such as TGF-β and PDGF that stimulate angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis.

In a healing wound the population shifts from the first type to the second. In chronic inflammation both may be present at once, so tissue is destroyed and scarred in the same place.

### Lymphocytes, plasma cells and others

**T lymphocytes** both respond to and drive macrophages. CD4 helper T cells of different types make different cytokines: Th1 cells make IFN-γ, which activates macrophages classically; Th2 cells make IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13, which activate eosinophils and macrophages of the alternative type; Th17 cells make IL-17, which recruits neutrophils. **B lymphocytes** develop into **plasma cells** that secrete antibody. In some long-standing inflammation, such as rheumatoid synovium, lymphocytes organise into structures resembling lymph node follicles. The immunology of these cells is developed in [[immunology/adaptive-responses]].

**Eosinophils** are characteristic of allergic inflammation and parasitic infection. Their granules contain major basic protein, which is toxic to parasites and also damages epithelium. **Mast cells** are present in connective tissue throughout the body and contribute to both acute and chronic allergic reactions.

::: theorem The macrophage–lymphocyte loop {#thm-loop}
Chronic inflammation is self-sustaining because macrophages and T lymphocytes stimulate each other. Macrophages present antigen to T cells and secrete IL-12, which promotes Th1 differentiation. Activated T cells secrete IFN-γ, which activates more macrophages. Activated macrophages release cytokines that recruit more monocytes and lymphocytes. While the antigen persists, the loop continues.
:::

This explains why chronic inflammation is hard to switch off, and why treatments for chronic inflammatory diseases target the loop at different points: corticosteroids broadly, methotrexate and other immunosuppressants, and biological drugs aimed at TNF, IL-12/23 or T cell co-stimulation.

## Granulomatous inflammation

A **granuloma** is a distinctive form of chronic inflammation that the body uses when it cannot destroy a persistent agent: it walls the agent off instead.

::: definition Granuloma {#def-granuloma}
A **granuloma** is a compact, organised collection of activated macrophages, often with multinucleated giant cells and a surrounding rim of lymphocytes. The activated macrophages are called **epithelioid cells** because they have abundant pink cytoplasm and lie closely together like epithelium. **Giant cells** form by fusion of macrophages; in **Langhans giant cells** the nuclei form a horseshoe at the edge of the cell, and in **foreign body giant cells** the nuclei are scattered.

Granulomas are described as **caseating** when they contain central caseous necrosis (typical of tuberculosis) or **non-caseating** when they do not (typical of sarcoidosis and Crohn's disease).
:::

Granulomas form in two settings. **Immune granulomas** form when a persistent antigen drives a T cell response, as in tuberculosis. **Foreign body granulomas** form around inert material too large to be phagocytosed, such as suture material, talc or keratin from a ruptured cyst; they need no specific immune response.

| Disease | Cause | Typical granuloma |
|---|---|---|
| Tuberculosis | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* | Caseating, with Langhans giant cells; acid-fast bacilli on Ziehl–Neelsen stain |
| Leprosy | *Mycobacterium leprae* | In skin and nerves; few or many bacilli depending on the host response |
| Syphilis (tertiary) | *Treponema pallidum* | Gumma: necrotic centre, plasma cells prominent |
| Fungal infection | *Histoplasma*, *Cryptococcus* and others | Often necrotising; organisms seen on special stains |
| Cat-scratch disease | *Bartonella henselae* | Stellate granulomas with central neutrophils in lymph nodes |
| Sarcoidosis | Unknown | Non-caseating, in lymph nodes, lung, skin and eyes |
| Crohn's disease | Immune reaction to gut contents | Non-caseating, in the bowel wall |
| Foreign body | Sutures, talc, crystals | Foreign body giant cells; material visible, sometimes in polarised light |

::: proof
Why does tuberculosis cause granulomas? *M. tuberculosis* is ingested by macrophages but survives inside them by preventing the phagosome from fusing with lysosomes. Infected macrophages present mycobacterial antigens to CD4 T cells and secrete IL-12. The T cells differentiate into Th1 cells and secrete IFN-γ, which activates the macrophages so that they can kill or at least contain the bacilli. Activated macrophages become epithelioid cells and some fuse into giant cells. **TNF** from macrophages and T cells holds the structure together and keeps recruiting cells. At the centre, the activity of the cells and the poor oxygen supply cause caseous necrosis. The granuloma therefore contains the infection at the cost of local tissue destruction. If the T cell response fails, as in advanced HIV infection, granulomas form poorly and the bacilli spread.
:::

::: example A cough and a cavity {#ex-tb}
A 32-year-old man who moved to the UK from a country with a high incidence of tuberculosis has had a cough, night sweats and weight loss for three months. A chest radiograph shows a cavity in the right upper lobe. A bronchoscopic biopsy shows granulomas with central caseous necrosis and Langhans giant cells. Explain the pathology and what the pathologist will do next.
::: solution
1. Granulomas with caseation and Langhans giant cells in the lung strongly suggest **tuberculosis**, although fungal infection can look similar.
2. The cavity forms when caseous material liquefies and is coughed out through a bronchus. The cavity wall contains many bacilli, so the person is likely to be infectious.
3. The pathologist will request a **Ziehl–Neelsen** stain for acid-fast bacilli, and the specimen should go for mycobacterial culture and molecular testing, which can detect the organism and resistance mutations faster than culture.
4. Systemic symptoms such as night sweats and weight loss are caused by cytokines, especially TNF.
5. Treatment, contact tracing and public health notification follow national guidance ([[infectious-diseases/tuberculosis]]).
:::
:::

::: example Hypercalcaemia in sarcoidosis {#ex-sarcoid}
A 41-year-old Black woman has enlarged lymph nodes at both lung hila, tender red nodules on her shins and a serum calcium of 2.85 mmol/L (reference range about 2.2 to 2.6). A lymph node biopsy shows tight non-caseating granulomas; stains and cultures for mycobacteria and fungi are negative. Explain why her calcium is raised.
::: solution
1. Non-caseating granulomas with negative stains for organisms, in a typical pattern of bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy and erythema nodosum, point to **sarcoidosis**. The diagnosis is one of exclusion, because tuberculosis, fungal infection and lymphoma can produce similar findings.
2. Activated macrophages in the granulomas express **1-alpha-hydroxylase**, the enzyme that converts 25-hydroxyvitamin D to the active hormone 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol).
3. Unlike the kidney's enzyme, the macrophage enzyme is not controlled by parathyroid hormone, so calcitriol is made in excess. It increases calcium absorption from the gut, and serum calcium rises while parathyroid hormone is suppressed.
4. Sunlight can worsen the hypercalcaemia by increasing vitamin D production. The same mechanism occurs in some lymphomas and in tuberculosis.
:::
:::

## The systemic effects of chronic inflammation

Chronic inflammation affects the whole body through circulating cytokines.

- **Anaemia of chronic disease.** IL-6 increases hepcidin from the liver, which traps iron in macrophages and the gut lining. Iron is present in the body but unavailable to make red cells ([[blood/anaemia]]).
- **Cachexia.** TNF and other cytokines cause loss of appetite and breakdown of muscle and fat.
- **Reactive (AA) amyloidosis.** Prolonged high levels of the acute-phase protein serum amyloid A can lead to deposition of its fragments as insoluble amyloid fibrils in the kidneys, liver and spleen. It complicates long-standing untreated rheumatoid arthritis, chronic infections and familial fever syndromes, and usually presents with protein in the urine. Amyloid shows apple-green birefringence when stained with Congo red and viewed in polarised light.

Low-grade chronic inflammation, without the classical signs, also contributes to common diseases. In **atherosclerosis**, lipoproteins trapped in the artery wall are oxidised, macrophages take them up and become foam cells, and the plaque behaves as a chronic inflammatory lesion ([[cardiovascular/coronary-artery-disease]]). The clinical relevance was supported by the CANTOS trial (2017), in which an antibody against IL-1β reduced recurrent cardiovascular events in people with a previous myocardial infarction and a raised CRP, although it increased fatal infections and has not entered routine use. **Obesity** is associated with macrophage infiltration of adipose tissue and insulin resistance. The increase of low-grade inflammation with age is sometimes called "inflammageing".

## Regeneration

After injury, tissue is restored in one of two ways. **Regeneration** replaces damaged cells with cells of the same type, restoring normal structure and function. **Repair by connective tissue** replaces them with a scar. Which happens depends on the ability of the cells to divide and on whether the supporting framework of the tissue survives.

::: definition Proliferative capacity of tissues {#def-cell-types}
- **Labile tissues** contain cells that divide continuously throughout life, replacing cells that are lost: the surface epithelia of the skin, mouth, gut and urinary tract, and the bone marrow.
- **Stable tissues** contain cells that are normally quiescent but can divide in response to injury: hepatocytes, renal tubular cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells.
- **Permanent tissues** contain cells that cannot divide in adult life to any useful extent: neurons and cardiac muscle cells. Injury to them is followed by scar (in the heart) or glial scar (in the brain). Skeletal muscle has a limited capacity to regenerate from satellite cells.
:::

Regeneration also needs the **extracellular matrix** to survive. The basement membrane and connective tissue framework guide regenerating cells into the right arrangement. The liver illustrates this. After surgical removal of part of a healthy liver, the remaining hepatocytes divide and the liver regains its original mass within weeks, a property used in living donor liver transplantation. But when hepatocytes are destroyed repeatedly and the framework collapses and is replaced by fibrous tissue, regeneration produces disorganised nodules instead of normal lobules. This is **cirrhosis**.

## Repair by scar

When regeneration is not possible, or injury is extensive, the body lays down connective tissue. This proceeds in overlapping stages.

1. **Inflammation and clearance.** A clot forms, and neutrophils then macrophages clear dead tissue and debris. Macrophages are essential to the rest of the process.
2. **Granulation tissue.** Within three to five days, the area fills with **granulation tissue**: new thin-walled capillaries, proliferating fibroblasts and loose, oedematous matrix. Its name comes from the red granular look of its surface, each granule being a capillary loop. It bleeds easily.
3. **Collagen deposition and scar formation.** Fibroblasts lay down collagen, first type III and then mainly type I. Blood vessels regress. The tissue becomes paler and firmer.
4. **Remodelling.** Over months, collagen is broken down and rebuilt by **matrix metalloproteinases** and fibroblasts, cross-linked and aligned along lines of stress, and the scar matures.

::: theorem Growth factors drive repair {#thm-repair}
Repair by scar is coordinated by growth factors released mainly by macrophages and platelets:

- **VEGF** (vascular endothelial growth factor) drives **angiogenesis**, the sprouting of new vessels from existing ones;
- **PDGF** and **FGF** stimulate fibroblast migration and proliferation;
- **TGF-β** (transforming growth factor beta) is the most important **fibrogenic** factor: it stimulates collagen synthesis, inhibits matrix breakdown and converts fibroblasts into **myofibroblasts**, which contract.

Persistent TGF-β activity is the common pathway of fibrosis in many organs.
:::

### Healing of skin wounds

Skin wounds show the same stages, and their healing is classified by how the edges meet.

**Healing by first intention** (primary union) occurs in a clean incision with edges brought together by sutures. Little tissue has been lost, so there is a small amount of granulation tissue and a thin scar. A typical timeline:

| Time | Events |
|---|---|
| 24 hours | Clot fills the incision; neutrophils at the margins; epidermis begins to grow across |
| 3 days | Macrophages replace neutrophils; granulation tissue starts to fill the gap |
| 5 days | Granulation tissue fills the space; new vessels are most abundant; collagen bridges the incision |
| 2 weeks | Collagen accumulates, vessels regress, the scar blanches |
| 1 month and later | A connective tissue scar covered by intact epidermis; remodelling continues for months |

**Healing by second intention** (secondary union) occurs when a wound is large or left open, as in an ulcer, a large abscess cavity or an infected wound. Much more granulation tissue forms, more inflammation occurs, and **wound contraction** by myofibroblasts reduces the size of the defect, sometimes by most of its area. The scar is larger.

### Tensile strength

A healing wound is weak for weeks. When skin sutures are removed at about one week, the wound has only about 10% of the strength of unwounded skin. Strength rises quickly over the next month as collagen accumulates and is cross-linked, and reaches a plateau of about 70 to 80% of normal by about three months. It never returns to 100%.

### Factors that impair healing

Healing is slowed or prevented by local and systemic factors.

- **Local**: infection, the most important single cause of delayed healing; poor blood supply (peripheral arterial disease, venous disease); foreign bodies such as sutures or fragments; mechanical stress or movement; previous radiotherapy; pressure, as in pressure ulcers.
- **Systemic**: diabetes, which impairs leucocyte function and microcirculation; poor nutrition, especially protein deficiency and **vitamin C deficiency** (vitamin C is needed to hydroxylate proline in collagen); **glucocorticoids**, which inhibit inflammation and TGF-β production and so weaken scars; smoking; advanced age.

::: warning Do not mistake granulation tissue for granulomas
The names are similar, but the structures are unrelated. **Granulation tissue** is the vascular, fibroblast-rich tissue of normal healing, seen in every repairing wound. A **granuloma** is a collection of activated macrophages produced by a particular type of chronic inflammation, often signalling tuberculosis, sarcoidosis or a foreign body. A report of "granulation tissue" in an ulcer biopsy is expected; a report of "granulomas" opens a differential diagnosis.
:::

## Abnormal repair and fibrosis

Repair can fail in three directions.

- **Deficient scar formation** leads to **wound dehiscence** (the wound reopens) or to chronic ulcers, as in venous leg ulcers and diabetic foot ulcers.
- **Excessive scar formation.** A **hypertrophic scar** is raised but stays within the original wound and often regresses with time. A **keloid** grows beyond the boundaries of the original wound and does not regress; keloids are more common in people of African and Asian descent and on the earlobes, chest and shoulders. Exuberant granulation tissue that rises above the skin and prevents epithelialisation is called "proud flesh".
- **Contracture.** Excessive contraction of a scar, especially after burns across joints, can fix a limb in a deformed position.

**Fibrosis** in internal organs is repair that does not stop. When injury persists, as in chronic viral hepatitis, alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, the repair signals persist too. In the liver, **hepatic stellate cells** respond to TGF-β by becoming myofibroblasts and laying down collagen. In the lung, fibroblasts in the alveolar walls do the same. Normal tissue is replaced by scar, and the organ fails. Drugs that slow fibrosis now exist for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (pirfenidone and nintedanib), and many more are being studied ([[respiratory/interstitial-vascular-disease]]).

Because fibrosis is the main determinant of prognosis in chronic liver disease, it is assessed non-invasively. One widely used score is **FIB-4**:

$$
\text{FIB-4} = \frac{\text{age (years)} \times \text{AST (U/L)}}{\text{platelets }(10^9/\text{L}) \times \sqrt{\text{ALT (U/L)}}}
$$ {#eq-fib4}

A value below 1.3 makes advanced fibrosis unlikely, and a value above 2.67 makes it likely; values between need further testing, such as the enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) blood test or elastography. The thresholds are validated mainly for people aged 35 to 65.

::: example A fibrosis score {#ex-fib4}
A 58-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes and a fatty liver on ultrasound has AST 45 U/L, ALT 50 U/L and a platelet count of $160 \times 10^9$/L. Calculate her FIB-4 and say what it means.
::: solution
1. Numerator: $58 \times 45 = 2610$.
2. Denominator: $160 \times \sqrt{50} = 160 \times 7.071 \approx 1131$.
3. FIB-4 $\approx 2610/1131 \approx 2.31$.
4. The value lies between 1.3 and 2.67, so it is **indeterminate**: advanced fibrosis is neither excluded nor confirmed.
5. The next step in many UK pathways is an ELF test or transient elastography. The score works because fibrosis lowers the platelet count (through portal hypertension and reduced thrombopoietin) and AST rises relative to ALT as the liver becomes scarred. Age is included because fibrosis accumulates with time ([[gastrointestinal/liver-disease]]).
:::
:::

::: example An ulcer that will not heal {#ex-ulcer}
A 74-year-old man with type 2 diabetes has had an ulcer on the sole of his foot for three months. He has reduced sensation in both feet, and his foot pulses are weak. A biopsy of the ulcer edge shows chronic inflammation, granulation tissue with few vessels and no malignancy. Explain why the ulcer is not healing.
::: solution
1. **Neuropathy** means he does not feel pressure or injury, so the ulcer is repeatedly traumatised by walking.
2. **Ischaemia** from peripheral arterial disease limits oxygen and nutrient delivery, so granulation tissue forms poorly; the biopsy shows few vessels.
3. **Hyperglycaemia** impairs neutrophil chemotaxis and killing, and diabetic wounds often become infected, which prolongs inflammation.
4. Diabetes also impairs the switch of macrophages from an inflammatory to a reparative state, and growth factor signalling is reduced.
5. The biopsy excludes malignant change, which can rarely occur in a long-standing ulcer. Management, following national guidance, needs a multidisciplinary foot care team: offloading pressure, treating infection, restoring blood flow where possible and controlling glucose ([[endocrine/diabetes-complications]]).
:::
:::

::: quiz
A surgeon asks why a patient taking long-term high-dose prednisolone has a weak abdominal scar. Which explanation is best?
- [ ] Glucocorticoids increase TGF-β and cause excess fibrosis.
- [x] Glucocorticoids suppress inflammation and reduce TGF-β and collagen synthesis, so less collagen is laid down.
- [ ] Glucocorticoids destroy fibroblasts directly.
- [ ] Glucocorticoids cause vitamin C deficiency.
::: solution
Glucocorticoids reduce the inflammatory phase, including the macrophages that drive repair, and they lower TGF-β production and collagen synthesis. The scar has less collagen and lower tensile strength. They do not cause excess fibrosis, do not destroy fibroblasts outright and do not cause scurvy.
:::

::: history
In 1868 the German pathologist Theodor Langhans described the multinucleated giant cells with peripheral nuclei that are found in tuberculous granulomas and now bear his name. In 1882 Robert Koch identified *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* as the cause of tuberculosis, linking the granuloma to a specific microbe. Élie Metchnikoff's work on phagocytes in the 1880s placed the macrophage at the centre of inflammation and defence. In 1971 Giulio Gabbiani and Guido Majno identified the **myofibroblast** in granulation tissue: a fibroblast with contractile filaments, which explained how wounds contract. In the same year Judah Folkman proposed that tumours depend on angiogenesis, a concept that also transformed the study of granulation tissue and repair.
:::

## Where this leads

Chronic inflammation and fibrosis appear throughout the organ systems: in the liver ([[gastrointestinal/liver-disease]]), the lung ([[respiratory/interstitial-vascular-disease]]), the joints ([[musculoskeletal/inflammatory-arthritis]]) and the kidney ([[renal/chronic-kidney-disease]]). The immune mechanisms are developed in [[immunology/autoimmunity]] and [[immunology/hypersensitivity]]. Repair of injured tissue after surgery and trauma is covered in [[surgery/wounds-surgical-infection]]. Chronic inflammation is also a risk factor for cancer, as seen in [[pathology/cancer-biology]].

::: summary
- Chronic inflammation is prolonged inflammation in which inflammation, tissue destruction and repair occur together, with macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells.
- Causes: persistent infections, prolonged exposure to toxic agents, and autoimmune and allergic diseases.
- Macrophages may be activated classically (killing, tissue damage) or alternatively (repair, fibrosis). Macrophages and T cells sustain each other through IL-12 and IFN-γ.
- A granuloma is an organised collection of epithelioid macrophages, giant cells and lymphocytes; caseating granulomas suggest tuberculosis, non-caseating ones sarcoidosis, Crohn's disease or a foreign body. TNF maintains granulomas.
- Systemic effects: anaemia of chronic disease, cachexia, AA amyloidosis; low-grade inflammation contributes to atherosclerosis.
- Tissues are labile, stable or permanent. Regeneration needs both dividing cells and an intact matrix framework.
- Scar formation: clot and inflammation, granulation tissue, collagen deposition, remodelling. VEGF drives angiogenesis and TGF-β drives fibrosis.
- Wounds heal by first or second intention; strength is about 10% at one week and about 70 to 80% at three months.
- Healing is impaired by infection, ischaemia, diabetes, malnutrition, vitamin C deficiency and glucocorticoids. Abnormal repair includes dehiscence, keloids, contractures and organ fibrosis.
:::

::: exercise Anti-TNF and tuberculosis {level=2}
Before starting an anti-TNF drug for rheumatoid arthritis, a patient is screened for latent tuberculosis. Explain why, using the biology of the granuloma.
::: solution
In latent tuberculosis, a small number of live bacilli are contained within granulomas. TNF is required to form and maintain granulomas: it recruits and activates macrophages and holds their organised structure together. Blocking TNF can cause the granulomas to break down, releasing the bacilli and allowing reactivation, which often presents with disseminated or extrapulmonary disease. Screening identifies people with latent infection so that they can be treated before or alongside the biological drug, following national guidance.

::: exercise A scar that keeps growing {level=3}
A 19-year-old woman of Nigerian heritage had her ears pierced. A year later a firm, shiny nodule has grown on one earlobe, extending well beyond the piercing. It was excised once and recurred larger. Name the lesion, explain how it differs from a hypertrophic scar, and suggest why it recurred.
::: hint
Compare the boundaries of the lesion with the original wound.
:::
::: solution
This is a **keloid**: an overgrowth of dense collagen that extends beyond the boundaries of the original wound and does not regress. A hypertrophic scar is raised but stays within the original wound and often flattens over a year or two. Keloids are more common in people of African and Asian descent and at sites such as the earlobe and chest, suggesting genetic predisposition. Fibroblasts in keloids respond excessively to TGF-β and make abnormal amounts of collagen. Excision is itself a new wound and triggers the same abnormal response, so keloids often recur unless excision is combined with other measures, such as steroid injections, pressure or radiotherapy, chosen by a specialist.
:::

::: exercise Granulomas in the bowel {level=3}
A 23-year-old man has diarrhoea and weight loss. Colonoscopy shows patchy ulceration with normal bowel between the ulcers, and biopsies show transmural chronic inflammation with non-caseating granulomas. Give a differential diagnosis for granulomas in the bowel, the investigations that would distinguish them, and explain why the inflammation is chronic.
::: solution
The differential includes **Crohn's disease** (the most likely, given skip lesions and transmural inflammation), intestinal **tuberculosis** (especially with relevant travel or origin; granulomas are often larger, confluent and caseating), other infections such as *Yersinia*, sarcoidosis (rare in the gut) and a foreign body reaction to bowel contents. Distinguishing tests include Ziehl–Neelsen staining and mycobacterial culture and PCR on biopsies, a chest radiograph and interferon-γ release assay for tuberculosis, stool cultures, and the overall pattern on imaging and endoscopy. The inflammation in Crohn's disease is chronic because the immune response to the bowel's own microbial contents cannot be resolved: the antigen is always present, and the macrophage–lymphocyte loop sustained by IL-12/23, IFN-γ and TNF keeps running. This is why biological drugs targeting TNF or IL-12/23 are used ([[gastrointestinal/bowel-disease]]).
:::
