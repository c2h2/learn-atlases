Growth is the single most useful sign in paediatrics. A child who is growing normally is very unlikely to have a serious chronic disease, and a child whose growth has slowed or stalled almost always has a reason that deserves to be found. Development is the second sign. Children acquire skills in a predictable order, and a child who is late, uneven or losing skills is telling us something about the brain, the senses or the environment.

This chapter explains how to measure a child, how to plot and read a growth chart, how to assess development, and how puberty works. The emphasis is on method: the measurements are simple, but the conclusions depend on doing them properly and on looking at the pattern over time rather than at a single point.

## Measuring growth

A single measurement says where a child is. Repeated measurements say where the child is going, and that is usually the more important question.

The routine measurements are weight, length or height, and head circumference. Babies are weighed naked on electronic scales. Length is measured lying down on a measuring board until the age of 2 years, and standing height is measured from then on with a stadiometer, heels together and the head in the Frankfort plane. Head circumference is the largest of three measurements around the forehead and occiput with a non-stretch tape. Each measurement has an error of a few millimetres or grams, which matters when the measurements are close together in time.

::: definition Centiles and the growth chart {#def-growth}
A **centile** (percentile) is the position of a measurement in a reference population of children of the same age and sex. A child on the 25th centile for height is taller than 25% of the reference children and shorter than 75%.

A **growth chart** plots centile lines against age. UK charts show nine lines: the 0.4th, 2nd, 9th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 91st, 98th and 99.6th centiles. Neighbouring lines are two-thirds of a standard deviation apart, so the space between two lines is called a **centile space**.

**Faltering growth** (formerly "failure to thrive") is a rate of weight gain in infancy or childhood that is slower than expected for the child's age and sex. It is a description, not a diagnosis.
:::

In the UK, children from birth to 4 years are plotted on the **UK-WHO charts**, introduced in 2009. They combine the World Health Organization Child Growth Standards, which describe how healthy breastfed children *should* grow, with UK data for babies born preterm. From 2 years onwards the **UK90** reference charts are used for height, weight and body mass index (BMI). The two systems overlap between 2 and 4 years.

::: theorem Centile lines are equally spaced in standard deviations {#thm-centiles}
For a measurement that is approximately normally distributed at each age, the centile lines on the UK charts correspond to z-scores of −2.67, −2, −1.33, −0.67, 0, +0.67, +1.33, +2 and +2.67. A child whose z-score stays constant over time is growing along a centile; a fall of 0.67 in z-score is a fall of one centile space.
:::

::: proof
A z-score is the number of standard deviations a measurement lies above or below the mean for age and sex. If the measurement is normally distributed, each z-score corresponds to a fixed centile: z = 0 is the 50th centile, z = −0.67 is about the 25th, z = −1.33 about the 9th, z = −2 about the 2nd and z = −2.67 about the 0.4th. The chart designers chose these lines so that the gaps are equal on the z-scale. Weight is skewed rather than normal, so the reference data are first transformed (the LMS method) to make them normal at each age. That is why weight centile lines look unevenly spaced in kilograms but are still evenly spaced in standard deviations. The practical consequence is that "crossing two centile lines" means the same amount of change, in standard deviations, at any position on the chart.
:::

The figure below shows the standard normal curve. Shade between −2.67 and +2.67 to see that only about 0.8% of healthy children lie outside the outer lines. A measurement below the 0.4th centile therefore always needs an explanation.

### Growth velocity and the shape of normal growth

Growth is fastest in fetal life and infancy. A typical term baby doubles birth weight by about 5 months and roughly triples it by 1 year, and length increases by about half in the first year. Through childhood height increases by about 5–6 cm per year. In puberty there is a growth spurt, with a peak height velocity of roughly 8–9 cm per year in girls and 9–10 cm per year in boys. Girls begin their spurt about two years earlier than boys, which is the main reason adult men are on average about 13 cm taller than women.

**Height velocity** is the change in height divided by the time between measurements, expressed in cm per year. Because measurement error is a few millimetres, velocity should be calculated over at least 6 months and preferably a year.

### Genetic potential: mid-parental height

Children's adult height depends largely on their parents' heights. The **mid-parental height** estimates a child's genetic target:

$$
\text{MPH (boy)} = \frac{\text{father} + \text{mother} + 13}{2}, \qquad \text{MPH (girl)} = \frac{\text{father} + \text{mother} - 13}{2}
$$ {#eq-mph}

with heights in centimetres. The 13 cm corrects for the average difference between men and women. The target range is roughly the MPH ± 8.5 cm. A child whose height centile is well below the centile expected from the parents deserves a closer look, even if the child is above the 2nd centile.

::: example A mid-parental height calculation {#ex-mph}
Amir is 9 years old. His height is on the 9th centile. His father is 183 cm tall and his mother is 170 cm. Calculate his mid-parental height and decide whether his height is consistent with it.
::: solution
For a boy, $\text{MPH} = (183 + 170 + 13)/2 = 366/2 = 183$ cm. The target range is about 174.5–191.5 cm.

An adult height of 183 cm lies between the 75th and 91st centiles on UK charts for men. Amir is on the 9th centile, which is about two centile spaces below his genetic expectation.

That gap does not make a diagnosis, but it means "he is short because his parents are short" is not available as an explanation. The next steps are to find earlier measurements to see whether he has always been on this centile or has fallen to it, to measure his height velocity over the next 6 months, to look at his pubertal status and the timing of puberty in the parents, and to take a history for chronic disease (for example coeliac disease or inflammatory bowel disease).
:::
:::

### Correcting for prematurity

A baby born early has not "lost" growth; the baby simply started earlier. For babies born before 37 weeks, measurements are plotted at **corrected age**: chronological age minus the number of weeks born early. On UK-WHO charts, correction continues until 1 year for babies born at 32–36 weeks and until 2 years for babies born before 32 weeks.

::: example Correcting age before plotting {#ex-corrected}
Ella was born at 28 weeks of gestation. She is now 26 weeks old. At what age should her measurements be plotted?
::: solution
Term is taken as 40 weeks. Ella was born $40 - 28 = 12$ weeks early. Her corrected age is $26 - 12 = 14$ weeks.

Plotted at 26 weeks, her weight might sit below the 2nd centile and suggest faltering growth. Plotted at 14 weeks, it may be on the 25th centile and entirely appropriate. Because she was born before 32 weeks, correction continues until she is 2 years old. The same correction applies to developmental milestones: she would be expected to smile responsively at around 6 weeks *corrected* age, which is about 18 weeks after birth.
:::
:::

### Faltering growth

NICE guideline NG75 (2017) defines concern about weight in infants and children by the number of centile spaces crossed. A fall across one or more centile spaces is a concern if the birth weight was below the 9th centile, two or more spaces if it was between the 9th and 91st, and three or more if it was above the 91st. A current weight below the 2nd centile is also a concern whatever the birth weight.

The large allowance for heavy babies reflects **regression to the mean**. Birth weight partly reflects the intrauterine environment, and many large babies settle onto a lower centile in the first months. Thin babies with small parents may also cross centiles upwards. Both are normal.

Most faltering growth has a simple cause: not enough milk or food going in, or not enough being retained. The first steps are a careful feeding history, observing a feed, and asking about vomiting, stools and illness. Organic causes are less common but important: coeliac disease, cow's milk protein allergy, cystic fibrosis, congenital heart disease, renal disease, hypothyroidism and chronic infection. Neglect is also a cause, and growth that recovers on admission to hospital is an important observation.

### Short stature and tall stature

**Short stature** is usually defined as a height below the 2nd centile, or below the 0.4th for referral purposes. Most short children are healthy and fall into one of two patterns:

- **Familial short stature**: the parents are short, the child grows parallel to the centiles at a normal velocity, and the bone age matches the chronological age.
- **Constitutional delay of growth and puberty**: the child is short in childhood, enters puberty late, and continues growing for longer. Bone age is delayed. There is often a family history of "late bloomers". Final height is usually within the family target.

Pathological short stature is suggested by a falling height centile, a low height velocity, disproportion between limbs and trunk, dysmorphic features, or a height well below the family target. Causes include hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, Turner syndrome in girls, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic kidney disease, long-term corticosteroids and psychosocial deprivation.

**Tall stature** is usually familial. Pathological causes include early puberty (tall now, but short as an adult because the growth plates close early), Klinefelter and Marfan syndromes, and rarely growth hormone excess.

::: warning One measurement is not a growth pattern
A single height or weight on the 2nd centile says almost nothing on its own. The commonest errors in growth assessment are plotting at the wrong age (forgetting to correct for prematurity, or plotting at the nearest birthday), using the wrong chart, and drawing conclusions from two measurements a few weeks apart that differ by less than the measurement error. Always find earlier measurements, check the plotting, and compare with the parents.
:::

## Development

Development means the acquisition of skills. It is described in four domains: **gross motor**, **fine motor and vision**, **speech, language and hearing**, and **social, emotional and behavioural**. In each domain the order of skills is very consistent between children, but the age at which each skill appears varies widely. That is why it is more useful to know the **limit age** (the age by which almost all children have a skill) than the average.

::: definition Developmental delay and regression {#def-development}
**Developmental delay** is the failure to reach milestones by the expected limit age. It can affect one domain (for example isolated speech delay) or several.

**Global developmental delay** is significant delay in two or more domains in a child under 5 years. In older children the term **intellectual disability** (learning disability in UK services) is used once formal assessment is possible.

**Developmental regression** is the loss of skills that the child had already acquired. It is always abnormal and always needs urgent assessment.
:::

### Milestones to know

The table gives typical ages and limit ages for a few key milestones. The figures follow commonly used UK teaching; individual sources vary by a few weeks.

| Domain | Skill | Typical age | Refer if not achieved by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social | Smiles responsively | 6 weeks | 8 weeks |
| Gross motor | Sits without support | 6–8 months | 9 months |
| Gross motor | Walks independently | 12 months | 18 months |
| Fine motor | Palmar grasp of a cube | 6 months | — |
| Fine motor | Pincer grip | 10 months | 12 months |
| Fine motor | Reaches for objects | 4 months | 6 months |
| Speech | Babbles with consonants ("baba") | 7 months | — |
| Speech | Single words with meaning | 12 months | 18 months |
| Speech | Joins two words | 20–24 months | 30 months |
| Social | Points to share interest | 12 months | 18 months |

Two patterns are always abnormal at any age: **hand preference before 12 months** (which suggests weakness of the other side, for example a hemiplegia), and **loss of skills**. Concerns from parents about hearing or vision should always be taken seriously, because they are often right.

### How development is assessed

In England, the **Healthy Child Programme** offers every family contacts with a health visitor, including reviews at about 9–12 months and 2–2½ years, when development is checked, often with the Ages and Stages Questionnaire. Doctors usually assess development by history and by observing the child at play. A useful order is: observe first, then ask the parents what the child does at home, then test specific skills with simple toys.

When a delay is found, the first questions are:

1. **Is it real?** Has the age been corrected for prematurity? Was the child unwell, tired or shy?
2. **Which domains are affected?** Isolated speech delay points first to hearing loss or a language disorder. Isolated motor delay points to a neuromuscular cause or cerebral palsy. Global delay points to a genetic, metabolic, structural or environmental cause.
3. **Is there regression?**
4. **Are the head size and growth normal?** Microcephaly and macrocephaly are both important clues.

Every child with speech delay needs a formal hearing test. Every child with global delay needs a careful examination for dysmorphic features, skin markings (for example café-au-lait patches) and neurological signs. First-line investigations commonly include a chromosomal microarray and fragile X testing, creatine kinase in boys with motor delay, and thyroid function. Neurodevelopmental conditions are covered in [[paediatrics/neurodevelopment]].

::: example The 20-month-old who still is not walking well {#ex-regress}
Kwame is 20 months old. He walked at 18 months but falls often and cannot run. His speech is a little behind: he has six words. On examination he has large calves. When asked to get up from sitting on the floor, he turns onto his front and pushes up with his hands on his thighs.
::: solution
**Step 1: which domain?** The main delay is gross motor, with milder speech delay.

**Step 2: what does the examination add?** Climbing up the thighs to stand is **Gowers' sign**. It shows weakness of the hip and thigh muscles. Large calves in a boy with proximal weakness suggest pseudohypertrophy.

**Step 3: the likely diagnosis.** This pattern is typical of **Duchenne muscular dystrophy**, an X-linked condition caused by loss of dystrophin. Speech and learning difficulties are common in Duchenne, which explains the speech delay.

**Step 4: investigation.** The first test is serum **creatine kinase**, which is greatly raised (often 10 to 100 times normal). Genetic testing of the *DMD* gene confirms the diagnosis. Referral is to a paediatric neuromuscular service.

**Lesson.** Any boy who is not walking by 18 months should have a creatine kinase measured. A delay in diagnosis means a delay in genetic counselling for the family and in starting treatment such as corticosteroids.
:::
:::

### Head circumference

Head growth reflects brain growth in infancy. **Microcephaly** (head circumference below the 0.4th centile) may be familial, but it may also reflect congenital infection, a genetic syndrome, fetal alcohol exposure or brain injury. **Macrocephaly** (above the 99.6th centile) is often familial (measure the parents) but may reflect hydrocephalus. The most important sign is a head that is **crossing centiles upwards**, especially with a bulging fontanelle, separated sutures, vomiting or "sunsetting" eyes.

::: example The 6-week-old whose head is growing too fast {#ex-headcirc}
At her 6-week check, Sofia's head circumference is on the 91st centile. At birth it was on the 50th. Her weight and length are on the 50th centile. She is feeding well. Her anterior fontanelle is full but not tense, and her parents both have average head sizes.
::: solution
Sofia's head has crossed two centile spaces in six weeks while her weight and length have stayed on the same centile. This is disproportionate head growth, and the parents' head sizes do not explain it.

The concern is **hydrocephalus**, an excess of cerebrospinal fluid causing the ventricles to enlarge. In an infant the sutures are open, so the head enlarges before the classic signs of raised pressure appear. Other causes include benign enlargement of the subarachnoid spaces and, rarely, a space-occupying lesion or subdural collections.

The next step is a **cranial ultrasound** through the open fontanelle, which is quick and needs no sedation, followed by MRI if it is abnormal. She should be re-measured carefully, because a single measurement error is common, but a repeat measurement should not delay imaging when the fontanelle is full. If subdural collections are found, the differential includes abusive head trauma (see [[paediatrics/safeguarding]]).
:::
:::

## Puberty and the growing skeleton

Puberty is the sequence of physical changes that produces sexual maturity and adult height. It is driven by the reactivation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis: pulsatile secretion of gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) stimulates the pituitary to release luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), which stimulate the gonads.

::: definition Puberty and its staging {#def-puberty}
**Tanner staging** describes pubertal development in five stages, from prepubertal (stage 1) to adult (stage 5), separately for breasts in girls, genitalia in boys, and pubic hair in both.

The first sign of puberty in girls is **thelarche** (breast budding), on average at about 11 years. The first sign in boys is **testicular enlargement** to a volume of 4 mL or more, measured with an orchidometer, on average at about 11.5–12 years.

**Precocious puberty** is the onset of puberty before 8 years in girls or 9 years in boys. **Delayed puberty** is the absence of breast development by 13 years in girls, or of testicular enlargement by 14 years in boys.
:::

The order of events differs between the sexes. In girls, the growth spurt comes early, at breast stage 2–3, and **menarche** comes late, about 2–2½ years after thelarche, when most of the growth spurt is over. In boys, the growth spurt comes late, at genital stage 4. This explains why girls at 12 are often taller than boys of the same age.

### Bone age

The growth plates fuse under the influence of oestrogen in both sexes. The maturity of the skeleton can be estimated from an X-ray of the left hand and wrist, by comparing the bones with standard images. This is the **bone age**. A delayed bone age means there is more growth still to come. It is typical of constitutional delay, hypothyroidism and growth hormone deficiency. An advanced bone age is typical of precocious puberty and obesity.

### Precocious puberty

In girls, most early puberty is **central** (gonadotrophin-dependent): the normal process has simply started too early, and no cause is found in most cases. In boys, a cause is much more often found, so early puberty in a boy always needs investigation of the brain (MRI) and of the testes and adrenal glands. Peripheral (gonadotrophin-independent) causes include congenital adrenal hyperplasia, adrenal or gonadal tumours and McCune–Albright syndrome.

Two benign variants should be recognised. **Premature thelarche** is isolated breast development, usually in girls under 2, without a growth spurt. **Premature adrenarche** is early pubic or axillary hair and body odour from adrenal androgens, without breast or testicular development, and is more common in children of South Asian and African-Caribbean heritage and in children with obesity.

::: example The short 13-year-old {#ex-shortgirl}
Leila is 13 years and 6 months old and has been referred because she is the shortest in her class. Her height is on the 2nd centile; at age 8 it was also on the 2nd centile. She has no breast development. Her height velocity over the last year is 5 cm. Her mother is 160 cm tall and had her first period at 15; her father is 172 cm. Bone age is 11 years.
::: solution
**Step 1: genetic target.** For a girl, $\text{MPH} = (172 + 160 - 13)/2 = 319/2 = 159.5$ cm. This lies near the 25th centile for women. Leila is below the family expectation.

**Step 2: the pattern.** Her height has followed the 2nd centile for five years, and her velocity of 5 cm/year is normal for a prepubertal child. She is not falling away from her centile.

**Step 3: puberty.** She has no breast development at 13.5 years, which meets the definition of delayed puberty. Her bone age is delayed by two and a half years, so she has more growth left than her classmates.

**Step 4: interpretation.** The combination of steady prepubertal growth, delayed bone age and a mother who had late menarche fits **constitutional delay of growth and puberty**. But two diagnoses must be excluded in a short girl with delayed puberty. **Turner syndrome** (45,X and variants) causes short stature and ovarian failure; it needs a karyotype in every short girl without an obvious cause. **Hypothyroidism** and chronic diseases such as coeliac disease must also be excluded.

**Step 5: plan.** Karyotype, thyroid function, coeliac serology, full blood count and inflammatory markers, and LH, FSH and oestradiol. A high FSH would point to ovarian failure. If all are normal, she can be reassured and followed. Some young people with constitutional delay benefit from a short course of low-dose sex steroids to start puberty, which is a specialist decision.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A 4-year-old boy's height has been on the 9th centile since infancy. His parents' mid-parental height lies on the 9th centile. His height velocity is normal. What is the most likely explanation?
- [x] Familial short stature: he is growing normally along his genetic centile.
- [ ] Constitutional delay: his bone age will be delayed.
- [ ] Growth hormone deficiency: his height is below the 25th centile.
- [ ] Faltering growth: he needs a feeding assessment.
::: solution
He has always been on the same centile, his velocity is normal, and his position matches his parents. That is normal growth for this family. The 9th centile is not short stature, and growth hormone deficiency would cause falling centiles and a low velocity. Faltering growth refers to weight, not height.
:::

::: history
Measuring children to judge their health has a long history. Between 1759 and 1777 Philibert Guéneau de Montbeillard measured his son every six months from birth to age 18; when the data were later plotted, they showed the shape of the human growth curve, including the pubertal spurt. In the twentieth century James Tanner and colleagues at the Institute of Child Health in London studied the growth of children in the Harpenden Growth Study. With R. H. Whitehouse and Michio Takaishi he published British standards for height, weight and growth velocity in 1966, and Tanner's descriptions of pubertal stages became the standard method of staging puberty. In 2006 the World Health Organization published standards based on healthy breastfed children in six countries, and the UK adopted them in 2009 for children under 4.
:::

::: summary
- Measure accurately, plot at the correct age on the correct chart, and judge growth by the pattern over time, not by one point.
- UK centile lines are two-thirds of a standard deviation apart; about 0.8% of healthy children lie outside the outer lines.
- Mid-parental height gives a genetic target; a child far below the family target needs assessment even above the 2nd centile.
- Correct for prematurity until 1 year (born 32–36 weeks) or 2 years (born before 32 weeks).
- Most short children have familial short stature or constitutional delay; falling centiles, low velocity and disproportion suggest disease. Every short girl without a clear cause needs a karyotype.
- Use limit ages for milestones. Regression, early hand preference and parental concern about hearing or vision always need assessment.
- Puberty is precocious before 8 in girls and 9 in boys, and delayed if absent by 13 in girls and 14 in boys. Early puberty in boys usually has a cause.
:::

::: exercise Calculate a mid-parental height {level=1 check="166"}
A girl's father is 180 cm tall and her mother 165 cm. Calculate her mid-parental height in centimetres.
::: hint
For a girl, subtract 13 cm before halving.
:::
::: solution
$\text{MPH} = (180 + 165 - 13)/2 = 332/2 = 166$ cm. The target range is about 157.5–174.5 cm.
:::

::: exercise Plot at the right age {level=1 check="7"}
A baby born at 31 weeks is now 16 weeks old. What is the baby's corrected age in weeks?
::: solution
Born $40 - 31 = 9$ weeks early, so the corrected age is $16 - 9 = 7$ weeks. Because the baby was born before 32 weeks, correction continues until 2 years.
:::
:::

::: exercise Height velocity {level=1 check="round(10*(131.2-128.0)/(9/12))/10"}
A boy measured 128.0 cm in March and 131.2 cm nine months later. Calculate his height velocity in cm per year, rounded to one decimal place.
::: solution
The gain is 3.2 cm in 0.75 years, so the velocity is $3.2/0.75 = 4.27$ cm/year, which rounds to **4.3** cm/year. This is low-normal for a prepubertal child. Because measurement error is a few millimetres, a velocity over nine months is reasonably reliable, but one more measurement would make it more so.
:::
:::

::: exercise Body mass index {level=2 check="round(10*30/1.3^2)/10"}
An 8-year-old girl weighs 30 kg and is 1.30 m tall. Calculate her BMI in kg/m² to one decimal place, and explain how it is interpreted in a child.
::: solution
$\text{BMI} = 30/1.30^2 = 30/1.69 = 17.75$, which rounds to **17.8** kg/m².

In children BMI changes with age, so it cannot be compared with adult cut-offs. It is plotted on a UK90 BMI centile chart. In clinical practice in the UK, a BMI at or above the 91st centile suggests overweight and at or above the 98th centile suggests obesity.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which babies have faltering growth? {level=2}
Three 4-month-old babies are reviewed. Baby A: birth weight 98th centile, now 50th. Baby B: birth weight 50th centile, now 9th. Baby C: birth weight 6th centile, now just below the 2nd. Using NICE thresholds, which are of concern?
::: hint
Count the centile spaces crossed, and remember that the threshold depends on the birth centile.
:::
::: solution
Baby A was above the 91st centile and has crossed three spaces (98th → 91st → 75th → 50th). That meets the threshold of three spaces for a heavy baby, so it is a concern, although regression to the mean is the likely explanation and a feeding history may be all that is needed.

Baby B started between the 9th and 91st centiles and has crossed three spaces (50th → 25th → 9th), above the threshold of two, so this is a concern.

Baby C started below the 9th centile and has crossed one space, to just below the 2nd centile. One space is the threshold for small babies, and any weight below the 2nd centile is a concern in its own right, so this is also a concern.

All three need a feeding assessment and review. The thresholds identify concern, not disease.
:::
:::

::: exercise A boy with early puberty {level=2}
A 7-year-old boy has pubic hair, an adult body odour and a growth spurt. His testes are 2 mL each. What does the testicular volume tell you, and what is the most important cause to exclude?
::: solution
Prepubertal testes (below 4 mL) with signs of androgen excess mean the androgens are not coming from the testes under gonadotrophin drive. This is **peripheral (gonadotrophin-independent)** precocious puberty. The source is likely to be the adrenal glands. The most important causes are **congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (non-classical forms can present like this) and an **adrenal tumour**. Investigations include 17-hydroxyprogesterone, adrenal androgens, bone age and adrenal imaging. Premature adrenarche causes milder changes without a marked growth spurt or rapidly advancing bone age.
:::
:::

::: exercise The child with speech delay {level=3}
A 2½-year-old girl has about ten words and does not join words. She follows simple instructions, plays imaginatively with a doll and points to show her mother things. Her motor development is normal. She had recurrent ear infections last winter. Discuss the differential diagnosis and the first investigation.
::: solution
This is an isolated delay in expressive language (she should be joining words by 30 months), with good comprehension, normal motor development and good social communication (pointing to share interest, pretend play).

**Differential diagnosis.** (1) **Conductive hearing loss** from glue ear (otitis media with effusion), suggested by the recurrent ear infections. (2) A **developmental language disorder**, which is common and often familial. (3) Lack of language exposure in the home. (4) Autism is less likely because her social communication is good, but should be kept in mind. (5) Global delay is unlikely because the other domains are normal.

**First investigation.** A formal **audiological assessment** including tympanometry, because hearing loss is treatable and every child with speech delay must have hearing tested. Referral to speech and language therapy can proceed at the same time. Developmental surveillance should continue, because a language disorder can affect learning and behaviour later.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a growth sequence {level=3}
Jamie, a 6-year-old boy, has these heights: age 3, 50th centile; age 4, 50th centile; age 5, 25th centile; age 6, 9th centile. His weight has stayed on the 50th centile, so his BMI centile is rising. He seems well, although his mother says he is tired and his school work has deteriorated. What is the likely category of disorder, and which tests would you do first?
::: hint
Compare what is happening to height and weight. Which disorders slow height while weight is maintained?
:::
::: solution
Jamie's height is falling across centiles (two spaces in two years, a low height velocity) while his weight is maintained, so he is becoming relatively overweight. This pattern points to an **endocrine cause**. Nutritional and chronic inflammatory diseases usually affect weight first and height later, so the child becomes thin.

The main endocrine causes are **acquired hypothyroidism** (tiredness and falling school performance fit), **growth hormone deficiency** (including from a tumour such as a craniopharyngioma, which may also cause headache and visual field loss) and **Cushing syndrome**, including from steroid treatment.

First tests: thyroid function (TSH and free T4), a bone age X-ray, and a careful history of headache, vision and steroid use with examination of the visual fields and fundi. If thyroid function is normal, he needs referral to a paediatric endocrinologist for growth hormone testing and probably MRI of the pituitary region.
:::
:::
