Neurodevelopment is the paediatrics of time: conditions defined not by a single abnormal test but by a child's trajectory across years, in multiple domains, starting early. **This chapter is how neurodevelopmental conditions are recognised and diagnosed - autism, developmental coordination disorder, speech and language disorders, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, intellectual disability, tic disorders and Tourette syndrome, and cerebral palsy as the motor member of the family - what the evidence says about treatments from parent-training to medication, how schools and local authorities are obliged to help, and why the same child can be 'behaviour problem' in one room and 'twice-exceptional' in another. The governing idea is that brains vary, some variations cause disability in particular environments, and the clinician's job is to describe the child accurately to systems that will otherwise describe them lazily.**

## Recognition and diagnosis

::: definition Principles and the common conditions {#def-conditions}
**Diagnosis is descriptive, dimensional and functional: development is domain-by-domain - gross and fine motor, speech and language (receptive versus expressive are different diseases in practice), cognition, social communication, adaptive behaviour, learning, attention and behaviour - and children are rarely globally delayed, so 'global developmental delay' under five and 'intellectual disability' after it (IQ roughly under 70 with adaptive-functioning limits) are summaries, not diagnoses. Cerebral palsy is the commonest cause of chronic motor disability (roughly 2 per 1,000 school-age children), a non-progressive injury or disorder of the developing brain classified by topography (spastic diplegia, hemiplegia, quadriplegia), by tone and movement type (spastic, dyskinetic, ataxic - and the dystonia and choreoathetosis that get mislabelled 'clumsy' or 'athetoid cerebral palsy' after hypoxic injury or severe jaundice) and by function with GMFCS levels I-V, with the essential caveat that the motor system is never alone: epilepsy, pain from hips and scoliosis, constipation, reflux, communication and intellectual disability travel with it, and the multidisciplinary team exists because no one specialty owns the child. Autism is a neurodevelopmental difference in social communication and restricted or repetitive behaviour, present from early development, present on a spectrum in support needs and in presentation, diagnosed developmentally with observation plus history (ADOS and ADI-R as research-backed aids, not magic) and increasingly recognised in girls whose social camouflage delays diagnosis, with high co-occurring anxiety, feeding differences, intellectual disability in a minority, and a substantial minority who are gifted - the twice-exceptional child (autistic AND bright) who is missed because nobody connects the distress to the profile. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder of inattention and or hyperactivity-impulsivity causing impairment across settings, with heritable neurobiology, and the debate about its existence is settled against the sceptics - the diagnosis is made by cross-setting history and function, not by a questionnaire score, and the differential to exclude is sleep-disordered breathing, absence seizures, anxiety and trauma, learning difficulty masked as not paying attention and the classroom that fails any child. Specific language impairment, developmental coordination disorder ('dyspraxia'), tic disorders including Tourette (the 'he blinks when nervous' that is actually a waxing-and-waning neurological condition starting around 6-8 with the ADHD and OCD baggage more disabling than the tics), and learning difficulties like dyslexia and dyscalculia complete the common set. Two structural rules: every new neurodevelopmental diagnosis in a child prompts the question 'does this child need hearing and vision assessed, and does the pattern need a genetic or metabolic or imaging cause' - neurodevelopmental conditions are a phenotype, and idiopathic is allowed but must be earned - and every diagnosis is made with the school in the loop because half the impairment lives there - [[paediatrics/growth-development]], [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]], [[genetics/common-disease-genetics]].**
:::

## Management, systems and medicines

::: definition Interventions and the system around them {#def-manage}
**Treatments work at three levels: the child's skills, the family's load and the environment's demands. For autism, the evidence supports parent-mediated approaches that improve communication and reduce distress for pre-schoolers, speech and occupational therapy aimed at function rather than hourly-drill rituals, and CBT adapted for autistic clients for the anxiety that often dominates the picture - while applied-behaviour-analysis-style programmes are adopted and audited with eyes open about their evidence base and their potential to pathologise normal autistic movement, and every therapy hour needs the question 'better at what, measured how'. For ADHD in school-age children, stimulant medication (methylphenidate first-line in most guidelines, lisdexamfetamine and the others in sequence; dexamfetamine and atomoxetine and guanfacine as further options) is among the most effective treatments in all of psychiatry for symptom levels, with monitoring of height, weight, pulse, blood pressure and sleep, cardiology review for the structural-heart minority, and the honest counselling that medication plus behavioural school measures plus sleep protection beats any one alone; adolescents need the adherence conversation (who holds the pharmacy box) and the driving and substance-misuse conversations early, not after the first suspension. For cerebral palsy, tone management is a ladder - physiotherapy and orthotics and schooling first, botulinum toxin for focal spasticity, oral baclofen and diazepam for generalised tone and sleep, intrathecal baclofen and selective dorsal rhizotomy for selected severe cases - with the hip-surveillance programme and the gastrostomy-and-fundoplication discussions that are really quality-of-life conversations wearing lab coats. For speech, language and coordination, the key intervention is access: the speech-and-language therapist training the teacher, the classroom accommodations (extra processing time, written instructions, seating, movement breaks) that cost nothing and are the entire reason occupational outcomes vary. And around all of it stands the legal architecture: in England the SEND system - the School Support Plan legacy, the Education, Health and Care plan that a local authority must assess within legally fixed timeframes, and the chronic national failure of that promise documented by tribunals and coroners after the cases of children harmed by unmet needs; the paediatrician's letter is often the difference between a plan and a tribunal, so it must describe function, evidence and needs in plain specific sentences, not diagnoses alone. Finally the health-inequities layer: adopted and looked-after children carry disproportionate neurodevelopmental burden (early adversity, attachment disruption and genuine neurobiology together), children in poverty are over-diagnosed as behavioural and under-diagnosed as needing help, and the system's default - that the articulate middle-class parent who threatens a tribunal gets the speech therapy - is the quietest access failure in medicine - [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]], [[nervous-system/motor-systems]], [[public-health/determinants-inequalities]].**
:::

## Living with the diagnosis

::: definition Adolescents, comorbidity and the long view {#def-long}
**Neurodevelopment does not end at puberty and clinic hand-over is where services fail. The autistic adolescent's depression is often under-treated because 'autistic people are like that' becomes an excuse for not asking; the ADHD adolescent's risk profile - unlicensed driving, substance misuse, school exclusion, self-harm - is the actual morbidity of the condition at 16 and belongs in every medication review; the girl whose autism was camouflaged until a school refusal or eating-disorder presentation at 14 needs the diagnosis as an explanation that rebuilds her self-narrative, and clinicians should be ready to offer it without apology even though 'you are autistic' arrives after years of 'you are lazy, rude, dramatic'. Eating disorders co-cluster with autism at rates that should change inpatient practice (sensory food lists, predictability, fewer fluorescent-lit chaos mealtimes), and 'atypical anorexia that does not look like the textbooks' in a rigid, special-interest-having teenager is a clue. Chronic pain and fatigue in neurodevelopmental children are under-assessed because communication differences are mistaken for absent suffering - pain scales must be adapted and the non-speaking child's distress behaviours read as data, and the hip-scan-and-physiotherapy schedule in GMFCS IV-V cerebral palsy exists because pain presents as 'increased spasticity and worse sleep', not as a complaint. Then the transitions: to adult neurology or learning-disability services where 'no seizure-free interval' and 'no active treatment needed' are used to discharge people who need a GP who understands their epilepsy and constipation; to college or work with the access-to-learning supports that the diagnosis letter unlocks; to sexual-health and capacity conversations the paediatrician should start at 14 with the parents' blessing, because consent, safeguarding from exploitation ('a stranger who buys them chips is the most common perpetrator story in the learning-disability service') and online vulnerability are neurodevelopmental issues as much as social ones. And for the family, the long view means the sibling who grows up parentified, the parent who stops working, and the parents' own ageing - 'what happens when we are gone' asked at 4am in every clinic waiting room and answered badly with silence or a generic leaflet; the answer is a personal life plan started in childhood and reviewed annually, legally literate (trusts, direct payments, lasting power of attorney where capacity permits planning) and honest about what support public services actually deliver. A neurodevelopmental clinic that only manages diagnoses is a form-filling service; the ones with value manage trajectories, expectations and the interfaces - school, social care, adult services - where a child's life is actually decided - [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]], [[medical-ethics-law/consent-capacity]].**
:::

## Where this leads

Normal variation is the baseline in [[paediatrics/growth-development]], the motor syndromes in [[nervous-system/motor-systems]], seizures in [[nervous-system/epilepsy]], the psychiatric comorbidity in [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]] and [[psychiatry/anxiety-disorders]], and the education-law interface in [[public-health/determinants-inequalities]].

::: history
**The neurodevelopmental disciplines grew out of three separate injustices. First the institutions: the nineteenth-century 'asylums for imbeciles' and the large-scale institutions of the twentieth century, whose exposed conditions - the Ely and Suffolk hospitals inquiries, the BBC's 1968 'Secret Children', Wolfensberger's normalisation principle - drove deinstitutionalisation from the 1970s and taught medicine the permanent lesson that 'care' without personhood is harm, and that family-based, community-supported services, however imperfect, are better - the closure of the institutions was paediatric and disability history's greatest reform and its least completed one. Second the taxonomy wars: 'minimal brain damage' and 'brain-injured child' of the 1940s-60s giving way to Kannner's 1943 and Asperger's 1944 papers on early-onset autism (and Asperger's own complicating biography under National Socialism, debated ever since), the Rutter criteria of the 1970s making autism an observable behavioural syndrome rather than a psychosis, the DSM-III marriage of description and research criteria, the 'recovery-rate' epidemic of the 1990s that was actually broadened recognition, and the 2013 DSM-5 collapse into one autism spectrum - the nosology absorbing the Asperger's community and the community's argument about being 'diagnosed out of existence' continuing to matter to how services speak to people today. Third the classrooms: the disability-rights and 'nothing about us without us' movements of the 1970s-90s, the school-refusal and SEND legislation from Warnock 1978 through the Children and Families Act 2014, the neurodiversity movement online (the autistic self-advocates who changed the language - identity-first 'autistic person' becoming standard in much of the field because the community chose it), and the ADHD fight from 'parental deficiency' behaviourism through Douglas and Bradley's stimulant trials of the 1930s-50s to the genetic and imaging literature that made scepticism professionally untenable while the prescribing data exploded - with the honest sub-chapter that stimulant supply and pressurised diagnostics have repeatedly outpaced services' ability to assess properly, producing exactly the caricature critics cite. The clinical-technique history is smaller but real: the hip-surveillance programmes that changed who walked at eighteen, the newborn-hearing screening that shifted the entire language-trajectory of deaf children, the GMFCS (1997) giving clinicians and families a shared functional language that changed research and school planning at once, and the recent recognition - still leaking into policy - that diagnostic delay in girls and in every minoritised group is an outcome variable, and measuring it is now malpractice to leave uncounted. The through-line: every generation's confident description of 'the troublesome child' turned out to be an environment mismatch plus a nervous system, and the specialty's progress is measured by how early it stopped blaming the child and started describing the mismatch - [[paediatrics/growth-development]], [[public-health/determinants-inequalities]], [[psychiatry/psychiatric-assessment]].**
:::

::: summary
- Neurodevelopmental diagnoses are descriptive, cross-setting and functional; the phenotype still asks whether hearing, vision, genetics or a safeguarding issue are hiding inside it.
- CP, autism, ADHD, language, coordination and tic disorders each have evidence-based paths - and their comorbidities (epilepsy, pain, anxiety, eating, sleep) are usually the true burden.
- Stimulants are first-line for ADHD after proper assessment; autism intervention is about communication, family load and school reality, not eliminating stimming.
- The environment is a treatment: access arrangements, EHC plans and trained teachers change trajectories as much as clinics do.
- Adolescents need the long view: camouflage-and-delay in girls, mood and eating comorbidity, driving and exploitation risk, and transition planning that names real services.
- Measure your own equity: who gets diagnosed at four and who at fourteen is a service-quality statistic as valid as any outcome.
:::
