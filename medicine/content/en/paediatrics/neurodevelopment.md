Neurodevelopmental conditions begin in childhood and affect how the brain develops: how a child moves, communicates, learns, pays attention or relates to other people. They include autism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, tic disorders and specific learning and language disorders. Together they affect more than one child in ten.

These conditions are lifelong, but their impact is not fixed. The right understanding at home and school, the right adjustments and, sometimes, the right medicine can change a child's experience a great deal. Many autistic people and people with ADHD see their condition as part of who they are rather than as a disease, and many prefer identity-first language ("an autistic child"). This chapter uses both forms. The aim is to recognise children who need help, to assess them properly, and to support them and their families without assuming that every difference is a deficit.

## Recognition and diagnosis

::: definition The main neurodevelopmental conditions {#def-conditions}
**Autism** (autism spectrum disorder) is defined by persistent differences in social communication and social interaction, together with restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, including unusual responses to sensory input. The features are present from early childhood, though they may not be recognised until social demands increase, and they cause significant difficulty in daily life.

**Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)** is a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity and impulsivity that is inconsistent with the child's developmental level, begins before the age of 12, occurs in two or more settings (for example home and school), and impairs functioning.

**Cerebral palsy** is a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture caused by a non-progressive disturbance of the developing fetal or infant brain. It is often accompanied by difficulties with sensation, communication, learning, behaviour and epilepsy.

**Intellectual disability** (in UK services, **learning disability**) is a significant limitation of intellectual functioning, usually an IQ below about 70, together with limitations in adaptive skills such as communication, self-care and social skills, beginning in the developmental period. It is graded as mild, moderate, severe or profound.

**Specific learning disorders** affect one area while general ability is typical: **dyslexia** (reading and spelling), **dyscalculia** (number), and **developmental coordination disorder** (motor coordination, sometimes called dyspraxia). **Developmental language disorder** is a persistent difficulty in understanding or using language without a known cause such as hearing loss.
:::

These conditions are common. Autism is diagnosed in at least 1 in 100 children in the UK, and recent studies suggest a higher figure. ADHD affects about 5% of children worldwide. Cerebral palsy occurs in about 2 per 1,000 live births. Developmental language disorder affects about 7% of children at school entry.

::: theorem Traits are dimensional; a diagnosis requires impairment {#thm-impairment}
The features of autism and ADHD are distributed continuously in the population, and many children show some of them. A diagnosis is made only when the features are pervasive, persistent and cause significant impairment in daily life.
:::

::: proof
Attention, activity, social reciprocity and flexibility of thinking vary between all children, and most of the genetic contribution to autism and ADHD comes from many common genetic variants, each with a small effect, which is the pattern seen for continuously distributed traits such as height. There is therefore no natural break between "having" and "not having" the condition. Diagnostic manuals (DSM-5-TR and ICD-11) draw the line using impairment: the features must cause real difficulty in social, educational or other functioning, across settings and over time. This is why the history from more than one setting matters, why a single clinic observation is not enough, and why the same traits may be a difficulty in one environment and not in another. It also explains why the threshold can be met at one age and not another, for example when the social demands of secondary school expose difficulties that were manageable in primary school.
:::

The conditions overlap. Between a third and a half of autistic children also have features of ADHD, many children with cerebral palsy have intellectual disability or epilepsy, and children with one learning disorder often have another. A child referred for one condition should be assessed for the others.

### The role of IQ

Intellectual ability is measured with standardised tests that are scaled to have a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 in the population. The figure shows that about 2% of people score below 70. An IQ score is only part of the assessment: adaptive functioning in daily life matters as much, and scores can be unreliable in children with communication, sensory or motor difficulties.

### How children are referred and assessed

Concerns may come from parents, nursery staff, teachers, health visitors or the child. NICE guidance sets out how assessment should be done: CG128 for autism in under 19s, NG87 for ADHD and NG62 for cerebral palsy in under 25s.

**Autism assessment** is done by a multidisciplinary team, often a paediatrician, a speech and language therapist and a psychologist. It includes a detailed developmental history from the parents, information from school or nursery, direct observation (often with a structured tool such as the ADOS-2), and a physical examination looking for features of genetic syndromes and for skin signs of neurocutaneous disorders. NICE recommends that assessment starts within 3 months of referral, though waits are often much longer.

**ADHD** is diagnosed by a specialist paediatrician or psychiatrist. Information is gathered from parents and teachers, often with rating scales, and other explanations are considered: hearing or vision problems, learning difficulties, sleep problems, anxiety, low mood, trauma and the effects of the child's environment.

**Cerebral palsy** is suggested by delayed motor milestones, abnormal tone, early hand preference, asymmetry, persistent primitive reflexes or feeding difficulties. Risk factors include preterm birth, low birth weight, neonatal encephalopathy, infection and stroke. MRI of the brain helps to identify the cause and timing of injury. NICE NG62 advises that babies at high risk should be in an enhanced developmental follow-up programme until at least 2 years.

Cerebral palsy is classified by type and by function. The main types are **spastic** (about 80–90%, described as unilateral or bilateral), **dyskinetic** and **ataxic**. The **Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS)** describes mobility in five levels:

| Level | Mobility |
|---|---|
| I | Walks without limitations |
| II | Walks with limitations, for example on uneven ground or long distances |
| III | Walks using a hand-held mobility device |
| IV | Self-mobility is limited; may use powered mobility |
| V | Transported in a manual wheelchair |

::: example The seven-year-old who "isn't trying" {#ex-adhd}
Jack is 7. His teacher says he does not finish his work, calls out in class, gets out of his seat and loses his belongings. At home he cannot sit through a meal, interrupts constantly and rushes into the road without looking. His parents have been told he is "lazy" and "badly behaved". The behaviour has been present since he started school and is causing him to be excluded from playtime and to lose friends.
::: solution
**Step 1: is the pattern consistent with ADHD?** Inattention (unfinished work, losing things) and hyperactivity and impulsivity (calling out, leaving his seat, interrupting, not waiting), present at home and school, persistent for over two years and causing impairment (exclusion, loss of friendships, danger on the road). This fits the definition.

**Step 2: what else could explain it?** Hearing or vision problems, a specific learning disorder (a child who cannot read will not finish written work), developmental language disorder, sleep problems such as obstructive sleep apnoea, anxiety, adverse experiences at home, and autism, which often co-occurs.

**Step 3: assessment.** Referral to a specialist. Rating scales from parents and school, a developmental and family history, a physical examination including growth, heart rate, blood pressure and cardiovascular history, and a hearing test if not done recently.

**Step 4: management if ADHD is confirmed** (see the next section): information and support for the family and school, environmental adjustments in class, and, because his difficulties are persistent and significant, a discussion of medicine.

**Lesson.** Words such as "lazy" and "naughty" are a description, not an explanation. Asking why a child behaves as he does is the start of the assessment.
:::
:::

## Management and the system around the child

::: definition Support, adjustment and treatment {#def-manage}
**Reasonable adjustments** are changes to the environment or the way services are delivered so that a disabled person is not at a disadvantage. In the UK they are a legal duty under the Equality Act 2010.

**SEN support** is extra help within a school for a child with special educational needs, planned with the school's special educational needs coordinator (SENCO).

An **education, health and care (EHC) plan** is a legal document in England, for children and young people aged 0–25, that sets out their needs and the support that must be provided, after a needs assessment by the local authority. It follows the SEND Code of Practice (2015).
:::

**Autism.** There is no medicine for the core features of autism. Support aims to help communication and social understanding, to reduce distress, and to make environments more predictable and less overwhelming. NICE recommends interventions that help parents and teachers adapt their communication and support play-based social learning. Co-occurring difficulties are treated in their own right: sleep problems (with behavioural approaches and, if needed, melatonin), anxiety, epilepsy, gastrointestinal problems and feeding difficulties. Antipsychotics are sometimes used for severe irritability or aggression, under specialist supervision, but not as a first step.

**ADHD.** NICE NG87 recommends information and support for families, and adjustments at school, for all children. For children under 5, a parent training programme is the first step, and medicine is used only with specialist advice. For children aged 5 and over with persistent, significant impairment despite these measures, medicine can be offered:

- **Methylphenidate** is first line. It blocks the reuptake of dopamine and noradrenaline.
- **Lisdexamfetamine** is the second-line stimulant.
- Non-stimulants, such as **atomoxetine** and **guanfacine**, are alternatives when stimulants are not tolerated or not effective.

Stimulants commonly reduce appetite and can affect sleep, growth, heart rate and blood pressure. Before treatment, height, weight, heart rate and blood pressure are recorded and a cardiovascular history is taken. During treatment, they are monitored regularly and plotted on centile charts. Doses come from the BNF for Children and are titrated by the specialist. Stimulants are controlled drugs, and the young person's views count in decisions about treatment.

**Cerebral palsy.** Care is multidisciplinary: physiotherapy for movement and posture, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy for communication and swallowing, orthotics and equipment. Spasticity may be treated with oral medicines such as baclofen, botulinum toxin injections into specific muscles, and in some children surgery, including selective dorsal rhizotomy. Children with cerebral palsy need **hip surveillance** with regular X-rays, because hip displacement is common in those who do not walk, and attention to nutrition, drooling, constipation, pain, sleep and epilepsy.

**Intellectual disability.** The cause is sought with genetic tests (chromosomal microarray, fragile X testing and, if these are normal, exome or genome sequencing), and support focuses on education, communication, independence and the family. People with learning disabilities die younger than the general population, often from treatable causes, and the NHS Learning from Lives and Deaths programme (LeDeR) reviews these deaths to improve care.

::: warning Diagnostic overshadowing
**Diagnostic overshadowing** is the error of attributing a new symptom to a person's known condition instead of investigating it. A non-verbal autistic child who starts banging his head may have toothache, otitis media or constipation. A child with cerebral palsy who becomes distressed when moved may have a dislocated hip or a fracture. A child with learning disability who stops eating may have reflux or an oesophageal problem. Behaviour is communication. Always look for a physical cause of a change in behaviour.
:::

::: example The 14-year-old who stopped going to school {#ex-autism-girl}
Ayesha is 14. She has not attended school for six weeks. At home she spends long periods in her room with her drawings and can talk in great detail about a particular series of novels. At primary school she was quiet and well behaved and was never seen as having difficulties. Since moving to a large secondary school she has had daily headaches and stomach aches, panics in crowded corridors, and says she is "exhausted from pretending to be normal". Her mother says that as a toddler she lined up toys and became very upset at changes in routine.
::: solution
**Pattern.** Social difficulties that have become apparent as social demands increased, an intense focused interest, early repetitive behaviour and distress at change, sensory overload in crowds, and the exhaustion of **masking** (consciously copying others to fit in). This pattern is typical of autism in girls, who are often diagnosed later than boys because they may mask their difficulties.

**Differential and co-occurring conditions.** Anxiety disorder and depression are common in autistic young people and may be the main presenting problem. Bullying, adverse experiences, and physical causes of headaches and abdominal pain should be considered. An eating disorder should be asked about.

**Plan.** Refer for an autism assessment. Screen sensitively for low mood and self-harm. Work with the school on a gradual return with adjustments: a quiet space, permission to leave lessons early to avoid crowded corridors, a named key person, and clear routines. Treat anxiety with approaches adapted for autistic young people.

**Lesson.** Absence from school in a teenager is a symptom to understand. For some young people, the reason is unrecognised autism.
:::
:::

## Tics and other conditions

**Tics** are sudden, rapid, repeated movements or sounds. Simple tics, such as blinking or sniffing, are very common in childhood and often come and go. **Tourette syndrome** is diagnosed when there have been multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic for more than a year, beginning before the age of 18. Tics typically start at 5–7 years, peak in early adolescence and often improve in adulthood. They are often preceded by an urge that is relieved by the tic, and they can be suppressed for a while at the cost of effort. ADHD, obsessive–compulsive disorder and anxiety commonly co-occur and are often more disabling than the tics.

::: example The nine-year-old with worse tics and a worse temper {#ex-tics}
Ollie is 9. He has blinked, grimaced and shrugged his shoulders for three years, and for 18 months he has made throat-clearing and sniffing noises. His tics are worse when he is tired or excited. Recently his teachers report outbursts of anger, he cannot concentrate, and he washes his hands many times a day.
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic for more than a year, beginning in childhood, meet the criteria for **Tourette syndrome**.

**Co-occurring conditions.** Poor concentration suggests ADHD, and repeated handwashing suggests obsessive–compulsive disorder. Anger outbursts are common and often relate to these conditions, to the effort of suppressing tics, or to teasing. These co-occurring conditions often matter more to daily life than the tics.

**Management.** Explanation (psychoeducation) for Ollie, his family and school is the first and often the most helpful step. Teachers should not draw attention to tics. If tics cause distress or impairment, **behavioural therapy** (habit reversal training or comprehensive behavioural intervention for tics) is first line. Medicines such as aripiprazole or clonidine are used by specialists when behavioural therapy is not enough. The ADHD and OCD symptoms need their own assessment and treatment.
:::
:::

::: example The three-year-old born preterm who is "just clumsy" {#ex-premzy}
Leo was born at 29 weeks. He is now 3. He walked at 24 months (21 months corrected). He walks on his toes, trips often and cannot jump. His hands are fine. Examination shows increased tone and brisk reflexes in both legs, with tight calf muscles, and extensor plantar responses. His speech and understanding are normal for his age.
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Increased tone and brisk reflexes affecting both legs much more than the arms, in a child born very preterm, suggest **bilateral spastic cerebral palsy** (spastic diplegia). It is commonly caused by periventricular white matter injury (periventricular leukomalacia), because the fibres to the legs pass closest to the ventricles.

**Classification.** He walks without aids but with limitations, so he is at **GMFCS level I or II**.

**Assessment.** MRI of the brain to confirm the cause. Check vision and hearing, which are often affected in preterm children. Monitor hips and look for other problems: learning difficulties, epilepsy, and later ADHD, which are more common after preterm birth.

**Management.** Physiotherapy to maintain muscle length and improve strength and function, ankle–foot orthoses, and consideration of botulinum toxin injections to the calves. Plan support for nursery and school.

**Lesson.** "Just clumsy" can hide a diagnosis. Toe walking with brisk reflexes is not idiopathic toe walking.
:::
:::

## Living with the diagnosis

::: definition Long-term needs and transition {#def-long}
**Transition** is the planned, purposeful move of a young person from children's to adult services. NICE guideline NG43 recommends that planning begins by age 13–14, that it is led by a named worker, and that the young person is involved in decisions.

**Co-occurring conditions** are conditions present alongside the main diagnosis, such as epilepsy, anxiety, sleep disorders and gastrointestinal problems, which are more common in neurodevelopmental conditions and often need their own treatment.
:::

Families of disabled children face practical and emotional demands: appointments, equipment, sleep loss, battles for school support and money worries. They are entitled to a carer's assessment, and short breaks and parent support groups can help. Siblings also need attention.

Adolescence brings new challenges: puberty, sexuality, safety online and in relationships, the risk of exploitation, and decisions about independence and capacity. At 16, the Mental Capacity Act 2005 applies in England and Wales, and parents do not automatically make decisions for a young person who has capacity. Adult services are often less well developed, particularly for ADHD and autism, so transition planning matters.

::: quiz
A 4-year-old has been diagnosed with ADHD. His parents ask about medicine. According to NICE NG87, what is the first-line treatment at this age?
- [ ] Methylphenidate
- [ ] Atomoxetine
- [x] A parent training programme
- [ ] Melatonin
::: solution
For children under 5 with ADHD, NICE recommends an ADHD-focused group parent training programme first. Medicine is not offered to children under 5 without a second specialist opinion. Methylphenidate is first-line medicine for children aged 5 and over when environmental and educational measures have not been enough.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1861 the English orthopaedic surgeon William Little described children with stiffness of the legs after difficult or premature births, and the condition was long called Little's disease; Sigmund Freud later noted that many cases could not be explained by birth injury alone. In 1943 Leo Kanner in Baltimore described 11 children with "autistic disturbances of affective contact", and in 1944 Hans Asperger in Vienna described a group of boys with social difficulties and intense interests. In 1979 Lorna Wing and Judith Gould in London described the "triad of impairments" and the idea of an autistic spectrum. In 1902 the paediatrician George Still described children with problems of "moral control" and attention in lectures to the Royal College of Physicians, an early description of what is now ADHD. Georges Gilles de la Tourette described the tic disorder that bears his name in 1885.
:::

::: summary

- Autism and ADHD traits are dimensional; diagnosis requires persistent features across settings with significant impairment.
- Autism assessment is multidisciplinary (NICE CG128); girls are often diagnosed late because they mask.

## Exercises

::: exercise Name the condition {level=1}
For each description, name the most likely condition: (a) a 6-year-old with persistent difficulty learning to read despite good teaching and normal intelligence; (b) a 5-year-old with blinking and sniffing for 3 months; (c) a child who walks with a hand-held frame because of cerebral palsy.
::: solution
(a) Dyslexia (a specific learning disorder). (b) A provisional or transient tic disorder; Tourette syndrome needs motor and vocal tics for more than a year. (c) Cerebral palsy at GMFCS level III.
:::
:::

::: exercise IQ below 70 {level=1 check="round(1000*0.02275)/10"}
IQ scores have a mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15. Using the normal distribution, what percentage of the population scores below 70? Give one decimal place.
::: hint
70 is two standard deviations below the mean.
:::
::: solution
$z = (70 - 100)/15 = -2$. The proportion below $z = -2$ is 0.02275, that is about **2.3%**. Because adaptive functioning must also be limited, the prevalence of intellectual disability is lower than this, about 1–2%.
:::
:::

::: exercise Before starting a stimulant {level=1}
List the measurements and history needed before a child starts methylphenidate.
::: solution
Height and weight plotted on a centile chart; heart rate and blood pressure plotted on a centile chart; a cardiovascular history (fainting, palpitations, chest pain on exercise, breathlessness) and a family history of sudden cardiac death or heart disease, with an ECG and cardiology advice if any are present; a history of tics, seizures, sleep problems, appetite and substance use; and a discussion of the young person's views.
:::
:::

::: exercise Weight on treatment {level=2 check="round(1000*(32.0-30.4)/32.0)/10"}
A 9-year-old on methylphenidate weighed 32.0 kg before treatment and 30.4 kg six months later. What percentage of body weight has he lost, to one decimal place? What should be done?
::: solution
$(32.0 - 30.4)/32.0 = 0.05$, that is **5.0%**. Weight loss in a growing child is significant, especially if he crosses centiles. Ask about appetite and meals; give the medicine with or after breakfast; offer a large meal in the evening when the effect wears off; and consider a dose reduction, planned breaks, or a change of medicine if growth continues to be affected. Height velocity should also be monitored.
:::
:::

::: exercise A head-banging child {level=2}
A 6-year-old autistic boy who does not speak has started hitting the side of his head and crying at night over the past week. His parents ask whether his autism is getting worse. How would you approach this?
::: solution
Autism is not progressive, and a new behaviour is a change that needs an explanation. Avoid diagnostic overshadowing and look for a physical cause: otitis media, dental pain, headache, constipation, reflux, injury, or a seizure. Examine his ears, mouth and abdomen, and ask about bowel habit, sleep and any change at home or school. Use the parents' knowledge of how he shows pain. Changes in routine, such as a new teacher or class, can cause distress too, but should not be assumed until physical causes have been considered.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which pupils need what? {level=2}
A primary school asks for advice. Explain the difference between SEN support and an EHC plan, and give an example of a child who might need each.
::: solution
**SEN support** is provided within the school's own resources, coordinated by the SENCO, using a cycle of assess, plan, do and review. Example: a child with dyslexia who needs small-group reading support and extra time. An **EHC plan** follows a statutory assessment by the local authority when a child's needs cannot be met by SEN support alone. It is legally binding and covers education, health and social care. Example: a child with cerebral palsy at GMFCS level IV with communication difficulties, who needs a specialist school place, therapy and equipment.
:::
:::

::: exercise Planning transition {level=3}
A 15-year-old with spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy (GMFCS V), epilepsy and a gastrostomy is cared for by a paediatrician, a neurologist, a gastroenterologist, an orthopaedic surgeon and community services. Outline a transition plan to adult services.
::: solution
**Timing and leadership.** Planning should already have started, by 13–14 years, led by a named worker, following NICE NG43.

**The young person.** Involve him as much as possible using his communication methods. Assess his capacity for specific decisions; from 16 the Mental Capacity Act applies, and decisions he cannot make are made in his best interests, with his parents consulted.

**Services.** Identify adult services for each need: an adult neurologist for epilepsy, gastroenterology or nutrition support for the gastrostomy, orthopaedics and physiotherapy for hips, spine and contractures, respiratory care, and the GP as coordinator. Arrange joint clinics or handover meetings, and a written health passport.

**Social care and education.** An EHC plan can continue to 25. Adult social care assessment, continuing healthcare funding, equipment and housing should be arranged before services end.

**Family.** Carer's assessment and support; plans for emergencies; and discussion of future care, including advance care planning if appropriate.
:::
:::

::: exercise A waiting list problem {level=3}
A community paediatric service receives 600 referrals a year for autism assessment and can complete 350 assessments a year. Families are waiting more than 2 years. Discuss the consequences and possible responses.
::: solution
**Arithmetic.** With 600 referrals and 350 assessments a year, the waiting list grows by 250 a year, so waits will keep lengthening unless capacity or demand changes.

**Consequences.** Children miss early support, families experience stress, schools may wait for a diagnosis before providing help, mental health problems may develop, and inequalities widen because families with resources can pay privately.

**Responses.** Provide support based on need, not diagnosis: schools should meet needs through SEN support whether or not a diagnosis has been made, which is what the SEND Code of Practice expects. Triage referrals to prioritise young children and those at risk. Increase capacity with additional trained staff, skill-mix teams and efficient pathways that use information from school. Offer support to families while waiting, including parent programmes and advice on sleep and anxiety. Monitor who is waiting and whether some groups (for example girls, or families from minority ethnic backgrounds) are under-referred.
:::
