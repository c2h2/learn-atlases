About 2–3% of babies are born with a major structural anomaly, and many more have a genetic condition that becomes apparent later in childhood. Congenital and genetic conditions are now among the leading causes of death in infancy in the UK, and they account for a large share of the children seen in hospital clinics. Many of these children grow up into adults with the same conditions, so the work of paediatrics includes planning their future.

This chapter takes a practical view. It explains how to approach a child with unusual features, then covers three groups of conditions that every doctor meets: congenital heart disease, cystic fibrosis and the common chromosomal syndromes. It ends with inherited metabolic disease, newborn screening and genetic counselling. The underlying mechanisms of inheritance and chromosome disorders are covered in the genetics course.

## Recognising a congenital condition

::: definition Anomalies, syndromes and sequences {#def-recognise}
A **major anomaly** is a structural difference with medical, surgical or cosmetic importance, such as a cleft lip or a heart defect. A **minor anomaly** has no serious consequence by itself, such as a single palmar crease or a small skin tag in front of the ear. Three or more minor anomalies together increase the chance of an underlying major anomaly or syndrome.

**Dysmorphism** means physical features that differ from those usually seen, especially of the face, hands and feet.

A **syndrome** is a recognisable pattern of anomalies with a single underlying cause, such as Down syndrome. A **sequence** is a cascade of anomalies that follow from a single initial problem: in the **Pierre Robin sequence**, a small jaw pushes the tongue back, which prevents the palate from closing. An **association** is a group of anomalies that occur together more often than by chance without a single known cause, such as VACTERL (vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheo-oesophageal, renal and limb anomalies).
:::

The assessment of a child with possible dysmorphism follows a routine. Take a **three-generation family history**, asking about miscarriages, early deaths, learning difficulties and consanguinity. Take a **pregnancy history**, including scans, infections, alcohol and medicines. Measure height, weight and head circumference and plot them. Examine systematically from head to toe, comparing features with the parents, because many features are simply familial. Photographs, taken with consent, help colleagues and are useful later.

Genetic testing has changed rapidly. In England, testing is ordered through the NHS Genomic Medicine Service according to the National Genomic Test Directory. A **chromosomal microarray**, which detects small deletions and duplications, has replaced the karyotype as the first test for most children with developmental delay or multiple anomalies. **Exome** or **whole genome sequencing** is used when microarray is normal, and rapid whole genome sequencing is available for acutely unwell babies and children in intensive care, with results in days.

## Congenital heart disease

Congenital heart disease affects about 8 in every 1,000 babies. It is the commonest major congenital anomaly. Some lesions are detected on the antenatal anomaly scan, some at the newborn examination, and some only when the baby becomes unwell.

::: definition Groups of congenital heart disease {#def-conditions}
**Left-to-right shunts** allow oxygenated blood to pass back to the lungs: ventricular septal defect (VSD), atrial septal defect (ASD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD). The baby is pink, but the lungs receive too much blood.

**Obstructive lesions** narrow the outflow from a ventricle: coarctation of the aorta, aortic stenosis and pulmonary stenosis.

**Cyanotic lesions** allow deoxygenated blood to reach the body: tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, and other complex lesions, including those with a single functional ventricle.

A **duct-dependent lesion** is one in which blood flow to the lungs or to the body depends on the ductus arteriosus. These babies become critically ill when the ductus closes in the first days of life.
:::

::: theorem A left-to-right shunt grows as pulmonary resistance falls {#thm-shunt}
The flow through a ventricular septal defect or a patent ductus arteriosus depends on the difference in resistance between the pulmonary and systemic circulations. Because pulmonary vascular resistance falls over the first weeks of life, a large shunt often causes no symptoms at birth and presents with heart failure at about 4–6 weeks.
:::

::: proof
At birth, pulmonary vascular resistance is still relatively high. Over the following weeks the muscular walls of the small pulmonary arteries thin, and resistance falls towards adult values. Blood flows through a defect towards the side with lower resistance, so as pulmonary resistance falls, more blood passes from the left ventricle through the defect into the pulmonary circulation. The ratio of pulmonary to systemic blood flow, $Q_p/Q_s$, rises. When it is large (above about 2), the left heart must pump much more blood than the body needs, the lungs become congested, and the baby develops **heart failure**: breathlessness, sweating and tiring during feeds, poor weight gain and an enlarged liver. A murmur may also become louder as the flow increases. If a large shunt is not repaired, the pulmonary arteries are damaged by the high flow and pressure, and resistance eventually rises above systemic. The shunt then reverses, the child becomes cyanosed, and the condition, called **Eisenmenger syndrome**, is no longer correctable.
:::

The size of a shunt can be estimated from oxygen saturations measured at cardiac catheterisation, using the Fick principle:

$$
\frac{Q_p}{Q_s} = \frac{S_{\text{aorta}} - S_{\text{mixed venous}}}{S_{\text{pulmonary vein}} - S_{\text{pulmonary artery}}}
$$ {#eq-qpqs}

where each $S$ is an oxygen saturation. In practice, echocardiography and MRI are used much more often than catheterisation, but the formula explains what "a big shunt" means.

::: example A baby who sweats when she feeds {#ex-vsd}
Amina is 6 weeks old. She takes a long time to feed, sweats and breathes fast during feeds, and has gained only 300 g since birth. She has a loud pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge and a liver edge 3 cm below the costal margin. At cardiac catheterisation, the saturations are: aorta 97%, superior vena cava (mixed venous) 67%, pulmonary veins 97%, pulmonary artery 87%. Calculate $Q_p/Q_s$ and explain her symptoms.
::: solution
**Calculation.** $Q_p/Q_s = (97 - 67)/(97 - 87) = 30/10 = 3.0$. Her lungs receive three times as much blood as her body.

**Explanation.** This is a large **VSD**. At birth pulmonary resistance was high, so the shunt was small. By 6 weeks resistance has fallen and the shunt has increased. The extra flow causes pulmonary congestion (fast breathing), and the extra work of the heart, combined with poor feeding, causes faltering growth. Sweating reflects increased sympathetic activity.

**Management.** Diuretics to reduce congestion, and high-energy feeds, by nasogastric tube if necessary, to support growth. A large VSD with heart failure usually needs surgical closure in the first months of life, before pulmonary vascular disease develops. Small muscular VSDs, by contrast, often close on their own.
:::
:::

### Cyanosis and the duct

A cyanosed newborn may have a heart or a lung problem. In heart disease the cyanosis is not corrected by oxygen. Babies with **transposition of the great arteries** become cyanosed within hours as the ductus closes, and need prostaglandin to keep the ductus open and an emergency balloon atrial septostomy to mix blood at atrial level, followed by an arterial switch operation. **Tetralogy of Fallot** (VSD, overriding aorta, pulmonary stenosis and right ventricular hypertrophy) causes cyanosis that varies with the degree of pulmonary stenosis; older infants may have "tet spells" of increased cyanosis when crying.

### Innocent murmurs

Many healthy children have an **innocent murmur**, caused by normal blood flow. It is soft, systolic, heard in a small area, varies with posture, and occurs in a child with no symptoms and otherwise normal cardiovascular examination. Murmurs that are diastolic, loud (with a thrill), pansystolic, or accompanied by symptoms, cyanosis or abnormal pulses need referral for echocardiography.

## Cystic fibrosis

**Cystic fibrosis** (CF) is the commonest life-limiting autosomal recessive condition in people of Northern European ancestry. About 1 in 25 people of white European ancestry is a carrier, and about 1 in 2,500 babies in the UK is affected. It occurs in all ethnic groups but is less common in others, and it is easier to miss in children from groups in which it is thought to be rare.

CF is caused by variants in the *CFTR* gene, which encodes a chloride channel in epithelial cells. Reduced chloride secretion makes secretions thick and dehydrated. In the lungs, mucus is cleared poorly, leading to chronic infection (with *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Haemophilus influenzae* and later *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*) and bronchiectasis. In the pancreas, ducts block, causing **pancreatic insufficiency** with fatty stools and poor growth, and later CF-related diabetes. About 10–20% of babies present with **meconium ileus**. Most men with CF are infertile because the vas deferens is absent.

CF is detected on the newborn blood spot test: a raised **immunoreactive trypsinogen** (IRT) is followed by testing for common *CFTR* variants. The diagnosis is confirmed by a **sweat test**: a sweat chloride of 60 mmol/L or more is diagnostic.

Treatment is lifelong and multidisciplinary: daily airway clearance physiotherapy, inhaled mucolytics, antibiotics, pancreatic enzyme replacement with meals, fat-soluble vitamins and high-energy diet. The biggest change in recent years has been **CFTR modulators**. These drugs correct or potentiate the faulty channel. The triple combination elexacaftor, tezacaftor and ivacaftor improves lung function and weight substantially in people with at least one *F508del* variant, and has been available on the NHS since 2020, now including young children. Median predicted survival for people with CF in the UK was already around 50 years before modulators, and is expected to rise further.

::: example A positive newborn screen {#ex-cfscreen}
Oliver's blood spot test shows a high IRT and two *CFTR* variants, including *F508del*. He is 3 weeks old, breastfeeding well, and gaining weight. His parents, who have no family history of CF, are shocked. What happens next?
::: solution
**Confirmation.** A sweat test at the CF centre. A sweat chloride of 60 mmol/L or more confirms CF.

**Explanation to the parents.** CF is autosomal recessive, so each parent must be a carrier. Carriers have no symptoms, which is why there was no family history. Each future pregnancy has a 1 in 4 chance of CF, a 1 in 2 chance of a carrier and a 1 in 4 chance of a child who is neither.

**Early care.** Referral to a specialist CF team. Faecal elastase to test pancreatic function, with enzyme replacement if it is low. Regular respiratory samples and physiotherapy. Discussion of CFTR modulator treatment, which depends on his variants and age.

**Wider family.** Each parent's siblings have a 1 in 2 chance of being carriers, and cascade testing can be offered. Genetic counselling is offered for future pregnancies, including the options for prenatal diagnosis.

**Lesson.** Newborn screening allows treatment before damage is done. A screen-positive baby who looks well still needs prompt confirmation.
:::
:::

## Chromosomal and microdeletion syndromes

### Down syndrome

**Down syndrome** (trisomy 21) affects about 1 in 1,000 live births in the UK. Babies have hypotonia, a flat facial profile, upslanting palpebral fissures, epicanthic folds, small ears, a single palmar crease and a sandal gap between the first and second toes. Learning disability is usual, but its degree varies widely, and early support makes a large difference.

The care of a child with Down syndrome is mostly **surveillance** for associated conditions, following the Down Syndrome Medical Interest Group guidance:

- **Heart**: about half have congenital heart disease, most often an atrioventricular septal defect. Every baby has an echocardiogram in the first weeks, even if the examination is normal.
- **Gut**: duodenal atresia and Hirschsprung disease are more common.
- **Thyroid**: hypothyroidism is common; thyroid function is checked regularly throughout life.
- **Hearing and vision**: glue ear, cataract and refractive errors are common, so hearing and eyes are checked regularly.
- **Blood**: transient abnormal myelopoiesis in the newborn and a higher risk of leukaemia.
- **Sleep**: obstructive sleep apnoea is common and affects behaviour and learning.
- **Growth**: plotted on Down syndrome-specific growth charts.
- Coeliac disease and atlanto-axial instability are also more common.

Adults with Down syndrome now commonly live into their sixties. Alzheimer disease develops earlier than in the general population, partly because the *APP* gene is on chromosome 21.

### 22q11.2 deletion syndrome

::: example A newborn with a heart defect and low calcium {#ex-diGeorge}
Kofi was born at term. On day 2 he has a seizure. His calcium is low and his parathyroid hormone is inappropriately low. An echocardiogram shows an interrupted aortic arch. He has a slightly long face and small, low-set ears.
::: solution
**Pattern.** A conotruncal heart defect (interrupted aortic arch, truncus arteriosus or tetralogy of Fallot) with hypocalcaemia from hypoparathyroidism is a classic presentation of **22q11.2 deletion syndrome** (DiGeorge syndrome).

**Test.** Chromosomal microarray, which detects the deletion. A standard karyotype usually does not.

**Other features to look for.** Thymic hypoplasia with low T cells and a risk of infection; a cleft or poorly functioning palate causing feeding problems and nasal speech; kidney anomalies; learning difficulties; and, later, a higher risk of anxiety, ADHD, autism and psychosis.

**Immediate precautions.** Check T-cell numbers before giving live vaccines. Give only **irradiated** blood products until immune function is known, because non-irradiated products can cause transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease in a child without functioning T cells. Treat the hypocalcaemia.

**Family.** Most deletions are new, but about 1 in 10 is inherited from a parent who may have mild features. If a parent carries the deletion, each child has a 50% chance of inheriting it, so parental testing is offered.
:::
:::

### Turner, Edwards and Patau syndromes

**Turner syndrome** (45,X and variants) affects about 1 in 2,500 girls. It causes short stature and ovarian insufficiency, and is associated with coarctation, bicuspid aortic valve, horseshoe kidney, hypothyroidism and hearing loss. It may present with lymphoedema of the hands and feet at birth, or with short stature or absent puberty later (see [[paediatrics/growth-development]]). Growth hormone and oestrogen replacement are the main treatments.

**Edwards syndrome** (trisomy 18) and **Patau syndrome** (trisomy 13) are much rarer and cause multiple severe anomalies. Most affected babies die in the first weeks or months, although some live longer. Care is planned with the parents and often focuses on comfort.

## Inborn errors of metabolism and genetic counselling

**Inborn errors of metabolism** are inherited defects of enzymes or transporters, mostly autosomal recessive. Individually rare, together they are an important cause of illness in newborns. The classic presentation is a baby who is well at birth (the placenta cleared the toxic metabolites in pregnancy) and then becomes unwell after starting feeds: poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy, seizures and coma.

The key bedside tests in an encephalopathic baby are blood glucose, blood gas, lactate, ketones and **ammonia**. Hyperammonaemia suggests a urea cycle disorder or organic acidaemia, and a very high level is an emergency that may need dialysis. Emergency treatment principles are to stop protein intake, give intravenous glucose to stop catabolism, and seek urgent advice from a metabolic team.

**Phenylketonuria** (PKU) is the model of successful screening. Without treatment it causes severe learning disability; with a low-phenylalanine diet started in the first weeks, children develop normally. **Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency** (MCADD) causes hypoglycaemia without ketones during fasting or illness, and can cause sudden death; once diagnosed, it is managed by avoiding fasting and giving an emergency glucose regimen during illness.

::: definition Genetic counselling {#def-counsel}
**Genetic counselling** is a communication process that helps people understand a genetic condition, how it is inherited, the chance that it will occur or recur in the family, and the options available. It is non-directive: the counsellor supports the family to make their own decisions.

A **recurrence risk** is the chance that a future child of the same parents will be affected. It depends on the mode of inheritance: 25% for each pregnancy when both parents are carriers of an autosomal recessive condition, 50% when a parent carries an autosomal dominant condition, and low (often around 1%) for most new chromosomal abnormalities and new dominant variants.

**Cascade testing** offers testing to relatives of a person with a confirmed genetic diagnosis.
:::

::: example A carrier risk calculation {#ex-carrier}
Hannah's brother has cystic fibrosis. Hannah does not. Her partner, Sam, has no family history and is of white British ancestry. What is the chance that their first child will have CF before any testing?
::: solution
**Hannah's carrier risk.** Her parents are both carriers. Of their children, $1/4$ are affected, $1/2$ carriers and $1/4$ non-carriers. Hannah is not affected, so she is one of the three remaining possibilities, of which two are carriers. Her carrier risk is $2/3$.

**Sam's carrier risk.** The population carrier frequency for his ancestry, about $1/25$.

**Child's risk.** If both are carriers, each child has a $1/4$ chance of CF. So the risk is
$$
\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{1}{25} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{2}{300} = \frac{1}{150}.
$$

**Next step.** Carrier testing. Testing Hannah for her family's variants is highly accurate. If Sam tests negative on a panel of common variants, his residual risk falls well below 1 in 25 but not to zero, because panels do not detect every variant.
:::
:::

::: warning A normal karyotype does not exclude a genetic diagnosis
A karyotype detects only large chromosome changes, several million base pairs in size. Microdeletions such as 22q11.2, single-gene disorders and many imprinting disorders are invisible to it. Telling a family that "the genetic tests were normal" after a karyotype alone is misleading. The right test depends on the question, and genetic results should be interpreted with a clinical genetics team.
:::

::: quiz
A baby boy is born with a cleft palate and a very small jaw. On his first day he has episodes of airway obstruction when lying on his back. What is the best description of this pattern?
- [ ] A syndrome, because there is more than one anomaly
- [x] A sequence, because one primary anomaly leads to the others
- [ ] An association, because the anomalies occur together by chance
- [ ] Minor anomalies with no significance
::: solution
This is the **Pierre Robin sequence**. A small jaw (micrognathia) displaces the tongue backwards, which prevents the palate from closing and obstructs the airway. Babies are nursed on their front or side and may need airway support. It can occur alone or as part of a syndrome, such as Stickler syndrome or 22q11.2 deletion, so a genetic assessment is still needed.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1934 Asbjørn Følling, a Norwegian physician, found phenylpyruvic acid in the urine of two children with learning disability, describing phenylketonuria. In 1953 Horst Bickel and colleagues in Birmingham showed that a low-phenylalanine diet improved affected children, and in 1963 Robert Guthrie published a simple bacterial test for phenylalanine on dried blood spots, which made screening of every newborn possible. In 1938 Dorothy Andersen described cystic fibrosis of the pancreas, and in 1989 Lap-Chee Tsui, John Riordan, Francis Collins and colleagues identified the *CFTR* gene. In 1944 Alfred Blalock and Helen Taussig, with the technician Vivien Thomas, performed the first operation to relieve cyanosis in tetralogy of Fallot, the start of paediatric cardiac surgery.
:::

::: summary
- Assess a child with possible dysmorphism with a three-generation family history, pregnancy history, measurements and a systematic examination; compare with the parents.
- Microarray is the first genetic test for most children with delay or multiple anomalies; exome and genome sequencing follow.
- Congenital heart disease affects about 8 per 1,000 babies. Large left-to-right shunts present with heart failure at 4–6 weeks as pulmonary resistance falls; duct-dependent lesions present with collapse or cyanosis in the first days.

- Recurrence risk depends on mechanism, and counselling is non-directive.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Recurrence risks {level=1}
Give the recurrence risk for the next pregnancy in each case: (a) both parents are carriers of an autosomal recessive condition; (b) the father has an autosomal dominant condition; (c) the child has a new 22q11.2 deletion and both parents test negative.
::: solution
(a) 1 in 4 (25%). (b) 1 in 2 (50%). (c) Low, around 1%, allowing for the small possibility that a parent carries the deletion in some of their egg or sperm cells (germline mosaicism).
:::

::: exercise Shunt size {level=1 check="(98-70)/(98-91)"}
At catheterisation a child with a patent ductus arteriosus has saturations: aorta 98%, mixed venous 70%, pulmonary veins 98%, pulmonary artery 91%. Calculate $Q_p/Q_s$.
::: solution
$Q_p/Q_s = (98 - 70)/(98 - 91) = 28/7 = $ **4**. This is a large shunt.
:::

::: exercise Down syndrome surveillance {level=1}
A baby with Down syndrome has a normal heart examination at the NIPE. Does he need an echocardiogram? Name four other conditions that are screened for during childhood.
::: solution
Yes. About half of babies with Down syndrome have congenital heart disease, and an atrioventricular septal defect may not cause a murmur in the first days, so every baby has an echocardiogram. Others: hypothyroidism, hearing loss, visual problems (cataract, refractive error), obstructive sleep apnoea, coeliac disease and growth on Down syndrome-specific charts.
:::
:::

::: exercise A carrier risk {level=2 check="2/3*1/25*1/4"}
A woman whose sister has CF (and who is unaffected herself) has a partner with no family history and a population carrier frequency of 1 in 25. What is the probability that their first child has CF? Give the answer as a fraction or decimal.
::: solution
$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{1}{25} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{150} \approx 0.0067$.
:::
:::

::: exercise The cyanosed baby {level=2}
A term baby becomes cyanosed at 6 hours of age with oxygen saturations of 70%, which do not rise with oxygen. He is breathing comfortably and has no murmur. What is the most likely diagnosis and the immediate treatment?
::: solution
Severe cyanosis that does not respond to oxygen, without respiratory distress, suggests cyanotic congenital heart disease, most likely **transposition of the great arteries**, in which the systemic and pulmonary circulations run in parallel and mixing depends on the ductus and the foramen ovale. A murmur is often absent. Start an infusion of **prostaglandin E** to keep the ductus open, support breathing if apnoea occurs, and arrange urgent echocardiography and transfer to a cardiac centre for balloon atrial septostomy and later arterial switch.
:::
:::

::: exercise The baby who became drowsy after feeds {level=2}
A 3-day-old girl, well at birth, has become sleepy and is feeding poorly. She is breathing fast. Blood glucose is 4.5 mmol/L, pH 7.48, and ammonia 450 µmol/L. Interpret the findings and outline immediate management.
::: hint
A respiratory alkalosis with very high ammonia points to a particular group of disorders.
:::
::: solution
Very high ammonia with a respiratory alkalosis (ammonia stimulates breathing) and no acidosis suggests a **urea cycle disorder**. Organic acidaemias usually cause metabolic acidosis. Sepsis must be considered in parallel. Management: stop all protein, give intravenous glucose (often with insulin) to stop catabolism, start antibiotics after cultures, contact the metabolic team urgently for ammonia-scavenging drugs (sodium benzoate, sodium phenylbutyrate), and prepare for haemofiltration or dialysis if ammonia stays high. Collect samples for specialist tests. Time matters, because high ammonia damages the brain.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why screen in two stages? {level=3 check="round(1000*0.0004*0.95/(0.0004*0.95+0.9996*0.005))/10"}
A screening test for a condition with prevalence 1 in 2,500 has a sensitivity of 95% and a specificity of 99.5%. Calculate its positive predictive value as a percentage, to one decimal place, and explain why a second-stage test is used.
::: solution
In 1,000,000 babies, 400 are affected and 999,600 are not. True positives: $400 \times 0.95 = 380$. False positives: $999{,}600 \times 0.005 = 4{,}998$. $\text{PPV} = 380/(380 + 4{,}998) = 0.0707$, that is **7.1%**.

More than 90% of positive results would be false. Telling thousands of families that their baby may have a serious condition causes real anxiety and costs. A second-stage test applied only to first-stage positives, such as *CFTR* variant testing after a high IRT, raises the prior probability for that test to about 7%, so its positive predictive value is much higher. The trade-off is that some affected babies are missed if they do not carry the variants tested.
:::
:::

::: exercise A new diagnosis and the family {level=3}
A 7-year-old boy with mild learning difficulties and a repaired tetralogy of Fallot is found on microarray to have a 22q11.2 deletion. His mother had a cleft palate repaired as a child and has had "anxiety" since her teens. Discuss the implications of the diagnosis for the boy and his family.
::: solution
**For the boy.** The diagnosis explains his heart defect and learning difficulties, and adds surveillance: calcium and parathyroid function, immune function (T-cell subsets) before any further live vaccines and irradiated blood if transfusion is needed, hearing and palate assessment, kidney ultrasound, thyroid function, and monitoring of mental health into adolescence and adulthood because of the higher risk of anxiety, ADHD and psychosis. Extra educational support, often with an education, health and care plan, should be considered.

**For the mother.** Her cleft palate and anxiety raise the possibility that she carries the same deletion. With her consent she should be offered testing and genetic counselling. If she carries it, she may need her own checks (calcium, thyroid, heart, mental health), and each of her children has a 50% chance of inheriting it.

**For the wider family.** Cascade testing for her relatives can be discussed. The consultation must be handled sensitively: a diagnosis in a child can lead to an unexpected diagnosis in a parent, and parents may feel guilt. Non-directive counselling and support are essential.
:::
:::
