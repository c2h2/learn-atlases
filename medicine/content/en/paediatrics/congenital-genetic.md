Congenital disease is where paediatrics meets the family at their most vulnerable moment - often the moment they were promised would be joyful. **This chapter covers how to recognise and diagnose a child with anomalies, how the common surgical and structural conditions are managed, and how genetics is practised as a family medicine: recurrence risks explained honestly, results that arrive years apart, variants of uncertain significance, and the conversation about a life-limiting condition. The clinical skill is parallel processing - treating the baby while diagnosing the baby while supporting the parents - and the professional skill is staying with uncertainty without either pretending to certainty or abandoning the family to it.**

## Recognition and diagnosis

::: definition The dysmorphic newborn {#def-recognise}
**Recognition is a disciplined craft. Major and minor anomalies matter for different reasons: a major structural anomaly (a cleft, a clubfoot, a septal defect) each carries a several-fold increase in the chance of a second anomaly or a chromosomal or single-gene diagnosis, while minor anomalies - a single palmar crease, a low-set or posteriorly rotated ear, a short phalanx, a skin tag - individually mean little but cluster meaningfully; the population baseline is that around 3-5 percent of newborns have a recognisable anomaly and another 10-15 percent have a minor feature, which is why the dysmorphologist counts patterns, not features. The first-pass method is systematic: measurements (head circumference, length, weight with custom charts), the three gates of growth (microcephaly and macrocephaly each open their own differential), then skin, eyes (red reflex, fundi), ears (shape, position, pits - a preauricular pit plus ear shape anomaly plus hearing risk is a renal-imaging question), mouth and palate (a submucous cleft hides behind a bifid uvula), hands (creases, digits, thumbs - absent or triphalangeal thumbs are a haematology alert), spine and sacrum (a dimple with a tuft, a deviated natal cleft, a lipoma), genitalia (ambiguous genitalia is an endocrine emergency in the first week because of the salt-losing possibility) and the fundus, heart and hips of every syndrome-having baby. The diagnostic ladder has reordered in fifteen years: karyotype for aneuploidy suspicion, chromosomal microarray now first-tier for unexplained multiple anomalies and developmental delay with a diagnostic yield around 15-20 percent, targeted gene panels, and exome or genome sequencing with yields of 30-50 percent in selected cohorts - available in many countries through national programmes such as the Genomic Medicine Service, which means 'we will never know' is now a statement to test, not accept. Yet the phenotype still drives the test: the baby with hypoplastic nails and small patellae needs a different gene than the baby with a cleft and periumbilical skin tags, and a negative exome in a recognisable syndrome is still that syndrome. Bedside tools are unglamorous and decisive - photographs (with consent) for the tele-dysmorphology call, a three-generation pedigree drawn properly with miscarriages and stillbirths and consanguinity asked without flinching, and the older-sibling examination that answers inheritance questions in seconds. The moment of recognition is also a communication event: parents remember the first ten sentences, so 'I can see something different, I do not yet know what it means, and here is who I am calling' beats either false reassurance or premature naming - [[genetics/genetic-testing]], [[genetics/chromosomal-disorders]], [[clinical-skills/history-taking]].**
:::

::: definition Common congenital conditions and their management arcs {#def-conditions}
**Each common condition has a predictable arc that families should be told early. Congenital heart disease (see [[cardiovascular/valve-congenital-disease]]): the pulse-oximetry screening programme catches critical left-sided and duct-dependent lesions before discharge, prostaglandin E1 keeps the duct open for duct-dependent lesions while echoing and transferring, and the big lesson is that a shocked neonate with a pink body and grey periphery was 'feeding poorly' somewhere in the notes a day earlier. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia: antenatal diagnosis, delivery-planning with paediatric surgery and neonatology present, gentle ventilation with permissive carbon dioxide, delayed cord clamping where possible, avoiding bag-mask inflation of a stomach, and ECMO for the refractory - the counselling numbers (survival now 80-90 percent in isolated cases in experienced centres, worse with liver herniation and a small lung) must be honest about which numerator is being quoted. Oesophageal atresia with or without fistula: the catheter coiled in the pouch is the diagnosis, the first feed is never allowed, and the operative repair is the beginning of a story about reflux, swallow therapy and fundoplication decisions. Gastroschisis (the paraumbilical defect with no sac, free bowel, usually a normal baby) versus exomphalos (the midline sac-covered defect that demands a chromosomal and cardiac hunt): the 'silo then closure' pathway, temperature and fluid losses as the immediate physiologic enemies, and the long enteral-feeding road. Omphalocele-small can be closed; large is 'grossed down' slowly with a silo. The neural tube defects: open spina bifida operated within the first days (or fetal surgery in selected centres), the neurogenic bowel, bladder and kidney programme that determines adult life more than the cord closure does, latex allergy avoidance, shunt or endoscopic management of hydrocephalus, and the transition clinic that keeps twenty-year-olds out of renal failure. Cleft lip and palate: the eight-week lip, the nine-to-twelve-month palate, the speech therapist and orthodontist and ENT surgeon and audiology for life-in-miniature, with parental feeding teaching before the first surgical date. Clubfoot: the Ponseti casting that works in the hands of a trained plaster technician more reliably than any operating theatre. Developmental dysplasia of the hip: the examination at birth and six weeks, the ultrasound controversy, the Pavlik harness and the late presentation that no screening programme fully prevents. Hypospadias and ambiguous genitalia: no circumcision before repair, the 'top-to-bottom' inspection that finds the proximal meatus, and the multidisciplinary gender question handled by a team and a family, not by a weekend decision. Each condition now has a named nurse coordinator, and one of the highest-value actions a junior doctor takes is learning which one to phone - [[paediatrics/newborn]], [[surgery/gastrointestinal-surgery]], [[renal/urinary-tract]].**
:::

## Families, risk and the long tail

::: definition Genetic counselling, uncertainty and ethics {#def-counsel}
**A diagnosis is a family event, and counselling is its medicine. Recurrence risk must be derived, not guessed: an autosomal recessive condition carries the one-in-four figure the parents can usually hear only after the shock has been given time; de novo dominant conditions carry low recurrence but nonzero risk from parental mosaicism and germ-layer effects, which is why 'it just happened' needs the caveat that makes siblings safe; balanced translocations found in a parent after a miscarrying-or-anomalous-child pregnancy change everything about the next pregnancy and should be looked for. Counselling is done in the family's language, often across two sessions, often with letters summarised so the parents can re-read the numbers at 2am without mishearing them again, and it must survive the modern complications: the variant of uncertain significance that cannot be repeated as 'probably not the cause' without becoming, to the family, 'they do not know', the incidental finding whose disclosure rules differ by laboratory and jurisdiction and that must be pre-negotiated before sequencing, the result that reframes a parent's own health (the 'child with a laminopathy and the father who now needs a cardiologist' pattern), and the secondary finding in a sibling conceived to be a donor. Prenatal diagnosis brings its own practice: a findings conversation after an increased nuchal translucency or a soft marker should be a genetics discussion, not a leaflet; the term 'incompatible with life' is used loosely and has destroyed wanted pregnancies; and the decision to continue or terminate after an anomaly scan requires the same non-directive counselling skill and same access to paediatric palliative detail as any other serious diagnosis - reproductive autonomy means the parents decide, and the clinician's job is accurate information, prognostic honesty about the specific family's variant where possible, and no abandonment either way. Newborn screening (see [[public-health/screening]]) sits under this chapter's logic: conditions are screened where early treatment changes outcome, consent is opt-out with real information, and the false-positive pathway is a family crisis that services manage badly and can manage well by phone call design alone. The ethical spine of the whole chapter is that reproductive decisions belong to parents and prognosis decisions belong with families in real time - 'quality of life' is asked of the family's child, not of a textbook silhouette; withholding or withdrawing treatment is decided condition by condition, usually better with a nurse who has bathed the baby in the room; and palliative paediatrics is not giving up but a specialty that makes symptom burden small and time at home large for the 3-5 percent of children with life-limiting conditions. Finally, the safeguarding overlap: multiple fractures, subdural collections and retinal haemorrhages are non-accidental injury until investigated - with osteogenesis imperfecta, glutaric aciduria type 1 and bleeding disorders genuinely excluded by the right people at the right time - because the child with a skeletal dysplasia and the child being harmed present in the same clinic and both errors are catastrophic. Every 'unexplained deterioration' in an infant is simultaneously a metabolic, a genetic and a protection question until answered, and the paediatric geneticist, the radiologist and the paediatric safeguarding team should be in the same conversation, usually within hours - [[genetics/genetic-testing]], [[medical-ethics-law/beginning-of-life]], [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], [[genetics/chromosomal-disorders]].**
:::

## Where this leads

Testing technology in [[genetics/genetic-testing]] and [[genetics/chromosomal-disorders]], inheritance arithmetic in [[genetics/mendelian-inheritance]], the newborn's other emergencies in [[paediatrics/newborn]], the safeguarding boundary in [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], and screening policy in [[public-health/screening]].

::: history
**Congenital disease moved from fatalism to family medicine in four shifts. First, teratology's hard lesson: the phocomelia of 1958-1961, when thalidomide crossed the placenta and thousands of babies were born without limbs, converting scattered 'freak' case reports into the science of critical windows in embryonic development, drug teratogenicity regulation, and the birth of the UK national congenital malformations survey and the modern teratology information service - the proof that a chemical passed through the placenta in a defined week, and that prescription practice was a public-health variable. Second, the surgical rescue: the classical operations of the 1940s-1950s - Cleft repair and oesophageal atresia and exomphalos and later the shunt for hydrocephalus - most of which initially failed almost always in the very young and the very severe, until the pattern 'the survivable minority gets better and the sickest still die' forced the ethical question openly, with the infant-surgery founders' published survival curves used in court in the infant-treatment decisions of the early 1980s, which is the direct ancestor of every modern 'comfort care versus operation' conversation. Third, the chromosome era: 1956's correct human chromosome number, 1959's trisomy 21, 1960s Philadelphia chromosome and the sex-chromosome work, the 1970s Q- and G-banding that made 'the phenotype' a testable hypothesis, the 1990s microdissection and FISH that found the microdeletions - including the deletion on 22q that DiGeorge had described in 1968 as a syndrome of 'no thymus, no parathyroid' and which now bears a number rather than a debate - and the 2000s microarray that replaced many karyotypes. Fourth, sequencing: the 2003 Human Genome Project, the 2008-2010 exome case reports that solved previously unsolvable families one pedigree at a time, and the 2010s-2020s national programmes (the 100 000 Genomes Project, the Genomic Medicine Service test directory, the worldwide newborn exome trials for the critically ill) that changed the question from 'can we sequence her' to 'which baby, when, who consents, who interprets at midnight and who lives with the uncertain variant for twenty years'. Parallel social history matters too: the eugenics shadow over early 'genetic counselling', the deinstitutionalisation movement and the parents' organisations that replaced 'institutionalise and advise' with 'include and support', the near-elimination of open neural tube defects by folic-acid fortification arguments still unfinished in many countries, and the disability-rights critique that forced the language of 'affected' and 'normal' toward 'child with a condition' and forced prognostic honesty instead of either inspiration or despair. The specialty's lesson is that diagnosis is power only when paired with support - a name without a plan is just a prognosis with better spelling - [[genetics/genomic-medicine]], [[public-health/screening]], [[medical-ethics-law/beginning-of-life]].**
:::

::: summary
- Major anomalies cluster; minor anomalies mean little alone - count patterns, measure growth, examine the eyes, ears, palate, spine, hips and heart systematically.
- The diagnostic ladder now runs microarray then exome or genome, but phenotype drives the test and a negative result never deletes a recognisable syndrome.
- Each congenital condition has a predictable arc and a named coordinator - learn the arc and the phone number before the shift.
- Recurrence risk is derived from inheritance, parental studies and mosaicism caveats - never guessed, always written down and re-read at home.
- Prenatal and newborn screening multiply decisions: accurate prognosis for this family's variant, non-directive counselling, no abandonment either way.
- Uncertain variants are a lifelong information-management problem - fix the disclosure policy before the sequencer runs.
:::
