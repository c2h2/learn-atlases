Children deteriorate fast and recover faster, and the illness is often more obvious to the parents than to the first clinician. **Acute childhood illness is pattern-recognition against a short list of killers: sepsis and meningococcal disease, bronchiolitis and asthma, dehydration from gastroenteritis, the fits and fevers, the non-blanching rashes, the young-febrile-infant rules and the child-protection overlay that belongs on every unexplained deterioration. Its practical core is the paediatric early-warning approach - look at the child before you look at the numbers, treat hypoxia and glucose and perfusion first, escalate by trajectory not threshold, and know exactly when a 'viral illness' becomes a referral.**

::: summary
- Look at the child before the numbers: work of breathing, perfusion, alertness and parental 'different' decide escalation before any laboratory value.
- Under-3-month fever follows strict rules; hypothermia is sepsis, and 'settled and sleepy' is deterioration language.
- A blanching rash or a low CRP never excludes early meningococcal disease; trajectory plus re-examination plus a written safety net is the standard.
- Simple febrile convulsions need no scans and no anticonvulsants; complex features or meningism change the pathway entirely.
- Repeated 'viral wheeze' demands one serious differential review; the child not responding to salbutamol is not having asthma.
- Recurrent attendances are themselves a symptom - of disease, of deprivation or of danger - and every discharge needs legible, translated, booked follow-up.

## Taking it further

Two closing habits distinguish the excellent acute-care paediatrician from the adequate one. The first is deliberate uncertainty management: when a febrious infant's trajectory is unclear, name the uncertainty out loud, state what you would do if it resolved each way, set the review time, and hand over the doubt as information rather than smoothing it away - the deterioration literature's consistent finding is that delays follow decisions nobody was allowed to doubt, not decisions that were wrong. The second is developmental situational awareness: every admission is an interruption of growth, so the acute paediatrician asks about school, feeds, mobility and the sibling at home while the intravenous line is still in, because the discharge that restores the child to a stalled environment simply schedules the next admission. Both habits cost nothing except conversation, and both are invisible on every laboratory result.
