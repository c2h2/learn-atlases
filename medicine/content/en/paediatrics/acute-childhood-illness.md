Most acutely ill children have a self-limiting viral infection. A few have a serious bacterial infection, a surgical problem or a metabolic emergency, and these children can look surprisingly well early on. The skill of acute paediatrics is to tell the two groups apart quickly and safely, to treat the sick child before the diagnosis is certain, and to send the well child home with clear advice about what to watch for.

This chapter teaches a structured assessment of the sick child, the approach to fever, the common respiratory and gastrointestinal illnesses, and the calculation of fluids. It follows UK practice: NICE guidance on fever in under 5s (NG143), suspected sepsis in under 16s (NG254), bronchiolitis (NG9), diarrhoea and vomiting (CG84) and intravenous fluids (NG29). Doses mentioned are illustrative; real prescribing uses the BNF for Children and local guidance.

## Recognising the sick child

Children are not small adults, and the differences matter in an emergency. Their airways are narrow, so a little swelling causes a large rise in resistance. They have a high metabolic rate and small glycogen stores, so they become hypoglycaemic quickly. Their normal heart and breathing rates are faster, and the normal range changes with age.

| Age | Breathing rate (per min) | Heart rate (per min) |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 year | 30–40 | 110–160 |
| 1–2 years | 25–35 | 100–150 |
| 2–5 years | 25–30 | 95–140 |
| 5–12 years | 20–25 | 80–120 |
| Over 12 years | 15–20 | 60–100 |

These ranges follow those taught on the Advanced Paediatric Life Support course. A sleeping child has lower rates, and a crying, frightened or febrile child has higher rates, so observations are repeated when the child is calm.

::: definition Structured assessment and early warning scores {#def-assess}
The **ABCDE assessment** examines and treats problems in order of threat to life: **A**irway, **B**reathing, **C**irculation, **D**isability (conscious level, pupils, posture and blood glucose) and **E**xposure (rash, temperature, injuries). A problem found at each step is treated before moving on.

A **paediatric early warning score (PEWS)** turns routine observations into a score that triggers review when it rises. In England, a national PEWS (the System-wide Paediatric Observations Tracking, or SPOT, programme) has been introduced, with age-specific observation charts and a way to record parents' and nurses' concern.

The **AVPU scale** assesses conscious level quickly: **A**lert, responds to **V**oice, responds to **P**ain, **U**nresponsive. A child who responds only to pain or less has a Glasgow Coma Scale score of about 8 or below and may not be able to protect the airway.
:::

::: theorem Children maintain blood pressure until late in shock {#thm-compensation}
In children with shock, tachycardia, prolonged capillary refill, cool peripheries and reduced urine output appear early, while blood pressure stays normal until late. Hypotension in a child is a sign of decompensated shock and is a pre-terminal finding.
:::

::: proof
Blood pressure is the product of cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, and cardiac output is heart rate times stroke volume. When circulating volume falls, the sympathetic nervous system raises heart rate and constricts peripheral vessels. Children have healthy blood vessels and a strong sympathetic response, so vasoconstriction can hold blood pressure steady even after a large loss of volume. The cost is seen elsewhere: the skin is cool and mottled, capillary refill is slow, the kidneys make less urine, and the brain may be less perfused, making the child irritable or drowsy. Only when these mechanisms are exhausted does blood pressure fall, and by then the child is close to cardiac arrest. Children also have a limited ability to increase stroke volume, so they depend on heart rate; a falling heart rate in a sick child (bradycardia) is an ominous sign, often caused by hypoxia.
:::

### Estimating weight

Drug doses and fluid volumes depend on weight. The child should be weighed whenever possible. In an emergency, when weighing is impossible, weight can be estimated from age using the formulas taught on the APLS course:

- 0–12 months: weight (kg) $= 0.5 \times \text{age in months} + 4$
- 1–5 years: weight (kg) $= 2 \times \text{age in years} + 8$
- 6–12 years: weight (kg) $= 3 \times \text{age in years} + 7$

These are only estimates. A length-based tape or the parents' recent knowledge of the weight is often better, and the child should be weighed as soon as possible.

## Fever

::: definition Fever and the NICE traffic-light system {#def-fever}
**Fever** in a child is a temperature of 38 °C or above. In children under 4 weeks it is measured with an electronic thermometer in the axilla; from 4 weeks to 5 years an electronic or chemical dot thermometer in the axilla or an infrared tympanic thermometer can be used.

NICE guideline NG143 sorts clinical features into a **traffic-light system** to estimate the risk of serious illness in febrile children under 5:

- **Red (high risk)** features include: pale, mottled, ashen or blue colour; no response to social cues, appearing ill to a healthcare professional, not waking or not staying awake, a weak, high-pitched or continuous cry; grunting, a breathing rate above 60 per minute or moderate to severe chest indrawing; reduced skin turgor; a temperature of 38 °C or more in a baby **under 3 months**; a non-blanching rash; a bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, status epilepticus or focal neurological signs.
- **Amber (intermediate risk)** features include: pallor reported by parents; not responding normally or reduced activity; nasal flaring, tachypnoea or oxygen saturation of 95% or less in air; tachycardia, dry mucous membranes, poor feeding in infants, capillary refill of 3 seconds or more, reduced urine output; a temperature of 39 °C or more at **3–6 months**; fever for **5 days or longer**; rigors; swelling of a limb or joint.
- **Green (low risk)** means none of the amber or red features.

A **febrile seizure** is a seizure with fever in a child aged 6 months to 5 years, without central nervous system infection and without previous afebrile seizures. A **simple** febrile seizure is generalised, lasts less than 15 minutes and does not recur within 24 hours. A **complex** febrile seizure is focal, prolonged or repeated.
:::

The traffic lights guide action. A child with any red feature needs urgent face-to-face assessment by a paediatric specialist. A child with amber features needs assessment or a clear safety net. A child with only green features can usually be managed at home with advice.

Fever itself is not dangerous, and lowering it does not prevent febrile seizures. Antipyretics (paracetamol or ibuprofen) are given for distress, not to normalise the temperature. Parents should be told which features mean they should seek help again: a non-blanching rash, a child who is hard to wake, breathing difficulty, signs of dehydration, fever lasting 5 days or more, or simply that they are more worried than before.

Particular causes to keep in mind:

- **Meningococcal disease** can kill within hours. A non-blanching rash in a febrile child is meningococcal septicaemia until proved otherwise. In the community, a single dose of intramuscular or intravenous benzylpenicillin should be given before transfer, as long as it does not delay transfer.
- **Urinary tract infection** is the commonest serious bacterial infection in infants and often has no localising signs. A urine sample should be tested in any infant with unexplained fever.
- **Kawasaki disease** should be considered when fever lasts 5 days or more. The features are fever with conjunctival injection, changes in the lips and mouth, a rash, changes in the hands and feet, and cervical lymphadenopathy. Early treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin reduces the risk of coronary artery aneurysms.

::: example The 6-week-old with a temperature {#ex-youngfever}
Zainab is 6 weeks old. Her mother brings her to the emergency department because she has felt hot and fed less today. Her temperature is 38.3 °C, heart rate 175 per minute and breathing rate 50 per minute. She is alert, with a normal cry and normal colour, and there is no rash.
::: solution
**Step 1: traffic light.** A temperature of 38 °C or more in a baby under 3 months is a **red** feature, however well the baby appears. Young infants show little sign of serious infection early, and their immune systems are immature.

**Step 2: what could this be?** Urinary tract infection, bacteraemia (group B streptococcus, *E. coli*), meningitis, and viral infections including enterovirus and influenza. Herpes simplex virus is a consideration in the first weeks of life.

**Step 3: investigations.** NG143 recommends that babies under 3 months with fever have a full blood count, blood culture, C-reactive protein and urine testing. Babies under 1 month, and babies of 1–3 months who appear unwell or have a white cell count below $5$ or above $15 \times 10^9$/L, should also have a **lumbar puncture**. Many UK units follow this rule for all febrile infants under 3 months who have any concerning finding.

**Step 4: treatment.** If Zainab is unwell or the tests suggest bacterial infection, she needs intravenous antibiotics without delay. NG143 recommends a third-generation cephalosporin, with an antibiotic active against *Listeria* (such as amoxicillin) for babies under 3 months. Doses come from the BNF for Children.

**Step 5: observation.** Even if the first tests are reassuring, a febrile baby of this age is usually observed in hospital until cultures are available.
:::
:::

## Breathing problems

Respiratory infections are the commonest reason for children to be admitted to hospital in winter.

### Bronchiolitis

**Bronchiolitis** is a viral infection of the small airways in infants, most often caused by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). It starts with a runny nose and cough, and over a few days develops into breathlessness, with crackles and sometimes wheeze. Symptoms are usually worst on days 3–5, and the cough can last for up to 3 weeks.

Management is supportive. NICE NG9 advises:

- Give oxygen if saturations are persistently below 90% in children aged 6 weeks and over, or below 92% in babies under 6 weeks or children with underlying health conditions.
- Give fluids by nasogastric tube if the infant cannot take enough by mouth.
- Do **not** use salbutamol, adrenaline, corticosteroids, antibiotics or hypertonic saline. Trials have not shown that these help.
- Refer urgently for apnoea, severe respiratory distress (grunting, marked recession or a breathing rate above 70 per minute), central cyanosis, or persistently low saturations.

Babies under 3 months, babies born preterm, and those with congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease or immunodeficiency are at higher risk of severe disease. The RSV vaccine given in pregnancy (see [[paediatrics/immunisation]]) protects young infants.

::: example Bronchiolitis or something else? {#ex-wheeze}
Tom is 4 months old. For three days he has had a cold and cough, and today he is breathing fast and taking only half his usual feeds. His temperature is 37.9 °C, breathing rate 58 per minute, heart rate 160, and oxygen saturation 91% in air. He has subcostal recession and fine crackles throughout both lungs.
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** A coryzal illness followed by tachypnoea, recession and widespread fine crackles in an infant in winter is typical of **bronchiolitis**.

**Severity.** His saturation of 91% is above the threshold of 90% for oxygen at his age, so he does not yet need oxygen. But he is taking only half his feeds, which is a reason to admit: NG9 suggests considering admission when intake is below 50–75% of usual. His breathing rate is high but below 70.

**Plan.** Admit for observation and feeding support, with nasogastric feeds if he cannot feed enough. Give oxygen if saturations fall persistently below 90%. Do not give bronchodilators, steroids or antibiotics. Watch for apnoea and exhaustion. Explain to the parents that he is likely to get worse for a day or two before he improves.

**Red flags that would change the picture.** A high fever (above 39 °C) or focal chest signs suggest pneumonia. A heart murmur, an enlarged liver or a very fast heart rate suggest heart failure from congenital heart disease, which can be mistaken for bronchiolitis.
:::
:::

### Croup

**Croup** (laryngotracheobronchitis) is a viral infection, usually caused by parainfluenza virus, that causes swelling below the vocal cords. It typically affects children aged 6 months to 3 years. The features are a barking cough, a hoarse voice and **stridor**, a harsh sound on breathing in, often worse at night.

Most croup is mild. A single dose of oral **dexamethasone** reduces symptoms and the need for hospital care, and is given to children with croup of any severity. In severe croup, with stridor at rest, marked recession, agitation or drowsiness, nebulised **adrenaline** gives temporary relief while the steroid takes effect, and senior help is needed.

Children with stridor should not have their throat examined with a tongue depressor, and should be kept calm on a parent's lap. **Epiglottitis** and **bacterial tracheitis** are rare but more dangerous: a child who is toxic, drooling, unable to swallow and sitting forward needs an anaesthetist and an ENT surgeon, not an examination of the mouth. Epiglottitis has become rare since Hib vaccination. An inhaled **foreign body** is a key alternative cause of sudden stridor in a toddler.

### Asthma and pneumonia

Acute asthma is assessed by the child's ability to talk, the breathing rate, the use of accessory muscles, oxygen saturation and, in older children, peak flow. Treatment uses inhaled salbutamol through a spacer, oral corticosteroids and, in severe attacks, oxygen and further treatments in hospital, following the British Thoracic Society, SIGN and NICE guidance. **Pneumonia** is suggested by fever, tachypnoea and focal signs; in young children the commonest bacterial cause is *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and amoxicillin is the usual first-line oral antibiotic.

## Vomiting, diarrhoea and fluids

::: definition Dehydration, shock and maintenance fluids {#def-syndromes}
**Clinical dehydration** is a loss of body water with features such as altered responsiveness, sunken eyes, dry mucous membranes, tachycardia, tachypnoea and reduced skin turgor. **Clinical shock** adds signs of poor perfusion: pale or mottled skin, cold extremities, prolonged capillary refill, weak peripheral pulses, a decreased conscious level and, late, hypotension.

**Maintenance fluid** is the volume a child needs each day to replace normal losses. It is calculated with the **Holliday–Segar formula**: 100 mL/kg/day for the first 10 kg of body weight, 50 mL/kg/day for the next 10 kg, and 20 mL/kg/day for each kilogram above 20 kg.
:::

caption: The Holliday–Segar formula for daily maintenance fluid. The slope falls at 10 kg and again at 20 kg, because metabolic rate per kilogram falls as children grow. Hospitals often cap adult-sized children at 2–2.5 litres a day and restrict fluids when antidiuretic hormone secretion is likely to be increased.
:::

**Gastroenteritis** is the commonest cause of vomiting and diarrhoea, usually caused by viruses such as norovirus (rotavirus has become much less common since vaccination). Vomiting usually settles within 3 days and diarrhoea within 7.

Most children with gastroenteritis can be managed at home with frequent small drinks and continued breastfeeding. Children with clinical dehydration are rehydrated with **oral rehydration solution (ORS)**: NICE CG84 suggests 50 mL/kg over 4 hours, plus maintenance fluid, given little and often, by nasogastric tube if necessary. Intravenous fluids are needed for shock, for children who cannot keep ORS down, and for those who deteriorate.

For shock, NICE NG29 recommends a bolus of **10 mL/kg** of a glucose-free isotonic crystalloid, such as 0.9% sodium chloride, given over less than 10 minutes, with reassessment after each bolus. Intravenous maintenance fluids should also be isotonic, with sodium 131–154 mmol/L, because hypotonic fluids can cause dangerous hyponatraemia in sick children, who often secrete excess antidiuretic hormone.

Not every vomiting child has gastroenteritis. Features that suggest another diagnosis are:

- **bile-stained (green) vomiting**, which suggests intestinal obstruction, for example malrotation with volvulus, and is a surgical emergency;
- **projectile vomiting** in a hungry baby aged 2–8 weeks, suggesting **pyloric stenosis**, with a hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis;
- intermittent colicky pain, pallor during attacks and "redcurrant jelly" stool in a child aged about 3 months to 2 years, suggesting **intussusception**;
- headache, a bulging fontanelle or a decreased conscious level, suggesting raised intracranial pressure or meningitis;
- abdominal pain, fast deep breathing and weight loss, suggesting **diabetic ketoacidosis**.

::: example Calculating fluids for a dehydrated child {#ex-ors}
Maya is 3 years old and weighs 14 kg. She has had diarrhoea and vomiting for two days. She is alert but has dry mucous membranes and sunken eyes, with a heart rate of 135 and a capillary refill of 2 seconds. She has no signs of shock. Calculate her oral rehydration plan for the first 4 hours.
::: solution
**Assessment.** She has clinical dehydration without shock, so oral rehydration is appropriate.

**Deficit replacement.** $50 \text{ mL/kg} \times 14 \text{ kg} = 700$ mL over 4 hours.

**Maintenance.** By Holliday–Segar: $10 \times 100 = 1000$ mL for the first 10 kg, plus $4 \times 50 = 200$ mL for the next 4 kg, giving 1,200 mL/day, or $1200/24 = 50$ mL/h. Over 4 hours, that is 200 mL.

**Total.** $700 + 200 = 900$ mL of ORS over 4 hours, about **225 mL/h**, given as small frequent amounts, for example 5–10 mL every few minutes from a syringe or cup.

**Monitoring.** Reassess regularly. If she vomits persistently, a nasogastric tube can be used. If signs of shock appear, she needs intravenous fluid resuscitation. Once rehydrated, she returns to her normal diet and fluids, with ORS after each large watery stool.
:::
:::

::: example The 14-year-old with abdominal pain and fast breathing {#ex-teen}
Daniel is 14. For a week he has been drinking a lot and passing urine often, and he has lost weight. Today he has abdominal pain and has vomited three times. He is drowsy, breathing deeply at 32 per minute, with a heart rate of 125. His capillary blood glucose is 28 mmol/L.
::: solution
**Recognition.** Thirst, polyuria and weight loss are the classic symptoms of new **type 1 diabetes**. Abdominal pain, vomiting, drowsiness and deep sighing breathing (Kussmaul respiration) suggest **diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)**. DKA is a common way for type 1 diabetes to present in children, and it is easily mistaken for gastroenteritis or an acute abdomen.

**Confirmation.** A venous blood gas and blood ketones. DKA in children is defined in the British Society for Paediatric Endocrinology and Diabetes (BSPED) guideline by acidosis (venous pH below 7.3 or bicarbonate below 15 mmol/L) with ketonaemia (blood ketones above 3 mmol/L), in a child with hyperglycaemia or known diabetes.

**Management principles.** Treat according to the BSPED DKA guideline and calculator: careful fluid resuscitation and replacement over 48 hours, insulin infusion started 1–2 hours after fluids begin, frequent monitoring of glucose, ketones, electrolytes (especially potassium) and neurological state.

**Danger.** **Cerebral oedema** is the most feared complication in children. Headache, slowing heart rate, rising blood pressure, falling conscious level or new confusion need immediate senior review and treatment.
:::
:::

::: warning Do not dismiss bilious vomiting
Green vomiting in a baby or child is a surgical emergency until proved otherwise. Malrotation with volvulus can cut off blood supply to the whole midgut within hours, and the baby may otherwise look well at first. Any bile-stained vomit needs urgent surgical review and, usually, an upper gastrointestinal contrast study. Do not label it reflux or gastroenteritis.
:::

::: quiz
A 2-year-old has had a fever for 6 days. She is irritable, with red eyes without discharge, cracked red lips, a rash on her trunk and swollen hands. Which diagnosis must be considered and treated urgently?
- [ ] Measles
- [x] Kawasaki disease
- [ ] Scarlet fever
- [ ] Viral exanthem; treat with antipyretics
::: solution
Fever for 5 days or more with conjunctival injection, changes in the lips and mouth, a rash and changes in the hands meets the criteria for **Kawasaki disease**. Treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin (and aspirin, under specialist guidance) reduces the risk of coronary artery aneurysms, and echocardiography is needed. Prolonged fever is an amber feature in NG143 precisely so that this diagnosis is not missed.
:::
:::

::: history
The Holliday–Segar formula comes from a 1957 paper in *Pediatrics* by Malcolm Holliday and William Segar, who linked water needs to calorie expenditure: about 100 mL of water for every 100 kcal used. Their method lasted for decades, but the hypotonic fluids then used for maintenance were later found to cause hyponatraemia and deaths in hospitalised children, and in 2015 NICE recommended isotonic fluids. Oral rehydration solution was developed in the 1960s, and trials in Bangladesh and in Indian refugee camps during the 1971 war showed that it could treat cholera and save lives without intravenous fluids; it is often called one of the most important medical advances of the twentieth century. Tomisaku Kawasaki described the disease that bears his name in Japan in 1967.
:::

::: summary
- Children compensate well: tachycardia, slow capillary refill and reduced urine output appear early, and hypotension is a late, pre-terminal sign.
- Use ABCDE, age-specific normal values and early warning scores; weigh the child, or estimate weight in an emergency.
- Fever of 38 °C or more under 3 months is a red feature; fever for 5 days or more is amber and should prompt consideration of Kawasaki disease.
- A non-blanching rash with fever is meningococcal disease until proved otherwise.
- Bronchiolitis is supportive care: oxygen below 90% (92% under 6 weeks or with risk factors), feeding support, and no bronchodilators, steroids or antibiotics.
- Croup is treated with a single dose of oral dexamethasone; do not examine the throat of a child who may have epiglottitis.
- Holliday–Segar gives maintenance fluid: 100, 50 and 20 mL/kg/day. Oral rehydration is first line; shock is treated with 10 mL/kg isotonic boluses, and maintenance fluids are isotonic.
- Bilious vomiting is a surgical emergency, and DKA can masquerade as gastroenteritis.
:::

::: exercise The child with a barking cough {level=2}
A 2-year-old has had a barking cough and hoarse voice for a day. Tonight he has stridor when upset but not at rest, and mild recession. Saturation is 97%. Outline management and the advice you would give.
::: solution
This is mild to moderate **croup**. Give a single dose of oral dexamethasone (dose from the BNF for Children). Keep him calm, avoid examining the throat with a spatula, and observe; most children improve within a few hours and can go home. Advise the parents to return immediately if stridor occurs at rest, if he becomes pale, drowsy or blue, if he drools or cannot swallow, or if they are worried. Steam inhalation is not recommended.
:::

::: exercise A first febrile seizure {level=2}
A 20-month-old girl has a generalised seizure lasting 3 minutes at home during a fever. On arrival she is alert, playing and has a temperature of 38.8 °C with a red throat. What type of seizure is this, what should be excluded, and what should parents be told?
::: solution
This is a **simple febrile seizure**: generalised, under 15 minutes, in a child aged 6 months to 5 years. The priorities are to find the cause of the fever and to exclude central nervous system infection: look for meningism, a bulging fontanelle, a non-blanching rash, and a prolonged post-ictal period, and have a lower threshold for lumbar puncture in a child who has received antibiotics or is unwell. Here a viral throat infection is likely. Parents should be told that febrile seizures are common (about 1 in 30 children), that they do not cause brain damage, that about 1 in 3 children has another, that antipyretics do not prevent them, and what to do in a seizure: place the child on her side, time it, and call an ambulance if it lasts more than 5 minutes.
:::
:::

::: exercise The vomiting baby {level=3}
A 5-week-old boy has vomited forcefully after feeds for 4 days. He is hungry after vomiting and has lost weight. A blood gas shows pH 7.52, bicarbonate 34 mmol/L, chloride 88 mmol/L and potassium 3.0 mmol/L. Explain the blood results, the likely diagnosis and the order of management.
::: hint
Think about what is lost in gastric vomit and how the kidney responds.
:::
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Projectile non-bilious vomiting in a hungry baby of this age suggests **pyloric stenosis**, hypertrophy of the pyloric muscle. A test feed may reveal a palpable olive-shaped mass, and ultrasound confirms it.

**Blood results.** Vomiting gastric contents loses hydrochloric acid, causing **hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis**. Dehydration activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system; aldosterone makes the kidney retain sodium in exchange for potassium and hydrogen ions, causing hypokalaemia and worsening the alkalosis (paradoxical aciduria).

**Management order.** This is not an emergency operation. First correct dehydration and electrolytes with intravenous fluids containing sodium chloride and potassium, until bicarbonate and chloride are normal, because a baby with metabolic alkalosis is at risk of apnoea after anaesthesia. Then the surgeon performs a **pyloromyotomy**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Plan a winter ward round {level=3}
On a winter morning a paediatric registrar has five new patients: (1) a 7-week-old with bronchiolitis, saturation 89% in air, feeding 40% of normal; (2) a 4-year-old with a fever of 39 °C for 2 days, cough, saturation 97% and a normal examination apart from a runny nose; (3) a 9-month-old with fever and a non-blanching rash on the legs; (4) a 2-year-old with croup and stridor at rest; (5) a 6-year-old with vomiting and green vomit since the night. Rank them by urgency and state the immediate action for each.
::: solution
1. **The 9-month-old with fever and non-blanching rash (3):** presumed meningococcal sepsis. ABCDE, blood culture, intravenous antibiotics immediately, fluid bolus if shocked, senior and intensive care involvement.
2. **The 2-year-old with stridor at rest (4):** severe croup. Keep calm with a parent, give nebulised adrenaline and dexamethasone, call senior help and an anaesthetist if not improving.
3. **The 6-year-old with bilious vomiting (5):** possible obstruction such as volvulus or adhesions. Nil by mouth, intravenous access and fluids, urgent surgical review and imaging.
4. **The 7-week-old with bronchiolitis (1):** saturation below 90% and poor feeding. Start oxygen and nasogastric feeds, watch for apnoea.
5. **The 4-year-old with fever and runny nose (2):** green features. Likely viral; antipyretics for comfort, safety-net advice and discharge if he remains well.

Ranking depends on reassessment, and the order would change at once if any child deteriorated.
:::
