Adolescence is a developmental stage misdiagnosed as a behaviour problem, and adolescent medicine treats it as physiology. **The adolescent brain - limbic reward sensitivity ahead of prefrontal control, shifted circadian timing, sleep debt as a population norm - explains much of what adults call laziness or risk-taking. The clinical consequences are concrete: consultations that work require confidentiality the young person actually believes, a systems approach to self-harm, eating disorders and substance use, and the ability to manage adult-sized problems - contraception, transition of chronic disease, gender distress, obesity without iatrogenic harm - in a patient who is neither a child nor politely silent about it. This chapter is about seeing the whole adolescent - school, family, online life, sexuality, mood - in twenty minutes without losing the medicine.**

## The consultation and confidentiality

::: definition Consent, confidentiality and the adolescent consult {#def-consult}
**The two currencies of adolescent medicine are trust and time, and both are spent on the confidentiality conversation. In England a young person under 16 can consent to treatment if they have sufficient understanding and intelligence for the decision (Gillick competence), and the Fraser guidelines specifically govern contraceptive advice without parental knowledge; the practical test is comprehension, retention, weighing and communication, plus the clinician's judgement that the young person will likely use the treatment, that their health is likely to suffer without it, and that their best interests require it - with parent involvement encouraged, not required. Confidentiality is promised in the first two minutes and then kept: what we talk about stays between us, with three exceptions I will tell you now - if someone is being hurt, if you are in danger of serious harm, or the bounded infectious-disease contact tracing that we do with you, not about you. The exceptions are rare, and they are explained before being used rather than after. The legal floor matters for the record: a competent 15-year-old's STI treatment kept quiet from parents is lawful; a 14-year-old's request for paracetamol after self-harm is not a confidentiality crisis and should not become one; and information-sharing works better when it is rehearsed with the young person - who do you want to know, and who must not - than when it surprises them. The consultation structure: part of every visit alone with the clinician (phone translators, siblings and mothers out of the room for the sensitive half), a chaperone offered always, examinations explained before hands move, and the psychosocial screen that takes three minutes and finds most of the disease: school attendance and achievement, home relationships, eating and body image, sleep (bedtime, wake time, screens - one hour of later bedtime can present as depression), activity, drugs, alcohol and vaping, self-harm, mood and anxiety, conduct and the law, sexuality as described by them rather than presumed by a checkbox, and online safety including the bullying and the exploitation that arrives dressed as 'a relationship' with someone twenty years older. The final rule is that adolescents are expert at detecting whether you have met teenagers before: 'most people your age...' opens more histories than any questionnaire, and the consultation that ends with 'what should I write to your GP, and what can I tell your mum?' is the one that produces the next attendance - [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], [[psychiatry/psychiatric-assessment]], [[clinical-skills/communication]].**
:::

## The common presentations

::: definition Presentations, treatments and adolescent-specific traps {#def-presentations}
**The adolescent caseload repeats: contraception and sexual health - the patient on the pill, the implant or the IUD with the side-effect question that determines continuation; pregnancy options counselling and antenatal care (see [[obstetrics-gynaecology/pregnancy-antenatal]]); the STI screen and the 'I forgot my injection' adherence rescue; the binge-purge and restriction patterns ([[psychiatry/eating-personality-disorders]]); self-harm with suicide-risk assessment and safety planning ([[psychiatry/self-harm-suicide-prevention]]); the vape-shaped hole where smoking used to be, with the cannabis and nitrous-oxide questions asked factually; and the obesity consultation done without producing an eating disorder - weighing in private or not at all, discussing behaviours rather than numbers, treating the family's food environment rather than the child's shame. Then chronic disease: the adolescent whose adherence collapses as a developmentally normal assertion of control - the teenager with type 1 diabetes 'forgetting' injections is doing identity work, managed by naming that out loud rather than by surveillance. The three adolescent-specific traps that catch generalists: first, the acute hip - a slipped upper femoral epiphysis in the heavy 13-year-old with a limp labelled 'growing pain', where the x-ray is a decision not a courtesy and the answer is non-weight-bearing and orthopaedics tonight before a fixable slip becomes a destroyed hip; second, testicular torsion - the 14-year-old too embarrassed to point at his groin while vomiting, where 'examine the genitals in every unwell adolescent boy' is the rule and the six-hour window makes fast history-taking a clinical intervention; third, menstrual disturbance - the iron-deficient girl whose 'heavy periods' were called normal for three years while her ferritin emptied, the endometriosis that presents as school absence, and the polycystic ovary syndrome that must be diagnosed carefully in adolescence because normal pubertal features mimic it. Menstrual history - pain, length, flooding, regularity - is a vital sign in adolescent girls and belongs in every note. Add the conditions that define this chapter's era: gender distress, where good practice means thorough exploration, treatment of co-occurring autism, neurodevelopmental and mental-health conditions, a real fertility conversation, and decisions made within the evidence base rather than around it; and the multi-symomatic adolescent - headaches, tummy aches, dizziness, normal examination - who needs the formulation 'real symptoms, understandable mind-body amplification, graded recovery, one coordinating clinician' rather than another cascade of tests, because the referral treadmill is itself the treatment failure - [[paediatrics/acute-childhood-illness]], [[endocrine/reproductive-endocrinology]], [[musculoskeletal/fractures]].**
:::

## The systems adolescent medicine needs

::: definition Transition, schools and population tools {#def-systems}
**Three things determine adolescent outcomes more than any drug: the school, the handover and the population infrastructure. Schools are the delivery channel for almost everything - immunisation programmes, the absence and exclusion data that are early-warning systems for safeguarding and for undiagnosed learning and mental-health problems, and the special-educational-needs interface where an EHC plan either holds or fails (see [[paediatrics/neurodevelopment]]); the paediatrician who never speaks to a head of year is practising in a vacuum, and the school nurse is the most under-used clinician in the country. Transition from children's to adult services is a process, not a letter: a named key-worker, transition passports, a young-person-led planning meeting at 14-16, medical summaries written for adult clinicians, and the honest conversations about who prescribes contraception at 16 and whose clinic takes the teenager whose diabetic control collapsed at college. The audit trail of transition quality is the admission data, which spike in the handover years; good services drive them down with GP registers and named workers rather than lovely discharge summaries. At population level, adolescence is where prevention either lands or dies: immunisation catch-up, the smoking and alcohol guidelines, the helmet and seat-belt and knife and drowning conversations that are tedious to give and measurably save the exact demographic that does not wear seat-belts; and the access architecture - young people do not use services where they must queue next to their parents, so confidential drop-in sexual health, youth advice centres, phone-first mental-health triage and honest online signposting are the interface layer without which every policy above becomes a PDF. Finally the measurement disciplines: adolescent services should track self-reported access ('could you get an appointment without a parent'), continuity ('do you know your named worker'), attendance equity by deprivation and ethnicity, and the outcome young people themselves report - whether the system knows them - because the best predictor of whether a 17-year-old in trouble seeks help at 19 is whether a clinician helped them once before without humiliating them - [[public-health/prevention-promotion]], [[public-health/determinants-inequalities]], [[clinical-skills/records-handover]].**
:::

::: example The 15-year-old with abdominal pain and a secret {#ex-teenpain}
A 15-year-old girl, four ED attendances in two months for periumbilical pain and normal bloods; today she is tearful, quiet, and asks to speak 'without my mum'.
::: solution
**The 'without my mum' sentence ([[#def-consult]], [[#def-presentations]]): the first five minutes decide everything - the practice script that separates the mother kindly ('she waits right here, we will see her together after'), the chaperone present, and the question that adolescents can actually answer: 'has anything happened that you have not told the doctors?' The history then covers the triad that recurrent abdominal pain hides: abuse or sexual violence (often presented as 'a boyfriend' who is older, or a 'freezing' story she believes is her fault), anxiety and school refusal with somatic amplification, and gynaecological causes - ovarian cysts, mid-cycle and menstrual pathology, and pregnancy whatever the stated history. The actions: believe first and investigate as indicated; involve the sexual-violence pathway (SARC or IDAS in England) rather than improvising forensic questions; agree a safety plan with her, not with her parent; document in her words; and explain exactly what will be shared, with whom, and why - because a girl who has to bring her mother into a disclosure is a girl who never discloses, and four ED attendances were the system failing to be alone with her. The somatic pain usually resolves when the secret is safe and believed - 'I believe you, this explains your stomach, and here is what happens next' are three sentences that break a somatisation spiral - [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], [[clinical-skills/communication]].**
:::
:::

::: example The 16-year-old cricketer with a sore hip {#ex-suFE}
A 16-year-old, BMI 34, three weeks of 'growing-pain' thigh and knee discomfort, walking with an out-toeing limp; radiographs at urgent care were 'normal'; his mother says 'he has just grown a lot'.
::: solution
**A slipped upper femoral epiphysis until proven otherwise ([[#def-presentations]], [[musculoskeletal/fractures]]): the recognition - 'referred knee pain is hip pain until proven otherwise, the obligate external rotation and out-toeing gait is the sign, and in an adolescent the hip is examined with radiographs standing AP and frog-leg - a "normal" film read at urgency level is re-read by a senior, because the early slip is a subtle physeal widening that is career- and hip-ending to miss'; the action tonight - 'non-weight-bearing from now, no more cricket, no "try ibuprofen and see", same-night or next-morning paediatric orthopaedic review, and pinning in theatre before a minor slip becomes a major one, because the whole disease is a mechanical problem with a small window and a lifetime of consequences - chondrolysis and avascular necrosis are the complications that decide his thirties'; the endocrine footnote - 'a SUFE at an unusual age or habitus prompts a thyroid and growth-hormone look, and the obesity conversation happens later, privately, with him, framed around this hip and his sport, not around a BMI number in front of his mother'; and the follow-through - 'the other hip gets surveillance and education because a substantial minority slip contralaterally, the school PE exemption is written today, and the physiotherapy and return-to-sport plan is owned by a named team, because these boys return to sport too early or give up entirely and both are service failures' - [[paediatrics/acute-childhood-illness]], [[endocrine/hypothalamus-pituitary]].**
:::

::: example The 17-year-old with chest pain after vaping {#ex-vape}
A 17-year-old boy, three months of daily THC vaping, presents with pleuritic chest pain and breathlessness; sats 93 percent, clear chest x-ray, and a father in the consult room who speaks first every time.
::: solution
**EVALI-shaped illness and the sub-consult ([[#def-consult]], [[#def-presentations]]): the medicine - 'maintain the oxygen and examine for the pneumomediastinum and the small effusion the x-ray misses and CT or echocardiogram guided by findings, with the differentials of myocarditis, pulmonary embolism in the vaper who also smoked a long-haul flight, and the chemical pneumonitis of vitamin-E-acetate-adjacent black-market cartridges; send him home only with a follow-up you own, and treat the vaping cessation as part of the prescription' - and the consult shape - 'separate the father within the first two minutes as standard practice, because the son's "I don't know" to every question is a translation problem: the clinician must ask the things a son cannot say in front of dad - what is in the vape, who buys it, is he being sold it or bullied into it - and then bring the father back for the plan, because demonising the son would have ended the history early. The cessation conversation: vaping THC is not 'nothing', and the motivational style that works at 17 is factual and future-oriented (sport, money, the chest-pain scare, the unknown additive) rather than parental; the documented harm-reduction step ('stop all THC cartridges now, especially informal-source ones, and here is what would make me worry tonight') plus a quit-line app plus a two-week recheck outperforms the one-off lecture. If the saturations or pain worsen, admit and re-ask the exposure history with a toxicology or lung-injury protocol, because EVALI is a diagnosis of exposure and exclusion, and the exposure is obtained alone - [[respiratory/asthma-copd]], [[psychiatry/substance-use]].**
:::
:::

::: example The 14-year-old who will not go to school {#ex-sr}
A 14-year-old girl, absent 11 weeks; somatic headaches and belly aches every morning that 'vanish by lunchtime when she is allowed to stay home'; no psychiatric history; the parents are exhausted and 'just want the doctor to say it is physical so the school stops calling'.
::: solution
**School refusal as a system diagnosis ([[#def-consult]], [[#def-systems]]): the formulation - 'the symptoms are real, the mechanism is anxiety with somatic amplification, and the maintaining circuit is the morning escape: symptoms, absence, short-term relief, longer-term collapse of school self-efficacy and friendship contact, and a darker version of the same anxiety in six months'; the ask from the parents - 'give them the honest physical answer: nothing dangerous is happening, no tests beyond the examination, and here is why "physical" and "anxiety" are the same sentence about her body'. The plan has four owners and a timetable, not a hope: graded return (day-one: two lessons and the library; the plan written by the school with a named mentor; no assemblies-and-corridors exposure therapy in week one), the parents' script for the morning (symptoms checked at the school gate, not at home in bed), the family work on sleep and the weekend reversal, and the psychological treatment - CBT for anxiety with the school accommodations, and a referral that is chased weekly because a fourteen-week wait is a treatment failure by arithmetic. The screens that must not be skipped: bullying including online, a learning difficulty unmasked by the work getting harder (the 'refusal' that is 'cannot read'), abuse or exploitation that presents as school refusal in a minority, pregnancy, self-harm and the eating that often co-arrives at this age. Reattendance at one month is the outcome measure; the school's attendance percentage is the scoreboard; and the sentence the parents needed on day one is 'you have not failed her, and staying out is the one thing that makes this worse - we will get her back in small pieces, starting Thursday' - [[psychiatry/anxiety-disorders]], [[clinical-skills/communication]].**
:::

## Where this leads

The acute traps connect to [[paediatrics/acute-childhood-illness]], the psychiatric comorbidity to [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]] and [[psychiatry/self-harm-suicide-prevention]], sexual health to [[infectious-diseases/sexually-transmitted-infections]] and [[obstetrics-gynaecology/contraception-fertility]], and the protection overlay to [[paediatrics/safeguarding]].

::: history
**Adolescent medicine is the youngest paediatric sub specialty and its history is the story of medicine noticing teenagers at all. The nineteenth-century view - that childhood ended at whatever age the factory or the counting house required - gave way slowly: the age-of-consent and compulsory-schooling battles of the 1870s-1900s created 'the adolescent' as a legal and then a scientific category, with G. Stanley Hall's 1904 treatise giving the new stage its (now-cringeable) storm-and-stress theory, and the child-guidance clinics of the 1920s-30s producing the first 'problem boy' literature that pathologised normality for three generations. The medical turn came from two directions: paediatricians who could no longer discharge cystic fibrosis and leukaemia survivors into adulthood with no service in between, and the visible epidemics of the 1960s-70s - pregnancy, drugs and suicide - which created hospital adolescent units against hospital culture, with the pioneer wards (the Harriet Lane and Great Ormond Street lines, the US ambulatory-clinic movement) fighting for overnight beds, same-sex visitation, and schooling in hospital. The confidentiality jurisprudence - the Gillick case of 1985-86, which began with contraceptive access and ended as the law of children's competence - is the anglophone specialty's founding document, and its Fraser guidelines still govern clinic practice weekly. The epidemiological contributions are humbling: the recognition that most mental illness starts by 14, that suicide and self-harm climbed steadily from the 1980s in some populations, that adolescent idiopathic scoliosis screening succeeded when done with the right curve thresholds and failed as a general-health excuse, and that the biggest killers at 16 are not diseases at all but collisions, drownings, overdoses and self-harm - which is why the field's treatments include a seat-belt law, a Graduated Drivers scheme, a smoke-free car law and a school start time more than any prescription. The newest chapter - the internet as developmental environment - is still unwritten: the 2010s evidence on social media and adolescent mood arrived contested, the regulator has turned to design codes and age-appropriate design rather than clinical consensus, and clinicians now take 'what do you watch, who do you talk to, when does the phone go' as seriously as diet history, because the consultations of the 2020s are unrecognisable without it - [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]], [[public-health/prevention-promotion]], [[clinical-skills/communication]].**
:::

::: summary
- Confidentiality is the intervention: promise it early, keep it visibly, share information with the young person rather than about them.
- Gillick competence and the Fraser criteria govern under-16 consent; competence is decision-specific and documented, not a birthday.
- The classic adolescent traps - SUFE, torsion, ovarian pathology, pregnancy, iron-deficient menorrhagia - are missed by histories rushed in front of parents.
- Obesity, gender distress and multi-symomatic presentations are consultation-shape problems: treat with privacy, formulation and one coordinating clinician, never with the referral treadmill.
- Transition is a funded process with a named owner; the school is a co-therapist or a co-stressor, and the absence data tell you which.
- Measure access the way adolescents experience it: without a parent, without a queue, without shame.
:::

::: exercise Structure the first-ever adolescent clinic visit {level=1}
A 13-year-old with asthma attends alone for the first time. List what to do in the first three minutes, and the four psychosocial questions most likely to change management in this age band.
::: solution
**The-alone-13-year-old ([[#def-consult]]): first three minutes - see them alone for at least part of the visit as standard, state confidentiality and its three exceptions in one breath, check who else is in the family and why they came today in their words, and hand over the inhaler technique check as the trust-building physical task. The four questions: smoking and vaping (the relapse-relevant one for asthma), cannabis (secret, stigmatised, and the commonest illicit exposure at this age), bullying and online life (the attendance-and-sleep-changing one), and alcohol (asked factually: what have you tried, with whom, how often) - because at 13 these four determine more morbidity than any inhaler brand, and a clinician who has asked once will be asked about everything else later - [[respiratory/asthma-copd]], [[psychiatry/substance-use]].**

::: exercise Advise on the missed-pill-and-assault question {level=2}
A 17-year-old discloses a sexual assault three nights ago, is on the combined pill and vomited it twice, is 'fine really' and asks for 'emergency contraception and no drama'. Manage her requests and the next steps.
::: solution
**Post-assault-care-with-autonomy ([[#def-consult]], [[#def-presentations]]): honour both requests precisely - she is the author of the pace and you are the owner of the completeness. Offer emergency contraception urgently (the levonorgestrel window is 72 hours, ulipristal 120 hours and works better late; the copper IUD is the most effective and is also the STI-safety-and-future-contraception conversation at once), explain the drug interactions with her regular pill, and give clear pregnancy-test timing. Offer, do not require: STI screening including chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAATs, HIV and hepatitis bloods with the window-period explanation and the PEP question discussed honestly inside 72 hours because it may still be relevant, pregnancy options counselling regardless of stated certainty, and a same-day referral to the sexual-violence service with a named contact - with her present, on speaker if she wants, with the option to decline everything except the contraception documented without judgement. Safeguarding note: '17 and assaulted' is not automatically a child-protection referral, but '17, assaulted by an older man who 'buys her things'' is a exploitation pattern that the safeguarding team should hear about with her knowledge - the difference between honouring a request and abandoning a duty is a sentence you say out loud: 'I will do exactly what you want tonight, and there are two things I will not stop doing: keeping you safe from him, and being here in two weeks' - [[obstetrics-gynaecology/contraception-fertility]], [[paediatrics/safeguarding]].**

::: exercise Run the transition-planning meeting {level=2}
A 16-year-old with spina bifida, a neurogenic bladder, two shunts and a college place booked has 'no idea' who does his referrals next year. Chair the meeting: who is in the room, the five questions he must be able to answer at the end, and the documents that must exist.
::: solution
**The-transition-meeting ([[#def-systems]]): in the room - him (speaking first), the parents at his invitation, the key-worker or nurse-led transition coordinator, the school or college SENCo, and the GP practice nurse or named GP by video if not in person; urology and neurosurgery send written status. The five questions he can answer at the end: who prescribes my catheters and what happens at 2am if they run out; who organises my annual renal ultrasound and my shunt checks and who does it when I am at college two hours away; how I get the pill or a clinic or a prescription confidentially as an adult; what my urosepsis or shunt-malfunction red flags are and which ED; and what happens in five years when I want contraception, fertility advice or a pregnancy plan - because these questions are the adult service's intake form and asking them at 16 prevents the 19-year-old septic-and-unregistered presentation. The documents: a one-page health passport he controls, the named-worker details for each adult service, the GP summary with the inheritance-and-anesthesia-relevant details, a copy of the capacity-and-consent note, and the college access plan - and one service owns the completeness check, auditable by name, because the transition that nobody audits is the admission that everybody predicts - [[public-health/health-systems-economics]], [[clinical-skills/records-handover]].**

::: exercise Design a school mental-health triage service {level=3}
Your town's ED sees 300 under-18 self-harm or crisis presentations a year; 40 percent re-attend within six months; CAMHS waits 14 weeks. Design a school-based triage and low-intensity service with its governance, equity and coercion safeguards.
::: solution
**School-based-mental-health-service ([[#def-systems]]): the design - embedded clinicians in the two secondary schools and the college with phone-first access, a 48-hour assessment promise for referred crisis cases, low-intensity CBT-informed therapists trained for the autism and trauma adaptations, a direct escalation route to the crisis team that bypasses the 14-week queue, and an ED-liaison protocol where every self-harm attendance gets a named worker before discharge. The governance: competency frameworks for what a school counsellor may and may not do, mandatory referral rules when risk crosses defined thresholds, clinical supervision funded in the model, and data-sharing agreements that tell students in plain English what their counsellor can and cannot keep - because 'confidential except...' is a promise, while 'we share with parents if we think it's best' is a trap that shuts the service. The equity test: the self-referral route must work for the pupil who will not be seen entering a counselling room (drop-in 'walk-in Wednesday' with a reason attached, online entry, sports-coach signposting), and uptake is measured by deprivation quintile and ethnicity against the population, because school services default to the articulate. The coercion safeguard: the service is help, not surveillance - a student referred for 'behaviour' gets the same assessment as one who self-refers for 'mood', records sit in the clinical file with a firewall from the exclusion process, and the referral criteria are published to students and parents - because a mental-health service that schools learn to use for discipline will never be trusted by a fourteen-year-old again, and the outcome measure that exposes that failure is the re-attendance rate - [[psychiatry/self-harm-suicide-prevention]], [[public-health/determinants-inequalities]].**

::: exercise Do the adolescent examination {level=1}
List the five components of an adolescent examination that differ from adult or child practice, and the two that most often change the diagnosis.
::: solution
**The adolescent exam ([[#def-presentations]]): the five differences - growth and pubertal staging done with a chaperone and an explanation and an opt-out, spine and posture (scoliosis and the SUFE hip), the skin (acne severity, the self-harm scars the sleeves hide, the knuckle calluses of purging), the fundus when headaches or medications demand it, and genital and testicular examination in every unwell boy and every scrotal or abdominal pain. The two that most often change the diagnosis: the testis - torsion, tumour and the episodic 'it twisted and untwisted' history that only an examination confirms; and the skin of the thighs and abdomen read with consent and context, because the self-harm and the purging marks are the psychiatric diagnoses arriving in dermatological dress. All of it governed by the three rules: explain before touching, chaperone offered always, and 'you can stop me at any time' said out loud - which is how adolescents tolerate examinations at all - [[clinical-skills/physical-examination]], [[paediatrics/safeguarding]].**

::: exercise Prescribe the first inhaler... or the first vaccine {level=1}
A 15-year-old asthmatic vaper is due the routine adolescent boosters and the HPV catch-up; he asks 'do any of these affect fertility or vaping or my inhalers'. Answer him.

::: exercise Handle the 'breaching' 16-year-old at 2am {level=2}
A 16-year-old with type 1 diabetes arrives at 2am intoxicated, ketones 6, glucose 19, 'forgot' his insulin for two days; his parents are furious and want the team to 'ground him for his own good'. Write the admission and discharge plan.
::: solution
**The-diabetes-and-vodka-admission ([[#def-consult]], [[#def-presentations]]): the medicine first - 'treat the ketosis with the protocol and do not moralise it; check for the hypoglycaemia-after-alcohol delay that strikes at hour 8-12 and admit for observation because alcohol plus type 1 diabetes is a genuine delayed-hypoglycaemia risk and "he is fine now" is not the endpoint'. The conversation after the fluids - with him alone: what happened, what is the pattern (this is not forgetfulness, it is the adolescent adherence collapse in its classic form, and naming it kindly - 'whose diabetes is it this year?' - is the intervention), the vaping-and-insulin interaction stated factually, the driving and the university plans asked about, and the 'what would make taking your insulin easier in your life as you actually live it' plan he designs. With the parents: their fear is real, and grounding is a family negotiation, not a prescription; what works is the structure they control - phone-and-carb-and-testing routines - plus a supervised period agreed with him, not imposed behind him. The discharge: diabetes-team follow-up within a week, a written sick-day-and-alcohol plan, the emergency glucagon renewed, the college or school nurse informed with his agreement, the CAMHS or young-diabetes-psychologist referral offered because two such episodes is a pattern, and one named adult clinician who owns the next six months - because the audit of this admission is whether he attends it, and teenagers attend services that did not humiliate them - [[endocrine/diabetes]], [[psychiatry/lifespan-psychiatry]].**

::: exercise Design an online-safety consultation protocol {level=3}
Your service sees the fallout of image-based abuse ('nude selfie sent, ex circulated it, school is involved, police 'advice only''). Write the clinical protocol: immediate steps, consent and information governance, the school interface, and the harm-reduction script for the next patient.
::: solution
**The-image-abuse-protocol ([[#def-consult]], [[#def-systems]]): the immediate steps - see the young person alone first; establish who did what to whom and with what intent, because the 'perpetrator' is often a 14-year-old classmate and the victim is simultaneously a suspect in someone's sexting narrative; document without requiring the image; explain the police position in your country plainly (in England creation of an indecent image of a person under 18 is technically an offence with a published charging-context policy and explicit diversion practice - the clinician must know the local reality rather than the headline law); refer to the reporting platform that actually removes content (the UK Internet Watch Foundation and the major-platform reporting routes work) with the young person present, not by telling them to do it; and screen for the coercion, exploitation and trafficking pattern that hides inside 'relationship' stories. Governance: images are never stored in clinical records; the consent conversation covers what the school must be told (safety and the no-retaliation plan) versus what it must not (the clinical detail, because the rumour mill is the harm's amplifier); and the young person's own device hygiene is discussed without shame - the block-and-report sequence, the trusted-adult list. The school interface: one letter from one clinician stating safe-use expectations and the anti-bullying duty, with the victim's education plan, because 'both kids punished equally' is how services revictimise. The harm-reduction script for the next consultation, delivered without a sermon: 'before you send the next photo, know three things - a photo you send can outlive the relationship, your consent travels with it and so does your right to report its theft, and if it ever leaks I would rather hear about it from you in the first hour than in the first month' - which is the clinical sentence that converts a future crisis into a consultation - [[paediatrics/safeguarding]], [[clinical-skills/records-handover]].**
