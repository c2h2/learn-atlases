Adolescence, which the World Health Organization defines as the years from 10 to 19, is a time of rapid change in body, brain and social life. Most young people are physically healthy, but this is the age at which many mental health problems begin, at which habits such as smoking, drinking and drug use start, and at which young people with chronic illness begin to take over their own care. The leading causes of death among adolescents in the UK are injury and suicide, not disease.

Adolescents are also the patients that clinicians most often find difficult to talk to. They may be embarrassed, guarded or worried about what will be passed on to their parents. The skills in this chapter are therefore as much about the consultation as about the conditions: how to see a young person alone, how confidentiality and consent work, and how to ask about the things that matter most.

## The consultation and confidentiality

The adolescent brain is still developing. The limbic system, which drives reward-seeking and emotion, matures early in puberty, while the prefrontal cortex, which supports planning and self-control, continues to mature into the mid-twenties. This helps to explain why adolescents take more risks, especially with peers, and why they respond better to being treated as partners than to being lectured.

::: definition Consent and confidentiality for young people {#def-consult}
**Confidentiality** means that information shared by a young person is not passed on without their agreement. It applies to young people as it does to adults. It can be broken only when there is a risk of serious harm to the young person or others, or when the law requires it, and the young person should usually be told first.

A young person aged **16 or 17** is presumed to be able to consent to their own medical treatment, under section 8 of the Family Law Reform Act 1969, as long as they have capacity.

A young person **under 16** can consent to treatment if they have enough understanding and intelligence to understand fully what is proposed. This is called **Gillick competence**, after the House of Lords judgment of 1985. Competence relates to a specific decision; a child may be competent to consent to a simple treatment but not to a complex one.

The **Fraser guidelines** apply specifically to advice and treatment about contraception and sexual health for under-16s. The clinician must be satisfied that the young person understands the advice, cannot be persuaded to tell their parents or allow the clinician to do so, is likely to have sex with or without contraception, and that their health is likely to suffer without it, and that it is in their best interests.
:::

A refusal of treatment by a young person under 18 is treated differently from consent. Courts can override a refusal of life-saving treatment if it is in the young person's best interests. These situations are rare and need senior and legal advice.

There are some fixed limits. A child **under 13** cannot legally consent to sexual activity, under the Sexual Offences Act 2003, so sexual activity in a child under 13 always requires a safeguarding referral. For young people aged 13–15, sexual activity is also illegal, but the law is not intended to prosecute consensual activity between young people of similar age; clinicians assess for exploitation and abuse, for example a much older partner, coercion, gifts or the use of drugs or alcohol.

### Seeing the young person alone

Good practice is to see a young person with their parent for part of the consultation and alone for part of it. Explaining this as routine ("I see all young people on their own for part of the appointment") avoids suggesting that anyone is under suspicion. At the start of the time alone, explain confidentiality and its limits clearly.

A structured psychosocial history helps to cover the important topics without making assumptions. The **HEEADSSS** framework asks about:

- **H**ome: who lives at home, relationships, safety.
- **E**ducation and employment: school attendance, performance, bullying, plans.
- **E**ating: weight and body image, dieting, bingeing or vomiting.
- **A**ctivities: friends, hobbies, sport, screen and social media use.
- **D**rugs: tobacco, vaping, alcohol and other drugs.
- **S**exuality: relationships, sexual activity, contraception, consent, sexual orientation and gender identity.
- **S**uicide and mood: low mood, anxiety, self-harm, thoughts of suicide.
- **S**afety: injuries, online safety, violence, exploitation.

Start with the less sensitive topics (home, school, activities) and move to the more sensitive ones. Asking directly about self-harm and suicidal thoughts does not increase risk, and many young people are relieved to be asked.

::: example A 15-year-old with abdominal pain and a secret {#ex-teenpain}
Chloe, aged 15, attends the emergency department with her mother because of lower abdominal pain for 2 days. When seen alone, after confidentiality has been explained, she says that she has had sex with her boyfriend and that her last period was 7 weeks ago. Her boyfriend is 16. She asks the doctor not to tell her mother.
::: solution
**Clinical priority.** Lower abdominal pain with a late period in a sexually active young person requires a **pregnancy test** to exclude **ectopic pregnancy**, which can be life-threatening. Examine her and check her observations. Also consider pelvic inflammatory disease, urinary tract infection, ovarian cyst accident and appendicitis.

**Consent and confidentiality.** Chloe is 15. If she understands the tests and their implications, she is Gillick competent to consent to them. Her confidentiality should be respected; there is no automatic duty to tell her mother.

**Safeguarding.** Sexual activity at 15 is below the age of consent, so assess the relationship. A partner of similar age, with no coercion, gifts, alcohol or drugs, and no other concerns does not automatically require a referral. A large age gap, signs of control or exploitation, or concern from Chloe would change that. Record the assessment.

**Further care.** Offer testing for sexually transmitted infections, including chlamydia and gonorrhoea, and a discussion of contraception. Encourage her to involve her mother or another trusted adult, and offer support to do so, while respecting her decision.

**If the test is positive,** she needs urgent assessment for ectopic pregnancy, and then confidential, non-judgemental support with all her options.
:::
:::

## Common presentations

::: definition Eating disorders, depression and self-harm {#def-presentations}
**Anorexia nervosa** is restriction of food intake leading to a significantly low body weight, with an intense fear of gaining weight and a disturbed experience of body shape or weight.

**Bulimia nervosa** is recurrent binge eating with compensatory behaviours such as vomiting, laxative use or excessive exercise, with self-evaluation unduly influenced by shape and weight; weight is usually in the normal range.

**Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID)** is restricted eating driven by sensory sensitivity, lack of interest in food or fear of consequences such as choking, without concern about body shape.

**Depression** in young people is persistent low mood or irritability, or loss of interest and pleasure, with other symptoms such as changes in sleep, appetite, energy and concentration, feelings of worthlessness and thoughts of death, lasting at least two weeks and affecting functioning.

**Self-harm** is intentional self-poisoning or self-injury, irrespective of the apparent purpose.
:::

### Eating disorders

Eating disorders usually begin in adolescence, and anorexia nervosa has one of the highest mortality rates of any mental illness. They affect boys and young people from all backgrounds, not only white girls, and are often missed in these groups.

In children and young people, weight is assessed using the **percentage of median BMI (%mBMI)**: the young person's BMI divided by the median BMI for their age and sex, multiplied by 100. Adult BMI cut-offs do not apply because BMI changes with age.

The medical risk of anorexia nervosa is assessed using the Royal College of Psychiatrists' guidance **Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders (MEED)**, published in 2022, which replaced the earlier MARSIPAN and Junior MARSIPAN guidance. It considers weight and the rate of weight loss, heart rate, blood pressure (including postural change), temperature, blood tests (especially electrolytes, glucose and phosphate), the ECG and the ability to stand up from squatting. A %mBMI below 70% is one marker of high risk.

Starvation slows the heart, lowers blood pressure and body temperature, and depletes phosphate, potassium and magnesium. When feeding restarts, insulin drives these into cells, and blood levels can fall dangerously; this is **refeeding syndrome**. Admission for medical stabilisation, with careful monitoring of electrolytes during refeeding, is needed for young people at high risk.

The main treatment for anorexia nervosa in young people, recommended by NICE NG69, is **eating disorder-focused family therapy**, in which parents are supported to take charge of their child's eating while the young person recovers.

::: example Assessing a young person with weight loss {#ex-ed}
Priya is 16. She has lost 9 kg over 4 months by restricting food and running every day. She weighs 41.0 kg and is 1.65 m tall. Her heart rate is 44 lying and 72 standing, blood pressure 88/52, temperature 35.6 °C. For this example, take the median BMI for her age and sex as 20.5 kg/m².
::: solution
**BMI.** $41.0/1.65^2 = 41.0/2.7225 = 15.06$ kg/m².

**%mBMI.** $15.06/20.5 \times 100 = 73.5\%$.

**Interpretation.** Her %mBMI is just above 70%, but her weight loss has been rapid (about 2 kg a month), her resting heart rate is very low with a large postural rise, her blood pressure is low and she is cool. Rapid weight loss and cardiovascular instability are markers of high medical risk in MEED even when %mBMI is not at its lowest. Athletic training can lower resting heart rate, but it does not explain these findings together.

**Action.** Urgent assessment with blood tests (electrolytes, phosphate, magnesium, glucose, full blood count, liver function), an ECG, and discussion with the paediatric team about admission for medical stabilisation and supervised refeeding, alongside urgent referral to the community eating disorder service. Talk with Priya honestly and kindly about the reasons, and involve her parents. Assess her mood and risk of self-harm.
:::
:::

A short screening questionnaire, such as the five-question SCOFF, can help to identify young people who need a fuller assessment. Like any screening test, its usefulness depends on how common the condition is in the group being tested.

### Depression, anxiety and self-harm

Low mood and anxiety are common in adolescence, and have become more common over the last decade. Depression in young people may present as irritability, withdrawal, falling school performance, physical symptoms or substance use rather than obvious sadness.

NICE NG134 (2019) recommends a stepped approach. Mild depression may be managed initially with active monitoring and, if it persists, digital cognitive behavioural therapy, group therapy or guided self-help. Moderate to severe depression needs referral to child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS) for a specific psychological therapy. An antidepressant, usually fluoxetine, is offered only after specialist assessment and in combination with psychological therapy, with close monitoring for agitation or increased suicidal thoughts, especially in the first weeks.

Self-harm is common in adolescents and is a strong risk factor for later suicide. NICE NG225 (2022) emphasises that every young person who has self-harmed should be treated with compassion and respect, should have a **psychosocial assessment** that explores their needs and circumstances, and should have a care plan. NICE advises against using risk assessment tools or scales to predict future suicide or to decide who receives care, because they predict poorly.

If you are affected by the issues in this section, support is available in the UK from Childline (0800 1111), Samaritans (116 123) and Papyrus HOPELINE247 (0800 068 4141) for young people under 35.

::: example A 14-year-old who stopped going to school {#ex-sr}
Marcus, aged 14, has missed most of the last month of school. His father says he stays in his room, is irritable and has stopped playing football. When seen alone, he says he feels "pointless", cannot sleep and has been cutting his arm. He has been bullied online about his sexuality.
::: solution
**Assessment.** Persistent low mood, loss of interest, sleep disturbance and withdrawal for over a month suggest **depression**. He has self-harmed. Online bullying and his feelings about his sexuality are important stressors.

**Ask directly about suicidal thoughts.** Ask whether he has thought about ending his life, whether he has made any plans, and what helps him to keep going. Asking does not increase risk.

**Immediate care.** Look at and treat the wounds. Discuss with Marcus who else needs to know. His safety is the priority, and his father will need to be involved in keeping him safe, but Marcus can be part of deciding what is shared and how.

**Referral.** Urgent referral to CAMHS for assessment and therapy, with the urgency depending on what he has said about suicidal thoughts and plans. Agree a **safety plan** with him: warning signs, things he can do, people he can contact, and crisis numbers. Work with the school on the bullying and on a gradual return.

**Respect.** Use his own words for his identity, avoid assumptions, and make it clear that his sexuality is not the problem; the bullying is.
:::
:::

### Substance use and sexual health

Experimentation with alcohol, tobacco and cannabis is common in adolescence, and vaping has become widespread. Questions should be asked in a non-judgemental way. Risks include accidents, unprotected sex, exploitation and, for some, dependence and mental health problems; regular cannabis use is associated with psychosis in vulnerable young people. Brief advice, harm reduction and referral to young people's substance misuse services are the main tools, and patterns of use that suggest exploitation, such as carrying drugs for others, are a safeguarding concern.

Sexual health care for young people includes contraception, testing for sexually transmitted infections and advice about consent and healthy relationships. **Long-acting reversible contraception** (implants and intrauterine methods) is the most effective and is suitable for young people. **Emergency contraception** options are the copper intrauterine device (the most effective, up to 5 days after unprotected sex), ulipristal acetate (up to 120 hours) and levonorgestrel (up to 72 hours). Chlamydia is the commonest bacterial sexually transmitted infection in young people and is often asymptomatic.

### An orthopaedic trap

::: example A 13-year-old with knee pain {#ex-suFE}
Daniel, aged 13, has had pain in his left knee for 3 weeks and has started to limp. He is overweight. Examination of the knee is normal. Hip examination shows that his left hip turns outwards when it is flexed, and internal rotation is limited and painful.
::: solution
**Key principle.** Pain from the hip is often felt in the knee, because the obturator nerve supplies both. **Always examine the hip in a child with knee pain.**

**Diagnosis.** Limited internal rotation, and obligatory external rotation on flexion, in an overweight young adolescent suggest a **slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)**: the femoral head slips backwards at the growth plate during the growth spurt.

**Investigation.** X-rays of both hips, including a frog-leg lateral view, because the slip may be seen only on the lateral view and both sides can be affected.

**Management.** Stop weight-bearing and refer urgently to orthopaedics for surgical fixation. Delay risks further slip and avascular necrosis of the femoral head.
:::
:::

## Chronic illness and transition

::: definition Transition and adolescent services {#def-systems}
**Transition** is the planned move of a young person with a long-term condition from child-centred to adult-oriented health care. NICE NG43 (2016) recommends starting planning by age 13–14, having a named worker to coordinate it, involving the young person in decisions, and arranging that adult services meet the young person before transfer.

**Adherence** is the extent to which a person's behaviour, such as taking medicine, matches agreed recommendations. It commonly falls in adolescence.
:::

Many chronic conditions become harder to manage in adolescence. Growth, puberty and changing routines alter the disease and its treatment: insulin requirements rise in puberty, for example. Young people want to be like their peers, may resent their condition, and are learning to manage it themselves. Poor adherence is common and is better understood than blamed.

Transfer to adult services is a time when young people often drop out of care, and outcomes can worsen. Good transition involves building self-management skills in stages, seeing the young person alone, giving them responsibility for appointments and prescriptions, and joint clinics with adult teams. Structured programmes such as "Ready Steady Go" help to track this.

::: warning Do not exclude young people from their own care
It is easy to direct questions to a parent and to discuss a young person over their head. Doing so undermines their developing autonomy and makes them less likely to disclose important information. Address the young person first, see them alone for part of every consultation from early adolescence, and explain confidentiality. Parents remain important partners, and the aim is to bring both together, not to replace one with the other.
:::

::: quiz
A 15-year-old asks for contraception and does not want her parents told. She understands the advice, is in a relationship with a 16-year-old, and intends to continue having sex. There are no signs of exploitation. What is the correct approach?
- [ ] Refuse, because she is under the age of consent.
- [ ] Prescribe only after informing her parents.
- [x] Assess her using the Fraser guidelines and, if they are met, provide contraception confidentially.
- [ ] Refer to children's social care because sexual activity under 16 is illegal.
::: solution
The Fraser guidelines allow contraception to be provided to a competent young person under 16 without parental involvement when they cannot be persuaded to involve their parents, are likely to have sex anyway, and their health would otherwise suffer. A safeguarding assessment is part of the consultation, but consensual activity between young people of similar age, without signs of exploitation, does not by itself require a referral.
:::
:::

::: history
In 1985 the House of Lords ruled in *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech Area Health Authority*. Victoria Gillick, a mother of ten, had challenged government guidance that allowed doctors to give contraceptive advice to girls under 16 without parental consent. Lord Scarman set out the test of competence that now bears the Gillick name, and Lord Fraser set out the specific guidelines for contraception. The Family Law Reform Act 1969 had already given 16- and 17-year-olds the right to consent to medical treatment. The idea that adolescents need their own services developed through the second half of the twentieth century, and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, adopted in 1989, established the right of children to express their views on matters affecting them.
:::

::: solution
(a) Presumed to have capacity and able to consent under section 8 of the Family Law Reform Act 1969. (b) Gillick competence. (c) The Fraser guidelines, together with an assessment of competence and of safeguarding.

List what each letter of HEEADSSS stands for and explain why the order matters.

Home; Education and employment; Eating; Activities; Drugs; Sexuality; Suicide and mood; Safety. The order moves from less sensitive to more sensitive topics, giving time to build rapport before asking about drugs, sex and self-harm.

A 15-year-old weighs 38 kg and is 1.58 m tall. The median BMI for her age and sex is taken as 19.8 kg/m². Calculate her %mBMI to one decimal place.
::: solution
$\text{BMI} = 38/1.58^2 = 38/2.4964 = 15.22$ kg/m². $\%\text{mBMI} = 15.22/19.8 \times 100 = $ **76.9%**.

::: exercise Refeeding syndrome {level=2}
Explain why phosphate falls when a starved young person starts to eat, and what the consequences can be.
::: solution
During starvation the body uses fat and protein, insulin is low, and intracellular phosphate, potassium and magnesium are depleted, although blood levels may be normal. When carbohydrate is eaten, insulin rises and drives glucose, phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells, where phosphate is needed to make ATP and other phosphorylated intermediates. Blood levels then fall. Severe hypophosphataemia can cause muscle weakness, respiratory failure, heart failure, arrhythmias, seizures and death. Prevention is careful refeeding with monitoring of electrolytes and supplementation when needed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Emergency contraception {level=2}
A 17-year-old had unprotected sex 4 days (96 hours) ago. Which emergency contraception options remain, and which is most effective?
::: solution
Levonorgestrel is licensed only up to 72 hours, so it is no longer appropriate. Ulipristal acetate (up to 120 hours) and the **copper intrauterine device** (up to 5 days after unprotected sex, or up to 5 days after the earliest expected ovulation) remain. The copper IUD is the most effective and also provides ongoing contraception. Offer testing for sexually transmitted infections and a discussion of ongoing contraception and, if relevant, consent and safety.
:::
:::

::: exercise Low mood or something else? {level=2}
A 16-year-old has been tired, low and withdrawn for 3 months, and has lost 4 kg. Before diagnosing depression, what physical causes and other problems should be considered?
::: solution
Physical: hypothyroidism, anaemia (including iron deficiency from heavy periods), coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, type 1 diabetes, infectious mononucleosis and other post-viral fatigue, and, rarely, malignancy. Weight loss also raises the possibility of an eating disorder. Other problems: substance use, sleep problems (including late sleep timing and heavy screen use), bullying, abuse or exploitation, and pregnancy. A HEEADSSS history, examination and simple blood tests help.
:::
:::

::: exercise Plan a transition clinic {level=3}
A hospital asks you to improve the transition of young people with type 1 diabetes, many of whom stop attending clinic after transfer. Design a plan.
::: solution
**Start early.** Begin preparing at 12–13 years, following NICE NG43, with a named worker and a structured programme (for example Ready Steady Go) to track skills: understanding the condition, adjusting insulin, ordering prescriptions, booking appointments, managing alcohol, driving, sex and contraception.

**Change the consultation.** See young people alone for part of each appointment, use HEEADSSS, and screen for disordered eating (including insulin omission to lose weight) and low mood.

**Bridge the services.** Joint young adult clinics with paediatric and adult teams, flexible times (evenings, after school or college), text or app reminders, and a named contact in the adult service.

**Follow-up.** Track young people who miss appointments and contact them rather than discharging them. Monitor outcomes such as attendance, HbA1c, admissions for diabetic ketoacidosis and patient experience, and involve young people in designing the service.
:::
:::

::: exercise A safeguarding question in sexual health {level=3}
A 14-year-old girl attends a sexual health clinic asking for emergency contraception. She says her boyfriend is 22, buys her gifts and takes her to parties where she is given alcohol. She asks that nobody is told. Discuss the clinical and safeguarding response.
::: solution
**Clinical care.** Provide emergency contraception, test for sexually transmitted infections and pregnancy, and discuss ongoing contraception. She may be competent to consent to these.

**Safeguarding.** A much older partner, gifts, alcohol and parties are indicators of **child sexual exploitation**. Sexual activity between an adult and a 14-year-old is a criminal offence. Her safety outweighs her request for confidentiality, so information must be shared with children's social care (and the police, through the agreed local process) even without her agreement.

**How.** Explain honestly to her why you must share the information and what will happen next, involve her as far as possible, and do not promise secrecy you cannot keep. Make a careful, factual record. Follow the clinic's safeguarding procedure and seek advice from the named safeguarding lead. Keep the door open for future care: young people who are being exploited often do not see themselves as victims, and a trusting relationship with a service can be protective.
:::
:::
