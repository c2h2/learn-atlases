Vaccines are the most successful medical intervention in human history and the most misunderstood. **Immunisation combines basic immunology - presenting antigens safely and generating memory - with population science: schedules, cold chains, coverage thresholds, adverse-event surveillance and the communication that decides whether hard-won herd immunity survives a rumour. The clinical job is smaller and larger than it looks: give the right doses at the right ages, know when to modify for immune deficiency and pregnancy, manage the faint and the anaphylactic competently, and answer worried parents with exact numbers delivered kindly.**

::: summary
- Vaccine platforms dictate rules: live-attenuated vaccines are restricted in pregnancy and profound immunosuppression; conjugates exist because infants cannot answer pure polysaccharides.
- Schedules encode immunology - maternal antibody interference, minimum intervals, adolescent boosters and maternity vaccination as transplacental neonatal prophylaxis.
- Most acute 'reactions' are syncope or stress responses; manage them as what they are, and have adrenaline ready for the genuine rarity.
- Programme success is infrastructure: cold chain, registers, recall, equity-mapped coverage, outbreak drills and honest pharmacovigilance.
- Answer parents with exact comparative risks, keep the door open for delayers, and document offers and refusals without shame.
- Immunodeficient children prove why coverage is a collective duty: they are protected only by everyone else's doses.

## Taking it further

Three habits complete the immunisation curriculum beyond the schedule itself. First, learn the exception tables rather than only the routine ones: the severely immunosuppressed child (live vaccines withheld, household contacts vaccinated), the asplenic and complement-deficient patient (meningococcal and pneumococcal additions with boosters), the HIV-exposed infant, the preterm baby whose doses are given by chronological age at full size, and the travel-and-outbreak scenarios with their ring-dose arithmetic. Most clinical errors in this field are exception failures, not schedule failures. Second, rehearse the hesitancy conversation until it is fluent: ask what the family has heard and from whom, endorse the question (protecting a child is the shared motive), state your recommendation as a recommendation rather than a menu, tell the story of one harmed patient you have actually seen, and book the next visit rather than losing the family to a binary argument. The evidence from the motivational-interviewing trials is that directive empathy moves uptake and confrontation does not, and the audit of any practice's coverage usually tracks how its clinicians handle the fifteen-minute conversation better than how they handle the cold chain. Third, treat every vaccination encounter as surveillance real-time: the fever that follows is an opportunity to ask about the milestones nobody scheduled, the rash that does not match a vaccine reaction is a call to the health-protection team, and the child who missed four visits is a signal worth a safeguarding temperature-take. Immunisation is the rare specialty where excellent practice is measurable in diseases that no longer appear on your ward, which makes its discipline easy to lose; the clinicians who keep it are the ones who still ask, on a quiet Tuesday, what they would do if the unvaccinated toddler with fever walked in now.
