Birth is the largest physiological change any person goes through. Within minutes the baby must start breathing air, redirect the circulation through the lungs, and keep warm and fed without the placenta. Most babies manage this without help. The job of the clinician is to support the transition, to examine the baby carefully, and to recognise early the small number of babies who are becoming unwell, because newborn babies show illness in quiet, non-specific ways.

This chapter covers the transition at birth, the newborn examination and screening, feeding and jaundice, infection, and the care of babies born preterm. UK practice follows NICE and the national screening programmes; guidance changes, so dated statements should be checked against current versions.

## Transition and the newborn examination

::: definition The transition at birth {#def-transition}
The **transition** is the set of changes that adapt the fetus to life outside the uterus. The three most important are:

- **Lung aeration.** Fluid in the airways is absorbed and the first breaths fill the alveoli with air.
- **Circulatory change.** Pulmonary vascular resistance falls, pulmonary blood flow rises, and the fetal shunts (the foramen ovale and the ductus arteriosus) close.
- **Metabolic independence.** The baby must keep its own temperature and blood glucose without a continuous supply from the placenta.
:::

::: theorem Oxygen and pressure close the fetal shunts {#thm-shunts}
After birth the fetal shunts close because the pressures that held them open reverse and because the ductus constricts in response to oxygen. Anything that keeps pulmonary vascular resistance high, or that makes the circulation depend on the ductus, can make a baby unwell in the first days.
:::

::: proof
In the fetus the lungs are full of fluid and pulmonary vascular resistance is high, so most blood from the right ventricle passes through the ductus arteriosus into the aorta. Oxygenated blood from the placenta enters the right atrium and crosses the foramen ovale to the left side. At birth, lung expansion and the rise in oxygen tension dilate the pulmonary vessels, and pulmonary resistance falls. Clamping the cord removes the low-resistance placenta, so systemic resistance rises. Left atrial pressure now exceeds right atrial pressure, which pushes the flap of the foramen ovale shut. The ductus arteriosus constricts as oxygen rises and placental prostaglandins fall, and it usually closes functionally within a few days. If the lungs do not aerate, or the baby is hypoxic or cold, pulmonary resistance can stay high and blood keeps shunting right to left: this is persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn. And if a baby has a heart defect in which blood flow to the body or lungs depends on the ductus, the baby may look well at birth and collapse when the ductus closes, often on day 2 or 3.
:::

### The Apgar score

The **Apgar score** is a quick summary of the baby's condition at 1, 5 and, if needed, 10 minutes after birth. It scores five features from 0 to 2: heart rate, breathing effort, muscle tone, reflex response and colour. A score of 7–10 is reassuring. The score describes the baby; it does not decide resuscitation, which is guided by heart rate, breathing and tone from the first seconds.

Most babies need only drying, warming and a check of breathing and heart rate. A baby who is not breathing is given **inflation breaths** to aerate the lungs. Opening the lungs is the most important step in newborn resuscitation, because most neonatal bradycardia is caused by lack of aeration, not by heart disease. For babies who do not need resuscitation, the cord is clamped after a delay of at least a minute, which improves iron stores in term babies and reduces mortality in preterm babies.

::: example Scoring a baby at one minute {#ex-apgar}
At one minute after a vaginal birth, a baby has a heart rate of 90 per minute, a weak irregular cry, some flexion of the limbs, grimaces when the nose is suctioned, and has a pink body with blue hands and feet. Calculate the Apgar score.
::: solution
Score each feature:

- Heart rate below 100 per minute: **1** (absent is 0; 100 or more is 2).
- Breathing weak and irregular: **1** (absent 0; strong cry 2).
- Some flexion: **1** (limp 0; active movement 2).
- Grimace on stimulation: **1** (no response 0; cough, sneeze or cry 2).
- Pink body with blue extremities: **1** (blue or pale all over 0; completely pink 2).

The score is **5** out of 10. The heart rate below 100 is the key finding: it means the baby needs help to aerate the lungs, with drying, stimulation and if needed inflation breaths, without waiting for the score. Peripheral cyanosis of the hands and feet (acrocyanosis) is common and normal in the first day.
:::
:::

### The newborn and infant physical examination

Every baby in England is offered the **newborn and infant physical examination (NIPE)** within 72 hours of birth, repeated at 6–8 weeks by the GP. It is a full examination, but four screening elements are recorded nationally:

- **Eyes**: the red reflex, to detect congenital cataract and, rarely, retinoblastoma.
- **Heart**: murmurs, heart sounds, femoral pulses and colour, to detect congenital heart disease.
- **Hips**: the Barlow and Ortolani manoeuvres, to detect developmental dysplasia of the hip. Babies with risk factors (breech presentation, a family history) are offered a hip ultrasound.
- **Testes**: to detect undescended testes in boys.

Two other screens are offered to every baby. The **newborn hearing screen** uses an automated otoacoustic emission test and, if needed, an auditory brainstem response test. The **newborn blood spot** (heel prick) test is taken on day 5, ideally. In England it screens for nine conditions: sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, congenital hypothyroidism, and six inherited metabolic diseases (phenylketonuria, MCADD, maple syrup urine disease, isovaleric acidaemia, glutaric aciduria type 1 and homocystinuria). Screening for severe combined immunodeficiency has been evaluated in parts of England since 2021. All babies are also offered **vitamin K** at birth to prevent vitamin K deficiency bleeding.

::: warning A normal examination at 24 hours does not exclude heart disease
A baby with a duct-dependent heart lesion, such as coarctation of the aorta or hypoplastic left heart, can have a normal examination while the ductus is open. The baby then presents a few days later with poor feeding, breathlessness, pallor and weak or absent femoral pulses. Parents should be told what to look for, femoral pulses should always be felt, and any baby who becomes unwell after a normal check should be reassessed from scratch, not reassured by the earlier normal examination. Many units add pulse oximetry screening for this reason, although it is not part of the national programme in England.
:::

## Feeding and jaundice

::: definition Normal feeding and weight in the first weeks {#def-feeding}
Most babies lose weight after birth as they pass urine and meconium and before milk supply is established. A loss of up to about **10% of birth weight** in the first week is common. Most babies regain their birth weight by **2 weeks**. A loss of more than 10%, or a baby who has not regained birth weight by 3 weeks, needs a feeding assessment.

**Breastfeeding** is recommended exclusively for about the first 6 months. Formula-fed and tube-fed babies are given volumes that rise over the first days, commonly to about 150 mL/kg/day by the end of the first week, following local guidance.
:::

::: example A breastfed baby who has lost too much weight {#ex-weightloss}
Priya was born at term weighing 3.40 kg. She is exclusively breastfed. On day 4 she weighs 3.02 kg. She is sleepy, has had two wet nappies in the last 24 hours, and is yellow to the abdomen. What percentage of her birth weight has she lost, and what should happen next?
::: solution
**Calculation.** The loss is $3.40 - 3.02 = 0.38$ kg. As a percentage of birth weight, $0.38/3.40 \times 100 = 11.2\%$.

**Interpretation.** A loss of more than 10% is outside the normal range. Together with few wet nappies (by day 4 there should be about six) and sleepiness, it suggests she is not getting enough milk. Dehydration in breastfed babies causes **hypernatraemia**, which can be dangerous, and poor intake makes jaundice worse because less bilirubin is excreted in stool.

**Next steps.** Check serum sodium and bilirubin. Observe a breastfeed, with attention to positioning and attachment, and assess the mother's milk supply. A feeding plan may include more frequent feeds, expressing, and supplementation with expressed milk or formula. Weigh again within 24–48 hours. Support for the mother to keep breastfeeding is part of the treatment, not an optional extra.
:::
:::

### Neonatal jaundice

About 60% of term babies and 80% of preterm babies become visibly jaundiced in the first week. Most of this is **physiological jaundice**: the newborn has a high red cell mass, red cells with a short life span, and a liver whose conjugating enzyme (UGT1A1) is still immature. Bilirubin rises from day 2, peaks around day 3–5 and fades within two weeks. Breastfed babies are more often jaundiced and for longer.

Unconjugated bilirubin can cross the blood–brain barrier. At very high levels it causes **acute bilirubin encephalopathy** and permanent damage called **kernicterus**: athetoid cerebral palsy, hearing loss and gaze palsy. Treatment aims to prevent this.

The key rules from NICE guideline NG98 are:

- **Jaundice in the first 24 hours is always pathological.** Bilirubin must be measured urgently, within 2 hours. The commonest causes are haemolysis from blood group incompatibility (rhesus or ABO), G6PD deficiency and infection.
- **Do not rely on visual inspection** to estimate the level, especially in babies with darker skin, in whom jaundice is harder to see. Look at the sclerae, gums and blanched skin, and measure bilirubin with a transcutaneous meter (in babies of 35 weeks or more, older than 24 hours) or a serum sample.
- Plot the result on the NICE **treatment threshold graph** for the baby's gestation. The graph gives the bilirubin level for **phototherapy** and, at higher levels, for **exchange transfusion**, both of which rise with the baby's age in hours.
- **Prolonged jaundice** is jaundice beyond 14 days in term babies or 21 days in preterm babies. These babies need a measurement of **conjugated bilirubin**. A conjugated level above 25 µmol/L suggests liver disease and needs urgent referral.

Bilirubin in the UK is reported in µmol/L. To convert to mg/dL, divide by 17.1.

**Phototherapy** works because blue-green light converts bilirubin in the skin into water-soluble isomers that can be excreted without conjugation. Exchange transfusion replaces the baby's blood with donor blood and is reserved for very high levels or signs of encephalopathy.

::: example The pale stools {#ex-palestools}
Tomasz is 4 weeks old and still jaundiced. He is breastfed and gaining weight well. His mother says his stools have become pale cream in colour and his urine is dark yellow. His total bilirubin is 190 µmol/L with a conjugated fraction of 110 µmol/L.
::: solution
**Step 1.** This is prolonged jaundice, so the first question is whether it is conjugated or unconjugated. Here the conjugated bilirubin is 110 µmol/L, far above 25 µmol/L. This is **conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia**, which is never physiological and never due to breast milk.

**Step 2.** Pale stools and dark urine show that bilirubin is not reaching the gut and is being excreted in the urine instead. This is cholestasis.

**Step 3.** The most important cause is **biliary atresia**, a progressive obliteration of the bile ducts. Other causes include alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, infection, Alagille syndrome and metabolic disease.

**Step 4.** Urgent referral to a specialist liver unit for ultrasound and further tests. Biliary atresia is treated by surgery (the Kasai portoenterostomy), and the results are much better when it is done early, ideally in the first 6–8 weeks of life. Give vitamin K, because cholestasis impairs its absorption and these babies can bleed. Stool colour charts help parents recognise pale stools.
:::
:::

## Infection and other early problems

### Early-onset neonatal infection

Infection in a newborn baby can progress to sepsis within hours. Early-onset infection, within 72 hours of birth, is usually acquired from the mother's genital tract; **group B streptococcus (GBS)** is the commonest cause in the UK, and *Escherichia coli* is next. Late-onset infection is more often acquired from the environment.

The signs are non-specific: altered behaviour or tone, poor feeding, temperature instability, breathing difficulty or apnoea, jaundice in the first 24 hours, or a seizure. NICE guideline NG195 (2021) lists **risk factors** (for example maternal GBS colonisation, preterm prelabour rupture of membranes, maternal fever in labour, and suspected infection in a twin) and **clinical indicators**, with some marked as **red flags**, such as respiratory distress starting more than 4 hours after birth, a seizure, signs of shock, or a confirmed invasive infection in the mother.

The principles of management are:

- Babies with a red flag, or with two or more risk factors or clinical indicators, should have a blood culture and start antibiotics within 1 hour. The first-line combination in NG195 is intravenous **benzylpenicillin with gentamicin**; doses come from the current formulary.
- Babies at lower risk are observed closely. NG195 also allows the use of a validated **sepsis risk calculator** for babies born at 34 weeks or later, as part of a structured programme.
- C-reactive protein is measured at the start and again 18–24 hours later. If the blood culture is negative at 36 hours, the baby is well and the CRP results are reassuring, antibiotics can usually be stopped. Stopping unnecessary antibiotics matters, because they disturb the baby's gut flora and promote resistance.

::: example A sleepy baby on day 2 {#ex-sepsis}
Ade was born at 39 weeks, 20 hours after his mother's membranes ruptured. His mother had a temperature of 38.4 °C in labour. On day 2 the midwife notices that he is reluctant to feed, his temperature is 35.9 °C and his breathing rate is 70 per minute with grunting.
::: solution
**Risk factors:** maternal fever in labour, and prolonged rupture of membranes (more than 18 hours).

**Clinical indicators:** poor feeding, low temperature, and respiratory distress beginning well after birth. Respiratory distress starting more than 4 hours after birth is a red flag.

**Interpretation.** Ade has a red flag, so he must be treated as having early-onset sepsis until proved otherwise. GBS is the most likely organism.

**Action.** Escalate to the neonatal team. Take a blood culture, then give intravenous benzylpenicillin and gentamicin within 1 hour, without waiting for results. Measure CRP and blood glucose, and give respiratory support as needed. Consider a lumbar puncture if the culture is positive, if the baby is very unwell, or if there are neurological signs. Review the plan at 36 hours with the culture and CRP results.

A low temperature is as significant as a high one in a newborn baby.
:::
:::

### Hypoglycaemia

Healthy term babies have lower blood glucose in the first days than older children, and use ketones as an alternative fuel. Babies at risk of significant hypoglycaemia are those with **impaired metabolic adaptation**: babies of mothers with diabetes or taking beta-blockers, growth-restricted babies, preterm babies, and babies who are cold or unwell. These babies have glucose checked before the second feed. UK practice follows the British Association of Perinatal Medicine framework (2017), which uses an operational threshold of 2.0 mmol/L in at-risk term babies in the first 48 hours, with lower tolerance for babies with abnormal signs. Treatment is feeding first, then buccal glucose gel or intravenous dextrose depending on the level and the baby's condition.

::: example The big baby who is jittery {#ex-idm}
Grace's mother has type 1 diabetes, with poor control in late pregnancy. Grace was born at 38 weeks weighing 4.6 kg. At 3 hours she is jittery and her blood glucose is 1.4 mmol/L.
::: solution
**Mechanism.** High maternal glucose crosses the placenta, so the fetus makes extra insulin. Insulin is a growth hormone in the fetus, which is why Grace is large (macrosomia). After the cord is cut, the glucose supply stops but her insulin level stays high, so her glucose falls quickly.

**Assessment.** Jitteriness is a clinical sign that may reflect hypoglycaemia, and her glucose of 1.4 mmol/L is well below the operational threshold. Babies of diabetic mothers are also at risk of hypocalcaemia, polycythaemia, jaundice, respiratory distress and congenital heart disease.

**Management.** Admit to the neonatal unit for intravenous glucose, because she is symptomatic with a very low level. Continue feeding. Monitor glucose until it is stable, and check calcium. Persistent or recurrent hypoglycaemia after the first days needs investigation for other causes.
:::
:::

## Babies born preterm

::: definition Preterm birth and birth weight {#def-preterm}
A **preterm** birth is a birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation. **Very preterm** is before 32 weeks, and **extremely preterm** is before 28 weeks.

**Low birth weight** is below 2,500 g, **very low birth weight** below 1,500 g and **extremely low birth weight** below 1,000 g. A baby can be of low birth weight because of preterm birth, because of fetal growth restriction, or both.
:::

About 7–8% of births in England and Wales are preterm. The risk of death and long-term disability rises steeply with lower gestation. In the UK, more than half of babies born at 24 weeks who receive intensive care now survive, but a substantial proportion of survivors have a disability.

Several interventions before and at birth improve outcomes. **Antenatal corticosteroids** given to the mother accelerate lung maturation and reduce respiratory distress, brain haemorrhage and death. **Magnesium sulfate** given before very preterm birth reduces the risk of cerebral palsy. **Delayed cord clamping**, **keeping the baby warm** (a plastic bag or wrap for very preterm babies) and birth in a centre with a neonatal intensive care unit all help.

The main problems of prematurity follow from the immaturity of each organ:

| System | Problem | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Lungs | Respiratory distress syndrome | Surfactant deficiency; treated with CPAP and surfactant |
| Lungs | Bronchopulmonary dysplasia | Need for oxygen at 36 weeks corrected age |
| Breathing control | Apnoea of prematurity | Treated with caffeine |
| Brain | Intraventricular haemorrhage, white matter injury | Detected by cranial ultrasound; risk of cerebral palsy |
| Gut | Necrotising enterocolitis | Abdominal distension, bloody stools; breast milk is protective |
| Eyes | Retinopathy of prematurity | Screened in babies born before 31 weeks or under 1,501 g |
| Heart | Patent ductus arteriosus | May cause heart failure or worsen lung disease |
| Immunity | Late-onset sepsis | Often linked to central lines |
| Temperature | Hypothermia | Thin skin, little fat, large surface area |

**Retinopathy of prematurity** is an abnormal growth of retinal blood vessels. It is caused by immaturity and is made worse by excessive oxygen. Screening by an ophthalmologist allows laser or anti-VEGF treatment before the retina detaches. This is why oxygen saturation targets in preterm babies are set carefully, avoiding both too much and too little oxygen.

Babies born preterm need follow-up after discharge. Their growth and development are assessed at corrected age (see [[paediatrics/growth-development]]), and they are at higher risk of cerebral palsy, learning difficulties, ADHD and autism. They receive their routine vaccines at the usual chronological age, not at corrected age.

::: example Choosing what to say before an extremely preterm birth {#ex-extreme}
A woman is in preterm labour at 23 weeks and 4 days. The neonatal consultant meets the parents before the birth. What does the consultant need to discuss?
::: solution
This conversation follows the British Association of Perinatal Medicine framework (2019) for extremely preterm birth, which recommends that decisions are based on an assessment of the baby's overall risk, not on gestation alone, and are made together with the parents.

**Information.** The consultant explains the likely chances of survival and of severe disability for a baby of this gestation, adjusted for factors that change the risk: estimated weight, whether steroids have been given, sex, single or multiple pregnancy, and the place of birth. Numbers are given as plainly as possible, with honesty about uncertainty.

**Options.** At 22–23 weeks, both active stabilisation and comfort-focused care can be reasonable. The parents' values are central. If active care is chosen, steroids and magnesium sulfate are given and the birth should take place in a centre with a neonatal intensive care unit.

**Plans.** The discussion covers what will happen at birth, that decisions can be revisited as the baby's condition becomes clear, and what palliative care looks like if it is needed. The conversation is documented, and the parents are offered time, written information and further discussion.
:::
:::

::: history
Care for premature babies began with warmth. In 1880 the Paris obstetrician Stéphane Tarnier introduced an incubator adapted from those used for hatching chickens, and his colleague Pierre Budin showed that warmth, careful feeding and hygiene improved survival. In 1952 the American anaesthetist Virginia Apgar proposed her score for assessing the newborn, published the following year. In 1959 Mary Ellen Avery and Jere Mead showed that the lungs of babies who died of respiratory distress syndrome lacked surfactant. Graham Liggins and Ross Howie reported in 1972 that antenatal corticosteroids reduced respiratory distress in preterm babies, and in 1980 Tetsuro Fujiwara and colleagues reported successful treatment with artificial surfactant. Phototherapy for jaundice also has a simple origin: in the 1950s, nurses at Rochford Hospital in Essex noticed that babies placed in sunlight became less yellow, and Richard Cremer and colleagues published their trial of light treatment in 1958.
:::

- Aerating the lungs is the most important step in newborn resuscitation. The Apgar score describes the baby but does not guide resuscitation.

- Preterm birth affects every organ. Antenatal steroids, magnesium sulfate, delayed cord clamping and warmth improve outcomes, and survivors need developmental follow-up.

::: exercise Stopping antibiotics {level=2}
A term baby was started on benzylpenicillin and gentamicin at 2 hours of age because the mother had a fever in labour. At 36 hours the baby is feeding well with normal observations, the blood culture shows no growth, and the CRP was 4 mg/L at the start and 6 mg/L at 20 hours. What should happen, and why does it matter?
::: solution
The baby is well, the culture is negative at 36 hours and both CRP results are low, so antibiotics should be **stopped**, following NG195, and the baby can usually go home with advice to the parents about warning signs. Stopping matters because unnecessary antibiotics disturb the developing gut microbiota, promote antibiotic resistance, keep mother and baby in hospital, and expose the baby to the risks of cannulas and gentamicin.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why light treats jaundice {level=2}
Explain why phototherapy lowers bilirubin in a newborn without needing the liver to conjugate it, and why it is used for unconjugated but not conjugated jaundice.
::: solution
Unconjugated bilirubin is fat-soluble and cannot be excreted in bile or urine until the liver conjugates it. Blue-green light absorbed by bilirubin in the skin converts it into isomers (configurational isomers and lumirubin) that are more water-soluble and can be excreted in bile and urine without conjugation. Phototherapy treats the risk of kernicterus, which comes from unconjugated bilirubin crossing the blood–brain barrier. In conjugated jaundice the problem is cholestasis: the bilirubin is already conjugated and is not neurotoxic in the same way, and the task is to find and treat the liver or bile duct disease.
:::
:::

::: exercise The baby who collapses on day 3 {level=3}
A term baby had a normal NIPE at 18 hours and went home. On day 3 she is brought to the emergency department grey, breathless and feeding poorly. Her liver is enlarged and her femoral pulses cannot be felt. Discuss the most likely diagnosis, the immediate treatment, and how the earlier normal examination can be explained.
::: hint
Think about what changes in the circulation over the first days of life.
:::
::: solution
**Likely diagnosis.** Weak femoral pulses with shock and heart failure on day 3 strongly suggest a **duct-dependent systemic circulation**, most often **coarctation of the aorta** or a critical aortic stenosis or hypoplastic left heart. The differential includes sepsis and an inborn error of metabolism, which must be treated in parallel.

**Why the examination was normal.** While the ductus arteriosus was open, blood from the pulmonary artery reached the descending aorta through it, so the femoral pulses were present. As the duct closed, flow to the lower body fell.

**Immediate treatment.** Resuscitate (airway, breathing, circulation), and start an infusion of **prostaglandin E** (alprostadil or dinoprostone) to reopen the duct, under specialist advice; apnoea is a side effect, so be ready to support breathing. Give antibiotics after a blood culture, check glucose and blood gases, and arrange urgent echocardiography and transfer to a cardiac centre.

**Lesson.** Femoral pulses must be felt at every examination, parents should know the warning signs, and a normal early check does not exclude duct-dependent disease.
:::
:::

::: exercise Plan for a 26-week baby {level=3}
A baby born at 26 weeks weighing 850 g is now 4 weeks old. List the problems of prematurity the team will be watching for over the coming weeks, and the screening the baby needs before and after discharge.
::: solution
**During the admission.** Respiratory distress syndrome and the risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (oxygen need at 36 weeks corrected age); apnoea of prematurity (treated with caffeine); patent ductus arteriosus; necrotising enterocolitis as feeds are increased (breast milk is protective); late-onset sepsis, especially with central lines; intraventricular haemorrhage and white matter injury (cranial ultrasound); anaemia and metabolic bone disease of prematurity; nutrition and growth; and temperature control.

**Screening.** **Retinopathy of prematurity** screening, because the baby was born before 31 weeks and under 1,501 g. Hearing screening (preterm babies in intensive care have a higher risk of hearing loss). The routine blood spot test (with repeat samples as required in preterm babies). Immunisations at the normal chronological age, starting at 8 weeks, while still in hospital.

**After discharge.** Follow-up of growth and development at corrected age until 2 years, with formal developmental assessment, because of the increased risk of cerebral palsy, learning difficulty, autism and ADHD. Support for the parents, who have been through a long and stressful admission, is part of the care.
:::
