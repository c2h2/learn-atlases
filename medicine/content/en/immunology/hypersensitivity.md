Most of the time the immune system's weapons are aimed correctly: at microbes, at a level of force that clears the infection without much harm. **Hypersensitivity** is the term for immune responses that cause tissue damage out of proportion to any benefit. The target may be a harmless environmental substance (pollen, peanut, nickel), a drug, transfused blood, or self, and the damage is done by the same effector mechanisms described in the previous chapters.

In 1963 Philip Gell and Robin Coombs grouped these reactions into four types according to the immune mechanism. The classification is old and imperfect, and many real diseases mix several types. But it remains useful because each type has a characteristic timing, a characteristic set of tests and a characteristic treatment. If you can tell which mechanism is operating, you can usually predict how fast the reaction will develop, which test will confirm it, and which drug will work.

This chapter describes the four types, with most space given to type I reactions and anaphylaxis because they are common and can kill within minutes.

::: definition The Gell and Coombs classification {#def-types}
- **Type I (immediate, IgE-mediated)**: allergen cross-links IgE bound to mast cells and basophils, which release histamine and other mediators within minutes. Examples: allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, food allergy, anaphylaxis.
- **Type II (antibody-mediated cytotoxic)**: IgG or IgM binds antigens on cell surfaces or in the extracellular matrix, causing cell destruction or altered receptor function. Examples: ABO-incompatible transfusion, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, Goodpasture disease, myasthenia gravis, Graves disease.
- **Type III (immune complex)**: soluble antigen–antibody complexes deposit in vessel walls, glomeruli and joints and activate complement. Examples: serum sickness, the Arthus reaction, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, lupus nephritis.
- **Type IV (delayed-type, T-cell-mediated)**: sensitised T cells release cytokines or kill cells, with a peak 24 to 72 hours after exposure. Examples: allergic contact dermatitis, the tuberculin skin test, many drug eruptions, granulomatous inflammation.
:::

Types I to III are mediated by antibody and can be transferred between animals with serum. Type IV is mediated by T cells and can be transferred only with cells.

## Type I: immediate hypersensitivity

### Sensitisation and the effector phase

A type I reaction has two stages.

**Sensitisation.** On first exposure to an allergen, dendritic cells present it to naive CD4 T cells, which in people predisposed to allergy develop into Th2 cells. Th2 cells provide IL-4 and IL-13, which cause B cells to switch to **IgE**. The IgE binds to the high-affinity receptor **FcεRI** on mast cells and basophils, where it remains for weeks to months. The person is now sensitised but has no symptoms.

**Effector phase.** On re-exposure, allergen binds and **cross-links** IgE molecules on the mast cell surface. Cross-linking, not mere binding, is what activates the cell. Within minutes the mast cell releases three groups of mediators.

::: definition Mast-cell mediators {#def-mast}
- **Preformed mediators** stored in granules and released within seconds to minutes: **histamine**, which causes vasodilatation, increased vascular permeability, itch and bronchoconstriction; **tryptase**, a protease used as a laboratory marker of mast-cell activation; heparin; and TNF.
- **Newly synthesised lipid mediators**, made from membrane arachidonic acid within minutes: **cysteinyl leukotrienes** (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4), which are potent bronchoconstrictors; **prostaglandin D2**; and **platelet-activating factor**.
- **Cytokines and chemokines**, transcribed over hours: IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 and others, which recruit eosinophils, basophils and Th2 cells and cause the **late-phase reaction** 4 to 12 hours after exposure.
:::

The late phase explains why an asthmatic person exposed to an allergen may improve after the first hour and then deteriorate again hours later, and why allergic rhinitis becomes persistent nasal blockage after repeated exposures.

**Atopy** is the inherited tendency to make IgE responses to common environmental allergens. It underlies the "atopic march" of eczema, food allergy, asthma and allergic rhinitis. Genetic contributors include variants in the skin barrier protein filaggrin, which allow allergens to cross the skin and favour sensitisation; environmental contributors include reduced microbial exposure in early life.

Not every mast-cell reaction needs IgE. Some drugs, including opioids, vancomycin, neuromuscular blocking agents and radiocontrast media, can activate mast cells directly, for example through the receptor MRGPRX2. NSAIDs can trigger reactions in susceptible people by inhibiting cyclo-oxygenase 1 and shifting arachidonic acid metabolism towards leukotrienes. These reactions can look exactly like IgE-mediated allergy, but skin tests and specific IgE are negative.

### Anaphylaxis

::: definition Anaphylaxis {#def-anaphylaxis}
**Anaphylaxis** is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction. It is characterised by the rapid onset of **airway** (laryngeal oedema, stridor, hoarseness), **breathing** (bronchospasm, wheeze, hypoxaemia) and/or **circulation** (hypotension, collapse) problems, usually but not always with skin or mucosal changes such as urticaria, flushing or angioedema. Most cases are IgE-mediated, but non-IgE mast-cell activation produces the same clinical picture.
:::

The commonest triggers are foods (especially peanut, tree nuts, milk, egg, fish and shellfish) in children and young adults, drugs (especially antibiotics, NSAIDs and agents used in anaesthesia) in older adults, and insect venom (wasp and bee). In many adults no trigger is found. Skin features are absent in some cases, especially when hypotension is the main problem, so their absence does not exclude anaphylaxis.

::: theorem Why adrenaline is the treatment {#thm-adrenaline}
Adrenaline is the first-line drug for anaphylaxis because it counteracts every life-threatening effect of mast-cell mediators and also reduces further mediator release:

- **α1-adrenergic** effects cause vasoconstriction, raising blood pressure and reducing mucosal oedema, including laryngeal oedema;
- **β1** effects increase cardiac contractility and rate;
- **β2** effects relax bronchial smooth muscle and **inhibit further degranulation** of mast cells.

Antihistamines block only H1 receptors, act slowly, and do nothing for hypotension or bronchospasm caused by leukotrienes; corticosteroids act over hours. Neither is a substitute for adrenaline.
:::

The UK guideline is the Resuscitation Council UK's *Emergency treatment of anaphylaxis* (2021). Its principles, summarised here, are covered in practical detail in [[emergency-critical-care/anaphylaxis]]:

- Recognise anaphylaxis using an ABCDE approach and call for help.
- Give **intramuscular adrenaline** into the anterolateral thigh as soon as anaphylaxis is suspected; the dose depends on age and is taken from the guideline (for adults, 500 micrograms, that is 0.5 mL of a 1 mg/mL solution, is the standard dose). Repeat after 5 minutes if airway, breathing or circulation problems persist.
- Lay the patient flat (or sitting if breathing is difficult) and do not let them stand or walk, because sudden posture change can cause cardiac arrest.
- Give high-flow oxygen and an intravenous fluid bolus for shock.
- If problems persist after two doses of intramuscular adrenaline (**refractory anaphylaxis**), seek expert help early for an adrenaline infusion.
- Antihistamines are for skin symptoms after stabilisation; routine corticosteroids are no longer recommended in the emergency treatment.

After the emergency, a person needs a period of observation (in England and Wales, NICE guideline NG258 of 2026 adopted the Resuscitation Council's risk-stratified approach to its length), **two adrenaline auto-injectors** with training before discharge, written information, and referral to a specialist allergy service to identify the trigger.

::: example Collapse in a restaurant {#ex-anaphylaxis}
A 22-year-old man with known peanut allergy collapses in a restaurant 10 minutes after eating a curry. He has widespread urticaria, swollen lips, a hoarse voice and audible wheeze. His pulse is 130 per minute and his blood pressure is 70/40 mmHg. Explain what is happening at the cellular level and outline the immediate management.
::: solution
**Mechanism.** Peanut protein absorbed from his gut has reached the blood and cross-linked peanut-specific IgE on mast cells throughout the body and on blood basophils. Histamine causes vasodilatation and capillary leak, producing hypotension and urticaria; leakage into the larynx causes hoarseness; histamine and leukotrienes constrict the bronchi. Fluid lost into the tissues and vasodilatation together reduce venous return, so cardiac output falls.

**Immediate management.** This is anaphylaxis with airway, breathing and circulation problems. Call for help. Give intramuscular adrenaline into the anterolateral thigh immediately (500 micrograms for an adult in the Resuscitation Council UK guideline) and repeat after 5 minutes if there is no improvement. Lay him flat with legs raised if breathing allows, and do not sit him up or stand him. Give high-flow oxygen, obtain intravenous access and give a crystalloid fluid bolus. Monitor continuously. If he remains unstable after two doses, call critical-care help for an adrenaline infusion. Take timed blood samples for mast-cell tryptase without delaying treatment.

**Afterwards.** He needs a period of observation, two adrenaline auto-injectors with training, an allergy action plan, and review in a specialist allergy clinic.
:::
:::

### Measuring mast-cell activation: tryptase

Serum **tryptase** rises during anaphylaxis because it is released from mast-cell granules. It peaks about 1 to 2 hours after the onset of symptoms and falls with a half-life of about 2 hours, so it may be back to normal 6 to 8 hours later. The Resuscitation Council UK therefore recommends up to three timed samples:

1. as soon as possible after treatment has started (without delaying it);
2. ideally 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset, and no later than 4 hours;
3. a **baseline** sample at least 24 hours after full recovery, or at the allergy clinic.

The baseline matters because tryptase levels differ between people. Some people have a constitutionally raised baseline, for example from inherited extra copies of a tryptase gene (*TPSAB1*), and a raised baseline may also indicate a mast-cell disorder such as systemic mastocytosis. An international consensus proposes that an acute rise to more than **120% of baseline plus 2 µg/L** indicates clinically significant mast-cell activation:

$$
\text{threshold} = 1.2 \times \text{baseline} + 2\ \mu\text{g/L}.
$$ {#eq-tryptase}

::: example Interpreting tryptase {#ex-tryptase}
A 46-year-old woman has a hypotensive reaction 10 minutes after intravenous co-amoxiclav. Her tryptase is 15 µg/L at 90 minutes after onset. Her baseline tryptase, measured 6 weeks later, is 11 µg/L. Was there significant mast-cell activation, and what does the baseline suggest?
::: solution
Using the consensus formula, the threshold is $1.2 \times 11 + 2 = 13.2 + 2 = 15.2$ µg/L. Her acute value of 15 µg/L is just **below** this threshold, so the tryptase result does not by itself confirm mast-cell activation, even though the absolute value of 15 µg/L lies above many laboratories' reference range.

The interpretation must be clinical. A hypotensive reaction within minutes of an intravenous antibiotic is highly suggestive of anaphylaxis, and a normal or borderline tryptase does not exclude it: tryptase rises less in some reactions, especially food-induced ones, and timing affects the result. Her **baseline of 11 µg/L** is at the top of the usual reference range (often quoted as up to about 11.4 µg/L, depending on the assay). A baseline this high raises the possibility of hereditary alpha-tryptasaemia or a clonal mast-cell disorder. These conditions are associated with more severe reactions, and the allergy service would consider further investigation.
:::
:::

### Diagnosing allergy

**Sensitisation is not the same as allergy.** A positive skin-prick test or specific IgE result shows only that IgE is present. Many people with positive tests eat the food or tolerate the drug without problems. Tests must therefore be chosen to answer a question raised by the clinical history.

- **Skin-prick testing**: a drop of allergen is introduced into the epidermis; a wheal and flare within 15 minutes indicates IgE on skin mast cells. Antihistamines must be stopped beforehand.
- **Specific IgE** in serum: useful when skin testing is impossible (extensive eczema, antihistamine use). **Component-resolved diagnostics** measure IgE against individual proteins; for example, IgE to the peanut storage protein Ara h 2 predicts clinical peanut allergy much better than IgE to whole peanut extract, which often reflects cross-reactivity with pollen proteins.
- **Oral food challenge** or **drug challenge** under supervision: the reference standard, used when the history and tests leave doubt.

About one in ten people in the UK carry a label of **penicillin allergy**, and most of them, when properly tested, are found not to be allergic. The label leads to the use of broader-spectrum antibiotics, more *C. difficile* and MRSA infection and worse outcomes, so "de-labelling" by risk assessment and direct oral challenge is now part of antimicrobial stewardship.

### Treatments that change the response

**Allergen immunotherapy** gives gradually increasing then maintenance doses of allergen, by injection or under the tongue, over about 3 years. It shifts the response from Th2 to regulatory T cells producing IL-10, and induces allergen-specific **IgG4**, which competes with IgE for allergen ("blocking antibody"). It is highly effective for venom allergy and for pollen-induced rhinitis.

**Omalizumab**, a monoclonal antibody against IgE, prevents IgE from binding FcεRI and reduces FcεRI expression on mast cells. It is used in severe allergic asthma and chronic spontaneous urticaria.

## Type II: antibody against cells and matrix

::: definition Type II mechanisms {#def-type2}
In type II hypersensitivity, IgG or IgM binds antigen that is part of a cell surface or the extracellular matrix. Damage follows by one of three routes:

1. **Complement-mediated lysis** in the blood vessels (intravascular), when the antibody fixes complement efficiently, as with IgM anti-A and anti-B.
2. **Opsonisation and phagocytosis**: IgG- or C3b-coated cells are removed by macrophages, mainly in the spleen and liver (extravascular), as in warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and immune thrombocytopenia.
3. **Inflammation** at the site, when antibody binds matrix and activates complement and Fc-receptor-bearing cells, as in anti-glomerular basement membrane disease.

A subgroup of antibodies does not destroy cells but **alters receptor function**: stimulating it (thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor antibodies in Graves disease) or blocking and depleting it (acetylcholine receptor antibodies in myasthenia gravis). Some classifications call this type V.
:::

The **direct antiglobulin test** (DAT, direct Coombs test) shows whether a patient's red cells are coated with IgG or complement in the body. Antiglobulin reagent cross-links the coated cells and makes them agglutinate. It is positive in autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, haemolytic disease of the newborn and haemolytic transfusion reactions.

::: example The wrong unit of blood {#ex-transfusion}
A 74-year-old woman of blood group O is given a unit of group A red cells intended for another patient. Within 10 minutes she develops fever, rigors, pain in her back and at the infusion site, hypotension and dark red urine. Explain the mechanism and the immediate actions.
::: solution
**Mechanism.** People of group O make naturally occurring **IgM anti-A and anti-B** antibodies, probably in response to similar carbohydrate structures on gut bacteria. The transfused group A red cells are bound immediately by IgM anti-A, which activates the classical complement pathway very efficiently. The membrane attack complex lyses the red cells **inside the vessels** (intravascular haemolysis). Free haemoglobin appears in plasma and urine. C3a and C5a and the inflammatory cytokines released cause fever, rigors and hypotension, and the reaction can progress to disseminated intravascular coagulation and acute kidney injury.

**Immediate actions.** Stop the transfusion at once, keeping the line open with saline. Resuscitate (ABCDE), check the patient's identity against the unit, and inform the transfusion laboratory, which will also check whether the intended recipient of the unit is at risk of receiving the wrong blood. Send samples for repeat grouping and crossmatch, DAT, full blood count, haemolysis markers, coagulation screen, renal function and urinalysis. Maintain urine output and seek senior and haematology help. The event is reported through the hospital system and to SHOT (Serious Hazards of Transfusion), the UK haemovigilance scheme.

ABO-incompatible transfusion is almost always the result of identification errors, which is why bedside identity checks matter more than any laboratory test. See [[blood/transfusion]].
:::
:::

Other type II examples include **haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn** (maternal anti-D IgG crossing the placenta), **drug-induced immune haemolysis and thrombocytopenia**, **anti-GBM disease** (antibodies to the α3 chain of type IV collagen in glomerular and alveolar basement membranes, causing rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and lung haemorrhage), and **pemphigus vulgaris** (antibodies to desmoglein 3 separating keratinocytes). These are covered in [[immunology/autoimmunity]].

## Type III: immune-complex disease

Antibodies bind soluble antigens all the time, and the resulting immune complexes are usually removed harmlessly by complement and phagocytes in the liver and spleen. Disease occurs when complexes are formed in large amounts, are of a size that is not cleared well (small complexes formed in slight antigen excess), or when clearance fails, as in complement deficiency. The complexes deposit in sites where blood is filtered at high pressure or turbulence: **glomeruli, small vessel walls, joints and the choroid plexus**. There they activate complement and attract neutrophils through Fc receptors and C5a, causing vasculitis, glomerulonephritis and arthritis.

::: theorem Features of immune-complex disease {#thm-type3}
Immune-complex diseases share a recognisable clinical and laboratory pattern:

- the lesions are where complexes deposit: **glomerulonephritis, small-vessel vasculitis (often palpable purpura), arthritis or arthralgia**, and fever;
- **complement is consumed**, so serum C3 and C4 are often low during active disease;
- biopsy shows **granular ("lumpy-bumpy") deposits** of immunoglobulin and complement on immunofluorescence, in contrast to the smooth **linear** deposits of type II anti-GBM disease.
:::

::: proof
The pattern follows from the mechanism. Because the antigen is soluble and circulating, the complexes can lodge in any vascular bed, but they are trapped preferentially where filtration and turbulence are greatest. Once deposited, they fix complement through the classical pathway, consuming C4 and C3, and the C5a generated recruits neutrophils, whose enzymes damage the vessel wall. The deposits are discrete lumps, because complexes deposit as particles, whereas antibody binding to an antigen that is evenly distributed along a basement membrane gives a continuous line.
:::

**Serum sickness** is the classic systemic example. It was described in children given horse antitoxin serum against diphtheria in the early 1900s. About 1 to 2 weeks after a large dose of foreign protein, the person makes antibody while plenty of antigen is still circulating. Complexes form and cause fever, rash, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy and sometimes proteinuria. It resolves as the antigen is cleared. Modern causes include anti-thymocyte globulin and other animal-derived antisera such as some antivenoms. A clinically similar "serum sickness-like reaction" occurs with some drugs, especially in children.

The **Arthus reaction** is the local equivalent: injecting antigen into the skin of a person who already has high levels of IgG against it causes local complex formation in vessel walls, with painful swelling and sometimes necrosis 4 to 12 hours later. It can follow tetanus- or diphtheria-containing boosters given too frequently.

Other immune-complex diseases include **post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis**, **lupus nephritis**, **IgA vasculitis** (Henoch–Schönlein purpura), **cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis** (often associated with hepatitis C infection) and the glomerulonephritis of infective endocarditis.

## Type IV: delayed-type hypersensitivity

Type IV reactions are mediated by T cells, not antibody. Because they require antigen-specific T cells to be recruited and activated in the tissue, they take **24 to 72 hours** to develop after re-exposure.

::: definition Delayed-type hypersensitivity {#def-type4}
**Type IV hypersensitivity** is tissue damage caused by antigen-specific T cells. CD4 T cells (mainly Th1, sometimes Th2 or Th17) release cytokines that recruit and activate macrophages, eosinophils or neutrophils; CD8 T cells kill cells directly. Reactions develop over 24 to 72 hours and cannot be transferred by serum.
:::

Important examples:

- **Allergic contact dermatitis.** Small reactive chemicals (haptens) such as nickel, chromate, fragrances, preservatives, hair-dye ingredients and the poison ivy toxin urushiol penetrate the skin and bind self proteins. Langerhans cells and dermal dendritic cells carry the modified proteins to lymph nodes and sensitise T cells. On re-exposure, T cells cause an itchy eczematous reaction at the site of contact. The diagnostic test is the **patch test**: the suspected allergens are applied under occlusion for 48 hours and read at about 48 and 96 hours.
- **The tuberculin skin test.** Purified protein derivative injected into the skin of a person with prior mycobacterial exposure or BCG produces induration that peaks at 48 to 72 hours.
- **Granulomatous inflammation**, as in tuberculosis, leprosy, sarcoidosis and Crohn's disease, is a chronic Th1-driven reaction (see [[immunology/adaptive-responses]]).
- **Delayed drug reactions**, from a mild maculopapular rash to severe reactions such as drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) and Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis. In SJS/TEN, cytotoxic CD8 T cells and NK cells kill keratinocytes. Some are strongly associated with HLA alleles (see [[immunology/antigen-recognition]]).
- **Coeliac disease** has a central T-cell component, with gluten-specific CD4 T cells driving damage, although it also has autoantibodies.

::: example A hairdresser's hands {#ex-contact}
A 27-year-old hairdresser has had itchy, scaly, fissured dermatitis on the backs of her hands and between her fingers for 6 months. It improves on holiday and flares within 1 to 2 days of returning to work. Skin-prick tests to common aeroallergens are negative. Explain the likely mechanism, why the skin-prick test does not help, and how the cause would be identified.
::: solution
**Mechanism.** The pattern suggests **allergic contact dermatitis**, a type IV reaction. A small reactive chemical used at work, most likely *para*-phenylenediamine or a related hair-dye ingredient, or a preservative or fragrance in shampoo, acts as a hapten: it penetrates damaged skin, binds self proteins and is carried by Langerhans cells to lymph nodes, sensitising T cells. Each re-exposure brings sensitised T cells back to the skin, where their cytokines cause eczematous inflammation over 24 to 72 hours, matching the delay after returning to work. Wet work and detergents (irritant contact dermatitis) often coexist and help haptens enter the skin.

**Why skin-prick testing is unhelpful.** Skin-prick tests detect IgE on mast cells, a type I mechanism with a reaction in 15 minutes. They say nothing about T-cell sensitisation.

**Identifying the cause.** **Patch testing**: a standard series plus a hairdressing series is applied to the back under occlusion for 48 hours and read at about day 2 and day 4. A positive result is an eczematous reaction at the site of the relevant allergen. Management is avoidance (gloves suitable for the chemical, changes to work practice, sometimes a change of role), emollients and topical corticosteroids; occupational dermatitis also has employment and reporting implications.
:::
:::

:::warning
The time from exposure to reaction is one of the most useful clues to mechanism, and it is often misused. A rash that develops 7 days into a course of amoxicillin is not an immediate (IgE) reaction; it is usually a delayed T-cell reaction, and the question of later anaphylaxis is quite different. A child given amoxicillin during infectious mononucleosis (EBV) often develops a widespread rash that does not mean lifelong penicillin allergy. Record the timing, the type of rash and the other features precisely, rather than writing "allergic to penicillin".
:::

## Bringing the types together

| | Type I | Type II | Type III | Type IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mediator | IgE, mast cells | IgG or IgM against cell or matrix | IgG–antigen complexes, complement | T cells |
| Timing after exposure | Minutes (late phase 4–12 h) | Minutes to days | Hours (Arthus) to 1–2 weeks (serum sickness) | 24–72 hours |
| Prototype | Anaphylaxis | ABO transfusion reaction | Serum sickness | Contact dermatitis |
| Key test | Skin-prick test, specific IgE, tryptase | Direct antiglobulin test, specific autoantibody | C3/C4, biopsy immunofluorescence | Patch test |

Transfusion reactions illustrate several types at once. An **acute haemolytic** reaction to ABO-incompatible blood is type II. A **delayed haemolytic** reaction 5 to 10 days after transfusion is a secondary (anamnestic) antibody response to a minor red-cell antigen to which the recipient was previously sensitised. An **allergic** reaction with urticaria is type I. Rare severe anaphylactic reactions occur in people with IgA deficiency who have anti-IgA antibodies. **Transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease**, in which donor T cells attack an immunocompromised recipient, is a type IV mechanism and is prevented by irradiating blood components for at-risk patients.

::: quiz
A 30-year-old woman develops fever, an urticarial rash, arthralgia and mild proteinuria 10 days after starting treatment with rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin. Her C3 and C4 are low. Which type of hypersensitivity is most likely?
- [ ] Type I
- [ ] Type II
- [x] Type III
- [ ] Type IV
::: solution
Fever, rash, joint symptoms and proteinuria 1 to 2 weeks after a large dose of a foreign protein, with complement consumption, is **serum sickness**, the prototype type III immune-complex disease. The delay reflects the time needed to make antibody against the rabbit protein while plenty of it is still circulating. The urticaria does not make this a type I reaction; urticarial rashes are common in serum sickness.
:::
:::

::: history
Charles Richet and Paul Portier tried in 1902 to immunise dogs against sea-anemone toxin. A second, small dose given weeks later killed the dogs within minutes. Richet called the phenomenon *anaphylaxis* ("against protection") and received the Nobel Prize in 1913. In 1906 the paediatrician Clemens von Pirquet coined the word *allergy* for altered reactivity and, with Béla Schick, described serum sickness in children treated with horse antiserum. Maurice Arthus described the local reaction that bears his name in 1903.

The cause of immediate allergy remained mysterious until 1966, when Kimishige and Teruko Ishizaka identified a new class of antibody, IgE, as the "reagin" responsible; the Swedish group of Gunnar Johansson and Hans Bennich independently found the same protein in a myeloma. Philip Gell and Robin Coombs, who had developed the antiglobulin test with Arthur Mourant and Rob Race in 1945, published their four-type classification in 1963.
:::

## Where this leads

Type II and type III mechanisms directed against self underlie the autoimmune diseases in [[immunology/autoimmunity]]. The emergency management of anaphylaxis is covered in [[emergency-critical-care/anaphylaxis]], drug allergy in [[pharmacology/adverse-reactions]], and the asthma that is often its companion in [[respiratory/asthma-copd]].

::: summary
- Hypersensitivity is immune-mediated tissue damage. Gell and Coombs classified it into four types by mechanism: I (IgE, mast cells), II (antibody against cells or matrix), III (immune complexes) and IV (T cells).
- Type I needs prior sensitisation (Th2, IgE on FcεRI). Cross-linking of IgE releases histamine and tryptase, then leukotrienes and prostaglandins, then cytokines causing a late phase.
- Anaphylaxis is a rapid, life-threatening airway, breathing or circulation problem. Intramuscular adrenaline is the first-line treatment, repeated after 5 minutes if needed; antihistamines and steroids are not substitutes.
- Tryptase peaks at 1 to 2 hours and has a half-life of about 2 hours; timed samples and a baseline are needed, and a rise above $1.2 \times \text{baseline} + 2$ µg/L indicates significant mast-cell activation.
- Sensitisation is not allergy. Tests answer questions raised by the history; challenge is the reference standard. Most penicillin allergy labels are wrong.
- Type II reactions cause intravascular lysis, splenic clearance or receptor stimulation or blockade; the direct antiglobulin test detects antibody on red cells.
- Type III reactions deposit immune complexes in glomeruli, vessels and joints, consume complement and show granular immunofluorescence; serum sickness appears 1 to 2 weeks after foreign protein.
- Type IV reactions are T-cell-mediated and peak at 24 to 72 hours: contact dermatitis (patch test), tuberculin reactions, granulomas and delayed drug eruptions.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Tryptase threshold {level=1 check="1.2*6.5 + 2"}
A patient's baseline tryptase is 6.5 µg/L. Using the consensus formula, above what acute tryptase value (in µg/L) would a reaction be considered to show significant mast-cell activation?
::: solution
$1.2 \times 6.5 + 2 = 7.8 + 2 = 9.8$ µg/L. An acute value above 9.8 µg/L meets the criterion, even though many laboratories' upper reference limit is about 11 µg/L. This is why a baseline sample is useful: a value within the "normal range" can still be a significant rise for that individual.
:::
:::

::: exercise Classify the reaction {level=1}
Name the Gell and Coombs type for each: (a) itchy eczema under a watch strap that developed 2 days after wearing a new watch; (b) wheeze and urticaria 5 minutes after a wasp sting; (c) haemolytic anaemia with a positive DAT after starting a drug; (d) glomerulonephritis with granular IgG and C3 deposits 2 weeks after a streptococcal sore throat.
::: solution
(a) Type IV (nickel contact dermatitis). (b) Type I. (c) Type II. (d) Type III (post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis).
:::
:::

::: exercise Tryptase half-life {level=1 check="40*0.5^3"}
A tryptase is 40 µg/L at its peak. Assuming the excess above baseline falls with a half-life of 2 hours and the baseline is negligible, what would the tryptase be 6 hours after the peak, in µg/L?
::: solution
6 hours is 3 half-lives, so $40 \times (1/2)^3 = 40/8 = 5$ µg/L. A sample taken this late could easily be within the normal range, which is why the second sample should be taken ideally 1 to 2 hours after the onset of symptoms and no later than 4 hours.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why antihistamines fail in anaphylaxis {level=2}
A junior doctor gives intravenous chlorphenamine as the first drug to a patient with anaphylaxis and hypotension. Explain why this is inadequate, with reference to the mediators involved.
::: solution
Antihistamines block only histamine H1 receptors. Hypotension in anaphylaxis is caused not only by histamine but by platelet-activating factor, leukotrienes, prostaglandins and other mediators that cause vasodilatation and capillary leak; bronchospasm is driven largely by cysteinyl leukotrienes. Antihistamines also take time to work and do not stop further mast-cell degranulation. Intramuscular adrenaline is needed because its α1 effects reverse vasodilatation and mucosal oedema, its β1 effects support the heart, and its β2 effects relax bronchial smooth muscle and inhibit further mediator release. Delay in giving adrenaline is associated with fatal outcomes. Antihistamines may be used after stabilisation for skin symptoms.
:::

::: exercise Linear or granular? {level=2}
A 24-year-old man who smokes presents with haemoptysis and rapidly rising creatinine. A kidney biopsy shows crescentic glomerulonephritis with smooth linear IgG along the glomerular basement membrane. Explain the mechanism, why the lungs are involved, and how this differs from lupus nephritis.
::: solution
Linear IgG along the basement membrane indicates **anti-GBM (Goodpasture) disease**, a type II reaction. Autoantibodies bind the non-collagenous domain of the α3 chain of type IV collagen, which is evenly distributed along the glomerular basement membrane, producing a continuous line on immunofluorescence. The same antigen is present in the alveolar basement membrane; it is normally hidden, but damage from smoking or infection exposes it, causing pulmonary haemorrhage. Lupus nephritis is a type III disease: immune complexes deposit as discrete particles, giving granular deposits of IgG, IgA, IgM, C3 and C1q, with low complement. Anti-GBM disease is a medical emergency, treated with plasma exchange to remove antibody plus immunosuppression.
:::

::: exercise A late wheeze {level=2}
A 9-year-old boy with asthma and cat allergy visits a friend with two cats. He has sneezing and mild wheeze within 20 minutes, which settles with his reliever inhaler. He is fine by bedtime but wakes at 3 a.m., 9 hours after the visit, with worse wheeze. Explain both episodes.
::: solution
The first episode is the **early phase** of a type I reaction: cat allergen (Fel d 1) cross-linked IgE on mast cells in his airway, which released histamine and leukotrienes within minutes, causing bronchoconstriction that responded to a β2 agonist. The second is the **late-phase reaction**: mast-cell cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) and chemokines released over hours recruited eosinophils, basophils and Th2 cells into the airway, causing inflammation, oedema and mucus with renewed bronchoconstriction 4 to 12 hours later. The late phase responds poorly to bronchodilators alone and is the target of inhaled corticosteroids. It is a reminder that a person who improves quickly after an allergic exposure may still deteriorate later.
:::

::: exercise Applying the tryptase rule {level=3}
Two patients have reactions during anaesthesia. Patient A: baseline 4 µg/L, acute 9 µg/L. Patient B: baseline 14 µg/L, acute 17 µg/L. Apply the consensus criterion to each and discuss the clinical significance of patient B's baseline.
::: solution
**Patient A.** Threshold $= 1.2 \times 4 + 2 = 6.8$ µg/L. The acute value of 9 µg/L exceeds it, so there was significant mast-cell activation, even though 9 µg/L lies within many reference ranges.

**Patient B.** Threshold $= 1.2 \times 14 + 2 = 18.8$ µg/L. The acute value of 17 µg/L does not reach it, so the tryptase does not confirm mast-cell activation; the clinical picture still decides whether this was anaphylaxis. A baseline of 14 µg/L is raised. Causes include hereditary alpha-tryptasaemia (extra copies of *TPSAB1*), systemic mastocytosis and other myeloid neoplasms, and chronic kidney disease. Raised baseline tryptase and mastocytosis are associated with more severe anaphylaxis, especially to insect venom and in the perioperative setting, so patient B should be assessed for a mast-cell disorder in the allergy clinic.

This example shows why both an acute and a baseline sample are needed: the same absolute value can be significant in one person and not in another.
:::

::: exercise The differential of a rash on antibiotics {level=3}
A 19-year-old student with a sore throat, fever and cervical lymphadenopathy is prescribed amoxicillin. Five days later he develops a widespread itchy maculopapular rash. He has no breathing difficulty, mucosal involvement or blistering. His blood film shows atypical lymphocytes. (a) What is the most likely explanation? (b) What features would make you worried about a severe cutaneous adverse reaction? (c) Should he be labelled penicillin-allergic for life?
::: solution
(a) The most likely explanation is the maculopapular rash that develops in most people with **infectious mononucleosis (EBV)** who are given amoxicillin or ampicillin. It is a delayed T-cell-mediated reaction, probably reflecting virus-driven immune activation, and it occurs days after starting the drug, which excludes an immediate IgE reaction.

(b) Warning features of a severe cutaneous adverse reaction include mucosal involvement (mouth, eyes, genitals), skin pain, blistering or skin detachment, a positive Nikolsky sign (SJS/TEN), and facial oedema, fever, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia and organ involvement such as hepatitis, appearing 2 to 8 weeks after starting the drug (DRESS). Any of these would require stopping the drug, urgent specialist assessment and a permanent allergy record.

(c) Not automatically. The reaction should be documented precisely (drug, timing, rash type, the EBV context). Many such patients tolerate amoxicillin later, and an allergy assessment, often with oral challenge, can remove an unnecessary penicillin allergy label, which would otherwise expose him to broader-spectrum antibiotics for the rest of his life.
