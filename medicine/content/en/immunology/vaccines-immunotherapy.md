Vaccination is the most successful application of immunology. Smallpox has been eradicated; two of the three types of wild poliovirus no longer exist; diphtheria, tetanus, measles, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b meningitis and congenital rubella, once common causes of childhood death and disability, have become rare wherever vaccine coverage is high. Vaccination against hepatitis B and human papillomavirus is preventing liver and cervical cancer.

A vaccine works by giving the adaptive immune system a safe rehearsal, so that the first real encounter with the pathogen triggers a secondary rather than a primary response. Everything in the earlier chapters is relevant: the innate signals that decide whether a response starts at all ([[immunology/innate-immunity]]), the need for T-cell help and germinal centres to produce high-affinity, class-switched antibody and memory ([[immunology/adaptive-responses]]), and the defects that make some people unable to respond ([[immunology/immunodeficiency]]).

The second half of the chapter turns to **immunotherapy**: the deliberate use of antibodies, cytokines, cells and small molecules to strengthen, redirect or suppress immune responses. Monoclonal antibodies are now among the most widely used medicines.

## Passive and active immunisation

::: definition Passive and active immunisation {#def-immunisation}
**Passive immunisation** is the transfer of ready-made antibody. Protection is immediate but temporary, because the antibody is gradually broken down (IgG with a half-life of about 3 weeks) and no memory is formed. Natural passive immunity comes from maternal IgG crossing the placenta and IgA in breast milk. Artificial passive immunisation uses human immunoglobulin (pooled or specific, such as hepatitis B, rabies, tetanus or varicella-zoster immunoglobulin), monoclonal antibodies, or animal antisera such as antivenoms.

**Active immunisation** is the stimulation of the person's own immune response, by infection or by a vaccine. It takes days to weeks to develop but produces immunological memory, which may last for decades.
:::

The two are often combined. A baby born to a mother with hepatitis B is given hepatitis B vaccine and, if the mother is highly infectious, hepatitis B immunoglobulin at birth: the immunoglobulin protects at once, while the vaccine generates lasting immunity. After a tetanus-prone wound in a person with incomplete vaccination, a tetanus-containing vaccine and, for high-risk wounds, human tetanus immunoglobulin are given. After a bite from a possibly rabid animal, rabies vaccine is given with rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated around the wound if the exposure is high-risk and the person has not been vaccinated before.

::: example A garden fork through the foot {#ex-tetanus}
A 78-year-old woman stands on a garden fork, which pierces her foot through her shoe. The wound is deep and contaminated with soil and manure. She thinks she had "a tetanus jab" after a cut in the 1970s but has no record of a full course. Explain the immunological reasoning behind her wound management.
::: solution
**The threat.** Spores of *Clostridium tetani* in soil germinate in deep, poorly oxygenated, contaminated wounds. The bacteria stay local, but they release **tetanospasmin**, a toxin that travels up nerves to the spinal cord and blocks inhibitory neurotransmission, causing rigidity and spasms. Disease follows toxin, not invasion, so protection depends on **neutralising IgG antitoxin** in the blood before the toxin binds nerve terminals. Tetanus does not reliably immunise the survivor, because the amount of toxin that causes disease is too small to provoke a strong response.

**Her immunity.** With an unknown or incomplete primary course, she cannot be assumed to have protective antitoxin (at least 0.1 IU/mL). Older people, especially women who were not vaccinated in military service, are the group with the lowest immunity in the UK.

**Management principles.** Thorough cleaning and debridement remove spores and dead tissue. Because a vaccine needs days to weeks to induce antibody, a high-risk wound in a person who is not fully immunised needs **passive** protection: human tetanus immunoglobulin, giving immediate antitoxin. She is also given a dose of tetanus-containing vaccine (**active** immunisation) at a different site, and the course is completed later so that she is protected in future. The exact choice follows the Green Book's table for tetanus-prone wounds, which depends on immunisation status and wound risk.
:::
:::

A long-acting monoclonal antibody against RSV, **nirsevimab**, given to infants, is an example of passive immunisation developed into a population programme. In the UK, RSV protection for infants is provided by a different route: a vaccine given to pregnant women from 28 weeks, so that maternal IgG crosses the placenta (this vaccine is also offered to adults at age 75).

## Types of vaccine

::: definition Vaccine platforms {#def-vaccine}
- **Live attenuated vaccines** contain a weakened form of the pathogen that replicates in the body without causing disease (or causing only mild disease). Examples: measles, mumps, rubella and varicella (MMRV), rotavirus, BCG, nasal live attenuated influenza vaccine, yellow fever, oral typhoid. They usually produce strong, long-lasting immunity, including T-cell responses, often after one or two doses.
- **Inactivated whole-pathogen vaccines** contain killed organisms: inactivated polio vaccine (IPV), hepatitis A, rabies, some influenza vaccines.
- **Subunit and recombinant protein vaccines** contain only selected proteins: hepatitis B surface antigen made in yeast, acellular pertussis, recombinant zoster vaccine. **Virus-like particles** are self-assembling viral capsid proteins with no genetic material, as in HPV vaccines.
- **Toxoid vaccines** contain toxins inactivated with formaldehyde: tetanus and diphtheria.
- **Polysaccharide vaccines** contain bacterial capsular polysaccharides (the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine, PPV23). **Conjugate vaccines** link the polysaccharide to a carrier protein: Hib, pneumococcal conjugate (PCV), meningococcal ACWY and C.
- **Viral vector vaccines** use a harmless, non-replicating virus (usually an adenovirus) to deliver a gene for a pathogen protein into host cells: some COVID-19 and Ebola vaccines.
- **Nucleic acid vaccines** deliver messenger RNA in lipid nanoparticles; host cells make the encoded protein: COVID-19 vaccines and some RSV vaccines.
:::

Each platform has strengths and weaknesses that follow from immunology.

**Live vaccines** replicate, so they provide a sustained dose of antigen in the right place with natural innate signals, and the antigen is made inside cells and presented on MHC class I, giving CD8 T-cell responses. But because they replicate, they can cause disease in people who cannot control them. They are **contraindicated in people with severe immunosuppression** (for example, SCID, high-dose corticosteroids, chemotherapy, many biologics) and in **pregnancy**, and rarely they revert to virulence, as with vaccine-derived poliovirus from oral polio vaccine.

**Non-replicating vaccines** are safe in immunosuppression, but they usually need several doses and an **adjuvant** to activate innate immunity. The antigen is taken up from outside cells, so it is presented mainly on class II, giving antibody and CD4 T-cell responses rather than strong CD8 responses. Viral vector and mRNA vaccines are exceptions: the protein is made inside the cell, so they induce CD8 T cells as well, and the nucleic acid itself activates innate sensors.

**Conjugate vaccines** solve the problem of polysaccharide antigens in infants, explained in [[immunology/adaptive-responses]]. Polysaccharide alone gives a T-independent response that is poor under 2 years and leaves no memory; linking it to a protein recruits T-cell help, so infants make class-switched antibody and memory. Conjugate vaccines also reduce nasopharyngeal carriage of the bacteria, so they protect unvaccinated people too.

### Adjuvants

An **adjuvant** is a substance added to a vaccine to enhance the immune response. Adjuvants provide the innate "danger" signal (signal 2 and signal 3) that purified proteins lack. **Aluminium salts**, used for decades, form a depot and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, favouring antibody responses. **Oil-in-water emulsions** (MF59, AS03) are used in some influenza vaccines. **AS01**, which combines a TLR4 agonist (monophosphoryl lipid A, a detoxified form of LPS) with the saponin QS-21, gives the recombinant zoster vaccine its high efficacy in older adults, whose responses to non-adjuvanted vaccines are weak.

## How vaccines protect

::: theorem What a vaccine must achieve {#thm-vaccine-principles}
A successful vaccine must:

1. **activate innate immunity**, through the pathogen's own molecules or an adjuvant, so that dendritic cells provide costimulation and cytokines;
2. **present the right antigens**: those targeted by protective antibodies (often surface proteins needed for entry, or toxins) or by protective T cells;
3. **engage T-cell help** to drive germinal-centre responses, class switching, affinity maturation and memory;
4. **produce a protective effector** at the right place and time: neutralising IgG in the blood for toxins and viraemic infections, mucosal IgA for some gut and respiratory infections, T cells for intracellular pathogens;
5. **last**, through long-lived plasma cells and memory B and T cells, with boosters where these wane.
:::

A **correlate of protection** is a measurable immune response that predicts protection. For tetanus, an antitoxin level of at least 0.1 IU/mL is considered protective; for hepatitis B, an anti-HBs level of at least 10 mIU/mL after the primary course indicates a response. For other infections, including pertussis and tuberculosis, no simple correlate exists, which makes vaccine development harder.

### Why schedules look the way they do

- **Several doses** of non-live vaccines are needed for a primary series: the first doses prime, later doses boost and drive affinity maturation. Doses must be separated by a **minimum interval** (often 4 weeks) to allow the germinal-centre reaction to proceed; giving them closer together gives a poorer response, not a faster one.
- **Maternal antibody** can neutralise live vaccines given too early. This is the main reason measles-containing vaccine is given at about 12 months in the UK.
- **Boosters** are given where immunity wanes, as with pertussis and tetanus.
- **Pregnancy** is an opportunity to protect the newborn through transplacental IgG: in the UK pertussis vaccine is offered from 16 weeks of pregnancy (ideally between about 20 and 32 weeks), influenza vaccine at any stage in the season, and RSV vaccine from 28 weeks.

The UK childhood schedule, updated in July 2025 and January 2026 (including the introduction of MMRV), is described in [[paediatrics/immunisation]]; programme organisation is covered in [[public-health/vaccination-programmes]].

## Herd immunity

Vaccines protect not only the people who receive them. If enough people are immune, each infection gives rise on average to less than one new infection, and outbreaks die out, protecting people who cannot be vaccinated or did not respond.

::: definition Basic reproduction number and herd immunity threshold {#def-herd}
The **basic reproduction number** $R_0$ is the average number of secondary cases caused by one infectious person in a fully susceptible population. If a proportion $p$ of the population is immune, the effective reproduction number is $R = R_0(1 - p)$. Transmission declines when $R < 1$, which gives the **herd immunity threshold**

$$
p_c = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}.
$$ {#eq-hit}

Because vaccines are not 100% effective, the **critical vaccination coverage** needed is higher: with vaccine effectiveness $E$ against infection,

$$
V_c = \frac{1 - 1/R_0}{E}.
$$ {#eq-coverage}
:::

These are simplified results. They assume random mixing, lifelong immunity and a homogeneous population. In reality, people cluster by family, school, community and belief, so an area with low coverage can sustain outbreaks even when national coverage is high.

::: example Measles in a school {#ex-measles}
Measles has an $R_0$ of about 15. Two doses of MMR are about 97% effective in preventing measles. (a) What is the herd immunity threshold? (b) What two-dose coverage is needed to reach it? (c) A secondary school has 1000 pupils, of whom 850 have had two doses and the rest none. Can measles spread there?
::: solution
(a) $p_c = 1 - 1/15 = 14/15 \approx 0.933$, so about 93% of people must be immune.

(b) $V_c = 0.933/0.97 \approx 0.962$, so about 96% two-dose coverage is needed. This is the basis of the WHO target of at least 95% coverage with two doses.

(c) Immune pupils: $850 \times 0.97 \approx 824.5$, so about 82% are immune. This is well below 93%, so $R = 15 \times (1 - 0.8245) \approx 2.6$: each case would on average infect more than two others, and an outbreak could spread among the roughly 175 susceptible pupils. Unvaccinated pupils are clustered in the same classrooms and friendship groups, which in reality makes spread even more likely. Public health action would include offering MMR to unvaccinated pupils and staff, and exclusion and post-exposure advice for susceptible contacts, especially those who are pregnant or immunosuppressed, following UKHSA guidance.
:::
:::

## Vaccine safety

Vaccines are given to healthy people, often children, so the standard of safety is very high. Most adverse events are **reactogenicity**: local pain, swelling and redness, and short-lived fever and malaise, caused by the same innate activation that makes the vaccine work. Febrile seizures can follow fever, especially 7 to 10 days after MMR (when the attenuated virus replicates), and are not associated with long-term harm.

True contraindications are few:

- **anaphylaxis** to a previous dose of the vaccine or to one of its components;
- for **live vaccines**: severe immunosuppression and pregnancy.

Many conditions are wrongly regarded as contraindications. Egg allergy is **not** a contraindication to MMR (which is grown in chick fibroblasts, not eggs) and, in the UK, is not a contraindication to most influenza vaccines. A minor illness without fever, prematurity, a family history of adverse reactions, stable neurological conditions, and breastfeeding are not reasons to withhold vaccines. The UKHSA *Green Book* is the reference for contraindications in the UK.

Rare serious adverse events are monitored by surveillance systems, such as the MHRA **Yellow Card** scheme in the UK. Examples show that the system works: a small increase in **intussusception** after rotavirus vaccines led to strict upper age limits for the first dose; **vaccine-induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis**, caused by antibodies against platelet factor 4, was recognised within weeks in 2021 after adenovirus-vector COVID-19 vaccines; and **myocarditis**, mostly mild, was identified after mRNA COVID-19 vaccines, mainly in young men after the second dose. In each case the risks were quantified and weighed against the benefits of vaccination for different groups.

:::warning
Do not confuse "after" with "because of". A vaccine given to millions of infants will be followed, by chance, by every condition that infants develop at that age. The claim that MMR causes autism came from a 1998 paper that was later shown to be fraudulent and was retracted in 2010; many large studies have found no association. Assessing whether a vaccine causes an event requires comparison of rates in vaccinated and unvaccinated people, or in the same people in risk and control periods, not individual stories. When parents raise concerns, listening and explaining the evidence respectfully is more effective than dismissal.
:::

## Vaccinating people with impaired immunity

People with immunodeficiency are both those who most need protection and those in whom vaccines work least well or are least safe.

- **Live vaccines** are avoided in severe primary immunodeficiency, during and for a period after immunosuppressive therapy (the intervals are given in the Green Book), and in HIV infection with a low CD4 count.
- **Inactivated vaccines** are safe but may give weaker responses; it is best to give them before immunosuppression starts, at least 2 weeks before where possible.
- **Household contacts** should be fully vaccinated (including annual influenza vaccination) to "cocoon" the vulnerable person. Most live vaccines, including MMR and varicella, can be given to contacts.
- **Asplenia** needs specific vaccines, described in [[immunology/immunodeficiency]].

::: example A baby exposed to infliximab {#ex-infant}
A woman with Crohn's disease received infliximab throughout pregnancy because her disease was severe. Her baby, born at term, is due to start routine immunisations at 8 weeks and is eligible for BCG because of family TB risk. Which vaccines raise a concern, and why?
::: solution
**Mechanism.** Infliximab is an IgG1 antibody. Like maternal IgG, it is actively transported across the placenta by the neonatal Fc receptor, mainly in the third trimester, and the baby is born with drug levels that can be higher than the mother's and that persist for months. The baby is therefore effectively receiving anti-TNF therapy.

**BCG.** BCG is a live attenuated mycobacterium. Control of mycobacteria depends on TNF-dependent granulomas, and disseminated BCG infection, including deaths, has been reported in infants exposed to infliximab in utero who received BCG early. UK guidance has been to defer BCG in infants exposed to immunosuppressive biologics in pregnancy until 6 months of age.

**Rotavirus.** This is also a live vaccine, but it causes mild disease even in exposed infants, and the evidence for harm is much weaker. Advice on rotavirus has varied over time and differs for different drugs (for example, it is avoided after exposure to B-cell-depleting drugs such as rituximab), so the decision follows the current Green Book chapter and specialist advice.

**Everything else.** All the non-live vaccines (the 6-in-1, pneumococcal conjugate, MenB) should be given on time. Breastfeeding can continue: very little infliximab passes into milk and it is not absorbed well from the infant gut. Ideally this plan is made before birth, so the baby is not given BCG on the postnatal ward by default.
:::
:::

## Immunotherapy

### Monoclonal antibodies

**Monoclonal antibodies** are identical antibodies produced by a single clone of cells. The first therapeutic monoclonals were mouse antibodies, which provoked human anti-mouse antibodies that neutralised them and caused reactions. Genetic engineering then produced **chimeric** antibodies (mouse variable regions on human constant regions), **humanised** antibodies (only the mouse CDRs grafted into a human framework) and fully **human** antibodies. Their generic names traditionally showed this: *-ximab* (chimeric, such as infliximab and rituximab), *-zumab* (humanised, such as tocilizumab and pembrolizumab) and *-umab* (human, such as adalimumab). Since 2021 new names use a different system, but the older names remain in use.

::: definition Mechanisms of therapeutic antibodies {#def-mab}
Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies act by:

- **neutralising a soluble molecule**: a cytokine (anti-TNF infliximab and adalimumab; anti-IL-5 mepolizumab; anti-IL-17 secukinumab), IgE (omalizumab) or complement C5 (eculizumab);
- **blocking a receptor**: IL-6 receptor (tocilizumab), IL-4 receptor α (dupilumab), α4 integrin on lymphocytes (natalizumab), or the checkpoint receptors CTLA-4 and PD-1;
- **depleting target cells** through ADCC, complement and phagocytosis: CD20 on B cells (rituximab, ocrelizumab), CD52 on lymphocytes (alemtuzumab), CD38 on myeloma cells (daratumumab);
- **delivering a toxin** to a target cell (antibody–drug conjugates such as trastuzumab emtansine);
- **bridging two cells** (bispecific antibodies such as blinatumomab, linking CD3 on T cells to CD19 on leukaemia cells).

**Fusion proteins** join a receptor to an IgG Fc region, giving an antibody-like half-life: etanercept (TNF receptor–Fc) and abatacept (CTLA-4–Fc).
:::

The predictable risks of these drugs follow from their targets:

- **infection** related to the pathway blocked: tuberculosis reactivation with anti-TNF drugs; hepatitis B reactivation and prolonged hypogammaglobulinaemia with rituximab; meningococcal disease with C5 inhibitors; **progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy** (PML), a brain infection by JC virus, with natalizumab, which blocks lymphocyte entry into the brain;
- **infusion reactions**, especially with the first dose of lymphocyte-depleting antibodies, because of cytokine release;
- **immunogenicity**: patients can make **anti-drug antibodies**, which reduce drug levels and efficacy. Combining an anti-TNF antibody with methotrexate or azathioprine reduces this.

Before starting a biologic, patients are screened for latent TB, hepatitis B and C and HIV, and their vaccinations are updated (live vaccines before, not during, treatment). **Biosimilars**, highly similar versions of a biologic made after patent expiry, have greatly reduced costs, allowing wider use.

### Other immunomodulatory treatments

- **Immunoglobulin** pooled from thousands of donors is given at replacement doses (intravenous every 3 to 4 weeks, or subcutaneous weekly) in antibody deficiency, and at high doses as an immunomodulator in immune thrombocytopenia, Kawasaki disease, Guillain–Barré syndrome and some other conditions, where it saturates FcRn and Fc receptors among other actions.
- **Cytokines** are used as drugs: interferon-β in relapsing multiple sclerosis, peginterferon-α in some hepatitis B, interferon-γ in chronic granulomatous disease, and G-CSF to shorten neutropenia.
- **Small-molecule immunomodulators**: **JAK inhibitors** (tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib) block signalling from many cytokine receptors at once and are used in rheumatoid arthritis, atopic dermatitis and inflammatory bowel disease; their risks include herpes zoster, thrombosis and, in older people with cardiovascular risk factors, major cardiovascular events and cancer, which led regulators to restrict their use in 2022 and 2023.
- **Allergen immunotherapy** induces tolerance to allergens (see [[immunology/hypersensitivity]]).
- **Cell therapies** such as CAR-T cells (see [[immunology/transplant-tumour-immunology]]).

::: example Weakness on natalizumab {#ex-pml}
A 38-year-old woman has relapsing multiple sclerosis treated with natalizumab for 4 years. She is JC virus antibody-positive and previously took azathioprine. Over 3 weeks she develops a gradually worsening left hemiparesis and difficulty finding words. MRI shows a new, large, non-enhancing lesion in the white matter without mass effect. What is the most important diagnosis to consider, and why is she at risk?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** **Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy** (PML), a demyelinating brain infection by JC polyomavirus, which infects oligodendrocytes.

**Why she is at risk.** Natalizumab blocks α4 integrin (VLA-4) on lymphocytes, preventing them from binding VCAM-1 on brain endothelium and entering the central nervous system. This is how it reduces MS relapses, but it also prevents T-cell surveillance of the brain, allowing latent JC virus to reactivate and replicate. The risk of PML on natalizumab increases with three factors, all present here: a positive anti-JC virus antibody (showing previous infection), treatment for more than 2 years, and previous immunosuppressant use.

**Investigation and management principles.** CSF PCR for JC virus and specialist neurology review. Natalizumab is stopped, and plasma exchange may be used to remove it more quickly so that immune surveillance can return; the restored immune response can cause an immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, which needs careful management. This case illustrates why PML risk stratification, monitoring by MRI, and consideration of extended-interval dosing or a switch of drug are part of natalizumab treatment.
:::
:::

Which of the following vaccines is safe to give to a 30-year-old man receiving high-dose prednisolone and mycophenolate after a kidney transplant?
- [ ] MMR
- [ ] Varicella (live)
- [x] Inactivated influenza vaccine
- [ ] Yellow fever
::: solution
Inactivated influenza vaccine contains no replicating virus and is recommended annually for transplant recipients, although the response may be reduced by immunosuppression. MMR, live varicella and yellow fever vaccines are live attenuated vaccines, which can cause disseminated infection in a person with severe immunosuppression and are contraindicated. His household contacts should be vaccinated, including against influenza. The non-live recombinant zoster vaccine can be offered to protect against shingles.

::: history
People in China, India and the Ottoman Empire practised **variolation**, deliberate infection with material from smallpox sores, long before vaccines; Lady Mary Wortley Montagu promoted it in England in the 1720s. In 1796 Edward Jenner inoculated James Phipps with material from a cowpox lesion on the hand of the dairymaid Sarah Nelmes, and later showed that the boy was protected against smallpox. The word vaccine comes from *vacca*, cow. Louis Pasteur extended the principle in the 1880s with attenuated vaccines against chicken cholera, anthrax and rabies, and named them vaccines in Jenner's honour. In 1890 Emil von Behring and Kitasato Shibasaburō introduced antitoxin serum therapy, and in the 1920s Gaston Ramon developed toxoids and the first adjuvants.

The WHO's intensified smallpox eradication programme, using surveillance and ring vaccination, achieved the last natural case in 1977 in Somalia, and smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980. In 1975 Georges Köhler and César Milstein made the first hybridomas producing monoclonal antibodies (Nobel Prize 1984). Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman found that modifying the nucleosides in mRNA reduced its activation of innate sensors, work that made mRNA vaccines possible and was recognised by the 2023 Nobel Prize.
:::

## Where this leads

Vaccination programmes and their population effects are covered in [[public-health/vaccination-programmes]] and [[public-health/communicable-disease-control]], and the childhood schedule in [[paediatrics/immunisation]]. The pharmacology of immunosuppressive drugs is discussed in [[pharmacology/adverse-reactions]] and in the organ-system courses where they are used.

::: summary
- Passive immunisation transfers antibody for immediate, temporary protection; active immunisation induces memory. They are often combined after exposure (hepatitis B, tetanus, rabies).
- Vaccine platforms are live attenuated, inactivated, subunit and virus-like particle, toxoid, polysaccharide and conjugate, viral vector and mRNA. Live vaccines give strong, broad immunity but are contraindicated in severe immunosuppression and pregnancy.
- Adjuvants supply the innate danger signal; conjugation recruits T-cell help for polysaccharides in infants.
- Schedules reflect immunology: priming and boosting with minimum intervals, timing to avoid maternal antibody, and maternal vaccination in pregnancy to protect newborns through IgG.
- Herd immunity threshold $p_c = 1 - 1/R_0$; critical coverage $V_c = (1 - 1/R_0)/E$. Measles needs about 95% two-dose coverage.
- Most vaccine adverse events are reactogenicity; true contraindications are few. Surveillance detects rare events, and causation requires comparison of rates, not anecdotes.
- Monoclonal antibodies neutralise cytokines, block receptors, deplete cells, deliver toxins or bridge cells; their infection risks follow their targets (TB with anti-TNF, hepatitis B with rituximab, meningococcus with C5 inhibitors, PML with natalizumab).
- Screen for latent infection and update vaccines before biologics; give live vaccines before, not during, immunosuppression.
:::

Classify each vaccine as live attenuated or non-live: (a) BCG; (b) the 6-in-1 infant vaccine; (c) rotavirus; (d) HPV; (e) recombinant zoster vaccine; (f) yellow fever.

(a) Live. (b) Non-live (toxoids, acellular pertussis, inactivated polio, Hib conjugate and hepatitis B surface antigen). (c) Live (oral). (d) Non-live (virus-like particles). (e) Non-live (recombinant glycoprotein E with AS01 adjuvant). (f) Live.

::: exercise Vaccine effectiveness {level=1 check="1 - 0.02/0.2"}
In an outbreak, the attack rate is 2% among vaccinated people and 20% among unvaccinated people. Vaccine effectiveness is $1 - (\text{attack rate in vaccinated})/(\text{attack rate in unvaccinated})$. Calculate it as a decimal.
::: solution
$1 - 0.02/0.20 = 1 - 0.1 = 0.9$, or 90%. Note that in a highly vaccinated population, many cases may still be in vaccinated people simply because they are the majority; effectiveness depends on rates, not on the share of cases.

::: exercise Critical coverage {level=2 check="(1 - 1/10)/0.9"}
An infection has $R_0 = 10$ and the vaccine is 90% effective against infection. What vaccine coverage is needed to reach the herd immunity threshold? Give the unrounded decimal and comment on the result.
::: solution
$V_c = (1 - 1/10)/0.9 = 0.9/0.9 = 1$. Coverage of 100% would be needed, which is unattainable in practice. With a slightly less effective vaccine or a higher $R_0$, herd immunity would be impossible even with universal coverage. For such infections, the vaccine still protects the individuals who are vaccinated, but elimination would need a more effective vaccine or additional measures.
:::
:::

::: exercise Why conjugate pneumococcal vaccine for infants {level=2}
A parent asks why her 1-year-old is given a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine covering fewer serotypes, while her grandmother received a polysaccharide vaccine covering 23. Explain the immunological reasons.
::: solution
Pure polysaccharide antigens activate B cells without T-cell help. Children under about 2 years respond very poorly to these T-independent antigens, because their marginal-zone B cells are immature, so the 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine would not protect the infant. Conjugating each polysaccharide to a carrier protein allows polysaccharide-specific B cells to receive help from carrier-specific T cells, producing class-switched, high-affinity antibody and memory even in infancy. Conjugation is technically more demanding, so conjugate vaccines cover fewer serotypes, chosen as the commonest causes of invasive disease. Conjugate vaccines also reduce carriage of the included serotypes in the nasopharynx, which protects unvaccinated people (including grandparents) through herd effects, although other serotypes can partly replace them over time. Newer conjugate vaccines cover more serotypes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Before rituximab {level=2}
A 50-year-old man is about to start rituximab for rheumatoid arthritis. Which infections should be screened for, which vaccines should be offered and when, and why?
::: solution
**Screening.** Hepatitis B (HBsAg and anti-HBc): rituximab depletes B cells and can cause fatal hepatitis B reactivation, including in people with resolved infection (anti-HBc positive), who may need antiviral prophylaxis. Hepatitis C and HIV testing are also standard before biologic therapy, and latent TB screening is commonly done.

**Vaccines.** Rituximab depletes B cells for 6 to 12 months, during which antibody responses to vaccines are very poor. So non-live vaccines (pneumococcal, annual influenza, COVID-19 as recommended, recombinant zoster if eligible) should ideally be given at least 2 weeks, and preferably longer, before the first infusion. Live vaccines are avoided during and for a period after treatment, according to the Green Book. Household contacts should be vaccinated.

**Why.** Protection after rituximab depends largely on memory and antibody already present before treatment; long-lived plasma cells lack CD20 and keep making existing antibody, but new responses cannot be generated until B cells return.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interpreting a vaccine response {level=3}
A 34-year-old healthcare worker has had three doses of hepatitis B vaccine. Her anti-HBs measured 2 months after the third dose is 4 mIU/mL. (a) What does this mean? (b) What factors are associated with poor response? (c) What should happen next?
::: solution
(a) An anti-HBs level below 10 mIU/mL 1 to 2 months after a complete course means she is a **non-responder** (or poor responder) and cannot be assumed to be protected.

(b) Poor response is associated with older age, male sex, obesity, smoking, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, immunosuppression, and certain HLA types (some HLA class II alleles present hepatitis B surface antigen peptides poorly). Undiagnosed chronic hepatitis B infection is also a cause of apparent non-response, because the virus produces excess surface antigen that binds the antibody.

(c) She should be tested for HBsAg and anti-HBc to exclude current or past infection. If negative, a further course of vaccine (often a different or more immunogenic vaccine) is given and anti-HBs rechecked. If she still fails to respond, she is managed as susceptible: after any significant exposure she would need hepatitis B immunoglobulin (passive immunisation). This follows the Green Book and occupational health guidance.

::: exercise Designing a vaccine {level=3}
Explain, using the principles of this course, why it has been much easier to make effective vaccines against tetanus and measles than against tuberculosis or HIV.
::: solution
**Tetanus and measles** have features that favour vaccination. Disease in tetanus is caused entirely by a single toxin, and a neutralising IgG antibody against it (induced by toxoid) is sufficient for protection; there is a clear correlate of protection. Measles virus has stable surface proteins with little antigenic variation, natural infection gives lifelong immunity (showing that immunity is achievable), and neutralising antibody plus T cells prevent infection; a live attenuated vaccine reproduces the natural immune response.

**Tuberculosis** is an intracellular infection in macrophages, controlled mainly by Th1 cells, interferon-gamma and granulomas rather than by antibody, and natural infection does not reliably prevent reinfection. There is no established correlate of protection, so candidate vaccines must be tested in large, long trials. BCG protects young children against severe disseminated forms but gives variable protection against adult pulmonary disease.

**HIV** has extreme sequence diversity and mutates rapidly within each person, its envelope protein is heavily glycosylated and conformationally masked so that broadly neutralising antibodies are rare and arise only after years of affinity maturation, it integrates into the host genome and establishes latent reservoirs within days, and it infects the CD4 T cells that coordinate the immune response. No one naturally clears HIV, so there is no natural model of protective immunity to copy. Current research aims to guide germinal centres through the many rounds of affinity maturation needed to make broadly neutralising antibodies.
