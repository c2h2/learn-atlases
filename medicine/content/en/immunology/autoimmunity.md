**Autoimmune disease** is tissue damage caused by an adaptive immune response against self. It is common. In a 2023 study of the primary care records of 22 million people in the UK, Conrad and colleagues found that about 1 in 10 people had at least one of 19 common autoimmune diseases, with women affected nearly twice as often as men. The list includes type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, coeliac disease, psoriasis, multiple sclerosis, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo, inflammatory bowel disease and systemic lupus erythematosus.

The previous chapter, [[immunology/tolerance-regulation]], described how tolerance normally keeps self-reactive lymphocytes in check. This chapter asks why it fails in common disease, how the damage is done, how autoantibodies are used and misused in diagnosis, and what the principles of treatment are. The clinical detail of individual diseases belongs to the organ-system courses; here the emphasis is on the immunological reasoning that is common to all of them.

## What counts as autoimmune

::: definition Autoimmunity and autoimmune disease {#def-autoimmunity}
**Autoimmunity** is the presence of an adaptive immune response, antibodies or T cells, against self antigens. It is common and often harmless: low-titre autoantibodies are found in many healthy people, and their frequency increases with age.

**Autoimmune disease** is present when that response causes tissue damage or dysfunction. The best evidence that a disease is autoimmune is that the antibody or T cells can transfer the disease. In humans this is seen when maternal IgG crosses the placenta and causes transient disease in the newborn, as in neonatal Graves disease, neonatal myasthenia and neonatal lupus.
:::

Autoimmune diseases are traditionally divided into two groups:

- **organ-specific** diseases, in which the immune response targets antigens of one organ: type 1 diabetes, Hashimoto thyroiditis, Graves disease, Addison disease, pernicious anaemia, myasthenia gravis, multiple sclerosis;
- **systemic** diseases, in which the targets are widely distributed, often intracellular, antigens such as nuclear proteins and DNA: systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren disease, systemic sclerosis, inflammatory myositis and, with a large joint component, rheumatoid arthritis.

Organ-specific diseases cluster together in the same people and families. A person with type 1 diabetes has an increased risk of autoimmune thyroid disease, coeliac disease and Addison disease, which is why screening for thyroid and coeliac disease is part of routine diabetes care.

## Why tolerance fails

Common autoimmune diseases are **multifactorial**: they need a genetic predisposition, an environmental trigger and often chance. No single factor is sufficient.

::: theorem The multifactorial model of autoimmunity {#thm-multifactorial}
Common autoimmune disease arises when several partial failures of tolerance coincide:

1. **Genetic susceptibility**, mostly from many variants of small effect, the largest usually in the HLA region;
2. **Environmental triggers** that provide antigen, inflammation or costimulation, such as infection, smoking, drugs and ultraviolet light;
3. **Amplification** once a response has started, through tissue damage releasing new self antigens (**epitope spreading**).

Because each factor contributes only partly, concordance in identical twins is incomplete, typically between about 15% and 50% depending on the disease, but much higher than in non-identical twins.
:::

::: proof
If genes alone determined autoimmune disease, identical twins, who share all their genes, would be concordant almost always; they are not, so non-genetic factors must contribute. If environment alone determined it, identical twins would be no more concordant than non-identical twins or siblings; they are more concordant, so genes contribute. The intermediate concordance is what a model of several interacting partial causes predicts. The same model explains why autoantibodies can be present for years before disease appears: the response starts, but a further event is needed to turn autoimmunity into disease.
:::

### Genes

The strongest genetic associations are with **HLA** alleles, because these determine which self peptides are presented to T cells: HLA-DR3 and DR4 with DQ2 and DQ8 in type 1 diabetes, HLA-DRB1 shared-epitope alleles in rheumatoid arthritis, HLA-B27 in ankylosing spondylitis. Outside HLA, many risk variants lie in genes that set the thresholds for lymphocyte activation and regulation. A variant of the phosphatase **PTPN22** increases the risk of type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE and autoimmune thyroid disease, and variants near **CTLA4** and the **IL-2 receptor** have similar shared effects. These shared genes explain why different autoimmune diseases cluster in the same families.

### Sex

Most autoimmune diseases are more common in women. The ratio is about 9 to 1 in SLE and Sjögren disease and about 3 to 1 in rheumatoid arthritis. Oestrogens enhance B-cell survival and antibody production, and some immune genes on the X chromosome, such as the RNA sensor **TLR7**, partly escape X inactivation, so women can express more of them. SLE often begins in the reproductive years and can flare in pregnancy.

### Environment

- **Infection.** Through **molecular mimicry**, an immune response against a microbe cross-reacts with self: antibodies to group A streptococcal M protein cross-react with cardiac myosin in rheumatic fever, and antibodies to *Campylobacter jejuni* lipo-oligosaccharide cross-react with nerve gangliosides in some cases of Guillain–Barré syndrome. Infection can also cause **bystander activation** by providing costimulation. A very large longitudinal study of US military personnel (Bjornevik and colleagues, 2022) found that the risk of multiple sclerosis increased about 32-fold after infection with Epstein–Barr virus, strong evidence that EBV is a leading cause.
- **Smoking** increases citrullination of proteins in the lung, and in people with shared-epitope HLA alleles it greatly increases the risk of rheumatoid arthritis with **anti-citrullinated protein antibodies** (ACPA).
- **Drugs** can cause autoimmunity. Hydralazine, procainamide and isoniazid cause **drug-induced lupus**, typically with anti-histone antibodies; checkpoint inhibitors cause organ-specific autoimmunity, as described in [[immunology/tolerance-regulation]].
- **Ultraviolet light** damages keratinocytes and releases nuclear antigens, which is why sunlight can trigger lupus flares.

::: example Joint pains on an old drug {#ex-drug-lupus}
A 68-year-old man has taken hydralazine for resistant hypertension for 2 years. Over 2 months he develops pain in the small joints of his hands, pleuritic chest pain and a low-grade fever. ANA is positive at 1:640 with a homogeneous pattern, anti-histone antibodies are positive, anti-dsDNA is negative and complement is normal. Kidney function and urinalysis are normal. What is the likely diagnosis and how does it differ from idiopathic SLE?
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Arthralgia, serositis and fever with a high-titre homogeneous ANA and anti-histone antibodies, in a person taking hydralazine, suggest **drug-induced lupus**.

**How it differs from SLE.** Drug-induced lupus typically affects older people and men as often as women, reflecting who takes the drugs; it usually causes joint, serosal and constitutional features and rarely affects the kidneys or central nervous system; anti-histone antibodies are present in most cases, while anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are usually absent and complement is normal. Hydralazine and procainamide are metabolised by acetylation, and people who are genetically **slow acetylators** are at higher risk, which illustrates how a genetic factor and an environmental trigger combine. Anti-TNF drugs can also induce a lupus-like syndrome, which is more often anti-dsDNA-positive.

**Management principles.** Stop the drug and choose an alternative antihypertensive. Symptoms usually resolve over weeks to months, although the ANA can remain positive much longer. NSAIDs or a short course of corticosteroids may be used for symptoms.
:::
:::

## How the damage is done

Autoimmune diseases use the same mechanisms as hypersensitivity, described in [[immunology/hypersensitivity]]. Identifying the mechanism tells you which tests make sense and which treatments are likely to work.

| Mechanism | Example | Target | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody destroying cells (type II) | Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, immune thrombocytopenia | Red cell or platelet antigens | Splenic clearance |
| Antibody stimulating a receptor (type II) | Graves disease | TSH receptor | Thyrotoxicosis, goitre |
| Antibody blocking or removing a receptor (type II) | Myasthenia gravis | Acetylcholine receptor | Fatigable weakness |
| Antibody against matrix (type II) | Anti-GBM disease | Type IV collagen α3 chain | Glomerulonephritis, lung haemorrhage |
| Immune complexes (type III) | SLE | Nuclear antigens | Nephritis, vasculitis, arthritis, rash |
| T cells (type IV) | Type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis | Islet β-cell antigens, myelin | Destruction of β cells, demyelination |

Many diseases combine mechanisms. In rheumatoid arthritis, T cells, B cells, autoantibodies and cytokines such as TNF and IL-6 all contribute to synovial inflammation. In Hashimoto thyroiditis, antibodies to thyroid peroxidase are good markers, but the gland is destroyed mainly by T cells.

::: example Weakness at the end of the day {#ex-myasthenia}
A 34-year-old woman notices double vision and drooping eyelids that worsen in the evening, and difficulty chewing by the end of a meal. Examination shows ptosis that increases on sustained upward gaze. Explain the mechanism, the antibody tests, and why the thymus is relevant.
::: solution
**Mechanism.** Fatigable weakness of the ocular and bulbar muscles suggests **myasthenia gravis**. In most patients, IgG antibodies against the **acetylcholine receptor** (AChR) at the neuromuscular junction reduce the number of functional receptors in three ways: they cross-link receptors and accelerate their internalisation, they activate complement and damage the postsynaptic membrane, and some block acetylcholine binding. Each nerve impulse releases a little less acetylcholine with repeated firing, and with fewer receptors the safety margin is lost, so weakness appears with sustained use.

**Antibody tests.** AChR antibodies are found in about 85% of patients with generalised myasthenia, fewer in purely ocular disease. Of those without them, some have antibodies against **muscle-specific kinase** (MuSK), which is needed to cluster receptors; MuSK myasthenia is IgG4-mediated, does not fix complement, often predominantly bulbar, and responds particularly well to rituximab.

**The thymus.** Thymic abnormalities are common: hyperplasia in young-onset AChR-positive disease and thymoma in about 10 to 15% of patients. The thymus contains muscle-like (myoid) cells expressing AChR, and it is thought to be a site where the autoimmune response is initiated. A CT of the chest is done to look for thymoma, and thymectomy benefits selected patients. Treatments include acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunosuppression, and newer agents that block complement or the neonatal Fc receptor. Neurological details are in [[nervous-system/neuromuscular]].
:::
:::

## Autoantibodies in diagnosis

Autoantibodies are among the most useful tests in medicine and among the most misused. They can be very sensitive, very specific, or both, and many appear years before disease. But a positive autoantibody test in a person with a low probability of disease is usually a false alarm.

| Anti-double-stranded DNA | SLE | Specific; level often reflects activity, especially nephritis |

### The antinuclear antibody test

The **ANA** test is performed by **indirect immunofluorescence** on HEp-2 cells, a human epithelial cell line with large nuclei. The patient's serum is diluted in a series, applied to the cells, and any bound IgG is detected with a fluorescent anti-human IgG. The result is reported as the highest dilution (**titre**) that still gives fluorescence, together with the staining **pattern** (homogeneous, speckled, centromere, nucleolar), which hints at the target. Positive results are followed by tests for specific antibodies such as anti-dsDNA and the extractable nuclear antigens (Ro, La, Sm, RNP, Scl-70, Jo-1).

Two facts dominate its interpretation. First, ANA is positive in almost everyone with active SLE, so a negative ANA makes SLE very unlikely. Second, ANA is common in healthy people. In a multicentre study of healthy individuals (Tan and colleagues, 1997), 31.7% were positive at a dilution of 1:40, 13.3% at 1:80, 5.0% at 1:160 and 3.3% at 1:320. Positive results are more frequent in women, in older people and in relatives of people with autoimmune disease. A positive ANA is therefore only meaningful in a person whose clinical features already make an ANA-associated disease reasonably likely.

::: example "Your ANA is positive" {#ex-ana}
A 29-year-old woman visits her GP with tiredness for 3 months after a viral illness. She has no joint swelling, rash, mouth ulcers, hair loss, Raynaud phenomenon or other features. Her full blood count, renal function, urinalysis and CRP are normal. An ANA was requested and is positive at 1:80 with a speckled pattern. Assume, for illustration, that the probability she has SLE before the test is 1 in 50, and that ANA at this titre has a sensitivity of 98% and specificity of 87% for SLE. What is the probability that she has SLE after the positive test?
::: solution
Work with 1000 similar women.

- With SLE: $1000 \times 0.02 = 20$. True positives: $20 \times 0.98 = 19.6$.
- Without SLE: $980$. False positives: $980 \times (1 - 0.87) = 980 \times 0.13 = 127.4$.

The positive predictive value is

$$
\frac{19.6}{19.6 + 127.4} = \frac{19.6}{147} \approx 0.133.
$$

After a positive result, the probability of SLE is only about 13%; about 87% of women like her with a positive ANA do not have SLE. The test has not made the diagnosis. It has slightly raised a low probability.

**What to do.** Review the history and examination for specific features, and request anti-dsDNA and extractable nuclear antigen antibodies, complement levels and urinalysis if any clinical suspicion remains. If all are normal and she has no specific features, the positive ANA is most likely incidental and she can be reassured, with advice to return if new features develop. The deeper lesson is that the ANA should not be used as a screening test for fatigue alone: the commonest consequence of testing a low-probability patient is anxiety and unnecessary referral.
:::
:::

:::warning
Do not equate an autoantibody with a disease. Rheumatoid factor is positive in a large proportion of healthy older people and in chronic infections such as endocarditis and hepatitis C; anti-TPO antibodies are found in up to about one in ten healthy women; a positive ANA at low titre is found in up to a third of healthy people. Interpret every autoantibody result in light of the pre-test probability, the titre and the specific antibody, and do not order an autoimmune "screen" without a specific clinical question.
:::

### Classification criteria are not diagnostic criteria

Rheumatological diseases have **classification criteria**, designed to define uniform groups of patients for research. They are deliberately specific, and a patient can have a disease without meeting them, especially early. They are nevertheless a useful summary of what matters.

- The **2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for SLE** require an ANA titre of at least 1:80 on HEp-2 cells (or an equivalent test) at any time as an **entry criterion**. Weighted clinical items (fever, haematological, neuropsychiatric, mucocutaneous, serosal, musculoskeletal and renal) and immunological items (antiphospholipid antibodies, low complement, anti-dsDNA or anti-Sm) are then scored; SLE is classified with a total of **10 or more points** and at least one clinical criterion.
- The **2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for rheumatoid arthritis** apply to a person with at least one joint with definite synovitis not better explained by another disease. Points are given for joint involvement, serology (rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP), acute-phase reactants (CRP or ESR) and symptom duration of at least 6 weeks; a score of **6 or more out of 10** classifies the patient as having rheumatoid arthritis.

## Systemic lupus erythematosus as a model

SLE shows how several immune abnormalities combine into disease. A useful sequence is:

1. **Increased exposure to nuclear antigen.** Apoptotic cells are produced in greater amounts (ultraviolet light, infection) or cleared less efficiently (deficiency of C1q or other early complement components; see [[immunology/innate-immunity]]).
2. **Innate sensing of nucleic acid.** Immune complexes containing DNA or RNA are taken up by plasmacytoid dendritic cells, where TLR7 and TLR9 sense the nucleic acid and drive large amounts of **type I interferon**. Most patients have an "interferon signature" in their blood cells.
3. **Loss of B-cell tolerance.** Interferon and the B-cell survival factor **BAFF** promote the survival and activation of autoreactive B cells, which make antibodies to DNA, histones and ribonucleoproteins, with help from autoreactive T cells.
4. **Immune-complex injury.** Complexes deposit in glomeruli, skin, joints and serosa and activate complement (type III), while some antibodies destroy blood cells directly (type II).

Each step is a treatment target. **Hydroxychloroquine** interferes with endosomal TLR signalling and is recommended for almost all patients with SLE, unless contraindicated, with regular retinal screening. **Belimumab** blocks BAFF; **anifrolumab** blocks the type I interferon receptor; **rituximab** depletes B cells. Corticosteroids are used for flares at the lowest effective dose, and mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide for severe lupus nephritis, according to EULAR and national guidance.

SLE is more common and often more severe in women of Black African, Black Caribbean, South Asian and East Asian ancestry than in women of white European ancestry, with higher rates of nephritis. The malar rash may be harder to see on darker skin, where it may appear as hyperpigmentation or a violaceous rather than red rash; this is one reason for delayed diagnosis.

::: example A young woman with proteinuria {#ex-sle}
A 23-year-old woman of Black Caribbean ancestry has had painful swollen joints in her hands and recurrent mouth ulcers for 3 months. She now has ankle swelling. Her blood pressure is 152/96 mmHg. Urinalysis shows 3+ protein and blood; her urine protein:creatinine ratio is raised. Her haemoglobin is 98 g/L, platelets $85 \times 10^9$/L and lymphocytes $0.6 \times 10^9$/L. ANA is positive at 1:640, homogeneous; anti-dsDNA is strongly positive; C3 and C4 are low; CRP is normal. Explain the immunological picture and the next steps.
::: solution
**Diagnosis.** Arthritis, oral ulcers, cytopenias and renal involvement with a high-titre ANA, anti-dsDNA and low complement strongly suggest **SLE with lupus nephritis**. Under the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria she has the entry ANA and far more than 10 points.

**Mechanisms.** Anti-dsDNA antibodies form immune complexes that deposit in glomeruli and activate the classical complement pathway, consuming C3 and C4 (type III). Antibodies against red cells and platelets cause cytopenias (type II). Lymphopenia is common in active SLE. The normal CRP with active disease is typical of SLE, partly because type I interferon suppresses CRP production; a high CRP in a patient with lupus should raise the possibility of infection or serositis.

**Next steps.** This is potentially organ-threatening disease. She needs urgent referral to rheumatology and nephrology, and a **kidney biopsy** to determine the class of lupus nephritis, which guides treatment. Blood pressure control and reduction of proteinuria are important. Treatment usually includes hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids and an induction agent such as mycophenolate, according to current guidelines. Pregnancy planning is part of care, because SLE and many of its treatments affect pregnancy, and antiphospholipid and anti-Ro antibodies need to be checked.
:::
:::

## Organ-specific disease: two examples

**Autoimmune thyroid disease** is the commonest organ-specific autoimmunity. In **Hashimoto thyroiditis**, T cells destroy the gland, causing hypothyroidism; anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies are markers. In **Graves disease**, IgG antibodies **stimulate** the TSH receptor, mimicking TSH and causing thyrotoxicosis and a diffuse goitre; the same autoimmune process in the orbit causes thyroid eye disease. Because TRAb are IgG, they cross the placenta, and a woman with current or past Graves disease can have a baby with neonatal thyrotoxicosis even if she herself is now hypothyroid after treatment. See [[endocrine/thyroid]].

**Type 1 diabetes** is a T-cell-mediated destruction of pancreatic β cells. Autoantibodies to insulin, GAD, IA-2 and ZnT8 appear months or years before diagnosis; a child with two or more such antibodies has a very high lifetime risk of clinical diabetes. The anti-CD3 antibody **teplizumab** has been shown to delay the onset of clinical type 1 diabetes in high-risk relatives with two or more autoantibodies and abnormal glucose tolerance, the first treatment to change the course of the disease before diagnosis. See [[endocrine/diabetes]].

## Principles of treatment

Treatment aims to replace lost function, to suppress the immune response, or both.

- **Replacement** is enough for many organ-specific diseases in which the organ is destroyed: thyroxine in Hashimoto thyroiditis, insulin in type 1 diabetes, vitamin B12 in pernicious anaemia, glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid in Addison disease.
- **Broad immunosuppression**: corticosteroids, then steroid-sparing drugs such as methotrexate, azathioprine and mycophenolate.
- **Targeted therapies** directed at the mechanism: anti-TNF antibodies, IL-6 receptor blockade (tocilizumab), B-cell depletion (rituximab), costimulation blockade (abatacept), JAK inhibitors, and the newer drugs that target BAFF, type I interferon, complement or FcRn.
- **Removing antibody**: plasma exchange in anti-GBM disease and myasthenic crisis; intravenous immunoglobulin in some antibody-mediated conditions.

Every immunosuppressive strategy increases infection risk and needs screening (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, latent TB), vaccination before treatment where possible, and monitoring.

::: quiz
A 52-year-old woman has tiredness, a large tender tongue and a macrocytic anaemia. Her vitamin B12 is low. Which result most specifically supports pernicious anaemia as the cause?
- [ ] Positive gastric parietal cell antibody
- [x] Positive intrinsic factor antibody
- [ ] Positive ANA at 1:80
- [ ] Positive anti-TPO antibody
::: solution
Intrinsic factor antibodies are highly specific for pernicious anaemia, although present in only about half of patients. Parietal cell antibodies are more sensitive but much less specific, being common in older people and in other autoimmune diseases. ANA and anti-TPO antibodies do not identify the cause of B12 deficiency, although autoimmune thyroid disease frequently coexists with pernicious anaemia.

::: history
In 1901 Paul Ehrlich and Julius Morgenroth found that goats injected with their own red cells did not make antibodies to them, and Ehrlich coined the phrase *horror autotoxicus* for the body's aversion to self-attack. The phrase was often misread as a claim that autoimmunity was impossible, although Ehrlich recognised that regulatory mechanisms could fail. In 1904 Julius Donath and Karl Landsteiner described the antibody that causes paroxysmal cold haemoglobinuria, the first autoantibody linked to a disease.

The modern field began in the 1950s. In 1956 Ivan Roitt, Deborah Doniach, Peter Campbell and Vivian Hudson found antibodies to thyroglobulin in patients with Hashimoto thyroiditis, the disease Hakaru Hashimoto had described in 1912; in the same year Noel Rose and Ernest Witebsky produced thyroiditis in rabbits by immunising them with thyroid extract. In 1948 Malcolm Hargraves described the LE cell in the bone marrow of patients with lupus, which led to the discovery of antinuclear antibodies, and in the 1950s George Friou introduced immunofluorescence testing for them.
:::

::: summary
- Autoimmune disease is tissue damage from an adaptive response against self; about 1 in 10 people in the UK has at least one common autoimmune disease, women more often than men.
- It is multifactorial: HLA and many small-effect genes (PTPN22, CTLA4), female sex, and environmental triggers such as infection (molecular mimicry, bystander activation; EBV in multiple sclerosis), smoking (citrullination in rheumatoid arthritis), drugs and ultraviolet light.
- Damage follows hypersensitivity mechanisms: antibodies against cells or receptors (type II), immune complexes (type III) and T cells (type IV).
- Autoantibodies are diagnostic tools whose value depends on pre-test probability. ANA is sensitive for SLE but common in healthy people (13.3% at 1:80, 5% at 1:160); anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are specific.
- Classification criteria (2019 EULAR/ACR for SLE, 2010 ACR/EULAR for rheumatoid arthritis) define research populations and summarise key features; they are not required for diagnosis.
- In SLE, impaired clearance of apoptotic material, TLR7/9 sensing and type I interferon, BAFF-driven B-cell survival and immune-complex deposition combine; each is a treatment target.
- Treatment replaces lost function, suppresses the immune response broadly or targets a specific mechanism, with screening and vaccination to limit infection risk.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Positive predictive value of ANA {level=1 check="0.1*0.98/(0.1*0.98 + 0.9*0.13)"}
A rheumatologist sees a group of patients in whom the pre-test probability of SLE is 10%. ANA at 1:80 has sensitivity 98% and specificity 87%. What is the positive predictive value of a positive ANA? Give the unrounded decimal.
::: hint
Work with 1000 patients, or use $\text{PPV} = \dfrac{p \cdot \text{sens}}{p \cdot \text{sens} + (1-p)(1-\text{spec})}$.
:::
::: solution
True positives $= 0.1 \times 0.98 = 0.098$; false positives $= 0.9 \times 0.13 = 0.117$. PPV $= 0.098/(0.098 + 0.117) = 0.098/0.215 \approx 0.456$. With a 10% pre-test probability the PPV is about 46%, compared with about 13% at 2%. Pre-test probability matters as much as the test itself.
:::
:::

::: exercise Match the antibody {level=1}
Give the disease most strongly associated with each antibody: (a) anti-centromere; (b) anti-MuSK; (c) anti-PR3 ANCA; (d) antimitochondrial; (e) anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA.
::: solution
(a) Limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis. (b) Myasthenia gravis (MuSK subtype). (c) Granulomatosis with polyangiitis. (d) Primary biliary cholangitis. (e) Coeliac disease.
:::
:::

::: exercise Negative predictive value {level=1 check="0.98*0.87/(0.98*0.87 + 0.02*0.02)"}
Using a pre-test probability of 2%, sensitivity 98% and specificity 87%, calculate the negative predictive value of a negative ANA for SLE (unrounded decimal).
::: solution
True negatives $= 0.98 \times 0.87 = 0.8526$; false negatives $= 0.02 \times 0.02 = 0.0004$. NPV $= 0.8526/(0.8526 + 0.0004) \approx 0.99953$. A negative ANA in a low-probability patient virtually excludes SLE, which is why ANA is useful for ruling out rather than ruling in.
:::
:::

::: exercise A falsely reassuring coeliac test {level=2}
A 12-year-old boy with poor growth and iron deficiency has a negative anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA. His total IgA is undetectable. Explain why the antibody result cannot be relied on and what should be done instead.
::: solution
Anti-tTG IgA is an IgA antibody. A person with selective IgA deficiency cannot make any IgA, so the test will be negative whether or not he has coeliac disease. IgA deficiency is itself associated with coeliac disease (it is about ten times more common in people with coeliac disease than in the general population), so his risk is if anything higher. This is why total IgA is measured with every coeliac IgA serology test. Instead, an IgG-based test should be done (IgG anti-tTG, IgG anti-deamidated gliadin peptide or IgG endomysial antibody), with referral for duodenal biopsy while he is still eating gluten if suspicion remains.
:::
:::

::: exercise Graves disease in pregnancy {level=2}
A 31-year-old woman had Graves disease treated with radioactive iodine 4 years ago and now takes levothyroxine. She is 20 weeks pregnant. Why is her baby still at risk of thyroid disease, and which test answers the question?
::: solution
Radioactive iodine destroyed her thyroid tissue, but it does not remove the B-cell clones and long-lived plasma cells making **TSH-receptor antibodies** (TRAb). TRAb levels can stay high for years, especially after radioiodine. These are IgG antibodies, transported across the placenta by FcRn, mainly in the second half of pregnancy. They can stimulate the fetal thyroid, causing fetal and neonatal thyrotoxicosis, even though the mother is hypothyroid and takes levothyroxine. Measuring maternal TRAb in pregnancy identifies the risk; if it is raised, the fetus is monitored (heart rate, growth, goitre) and the newborn is assessed after birth. The neonatal disease is transient, resolving as maternal IgG is cleared over weeks to months.
:::
:::

::: exercise Scoring rheumatoid arthritis {level=2}
Under the 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria, points are given for joint involvement (0 to 5), serology (0 to 3), acute-phase reactants (0 to 1) and duration of symptoms (0 to 1). A patient has 12 swollen and tender joints including small joints (5 points), high-positive anti-CCP (3 points), a normal CRP and ESR (0 points) and symptoms for 4 weeks (0 points). What is the score, and is rheumatoid arthritis classified? Does this mean she does not have it?
::: solution
The score is $5 + 3 + 0 + 0 = 8$, which is at least 6 out of 10, so the patient is classified as having rheumatoid arthritis. Classification criteria are designed for research and early identification; a patient scoring less than 6 can still be diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis by a rheumatologist on clinical grounds, and should be followed up because she may meet the criteria later. Early specialist referral matters because treatment within the first months of disease improves outcomes.

::: exercise A family with clustering of disease {level=3}
A 9-year-old girl has type 1 diabetes. Her father has Graves disease, her aunt has pernicious anaemia, and her older brother has vitiligo. Explain why these diseases cluster in the family, why they mostly affect different organs, and which screening tests the girl should have as part of her routine care.
::: solution
**Clustering.** Organ-specific autoimmune diseases share genetic susceptibility, including HLA haplotypes (especially those containing DR3/DQ2 and DR4/DQ8) and non-HLA variants that alter immune regulation, such as *PTPN22* and *CTLA4* variants. These genes raise the general tendency to break tolerance rather than determining which organ is targeted. Which organ is attacked depends on other factors, including specific HLA alleles that present peptides from particular organs, organ-specific genes (such as variants affecting insulin expression in the thymus), environmental triggers and chance.

**Screening for the girl.** Under routine diabetes care she should have thyroid function tests (and often anti-TPO) at diagnosis and periodically, and coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA with total IgA) at diagnosis and if symptoms develop. Clinicians should be alert to features of Addison disease (unexplained hypoglycaemia, falling insulin requirement, pigmentation, salt craving) and pernicious anaemia, although these are much rarer in childhood. Rare monogenic syndromes such as APS-1 or IPEX should be considered if the pattern is unusual or very early.

::: exercise Interpreting a low complement {level=3}
A 35-year-old woman with known SLE has a rising creatinine and new proteinuria. Her anti-dsDNA has doubled and C3 and C4 are low. A colleague says the low complement shows she has an inherited complement deficiency. Evaluate this claim and explain how you would tell the difference.
::: solution
The claim is probably wrong. In active SLE, especially nephritis, immune complexes activate the classical pathway and **consume** C3 and C4, so low levels are a marker of disease activity. The rise in anti-dsDNA and the new renal involvement support a flare with consumption. An inherited deficiency is different: a homozygous deficiency of C1q, C2 or C4 causes absent or very low levels of the single affected component, persisting when the disease is quiet, and CH50 is absent. Partial C4 deficiency from variation in C4 gene copy number is common and can coexist with consumption. To tell the difference: measure C3 and C4 again when the disease is controlled (consumption normalises; inherited deficiency persists), measure CH50 and individual components, and in suspected C1q deficiency measure C1q directly. Early-onset SLE, a strong family history and severe skin disease raise the suspicion of an inherited early-component deficiency.
