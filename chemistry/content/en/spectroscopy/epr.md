Put a frozen solution of a copper(II) salt, a sample of irradiated sugar, or a drop of blood serum containing a nitroxide in a magnetic field of about a third of a tesla, bathe it in microwaves near $9.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$, and slowly sweep the field. At one field — and only there — the sample absorbs. Most molecules are silent: their electrons are paired, and paired spins have nothing to say. But any species with an unpaired electron — an organic radical, a transition-metal ion, a defect in a crystal, a triplet state — answers, and it answers with a wealth of detail. The position of the absorption tells you how the electron's orbital motion couples to its spin; the splitting of the line into a pattern of $2, 3, 4, \ldots, 25$ components tells you which magnetic nuclei the electron visits and how much time it spends on each. **Electron paramagnetic resonance** (EPR, also called electron spin resonance, ESR) is NMR's sibling for unpaired electrons, and because only paramagnetic species respond it can pick out a radical at micromolar concentration in a sea of diamagnetic solvent.

This lesson builds the resonance condition and the $g$ value, explains why $g$ departs from the free-electron value, derives the hyperfine patterns produced by equivalent and inequivalent nuclei, connects the measured couplings to Hückel spin densities through the McConnell relation, and finishes with spin labels, spin traps and transition-metal ions. The magnetic-resonance language — Larmor precession, populations, relaxation, the rotating frame — is shared with [[spectroscopy/nmr-principles]], and the general framework of transitions and line widths is that of [[spectroscopy/spectroscopy-principles]]. Constants are CODATA 2018 values: $\mu_\mathrm{B} = 9.274\,010\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}$, $g_\mathrm{e} = 2.002\,319$, $\mu_\mathrm{N} = 5.050\,784\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}$, and $h$, $k_\mathrm{B}$ exact. Hyperfine couplings are the standard literature values collected in Weil and Bolton, *Electron Paramagnetic Resonance* (2007), unless stated otherwise.

## The electron Zeeman effect and the resonance condition

An electron carries spin angular momentum $\hbar\sqrt{S(S+1)}$ with $S = \tfrac12$, and with it a magnetic moment that points *opposite* to the spin because the electron's charge is negative. In a field $\mathbf{B}$ along $z$, the energy of the moment is $-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B}$, and the two spin states $m_S = \pm\tfrac12$ separate.

::: definition The g value and the EPR resonance condition {#def-g}
For a paramagnetic species with effective spin $S = \tfrac12$ in a magnetic field $B$, the spin Hamiltonian is $\hat H = g\mu_\mathrm{B}B\hat S_z$, with eigenvalues
$$
E_{m_S} = g\mu_\mathrm{B}B\,m_S, \qquad m_S = \pm\tfrac12 ,
$$ {#eq-zeeman}
where $\mu_\mathrm{B} = e\hbar/2m_\mathrm{e}$ is the Bohr magneton and the dimensionless **g value** (g factor) characterises the species. The $m_S = -\tfrac12$ state ("$\beta$") lies lower. Absorption of a photon of frequency $\nu$ drives $\Delta m_S = \pm 1$ when the **resonance condition**
$$
h\nu = g\mu_\mathrm{B}B_\mathrm{res}
$$ {#eq-resonance}
is satisfied. For the free electron $g = g_\mathrm{e} = 2.002\,319$; in molecules $g$ is a property of the species, like a chemical shift.
:::

Equation [[#eq-resonance]] fixes the working combinations of frequency and field. Since $\mu_\mathrm{B}/h = 13.996\ \mathrm{GHz\,T^{-1}}$, a free electron resonates at $28.025\ \mathrm{GHz}$ per tesla, or $28.025\ \mathrm{MHz}$ per millitesla. Commercial spectrometers work in fixed bands named after radar conventions: **X-band** ($9$–$10\ \mathrm{GHz}$, resonance near $340\ \mathrm{mT}$), **Q-band** ($\approx 34\ \mathrm{GHz}$, $\approx 1.2\ \mathrm{T}$) and **W-band** ($94\ \mathrm{GHz}$, $3.35\ \mathrm{T}$). Unlike NMR, the frequency is held fixed — a microwave resonant cavity is efficient only at one frequency — and the *field* is swept. That is why EPR spectra are plotted against $B$ in millitesla (or gauss, $1\ \mathrm{mT} = 10\ \mathrm{G}$) and why a $g$ value, not a field, is the transferable quantity: the same radical resonates at $339\ \mathrm{mT}$ at $9.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$ and at $1.21\ \mathrm{T}$ at $34\ \mathrm{GHz}$.

::: example Measuring a g value {#ex-g-value}
A radical generated by oxidising a hydroquinone in basic solution gives a spectrum centred at $B = 338.40\ \mathrm{mT}$ in a spectrometer running at $\nu = 9.5000\ \mathrm{GHz}$. Find its $g$ value and comment on it. At what field would a free electron resonate?
::: solution
Rearranging equation [[#eq-resonance]],
$$
g = \frac{h\nu}{\mu_\mathrm{B}B} = \frac{6.626\,070\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s} \times 9.5000\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}}{9.274\,010\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J\,T^{-1}} \times 0.338\,40\ \mathrm{T}} = 2.0058 .
$$
The free electron would resonate at $B = h\nu/g_\mathrm{e}\mu_\mathrm{B} = 338.98\ \mathrm{mT}$, $0.58\ \mathrm{mT}$ higher. A shift $g - g_\mathrm{e} = +0.0035$ is too large for a carbon-centred radical (those have $g$ within about $0.001$ of $g_\mathrm{e}$) and typical of a semiquinone radical anion, in which a substantial part of the unpaired spin sits on the oxygen atoms, whose spin–orbit coupling is stronger. Note the precision needed: $g$ values are quoted to four or five decimal places, so the field must be measured to better than $0.01\ \mathrm{mT}$ — in practice with an NMR gaussmeter or against a standard such as DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl, $g = 2.0036$).
:::
:::

### Why EPR is sensitive: populations

The energy gap at X-band is small by chemical standards but large by NMR standards. The electron's magnetic moment is $658$ times that of a proton, so at a given field the electron Zeeman splitting is $658$ times larger; even though NMR magnets are about thirty times stronger than EPR electromagnets, the EPR transition at $9.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$ corresponds to an energy $24$ times that of a $400\ \mathrm{MHz}$ proton transition.

::: example Boltzmann populations at X-band {#ex-boltzmann}
Compute the ratio of upper to lower spin populations, and the fractional population difference $(N_\beta - N_\alpha)/(N_\beta + N_\alpha)$, for an $S = \tfrac12$ species at $9.50\ \mathrm{GHz}$ and $298\ \mathrm{K}$. Compare with protons in a $400\ \mathrm{MHz}$ NMR spectrometer, and with the EPR sample cooled to $4.2\ \mathrm{K}$ in liquid helium.
::: solution
The exponent is
$$
\frac{h\nu}{k_\mathrm{B}T} = \frac{6.626\,070\times10^{-34} \times 9.50\times10^{9}}{1.380\,649\times10^{-23} \times 298} = 1.530\times10^{-3},
$$
so $N_\alpha/N_\beta = \mathrm{e}^{-1.530\times10^{-3}} = 0.998\,47$ and the polarisation is $\tanh(h\nu/2k_\mathrm{B}T) = 7.65\times10^{-4}$ — fewer than one spin in a thousand is "in excess" in the lower level. For protons at $400\ \mathrm{MHz}$ the exponent is $6.44\times10^{-5}$ and the polarisation $3.2\times10^{-5}$, $24$ times smaller. Cooling the EPR sample to $4.2\ \mathrm{K}$ raises the exponent to $0.109$ and the polarisation to $5.4\,\%$, a seventy-fold gain: this, together with slower relaxation, is why transition-metal EPR is routinely measured at liquid-helium temperature. Higher polarisation, a larger magnetic moment and a higher frequency together make EPR intrinsically far more sensitive per spin than NMR; a modern X-band spectrometer detects of order $10^{10}$–$10^{11}$ spins in a narrow line.
:::
:::

### Field modulation and the first-derivative spectrum

Continuous-wave (CW) EPR spectrometers record not the absorption line itself but its **first derivative**. A small oscillating field, typically at $100\ \mathrm{kHz}$ and with an amplitude smaller than the line width, is added to the slowly swept main field; a phase-sensitive (lock-in) detector picks out the component of the microwave signal that oscillates at the modulation frequency. That component is proportional to the *slope* of the absorption at the current field, so the recorded trace crosses zero at the line centre, has a positive lobe on the low-field side and a negative lobe on the high-field side. The trick suppresses noise at all other frequencies and sharpens overlapping lines, at the price of a trace that takes some practice to read.

The derivative crosses zero at the absorption maximum; its two extrema lie at $\pm w/\sqrt{3}$, so the **peak-to-peak width** is $\Delta B_\mathrm{pp} = 2w/\sqrt{3}$ and the full width at half height is $\sqrt{3}\,\Delta B_\mathrm{pp}$ (for a Gaussian line the factor is $\sqrt{2\ln 2} = 1.177$). ::: warning The EPR trace is not the absorption {#warn-derivative}
Three errors follow from reading a derivative spectrum as if it were an absorption spectrum. First, the line position is the **zero crossing**, not either peak. Second, the *height* of a derivative line is not proportional to the number of spins: for lines of the same shape it scales as (number of spins)/(width)$^{2}$, so a line twice as broad looks four times weaker. To compare concentrations you must integrate twice (derivative → absorption → area). Third, in a hyperfine multiplet the relative peak heights match the binomial intensities only if all components have the same width — which is usually true for a small organic radical in fluid solution and often false for metal complexes.
:::

## Why g differs from 2: spin–orbit coupling

If the unpaired electron behaved as a free spin, every radical would resonate at $g_\mathrm{e}$ and the $g$ value would carry no chemistry. ($g_\mathrm{e}$ itself exceeds $2$ by $0.0023$, a quantum-electrodynamic correction, $g_\mathrm{e} \approx 2(1 + \alpha/2\pi + \cdots)$ with $\alpha$ the fine-structure constant, first calculated by Schwinger in 1948.) In a molecule, however, the electron also has orbital angular momentum, and the orbital motion carries a magnetic moment of its own. In most molecules the orbital angular momentum is **quenched**: the ground state is a real orbital, non-degenerate, with $\langle \hat L_z \rangle = 0$. Spin–orbit coupling, with coupling constant $\zeta$ or $\lambda$, mixes a little excited-state orbital angular momentum back into the ground state, and the electron's effective magnetic moment changes.

::: proposition The g shift from spin–orbit coupling {#prop-g-shift}
For a radical or ion with a non-degenerate orbital ground state, second-order perturbation theory in the spin–orbit coupling $\lambda\hat{\mathbf{L}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ and the orbital Zeeman term gives, for a field along axis $i$,
$$
g_{ii} = g_\mathrm{e} - 2\lambda\sum_{n \neq 0}\frac{\lvert\langle 0\vert\hat L_i\vert n\rangle\rvert^{2}}{E_n - E_0} ,
$$ {#eq-g-shift}
where the sum runs over excited orbital states $n$. The shift is of order $\lambda/\Delta E$: small when the spin–orbit coupling is weak (light atoms) or the excited states are far away, and positive or negative according to the sign of $\lambda$ — negative for a shell less than half full ($\lambda > 0$), positive for a shell more than half full ($\lambda < 0$, as for $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$, $d^{9}$, and for holes on oxygen).
:::

::: proof Origin of the formula (outline) {#proof-g-shift}
Write the Hamiltonian as $\hat H_0 + \mu_\mathrm{B}B(\hat L_i + g_\mathrm{e}\hat S_i) + \lambda\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}$. In the ground orbital state $\vert 0\rangle$ the first-order orbital term vanishes because $\langle 0\vert\hat L_i\vert 0\rangle = 0$ for a real non-degenerate wavefunction ($\hat L_i$ is purely imaginary, so its expectation value in a real state is both real and imaginary, hence zero). At second order, the cross term between the orbital Zeeman operator and spin–orbit coupling gives the energy
$$
E^{(2)} = -2\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{\langle 0\vert\mu_\mathrm{B}B\hat L_i\vert n\rangle\langle n\vert\lambda\hat L_i\hat S_i\vert 0\rangle}{E_n - E_0} = -2\lambda\mu_\mathrm{B}B\,\hat S_i\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{\lvert\langle 0\vert\hat L_i\vert n\rangle\rvert^{2}}{E_n - E_0},
$$
(the factor 2 counts the two orderings of the operators). This term is linear in $B$ and in $\hat S_i$, exactly like the spin Zeeman term $g_\mathrm{e}\mu_\mathrm{B}B\hat S_i$, so it can be absorbed into an effective $g_{ii}$: equation [[#eq-g-shift]]. The other second-order terms are either independent of the spin (diamagnetism) or quadratic in $\lambda$ (zero-field effects for $S > \tfrac12$).
:::

The proposition explains the empirical ranges at a glance. Carbon has an atomic spin–orbit coupling constant of only about $28\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and organic $\pi$ radicals have excited states $\sim 3\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ above the ground state, so $\lvert g - g_\mathrm{e}\rvert \sim 10^{-3}$: hydrocarbon radicals lie at $g = 2.0025$–$2.0030$ (methyl $2.0026$, the benzene radical anion $2.0028$). Nitrogen ($\zeta \approx 76\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) and oxygen ($\zeta \approx 151\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) push $g$ higher: nitroxides $2.006$, semiquinones $2.004$–$2.005$, peroxyl radicals about $2.015$. Heavier atoms and transition-metal ions, with $\lambda$ of hundreds of $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and low-lying $d$–$d$ excited states, give shifts of $0.1$ and more: $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ complexes have $g$ between $2.05$ and $2.4$. A $g$ value is therefore a first, coarse fingerprint of *where* the unpaired electron lives.

### Anisotropy: g as a tensor

In equation [[#eq-g-shift]] the matrix elements depend on the axis $i$, so in general $g$ depends on the orientation of the molecule in the field — $g$ is a symmetric tensor with three principal values $g_{xx}, g_{yy}, g_{zz}$ along molecule-fixed axes. In fluid solution, molecular tumbling (on a picosecond-to-nanosecond timescale) averages the tensor to its isotropic value $g_\mathrm{iso} = \tfrac13(g_{xx} + g_{yy} + g_{zz})$. In a single crystal the line moves as the crystal is rotated. In a frozen solution or powder every orientation is present at once, and the spectrum becomes a **powder pattern**: absorption spreads over the whole range of fields from $h\nu/g_\mathrm{max}\mu_\mathrm{B}$ to $h\nu/g_\mathrm{min}\mu_\mathrm{B}$, with the derivative showing features at the fields corresponding to the principal values.

For a species with an axis of threefold or higher symmetry (an **axial** system) two principal values coincide: $g_\parallel$ for the field along the unique axis and $g_\perp$ for the field anywhere in the perpendicular plane, with $g(\theta)^{2} = g_\parallel^{2}\cos^{2}\theta + g_\perp^{2}\sin^{2}\theta$. Because orientations perpendicular to the axis form a whole circle while parallel orientations form only two poles, the $g_\perp$ feature dominates the powder spectrum — a large derivative-shaped peak — while $g_\parallel$ appears as a weaker step at the other end. A tetragonally elongated $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ complex, whose unpaired electron is in $d_{x^2-y^2}$, typically shows $g_\parallel \approx 2.2$–$2.4 > g_\perp \approx 2.05 > g_\mathrm{e}$; the order $g_\parallel > g_\perp > 2$ is itself diagnostic of a $d_{x^2-y^2}$ ground state, as discussed for ligand fields in [[coordination-chemistry/crystal-field-theory]].

- [ ] A peroxyl radical $\mathrm{ROO^\bullet}$.
- [ ] A species with $S = 0$.
::: solution
A $g$ value within $0.0005$ of $g_\mathrm{e}$ means almost no orbital angular momentum is mixed in: the spin sits on light atoms with weak spin–orbit coupling, as in hydrocarbon radicals. $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ ($g \approx 2.05$–$2.4$) and peroxyl radicals ($g \approx 2.015$, spin largely on oxygen) are shifted far more. An $S = 0$ species has no EPR spectrum at all.
:::
:::

## Hyperfine coupling

The richest information in an EPR spectrum comes from the interaction of the electron spin with magnetic nuclei — the **hyperfine interaction**. A nucleus of spin $I$ has $2I + 1$ orientations $m_I = -I, \ldots, +I$, each producing a slightly different local magnetic field at the electron. Each orientation therefore shifts the electron's resonance by a different amount, and one line becomes $2I + 1$.

::: definition The isotropic hyperfine coupling {#def-hyperfine}
For an $S = \tfrac12$ species tumbling rapidly in solution, interacting with nuclei $k$ of spin $I_k$, the spin Hamiltonian is
$$
\hat H = g\mu_\mathrm{B}B\hat S_z + \sum_k h A_k\,\hat{\mathbf S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf I}_k ,
$$
where $A_k$ (in $\mathrm{MHz}$) is the **isotropic hyperfine coupling constant** of nucleus $k$. When $A_k \ll g\mu_\mathrm{B}B/h$ the energies are, to first order, $E = g\mu_\mathrm{B}Bm_S + \sum_k hA_k m_S m_{I,k}$, and the allowed transitions ($\Delta m_S = \pm1$, $\Delta m_I = 0$) occur at the fields
$$
B = B_0 - \sum_k a_k\,m_{I,k}, \qquad a_k = \frac{hA_k}{g\mu_\mathrm{B}}, \qquad B_0 = \frac{h\nu}{g\mu_\mathrm{B}} ,
$$ {#eq-hf-lines}
with the **hyperfine splitting** $a_k$ in millitesla. For $g = g_\mathrm{e}$, $A/\mathrm{MHz} = 28.025\times a/\mathrm{mT}$.
:::

The nuclear spin states are almost equally populated (their splitting is a thousand times smaller than the electron's), so all $2I+1$ components of a hyperfine multiplet from one nucleus have equal intensity. The nuclear spin is not flipped in an allowed EPR transition — it is a spectator whose orientation shifts the resonance field, much as a neighbouring spin shifts a proton's NMR line in [[spectroscopy/nmr-principles]], but with couplings a thousand times larger and expressed in field units.

### Where the isotropic coupling comes from: the Fermi contact interaction

A magnetic dipole interacting with another dipole through space gives an anisotropic energy that averages to zero when the molecule tumbles. An isotropic coupling that survives tumbling must have a different origin: the electron must have a finite probability density *at* the nucleus.

::: proposition The Fermi contact interaction {#prop-fermi}
The isotropic hyperfine coupling of a nucleus with nuclear $g$ factor $g_N$ is proportional to the unpaired-spin density at the nucleus,
$$
a_\mathrm{iso} = \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\,g_N\mu_\mathrm{N}\,\rho_\mathrm{s}(0), \qquad \rho_\mathrm{s}(0) = \lvert\psi(0)\rvert^{2}\ \text{for a single unpaired electron in orbital }\psi ,
$$ {#eq-fermi}
in field units (tesla). Only orbitals with $s$ character have $\psi(0) \neq 0$, so a pure $p$, $d$ or $\pi$ orbital contributes nothing directly; isotropic couplings to nuclei in the nodal plane of a $\pi$ radical arise indirectly, through spin polarisation, and can be negative.
:::

::: proof The hydrogen atom as a test {#proof-fermi}
The Fermi result follows from the magnetic field inside a uniformly magnetised sphere, $\tfrac{2}{3}\mu_0\mathbf{M}$, applied to the nuclear moment smeared over the region the electron occupies; we check it on the one case where $\psi(0)$ is known exactly. For hydrogen in its $1s$ state, $\lvert\psi(0)\rvert^{2} = 1/\pi a_0^{3}$ ([[quantum-chemistry/hydrogen-atom]]), and with $g_N = 5.585\,695$ for the proton,
$$
a_\mathrm{iso} = \frac{2 \times 1.256\,637\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}}{3} \times \frac{5.585\,695 \times 5.050\,784\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}}{\pi \times (5.291\,772\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m})^{3}} = 50.77\ \mathrm{mT}.
$$
Using the reduced-mass Bohr radius, $a_0(1 + m_\mathrm{e}/m_\mathrm{p})$, multiplies $\lvert\psi(0)\rvert^{2}$ by $(1 + m_\mathrm{e}/m_\mathrm{p})^{-3}$ and gives $50.69\ \mathrm{mT}$, against the measured $50.68\ \mathrm{mT}$ ($1420.41\ \mathrm{MHz}$ — the famous $21\ \mathrm{cm}$ line of interstellar hydrogen). The agreement to $0.02\,\%$ confirms that the isotropic coupling measures $s$-electron density at the nucleus.
:::

The hydrogen atom shows the scale: an electron *entirely* in a hydrogen $1s$ orbital produces $50.7\ \mathrm{mT}$. A radical whose proton coupling is $2.3\ \mathrm{mT}$ therefore has only about $4.5\,\%$ effective $1s$ spin density on that hydrogen. Hyperfine couplings are a map of the singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO), drawn atom by atom.

### Patterns from equivalent nuclei

::: proposition Hyperfine patterns from equivalent nuclei {#prop-patterns}
A set of $n$ equivalent nuclei of spin $I$, each with coupling $a$, splits an EPR line into
$$
2nI + 1
$$
equally spaced lines, separated by $a$, spanning a total width $2nIa$. The intensities are the number of ways of obtaining each total $M_I = \sum m_I$: for $I = \tfrac12$ they are the binomial coefficients $\binom{n}{k}$ (Pascal's triangle: $1{:}1$, $1{:}2{:}1$, $1{:}3{:}3{:}1$, …); for $I = 1$ ($^{14}\mathrm{N}$, $^{2}\mathrm{H}$) they are the coefficients of $(1 + x + x^{2})^{n}$ ($1{:}1{:}1$, $1{:}2{:}3{:}2{:}1$, …). Inequivalent sets multiply: sets with $n_1, n_2, \ldots$ nuclei of spins $I_1, I_2, \ldots$ give up to $\prod_j(2n_jI_j + 1)$ lines.
:::

::: proof Counting the orientations {#proof-patterns}
By equation [[#eq-hf-lines]] the shift of a line depends only on the sum $M_I = \sum_k m_{I,k}$ over the equivalent nuclei, which runs from $-nI$ to $+nI$ in integer steps: $2nI + 1$ values, separated by $a$ in field. All $(2I+1)^{n}$ combinations of nuclear orientations are equally populated, so the intensity of the line with total $M_I$ is the number of combinations giving that sum. For $I = \tfrac12$ choosing which $k$ of the $n$ spins point "up" gives $\binom{n}{k}$; for general $I$ the count is the coefficient of $x^{M_I + nI}$ in $(1 + x + \cdots + x^{2I})^{n}$, since each nucleus contributes one factor. For inequivalent sets the shifts add independently, so each line of the first pattern is split into the full pattern of the second, and so on; the line count is the product unless accidental coincidences merge lines.
:::

::: example The methyl radical {#ex-methyl}
The methyl radical $\mathrm{CH_3^\bullet}$, made by photolysis or radiolysis and trapped in a low-temperature matrix, has $g = 2.0026$ and $a_\mathrm{H} = 2.30\ \mathrm{mT}$ for its three equivalent protons. Predict the X-band spectrum at $9.500\ \mathrm{GHz}$: number of lines, positions and relative intensities. What would $\mathrm{CD_3^\bullet}$ show?
::: solution
Three equivalent $I = \tfrac12$ nuclei give $2 \times 3 \times \tfrac12 + 1 = 4$ lines with intensities $1{:}3{:}3{:}1$. The centre is at $B_0 = h\nu/g\mu_\mathrm{B} = 338.94\ \mathrm{mT}$, and the lines lie at $B_0 - a M_I$ with $M_I = +\tfrac32, +\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac32$:
$$
335.49,\quad 337.79,\quad 340.09,\quad 342.39\ \mathrm{mT},
$$
spaced by $2.30\ \mathrm{mT}$ and spanning $3a = 6.90\ \mathrm{mT}$. For $\mathrm{CD_3^\bullet}$ the electronic structure, and hence the spin density at each nucleus, is unchanged, so by equation [[#eq-fermi]] the coupling scales with the nuclear $g$ factor: $a_\mathrm{D} = a_\mathrm{H}\times g_N(\mathrm{D})/g_N(\mathrm{H}) = 2.30 \times 0.1535 = 0.353\ \mathrm{mT}$. Deuterium has $I = 1$, so three of them give $2\times3\times1 + 1 = 7$ lines with intensities $1{:}3{:}6{:}7{:}6{:}3{:}1$, spread over only $6 \times 0.353 = 2.12\ \mathrm{mT}$. Deuteration is the standard way of proving which hydrogens a coupling belongs to.
:::
:::

When there are several sets of equivalent nuclei the spectrum can contain dozens of lines, and building it by hand is a routine skill.

::: algorithm Building a hyperfine stick diagram {#alg-stick}
To predict the isotropic spectrum of a radical with several sets of equivalent nuclei:
1. Group the magnetic nuclei into sets of symmetry-equivalent nuclei and look up each set's spin $I_j$ and coupling $a_j$.
2. Order the sets by decreasing $a_j$.
3. Start with one line of intensity 1 at $B_0 = h\nu/g\mu_\mathrm{B}$.
4. Split it by the largest set into $2n_1I_1 + 1$ lines spaced $a_1$, with the intensities of [[#prop-patterns]].
5. Split *every* line of the result by the next set, multiplying intensities, and repeat for every set.
6. Add the intensities of any lines that fall at the same field; the total number of distinct lines is at most $\prod_j(2n_jI_j + 1)$, and the total width is $\sum_j 2n_jI_ja_j$.
7. To compare with a measured trace, convert the sticks to derivative lines of the observed width; to analyse a measured trace, run the steps in reverse — the outermost lines (intensity 1) give the smallest coupling by their spacing from the next line, and the total width must equal $\sum_j 2n_jI_ja_j$.
:::

::: example The ethyl radical {#ex-ethyl}
The ethyl radical $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2^\bullet}$ in solution has $a(\alpha\text{-H}, 2\mathrm{H}) = 2.24\ \mathrm{mT}$ and $a(\beta\text{-H}, 3\mathrm{H}) = 2.69\ \mathrm{mT}$ (Fessenden and Schuler, 1963). Construct the stick spectrum.
::: solution
Following [[#alg-stick]]: the larger coupling belongs to the three $\beta$ protons, which split the central line into a $1{:}3{:}3{:}1$ quartet at $\pm1.345$ and $\pm4.035\ \mathrm{mT}$ from the centre. Each quartet line is split by the two $\alpha$ protons into a $1{:}2{:}1$ triplet spaced $2.24\ \mathrm{mT}$. The result has $4 \times 3 = 12$ lines; relative to $B_0$, with intensities in brackets,
$$
\pm0.895\,(3),\quad \pm1.345\,(6),\quad \pm1.795\,(1),\quad \pm3.585\,(3),\quad \pm4.035\,(2),\quad \pm6.275\,(1)\ \mathrm{mT}.
$$
The total width is $3(2.69) + 2(2.24) = 12.55\ \mathrm{mT} = 2 \times 6.275$, as it must be. Because the two couplings are similar, the triplets interleave: the spectrum does not look like "a quartet of triplets" at first sight, and the stick diagram is the only safe way to assign it. A notable fact is hidden here — the $\beta$ protons, one bond further from the radical centre, couple *more* strongly than the $\alpha$ protons — and the next section explains why.
:::
:::

## Spin density and the McConnell relation

In a planar $\pi$ radical such as the methyl radical or the benzene radical anion, the unpaired electron occupies a $\pi$ orbital built from carbon $2p_z$ orbitals, which have a node in the molecular plane — exactly where the hydrogen nuclei sit. By equation [[#eq-fermi]] there should be no contact coupling to these **α protons**, yet the methyl radical shows $2.30\ \mathrm{mT}$. The explanation is **spin polarisation**: the unpaired $\pi$ electron interacts by exchange slightly differently with the two electrons of the $\mathrm{C{-}H}$ $\sigma$ bond. Hund-type exchange favours the $\sigma$ electron near carbon having the *same* spin as the $\pi$ electron, which leaves a small excess of the *opposite* spin at the hydrogen. The induced spin density at the proton is negative and proportional to the $\pi$ spin density on the attached carbon.

::: proposition The McConnell relation {#prop-mcconnell}
For an α proton attached to an $sp^{2}$ carbon atom of a $\pi$ radical,
$$
a_\mathrm{H} = Q\,\rho_\mathrm{C} ,
$$ {#eq-mcconnell}
where $\rho_\mathrm{C}$ is the unpaired $\pi$ spin density on the carbon and $Q$ is an empirical constant, about $-2.3$ to $-2.7\ \mathrm{mT}$ (calibrations: $Q = -2.30\ \mathrm{mT}$ from the methyl radical, $\rho = 1$; $Q = -2.25\ \mathrm{mT}$ from the benzene radical anion, $\rho = \tfrac16$; a value near $-2.7\ \mathrm{mT}$ fits the larger aromatic ions). In simple Hückel theory, $\rho_\mathrm{C} = c_\mathrm{C}^{2}$, the squared coefficient of that carbon's $p$ orbital in the SOMO. The relation is empirical — $Q$ depends somewhat on charge and geometry — and applies to α protons only; it is accurate to roughly $10\,\%$.
:::

Since a CW spectrum gives only $\lvert a\rvert$, the negative sign of $Q$ is invisible in an ordinary spectrum (it was established by NMR of radicals and by ENDOR); for the line pattern only magnitudes matter. **β protons** — those on a carbon adjacent to the radical centre, as in the $\mathrm{CH_3}$ group of ethyl — are coupled differently, by **hyperconjugation**: the $\mathrm{C{-}H}$ bond overlaps directly with the singly occupied $p$ orbital, putting positive spin density into the hydrogen $1s$ orbital. The coupling depends on the dihedral angle $\theta$ between the $\mathrm{C{-}H}$ bond and the $p$ orbital axis, $a_\beta \approx B\rho_\mathrm{C}\cos^{2}\theta$ with $B$ of roughly $5\ \mathrm{mT}$; for a freely rotating methyl group $\langle\cos^{2}\theta\rangle = \tfrac12$, which is why $a_\beta$ for ethyl ($2.69\ \mathrm{mT}$) is comparable to, and even larger than, $a_\alpha$. In rigid radicals the angular dependence is a conformational probe.

::: example Spin densities in the naphthalene radical anion {#ex-naphthalene}
Reducing naphthalene with sodium in an ether solvent gives the naphthalene radical anion, $\mathrm{C_{10}H_8^{\bullet-}}$. Its extra electron enters the Hückel LUMO ($x = -0.618$, [[quantum-chemistry/huckel-theory]]), whose squared coefficients are $0.181$ at the four α positions (1, 4, 5, 8), $0.069$ at the four β positions (2, 3, 6, 7) and $0$ at the bridgehead carbons. (a) Predict the couplings with $Q = -2.7\ \mathrm{mT}$. (b) How many lines does the spectrum have? The measured couplings are $0.490$ and $0.183\ \mathrm{mT}$.
::: solution
(a) By equation [[#eq-mcconnell]], $\lvert a_\alpha\rvert = 2.7 \times 0.181 = 0.49\ \mathrm{mT}$ and $\lvert a_\beta\rvert = 2.7 \times 0.069 = 0.19\ \mathrm{mT}$, in excellent agreement with $0.490$ and $0.183\ \mathrm{mT}$. The measured couplings imply $\lvert Q\rvert = 0.490/0.181 = 2.71$ and $0.183/0.069 = 2.65\ \mathrm{mT}$: a single $Q$ fits both positions, which is the real test of the relation.

(b) Two sets of four equivalent protons give $(2\times4\times\tfrac12 + 1)^{2} = 5 \times 5 = 25$ lines: a $1{:}4{:}6{:}4{:}1$ quintet of spacing $0.490\ \mathrm{mT}$, each line split into a $1{:}4{:}6{:}4{:}1$ quintet of spacing $0.183\ \mathrm{mT}$, with intensities from $1$ to $36$. The bridgehead carbons carry no hydrogen and, in Hückel theory, no spin; the spectrum is a direct experimental picture of the LUMO's nodal structure — and the fact that it agrees with Hückel theory in 1953–56 was one of the first quantitative confirmations of molecular-orbital theory for $\pi$ systems.
:::
:::

::: warning Counting lines is not the same as counting nuclei {#warn-counting}
A measured spectrum often shows fewer lines than $\prod(2n_jI_j + 1)$. Outer lines of large multiplets are weak (in the naphthalene anion the outermost lines have $1/36$ of the central intensity) and may be lost in the noise; couplings that happen to be nearly in simple ratio make lines coincide; a coupling smaller than the line width shows up only as broadening. Conversely, impurities or a second radical add lines. Always check a proposed assignment three ways: the number of lines, their relative intensities, and the total width $\sum 2n_jI_ja_j$ — and, when possible, simulate the spectrum and compare it with the trace.
:::

A neat consequence of the McConnell relation is a sum rule. Hückel spin densities in a single SOMO sum to 1 over all carbon atoms, so for a hydrocarbon ion in which every spin-bearing carbon carries one hydrogen, the total spectral width $\sum_j n_j\lvert a_j\rvert = \lvert Q\rvert\sum\rho = \lvert Q\rvert$. The benzene anion spans $6 \times 0.375 = 2.25\ \mathrm{mT}$, the naphthalene anion $4(0.490) + 4(0.183) = 2.69\ \mathrm{mT}$: the width of the whole spectrum is a direct estimate of $\lvert Q\rvert$.

## Spin labels and spin traps

Most molecules of interest are not radicals, but EPR can still study them by attaching or catching one.

**Nitroxide spin labels.** Nitroxides $\mathrm{R_2N{-}O^\bullet}$ with bulky alkyl groups, such as TEMPO (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl), are stable radicals that can be stored in a bottle. The unpaired electron sits in a $\pi^{*}$ orbital shared by nitrogen and oxygen, so the spectrum is a $1{:}1{:}1$ triplet from $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ ($I = 1$) with $a_\mathrm{N} \approx 1.5$–$1.7\ \mathrm{mT}$ (larger in polar, hydrogen-bonding solvents) and $g_\mathrm{iso} \approx 2.006$. The nitrogen hyperfine and $g$ tensors are strongly anisotropic, so the shape of the triplet depends on how fast the nitroxide tumbles: three sharp, equal lines when the rotational correlation time is below about $0.1\ \mathrm{ns}$, increasingly unequal and broadened lines up to a few nanoseconds, and a broad rigid-limit powder pattern spanning about $7\ \mathrm{mT}$ when motion is frozen. Attached covalently to a cysteine of a protein (site-directed spin labelling), a nitroxide reports local mobility and polarity, and the dipolar interaction between two labels measures distances of $1.5$–$8\ \mathrm{nm}$ in pulsed experiments.

**Spin trapping.** Short-lived radicals such as $\mathrm{HO^\bullet}$ or superoxide never build up to detectable concentrations. A **spin trap**, a nitrone or nitroso compound, adds the transient radical to give a persistent nitroxide whose couplings identify what was trapped. With DMPO (5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide), hydroxyl radical gives the DMPO–OH adduct with $a_\mathrm{N} = a_\mathrm{H} \approx 1.49\ \mathrm{mT}$: one nitrogen ($1{:}1{:}1$) and one β proton ($1{:}1$) with equal couplings produce, by [[#alg-stick]], a four-line $1{:}2{:}2{:}1$ pattern that is a textbook fingerprint. Spin trapping is how the radical intermediates of [[organic-1/radical-reactions]], of lipid peroxidation and of enzymatic redox chemistry are detected in practice — with the caution that adducts can also form by non-radical routes, so controls are essential.

## Transition-metal ions

Transition-metal ions with partly filled $d$ shells are the other great family of EPR-active species, and here every term of the spin Hamiltonian becomes large: $g$ shifts of $0.1$ or more, hyperfine couplings of $5$–$20\ \mathrm{mT}$ to the metal nucleus, and, for $S > \tfrac12$, zero-field splittings that can exceed the microwave quantum. Two general results organise the field.

::: proposition Kramers' theorem {#prop-kramers}
In the absence of a magnetic field, every energy level of a system with an **odd** number of electrons is at least doubly degenerate, whatever the symmetry of the electric (ligand) field. Such **Kramers ions** ($d^{1}$, $d^{3}$, $d^{5}$, $d^{7}$, $d^{9}$ and all radicals) always have a degenerate pair of levels that a magnetic field splits, and so always give an EPR signal if the relaxation is slow enough (usually at low temperature). Ions with an **even** number of electrons and $S \geq 1$ (non-Kramers ions, e.g. high-spin $d^{6}$ $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ or $d^{8}$ $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$) can have all their degeneracies removed by low-symmetry fields and zero-field splitting, and are often **EPR-silent** at X-band.
:::

The theorem is a consequence of time-reversal symmetry, which an electric field cannot break: for half-integer total spin, the time-reversed partner of a state is a different, orthogonal state with the same energy (Kramers, 1930). Only a magnetic field lifts the degeneracy. This is why biological EPR concentrates on $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$, low-spin $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ in haem proteins, iron–sulfur clusters with odd electron counts, $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Mo^{5+}}$, and why reduced $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ sites are often "invisible" ([[coordination-chemistry/bioinorganic]]).

Two cases show the metal hyperfine structure clearly.

- **Copper(II)**, $d^{9}$, $S = \tfrac12$. Both natural isotopes, $^{63}\mathrm{Cu}$ ($69\,\%$) and $^{65}\mathrm{Cu}$ ($31\,\%$), have $I = \tfrac32$, so each $g$ feature splits into **four** equally spaced lines of equal intensity; the two isotopes have magnetic moments differing by only $7\,\%$, so their quartets overlap almost exactly. In a frozen solution of a tetragonal complex the $g_\parallel$ region at low field shows four well-separated lines with $A_\parallel$ typically $13$–$20\ \mathrm{mT}$, while the $g_\perp$ region is a strong, often poorly resolved peak. The values of $g_\parallel$ and $A_\parallel$ together classify copper sites — the "blue" copper proteins, for instance, have unusually small $A_\parallel$.
- **Manganese(II)**, high-spin $d^{5}$, $S = \tfrac52$, $^{55}\mathrm{Mn}$ ($100\,\%$, $I = \tfrac52$). Its half-filled shell is an orbital singlet ($^{6}S$), so spin–orbit effects are tiny and $g \approx 2.00$; in aqueous solution, where zero-field splitting averages out, $[\mathrm{Mn(H_2O)_6}]^{2+}$ gives a clean sextet of six equal lines, separated by about $9.5\ \mathrm{mT}$ — a common contaminant signal in biological and environmental samples, and a convenient field marker.

::: example Reading a metal hyperfine pattern {#ex-metal}
A frozen-solution spectrum of a metalloprotein at $9.40\ \mathrm{GHz}$ shows, at low field, four equally intense, equally spaced lines centred at $B = 298.5\ \mathrm{mT}$ with spacing $17.0\ \mathrm{mT}$, and a strong derivative feature at $327.6\ \mathrm{mT}$. Identify the metal ion and compute $g_\parallel$, $g_\perp$ and $A_\parallel$ in $\mathrm{MHz}$.
::: solution
Four equal lines mean one nucleus with $2I + 1 = 4$, $I = \tfrac32$; together with an axial spectrum in which the low-field ($g_\parallel$) feature carries the large splitting, this is the signature of $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$. From equation [[#eq-resonance]],
$$
g_\parallel = \frac{h\nu}{\mu_\mathrm{B}B_\parallel} = \frac{6.626\,070\times10^{-34} \times 9.40\times10^{9}}{9.274\,010\times10^{-24} \times 0.2985} = 2.250, \qquad
g_\perp = \frac{h\nu}{\mu_\mathrm{B}\times 0.3276\ \mathrm{T}} = 2.050 .
$$
The order $g_\parallel > g_\perp > g_\mathrm{e}$ indicates a $d_{x^2-y^2}$ ground state (tetragonally elongated or square-planar copper). The coupling in frequency units uses the $g$ of that feature: $A_\parallel = g_\parallel\mu_\mathrm{B}a_\parallel/h = 2.250 \times 13.996\ \mathrm{GHz\,T^{-1}} \times 0.0170\ \mathrm{T} = 535\ \mathrm{MHz}$ (about $0.0179\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$). Converting with the free-electron factor $28.0\ \mathrm{MHz\,mT^{-1}}$ would underestimate it by $11\,\%$ — when $g$ differs from 2, always convert field splittings with the actual $g$.
:::
:::

Estimating such $g$ values from equation [[#eq-g-shift]] with the free-ion $\lambda(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}) \approx -830\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $d$–$d$ excitation energies of $(1.2$–$1.5)\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ gives $g_\parallel = g_\mathrm{e} - 8\lambda/\Delta_{xy} \approx 2.45$–$2.56$, somewhat larger than observed; the difference is a measure of covalency, which delocalises the unpaired electron onto the ligands and reduces the effective orbital angular momentum. EPR is thus one of the most direct experimental probes of metal–ligand covalency, a theme taken up in [[coordination-chemistry/ligand-field-mo]] and [[coordination-chemistry/magnetism]].

::: remark Practical and safety notes
EPR samples are usually in sealed quartz tubes (glass contains paramagnetic impurities and absorbs microwaves); aqueous samples need flat cells or capillaries because water absorbs microwaves strongly and detunes the cavity. Microwave powers are low (milliwatts to about $200\ \mathrm{mW}$) and enclosed in waveguides, but the electromagnet's stray field attracts ferromagnetic tools and can affect cardiac pacemakers and other implants, so access is controlled. Low-temperature work uses liquid nitrogen ($77\ \mathrm{K}$) or liquid helium ($4.2\ \mathrm{K}$) cryostats: cryogens cause cold burns and, by evaporating, can displace oxygen in a closed room, so they are handled with face shield and cryogenic gloves in ventilated rooms with oxygen monitors. As with all practical work, measurements are done under supervision and after a risk assessment.
:::

## Where this leads

EPR shares its formalism with NMR, and the two meet in double-resonance methods: **ENDOR** (electron–nuclear double resonance) measures hyperfine couplings as NMR-like frequencies with far higher resolution, and **dynamic nuclear polarisation** transfers the large electron polarisation of [[#ex-boltzmann]] to nuclei to boost NMR signals by orders of magnitude ([[spectroscopy/nmr-principles]]). Pulsed EPR, the analogue of Fourier-transform NMR, measures relaxation times and electron–electron distances. The $g$ and hyperfine tensors are computed routinely by the density-functional methods of [[computational-chemistry]], and the spin densities that EPR measures are the experimental face of the Hückel and molecular-orbital pictures of [[quantum-chemistry/huckel-theory]]. In inorganic and biological chemistry, EPR identifies the oxidation and spin states of metal centres in catalysts and enzymes, from the iron–sulfur clusters of [[biochemistry/oxidative-phosphorylation]] to the copper sites of [[coordination-chemistry/bioinorganic]]; in materials science it counts defects and dangling bonds in semiconductors; and in organic chemistry it detects the radical intermediates whose existence kinetics can only infer.

::: history
The idea that an electron has a spin and a magnetic moment of about one Bohr magneton came from the Stern–Gerlach experiment (1922) and from Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit's interpretation of atomic spectra (1925). Fermi derived the contact formula for hyperfine structure in 1930, the same year Kramers proved the degeneracy theorem that bears his name. Electron paramagnetic resonance itself was first observed by Yevgeny Zavoisky in Kazan in 1944, in salts of copper and manganese, at radio frequencies and fields of a few millitesla; the wartime development of microwave radar then made X-band spectrometers possible, and Brebis Bleaney's group in Oxford established the spin-Hamiltonian description of transition-metal ions in the late 1940s and 1950s. Schwinger's 1948 calculation of the anomalous moment explained why $g_\mathrm{e}$ is not exactly 2. In 1953 Samuel Weissman and co-workers resolved the hyperfine structure of the naphthalene radical anion, and in 1956 Harden McConnell related proton couplings to $\pi$ spin densities; McConnell's group introduced nitroxide spin labels for biological membranes and proteins in 1965.
:::

::: summary
- EPR detects species with unpaired electrons; the resonance condition is $h\nu = g\mu_\mathrm{B}B$, with $28.025\ \mathrm{MHz\,mT^{-1}}$ for $g_\mathrm{e} = 2.0023$, so X-band ($9.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$) resonance lies near $340\ \mathrm{mT}$. Spectra are field-swept and recorded as first derivatives; positions are zero crossings.
- The $g$ value departs from $g_\mathrm{e}$ through spin–orbit mixing, $\Delta g \sim -2\lambda\lvert L\rvert^{2}/\Delta E$: carbon radicals $2.0025$–$2.003$, O- and N-centred radicals $2.004$–$2.02$, metal ions $0.1$ or more away; $g$ is a tensor, averaged by tumbling in solution and spread into a powder pattern ($g_\parallel$, $g_\perp$) in frozen samples.
- The isotropic hyperfine coupling is the Fermi contact interaction, $a = \tfrac{2}{3}\mu_0 g_N\mu_\mathrm{N}\lvert\psi(0)\rvert^{2}$: $50.7\ \mathrm{mT}$ for a hydrogen atom, a direct measure of $s$ spin density at a nucleus.
- $n$ equivalent nuclei of spin $I$ give $2nI + 1$ lines (binomial $1{:}3{:}3{:}1$ for protons, $1{:}1{:}1$ for one $^{14}\mathrm{N}$); inequivalent sets multiply, and stick diagrams built from the largest coupling down assign the spectrum (ethyl: 12 lines; naphthalene anion: 25).
- α-proton couplings in $\pi$ radicals follow McConnell's empirical relation $a_\mathrm{H} = Q\rho_\mathrm{C}$, $Q \approx -2.3$ to $-2.7\ \mathrm{mT}$, with Hückel $\rho = c^{2}$; β protons couple by hyperconjugation as $\cos^{2}\theta$.
- Nitroxide spin labels report motion and distances; spin traps convert transient radicals (DMPO–OH: $1{:}2{:}2{:}1$) into persistent, identifiable nitroxides.
- Kramers ions (odd electron count) always give EPR; $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ shows four-line metal hyperfine ($I = \tfrac32$) with $g_\parallel > g_\perp > 2$, $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ a six-line sextet ($I = \tfrac52$).
- EPR's polarisation at X-band and $298\ \mathrm{K}$ is $7.6\times10^{-4}$, about 24 times that of protons at $400\ \mathrm{MHz}$; cooling to $4.2\ \mathrm{K}$ raises it to about $5\,\%$.
:::

::: exercise The width sum rule {level=3 check="2.69"}
(a) Show that for a planar hydrocarbon radical ion in which every carbon with non-zero Hückel spin density carries exactly one hydrogen, the McConnell relation predicts a total spectral width (outermost line to outermost line) of $\lvert Q\rvert$. (b) Using the measured naphthalene anion couplings ($4 \times 0.490$ and $4 \times 0.183\ \mathrm{mT}$), compute the total width, which estimates $\lvert Q\rvert$. (Enter the width in mT.) (c) Why do the bridgehead carbons not spoil the rule?
::: solution
(a) For protons ($I = \tfrac12$) the total width is $\sum_j 2n_jIa_j = \sum_j n_j\lvert a_j\rvert$, which is the sum of $\lvert a\rvert$ over all protons. With $\lvert a_k\rvert = \lvert Q\rvert\rho_k$ for the carbon bearing proton $k$, this is $\lvert Q\rvert\sum_k\rho_k$. The SOMO is normalised, $\sum_\mathrm{C} c_\mathrm{C}^{2} = 1$ in Hückel theory (overlap neglected), and if every carbon with $\rho \neq 0$ carries one proton, the sum over protons equals the sum over all carbons: width $= \lvert Q\rvert$.
(b) $4(0.490) + 4(0.183) = 1.960 + 0.732 = 2.69\ \mathrm{mT}$, so $\lvert Q\rvert \approx 2.69\ \mathrm{mT}$ — close to the $2.7\ \mathrm{mT}$ used in [[#ex-naphthalene]], and larger than the $2.25\ \mathrm{mT}$ of the benzene anion (six protons, $6 \times 0.375$): $Q$ is not perfectly constant.
(c) In the naphthalene LUMO the bridgehead coefficients are exactly zero (the orbital has a node through the central bond), so the carbons without hydrogens carry no spin and nothing is lost from the sum. For the anthracene anion the bridgehead atoms carry a small density ($4 \times 0.008$ in Hückel theory), so the measured width falls slightly short of $\lvert Q\rvert$ — and spin-polarisation effects beyond Hückel theory, which put small negative spin densities on some atoms, make the rule approximate in any case.
:::

::: exercise Line width and relaxation time {level=3 check="65.6"}
A Lorentzian EPR line with $g = g_\mathrm{e}$ has a peak-to-peak width $\Delta B_\mathrm{pp} = 0.100\ \mathrm{mT}$. (a) Derive the relation between $\Delta B_\mathrm{pp}$ and the half-width at half height $\Gamma$ of the absorption. (b) For a homogeneously broadened line, $\Gamma = 1/(\gamma_\mathrm{e}T_2)$ with $\gamma_\mathrm{e} = g_\mathrm{e}\mu_\mathrm{B}/\hbar$. Find $T_2$. (Enter $T_2$ in ns.)
::: solution
(a) The Lorentzian is $L(x) = \Gamma^{2}/(x^{2} + \Gamma^{2})$ with $x = B - B_\mathrm{res}$. Its derivative $L'(x) = -2x\Gamma^{2}/(x^{2} + \Gamma^{2})^{2}$ has extrema where $L''(x) = 0$: differentiating, $(x^{2} + \Gamma^{2}) - 4x^{2} = 0$, so $x = \pm\Gamma/\sqrt{3}$. Hence $\Delta B_\mathrm{pp} = 2\Gamma/\sqrt{3}$, i.e. $\Gamma = (\sqrt{3}/2)\Delta B_\mathrm{pp}$ and the full width at half height is $\sqrt{3}\,\Delta B_\mathrm{pp}$.
(b) $\Gamma = 0.866 \times 0.100\ \mathrm{mT} = 8.66\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{T}$; $\gamma_\mathrm{e} = 2.002\,319 \times 9.274\,010\times10^{-24}/1.054\,572\times10^{-34} = 1.7609\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}\,T^{-1}}$. Then $T_2 = 1/(1.7609\times10^{11} \times 8.66\times10^{-5}) = 6.56\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s} = 65.6\ \mathrm{ns}$. Electron relaxation is a million times faster than proton relaxation in NMR (seconds), which is why pulsed EPR needs nanosecond pulses, and why fast-relaxing metal ions (with $T_2$ in picoseconds at room temperature) give lines too broad to see until the sample is cooled.
:::
