A pressure cooker cooks stew in half the time; a spread of salt on an icy path clears it on a cold morning; a bottle of soda fizzes the moment you open it; and a cell placed in pure water swells until it bursts. None of these is a story about chemistry — they are stories about *physics of the condensed phase*, and all of them are told by the same handful of numbers: the vapour pressure of a liquid, the slope of a phase boundary, and the concentration of dissolved particles. This chapter is where liquids stop being the invisible background of chemistry and become the subject itself.

The organizing idea is simple. A liquid is a phase held together by intermolecular forces that are weak enough to be constantly losing molecules to the gas above it, and any property of a solution that depends only on *how many* solute particles are present — not on what they are — is called **colligative**. Vapour-pressure lowering, boiling-point elevation, freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure are the four colligative properties, and they are all one phenomenon seen from four directions: the solute dilutes the solvent, and the solvent responds by evaporating, freezing and osmosing less readily.

## Intermolecular forces and vapour pressure

::: definition Intermolecular forces {#def-imf}
The **intermolecular forces** that hold a liquid together are, in order of typical strength: **hydrogen bonding** (a $\mathrm{H}$ atom bonded to $\mathrm{F}$, $\mathrm{O}$ or $\mathrm{N}$ attracted to a lone pair on another such atom, roughly $10$–$40\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$); **dipole–dipole** interaction between permanent molecular dipoles (roughly $1$–$10\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$); and **dispersion (London) forces**, generated by correlated instantaneous fluctuations of the electron cloud (under $\sim 5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for small molecules but dominant for large or polarizable ones, and the only force in noble gases, $\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2}$ and hydrocarbons). Dispersion forces grow with the number of electrons and with molecular surface area, which is why the alkanes boil in order of chain length.
:::

Because these forces are so much weaker than covalent bonds ($\sim 400\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), a liquid is a crowded, constantly rearranging assembly in which molecules are forever evaporating from the surface and condensing back. In a closed vessel the two directions of this exchange eventually proceed at equal rates, and the vapour above the liquid then has a steady pressure.

::: definition Vapour pressure and volatility {#def-vapour-pressure}
The **vapour pressure** $p^{*}$ of a liquid is the equilibrium pressure of its vapour above the pure liquid at a given temperature. A liquid with a high vapour pressure at room temperature is called **volatile**. Vapour pressure depends on temperature and on the strength of the intermolecular forces, and on nothing else: not on the amount of liquid, not on the volume of the container, not on the presence of other gases (to the accuracy of first-year chemistry).
:::

::: example Ranking boiling points {#ex-imf-ranking}
Arrange propane $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_3}$, dimethyl ether $\mathrm{CH_3OCH_3}$, ethanol $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH}$ and ethane-1,2-diol (ethylene glycol) $\mathrm{HOCH_2CH_2OH}$ in order of increasing normal boiling point, and account for the order. These four molecules have molar masses $44$, $46$, $46$ and $62\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ — nearly the same size.
::: solution
Propane is nonpolar: dispersion forces only, and it boils lowest, at $-42\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Dimethyl ether has a permanent dipole but no $\mathrm{O–H}$ bond, so it cannot hydrogen-bond to itself; dipole–dipole forces lift its boiling point only to $-24\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Ethanol hydrogen-bonds through its single $\mathrm{O–H}$ group and boils at $78.4\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Ethylene glycol has two $\mathrm{O–H}$ groups and forms roughly twice the hydrogen-bond network per molecule; it boils at $197\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — over a hundred degrees higher than ethanol of nearly the same mass. Two molecules of almost identical mass and formula differ by $220\ \mathrm{K}$ of boiling point purely because of how many hydrogen bonds each can make: intermolecular forces, not covalent structure, decide the boiling point.
:::
:::

::: warning Evaporation, boiling, and what vapour pressure is not {#warn-vapour-pressure}
Three confusions are worth killing now. First, **evaporation and boiling are different processes**: evaporation is surface loss below the boiling point and happens at any temperature; boiling is the formation of vapour *bubbles within the bulk*, which can only happen when the vapour pressure equals the external pressure. Second, the bubbles in a kettle of boiling water are **water vapour, not air** — the dissolved air has already been driven off long before the first bubble. Third, vapour pressure is an **intensive equilibrium property**: pouring the liquid into a bigger container, or adding helium above it, does not change it, and a drop of water and a lake have the same vapour pressure at the same temperature. The mistake of "heating increases the vapour pressure, so the vapour pressure pushes the boiling point up" gets the causality backwards — the liquid boils when its (temperature-determined) vapour pressure reaches the (externally fixed) pressure.
:::

::: quiz
A sealed flask half-filled with water is at equilibrium at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ with a vapour pressure of $3.17\ \mathrm{kPa}$. Some of the water is carefully evaporated away in the dark so that only a thin film remains, still at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. What is the vapour pressure now?
- [x] Still $3.17\ \mathrm{kPa}$, as long as any liquid water remains.
- [ ] Proportional to the fraction of liquid left.
- [ ] Zero, because there is no free surface.
- [ ] It depends on the volume of the flask.
::: solution
Still $3.17\ \mathrm{kPa}$. Equilibrium vapour pressure is set by temperature alone; the amount of liquid only determines how long the system takes to reach equilibrium and whether any liquid survives. If *all* the liquid were removed, the pressure would fall below $p^{*}$ and the vapour would no longer be saturated.
:::
:::

## The Clausius–Clapeyron equation

Vapour pressure is tabulated at a few temperatures, but the useful science is predicting it at temperatures nobody measured. That is the job of the Clapeyron equation and its famous approximation.

::: proposition The Clapeyron equation {#prop-clapeyron}
Along any line of two-phase equilibrium of a pure substance, pressure and temperature are tied together by
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\Delta H}{T\,\Delta V},
$$
where $\Delta H$ and $\Delta V$ are the enthalpy and volume changes of the transition at temperature $T$. The coexistence curves of a pure substance are therefore one-dimensional lines, not regions — a fact we will use again when we meet phase diagrams.
:::

::: proposition The Clausius–Clapeyron equation {#prop-clausius-clapeyron}
For liquid–vapour (or solid–vapour) equilibrium, with the vapour treated as an ideal gas and the condensed-phase volume neglected, the Clapeyron equation becomes
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln p}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}{R\,T^{2}}
\qquad\text{and, if } \Delta_\mathrm{vap}H \text{ is taken constant,}\qquad
\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1} = -\frac{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right).
$$
A plot of $\ln p$ against $1/T$ is a straight line of slope $-\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H/R$; this is the standard way of measuring enthalpies of vaporization.
:::

::: proof
Start from the Clapeyron equation with the transition liquid $\rightarrow$ vapour. Because one mole of vapour occupies some $24\ \mathrm{dm^3}$ at ordinary conditions while one mole of liquid occupies a few tens of $\mathrm{cm^3}$, $\Delta V \approx V_\mathrm{m,gas}$. Treating the vapour as ideal, $V_\mathrm{m,gas} = RT/p$, so
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}{T \cdot RT/p} = \frac{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}{R}\,\frac{p}{T^{2}}.
$$
Separating the variables gives $\mathrm{d}\ln p = (\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H/R)\,\mathrm{d}T/T^{2}$. If $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$ is independent of temperature over the range considered, integration between $(T_1, p_1)$ and $(T_2, p_2)$ yields the two-point form. $blacksquare$
:::

::: example An enthalpy of vaporization from two points {#ex-dHvap-water}
The vapour pressure of water is $3.17\ \mathrm{kPa}$ at $25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $47.36\ \mathrm{kPa}$ at $80.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Estimate $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$ over this range.
::: solution
Rearrange the two-point form:
$$
\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H = \frac{R\,\ln(p_2/p_1)}{1/T_1 - 1/T_2}
= \frac{8.314\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \times \ln(47.36/3.17)}{\left(1/298.15 - 1/353.15\right)\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}}
= \frac{8.314 \times 2.704}{5.223\times10^{-4}} = 4.30\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
So $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H \approx 43.0\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. That is neither the tabulated $44.0\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ nor the $40.7\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ at $100\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — it is the *average* over the interval, and it lies neatly between the two, which is the correct way to read the approximation. The assumption of constant $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$ is the only approximation made, and over $55\ \mathrm{K}$ it costs about $3\ \%$.
:::
:::

::: example Cooking at altitude, cooking under pressure {#ex-altitude}
In a town where the atmospheric pressure is $75.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$, at what temperature does water boil? And in a sealed cooker that reaches $2.00\ \mathrm{atm}$ total pressure, at what temperature does it boil? Take $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H = 40.7\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.
::: solution
Solve the two-point form for $T_2$ with $p_1 = 101.325\ \mathrm{kPa}$, $T_1 = 373.15\ \mathrm{K}$:
$$
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{373.15} - \frac{R}{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}
= 2.6799\times10^{-3} - \frac{8.314}{40\,700}\ln\frac{75.0}{101.325}
= 2.6799\times10^{-3} + 0.6145\times10^{-4} = 2.7413\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}},
$$
so $T_2 = 364.8\ \mathrm{K} = 91.6\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Water boils nearly nine degrees cooler, which is why pasta and pulses take much longer at altitude — the food never gets hotter than the boiling point. For the cooker, $\ln(202.65/101.325) = 0.693$ gives $1/T_2 = 2.6799\times10^{-3} - 1.416\times10^{-4} = 2.5383\times10^{-3}$, $T_2 = 394.0\ \mathrm{K} = 121\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Twenty per cent more pressure buys twenty-eight degrees of cooking temperature and roughly doubles the rate of most kitchen chemistry — that is the whole idea of the pressure cooker.
:::
:::

::: history
The quantitative study of vapours begins with Dalton, who in 1801–1802 measured the "tension" of water vapour and of other liquids and recognized that each vapour in a mixture behaves as if the others were absent. Faraday liquefied chlorine and carbon dioxide by pressure and cold in 1823, and for decades afterwards these "permanent gases" resisted liquefaction, which made the discovery by Thomas Andrews in 1869 of a **critical temperature** above which no pressure liquefies a gas so decisive: every gas has one, and it is a property of the molecule, not of technique. Van der Waals gave the first molecular account in 1873. The solution laws came in the 1880s: Raoult's vapour-pressure measurements of 1887 and van 't Hoff's recognition in 1886–1887 that dilute solutions obey the gas laws, $\Pi V = nRT$, gave solution chemistry the quantitative form you are about to use.
:::

## Phase diagrams

::: definition The phase diagram {#def-phase-diagram}
A **phase diagram** plots pressure against temperature and shows which phase (or mixture of phases) of a pure substance is stable at each point. The three **coexistence curves** — sublimation (solid–vapour), vaporization (liquid–vapour) and fusion (solid–liquid) — meet at the **triple point**, the unique $(T, p)$ at which three phases coexist; the vaporization curve ends at the **critical point** $(T_\mathrm{c}, p_\mathrm{c})$, beyond which the substance is a single **supercritical fluid**. Each curve is a line, not a region, because a one-component system with two phases in equilibrium has one degree of freedom by the phase rule $F = C - P + 2 = 1$.
:::

The two diagrams every student should know by heart are those of water and carbon dioxide. Water's triple point is at $0.01\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $611.7\ \mathrm{Pa}$ — *below* atmospheric pressure — so at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ ice melts to a liquid and the liquid boils at $100\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Carbon dioxide's triple point is at $5.11\ \mathrm{bar}$ and $-56.6\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — *above* atmospheric pressure — so at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ liquid $\mathrm{CO_2}$ does not exist at all: solid $\mathrm{CO_2}$ ("dry ice") sublimes at $194.7\ \mathrm{K}$, and gaseous $\mathrm{CO_2}$ cannot be liquefied by pressure alone above $31.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, its critical temperature. Dry ice is a serious asphyxiation hazard in unventilated spaces because the subliming gas is dense, invisible and displaces air; handle it with insulating gloves because at $194.7\ \mathrm{K}$ it freezes skin on contact.

::: example Why the ice–water line slopes backwards {#ex-ice-slope}
For ice melting at $273.15\ \mathrm{K}$, $\Delta_\mathrm{fus}H = 6.01\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, and the molar volumes are $19.66\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$ (ice) and $18.02\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$ (water). Find the slope of the melting curve and the pressure needed to lower the melting point by $1\ \mathrm{K}$.
::: solution
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\Delta_\mathrm{fus}H}{T\,\Delta_\mathrm{fus}V}
= \frac{6010\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}}{273.15\ \mathrm{K} \times (18.02 - 19.66)\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}}
= \frac{6010}{-4.48\times10^{-4}} = -1.35\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{Pa\,K^{-1}},
$$
i.e. $-135\ \mathrm{bar\,K^{-1}}$. The slope is negative because ice is *less* dense than water — melting shrinks the sample, so pressure favours the liquid. A $1\ \mathrm{K}$ depression needs $135\ \mathrm{bar}$, a hundred and thirty times the atmosphere.
:::
:::

::: warning Ice skating is not pressure melting {#warn-ice-skating}
The old explanation of ice skating — the blade melts the ice because its pressure lowers the melting point — fails the arithmetic. A $70\ \mathrm{kg}$ skater on blades $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ wide and $8\ \mathrm{cm}$ long in contact exerts about $86\ \mathrm{bar}$, which lowers the melting point by only $0.6\ \mathrm{K}$: useless on ice at $-10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and the blade sinks in until the contact area is far larger and the pressure smaller anyway. The accepted explanation is a combination of frictional heating and **surface premelting** — the outermost molecular layers of ice are disordered and quasi-liquid well below $0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — with a thin water film providing the lubrication. The episode is a useful caution: a mechanism that is *real* (pressure does lower the melting point of ice) can still be the *wrong explanation* of an everyday phenomenon by two orders of magnitude.
:::

::: quiz
Why does a block of dry ice at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ and room temperature turn directly into gas, while a block of ice turns into water?
- [x] The triple-point pressure of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ ($5.11\ \mathrm{bar}$) lies above $1\ \mathrm{atm}$, so no liquid phase is stable at atmospheric pressure.
- [ ] $\mathrm{CO_2}$ has no liquid phase at any pressure.
- [ ] Dry ice is warmer than ice because sublimation is exothermic.
- [ ] The atmosphere contains $\mathrm{CO_2}$, which prevents melting.
::: solution
The triple point decides. At pressures below the triple-point pressure, heating a solid takes it straight from solid to vapour: the liquid region simply is not reached. $\mathrm{CO_2}$ *does* have a liquid phase — above $5.11\ \mathrm{bar}$, as any fire extinguisher shows — and above its critical temperature of $31.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ it becomes a supercritical fluid instead. Sublimation, like all vaporization-like transitions, is endothermic.
:::
:::

## Solubility and Raoult's law

Dissolution competes two energies against one entropy: solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions must be broken (endothermic) while solute–solvent attractions form (exothermic), and the mixing entropy drives the process regardless. "Like dissolves like" is the bookkeeping rule: polar and hydrogen-bonding solutes dissolve in polar solvents because the new interactions pay for the old; hydrocarbons dissolve in hydrocarbons because dispersion forces are all around and only the entropy of mixing is at work.

::: definition Henry's law {#def-henry}
The solubility of a gas in a liquid is proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid:
$$
c = k_\mathrm{H}\,p,
$$
where $k_\mathrm{H}$ is the Henry's-law constant (for $\mathrm{O_2}$ in water at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $k_\mathrm{H} = 1.25\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,bar^{-1}}$; for $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $3.4\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,bar^{-1}}$ — standard tabulated values). Henry's law is a limiting law: it holds for dilute solutions of non-reacting gases, and it fails for gases like $\mathrm{NH_3}$ or $\mathrm{HCl}$ that react with water.
:::

::: example The oxygen in a river and the gas in a soda {#ex-henry}
(a) River water at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is in equilibrium with air at $1.00\ \mathrm{bar}$, in which $p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 0.200\ \mathrm{bar}$. Find the dissolved oxygen concentration in $\mathrm{mg\,dm^{-3}}$. (b) A soda is bottled under $\mathrm{CO_2}$ at $3.0\ \mathrm{bar}$. Find the dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$ concentration, and say what happens when the bottle is opened to air, where the effective $\mathrm{CO_2}$ partial pressure is about $3.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{bar}$.
::: solution
(a) $c = k_\mathrm{H} p = 1.25\times10^{-3} \times 0.200 = 2.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$, and with $M(\mathrm{O_2}) = 32.0\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$,
$$
c = 2.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \times 32.0\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 8.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{g\,dm^{-3}} = 8.0\ \mathrm{mg\,dm^{-3}}.
$$
That is the whole oxygen budget of warm aerobic water — which is why warm, polluted (oxygen-consuming) water kills fish, and why the number is a legally monitored quantity. (b) Bottled: $c = 3.4\times10^{-2} \times 3.0 = 0.10\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$. Opened: equilibrium demands only $3.4\times10^{-2} \times 3.5\times10^{-4} = 1.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$, a factor of $8600$ less. The soda is enormously supersaturated with respect to air, and the excess leaves as bubbles — the fizz. Henry's law also explains "the bends": dissolved nitrogen in a diver's blood comes out of solution as the pressure falls.
:::
:::

::: definition Ideal solutions and Raoult's law {#def-raoult}
A solution is **ideal** if the solute–solvent interactions equal the solute–solute and solvent–solvent interactions they replace. An ideal solution obeys **Raoult's law**: the partial vapour pressure of each component equals its mole fraction times the vapour pressure of the pure liquid,
$$
p_\mathrm{A} = x_\mathrm{A}\,p_\mathrm{A}^{*}, \qquad p_\mathrm{B} = x_\mathrm{B}\,p_\mathrm{B}^{*},
$$
and the total vapour pressure is $p = x_\mathrm{A}p_\mathrm{A}^{*} + x_\mathrm{B}p_\mathrm{B}^{*}$. Real solutions deviate: ethanol–water shows a positive deviation (the hydrogen-bond network is broken, raising $p$), hydrochloric acid–water a large negative one (ionisation and strong hydration, lowering $p$).
:::

::: proposition Vapour-pressure lowering {#prop-raoult-lowering}
For a solution of a **non-volatile** solute, the vapour pressure is that of the solvent alone, $p = x_\mathrm{A} p_\mathrm{A}^{*}$, so the lowering is
$$
\Delta p = p_\mathrm{A}^{*} - p = x_\mathrm{B}\,p_\mathrm{A}^{*},
$$
proportional to the mole fraction of solute. For dilute solutions this is proportional to molality, and for electrolytes each formula unit contributes approximately $i$ particles, where $i$ is the **van 't Hoff factor**.
:::

::: example Vapour-pressure lowering by sucrose {#ex-sucrose}
Calculate the vapour pressure at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ of a solution of $45.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of sucrose $\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}}$ ($M = 342.3\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$) in $100.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of water. The vapour pressure of pure water at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is $3.17\ \mathrm{kPa}$.
::: solution
$n_\mathrm{sucrose} = 45.0/342.3 = 0.1315\ \mathrm{mol}$ and $n_\mathrm{water} = 100.0/18.02 = 5.551\ \mathrm{mol}$, so
$$
x_\mathrm{water} = \frac{5.551}{5.551+0.1315} = 0.9769,
\qquad
p = x_\mathrm{water}\,p^{*} = 0.9769 \times 3.17 = 3.10\ \mathrm{kPa}.
$$
Sucrose is non-volatile (its vapour pressure is negligible), so the whole pressure is water's, reduced by $2.3\ \%$ — exactly the mole fraction of sucrose. Note the size of the effect: even a syrupy solution barely changes its vapour pressure, which already warns us that vapour-pressure measurements will need a sensitive balance for molar-mass work, and that the *colligative* properties built on this same dilution (next section) are more convenient levers.
:::
:::

## Colligative properties

::: definition Colligative properties and the van 't Hoff factor {#def-colligative}
A **colligative property** depends only on the *number* of dissolved particles, not their identity. The four are: vapour-pressure lowering, boiling-point elevation, freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure. For an electrolyte that dissociates into $\nu$ ions, the particle count is multiplied by the **van 't Hoff factor** $i$, which is $\nu$ for a strong electrolyte at infinite dilution but smaller in real solutions because of ion pairing ($i \approx 1.87$ for $0.1\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\ \mathrm{NaCl}$, not $2$).
:::

::: proposition The colligative laws {#prop-colligative}
For a dilute ideal solution of a non-volatile solute:
$$
\Delta T_\mathrm{b} = i\,K_\mathrm{b}\,m, \qquad
\Delta T_\mathrm{f} = i\,K_\mathrm{f}\,m, \qquad
\Pi = i\,c\,R\,T,
$$
where $m$ is the molality ($\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$) in the first two laws and $c$ the molar concentration ($\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$) in the second, and $\Pi$ is the osmotic pressure. The molal constants are properties of the *solvent only*: $K_\mathrm{b} = RT_\mathrm{b}^{2}M_\mathrm{A}/\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$ and $K_\mathrm{f} = RT_\mathrm{f}^{2}M_\mathrm{A}/\Delta_\mathrm{fus}H$. For water, $K_\mathrm{b} = 0.512$ and $K_\mathrm{f} = 1.86\ \mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$; for benzene, $K_\mathrm{b} = 2.53$ and $K_\mathrm{f} = 5.12\ \mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$ (standard tabulated data).
:::

::: proof Boiling-point elevation
At the boiling point of the solution, the solvent's vapour pressure must equal the external pressure $p^\circ$. By Raoult's law the solution reaches $p^\circ$ at a higher temperature than the pure solvent because $p_\mathrm{A} = x_\mathrm{A}p_\mathrm{A}^{*} < p_\mathrm{A}^{*}$. Write $p_\mathrm{A}^{*}(T_\mathrm{b} + \Delta T_\mathrm{b}) = p^\circ/x_\mathrm{A} = p^\circ(1 + x_\mathrm{B} + \cdots)$, and expand the left side with the Clausius–Clapeyron equation:
$$
\ln\frac{p_\mathrm{A}^{*}(T_\mathrm{b}+\Delta T_\mathrm{b})}{p_\mathrm{A}^{*}(T_\mathrm{b})}
= -\frac{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_\mathrm{b}+\Delta T_\mathrm{b}} - \frac{1}{T_\mathrm{b}}\right)
\approx \frac{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}{R\,T_\mathrm{b}^{2}}\,\Delta T_\mathrm{b}.
$$
The left side is $\ln(1/x_\mathrm{A}) = -\ln x_\mathrm{A} \approx x_\mathrm{B}$ for dilute solution. Hence
$$
\Delta T_\mathrm{b} = \frac{R\,T_\mathrm{b}^{2}}{\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H}\,x_\mathrm{B}.
$$
Converting mole fraction to molality ($x_\mathrm{B} \approx n_\mathrm{B}/n_\mathrm{A} = M_\mathrm{A} m$, with $M_\mathrm{A}$ the solvent molar mass in $\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$) gives $\Delta T_\mathrm{b} = K_\mathrm{b} m$ with $K_\mathrm{b} = RT_\mathrm{b}^{2}M_\mathrm{A}/\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$. For water: $K_\mathrm{b} = 8.314 \times 373.15^{2} \times 0.01802 / 40\,700 = 0.512\ \mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$, reproducing the tabulated value from molecular data. The identical argument run on the *fusion* curve — the solution freezes when the solvent's chemical potential in the liquid falls to that of the solid — gives $\Delta T_\mathrm{f} = K_\mathrm{f} m$ with $K_\mathrm{f} = RT_\mathrm{f}^{2}M_\mathrm{A}/\Delta_\mathrm{fus}H = 1.86\ \mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$ for water. $blacksquare$
:::

At $i = 1$ (glucose, urea — non-electrolytes) a $1.0\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ solution freezes at $-1.86\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$; at $i = 1.87$ (measured, $0.1\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\ \mathrm{NaCl}$) the same molality depresses the freezing point by $3.5\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Road-salt crews and ice-cream makers live on the right-hand end of this line; car radiators need the whole line, because $2\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ of *salt* would be unacceptably corrosive, while $10.8\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ of ethylene glycol — $i = 1$, non-corrosive — protects to $-20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

::: example Antifreeze by the arithmetic {#ex-antifreeze}
How much ethane-1,2-diol (ethylene glycol, $M = 62.07\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, $i = 1$) must be added to $4.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water in a car radiator to protect down to $-20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$?
::: solution
The required molality is
$$
m = \frac{\Delta T_\mathrm{f}}{i\,K_\mathrm{f}} = \frac{20\ \mathrm{K}}{1 \times 1.86\ \mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}} = 10.8\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}},
$$
so $n = 10.8 \times 4.0 = 43\ \mathrm{mol}$ and $m_{\mathrm{glycol}} = 43 \times 62.07 = 2.7\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{g} = 2.7\ \mathrm{kg}$, about $2.4\ \mathrm{L}$ of the liquid (density $1.11\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$). That is a large volume — the radiator ends up roughly half glycol — and it shows how weak the colligative lever is per mole: freezing-point depression is $1.86\ \mathrm{K}$ per mole of *particles*, so protection to $-20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ costs ten moles. A safety note in the same breath: methanol gives the same depression at half the mass but is oxidized in the body to formaldehyde and formic acid, and is toxic by ingestion and inhalation; ethylene glycol is itself poisonous (it is metabolized to oxalate, which attacks the kidneys) and is handled as a hazardous liquid, never stored in drink containers. Both belong in labelled containers and a fume-aware workshop, and spills are cleaned up, not hosed to the drain.
:::
:::

::: example A molar mass from the freezing point {#ex-molar-mass-dtf}
A $0.520\ \mathrm{g}$ sample of an unknown non-electrolyte dissolved in $50.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of water depresses the freezing point by $0.108\ \mathrm{K}$. Find the molar mass.
::: solution
$$
m = \frac{\Delta T_\mathrm{f}}{K_\mathrm{f}} = \frac{0.108}{1.86} = 0.0581\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}},
\qquad
n = 0.0581 \times 0.0500 = 2.90\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol},
$$
$$
M = \frac{0.520\ \mathrm{g}}{2.90\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol}} = 179\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
Cryoscopy (freezing-point measurement) is a genuinely useful molar-mass method precisely because $K_\mathrm{f}$ is large for many solvents — benzene's $5.12$ makes it four times more sensitive than water per mole. The precision is set by how well $\Delta T_\mathrm{f}$ can be measured: a thermometer readable to $0.01\ \mathrm{K}$ limits this determination to a few per cent.
:::
:::

::: example Osmotic pressure: the most sensitive lever {#ex-osmotic}
Find the osmotic pressure of $0.100\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ glucose at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and compare the sensitivity of osmometry with cryoscopy for a $0.100\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ solution.
::: solution
$$
\Pi = i c R T = 1 \times 0.100\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \times 8.314\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \times 298.15\ \mathrm{K}
= 248\ \mathrm{J\,dm^{-3}} = 248\ \mathrm{kPa},
$$
about $2.45\ \mathrm{atm}$ — enough to drive a column of water $25\ \mathrm{m}$ high. The same solution's freezing-point depression is only $0.186\ \mathrm{K}$, and its boiling-point elevation $0.051\ \mathrm{K}$. Osmotic pressure is hundreds of times more sensitive per mole, which is why it is the method of choice for large molecules: for a protein of $M \sim 5\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ even, a $1\ \mathrm{g\,dm^{-3}}$ solution gives a measurable $\Pi$ while $\Delta T_\mathrm{f}$ would be $10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K}$. Biologically, $248\ \mathrm{kPa}$ is the scale of the turgor pressure that holds up a herbaceous plant; osmosis is the reason a cell in pure water bursts and a lettuce leaf wilts, and reverse osmometry — applying more than $\Pi$ to force water *out* of salt water — is how seawater is desalinated industrially.
:::
:::

::: warning Colligative traps {#warn-colligative}
Four errors account for most lost marks. (1) **$i$ is not an integer.** A $0.100\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ $\mathrm{NaCl}$ solution depresses the freezing point by $0.348\ \mathrm{K}$, not $0.372\ \mathrm{K}$: ion pairing makes $i = 1.87$. Use the measured or given $i$, and never invent $i = 2$ for a concentrated electrolyte. (2) **Molality in the $\Delta T$ laws, molarity in $\Pi = cRT$.** The boiling and freezing laws are temperature-independent because molality is; osmometry is done at one temperature, so molarity is natural there. (3) **Osmosis goes the other way from intuition.** Solvent crosses the membrane *towards* the more concentrated solution — the side with more particles ends up with more volume. (4) **Colligative means particles, not molecules.** For $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ count three ions per formula unit (ideal $i = 3$); for a covalent non-electrolyte count one; and for a weak electrolyte like acetic acid count $1 + \alpha$.
:::

## Where this leads

Vapour pressure is the equilibrium pressure of a phase change, and equilibrium in general is [[general-chemistry-2/chemical-equilibrium]]; the acid–base and solubility equilibria built on it begin with [[general-chemistry-2/acids-bases]]. The gas-law algebra used here comes from [[general-chemistry-1/gases]], the solution stoichiometry from [[general-chemistry-1/solutions-reactions]], and the enthalpies behind the Clausius–Clapeyron equation from [[general-chemistry-1/thermochemistry]]. The phase rule and the rigorous chemical-potential derivation of the colligative laws are in [[thermodynamics/phase-equilibria]] and [[thermodynamics/mixtures]].

::: summary
- Vapour pressure is an intensive equilibrium property fixed by temperature and intermolecular forces; a liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure.
- The Clausius–Clapeyron equation $\ln(p_2/p_1) = -(\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H/R)(1/T_2 - 1/T_1)$ turns two vapour-pressure measurements into an enthalpy of vaporization, and explains altitude cooking and pressure cookers.
- Phase diagrams are maps of these equations: water's triple point lies below $1\ \mathrm{atm}$, carbon dioxide's above it, which is why dry ice sublimes; the ice–water line slopes backwards at $-135\ \mathrm{bar\,K^{-1}}$, but pressure melting does not explain ice skating.
- Gas solubility follows Henry's law $c = k_\mathrm{H}p$; oxygen in rivers and fizz in soda are the same equation.
- Raoult's law $p_\mathrm{A} = x_\mathrm{A}p_\mathrm{A}^{*}$ says an ideal solution's vapour pressure is the mole-fraction-weighted sum of the pure values; a non-volatile solute lowers it by $x_\mathrm{B}p_\mathrm{A}^{*}$.
- The four colligative properties count particles, not species: $\Delta T_\mathrm{b} = iK_\mathrm{b}m$, $\Delta T_\mathrm{f} = iK_\mathrm{f}m$, $\Pi = icRT$, with $K_\mathrm{b} = RT_\mathrm{b}^2 M_\mathrm{A}/\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$ and $K_\mathrm{f} = RT_\mathrm{f}^2 M_\mathrm{A}/\Delta_\mathrm{fus}H$ properties of the solvent alone.
- Osmotic pressure is by far the most sensitive colligative lever and the one that moves water through cell membranes and desalination plants.
:::

::: exercise Freezing point of a glucose drip {level=1 check="0.465"}
Calculate the freezing-point depression of $0.250\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ glucose in water. (Enter $\Delta T_\mathrm{f}$ in $\mathrm{K}$.)
::: solution
Glucose is a non-electrolyte, $i = 1$: $\Delta T_\mathrm{f} = iK_\mathrm{f}m = 1 \times 1.86 \times 0.250 = 0.465\ \mathrm{K}$. The solution freezes at $-0.465\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

::: exercise Vapour pressure of a sugar solution {level=1 check="3.16"}
Calculate the vapour pressure at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ of a solution of $0.200\ \mathrm{mol}$ sucrose in $1.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water. $p^{*}(\mathrm{H_2O}, 25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}) = 3.17\ \mathrm{kPa}$. (Enter $p$ in $\mathrm{kPa}$.)
::: solution
$n_\mathrm{water} = 1000/18.02 = 55.5\ \mathrm{mol}$, so $x_\mathrm{water} = 55.5/(55.5+0.200) = 0.9964$ and $p = 0.9964 \times 3.17 = 3.16\ \mathrm{kPa}$.

::: exercise Osmotic pressure of blood substitute {level=1 check="387"}
A glucose solution for intravenous use is $0.150\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$. Calculate its osmotic pressure at $37\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. (Enter $\Pi$ in $\mathrm{kPa}$.)
::: solution
$\Pi = icRT = 1 \times 0.150 \times 8.314 \times 310.15 = 387\ \mathrm{kPa}$, about $3.8\ \mathrm{atm}$ — close to the osmotic pressure of blood, which is why this concentration is called isotonic.

::: exercise Enthalpy of vaporization of benzene {level=2 check="31.1"}
Benzene has vapour pressure $26.1\ \mathrm{kPa}$ at $40.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and boils at $80.1\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at $101.325\ \mathrm{kPa}$. Estimate $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$. (Enter $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$ in $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.)
::: solution
$\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H = R\ln(p_2/p_1)/(1/T_1 - 1/T_2) = 8.314 \times \ln(101.325/26.1)/(1/313.15 - 1/353.25) = 8.314 \times 1.357/3.625\times10^{-4} = 31.1\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, in good agreement with the tabulated $30.8\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.
:::

::: exercise Boiling on a mountain {level=2 check="96.0"}
At a hill station the barometer reads $88.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$. At what temperature does water boil there? Take $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H = 40.7\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. (Enter $T$ in $^\circ\mathrm{C}$.)
::: solution
$1/T = 1/373.15 - (R/\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H)\ln(88.0/101.325) = 2.6799\times10^{-3} + 0.287\times10^{-4} = 2.7086\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$, so $T = 369.2\ \mathrm{K} = 96.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Tea brews, but pulses do not soften — the reason hill-station kitchens keep pressure cookers.
:::

::: exercise Derive the Clausius–Clapeyron equation {level=3}
State the Clapeyron equation and derive the Clausius–Clapeyron equation from it, naming every approximation. Why does the approximation fail badly near the critical point?
::: solution
The Clapeyron equation $\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}T = \Delta H/(T\Delta V)$ follows from equality of chemical potentials of the two phases along the coexistence line. Three approximations convert it to the Clausius–Clapeyron form: (i) $\Delta V \approx V_\mathrm{m,gas}$, dropping the liquid volume — good to about $0.2\ \%$ at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$; (ii) the ideal gas law $V_\mathrm{m,gas} = RT/p$ — good at low pressure, and the dominant error near the boiling point of polar liquids; (iii) $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H$ independent of $T$, which introduces an error of order $(C_{p,\mathrm{g}} - C_{p,\mathrm{l}})\Delta T/T$ over a range $\Delta T$. Near the critical point all three fail at once: the liquid and vapour molar volumes become equal, so $\Delta V \rightarrow 0$ and the Clapeyron slope stays finite while the ideal-gas replacement is meaningless; $\Delta_\mathrm{vap}H \rightarrow 0$ at $T_\mathrm{c}$. A plot of $\ln p$ against $1/T$ for water is visibly curved over $0$–$370\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and the straight-line treatment is only a local device.
:::
:::

::: exercise Boil and freeze: identify the solute {level=3}
A solution of $30.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of an unknown non-electrolyte in $250\ \mathrm{g}$ of water freezes at $-0.930\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Find the molality and the molar mass of the solute, and predict the boiling point of the same solution.
::: solution
The molality follows from the freezing point alone: $m = \Delta T_\mathrm{f}/K_\mathrm{f} = 0.930/1.86 = 0.500\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. The amount of solute is $0.500 \times 0.250 = 0.125\ \mathrm{mol}$, so $M = 30.0/0.125 = 240\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. The boiling-point elevation is then $\Delta T_\mathrm{b} = 0.512 \times 0.500 = 0.256\ \mathrm{K}$, so the solution boils at $100.256 \approx 100.26\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The point of the problem: one colligative measurement fixes the particle concentration, and *every other* colligative property follows from it — the four properties are one fact measured four ways, and consistency between them is the classic test that the solute is a non-electrolyte.
:::
:::

::: exercise Molar mass of a protein by osmometry {level=3 check="50.5"}
A solution of $25.0\ \mathrm{mg}$ of a protein in $25.0\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of water ($\rho = 1.00\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$) develops an osmotic pressure at $25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ that supports a column of water $5.00\ \mathrm{mm}$ high. Calculate the molar mass of the protein. (Enter $M$ in $\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$.)
::: solution
The osmotic pressure is $\Pi = \rho g \Delta h = 1000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \times 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \times 5.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m} = 49.1\ \mathrm{Pa}$. The mass concentration is $c = 25.0\ \mathrm{mg}/25.0\ \mathrm{cm^3} = 1.00\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and from $\Pi V = nRT$,
$$
M = \frac{cRT}{\Pi} = \frac{1.00 \times 8.314 \times 298.15}{49.1} = 50.5\ \mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}},
$$
i.e. $5.05\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. The same solution's freezing-point depression would be $2.7\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K}$ — unmeasurable — which is the whole reason osmometry is the classical method for protein molar masses.
:::
