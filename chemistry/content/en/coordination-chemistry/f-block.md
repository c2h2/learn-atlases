The red of a television screen in the 1960s, the green of a fluorescent tube, the light from a white LED, the magnets in a wind-turbine generator and an electric car's motor, the anti-counterfeiting marks on a euro banknote and the contrast agent of an MRI scan all depend on the **lanthanides**, the fifteen elements from lanthanum to lutetium. For a century and a half they were the despair of analytical chemists. Their trivalent ions are so alike that separating neighbours took thousands of fractional crystallisations, and the true number of elements in the series was settled only when Henry Moseley's X-ray spectra fixed their atomic numbers in 1913. The **actinides**, from actinium to lawrencium, are the elements of nuclear energy; all are radioactive, and those beyond uranium are made artificially.

Both series fill an $f$ subshell, and the chemistry of each follows from one property of its $f$ orbitals: how far they reach out from the nucleus. The $4f$ orbitals of the lanthanides are buried inside the filled $5s$ and $5p$ shells. They hardly take part in bonding, so the lanthanides behave as a series of hard, nearly identical $\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ ions whose $f$ electrons keep the sharp spectra and large magnetic moments of free ions. The early $5f$ orbitals reach further, so uranium, neptunium and plutonium show the varied oxidation states and covalent bonding of transition metals, before the later actinides become lanthanide-like. This chapter develops both pictures, using the term symbols of [[coordination-chemistry/spectra-of-complexes]] and the magnetism of [[coordination-chemistry/magnetism]].

## The 4f orbitals and the lanthanide contraction

::: definition The f block and its radial problem {#def-f-block}
The **lanthanides** (Ln) are the elements La–Lu ($Z = 57$–$71$), whose $\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ ions have configurations $[\mathrm{Xe}]4f^n$ with $n = 0$ (La) to $14$ (Lu); with scandium and yttrium they form the **rare-earth elements**. The **actinides** (An) are Ac–Lr ($Z = 89$–$103$), filling $5f$. The seven $f$ orbitals ($l = 3$) have three angular nodes each. The $4f$ radial function has its maximum *inside* the maxima of the filled $5s$ and $5p$ orbitals, so $4f$ electrons are shielded from the ligands by eight electrons further out; they interact with ligands only weakly (crystal field splittings of about $100\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, against $10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ for $3d$ ions). The $5f$ orbitals extend further relative to the $6s$ and $6p$ shells, especially for the early actinides, and take part in bonding.
:::

Because the $4f$ electrons are inner electrons, they shield one another poorly from the nucleus. Each step from one lanthanide to the next adds one proton and one $4f$ electron, the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons rises, and the ions shrink: the **lanthanide contraction**.

About 10–20 % of the contraction is a relativistic effect: inner $s$ and $p$ electrons of heavy atoms move fast enough that their mass increase contracts their orbitals, and the outer shells contract with them. The rest is the poor shielding by $4f$ electrons.

## The chemistry of the lanthanide ions

### Oxidation states

The +3 state dominates the whole series. Its stability is not explained by the $4f$ configuration, which varies from $f^0$ to $f^{14}$, but by energetics: removing the three outer electrons ($5d^16s^2$ or $4f\,6s^2$) is affordable, the fourth ionisation energy (from the $4f$ shell) is large, and the lattice and hydration energies of a small $3+$ ion are large. A few other oxidation states appear where they reach an empty, half-filled or full $4f$ shell:

- **cerium(IV)**, $4f^0$: $E^\circ(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}/Ce^{3+}}) \approx +1.72\ \mathrm{V}$ (formal potentials $+1.44$ to $+1.70\ \mathrm{V}$, depending on the acid), a strong one-electron oxidant used in cerimetric titrations and organic synthesis (as ceric ammonium nitrate, $\mathrm{(NH_4)_2[Ce(NO_3)_6]}$);
- **europium(II)**, $4f^7$: $E^\circ(\mathrm{Eu^{3+}/Eu^{2+}}) = -0.35\ \mathrm{V}$, stable enough to exist in water for some time;
- **ytterbium(II)**, $4f^{14}$, and **samarium(II)**, $4f^6$: $E^\circ = -1.05$ and $-1.55\ \mathrm{V}$, powerful reductants; samarium(II) iodide in THF (Kagan's reagent) is widely used in organic synthesis;
- terbium(IV) and praseodymium(IV) in oxides and fluorides only.

### Coordination chemistry of Ln³⁺

$\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ ions are large, highly charged, hard Lewis acids ([[coordination-chemistry/complex-stability#prop-hsab]]) with no ligand field preferences. Their bonding is predominantly ionic, so their complexes have high and variable coordination numbers set by the size of the ion and the steric demands of the ligands: nine for the early aqua ions $\mathrm{[Ln(H_2O)_9]^{3+}}$ (tricapped trigonal prism), eight for the later ones, up to twelve with small chelating ligands such as nitrate. They prefer oxygen donors (water, carboxylates, phosphates, β-diketonates) and fluoride; amine and sulfur donors bind weakly in water, which they cannot displace. Ligand exchange is very fast ($k_\mathrm{ex}$ for water about $10^8$–$10^9\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$), and geometric isomers are rarely isolable.

::: example Separating the lanthanides {#ex-separation}
Two neighbouring lanthanides differ in radius by about 1 pm (0.1 %) and form complexes whose $\log K$ values with a chelating ligand typically differ by 0.1–0.3. (a) If $\log K$ differs by 0.20 between neighbours, what separation factor $\alpha$ does one equilibrium stage of a solvent-extraction process give? (b) How many ideal stages are needed to enrich a 50:50 mixture to 99.9 % purity of one component? (c) Why does the order of elution in cation-exchange chromatography with a complexing eluent run from Lu to La?
::: solution
(a) The separation factor of one stage is the ratio of distribution ratios, which for complexation-controlled extraction is the ratio of formation constants: $\alpha = 10^{0.20} = 1.58$. (b) After $N$ stages the ratio of the two elements changes from 1 to $\alpha^N$. For 99.9 % purity the ratio must be $999$: $N = \log 999/\log 1.58 = 3.00/0.20 = 15$ ideal stages (in practice several times more, because real stages are not ideal and the yield must be high). Industrial lanthanide separation plants use hundreds of mixer-settler stages in series. (c) The heavier, smaller ions (towards Lu) form the stronger complexes with the eluent (for example citrate or α-hydroxyisobutyrate), so they spend more time in solution and less on the resin, and they come off first. Frank Spedding's ion-exchange separations of the 1940s, using exactly this principle, produced the first kilogram quantities of pure lanthanides.
:::
:::

## Spectra and magnetism: free-ion behaviour

Because the ligand field is tiny compared with spin–orbit coupling (which for $4f$ electrons is about $10^3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), the lanthanide ions behave almost as free ions. The relevant states are Russell–Saunders terms split by spin–orbit coupling into levels $^{2S+1}L_J$, and the ligand field only splits each level slightly. The order is the reverse of the $d$ block, where the ligand field dominates and spin–orbit coupling is a small correction.

::: proposition Ground levels of the Ln³⁺ ions (Hund's rules) {#prop-hund-j}
The ground level of a $4f^n$ ion is found by (1) maximising $S$, (2) maximising $L$ for that $S$, and (3) taking $J = |L - S|$ for $n < 7$ (less than half-filled) and $J = L + S$ for $n > 7$. For $f^7$, $L = 0$ and $J = S = \tfrac72$. The excited levels of the ground term lie at energies given by the Landé interval rule, $E(J) - E(J - 1) = \lambda J$, with typical separations of $10^3$–$10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, except for $\mathrm{Sm^{3+}}$ and $\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}$, whose first excited levels are only a few hundred $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ up.
:::

::: proof
Rules (1) and (2) are those of [[coordination-chemistry/spectra-of-complexes#ex-ground-terms]], applied to the seven $f$ orbitals ($m_l = 3, \dots, -3$): fill singly with parallel spins first, starting at the largest $m_l$. For rule (3), spin–orbit coupling has the form $\lambda\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S}$ within a term, and $\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S} = \tfrac12[J(J+1) - L(L+1) - S(S+1)]$, so the energy rises with $J$ if $\lambda > 0$ and falls if $\lambda < 0$. For a single electron the spin–orbit constant is positive, and for a less than half-filled shell the term constant $\lambda = \zeta/2S$ is positive, so the lowest $J$ lies lowest. For a more than half-filled shell the holes behave as positive charges and the sign of $\lambda$ reverses, so the highest $J$ lies lowest. The interval rule follows from the same expression: $E(J) - E(J - 1) = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}[J(J+1) - (J-1)J] = \lambda J$.
:::

For example, $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$, $4f^3$: three electrons in $m_l = 3, 2, 1$, $S = \tfrac32$, $L = 6$ (I), $J = L - S = \tfrac92$: $^4I_{9/2}$. $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$, $4f^9$: seven electrons with parallel spins and two paired in $m_l = 3, 2$, so $S = \tfrac52$, $L = 5$ (H), $J = L + S = \tfrac{15}{2}$: $^6H_{15/2}$.

### Magnetic moments

When $J$ is a good quantum number and only the ground level is populated, the moment is determined by $J$ and the **Landé $g$ factor**, not by the spin alone.

::: proposition Magnetic moments of the lanthanide ions {#prop-lande}
For an ion in a level with quantum numbers $S$, $L$, $J$, with only that level populated,
$$
\mu_\mathrm{eff} = g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}\ \mu_\mathrm{B}, \qquad g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J+1) + S(S+1) - L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)},
$$ {#eq-lande}
taking $g_e = 2$. The Curie law of [[coordination-chemistry/magnetism#prop-curie]] holds with $g_J^2J(J+1)$ in place of $g^2S(S+1)$.
:::

::: proof
The magnetic moment operator is $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} = -\mu_\mathrm{B}(\hat{\mathbf L} + 2\hat{\mathbf S})/\hbar$: the orbital angular momentum contributes with $g = 1$ and the spin with $g_e = 2$. Because $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ precess rapidly about their resultant $\mathbf{J}$, only the projection of the moment onto $\mathbf{J}$ survives on average (the vector model, or formally the projection theorem for vector operators within a level of fixed $J$):
$$
\langle\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\rangle = -\mu_\mathrm{B}\,\frac{(\mathbf{L} + 2\mathbf{S})\cdot\mathbf{J}}{J(J+1)}\,\mathbf{J} = -g_J\mu_\mathrm{B}\mathbf{J},
$$
with $\hbar = 1$. Now $(\mathbf{L} + 2\mathbf{S})\cdot\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{J}^2 + \mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{J}$, and from $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{J} - \mathbf{S}$, $\mathbf{L}^2 = \mathbf{J}^2 + \mathbf{S}^2 - 2\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{J}$, so $\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{J} = \tfrac12[J(J+1) + S(S+1) - L(L+1)]$. Dividing by $J(J+1)$ gives $g_J$ as stated. The Zeeman levels of the ground level are then $E = g_J\mu_\mathrm{B}BM_J$, and the derivation of [[coordination-chemistry/magnetism#prop-curie]] goes through with $S \rightarrow J$ and $g \rightarrow g_J$, giving $\mu_\mathrm{eff}^2 = g_J^2J(J+1)$. Check: for $L = 0$ (Gd³⁺), $J = S$ and $g_J = 2$, the spin-only result.
:::

With no orbital moment, gadolinium(III) has very slow electron spin relaxation, the property exploited in MRI contrast agents. (c) $4f^{11}$: three holes, $S = \tfrac32$, $L = 6$ (I), $J = L + S = \tfrac{15}{2}$: $^4I_{15/2}$. $g_J = 1 + [\tfrac{15}{2}\cdot\tfrac{17}{2} + \tfrac32\cdot\tfrac52 - 42]/(2 \times 63.75) = 1 + (63.75 + 3.75 - 42)/127.5 = 1.200$; $\mu = 1.2\sqrt{63.75} = 9.58\ \mu_\mathrm{B}$, against spin-only $3.87$.

### f–f spectra and luminescence

Transitions within the $4f^n$ configuration are Laporte-forbidden (all $f$ states have the same parity) and, since the $4f$ orbitals barely mix with ligand orbitals, they gain little intensity: molar absorption coefficients are usually below $10\ \mathrm{dm^3\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$. Because the $4f$ electrons are not involved in bonding, the equilibrium geometry hardly changes on excitation, there is little vibrational broadening, and the bands are **sharp** (widths of a few nanometres in solution) and almost independent of the ligands. These sharp, characteristic lines are why neodymium-doped glass is used to calibrate spectrophotometers and why each lanthanide can be identified spectroscopically. A few **hypersensitive** transitions, such as one near $575\ \mathrm{nm}$ in $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$, do change intensity strongly with the ligands.

The excited states can also emit. $\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}$ emits red light, chiefly from its $^5D_0$ level to the $^7F_2$ level near $612\ \mathrm{nm}$; $\mathrm{Tb^{3+}}$ emits green, from $^5D_4$ to $^7F_5$ near $545\ \mathrm{nm}$. Because direct absorption is so weak, practical lanthanide emitters use the **antenna effect**: an organic ligand or a host lattice absorbs light strongly, transfers the energy to the lanthanide by intersystem crossing and energy transfer, and the lanthanide emits its sharp lines with a long lifetime (milliseconds, against nanoseconds for organic fluorophores). Water and other O–H oscillators quench the emission by vibrational energy transfer, so good emitters shield the ion with chelating ligands. Applications include the red phosphor $\mathrm{Y_2O_3:Eu^{3+}}$ of fluorescent lamps, the yellow phosphor $\mathrm{Y_3Al_5O_{12}:Ce^{3+}}$ (Ce:YAG) that turns the blue light of an LED white, time-resolved luminescence bioassays, and the Nd:YAG laser at $1064\ \mathrm{nm}$.

Which ion is better sensitised by this ligand, and why?

(a) $\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda$: $1/(612\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{cm}) = 16\,340\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $1/(545\times10^{-7}) = 18\,350\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Multiplying by $N_\mathrm{A}hc = 0.01196\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ per $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$: $195\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $219\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. (The emissions end on excited levels of the $^7F$ term, which is why the photon energies are smaller than the emitting levels' energies above the ground state.) (b) Energy transfer from the triplet to the lanthanide is efficient when the triplet lies somewhat above the emitting level, typically by $2000$–$4000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$: too small a gap allows thermally activated back-transfer, and too large a gap makes transfer slow. For europium the gap is $20\,500 - 17\,250 = 3250\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, ideal. For terbium it is only $100\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, so energy flows back to the ligand at room temperature and terbium emission is weak. A terbium antenna needs a higher triplet, typically above $22\,000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.
:::

::: warning Lanthanide complexes are not like d-block complexes {#warn-lanthanides}
It is natural to carry $d$-block habits into $f$-block chemistry, and almost every one of them fails. There is no useful crystal field stabilisation (splittings of order $100\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), so there are no preferred geometries, no spin states to choose between, no Jahn–Teller distortions of chemical consequence and no 18-electron rule. Colours are pale and do not change much with ligands. Magnetic moments follow $J$, not spin-only. Complexes are labile, so isomers cannot be isolated, and bonding is mostly ionic, so coordination numbers are set by size and sterics. And the "rare earths" are not rare: cerium is about as abundant in the Earth's crust as copper. What is difficult is separating them from each other.
:::

## The actinides

The early actinides look like transition metals, the later ones like lanthanides. The difference is the $5f$ orbitals: for thorium to plutonium they are close in energy to $6d$ and $7s$ and extend far enough to overlap with ligand orbitals, so many electrons can be removed and covalent bonding is significant. As the nuclear charge rises across the series the $5f$ orbitals contract and drop in energy, and from americium onwards the +3 state dominates, as in the lanthanides.

| element | Th | Pa | U | Np | Pu | Am | Cm |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| common oxidation states | IV | V | IV, **VI** | IV, V, VI | III, **IV**, V, VI | **III** | III |
| highest known | IV | V | VI | VII | VII (VIII disputed) | VI (VII reported) | IV |

**Uranium** is the best-known actinide. Its most stable state in aerated water is uranium(VI) as the **uranyl** ion, $\mathrm{[UO_2]^{2+}}$, a linear $\mathrm{O{=}U{=}O}$ unit with short U–O bonds (about $176\ \mathrm{pm}$), to which four to six further ligands bind in the equatorial plane. Its yellow-green luminescence gave the nineteenth century its "uranium glass". The linearity and the strength of the U–O bonds (a formal triple bond, one σ and two π, using $5f$ and $6d$ orbitals) are a direct sign of $5f$ participation in bonding. Uranium(IV) is green and forms $\mathrm{UO_2}$, the ceramic fuel of most nuclear reactors, and $\mathrm{UF_4}$; uranium hexafluoride, $\mathrm{UF_6}$, a volatile solid that sublimes at $56.5\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, is used for isotope enrichment because it is a molecular compound of a single element with monoisotopic fluorine. **Uranocene**, $\mathrm{[U(\eta^8\text{-}C_8H_8)_2]}$, made by Andrew Streitwieser and Ulrich Müller-Westerhoff in 1968, was designed to test $5f$ covalency, the cyclooctatetraenide dianion offering orbitals of the right symmetry to overlap with $5f$.

**Plutonium** is the most complex: its III, IV, V and VI states have reduction potentials within a few tenths of a volt of each other near $1\ \mathrm{V}$, so in acidic solution several oxidation states can coexist in equilibrium, with disproportionation and comproportionation reactions linking them. The PUREX process for reprocessing spent nuclear fuel exploits the different extractabilities of the oxidation states: uranium(VI) and plutonium(IV) nitrates are extracted from nitric acid into tributyl phosphate in kerosene, leaving most fission products behind, and plutonium is then separated from uranium by reducing it to the poorly extracted plutonium(III).

::: example Why plutonium is so complicated {#ex-plutonium}
Approximate formal potentials in $1\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ perchloric acid are $\mathrm{PuO_2^{2+}/PuO_2^+}$ $+0.92\ \mathrm{V}$, $\mathrm{PuO_2^+/Pu^{4+}}$ $+1.17\ \mathrm{V}$ and $\mathrm{Pu^{4+}/Pu^{3+}}$ $+0.98\ \mathrm{V}$. (a) Is plutonium(V) stable towards disproportionation? (b) Find $\log K$ for $2\,\mathrm{PuO_2^+ + 4\,H^+ \rightleftharpoons Pu^{4+} + PuO_2^{2+} + 2\,H_2O}$. (c) Explain why plutonium(IV) also disproportionates slightly in dilute acid.
::: solution
(a) Using [[coordination-chemistry/d-block-chemistry#prop-disproportionation]] with the Latimer sequence $\mathrm{PuO_2^{2+}}\ (0.92)\ \mathrm{PuO_2^+}\ (1.17)\ \mathrm{Pu^{4+}}$: the right-hand potential (1.17) exceeds the left-hand one (0.92), so $\mathrm{PuO_2^+}$ disproportionates in $1\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ acid. (b) $E^\circ_\mathrm{cell} = 1.17 - 0.92 = 0.25\ \mathrm{V}$, $\log K = 0.25/0.05916 = 4.2$. Because four protons appear on the left, the equilibrium shifts back towards plutonium(V) as the acidity falls, and plutonium(V) is the dominant state in some near-neutral natural waters. (c) The relevant reaction is $3\,\mathrm{Pu^{4+}} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{Pu^{3+}} + \mathrm{PuO_2^{2+}} + 4\,\mathrm{H^+}$. The two-electron couple $\mathrm{PuO_2^{2+}/Pu^{4+}}$ has the average potential $(0.92 + 1.17)/2 = 1.045\ \mathrm{V}$ (free energies, not potentials, add; for two one-electron steps this is the mean). So $E^\circ_\mathrm{cell} = 0.98 - 1.045 = -0.065\ \mathrm{V}$ for $n = 2$, and $\log K = 2(-0.065)/0.05916 = -2.2$. The equilibrium lies to the left at unit activities, but only slightly, and because four protons are released it moves to the right as the acidity falls: in dilute acid plutonium(IV) partly disproportionates into plutonium(III) and plutonium(VI). All four oxidation states can therefore be present together, each with different chemistry, which makes the environmental behaviour of plutonium hard to predict.
:::

### Safety

All actinides are radioactive, and their chemistry is practised only in licensed laboratories with containment matched to the isotope. The main hazards of the transuranium elements, and of plutonium in particular, are internal: alpha-emitting isotopes are dangerous when inhaled or ingested, and plutonium accumulates in bone and liver. Natural and depleted uranium are weakly radioactive, and their chemical toxicity as heavy metals (mainly to the kidney) is often the more important hazard. Lanthanide compounds are of low to moderate toxicity, although free gadolinium(III) is toxic, which is why it is given only as very stable, inert chelates ([[coordination-chemistry/bioinorganic]]).

## Where this leads

The large magnetic moments and anisotropy of the lanthanides make them central to modern magnets ($\mathrm{Nd_2Fe_{14}B}$, $\mathrm{SmCo_5}$) and to single-molecule magnets, and their sharp luminescence to phosphors and lasers; both topics continue in [[materials-chemistry/magnetic-optical]]. Gadolinium(III) MRI agents combine the magnetism of $^8S_{7/2}$ with the thermodynamic and kinetic stability of [[coordination-chemistry/complex-stability]] and [[coordination-chemistry/inorganic-mechanisms]]. Nuclear chemistry, including the decay series of uranium and thorium and the fission products, is in [[general-chemistry-2/nuclear-chemistry]], and the environmental fate of radionuclides in [[environmental-chemistry/pollutants-fate]].

::: history
Johan Gadolin isolated "yttria" from a mineral found at Ytterby in Sweden in 1794, and the next century was spent discovering that it and Berzelius's "ceria" were mixtures of many oxides; Carl Gustaf Mosander separated lanthanum, "didymium", erbium and terbium in the 1840s, and Carl Auer von Welsbach split didymium into praseodymium and neodymium in 1885. Henry Moseley's 1913 X-ray spectra showed that there must be exactly fifteen elements from lanthanum to lutetium, with element 61 missing; promethium was finally identified in 1945 among the fission products of uranium. Victor Goldschmidt named the lanthanide contraction in 1925. Uranium was named by Martin Heinrich Klaproth in 1789, and Henri Becquerel discovered its radioactivity in 1896. Glenn Seaborg and his colleagues made plutonium in 1940–41 and proposed the actinide concept in 1944, placing the new elements as a second $f$ series beneath the lanthanides; Seaborg shared the 1951 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Edwin McMillan. Frank Spedding's ion-exchange separation of the lanthanides at Iowa State in the 1940s made pure rare earths available in quantity for the first time.
:::

::: summary
- The $4f$ orbitals lie inside the filled $5s$ and $5p$ shells, so lanthanide ions interact weakly with ligands; their chemistry is that of hard, labile, mainly ionic $\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ ions with coordination numbers of 8–9.
- Poor shielding by $4f$ electrons causes the lanthanide contraction (103 to 86 pm from $\mathrm{La^{3+}}$ to $\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}$), which makes neighbouring lanthanides hard to separate and makes Zr and Hf twins.
- +3 dominates; Ce(IV) ($f^0$) is a strong oxidant and Eu(II) ($f^7$), Yb(II) ($f^{14}$) and Sm(II) are reductants.
- Spin–orbit coupling exceeds the ligand field: ground levels $^{2S+1}L_J$ follow Hund's rules, and moments follow $\mu_\mathrm{eff} = g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}$, not the spin-only formula (exceptions Sm³⁺, Eu³⁺ from low-lying levels).
- $f$–$f$ bands are weak, sharp and ligand-insensitive; Eu³⁺ (red) and Tb³⁺ (green) luminescence, sensitised by antenna ligands, is used in lighting, displays and bioassays.
- The early actinides (Th–Pu) have accessible $5f$ orbitals, many oxidation states and covalent bonding (linear uranyl, uranocene); from Am on they resemble the lanthanides. Plutonium's oxidation states coexist in solution.
- All actinides are radioactive; their chemistry needs licensed facilities and appropriate containment.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Ground level of neodymium(III) {level=1 check="4.5"}
Derive the ground level of $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$ and give its value of $J$. (Enter $J$.)
::: solution
$4f^3$: three parallel spins in $m_l = 3, 2, 1$; $S = \tfrac32$, $L = 6$ (I). The shell is less than half full, so $J = L - S = \tfrac92$: $^4I_{9/2}$.
:::

::: exercise The moment of gadolinium(III) {level=1 check="7.94"}
Calculate $\mu_\mathrm{eff}$ for $\mathrm{Gd^{3+}}$ from [[#eq-lande]]. (Enter $\mu_\mathrm{eff}$ in $\mu_\mathrm{B}$, to three significant figures.)
::: solution
$4f^7$, $^8S_{7/2}$: $L = 0$, $J = S = \tfrac72$, $g_J = 2$, $\mu = 2\sqrt{\tfrac72 \cdot \tfrac92} = 2\sqrt{15.75} = 7.94\ \mu_\mathrm{B}$.
:::

::: exercise Unusual oxidation states {level=1}
Predict which lanthanides are most likely to show (a) a stable +4 state and (b) a +2 state in aqueous solution, and explain.
::: solution
(a) Cerium: $\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}$ is $4f^0$ (the xenon configuration), and the fourth ionisation energy of cerium is the lowest of the series. Terbium(IV) ($4f^7$) and praseodymium(IV) exist in solids but are too strongly oxidising for water. (b) Europium: $\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}$ is $4f^7$, half-filled, with $E^\circ(\mathrm{Eu^{3+}/Eu^{2+}}) = -0.35\ \mathrm{V}$, so it survives for a time in water. Ytterbium(II) ($4f^{14}$) and samarium(II) are much stronger reductants ($-1.05$ and $-1.55\ \mathrm{V}$) and reduce water, but are useful in non-aqueous solvents.
:::

::: exercise The moment of dysprosium(III) {level=2 check="10.65"}
Derive the ground level of $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$, calculate $g_J$ and $\mu_\mathrm{eff}$, and compare with the spin-only value. (Enter $\mu_\mathrm{eff}$ in $\mu_\mathrm{B}$, to four significant figures.)
::: solution
$4f^9$: seven electrons with spin up in $m_l = 3$ to $-3$ (total $m_l = 0$), two with spin down in $m_l = 3, 2$. $S = (7 - 2)/2 = \tfrac52$, $L = 5$ (H), more than half full, $J = L + S = \tfrac{15}{2}$: $^6H_{15/2}$. $g_J = 1 + [\tfrac{15}{2}\cdot\tfrac{17}{2} + \tfrac52\cdot\tfrac72 - 30]/(2 \times 63.75) = 1 + (63.75 + 8.75 - 30)/127.5 = 1 + 0.3333 = 1.3333$. $\mu = 1.3333\sqrt{63.75} = 1.3333 \times 7.984 = 10.65\ \mu_\mathrm{B}$, against spin-only $\sqrt{35} = 5.92$ for five unpaired electrons.
:::

::: exercise Europium(III) is not diamagnetic {level=2}
$\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}$ has a $^7F_0$ ground level, for which [[#eq-lande]] predicts $\mu_\mathrm{eff} = 0$, yet europium(III) compounds have $\mu_\mathrm{eff} \approx 3.4\ \mu_\mathrm{B}$ at room temperature. Explain, and predict how $\mu_\mathrm{eff}$ changes on cooling to $4\ \mathrm{K}$.
::: solution
The $^7F$ term of $4f^6$ is split by spin–orbit coupling into levels $J = 0, 1, \dots, 6$, with the interval rule $E(J) - E(J - 1) = \lambda J$ and $\lambda \approx 350\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. The $^7F_1$ level lies only about $350\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ above $^7F_0$, comparable to $k_\mathrm{B}T = 207\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ at room temperature, and $^7F_2$ about $1050\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ up. These magnetic levels are thermally populated, and there is also a temperature-independent contribution from field-induced mixing of $^7F_1$ into $^7F_0$. On cooling, the excited levels empty and $\mu_\mathrm{eff}$ falls towards a small value due only to the temperature-independent term; at $4\ \mathrm{K}$ the measured moment is well below $1\ \mu_\mathrm{B}$. A single-level Curie law does not apply.
:::

::: exercise A lanthanide emission line {level=2 check="16340"}
The strongest emission of $\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}$ in many complexes is at $612\ \mathrm{nm}$. Give the wavenumber of the emitted light, and explain why the wavelength hardly changes from one europium complex to another. (Enter the wavenumber in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, to four significant figures.)
::: solution
$\tilde\nu = 1/(612\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{cm}) = 16\,340\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. The transition $^5D_0 \rightarrow {}^7F_2$ takes place within the $4f^6$ configuration, whose electrons are shielded by the $5s^25p^6$ shell; the ligand field shifts and splits the levels by only tens to hundreds of $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, so the line moves by at most a few nanometres. Its *intensity* is hypersensitive to the environment (it is strongest when the europium site lacks an inversion centre), which is why europium emission spectra are used to probe site symmetry.
:::

::: exercise How many extraction stages? {level=3 check="23"}
Two adjacent lanthanides have a separation factor of $\alpha = 1.35$ per ideal extraction stage. (a) How many ideal stages are needed to raise the ratio of the more extractable element from 1:1 to 999:1? (b) If the feed already contains the two in the ratio 1:9, how many are needed? (c) Explain why lanthanide separation plants are built as continuous countercurrent cascades. (Enter the number of stages for (a), rounded up to a whole number.)
::: solution
(a) $\alpha^N \ge 999$: $N \ge \log 999/\log 1.35 = 2.9996/0.13033 = 23.0$, so **23** stages. (b) From $1/9$ to $999/1$ is a factor of $8991$: $N \ge \log 8991/\log 1.35 = 3.954/0.1303 = 30.3$, so 31 stages. (c) In a countercurrent cascade the aqueous and organic phases flow in opposite directions through a series of mixer-settlers, so that each stage receives a feed already partly enriched; the cascade reaches high purity and high recovery at the same time and runs continuously. With $\alpha$ only slightly above 1, separating a full mixture of rare earths needs hundreds to over a thousand stages, which is a major reason for the cost and environmental burden of rare-earth production.
:::

::: exercise Uranyl and the participation of 5f orbitals {level=3}
(a) The uranyl ion $\mathrm{[UO_2]^{2+}}$ is linear, while the dioxo units of $d^0$ transition metals, such as the $\mathrm{[MoO_2]^{2+}}$ core of molybdenum(VI) complexes, are almost always *cis* (bent). Suggest why, in terms of the orbitals available. (b) Why does the luminescence of uranyl, unlike that of the lanthanides, depend strongly on its ligands? (c) Why do the later actinides (Am onwards) behave more like lanthanides?
::: solution
(a) A $d^0$ metal forming two π-bonded oxo ligands prefers a *cis* arrangement, because in a *cis* geometry the two oxygens can use different $d$ orbitals for their π bonds and do not compete for the same one (and $p$ orbitals, which would favour *trans*, lie too high). For uranium both $6d$ and $5f$ orbitals are available, and the $5f$ set includes orbitals ($f_{z^3}$ for σ, and $f_{xz^2}$, $f_{yz^2}$ for π) that interact strongly along a single axis, and combine with $6d$ to favour the linear *trans* arrangement with a formal $\mathrm{U{\equiv}O}$ triple bond to each oxygen. Linearity is therefore evidence that $5f$ orbitals take part in bonding. (b) The uranyl emission is a ligand-to-metal charge-transfer transition (oxygen non-bonding orbitals to empty $5f$), involving the bonding orbitals themselves; changes in the equatorial ligands perturb these orbitals directly, unlike the shielded $4f$–$4f$ transitions of the lanthanides. (c) Across the actinide series the $5f$ orbitals contract and fall in energy, as the $4f$ orbitals do in the lanthanides, so from americium onwards they are core-like, ionisation beyond +3 is costly, and the chemistry becomes that of $\mathrm{An^{3+}}$ ions, closely similar in size to the lanthanides.
