Groups 13 and 14 run from two of the hardest non-metals known — boron and diamond — down to two soft, dense metals, thallium and lead. Along the way they supply the metal of aircraft and drinks cans (aluminium), the element of life (carbon), the element of the information age (silicon) and the bulk of the Earth's crust (the silicates). Two themes organise them. Group 13 has only three valence electrons for four valence orbitals, so its lighter members are **electron-deficient**: they are Lewis acids, they bridge, they cluster. Group 14 has exactly four electrons for four orbitals, so its compounds are electron-precise, and the interesting contrast is the one between carbon and everything below it — the $\pi$ bonds and long chains of carbon against the single-bonded networks of silicon. Down both groups the inert-pair effect of [[main-group/periodic-trends#def-inert-pair]] gradually takes over, until thallium prefers $+1$ and lead prefers $+2$. The bonding models used here — three-centre bonds, Wade's rules and the double-bond rule — come from [[main-group/main-group-bonding]].

## Boron: a non-metal with too few electrons

Elemental boron is a black, very hard, high-melting ($2076\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$) semiconductor. Every one of its several crystalline forms is built from $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ icosahedra, linked to one another by two-centre and three-centre bonds. That structure is the first sign of electron deficiency: boron has too few electrons to give each atom four ordinary two-centre bonds, so it builds polyhedra in which electrons are shared over faces rather than edges — the cluster bonding that Wade's rules count ([[main-group/main-group-bonding#prop-wade]]).

Boron's compounds with electronegative partners are dominated by the empty $2p$ orbital of trigonal-planar $\mathrm{BX_3}$. The trihalides are Lewis acids (with the inverted order $\mathrm{BF_3} < \mathrm{BCl_3} < \mathrm{BBr_3}$ explained in [[main-group/inorganic-acids-bases#ex-bx3]]); $\mathrm{BF_3}$ forms $\mathrm{[BF_4]^-}$ with fluoride and stable adducts with ethers and amines. **Boric acid**, $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$, is a weak acid of an unusual kind, since it acts by accepting hydroxide rather than giving up a proton,
$$
\mathrm{B(OH)_3 + 2\,H_2O \rightleftharpoons [B(OH)_4]^- + H_3O^+}, \qquad \mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 9.24,
$$
and its salts, the **borates**, build rings and chains of trigonal $\mathrm{BO_3}$ and tetrahedral $\mathrm{BO_4}$ units (borax, the commonest ore, is $\mathrm{Na_2[B_4O_5(OH)_4]\cdot 8H_2O}$). Fused with silica, boron oxide gives **borosilicate glass**, whose low thermal expansion makes laboratory glassware survive heating.

### Boranes and borohydrides

The boron hydrides are the classic electron-deficient molecules. The simplest isolable one is diborane, $\mathrm{B_2H_6}$, with its two $\mathrm{B{-}H{-}B}$ three-centre two-electron bridges ([[main-group/main-group-bonding#def-3c2e]]); the higher boranes ($\mathrm{B_4H_{10}}$, $\mathrm{B_5H_9}$, $\mathrm{B_{10}H_{14}}$) are fragments of deltahedra, and the closo anions $\mathrm{B_nH_n^{2-}}$ ($n = 6$–$12$) are complete ones — $\mathrm{B_{12}H_{12}^{2-}}$, a perfect icosahedron, is so stable that it survives boiling in strong acid or base. Diborane is made by transferring hydride to a boron halide in an ether solvent,
$$
\mathrm{3\,NaBH_4 + 4\,BF_3 \rightarrow 2\,B_2H_6 + 3\,NaBF_4},
$$
and it is a toxic, pyrophoric gas (it can ignite spontaneously in moist air), handled only in closed apparatus by trained workers.

The bridge bonds are where diborane reacts. A Lewis base splits the dimer symmetrically into two adducts, $\mathrm{B_2H_6 + 2\,THF \rightarrow 2\,H_3B{\cdot}THF}$, and the resulting "$\mathrm{BH_3}$" equivalents add across alkenes in the **hydroboration** reaction (boron to the less substituted carbon, hydrogen to the more substituted one), the entry point to anti-Markovnikov alcohols in [[organic-1/alkenes-alkynes]]. Hydride acceptance gives the **tetrahydroborate** (borohydride) anion $\mathrm{[BH_4]^-}$, isoelectronic with methane; sodium borohydride is the mild, water-tolerant reducing agent that converts aldehydes and ketones to alcohols. The $\mathrm{B{-}H}$ bond is hydridic ($\chi(\mathrm{B}) = 2.04 < \chi(\mathrm{H}) = 2.20$), which is why boranes are hydride donors and why they react with water and alcohols to release $\mathrm{H_2}$ — the hydride classification of [[main-group/hydrogen-s-block#def-hydrides]].

### Boron nitride and borazine: isoelectronic with carbon

A $\mathrm{B{-}N}$ unit has $3 + 5 = 8$ valence electrons, the same as $\mathrm{C{-}C}$. Boron nitride therefore mimics carbon: **hexagonal BN** has graphite-like layers of fused $\mathrm{B_3N_3}$ hexagons, and **cubic BN**, made at high pressure, has the diamond structure and is second only to diamond in hardness. The mimicry is structural, not electronic. Hexagonal BN is white and an electrical insulator, unlike black, conducting graphite, because the electronegativity difference opens a large gap between the filled $\pi$ band (on nitrogen) and the empty one (on boron); and its layers stack with boron directly above nitrogen, where opposite partial charges meet, rather than offset as in graphite.

::: definition Isoelectronic analogues {#def-isoelectronic}
Two species are **isoelectronic** when they have the same number of valence electrons distributed over the same skeleton of atoms: $\mathrm{BH_4^-}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$ and $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$; $\mathrm{N_2}$, $\mathrm{CO}$, $\mathrm{CN^-}$ and $\mathrm{NO^+}$; $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2O}$ and $\mathrm{NO_2^+}$; benzene and borazine $\mathrm{B_3N_3H_6}$; graphite and hexagonal BN. Isoelectronic species usually share a shape and a bonding pattern (the same Lewis structure and VSEPR count); they do **not** in general share reactivity, polarity or colour, because those depend on how the electrons are distributed between atoms of different electronegativity.
:::

::: example Borazine is not benzene {#ex-borazine}
Borazine, $\mathrm{B_3N_3H_6}$, is a planar six-membered ring of alternating $\mathrm{BH}$ and $\mathrm{NH}$ units, isoelectronic with benzene and nicknamed "inorganic benzene". Benzene does not react with hydrogen chloride at room temperature, but borazine adds three molecules of $\mathrm{HCl}$ to give $\mathrm{B_3N_3H_9Cl_3}$. Predict where each H and Cl goes, and explain the difference in terms of electronegativity.
::: solution
In a $\mathrm{B{-}N}$ bond the $\pi$ electrons come from nitrogen's lone pair (in the Lewis structure without formal charges, boron has an empty $p$ orbital and nitrogen a lone pair), and they remain concentrated on the more electronegative nitrogen ($\chi = 3.04$ against $2.04$). The ring is therefore polar: boron is $\delta+$ and Lewis-acidic, nitrogen $\delta-$ and basic. $\mathrm{HCl}$ adds across each $\mathrm{B{=}N}$ bond in the polar sense, $\mathrm{H^{\delta+}}$ to nitrogen and $\mathrm{Cl^{\delta-}}$ to boron, giving the saturated cyclohexane analogue $\mathrm{(ClHB{-}NH_2)_3}$, in which every boron carries $\mathrm{H}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}$ and every nitrogen two hydrogens. Benzene's ring is non-polar, and its $\pi$ delocalisation energy (about $150\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ of aromatic stabilisation) would be lost by addition, so it resists. Borazine has much less delocalisation to lose because its $\pi$ density is localised on the nitrogens. The example is the definition's caveat in action: the same electron count gives the same shape, but not the same chemistry.
:::
:::

## Aluminium and the heavier metals of group 13

Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, yet it was a precious metal until the 1880s, because its oxide is extraordinarily stable. With $E^\circ(\mathrm{Al^{3+}/Al}) = -1.66\ \mathrm{V}$ and a line far down the Ellingham diagram, alumina cannot be reduced by carbon at any practical temperature ([[main-group/inorganic-redox#ex-zn-mg-smelting]]), and aqueous electrolysis would only make hydrogen. The modern industry has two stages.

**The Bayer process** purifies bauxite, a mixture of aluminium hydroxide–oxides with iron oxides and silica. Hot concentrated sodium hydroxide dissolves the amphoteric aluminium as aluminate, $\mathrm{Al(OH)_3(s) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow [Al(OH)_4]^-(aq)}$, while the basic iron(III) oxide stays behind as "red mud". On cooling and seeding, the equilibrium is reversed and pure $\mathrm{Al(OH)_3}$ crystallises; calcination above $1000\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ gives alumina, $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$. The whole separation rests on the amphoteric band of [[main-group/inorganic-acids-bases#def-oxides]]: aluminium's oxide is acidic enough to dissolve in base, iron's is not.

**The Hall–Héroult process** electrolyses alumina dissolved in molten cryolite, $\mathrm{Na_3AlF_6}$, at about $960\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ — far below alumina's melting point of $2072\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$. Aluminium is deposited at the carbon-lined cathode, and oxygen reacts with the carbon anodes, which are consumed:
$$
\mathrm{2\,Al_2O_3 + 3\,C \rightarrow 4\,Al + 3\,CO_2}.
$$
Cells run at about $4$–$4.5\ \mathrm{V}$ and several hundred kiloamperes, with current efficiencies near $95\ \%$, and use roughly $13$–$15\ \mathrm{kWh}$ of electricity per kilogram of metal; that energy is why recycling aluminium, which needs about a twentieth of it, matters so much.

Aluminium metal is protected by a coherent film of oxide a few nanometres thick, which re-forms instantly if scratched; destroy the film (with mercury, or in strongly basic solution, where the oxide dissolves as aluminate) and the metal reacts vigorously with water. The same oxide film, thickened electrolytically, is **anodised** aluminium.

### Acidic aqua ions

::: definition The acidity of a hydrated cation {#def-aqua-acidity}
A metal ion in water is a Lewis acid bound to six (or four) water molecules, and the bound water is a stronger Brønsted acid than free water because the cation withdraws electron density from the $\mathrm{O{-}H}$ bonds:
$$
\mathrm{[M(H_2O)_6]^{z+} + H_2O \rightleftharpoons [M(OH)(H_2O)_5]^{(z-1)+} + H_3O^+}.
$$
The acidity increases with the cation's charge-to-radius ratio $z/r$ (its polarising power): $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ is about $14$ for $\mathrm{[Na(H_2O)_n]^+}$ (practically neutral), $11.4$ for $\mathrm{[Mg(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$, $5.0$ for $\mathrm{[Al(H_2O)_6]^{3+}}$ and about $2$ for $\mathrm{[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{3+}}$, where the partly covalent $\mathrm{Fe{-}O}$ bond adds to the effect.
:::

::: example Why aluminium salts are acidic {#ex-al-hydrolysis}
Calculate the pH of $0.10\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ aluminium chloride solution, taking $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 5.0$ for $\mathrm{[Al(H_2O)_6]^{3+}}$ and ignoring further hydrolysis steps. Then explain what happens when sodium hydroxide is added slowly.
::: solution
$K_\mathrm{a} = 1.0\times10^{-5}$ and $c = 0.10\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$. Since $c/K_\mathrm{a} = 10^4$, the weak-acid approximation $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = \sqrt{K_\mathrm{a}c} = \sqrt{1.0\times10^{-6}} = 1.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ is good (the exact quadratic gives $9.95\times10^{-4}$), so the pH is $3.00$ — as acidic as a dilute solution of acetic acid. Adding base removes protons step by step from the aqua ion, giving neutral, insoluble $\mathrm{Al(OH)_3(H_2O)_3}$ (a white gelatinous precipitate, which in practice is a polymerised hydroxide) around neutral pH; with excess base a fourth proton goes, and the precipitate redissolves as $\mathrm{[Al(OH)_4]^-}$. Acid, precipitate, redissolution: that sequence is amphoterism observed in one test tube, and it is exactly the chemistry the Bayer process runs forwards and backwards. Water treatment uses the same precipitate: aluminium sulfate added to raw water forms a gelatinous hydroxide that sweeps fine particles out of suspension.
:::
:::

Aluminium halides show the same electron deficiency as boron's. Aluminium chloride is a layer lattice in the solid, a dimer $\mathrm{Al_2Cl_6}$ with chloride bridges in the melt and vapour ([[main-group/main-group-bonding]] explains why these bridges, unlike diborane's, are ordinary two-electron bonds), and a monomer only at high temperature. Its Lewis acidity drives the Friedel–Crafts reactions of [[organic-2/aromatic-substitution]], where it pulls a halide from an alkyl or acyl halide to make the electrophile.

Below aluminium the group becomes more metallic and the inert pair appears. **Gallium** melts at $29.8\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ but boils above $2000\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, the longest liquid range of any element; its compounds gallium arsenide and gallium nitride are the semiconductors of lasers and light-emitting diodes. **Indium** gives indium tin oxide, the transparent conductor of touchscreens. **Thallium** is mostly $+1$: $\mathrm{Tl^+}$ ($150\ \mathrm{pm}$) resembles $\mathrm{K^+}$ in size and $\mathrm{Ag^+}$ in the low solubility of its halides, and its resemblance to potassium is the root of its high toxicity — the body takes it up through potassium channels. Thallium compounds are classed as acutely toxic and cumulative poisons; they are not used in teaching laboratories.

## Carbon

### The allotropes

Carbon's allotropes are a lesson in how bonding sets properties.

| | bonding | C–C distance / pm | density / $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ | character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| diamond | $sp^3$, 3D network | 154 | 3.515 | hardest natural material, insulator, highest thermal conductivity of any bulk solid at room temperature |
| graphite | $sp^2$ layers, $\pi$ delocalised | 142 in layer; 335 between layers | 2.266 | soft, lubricating, conducts in the layer plane |
| $\mathrm{C_{60}}$ | curved $sp^2$ cage | 139 and 145 | 1.65 | molecular solid; soluble in aromatic solvents |
| graphene | one graphite layer | 142 | — | semimetal with extremely high carrier mobility |

The fullerenes, carbon nanotubes and graphene were discovered between 1985 and 2004 and are developed in [[materials-chemistry/nanomaterials]]. The classical question is the relation of the first two. At $298\ \mathrm{K}$ and $1\ \mathrm{bar}$ graphite is the stable form: for $\mathrm{C(graphite) \rightarrow C(diamond)}$, $\Delta_\mathrm{r}H^\circ = +1.895\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ = +2.900\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Diamonds survive because the conversion requires breaking and remaking every bond of a covalent network; the barrier is so high that diamond is indefinitely metastable at room temperature. But diamond is denser, and pressure favours the denser phase.

::: proposition The pressure dependence of a reaction Gibbs energy {#prop-dg-pressure}
At constant temperature, for a transformation whose molar volume change is $\Delta_\mathrm{r}V$,
$$
\left(\frac{\partial \Delta_\mathrm{r}G}{\partial p}\right)_T = \Delta_\mathrm{r}V, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \Delta_\mathrm{r}G(p) \approx \Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ + \Delta_\mathrm{r}V\,(p - p^\circ)
$$
when the phases are condensed and their compressibilities are neglected. A transformation to a denser phase ($\Delta_\mathrm{r}V < 0$) becomes more favourable as pressure rises, and becomes spontaneous above $p \approx p^\circ - \Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ/\Delta_\mathrm{r}V$.
:::

::: proof From the fundamental equation
For each phase, $\mathrm{d}G_\mathrm{m} = V_\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{d}p - S_\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{d}T$ ([[thermodynamics/free-energy]]), so at constant $T$, $(\partial G_\mathrm{m}/\partial p)_T = V_\mathrm{m}$. Subtracting the equation for the reactant phase from that for the product phase gives $(\partial \Delta_\mathrm{r}G/\partial p)_T = \Delta_\mathrm{r}V$. If $\Delta_\mathrm{r}V$ is independent of pressure (an incompressible-solid approximation, good to a few per cent for diamond and graphite over the pressures involved), integrating from $p^\circ$ to $p$ gives the linear form. Setting $\Delta_\mathrm{r}G(p) = 0$ gives the equilibrium pressure.
:::

::: example At what pressure is diamond stable? {#ex-diamond}
Using the densities in the table ($M = 12.011\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$) and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ = +2.900\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, estimate the pressure above which diamond is thermodynamically stable at $298\ \mathrm{K}$.
::: solution
Molar volumes: graphite $12.011/2.266 = 5.301\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$; diamond $12.011/3.515 = 3.417\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$. So $\Delta_\mathrm{r}V = -1.883\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}} = -1.883\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$. Then
$$
p - p^\circ = -\frac{\Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ}{\Delta_\mathrm{r}V} = \frac{2900\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}}{1.883\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}} = 1.54\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{Pa},
$$
about $1.5\ \mathrm{GPa}$ or $15\,000$ times atmospheric pressure ($p^\circ$, $10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, is negligible beside it). Note the unit bookkeeping: $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}} = \mathrm{Pa}$. At room temperature that pressure would make diamond stable but not make it *form*, because the conversion is so slow. Industrial synthesis therefore works at higher temperature, where the rate is useful, and because the equilibrium pressure rises with temperature (graphite has the higher entropy) it needs about $5$–$6\ \mathrm{GPa}$ at $1500$–$2000\ \mathrm{K}$, with a molten transition metal as a solvent that dissolves graphite and precipitates diamond. Natural diamonds formed in the same way more than $150\ \mathrm{km}$ down in the mantle and were carried up rapidly by volcanic eruptions.
:::
:::

### Oxides and carbides

Carbon's oxides show the double-bond rule from the favourable side. **Carbon monoxide**, isoelectronic with $\mathrm{N_2}$, has a bond enthalpy of $1076\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, the strongest of any diatomic molecule; it binds to low-oxidation-state metals through carbon (the carbonyls of [[organometallics-catalysis/ligands-complexes]]) and to the iron of haemoglobin about two hundred times more strongly than oxygen does, which makes it a dangerous asphyxiant (GHS06, acute toxicity) that cannot be seen or smelled. **Carbon dioxide** is the molecular acid anhydride of carbonic acid, whose strange $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ was dissected in [[main-group/inorganic-acids-bases#warn-oxoacid-exceptions]].

The **carbides** sort themselves by the electronegativity of the partner, like the hydrides. With the most electropositive metals they are saline: calcium carbide, $\mathrm{CaC_2}$, contains the acetylide ion $\mathrm{C_2^{2-}}$ (isoelectronic with $\mathrm{N_2}$ and CO) and gives ethyne with water, $\mathrm{CaC_2 + 2\,H_2O \rightarrow Ca(OH)_2 + C_2H_2}$. With silicon and boron they are covalent networks: silicon carbide (carborundum) and boron carbide are abrasives and armour ceramics. With the larger transition metals they are interstitial: tungsten carbide, $\mathrm{WC}$, is the hard metal of cutting tools.

## Silicon, silicates and silicones

Silicon is made by reducing quartz with carbon in an electric arc furnace at about $2000\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, $\mathrm{SiO_2 + 2\,C \rightarrow Si + 2\,CO}$, giving metallurgical-grade silicon about $99\ \%$ pure. For electronics that is far too impure. The metal is converted with hydrogen chloride into trichlorosilane, $\mathrm{Si + 3\,HCl \rightarrow HSiCl_3 + H_2}$, a liquid (bp $32\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$) that is purified by fractional distillation and decomposed on hot silicon rods back to the element (the Siemens process). Single crystals are then pulled from the melt (the Czochralski method), giving silicon whose impurity content is measured in parts per billion or less — the material of [[materials-chemistry/electronic-structure-solids]].

### Silicates

About 90 % of the Earth's crust consists of silicate minerals, and their bewildering formulas are ruled by a single building block: the $\mathrm{SiO_4}$ tetrahedron. By the double-bond rule ([[main-group/main-group-bonding#prop-double-bond-rule]]) silicon makes four single bonds to oxygen rather than double bonds, so its oxides polymerise: tetrahedra link by sharing corner oxygens to build islands, rings, chains, sheets and frameworks. One counting rule turns the structure into a formula.

::: algorithm Formula of a silicate from its connectivity {#alg-silicate}
1. Identify the number $s$ of corners each $\mathrm{SiO_4}$ tetrahedron shares with other tetrahedra ($0 \le s \le 4$; use the average if the tetrahedra differ).
2. Each shared (bridging) oxygen belongs half to each of two tetrahedra; each unshared (terminal) oxygen belongs wholly to one. Oxygens per silicon: $(4 - s) + s/2 = 4 - s/2$.
3. With silicon $+4$ and oxygen $-2$, the charge per silicon is $4 - 2(4 - s/2) = s - 4$. The repeat unit is therefore $[\mathrm{SiO}_{4 - s/2}]^{(4 - s)-}$.
4. Multiply by the number of tetrahedra in the true repeat unit (ring size, or chain repeat) to clear fractions, and check the overall charge against the cations.
5. If aluminium replaces silicon in a tetrahedron, each substitution adds one negative charge, which must be balanced by an extra cation.

| $s$ | unit | structure type | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $\mathrm{SiO_4^{4-}}$ | isolated tetrahedra (orthosilicates) | olivine $\mathrm{(Mg,Fe)_2SiO_4}$ |
| 1 | $\mathrm{Si_2O_7^{6-}}$ | pairs | thortveitite $\mathrm{Sc_2Si_2O_7}$ |
| 2 | $\mathrm{(SiO_3)}_n^{2n-}$ | rings or single chains | beryl $\mathrm{Be_3Al_2Si_6O_{18}}$; pyroxenes such as $\mathrm{MgSiO_3}$ |
| 2.5 | $\mathrm{(Si_4O_{11})}_n^{6n-}$ | double chains | amphiboles (asbestos minerals) |
| 3 | $\mathrm{(Si_2O_5)}_n^{2n-}$ | sheets | micas, clays, talc $\mathrm{Mg_3Si_4O_{10}(OH)_2}$ |
| 4 | $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ | three-dimensional framework | quartz; feldspars and zeolites with Al |
:::

::: example Reading three mineral formulas {#ex-silicates}
Use [[#alg-silicate]] to identify the silicate type in (a) beryl, $\mathrm{Be_3Al_2Si_6O_{18}}$; (b) talc, $\mathrm{Mg_3Si_4O_{10}(OH)_2}$; (c) orthoclase feldspar, $\mathrm{KAlSi_3O_8}$.
::: solution
(a) The cations carry $3(+2) + 2(+3) = +12$, so the silicate unit is $\mathrm{Si_6O_{18}^{12-}}$: three oxygens and a $2-$ charge per silicon, which is $s = 2$. With exactly six silicons in the formula unit and no chain, it is a **ring** of six tetrahedra — the hexagonal rings stack into channels, and beryl crystals are hexagonal prisms (emerald and aquamarine are coloured beryls). (b) Removing the hydroxides, which belong to the magnesium layer rather than to the tetrahedra, leaves $\mathrm{Si_4O_{10}}$; three $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ ($+6$) less two $\mathrm{OH^-}$ ($-2$) leave $+4$ to balance, so the unit is $\mathrm{Si_4O_{10}^{4-}}$, i.e. $\mathrm{Si_2O_5^{2-}}$ per two silicons: $2.5$ oxygens per silicon, $s = 3$, a **sheet**. Talc's sheets are electrically neutral and held together only by weak dispersion forces, which is why it is the softest mineral and feels soapy. (c) Count tetrahedral atoms, $\mathrm{Al} + 3\,\mathrm{Si} = 4$, against eight oxygens: two oxygens per tetrahedral atom, $s = 4$, a **framework**. A pure $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ framework would be neutral; replacing one silicon in four by aluminium makes the framework $\mathrm{[AlSi_3O_8]^-}$, and the potassium ion sits in its cavities to balance the charge (step 5). Feldspars, built this way, make up about half of the crust.
:::
:::

Aluminium's substitution into the frameworks (step 5) creates the **aluminosilicates**: feldspars, the clays (sheet aluminosilicates that swell with water and exchange cations) and the **zeolites**, open frameworks whose cavities and channels of molecular size make them ion exchangers in detergents, molecular sieves and the acid catalysts of oil refining ([[materials-chemistry/porous-materials]]). One empirical regularity, **Löwenstein's rule**, says that two aluminium-centred tetrahedra almost never share an oxygen ($\mathrm{Al{-}O{-}Al}$ links are avoided), so no zeolite framework has more aluminium than silicon. It is an observation about the minerals and synthetic zeolites, not a law; a few exceptions are known.

::: warning Silicate structures are not molecules in water {#warn-silicates}
Writing $\mathrm{SiO_4^{4-}}$ or $\mathrm{Si_6O_{18}^{12-}}$ tempts one to imagine these anions floating free, as sulfate does. They do not. A $4-$ anion is far too basic to survive in water (orthosilicic acid, $\mathrm{Si(OH)_4}$, has $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} \approx 9.8$, and successive deprotonations get much harder), and in solution silicates condense and depolymerise reversibly into a shifting mixture of oligomers. The formulas in the table describe the connectivity of tetrahedra inside a crystal, where the negative charge is balanced by cations bonded to the terminal oxygens. Similarly, a "chain" in a pyroxene is a structural motif that runs through the crystal, not a polymer molecule that could be isolated. Use the algorithm to read structures and formulas, never to predict solution species.
:::

### Silicones

Silicones are polymers with an inorganic backbone, $\mathrm{({-}SiR_2{-}O{-})}_n$, and organic side groups (usually methyl). They are made by the **direct process** (Rochow–Müller), in which silicon reacts with chloromethane over a copper catalyst at about $300\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$,
$$
\mathrm{Si + 2\,CH_3Cl \rightarrow (CH_3)_2SiCl_2},
$$
together with smaller amounts of $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3SiCl}$ and $\mathrm{CH_3SiCl_3}$. Hydrolysis replaces each $\mathrm{Si{-}Cl}$ by $\mathrm{Si{-}OH}$, and the silanols condense with loss of water, so dimethyldichlorosilane (two functional groups, "D") gives chains and rings, trimethylchlorosilane (one, "M") caps chain ends, and methyltrichlorosilane (three, "T") makes branch points and cross-links. Choosing the mixture sets the product: oils, greases, rubbers or resins. The properties come from the backbone. The $\mathrm{Si{-}O}$ bond is strong ($452\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), giving thermal and oxidative stability; the $\mathrm{Si{-}O{-}Si}$ angle is wide (about $140$–$150^\circ$) and very easily bent, and the chain rotates freely, so poly(dimethylsiloxane) stays flexible far below room temperature (its glass transition is near $-125\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$); and the methyl groups face outwards, making the material water-repellent and chemically inert. Silicones are inorganic chemistry's reward for the double-bond rule: what silicon cannot do as a molecule ($\mathrm{R_2Si{=}O}$, the analogue of a ketone, is not isolable) it does as a polymer.

## Germanium, tin and lead

Germanium is a semiconductor (the first transistor, in 1947, was made from it), with chemistry close to silicon's. Tin and lead are metals, and the inert-pair effect governs them: $\mathrm{Sn(IV)}$ is the stable state of tin, with $\mathrm{Sn(II)}$ a mild reductant, while $\mathrm{Pb(II)}$ is the stable state of lead, with $\mathrm{Pb(IV)}$ a strong oxidant ([[main-group/periodic-trends#ex-lead]]).

Tin also shows how close two allotropes can be. Grey $\alpha$-tin, with the diamond structure and semiconducting, is stable below $13.2\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$; ordinary metallic white $\beta$-tin is stable above. The transformation is very slow, and white tin can be kept in the cold for a long time, but once grey tin nucleates it spreads as a crumbling powder ("tin pest"). That is one reason modern tin solders contain small additions of other metals.

Lead is the element of the **lead–acid battery**, still the commonest rechargeable battery by mass. Discharge reduces lead(IV) and oxidises lead(0), and both end as lead(II) sulfate:
$$
\mathrm{Pb(s) + PbO_2(s) + 2\,H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow 2\,PbSO_4(s) + 2\,H_2O(l)}, \qquad E^\circ = 1.69 - (-0.36) = 2.05\ \mathrm{V},
$$
a comproportionation, the reverse of disproportionation, made favourable by the stability of the middle state, the inert-pair state $\mathrm{Pb(II)}$. Lead and its compounds are toxic to the nervous system and to reproduction (GHS08); their use in paint, petrol and plumbing has been largely phased out, and lead-acid batteries are among the most completely recycled products of industry.

::: quiz
Which statement about the group-14 elements is correct?
- [ ] Silicon forms $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ molecules analogous to $\mathrm{CO_2}$, but they condense in the solid.
- [x] The stable oxidation state changes from $+4$ for carbon, silicon, germanium and tin to $+2$ for lead.
- [ ] Diamond is the thermodynamically stable form of carbon at room temperature and pressure.
- [ ] Hexagonal boron nitride conducts electricity like graphite because the two are isoelectronic.
::: solution
The inert-pair effect makes $\mathrm{Pb(II)}$ the stable state of lead while the lighter members, tin included, prefer $+4$. Silica is a network of single $\mathrm{Si{-}O}$ bonds, not a solid of $\mathrm{O{=}Si{=}O}$ molecules (the double-bond rule); diamond is metastable, with $\Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ = +2.9\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for its formation from graphite; and hexagonal BN is an insulator, because the electronegativity difference between boron and nitrogen opens a large band gap — isoelectronic structures do not imply isoelectronic properties.

## Where this leads

The electron deficiency of boron and aluminium returns as Lewis acidity throughout organic chemistry (hydroboration, Friedel–Crafts, borohydride reductions) and in the metal–carbon chemistry of [[organometallics-catalysis/main-group-organometallics]]. Silicates, zeolites and semiconductors are the core of [[materials-chemistry/porous-materials]], [[materials-chemistry/electronic-structure-solids]] and [[materials-chemistry/nanomaterials]], and silicones join the inorganic polymers of [[polymer-chemistry/polymer-structure]]. The carbonate and silicate weathering cycle that controls atmospheric $\mathrm{CO_2}$ over geological time is part of [[environmental-chemistry/earth-system-chemistry]]. In this course, the next chapter, [[main-group/groups-15-16]], carries the double-bond rule into nitrogen and phosphorus, where it explains why one element is a gas of triple-bonded molecules and the next a solid of $\mathrm{P_4}$ tetrahedra.

::: history
Boron was isolated in 1808, independently by Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac and Louis Jacques Thénard in Paris and by Humphry Davy in London, by reducing boric acid with potassium. Hans Christian Ørsted made impure aluminium in 1825 and Friedrich Wöhler a purer product in 1827, but the metal stayed precious until 1886, when Charles Martin Hall and Paul Héroult independently invented its electrolysis in molten cryolite; Karl Josef Bayer's alumina process followed in 1888. Alfred Stock prepared and characterised the volatile boron hydrides from 1912 onwards, inventing the glass vacuum line to handle them, and with Erich Pohland made borazine in 1926; William Lipscomb's structural work on the boranes earned the 1976 Nobel Prize, and Herbert C. Brown's hydroboration (1956) shared the 1979 prize. Eugene Rochow at General Electric and Richard Müller in Germany independently discovered the direct process for methylchlorosilanes in the early 1940s, making silicones an industry. Diamond was first synthesised reproducibly at General Electric by a team including H. Tracy Hall (announced in 1955). The carbon allotropes were rewritten twice in twenty years: $\mathrm{C_{60}}$ was discovered by Harold Kroto, Robert Curl, Richard Smalley and co-workers in 1985 (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1996), and graphene was isolated by Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov in 2004 (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2010).
:::

::: summary
- Group 13 is electron-deficient: $\mathrm{BX_3}$ and $\mathrm{AlX_3}$ are Lewis acids, boric acid accepts hydroxide ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}\ 9.24$), and the boranes are held together by three-centre bonds and counted by Wade's rules.
- $\mathrm{B{-}N}$ is isoelectronic with $\mathrm{C{-}C}$: BN mimics graphite and diamond and borazine mimics benzene in structure, but polarity changes colour, conductivity and reactivity.
- Aluminium is extracted in two stages — the Bayer process exploits amphoterism, the Hall–Héroult process electrolyses alumina in cryolite — and its aqua ion is a weak acid ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} \approx 5$) because of high $z/r$.
- Graphite is the stable form of carbon; diamond becomes stable above about $1.5\ \mathrm{GPa}$ at room temperature, by $(\partial\Delta_\mathrm{r}G/\partial p)_T = \Delta_\mathrm{r}V$, and is made industrially near $5$–$6\ \mathrm{GPa}$.
- Silicate formulas follow from the number $s$ of shared corners per $\mathrm{SiO_4}$ tetrahedron: $[\mathrm{SiO}_{4-s/2}]^{(4-s)-}$, from islands ($s = 0$) to frameworks ($s = 4$); aluminium substitution adds charge and creates feldspars, clays and zeolites.
- Silicones exploit the strong, flexible $\mathrm{Si{-}O}$ backbone; their structure is set by the mixture of M, D and T chlorosilanes.
- Down both groups the inert pair takes over: $\mathrm{Tl(I)}$ and $\mathrm{Pb(II)}$ are the stable states, $\mathrm{Pb(IV)}$ is an oxidant, and the lead–acid battery runs on comproportionation to $\mathrm{Pb(II)}$.
:::

Use [[#alg-silicate]] to classify the silicate in each mineral: (a) zircon, $\mathrm{ZrSiO_4}$; (b) the pyroxene enstatite, $\mathrm{MgSiO_3}$; (c) kaolinite, $\mathrm{Al_2Si_2O_5(OH)_4}$; (d) quartz.

(c) Set aside the hydroxides on aluminium: $\mathrm{Si_2O_5^{2-}}$, $2.5$ oxygens per silicon, $s = 3$, a sheet; kaolinite is a layer of silicate sheets bonded to a layer of aluminium hydroxide, the commonest clay mineral. (d) $\mathrm{SiO_2}$: $s = 4$, a neutral three-dimensional framework.

::: exercise A dilute aluminium salt {level=1 check="3.51"}
Calculate the pH of a $0.010\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ solution of aluminium nitrate, taking $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 5.0$ for $\mathrm{[Al(H_2O)_6]^{3+}}$, solving the quadratic exactly and ignoring further hydrolysis. (Enter the pH to two decimal places.)
::: solution
$x^2/(0.010 - x) = 1.0\times10^{-5}$, so $x^2 + 1.0\times10^{-5}x - 1.0\times10^{-7} = 0$ and $x = [-10^{-5} + \sqrt{10^{-10} + 4\times10^{-7}}]/2 = 3.11\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$. pH $= 3.51$. (The approximation $\sqrt{K_\mathrm{a}c}$ gives $3.16\times10^{-4}$, pH $3.50$; the difference appears in the second decimal place because $c/K_\mathrm{a}$ is only $10^3$.) Tenfold dilution raises the pH by about half a unit, the square-root behaviour of a weak acid.

::: exercise Decaborane {level=1 check="12"}
Count the skeletal electron pairs of decaborane, $\mathrm{B_{10}H_{14}}$, by Wade's rules and classify its structure. (Enter the number of skeletal electron pairs.)
::: solution
Ten $\mathrm{BH}$ units contribute $10 \times 2 = 20$ electrons, and the four extra hydrogens $4$ more: $24$ electrons, or $12$ pairs. With $n = 10$, $12 = n + 2$: **nido**, a closo eleven-vertex deltahedron (the octadecahedron) with one vertex removed — a basket-shaped cluster with the four extra hydrogens bridging around its open face.

::: exercise A day in an aluminium cell {level=2 check="2295"}
An aluminium smelting cell runs at $300\ \mathrm{kA}$ with a current efficiency of $95.0\ \%$. What mass of aluminium ($M = 26.98\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$) does it produce in $24.0\ \mathrm{h}$? ($F = 96\,485\ \mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$.) Estimate the electrical energy per kilogram if the cell voltage is $4.2\ \mathrm{V}$. (Enter the mass in kg to the nearest kilogram.)
::: solution
Charge $= 3.00\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{A} \times 86\,400\ \mathrm{s} = 2.592\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{C}$, of which $95.0\ \%$, $2.462\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{C}$, deposits metal. Moles of electrons $= 2.462\times10^{10}/96\,485 = 2.552\times10^{5}$; aluminium needs three per atom, so $8.507\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{mol}$, or $8.507\times10^{4} \times 26.98\ \mathrm{g} = 2.295\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{g} = 2295\ \mathrm{kg}$ — over two tonnes a day from one cell. Energy $= 4.2\ \mathrm{V} \times 2.592\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{C} = 1.089\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{J}$, or $30\,240\ \mathrm{kWh}$, which is $13.2\ \mathrm{kWh}$ per kilogram, within the range quoted in the text.

::: exercise The slope of the diamond line {level=2 check="1.79"}
For $\mathrm{C(graphite) \rightarrow C(diamond)}$, $\Delta_\mathrm{r}S^\circ = -3.363\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}V = -1.883\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$. Use the Clapeyron equation, $\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}T = \Delta S/\Delta V$, to find the slope of the graphite–diamond equilibrium line, and use it to estimate the equilibrium pressure at $1500\ \mathrm{K}$, starting from $1.54\ \mathrm{GPa}$ at $298\ \mathrm{K}$. (Enter the slope in $\mathrm{MPa\,K^{-1}}$ to three significant figures.)
::: solution
$\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}T = (-3.363\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}})/(-1.883\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}) = 1.786\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{Pa\,K^{-1}} = 1.79\ \mathrm{MPa\,K^{-1}}$. Over $1202\ \mathrm{K}$ the pressure rises by $1.79 \times 1202 = 2150\ \mathrm{MPa}$, so $p \approx 1.54 + 2.15 = 3.7\ \mathrm{GPa}$ at $1500\ \mathrm{K}$. The measured boundary is somewhat higher (near $4.5$–$5\ \mathrm{GPa}$) because $\Delta_\mathrm{r}S$ and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}V$ both change with temperature and pressure; the linear estimate gets the direction and the order of magnitude, which is what it is for.

::: exercise Designing a silicone oil {level=2 check="3870"}
A silicone oil is made by co-hydrolysing $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3SiCl}$ and $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2SiCl_2}$ in the mole ratio $2 : 50$, giving on average the linear molecule $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3SiO[(CH_3)_2SiO]_{50}Si(CH_3)_3}$. Calculate its molar mass ($\mathrm{C}$ $12.011$, $\mathrm{H}$ $1.008$, $\mathrm{O}$ $15.999$, $\mathrm{Si}$ $28.086\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$), and say what adding some $\mathrm{CH_3SiCl_3}$ would do. (Enter the molar mass in $\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ to the nearest unit.)
::: solution
Split the molecule into two end groups $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3SiO_{1/2}}$ (an "M" unit: $3(12.011) + 9(1.008) + 28.086 + 7.9995 = 81.19\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$) and fifty $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2SiO}$ "D" units ($2(12.011) + 6(1.008) + 28.086 + 15.999 = 74.155\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$). $M = 2(81.19) + 50(74.155) = 162.38 + 3707.75 = 3870\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. (Check: the formula is $\mathrm{C_{106}H_{318}O_{51}Si_{52}}$.) The ratio of chain-stopping M to chain-extending D units sets the chain length and hence the viscosity. Adding trifunctional T units creates branch points; with enough of them the chains join into a network, and the oil becomes a rubber or, with many cross-links, a rigid resin.

::: exercise Borazine and boron nitride {level=2}
(a) Draw the Lewis structure of borazine without formal charges, and the resonance form with $\mathrm{B{=}N}$ double bonds; assign formal charges in the second. (b) Explain why hexagonal BN layers stack with B over N while graphite layers are offset. (c) Suggest why hexagonal BN is used as a high-temperature lubricant and crucible material in air, where graphite would burn.
::: solution
(a) Without formal charges: a six-membered ring of alternating $\mathrm{BH}$ and $\mathrm{NH}$ joined by single bonds, each nitrogen carrying a lone pair and each boron an empty $p$ orbital. In the form with three $\mathrm{B{=}N}$ bonds each nitrogen has four bonds (formal charge $+1$) and each boron four bonds (formal charge $-1$) — the formal charges run *against* electronegativity, which is why this form contributes only partly and the $\pi$ density stays mainly on nitrogen. (b) In BN the boron atoms are $\delta+$ and the nitrogens $\delta-$; stacking each boron over a nitrogen of the next layer maximises electrostatic attraction between layers. Graphite has no partial charges, and its offset (ABAB) stacking minimises repulsion between the $\pi$ clouds. (c) BN is chemically the "burnt" form already in one sense — its strong, polar $\mathrm{B{-}N}$ bonds and wide band gap make it resistant to oxidation up to about $1000\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ in air (a protective boron oxide skin forms), while graphite burns to $\mathrm{CO_2}$ at much lower temperatures. Its layers slide over one another like graphite's, giving the lubricating behaviour.
:::

::: exercise Deriving the silicate rule {level=3}
(a) Prove the formula $[\mathrm{SiO}_{4-s/2}]^{(4-s)-}$ of [[#alg-silicate]] from oxygen counting and charge neutrality. (b) Apply it to an amphibole double chain, in which half the tetrahedra share two corners and half share three, and give the repeat unit with four silicon atoms. (c) Show that a framework with one aluminium for every three silicons has charge $1-$ per four tetrahedra, and give the formula of the sodium feldspar albite.
::: hint
For (b), average $s$ over the tetrahedra before applying the formula, then multiply by four.
:::
::: solution
(a) Each tetrahedron has four oxygens. The $4 - s$ unshared oxygens belong to it alone, and each of the $s$ shared oxygens is split between two tetrahedra, so oxygens per silicon $= (4 - s) + s/2 = 4 - s/2$. The oxidation states give a charge $4 + (4 - s/2)(-2) = s - 4$, i.e. an anion of charge $(4 - s)-$ per silicon. (b) Average $s = (2 + 3)/2 = 2.5$: oxygens per silicon $= 4 - 1.25 = 2.75$, charge $1.5-$ per silicon. For four silicons: $\mathrm{Si_4O_{11}^{6-}}$, the amphibole unit (as in tremolite, $\mathrm{Ca_2Mg_5(Si_4O_{11})_2(OH)_2}$, whose cations supply $4 + 10 = 14$ positive charges against $12 + 2 = 14$ negative). (c) With $s = 4$, a framework of four tetrahedra has eight oxygens. With three Si(IV) and one Al(III) the positive charge is $3(4) + 3 = 15$ against $16$ negative, so the framework is $\mathrm{[AlSi_3O_8]^-}$, one negative charge per aluminium, and albite is $\mathrm{NaAlSi_3O_8}$. Replacing a second silicon by aluminium needs a doubly charged cation, as in anorthite, $\mathrm{CaAl_2Si_2O_8}$, which still obeys Löwenstein's rule.
:::

::: exercise What the Bayer process leaves behind {level=3}
Bauxite contains aluminium hydroxide–oxides, iron(III) oxide, silica and small amounts of gallium. (a) Explain, using the oxide classification of [[main-group/inorganic-acids-bases#def-oxides]] and the aqua-ion acidity of [[#def-aqua-acidity]], why hot sodium hydroxide dissolves the aluminium but not the iron. (b) Silica is attacked too, and then reacts with aluminate to precipitate a sodium aluminosilicate; why does this cost the process both sodium hydroxide and aluminium? (c) Most of the world's gallium is recovered from Bayer liquors. Why does gallium follow aluminium?
::: hint
Gallium sits directly below aluminium; compare the sizes of $\mathrm{Al^{3+}}$, $\mathrm{Ga^{3+}}$ and $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ (53.5, 62 and 64.5 pm) and recall the d-block contraction.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\mathrm{Al^{3+}}$ is small and highly charged, so $\mathrm{Al(OH)_3}$ is amphoteric: in strong base it gives up a proton from a bound water (equivalently, accepts $\mathrm{OH^-}$) to form soluble $\mathrm{[Al(OH)_4]^-}$. Iron(III) oxide is basic; it does not form a stable soluble hydroxo-anion at the hydroxide concentrations and temperatures of the process, so it remains as red mud. (b) Silica, an acidic oxide, dissolves as silicate, but silicate and aluminate then combine with sodium ions to precipitate an insoluble sodium aluminosilicate ("desilication product"); every unit of it removes sodium (as NaOH lost) and aluminium (as product lost) from the liquor, which is why high-silica bauxites are expensive to process. (c) $\mathrm{Ga^{3+}}$ has the same charge as $\mathrm{Al^{3+}}$ and, because of the d-block contraction, is not much larger; $\mathrm{Ga(OH)_3}$ is amphoteric too and dissolves as $\mathrm{[Ga(OH)_4]^-}$. Gallium therefore travels with aluminium into the liquor, where it accumulates as the liquor is recycled, and is recovered from it. Iron(III), though similar in size to gallium(III), stays behind because its oxide is basic; size and charge alone do not decide amphoterism, which also depends on the covalency of the $\mathrm{M{-}O}$ bond.
:::
