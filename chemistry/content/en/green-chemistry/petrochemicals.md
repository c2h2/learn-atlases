A modern steam cracker is a row of furnaces, each containing tubes a few centimetres in diameter through which a mixture of hydrocarbon and steam flows at about $850\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The gas spends a few tenths of a second in the hot zone and is then quenched within milliseconds. In that fraction of a second, ethane, propane or naphtha is broken into ethylene, propylene, butadiene, aromatics and hydrogen, and the plant beyond the furnaces — a cold box at $-100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and a train of distillation columns — separates them. A single cracker can make more than a million tonnes of ethylene a year, and ethylene is the largest-volume organic chemical: the monomer of polyethylene and the starting point of ethylene oxide, ethylene glycol, vinyl chloride and styrene.

Most of the carbon in the organic chemical industry comes from oil and natural gas, and travels through a remarkably small number of molecules. The **petrochemical** industry converts fractions of crude oil and natural-gas liquids into a handful of building blocks — ethylene, propylene, the C₄ olefins and butadiene, benzene, toluene and the xylenes, and methanol from synthesis gas — and these into the intermediates and polymers on which much of modern material life depends. This chapter follows that path. It starts with refining, where the fractions are made; analyses steam cracking, an endothermic reaction controlled by thermodynamics and by the kinetics of a radical chain; and then surveys the principal derivatives, using atom economy and selectivity to explain why some routes have been abandoned and others adopted. The comparison of old and new routes — chlorohydrin against direct oxidation, the balanced vinyl chloride process, hydrogen peroxide for propylene oxide — is green chemistry practised by the bulk industry under economic pressure. The vocabulary of conversion, selectivity and recycle is that of [[green-chemistry/chemical-industry]]; the catalytic chemistry behind several processes is in [[organometallics-catalysis]].

## From crude oil to fractions

Crude oil is a mixture of thousands of hydrocarbons — alkanes (paraffins), cycloalkanes (naphthenes) and aromatics — with small amounts of sulfur, nitrogen and oxygen compounds and traces of metals. A refinery's first step is **atmospheric distillation**, which separates it by boiling range into fractions: refinery gas and liquefied petroleum gas (C₁–C₄), **naphtha** (roughly C₅–C₁₀, boiling up to about $180\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$), kerosene, gas oil (diesel), and an atmospheric residue that is further distilled under vacuum. Most of the crude becomes fuels; only around a tenth or so of oil demand goes to petrochemical feedstocks, but that fraction is growing faster than fuel demand.

The fractions are then reshaped by conversion processes.

::: definition Cracking, reforming and hydrotreating {#def-refining}
- **Thermal (steam) cracking** breaks C–C and C–H bonds by free-radical reactions at high temperature without a catalyst; with steam dilution and short residence times it maximises light olefins.
- **Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC)** cracks heavy gas oils over a zeolite catalyst (faujasite, zeolite Y, in a matrix) at about $500\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ by carbocation chemistry; it makes mainly gasoline, with propylene and butenes as by-products. The catalyst is coked in seconds and continuously regenerated by burning the coke in a fluidised bed.
- **Catalytic reforming** converts naphtha over platinum (often with rhenium or tin) on chlorided alumina at about $500\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, dehydrogenating cycloalkanes and dehydrocyclising alkanes into aromatics, with hydrogen as a valuable by-product. It is the main source of benzene, toluene and xylenes (BTX).
- **Hydrotreating** removes sulfur, nitrogen and metals by reaction with hydrogen over cobalt–molybdenum or nickel–molybdenum sulfide catalysts; the sulfur leaves as $\mathrm{H_2S}$, which is converted to elemental sulfur in the Claus process and largely becomes the feed of sulfuric acid plants ([[green-chemistry/industrial-inorganic]]).
:::

Carbocation and radical chemistry give quite different products. A carbocation in a zeolite rearranges to the most stable (tertiary) ion, and β-scission produces branched fragments and little ethylene, because a primary carbocation is too unstable to form; radicals do not rearrange easily, and β-scission of primary radicals gives ethylene. That is why ethylene is made by steam cracking rather than catalytic cracking.

## Steam cracking

### Thermodynamics

The simplest cracking reaction is the dehydrogenation of ethane:
$$
\mathrm{C_2H_6(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{C_2H_4(g)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)} .
$$
From NBS data ($\Delta_\mathrm{f}H^\circ$: ethane $-84.68$, ethylene $+52.26\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; $S^\circ$: ethane $229.60$, ethylene $219.56$, $\mathrm{H_2}$ $130.68\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}$), $\Delta_\mathrm{r}H^\circ = +136.9\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}S^\circ = +120.6\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}$. This is the mirror image of ammonia synthesis: endothermic, with an increase in the number of gas molecules. High temperature and low pressure favour the products, and $K = 1$ only at $T \approx \Delta_\mathrm{r}H^\circ/\Delta_\mathrm{r}S^\circ = 1135\ \mathrm{K}$.

::: proposition Equilibrium of a dehydrogenation with diluent {#prop-dilution}
For $\mathrm{A(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B(g)} + \mathrm{C(g)}$ fed as pure $\mathrm{A}$ diluted with $s$ moles of an inert gas (steam) per mole of $\mathrm{A}$, at total pressure $p$ and with ideal gases, the equilibrium conversion $X$ satisfies
$$
K = \frac{X^2}{(1 - X)(1 + X + s)}\,\frac{p}{p^\circ},
\qquad
X = \frac{-Ks + \sqrt{K^2s^2 + 4(p/p^\circ + K)K(1 + s)}}{2(p/p^\circ + K)} .
$$ {#eq-dilution}
At fixed $T$, $X$ increases as $p$ decreases and as $s$ increases: dilution acts like a reduction of pressure.
:::

::: proof
Per mole of A fed, at conversion $X$ the amounts are $1 - X$ of A, $X$ each of B and C, and $s$ of inert, total $n = 1 + X + s$. For ideal gases $K = (x_\mathrm{B}x_\mathrm{C}/x_\mathrm{A})(p/p^\circ)$, the pressure exponent being $\Delta\nu = +1$, and $x_\mathrm{B}x_\mathrm{C}/x_\mathrm{A} = [X^2/n^2]/[(1 - X)/n] = X^2/[(1 - X)n]$, which gives the first equation. Multiplying out, $(p/p^\circ)X^2 = K(1 - X)(1 + X + s) = K(1 + s - sX - X^2)$, a quadratic $(p/p^\circ + K)X^2 + KsX - K(1 + s) = 0$ whose positive root is [[#eq-dilution]]. Increasing $s$ increases $n$ and so lowers the partial pressures of B and C, exactly as lowering $p$ does; formally $X$ depends on $p$ and $s$ through the partial pressure of the reacting species.
:::

(a) $s = 0$: $X^2/(1 - X^2) = K/(p/p^\circ) = 0.315$, so $X^2 = 0.315/1.315 = 0.2395$ and $X = 0.489$.

$$
\frac{136.9\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}{28.05\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{g} = 4.88\ \mathrm{GJ},
$$
supplied at about $850\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ by burning fuel in the furnace — largely the methane and hydrogen by-products of cracking. Heating the feed and steam to reaction temperature and running the cryogenic separation roughly treble this; Ren, Patel and Blok (2006) estimated specific energy consumptions of about $17$–$21\ \mathrm{GJ}$ per tonne of ethylene for ethane cracking and $26$–$31\ \mathrm{GJ}$ for naphtha, on their basis of allocation. Steam cracking is the single most energy-intensive process in the organic chemical industry.
:::
:::

### Mechanism and selectivity

Cracking proceeds by a free-radical chain, first analysed for ethane by Rice and Herzfeld (1934):

1. **Initiation**: homolysis of the weakest bond, the C–C bond: $\mathrm{C_2H_6 \rightarrow 2\,CH_3^{\bullet}}$.
2. **Hydrogen abstraction**: $\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet} + C_2H_6 \rightarrow CH_4 + C_2H_5^{\bullet}}$.
3. **β-Scission** (propagation): the ethyl radical breaks the C–H bond β to the radical centre, giving ethylene and a hydrogen atom: $\mathrm{C_2H_5^{\bullet} \rightarrow C_2H_4 + H^{\bullet}}$.
4. **Propagation**: $\mathrm{H^{\bullet} + C_2H_6 \rightarrow H_2 + C_2H_5^{\bullet}}$, regenerating the chain carrier.
5. **Termination** by radical recombination or disproportionation.

Steps 3 and 4 form the chain that makes ethylene and hydrogen; the chain length is long, so the overall stoichiometry is close to $\mathrm{C_2H_6 \rightarrow C_2H_4 + H_2}$, with a little methane from initiation. The kinetics of such chains are developed in [[kinetics/chain-reactions]]. For larger alkanes, radicals formed by abstraction at different carbons undergo β-scission to give a distribution of smaller olefins and radicals, which is why naphtha gives ethylene, propylene, butadiene and aromatics together.

The products are themselves reactive. Ethylene and propylene can add radicals, oligomerise and cyclise to aromatics, and eventually form coke on the tube walls, which insulates the tube and must be burnt off periodically. The desired product is an *intermediate* in a sequence of reactions, so its yield passes through a maximum with residence time.

::: proposition Maximum yield of an intermediate {#prop-consecutive}
For consecutive first-order reactions $\mathrm{A} \xrightarrow{k_1} \mathrm{B} \xrightarrow{k_2} \mathrm{C}$ in a plug-flow or batch reactor with only $\mathrm{A}$ at $t = 0$, the yield of $\mathrm{B}$ is
$$
\frac{[\mathrm{B}]}{[\mathrm{A}]_0} = \frac{k_1}{k_2 - k_1}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t} - \mathrm{e}^{-k_2t}\right) \quad (k_1 \neq k_2),
$$ {#eq-consecutive}
which is greatest at $t_\mathrm{max} = \ln(k_2/k_1)/(k_2 - k_1)$, where it equals $(k_1/k_2)^{k_2/(k_2 - k_1)}$. The maximum yield depends only on the ratio $\kappa = k_2/k_1$, falling from $1$ as $\kappa \to 0$ to $1/\mathrm{e}$ at $\kappa = 1$ and towards $0$ as $\kappa$ becomes large.
:::

::: proof
The rate equations are $\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]/\mathrm{d}t = -k_1[\mathrm{A}]$ and $\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{B}]/\mathrm{d}t = k_1[\mathrm{A}] - k_2[\mathrm{B}]$. The first gives $[\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{A}]_0\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t}$; the second is linear, $\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{B}]/\mathrm{d}t + k_2[\mathrm{B}] = k_1[\mathrm{A}]_0\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t}$, and with the integrating factor $\mathrm{e}^{k_2t}$ and $[\mathrm{B}](0) = 0$ it gives [[#eq-consecutive]] ([[kinetics/rate-laws]]). Setting the derivative to zero, $k_1\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t} = k_2\mathrm{e}^{-k_2t}$, so $\mathrm{e}^{(k_2 - k_1)t} = k_2/k_1$ and $t_\mathrm{max} = \ln(k_2/k_1)/(k_2 - k_1)$. At this time $\mathrm{e}^{-k_2t} = (k_1/k_2)\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t}$, so the bracket is $\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t}(1 - k_1/k_2)$ and the yield is $(k_1/k_2)\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t_\mathrm{max}} = (k_1/k_2)(k_1/k_2)^{k_1/(k_2 - k_1)} = (k_1/k_2)^{k_2/(k_2 - k_1)}$. With $\kappa = k_2/k_1$ this is $\kappa^{-\kappa/(\kappa - 1)}$, which tends to $1/\mathrm{e}$ as $\kappa \to 1$.
:::

::: example How long in the furnace? {#ex-residence}
In an idealised model of a cracking coil at its outlet temperature, ethane is consumed with $k_1 = 20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and ethylene is converted into heavier products and coke precursors with $k_2 = 4.0\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, both treated as first order. Find the residence time that maximises the ethylene yield, the yield and ethane conversion at that time, and the yield if the gas stayed three times as long.
::: solution
With $\kappa = k_2/k_1 = 0.20$, [[#prop-consecutive]] gives
$$
t_\mathrm{max} = \frac{\ln(4.0/20)}{4.0 - 20}\ \mathrm{s} = \frac{-1.609}{-16}\ \mathrm{s} = 0.101\ \mathrm{s},
\qquad
Y_\mathrm{max} = \left(\frac{k_1}{k_2}\right)^{k_2/(k_2 - k_1)} = 5^{4/(-16)} = 5^{-0.25} = 0.669 .
$$
The ethane conversion at $t_\mathrm{max}$ is $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-20\times0.101} = 0.866$. At $0.30\ \mathrm{s}$ the ethane is almost all consumed ($99.8\,\%$) but the ethylene yield has fallen to $\tfrac{20}{-16}(\mathrm{e}^{-6} - \mathrm{e}^{-1.2}) = 0.373$. Real crackers aim for residence times of $0.1$–$0.5\ \mathrm{s}$ and somewhat lower ethane conversions (about $60$–$70\,\%$, with recycle of the unconverted ethane), because selectivity falls steeply as conversion approaches its maximum-yield point and because coking accelerates. The model ignores the reversibility and radical-chain nature of the real kinetics, but captures why the residence time is measured in tenths of a second and why the gas is quenched in milliseconds in transfer-line exchangers.
:::
:::

### Feedstocks and products

What comes out of a cracker depends on what goes in. Ethane gives mainly ethylene (ultimate yields around $80\,\%$ by mass with recycle of unconverted ethane) and hydrogen; propane and butane give more propylene; naphtha gives roughly $30\,\%$ ethylene, $15\,\%$ propylene, butadiene and other C₄s, and a "pyrolysis gasoline" rich in benzene, with methane and hydrogen burnt as fuel. The shale-gas boom in the United States from about 2010 made ethane cheap and shifted new capacity there towards ethane crackers, which produce little propylene and butadiene; the shortfall is met by **on-purpose** routes such as propane dehydrogenation over platinum–tin or chromium oxide catalysts, and in China by **methanol-to-olefins** (MTO) over the zeolite SAPO-34, with methanol made from coal-derived synthesis gas — a route with a much larger $\mathrm{CO_2}$ footprint.

Global plastics production, the largest outlet for the olefins, was about $431\ \mathrm{Mt}$ in 2024 (Plastics Europe, *Plastics – the fast Facts 2025*), the great majority from fossil feedstocks.

## Synthesis gas and methanol

The second carbon pathway runs through **synthesis gas** (syngas), mixtures of CO and $\mathrm{H_2}$ made by steam reforming of natural gas, partial oxidation of heavier oils, or gasification of coal. Methanol is made from syngas over copper–zinc oxide–alumina catalysts at about $250\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $50$–$100\ \mathrm{bar}$:
$$
\mathrm{CO + 2\,H_2 \rightleftharpoons CH_3OH}, \qquad \mathrm{CO_2 + 3\,H_2 \rightleftharpoons CH_3OH + H_2O}.
$$
Isotope-labelling studies showed that under industrial conditions most of the methanol is formed from the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ in the gas, the CO acting mainly through the water–gas shift — which makes the same catalysts candidates for $\mathrm{CO_2}$ utilisation ([[green-chemistry/carbon-hydrogen]]). Like ammonia synthesis, methanol synthesis is exothermic with a decrease in moles and is run in a recycle loop. Methanol is the feed for formaldehyde, acetic acid, methyl *tert*-butyl ether and, via MTO, olefins.

**Acetic acid** shows how catalysis has transformed atom economy. The Monsanto process (1970), using a rhodium iodide catalyst, and BP's iridium-based Cativa process (1996) carbonylate methanol, $\mathrm{CH_3OH + CO \rightarrow CH_3COOH}$, with $100\,\%$ atom economy and selectivities above $99\,\%$ based on methanol; the organometallic catalytic cycle (oxidative addition of methyl iodide, migratory insertion of CO, reductive elimination of acetyl iodide) is a standard example in [[organometallics-catalysis/elementary-reactions]]. Hydroformylation of propylene to butanal, another organometallic process, is treated in [[organometallics-catalysis/hydrogenation-hydroformylation]].

## Derivatives of ethylene

### Ethylene oxide

Ethylene oxide (oxirane), the precursor of ethylene glycol (for polyester and antifreeze), ethoxylate surfactants and ethanolamines, was first made by the **chlorohydrin route**: ethylene reacts with chlorine water to give 2-chloroethanol, which is cyclised with base (lime):
$$
\mathrm{C_2H_4 + Cl_2 + H_2O \rightarrow HOCH_2CH_2Cl + HCl}, \qquad 2\,\mathrm{HOCH_2CH_2Cl + Ca(OH)_2 \rightarrow 2\,C_2H_4O + CaCl_2 + 2\,H_2O}.
$$
All the chlorine ends as calcium chloride, with chlorinated organic by-products. Since the 1930s (Union Carbide, 1937) and almost exclusively now, ethylene oxide is made by **direct oxidation** with oxygen over silver supported on α-alumina, at about $230$–$280\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $10$–$30\ \mathrm{bar}$:
$$
\mathrm{C_2H_4 + \tfrac12O_2 \rightarrow C_2H_4O} \quad (\mathrm{AE} = 100\,\%), \qquad \mathrm{C_2H_4 + 3\,O_2 \rightarrow 2\,CO_2 + 2\,H_2O}.
$$
The competing total combustion, and the consecutive combustion of ethylene oxide itself, limit the selectivity. Silver is unique among metals in giving useful selectivity; trace chlorine compounds (ppm levels of chloroethane in the feed) and caesium and rhenium promoters raise it from about $70\,\%$ for the early catalysts to $85$–$90\,\%$ today, with per-pass conversions of $10\,\%$ or less and recycle of unreacted ethylene.

::: example Selectivity as carbon dioxide {#ex-eo}
An ethylene oxide plant operates at $85\,\%$ selectivity, the rest of the ethylene consumed being burnt to $\mathrm{CO_2}$. Find the ethylene consumed and the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ formed per tonne of ethylene oxide, and the saving from raising the selectivity to $90\,\%$. Compare the atom economy of the chlorohydrin route.
::: solution
Per mole of ethylene oxide, $1/0.85 = 1.176\ \mathrm{mol}$ of ethylene is consumed, of which $0.176\ \mathrm{mol}$ is burnt to $2\times0.176 = 0.353\ \mathrm{mol}\ \mathrm{CO_2}$. Per tonne ($M$: $\mathrm{C_2H_4O}$ $44.05$, $\mathrm{C_2H_4}$ $28.05$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ $44.01$):
$$
m(\mathrm{C_2H_4}) = 1.176\times\frac{28.05}{44.05} = 0.749\ \mathrm{t}, \qquad m(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.353\times\frac{44.01}{44.05} = 0.353\ \mathrm{t}.
$$
At $90\,\%$: $0.708\ \mathrm{t}$ of ethylene and $0.222\ \mathrm{t}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ — five points of selectivity save $41\ \mathrm{kg}$ of ethylene and $131\ \mathrm{kg}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ per tonne, which over a plant making several hundred thousand tonnes a year justifies a large research effort on catalysts. This combustion $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is a concentrated stream removed from the recycle gas, and is one of the cheaper sources of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ for capture.

For the chlorohydrin route, the overall equation $\mathrm{C_2H_4 + Cl_2 + Ca(OH)_2 \rightarrow C_2H_4O + CaCl_2 + H_2O}$ gives $\mathrm{AE} = 44.05/(28.05 + 70.90 + 74.09) = 25.5\,\%$, with about $2.5\ \mathrm{t}$ of calcium chloride and water per tonne of product even at perfect yield.
:::
:::

Ethylene oxide is a highly flammable gas and a carcinogen; it is handled in closed systems, and the reactor is operated outside the flammable range of the ethylene–oxygen mixture with methane as ballast gas. Most of it is converted on site to ethylene glycol by hydrolysis; Shell's OMEGA process goes via ethylene carbonate, from ethylene oxide and $\mathrm{CO_2}$, which hydrolyses with much higher selectivity to the monoglycol than direct hydration.

### Vinyl chloride and the balanced process

Vinyl chloride (chloroethene), the monomer of PVC, is made from ethylene and chlorine via 1,2-dichloroethane (EDC). Direct chlorination, $\mathrm{C_2H_4 + Cl_2 \rightarrow ClCH_2CH_2Cl}$, is followed by thermal cracking of EDC at about $500\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $\mathrm{ClCH_2CH_2Cl \rightarrow CH_2{=}CHCl + HCl}$, which releases half of the chlorine as hydrogen chloride. Rather than selling or neutralising the HCl, the **balanced process** feeds it to **oxychlorination**, $\mathrm{C_2H_4 + 2\,HCl + \tfrac12O_2 \rightarrow ClCH_2CH_2Cl + H_2O}$, over a copper(II) chloride catalyst, making more EDC. With equal amounts of EDC from the two routes, the HCl is consumed as fast as it is formed.

::: example Atom economy of the balanced VCM process {#ex-vcm}
Write the overall equation of the balanced process and find its atom economy ($M$: $\mathrm{C_2H_4}$ $28.05$, $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ $70.90$, $\mathrm{O_2}$ $32.00$, $\mathrm{C_2H_3Cl}$ $62.50$).
::: solution
Add direct chlorination, oxychlorination and the cracking of two EDC:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{C_2H_4 + Cl_2} &\rightarrow \mathrm{C_2H_4Cl_2}\\
\mathrm{C_2H_4 + 2\,HCl + \tfrac12O_2} &\rightarrow \mathrm{C_2H_4Cl_2 + H_2O}\\
2\,\mathrm{C_2H_4Cl_2} &\rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{C_2H_3Cl} + 2\,\mathrm{HCl}\\ \hline
2\,\mathrm{C_2H_4 + Cl_2 + \tfrac12O_2} &\rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{C_2H_3Cl} + \mathrm{H_2O}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\mathrm{AE} = 2\times62.50/(2\times28.05 + 70.90 + 16.00) = 125.0/143.0 = 87.4\,\%$, with water as the only by-product. Without oxychlorination, the cracking step alone makes one HCl per vinyl chloride, and the atom economy of $\mathrm{C_2H_4 + Cl_2 \rightarrow C_2H_3Cl + HCl}$ is $62.50/98.95 = 63.2\,\%$. In practice by-products — chlorinated ethanes and heavier compounds from oxychlorination and cracking — are incinerated with HCl recovery. Vinyl chloride is a carcinogen (angiosarcoma of the liver in exposed workers was recognised in the 1970s), and occupational exposure limits were cut by orders of magnitude after that discovery: a case where risk management ([[green-chemistry/green-principles#def-hazard-risk]]) transformed the operation of an entire industry.
:::
:::

## Derivatives of propylene and aromatics

**Propylene oxide** illustrates three generations of process chemistry. The **chlorohydrin** route, analogous to that for ethylene oxide, produces about two tonnes of calcium chloride per tonne of product. The **co-product (Halcon/ARCO) routes** oxidise isobutane or ethylbenzene to a hydroperoxide, which transfers an oxygen atom to propylene over a molybdenum or titanium catalyst; the co-products, *tert*-butanol (to MTBE) or styrene (via 1-phenylethanol), are made in fixed ratio, about $2.2$–$2.5\ \mathrm{t}$ of styrene per tonne of propylene oxide, tying the plant to two markets. The **HPPO process**, first operated commercially in 2008 (BASF and Dow at Antwerp; Evonik and Uhde in Korea), epoxidises propylene with hydrogen peroxide over titanium silicalite-1 (TS-1), a zeolite in which isolated titanium atoms activate $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$:
$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 + H_2O_2 \rightarrow CH_3CH(O)CH_2 + H_2O} .
$$
Its by-product is water, and the Presidential Green Chemistry Challenge recognised it in 2010. The hydrogen peroxide itself is made by the anthraquinone autoxidation process, whose own solvent, energy and hydrogen use must be included in a fair life-cycle comparison ([[green-chemistry/green-metrics#alg-lca]]).

**Phenol and acetone** are made together by the **cumene process** (Hock, 1944): benzene is alkylated with propylene over a zeolite to give isopropylbenzene (cumene), which is autoxidised by air to cumene hydroperoxide; acid-catalysed rearrangement (the Hock rearrangement, a migration of the phenyl group to electron-deficient oxygen) cleaves it to phenol and acetone. The overall stoichiometry, $\mathrm{C_6H_6 + C_3H_6 + O_2 \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + (CH_3)_2CO}$, has $100\,\%$ atom economy to the two products — but in fixed ratio, $58.08/94.11 = 0.617\ \mathrm{t}$ of acetone per tonne of phenol, whatever the demand for each. Phenol goes largely to bisphenol A (with acetone, conveniently) and phenolic resins.

**Benzene, toluene and xylenes** feed ethylbenzene and styrene (polystyrene), cumene, cyclohexane (for nylon intermediates), nitrobenzene and aniline (for polyurethane isocyanates) and, above all, *para*-xylene, oxidised in the Amoco/Mid-Century process by air with a cobalt–manganese–bromide catalyst in acetic acid to terephthalic acid, the monomer of poly(ethylene terephthalate). Toluene, in surplus, is disproportionated to benzene and xylenes. Benzene is a human carcinogen, which is why its content in gasoline is regulated and why it is handled in closed systems.

::: warning A 100 % atom economy can hide a co-product problem {#warn-coproduct}
The cumene process and the co-product propylene oxide routes are perfectly or nearly atom-economical, yet both are vulnerable: they produce two products in a fixed ratio, and if the market for one collapses its price falls, it must be stored or burnt, and the economics and the real waste of the process deteriorate. Atom economy counts the co-product as product; the market may not. Conversely, a process with a lower atom economy whose by-product is benign and saleable may be more robust. Process designers value **flexibility** — routes such as HPPO or on-purpose propylene that make one product — for this reason.
:::

::: quiz
Why is ethylene made by thermal (steam) cracking rather than by catalytic cracking over zeolites?
- [ ] Zeolites are destroyed by ethylene
- [x] Carbocations rearrange to branched, more stable ions and rarely form primary cations, so acid catalysis gives branched and larger fragments; radical β-scission readily gives ethylene
- [ ] Catalytic cracking is endothermic and thermal cracking exothermic
- [ ] Thermal cracking is carried out at lower temperature
::: solution
The mechanisms differ. In catalytic cracking, carbocations rearrange by hydride and methyl shifts towards tertiary ions, and β-scission yielding a primary cation (which would be needed to form ethylene) is strongly disfavoured, so the products are gasoline-range branched alkanes, propylene and butenes. Free radicals do not rearrange easily and primary radicals undergo β-scission to give ethylene. Both processes are endothermic, and thermal cracking runs hotter (about $850$ against $500\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$).
:::
:::

## Carbon, energy and the future of petrochemicals

Petrochemicals face two related challenges. The first is **emissions**: the high-value chemicals (olefins and aromatics) accounted for about $27\,\%$ of the roughly $935\ \mathrm{Mt}$ of direct $\mathrm{CO_2}$ from primary chemicals in 2022 (IEA, latest dated value as of 2026), mostly from cracker furnaces. Options under development include electrically heated cracking furnaces (a demonstration plant was started by BASF, SABIC and Linde at Ludwigshafen in 2024), capture of the furnace $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and the use of hydrogen-rich fuel gas. The second is **feedstock**: even with zero process emissions, the carbon in the products is fossil, and it is released when they are incinerated at the end of their life. Replacing it requires biomass ([[green-chemistry/renewable-feedstocks]]), captured $\mathrm{CO_2}$ with low-emission hydrogen ([[green-chemistry/carbon-hydrogen]]) or recycled plastics ([[green-chemistry/circular-economy]]), each with its own constraints on scale and energy.

::: history From coal tar to the cracker {#hist-petrochem}
The first organic chemical industry ran on coal tar, the by-product of town gas, which supplied benzene, toluene, naphthalene and phenol for dyes from the 1850s. Thermal cracking of heavy oil to gasoline was patented by William Burton of Standard Oil of Indiana in 1913, and Eugène Houdry introduced catalytic cracking in 1937; fluid catalytic cracking followed in 1942, and zeolite catalysts, developed at Mobil, in the early 1960s. Union Carbide began making ethylene and its derivatives from natural-gas liquids at Clendenin, West Virginia, in 1920, generally taken as the start of the petrochemical industry, and commercialised silver-catalysed ethylene oxide in 1937. Heinrich Hock and Shon Lang described the cumene hydroperoxide rearrangement in 1944. Karl Ziegler's low-pressure polymerisation of ethylene and Giulio Natta's stereoregular polypropylene (1953–1954, Nobel Prize 1963) and the Phillips chromium catalyst (Hogan and Banks, 1951) created the polyolefin industry. Monsanto's rhodium-catalysed methanol carbonylation started in 1970, and the HPPO process in 2008. Wittcoff, Reuben and Plotkin's *Industrial Organic Chemicals* describes the resulting network product by product.
:::

## Where this leads

The building blocks of this chapter are the monomers of [[polymer-chemistry]], whose end-of-life fate is the subject of [[green-chemistry/circular-economy]]. The oxygen-free character of petrochemical feedstocks, against the oxygen-rich structure of biomass, frames the challenge of [[green-chemistry/renewable-feedstocks]]. Synthesis gas, methanol and the hydrogen of reforming lead to [[green-chemistry/carbon-hydrogen]]. Many of the separations — distillation of close-boiling hydrocarbons, extraction of aromatics with sulfolane — and the solvent choices in derivatives connect to [[green-chemistry/safer-solvents]].

::: summary
- Refining separates crude oil by distillation and reshapes it by catalytic cracking (carbocations, zeolites, gasoline and propylene), reforming (Pt, aromatics and $\mathrm{H_2}$) and hydrotreating (sulfur to $\mathrm{H_2S}$ and then to sulfur).
- Ethane cracking is endothermic ($+136.9\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) with an increase in moles; $K = 1$ near $1135\ \mathrm{K}$, and steam dilution acts like lower pressure ($X = 0.49$ without and $0.54$ with $0.5$ steam at $1100\ \mathrm{K}$ and $2\ \mathrm{bar}$).
- Cracking is a radical chain (Rice–Herzfeld); the olefin products react further, so the yield of an intermediate peaks at $t_\mathrm{max} = \ln(k_2/k_1)/(k_2 - k_1)$ — hence millisecond quenching and limited per-pass conversion.
- Synthesis gas gives methanol (Cu/ZnO, mostly via $\mathrm{CO_2}$) and, by carbonylation (Rh, Ir), acetic acid with $100\,\%$ atom economy.
- Direct silver-catalysed ethylene oxide ($\mathrm{AE} = 100\,\%$, $85$–$90\,\%$ selectivity) replaced the chlorohydrin route ($25.5\,\%$); the balanced vinyl chloride process recycles HCl ($87.4\,\%$); HPPO gives propylene oxide with water as by-product.
- Fixed co-product ratios (phenol/acetone $1 : 0.617$, propylene oxide/styrene) make perfectly atom-economical processes vulnerable to markets.
- High-value chemicals produced about a quarter of primary-chemical $\mathrm{CO_2}$ in 2022; electrified cracking, capture and non-fossil carbon are the main options.
:::

::: exercise When does ethane crack? {level=2 check="1135"}
Using $\Delta_\mathrm{r}H^\circ = 136.94\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}S^\circ = 120.64\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}$, taken as independent of temperature, find the temperature at which $K = 1$ for $\mathrm{C_2H_6 \rightleftharpoons C_2H_4 + H_2}$. (Enter $T$ in K, to four significant figures.)
::: solution
$K = 1$ when $\Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ = 0$: $T = 136\,940/120.64 = 1135\ \mathrm{K}$ ($862\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$). At $1\ \mathrm{bar}$ with no diluent, $X^2/(1 - X^2) = 1$, so $X = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.707$ at this temperature.

::: exercise An intermediate's peak {level=2 check="66.9"}
For $\mathrm{A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C}$ with $k_1 = 0.50\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $k_2 = 0.10\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, find the time of maximum yield of B and the maximum yield. (Enter the maximum yield in per cent, to three significant figures.)
::: solution
$t_\mathrm{max} = \ln(0.10/0.50)/(0.10 - 0.50) = (-1.609)/(-0.40) = 4.02\ \mathrm{s}$. The maximum yield is $(k_1/k_2)^{k_2/(k_2 - k_1)} = 5^{0.10/(-0.40)} = 5^{-0.25} = 0.669$, i.e. $66.9\,\%$. It depends only on $\kappa = 0.2$; doubling both rate constants halves $t_\mathrm{max}$ but leaves the maximum yield unchanged.
:::

::: exercise The effect of steam {level=3 check="81.8"}
Find the equilibrium conversion of ethane at $1100\ \mathrm{K}$ and a total pressure of $1.0\ \mathrm{bar}$ (a) with no diluent and (b) with $4.0\ \mathrm{mol}$ of steam per mole of ethane, using $K(1100\ \mathrm{K}) = 0.630$. Show that (b) is equivalent to (a) at a lower pressure, and find that pressure for the conversion of (b). (Enter the conversion for (b) in per cent, to three significant figures.)
::: hint
Use [[#eq-dilution]]; for the equivalent pressure, set $s = 0$ and solve $K = X^2 p/[(1 - X^2)p^\circ]$ for $p$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $X^2/(1 - X^2) = 0.630$, $X^2 = 0.630/1.630 = 0.3865$, $X = 0.622$.

(b) $X = [-0.630\times4 + \sqrt{(0.630\times4)^2 + 4(1 + 0.630)(0.630)(5)}]/[2(1.630)] = [-2.520 + \sqrt{6.350 + 20.54}]/3.260 = (-2.520 + 5.186)/3.260 = 0.818$, i.e. $81.8\,\%$.

Equivalent pressure: with $s = 0$, $p/p^\circ = K(1 - X^2)/X^2 = 0.630\times(1 - 0.669)/0.669 = 0.312$: four moles of steam per mole of ethane at $1\ \mathrm{bar}$ are equivalent to cracking undiluted ethane at about $0.31\ \mathrm{bar}$, because the hydrocarbon partial pressures are similar. Operating a cracker under vacuum would be hazardous (air ingress into a hot hydrocarbon stream) and would need enormous equipment; steam achieves the same effect safely. Industrial ratios are lower than $4$ (about $0.3$–$0.5$ by mass for ethane), balancing equilibrium and coking against the energy of raising steam.
:::
:::

::: exercise Furnace duty {level=3 check="4.88"}
Compute the reaction enthalpy, in GJ, that must be supplied per tonne of ethylene for $\mathrm{C_2H_6 \rightarrow C_2H_4 + H_2}$ ($\Delta_\mathrm{r}H^\circ = 136.94\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$). If this heat is supplied by burning methane ($\Delta_\mathrm{c}H = -802\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, products gaseous) with a furnace efficiency of $90\,\%$ for transferring heat into the reaction, what mass of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is released per tonne of ethylene for the reaction enthalpy alone? (Enter the enthalpy in GJ per tonne.)
::: solution
$136.94\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\times(10^6/28.05)\ \mathrm{mol} = 4.88\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{kJ} = 4.88\ \mathrm{GJ}$ per tonne. The fuel needed is $4.88\times10^{6}/(0.90\times802) = 6.76\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{mol}$ of methane, releasing the same amount of $\mathrm{CO_2}$: $6.76\times10^{3}\times44.01\ \mathrm{g} = 0.298\ \mathrm{t\,CO_2}$ per tonne of ethylene. Heating feed and steam and driving the compressors of the separation train add several times this, which is why typical emissions are of the order of $1\ \mathrm{t\,CO_2}$ or more per tonne of ethylene. If the furnace burns the hydrogen co-product instead, the reaction duty can in principle be met with no $\mathrm{CO_2}$ — one of the routes to lower-emission cracking.
:::
