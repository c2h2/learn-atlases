Table salt cleaves into cubes, diamond cuts glass, and copper draws into kilometres of wire. Three everyday solids, three completely different answers to the same question — *how do the building blocks arrange themselves?* — and each answer follows from the type of bonding in [[bonding-structure/ionic-bonding]], [[bonding-structure/covalent-lewis]] and [[bonding-structure/metallic-bonding]] applied to a huge crowd of particles. A solid is where this course's bonding models are paid off: the melting point, hardness, conductivity and cleavage of a material are read directly off its structure. The chapter builds the geometric vocabulary (lattices, unit cells, close packing), derives the quantitative rules that connect a crystal's architecture to numbers you can measure (packing fractions, radius ratios, densities), and finishes with the experimental trick that made all of it visible: X-ray diffraction. One distinction first. A **crystalline** solid has its building blocks arranged in a pattern that repeats without limit in three dimensions — long-range order. An **amorphous** solid (glass, most polymers, gels) has order only over a few atomic spacings: it is a liquid that was cooled so fast its molecules froze in place before they could organize, a disordered network held rigid by its own connectivity — not, despite the old story about sagging cathedral windows, a liquid that flows.

## Lattices and unit cells

::: definition Lattice, basis and unit cell {#def-lattice-basis}
A **lattice** is an infinite array of points generated by translations $\mathbf{R} = n_1\mathbf{a} + n_2\mathbf{b} + n_3\mathbf{c}$ with $n_i$ integers: every lattice point has an environment identical to every other. A **basis** is the atom, ion or group of atoms attached to each lattice point. A **crystal** is lattice + basis: the lattice is the scaffolding, the basis is what hangs on it, and two crystals with the same lattice and different bases are completely different materials. A **unit cell** is a parallelepiped spanned by $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$ that tiles space by translation alone; the **conventional cell** is the smallest convenient shape that also shows the crystal's symmetry, and it may contain more than one lattice point (body-centred, face-centred, edge-centred). Atoms shared between cells are counted fractionally: a corner atom belongs $\tfrac18$ to each of eight cells, an edge atom $\tfrac14$, a face atom $\tfrac12$, an interior atom wholly.
:::

The fractional bookkeeping is worth doing once by hand, because every density calculation in this chapter rests on it.

::: example Counting atoms in the cubic cells {#ex-cell-counting}
How many atoms sit in the conventional cubic cell of the simple cubic (SC), body-centred cubic (BCC) and face-centred cubic (FCC) metals, and what is each atom's coordination number (the number of nearest neighbours)?
::: solution
**SC:** eight corners $\times\ \tfrac18 = 1$ atom. An atom has neighbours along $\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$: **CN 6**. **BCC:** corners $8\times\tfrac18 = 1$ plus a whole body-centre atom $= 2$ atoms. The centre atom touches the eight corners: **CN 8**. **FCC:** corners $8\times\tfrac18 = 1$ plus six faces $\times\ \tfrac12 = 3$, total **4 atoms**. An atom at a face centre touches the four corner atoms of its face and eight other face-centre atoms at distance $a/\sqrt{2}$: **CN 12**. The three structures differ in how many atoms they pack into a cell and how many neighbours each atom enjoys — and those two numbers, as we will see, are not independent: higher coordination means denser packing.
:::
:::

::: definition The seven crystal systems and the fourteen Bravais lattices {#def-crystal-systems}
Every lattice falls into one of **seven crystal systems**, classified by the metric relations of its conventional cell (lengths $a,b,c$; angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$): **cubic** $a=b=c$, all angles $90^\circ$; **tetragonal** $a=b\neq c$, all $90^\circ$; **orthorhombic** $a\neq b\neq c$, all $90^\circ$; **hexagonal** $a=b\neq c$, $\alpha=\beta=90^\circ$, $\gamma=120^\circ$; **trigonal (rhombohedral)** $a=b=c$, $\alpha=\beta=\gamma\neq 90^\circ$; **monoclinic** $a\neq b\neq c$, $\alpha=\gamma=90^\circ\neq\beta$; **triclinic** no metric constraints. Adding the possible centring types — primitive P, body-centred I, face-centred F, one-face-centred C — yields exactly **fourteen Bravais lattices** (cubic P, I, F; tetragonal P, I; orthorhombic P, I, F, C; hexagonal P; trigonal P; monoclinic P, C; triclinic P): only fourteen distinct translation symmetries are compatible with the seven metric classes.
:::

::: definition Coordination number in ionic structures {#def-coordination-ionic}
In an ionic structure the **coordination number** of an ion is the number of oppositely charged nearest neighbours around it; structures are described as "$m:n$" (cation CN : anion CN), and charge balance forces $N_+/N_- = n_\mathrm{anion\,CN}/n_\mathrm{cation\,CN}$ — the ion present in fewer copies needs more neighbours of the opposite kind. A cation sitting in a **hole** (interstitial site) of a close-packed or simple array of anions has a coordination number fixed by the hole type: 3 (triangular), 4 (tetrahedral), 6 (octahedral) or 8 (cubic).
:::

::: proof The atomic packing factors of SC, BCC and FCC {#proof-packing}
Treat the atoms of a metallic element as equal hard spheres of radius $r$ that touch along the densest available direction of the cell; the **atomic packing factor** is $\mathrm{APF} = N V_\mathrm{atom}/V_\mathrm{cell}$ with $V_\mathrm{atom} = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$.
**SC.** Atoms touch along the cube edge: $a = 2r$. With $N = 1$,
$$
\mathrm{APF} = \frac{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}}{(2r)^{3}} = \frac{\pi}{6} = 0.524 .
$$
**BCC.** The body diagonal $\sqrt{3}\,a$ passes through two corner atoms and the whole centre atom, which all touch: $4r = \sqrt{3}\,a$, so $a = 4r/\sqrt{3}$. With $N = 2$,
$$
\mathrm{APF} = \frac{2\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}}{\left(4r/\sqrt{3}\right)^{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,\pi}{8} = 0.680 .
$$
**FCC.** Atoms touch along the face diagonal: $4r = \sqrt{2}\,a$, so $a = 2\sqrt{2}\,r$. With $N = 4$,
$$
\mathrm{APF} = \frac{4\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}}{\left(2\sqrt{2}\,r\right)^{3}} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = 0.740 .
$$
The numbers rise with coordination number — $0.524$ at CN 6, $0.680$ at CN 8, $0.740$ at CN 12 — and the last value turns out to be the maximum possible for equal spheres (the Kepler conjecture of 1611, announced as proved by Thomas Hales in 1998 and formally verified in 2014): no arrangement of equal spheres fills more than $74.05\ \%$ of space.
:::

## Close packing

::: proposition Close packing of equal spheres {#prop-close-packing}
Stack equal spheres in a plane so each touches six others (a **hexagonal layer**, the densest possible 2-D packing); drop the second layer into the hollows of the first, and a third layer has a choice. If it sits over the *first* layer's hollows the sequence is **ABAB…**, the **hexagonal close-packed (hcp)** structure (Mg, Zn, Cd); if it occupies the *third*, previously unused set of hollows the sequence is **ABCABC…**, the **cubic close-packed (ccp)** structure — which is the FCC lattice viewed along its body diagonal (Cu, Ag, Au, Ni, Al). Both stackings have CN 12 and fill $74.05\ \%$ of space; they differ only in stacking sequence, which is why polytypism — mixed sequences — is common. A packing of $N$ spheres contains exactly $N$ **octahedral holes** (CN 6, radius ratio $0.414$) and $2N$ **tetrahedral holes** (CN 4, radius ratio $0.225$): in the FCC cell, four spheres own the four octahedral sites (twelve edge centres $\times\ \tfrac14$ plus the body centre) and the eight tetrahedral sites wholly inside the cell.
:::

The hole count is not a footnote — it is the bookkeeping that builds every ionic structure below, because ionic structures *are* close packings (or simple packings) of the larger ion with the smaller ion in the holes.

## Ionic crystal structures

::: proposition The radius-ratio rules {#prop-radius-ratio}
For a cation in a hole of a close-packed or simple array of touching anions, stable contact with all neighbours (and no anion–anion squeeze) requires the cation/anion radius ratio $r_+/r_-$ to exceed a geometric limit that depends only on the hole's coordination:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{coordination of cation} & \text{hole} & \text{minimum } r_+/r_- \\ \hline
3\ (\text{triangular}) & \text{planar} & 0.155 \\
4\ (\text{tetrahedral, zinc blende}) & \text{tetrahedral} & 0.225 \\
6\ (\text{octahedral, rock salt}) & \text{octahedral} & 0.414 \\
8\ (\text{cubic, CsCl type}) & \text{cubic} & 0.732
\end{array}
$$
Given a ratio, the rule predicts the structure type; it is an **empirical geometric guide**, not a law — see the warning below.
:::

::: proof The radius-ratio limits {#proof-radius-ratio}
All four limits come from one geometry: the cation at the centre of a symmetric cage of anions that themselves touch.
**Octahedral (CN 6).** Take the square plane of four anions with the cation at the centre. Adjacent anions touch along the *edge* of the square of side $a$: $2r_- = a$, while cation–anion contact along the half-diagonal gives $r_+ + r_- = \sqrt{2}\,a/2 = \sqrt{2}\,r_-$. Hence
$$
\frac{r_+}{r_-} = \sqrt{2} - 1 = 0.414 .
$$
**Cubic (CN 8).** Eight anions at the corners of a cube of edge $a$, touching along the cube *edges*: $2r_- = a$. The cation at the body centre touches them along the body diagonal: $2(r_+ + r_-) = \sqrt{3}\,a = \sqrt{3}\cdot 2r_-$, so
$$
\frac{r_+}{r_-} = \sqrt{3} - 1 = 0.732 .
$$
**Tetrahedral (CN 4).** Four anions at alternating corners of a cube of edge $a$ (a regular tetrahedron), touching along the face diagonals: $2r_- = \sqrt{2}\,a$. The cation at the cube centre is at distance $\sqrt{3}\,a/2$: $r_+ + r_- = \sqrt{3}\,a/2 = \sqrt{3}\,r_-/\sqrt{2}$, so $r_+/r_- = \sqrt{6}/2 - 1 = 0.225$.
**Triangular (CN 3).** Three touching anions with the cation in the centre of the triangle: the centre-to-corner distance is $r_-/\cos 30^\circ$, so $r_+ + r_- = 2r_-/\sqrt{3}$ and $r_+/r_- = 2/\sqrt{3} - 1 = 0.155$.
Below a limit the cation is too small to touch its cage — it rattles, the anions come into mutual contact and the like-charge repulsion destabilises the arrangement, and a lower-coordination structure wins.
:::

With the holes counted and the ratios in hand, the classic structure types are short descriptions. **Rock salt, NaCl:** ccp (FCC) of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ with $\mathrm{Na^+}$ in *all* the octahedral holes — 4 octahedral holes per 4 anions, exactly enough — giving 6:6 coordination and $Z = 4$ formula units per cell. **Caesium chloride, CsCl:** two interpenetrating *simple cubic* lattices, each ion cubic (8:8), $Z = 1$; it is *not* a BCC lattice, because the two sublattices carry different ions and a lattice point must have an identical environment. **Zinc blende, ZnS:** FCC of $\mathrm{S^{2-}}$ with $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$ in *half* the tetrahedral holes (4 of 8), 4:4, $Z = 4$; the **wurtzite** polymorph is the hcp stacking of the same idea. **Fluorite, $\mathrm{CaF_2}$:** FCC of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ with $\mathrm{F^-}$ in *all* eight tetrahedral holes — 4 Ca : 8 F — so Ca is 8-coordinate and F 4-coordinate (8:4), $Z = 4$; **antifluorite** ($\mathrm{Na_2O}$) swaps cations and anions.

::: example Predicting structures from radius ratios {#ex-radius-ratio-predict}
Using Shannon-type ionic radii (which depend on coordination; standard tabulated data): $\mathrm{Na^+}$ $102\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{Cs^+}$ $167\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$ $74\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ $100\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ $181\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{S^{2-}}$ $184\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{F^-}$ $133\ \mathrm{pm}$ — predict the structure type of $\mathrm{NaCl}$, $\mathrm{CsCl}$, $\mathrm{ZnS}$ and $\mathrm{CaF_2}$.
::: solution
$$
\mathrm{NaCl:}\ \frac{102}{181} = 0.56;\qquad
\mathrm{CsCl:}\ \frac{167}{181} = 0.92;\qquad
\mathrm{ZnS:}\ \frac{74}{184} = 0.40;\qquad
\mathrm{CaF_2:}\ \frac{100}{133} = 0.75 .
$$
$0.56$ lies in $[0.414, 0.732)$: octahedral holes, CN 6 — rock salt, as observed. $0.92 \geq 0.732$: cubic holes, CN 8 — the CsCl type, as observed. $0.40 < 0.414$: tetrahedral holes, CN 4 — zinc blende, as observed (and only just: $\mathrm{ZnS}$ sits near the boundary, which is why it also exists as wurtzite). $0.75 \geq 0.732$: the calcium cation wants CN 8, and fluorite delivers exactly that while keeping F at CN 4 — consistent with the $8{:}4$ structure. Four compounds, four correct predictions from four divisions: the rules work remarkably well *as guides*, which is precisely the qualifier the warning attaches.
:::
:::

## Density from a unit cell

::: algorithm Density of a crystalline solid from its cell {#alg-cell-density}
**Input:** the chemical formula, the conventional-cell edge $a$ (from diffraction), and the structure type. **Output:** the theoretical density.
1. Count the **formula units per cell, $Z$**, by the fractional bookkeeping of [[#def-lattice-basis]] (rock salt $Z = 4$; CsCl type $Z = 1$; fluorite $Z = 4$).
2. Compute the cell mass $Z\,M/N_\mathrm{A}$, where $M$ is the molar mass of the *formula unit*.
3. Divide by the cell volume:
$$
\rho = \frac{Z\,M}{N_\mathrm{A}\,a^{3}}.
$$
4. Keep units honest: convert $a$ from $\mathrm{pm}$ to $\mathrm{cm}$ ($1\ \mathrm{pm} = 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{cm}$) before cubing, and quote $\rho$ in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.
The measured crystal density *is* this number (defects change it by far less than a per cent), so density closes the loop between the cell model and the balance — historically, it was one of the first checks on unit-cell determinations.
:::

::: example The density of rock salt {#ex-nacl-density}
$\mathrm{NaCl}$ is face-centred cubic with $a = 564\ \mathrm{pm}$ and $Z = 4$. Compute its density ($M = 58.44\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$).
::: solution
$$
\rho = \frac{Z M}{N_\mathrm{A} a^{3}} = \frac{4 \times 58.44\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}{6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}} \times \left(5.64\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm}\right)^{3}} = \frac{233.8}{108.1} = 2.16\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}},
$$
against a measured $2.17\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (standard tabulated data). Four formula units — four $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and four $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ — in a cube $564\ \mathrm{pm}$ on a side reproduce the balance to better than half a per cent. The same arithmetic run backwards (measured $\rho$ gives $Z$, or known $Z$ gives $a$) is how unit cells were first pinned down.
:::
:::

## X-ray diffraction: measuring the cell

::: proposition Bragg's law {#prop-bragg}
A crystal diffracts X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$ strongly at angles $\theta$ satisfying
$$
n\lambda = 2 d \sin\theta ,
$$
where $d$ is the spacing of a family of parallel lattice planes and $\theta$ is the **glancing angle** between beam and plane (diffractometers report $2\theta$, the angle between incident and detected beam). The law is the condition that waves scattered from successive planes return *in phase*; it treats diffraction as mirror-like reflection from planes, which is why it needs no more than the plane spacing.
:::

::: proof The path difference $2d\sin\theta$ {#proof-bragg}
Two parallel rays graze a family of planes at angle $\theta$, separated by the interplanar spacing $d$. The lower ray penetrates one plane deeper: on the way in it travels an extra $d\sin\theta$, and on the way out another $d\sin\theta$ — read off the two right triangles formed by dropping perpendiculars from the upper ray's path. The total path difference is therefore $2d\sin\theta$. Waves add constructively when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:
$$
2d\sin\theta = n\lambda, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$
Because $\sin\theta \le 1$, a reflection $n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$ exists only when $n\lambda \le 2d$: too-small spacings or too-long wavelengths simply do not diffract. And since $\lambda$ must be comparable to $d$ (a few hundred $\mathrm{pm}$) for $\theta$ to be measurable, X-rays — whose wavelengths match atomic spacings — are the right probe; visible light, $10^{5}$ times too long, sees only a blur.
:::

::: example A Bragg angle for rock salt {#ex-bragg-nacl}
$\mathrm{NaCl}$ is diffracted by $\mathrm{Cu\ K\alpha}$ radiation, $\lambda = 154\ \mathrm{pm}$. The $\{200\}$ planes of the rock-salt cell have spacing $d = a/2 = 282\ \mathrm{pm}$. At what $2\theta$ does the first-order reflection appear?
::: solution
$$
\sin\theta = \frac{n\lambda}{2d} = \frac{1 \times 154\ \mathrm{pm}}{2 \times 282\ \mathrm{pm}} = 0.273
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\theta = 15.8^\circ,
$$
so the diffractometer records the peak at $2\theta = 31.7^\circ$. (Powder patterns are always reported in $2\theta$ because the detector, not the crystal, is what moves.) One measured angle has determined $d$, and with it the cell edge $a = 2d = 564\ \mathrm{pm}$ — the number the density example consumed. Diffraction gives the cell; the cell gives the density; the balance agrees: the model is closed.
:::
:::

Higher orders appear at larger angles for the same planes — which is exactly how a diffractometer distinguishes $n = 2$ from a different $d$.
:::

## The four classes of crystalline solid

Every crystalline solid is one of four classes, distinguished by what sits on the lattice and what holds it — and each class is a chapter of this course wearing a different hat. **Ionic solids** (NaCl, MgO): ions held by the Coulomb energy of [[bonding-structure/ionic-bonding]]; hard but brittle (slip brings like charges together and cleaves the crystal), high-melting, insulating until molten or dissolved, when the mobile ions carry the current. **Metallic solids** (Cu, Fe): cations in a sea of delocalised bands — [[bonding-structure/metallic-bonding]] explains the malleability (the electron gas accommodates slip), the lustre and the conductivity. **Covalent-network solids** (diamond, quartz $\mathrm{SiO_2}$, SiC): atoms linked by directional covalent bonds into one giant molecule — [[bonding-structure/covalent-lewis]] and [[bonding-structure/valence-bond]] extended to infinity. Diamond (sp³ carbon, CN 4) is the hardest natural material and does not melt at ordinary pressure but sublimes around $3550\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$; graphite is the instructive exception — sp² layers bonded strongly in two dimensions and held *between* layers only by dispersion, so it is soft, slippery and conducting within its planes. **Molecular solids** (ice, solid $\mathrm{I_2}$, dry ice): discrete molecules held by the forces of [[bonding-structure/intermolecular-forces]] — soft, low-melting (dry ice sublimes at $-78.5\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$), insulating, volatile. The class is read off the properties in seconds and off the diffraction pattern in an afternoon; being able to move between the two is the skill.

::: quiz
How many formula units of $\mathrm{NaCl}$ are contained in the conventional rock-salt unit cell?
- [x] 4: four $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and four $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ — the anions form an FCC array (4 per cell) and the cations fill all four octahedral holes.
- [ ] 1: one at the centre, as in the CsCl structure.
- [ ] 8: eight corners $\times\ \tfrac18$ of each ion.
- [ ] 2: one formula unit per interpenetrating FCC sublattice.
::: solution
The $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions sit on an FCC lattice: $8\times\tfrac18 + 6\times\tfrac12 = 4$. The $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ions occupy all four octahedral holes (body centre $+ $ twelve edge centres $\times\ \tfrac14 = 1 + 3 = 4$). So $Z = 4$ formula units — the number the density calculation of [[#ex-nacl-density]] depends on. The CsCl structure, by contrast, has $Z = 1$; confusing the two is the most common cell-counting error.
:::
:::

::: warning Radius ratios are guides; cells are bookkeeping; glass does not flow {#warn-solid-structures}
Three overstatements to unlearn. **The radius-ratio rules are geometry, not thermodynamics.** They ignore the covalent contribution to the lattice energy, the polarisability of large anions, and temperature and pressure entirely — and compounds whose ratio clears even the top limit can stay rock-salt: $\mathrm{RbCl}$ ($152/181 = 0.84$) is 6:6 at ambient pressure and adopts the CsCl type only when squeezed, because the energy difference between the two structure types is a few $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ while the rules resolve nothing at that resolution. Use them to *organise* predictions, never to settle arguments; CsCl itself is the 8:8 structure it is because caesium is genuinely large, not because the rule says so. **The conventional cell is a choice, the primitive cell is the minimum.** FCC's conventional cell holds 4 lattice points because it is chosen to show cubic symmetry; its primitive cell is an oblique rhombohedron holding exactly 1 — different shape, same crystal. Never confuse "atoms in the conventional cell" with "atoms in the primitive cell". **Amorphous solids are rigid, not slow liquids.** The medieval-window story is a myth: old glass is thick-and-thin because glassblowers made it that way, and the relaxation times of window glass exceed the age of the cathedral. A glass is a frozen, disordered network — short-range order, no long-range order, and no flow.
:::

::: history
In 1784 René Just Haüy, famously rattled by a dropped hand specimen of calcite, noticed that its fragments were all rhombohedra — and proposed that every crystal is built by stacking identical *molécules intégrantes*, the first lattice theory of solids. A century later the scaffolding became visible: in 1912 Max von Laue proposed that crystals diffract X-rays, and Walter Friedrich and Paul Knipping obtained the first Laue photograph, proving both that X-rays are waves and that crystals are periodic. In 1913 William Lawrence Bragg, aged 22, wrote down $n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$, and with his father William Henry Bragg he solved the first crystal structures — reporting in 1914 that rock salt is a lattice of alternating $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions with no NaCl *molecules* anywhere in it, settling a long dispute about the ionic bond by direct measurement. The 1915 Nobel Prize in Physics followed. In 1929 Linus Pauling published his rules for the structures of ionic crystals, turning radius ratios, electrostatic valence and shared polyhedra into a working chemistry of solids — the framework this chapter still teaches.
:::

::: summary
- A crystal is a lattice (a translationally periodic array of points) plus a basis; the conventional unit cell is a bookkeeping choice, and atoms shared between cells count fractionally.
- Seven metric classes of cell give seven crystal systems and, with centring, exactly fourteen Bravais lattices.
- SC, BCC and FCC hold 1, 2, 4 atoms with CN 6, 8, 12 and pack $52.4$, $68.0$, $74.0\ \%$ of space; close packing (hcp ABAB, ccp ABCABC) attains the $74.05\ \%$ maximum with CN 12.
- A packing of $N$ spheres offers $N$ octahedral and $2N$ tetrahedral holes; ionic structures are anion packings with cations in the holes, and the radius-ratio limits $0.155/0.225/0.414/0.732$ follow from one geometry each.
- Rock salt (6:6, $Z=4$), CsCl (8:8, $Z=1$), zinc blende (4:4, $Z=4$) and fluorite (8:4, $Z=4$) are the four archetypes; radius ratios predict them well as guides, not laws.
- Density follows from the cell: $\rho = ZM/(N_\mathrm{A}a^{3})$ — NaCl's $564\ \mathrm{pm}$ cell gives $2.16\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.
- Bragg's law $n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$ turns a measured $2\theta$ into a plane spacing; X-rays work because their wavelengths match atomic spacings.
- Ionic, metallic, covalent-network and molecular solids: the four classes, each a bonding model of this course read out in bulk.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Cells and coordinations {level=1}
For each of the three cubic metallic structures (SC, BCC, FCC), state the number of atoms in the conventional cell and the coordination number, and verify the atom count by fractional bookkeeping.
::: solution
SC: $8\times\tfrac18 = 1$ atom, CN 6. BCC: $8\times\tfrac18 + 1 = 2$ atoms, CN 8. FCC: $8\times\tfrac18 + 6\times\tfrac12 = 4$ atoms, CN 12. The coordination numbers follow from the contact directions used in the packing-factor proof: along the edge (SC), along the body diagonal (BCC), along the face diagonal (FCC).
:::

::: exercise Classify the solid {level=1}
Classify each as ionic, metallic, covalent-network or molecular, and justify from the properties given: (a) melts at $801\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, conducts when molten, not when solid; (b) sublimes at $-78.5\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, non-conducting; (c) malleable, conducting, lustrous; (d) hardest known natural material, insoluble, sublimes near $3550\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$.
::: solution
(a) Ionic — mobile ions only when the lattice melts (NaCl). (b) Molecular — discrete molecules held by dispersion alone (dry ice, $\mathrm{CO_2}$). (c) Metallic — delocalised electrons give conductivity, malleability and lustre at once (copper). (d) Covalent network — one giant sp³-bonded molecule (diamond). The classification algorithm is: does it conduct? (metal or molten ionic); does it melt high? (network or ionic); does it melt or sublime low? (molecular).
:::

::: exercise Count the holes {level=1}
The FCC (ccp) cell of close-packed anions contains 4 anions. How many octahedral and how many tetrahedral holes does the cell contain, and where are they? Does the count match the general rule?
::: solution
Octahedral: the body centre (1, wholly inside) plus twelve edge centres $\times\ \tfrac14 = 3$, total **4** — one per anion. Tetrahedral: eight sites wholly inside the cell, one under each corner, at $(\pm\tfrac14,\pm\tfrac14,\pm\tfrac14)$ from each corner — **8**, two per anion. The rule "$N$ spheres give $N$ octahedral and $2N$ tetrahedral holes" holds exactly, and it is the inventory that lets $\mathrm{Na^+}$ fill *all* the octahedral holes of rock salt and $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$ fill exactly *half* the tetrahedral holes of zinc blende.
:::
:::

::: exercise Density of KCl {level=2 check="1.99"}
$\mathrm{KCl}$ adopts the rock-salt structure with $a = 629\ \mathrm{pm}$ ($M = 74.55\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$). Calculate its density. (Enter it in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.)
::: solution
Rock salt means $Z = 4$:
$$
\rho = \frac{4 \times 74.55\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}{6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}} \times \left(6.29\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm}\right)^{3}} = \frac{298.2}{149.9} = 1.99\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}},
$$
in excellent agreement with the measured $1.98\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (standard tabulated data).
:::

::: exercise Predict the structure {level=2}
Using Shannon-type radii $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ $72\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{Cs^+}$ $167\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ $140\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\mathrm{I^-}$ $206\ \mathrm{pm}$ (standard tabulated data), predict the coordination number of the cation and the likely structure type of $\mathrm{MgO}$ and of $\mathrm{CsI}$.
::: solution
$\mathrm{MgO}$: $72/140 = 0.51$, inside $[0.414, 0.732)$ → CN 6, rock-salt type — correct, the same prediction as $\mathrm{NaCl}$ from a smaller ratio. $\mathrm{CsI}$: $167/206 = 0.81 \geq 0.732$ → CN 8, the CsCl type — also correct: caesium iodide really is the 8:8 structure. (For contrast, $\mathrm{ZnO}$ has ratio $74/140 = 0.53$, which the rules place in the rock-salt window, yet it adopts the 4:4 wurtzite structure — the classic reminder that directional, partly covalent bonding is not in the radius-ratio ledger at all.)

::: exercise A Bragg angle {level=2 check="18.3"}
A reflection from planes with $d = 245\ \mathrm{pm}$ is observed in the first order with $\mathrm{Cu\ K\alpha}$ radiation, $\lambda = 154\ \mathrm{pm}$. Find $\theta$. (Enter $\theta$ in degrees.)
::: solution
$\sin\theta = \lambda/(2d) = 154/(2\times245) = 0.314$, so $\theta = 18.3^\circ$ (the diffractometer would report $2\theta = 36.6^\circ$). Note the discipline the law imposes: had the planes been spaced $70\ \mathrm{pm}$, $\sin\theta$ would exceed 1 and no first-order reflection could exist at this wavelength.
:::

::: exercise Derive the cubic limit {level=3}
Derive the radius-ratio limit for a cation in a cubic hole (CN 8) from first principles, and explain why the anions are taken to touch along the cube *edge* rather than along the face diagonal.
::: solution
Place anions of radius $r_-$ at the eight corners of a cube of edge $a$ and the cation at the body centre. The nearest anion–anion contacts are along the cube edges (length $a$), shorter than the face diagonals ($\sqrt2 a$), so the close-packed direction — the one that sets the contact condition — is the edge: $2r_- = a$. The cation touches the corner anions along the body diagonal, length $\sqrt3\,a$: $2(r_+ + r_-) = \sqrt3\,a = \sqrt3\cdot 2r_-$, hence $r_+/r_- = \sqrt3 - 1 = 0.732$. Below this ratio the cation loses contact with all eight anions simultaneously (it "rattles"), the electrostatic payoff drops, and the structure switches to the octahedral 6:6 type. The same edge-versus-diagonal reasoning is what made the octahedral limit come from the square's diagonal and the tetrahedral limit from the face diagonal: always let the *shortest* anion–anion vector set the scale.
:::

::: exercise The fluorite cell, completely {level=3}
Fluorite, $\mathrm{CaF_2}$, has $a = 546\ \mathrm{pm}$, with $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ on an FCC lattice and $\mathrm{F^-}$ in all the tetrahedral holes. Find $Z$, the coordination numbers, the $\mathrm{Ca-F}$ bond length in terms of $a$, and the density ($M = 78.08\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$).
::: solution
FCC Ca: $8\times\tfrac18 + 6\times\tfrac12 = 4$. Eight tetrahedral holes, all filled by F: 8. So the cell holds $\mathrm{Ca_4F_8}$ — $Z = 4$ formula units, consistent with the formula. Each F at $(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14)$-type positions sits under one corner and is tetrahedrally coordinated by 4 Ca; each Ca is surrounded by all 8 F of its cube: **8:4 coordination**, as the radius ratio $100/133 = 0.75$ demands. The Ca–F distance is half the body diagonal of the small octant cube: $d = \sqrt3\,a/4 = 236\ \mathrm{pm}$. Density:
$$
\rho = \frac{4 \times 78.08\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}{6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}} \times \left(5.46\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm}\right)^{3}} = \frac{312.3}{98.0} = 3.19\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}},
$$
against the measured $3.18\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (standard tabulated data).
:::

::: exercise Packing fraction of hcp {level=3}
The hexagonal close-packed structure has lattice parameters $a = 2r$ and $c = \sqrt{8/3}\,a$ (the ideal ratio, $c/a = 1.633$). Its primitive cell is a parallelepiped of the hexagonal lattice with volume $V = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a^{2}c$ containing 2 spheres. Derive its atomic packing factor and explain why it must equal the ccp value.
::: solution
$$
\mathrm{APF} = \frac{2\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}}{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a^{2}c} = \frac{\tfrac{8}{3}\pi r^{3}}{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(2r)^{2}\sqrt{\tfrac{8}{3}}\,(2r)} = \frac{\tfrac{8}{3}\pi}{4\sqrt{3}\cdot\sqrt{\tfrac{8}{3}}} = \frac{\tfrac{8}{3}\pi}{8\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = 0.740 .
$$
Identical to ccp — and it must be: both stackings are built from identical close-packed layers with identical in-layer contacts and identical inter-layer sphere positions; only the *registry* of successive layers differs, and registry does not change the volume per sphere. Every local environment is the same (CN 12), so every bulk density-like property coincides — which is why hcp and ccp metals differ in slip systems and ductility, not in packing fraction.
:::
