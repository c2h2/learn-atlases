Strontium titanate, $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$, is a colourless, rather unremarkable-looking oxide. Yet the same atomic arrangement, with barium in place of strontium, gives the ferroelectric in billions of ceramic capacitors; with lanthanum, manganese and a little calcium, a material whose resistance collapses in a magnetic field; with copper and yttrium in a layered variant, a superconductor above the boiling point of nitrogen; and with lead, iodide and methylammonium, a solar-cell absorber. All of these are **perovskites**. Learning to recognise a handful of such structure types, to describe them exactly and to predict which elements will adopt them is the first skill of materials chemistry, because almost every property in the later chapters (diffraction patterns, defect chemistry, band structures, magnetism, ion transport) is a property of a particular arrangement of atoms.

The chapter [[bonding-structure/solid-structures]] introduced unit cells, close packing, radius ratios and the density calculation. Here we make the description of crystals precise and general. We define lattices, cells and fractional coordinates for all seven crystal systems, prove why only certain rotational symmetries are possible in a crystal, introduce Miller indices and derive the spacing of lattice planes, the quantity that X-ray diffraction measures in [[materials-chemistry/diffraction]]. We then build the important structure types from close-packed arrays and their holes, and finish with three semi-quantitative tools for deciding whether a proposed structure is chemically reasonable: Pauling's rules, the Goldschmidt tolerance factor for perovskites, and bond valence sums.

## Lattices, cells and coordinates

A crystal is a pattern that repeats by translation in three dimensions. The abstract set of repeat points is the lattice; what sits at each point is the basis (or motif). The distinction matters because many different crystals share the same lattice: copper metal and sodium chloride both have a face-centred cubic lattice, with a basis of one Cu atom in the first case and one $\mathrm{Na^+}$ plus one $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ in the second.

::: definition Lattice, unit cell and fractional coordinates {#def-lattice}
A **lattice** is the infinite set of points $\mathbf{R} = n_1\mathbf{a} + n_2\mathbf{b} + n_3\mathbf{c}$ ($n_i$ integers) generated by three non-coplanar **lattice vectors** $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$. The parallelepiped they span is a **unit cell**, described by six **lattice parameters**: the lengths $a, b, c$ and the angles $\alpha$ (between $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$), $\beta$ (between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{c}$) and $\gamma$ (between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$). Its volume is
$$
V = abc\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\alpha - \cos^2\beta - \cos^2\gamma + 2\cos\alpha\cos\beta\cos\gamma}.
$$ {#eq-volume}
A cell containing one lattice point is **primitive** (P); conventional cells chosen to show the symmetry may be centred: body (I), face (F) or one face (A, B, C). An atom at position $\mathbf{r} = x\mathbf{a} + y\mathbf{b} + z\mathbf{c}$ has **fractional coordinates** $(x, y, z)$, each between 0 and 1 within the cell. The number of formula units per cell is written $Z$.
:::

Formula [[#eq-volume]] reduces to $V = abc$ for orthogonal axes, $V = abc\sin\beta$ for a monoclinic cell ($\alpha = \gamma = 90^\circ$), and $V = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a^2c$ for a hexagonal cell ($a = b$, $\gamma = 120^\circ$). It is the square root of the determinant of the **metric tensor**, the matrix of dot products $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}, \dots$, which is how crystallographic software computes all distances and angles from fractional coordinates: the distance between two atoms is $\lvert\Delta\mathbf{r}\rvert$ with $\Delta\mathbf{r} = \Delta x\,\mathbf{a} + \Delta y\,\mathbf{b} + \Delta z\,\mathbf{c}$ and $\lvert\Delta\mathbf{r}\rvert^2 = \Delta x^2a^2 + \Delta y^2b^2 + \Delta z^2c^2 + 2\Delta x\Delta y\,ab\cos\gamma + 2\Delta x\Delta z\,ac\cos\beta + 2\Delta y\Delta z\,bc\cos\alpha$.

The seven **crystal systems** are defined by the minimum symmetry of the lattice, not by the lattice parameters themselves (a monoclinic crystal may by accident have $\beta$ within measurement error of $90^\circ$). The required symmetry forces relations between the parameters: cubic ($a = b = c$, all angles $90^\circ$; four threefold axes), tetragonal ($a = b \ne c$; one fourfold axis), orthorhombic (three twofold axes), hexagonal (one sixfold axis), trigonal (one threefold axis), monoclinic (one twofold axis) and triclinic (none). Combining the systems with the centring types gives the **fourteen Bravais lattices**; combining point symmetry with translations, including screw axes and glide planes, gives the **230 space groups**, one of which describes every crystal. The full machinery belongs to a crystallography course, but one result about which symmetries are possible at all is worth proving here.

::: proposition The crystallographic restriction {#prop-restriction}
A lattice can be invariant under a rotation by $\theta = 2\pi/n$ only if $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$ or $6$. Fivefold, sevenfold and higher rotation axes are impossible in a periodic crystal.
:::

::: proof
Suppose a rotation $\mathsf{R}$ maps the lattice onto itself. Then it maps each lattice vector $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$ to a lattice vector, which is an integer combination of $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$. Written in the lattice basis, the matrix of $\mathsf{R}$ therefore has integer entries, and its trace is an integer. The trace of a matrix does not depend on the basis, and in a Cartesian basis with the rotation axis along $z$ the rotation matrix is
$$
\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$
with trace $1 + 2\cos\theta$. So $2\cos\theta$ must be an integer, and since $-2 \le 2\cos\theta \le 2$ the only possibilities are $2\cos\theta = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$, that is $\theta = 180^\circ, 120^\circ, 90^\circ, 60^\circ, 0^\circ$: $n = 2, 3, 4, 6, 1$. A fivefold axis would need $2\cos72^\circ = 0.618$, which is not an integer.
:::

The restriction explains why there are only 32 crystallographic point groups, against the infinitely many of molecules ([[quantum-chemistry/molecular-symmetry]]): a ferrocene molecule may have a fivefold axis, but a crystal of ferrocene cannot. It also explains the astonishment caused in 1984 by Dan Shechtman's electron diffraction patterns of rapidly cooled Al–Mn alloys, which showed sharp spots with tenfold symmetry. Such **quasicrystals** are ordered but not periodic, so the proof (which assumes a lattice) does not apply to them; the International Union of Crystallography redefined a crystal in 1992 as any solid with an essentially discrete diffraction pattern.

The rotation sends $\mathbf{a}_1 \to \mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_2$ and $\mathbf{a}_2 \to -\mathbf{a}_1$, so its columns are $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, 0)$: integers, as [[#prop-restriction]] requires, with trace $1 = 2\cos60^\circ$ and determinant 1. ## Miller indices and the spacing of lattice planes

The lattice points can be grouped into families of parallel, equally spaced planes in infinitely many ways. Such planes govern the external faces of crystals (the faces of a rock salt cube, the octahedral faces of an alum crystal), the slip planes along which metals deform, cleavage, and above all diffraction, which treats each family as a mirror.

::: definition Miller indices {#def-miller}
Take the plane of a family that lies nearest to the origin without passing through it, and let it cut the axes at $a/h$, $b/k$ and $c/l$. The integers $(hkl)$, with no common factor, are the **Miller indices** of the family. In practice: find the intercepts as multiples of $a, b, c$; take reciprocals; clear fractions. A plane parallel to an axis has intercept $\infty$ and index 0; a negative intercept is written with a bar, $(\bar1 10)$. The notation $\{hkl\}$ denotes all families equivalent by symmetry (in a cubic crystal $\{100\}$ is the six cube faces), $[uvw]$ a lattice direction $u\mathbf{a} + v\mathbf{b} + w\mathbf{c}$, and $\langle uvw\rangle$ the set of equivalent directions.
:::

For diffraction it is convenient to allow indices with a common factor: $(200)$ means the family with half the spacing of $(100)$, which includes the $(100)$ planes and a plane midway between each pair. The reflection labelled 200 in a powder pattern is the second-order reflection from (100) planes, and the language of [[materials-chemistry/diffraction]] treats it as first-order reflection from the (200) planes.

::: proposition Interplanar spacing {#prop-dspacing}
For a cell with orthogonal axes (cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic) the spacing of the $(hkl)$ planes is given by
$$
\frac{1}{d_{hkl}^2} = \frac{h^2}{a^2} + \frac{k^2}{b^2} + \frac{l^2}{c^2},
$$ {#eq-dspacing}
which for a cubic cell becomes $d_{hkl} = a/\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}$. For a hexagonal cell,
$$
\frac{1}{d_{hkl}^2} = \frac{4}{3}\,\frac{h^2 + hk + k^2}{a^2} + \frac{l^2}{c^2}.
$$ {#eq-dhex}
:::

::: proof
Use Cartesian coordinates along the orthogonal axes. The plane of the family nearest the origin passes through $(a/h, 0, 0)$, $(0, b/k, 0)$ and $(0, 0, c/l)$, so its equation is
$$
\frac{h}{a}X + \frac{k}{b}Y + \frac{l}{c}Z = 1.
$$
A plane $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{X} = 1$ with normal vector $\mathbf{n} = (h/a, k/b, l/c)$ lies at perpendicular distance $1/\lvert\mathbf{n}\rvert$ from the origin. The origin itself lies on the next plane of the family (the equation with right-hand side 0), so this distance is the spacing: $d_{hkl} = 1/\lvert\mathbf{n}\rvert$, and squaring gives [[#eq-dspacing]]. For the hexagonal cell, put $\mathbf{a} = a(1, 0, 0)$, $\mathbf{b} = a(-\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, 0)$, $\mathbf{c} = c(0, 0, 1)$. The normal to the plane through $\mathbf{a}/h$, $\mathbf{b}/k$, $\mathbf{c}/l$ is $\mathbf{n}$ with $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a} = h$, $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{b} = k$, $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{c} = l$ (so that each intercept satisfies $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{X} = 1$). Solving, $n_x = h/a$, $n_y = (2k + h)/(\sqrt3\,a)$, $n_z = l/c$, and $\lvert\mathbf{n}\rvert^2 = [h^2 + (2k + h)^2/3]/a^2 + l^2/c^2 = \tfrac43(h^2 + hk + k^2)/a^2 + l^2/c^2$.
:::

The vector $\mathbf{n}$ in the proof, perpendicular to the planes with length $1/d_{hkl}$, is a **reciprocal lattice vector** $\mathbf{G}_{hkl} = h\mathbf{a}^* + k\mathbf{b}^* + l\mathbf{c}^*$, where the reciprocal basis is defined by $\mathbf{a}^*\cdot\mathbf{a} = 1$, $\mathbf{a}^*\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0$ and so on. Every family of lattice planes corresponds to one point of the reciprocal lattice, a correspondence that turns diffraction geometry into vector algebra in the next chapter.

::: example Miller indices and spacings in rutile {#ex-rutile-d}
Rutile, $\mathrm{TiO_2}$, is tetragonal with $a = 4.594\ \text{Å}$ and $c = 2.959\ \text{Å}$. (a) A plane cuts the axes at $a$, $b$ and $\infty$; another at $a$, $\infty$ and $c$. Give their Miller indices. (b) Calculate $d_{110}$, $d_{101}$ and $d_{211}$.
::: solution
(a) Intercepts $1, 1, \infty$; reciprocals $1, 1, 0$: the plane is $(110)$. Intercepts $1, \infty, 1$: $(101)$.

(b) By [[#eq-dspacing]] with $a = b$,
$$
\frac{1}{d_{110}^2} = \frac{2}{4.594^2} = 0.09477\ \text{Å}^{-2}, \qquad d_{110} = 3.248\ \text{Å};
$$
$$
\frac{1}{d_{101}^2} = \frac{1}{4.594^2} + \frac{1}{2.959^2} = 0.04738 + 0.11421 = 0.16159\ \text{Å}^{-2}, \qquad d_{101} = 2.488\ \text{Å};
$$
$$
\frac{1}{d_{211}^2} = \frac{5}{4.594^2} + \frac{1}{2.959^2} = 0.23691 + 0.11421 = 0.35112\ \text{Å}^{-2}, \qquad d_{211} = 1.688\ \text{Å}.
$$
These are the three strongest lines of the rutile powder pattern, at $3.25$, $2.49$ and $1.69\ \text{Å}$, which is how a white-pigment manufacturer checks that a batch is rutile and not anatase. Lattice parameters are known to four figures, so four-figure spacings are justified.
:::
:::

::: warning Miller indices are not coordinates, and (200) is not (100) {#warn-miller}
Three errors recur. (1) Using the intercepts instead of their reciprocals: a plane with intercepts $1, 2, \infty$ is $(210)$, not $(120)$. (2) Treating $(hkl)$ as a direction: in a cubic crystal $[hkl]$ is perpendicular to $(hkl)$, but in other systems it generally is not, because the axes are not orthogonal or not of equal length. (3) Reducing diffraction indices: in a body-centred cubic metal there is no 100 reflection but there is a strong 200, and dividing by 2 to "simplify" would hide exactly the information (the centring) that the absence reveals.
:::

## Structure types from close-packed arrays

Most simple inorganic solids can be pictured as a close-packed array of the larger ions, usually the anions, with the smaller ions in some of the holes. Close packing of spheres in layers ABAB… gives hexagonal close packing (hcp), and ABCABC… gives cubic close packing (ccp), which is the face-centred cubic lattice with one sphere per lattice point. As shown in [[bonding-structure/solid-structures]], an array of $N$ close-packed spheres contains $N$ octahedral holes and $2N$ tetrahedral holes. In a ccp cell ($4$ spheres) the octahedral holes are at the cell centre $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and the edge midpoints, and the eight tetrahedral holes at $(\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)$ and its equivalents, the centres of the eight small cubes into which the cell can be divided. Which holes are filled, and in what pattern, defines the structure type.

| structure type | anion array | holes filled | coordination (cation:anion) | examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| rock salt (NaCl) | ccp | all octahedral | 6:6 | MgO, NiO, FeO, TiC, LiF, AgCl |
| zinc blende (sphalerite, ZnS) | ccp | half tetrahedral | 4:4 | GaAs, CdTe, CuCl; Si and diamond if all atoms are the same |
| antifluorite ($\mathrm{Li_2O}$) | ccp | all tetrahedral | 4:8 | $\mathrm{Na_2O}$, $\mathrm{K_2S}$ |
| fluorite ($\mathrm{CaF_2}$) | cations ccp | anions in all tetrahedral | 8:4 | $\mathrm{UO_2}$, $\mathrm{CeO_2}$, $\mathrm{ZrO_2}$ (cubic) |
| NiAs | hcp | all octahedral | 6:6 | FeS, CoS, NiS |
| wurtzite (ZnS) | hcp | half tetrahedral | 4:4 | ZnO, GaN, AlN, CdS |
| $\mathrm{CdCl_2}$ / $\mathrm{CdI_2}$ | ccp / hcp | octahedral in alternate layers | 6:3 | $\mathrm{MgCl_2}$, $\mathrm{TiS_2}$, $\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2}$ |
| corundum ($\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$) | hcp | two-thirds octahedral | 6:4 | $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$, $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$ |
| rutile ($\mathrm{TiO_2}$) | distorted hcp | half octahedral, in chains | 6:3 | $\mathrm{SnO_2}$, $\mathrm{MnO_2}$, $\mathrm{MgF_2}$ |
| spinel ($\mathrm{AB_2O_4}$) | ccp | ⅛ tetrahedral (A), ½ octahedral (B) | A 4, B 6 | $\mathrm{MgAl_2O_4}$, $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$, $\mathrm{LiMn_2O_4}$ |
| caesium chloride | simple cubic, not close packed | cube centre | 8:8 | CsBr, CsI, NH₄Cl, β-brass CuZn |

The layered $\mathrm{CdI_2}$ type deserves special mention because it reappears in [[materials-chemistry/energy-materials]] and [[materials-chemistry/nanomaterials]]: filling every octahedral hole between *alternate* pairs of anion layers leaves empty van der Waals gaps, into which lithium ions can be inserted ($\mathrm{TiS_2}$, the first rechargeable lithium battery cathode), and across which the solid can be peeled into single sheets ($\mathrm{MoS_2}$, which has a related structure with trigonal prismatic metal coordination). The same idea with oxygen layers and alternating Li and Co layers gives $\mathrm{LiCoO_2}$, which is rock salt with cation ordering in alternate (111) planes.

::: example Counting formula units and checking a structure by density {#ex-spinel-density}
Spinel, $\mathrm{MgAl_2O_4}$, has a cubic cell with $a = 8.083\ \text{Å}$ and measured density $3.58\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. (a) The oxide ions form a ccp array with the cell edge equal to twice that of the simple ccp cell. How many oxide ions, tetrahedral holes and octahedral holes does the cell contain, and how many formula units? (b) Check the density.
::: solution
(a) Doubling the edge of an fcc cell multiplies its volume by 8, so the cell holds $8 \times 4 = 32$ oxide ions, hence $32$ octahedral and $64$ tetrahedral holes. The formula has four oxygens, so $Z = 32/4 = 8$: eight $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ in $8$ of the $64$ tetrahedral holes (one-eighth) and sixteen $\mathrm{Al^{3+}}$ in $16$ of the $32$ octahedral holes (half).

(b) $M = 24.305 + 2(26.982) + 4(15.999) = 142.27\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, $V = (8.083\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm})^3 = 5.281\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, so
$$
\rho = \frac{ZM}{N_\mathrm{A}V} = \frac{8 \times 142.27}{6.022\times10^{23} \times 5.281\times10^{-22}} = 3.58\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}},
$$
in agreement with experiment. A density check like this is the quickest test of a proposed $Z$: had we guessed $Z = 4$, the density would have been half the measured value.
:::
:::

### Normal and inverse spinels

In a **normal** spinel $\mathrm{A^{II}[B^{III}_2]O_4}$ the divalent ion occupies the tetrahedral sites (square brackets mark octahedral sites). In an **inverse** spinel $\mathrm{B[AB]O_4}$ the tetrahedral sites hold half the trivalent ions and the divalent ions move to octahedral sites; intermediate distributions are common and are described by the **inversion parameter** $x$, the fraction of tetrahedral sites occupied by B. $\mathrm{MgAl_2O_4}$ is nearly normal; magnetite, $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4} = \mathrm{Fe^{3+}[Fe^{2+}Fe^{3+}]O_4}$, and $\mathrm{NiFe_2O_4}$ are inverse. Electrostatics and size alone mildly favour the normal arrangement. For transition-metal ions the decisive factor is often ligand field stabilisation ([[coordination-chemistry/crystal-field-theory]]).

::: proposition Octahedral site preference energy {#prop-ospe}
For a high-spin transition-metal ion, the **octahedral site preference energy** is the difference between its ligand field stabilisation energies in octahedral and tetrahedral sites,
$$
\mathrm{OSPE} = \lvert\mathrm{CFSE_{oct}}\rvert - \lvert\mathrm{CFSE_{tet}}\rvert,
$$
estimated with $\Delta_\mathrm{t} \approx \tfrac49\Delta_\mathrm{o}$ for the same ion and ligand at the same distance. In a spinel the ion with the larger OSPE tends to take the octahedral sites. The rule is empirical: it neglects differences in Madelung energy, ion size and covalency, which can dominate when OSPE differences are small.
:::

::: proof
In an octahedral site each $t_{2g}$ electron is stabilised by $0.4\Delta_\mathrm{o}$ and each $e_g$ electron destabilised by $0.6\Delta_\mathrm{o}$; in a tetrahedral site each $e$ electron is stabilised by $0.6\Delta_\mathrm{t}$ and each $t_2$ electron destabilised by $0.4\Delta_\mathrm{t}$. These weights follow from the barycentre rule: the splitting preserves the average energy of the five $d$ orbitals. For high-spin $d^8$, octahedral $t_{2g}^6e_g^2$ gives $6(-0.4) + 2(0.6) = -1.2\Delta_\mathrm{o}$; tetrahedral $e^4t_2^4$ gives $4(-0.6) + 4(0.4) = -0.8\Delta_\mathrm{t} = -0.36\Delta_\mathrm{o}$; so OSPE $= 0.84\Delta_\mathrm{o}$. For high-spin $d^5$ both stabilisations are zero and OSPE $= 0$. Doing the same for each $d^n$ gives OSPEs (in units of $\Delta_\mathrm{o}$) of 0 for $d^0$, $d^5$ and $d^{10}$, and the largest values, $0.84\Delta_\mathrm{o}$, for $d^3$ and $d^8$. Since the preference energy of an ion enters the lattice energy once per ion, the arrangement that places the ion with the larger OSPE on octahedral sites has the lower energy, other things being equal.
:::

Using values of $\Delta_\mathrm{o}$ from the spectra of oxide complexes, Jack Dunitz and Leslie Orgel (and independently Donald McClure) estimated OSPEs in 1957 of about $158\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$, $86$ for $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$, $95$ for $\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}$, $31$ for $\mathrm{Co^{2+}}$, $17$ for $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and zero for $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$, $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ and $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$. Every chromium(III) spinel is normal; $\mathrm{NiFe_2O_4}$ and $\mathrm{NiAl_2O_4}$ are largely inverse, because nickel(II) gains more from an octahedral site than iron(III) or aluminium(III) loses.

::: example Normal or inverse? {#ex-spinel-inversion}
Predict whether (a) $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$, (b) $\mathrm{Mn_3O_4}$ and (c) $\mathrm{ZnFe_2O_4}$ are normal or inverse spinels.
::: solution
(a) Magnetite contains $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ ($d^6$, high spin) and $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ ($d^5$). Octahedral $t_{2g}^4e_g^2$: $4(-0.4) + 2(0.6) = -0.4\Delta_\mathrm{o}$; tetrahedral $e^3t_2^3$: $3(-0.6) + 3(0.4) = -0.6\Delta_\mathrm{t} = -0.27\Delta_\mathrm{o}$; OSPE $= 0.13\Delta_\mathrm{o}$, small but positive. $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ has none. So $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ goes octahedral: **inverse**, $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}[Fe^{2+}Fe^{3+}]O_4}$, as observed. The mixed-valence octahedral sites are why magnetite conducts electricity, by electron hopping between $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$.

(b) $\mathrm{Mn_3O_4}$ contains $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ ($d^5$, OSPE 0) and $\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}$ ($d^4$: octahedral $t_{2g}^3e_g^1 = -0.6\Delta_\mathrm{o}$, tetrahedral $e^2t_2^2 = -0.4\Delta_\mathrm{t} = -0.18\Delta_\mathrm{o}$, OSPE $0.42\Delta_\mathrm{o}$). $\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}$ stays octahedral: **normal**, $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}[Mn^{3+}_2]O_4}$. It is in fact tetragonally distorted (hausmannite), a cooperative Jahn–Teller effect of the $e_g^1$ ions.

(c) $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$ ($d^{10}$) and $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ ($d^5$) both have zero OSPE, so ligand field arguments are silent. $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$ has a strong preference for tetrahedral coordination for covalent reasons (as in ZnO and ZnS), so the spinel is **normal**, which is observed. This case shows the limit of [[#prop-ospe]].
:::
:::

## Perovskites and the tolerance factor

The ideal **perovskite** $\mathrm{ABX_3}$ is cubic with B at the cell corner $(0, 0, 0)$, X at the edge midpoints $(\tfrac12, 0, 0)$ and equivalents, and A at the body centre $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, with $Z = 1$. Equivalently, it is a three-dimensional network of corner-sharing $\mathrm{BX_6}$ octahedra with a large A cation in each cuboctahedral cavity, coordinated by twelve X; or a ccp array of composition $\mathrm{AX_3}$ with B in the quarter of the octahedral holes surrounded only by X. A and X together build the close-packed layers, so A must be about the same size as X.

::: proposition The Goldschmidt tolerance factor {#prop-tolerance}
For ions treated as hard spheres of radii $r_\mathrm{A}$, $r_\mathrm{B}$ and $r_\mathrm{X}$, the ideal cubic perovskite requires $t = 1$, where
$$
t = \frac{r_\mathrm{A} + r_\mathrm{X}}{\sqrt2\,(r_\mathrm{B} + r_\mathrm{X})}.
$$ {#eq-tolerance}
Empirically, perovskites form for about $0.8 < t < 1.06$ (oxides, with Shannon radii): close to 1 the structure is cubic or nearly so; for $t < 1$ the octahedra tilt and the symmetry drops (orthorhombic $\mathrm{CaTiO_3}$, $\mathrm{GdFeO_3}$); for $t > 1$ the B ion rattles in a cage too large for it, and the structure may become polar or adopt hexagonal stacking. The bounds are empirical guides, not laws.
:::

::: proof
In the cubic cell of edge $a$ with B at the corner, the B–X distance runs along a cell edge from the corner to the edge midpoint $(\tfrac12, 0, 0)$: it is $a/2$. The A ion at $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ is at distance $\sqrt{0 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14}\,a = a/\sqrt2$ (half a face diagonal) from that same X ion, and there are twelve edge midpoints at this distance, which gives A its twelve-coordination. For touching hard spheres, $r_\mathrm{B} + r_\mathrm{X} = a/2$ and $r_\mathrm{A} + r_\mathrm{X} = a/\sqrt2$. Eliminating $a$, $r_\mathrm{A} + r_\mathrm{X} = \sqrt2\,(r_\mathrm{B} + r_\mathrm{X})$, that is $t = 1$. If $t < 1$ the A ion is too small for its cavity when the B–X bonds are at their ideal length; rotating the octahedra about their shared corners shortens some A–X distances without changing B–X, which is the tilting observed. If $t > 1$ the reverse holds: the A–X contacts set the cell size and the B–X bonds are stretched.
:::

Choosing A at the origin instead puts X at the face centres and B at the body centre; this is the other common setting of the same structure, and it is worth being able to switch between the two pictures.

::: example Tolerance factors of three titanates {#ex-tolerance}
Using Shannon's ionic radii ($\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ $1.40\ \text{Å}$; $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ six-coordinate $0.605\ \text{Å}$; twelve-coordinate $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ $1.34$, $\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}$ $1.44$, $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ $1.61\ \text{Å}$), calculate $t$ for $\mathrm{CaTiO_3}$, $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ and $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ and relate it to their structures at room temperature. Then use $a = 3.905\ \text{Å}$ to find the density of $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$.
::: solution
The denominator is common: $\sqrt2(0.605 + 1.40) = 1.4142 \times 2.005 = 2.8355\ \text{Å}$. Then
$$
t(\mathrm{Ca}) = \frac{2.74}{2.8355} = 0.966, \qquad t(\mathrm{Sr}) = \frac{2.84}{2.8355} = 1.002, \qquad t(\mathrm{Ba}) = \frac{3.01}{2.8355} = 1.062.
$$
$\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ ($t \approx 1.00$) is the textbook cubic perovskite. $\mathrm{CaTiO_3}$ ($t < 1$) is orthorhombic with tilted octahedra, the mineral perovskite itself. $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ ($t > 1$) has a large cell in which $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ is loose; below $120\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ it shifts off-centre in each octahedron, giving a tetragonal, polar, ferroelectric structure ([[materials-chemistry/magnetic-optical]]).

Density of $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$: $M = 87.62 + 47.867 + 3(15.999) = 183.48\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, $Z = 1$, $V = (3.905\times10^{-8})^3 = 5.955\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, $\rho = 183.48/(6.022\times10^{23} \times 5.955\times10^{-23}) = 5.12\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (measured $5.12$). The radii are given to two or three decimals and the bounds on $t$ are fuzzy, so quoting $t$ to three figures is the most that is meaningful.
:::

::: quiz
Which statements about the ideal cubic perovskite $\mathrm{ABX_3}$ are correct?
- [x] The B cation is six-coordinate and the A cation twelve-coordinate.
- [ ] The $\mathrm{BX_6}$ octahedra share edges.
- [x] The cell contains one formula unit.
- [x] A and X together form a cubic close-packed array.
- [ ] A tolerance factor $t < 1$ means the A cation is too large for its site.
::: solution
The octahedra share corners (each X is shared by two B), not edges. The cell has one B (8 corners × ⅛), three X (12 edges × ¼) and one A (body centre): $Z = 1$. A and X ($\mathrm{AX_3}$) form ccp layers. $t < 1$ means $r_\mathrm{A} + r_\mathrm{X}$ is too *small* compared with $\sqrt2(r_\mathrm{B} + r_\mathrm{X})$: the A ion is too small, and the octahedra tilt to close up around it.
:::

## Is the structure chemically reasonable? Pauling's rules and bond valence

A refined crystal structure, or one proposed by a computer search, must also make chemical sense. In 1929 Linus Pauling summarised the regularities of ionic structures in five rules, of which the first two are the most used. **Rule 1**: a coordination polyhedron of anions forms around each cation, the cation–anion distance being set by the sum of radii and the coordination number by the radius ratio. **Rule 2**, the electrostatic valence principle, is the quantitative one.

::: definition Electrostatic bond strength and bond valence {#def-bond-valence}
In Pauling's form, a cation of charge $z$ and coordination number $N$ gives each of its bonds an **electrostatic bond strength** $s = z/N$, and in a stable ionic structure the bond strengths reaching each anion sum to its charge (**Pauling's second rule**, empirical). The modern **bond valence** model (Brown and Altermatt, 1985) makes the bond strength depend on bond length:
$$
s_{ij} = \exp\!\left(\frac{R_0 - R_{ij}}{b}\right), \qquad V_i = \sum_j s_{ij},
$$ {#eq-bvs}
where $R_{ij}$ is the observed bond length, $b = 0.37\ \text{Å}$, and $R_0$ is a tabulated constant for each cation–anion pair (for example $1.815\ \text{Å}$ for $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$–O, $2.118\ \text{Å}$ for $\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}$–O, $1.759\ \text{Å}$ for $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$–O). The **bond valence sum** $V_i$ should equal the formal oxidation state of atom $i$, typically within about 0.1–0.2 valence units in a well-determined structure. The parameters are empirical fits to many structures.
:::

Pauling's remaining rules are qualitative: polyhedra prefer to share corners rather than edges, and edges rather than faces, because sharing brings the cations closer and raises their repulsion (rule 3), especially for highly charged cations with small coordination numbers (rule 4); and structures tend to contain few kinds of distinct site (rule 5, parsimony). Rule 3 explains why rutile, with each octahedron sharing two edges, distorts so as to lengthen those shared edges' Ti···Ti separations, and why silicates almost never share edges between $\mathrm{SiO_4}$ tetrahedra.

::: example Checking perovskite by Pauling and by bond valence {#ex-bvs}
(a) Check Pauling's second rule for an oxide ion in $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ and for one in spinel $\mathrm{MgAl_2O_4}$, in which each oxide is bonded to three Al and one Mg. (b) Calculate the bond valence sums of Ti and Sr in cubic $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ ($a = 3.905\ \text{Å}$).
::: solution
(a) In $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$, each O sits between two Ti (a straight Ti–O–Ti link) and is surrounded by four Sr. Ti bonds have $s = 4/6$ and Sr bonds $s = 2/12$, so the sum is $2(\tfrac23) + 4(\tfrac16) = \tfrac43 + \tfrac23 = 2$: exactly the oxide charge. In spinel, Al bonds have $s = 3/6 = \tfrac12$ and Mg (tetrahedral) bonds $s = 2/4 = \tfrac12$, so the sum is $3(\tfrac12) + \tfrac12 = 2$. Both structures satisfy rule 2 exactly, which is part of why they are so widespread.

(b) Ti–O $= a/2 = 1.9525\ \text{Å}$: $s = \exp[(1.815 - 1.9525)/0.37] = \exp(-0.3716) = 0.690$, and six bonds give $V_\mathrm{Ti} = 4.14$. Sr–O $= a/\sqrt2 = 2.761\ \text{Å}$: $s = \exp[(2.118 - 2.761)/0.37] = \exp(-1.738) = 0.176$, and twelve give $V_\mathrm{Sr} = 2.11$. Both are a little above the formal values 4 and 2, meaning the bonds are slightly short: both cations are slightly over-bonded, as the empirical parameters often make them in close-packed oxides. The tolerance factor, $t = 1.00$, says the same thing in another language. When a bond valence sum is far from the oxidation state (for example $V = 2.6$ for a supposed $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$), the site is under strain, the oxidation state has been mis-assigned, or the refinement is wrong.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why bond valence works
Equation [[#eq-bvs]] says that a cation distributes a fixed amount of "bonding power" (its valence) over its bonds, and a shorter bond takes a larger share. A seven-coordinate site, or one with one short and one long bond, still sums to the same valence. This is the solid-state counterpart of bond-order–bond-length correlations in molecules, and it makes oxidation-state assignment in mixed-valence oxides (which Mn in $\mathrm{LaMnO_3}$ is $\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}$?) a matter of measuring distances.
:::

## Where this leads

The vocabulary of this chapter is used throughout the course. [[materials-chemistry/diffraction]] measures lattice parameters and atomic positions through the spacings of [[#prop-dspacing]] and the arrangement of atoms within the cell. [[materials-chemistry/defects]] asks what happens when sites of these structures are empty or wrongly occupied, with fluorite-type $\mathrm{ZrO_2}$ and $\mathrm{CeO_2}$ as the key ionic conductors. [[materials-chemistry/electronic-structure-solids]] builds bands from the orbitals of these lattices; [[materials-chemistry/magnetic-optical]] explains the ferrimagnetism of inverse spinels and the ferroelectricity of $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$; [[materials-chemistry/porous-materials]] meets frameworks so open that most of the cell is empty; and [[materials-chemistry/energy-materials]] uses layered, spinel and olivine structures as battery electrodes and halide perovskites as solar absorbers. The physics of lattices and the reciprocal lattice is developed in Physics Atlas ([condensed matter: crystals](/learn/physics/lesson.php?c=condensed-matter&l=crystals)).

::: history
René-Just Haüy proposed in 1784, in his *Essai d'une théorie sur la structure des crystaux*, that crystals are built by stacking identical small units ("molécules intégrantes"), and William Hallowes Miller introduced the indices that bear his name in *A Treatise on Crystallography* (1839). Auguste Bravais enumerated the fourteen lattices in 1850, and the 230 space groups were derived independently by Evgraf Fedorov and Arthur Schoenflies in 1891, with William Barlow following in 1894. The first structures determined by X-rays, those of the alkali halides, came from William Henry and William Lawrence Bragg in 1913, and the spinel structure was solved by W. H. Bragg and, independently, Shoji Nishikawa in 1915. Victor Moritz Goldschmidt's geochemical studies of the 1920s introduced the tolerance factor (1926) and systematic ionic radii; Linus Pauling published his rules in the *Journal of the American Chemical Society* in 1929. Robert Shannon's critically revised radii (1976) and I. David Brown and Dov Altermatt's bond valence parameters (1985), both in *Acta Crystallographica*, are the tables used today.
:::

::: summary
- A crystal is a lattice plus a basis; a cell is described by $a, b, c, \alpha, \beta, \gamma$, atoms by fractional coordinates, and the contents by $Z$, checked against density with $\rho = ZM/N_\mathrm{A}V$.
- Seven crystal systems, fourteen Bravais lattices and 230 space groups classify all crystals; only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotations are compatible with a lattice, because the trace $1 + 2\cos\theta$ must be an integer.
- Miller indices $(hkl)$ are the reciprocals of the intercepts; for orthogonal axes $1/d^2 = h^2/a^2 + k^2/b^2 + l^2/c^2$, and for cubic cells $d = a/\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}$.
- Most simple structures are close-packed anion arrays with cations in octahedral or tetrahedral holes: rock salt, zinc blende, fluorite, NiAs, wurtzite, $\mathrm{CdI_2}$, corundum, rutile and spinel.
- In spinels, octahedral site preference energies from ligand field theory decide between normal and inverse distributions ($\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$ and $\mathrm{NiFe_2O_4}$ inverse, $\mathrm{Mn_3O_4}$ and chromites normal).
- The perovskite $\mathrm{ABX_3}$ is a corner-sharing octahedral network; the tolerance factor $t = (r_\mathrm{A} + r_\mathrm{X})/\sqrt2(r_\mathrm{B} + r_\mathrm{X})$ predicts cubic ($t \approx 1$), tilted ($t < 1$) or polar/hexagonal ($t > 1$) variants.
- Pauling's second rule and bond valence sums $V_i = \sum\exp[(R_0 - R_{ij})/b]$ test whether a structure and its oxidation states are chemically reasonable.
:::

Zinc oxide (wurtzite) is hexagonal with $a = 3.250\ \text{Å}$, $c = 5.207\ \text{Å}$ and $Z = 2$. (a) Calculate the cell volume from [[#eq-volume]]. (b) Calculate the density. (c) Calculate $d_{100}$ and $d_{002}$. (Enter the density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, to three significant figures.)

(c) By [[#eq-dhex]], $1/d_{100}^2 = \tfrac43/3.250^2$, $d_{100} = 3.250 \times \sqrt{3}/2 = 2.815\ \text{Å}$; $d_{002} = c/2 = 2.604\ \text{Å}$.

::: exercise Which perovskite? {level=2 check="1.05"}
Using Shannon radii $r(\mathrm{K^+}, \mathrm{XII}) = 1.64\ \text{Å}$, $r(\mathrm{Nb^{5+}}, \mathrm{VI}) = 0.64\ \text{Å}$ and $r(\mathrm{O^{2-}}) = 1.40\ \text{Å}$, calculate the tolerance factor of $\mathrm{KNbO_3}$ and predict whether it is likely to be cubic, tilted or polar at room temperature. Check Pauling's second rule for its oxide ion. (Enter $t$ to three significant figures.)
::: solution
$t = (1.64 + 1.40)/[\sqrt2(0.64 + 1.40)] = 3.04/2.885 = 1.05$. Since $t > 1$, $\mathrm{Nb^{5+}}$ is in an oversized octahedron and is expected to displace off-centre: $\mathrm{KNbO_3}$ is indeed ferroelectric at room temperature (orthorhombic, polar), like $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$. Rule 2: each O receives $2 \times \tfrac56$ from Nb and $4 \times \tfrac{1}{12}$ from K, total $\tfrac53 + \tfrac13 = 2$. (Note that $\mathrm{A^+B^{5+}}$ and $\mathrm{A^{2+}B^{4+}}$ both satisfy rule 2; so does $\mathrm{A^{3+}B^{3+}}$ in $\mathrm{LaAlO_3}$.)
:::

::: exercise Spinel site preferences {level=2}
Use octahedral site preference energies to predict whether (a) $\mathrm{NiCr_2O_4}$, (b) $\mathrm{CoFe_2O_4}$ and (c) $\mathrm{MgFe_2O_4}$ are normal or inverse, and say where the argument is weakest.
::: solution
(a) $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ($d^3$, OSPE $\approx 158\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) beats $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ ($86$): Cr keeps the octahedral sites, **normal** $\mathrm{Ni[Cr_2]O_4}$ (tetragonally distorted by tetrahedral $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$, a Jahn–Teller ion in that geometry). (b) $\mathrm{Co^{2+}}$ ($d^7$, OSPE $\approx 31$) against $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ (0): **inverse**, $\mathrm{Fe[CoFe]O_4}$, which is observed (with partial disorder depending on heat treatment). (c) $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ both have zero OSPE, so ligand field theory makes no prediction. $\mathrm{MgFe_2O_4}$ is in fact largely inverse ($x \approx 0.9$), for reasons of Madelung energy and covalency; its inversion depends on the cooling rate, because cation exchange between sites is thermally activated. This is the weakest case.
:::

::: exercise Ideal axial ratio of hcp {level=3 check="1.633"}
In hexagonal close packing of spheres of radius $r$, the hexagonal cell has $a = 2r$ and contains spheres at $(0, 0, 0)$ and $(\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac12)$. Using the distance formula in the text, derive the ideal ratio $c/a$, and use it to find the ideal $c$ for a metal with $a = 2.507\ \text{Å}$ (cobalt). (Enter $c/a$ to four significant figures.)
::: hint
The sphere at $(\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac12)$ must touch the sphere at the origin: their distance is $2r = a$.
:::
::: solution
With $\Delta x = \tfrac13$, $\Delta y = \tfrac23$, $\Delta z = \tfrac12$, $a = b$, $\gamma = 120^\circ$ and $\alpha = \beta = 90^\circ$:
$$
\lvert\Delta\mathbf{r}\rvert^2 = a^2\left(\tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + 2 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac23 \cdot (-\tfrac12)\right) + \tfrac14c^2 = a^2\left(\tfrac59 - \tfrac29\right) + \tfrac14c^2 = \tfrac13a^2 + \tfrac14c^2.
$$
Setting this equal to $a^2$: $\tfrac14c^2 = \tfrac23a^2$, $c/a = \sqrt{8/3} = 1.633$. For cobalt, ideal $c = 1.633 \times 2.507 = 4.094\ \text{Å}$; the measured value, $4.070\ \text{Å}$ ($c/a = 1.623$), is close. Zinc and cadmium ($c/a \approx 1.86$) deviate strongly, a sign of directional bonding in those metals.
:::

::: exercise Bond valence sum in rutile {level=3 check="4.08"}
In rutile each titanium has four O at $1.946\ \text{Å}$ and two at $1.983\ \text{Å}$. (a) Calculate the bond valence sum of Ti with $R_0 = 1.815\ \text{Å}$ and $b = 0.37\ \text{Å}$. (b) Each O in rutile is bonded to three Ti. Check Pauling's second rule. (c) Explain why the bond valence sum is more informative than Pauling's rule for a distorted site. (Enter the bond valence sum to three significant figures.)
::: solution
(a) $s(1.946) = \exp(-0.131/0.37) = \exp(-0.3541) = 0.7018$; $s(1.983) = \exp(-0.168/0.37) = \exp(-0.4541) = 0.6351$. $V_\mathrm{Ti} = 4(0.7018) + 2(0.6351) = 2.807 + 1.270 = 4.08$, close to the formal $+4$. (b) Each Ti–O bond has Pauling strength $4/6 = \tfrac23$, and three bonds give $2$: rule 2 is satisfied. (c) Pauling's rule gives every bond of a site the same strength regardless of length, so it cannot tell whether a distorted octahedron is reasonable; the bond valence model weights each bond by its length, and would immediately flag, say, a "Ti" site whose bonds sum to $3.3$, revealing $\mathrm{Ti^{3+}}$, a misassigned atom or a wrong structure.
:::
