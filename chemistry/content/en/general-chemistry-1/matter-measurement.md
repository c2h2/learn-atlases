A copper wire, a beaker of seawater, a crystal of sugar and the air above the bench are all matter, and the word alone does no chemical work. What you can do with a sample — decompose it, dissolve it, or predict its mass from its volume — depends on what kind of matter it is, and on whether the number you write down means what you think it means. This chapter answers the first question at the level of particles, then fixes the measuring habits the rest of the course will not relax: SI units, density, the Celsius and kelvin scales, significant figures, and unit conversions that do not change the quantity you started with.

## Elements, compounds and mixtures

::: definition Elements, compounds and mixtures {#def-matter-kinds}
An **element** is a pure substance whose atoms all have the same atomic number. Those atoms may stand alone, as in helium, or be bound into molecules of a single element, as in $\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2}$ or $\mathrm{S_8}$, or packed in a metallic lattice, as in copper or aluminium. Oxygen is an element even though the particle you breathe is a molecule: both nuclei are oxygen, and no chemical reaction turns oxygen into anything simpler.

A **compound** is a pure substance in which two or more elements are chemically combined in a fixed ratio. Every water molecule is $\mathrm{H_2O}$; in sodium chloride the ions $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ occur one to one throughout the crystal. The ratio belongs to the substance, not to the particular sample. A compound has properties of its own: hydrogen burns, oxygen supports burning, and water puts a flame out.

A **mixture** is two or more pure substances physically together, in proportions that can vary from sample to sample. Seawater can be saltier and still be seawater; damp air can hold more water vapour and still be air. No new substance has formed. The particles of the components simply share a container.

A **pure substance** is an element or a compound: fixed composition, and, at a given temperature and pressure, fixed properties such as density and melting point. A mixture of different substances does not melt sharply at one temperature.
:::

The question to ask of an unlabelled sample is a particulate one. Is there one atomic number, or more than one? If more than one, are the atoms locked in a fixed ratio inside a single substance, or only sharing the bottle? Copper is one kind of atom. Water is two kinds, locked as $\mathrm{H_2O}$. Seawater is water molecules and several ions, in proportions the ocean decided, not a formula.

::: example Sorting eight samples {#ex-classify}
Classify each sample as an element, a compound or a mixture, and say what the particles are. (a) Helium in a balloon. (b) Distilled water. (c) Dry filtered air. (d) Crystals of sodium chloride. (e) Bronze, an alloy of copper and tin. (f) Granite. (g) Glucose, $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$. (h) Oxygen gas.
::: solution
(a) Element: helium atoms, one atomic number, no chemical partners.
(b) Compound: $\mathrm{H_2O}$ molecules, hydrogen and oxygen in the fixed ratio $2:1$.
(c) Mixture. Mostly $\mathrm{N_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$, with argon and a little $\mathrm{CO_2}$, in proportions no formula fixes. Filtered dry air is uniform, so the mixture is homogeneous, but uniformity is not purity.
(d) Compound. $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ occupy the lattice in a $1:1$ ratio. There is no $\mathrm{NaCl}$ molecule; the fixed ratio is enough to make the substance pure.
(e) Mixture. Copper and tin atoms share a metallic lattice, and the tin content of bronze can vary. Looking uniform does not make an alloy a compound.
(f) Mixture, and visibly heterogeneous: grains of quartz, feldspar and mica, each grain itself a compound.
(g) Compound: every molecule is $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$.
(h) Element. The particles are $\mathrm{O_2}$ molecules. Two atoms do not make a compound when both are the same element.

(d) and (h) are the pair of traps. A compound need not consist of molecules, and a molecule need not be a compound.
:::
:::

## One phase or several

::: definition Homogeneous and heterogeneous matter {#def-homo-hetero}
A **phase** is a portion of a sample throughout which composition and physical properties are uniform. Matter is **homogeneous** when the sample is a single phase, and **heterogeneous** when two or more phases are present. Salt dissolved in water, ethanol mixed with water, dry air and many alloys are homogeneous. Granite, oil floating on water, and sand stirred into water are heterogeneous: a boundary can be pointed at, or a filter can stop one phase and pass the other.

Homogeneous does not mean pure. Air is homogeneous and is a mixture; distilled water is homogeneous and is a compound. The two cuts are independent. While a pure substance is melting, ice and liquid water — two phases of the *same* substance — sit together; that is a change of state, not a mixture of different substances. Phase diagrams take up that case later. For a bottle on the bench, "one substance or several?" and "one phase or several?" are the questions that matter.
:::

Colloids sit on the boundary of ordinary observation. Milk looks uniform and does not settle in a few minutes, yet it is droplets of fat dispersed in water. In this course, a sample that looks uniform and does not settle on the time scale of the experiment is treated as homogeneous. Scattering of light, and the proper description of colloids, can wait.

::: example Three beakers {#ex-beakers}
Three unlabelled beakers stand on the bench. Beaker A is clear. When a portion is evaporated to dryness it leaves a white solid. Beaker B is cloudy; filter paper catches sand, and the liquid that passes through leaves nothing when evaporated. Beaker C is cloudy, the cloudiness passes through the paper, and overnight a solid settles to the bottom. What kind of matter is in each beaker?
::: solution
A holds a homogeneous mixture: a solid dissolved in water, one phase, separable because the water can be driven off and the solid cannot. Evaporating a salt solution is an ordinary teaching operation. The hazards are hot glassware and violent boiling if the liquid is heated too hard; the work belongs under supervision, with a risk assessment, and not as a procedure to invent at the bench from a textbook.

B is a heterogeneous mixture of sand and water. Filtration separates the two phases, and the water was essentially pure — nothing dissolved remained after evaporation.

C was heterogeneous as well, even while it merely looked cloudy. The particles were small enough to pass the paper and large enough to settle overnight. Failure of a quick filtration is not evidence of a single phase; it is evidence that the phases were finely divided. Leaving the beaker to stand was the observation that settled the classification.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-pure}
Which description is a pure compound?
- [ ] Dry air, uniform throughout the room, about four-fifths nitrogen and one-fifth oxygen.
- [x] Distilled water: every molecule is $\mathrm{H_2O}$, so the ratio of the elements is fixed and there is only one substance.
- [ ] Brass that looks perfectly uniform, made from copper and zinc in a proportion the foundry chose.
- [ ] Granite, because each of its minerals has a definite formula.
::: solution
Distilled water is one substance with a fixed composition, which is what "pure compound" requires. Dry air and brass are homogeneous *mixtures*: uniform, but the proportions are not fixed by a chemical formula. Granite contains compounds, yet the rock itself is a heterogeneous mixture of those minerals. Containing compounds is not the same thing as being one compound.
:::
:::

## The units a chemical measurement uses

The SI has seven base quantities. Five of them run through chemistry:

- length, the **metre**, symbol $\mathrm{m}$;
- mass, the **kilogram**, symbol $\mathrm{kg}$;
- time, the **second**, symbol $\mathrm{s}$;
- thermodynamic temperature, the **kelvin**, symbol $\mathrm{K}$;
- amount of substance, the **mole**, symbol $\mathrm{mol}$.

The ampere and the candela complete the list and will not be needed here. The mole is the chemist's counting unit; the next chapter gives it a definition and a use. Two features of the list cause needless mistakes. The base unit of mass is the kilogram, not the gram, so $1\,\mathrm{g} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}$. And the symbol of the kelvin is $\mathrm{K}$, with no degree sign: $298.15\,\mathrm{K}$, never $298.15\,^\circ\mathrm{K}$.

Prefixes turn a base unit into a convenient size. The ones that appear constantly are:

| prefix | symbol | factor |
|---|---|---|
| pico | $\mathrm{p}$ | $10^{-12}$ |
| nano | $\mathrm{n}$ | $10^{-9}$ |
| micro | $\mu$ | $10^{-6}$ |
| milli | $\mathrm{m}$ | $10^{-3}$ |
| centi | $\mathrm{c}$ | $10^{-2}$ |
| deci | $\mathrm{d}$ | $10^{-1}$ |
| kilo | $\mathrm{k}$ | $10^{3}$ |
| mega | $\mathrm{M}$ | $10^{6}$ |

A cubic metre is an enormous volume for a bench reaction. The unit the laboratory actually uses is the cubic decimetre, which has the special name **litre**: $1\,\mathrm{L} = 1\,\mathrm{dm^3}$ exactly, by definition. The cubic centimetre and the millilitre are the same volume, $1\,\mathrm{mL} = 1\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ exactly, and because a decimetre is ten centimetres,

$$
1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = (10\,\mathrm{cm})^3 = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 1000\,\mathrm{mL},
$$

also exactly. Defined equalities have no uncertainty. They never limit the significant figures of a result.

A balance, once calibrated, reports **mass**. **Weight** is the gravitational force on that mass, and it would change if the same object were carried up a mountain. In every calculation in this course, "weigh" means "measure the mass", and a result in grams is a mass. Do not multiply by $g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ unless someone has asked for a force.

## Significant figures and rounding

::: definition Significant figures {#def-sigfigs}
The **significant figures** of a measured number are the digits that carry information about its precision, starting at the first non-zero digit. Reading them off is a convention, not a law of nature, and it is this convention:

- every non-zero digit counts;
- a zero between non-zero digits counts ($4.05$ has three significant figures);
- leading zeros, which only locate the decimal point, do not count ($0.00450$ has three);
- trailing zeros count when a decimal point is written ($2.500$ has four), and a trailing zero in scientific notation counts ($4.50\times 10^{3}$ has three);
- a bare number such as $4500$ is ambiguous, and must be written as $4.5\times 10^{3}$, $4.50\times 10^{3}$ or $4.500\times 10^{3}$ according to what was actually known.

In multiplication and division the result has as many significant figures as the factor with the fewest. In addition and subtraction the result is limited by the least precise *decimal place*, not by the count of digits: $14.2 + 0.085$ is known only to the tenth. Counted numbers (12 students, 2 atoms in $\mathrm{O_2}$) and defined conversions ($100\,\mathrm{cm} = 1\,\mathrm{m}$ exactly) are exact and do not limit a result.

**Rounding**, in this course, is round half up. If the first digit you drop is $0$–$4$, leave the last kept digit unchanged; if it is $5$–$9$, increase the last kept digit by one. Metrology often uses round-half-to-even, to avoid a systematic drift when many fives are rounded; we do not, so that every exercise has a single numerical answer. Carry one extra guard digit through intermediate steps, and round only the value you report.
:::

Precision and accuracy are not the same virtue. Precision is how tightly repeated measurements cluster; accuracy is how close that cluster lies to the true value. Significant figures describe precision. A balance that is always $0.05\,\mathrm{g}$ high can be beautifully precise and still inaccurate, and no clever rounding repairs it. Calibration, not arithmetic, is what deals with accuracy.

::: example Reading and combining measured numbers {#ex-sigfigs}
(a) How many significant figures are in $0.00450$, in $4.050\times 10^{-3}$, and in $4500$? (b) Evaluate $14.2 + 0.085 + 3.11$ to the precision the addition rule allows. (c) A rectangular block measures $2.50\,\mathrm{cm}$ by $3.20\,\mathrm{cm}$ by $4.10\,\mathrm{cm}$. What is its volume, to the correct number of significant figures?
::: solution
(a) $0.00450$ has three significant figures: the leading zeros do not count, and the trailing zero does, because it is written after the decimal point. $4.050\times 10^{-3}$ is the same magnitude written honestly, and it has four significant figures — the zero between $5$ and the end is measured, not decorative. $4500$ cannot be assigned a count. It might be known to the nearest hundred or to the nearest unit. Write $4.5\times 10^{3}$ or $4.500\times 10^{3}$ and the ambiguity disappears.

(b) The sum is $14.2 + 0.085 + 3.11 = 17.395$. The least precise term, $14.2$, stops at the tenth, so the sum stops at the tenth: $17.4$. The hundredths in $3.11$ and the thousandths in $0.085$ do not survive contact with a number that was never known past $0.1$.

(c) $2.50\times 3.20 = 8.00$, and $8.00\times 4.10 = 32.8$. Each edge has three significant figures, so the volume is $32.8\,\mathrm{cm^3}$. Do not round the intermediate $8.00$ back to one significant figure, and do not quote $32.80$: the last edge, $4.10$, has three figures, and the product cannot grow a fourth.
:::
:::

## Density

::: definition Density {#def-density}
The **density** of a homogeneous sample is its mass divided by its volume,
$$
\rho = \frac{m}{V}.
$$
Density is **intensive**: if you take twice the sample you double both $m$ and $V$, and $\rho$ is unchanged. The SI unit is $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. In the laboratory, $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ is the usual unit for solids and liquids, and $1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ exactly, as proved below. Because substances expand when heated, a careful density belongs to a stated temperature. The numbers in this chapter are for ordinary bench temperatures unless a temperature is given; we will not apply thermal-expansion corrections.
:::

Density is the intensive property you can measure with a balance and a ruler, or a balance and a volumetric flask, and it is often enough to tell two metals apart. It will not tell apart two substances that happen to share a density, and it says nothing about *which* particles are present. Identity and density answer different questions.

::: example Ethanol in a volumetric flask {#ex-ethanol-density}
A $50.00\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ portion of ethanol has mass $39.45\,\mathrm{g}$ at the temperature of the measurement. What is the density?
::: solution
$$
\rho = \frac{39.45\,\mathrm{g}}{50.00\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 0.7890\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$
Both data have four significant figures and the quotient is exact at $0.7890$, so no rounding is required. The result is intensive: $100.00\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ of the same ethanol would have mass $78.90\,\mathrm{g}$ and the same density. Quoting it without the temperature would be slightly loose for careful work — ethanol expands visibly between $15$ and $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — but the arithmetic of the definition does not change.
:::
:::

::: example An aluminium block {#ex-al-block}
A rectangular block measures $2.50\,\mathrm{cm}$ by $3.20\,\mathrm{cm}$ by $4.10\,\mathrm{cm}$ and has mass $88.6\,\mathrm{g}$. Find its density, and decide whether the value is consistent with aluminium, whose density is $2.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.
::: solution
The volume was found in [[#ex-sigfigs]]: $V = 32.8\,\mathrm{cm^3}$, three significant figures. Then
$$
\rho = \frac{88.6\,\mathrm{g}}{32.8\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 2.701\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}},
$$
which rounds to $2.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. That matches the accepted density of aluminium at the precision of the mass, which had only three significant figures. A much denser metal of the same size would have weighed distinctly more than $88.6\,\mathrm{g}$; the measurement can rule those out, and it cannot prove the block is aluminium rather than some other substance of density $2.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. An intensive property narrows the field. It does not, by itself, name the substance.
:::
:::

::: proposition Converting a density between $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ {#prop-density-units}
Unit conversion multiplies a quantity by a ratio that equals $1$, arranged so that the unwanted unit cancels. Applied to density,
$$
1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}
$$
exactly. A numerical value of density expressed in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ is therefore multiplied by $1000$ to express the same density in $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and the significant figures are unchanged, because $1000$ here is a defined conversion, not a measurement.
:::

::: proof Factor-label derivation of the factor $1000$ {#proof-density-units}
Start from $1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and insert two ratios, each exactly $1$: there are $10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}$ in a gram, and $10^{2}\,\mathrm{cm}$ in a metre. Volume is a cube of a length, so the length ratio is cubed:
$$
1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}
= 1\,\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{cm^3}}
\times \frac{10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}}{1\,\mathrm{g}}
\times \left(\frac{10^{2}\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^{3}.
$$
The grams cancel, and the centimetres cancel. The powers of ten are
$$
10^{-3}\times (10^{2})^{3} = 10^{-3}\times 10^{6} = 10^{3},
$$
so
$$
1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}.
$$
Nothing in the arithmetic was approximate. The factor is exact, and it may be used without spending a significant figure. The same cancelling of units is the whole of the factor-label method: write the identity you need as a fraction, multiply, and refuse to stop until only the unit you were asked for remains.
:::

::: widget plot

x: 0, 10

caption: Mass $m$ (in $\mathrm{g}$) against volume $V$ (in $\mathrm{cm^3}$) for a homogeneous substance, with density $\rho$ equal to the slider $a$ in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. The widget is the definition drawn as a graph. Several pieces of one pure substance — different masses, different volumes — fall on one line, and the slope of that line is $\rho$. A mixture whose composition varied from piece to piece would not. Scatter about the line is experimental uncertainty, not a new law; a line that misses the origin means a systematic error, often a volume that includes an empty container you forgot to subtract.

## Temperature: Celsius and kelvin

::: proposition The Celsius–kelvin relation {#prop-temperature}
In the SI the Celsius temperature $\theta$ and the thermodynamic temperature $T$ are related by definition:
$$
\frac{\theta}{^\circ\mathrm{C}} = \frac{T}{\mathrm{K}} - 273.15,
$$
or equivalently $T/\mathrm{K} = \theta/^\circ\mathrm{C} + 273.15$. The offset $273.15$ is exact. A temperature *interval* therefore has the same numerical value on both scales.
:::

::: proof Why an interval does not pick up $273.15$ {#proof-temperature}
Write the defining equation at two temperatures, $T_2$ and $T_1$:
$$
\theta_2 = T_2 - 273.15, \qquad \theta_1 = T_1 - 273.15,
$$
where $\theta$ is in degrees Celsius and $T$ is in kelvin. Subtract:
$$
\theta_2 - \theta_1 = T_2 - T_1.
$$
The offset cancels. A rise of $7.4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is a rise of $7.4\,\mathrm{K}$, not of $7.4 + 273.15$. The size of the degree and the size of the kelvin are the same; only the zero of the scale differs. Celsius zero is defined to lie $273.15\,\mathrm{K}$ below the zero of thermodynamic temperature, which is absolute zero. Before 20 May 2019 the kelvin was defined by fixing the triple point of water at $273.16\,\mathrm{K}$, and the Celsius scale put $0.01\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at that triple point, so ice point and $273.15\,\mathrm{K}$ coincided by construction. Since the 2019 redefinition the kelvin is fixed by assigning an exact value to the Boltzmann constant, and the relation $\theta/^\circ\mathrm{C} = T/\mathrm{K} - 273.15$ is kept as the *definition* of the Celsius scale. A real ice bath, with dissolved air and at ordinary atmospheric pressure, is not a definition and need not sit at exactly $273.15\,\mathrm{K}$. The conversion you use in calculations is exact anyway.
:::

Gas laws, and every later formula in which temperature multiplies or divides an energy, demand the kelvin. Inserting $25$ where the temperature is $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is not a small slip: $298\,\mathrm{K}$ and $25\,\mathrm{K}$ differ by a factor of twelve.

::: example Converting temperatures, and not converting differences {#ex-temperature}
(a) Express $25.00\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $100.00\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ in kelvin. (b) A freezer stands at $-18.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Report the thermodynamic temperature to the tenth of a kelvin, using round half up. (c) A solution warms from $19.4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $26.8\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. What is the temperature change in kelvin?
::: solution
(a) The offset is exact, and both Celsius readings are given to a hundredth of a degree, which is the same decimal place as $273.15$. So
$$
25.00 + 273.15 = 298.15\,\mathrm{K}, \qquad 100.00 + 273.15 = 373.15\,\mathrm{K}.
$$
These are the numbers to substitute into a gas law. The symbols $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $298.15\,\mathrm{K}$ name one and the same temperature.

(b) $-18.0 + 273.15 = 255.15$. The reading $-18.0$ is known to a tenth, so the sum is known to a tenth. The first dropped digit is $5$; round half up increases the tenths digit from $1$ to $2$. The temperature is $255.2\,\mathrm{K}$.

(c) The change is $26.8 - 19.4 = 7.4$. By [[#prop-temperature]] the same number is the change in kelvin: $\Delta T = 7.4\,\mathrm{K}$. Adding $273.15$ here would describe a warming of about $281\,\mathrm{K}$, which is not what the solution did.
:::
:::

::: warning Three measurements that are not what they look like {#warn-measurement}
First: a temperature difference and a temperature are different kinds of number. $7.4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ of warming is $7.4\,\mathrm{K}$, already proved; $7.4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ *as a temperature* is $280.6\,\mathrm{K}$. The arithmetic that is right for one is badly wrong for the other, and formulas will not catch the mistake for you — they will quietly return a nonsense pressure or energy.

Second: mass is not weight, and a defined conversion is not a measurement. The factor $100\,\mathrm{cm}/1\,\mathrm{m}$ is exact. Treating it as a number with one or three significant figures throws away digits you were entitled to keep, or invents digits nobody measured.

Third: $4500$, written bare, does not have four significant figures merely because it has four digits, and $0.00450$ does not have six. Leading zeros are not measured. Ambiguous trailing zeros belong in scientific notation. Rounding an intermediate result to the final precision, and then calculating onwards, is a separate way to lose a real digit; keep a guard digit until the last line.
:::

## Dimensional analysis

The proof of [[#prop-density-units]] is one instance of a general habit. You are given a quantity in one unit and asked for it in another. You multiply by fractions equal to $1$, each fraction a defined or measured equivalence written so the units you do not want cancel. The method has several names — dimensional analysis, the factor-label method, unit cancellation. The discipline is the same: a unit is part of the quantity, and it must survive the algebra visibly.

::: example Three conversions {#ex-convert}
(a) Express $0.0450\,\mathrm{m^3}$ in cubic centimetres. (b) Express a density of $2.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ in $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. (c) A sample has mass $2.45\,\mathrm{g}$. What is that mass in milligrams?
::: solution
(a) One metre is exactly $100\,\mathrm{cm}$, so
$$
0.0450\,\mathrm{m^3}\times\left(\frac{100\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^3 = 0.0450\times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 4.50\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^3}.
$$
The factor $10^{6}$ is exact. The three significant figures of $0.0450$ are the three significant figures of the answer. Writing $45000\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ is numerically equal and stylistically worse, because the trailing zeros look uncertain; $4.50\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ says what you know.

(b) By [[#prop-density-units]], multiply by $10^{3}$:
$$
2.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 2.70\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}.
$$
Three significant figures in, three out. The conversion does not justify $2700.0$, and it does not force you down to $3\times 10^{3}$.

(c) $1\,\mathrm{g} = 10^{3}\,\mathrm{mg}$ exactly, so
$$
2.45\,\mathrm{g}\times\frac{10^{3}\,\mathrm{mg}}{1\,\mathrm{g}} = 2.45\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{mg}.
$$
The gram cancels. If the fraction had been written upside down, the units would have come out as $\mathrm{g^2\,mg^{-1}}$, which is the signal to invert the factor before you trust the number.
:::
:::

A converted number that you cannot check by units is not finished. The habit is worth more than any single factor in this chapter: every later formula, from the mole to the gas laws, will be applied by the same cancellation.

::: history {#hist-lavoisier}
Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier made the balance the referee of chemical explanation. Combustion had been described as the loss of a substance, phlogiston, from the burning material. The awkward fact was that a metal *gains* mass when it burns. Lavoisier, working in the 1770s, heated tin and phosphorus in sealed vessels and weighed the vessel, contents and all, before and after. The total mass did not change. Opened afterwards, the vessel drew in air, and the extra mass of the solid matched the mass of the air that had been used up. What had looked like creation of mass was a transfer out of the air. With Guyton de Morveau, Berthollet and Fourcroy he set out a new nomenclature in 1787, replacing traditional names with names built from the elements, and in the *Traité élémentaire de chimie* of 1789 he stated conservation of mass as the axiom of a quantitative chemistry: in a chemical operation on a closed system the total mass is unchanged, and only the combination of the elements changes.

Two boundaries keep the axiom honest. The system has to be closed. A beaker of boiling water loses mass because vapour leaves the bench, not because mass fails. And mass and energy are tied by $E = mc^2$. A change of a million joules — a large energy for one mole of a chemical reaction — corresponds to a mass of about $1.1\times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{g}$, which no balance used in this course can detect. For every weighing and every stoichiometry problem that follows, mass is conserved to far more digits than the significant figures will bear. Lavoisier was executed in 1794. The axiom was not.
:::

## Where this leads

The particles named in this chapter are counted, not by hand, but by weighing. [[general-chemistry-1/atoms-and-moles]] defines the mole and the molar mass that turn a balance reading into a number of atoms. Conservation of mass, which here is a statement about a closed vessel, becomes a balanced equation and a numerical prediction in [[general-chemistry-1/stoichiometry]]. Volume, which for a liquid or a solid is fixed once the mass and the density are known, is no such thing for a gas: [[general-chemistry-1/gases]] makes volume depend on pressure and on the kelvin temperature introduced above.

::: summary
- An element contains one atomic number; a compound contains two or more elements in a fixed ratio; a mixture has a variable composition. Pure means element or compound, not "homogeneous".
- Homogeneous matter is a single phase; heterogeneous matter is two or more. Solutions and alloys are homogeneous mixtures. A filter or a settling test beats a casual glance.
- Chemistry uses the metre, kilogram, second, kelvin and mole. $1\,\mathrm{L} = 1\,\mathrm{dm^3}$ and $1\,\mathrm{mL} = 1\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ exactly. The kelvin is written $\mathrm{K}$, with no degree sign.
- Density is $\rho = m/V$, intensive, and $1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ exactly. A mass–volume graph of a pure substance is a straight line through the origin whose slope is $\rho$.
- $T/\mathrm{K} = \theta/^\circ\mathrm{C} + 273.15$ exactly, so temperature intervals agree on the two scales. Absolute formulas need kelvin; differences do not get $273.15$ added.
- Significant figures: leading zeros do not count, trailing zeros count when the decimal point shows them, multiplication follows the fewest figures, addition follows the least precise decimal place. This course rounds half up, and keeps a guard digit until the end.
- Dimensional analysis multiplies by fractions equal to $1$ until the unwanted unit has cancelled. A result whose unit did not cancel is not a result.
- Mass is conserved in a closed chemical system to a precision no teaching balance can challenge. An open beaker is not a counter-example.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Density of a liquid portion {#exr-density-liquid level=1 check="0.9976"}
A $25.00\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ portion of a liquid has mass $24.94\,\mathrm{g}$. What is its density? (Enter the density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.)
::: solution
$$
\rho = \frac{24.94\,\mathrm{g}}{25.00\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 0.9976\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$
Both measurements have four significant figures, and the quotient needs no rounding. The value sits just under the familiar $1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ of water near room temperature, which is a useful sanity check and not a proof that the liquid is water.
:::

::: exercise Mass from a density and a volume {#exr-mass-vol level=2 check="29.6"}
A liquid has density $1.184\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. What mass is contained in $25.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}$? (Enter the mass in grams, to three significant figures.)
::: solution
$$
m = \rho V = (1.184\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}})(25.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}) = 29.6\,\mathrm{g}.
$$
The product $1.184\times 25.0 = 29.6$ is exact at three figures. The density has four significant figures and the volume has three, so the mass has three. Keeping $29.60$ would pretend the volumetric measurement was better than it was.
:::

::: exercise An addition, rounded properly {#exr-add-round level=2 check="17.4"}
Evaluate $14.2 + 0.085 + 3.11$ and round as the addition rule requires. (Enter the result.)
::: solution
The raw sum is $14.2 + 0.085 + 3.11 = 17.395$. The term $14.2$ is known only to the tenth of a unit, and addition cannot improve on the least precise decimal place. The first dropped digit is $9$, so the tenths digit rounds up: $17.4$. Reporting $17.40$ or $17.395$ keeps digits that $14.2$ never contained.

::: exercise Cubic metres to cubic centimetres {#exr-m3-cm3 level=2 check="45000"}
Convert $0.0450\,\mathrm{m^3}$ to cubic centimetres. The length conversion is exact. (Enter the volume in $\mathrm{cm^3}$, equal to $4.50\times 10^{4}$.)
::: solution
$$
0.0450\,\mathrm{m^3}\times\left(\frac{100\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^3 = 0.0450\times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 4.50\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^3}.
$$
Numerically that is $45000$. The three significant figures live in the coefficient $4.50$; the zeros of the power of ten are not uncertain digits. The factor $(100)^3 = 10^{6}$ is defined, so it contributes no uncertainty of its own.
:::

::: exercise Density of a cylinder {#exr-cylinder level=3 check="0.878"}
A solid cylinder has radius $1.25\,\mathrm{cm}$, height $8.40\,\mathrm{cm}$ and mass $36.2\,\mathrm{g}$. Find its density. Use the value of $\pi$ on your calculator. (Enter the density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, to three significant figures.)
::: hint
The volume of a cylinder is $V = \pi r^{2} h$. Square the radius before you worry about significant figures, carry $\pi$ and the intermediate volume with extra digits, and round only $\rho$.
:::
::: solution
$$
V = \pi r^{2} h = \pi (1.25\,\mathrm{cm})^{2} (8.40\,\mathrm{cm}) = \pi \times 1.5625 \times 8.40 = 41.233\ldots\,\mathrm{cm^3}.
$$
$$
\rho = \frac{36.2\,\mathrm{g}}{41.233\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 0.8779\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}},
$$
which is $0.878\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ to three significant figures. The mass has three figures, and so do $1.25$ and $8.40$; $\pi$ is exact for this purpose and does not limit the result. Using $\pi \approx 3.14$ changes the volume in the fourth figure and still rounds to $0.878$. Forgetting to square the radius does not.
:::
:::

::: exercise An irregular piece of metal {#exr-displacement level=3 check="2.62"}
An irregular metal piece has mass $48.75\,\mathrm{g}$. Lowered into a graduated cylinder, it displaces $18.6\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ of water. What is its density? (Enter the density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, to three significant figures.) Is the value consistent with aluminium, density $2.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$?
::: hint
The displaced volume is the volume of the metal only if the piece is fully submerged and non-porous. The precision is then limited by the poorer of the two measurements.
:::
::: solution
$$
\rho = \frac{48.75\,\mathrm{g}}{18.6\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 2.62097\ldots\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 2.62\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$
The volume has three significant figures and the mass has four, so the density has three. Aluminium at $2.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ differs already in the second significant figure: $2.62$ against $2.70$ is about three per cent, larger than the uncertainty suggested by the last digit of $18.6$ (about one part in $200$). The piece is not aluminium, unless the volume was mis-read. Displacement measures the volume of a non-porous object that is fully under the liquid; trapped air or a porous lump makes $V$ too large and $\rho$ too small, which is the systematic error to suspect before you announce a new metal.
:::
:::

::: exercise The factor of one thousand, applied {#exr-derive-1000 level=3 check="13600"}
Derive, by writing the cancelled units, the relation $1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Hence express $13.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, the density of mercury near room temperature, in $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. (Enter that density in $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$.)
::: solution
$$
1\,\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{cm^3}}
\times \frac{10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}}{1\,\mathrm{g}}
\times \left(\frac{10^{2}\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^{3}
= 10^{-3}\times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}
= 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}.
$$
The grams and the centimetres cancel, as in [[#proof-density-units]]. Multiplying the mercury value by the exact factor $1000$,
$$
13.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 1.36\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},
$$
which is $13600$ when written as an integer. Three significant figures went in, so the honest scientific notation is $1.36\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$; the conversion itself added none.
:::
