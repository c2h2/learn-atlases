What was a manor, and why is serfdom not one system? A familiar picture answers too quickly. From about 1000 to about 1300, it says, Latin Christendom was a pyramid: a king at the top, knights in the middle, and at the bottom a peasant who was the same person in every valley, owing the same labour to the same kind of lord. The picture is easy to draw and impossible to check against a record. Lordship was widespread. Unfreedom was widespread. Neither fact produces one legal status from Scotland to Sicily, and neither fact is a personality called "the peasant".

This chapter stays with rents, labour and courts. It treats the battle of 14 October 1066 and the survey of 1086 as a conquest and the record of that conquest, not as a photograph of Europe. It will not state a population of medieval Europe. This atlas has not locked one, and a total invented to fill the bottom of the pyramid would only add false precision to a diagram that is already doing too much work.

## Lordship is a right to dues

People in these centuries lived, overwhelmingly, by agriculture. That sentence is true and almost useless. It does not say who controlled the land, what was owed from it, whether the obligation was paid in days, in grain or in coin, or which court heard a dispute when the obligation was refused. Those are the questions a manor makes answerable. They are local questions. A true answer on one estate does not travel, free of charge, to the next valley, still less to another kingdom. [[sources/scale]] is the chapter that separates a local fact from the state story people build out of it. The separation starts here, because the cartoon of serfdom is exactly that kind of overreach.

Lords were not a single social type. A lord could be a king, a lay aristocrat, a bishop, or a monastery. The usual date for the foundation of Cluny is 910. Cluny was a reformed monastery and also a great landlord, with rights over land and people at a distance from the house itself. A bishop could hold estates in the same way a lay tenant held them. The peasant who owed week-work did not, by that fact, owe it to a knight who lived in a wooden tower at the edge of the fields. The lord was a holder of rights. The rights could be bought, given, disputed and recorded without the holder ever being the person the cartoon draws.

The word feudalism is older as a historian's bundle than as a medieval statute. Historians disagree about whether the bundle is worth keeping. It ties together land held from a superior, military service, jurisdiction, and a moral language of loyalty. Each of those things existed in some places. They did not arrive as one package, and they did not bind every cultivator. Some land, in parts of the continent, was held as an allod, with no lord at all. A pyramid has nowhere to put that land except by calling it an exception that the diagram may ignore. In the empire, some men who were legally unfree served as knights and administrators. Unfreedom was not a synonym for a cultivator at the bottom of a drawing. Status and occupation are different facts.

:::definition Manor {#def-manor}
A **manor** is a unit of lordship and rent: a lord's right to dues from land and people, often together with a court that hears the people who owe those dues. It is not a standard size of farm, not a village as a community of neighbours, and not a description of every rural household in Latin Christendom.
:::

:::definition Unfreedom {#def-unfreedom}
**Unfreedom**, in this chapter, means a bundle that can include labour services, restrictions on leaving the land, payments when a person marries or inherits, and the duty to answer in a lord's court. The bundle is not one legal status. A person can owe heavy labour and still not match the textbook serf. Another can owe mostly money and still be restricted in movement. Slavery, where a survey uses that harsher label, is not the same entry in the bundle.
:::

The two definitions are deliberately modest. They tell you what to look for in a record. They do not tell you what you will find. A custumal, the written custom of an estate, might specify days of work on the lord's demesne, the land the lord exploited more directly. It might specify coin. It might specify both, and then add harvest boons, a payment at death, and suit of mill. Another estate's custumal might lack the week-work and keep the court. Comparing those documents is the work. Averaging them into "three days a week, everywhere" is the cartoon.

:::theorem Serfdom was not one European system {#thm-not-one}
Unfreedom was widespread in Latin Christendom, and it was not one legal system from Scotland to Sicily. Records show different mixes of labour, money and jurisdiction. A pyramid that stacks king, lords and a single kind of peasant is a diagram, not a law those records obey. The claim does not say that most people were free, and it does not state a population.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the shape of the records that survive, not a mood about the Middle Ages. Surveys, charters and custumals fix obligations locally. They name dues that are not interchangeable: a day of labour is not a shilling, and a shilling is not a right to leave the estate. Where the same kingdom's survey uses several labels for rural people, as Domesday does in 1086, the labels are themselves evidence against one status. Ecclesiastical lords, allodial land and unfree knights are further evidence that "lord over serf" is not the whole of rural power.

What would count against the claim is a common rule, written and applied, that fixed one set of services and one status-name for the rural population across Latin Christendom. A family resemblance is not that rule. If every custumal we possessed stated the same days, the same payments and the same restriction on movement, the claim would fail. That is not what the records do. A population total, had this atlas locked one, still would not be the rule. A count of people is not a code of obligations.
:::

:::quiz
Which sentence follows from [[#thm-not-one]]?
- [ ] Most rural people in Latin Christendom were free of lords.
- [ ] Every estate owed the same three days of labour each week.
- [x] A resemblance among rents and courts is not, by itself, one legal system.
- [ ] The chapter has established a population for medieval Europe.
:::solution
The claim refuses a single system. It does not replace that system with a story in which lords disappear, and it does not smuggle in a census. Resemblances are granted. Identity is not. A population is not locked in this atlas, so no sentence that needs one can be a conclusion of the claim.
:::

## What a pyramid pretends to settle

The pyramid feels like an explanation because it answers "who commands?" with a staircase. The staircase hides the questions a court would actually ask. Commands about what? Owed by whom? Enforced where? Recoverable in coin if the labour was refused? Heritable with the holding, or reassessed when the lord changed? A tenant might hold land of one lord and owe suit to another's court. A monastery might hold the rent while a lay advocate claimed a different right in the same place. Crossed lines are normal in lordship. A pyramid draws them as if they had been tidied.

There is a fair objection. Across many regions a lord takes rent, a dependant is not free to walk away, and a court of some kind hears the resulting disputes. If "serfdom" only names that cluster, then there is one system, and the quarrel is about a word. We grant the cluster. Labour, restriction and jurisdiction often travel together, which is why comparison is possible at all. A cluster is still not a code. A code would fix the mix: how many days, whether marriage is taxed, whether the person can leave, which court hears the plea, and whether the answers are the same in Yorkshire and in Sicily. The cluster lets us ask those questions. Calling it one European system is the step that closes them.

What would count against that reply is the common rule already named in [[#thm-not-one]]. Local custom that differs is what we would expect if the reply is right. It is not an embarrassment to be averaged until the diagram looks clean. Manor, rent and court are useful because they can be filled in differently.

The same caution applies to freedom. "Free" in these records is often a specific package: freedom from certain dues, or freedom to answer in a public court rather than a lord's. A free tenant could still owe rent. An unfree tenant could still hold land heritably. The ministeriales of the empire, unfree and armed, make the staircase look especially foolish. Status was a legal fact. It was not a rung with one face.

## Domesday is one kingdom

On 14 October 1066 a Norman duke took the English kingdom by battle and then by settlement. The man who became William I did not, by winning that battle, survey Europe. In 1086 the Domesday survey recorded the kingdom he and his successors held. The year difference from 1066 to 1086 is twenty years. The survey is the conquest's record, made for a conqueror who wanted rights and values stated. It is not a diary of the battle, and it is not a peasant's account of the twenty years between.

:::widget timeline
start: 1050
end: 1100
window: 5
events: 1066,Hastings; 1086,Domesday
caption: Move the year. A battle and a survey twenty years later are the conquest and the record of the conquest. The survey is not Europe.
:::

Read the figure as two kinds of event, not as a rise. The first dot is a battle that changes who holds the kingdom. The second is a written inquiry into holdings, people and values. Nothing on the line is France, the empire, Sicily, Scotland or Scandinavia. If the window tempts you to see a European curve, the temptation is the error.

What the survey can support is limited and strong. It sorts rural people into more than one condition. Villeins, bordars, sokemen and slaves are not synonyms, and the survey's own practice of using several labels is evidence against a single serf. Sokemen, more visible in some eastern circuits, sit badly on the bottom step of a pyramid: they are inside a lord's jurisdiction and are not the same legal object as a slave. Slaves are in the survey too, so an unfree tenant with a holding does not exhaust even England in 1086. This chapter does not turn those categories into counts. A count used as a population of England would already be a further inference, and a population of Europe would be a different subject. [[sources/evidence]] separates a source from the inference. Domesday is the worked case of a survey made for a ruler.

The change the survey is built to record is largely a change of lords. Conquest here is a political fact, not a racial one. The cultivators were not replaced as a people because a duke won a battle. The document does not license a story of one population emptying the land and another filling it. It licenses a story about tenure: who holds of the king, what the holding is said to be worth, which dues are claimed. Even that story is the king's story. A value in pounds, shillings and pence is a value for assessment and for the claims of lordship. It is not a household budget kept by the tenant.

Women appear in the survey as holders more often than a pyramid of armed men would predict, and they disappear from it as workers, wives and the unrecorded poor. Both facts are facts about the record. A silence about a woman's opinion is not evidence that she had no mind. It is evidence that the king's inquiry did not ask her. The same rule applies to the villein whose labour is listed and whose judgement of the lord is not. We will not summarise "the medieval woman" from this silence. That refusal is continued, for other laws, in [[christendom/exclusion]]. Here the point is only that a manorial survey is not a biography.

:::example From Hastings to the survey {#ex-span}
Hastings is dated 14 October 1066. Domesday is dated 1086. The atlas treats the difference of two CE year-numbers as the later minus the earlier. How many years is that span? Do not convert it into a number of days. The survey's day is not locked.
:::solution
Subtract the year-numbers. 1086 minus 1066 is 20. The survey comes twenty years after the battle. The twenty years are a gap between a conquest and a record of the conquered kingdom. They are not a claim that the years in between were empty, and they are not a span across Europe. Because no day is locked for the survey, a count of days would pretend to a precision the chronology does not give.
:::
:::

:::example Two invented mixes {#ex-rents}
The following manors are invented, and so are the rents. They are not entries in Domesday, and they are not typical of anywhere.

Invented manor of Westfield. A holding owes three days of week-work, four shillings a year, and suit of the lord's court.

Invented manor of Eastmere. A holding of the same invented size owes no week-work, eighteen shillings a year, a payment when a daughter marries, and suit of the lord's court.

How many more shillings a year does Eastmere owe in money? Why does that difference not tell you which holding is more unfree?
:::solution
Eighteen minus four is fourteen. Eastmere owes fourteen shillings a year more in money. The difference does not measure unfreedom. Westfield owes three days of labour each week, which Eastmere does not. This atlas has not locked a wage that would turn a day into shillings, so the days and the coin must not be added into one burden. Both invented holdings answer in a lord's court. One also pays when a daughter marries. The mixes differ. [[#thm-not-one]] is the claim those mixes illustrate. They illustrate it only as a worked logic. An invented rent is not a source.
:::
:::

:::theorem A mix is not a diagram {#thm-mix}
You cannot read a manor's mix of labour and money off a general diagram of feudal society. The invented manors of Westfield and Eastmere are not evidence that any real village matched those figures. They show why a diagram that states one mix has already left the records.
:::
:::proof
Westfield and Eastmere, both labelled invented in [[#ex-rents]], share a court and differ in labour and coin. No staircase predicts which mix you will meet. Real custumals add further dues the pair leaves out on purpose: harvest boons, payments at death, suit of mill. The omissions keep the comparison readable. They also show that even a careful invented pair is not exhaustive.

What would count against the claim is a law, in force on the estates historians can actually study, that fixed one mix of days and coin everywhere the diagram is supposed to apply. A textbook sentence that repeats the diagram is not that law. If Domesday, or a run of continental custumals, stated one and the same mix, the invented contrast would be a trick and the claim would fail. The survey's several categories point the other way. The invented numbers themselves prove nothing about 1086. Only a record can do that.
:::

## What the twenty years are not

It is tempting to treat 1086 as the moment when England became a completed feudal order and to export that moment. The temptation fails twice. Lordship and dependent cultivation are older than Hastings. The survey records a kingdom that already had estates, dues and courts; the conquest changes holders and intensifies a particular kind of record. A date in one kingdom is not a European birthday. On the usual date of 910, Cluny is already a monastic landlord. From 910 to 1066 is 156 years, and from 910 to 1086 is 176. A house of the tenth century sits inside the history of rent. Nothing about either gap makes 1066 the start of lordship as such.

The conquest does change English lordship at the top. Tenants-in-chief hold of the king. Mesne lords hold of them. That hierarchy is real, and it is still not the pyramid of the cartoon, because the people on the land are not one legal residue at the bottom. A hierarchy of tenure among the free and the powerful can coexist with several kinds of peasant obligation. Collapsing both into one drawing is how a true account of Norman tenure becomes a false account of European serfdom.

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-sentence}
The following sentence is invented. "In the year of Hastings every peasant in Europe owed three days' labour each week to a knight."

What would it take to promote this sentence from an illustration into evidence? Which locked source is the wrong document to cite for it?
:::solution
Promotion would require a record that spoke of Europe rather than one kingdom, that dated the obligation to 1066 rather than using Hastings as a moral decoration, and that actually surveyed peasants' services rather than a ruler's claims. The sentence would also have to survive contact with a second manor whose mix was not three days. Domesday is the wrong document to cite. It is a survey of the English kingdom in 1086, twenty years after the battle, made for a conqueror. It does not say the sentence, and it could not say the sentence even if every English entry had matched the invented manor of Westfield. Sicily, Scotland and the empire are outside it.
:::
:::

:::example Battle beside survey {#ex-beside}
Set 14 October 1066 beside 1086. Name one thing the pair supports and two things it does not support.
:::solution
The pair supports a political sequence inside one kingdom: a conquest, then, twenty years later, a survey for the conqueror's regime. It does not support a racial replacement of the people who worked the land, and it does not support any sentence about Europe as a whole. It also does not support a claim about what peasants believed. Belief was not the question the king's commissioners were sent to ask. The two dates are different kinds of event. A window placed over both of them does not fuse them into one fact.
:::
:::

:::warning The pyramid and the identical peasant
The usual myth is that medieval Europe was a feudal pyramid of identical peasants, each owing the same labour to a knight. The myth fails because a manor is a bundle of rights, not a standard farm, and because the bundles differ. Domesday, the record people sometimes wave at the myth, is a survey of one conquered kingdom. It is not a continental law. Drawing the pyramid as if it were a statute does not make it one.
:::

There is a further misuse of the same picture. Once the peasant is identical, any later revolt can be told as "the peasants" waking up, and any earlier document can be told as the system already complete. The English Rising of 1381, which belongs to [[crises/war-and-schism]], was not only peasants. The path from a manor to that rising is not a single system expressing itself. Rents, labour and courts remain the right objects. They are not a script.

## A worked case

Take the invented pair in [[#ex-rents]] and watch a false sentence grow. A writer looks at Westfield, sees three days, and writes "the medieval peasant owed three days a week". Eastmere, still invented, already falsifies the sentence inside the thought experiment: a holding can be under a lord, answer in his court, and owe no week-work. The writer then widens the sentence to "Europe's peasants owed three days a week". That second step would fail even if every real English custumal had matched Westfield. Domesday is not Europe. A true description of one kingdom's survey is still a false description of a continent. This is the same scale error [[sources/scale]] isolates, worked here with rents rather than with a village anecdote.

We did not convert three days into coin, and we did not call fourteen shillings heavy or light. No price is locked. Both manors are invented, so the failure of the general sentence is visible before any archive is asked to confirm it. Confirmation would mean reading custumals and saying which dues are actually in the text. The claim is that those texts are allowed to differ. It is not a substitute for them.

A real holding would also force a choice the invented Eastmere leaves open: who owes the days, and whether a marriage payment falls only on an unfree tenant's daughter. A rent without the rule that says who pays it is not yet a status. The cartoon skips that rule, and the rule is where variety lives.

:::quiz
Eastmere owes more shillings than Westfield and owes no week-work. Both manors are invented. What follows?
- [ ] Eastmere is more unfree, because the coin is larger.
- [ ] Westfield is not a manor, because a manor must be paid only in coin.
- [x] Labour and money can be mixed differently, so one rent does not define serfdom.
- [ ] The figures are precise, so they describe Europe.
:::solution
Precision does not turn an invented illustration into a continental fact. The larger coin is one part of a mix. The missing week-work is another part. [[#def-unfreedom]] allows both mixes. [[#thm-mix]] says a diagram cannot choose between them in advance. Calling Westfield the real pattern and Eastmere a curiosity is how the identical peasant gets back in.
:::
:::

:::history A conquest and its survey
On 14 October 1066 a Norman duke, William I, takes the English kingdom by battle and then by settlement. The Domesday survey of 1086 records that kingdom for its ruler. Conquest here is a political fact, not a racial one, and the survey is not a picture of Europe.
:::

## Where this leads

[[christendom/popes-and-kings]] moves from a lord's court to a fight over office between popes and kings. The dues here do not cause that fight, and the fight does not abolish the dues. [[christendom/crusades]] is the wars preached in the same Latin Christian world, and [[christendom/exclusion]] is where a later council marks Jews and Muslims by dress. How a local fact becomes a false larger sentence is [[sources/scale]]. What a survey's silence does and does not prove is [[sources/evidence]]. The English Rising of 1381, in [[crises/war-and-schism]], was not only peasants. [[crises/plague]] is the later disaster this chapter has refused to number in advance.

:::summary
- A manor is a unit of lordship and rent, not a standard farm and not a village personality.
- Unfreedom is a bundle of labour, restriction, payments and courts, and the bundle varies.
- The feudal pyramid is a diagram. It is not a law from Scotland to Sicily.
- Domesday in 1086 surveys the English kingdom after Hastings in 1066. It does not survey Europe.
- An invented rent can show a logic. It becomes evidence only when a record says it.
- Days and shillings must not be added together unless a rate is actually known. This atlas locks none.
- A silence in a survey is evidence about who was asked, not evidence that a person had no mind.
:::

:::exercise Promoting a sentence {level=2}
The invented sentence in [[#ex-sentence]] says that every peasant in Europe owed three days to a knight in the year of Hastings. List three distinct things a source would have to be, or to say, before the sentence could count as evidence. Then say why a matching entry for one English manor would still be insufficient.

The source would have to concern Europe rather than one kingdom, address 1066 rather than a later survey, and record labour services of peasants rather than a ruler's military success. One English manor that really did owe three days would support a local sentence about that manor. It would not support "every peasant", and it would not support "Europe". :::exercise Crossed lines {level=2}
A holding is rented from a monastery. A different lord claims the court in which the tenant must answer. Why does this situation, which is the sort lordship often produces, not fit a feudal pyramid drawn as a law?
:::solution
A pyramid gives each person one place under one superior. Here the rent and the court have come apart, so two lines arrive at the same holding. The monastery is a lord and need not be a knight; Cluny's kind of landlord, from 910, is already a reminder that lords are holders of rights. The tenant's unfreedom, if the court restrains him, is not identical with the labour he may or may not owe the monastery. [[#thm-mix]] says no diagram chooses the mix in advance. Crossed lines are what we should expect once rent and jurisdiction are allowed to be separate rights.
:::

:::exercise What would defeat the claim {level=3}
[[#thm-not-one]] says serfdom was not one European system. What single kind of evidence would count against that claim? Why would a well-drawn pyramid in a modern textbook not be enough, and why would a resemblance among three real custumals not be enough either?
:::hint
Look for a rule that fixes the mix, not for a picture that asserts it. Ask what "one system" would have to determine: days, payments, movement, and the court.
:::
:::solution
The defeating evidence would be a common rule, written and applied from Scotland to Sicily, fixing one set of services and one status-name for the rural population. A textbook pyramid is a modern diagram. It is not a medieval law, so it cannot defeat a claim about what the laws and customs were. Three custumals that resemble each other show a cluster: rent, restriction, a court. [[#thm-not-one]] already grants clusters. It fails only if the mix itself is fixed and shared, so that comparison no longer finds different days, different coin and different courts. Resemblance is the start of the inquiry. A code would be the end of it.
:::
