What were popes and kings fighting over? They were fighting over office. Who appointed a bishop, who handed him the signs of spiritual office, who bestowed the lands and jurisdiction that came with the see, and which court could judge a king: those are the questions. They were not fighting a modern war between religion and the state, and they were not building democracy by accident. The instruments on the line are different from one another. Reading them as one constitution is the mistake this chapter exists to stop.

Gregory VII becomes pope in 1073. In January 1077 Henry IV does penance at Canossa. The Concordat of Worms is 1122. Magna Carta is a peace of June 1215 between a king and his barons, reissued in 1225. Simon de Montfort's parliament of 1265 sits in a civil war. Edward I's parliament of 1295 is later called the Model Parliament. The bull Unam sanctam is 1302. In 1309 Clement V begins the long papal residence at Avignon. A penance, a concordat, a baronial charter, a wartime assembly, a royal assembly, a bull and a change of residence do not become one story because they can be placed on one line.

## Appointment, not a personal quarrel

The reform that carries Gregory's name had a programme, and the programme was about how offices were filled and what the holders might do with church property. Simony, the purchase of office, was attacked because a bought office was not a free election and not a spiritual act. Clerical marriage was attacked because a married priest might treat church land as a family's inheritance. Lay investiture was attacked because a king who handed a bishop the ring and the staff was conferring the signs of spiritual office. Libertas ecclesiae, the freedom of the Church, meant freedom from that lay control. It did not mean a modern liberty of worship for everyone inside the kingdom. Jews, Muslims and people the Church called heretics were not the beneficiaries of this freedom. Their legal position is the subject of [[christendom/exclusion]].

Kings did not hear this programme as a private scruple. A bishop was a lord, in the sense [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] gives that word: a holder of lands, dues and courts. Whoever invested him shaped the political map. A king who supervised appointments claimed to be guarding the realm and, often, to be a protector of the Church rather than its enemy. A pope who forbade that supervision claimed a jurisdiction that reached the king. Both sentences are claims about office. Neither is a claim that "the state" had been invented and then defeated.

Earlier imperial coronations show that pope and ruler were already paired. On 25 December 800 Leo III crowns Charlemagne emperor in Rome. The claim is imperial, and it is contested from Constantinople, as [[after-rome/carolingians]] sets out. In 962 Otto I is crowned emperor. That is another coronation, not the same ceremony continued. From 962 to the penance at Canossa in 1077 is 115 years. Crowning a ruler and investing a bishop are different acts. The first makes an imperial claim. The second fills an ecclesiastical office. The investiture conflict is about the second, conducted by men who still lived with the memory of the first.

In 1054 mutual excommunications involving Humbert and Michael Cerularius mark a longer schism between the Latin and Greek churches. The year is a marker, not the whole story. The popes in this chapter are Latin popes. Their quarrels with kings do not describe the Greek church, and they do not make Constantinople a department of the Latin reform. The state that continued in the east is [[after-rome/byzantium]].

:::definition Investiture {#def-investiture}
**Investiture**, in this conflict, is the handing over of the signs of an ecclesiastical office, especially by a lay ruler. The ring and the staff are the signs usually in dispute. Investiture is not a name for every quarrel between a pope and a king, and it is not a synonym for taxation, war or a personal humiliation.
:::

:::definition Office {#def-office}
An **office** here is a jurisdiction with a procedure for filling it and a claim about who may judge the holder. An office is not a personality, not a nation, and not the modern state. Two people can fight over an office without either of them intending to found a constitution.
:::

:::theorem Canossa does not end the conflict {#thm-canossa}
The conflict from Gregory VII's accession in 1073 to the Concordat of Worms in 1122 is a conflict over appointment and jurisdiction. Henry IV's penance at Canossa in January 1077 is a scene inside that conflict, not its end, and not a victory of the pope over the state.
:::
:::proof
The dates refuse a single climax. Gregory becomes pope in 1073. Four years later, in January 1077, the king does penance and is absolved. Opposition in the German kingdom does not stop: a rival claim to the kingship is put forward after the penance. The chronology does not lock a day for that rivalry, and this chapter will not invent one. What it will say is that the conflict outlives the meeting. In 1122 the Concordat of Worms still has something to settle.

On the usual reading of that concordat, the spiritual signs and the temporal rights are separated. The elected man receives ring and staff from the church side. He receives the regalia, the temporal possessions and secular duties, from the ruler, by a sceptre rather than by the pastoral staff. In the German kingdom the ruler keeps a presence at the election, and temporal investiture comes before consecration. In Italy and Burgundy the usual reading places temporal investiture after consecration. This is a paraphrase of the settlement's structure, not a quotation. The structure itself is the evidence. A king remains inside the making of a bishop. A pope has not abolished lay power. Appointment has been argued into a distinction, not conceded entire.

What would count against the claim is a source in which both sides treat January 1077 as the close, or a concordat that removes lay rulers from appointment altogether. Worms does not do that. A later retelling that dwells on snow and humiliation can be vivid and still be the wrong kind of explanation. Weather is not a constitution. Absolution is not the end of a quarrel about who fills an office.
:::

What is Canossa, on the dating this atlas locks?
- [ ] The battle in which the pope defeated the empire and ended lay politics.
- [ ] The signing of a constitution that separated church and state for good.
- [x] A penance by Henry IV in January 1077, inside a conflict that continued.
- [ ] The foundation of the papal residence at Avignon.
:::solution
Canossa is dated January 1077. Henry IV does penance. [[#thm-canossa]] says the conflict is not over, and the Concordat of Worms in 1122 is the later settlement about investiture. Avignon begins in 1309, under Clement V, and is a different fact. No locked source makes the penance a victory over "the state".

## From a penance to a concordat

The forty-five years from 1077 to 1122 are the simplest check on the myth. If Canossa had ended the conflict, Worms would be unnecessary, or it would be a mere minute of a victory already won. It is neither. It is a compromise about signs and timing, negotiated by a later pope and a later king, Callixtus II and Henry V. Naming them matters because the conflict is not a duel between two men in one January. Offices outlast their holders. Gregory's claim and Henry IV's penance do not expire as a personal story and then get replaced by an unrelated charter. The question of who invests a bishop is still the question.

There is a fair objection. Surely the reform succeeded in part. Bishops were no longer supposed to receive ring and staff from a king as if the king were the source of spiritual office. That change is real, and we grant it. Success at separating two signs is not the abolition of royal influence, and it is not the birth of a secular state standing opposite a spiritual one. The concordat keeps the ruler in the process, especially in Germany. A reader who wants a clean victory has to delete those clauses. What would count against our reading is a text of Worms that said the king would neither attend nor endow. The usual reading of the concordat says otherwise.

The same years are not the whole of papal politics. The reform papacy is Latin. The schism marked in 1054 is not healed by a German concordat. A settlement about investiture in the empire does not reorganise the Greek church, and it does not decide the crusades that [[christendom/crusades]] begins from Clermont in 1095. From Canossa in 1077 to Clermont in 1095 is eighteen years. A penance and a sermon are different instruments. One does not explain the other.

The figure omits dates the argument still needs. Gregory's accession in 1073 is not a dot. The reissue of Magna Carta in 1225 is not a dot. De Montfort's parliament in 1265 is not a dot. Move from 1077 to 1122 and the kind of act changes. Move to 1215 and the parties change: a king and his barons, not a pope and an emperor. Move to 1295 and the act is an assembly a king summoned. Move to 1302 and the act is a bull about papal supremacy. Nothing in that sequence writes a constitution by accumulation.

:::example From the penance to the concordat {#ex-worms}
Canossa is January 1077. The Concordat of Worms is 1122. How many years lie between the year-numbers? What does the gap show about a story in which the penance is the ending?
:::solution
1122 minus 1077 is 45. Forty-five years later there is still a settlement to make, and the settlement distinguishes spiritual signs from temporal rights while leaving the ruler a role. If the penance had been the ending, the gap would be a silence after victory. It is not. It is the distance between a scene and a compromise. The answer is a difference of year-numbers, not a claim that every year between them was occupied by the same argument.
:::
:::

:::example An invented verdict on Canossa {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. "After Canossa the pope ruled the kings of Europe, and lay appointment ceased."

What would promote it into evidence, and which locked dates count against it as it stands?
:::solution
Promotion would require a record in which lay investiture actually stops in January 1077 and in which kings across Europe, not one penitent king, accept papal rule as a settled jurisdiction. The sentence is not that record. Worms in 1122 counts against it, because a concordat that still gives the ruler the regalia is not the cessation of lay appointment. Unam sanctam in 1302 counts against it in a second way: a pope still feels the need to claim supremacy more than two centuries later, which is an odd form of victory already won. The invented sentence borrows a real penance and adds a result the penance did not have.
:::

## A baronial peace

Magna Carta belongs on this line only if we refuse the usual promotion. In June 1215 a king and his barons make a peace. King John faces armed barons. The document limits some of the ways a king can take money and misuse feudal incidents, confirms a liberty of the English church, and sets procedures, including judgement by peers and by the law of the land, for the men it covers. It provides a committee of barons as a means of enforcement. It is a political settlement inside a rebellion. It is not a constitution of the modern state, not a grant of votes to every adult, and not the birth of democracy or of liberty as a general status.

Most people in the kingdom were not parties. Villeins are largely outside the procedures that later readers love. Women are not given a new political personality. Jews appear, where they appear, as objects of clauses about debt, not as authors of the peace. A charter can be important in English political history and still be a baronial peace. Importance is not the same as origin-of-democracy.

The charter was reissued. The reissue this atlas locks is 1225, ten years after the first sealing. From June 1215 to 1225 is a difference of ten in the year-numbers. The reissue is still a royal charter extracted in politics. Later English law treats the 1225 text as the Magna Carta that gets confirmed. That legal afterlife is real. It does not change the parties of 1215 into a democratic people. Later centuries reinterpreted clauses as a story about liberty. The reinterpretation is a later use. It is not what the peace was.

Innocent III is the pope this atlas names with 1215. He sided against the baronial charter. The fact is a useful obstruction. A pope could take a king's side against barons. The year also contains, in November, the Fourth Lateran Council, under the same pope. June and November are not one programme. The council's rules about dress and category are [[christendom/exclusion]]. Putting the charter and the council in one sentimental "birth of the West" deletes both the baronial limit and the council's exclusions.

Simon de Montfort's parliament in 1265 is often recruited into a later story called the mother of parliaments. The chronology's own correction stands: it was a baronial parliament in a civil war. Representatives of communities were summoned, which is why later stories notice the assembly. A summons issued while a king is under baronial constraint is not a founding constitution, and it is not evidence that England had become a democracy. Edward I's parliament of 1295 is a different political fact. A king summons it, for a king's purposes, including the needs of war. Later writers call it the Model Parliament. The word "model" is theirs. From 1265 to 1295 is thirty years. Two assemblies, two situations, one later habit of treating both as steps on a road to modern representation. The habit is the thing to resist.

:::theorem These instruments are not a constitution of liberty {#thm-instruments}
Magna Carta in June 1215, its reissue in 1225, the parliaments of 1265 and 1295, and the bull Unam sanctam in 1302 are instruments of office and jurisdiction. They are not one constitution of liberty, and they are not the birth of democracy.
:::
:::proof
Each instrument does a job for particular people. The 1215 charter is a peace between a king and his barons. The 1225 reissue is still that kind of text. The 1265 assembly is baronial politics in a civil war. The 1295 assembly is a king's parliament, later nicknamed a model. Unam sanctam is a papal claim about supremacy, not an English statute. A constitution of liberty, if the phrase means a settlement that gave every adult a political standing, is not what any of these texts does.

What would count against the claim is a clause, in the 1215 charter or the 1225 reissue, that enfranchised every adult, or a bull in 1302 that founded a secular constitution rather than asserting papal jurisdiction. Neither is the document we have. A later reinterpretation can be sincere and still be evidence about the later readers, not about June 1215. The figure's caption is the same argument in miniature: the line does not make them a constitution.
:::

:::example Charter and reissue {#ex-reissue}
June 1215 and 1225 are both Magna Carta in some sense. How many years lie between the year-numbers, and why does the reissue not turn the first text into a modern constitution?
:::solution
1225 minus 1215 is 10. The reissue shows that the charter had to be made again, in a later political moment, which is what happens to a peace rather than to a self-executing constitution. The parties are still a king and the political community that can force a king, not the adult inhabitants. Ten years of confirmation do not add a democratic franchise that neither text states. [[#thm-instruments]] is the claim this arithmetic serves.
:::
:::

:::example Two assemblies {#ex-parliaments}
Set 1265 beside 1295. Both are later called parliaments in stories about representation. What is the same, and what must not be collapsed?
:::solution
Both are assemblies in the English kingdom, and both are later given flattering names: the mother of parliaments for the first, the Model Parliament for the second. The names are later. What must not be collapsed is the politics. 1265 is Simon de Montfort's parliament in a civil war. 1295 is Edward I's parliament. The year-difference is 1295 minus 1265, which is 30. Thirty years and a change of who summons the assembly are enough to block a sentence in which "parliament" grows smoothly into democracy. Neither assembly is Unam sanctam, and neither is Worms.
:::
:::

## A bull, then a residence

Unam sanctam, in 1302, is the papal claim at the other end of the period. Boniface VIII issues it in the course of a quarrel with Philip IV of France. In paraphrase, and not as a quotation, the bull claims that salvation depends on the Roman church, that subjection to the Roman pontiff is necessary, and that temporal power stands under spiritual power. The two-swords image in the bull is part of that claim: both powers are said to belong under the pope. This is a sentence about jurisdiction. It tells you what the pope claimed. It does not tell you that kings obeyed, and it does not found a state.

The bull did not settle the quarrel with the French king. This atlas has not locked a year for the violence that followed against the pope's person, so none is invented here. What is locked is the sequel in 1309. Clement V begins the long papal residence at Avignon. From 1302 to 1309 is seven years. A papal court resident in the orbit of the French kingdom is a strange monument to a bull that had just declared every human creature subject to the Roman pontiff. The residence is not the bull's victory. It is a different fact about where the office sat. This chapter does not date the end of the residence. The start is enough to stop 1302 being told as a final supremacy.

:::example Bull and residence {#ex-avignon}
Unam sanctam is 1302. The Avignon residence begins in 1309. How many years is the gap, and why is the second event not the triumph of the first?
:::solution
1309 minus 1302 is 7. Seven years after a bull about papal supremacy, Clement V's residence at Avignon begins. The residence shows the office moving, under a different pope, into a city in the French orbit. It does not show that Philip IV had accepted the bull, and it does not show that temporal power had been permanently subordinated. A claim in a bull and a place of residence are different instruments. The figure stops at the bull. The residence is the next locked fact, and it changes the picture.
:::
:::

:::warning Canossa and Magna Carta as origins
Two myths travel together. The first says Canossa was the pope's victory over the state. The penance was real, and the victory was not: Worms, forty-five years later, still bargains over appointment. The second says Magna Carta was the birth of liberty or of democracy. It was a peace between a king and his barons in June 1215, reissued in 1225, and later reinterpreted. A baronial charter is not a democracy. Calling it one praises a document by deleting most of the people it did not include.
:::

- [ ] The constitution that gave every adult in England a vote.
- [ ] The bull in which Boniface VIII subordinated kings to the pope.
- [x] A peace of June 1215 between a king and his barons, later reissued and reinterpreted.
- [ ] The concordat that ended lay investiture everywhere in 1122.
:::solution
June 1215 is the baronial peace. The reissue of 1225 keeps it a royal charter in politics. Unam sanctam is 1302, and Worms is 1122. Neither is Magna Carta. [[#thm-instruments]] refuses the democratic birthday. The people omitted from the peace, including most rural tenants, are the reason a flattering origin story fails.
:::

## Where this leads

[[christendom/crusades]] takes up a papal sermon, at Clermont in November 1095, that is not an investiture concordat and not a baronial charter. [[christendom/exclusion]] stays with November 1215, when the Fourth Lateran Council legislates categories, in the same year as the June peace and not as the same act. [[christendom/towns-and-learning]] is the other kind of privilege, urban and academic, which is also not a constitution of a middle class. The discipline of a claim, a cause and a label is [[sources/argument]]. A label such as "the birth of the state" works the way [[sources/argument]] says a label works: it hides the sentence it would have to prove. The English assemblies of 1265 and 1295 are not yet the parliaments of later centuries. [[crises/war-and-schism]] is where authority comes under a different strain, including the Western Schism that begins in 1378.

:::summary
- The fight was about office: appointment, the signs of spiritual authority, temporal rights, and jurisdiction over kings.
- Canossa in January 1077 is a penance. It is not the end of the conflict and not a victory over the state.
- The Concordat of Worms in 1122 distinguishes ring and staff from the regalia and still leaves rulers a role.
- Magna Carta in June 1215 is a baronial peace, reissued in 1225. It is not the birth of democracy or of liberty.
- The parliament of 1265 is a civil-war assembly. The parliament of 1295 is a king's assembly. Both nicknames are later.
- Unam sanctam in 1302 claims papal supremacy. Avignon in 1309 is a residence, not that claim's triumph.
- A pope could side with a king, as Innocent III did against the baronial charter. "Church versus state" is too coarse.
:::
