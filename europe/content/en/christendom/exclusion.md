How did Latin Christian law mark people as outside? In November 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council, under Innocent III, legislates for a society it treats as Christian. Canon 68 requires Jews and Muslims in Latin Christendom to be marked by distinctive dress. In 1231 Gregory IX authorises the papal Inquisition, a procedure against heresy, in the bull often cited as Excommunicamus. A dress rule and a heresy procedure are sixteen years apart. 1231 minus 1215 is 16. They are not the same law. They are both laws about categories.

A category tells an officer whom to mark, whom to inquire about, and who counts as the ordinary Christian on the other side of the line. It does not tell you that every town obeyed, and it does not tell you what any one person believed. The Spanish Inquisition is a different institution, authorised by Sixtus IV on 1 November 1478, and it belongs to [[crises/year-1492]]. Women's legal position varies by status, marriage, town and vow. This chapter will not summarise "the medieval woman". Most lives are not these laws. A history written only about knights, or only about cathedrals, has already edited them out.

## A decree is a command, not a street

Canon 68 is evidence of a legal will. In paraphrase, and not as a quotation, the council requires a distinction of dress so that Jews and Muslims can be told apart from Christians in Latin Christian lands. The chronology's own wording is enough: they are to be marked by dress. The council does not, by that command, issue one uniform to every street. Later local rules specified badges, colours or garments, and the specifications differed. A single picture of one badge for all of Europe is a later simplification. This chapter will not invent a year for any one kingdom's version of the mark, and it will not treat a local rule as if the canon had photographed it.

The people named are not a legal fiction and not only a legal fiction. Jewish communities in Latin Christendom had their own worship, law and learning. Muslim communities lived under Christian kings in some conquered lands, including parts of Iberia and Sicily, while in al-Andalus Muslims were not a marked minority inside an Islamic polity. [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]] is that other map. Canon 68 imagines Jews and Muslims inside Latin jurisdictions. It does not describe Muslim rulers, and it does not exhaust the communities it marks. To write the history only as the mark is to repeat the council's reduction.

Enforcement is a further question, and it is not settled by the canon's existence. Some rulers and towns were keener than others. Some communities negotiated, paid, or lived for a time under a local custom that did not match the decree. A command that has to be issued is evidence that the mark was not already everyone's habit. It is not evidence that the command was carried out everywhere. Both mistakes are common. One pretends the canon was unnecessary because practice was already uniform. The other pretends practice was uniform because the canon existed. [[#thm-decree]] below refuses both, and the invented town in the worked example shows why one note cannot replace the other kind of evidence.

Las Navas de Tolosa, in 1212, is three years before the council. 1215 minus 1212 is 3. A battle in Iberia and a dress regulation are different acts. The battle does not create the canon, and the canon does not describe the battle. [[christendom/crusades]] has already refused the legend in which one battle saves Europe. Exclusion is not that legend continued by other means. It is law about people living under Latin Christian authority.

:::definition Category {#def-category}
A **category** here is a legal or theological label authorities can apply: Jew, Muslim, heretic, and the ordinary Christian the label implies by contrast. A category is not a census of belief, not a description of a household, and not a proof that every person so labelled matched the definition the law used.
:::

:::definition Papal Inquisition {#def-inquisition}
The **papal Inquisition**, as this atlas locks it in 1231, is a procedure against heresy authorised by Gregory IX, in the bull often cited as Excommunicamus, with men commissioned to inquire. It is not a technique of questioning, which this chapter does not describe. It is not the Spanish Inquisition authorised on 1 November 1478.
:::

Bishops had pursued heresy before a papal procedure existed. The bull of 1231 does not invent disagreement, and it does not invent the episcopal court. It makes inquiry a papal instrument, with commissioners, often friars, appointed for the work. That is a change in office, of the kind [[christendom/popes-and-kings]] studies in other fights: who holds the jurisdiction, and under what authority. The change is not a description of how questions were put. This chapter does not describe interrogation. It does not describe an execution. The procedure could end in penance or in a person being handed to a secular court. Those are outcomes of a legal process. They are not a manual.

:::theorem Categories are made usable {#thm-usable}
Canon 68 and the papal procedure of 1231 are acts that make categories usable in law. They do not prove that every community was marked in practice, and they do not prove that heresy was simply found rather than built as a charge. The claim does not say that dissent was imaginary.
:::
:::proof
R. I. Moore has argued that heresy became a category authorities could prosecute, built in these centuries rather than simply discovered as a ready-made enemy. This chapter presents that as an argument, not as a fact that ends debate. What supports the argument, on the dates locked here, is the work of definition itself. A council in November 1215 commands a mark for Jews and Muslims. A bull in 1231 commissions inquiry into heresy. Sixteen years apart, both are laws about categories. Authorities who must define a mark, or commission an inquiry, are doing something. Finding a fully formed enemy already present in every town would not need so much law.

What would count against Moore's argument, stated fairly, is evidence that the councils and bulls only copied a category already stable in every town's practice, with no work of definition left. A decree that has to command a distinction is poor evidence for that stability. It remains possible that dissenting preachers and communities existed, and this chapter grants the possibility. Waldensian preaching and poverty are a relatively clear case of a movement authorities came to treat as heretical. Historians disagree about whether the people called Cathars formed one dualist church. The disagreement matters. A constructed charge can still be aimed at real critics of the clergy, and a real critic is not the same thing as a unified counter-church. Both sentences can be true. The lesson's claim is the narrower one. Law made a category usable. Usability is not a transcript of every accused person's mind.

What would count against [[#thm-usable]] itself is a survey, comparable to a census, showing either that no legal definition was added in these years or that every town already practised one uniform mark and one uniform charge. This atlas has no such survey. Crusading war was also turned against people inside Latin Christendom whom the Church called heretics. This atlas has not locked a year for those campaigns, so none is invented. The locked instrument remains the procedure of 1231.
:::

## Sixteen years, two laws

:::widget timeline
start: 1200
end: 1250
window: 5
events: 1215,Fourth Lateran; 1231,Papal Inquisition
caption: Move the year. A dress regulation and a procedure against heresy are sixteen years apart. Both are laws about categories.
:::

The figure's sixteen years are 1231 minus 1215. The window is five years, so the two dots are not adjacent even on a short scale. A reader who remembers only "the Church persecuted outsiders" has already fused them. Dress is a rule about Jews and Muslims who are identified as such, not about Christians accused of doctrinal error. Heresy is a charge against people the Church claims as its own and then places outside orthodoxy. The categories differ. The powers differ too: a council legislating for Latin Christendom, and a pope commissioning a procedure. Fusing them makes every Jew into a heretic and every heresy charge into a dress code. Neither fusion fits the texts.

June 1215 is still Magna Carta, a peace between a king and his barons, as [[christendom/popes-and-kings]] argues. November 1215 is this council. The same year holds a baronial charter and a general council. Innocent III is the pope this atlas names with both, and he sided against the charter while presiding over the council. One year, two instruments, and a pope who is not on one side of a simple Church-against-king story. Exclusion is not added after the political history. It is in the same months.

:::example Sixteen years {#ex-sixteen}
How many years lie between November 1215 and the papal procedure of 1231? What fusion does that gap forbid?
:::solution
1231 minus 1215 is 16. The figure states the same gap in words. Canon 68 is a dress rule for Jews and Muslims. Excommunicamus is a procedure against heresy. Sixteen years, and the difference of subject, forbid a sentence in which "persecution" is one act with one target. The gap is a difference of year-numbers. It is not a claim that the years between were empty of law, and it is not a death total.
:::
:::

:::example The same year, two instruments {#ex-same-year}
Set June 1215 beside November 1215. Name each instrument and say what goes wrong if a history keeps only the first.
:::solution
June is Magna Carta, a peace between a king and his barons, not a modern constitution. November is the Fourth Lateran Council, including canon 68. A history that keeps only the charter can tell a story about barons limiting a king and never mention a dress rule or a Christian society being legislated in the same year. That edit is how exclusion becomes a stain added later, instead of part of the year's law. Innocent III connects the months without making them one programme: he sided against the charter. The council's other pastoral rules are not the charter's clauses either.
:::
:::

:::theorem A decree is not a town {#thm-decree}
A decree about dress is evidence of a legal will to mark Jews and Muslims. It is not evidence that every Jewish or Muslim community was marked in practice. Local practice is a further question, and it is not answered by repeating the canon.
:::
:::proof
The canon's text, as a command, shows what the council required. Obedience is a second fact, and the sources differ by town and decade. This chapter will not invent a compliance rate. What would count against the claim is a survey, after November 1215, showing one distinctive dress actually worn in every Latin town. No such survey is locked here. An anecdote of obedience in one town would not be that survey. An anecdote of neglect would not abolish the canon.

The invented case below is a limit on both mistakes. It is not evidence about any real place. It shows the logical gap: a decree and a local observation are different kinds of trace, and neither promotes itself into the other. [[sources/evidence]] is the general rule. A silence in a town's records is not proof that nobody was marked, and it is not proof that the canon was read aloud and obeyed.
:::

## Built, and also not the whole of a life

The counter-argument to a purely "constructed" story deserves its own space, because Moore's argument is easy to over-read. People criticised clergy. People formed followings. Some beliefs really did contradict what councils called orthodoxy. If "built" meant "invented out of nothing", the argument would be false, and this chapter does not use it that way. Built means made usable: named, listed, assigned to a court or a commissioner, attached to a penalty. A movement can be real and still be reshaped by the charge. Historians disagree, in particular, about the unity of those called Cathars. Presenting the disagreement is part of the argument, not a footnote that saves a neater sentence.

Women's legal position cuts across every category in this chapter. A noble heiress, a villein widow, a nun, a townswoman and a Jewish woman do not share one legal personality. Status, marital property, town custom and religious law divide them. Canon 68, where it was applied, marked Jewish and Muslim women as members of a marked community, not as "woman" in the abstract. A heresy charge could touch women as believers and as heads of households. That possibility is not a biography, and it is not a licence to describe questioning. Most women's lives are not canon 68 and not an inquisitorial file. The silence of those lives in these particular laws is evidence about what the laws were for, not evidence that the women had no minds. [[sources/evidence]] says a silence is evidence about who was asked.

"The medieval woman" fails for the same structural reason "the serf" fails in [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] and "the middle class" fails in [[christendom/towns-and-learning]]. It offers one subject so that a law, a market and a household can be treated as illustrations of her. They are not illustrations. They are different scales. A dress rule is a category. A heresy procedure is a category. A widow's control of a workshop is a local economic fact. None of the three is the truth of the others.

Knights and cathedrals are the edit this course has been refusing. A cathedral is a church and often a lord. A knight is an armed member of a small elite. Both are inside Latin Christendom. Neither is the content of the centuries. A history that keeps only them can admire stone and honour and never read canon 68, never meet the procedure of 1231, and never notice that most people were peasants, townsmen, women, Jews or Muslims under somebody's law. The edit is a choice. It is not what the record contains.

:::example Two inquisitions {#ex-two}
The papal procedure is 1231. Sixtus IV authorises the Spanish Inquisition on 1 November 1478. How many years lie between the year-numbers, and why is the second not a continuation under the same name only?
:::solution
1478 minus 1231 is 247. Two hundred and forty-seven years is not a continuity of one office. The first is a papal procedure against heresy inside the thirteenth-century Latin church. The second is an institution authorised for the Spanish crowns in 1478, taught in [[crises/year-1492]], not here. Sharing the word inquisition is how the fusion happens. [[#def-inquisition]] separates them. The gap is not a claim about how either procedure questioned people. This chapter does not describe that.
:::
:::

:::example An invented note {#ex-note}
The following note is invented. "In the invented town of Millbridge, in the year after the council, a traveller saw no distinctive dress on any Jewish or Muslim resident."

What can the note not do, even as a logic, and what would a real record of the same shape still not do to canon 68?
:::solution
Millbridge is invented, so the note proves nothing about 1215 or about any town. As a logic it shows a gap: a local observation, if genuine, would be evidence about one place, not a repeal of a council. A real record of that shape would still not erase canon 68. The canon would remain a command. The record would remain one town's trace, and only if it were authentic, dated and actually about dress. One town cannot demonstrate that every town ignored the canon, just as the canon cannot demonstrate that Millbridge obeyed it. [[#thm-decree]] is that separation. Promoting the note would require a real document. Using the canon as a photograph of Millbridge would require evidence the command was carried out there.
:::
:::

The later expulsions and conversions are not this canon grown up. On 2 January 1492 Granada surrenders. On 31 March 1492 the Alhambra Decree orders the Jews of Castile and Aragon to convert or leave, with a deadline of 31 July. Those are several events inside one year, and [[crises/year-1492]] refuses to collapse them into a birth of modern Europe. A dress rule of November 1215 and an expulsion order of 1492 are different acts. From 1215 to 1492 is 277 years. 1492 minus 1215 is 277. The distance is the point. Some kingdoms expelled Jewish communities in the years between. This atlas has not locked those years, so this chapter does not invent them, including for England. Absence of a locked year is not absence of the communities. It is a limit on what this paragraph may date.

Jan Hus is burned in 1415, during the Council of Constance, inside the crisis of authority taught in [[crises/war-and-schism]]. From 1231 to 1415 is 184 years. 1415 minus 1231 is 184. A burning at Constance is not the bull of Gregory IX, and it is not canon 68. The comparison stops a habit of treating every later prosecution as 1231 under another name. The Western Schism and the council that ends it are not this chapter's story. The year is enough to keep the distance.

:::warning One badge, one inquisition, one woman
Three myths tidy the same evidence. The first says that because canon 68 required a mark, every Jewish and Muslim community wore it. A decree is not a street. The second says that the papal procedure of 1231 is the Spanish Inquisition. The authorisation in 1478 is 247 years later and a different institution. The third says there was a medieval woman whose legal position can be summarised. Status, marriage, town and vow divide that figure until it is not a subject. This chapter also does not describe interrogation. A procedure is not a technique.
:::

:::quiz
Which pairing is historically one institution?
- [ ] Gregory IX's procedure of 1231 and the Spanish Inquisition authorised in 1478.
- [ ] Canon 68 and the battle of Las Navas in 1212.
- [x] Neither pairing. A dress rule, a battle, a papal procedure and a later Spanish institution are different acts.
- [ ] Magna Carta in June 1215 and canon 68, because they share a year.
:::solution
Sharing a year does not fuse June's baronial peace with November's council. Sharing a word does not fuse 1231 with 1478. 1215 minus 1212 is 3, and a battle is still not a dress code. [[#def-inquisition]] and [[#thm-decree]] are the separations. The right answer is that none of these pairings is one institution or one act.
:::

## Where this leads

[[crises/year-1492]] holds Granada, the Alhambra Decree and the Spanish Inquisition's world. They are not canon 68. [[crises/war-and-schism]] holds the burning of Jan Hus in 1415 and the schism that begins in 1378, a different crisis of authority from Gregory IX's procedure. [[christendom/crusades]] is the violence of expeditions, including attacks on Rhineland Jews during the First Crusade, which is not the same act as a dress regulation. [[christendom/towns-and-learning]] is where corporations and routes sit in the same centuries without being a middle class. [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] is the variety of unfreedom a knightly history also edits out. [[sources/evidence]] is how to read a file, a canon and a silence without promoting any of them into a mind it did not record.

:::summary
- In November 1215 canon 68 requires distinctive dress for Jews and Muslims in Latin Christendom. A decree is not every town's practice.
- In 1231 Gregory IX authorises the papal Inquisition, a procedure against heresy. It is not the Spanish Inquisition of 1 November 1478. The gap is 247 years.
- The two laws are sixteen years apart and concern different categories. Dress is not a heresy charge.
- Moore's argument, presented as an argument, is that heresy was made usable as a charge, not simply found. Dissent can still be real. Historians disagree about the unity of those called Cathars.
- This chapter does not describe interrogation or an execution. A procedure is not a technique.
- Women's legal position varies. There is no one medieval woman. Most lives are not these laws.
- June 1215 and November 1215 are the same year and not the same instrument. Exclusion is not a stain added after the political story.
- Later expulsions, including 1492, and the burning of Hus in 1415, are different acts at different distances. Years this atlas has not locked are not invented to fill the gaps.
