Whom did crusading violence strike? A one-word answer is already a distortion. In November 1095 Urban II at Clermont preaches the expedition later called the First Crusade. On 15 July 1099 that expedition takes Jerusalem. During the First Crusade, Jewish communities in the Rhineland are attacked. In 1187 Saladin wins at Hattin and takes Jerusalem. On 13 April 1204 the Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople, a Christian city. In 1212 Las Navas de Tolosa is fought in Iberia. In 1291 Acre falls, and the crusader principalities on the mainland end. Muslims in the Levant, Jews in the Rhineland, Greek Christians in Constantinople and Muslim forces in Iberia are not one target in different clothes.

The expeditions were preached as penance and fought as war. The religious framing is real. It does not choose the victims. This chapter will not give an army size or a death total. The chronology has locked none, and a number invented for effect would not tell you whom the violence struck. It would only make a sermon, a battle and a sack look like one measurable campaign.

## A sermon is not a transcript

Urban's preaching at Clermont is the usual start of the First Crusade. The accounts of what he said were written afterwards, and they do not match one another. This chapter will not invent a speech, and it will not treat a reported cry as a document. In paraphrase of the aim the accounts share, the expedition was preached as help to eastern Christians and as a journey to Jerusalem, with a spiritual privilege for those who took the vow. Historians disagree about the edges of the word crusade: how tightly it must be tied to a papal privilege, whether Iberian wars belong inside it, whether 1095 is a beginning or a name later stuck on a longer habit of holy war. The disagreement does not remove the Rhineland attacks or the sack of 1204 from the record of violence connected with these expeditions.

In 1098 the Cistercian start at Cîteaux marks a new monasticism beside the war, not a cause of the sermon and not a refuge from the fighting. From Clermont in 1095 to Cîteaux in 1098 is three years. A reformed monastery and a preached expedition can share a decade without sharing a motive. The landed monasticism of an earlier house, Cluny in 910, is a different story again, told in [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]].

The expedition took Jerusalem on 15 July 1099. People in the city were killed. Jews and Muslims were among those struck. This atlas does not state how many, and it does not describe the manner of the killing. The fact of killing is part of the taking. A penitential vow did not prevent it. Some people were spared or ransomed, so a sentence that says everyone died is a death total in disguise, and it is not used here. The sources disagree in detail. The disagreement is not a reason to omit the violence, and it is not a licence to invent a figure.

During the First Crusade, before the story can be told only at Jerusalem, Jewish communities in the Rhineland were attacked by people associated with the expedition. This atlas has not locked a year for those attacks, so none is invented. Some local bishops tried to shelter the communities. The shelter often failed. Some people were killed, and some were pressed towards baptism. That is the level of description the evidence here will bear. No death total, and no account of how the attacks were carried out. The attackers were not the enemy named in a sermon about Jerusalem. A motive stated at Clermont is not a list of the people struck on the road.

:::definition Crusade {#def-crusade}
A **crusade**, in this chapter, is an expedition preached by Latin Christian authority as a penitential war, with a privilege attached to the vow. The definition does not say that the violence had one target. It does not say that every war fought by a Christian king was a crusade. Historians disagree about borderline cases. The definition is a working limit, not a verdict on every campaign in the period.
:::

:::definition Target {#def-target}
A **target** here is a person or a community the violence struck. Targets are not identical with the enemy named in the preaching. A sermon can name Jerusalem while an armed band attacks a Rhineland community, and a later crusading army can sack a Christian city. Naming the target is a historical task. It is not settled by repeating the sermon.
:::

:::theorem The targets were plural {#thm-plural}
Crusading violence struck more than one kind of target. It was not only a defence of Latin Christendom against Muslims, and it was not only one crime with one victim. The claim covers battles, the taking of cities, and attacks on communities who were not the army's declared enemy. It does not flatten those kinds into a single moral type, and it states no death total.
:::
:::proof
The locked dates are the evidence, read for who is struck. On 15 July 1099 Jerusalem is taken, and people in the city, including Jews and Muslims, are killed. During the First Crusade, Rhineland Jews are attacked. In 1187 Saladin, a ruler with his own politics and not a symbol of "the East", defeats the Latin forces at Hattin and takes Jerusalem. On 13 April 1204 a crusading army sacks Constantinople. The victims include the Christian inhabitants of a Christian city. Enrico Dandolo is the Venetian doge associated with that expedition. In 1212 Las Navas de Tolosa is a battle in Iberia between Christian kings and an Almohad army. In 1291 Acre falls. The mainland principalities end. The list is not one war and not one enemy.

What would count against the claim is a record in which every expedition named the same enemy and no connected violence fell on Jews or on Greek Christians. The dates do not show that record. A sincere sermon would not refute the claim either. Sincerity is evidence about motive. Motive is not a victim list. What would also count against the claim is a demonstration that 1204 was not crusading violence in any usable sense, because the army had abandoned the vow so completely that the sack belongs only to Venetian and Byzantine politics. The reply is narrower than a definitional victory. The army was a crusade army. The preaching had not named Constantinople. The victims do not become unreal because the attackers had left an earlier aim. Historians who argue about the word still have the sack to explain.

A history that calls the crusades self-defence only has to delete 1099 as an offensive taking, delete the Rhineland, and delete 1204. A history that calls them a crime only has to delete the penitential vow, delete Hattin as a battle Saladin won, and treat Las Navas as if it were the same act as a massacre of non-combatants. Both deletions fail. Plurality is the argument. It is not an invitation to one indictment or one apology.
:::

:::quiz
Which conclusion follows from the dates 1099, the Rhineland attacks, 1204 and 1212?
- [ ] Crusading violence struck only Muslims who were attacking Europe.
- [ ] Crusading violence was only a crime against Greek Christians.
- [x] The people struck were not all of one kind, and no single enemy-word covers them.
- [ ] The attacks on Rhineland Jews have a locked year and a locked death total.
:::solution
Jerusalem in 1099, the Rhineland during the First Crusade, Constantinople on 13 April 1204 and Las Navas in 1212 do not share one target. The Rhineland attacks are not given a year or a death total in this atlas. [[#thm-plural]] is the claim. Self-defence only, and a crime only, each require deleting part of the list.
:::

## Jerusalem taken, Jerusalem lost

From the taking of Jerusalem on 15 July 1099 to Saladin's taking of the city in 1187 is eighty-eight years. 1187 minus 1099 is 88. Hattin and the loss of the city are the same year and not the same event: a battle, then a city. The eighty-eight years are not one siege with a moral ending. Latin principalities existed in between. This atlas has not locked the years of the expeditions that tried to recover Jerusalem after 1187, so those years are not invented here. Between the loss of the city and the fall of Acre in 1291, the chronology used in this chapter locks 1204, 1212 and 1291, not a continuous war.

Saladin is a political actor. He takes a city the First Crusade had taken. That symmetry of verbs is not a symmetry of justification, and it is not a reason to tell 1187 as the moment a spiritual project was judged. It is a change of who holds Jerusalem. Crusading does not stop because the city changes hands. Acre in 1291 is a later ending, of mainland principalities, not of the idea. From 1099 to 1291 is 192 years. The span is not one kingdom and not one war.

Move the year and refuse the single plot. Clermont is a sermon. 1099 is a city taken. 1187 is a battle and a city lost. The eighty-eight years in the caption are 1187 minus 1099, and they are a gap, not a mood. 1204 sits between the loss of Jerusalem and Las Navas, and it is not a chapter of either. 1212 is in Iberia. 1291 ends the mainland ports. Six dots, more than one kind of act. The window of eight years is small beside the gaps. That smallness is the point. Events that look adjacent in a summary are far apart on the line.

:::example Eighty-eight years {#ex-eighty}
Jerusalem is taken on 15 July 1099 and taken by Saladin in 1187. Using year-numbers, how long is the gap? What does the gap forbid you to say about "the crusade" as one war?
:::solution
1187 minus 1099 is 88. The caption's "eighty-eight years later" is that subtraction. The gap forbids a sentence in which the taking and the loss are one campaign with a single result. Hattin and the city's fall share 1187 with each other, not with 1099. The day in July 1099 has no twin day locked for 1187, so the eighty-eight is a difference of years, not a count of days. It is also not a death total and not an army size.
:::
:::

:::example Two cities {#ex-cities}
Set 15 July 1099 beside 13 April 1204. Both are violent entries into great cities. What is the same, and who is struck in the second case that a "war against Muslims" sentence cannot see?
:::solution
Both are dated acts of crusading violence against a city: Jerusalem taken, Constantinople sacked. The year-difference is 1204 minus 1099, which is 105. What is not the same is the city and the people. In 1099 the city is under Muslim rule and the people killed include Muslims and Jews. On 13 April 1204 the city is Christian, and the victims include Greek Christians. Enrico Dandolo's expedition is still a crusade army. A sentence that says the violence struck only Muslims cannot see them. The 105 years do not make the sack a sequel of the taking of Jerusalem. They make the distance visible.
:::
:::

## A Christian city

The Fourth Crusade is the clearest obstruction to a map with one enemy. The preaching had not named Constantinople. The army's leaders became entangled with Venetian interests and with a Byzantine succession dispute. This chapter does not date the steps of that entanglement, because those years are not locked here. It dates the sack: 13 April 1204. The eastern Roman state does not end on that day. A Latin regime is imposed on the city. Greek rule is not a footnote that 1204 erases. The state's end, on the dating used in this atlas, is the fall of Constantinople on 29 May 1453. That later date is not the meaning of the sack. [[after-rome/byzantium]] is the continuity the sack interrupted and did not finish.

Some readers try to save the word crusade by expelling 1204 from it. The expulsion is understandable and insufficient. If crusade means only violence against Muslims, then a crusade army's sack of a Christian city has to be called something else, and the something else will be whatever keeps the definition clean. [[#thm-plural]] refuses the cleanliness. The sack is crusading violence because of who did it and under what banner of vow and preaching, not because the victims matched the sermon. Venetian politics and a dynastic dispute explain how the army arrived. They do not explain the victims away.

There is a counter-argument worth keeping. Participants understood themselves as answering a papal call. They spent resources. They could understand death on the expedition as religiously meaningful. A history that says "a crime only" cannot hear that self-understanding, and so it fails as a description of the preaching. We grant the self-understanding. We do not let it edit the sack. What would count against the plural-targets claim is not a pious motive. It is evidence that Constantinople was not sacked, or was not sacked by this army. The date says otherwise.

:::example An invented defence {#ex-defence}
The following sentence is invented. "The crusaders fought only to defend Europe from Islam, and everyone they struck was an enemy at the gate."

Why can 1204, the Rhineland attacks and Las Navas not be promoted into support for this sentence?
:::solution
The sentence is invented, and promotion would require those three episodes to fit one defensive war against a single Muslim enemy at the edge of Europe. They do not. The Rhineland attacks, during the First Crusade, strike Jewish communities who were not an army at a gate, and this atlas will not invent a year or a death total to make them look like a battle. 1204 strikes a Christian city. Las Navas in 1212 is a battle in Iberia, not a defence of a continent at a single gate, and it does not finish Muslim political power in Iberia. The invented sentence uses "Europe" as a civilisation with one enemy. [[sources/naming-europe]] is why that use fails. The sentence also fails internally: defence and a gate do not describe an offensive taking of Jerusalem in 1099.
:::
:::

## Iberia is not a footnote, and Acre is not a mood

Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 is on the figure so that crusading is not only a Levantine story. Christian kings of the north fought an Almohad army. Crusading privileges and crusading language were available in Iberia. Historians disagree about how tightly the battle should be tied to a papal banner. On any reading used here it is a battle in Iberia, inside the same broad period as the expeditions to the east, eight years after the sack of Constantinople. 1212 minus 1204 is 8. Eight years do not make Las Navas a sequel of 1204. They put two different wars near each other so that neither can stand for both.

The battle did not save Europe. That legend, attached to an earlier fight in Gaul, is refused in [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]]. Las Navas is not a second copy of the legend. It also did not end Muslim rule in Iberia. Granada surrenders on 2 January 1492, a date that belongs to [[crises/year-1492]], not to a moral of 1212. Al-Andalus is a political history. A battle is one of its events, not its meaning.

Acre falls in 1291. The crusader principalities on the mainland end. From Clermont in 1095 to Acre is 196 years. 1291 minus 1095 is 196. Nothing about that span is one command, one kingdom or one outcome. Trade and pilgrimage do not become unthinkable because a port falls, which is why [[christendom/towns-and-learning]] can put Acre beside a theologian's death without making them one process. Later projects of crusade were preached. This atlas does not lock their years on this line, so they are not given a false date. The idea outlives the mainland principalities. The principalities do not outlive 1291.

:::example Not one span {#ex-span}
From Clermont in 1095 to the fall of Acre in 1291, how many years lie between the year-numbers? Name two locked events inside the span that stop it being one war, and say where each happened.
:::solution
1291 minus 1095 is 196. Inside the span, 13 April 1204 is the sack of Constantinople, a Christian city, and 1212 is Las Navas in Iberia. Either event is enough to break "one war in the Levant". 1187, Hattin and the loss of Jerusalem, breaks it again, from the other direction: the city taken in 1099 is not held for the whole span. The 196 years are a container for several wars. They are not a duration of a single campaign, and they are not a casualty figure.
:::
:::

:::warning Self-defence only, or a crime only
The usual pair of myths splits the same list in opposite directions. One myth says the crusades were Europe defending itself, and then has no room for the Rhineland, for an offensive taking in 1099, or for a Christian city sacked in 1204. The other says they were a single crime, and then has no room for a vow preached as penance, for Hattin as Saladin's victory, or for the difference between a battle and an attack on a community. Plural targets are the correction. They are not a licence to invent army sizes or death totals.
:::

A worked case keeps the kinds apart. Take four situations the dates actually support. A Muslim inhabitant of Jerusalem in July 1099 is inside a city that is taken, and may be killed, spared or ransomed: the sources vary, and we will not count. A Jewish household in the Rhineland during the First Crusade is attacked by people associated with the expedition, though the sermon had named a different goal. A Greek Christian in Constantinople on 13 April 1204 is in a city sacked by a crusade army. An Almohad soldier at Las Navas in 1212 is in a battle between armies, which is not the same act as the Rhineland attacks. "Enemies of Europe" covers none of this cleanly. "Muslims" misses the second and the third. "Victims of crusading violence" is usable only if the kinds stay distinct. [[#thm-plural]] is that usable sentence, with the limit written into it.

:::quiz
The fall of Acre in 1291 is best described as which of the following?
- [ ] The end of every idea of crusade, and the start of a Europe with no further such projects.
- [ ] Proof that the First Crusade had been a single war lasting from 1095.
- [x] The end of the crusader principalities on the mainland, not the end of one continuous war.
- [ ] The sack of Constantinople, which happened on the same day.
:::solution
The chronology says Acre falls in 1291 and the mainland principalities end. Constantinople was sacked on 13 April 1204, not in 1291. From 1095 to 1291 is 196 years and several kinds of event, which [[#ex-span]] calculates. Later projects existed. Their years are not invented here. An ending of principalities is not the death of an idea.
:::

:::history A sermon, a city, another city
In November 1095 Urban II preaches at Clermont the expedition later called the First Crusade, and Jerusalem is taken on 15 July 1099. On 13 April 1204 the Fourth Crusade, associated with Enrico Dandolo, sacks Constantinople. Crusading violence is not only violence against Muslims.
:::

## Where this leads

[[christendom/towns-and-learning]] puts the fall of Acre beside Lateran IV and the death of Aquinas, and refuses to make the three one process. [[christendom/exclusion]] is the legal marking of Jews and Muslims inside Latin Christendom, a different act from the Rhineland attacks and not a substitute for them. [[christendom/popes-and-kings]] is the quarrel over office that does not explain these targets. The eastern Roman state the Fourth Crusade sacked is [[after-rome/byzantium]]. The longer Islamic political history in Iberia and elsewhere is [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]]. Granada in 1492, not Las Navas, is the surrender that belongs to [[crises/year-1492]]. How a label such as "the crusades" hides several claims is the business of [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- Clermont in November 1095 preaches an expedition. Jerusalem is taken on 15 July 1099. The accounts of the sermon are later and do not match. No speech is invented here.
- During the First Crusade, Rhineland Jews are attacked. No year and no death total are locked for those attacks.
- In 1187 Hattin and Saladin's taking of Jerusalem are eighty-eight years after 1099. They are not one war with 1099.
- On 13 April 1204 a crusade army sacks Constantinople, a Christian city. The eastern Roman state does not end that day.
- Las Navas in 1212 is a battle in Iberia. It does not save Europe and does not end Muslim rule there.
- Acre falls in 1291. The mainland principalities end. The idea of crusade is not dated as dead.
- Self-defence only, and a crime only, each delete part of this list. Plural targets are the claim, without army sizes or death totals.
:::
