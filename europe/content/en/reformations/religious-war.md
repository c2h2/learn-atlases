What did Westphalia settle, and what have later readers claimed for it? On 24 October 1648 the treaties of Münster and Osnabrück adjusted the Peace of Augsburg and admitted Calvinists to the imperial religious peace. In January of the same year, at Münster, Spain recognised the United Provinces. Those are settlements: of an imperial constitution, of two public religions become three, and of a revolt's independence. Later readers claimed something else, a finished theory of modern sovereignty and a new state system. The treaties do not publish that theory. The wars that led to them were religious, and they were also fiscal, dynastic, and local. A peace can be real without being the birth of the modern world.

The French wars, usually bracketed 1562 to 1598, are the first place to practise the distinction. A massacre, a failed invasion, and a limited edict sit inside those decades. None of them is Westphalia. None of them is a death total this atlas will state.

## Brackets, a massacre, a limited edict

The usual brackets for the French wars of religion are 1562 to 1598. The difference of the year numbers is 36. Brackets are a label for a series, as the brackets of the Hundred Years' War are a label in [[crises/war-and-schism]]. They are not one battle and not one cause. The Reformed churches in France, supplied with pastors from Geneva, had grown under noble protection. They were Huguenots, not Lutherans. On the other side stood the house of Guise and a Catholic league. The crown, often a child king with Catherine de' Medici beside it, tried to balance and also to survive. Confession, clientage, and the weakness of the monarchy are three descriptions of the same decades. Dropping any one of them makes the wars look cleaner than they were.

:::definition Religious war {#def-religious-war}
A **religious war**, in this chapter, is a war in which confession is a real cause and a public banner. The name does not mean that fiscal need, dynasty, and local privilege were absent. Used as if it did, the name hides the Catholic crown of France fighting Catholic Habsburgs, and the princes who changed sides.
:::

On 24 August 1572, in Paris, Huguenot leaders who had gathered for the wedding of Henry of Navarre and Marguerite of Valois were killed. The killing spread through the city and then to other towns. Historians disagree about how far the first decision at court was meant to reach. They do not disagree that the killing spread. It was a massacre, not a pitched battle. This atlas states no death total, and it describes no method. The massacre did not end the wars. From 1572 to the end of the usual brackets in 1598 is 26 years. Henry of Navarre survived, became Henry IV, and accepted the Catholic mass as the political condition of being king in Paris. We do not quote a witticism, and we do not date that acceptance with a year the chronology has not locked.

:::definition Limited toleration {#def-nantes}
The **Edict of Nantes**, in April 1598, is a **limited toleration**. It gave Huguenots a measure of protection: worship in specified places, access to office, law courts that included their co-religionists, and, for a time, secure towns. It was not equality. It was not a grant to every religion. It was a royal edict, and a royal edict can be revoked.
:::

In April 1598 Henry IV issued that edict. Private conscience and public worship were not the same permission. Worship was located, not thrown open. The political guarantees were the king's to police. Catholics remained the public church of the kingdom. The pope did not love the compromise. Subjects who were neither Catholic nor Reformed were not the edict's beneficiaries. From 1562 to 1598 the brackets close on a grant, not on a victory of one doctrine. The grant's future is already in the chronology, and it belongs to the next course: on 18 October 1685 Louis XIV revoked Nantes at Fontainebleau. [[states/fiscal-military]] teaches that revocation beside the Code Noir of the same year. Here the revocation is only the proof that 1598 was revocable. From 1598 to 1685 is 87 years. A long tolerance can still be a favour.

:::theorem A grant, not a right {#thm-nantes}
The Edict of Nantes was a limited and revocable toleration for Huguenots. It was not equality, and it was not a settlement that later European law was obliged to keep. The revocation of 1685 is the evidence of revocability, not a surprise from outside the edict's character.
:::
:::proof
The text's limits are the evidence for the first half: placed worship, a Catholic public order, no general liberty of religion. The revocation on 18 October 1685 is the evidence for the second. What would count against [[#thm-nantes]] is an edict that entrenched Huguenot worship as a fundamental law the crown itself could not touch, or a revocation that proved impossible because the courts treated 1598 as supreme over the king. The king revoked it. What would also count against the claim is a reading of Nantes as equality in 1598. Equality would not have needed specified places of worship and would not have left the Catholics as the kingdom's public church. The edict is a peace inside a Catholic monarchy. [[#def-nantes]] is that peace, and nothing larger.
:::

:::example Twenty-six years after a massacre {#ex-bartholomew}
St Bartholomew's Day in Paris is 24 August 1572. The Edict of Nantes is April 1598. How many years is the difference, and what does the gap forbid you to say about the massacre?
:::solution
1598 minus 1572 is 26. The massacre did not finish the Huguenots, did not finish the wars, and did not make Nantes unnecessary or inevitable. The brackets 1562–1598 still have more than two decades to run after the killing spreads. You may call 24 August 1572 a massacre that began in Paris and spread. You may not give a death total, and you may not treat the day as the end of the series. The number of years is 26.
:::
:::

## One fleet is not a great power

In 1588 Philip II sent the Armada against Elizabeth's England. The fleet did not join the army it was built to carry across. English ships and the weather of the Atlantic broke the enterprise. We stop there. A lesson that becomes a naval manual has left its question. The defeat did not, by itself, make England a great power. Spain remained a great power, still at war in the Netherlands and still a Catholic monarchy with a world empire. England's later capacity to tax, borrow, and keep a fleet in being is a fiscal story. The Navigation Act of 1651 and the Bank of England of 1694 are instruments of that story, taught in [[states/fiscal-military]], not trophies washed up in 1588.

Elizabeth's acts of 1559, in [[reformations/england]], explain why the war could be narrated as Protestant island against Catholic empire. They do not explain a change in the rank of states. From 1559 to 1588 is 29 years. A statutory church can fight. Fighting once, and surviving, is not hegemony. The same caution applies inside France: 1588 is also a year of open conflict between the Catholic League and the French king. One calendar holds an ocean and a civil war. Neither event is the meaning of the other.

## Not only a war of religion

On 23 May 1618 officials of the Habsburg king were thrown from a window of Prague Castle. They survived the fall. The Defenestration opened the war this atlas dates 1618 to 1648. The quarrel was about churches and about the privileges of the Bohemian estates. In 1609, the year the Twelve Years' Truce began in the Netherlands, the Bohemian estates had a royal grant of religious rights. Ferdinand's government was accused of breaking it. The estates renounced him as king of Bohemia and offered the crown to Frederick, the elector Palatine, a Calvinist. Augsburg, on 25 September 1555, had not included Calvinists. A Calvinist prince on a Habsburg throne was a constitutional act as well as a confessional one. He was defeated. He lost the Bohemian crown and control of his own electoral lands. We do not date the battle. The widening is what matters.

A Danish king intervened and failed. A Swedish king, Gustavus Adolphus, intervened, won campaigns, and was killed in battle. We do not date the death. Imperial commanders, Wallenstein among them, were themselves expendable: he was killed on the emperor's side of the war, and we do not date that either. France, a Catholic kingdom, under Richelieu and then Mazarin, paid and then fought the Catholic Habsburgs of Spain and of the empire. That alliance is the clearest evidence in the chronology's warning. The war is not only a war of religion, because a Catholic crown fought Catholic crowns, because Protestant princes changed sides, and because the imperial constitution was at stake. The Golden Bull of 1356 had fixed the electors who chose an emperor. A war about who held a vote, who garrisoned a bishopric, and who paid an army is fiscal, dynastic, and local even when every banner carries a confession.

:::theorem More than one cause {#thm-causes}
The Thirty Years' War, dated 1618 to 1648, was religious and not only religious. Confession was a cause and a banner. Fiscal need, dynasty, and the constitution of the empire were causes as well. A single religious plot does not survive the list of who fought whom.
:::
:::proof
The Defenestration of 23 May 1618 is religious and constitutional at once: churches, a grant of 1609, and the estates' right to resist a king. Frederick's Calvinist kingship tests the omission written into Augsburg in 1555. The Swedish and Danish interventions are Protestant powers in a German war, which keeps religion in the story. France's war against the Habsburgs keeps it from being the whole story. What would count against [[#thm-causes]] is a war in which every Catholic power fought every Protestant power and no one changed sides for a province, a vote, or a subsidy. The coalitions were not like that. What would also count against it is a claim that religion was propaganda only. The Bohemian crisis began on a question of churches. Both reductions fail. Historians disagree about the scale of death and destruction. This atlas states no death total. Severity in some regions and relative sparing of others are compatible with everything just said. A percentage of the empire's population is not.
:::

:::example Thirty years by subtraction {#ex-thirty}
The Defenestration is 23 May 1618. The imperial treaties are 24 October 1648. How many years is the difference of the year numbers, and why is that difference not a claim that religion operated evenly throughout?
:::solution
1648 minus 1618 is 30. The count is the atlas's elapsed difference of two years of the common era. It is not a claim that every campaign had the same cause, and it is not a death total. Inside the thirty years a Bohemian revolt becomes a German war and then a European one, with Catholic France against Catholic Habsburgs. The number names the brackets. The reader has to open them. The number of years is 30.
:::
:::

## Two cities, and a January that is not October

Peace was negotiated in two cities because the parties would not sit as one congress under one mediator. Münster held the talks involving France and the Catholic powers, and it was where Spain and the United Provinces made their own treaty. Osnabrück held the talks involving Sweden and the Protestant estates of the empire. The papal nuncio was at Münster. He was not the judge of the result. On 24 October 1648 the imperial treaties were signed. They did not ask his permission. His protest is a fact about jurisdiction. It is not a philosophy of secular politics.

Spain's recognition of the United Provinces came in January 1648, at Münster, months before the imperial signature. The Dutch revolt had produced the Union of Utrecht in 1579 and the Act of Abjuration in 1581, taught in [[states/composite-monarchy]]. The Twelve Years' Truce ran from 1609 to 1621. This chapter does not invent a year for the revolt's beginning. It does say that January 1648 closed Spain's claim, and that this closing is not the same act as the imperial religious peace of 24 October. One year, two treaties, two problems.

:::proposition January is not October {#prop-two-treaties}
In 1648 Spain's recognition of the United Provinces and the imperial treaties of Münster and Osnabrück are both real and they are not one document. The first recognises a republic outside the Spanish monarchy. The second adjusts the empire.
:::
:::proof
The months are the evidence. January is the Spanish-Dutch treaty at Münster. 24 October is the imperial settlement. The Dutch Republic's independence from Spain did not require a new rule about Calvinists inside Bohemia, and the admission of Calvinists to the imperial peace did not require Spain to leave the Netherlands. What would count against [[#prop-two-treaties]] is a single text that does both jobs in one clause, signed once. The congress produced separate instruments. Later shorthand that says "Westphalia" for the whole year is a convenience. It becomes an error when it hides which parties promised what. Fighting between France and Spain did not stop on 24 October. The day is not the end of every war then in progress. We give no substitute year for that later peace.
:::

The October treaties admit the Reformed, the Calvinists, to the religious peace from which Augsburg had excluded them in 1555. From 1555 to 1648 is 93 years. That admission is the doctrinal change inside the empire: three public religions of princes and of imperial cities, not two. It is not a right of each subject to pick a fourth, or to pick against the prince in every case. The peace also wrote down limits on the emperor in favour of the estates, including a liberty to make alliances that were not directed against the emperor and the empire. Sweden gained a foothold in northern Germany. France gained rights in Alsace that were legally tangled, not a clean transfer of a modern province. The Swiss cantons were treated as outside imperial jurisdiction. The emperor, the electors, the diet, and the imperial courts remained. The empire outlived the peace. This lesson does not date its end.

:::theorem A peace, not a theory of sovereignty {#thm-peace}
The treaties of 24 October 1648 adjusted the Peace of Augsburg and admitted Calvinists in the empire. They did not publish a finished theory of modern sovereignty, and they did not create a modern state system. That reading is later.
:::
:::proof
What the treaties change can be listed from the instruments: Calvinists enter the religious peace; estates have a written liberty of alliance inside the empire; territory and rights move; Swiss exemption from imperial courts is confirmed; Spain's separate January act has already recognised the Dutch. What they do not contain is a definition of sovereign equality among states and a dissolution of the empire. The empire's offices continue. The pope's protest is answered by proceeding without him, which is a fact about this negotiation, not a chapter of a textbook on international relations. Later readers, especially in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, bundled the treaties with a philosophy of the modern state. Peter Wilson's history of the war treats them as a peace. What would count against [[#thm-peace]] is an article that defines sovereignty in that later sense and declares the imperial constitution over. There is no such article. What would count against the first sentence is a treaty that left the Reformed where Augsburg had left them. It did not. The admission is real. The metaphysics are not.
:::

:::example One year, two instruments {#ex-two-1648}
Spain recognises the United Provinces in January 1648. The imperial treaties are 24 October 1648. Why is it not enough to answer "What did 1648 do?" with one sentence?
:::solution
The year holds two peaces. January, at Münster, ends Spain's claim over the United Provinces. That story runs through the Union of Utrecht in 1579, the Act of Abjuration in 1581, and the truce of 1609 to 1621. 24 October adjusts the empire: Calvinists enter the religious peace, and the imperial constitution continues. A single sentence will either describe a republic leaving a composite monarchy or an empire rewriting Augsburg. It will not honestly do both unless it says that it is doing both. [[#prop-two-treaties]] is the distinction. The months are the method.
:::
:::

:::example An invented constitution {#ex-invented-1648}
The following sentence is invented. "On 24 October 1648 the kings of Europe signed a constitution of sovereign states, ended religious war, and dissolved the empire." Which claims fail?
:::solution
The signatories were not "the kings of Europe" in a single assembly. Münster and Osnabrück were parallel negotiations, and Spain's recognition of the Dutch was a January treaty, not the October text. There is no constitution of sovereign states in the treaties, and there is no sentence dissolving the empire. Religious coercion did not end: Nantes was already only a limited edict, and it was revoked on 18 October 1685. Franco-Spanish fighting outlived 24 October. The sentence takes a peace inside an empire and replaces it with a founding myth. [[#thm-peace]] is the correction.
:::

## A worked case: the Calvinists enter the peace

Follow one omission until it is repaired. In 1529 at Marburg, Luther and Zwingli did not agree on the eucharist, so "Protestant" was already more than one doctrine. That split is [[reformations/luther]] and [[reformations/reformed-cities]]. On 25 September 1555 Augsburg legalised a princely choice between Lutheran and Catholic and left the Reformed outside. In 1618 a Calvinist elector took the Bohemian crown the Habsburg king claimed. The war that followed was, in that chapter of it, about an exclusion becoming a revolution. On 24 October 1648 the exclusion was withdrawn. The Reformed religion became a legal public religion of the empire.

The case is narrow on purpose. It shows a real change: the list of recognised confessions grew. It does not show a modern liberty of conscience. A subject in a prince's territory still met a public church. It does not show a system exported to the world by the treaty's own text. Nothing in the admission of Calvinists defines sovereignty, borders in general, or the equality of all states. Readers who want a birthday for the international order have used this peace because it is famous, late, and full of crowns. Fame is not a definition. The worked test is simple. If you cannot say what happened to the Reformed between 1555 and 1648, you do not yet know what Westphalia changed. If you can say only that, you do not yet know what later theory added.

## Objections

The serious objection is that the later reading is not an invention but a summary. The Dutch leave the Spanish monarchy. The Swiss leave the imperial courts. Estates may ally. The pope does not ratify the peace. From those clauses, a reader can sketch a world of polities with no superior. The sketch uses real clauses. It becomes false when it pretends the clauses were written as that sketch, and when it says the treaties created the modern state system. They adjusted an empire that continued. They recognised two independences that had already been fought for. A turning point is not a birth. [[sources/argument]] is the general warning about labels that swallow the evidence they nickname. Westphalia is one of those labels.

A second objection treats the whole century as religion and nothing else. St Bartholomew, the Armada, and the Defenestration are then one plot. The plot cannot explain why Catholic France fought the Catholic emperor, why Nantes regulated a kingdom the empire did not rule, or why a Spanish-Dutch treaty in January 1648 could be signed by powers who were not settling the Palatinate. Religion made parties and justified taxes. It did not choose every ally. [[#thm-causes]] is the reply, and the absence of a death total is part of the same discipline. A number would only make the religious story look more scientific. We do not have a number this atlas will stand behind.

:::warning Not the birth of the state system
Do not say the Peace of Westphalia created the modern state system. It adjusted Augsburg, admitted Calvinists, and left the empire standing. A theory of sovereignty is a later reading. Do not give a death total for the Thirty Years' War or for St Bartholomew's Day. And do not let "religious war" erase the Catholic powers who fought each other, or the fiscal and dynastic stakes of 1618 to 1648.
:::

## Where this leads

The revocation of Nantes on 18 October 1685, and the fiscal-military states that could police a religion and fund a war, are [[states/fiscal-military]]. The Dutch revolt as a composite monarchy coming apart is [[states/composite-monarchy]]. How a label such as Westphalia can hide a claim is [[sources/argument]]. The English settlement that the Armada did not transform into a great power is [[reformations/england]]. The Reformed churches that entered the imperial peace only in 1648, having been outside it since 1555, are [[reformations/reformed-cities]]. A peace adjusted a list. It did not close the century, and it did not found a system.

:::summary
- The French wars are usually bracketed 1562–1598. They were religious, noble, and dynastic, not one battle.
- On 24 August 1572 the massacre of St Bartholomew's Day began in Paris and spread. No death total is stated. The wars continued.
- The Edict of Nantes, April 1598, was a limited and revocable toleration, not equality. Louis XIV revoked it on 18 October 1685.
- The Armada of 1588 failed. It did not, by itself, make England a great power.
- The Defenestration of Prague on 23 May 1618 opened a war dated 1618–1648. The war was not only religious. Catholic France fought Catholic Habsburgs. No death total is stated.
- In January 1648 Spain recognised the United Provinces at Münster. On 24 October the imperial treaties adjusted Augsburg and admitted Calvinists.
- Those treaties did not publish a theory of modern sovereignty. The claim that they created a modern state system is a later reading. The empire continued.
:::

:::exercise The later theory {level=3}
State, as fairly as you can, why serious readers have treated 1648 as the start of a modern state system. Then say what the treaties would have to contain for that treatment to be a reading of the text rather than a later theory.

:::solution
The fair statement is that several clauses point away from a single superior. Spain recognises the United Provinces in January 1648. The Swiss are outside imperial jurisdiction. Estates may ally if the alliance is not against the emperor and the empire. The treaties take effect without the pope's approval. A historian who wants the genealogy of independent states can start a chapter there. The clauses are real, and calling them nothing is as false as calling them a constitution.

They are not that constitution. A reading of the text would need an article that defines states as legally equal sovereigns and that ends the Holy Roman Empire. The treaties adjust the empire and leave its emperor, electors, diet, and courts in place. They admit Calvinists to a religious peace. They move territory and rights. None of that is a general theory, and none of it is the creation of a state system. The treatment becomes a later theory at the moment it supplies the definition the text lacks. [[#thm-peace]] is the line. What would move the line is the missing article, not a louder repetition of the nickname Westphalia.
