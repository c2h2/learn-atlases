What does the name Counter-Reformation hide? It hides the half of Catholic reform that is not a reply to Protestants, and it keeps the half that is. Both halves are true. The older label keeps only the second. From 1545 to 1563 a council at Trent defined doctrine and reformed practice, with a long gap in which it was not sitting. New orders, the Jesuits among them, belong to the same years. This atlas does not give those orders a foundation year. On 25 September 1555 the empire made a peace the council would not call the truth. A peace and a council were answering different questions.

Reform inside the Latin Church is older than 1517. Cluny's usual date is 910. Cîteaux, the Cistercian start, is 1098. Constance sat from 1414 to 1418 and burned Hus in 1415, which is a council used as an instrument of uniformity, not as a Protestant invention. The demand for a resident bishop and a literate priest did not begin as a reply to Luther. What begins as a reply is the drawing of a doctrinal line against teachings that had spread since the theses. An honest name has room for both sentences. Counter-Reformation has room for one.

## A council with a gap in it

Trent opened in 1545 and closed in 1563. The difference of the year numbers is 18. Eighteen is not eighteen years of continuous debate. The chronology of this atlas calls the interruption a long gap between sessions, and the lesson must not replace that gap with a list of years it has not locked. Bishops were hard to assemble. Emperor and pope did not want the same council. Charles V wanted reform of the papal court and a path back for the German estates. The papacy wanted doctrine defined, and defined under papal presidency, not reopened by a council that might repeat Constance's claim to stand over the pope. War between the great crowns, and the choice of a city both sides would endure, did the rest. Charles abdicated in 1556, before the council ended. The peace of the previous year was already in force in the empire. Trent closed under a different pope and a different emperor from the men who had opened the argument.

:::definition Catholic reform {#def-catholic-reform}
**Catholic reform** is the effort, inside the Latin Church and especially across the years 1545 to 1563, to define doctrine, to change the life of bishops and priests, and to found or renew religious orders. It includes a reaction to Protestant teaching. It is not only that reaction. The name does not mean "whatever answered Luther", and it does not mean a new church.
:::

:::definition Counter-Reformation {#def-counter}
**Counter-Reformation** is a later historians' label for the reactive half of that effort: controversy, censorship, and the recovery of territory and people from Protestant churches. Used as the name of the whole, it hides residence, seminaries, the reform of older orders, and missions to people who had never been Protestant. Hubert Jedin's formula, Catholic reform and Counter-Reformation, is the reminder that the two halves need both words.
:::

What the council decided can be paraphrased, and must not be pretended as a quotation. On scripture, it held that the gospel is handed on in written books and in unwritten traditions kept in the Catholic Church, and it treated the old Latin Vulgate as the authentic text for public teaching. That is a refusal of "scripture alone" as the Protestants had come to use it. On justification, it taught that sinners are justified by God's grace, for the sake of Christ, and that faith is the beginning of salvation, while refusing the claim that faith alone, without hope, charity, and the sacraments, is the whole account. Good works of the justified are not a side issue in that decree. On the sacraments, it kept seven, and it taught that they confer grace. On the eucharist, it affirmed transubstantiation. That rejects a purely symbolic reading. It also refuses to treat the Lutheran real presence, which had rejected transubstantiation, as sufficient. Reformed teaching, already distinct since Marburg in 1529, is further still from Trent's formula. See [[reformations/luther]] and [[reformations/reformed-cities]].

The reform of practice is the other pile of decrees, and it is not a cover story for the first. Bishops were to reside, to preach, and to visit. Holding many benefices at once was restricted. Each diocese was to train priests in a seminary. A marriage, to be valid, was to be contracted before the parish priest and witnesses, against the old problem of clandestine vows. None of these rules needs a Lutheran neighbour in order to be intelligible. A priest who cannot read the mass, and a bishop who never sees his diocese, were medieval complaints. They are still Catholic reform when Trent repeats them.

:::theorem The label keeps only the reaction {#thm-label}
Catholic reform is a reaction to Protestant teaching, and it is not only a reaction. The name Counter-Reformation keeps the first half and hides the second. Both halves are required for Trent and for the new orders.
:::
:::proof
The reactive half is visible in the doctrinal decrees just paraphrased. They draw lines against justification by faith alone, against scripture as the only channel of the gospel, and against any eucharist that will not say transubstantiation. Those lines have opponents, and the opponents are the churches of the decades since 1517. The other half is visible in residence, seminaries, marriage law, and in orders that also mission where there were no Protestants to counter. What would count against [[#thm-label]] is a council that only condemned and never reformed the clergy, or a council that only repeated fifteenth-century advice and never named the new doctrines. Trent does both. What would also count against it is a use of Counter-Reformation that already includes, without hiding, the seminary and the mission. Ordinary use does not. It means "the Catholic reply", and the reply is only half the record.
:::

:::example Eighteen years, not eighteen sessions {#ex-span-trent}
Trent opens in 1545 and ends in 1563. How many years is the difference, and why must you not treat the council as busy for all of them?
:::solution
1563 minus 1545 is 18. The chronology calls the interruption a long gap between sessions. Some of those eighteen years are years of sitting, and some are years of suspension, war, and a pope or an emperor who did not want the assembly where it was. Charles V abdicated in 1556, inside the span and before the end. The Peace of Augsburg, 25 September 1555, also falls inside it. A continuous council would not have a gap to explain, and it would not need the politics of absence. The number of years is 18.
:::
:::

## New orders, with no foundation year

The Jesuits, the Capuchins, the Ursulines, the reformed Carmelites, and the Oratory of Philip Neri are not one project wearing five uniforms. The Society of Jesus, associated with Ignatius of Loyola, bound its members to a special obedience to the pope in the matter of missions, ran colleges, and used a structured retreat, the Spiritual Exercises, as a training of choice. Francis Xavier is the name that stops the order being only a European police: his missions went out along the Iberian sea routes, to people who were not lapsed Lutherans. The Capuchins were a Franciscan reform of preaching and poverty. The Ursulines took the education of girls as their work, which is enough to stop a story in which Catholic reform is only a tale about priests. The Carmelites renewed an old order. The Oratory gathered secular priests in a common life. Together they are contemporary with Trent. That is the chronological claim this atlas will allow.

:::proposition Contemporary, and not dated further {#prop-orders}
The new orders, including the Jesuits, are part of Catholic reform and are contemporary with the council of 1545 to 1563. No foundation year is locked in the chronology. A textbook that prints one has added a numeral this lesson will not use.
:::
:::proof
The positive evidence is the work itself, set beside the council's years: colleges, missions, preaching, and the training of priests are what Catholic reform looks like when it is not a decree. The negative evidence is the rule of this atlas. Where the chronology does not lock a year, the lesson does not invent a substitute. What would count against [[#prop-orders]] is a chronology entry that dates the Society and authorises the numeral. There is none. What would count against the first sentence is evidence that these orders were only anti-Protestant squads with no schools, no missions beyond Christian Europe, and no reform of the religious life. Xavier's missions, the Ursuline schools, and the Capuchin return to Franciscan practice are the contrary. They are also, in Germany, France, and the empire, a reaction. The proposition says contemporary and part of Catholic reform. It does not say "untouched by Luther".
:::

:::example A date the page must not supply {#ex-no-year}
An invented examination question reads: "Give the foundation year of the Jesuits and quote the decree of Trent that answers it." Why does this atlas reject both halves of the question?
:::solution
The foundation year is not locked. Supplying one, even a famous one, invents a numeral. The right sentence is that the new orders are contemporary with Trent and that no foundation year is given. The decree is the other half of the trap. Trent's decisions can be paraphrased: scripture and tradition together, justification not by faith alone, seven sacraments, transubstantiation, resident bishops, seminaries, marriage before the parish priest and witnesses. Paraphrase is not a quotation, and this lesson will not dress a summary up as the council's wording. The invented question fails twice: once by a missing year, and once by a fake document.
:::
:::

## A peace the council would not call true

On 25 September 1555 the Peace of Augsburg let Lutheran and Catholic princes in the empire settle the public religion of their lands. The formula *cuius regio, eius religio* is the shorthand for that choice. Calvinists were not included. Subjects were not granted a private right to believe as they liked. The peace is not modern toleration, and it is not a doctrine. Trent did not accept it as the truth of the faith. A council says what the Church teaches. A diet says which public worship a prince may maintain without restarting a war inside the empire. A prince's choice does not make a doctrine true. A decree does not, by itself, rewrite the law of the estates. Charles V's abdication in 1556 left his successors living with a peace he had not wanted as a theological settlement. The empire and the council had diverged inside one decade of the council's own span.

:::theorem Two questions, two answers {#thm-two-questions}
Trent and the Peace of Augsburg answer different questions. The council defines Catholic doctrine and clerical practice. The peace allocates public religion between Lutheran and Catholic princes. Neither result is the other in disguise, and the council does not receive the peace as the truth.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the content of each act, not a theory about "church and state". Augsburg names two permitted public religions and omits the Reformed. Trent's eucharistic and justification decrees would not accept Lutheran teaching as Catholic truth, and they have nothing to say that turns a princely option into a sacrament. The dates interlock without merging: 1555 falls inside 1545–1563, ten years after the opening and eight years before the close. What would count against [[#thm-two-questions]] is a Tridentine decree that ratifies *cuius regio, eius religio*, or a clause of Augsburg that claims to define justification. Neither document does the other's job. A reader who calls Augsburg "Trent's policy for Germany" has confused a peace with a council.
:::

:::example Inside one span {#ex-inside}
Using only 1545, 1555, and 1563, say where Augsburg sits relative to Trent, and what a student should not conclude from the nesting.
:::solution
From 1545 to 1555 is 10 years. From 1555 to 1563 is 8. Augsburg is inside the council's span and not at either end. The nesting tempts the conclusion that the peace is a session of the council, or that the council paused in order to enact it. The gap in Trent is real, but the peace is an imperial law, not a decree. Trent does not accept the princely choice as the truth of doctrine. Sharing a calendar is not sharing an author.
:::
:::

:::example Three institutions, three dates {#ex-three-courts}
Set 1231, 1 November 1478, and the council of 1545–1563 side by side. An invented sentence says: "The Inquisition, founded once, sat at Trent and published the Index as its only book." What must you separate?
:::solution
1231 is Gregory IX's papal procedure, and the chronology says it is not yet the Spanish Inquisition. 1478 is Sixtus IV's authorisation of that Spanish tribunal, a royal institution. Trent is a council that defines doctrine and reforms practice. The Roman inquisitorial court of the council's years is a further office, and this lesson does not date its reorganisation with a numeral the chronology has not locked. The Index is one instrument of the same decades, not the only book and not the council. The invented sentence collapses four things into one nickname. Separation is the whole of the answer.
:::
:::

## The Index is an instrument, not the mind of the Church

Lists of prohibited books were one way the papacy tried to govern print. They belong to the reactive half: a technology that had multiplied the theses, as [[crises/print]] explains, could also multiply a reply, and it could be censored. Censorship is real. It is not the whole of Catholic intellectual life. Universities still disputed. The new colleges taught. Lawyers glossed. The council ordered a reform of the liturgical books, which is an editorial labour, not a bonfire. Biblical and patristic learning continued inside the church that issued the lists. A history that stops at the Index has described a police measure and called it a culture.

The same care is required with the word inquisition. In 1231 Gregory IX's procedure is a papal inquisition. It is not yet the Spanish Inquisition. On 1 November 1478 Sixtus IV authorised the Spanish Inquisition, a royal institution in Castile and Aragon, taught as part of [[crises/year-1492]]. In the years of Trent the papacy also reorganised inquisitorial authority at Rome. That Roman court is a third thing. This lesson does not describe the procedure of any of them. A procedure is not needed in order to see that a court of doctrine, a Spanish royal tribunal, and a council's decree are not one office under three nicknames.

:::corollary Censorship is not the culture {#cor-index}
The Index of prohibited books is part of Catholic reform's reactive half. It is not a sufficient description of Catholic thought, scholarship, or schooling in the same years.
:::
:::proof
The corollary follows from the existence of the other instruments already named: seminaries, colleges, the reform of liturgy, the new orders' teaching, and the council's own mass of doctrinal and practical text. An Index is a list. A list cannot be the activity it tries to limit. What would count against the corollary is a demonstration that Catholic publication, university disputation, and schooling ceased where the lists began. They did not cease. What would also count against it is a claim that the lists were trivial. They were not. The corollary says "not sufficient", not "unimportant".
:::

## A worked case: two decrees, one council

Take the seminary and the decree on justification as one case with two acts. The seminary answers a pastoral failure: priests untrained, bishops absent, parishes served by men who held the title and not the cure. A fifteenth-century reformer could have written the complaint. Nothing in it requires Wittenberg. The justification decree answers a sixteenth-century argument. It refuses to let faith alone, in the Protestant sense, be the account of how a sinner stands before God, and it keeps grace, the sacraments, and good works inside one teaching. A Lutheran negotiator could not sign it and remain Lutheran. A Catholic bishop could implement the seminary and preach the decree in the same year of his life.

The case is the test of the name. Call the council only a Counter-Reformation, and the seminary becomes camouflage for the condemnation. Call it only an internal tidy-up, and the condemnation becomes an embarrassment to skip. The worked claim is the conjunction in [[#thm-label]]. Evidence that would break the case: a seminary decree that in fact only authorised colleges for controversy and said nothing about the ordinary priest, or a justification decree that in fact offered the compromise Charles V had wanted for the German estates. The paraphrasable content runs the other way. Ordinary training on one side, a doctrinal wall on the other, one council.

## Objections

The sharpest objection is that the reactive half is the real half, because the decrees condemn Protestant positions and the Jesuits became the most visible Catholic agents in Protestant lands. The objection is half right, which is why [[#thm-label]] will not drop the reaction. It becomes false when it deletes the rest. Missions beyond Europe do not counter a German sermon. A rule about clandestine marriage does not counter Zwingli. Residence is not a pamphlet. The objection wins only if those acts can be shown to be pretences. Their later use against Protestants does not prove they had no other object.

The opposite objection says that Catholic reform was only continuity, and that "Counter" is a Protestant insult. Continuity is real, back to 1098 and to the complaints of any synod about ignorant clergy. It is not the whole. Before 1517 no council had to answer the *Institutes* of 1536 or the Zurich Supper of 1525. After those texts, silence was a choice, and Trent did not choose silence. A label with only "reform" in it, and no "counter", hides the wall. Jedin's pair of words is the objection's proper size.

:::warning Three substitutions
Do not let Counter-Reformation stand as the name of the whole. It keeps the reaction and hides the reform of practice and the new orders' wider work. Do not let the Index stand as Catholic intellectual life. And do not let 1545 to 1563 stand as an unbroken sitting. The gap is part of the council. A peace in 1555 and a council that ends in 1563 are neighbours on a timeline, not a single policy.
:::

## Where this leads

England's Catholic restoration is a queen and a parliament, not a session of Trent: [[reformations/england]]. The wars that a princely peace and a council both failed to prevent are [[reformations/religious-war]]. The theses they were and were not answering are [[reformations/luther]]. The city churches whose Supper Trent refused are [[reformations/reformed-cities]]. The Spanish Inquisition of 1478 is a different institution, in [[crises/year-1492]]. A reader who can say both halves of [[#thm-label]] will not be surprised that Europe after 1563 still had more than one church, and more than one way of being Catholic.

:::summary
- Counter-Reformation names the reactive half of Catholic reform and hides the rest. Both halves are true.
- Trent ran from 1545 to 1563, eighteen years with a long gap, not a continuous sitting. Charles V abdicated in 1556, before it ended.
- Doctrine, in paraphrase and not in a fake quotation: scripture and tradition, justification not by faith alone, seven sacraments, transubstantiation, and a real reform of bishops, seminaries, and marriage.
- New orders, Jesuits included, are contemporary with the council. No foundation year is given.
- On 25 September 1555 Augsburg let Lutheran and Catholic princes settle public religion. Trent did not accept that peace as the truth.
- The Index is one instrument of control. It is not the whole of Catholic scholarship, schooling, or worship.
- The papal inquisition of 1231, the Spanish Inquisition of 1478, and the Roman court of Trent's years are three institutions. Their procedure is not described here.
