What is established about 31 October 1517, and what is not? The Ninety-five Theses carry that date. They were a real text, and they circulated. The story that Martin Luther nailed them to the door of the castle church in Wittenberg comes later, from Philip Melanchthon, and is not established. A date on a set of academic propositions is not the same thing as a scene with a hammer. This chapter stays with what can be dated: the theses, the Diet of Worms, the Peasants' War, the sack of Rome, and the split at Marburg.

The singular name "the Reformation" is already a poor fit for these years. Luther's refusal to recant did not produce one doctrine, one church, or one relation between preacher and prince. By 1529 he and Huldrych Zwingli could not agree on the eucharist. The word Protestant, born the same year, names a protest in imperial politics, not a single confession. The next chapter takes the city reformations that are not Wittenberg. Catholic reform, which is not only a reply to Luther, is the chapter after that.

## A date, a text, and a later story

Wittenberg was a small university town in the electorate of Saxony. Luther was an Augustinian friar and a professor of theology. The theses are written in the form of a disputation: numbered propositions offered for academic argument, in Latin, not a sermon to a crowd and not a charter for a new church. What the date establishes is authorship and circulation. Copies moved, in print and in manuscript, beyond the university. Print is the multiplier. It is not, by itself, the cause. German Bibles already existed, and a printed Latin Bible is usually dated about 1454–1455, two generations before the theses. [[crises/print]] is the place for that technology. A press can spread a quarrel that is already under way. It cannot decide what the quarrel means.

:::definition Ninety-five Theses {#def-theses}
The **Ninety-five Theses** are propositions for a disputation, dated 31 October 1517. They are established as a text that circulated. They are not established as a paper nailed to the Wittenberg church door. The door scene is Melanchthon's later story. A university custom of posting theses, even if one existed, would still not be a witness to this day.
:::

The theses take their occasion from indulgence preaching. They ask, in substance, whether a papal indulgence can reach a soul in purgatory, whether a money payment can stand in for repentance, and whether the pope, if he has the power the preachers claim, would not empty purgatory for free. We are paraphrasing the line of argument, not quoting a script. The propositions still sit inside a Latin Church: they argue about penance, the pope, and the care of souls. They are not yet a rejection of every sacrament, and they are not a political programme for the German princes. The break widens in the arguments that follow, when Luther is pushed from one practice to the authority of popes and of councils.

That widening reaches back to a wound the Council of Constance had thought closed. The council sat from 1414 to 1418. Jan Hus was burned in 1415. The Western Schism, begun in 1378, ended with the election of Martin V in 1417. In the arguments after the theses, Luther was brought to say that popes and councils can err. A man burned by a council then became a precedent, not a closed case. [[crises/war-and-schism]] treats Constance on its own terms. Here the point is narrower: the theses are the start of a public quarrel, not the whole of Luther's later teaching, and not the whole of the reformations.

:::theorem What 31 October 1517 establishes {#thm-door}
The day establishes a dated text that circulated. It does not establish that Luther nailed the theses to the church door. The door story is later, it comes from Melanchthon, and it is not a contemporary record of the act.
:::
:::proof
The evidence for the text is the theses themselves and the fact that they circulated. The evidence against the door is the shape of the story: it is told later, by a man who was not writing a diary of 31 October 1517. A custom of posting theses would make the scene plausible. Plausibility is not testimony. What would count against [[#thm-door]] is a contemporary witness, independent of Melanchthon, who records the nailing on that day. No such witness is established. What would count against the first half is evidence that the theses did not circulate. Circulation is the part that is established. A portrait at a door adds an act we cannot show and hides print, university, and prince.
:::

:::quiz
What is established about 31 October 1517?
- [ ] Luther nailed the Ninety-five Theses to the door of the castle church, and the sound of the hammer opened the Reformation.
- [x] The theses are dated that day and they circulated. The nailing is a later story and is not established.
- [ ] Nothing about the theses is known. The date was invented in the nineteenth century.
- [ ] The theses were a German sermon which abolished the papacy on that afternoon.
:::solution
The text and its circulation are the settled facts. Melanchthon's door scene is later and is not established. The theses are Latin propositions for a disputation, not a sermon that founded a church in a day, and not a blank that later centuries filled in from nothing.
:::

## Indulgences are the occasion, not the whole church

An indulgence, in the teaching of the Latin Church, was a remission of temporal penalty for sin. It was not a licence to sin, and it was not, on that teaching, the sale of forgiveness of guilt. Guilt belonged to contrition and to the sacrament of penance. Penalty was what remained, including penalty associated with purgatory. Preachers offered indulgences in connection with gifts to pious works. The rebuilding of St Peter's in Rome was one such work. Johann Tetzel preached it in the provinces of Albert of Mainz, archbishop of Mainz and of Magdeburg. That campaign is the occasion of the theses. We give no sum of money. The abuse is in the structure of the preaching, not in a figure this atlas has not locked.

:::definition Indulgence {#def-indulgence}
An **indulgence** is a remission, under the authority the papacy claimed, of temporal penalty for sin. It is not a permit to sin, and it is not a complete description of late medieval religion. In 1517 it is the occasion of Luther's public quarrel. It is not the whole of his later critique, and it is not a cartoon of a church with no other life.
:::

The cartoon is the mistake to refuse. Latin Christian life in these decades was also parish masses, confraternities, pilgrimage, charity, and prayers for the dead. A campaign that told a buyer he had purchased a soul out of purgatory can be attacked without pretending that every altar was a shop. Duffy's warning about England, in [[reformations/england]], travels: a critique of one practice is not a photograph of a dead church. Catholic reform, in [[reformations/catholic-reform]], is unintelligible if nothing inside the old church was worth reforming except as a reply to Protestants.

The theses themselves do not stop at the cash-box. They tie the preaching to repentance, to the limits of papal power, and to the scandal of a pardon that makes grace sound cheap. Once the argument is about authority, it cannot stay a local complaint about one preacher. Defenders of the indulgence replied that Luther had misunderstood a legitimate theology and insulted the pope. He replied, in the years before Worms, by widening the ground: scripture against the preachers, and then scripture against the claim that pope or council cannot err. We do not assign those steps a year the chronology has not locked. The direction is what matters. By the time he stood at Worms he had been excommunicated. The diet is not the first day of the quarrel. It is the day the quarrel met the emperor.

:::example An invented sentence about the door {#ex-invented-door}
Read this sentence, which is invented and is not a source. "On 31 October 1517 Luther nailed the Ninety-five Theses to the church door and told the crowd that he would rather trust his conscience than the pope." What, if anything, in it is established, and what would it take to promote the rest into evidence?
:::solution
Only the date of the theses, and the fact that a text of that date existed to be talked about, touches an established fact. The nailing is the later story, not a fact we may tell. The speech to the crowd is invented. Luther's words are not ours to supply, at the door or at Worms. To promote the nailing, a historian would need a contemporary witness independent of Melanchthon's later account. To promote the speech, one would need a record of words said, not a sentence written to make the scene dramatic. Circulation of the theses can be shown from copies. A crowd, a hammer, and a motto cannot.
:::
:::

## Worms: a refusal, not a script

In April 1521 Luther appeared before the Diet of Worms, Charles V presiding. He was asked to recant. He refused. That refusal is the outcome this atlas will state. The famous sentences later hung on the scene are not a script we can quote, and this chapter will not invent them. A safe conduct had brought him to the diet and allowed him to leave it. The Edict of Worms, in May 1521, declared him an outlaw of the empire. Outlawry was a legal fact. It was not, in Saxony, an arrest.

The gap between edict and arrest is the political fact of this first generation. Frederick, elector of Saxony, protected his professor. In 1521 Luther was taken into that protection, at the Wartburg. He used the hiding to translate the New Testament into German. He was not the first to put scripture into German. The translation mattered because of who he now was, and because print could multiply it. We do not date the publication to a year the chronology has not locked. An empire that had outlawed him could not, or would not, take him out of a prince's hands. Charles V was also the ruler of a stack of kingdoms, taught in [[states/composite-monarchy]]. His abdications in 1556 lie far ahead. At Worms he could not enforce one religious law on every elector.

:::example From the theses to Worms {#ex-span-worms}
The theses are dated 31 October 1517. The diet is in April 1521. How many years is the difference of the year numbers, and why is that not the same as the span from October to April? (Enter the difference of the year numbers.)
:::solution
For two years of the common era, this atlas takes the elapsed count from year A to year B as B minus A. From 1517 to 1521 that count is 4. From 31 October 1517 to a day in April 1521 is three years and about six months, because October to April does not complete a fourth year. A sentence that says "four years later, at Worms" is using the year-number difference and stretching a date in October forward to a date in April. Both figures can be right if you say which question you asked. The number of years in the year-number count is 4.
:::
:::

After Worms the quarrel was no longer one friar's case. Preachers, councils, and some princes began to change worship. Saxony's protection made a public career possible. It did not make Luther the bishop of the empire. Zwingli in Zurich was already on another path. The split becomes unmistakable at Marburg in 1529.

## The Peasants' War is part of the story

In 1524 and 1525 a large rising ran through the German lands. It is called the Peasants' War. It was not only peasants, just as the English Rising of 1381 was not only peasants. Commons, rural people, and some townsmen acted against lords. Grievances mixed rents, serfdom, the use of woods and commons, and the choice of a pastor. In 1525 a widely copied list, the Twelve Articles, put some of those grievances in the language of the gospel: lordship was to be tested by scripture, burdens eased, and a community allowed to choose its minister. We paraphrase the kind of demand. We do not pretend the articles were one manifesto of one army. The rising was many risings.

Luther condemned the rebels. He had refused pope and emperor at Worms. He did not defend subjects who took up arms against their lords. The distinction he drew, in substance, was between a Christian freedom that does not need to remake the law, and a civil order that subjects must not overthrow in the name of the gospel. The princes put the rising down. The suppression was ferocious. This atlas states no death total. Thomas Müntzer, a preacher who had sided with the revolt, was killed after the defeat at Frankenhausen in 1525. We do not describe the manner of killing. The fact we keep is the alignment: evangelical language was available to rebels, and Luther spent it against them.

:::proposition Worms was not a licence for subjects {#prop-peasants}
Luther's refusal to recant in April 1521 does not entail a right of subjects to impose the gospel by arms. His condemnation of the rebels in the Peasants' War of 1524–1525 is the evidence, and it belongs in the same life as Worms.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the sequence the chronology locks: a refusal at Worms, then a condemnation of the rising. At Worms a summoned theologian refuses to withdraw his teaching. In 1525 subjects attack lordship and he sides with the lords. What would count against [[#prop-peasants]] is a text of his, in the years of the rising, that defends armed rebellion as the consequence of the gospel he had refused to recant. That is not what he published. Skip the war, and the portrait of liberty is edited. Treat the condemnation as the only fact, and you cannot explain why an outlaw was worth protecting. Both facts stand.
:::

Frederick of Saxony died in 1525, during the war. The electorate's protection did not die with him. A dynastic death, a social war, and a theological condemnation can share a year without being one event.

:::example Worms beside the Peasants' War {#ex-side}
Set April 1521 beside 1524–1525. An invented chronicle, labelled as invented, says: "Having broken with all authority at Worms, Luther led the peasants against their lords." What does the pairing of the real dates do to that sentence?
:::solution
The real dates put a refusal and a condemnation in one life, a few years apart. From 1521 to 1525 the year-number difference is 4. The invented sentence collapses them into one pose, "against all authority", and then invents a leadership Luther did not exercise. He refused to recant before the emperor. He condemned the rebels when they fought their lords. The pairing is the correction. A reader who knows only Worms will misread 1525. A reader who knows only the condemnation will misread Worms. The atlas keeps both.
:::
:::

## Rome, Speyer, and Marburg

In 1527 imperial troops sacked Rome. Pope Clement VII had opposed Charles V in Italy. The army was imperial, unpaid, and mixed, and it included German soldiers who had heard evangelical preaching. The sack humiliated the pope. It did not install Luther's doctrine, and Charles did not order it as a theological demonstration. He remained a Catholic prince. The man outlawed at Worms was not the commander. Emperor and pope could make war while both rejected Luther. The English consequence belongs to [[reformations/england]]: a pope bruised by the emperor was badly placed to annul the marriage of the emperor's aunt.

:::theorem Already more than one reformation {#thm-marburg}
By 1529 the evangelical movements were already more than one doctrine of the eucharist. The Marburg colloquy is the marker: Luther and Zwingli did not agree. The word Protestant, from the protest at Speyer in the same year, does not cancel that split.
:::
:::proof
In 1529 a diet at Speyer moved to enforce the Edict of Worms. Evangelical princes and cities protested. The name Protestant comes from that protest. It is a word in imperial law. The same year, Philip of Hesse brought Luther and Zwingli to Marburg. They could agree on a great deal. They could not agree on the eucharist. Luther held that Christ's body and blood are truly given in the bread and wine. Zwingli did not. We state the disagreement and supply no dialogue. The failed colloquy is the evidence for [[#thm-marburg]]. What would count against it is a protocol in which the two men state the same doctrine, or an alliance that did not need the agreement. The meeting was held because the agreement was missing. The alliance did not follow. The name Protestant does not tell you what a person believed about the Supper.
:::

:::corollary The name is not the doctrine {#cor-protestant}
Calling a prince, a city, or a preacher Protestant after 1529 identifies a side in the imperial quarrel. It does not identify a doctrine of the eucharist, and it does not identify a Reformed church of the Genevan kind.
:::
:::proof
The protest at Speyer opposed a recess. It was not a confession drafted to settle the Supper. Luther and Zwingli could both stand on the protesting side and still divide at Marburg. What would count against the corollary is a single confession, accepted at Speyer in 1529, that fixed the eucharist for every protesting estate. There was none. The Peace of Augsburg on 25 September 1555 still had to say which doctrines the empire would recognise. That peace is taught in [[reformations/religious-war]]. A word coined in 1529 cannot do the work of a church order.
:::

:::example One year, two facts {#ex-1529}
Speyer and Marburg both fall in 1529. From the theses in 1517 to that year, the year-number difference is 12. Why is it a mistake to treat 1529 as the year "Protestantism" acquired one theology?
:::solution
Twelve years is the difference 1529 minus 1517. Inside the twelfth year sit two facts that pull apart. Speyer gives a political name, Protestant, to princes and cities protesting an imperial recess. Marburg shows that the theologians those princes might have allied could not say the same thing about the eucharist. One theology would require the second meeting to have succeeded. It failed. The year is shared. The outcomes are not interchangeable. Add Zurich's later history, and Zwingli's death at Kappel in 1531, and the plural is already the story of [[reformations/reformed-cities]].
:::

## A worked case: an outlaw who is not arrested

Take the elector of Saxony as the case, not as a saint. After May 1521 Luther is an outlaw. In a story where imperial law describes the world, he is arrested or he flees. What happens instead is territorial protection. He is hidden in 1521 and returns to a public career in Saxony. The edict remains an edict. This is not yet the Peace of Augsburg. No one in 1521 has a legal right to choose a principality's religion. Electors and princes can obstruct and shelter. The Golden Bull of 1356 had made the electors the men who chose an emperor. Worms shows the other face of that constitution: the emperor they elect cannot always use them as policemen.

The case also limits Luther. He is free enough to condemn the peasants because a lord protects him. That fit does not prove mere self-interest. It does forbid a reading in which Worms is the birth of a right to resist. The same lands produce a protected reform and a crushed rising.

What would break the case is evidence that the Edict of Worms was executed uniformly, or that the peasants won a liberty Luther had preached. Neither is the record. The case stands between two legends: the hammer at the door, and the rebel angel.

## Objections

The door survives because a hammer is easier to remember than circulation. Ease is not evidence. Melanchthon's later story is not a diary of the day. The theses need readers, a university, a press, and a prince, which is [[#thm-door]].

The wholly corrupt church is the next objection. Indulgence preaching was open to attack. Ordinary religion was also real. A cash-box is not the whole church, and devotion is not a reason to ignore the cash-box. People who never became Lutheran still demanded resident bishops and trained priests. That demand is older than 1517.

The third objection makes Worms the birth of religious liberty. It was one man's refusal to recant, under a safe conduct. It was not a rule that every conscience may publish what it likes. The condemnation of the rebels is the counter-evidence in his own work. The Peace of Augsburg in 1555 is still a princely choice between Lutheran and Catholic, not a private right, and it omits the Reformed. That peace is not hiding inside the theses.

:::warning The door, and the cartoon church
The nailing on the Wittenberg door is a later story. Do not tell it as the opening fact. The other usual picture, a single corrupt church with no parish life, fails in the opposite direction. Indulgences are the occasion of the theses. They are not the whole of Luther's critique, and they are not the whole of the Latin Church he criticised. A lesson that needs either the hammer or the cartoon has not yet started work.
:::

## Where this leads

Zurich and Geneva are not Saxony. Zwingli's death at Kappel in 1531 opens [[reformations/reformed-cities]]. The council that answers Luther without merely chasing him is [[reformations/catholic-reform]]. England's statutes are a third constitution, in [[reformations/england]]. The wars the word Protestant cannot prevent, and the peace of 1648 that later readers over-read, are [[reformations/religious-war]]. Behind the theses sit [[crises/print]], the council that burned Hus in [[crises/war-and-schism]], and the papal claims of [[christendom/popes-and-kings]]. None of those chapters is a path that had to end in 1517. Worms could have ended in a recantation. Marburg could have ended in an agreement. Neither did.

- The Ninety-five Theses are dated 31 October 1517 and they circulated. The church-door nailing is Melanchthon's later story and is not established.
- An indulgence is a remission of temporal penalty, not a permit to sin. It is the occasion of the quarrel, not a portrait of the whole Latin Church.
- In April 1521 Luther refused to recant at Worms. The Edict of Worms is May 1521. This atlas does not invent his words.
- Princely protection, not the edict's enforcement, is why an outlaw continued to write. That is a fact about the empire, not yet a right of resistance.
- The Peasants' War of 1524–1525 belongs in the story. Luther condemned the rebels. No death total is stated.
- Imperial troops sacked Rome in 1527. The sack is Habsburg and papal politics, not Luther's victory.

:::exercise Princes as the cause {level=3}

:::solution
The strong form is real. Princes and nobles later took monastic lands, and Saxony's protection of Luther was political from the start. A theologian who increased a prince's independence from Rome was useful. Read backwards from the Peace of Augsburg in 1555, and the land-grab looks like the plot.

It is not the whole cause. In April 1521 Luther is an outlaw, not a minister of confiscation. Cities change worship under councils, where the prize is not an elector's new estate. The peasants of 1525 use evangelical language against lords, and Luther condemns them. A pure land-grab does not explain that condemnation, or the eucharistic split at Marburg, where Zwingli's side is a city rather than an electoral treasury. Princely interest is a cause. It is not a substitute for the texts. The objection would become the whole cause only if no one outside the chanceries had taken the religious arguments seriously. Marburg was held because they had.
