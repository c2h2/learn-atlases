How were Zurich and Geneva not Wittenberg? Wittenberg was a university under an elector, and after 1521 that elector's protection was the condition of Luther's public life. Zurich and Geneva were governed by councils. They changed a liturgy, they built a discipline, and they did not wait for a princely settlement of the kind the Peace of Augsburg later offered to Lutheran and Catholic rulers. The doctrinal marker is earlier than either city's finished order. At Marburg in 1529 Luther and Zwingli did not agree on the eucharist. Reformed and Lutheran are not one Protestantism with two accents. They are a split.

This chapter narrates institutions. It does not narrate Calvin's personality, and it does not describe criminal punishments in Geneva. A consistory that admonishes, and a council that governs, are the facts. The fear, the fame, and the scaffold are not a substitute for the ordinances.

## A council is not an elector

A **city reformation** is a change of public worship decided by a town's own government: a council, often after a disputation, producing a liturgy and a discipline. It is not the later imperial bargain in which a territorial prince chooses Lutheran or Catholic for his lands. Zurich had a council and also a subject countryside, so "city" does not mean that every peasant voted. Geneva was a small republic that had thrown off its bishop and the duke of Savoy and leaned on Bern. Neither city was an electorate of the empire. Neither was Wittenberg.

:::definition City reformation {#def-city}
A **city reformation** is a council's reform of worship: a public disputation or an equivalent hearing, a vernacular liturgy, and a discipline of morals and doctrine exercised with lay officers. It is not a territorial prince's settlement under the Peace of Augsburg, and it is not a private choice by each household.
:::

:::definition Reformed {#def-reformed}
**Reformed** names the churches that stand with Zwingli and, later, with Calvin on the far side of the Marburg disagreement: they do not teach Luther's bodily presence in the bread and wine, and they order the church through ministers and elders rather than through a prince's consistory alone. The word is not a synonym for Protestant. Protestant, from Speyer in 1529, is a political name. Reformed is a doctrinal and institutional one.
:::

The split can be stated without pretending that Zwingli and Calvin wrote the same sentence. Zwingli pressed the Supper as a signification, a thanksgiving, and a pledge of the community. Calvin, in the *Institutes* and in Geneva's liturgy, taught that Christ is truly given to faith by the Holy Spirit, while the bread remains bread. Both refused the claim that Christ's body is locally in the element, which is where Luther would not follow them. Marburg is the marker against Luther. It is not a claim that the Reformed were internally uniform.

## Zurich: a council, a liturgy, a battlefield

Zwingli was the people's priest at the Grossmünster. Zurich's changes were argued before the council and enacted by it. Images were removed under civic control rather than as a standing right of any crowd. In 1525, the year the Peasants' War was being fought and condemned in the empire, Zurich replaced the mass with a reformed Lord's Supper. The same year belongs to two constitutions. In the empire, evangelical language met lords and was put down, as [[reformations/luther]] recounts. In Zurich, a council abolished the central rite of the old church and set up a court for marriage and morals. The coincidence of the year is not a common cause. It is a warning against one timeline of "the Reformation".

The countryside of the canton did not simply agree. Zurich ruled subject communities. A city reformation can be urban at the centre and imposed at the edge. That limit matters when the same city tries to spread its reform into territories shared with Catholic cantons. The Swiss Confederation was a league, not a principality and not an empire in miniature. Catholic cantons remained Catholic. Pressure from Zurich produced war.

:::theorem Marburg divides Lutheran from Reformed {#thm-marburg-split}
The colloquy at Marburg in 1529 is the doctrinal marker between Luther and the Reformed. They did not agree on the eucharist. A shared rejection of the pope, or a shared use of the word Protestant, does not repair that disagreement.
:::
:::proof
[[reformations/luther]] states the meeting: Philip of Hesse wanted an alliance, and the Supper stopped it. Luther held a real bodily presence. Zwingli did not. Calvin was not at Marburg. His later teaching still belongs on Zwingli's side of that divide and not on Luther's, even where Calvin's sentences are fuller than Zwingli's. The evidence is the failed agreement, not a mood. What would count against [[#thm-marburg-split]] is a text in which Luther accepts a merely significative Supper, or Zwingli accepts Luther's presence, or a Reformed confession that treats the Marburg article as closed in Luther's favour. The Zurich church under Zwingli's successor Heinrich Bullinger did not become Lutheran. The split outlived the colloquy.
:::

In 1531 Zwingli was killed at Kappel. He had gone with Zurich's army. The defeat ended the attempt to force the reform on other cantons by war. Catholic worship remained in the confederacy. A preacher's death did not dissolve the city's church. Bullinger succeeded him and kept the Zurich order for a long tenure we do not date here. The institution, not the personality, is the survivor. Kappel is also the answer to a romantic reading of city reform: a council can lose a battle, and the liturgy does not protect the chaplain.

:::example The same year, two polities {#ex-1525}
In 1525 Zurich's council replaces the mass. In 1525 the Peasants' War is under way in the German lands, and Luther condemns the rebels. Why is it a mistake to call 1525 "the year of the Reformation"?
:::solution
The year is shared and the constitutions are not. Zurich's act is a council changing a city's rite, including a subject countryside that did not all ask for the change. The Peasants' War is a rising against lords, defeated, and disowned by Luther. One label would hide a liturgy, a social war, and a condemnation inside one slogan. From the theses in 1517 to this year the difference of the year numbers is 8. Those eight years contain Worms, the rising, and Zurich's Supper. They do not contain one event.
:::
:::

## The book of 1536 and the return of 1541

:::example Two years from Marburg to Kappel {#ex-kappel-span}
Marburg is 1529. Zwingli is killed at Kappel in 1531. How many years is the difference of the year numbers, and why does the short gap matter?
:::solution
1531 minus 1529 is 2. The doctrinal split and the military defeat are not a long maturation of one Swiss story. Zurich had already replaced the mass in 1525, four years before Marburg. The death does not found the reform and does not restore the mass in Zurich. Bullinger's leadership is the evidence that the institution survived the chaplain. The number of years is 2.
:::
:::

The first edition of Calvin's *Institutes* appeared in 1536. It is a book: a short Latin sum of doctrine, with a prefatory address to the French king defending the evangelical cause. We paraphrase that purpose. We do not quote the preface. The book is not a city. Geneva had its own break with its bishop, already accomplished when Calvin was pressed to stay and work. The council later expelled him. In 1541 the city called him back. From 1536 to 1541 the difference of the year numbers is 5. The book circulates while the pastor is out of office. That gap is the cleanest distinction between a text and an institution.

On his return the city adopted ecclesiastical ordinances. Four offices organise the church: pastors, who preach and administer the sacraments; doctors, who teach; elders, drawn from the civil councils; and deacons, who care for the poor. The consistory is pastors and elders together. It meets to examine doctrine and morals. Its own instruments are admonition and exclusion from the Lord's Supper. Matters the city treats as crimes go to the councils, which remain the government. This chapter stops at that line. It does not describe criminal penalties. Anyone who thinks the Reformed Reformation is a list of punishments has substituted a spectacle for an office.

:::proposition Council, liturgy, discipline {#prop-institutions}
A city reformation of the Zurich or Geneva kind is those three things: a council that decides, a liturgy that replaces the mass, and a discipline shared between ministers and lay elders. It is not the rule of a pastor in place of the magistrate, and it is not the Augsburg rule of a prince.
:::
:::proof
Zurich shows the council deciding the rite, in 1525, and making war, in 1531, with the preacher killed in the council's army. Geneva shows ordinances in 1541 that seat elders from the councils inside the consistory and leave civil jurisdiction with the councils. Calvin wanted the Supper more often than the council would grant. The refusal is evidence that the pastor did not command the calendar. What would count against [[#prop-institutions]] is a minute-book in which pastors tax, condemn criminal cases, and declare war with no council. That is not the Genevan or the Zurich record. What would also count against it is a city that adopted Calvin's book and left the mass, the images, and the old clergy untouched. The liturgy is part of the definition, not an optional style.
:::

The company of pastors examined ministers and sent them out, including to France. Geneva thus became a nursery of Reformed churches that were not themselves city-republics. That export is why the French wars in [[reformations/religious-war]] are Reformed wars, not Lutheran ones. In 1559 the city opened an academy to train that ministry. The same year, Elizabeth's parliament in England passed two acts of a quite different constitution, taught in [[reformations/england]]. A shared year is not a shared church order. The long edition of the *Institutes* also belongs to 1559. We still take 1536 as the first edition and 1541 as the return. A book that grows is not a change of regime.

:::example From the first *Institutes* to the return {#ex-span-geneva}
The first edition of the *Institutes* is 1536. Calvin returns to Geneva in 1541. How many years is the difference, and what does the gap show about book and city? (The difference is the figure to keep.)
:::solution
1541 minus 1536 is 5. During those years the book exists and the reformer is not in office, because the council that had used him had also expelled him. The return in 1541 is a city's decision, followed by ordinances. Five years is enough to separate a publication from a government. The number of years is 5.
:::
:::

## Augsburg is the contrast, not the model

On 25 September 1555 the Peace of Augsburg let Lutheran and Catholic princes in the empire settle the public religion. The formula *cuius regio, eius religio* — whose realm, his religion — is the shorthand for that princely choice. It is not a modern toleration. It does not include Calvinists. Zurich and Geneva were not princes of that peace, and the Reformed churches were not a party to it. A city council adopting a discipline in 1541 is a different legal object from an elector choosing Lutheran or Catholic in 1555.

The omission was not a theoretical quibble. In 1563, the year the Council of Trent ended, a Reformed catechism was published for the Palatinate, a principality inside the empire. From 1555 to 1563 is 8 years. A Reformed confession could exist in the empire before imperial law named it. The peace that finally admits Calvinists is 1648, not 1555. That admission is the work of [[reformations/religious-war]]. Here Augsburg serves only to stop a false generalisation: do not describe Zurich or Geneva as an early draft of a prince's religion.

:::corollary Outside the princely bargain {#cor-augsburg}
The Peace of Augsburg cannot be used as the pattern of Zurich or Geneva. It names Lutheran and Catholic princes. It does not name these councils, and it does not name the Reformed.
:::
:::proof
The corollary is the contrast the chronology itself draws. Augsburg's formula is for Lutheran and Catholic princes. Calvinists are not included. Zurich's reform was enacted by a Swiss council and limited by defeat at Kappel in 1531, twenty-four years before the peace. Geneva's ordinances are 1541, fourteen years before it. What would count against the corollary is a clause of the 1555 peace that ratifies city consistories, or that includes the Reformed by name. There is no such clause. Later readers who file every non-Roman church under one Protestant type are using a word from Speyer to erase a peace that was more precise than they are.
:::

:::example An invented merger {#ex-invented-merger}
The following sentence is invented, and it is not a source. "After Marburg, Luther and Calvin signed one Protestant confession, which the Peace of Augsburg enacted for every city." Which clauses fail, and against which dates?
:::solution
Marburg is 1529, and it failed on the eucharist. Calvin was not a party to it. The first *Institutes* are 1536, and the return to Geneva is 1541, so "after Marburg" cannot be a joint signing with Luther of one confession that includes Geneva's order. Augsburg is 25 September 1555, and it enacts a princely choice between Lutheran and Catholic only. It does not enact a city discipline, and it does not include the Reformed. Every clause of the invented sentence joins things the dates keep apart.
:::

## A worked case: two authorities in one city

Consider a townsman of Geneva after 1541. The name is unnecessary, and any anecdote of a particular sinner would be invented, so we keep the case institutional. He can be summoned by the consistory for staying away from preaching, for a public quarrel, or for contempt of the Supper. The consistory admonishes him. It may bar him from the Supper until he satisfies the church. If the matter is also a crime under the city's law, it goes to the council. Two records, two authorities. The pastor is not the sovereign. The elder beside him is a layman sent by the councils. The liturgy he is accused of neglecting is the public worship the council has established in place of the mass.

What the case will not do is follow him into a criminal court and describe a penalty. That refusal is a rule of this chapter, not a gap in the archives. Criminal justice existed. Describing it would turn a lesson about offices into a lesson about pain, and it would let one city's severity stand for every Reformed church. Zurich's morals court is not Geneva's consistory in every detail. French congregations under a Catholic king, supplied with pastors from Geneva, could not be Genevan republics at all. The exportable parts were the eldership, the discipline, and the doctrine of the Supper. The gallows were not exportable, because they were the city's.

What would break the case? Evidence that exclusion from the Supper was only a private snub with no public worship to be excluded from, or evidence that the consistory was a committee of pastors with no elders from the councils. The ordinances of the return in 1541 are drawn the other way: public liturgy, mixed consistory, civil government retained. Kappel is the Zurich version of the same lesson. The council made the war. The theologian died in it.

## Objections

The first objection says "Protestant" and stops. Speyer in 1529 gave the word a political birth, and Marburg in the same year showed that the men who might have shared it did not share a Supper. Augsburg in 1555 then drew a legal circle around Lutheran and Catholic only. A lecturer who answers "how did Protestantism win?" has asked a question this chapter refuses. Which Protestantism, in which constitution, recognised by which law?

The second objection says Geneva was a theocracy, meaning that Calvin was the state. The word saves a reader from learning the ordinances. Pastors did not hold the criminal sword. They sat with elders from the councils. They lost arguments about how often to hold the Supper. Calvin was expelled once and recalled once, which is a strange sovereignty. His force of character can be granted without being narrated. Character does not pass a law. What would make the theocracy claim true is a city with no council beside the pastor. Geneva was the opposite arrangement: a council that hired, fired, and rehired him.

The third objection treats Zwingli's death as the end of the Reformed experiment, or as proof that city reform was a personal adventure. Kappel killed the man in 1531 and limited Zurich's wars. It did not restore the mass in Zurich. Bullinger's long leadership is the counter-evidence, and so is Geneva's separate path from 1541, a decade after Kappel. From 1531 to 1541 is 10 years. The city reformation is a type, not a biography.

:::warning One Protestantism, and the scaffold as the story
Reformed and Lutheran are not one church that happens to argue about a ceremony. Marburg in 1529 is the divide. Augsburg in 1555 leaves the Reformed outside the imperial peace. The other mistake is to define Geneva by punishments. This chapter will not describe them. The offices are the consistory, the company of pastors, the liturgy, and the councils. A list of penalties is not a church order.
:::

## Where this leads

Catholic reform is the other half of the century, and it is not only a reply to these cities: [[reformations/catholic-reform]]. The imperial peace that left Calvinists out in 1555, and the French wars of congregations linked to Geneva, are [[reformations/religious-war]]. England's statutes are a third way of joining crown and church, in [[reformations/england]]. The Wittenberg story these cities are not is [[reformations/luther]]. A reader who keeps Marburg and the consistory can no longer hear "Protestant" as one thing.

:::summary
- Zurich and Geneva were council reformations: a liturgy, a discipline, and a civil government. They were not Wittenberg under an elector.
- In 1525 Zurich replaced the mass. The same year is the Peasants' War in the empire. One year is not one Reformation.
- Marburg in 1529 is the eucharistic split between Luther and Zwingli. Reformed is not a synonym for Lutheran or for Protestant.
- Zwingli was killed at Kappel in 1531. The city's church continued under Bullinger. A personality is not the institution.
- The first *Institutes* are 1536. Calvin returned to Geneva in 1541. The book and the office are five years apart.
- Geneva's consistory admonished and could bar people from the Supper. The councils kept civil jurisdiction. This chapter does not describe punishments.
- The Peace of Augsburg on 25 September 1555 is a contrast. It covers Lutheran and Catholic princes and omits the Reformed.
:::

:::exercise The theocracy objection {level=3}
Objection: Geneva was a theocracy, so the institutions are a disguise for personal rule. State the objection fairly, then say what evidence keeps the ordinances from being a disguise.

Use the expulsion and the return, the presence of elders from the councils, and the council's control of how often the Supper was held. Do not answer with a list of penalties.

:::solution
The fair statement is that Calvin's personal authority was real, that Geneva was famous for severity, and that a strong minister can dominate a small city on paper that still names a council. If the minutes were only a costume, "theocracy" would be the honest short word.

The ordinances keep the word from being the whole truth. Elders sat with pastors and were drawn from the councils. The consistory's church censure was not the criminal court. The council expelled Calvin and, in 1541, called him back, which is the act of an employer, not of a subject. It also refused him the frequency of communion he wanted. What would make the objection win is evidence of a pastor legislating, taxing, and judging crime with no council beside him. The record of 1541 is the contrary arrangement. Severity, whatever its degree, was the city's as well as the minister's, and this lesson does not describe it.
