How did England's settlements depend on the monarch and on parliament? They depended on both, and on the order of succession between them. In 1534 parliament named Henry VIII supreme head of the Church of England. The dissolution of the monasteries was a process, for which 1536 is a marker and not the whole. His son Edward, a minor, moved worship further in a Protestant direction. Mary, Edward's successor and Catherine of Aragon's daughter, restored the mass and papal obedience. This atlas does not date her accession. In 1559 Elizabeth's parliament passed a new Act of Supremacy and an Act of Uniformity. That sequence is statute and succession. It is not a national conversion, and it is not a step on a road to toleration.

A statute is not a diary of a parish. Eamon Duffy's *The Stripping of the Altars*, a book of 1992, is the warning against writing the English story only from London. Parish practice was slower, more attached to the old rites, and less interested in a theologian's timetable than a history of acts suggests. Historians disagree about how far, and how fast, parishes changed. The disagreement is a reason to keep the statutes and the parishes in different sentences.

## A supremacy is not a confession

The Act of Supremacy of 1534 transferred the highest jurisdiction in the English Church from the pope to the crown. It was a parliamentary act. Henry wanted an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, the emperor's aunt, so that a new marriage could settle the succession. After the sack of Rome in 1527, Clement VII was badly placed to defy Charles V. The statute is what the king could get from a parliament when he could not get it from a pope. It made him supreme head. It did not publish a Lutheran confession. Under Henry the mass, in large part, remained. Jurisdictional change ran ahead of doctrinal change.

Thomas More and John Fisher were killed for refusing the supremacy. They were not Protestants. Their deaths show that the settlement was enforced, and that refusal of the royal headship was not the same thing as joining Luther. We give no year for the killings and describe no method. The fact that matters here is the category: a Catholic conscience could reject the act without accepting Wittenberg.

:::definition Royal supremacy {#def-supremacy}
The **royal supremacy** is the claim, enacted in 1534, that the king is the supreme head of the Church of England, and, in the act of 1559, that the queen is its supreme governor. It is a jurisdictional statute. It is not a doctrinal confession, not a vote of the nation, and not a permission for subjects to choose their own religion.
:::

Parliament's role does not make the change popular. An English parliament was summoned by the monarch. It was lords and commons of a realm, not a democracy. The parliament of 1265, often dragged into a later story of English liberty, was a baronial assembly in a civil war. The parliament of 1534 is a king's instrument that is still the only way an English statute exists. Both facts are required. Without the king there is no supremacy. Without the statute there is no English form of it. A German prince under the Peace of Augsburg, and a council in Geneva, did their religious work by other laws. The widget in this chapter puts Augsburg on the same line so that 1534 and 1559 are not mistaken for the centre of Europe.

## 1536 marks a process

Monasteries were not closed on one morning. An act associated with 1536 began the suppression of the smaller houses. The greater houses followed over the years after. We do not date those later waves with a numeral this chronology has not locked. Lands and goods passed to the crown and, much of them, onward to lay buyers. A class of landowners acquired a material stake in the break with Rome. That stake later limited every attempt to put the monasteries back. Parish churches were not monasteries. Taking a house's land is not the same act as taking down a parish rood screen. The second change was slower, uneven, and often resented.

In 1536 a rising in the north, the Pilgrimage of Grace, opposed the dissolution and other grievances. It was suppressed. The rising is evidence that the realm did not experience the marker year as a willing conversion. It is also evidence that opposition, even armed opposition, did not write the settlement. The statutes remained. The houses continued to fall.

:::theorem A marker, not the day {#thm-marker}
1536 is a marker in the dissolution of the English monasteries. It is not the day monastic life ended, and it is not a measure of what parish religion had lost.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the shape of the process: a statute against smaller houses, further closures afterwards, and a parish religion that Duffy and others find still furnished, still ritual, and still attached to prayers for the dead. What would count against [[#thm-marker]] is a single instrument of 1536 that dissolved every house and emptied every parish church on one date, with inventories to match. The inventories that exist show a sequence, not a flash. What would also count against it is evidence that the Pilgrimage of Grace never happened, or that it was a small quarrel about something else. It was large enough to show dissent, and it was a rising of 1536, the marker year itself. The year can be both a statute and a rebellion. It cannot be a conversion.
:::

:::example What one year cannot hold {#ex-1536}
A chronicle sentence, invented and labelled as such, says: "In 1536 England closed its monasteries, stripped its parish altars, and became Protestant." Which clauses can you save, and which must you refuse?
:::solution
You can save a marker: 1536 is a real stage in the dissolution, especially the smaller houses. You cannot save "closed its monasteries" as if the process finished that year. You cannot save the stripping of parish altars, which is a longer and later pressure on a different kind of church, the subject of Duffy's book of 1992. You cannot save "became Protestant". Henry's supremacy was jurisdictional. The mass largely remained. Protestant worship belongs to his son's reign and, after Mary's reversal, to the acts of 1559. The invented sentence puts three tempos into one year.
:::
:::

## Succession is the plot

Edward's reign, which this atlas does not date, was a minority. Councillors advanced a Protestant reform Henry had not enacted: worship in English, pressure on altars, and a book of common prayer imposed by authority. This lesson does not quote the book. The direction is enough. A child king and a council are not a nation arriving at a conclusion. They are a regime using statute while it holds the crown.

Mary came next. Her reign restored the mass and reunion with Rome, and she married Philip of Spain. Monastic land, in large part, stayed with its new owners, so the restoration was already compromised by the dissolution it could not afford to undo. Her government burned people for heresy. This atlas states no number and describes no method. The burnings became, later, a Protestant memory. They were also a policy. Catholic restoration was coercive, just as the supremacy had been coercive. Mary is not an interruption on a road that had already been chosen. She is evidence that a different succession produced a different settlement. Leave her out, or date her with a year the chronology has not locked, and the story becomes a straight line from Henry to Elizabeth. It was not a straight line.

:::definition Settlement {#def-settlement}
A **settlement**, in this chapter, is a religious order enacted by parliament under a particular monarch: what public worship is required, and who holds the supreme jurisdiction. The settlements of Henry, of Edward, of Mary, and of Elizabeth in 1559 are successive. They are not stages of one conversion, and they are not tolerations.
:::

In 1559 parliament passed two acts. The Act of Supremacy named Elizabeth supreme governor rather than supreme head. The change of title is jurisdictional language, and it avoided a claim that had troubled some reformers: whether a lay monarch, and a woman, could be "head" of the church in a sacramental sense. Governor says who holds the crown's authority over the church. It does not describe the belief of a parish. The Act of Uniformity required a book of common prayer and attendance at the parish service. Absence could be punished. Later statutes raised that cost. The direction is towards a heavier conformity, not towards toleration. We do not quote the prayer book, and we do not invent a sentence from it.

John Foxe's book of martyrs, a work of 1563, taught many later readers to see Mary's burnings as the meaning of her reign. From 1559 to 1563 is 4 years. Memory organised itself quickly. Memory is not a census. Recusants refused the parish service and remained Catholic. Church papists conformed in the church and kept Catholic practice at home. People called Puritans wanted the settlement to go further against vestments and ceremonies. Three kinds of dissatisfaction fit inside one statutory church. None of them is a permission to worship as one likes. None of them is a Protestant nation in the sense of a people that had changed its mind together.

:::theorem Succession and statute, not conversion {#thm-succession}
England's religious changes from the Act of Supremacy of 1534 to the acts of 1559 follow the monarch and the statutes parliament passed under that monarch. They are not evidence of a national conversion, and they are not a step on a road to toleration.
:::
:::proof
The sequence is the evidence. Henry's act changes jurisdiction and leaves much of the mass in place. Edward's regime, undated here, pushes Protestant worship. Mary's regime reverses it and punishes Protestants. Elizabeth's two acts of 1559 establish a third order, supreme governor and a required prayer book, with penalties for absence. A national conversion would show one direction and a parish religion that matches the statute. Duffy's parish evidence is the counter-evidence: practice lags, resists, and splits. A road to toleration would show each act wider than the last. Mary's burnings and Elizabeth's uniformity are narrower demands, enforced. What would count against [[#thm-succession]] is a settlement that survived a change of monarch unchanged, or a statute of 1559 that legalised Catholic, Puritan, and prayer-book worship side by side. The reigns undid each other. The act of 1559 chose one public order.
:::

:::proposition Not a national identity {#prop-identity}
The acts of 1559 are not the founding fact of a Protestant national identity. Identity is a later reading of statutes, of Foxe's book of 1563, and of conflicts still to come. The fact available in 1559 is two acts of parliament.
:::
:::proof
A fact about identity would be a description of what people believed, measured in more than one county, and stable under the next crisis. What 1559 records is what a parliament required. Recusants, church papists, and Puritans are the contemporary evidence that requirement and belief were not the same. The Armada of 1588, in [[reformations/religious-war]], is often recruited as the moment a Protestant nation discovered itself. Even that reading is an interpretation of a later year, and the Armada does not by itself make England a great power. What would count against the proposition is evidence that in 1559 the parishes of the realm already matched the prayer book in practice and in conviction, with no recusant problem and no Puritan agitation. The parish records Duffy used are why historians do not grant that picture. Disagreement about the pace does not restore the picture.
:::

## Beside the empire, and below the statute

On 25 September 1555 the Peace of Augsburg let a Lutheran or Catholic prince settle the public religion of his lands. Elizabeth's acts are four years later. The year-number difference from 1555 to 1559 is 4. The comparison is the point of putting Augsburg on this chapter's timeline. A German prince did not need an English parliament. An English queen did not, by those acts, join the imperial peace or include Calvinism as Augsburg had failed to do. Geneva's councils, from 1541, were a third constitution again. Three legal forms can all be called reformations. Only one of them is a supremacy passed by parliament. England is not the pattern of the others, and it is not their destination.

Scotland's break with Rome is another polity's story, and this atlas does not lock its year. Ireland was not described by an English statute about an English parish. The settlements in this chapter are English. Stretching them into a British or Irish conversion is the same mistake as stretching them into a European one, at a smaller scale.

:::example Twenty-five years with a reign inside them {#ex-span-mary}
From 1534 to 1559 the difference of the year numbers is 25. Mary's reign sits inside that span. Why must the arithmetic not become a story of steady Protestant advance?
:::solution
25 is 1559 minus 1534. Inside it, Edward's undated reign pushes Protestant worship, and Mary's undated reign restores the mass, papal obedience, and the burning of Protestants. The chronology lets you name her and forbids you to invent an accession year. Steady advance would require each step to keep what the previous step had built. Mary demolished the Edwardian order. Elizabeth's acts of 1559 did not restore Henry's mass or Edward's book unchanged. They made a new statutory order. The number 25 measures a gap. It does not measure a slope. The number of years is 25.
:::
:::

:::example A statute beside a peace {#ex-beside-augsburg}
Set the Act of Supremacy of 1534 beside the Peace of Augsburg of 25 September 1555. What can the pair show, and what can it not show?
:::solution
Both are public religious settlements made by political authority. The English one is a parliamentary statute naming a royal head of the church, twenty-one years before Augsburg. The imperial one is a peace between estates, letting each Lutheran or Catholic prince choose the public religion, and omitting Calvinists. The pair shows that "reformation" had more than one constitution. It cannot show that England led the empire, or that Augsburg copied the supremacy, or that either act was a toleration. From 1534 to 1555 is 21 years. Distance on the timeline is not influence.
:::
:::

:::example Not a Protestant martyr {#ex-more}
An invented caption says: "Thomas More, Protestant martyr, died for the prayer book of 1559." What is wrong with every clause?
:::solution
More was killed for refusing Henry's supremacy. He was a Catholic, not a Protestant. The lesson gives no year and no method for that death, and it does not move him to Elizabeth's reign. The prayer book required in 1559 is a generation after the supremacy of 1534. From 1534 to 1559 is 25 years. More did not die for a book that belonged to a later settlement, and he did not die as a Protestant. The caption has changed both the man and the statute.
:::

## A worked case: an invented parish

Call the place Ashwick. The name is invented, and so is every detail that follows. It is a model, not evidence. In 1534 the supremacy is the law and the mass is still said. The priest has not become a Lutheran. In 1536 a small religious house nearby is under threat. Ashwick's parish church is not that house. The rood is still up. People still leave money for prayers. Under Mary, with no year attached, the mass is required again and a neighbour who had welcomed Edward's book keeps quiet or flees. In 1559 a prayer book is required. Some families conform. One household stays away. The churchwardens' answers to a visitation, which we are inventing and therefore cannot cite, would be cautious.

What would promote Ashwick into evidence is a real village's churchwardens' accounts, wills, and visitation presentments, read against the statutes rather than deduced from them. That is the method of Duffy's book. [[sources/evidence]] is the general rule: a source is not the inference. [[sources/scale]] is the special rule: a local fact can be true while the national sentence drawn from it is false, and an invented village proves nothing until it stops being invented. The case's only job is to slow the reader down. London passed an act. Ashwick, even as a fiction, does not change religion at the speed of the printing of the act.

## Objections

The first objection says that parliament's part makes the English Reformation popular. It confuses a representative institution of a monarchy with the population. Parliament was summoned, managed, and still indispensable. The Pilgrimage of Grace is the population, or a large part of the north, and it was not the author of the statutes. City reformations on the continent look more like civic participation than an English statute does. If "popular" means "enacted by parliament", say parliament. If it means "wanted in the parishes", the parish evidence is the argument against.

The second objection says Henry founded a Protestant nation in 1534. He founded a royal supremacy. Protestant doctrine advanced under Edward, was reversed under Mary, and was resettled in 1559 in a form Henry did not write. A nation, if the word means a people identified by one religion, is not a fact these statutes can carry. [[#prop-identity]] is the reply. The Armada, the Dutch war, and Foxe's readers built a story later. The story can be studied. It cannot be moved back to the day of the act.

:::warning Not the birth of a Protestant nation
1534 is not the conversion of England. 1536 is not the end of parish religion. 1559 is not toleration, and it is not the moment a Protestant national identity becomes a fact. Mary's reign, which you may name and must not date with an invented year, stands in the middle and points the other way. Duffy's warning remains: statutes passed in London are not a diary of what a parish believed.
:::

## Where this leads

The European wars into which Elizabeth's England was drawn, including the Armada of 1588, are [[reformations/religious-war]]. The Catholic reform Mary belonged to, without being a session of the council, is [[reformations/catholic-reform]]. Wittenberg and Geneva are the other constitutions this settlement is not: [[reformations/luther]] and [[reformations/reformed-cities]]. How to read a parish source against a statute is [[sources/evidence]] and [[sources/scale]]. A different succession would have produced a different statute. That is the whole political moral of the chapter, and it is not a moral about which church was right.

:::summary
- In 1534 the Act of Supremacy made Henry VIII supreme head by statute. It was jurisdiction, not a Lutheran confession, and not a national conversion.
- More and Fisher were killed for refusing that headship. They were not Protestants. No method and no year are supplied.
- The dissolution is a process. 1536 marks the smaller houses and the Pilgrimage of Grace. It does not empty the parishes.
- Edward's undated reign pushed Protestant worship. Mary's undated reign restored the mass and papal obedience and burned Protestants. No death total is stated.
- In 1559 Elizabeth's Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity made her supreme governor and required a prayer book. That is not toleration.
- Duffy's *The Stripping of the Altars* (1992) warns against reading London's statutes as parish belief. Practice was slower.
- These acts are not the founding fact of a Protestant national identity. Identity is a later reading.
:::

:::exercise Head and governor {level=2}
Why does it matter that the act of 1559 says supreme governor and the act of 1534 says supreme head?
:::solution
Both titles are jurisdictional, but they are not identical. Head, taken by Henry, could be heard as a claim about who stands at the top of the church in a sacramental sense, and some reformers doubted that a lay monarch could be that. Governor names the crown's authority over the church without repeating that claim. Neither title is a confession of the eucharist, and neither is a description of what a parish believed. The change shows that 1559 was a new statute, not a reprint of 1534.
:::

:::exercise Why the land mattered {level=2}
How did the alienation of monastic land limit Mary's Catholic restoration, even though you may not date her reign?
:::solution
From the marker of 1536 onward, monastic land passed to the crown and largely to lay owners. Those owners had a stake in not giving it back. Mary's restoration of the mass and of papal obedience therefore met a property settlement she could not simply undo, and in large part the land stayed put. The limit is structural. It does not need her accession year. It does need the dissolution to be understood as a process with beneficiaries, not as a purely spiritual gesture.
:::
:::

:::exercise Duffy's warning in one parish question {level=2}
You are shown only the Act of Uniformity of 1559 and asked what Ashwick believed that Sunday. Why is the question badly formed, even before you remember that Ashwick is invented?
:::solution
A statute says what authority required. It does not say what a congregation did, or believed, that week. Churchwardens' accounts, wills, and visitations are the kind of source that could speak to practice, and even they need interpretation. Duffy's argument is that those sources show traditional religion lasting longer, and being stripped from above, rather than fading because a nation had changed its mind. Ashwick adds a further ban: an invented village is a model of the tempo, not a source. The question treats a London act as a local mind.
:::

:::exercise Identity as a fact {level=3}
A museum caption says: "1559: the birth of Protestant England." Defend the caption's grain of truth, then say why this chapter still rejects it as a statement of fact.
:::hint
Separate a statutory church from a national identity. Use Mary, the recusants, and the gap between statute and parish. Do not invent a prayer-book sentence or an accession year.

:::solution
The grain of truth is that 1559 really did settle a public order different from Rome and different from Mary's restoration: a supreme governor and a required English prayer book, passed by parliament. Later conflicts, including the Armada of 1588, were lived by many people inside that order, and Foxe's book of 1563 had already begun to narrate Mary's reign as martyrdom. A tradition of Protestant England has materials.

It is not a fact about 1559 itself. The acts bind. They do not describe belief. Recusants and church papists remained. Puritans thought the settlement had not gone far enough. Parish change was slower than the statute, which is Duffy's warning. Mary had just shown that succession can reverse a religious policy, so 1559 is another succession's policy, not the unveiling of a nation that had been Protestant underneath. The caption would become a fact only if identity meant "the legal public church", in which case it should say that, and if the parishes already matched it. They are two sentences. The museum has glued them together.
:::

:::exercise Three constitutions {level=3}
Compare the English acts of 1559, the Peace of Augsburg of 1555, and Geneva's ordinances of 1541 as three ways of settling religion. What mistake does a single word, "Reformation", invite?
:::hint
For each, name who decides and what is decided. Then say which of the three includes Calvinists, which omits them, and which is a city.
:::
:::solution
In 1541 Geneva's councils recall a minister and adopt ordinances: pastors and elders, a liturgy in place of the mass, civil power still with the council. The decision is civic, and the doctrine is Reformed. In 1555 Augsburg lets a prince of the empire choose a Lutheran or a Catholic public religion and does not include Calvinists. The decision is princely and imperial. In 1559 an English parliament under Elizabeth requires one prayer book and names a supreme governor. The decision is statutory and monarchical, and it is not a clause of the imperial peace.

"Reformation" invites the mistake of treating these as three drafts of one event, usually with England as the liberal end of the series. They are three constitutions. None is a toleration. None makes the other inevitable, and none is the centre of the timeline except by the accident of which chapter one is reading. The widget puts Augsburg beside the English dates in order to block that accident.
:::
