Who was included when politics became mass politics?

Mass politics means that large numbers of people are addressed as a public, by a franchise, a party, a church, a press or a league, whether or not they can vote. It does not mean that democracy had been achieved. By 1914 it had not. The second and third British Reform Acts, in 1867 and 1884, widened a male franchise and left women out. The Paris Commune of March to May 1871 was a city government, and it was destroyed. Antisemitism in these decades was a political movement, and the Dreyfus affair is the case, not the origin of 1933. On 22 January 1905, New Style, a petition in St Petersburg was met with violence. In 1906, in the Grand Duchy of Finland, women gained the parliamentary vote and the right to stand. That is a European parliamentary first. It is not the first women's suffrage in the world, and it is not what the British acts had done.

The states made in [[nations/unifications]] are the containers for some of this. A proclamation is not an electorate. The census in [[nations/industry]] counts presence, not a ballot.

## What "included" cannot be allowed to mean

:::definition Mass politics {#def-mass}
**Mass politics** is the practice of treating large numbers of people as a public that can be enrolled, addressed, or shut out. The instruments include franchises, parties, churches, newspapers and leagues. The phrase does not mean that every adult chose the government, and it does not mean that inclusion was the only thing mass politics did.
:::

A public can be addressed in order to be enfranchised. It can also be addressed in order to be blamed. The same decades hold reform acts and antisemitic leagues. Anyone who defines the period as the arrival of the people has already deleted half the archive. The test, for each dated act, is who is inside the rule and who is outside it.

:::definition Political antisemitism {#def-antisemitism}
**Antisemitism**, as a political movement in these decades, is an organised claim that Jews are a collective enemy of the nation, carried by press, leagues and electoral politics. It is not only a private dislike, and it is not, in this lesson, the origin of the appointment of Hitler as chancellor on 30 January 1933.
:::

:::theorem The British Reform Acts widened a male franchise and did not include women {#thm-reform}
The Act of 1832 was a first widening and remained narrow. The Act of 1867 and the Act of 1884 widened the male franchise further, in boroughs and then in counties. Women are not included in any of the three. A larger male electorate is not democracy, and it is not the Finnish franchise of 1906.
:::
:::proof
The chronology states the three statutes and states that women are not included in 1867 or 1884. 1832 is already taught in [[revolutions/restoration-and-1848]] as a narrow franchise. No figure for the size of the electorate is locked, so none is invented. What the acts show is a direction: more men, under a property or household rule, and not women. What would count against the claim is a clause of 1867 or of 1884, in force, that gave women the parliamentary vote. There is none. What would also count against treating the acts as democracy is the Finnish statute of 1906, which did give women the parliamentary vote and the right to stand, and which Britain had not copied. The contrast is inside the locked years. It does not need a guess about how many men still could not vote, though the household rules themselves say the widening was not universal.
:::

From 1867 to 1884 is 1884 minus 1867, which is 17 years. Seventeen years of further reform, and the exclusion of women is intact at the end of them. The year 1867 is also the year of the Ausgleich. [[nations/unifications]] uses it for a Hungarian coronation. This chapter uses it for a British statute. Same year, two inclusions, neither of them a women's franchise, and neither of them a Czech kingdom. A date is not a synonym.

The third act extends a household franchise into the counties. It does not, on any date locked here, abolish plural voting or give the vote to women. The French republic of the late century rests on a wide male franchise whose revolutionary widening belongs with 1848, and it too leaves women out. In the German empire the Reichstag franchise and the Prussian franchise are not the same instrument. The Prussian vote was weighted. This lesson does not invent the weights. It is enough that a man could be inside one German electorate and not inside the other on equal terms, and that neither rule is the Finnish one.

:::example Seventeen years, and the same exclusion {#ex-seventeen}
The second Reform Act is 1867 and the third is 1884. Women enter the British parliamentary franchise in neither. How long is the gap in the year-labels, and what does the gap not soften?
:::solution
1884 minus 1867 is 17. The gap is not a pause before inclusion. At the later date the exclusion still holds. Finland's parliamentary franchise for women, including the right to stand, is 1906, which is 1906 minus 1884, or 22 years after the third British act, and it is a different state's rule. The British gap of 17 years is evidence of reform without women, not evidence that reform and inclusion are the same word.
:::
:::

- [ ] 1867 gave the vote to every adult in the United Kingdom
- [x] 1867 and 1884 widened a male franchise, and women were not included

:::solution

## A city, a party, a church

The Paris Commune lasts from March to May 1871. It is a government in the city during the aftermath of the war that [[nations/unifications]] ends, on the French side, with the Treaty of Frankfurt on 10 May. The treaty's day falls inside the Commune's months. The German Empire has already been proclaimed, on 18 January, before the Commune begins. Three facts share a season and do not share a meaning: an empire announced, a peace signed, a municipal revolution crushed.

The Commune acted as a city government and passed social measures. This lesson does not invent the text of a programme. Women took part in the political life of the city. That participation was not a durable national franchise, and it did not survive the Commune's defeat. The defeat was violent. No death total is stated, because none is locked, and a guessed total would be the wrong kind of precision. The Commune is not the proof that socialism must take the form of a city in arms, and it is not a mere crime story told to embarrass a doctrine. It is a dated episode of mass politics in which a public held a city and was then shut out of it.

Socialist parties belong to the same decades, and they are not the franchise either. The Communist Manifesto is published in London in February 1848. Parties that claim to speak for labour organise later, sit in some parliaments, and publish programmes. This lesson does not quote those programmes. A party can be large and still be harassed, excluded from the executive, or opposed by a weighted suffrage. Organisation is inclusion in one sense, the sense of [[#def-mass]]. It is not the sense in which Finland, in 1906, changes who may vote and who may stand.

Churches are mass institutions in the same plain meaning. They enrol, teach, publish and, where the law allows, elect. Catholic and Protestant associations are part of political life, not a residue left over after politics became modern. In the new German empire the state's conflict with the Catholic Church is itself politics. The lesson does not date the individual statutes of that conflict, because those days are not locked. It says the conflict is public. Belief, as [[sources/evidence]] would insist, is not the same thing as a ministry's policy. A church that can fill a hall is a fact about organisation. It is not a census of souls.

## Dreyfus, as a case and not as an origin

:::theorem The Dreyfus affair is a case of political antisemitism, not the origin of 1933 {#thm-dreyfus}
Dreyfus is arrested in 1894, Zola's article titled J'Accuse is published on 13 January 1898, and Dreyfus is exonerated in 1906. The affair shows a mass politics of press and league directed against a Jewish officer. It does not cause, and it is not the origin of, Hitler's appointment as chancellor on 30 January 1933.
:::
:::proof
The three dates are locked, and the title may be named. The article's sentences may not be invented, so they are not supplied. What the title itself does is enough for the public character of the act: an accusation in the first person, in a newspaper, aimed at readers. Exoneration in 1906 means the conviction did not stand. Twelve years on the labels, 1906 minus 1894, separate arrest from exoneration. The article does not acquit him. It is an intervention, eight years before the exoneration on the labels, 1906 minus 1898. What would count against calling this a political movement is evidence that the affair stayed a private dislike, with no press, no leagues and no effect on public office. The dated article counts against that quiet reading. What would count against refusing the link to 1933 is a chain, in evidence, from this French case to the German appointment. A shared prejudice is not a chain. The appointment belongs to [[wars/interwar]]. Treating 1894 as its origin would make one miscarriage the author of a later regime, which the dates do not say.
:::

Dreyfus was a Jewish officer in the French army, arrested and convicted on a charge of passing secrets. The lesson does not reconstruct the file. It records that the conviction was later undone, and that undoing it took until 1906. In the years between, the affair split French public life. Antisemitism operated as [[#def-antisemitism]] describes: a politics, not only a snub. Leagues and newspapers treated a Jew as a stand-in for a collective enemy. That is the worked case. It is a case of mass politics behaving badly, which is why "mass" must not be praised in advance.

:::example From arrest to exoneration {#ex-dreyfus}
Dreyfus is arrested in 1894. J'Accuse is published on 13 January 1898. Exoneration is 1906. Compute the two gaps in the year-labels, and say what you may not add about the article.
:::solution
1906 minus 1894 is 12. 1906 minus 1898 is 8. The article's title may be named, and its date may be used. Its sentences may not be written out from memory or from a paraphrase that pretends to be the text. The title is an accusation addressed to a public. It is not a verdict. Exoneration is a later legal act, not a consequence the article automatically produced. Neither gap is a reason to call the affair the origin of 30 January 1933.
:::
:::

You are not asked to admire the twelve years or to mine them for a moral about patience. You are asked to keep a legal result, a newspaper and a later German appointment in different boxes. Collapsing the boxes is how a French case of the years after 1894 gets told as if it explained a chancellorship in 1933. It does not.

## 1905 and 1906, just far enough apart

:::theorem Democracy had not been achieved by 1914 {#thm-notyet}
By 1914 the locked inclusions do not amount to democracy. Britain has reformed a male franchise and left women out. Russia has met a petition with violence on Bloody Sunday. Finland in 1906 shows a parliamentary rule Britain had not adopted. A European first is not a European norm.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the set of acts already dated. 1867 and 1884 exclude women. 22 January 1905, New Style, is Bloody Sunday in St Petersburg, which is also 9 January 1905, Old Style: one event, a public treated with violence rather than with a vote. No death total is stated. 1906 gives women in the Grand Duchy of Finland the parliamentary vote and the right to stand. The chronology calls that a European parliamentary first and forbids calling it the first women's suffrage in the world. This lesson therefore names no earlier foreign year. What would count against the claim is a locked general rule, in force by 1914 across the states this course has been following, under which adult women and men chose their governments. The British acts, the Russian day and the Finnish exception are not that rule. Finland is the counter-example to complacency and also the proof that the wider rule was not general. Both uses are fair.
:::

The two calendars are the method of [[sources/time-and-names]], applied to a political day. Russia has not yet made the switch of 1918, when 31 January is followed by 14 February. In 1905 the Old Style date and the New Style date name one morning. The numerals differ by 13: 9 plus 13 is 22. Anyone who tells the story twice, as if the petition were dispersed on two days, has mistaken a calendar for a sequence. The October Revolution's double date, later, is [[wars/revolutions-1917]]. This chapter stops at the January day.

The year 1905 contains more than that morning. This atlas locks Bloody Sunday and does not lock a day for the later grants and risings of the year, so those days are not invented. A revolution can be real and still have only one dated threshold in a given lesson. The threshold here is the violence offered to petitioners. A franchise is precisely what that morning was not.

Finland in 1906 is the other threshold. The Grand Duchy, under the Russian emperor, gets a parliamentary franchise that includes women as voters and as candidates. "Right to stand" is not a grace note. A vote without the right to be elected is a thinner inclusion, and Finland's rule has both. Britain's 1884 rule has neither, for women. The widget puts 1905 and 1906 side by side on purpose. A window of three years only just separates them. One is a refusal in the street. One is a statute about who may sit. Calling both "the rise of the masses" teaches nothing until you say which public, and which right.

:::example One morning, two numerals {#ex-sunday}
Bloody Sunday is 22 January 1905 New Style and 9 January 1905 Old Style. Show that the numerals differ by 13, and say what that difference is not.
:::solution
22 minus 9 is 13, and 9 plus 13 is 22. The difference is the gap between the Russian calendar and the Gregorian date of the same day. It is not a second petition and not a death total. The lesson uses the New Style date as the marker and keeps the Old Style date beside it. Both name one event.
:::
:::

The figure's lesson is the caption's lesson. Inclusion is not one reform. A reform act that leaves women out, a commune that is destroyed, an arrest, a newspaper, a violent morning and a Finnish statute are different kinds of dot. The line does not become a progress because the years increase.

:::example An invented verdict on 1884 {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. "In 1884 the British people entered politics at last, the Commune having shown the way in 1871, and democracy was complete in time for 1905." Separate the dates it borrows from the claims it adds.
:::solution
1884, March to May 1871 and 1905 are real. "The British people" is not the electorate of the third Reform Act, which is a wider male franchise and not women. The Commune does not "show the way" into that Act: it is a French city government, crushed, thirteen years before 1884 on the labels, 1884 minus 1871. Democracy was not complete in 1884, and 1905 in St Petersburg is Bloody Sunday, not a completed democracy. The Finnish franchise, which would be the interesting comparison, is missing from the invented sentence, and it is 1906. The sentence uses true years to smuggle a completed people.
:::
:::

:::quiz
Finland in 1906 is which of these?
- [ ] The first time any woman in the world could vote
- [ ] The year the British third Reform Act was extended to women
- [x] A European parliamentary first: women could vote and stand, which the British acts of 1867 and 1884 had not granted
- [ ] The origin of Hitler's appointment in 1933
:::solution
The chronology calls the Finnish rule a European parliamentary first and forbids calling it the first women's suffrage in the world. Britain's 1867 and 1884 acts do not include women. The appointment in 1933 is a different event, and [[#thm-dreyfus]] already refuses to hang it on the affair that runs from 1894 to 1906. The same refusal covers Finland.

## What the counter-argument has to face

The counter-argument says that 1867 and 1884 are democracy arriving, perhaps slowly, and that a slow arrival is still an arrival. It points at real statutes. More men could vote after each of them than before. Socialist parties and churches built organisations unknown to an older elite politics. Those are facts, and they belong in [[#def-mass]]. They do not reach [[#thm-notyet]].

What would count for the counter-argument is a rule, in Britain or as a European norm by 1914, under which the exclusions named in this chapter had lapsed. Women would be inside the British parliamentary franchise. A petition in the Russian capital would not be the picture of how a public met the state. Finland would be typical rather than first. None of that is the locked record. What the counter-argument can keep is more modest and more honest: the electorate grew, parties and churches organised, and some states were more open in 1906 than in 1832. Growth of that kind is mass politics. It is not a synonym for democracy, and the chapter will not sign the synonym.

There is a second counter-argument, about the affair. It says Dreyfus was one miscarriage, unfortunate and personal, and that politics in the ordinary sense went on elsewhere. A single defendant can be one person and still be the occasion of a movement. The test is not the number of defendants. The test is whether press, leagues and public office took the case as a national question about Jews. They did. Exoneration in 1906 closes the legal case against him. It does not rewind the movement into a private snub, and it does not project the case forward as the cause of 1933. Both exaggerations, "only personal" and "the origin of everything later", fail the same dates.

:::warning Democracy by 1914, and a French origin for 1933
Two myths use this chapter's dates and miss them. The first says democracy was achieved by 1914, because reform acts had passed and parties existed. Women are outside the British acts of 1867 and 1884. Bloody Sunday is a public met with violence. Finland's 1906 franchise is a first, not a norm. The second myth makes the Dreyfus affair the origin of 1933. The affair runs from an arrest in 1894 to an article, J'Accuse, on 13 January 1898, to an exoneration in 1906. The chancellorship is a later German act. Mass politics includes reform and it includes a campaign against a minority. Neither fact is a happy ending.
:::

:::history
The Paris Commune holds the city from March to May 1871, after the German Empire has already been proclaimed on 18 January and while the Treaty of Frankfurt, on 10 May, falls inside the Commune's months. The Commune is a government, and it is destroyed. It is not the meaning of 1871's empire, and it is not a franchise for the women who took part in it.
:::

## Where this leads

The states whose franchises are in question were made, and refused, in [[nations/unifications]]. The people a census could see, who are not yet voters, are in [[nations/industry]]. The narrow Act of 1832 and the Manifesto of February 1848 are in [[revolutions/restoration-and-1848]]. Calendars, including the reason 9 January and 22 January can be one day, are [[sources/time-and-names]]. The overseas empires of these same states are [[nations/empires]]. The war that shows how unfinished 1914's politics were is [[wars/first-world-war]], and the German appointment of 1933, which this chapter refuses to derive from Dreyfus, is [[wars/interwar]].

:::summary
- Mass politics addresses large publics through franchises, parties, churches, newspapers and leagues. It is not democracy, and democracy had not been achieved by 1914.
- The Reform Act of 1832 was a narrow widening. The Acts of 1867 and 1884 widened a male franchise. Women were not included. The year-labels are 17 years apart.
- The Paris Commune lasts from March to May 1871. It is a city government, destroyed, with no death total stated. It is not the German proclamation of 18 January and not the Treaty of Frankfurt on 10 May, though the treaty falls inside its months.
- Dreyfus is arrested in 1894. Zola's article, whose title is J'Accuse, appears on 13 January 1898. The text is not invented here. Exoneration is 1906. The affair is political antisemitism, not the origin of 30 January 1933.
- Bloody Sunday is 22 January 1905 New Style and 9 January Old Style, one event. The numerals differ by 13. No death total is stated.
- In 1906 women in the Grand Duchy of Finland gain the parliamentary vote and the right to stand. That is a European parliamentary first, not the first in the world, and not the British reform.
:::

:::exercise Parties, churches, and the vote {level=2}
Socialist parties and churches both organise large publics in these decades. Why is that fact not the same fact as the Finnish franchise of 1906, and why must you not quote a party programme to make the point?
:::solution
Organisation is mass politics in the sense of [[#def-mass]]: people enrolled and addressed. A franchise is a legal rule about who votes and who may stand. Finland's 1906 rule is the second kind, and it includes women. A party or a church can be huge while women, or most men, still cannot vote, and while a government still answers a petition with violence, as on Bloody Sunday. Programmes existed. Their texts are not locked, so they are not invented. February 1848's Manifesto is a published text of a different, earlier moment. It is not a quotation from a later party's platform.
