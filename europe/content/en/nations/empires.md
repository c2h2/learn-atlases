What did the Berlin Conference do, and what did it not do?

The Berlin Conference sits from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885 and regulates how European states will recognise one another's claims in Africa. It does not give a continent away in one meeting. Conquest is longer than the meeting, and it is violent. The French invasion of Algiers has already begun in 1830. The Indian rebellion is 1857, and the name you use for it is already an argument. Leopold II's Congo Free State is his personal rule from 1885 to 1908, then the Belgian Congo. Casement's report is 1904. In the same year, on 2 October, von Trotha issues an extermination order against the Herero. Historians classify the campaign against the Herero and the Nama, running to 1908, as genocide. The South African War is 1899 to 1902. None of these sentences contains a death total. This lesson will not add one.

A different meeting, the Congress of Berlin in 1878, is about the Balkans. It is not this conference. The two Berlins are easy to confuse and are not the same act.

## Claims are not a continent

:::definition The Berlin Conference {#def-conference}
The **Berlin Conference** is the meeting of 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885 that regulates European claims in Africa. It is not the Congress of Berlin of 1878. It is not a ceremony at which African sovereignty was handed over in an afternoon, and it is not a census of the people who lived under the claims.
:::

Diplomats can write rules about notification, about the idea that a claim on paper should be backed by presence, and about navigation on the Congo and the Niger. Historians summarise the meeting in those terms. The summary is a summary of a diplomatic act. It is not a transfer, completed on 26 February 1885, of inland government from African polities to European capitals. A rule about how claimants should behave is not the behaviour. Effective occupation, the phrase often used for the presence rule, is an instruction to empires. It is not itself an occupation.

:::theorem The conference regulates claims and does not give away a continent {#thm-regulates}
The Berlin Conference regulates European claims. It does not, in one meeting, transfer African sovereignty. Conquest takes longer than 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885, and it is violent. A signature is not the inland history.
:::
:::proof
The dates are the meeting. The chronology states the limit in the same breath: the conference does not give the continent away, and conquest is longer and violent. What would count against the claim is evidence that on 26 February 1885 the polities of the interior had in fact changed sovereign by the meeting alone, with no further war. The later record used here counts against that picture. Leopold's personal rule runs from 1885 to 1908. The South African War is still in the future, 1899 to 1902. The extermination order is 2 October 1904. A meeting that had finished the conquest would have left no work for those years. What would also count against a softer version, in which the meeting was merely talk and the violence was someone else's subject, is the point of regulating claims at all. The diplomats were organising a competition they expected states to pursue. Pursuit is not identical with the general act. Both halves belong in the sentence.
:::

The dates enclose a season, not a moment, and not a decade. Count the calendar days with both ends included. November, from the 15th through the 30th, is 16 days. December has 31. January has 31. February, from the 1st through the 26th, is 26. Then 16 plus 31 is 47, plus 31 is 78, plus 26 is 104. The meeting's dates enclose 104 calendar days. That count is not a claim that anyone sat on each of them. Elapsed time from 15 November to 26 February, not counting both ends as extra, is 103 days. Either way the conference is a season of diplomacy. It is not an afternoon, and it is not the whole of empire.

:::example One hundred and four days {#ex-days}
The conference opens on 15 November 1884 and ends on 26 February 1885. How many calendar days do those dates enclose if both ends are counted, and what does the number not mean?
:::solution
November contributes 16 days, from the 15th through the 30th. December contributes 31 and January 31. February contributes 26. The sum is 16 plus 31 plus 31 plus 26, which is 104. The number encloses the span. It does not say the delegates met daily, and it does not say African sovereignty passed on the last of the days. [[#thm-regulates]] is the claim the arithmetic must not be asked to inflate.
:::
:::

Nor does the meeting leave every African polity colonised. Some states are not conquered by a signature in Berlin, and conquest continues after February 1885. This lesson does not invent a year for those later victories and defeats beyond the ones the chronology locks. It says the obvious negative. A conference that regulated claims did not thereby finish a map.

:::solution

## Before Berlin, and the other Berlin

Empire in this century does not begin when the conference opens. In 1830 the French invasion of Algiers begins. The July Revolution in France is also 1830. The chronology does not lock a day for either, so this lesson does not say which came first and does not make one the cause of the other. Sharing a year is not sharing an event. The invasion is the start of a long conquest in North Africa, with settlers and soldiers and the people already there. No casualty total is stated. What 1830 does in the argument is chronological: a European empire is being extended by invasion more than fifty years before 15 November 1884. Fifty-four years separate the labels, 1884 minus 1830. The "scramble" is a label for a late intensification. It is not the birth of empire.

:::example Two events in 1830 {#ex-1830}
The July Revolution and the start of the French invasion of Algiers share 1830. What may you conclude, and what must you not?
:::solution
You may conclude that both are dated to that year in this atlas, and that a revolution in Paris and an invasion of Algiers are different kinds of event. You may not conclude that one caused the other, because no day is locked and no mechanism is given. You may not describe the fighting, and you may not add a casualty total. The use of the year in this chapter is to place conquest before the Berlin Conference, not to narrate 1830 in full. That revolution is also part of [[revolutions/restoration-and-1848]].
:::
:::

The Indian rebellion is 1857. The chronology's name for it is the rebellion. Two other names are in use, and each is an argument. "Mutiny" places the event inside British military law and centres soldiers of the Company's army. "First War of Independence" places it inside a later national story and centres a war for a state. Neither name is neutral, which is why this lesson prefers the chronology's word and then says what the other two are doing. The rebellion is a crisis of Company rule across a large part of the subcontinent. It is not only a soldiers' plot, and it is not only a later nation's origin myth. This atlas does not lock a year for the end of Company government, so that year is not supplied. A crisis can be taught without a sequel's statute.

The Congress of Berlin sits from 13 June to 13 July 1878. It is a Balkan settlement, after a Russian-Ottoman conflict this chronology does not date separately, and it is not a meeting about African coasts. Count it so the confusion has a number to fail against. June, from the 13th through the 30th, is 18 days. July, from the 1st through the 13th, is 13. The sum is 31 calendar days with both ends counted. One month of Balkan diplomacy is not three months of African claim-making, and 1878 is not 1884. Austria-Hungary's occupation of Bosnia and Herzegovina belongs to this congress's settlement. The annexation crisis of 1908, taught in [[nations/toward-1914]], is what a later government did with that occupation. One paragraph is enough to stop the two Berlins becoming one word. It is not enough, and it is not meant, to narrate the Ottoman empire's century.

:::example Two meetings, two spans {#ex-berlins}
Compare the Congress of Berlin, 13 June to 13 July 1878, with the Berlin Conference, 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885. What is each for, and how do the enclosed day-counts differ?
:::solution
The congress is a Balkan settlement. With both ends counted it encloses 18 days of June plus 13 days of July, which is 31. The conference regulates African claims and encloses 104 calendar days, as in [[#ex-days]]. The year-labels differ by 1884 minus 1878, which is 6, and the conference runs into 1885. Neither span is a death total, a conquest completed, or a licence to use "Berlin" as if the meetings were one.
:::
:::

## Congo, without a total

:::definition The Congo Free State {#def-congo}
The **Congo Free State** is Leopold II's personal rule from 1885 to 1908. It is not yet the Belgian Congo, which is the regime after 1908. It is not a colony in the ordinary sense that a parliament in Brussels governed it from the start. Atrocities under the personal rule are documented. A death total is not stated.
:::

The conference is part of how a Congo claim becomes internationally recognisable. Recognition is not the regime. From 1885 to 1908 the ruler is Leopold, personally. The coercion that extracts rubber is a system of rule, not a clause voted on 26 February 1885 and then forgotten. Roger Casement's report in 1904 documents atrocities. This atlas refuses the large round numbers that circulate as death totals. They are not settled enough to print. Refusal is not an acquittal. A crime can be documented by a report, by the structure of forced labour, and by the diplomacy that eventually criticised it, without a figure this book will not stand behind.

What would count against the refusal is a total the chronology itself accepted. None has been. What would count against softening "atrocities are documented" into "allegations were made" is the status the chronology gives the report: documentation, not a rumour the lesson is free to balance away. In 1908 the Belgian state takes the territory as the Belgian Congo. The sovereign changes. The record does not. Annexation is not a cleaning of the years before it.

:::theorem Documented violence does not wait on a death total {#thm-novictims}
Atrocities in Leopold's Congo, and the campaign against the Herero and the Nama, are part of this history. Historians classify the campaign of 1904 to 1908 as genocide. Neither case is taught with a death total, and the absence of a settled total is not a finding of innocence.
:::
:::proof
Casement's report is 1904. Von Trotha's extermination order against the Herero is 2 October 1904. The campaign against the Herero and the Nama runs to 1908. The chronology states that historians classify that campaign as genocide, and it forbids a death total for the campaign and for the Congo alike. The word genocide is later than the order: Raphael Lemkin's book of 1944 uses it, and the Genocide Convention is adopted on 9 December 1948. Applying a later legal category to an earlier campaign is a historian's judgement, which this atlas follows. It is not a claim that the order used the later word. The order is an order of extermination. That is evidence of intent. What would count against the classification is evidence that the order was not carried out and that the campaign was a limited war against combatants only. The chronology's classification stands against that reading. What would count against teaching either case without a number is a belief that only a total makes violence real. The reports, the order and the classification are the evidence. A borrowed round number would be a different, weaker, thing.
:::

The two files of 1904 must not be merged. Casement writes on the Congo, under Leopold's personal rule. Von Trotha's order is German, in German South-West Africa, and it names the Herero. The Nama are part of the campaign that runs to 1908. Same year, two empires, two crimes. The widget puts them on one dot because the year carries both, and the caption says the dot does not contain a death total. A reader who wants one African story for 1904 has already refused the geography.

The order, the report, the dates and the classification are the content. A procedure of killing is not. Naming the crime does not require it, and this lesson does not supply one.

## A war that is not a conference

The South African War runs from 1899 to 1902. It is fought between the British empire and two Boer republics. It is not a session of the Berlin Conference, which has been over since 26 February 1885, fourteen years before 1899 on the labels, 1899 minus 1885. It is empire as war, after the rules about claims had already been written. African inhabitants are not the parties to the diplomacy that named the republics, and they live inside the war anyway. No death total is stated. The point of including the war is the point of including Algiers and 1857: the conference is one instrument among others, and it is not the whole of European empire between 1830 and 1908.

Abolition is not the end of that empire either. The British Act of 1807 is against the slave trade, not against slavery. The Act of 1833 abolishes slavery in most of the British empire, with apprenticeship to 1838. The embarkation estimate for the Atlantic trade, 12,521,354 people over 1501 to 1866, lives in [[states/atlantic]]. It is not a description of the Congo Free State and not a description of German South-West Africa. Territorial empire continues after the British trade is illegal and after British colonial slavery is abolished. Anyone who treats 1833 as the end of the imperial story cannot account for 1884, or for 1904.

There is a counter-argument that tries to rescue the conference by its language about the slave trade. Diplomats did discuss commitments against the trade. A discussion is not a humanitarian compact, and a clause does not govern the Congo. What would count for a humanitarian reading is a meeting whose rules stopped the violence that followed. What followed includes Leopold's personal rule, Casement's report, and an extermination order. The clause does not win that test. [[nations/industry]] has already separated fibre, finance and empire as arguments about British industrialisation. Here empire is not an argument about cotton. It is rule, claim and war.

:::example An invented transfer {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. "On 26 February 1885 the people of the Congo became Belgian, and the conference's humanitarian clauses meant that no violence followed." What does the sentence get wrong about the sovereign, the date and the sequel?
:::solution
26 February 1885 is the end of the conference, not the start of Belgian rule. The Congo Free State from 1885 to 1908 is Leopold II's personal rule. The Belgian Congo begins in 1908, when the sovereign changes and the record of atrocities does not. Casement's report is 1904, inside the personal rule. Humanitarian discussion of the slave trade, whatever diplomats wrote, did not stop that rule and did not stop the extermination order of 2 October 1904 in a different colony. The invented sentence turns a regulation of claims into a change of nationality and a promise of peace. [[#thm-regulates]] and [[#thm-novictims]] are the corrections. No death total is added in order to make the correction look more severe.
:::

## A worked case: the last day of the meeting

Take 26 February 1885 as a worked case. It is the day the conference ends. A diplomat can leave Berlin with a rule about claims. A ruler in the Congo basin does not become a Belgian subject because the diplomat has left. Leopold's personal rule is dated from 1885, which is the same year as the ending, and it is his. The year-label can host both a signature and a personal sovereign without making them one government.

Ask what you would need before you told a local story as if it were the conference. You would need a source from a place, naming who governed on the day after the meeting, under what force, and with what resistance. The conference's own acts do not provide that source. [[sources/scale]] is the method: a true sentence about diplomats is often a false sentence about a village. The invented transfer in [[#ex-invented]] is the false sentence. The worked case is the same test without the invention. End of meeting, start of a personal rule, documentation of atrocities only in 1904, Belgian annexation only in 1908. Four beats. None of them is a death total, and none of them is an acquittal.

Set 2 October 1904 beside that case and the scale changes again. An extermination order is not a conference. It is a command in a war against the Herero, inside a campaign that also runs against the Nama until 1908. Historians call the campaign genocide. The later word, Lemkin's in 1944 and the convention's on 9 December 1948, does not make the order polite, and it does not require you to invent how many people died. Intent is in the order. Duration is in the dates. Classification is the historians' judgement the chronology tells you to follow. A student who will not call the campaign genocide without a number has added a condition the evidence does not need. A student who invents the number has broken a different rule. Both students have left the case.

:::warning An afternoon, and a number that would settle it
Two myths undo this chapter. The first says the Berlin Conference gave Africa away in an afternoon, so that 26 February 1885 is the partition. The meeting runs to 104 enclosed days, regulates claims, and leaves conquest undone. Algiers in 1830 and the rebellion of 1857 are empire before the meeting. The South African War and the order of 1904 are empire after it. The second myth says that without an agreed death total nothing serious happened in the Congo or to the Herero and the Nama. Casement's report documents atrocities. The order is an extermination order. Historians classify the campaign to 1908 as genocide. The totals in circulation are not settled, so they are not printed. A missing total is not an acquittal.
:::

:::history
The Berlin Conference sits from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885. It regulates European claims in Africa. Conquest is a longer and more violent process than the meeting. The Congress of Berlin in 1878 is a different act, about the Balkans, and must not be folded into this one.
:::

## Where this leads

The trade in enslaved people, which is not this chapter's Congo and not its German colony, is [[states/atlantic]]. The argument that empire was a condition of British industrialisation, which is not the same claim as a conference, is [[nations/industry]]. The publics inside the European states that claimed empires are [[nations/mass-politics]]. The Balkans after the congress of 1878 return as the annexation crisis of 1908 and the July decisions of 1914 in [[nations/toward-1914]]. The later legal category of genocide, which historians already apply to the campaign of 1904 to 1908, is set out for the crime of the 1940s in [[wars/genocide]]. Using the category here does not make the two campaigns one event.

:::summary
- The French invasion of Algiers begins in 1830, the same year as the July Revolution and not the same event. The Indian rebellion is 1857. "Mutiny" and "First War of Independence" are arguments. The chronology's name is the rebellion.
- The Congress of Berlin, 13 June to 13 July 1878, is a Balkan settlement. It is not the Berlin Conference.
- The conference runs from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885, 104 calendar days with both ends counted. It regulates claims. It does not give away a continent in one meeting. Conquest is longer and violent.
- The Congo Free State is Leopold II's personal rule from 1885 to 1908, then the Belgian Congo. Casement's report of 1904 documents atrocities. No death total is stated.
- On 2 October 1904 von Trotha issues an extermination order against the Herero. The campaign against the Herero and the Nama runs to 1908. Historians classify it as genocide. No death total is stated.
- The South African War is 1899 to 1902. Abolition of the British slave trade in 1807, and of slavery in most of the British empire in 1833, does not end empire.
:::
