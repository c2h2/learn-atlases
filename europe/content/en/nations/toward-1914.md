What choices remained in July 1914?

Franz Ferdinand is assassinated in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia on 28 July. Germany declares war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August. On 4 August Germany invades Belgium and Britain declares war on Germany. Those are choices with dates on them. The alliances of 1879, 1894, 1904 and 1907 are not one mechanism that made the choices for the governments. A German mobilisation plan, associated with Schlieffen and revised by Moltke, constrained a timetable. A constraint is not the absence of choice. One claim, often repeated, is that war was inevitable after 1879, or after the promise of support later called a blank cheque. That claim is someone's argument. The dates answer it. This lesson does not narrate the war that follows. The war is [[wars/first-world-war]].

The dual monarchy that declares on 28 July is the Austria-Hungary made by the Ausgleich, taught in [[nations/unifications]]. A dynasty and a Hungarian kingdom go to war together. They are not a nation waking into a destiny.

## Four agreements, not one machine

:::definition Alliance and entente {#def-alliance}
An **alliance**, here, is a dated agreement between named states, read for what it binds and for what it does not. An **entente** is also a dated agreement, and it is not the same instrument as the Dual Alliance. Neither word means a machine that declares war by itself. Italy is a European power in 1914 and is not an endpoint of the agreements drawn below.
:::

The Dual Alliance of 1879 joins Germany and Austria-Hungary. It does not join Britain, and it does not put Italy on that edge. The Franco-Russian alliance is 1894. The year is also the year of Dreyfus's arrest, which [[nations/mass-politics]] keeps as a different event: a French affair is not a military convention. The Entente Cordiale is 8 April 1904, between France and Britain. It settles outstanding colonial disputes. It is not, by itself, a promise to fight on the fourth day of a later August. The Anglo-Russian convention is 1907, another agreement, not a fusion of Britain and Russia into a single will aimed at Berlin. Four dates, four instruments, three edges that touch Britain or France or Russia and one that joins Germany to Austria-Hungary. Counting them as two blocs is already an interpretation. The interpretation has to survive the fact that the instruments are not alike.

:::theorem The agreements do not remove choice {#thm-agreements}
The Dual Alliance of 1879, the Franco-Russian alliance of 1894, the Entente Cordiale of 8 April 1904 and the Anglo-Russian convention of 1907 are not one mechanism. They raise the cost of some choices. They do not make war inevitable after 1879, and they do not act in place of the declarations of 1914.
:::
:::proof
The four instruments have different parties and different years. Italy is in none of the edges this lesson draws, and Italy is not among the states that declare war in the sequence from 28 July to 4 August. If the agreements were one machine, the machine would have the same shape at every crisis. It does not. The Bosnian annexation crisis of 1908 does not become a general European war. The Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 do not become one either. What would count against the claim is evidence that after 1879 no government could have acted otherwise than it did in 1914, because the texts of 1879, 1894, 1904 and 1907 had already exhausted the alternatives. The quiet crises count against that evidence. So does the gap between an entente that settles colonial arguments and an alliance between Germany and Austria-Hungary. Cost is real. Cost is not impossibility. The word "inevitable" belongs to the claim this paragraph refuses, not to the chronology.
:::

:::example Italy off the edges {#ex-italy}
Using only the agreements of 1879, 1894, 8 April 1904 and 1907, say what the figure claims about Italy, and what a reader must not add.
:::solution
Italy is not a party to the Dual Alliance, not a party to the Franco-Russian alliance, and not a party to either agreement with Britain. The figure therefore gives Italy no edge. You must not add a treaty year the chronology does not lock, and you must not infer that Italy declares war in the sequence from 28 July to 4 August. It does not appear in that sequence. Absence from these edges is not absence from European politics. It is absence from this particular machine.

## Crises that stopped short

In 1908 Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia and Herzegovina. The congress of 1878, a different Berlin meeting from the African conference, had placed those lands under Austro-Hungarian occupation. Annexation is a change of status, and it is a crisis: Serbia and Russia object. It is not 28 July 1914. No general European war begins. The Dual Alliance can operate inside a Balkan quarrel and still leave the other governments short of the declarations this chapter lists. That is what a mechanism would not have allowed, if the mechanism were real.

The Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 are wars in Ottoman Europe, among Balkan states and the Ottoman empire, and then over the settlement. This lesson does not narrate the campaigns and does not state a casualty total. It uses the bracket the chronology gives. A Balkan war, even a large one, is not yet the European war of 1914. Twice, if one counts 1908 as well as 1912–1913, a south-eastern crisis fails to become the sequence of late July and early August. The failure is evidence. It does not make 1914 impossible. It makes 1914 a further set of decisions, taken when earlier crises had been survivable.

:::example Two crises that are not August {#ex-crises}
Set the Bosnian annexation crisis of 1908 beside the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913. What do they show about the Dual Alliance of 1879, and what do they not show about 1914?
:::solution
Both are Balkan crises after 1879, and neither becomes a general European war. They count against the claim that the Dual Alliance had already made such a war the only available act. They do not show that 1914 was impossible, and they do not show that the decisions of 28 July, 1 August, 3 August and 4 August were the same decision as 1908. No casualty total for the Balkan Wars is part of the answer. The occupation that 1908 annexes belongs to the congress of 1878, which [[nations/empires]] keeps separate from the African conference.
:::
:::

Historians disagree about how tightly the rivalry of Austria-Hungary and Russia in the Balkans bound the later choices. The disagreement is part of the prose, not a footnote you may skip. Rivalry is a condition. A condition is the sort of thing that could have been otherwise, which is the test [[sources/argument]] gives a cause. Russia can object in 1908 and not be at war with Austria-Hungary. Balkan states can fight in 1912 and 1913 while the great powers stay short of the August declarations. The rivalry explains why the region is dangerous. It does not choose the day.

## A timetable is not a fate

:::definition A mobilisation plan {#def-plan}
A **mobilisation plan** is a staff scheme for calling up reserves and moving armies on a prepared schedule. The German plan associated with Schlieffen, and revised by Moltke, put the first weight of a two-front war on the western side and therefore on a movement through Belgium. The plan constrains a government that orders it. It is not a law of geography, and it is not a proof that no other order could have been written.
:::

:::theorem A constraint is not the absence of choice {#thm-choice}
The German mobilisation plan constrained a timetable once it was the plan in force. It did not abolish choice, in July 1914 or before. The declarations and the invasion remain dated acts of governments. Christopher Clark's Sleepwalkers is one interpretation of those acts, not a verdict.
:::
:::proof
The plan is a timetable: railways, reserves, a western movement through Belgium if a two-front war is fought on the plan's terms. Once a government orders that mobilisation, the next hours are pressed. The pressure is the constraint. It was made by staff officers and ministers, and Moltke revised it, so it was not nature. Revision is itself evidence that the scheme could be otherwise. It had been otherwise. What would count against the constraint is evidence that, in the last days, Berlin could mobilise partially, stop, or face only east, and that the government both believed so and acted so. Historians disagree about how open those alternatives were. The disagreement stays open here. What would count against "no choice" is the list of acts that still had to be performed after any promise of support: the ultimatum of 23 July, the declaration on Serbia on 28 July, the declaration on Russia on 1 August, the declaration on France on 3 August, the invasion of Belgium and the British declaration on 4 August. Austria-Hungary's war on Serbia does not, by itself, put German troops in Belgium. Someone orders that movement. Clark's book reads the crisis as a set of steps that were locally intelligible and collectively disastrous. The sleepwalker is his image, not a finding that nobody was awake. Other historians argue that German leaders used the crisis to seek a war they wanted. That is a claim about aims. Aims can be real and still leave a day on which a different order could have been given. This lesson's claim is narrower than either book. On the locked dates, choices remained.
:::

The promise of support from Berlin to Vienna, often called a blank cheque, belongs in July, before the ultimatum of 23 July. This atlas does not lock a day for the promise, so no day is invented and no text of the promise is supplied. A cheque, even a blank one, is an assurance a government gives. It is not yet a declaration. The claim that choice ended when the assurance was given has to explain the acts that come after it. They have dates. They are not the assurance repeating itself.

The ultimatum is 23 July. Its text is not invented here. It was a demand to Serbia with a short deadline. The length of the deadline in hours is not locked, so it is not stated. Governments then disagreed about whether Serbia's reply was enough. The declaration follows on 28 July. Five days on the calendar, from the 23rd to the 28th, are enough to hold a judgement. A judgement is a choice. You are not given the clauses, and you do not need them to see that a declaration is a further act.

:::example Twenty-five days, then five {#ex-span}
Franz Ferdinand is assassinated on 28 June 1914. The ultimatum is 23 July. The declaration on Serbia is 28 July. How many days lie between the assassination and the ultimatum, and how many between the ultimatum and the declaration?
:::solution
From 28 June to 23 July is 25 days. July 23 is five days before July 28, because 28 minus 23 is 5. The 25 days are a political interval: a murder has happened, and a European war has not yet been declared. The five days are a further interval, after a demand whose text this lesson does not invent. Neither interval is empty enough to be called automatic. The blank cheque, undated here on purpose, falls inside July and before the ultimatum. It does not consume the 25 days or the five.
:::
:::

## The worked case: four dates

Read the case as a list, not as a slide.

On 28 June the Habsburg heir is killed in Sarajevo. The lesson does not describe the weapon and does not invent a speech. A murder of an heir is a political fact. It is not, on that day, a war between Austria-Hungary and Serbia, and it is not a war between Germany and Russia. Anyone who starts the war on 28 June has skipped the month in which choices were made.

On 23 July the ultimatum is delivered. On 28 July Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia. The dual monarchy chooses to treat the assassination as a reason for war, and chooses the day. Russia is not declared upon yet. Germany has not invaded Belgium. The Balkan pattern of 1908 and of 1912–1913 shows that a quarrel in this region can remain a quarrel. Choosing otherwise is intelligible. It is still a choice.

On 1 August Germany declares war on Russia. On 3 August Germany declares war on France. From 28 July to 1 August is four days. From 1 August to 3 August is two. Each declaration adds a state. The mobilisation plan makes a western movement the staff's preferred sequence for a two-front war. Preferred, prepared and revised is not the same as unable to be otherwise. Historians disagree about the room left in Berlin. The lesson does not close their disagreement by announcing that the plan was destiny, and it does not close it by announcing that the plan was a pretext with no grip. The grip is the constraint. The declarations are the choices.

On 4 August Germany invades Belgium, and Britain declares war on Germany. One day holds two acts. The invasion is what the revised plan's western movement requires if the plan is followed into Belgium. The British declaration is a decision in London, connected to Belgium and to the ententes, and not a clause written in 1879. The Entente Cordiale does not declare war. A cabinet does. From 3 August to 4 August is one day. One day is long enough for an invasion and a declaration to be told apart, which is all the chronology asks.

:::example From Sarajevo to the British declaration {#ex-thirty-seven}
How many days lie between 28 June 1914 and 4 August 1914? Check the result by adding the steps 25, 5, 4, 2 and 1.
:::solution
From 28 June to 4 August is 37 days. The steps meet: 25 days from 28 June to 23 July, 5 days from 23 July to 28 July, 4 days from 28 July to 1 August, 2 days from 1 August to 3 August, and 1 day from 3 August to 4 August. 25 plus 5 is 30, plus 4 is 34, plus 2 is 36, plus 1 is 37. Thirty-seven days of political time, not one detonation. The sum does not include a death total, a text of the ultimatum, or a claim that any step was the only step that could have been taken.
:::
:::

:::example An invented machine {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. "Once the Dual Alliance was signed in 1879, and again once the blank cheque was sent, every later act was the only act left, so 28 July and 4 August are the same decision." What does the sentence erase?
:::solution
It erases the crises of 1908 and 1912–1913, which did not become this war. It erases the missing day of the blank cheque, which this atlas will not invent, and it treats that assurance as if it were the declarations. It erases the differences among 28 July, 1 August, 3 August and 4 August: Serbia, then Russia, then France, then Belgium and Britain. It erases Moltke's revision, which shows that the plan was a plan someone changed. [[#thm-agreements]] and [[#thm-choice]] are the answers. Clark's Sleepwalkers and the argument that German leaders sought a war are both interpretations of aims and muddle. Neither is a licence to call the dated acts one act. The sentence is invented, and the chronology is enough to break it.
:::
:::

:::quiz
Austria-Hungary's declaration of war on Serbia on 28 July 1914 proves which of the following?
- [ ] That Germany was already invading Belgium
- [ ] That the Entente Cordiale had declared war
- [x] That one government had chosen war on Serbia, with further choices still dated after that day
- [ ] That war had been inevitable since the Dual Alliance of 1879
:::solution
28 July is a declaration on Serbia. The invasion of Belgium and the British declaration are 4 August. Germany's declarations on Russia and France are 1 August and 3 August. The ententes do not declare anything by themselves. The claim of inevitability since 1879 is what [[#thm-agreements]] refuses, using 1908 and 1912–1913 as part of the refusal.
:::
:::

The narrow claim is easy to slide past, so it is worth stating once more without the narrative. Governments in July and early August could take the steps the chronology lists, and they could have refrained from particular steps. Refraining would have had costs, alliance costs and timetable costs among them. A cost is a reason. It is not a proof that the alternative was absent. Historians who stress German aims and historians who stress a shared failure are arguing about how to distribute those reasons. You can prefer one distribution and still refuse "inevitable". The refusal is the chapter's. It is not a plea for any capital, and it is not advice about any later war.

:::warning Inevitable after 1879, or after the cheque
The myth says the war was waiting, either from the Dual Alliance of 1879 or from the blank cheque of July 1914, and that the dates only record a mechanism firing. The myth is a claim, and it fails. 1908 and the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 do not fire it. The ultimatum, the four beats from 28 July to 4 August, and a plan that Moltke had revised are all things a mechanism would not have needed to decide. Sleepwalkers, in Clark's argument, is one interpretation of choices, not a verdict that choice was absent. A staff timetable constrained Germany. It did not abolish the list.
:::

:::history
Franz Ferdinand is killed in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia on 28 July. By 4 August Germany has invaded Belgium and Britain has declared war on Germany. Between those dates Germany has also declared war on Russia, on 1 August, and on France, on 3 August. The sequence is a set of choices under a constraint, not an appointment the nineteenth century had already kept.
:::

## Where this leads

How a cause differs from a timetable was pointed forward from [[sources/argument]] to this chapter. The Austria-Hungary that declares war is the dual monarchy of [[nations/unifications]]. The congress of 1878, which is not the African conference, is in [[nations/empires]], and so is the difference between regulating a claim and fighting a war. The publics those governments faced, which were not yet democracies, are in [[nations/mass-politics]]. The fighting itself, which this lesson does not narrate, is [[wars/first-world-war]].

:::summary
- The Dual Alliance is 1879, the Franco-Russian alliance is 1894, the Entente Cordiale is 8 April 1904, and the Anglo-Russian convention is 1907. They are not one mechanism. Italy is on none of those edges.
- The Bosnian annexation crisis is 1908. The Balkan Wars are 1912–1913. Neither becomes a general European war. Both count against calling war inevitable after 1879.
- Franz Ferdinand is assassinated on 28 June 1914. The ultimatum is 23 July. Its text is not invented. Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia on 28 July.
- Germany declares war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August. On 4 August Germany invades Belgium and Britain declares war on Germany. From 28 June to 4 August is 37 days.
- The promise of support called a blank cheque has no locked day here, so none is invented. It does not end choice.
- The mobilisation plan associated with Schlieffen and revised by Moltke constrained a German timetable. Historians disagree about how much room remained. A constraint is not the absence of choice. Clark's Sleepwalkers is one interpretation, not a verdict.

What does the German mobilisation plan constrain, and why does Austria-Hungary's declaration on 28 July not already equal the invasion of Belgium on 4 August?
:::solution
The plan associated with Schlieffen and revised by Moltke constrains the timetable of a German mobilisation for a two-front war whose first weight lies in the west, through Belgium. The constraint bites when that plan is ordered. 28 July is Austria-Hungary's declaration on Serbia. Belgium is invaded on 4 August, seven days later, as a German act. One ally's war on Serbia is not the other ally's invasion. Moltke's revision shows the scheme had already been changed once, so it was not geography. Historians disagree about how much room remained in the last days. [[#thm-choice]] keeps both the constraint and the disagreement.

Historians disagree, and the disagreement belongs in the answer. What both readings must still leave standing is [[#thm-choice]]: the ultimatum, the declarations and the invasion are dated acts. Neither book is a licence to say the war was inevitable after 1879 or after the blank cheque. The cheque has no locked day and no invented text.
