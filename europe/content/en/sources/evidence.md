What can a source support, and what is a silence?

[[sources/naming-europe]] refused a single outline of the continent. That refusal is only as strong as the next question. When a story treats someone as absent, is the absence in the world, or in the record? A charter, a chronicle, a coin, a parish register and an inquisition file are not four ways of saying the same thing. Each was made by someone, for a purpose, and each fails in its own way. The sentence a historian writes afterwards is a further object. It can be careful, and it can still be false. A silence is a third object. No record of a peasant's opinion, and no women's votes on a roll, are facts about the archive. They are not facts about an empty mind.

The skill is easy to name and easy to drop the moment a story becomes exciting. A survey starts to look like a photograph of a society. A description of sickness starts to look like a count of the dead. A moral at the end of a narrative starts to look like something the writer watched happen. This chapter keeps those slides from going through. It does not teach a general scepticism, as if nothing in the past could be known. It teaches a public test: what the trace can support, what inference you have added, and what would count against the sentence you have just written.

## The trace and the sentence

:::definition Source {#def-source}
A **source** is a surviving trace made by someone for a purpose. In this chapter the working examples are a charter, a chronicle, a coin, a parish register and an inquisition file. The trace is not the inference a historian draws from it, and it is not a window with no maker.
:::

:::definition Inference {#def-inference}
An **inference** is the sentence a historian adds. It says what the trace is being asked to support about the past. A true inference names the trace, stays inside what the maker could record, and says what would count against it. A false inference keeps the trace's prestige and changes the subject.
:::

:::definition Silence {#def-silence}
A **silence** is an absence where a modern reader expected a voice: no record of a peasant's opinion in a survey, no women's votes on a roll of a political act. It is evidence about who was asked and what the record was for. It is not evidence that the omitted person had no mind.
:::

:::theorem The trace and the inference are different objects {#thm-separate}
A charter, a chronicle, a coin, a parish register or an inquisition file supports only what its maker was in a position to record, for the purpose the maker had. The historian's sentence is a second object. Genuine traces do not make careless inferences true, and the separation is not a claim that traces are useless.
:::
:::proof
The five kinds fail in different places, which is the evidence for keeping them apart from the sentences we hang on them. A charter is an instrument. It records a grant, a privilege or a judgement in the form the parties, or the party who kept the copy, wanted preserved. It can support a sentence about that claim of right. It cannot, by itself, support a sentence about what the neighbours felt. A chronicle is a narrative, usually written with a moral and often later than the events. It can support a sentence about what that writer chose to tell and how the writer judged it. The moral is not a fact the writer witnessed, even when some of the events were in reach. A coin is an object struck to circulate and to carry a name or a title. It can support a sentence about a mint and a claim of authority. It cannot support a sentence about whether the people who handled it believed the claim. A parish register records baptisms, marriages and burials that a parish chose to enter. It can support sentences about recorded rites in that place. A gap can be a lost book as easily as an empty village. An inquisition file is the record of a procedure that already classified some beliefs as heresy. In 1231 Gregory IX's papal Inquisition is that kind of procedure. The Spanish Inquisition, authorised on 1 November 1478, is a later and different institution, taught in [[crises/year-1492]]. Either file shows what the court wrote down. It does not show an inner life with the court subtracted.

What would count against [[#thm-separate]] is a trace with no maker and no purpose that somehow arrived as a pure statement of the past. We do not have one. What would count against a cruder reading, in which sources are dismissed because each has a purpose, is a charter that does settle the grant it records, or a register that does settle the burials it lists. Purpose limits the inference. It does not empty the trace. What would also count against the claim is a habit of treating the historian's sentence as if it were the trace. That habit is the one this chapter exists to stop.
:::

The test that follows from [[#def-source]] and [[#def-inference]] can be written as a short list, and it is the list we will use on an invented sentence later. A sentence may be treated as evidence only when there is a surviving trace, a maker, a date or an honest statement that the date is not locked, and a purpose. Even then, the trace and the inference stay apart. You say what the trace shows, you say what you have added, and you say what would count against the addition. An invented sentence has no maker and no archive behind it. Labelling it invented is not a courtesy. It is the difference between a classroom example and a fake quotation. This atlas does not invent quotations from chroniclers, and it does not invent words for the people the files concern.

[[#thm-separate]] is also why a vivid detail is not automatically a strong source. Vividness is a property of prose. A chronicle can be vivid about a moral and thin about the event. A register can be dull and precise about a burial. Strength is the fit between the question and the purpose of the trace, not the temperature of the writing. [[sources/scale]] will ask a related question at the level of a life, a manor, a kingdom and a trade. Here the question is prior. Before you ask whether one village illustrates a kingdom, you ask what the village's own record can support at all.

## A survey made for a conqueror

In 1086 the Domesday survey recorded the English kingdom. It is a source in the strict sense: a great inquiry, made for a conqueror, about land, lords and the resources lordship lived on. The conquest it follows has a date of its own. On 14 October 1066 the battle of Hastings was fought, and a conqueror's dynasty took the kingdom. From 1066 to 1086 is twenty years, the difference of the year numerals, the same rule by which this atlas counts from 800 to 843 as 43 years. The survey is not the battle. It is a later instrument of the regime the battle installed. [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] teaches the manor as a unit of lordship and rent, and it teaches Domesday as a survey of that kingdom, not as a picture of Europe. This chapter uses the same limit for a different purpose. We are asking what kind of sentence the survey can carry.

It can carry sentences about the things the commissioners were told to ask: who held the land, what the manors were recorded as rendering, how the new lordship sat on the old. It can carry sentences about the fact of the inquiry itself, which is evidence that the conqueror wanted a written account of a kingdom. It cannot carry a sentence about whether the peasants thought the conquest just. No column in a survey of that kind is a column for their opinion. The missing opinion is a silence in the sense of [[#def-silence]]. Using it as evidence that peasants had no view is a change of subject. Using it as evidence that the survey was not built to collect their view is the sentence the silence actually supports.

Nor does Domesday become more powerful by being large. A kingdom-wide survey is still a survey of that kingdom. Readers who want a picture of Europe in 1086 will not find it by stretching the English returns until the map looks full. The rock of the claim is the purpose. A conqueror's inquiry into one realm does not become a continental census because a later atlas is continental. If a return seems to speak beyond the questions that were asked, the first suspicion is that the inference has slipped, not that the survey secretly answered a question it was not designed to ask.

A charter from the same world makes the distinction smaller and sharper. Suppose the document grants a manor to a follower. The grant is evidence of the grantor's claimed right to give, and of the form in which the gift was recorded. Witnesses named in the charter are evidence that those names were attached to the act. They are not evidence that a peasant on the manor consented, and they are not evidence that the boundary stones were walked in the way a later reader imagines. If the charter's date is a regnal year rather than a year in our count, the date is already a claim about who reigns. [[sources/time-and-names]] takes up that kind of label. Here it is enough that the charter does not shed its purpose when it is copied into a book.

## A disaster report is not a census

In October 1347 plague was reported at Messina. In 1348 and 1349 it was in much of western Europe, and further waves followed. Those reports are sources. They witness a disaster: sickness that people at the time recognised as a catastrophe, moving between ports and then inland. They are not a census. A witness who writes that the dead are many, or that a town is stricken, has recorded an alarm and, at best, a local sight of death. The witness has not conducted a count of a continent.

This atlas states no European death total. The chronology does not give one, and a false precision would only dress the disaster up as a table. In many of the regions where the evidence has been studied, estimates fall between about a third and about a half of the population, and some places differ. That range is not a number hiding inside the Messina report. It is a later inference, drawn from different kinds of trace, stronger in some places than in others, and not a licence to say that half of Europe died. [[crises/plague]] is where the range, the famine that came before, and the consequences for labour and rent are taught. This chapter's point is the type of the 1347 source. A report of arrival is evidence about arrival. A spread through 1348 and 1349 is evidence about spread. Neither is a headcount.

The Great Famine of 1315 to 1317 is a different disaster, a generation earlier. From 1315 to 1347 is 32 years. A chronicle that moralises "the age" as one punishment has bundled a famine and a plague. The bundle is the writer's shape, or a later reader's. It is not something either catastrophe, by itself, witnessed. Keeping 1315 and 1347 apart is a small case of [[#thm-separate]]. Two traces, two dates, two inferences. A mood called decline is not a third trace.

Parish registers, where they exist and where they survive, are one of the tools later historians use to study death in a place. They are still not the 1347 report, and they are still not Europe. A register records the burials a clerk entered. It can support a sentence about recorded burials in that parish. It cannot, without more argument, support a sentence about a kingdom, and it cannot support a sentence about the faith of the dead. A gap in a register is a silence about entries. The gap may be a year of extraordinary death that overwhelmed the writing, or a lost quire, or a parish that never kept the book. Choosing among those explanations is an inference. It has to be argued. The blank page will not argue it for you.

## What a moral cannot do

A chronicle's moral is the part readers most want to promote, because it arrives as a finished judgement. The pattern, paraphrased rather than quoted, is that the sufferers deserved the blow, or that a king was a tyrant and heaven replied, or that the chronicler's own side was chastised and then restored. You may describe that pattern without pasting words the writer did not write. The pattern is common. The particular sentences, in this chapter, will be invented and labelled, or they will not be quoted at all.

The moral is evidence about the writer. It shows the lesson the narrative was built to teach, the side the writer took, and sometimes the audience the writer feared or served. It is not evidence that the world had that shape. A punishment is a theological or political inference. Even a writer who lived through a plague did not witness "desert" in the way the writer may have witnessed carts, closed houses or a court leaving town. Those sights, if the writer was in a position to have them, are a different layer of the same text. A careful reading splits the layers. A careless reading quotes the moral and calls it the event.

This is the prohibition that keeps the chapter honest. We do not treat a chronicle's moral as a fact it witnessed. We also do not invent a quotation from a chronicler in order to have something impressive to disagree with. An invented sentence can be used in an example only with the label on it, and the example has to show the road by which a real trace would replace it. There is no short cut in which a plausible voice counts as a source because it sounds old.

Coins and files belong in the same discipline. A coin of a ruler whose title we otherwise know is a small, hard chronicle of a claim: this name, this authority, this metal, struck to pass from hand to hand. Survival of the coin shows that it was made and that it outlasted use. It does not show that the title was loved, or that the mint's politics were the politics of the village where the coin was lost. An inquisition file shows the questions a court thought worth asking and the answers a clerk thought worth writing. The file is hostile by design. Hostility does not make it empty, and it does not make it a diary. [[christendom/exclusion]] teaches the thirteenth-century procedure as a category authorities built. [[sources/scale]] will refuse to let one Friulian miller's file stand for a people. Neither chapter needs us to pretend the file is a transparent mind, and neither needs a description of interrogation. The file is a source about a procedure and about what that procedure recorded. The inference starts after that sentence, not inside it.

:::theorem A silence is about the asking {#thm-silence}
Where a survey records no peasant's opinion, or a roll records no women's votes, the supported sentence is that the makers did not ask those people, or did not count their answers as part of the act. The unsupported sentence is that the omitted people had no mind, no judgement, or no stake.
:::
:::proof
Domesday is the clear case. The commissioners of 1086 were not collecting a peasant review of the conquest of 1066. The absence of that review matches the purpose in [[#def-source]]. A roll of a political act that names men and does not name women voters matches a political body defined so as to leave women out, of the kind [[greece/athenian-democracy]] studies in another century and [[sources/naming-europe]] meets whenever a map of the worthy is mistaken for a map of the inhabitants. The silence sits where the purpose predicts it. That is positive evidence about the archive. It is not positive evidence about an inner vacancy.

What would count against [[#thm-silence]] is a record built to gather the opinions of the people in question, surviving intact, with a studied blank that other evidence shows to mean "chose not to have a view" rather than "was not admitted". A burned register does not meet that test. A roll that never had a column for women does not meet it either. What would also count against a careless expansion of the claim is the habit of calling every gap a plot. Some gaps are decay, fire and loss. A loss supports a sentence about the survival of traces. It still does not support a sentence about an empty mind. The claim is the narrow one. Who was asked is a historical fact. What the unasked person thought is a different question, and this silence does not answer it.
:::

## A book, a murder, and the craft

Marc Bloch's *The Historian's Craft* was published in 1949. The atlas lists it as a book on evidence, criticism and the legitimacy of the craft. It was written during the war and published after Bloch was murdered. We will not invent the manner of the murder, and we will not invent his sentences. There is no quotation to memorise here, because a made-up line would break the rule it pretends to illustrate. The fact we use is smaller and harder. The craft has a history. The person who wrote a defence of critical history could be killed, and the book could appear afterwards, as a book, with a date. Method is not a view from nowhere. It is something people do, inside institutions, under governments, and sometimes against them.

1949 is also, in this chronology, a year of other acts: the Berlin blockade runs to 12 May 1949, the North Atlantic Treaty is 4 April 1949, and the two German states of that year are dated 23 May and 7 October. Those are not the book. Putting them on one year-number does not make a survey, a plague report, a treaty and a work of method into one event. The timeline below is a set of kinds of trace, not a ranking and not a plot. A reader who wants the book to "lead to" the treaty, or the treaty to explain the book, has already left [[#thm-separate]].

What the 1949 publication can support is modest. There is a book. It has an author. It has a year. The author was murdered, and the publication follows the murder. The craft it names is therefore not outside the history it studies. What the publication cannot support is a paraphrase we have not earned. If you have not got his sentence, you do not improve the chapter by composing one and admiring it. The reminder is the historical fact of the book and the death. Criticism of sources is itself something that happens in time.

Move the window before you join the dots. From 1086 to 1347 is 261 years. From 1347 to 1582 is 235 years. From 1582 to 1949 is 367 years. The calendar reform of 1582, in which Rome followed 4 October with 15 October, is a different kind of trace again, and [[sources/time-and-names]] teaches it. A window of forty years will not make neighbours of a survey and a book. The figure is here so that four legitimate dates can sit in one frame without becoming one story. The frame is a tool. The story would be an inference, and it would be a bad one.

:::example An invented moral {#ex-invented-moral}
The following sentence is invented for the example. It is not a quotation, not a chronicle, and not a source. "When the plague came, the chronicler saw that the kingdom had deserved it, and heaven had answered." What would it take to promote this sentence into evidence, and what would it still not be evidence of?
:::solution
Promotion requires a surviving trace that contains this sentence or of which this is a fair paraphrase; a maker; a date, or a stated reason why no date is locked; and a purpose. You would then separate layers. If the maker was in a place where the sickness had arrived, the trace might support a sentence about that arrival, and only within the limits of one witness. The clause about desert, and the clause about heaven answering, are the moral. They are evidence that the maker offered that lesson. They are not a fact the maker witnessed in the way a cart or a shut house might have been witnessed. [[#thm-separate]] is the rule. Even after promotion, the invented lesson does not become a census of guilt, and it does not become a death total. This atlas has no European death total to attach to 1347, 1348 or 1349. A real chronicle that moralised the plague would still be a chronicle. The label "invented" is what keeps the present sentence from being mistaken for that chronicle.
:::
:::

:::example What Domesday is being asked to do {#ex-domesday}
A reader writes two sentences. First: "In 1086 a survey recorded the English kingdom for a conqueror, twenty years after Hastings." Second: "The survey shows that the peasants accepted the conquest, because it records no complaint." Which sentence is tied to the source, and which is an inference the silence does not allow?
:::solution
The first stays with the source. Domesday is dated 1086. Hastings is dated 14 October 1066. The difference of the year numerals is 20. The survey is an instrument of the regime that followed the conquest, not a picture of Europe, which is the limit [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] will use again. The second sentence takes the silence identified in [[#thm-silence]] and reverses it. No column of complaint means the commissioners were not collecting peasant judgements. It does not mean the judgements were favourable, or that they did not exist. Acceptance is an inference of a different kind, and it would need a trace built to record consent. Domesday is not that trace. The second sentence has borrowed the authority of a famous survey for a question the survey was not asked.
:::
:::

:::example Reports and a later range {#ex-plague-range}
October 1347 is a report of plague at Messina. A textbook adds, "so we know that half of Europe died." Using only what this atlas locks, what may you say about the reports of 1347, 1348 and 1349, and what must you refuse?
:::solution
You may say that plague was reported at Messina in October 1347, that it was in much of western Europe in 1348 and 1349, and that further waves followed. Those are witnesses of a disaster and of a spread, not a census. You may say that in many studied regions estimates fall between about a third and about a half, and that some places differ. You must refuse a single European death total, and you must refuse "half of Europe died" as a fact. The locked range is not a secret number inside the Messina report. It is a later, regional kind of inference, and it is not uniform. The textbook sentence has replaced a dated report with a census the sources do not support. [[crises/plague]] keeps the distinction. So does [[#def-inference]].
:::
:::

:::example A roll without women {#ex-roll}
This roll is invented for the example, and it is not a document in any archive. It lists twelve men as present at a court and lists no women as voters. A commentator, also invented, concludes that women in that place had no political judgement. What does the invented roll support, once it is admitted as a toy, and what would a real roll of the same shape support?
:::solution
Inside the toy, the roll supports only its own stipulation: twelve male names, no women voters, purpose unspecified until we add one. The commentator's conclusion breaks [[#thm-silence]]. Absence from a voting list is evidence about who was entered as a voter. It is not evidence about a mind. If the toy were replaced by a real roll, promotion would still only yield a sentence about that act: these names were recorded as participants, women were not recorded as voters, and the record matches a body that did not ask them. A further sentence about intelligence, consent or indifference would remain an inference, and a bad one, unless some other trace was built to ask the question. Twelve is part of the invention. It is not a statistic of any real court. The example is here to keep a clean roll from being promoted into a psychology.
:::
:::

- [ ] A chronicle's closing moral is the event the writer watched
- [x] No women's names on a voting roll are evidence about who was asked, not about whether women had minds
- [ ] A plague report in 1347 is a census of Europe

[[#thm-silence]] allows the third sentence and blocks the first. A moral is not a witnessed fact, which blocks the second, and a disaster report is not a census, which blocks the fourth. Domesday in 1086 and the Messina report of October 1347 stay sources only for what their makers were doing.

- [x] A surviving trace, a maker, a date or an honest gap, a purpose, and a separation between the trace and the inference
- [ ] A famous book published in 1949, which can lend its authority to the sentence

The second answer is the test attached to [[#def-source]] and [[#thm-separate]]. Tone is not a maker. Bloch's book of 1949 is a real book about the craft, not a source for sentences we compose in its shadow. A silence supports a claim about who was asked, which is [[#thm-silence]], and does not become evidence by being repeated.

:::warning The moral promoted, and the silence reversed
The usual myth says that a chronicle's lesson is what happened, and that people missing from the record had nothing to think. The myth fails twice. The moral is the writer's work, not a layer of the event, even when the writer saw part of what the narrative describes. The silence is the shape of the asking. Domesday records a kingdom for a conqueror in 1086. It does not record peasant consent, and it does not record Europe. The Messina report of October 1347 records an alarm of plague, not a European total this atlas refuses to invent. A murdered historian's book, published in 1949, is a reminder that criticism has a date. It is not a fund of quotations we are free to compose.
:::

## What the test changes

Apply the test to the outline refused in the previous chapter and it becomes less rhetorical. A map that leaves out Jewish communities, Roma, al-Andalus or the Roman state in the east is often defended by a silence: the birthday story did not mention them, so they were not really part of the thing. [[#thm-silence]] says the birthday story shows who the storyteller asked. Canon 68 of the Fourth Lateran Council, in November 1215, requires distinctive dress for Jews and Muslims in Latin Christendom. That canon is a source about a decree. It is not a description of every town, and it is not evidence that the people named in it began to exist when the canon noticed them. Presence is not the same as being asked.

The same test limits generosity as well as exclusion. A reader who wants the past to be kinder than the record may fill every silence with the voice they prefer. That filling is also an inference, and it is also unearned. We do not know, from the blank, that the peasant opposed the conquest, any more than we know that the peasant accepted it. We know the survey did not ask. Holding that line is not cold. It is what leaves the person in place, with a mind the archive failed to collect, instead of recruiting the person as a mute extra in someone else's moral.

Bloch's year belongs in that discipline rather than in a gallery of great books. The craft's legitimacy is itself a historical question: who is allowed to criticise a document, who is killed for the company they keep, what a publication after a murder can and cannot prove. None of those questions is answered by a sentence we wish the book had contained. They are answered, where they are answered at all, by traces with makers. The next chapter turns to a tool that looks even more neutral than a charter. A date can be wrong in a way that is not an error, and two calendars can name one day. That problem is [[sources/time-and-names]].

## Where this leads

The separation of trace, inference and silence is the method the rest of the atlas keeps using. [[sources/time-and-names]] asks why a date is not a neutral label. [[sources/argument]] asks how a claim carries its own counter-evidence, and [[sources/scale]] asks when a true sentence at one scale becomes false at another. Domesday returns as a survey of a kingdom in [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]]. The plague returns in [[crises/plague]]. The inquisitorial procedure returns in [[christendom/exclusion]], and one miller's file, which this chapter has not quoted, is the scale problem of [[sources/scale]].

:::summary
- A source is a trace with a maker and a purpose. An inference is the sentence a historian adds. They are not the same object.
- A charter, a chronicle, a coin, a parish register and an inquisition file fail in different ways. None of them is a window with no one standing at it.
- Domesday in 1086 is a survey made for a conqueror. It can support sentences about that inquiry. It cannot support a peasant's consent, and it does not describe Europe.
- The plague reports of October 1347, and the spread in 1348 and 1349, witness a disaster. They are not a census, and this atlas states no European death total.
- A chronicle's moral is evidence about the writer. It is not a fact the chronicle witnessed. Invented sentences stay labelled invented.
- A silence is evidence about who was asked. It is not evidence that the omitted person had no mind.
- Bloch's *The Historian's Craft*, published in 1949 after he was murdered, is a reminder that the craft has a history. It is not a stock of lines we may invent.
- Promotion into evidence needs a trace, a maker, a date or an honest gap, a purpose, and a statement of what would count against the inference.
:::

:::exercise The famine is not the plague {level=2}
The Great Famine is dated 1315 to 1317. Plague is reported at Messina in October 1347. Why is a chronicle moral about a single punished age a bad inference from those two traces?
:::solution
The traces are a generation apart: 1347 minus 1315 is 32 years. One is a famine, the other a plague. Each can be witnessed, in the limited way a report witnesses a disaster, without either report witnessing a single moral shape called the age. The moral bundles them. [[#thm-separate]] puts the bundle on the writer's side of the line, or on ours, and not inside either event. Treating the moral as a fact that was seen would break the rule against promoting a chronicle's lesson into the event itself. No death total is required for the argument, and none is stated.

:::exercise A file is not a diary {level=2}
The papal Inquisition associated with Gregory IX is dated 1231. The Spanish Inquisition is authorised on 1 November 1478. What can an inquisition file support, and why does neither date make the file a diary of an unmediated mind?
:::solution
A file supports sentences about the procedure and about what the clerk recorded: the categories the court used, the questions it thought worth asking, the answers it preserved. 1231 and 1478 are different institutions, so a file from one is not a source for the other. Hostility is part of the purpose. It limits the inference. It does not erase the trace, and it does not convert the trace into the person's own account with the court removed. We do not describe interrogation, and we do not invent the words of anyone examined. [[christendom/exclusion]] takes up the earlier procedure. [[sources/scale]] refuses to let one person's file illustrate a whole people. Both limits follow from [[#def-source]].
:::

:::exercise Promote, or refuse {level=3}
The following sentence is invented for the exercise. It is not a source. "The peasants told the Domesday commissioners that they welcomed the new king." What is missing from the promotion test, and which real feature of 1086 already counts against the inference even before a trace is found?
:::hint
List maker, trace, date and purpose. Then ask whether a survey of lordship was built to collect that welcome.
:::
:::solution
The sentence has no surviving trace, no named maker, and no purpose beyond our invention, so it fails the promotion test at the start. Labelling it invented is mandatory, and it has been labelled. Even if a line of greeting turned up in a genuine return, [[#thm-separate]] would still ask whether the line was the commissioners' formula, a lord's formula, or a peasant's speech, and we would not invent the speech. What already counts against the inference is the purpose of Domesday. The survey of 1086 records a kingdom for a conqueror after 1066. It is not an inquiry into popular welcome. A silence where welcome would have been is [[#thm-silence]]: evidence about who was asked. The invented sentence reverses that silence into a quotation. That is the move the chapter forbids.
:::
:::

:::exercise Loss or exclusion {level=3}
A parish register for a given year has no burials entered. One historian says the pages were lost. Another says nobody died. A third says the blank shows that the poor had no feelings about death. Sort the three sentences. You need not invent the year or the parish.
:::hint
Only one of these sentences might be an inference about the survival of a trace. The others change the subject. Feelings are a mind, and a blank is not a mind.
:::
:::solution
The lost-pages sentence is an inference about survival. It may be right or wrong, and it needs evidence about the book as an object: a broken binding, a scribe who says the quire is gone, a comparison with another year. It does not, by itself, describe the parish's health. The nobody-died sentence treats a blank as a census result. [[#def-inference]] blocks that unless the register is known to be complete and the blank is known to mean zero burials rather than zero writing. The feelings sentence breaks [[#thm-silence]] directly. A register was never a record of feelings. A blank in it is about entries, or about the loss of entries. It is not about whether the poor had minds. No death total, real or invented, would repair the third sentence. The exercise uses no year because none was locked for this imaginary book, and a fake year would have pretended to be a source.
:::
:::
