When does a true story at one scale fail at another?

A sentence can be true and still be the wrong size. [[sources/argument]] asked a claim to carry its own counter-evidence, and to refuse a cause that could not have been otherwise. The next failure is quieter. The sentence is true of a life, and someone treats it as true of a kingdom. It is true of a kingdom's survey, and someone treats it as true of a continent. It is true of a trade's embarkations, and someone treats it as a biography, or as a total of every slave system in the world. A life, a manor, a kingdom and a trade do not illustrate one another for free. The illustration is a further argument. Most of the time it has not been made.

The temptation has two faces, and this chapter refuses both. One face says that only the vivid small story is real, and that a total or a survey is a fiction of clerks. The other face says that only the large number knows anything, and that a single file is an anecdote. Microhistory does not disprove all large-scale history. Large-scale history does not make a life unreal. Each scale answers the question that matches its traces. Using one to cancel the other is how a true sentence becomes a false one without a single date being wrong.

## Four sizes of object

:::definition Scale {#def-scale}
**Scale** is the size of the object a sentence is about. In this chapter the sizes are a life, a manor, a kingdom and a trade. A manor, as the atlas uses the word elsewhere, is a unit of lordship and rent, not a miniature kingdom. Moving a true sentence from one size to another is a new claim, not a restatement.
:::

:::definition Illustration {#def-illustration}
An **illustration** is the claim that a story at one scale stands for a story at another. It is an inference in the sense of [[sources/evidence]]. It is earned only when you can say how the smaller trace was chosen, what would count against the larger sentence, and which object you have stopped talking about. It is not a free gift of a vivid example.
:::

:::theorem Truth does not travel free between scales {#thm-travel}
A true sentence about a life, a manor, a kingdom or a trade does not become a true sentence about any of the others merely by being true. Domesday describes a kingdom's survey. It does not describe Europe. One inquisition file describes what that file records. It does not describe a people. An embarkation total describes a trade's estimate. It does not describe a person, and it does not describe every slave system there has been.
:::
:::proof
The three objects are the evidence, and they refuse one another's jobs. In 1086 the Domesday survey recorded the English kingdom for a conqueror, twenty years after Hastings on 14 October 1066. [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] teaches it as a survey of that kingdom. A reader who concludes that Europe in 1086 had the shape of those returns has changed the object from a realm to a continent. Nothing in the commissioners' purpose, which [[sources/evidence]] insisted on keeping, asked them to describe Europe. The sentence "this manor owed what the return says" can be true while the sentence "Europe was this manor" is false.

Carlo Ginzburg's *The Cheese and the Worms*, published in 1976, is a study built on an inquisition record of one Friulian miller, Menocchio. That is the whole of what this chapter will say about him. We do not invent his words, and we do not retell the testimony. The allowed sentence is already a scale. It is one file, one miller, one study. It is not the peasantry of Europe, and it is not a refutation of every kingdom-sized history. A question about that miller is a question at the scale of a life and a file. A question about a trade or a state needs other traces. Using the file to cancel the survey, or the survey to cancel the file, fails [[#def-illustration]].

The Slave Voyages embarkation-region table covering 1501 to 1866 sums to 12,521,354 people embarked. The figure is an estimate from that project's published table, not a headcount taken at the time. From 1501 to 1866 is 365 years, the later year minus the earlier, the same rule by which 843 minus 800 is 43. We do not divide the total by 365 to invent an annual rate. The total is not a biography of any one person embarked. It is not a world total of all slavery. Other slave trades existed, and this number does not absorb them. Fewer people disembarked than embarked, because people died on the voyage. We state no disembarkation total, no national share and no death total. We do not describe a ship, a hold or a procedure. [[states/atlantic]] is where the trade is taught. Here the number is a scale: a trade, across centuries, and nothing smaller or larger unless a new argument is written.

What would count against [[#thm-travel]] is a rule by which any true local sentence is automatically true at the next size up, or any true total is automatically true of each life inside it. There is no such rule. What would count against a harsher reading, in which the scales cannot touch at all, is a real argument of illustration: a sample designed to represent a kingdom, a survey that says what it counted, a statement of what variation would refute the larger sentence. Domesday is that kind of instrument for England, and still not for Europe. The claim is that the illustration has to be built. It is not that building one is impossible. Microhistory does not disprove the large scale by existing, and the large scale does not disprove the file.
:::

The figure is the same point as a shaded overlap. One circle is a local fact. The other is a state story. The shading is the rare case in which the local fact is also the state story, because someone has done the work of showing it. Most of each circle is not in the other. A life can be entirely true and sit outside the state story. A state story can be entirely true and contain no particular life you happen to have read. Changing the expression moves the shading. It does not move the historical burden of proof.

[[#thm-travel]] is what you have left when the shading is gone. A true local fact, unshaded, is still true. It has merely stopped pretending to be the kingdom.

## A kingdom is not a continent

Domesday is the cleanest case because the trace is famous and the overreach is tempting. Fame makes readers generous. A book that large, they feel, ought to be about everything that matters in its century. The commissioners were not asked about everything that matters. They were asked about a conquered kingdom's land and lordship. A manor inside the survey is a unit of lordship and rent. Its return can support a sentence about that unit, within the limits of what the inquiry recorded. The kingdom's survey can support sentences about the kingdom the inquiry covered. Europe is a further object, and [[sources/naming-europe]] has already refused to treat it as one natural thing. Stretching Domesday until it covers that name does not make the name more precise. It makes the survey less honest.

The step from manor to kingdom is not free either, which is why Domesday had to be a survey rather than a single court roll held up as typical. One manor's mix of labour and rent does not illustrate the next valley. [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] will show that unfreedom varied, and it will not do so by declaring a continent of identical peasants. This chapter only needs the logic. If the scales were interchangeable, a survey would be a waste of parchment. The reason to visit more than one manor is that the first manor's truth does not travel. The reason to stop at the kingdom's edge is that the survey's truth does not travel past the questions that were asked.

A life inside the kingdom is a third size again. The survey can name a holder. It cannot, on the rule in [[sources/evidence]], tell you that the unasked person had no mind. Nor can one holder's file, if a file existed, tell you the kingdom's totals. The holder and the survey are both real. They illustrate each other only where a claim says how. Most of the time the honest sentence is the smaller one: this return, this place, this year, 1086.

## One file is not a people

Menocchio is easy to misuse in both directions, which is why the limit on what we may say is so tight. There is an inquisition record of one Friulian miller. Ginzburg's book of 1976 is a study of that record and of what such a record can and cannot show about a life. We do not quote the miller. We do not describe the beliefs the court wrote down. We do not explain the book's title. A title is a title. Glossing it would be a back door into the testimony, and the testimony is not ours to invent. The scale point can be made with no detail at all.

At the scale of a life, the file is a source of the kind an inquisition produces: a court that already classified some beliefs as heresy, a clerk, a purpose. [[sources/evidence]] separated that file from a diary. The separation stands. At the scale of a people, the file has not even started. One miller in Friuli is not the peasants of the peninsula, not the peasants of Latin Christendom, and not a type called the medieval mind. Calling him typical would be an illustration, and we have not offered the argument that would earn it. Calling him a refutation of kingdoms, trades and surveys would be the opposite illustration, and it is the one the warning below forbids. Microhistory at its own scale is a way of asking what one record can hold. It is not a victory over every other way of asking.

Who benefits from stretching the file? A teacher who wants a human face on a century benefits, and the benefit is real as a motive, and it is not an argument. A reader who is tired of totals benefits from the claim that the total has been defeated by a story. The total has not been defeated. It was about something else. Who benefits from ignoring the file? A history that wants only states, and that finds a miller embarrassing because he does not illustrate the state, benefits from dropping him. The file remains a source for questions at its size. Embarrassment is not a scale.

The inquisition that produced such files is not, by that fact, the papal procedure of 1231 or the Spanish institution authorised on 1 November 1478. Those are dated institutions with their own chapters. We do not assign this miller's record to either year. No year for the hearing is locked in the chronology we are using, and inventing one would fake a precision the scale lesson does not need. The object is one file. The date of the book we may name is 1976. That is a date of publication, not a date of the life.

## A trade is not a life

:::theorem The embarkation total is a trade, and only a trade {#thm-embarked}
The Slave Voyages figure of 12,521,354 people embarked, covering 1501 to 1866, is an estimate of embarkations in the Atlantic trade as that table defines it. It is not a contemporary headcount, not a biography, not a national share, not a death total, and not a world total of all slavery. Fewer people disembarked than embarked, because people died. The number does not describe a ship.
:::
:::proof
The published table is the evidence, and the chronology prints the sum with its warnings beside it. An estimate is a later construction from voyage records. It is not a roll called on a beach in a single year. Treating it as a headcount taken at the time promotes a modern total into a contemporary census, which is the error [[sources/evidence]] named when a plague report was asked to be a census. The span is 365 years. A person embarked in the sixteenth century and a person embarked in the nineteenth are both inside the sum and are not one life. A biography would need a name, a voyage and a trace of that person. The sum has none of those roles. It counts embarkations.

The sum is not all the slavery in the world, because it is one trade's estimate, the Atlantic embarkations in that table, and the atlas has already recorded that it is not a count of every slave trade. Saying "fewer disembarked than embarked, because people died" is as far as the death sentence may go. A percentage, a national share or a count of the dead would be a new quantity, and this chronology does not lock one. Inventing it would be false precision of the kind a scale error loves, because a precise-looking death total feels like it has explained the trade. It has not. It has decorated it. [[states/atlantic]] can teach the legal sequence around the trade, the Code Noir, the abolitions, the revolt in Saint-Domingue, without our describing a hold or a procedure here. Description of that kind is refused. The refusal does not shrink the number. The number stays 12,521,354 people embarked, as an estimate, with the limits in the same sentence.

What would count against [[#thm-embarked]] is a contemporary global headcount, locked in this chronology, of which this figure is the transcription, or a locked disembarkation total that this chapter has been hiding. There is none. What would also count against it is evidence that no one died on the voyage, so that embarked and disembarked were the same quantity. The chronology tells us to say the opposite: fewer disembarked, because people died. What would count against using the figure as a moral about a civilisation is the figure's own narrowness. It is one trade. It is not a verdict on a continent's character, and [[sources/naming-europe]] has already refused that kind of character. A true total at the scale of a trade becomes false when it is asked to be a soul, a nation or a world.
:::

The arithmetic of the span is worth doing once so that the 365 does not turn into a year of days or a licence to divide. 1866 minus 1501 is 365. The result is a count of years of coverage, not a claim that the trade was the same size in each of them, and not a claim that the path from the first year to the last was a straight line. The widget in [[states/atlantic]] will refuse to invent that path. We refuse here, in prose, to invent the annual average the division would produce. An average not in the chronology is an invented quantity. Scale errors often arrive as averages: the total divided by the years, then offered as "the typical voyage" or "the typical life". Typical is an illustration. We have not earned it.

A manor and a trade fail each other in the same way. A manor's court can fine a miller, and the fine, if it is in a real roll, is a local fact. It does not tell you how many people were embarked across the Atlantic, and the embarkation total does not tell you what the fine was. Readers reach for these crossings because both subjects are about power and labour. The subject matter is not the scale. Shared themes are not an illustration. [[#def-illustration]] demands a path from one trace to the other. A theme is not a path.

:::example An invented village {#ex-northmere}
Northmere is invented for this example. It is not in Domesday, and it is not a place you can visit. Inside the invention, one fact is stipulated to be true: Northmere's manor court fined its only miller for a short measure. A national sentence is then drawn: "The kingdom's economy was a single mill, and the kingdom's justice was that fine." The local fact is true inside the example. Why is the national sentence false, and what would have to be added before a local fact could support a kingdom sentence?
:::solution
The national sentence changes the object. A miller and a fine are a life and a manor, in the sense of [[#def-scale]]. A kingdom's economy and a kingdom's justice are larger objects. [[#thm-travel]] says the truth does not travel free. The invention never stipulated that any other place matched Northmere, so the word "kingdom" is doing the illustrating all by itself. That is [[#def-illustration]] with none of the work done. What would have to be added is an argument about the sample: how Northmere was chosen, what was counted in other places, and what variation would count against the national sentence. Domesday in 1086 is a real survey that tries to be that kind of instrument for the English kingdom, and even Domesday does not describe Europe. A vivid fine is not a shortcut around a survey. The fine, the mill and the village remain invented. They are not statistics, and they are not a source.
:::
:::

:::example The survey stops at the realm {#ex-domesday-europe}
Which sentences can the Domesday survey of 1086 support, and which sentence changes the scale to Europe without an argument?
:::solution
It can support sentences about the inquiry itself and about the English kingdom the commissioners were sent to record, within the purpose of a conqueror's survey after 1066. It can support a manor-sized sentence only where a particular return is the trace, and even then the manor is a unit of lordship and rent, not a model of every other manor. It cannot support "this is Europe in 1086". That sentence keeps the year, which is real, and replaces the kingdom with a continent the survey did not describe. [[sources/naming-europe]] is why the continent was never a single object waiting for an English return to reveal it. [[#thm-travel]] is why a true kingdom survey is still false as a continental picture. The shaded overlap in the figure would be the wrong tool here. Europe is not the state story of which an English manor is the local fact, unless a separate argument says so. No such argument is available from the survey alone.
:::
:::

State the Slave Voyages embarkation total, the years it covers, and four things it must not be asked to be. Include the sentence about disembarkation that the chronology does allow.

The total must not be asked to be a biography, a national share, a death total, or a world total of all slavery. Fewer people disembarked than embarked, because people died on the voyage. No disembarkation figure is stated, because none is locked. No ship, hold or procedure is described. [[#thm-embarked]] is the claim. The place where the trade is taught, rather than used as a scale lesson, is [[states/atlantic]]. Other slave trades are outside the sum, which is another reason the sum is not the world.
:::

:::example The file and the survey {#ex-file-survey}
Set the allowed sentence about Menocchio beside the allowed sentence about Domesday. What does each sentence forbid the other to do, and what would it mean to say that microhistory had disproved the survey?
:::solution
The allowed sentence about the miller is that there is an inquisition record of one Friulian miller, studied in Ginzburg's *The Cheese and the Worms*, published in 1976. It forbids Domesday to be a source for that man's mind, and it forbids us to invent his words so that he can illustrate 1086 or any other year. The allowed sentence about Domesday is that the 1086 survey describes an English kingdom for a conqueror and does not describe Europe. It forbids the miller's file to stand in for the kingdom, and it forbids the kingdom's survey to stand in for the miller. Saying that microhistory disproves the survey would treat one file as a refutation of a different object. [[#thm-travel]] blocks that. The two truths sit in different circles of the figure. Their overlap is not given. It would have to be argued, and this chapter does not argue it, because the file and the survey do not answer one question.
:::

:::warning The vivid life that cancels the map
The usual myth says that a story is serious only when it is small enough to imagine, and that a total or a survey is a clerk's fiction which one good life can explode. The myth fails in both directions. Menocchio's file, which we do not quote, is real at the scale of one Friulian miller and one book of 1976. It does not explode Domesday, and Domesday does not explode it. The embarkation estimate is real at the scale of a trade and false as a biography or as all the slavery in the world. Northmere, being invented, is real inside its example and false as a kingdom. Microhistory does not disprove large-scale history. It occupies a scale. The shaded overlap on the figure is rare because illustration is work, not because one of the circles is a lie.
:::

## What Greece will ask of the same rule

The rule leaves this course and enters the next one without a change of standard. A polis is a community of citizens and a territory. It is not a Greek nation, and a true sentence about Athens is not yet a sentence about "the Greeks" as a state. There was no Greek state. Shared language, cult and games are real at their scale. They do not illustrate a nation that was not there. [[greece/poleis]] is where that category is taught. The method you carry is the one you have just used on a manor and a kingdom. Do not let a city stand for a people it did not govern. Do not let a people-word stand for a constitution the cities never had.

The same rule will sit under every later total. A census of one realm is not a census of a continent. A battle is not a war, and a war is not a civilisation. A membership list of a treaty organisation is not the coastline, which is where [[sources/naming-europe]] began. Scale is how those refusals stay practical when the material is no longer a method chapter but a story you want to believe. The test is repetitive on purpose. What is the object of this sentence? What trace is the right size for that object? What true sentence at the neighbouring size have I just conscripted without an argument? If you cannot answer, the sentence is not ready. It may be beautiful. Beauty is not a scale.

The overlap remains available. Sometimes a local fact is the state story, because the state was small, or because the trace was built as a survey, or because the law you are describing is a law about the whole body and not about one household. The figure shades that case so you do not forget it exists. Rarity is not impossibility. [[#thm-travel]] forbids the free ride. It does not forbid the journey. When you make the journey, you say so, and you say what would count against the illustration. That sentence is the whole craft, at every size the atlas still has to cross.

## Where this leads

Scale is the last piece of method before the history proper. [[greece/poleis]] asks what a polis was, if it was not a Greek nation: a true city is not yet a nation, and a shared language is not yet a state. The survey that must not be stretched to Europe returns in [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]]. The embarkation total returns, still an estimate and still not a biography, in [[states/atlantic]]. The claim that has to survive the change of scale was built in [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- Scale is the size of the object: a life, a manor, a kingdom, a trade. A true sentence at one size is not automatically true at the next.
- An illustration is a further argument. It has to say how the trace was chosen and what would count against the larger sentence.
- Domesday in 1086 describes an English kingdom's survey for a conqueror. It does not describe Europe, and one manor inside it does not describe the kingdom for free.
- There is an inquisition record of one Friulian miller, Menocchio, studied in Ginzburg's *The Cheese and the Worms* (1976). We do not invent his words. One file is not a people.
- Microhistory does not disprove large-scale history. Each scale answers the question its traces can bear.
- Slave Voyages estimates 12,521,354 people embarked from 1501 to 1866. The figure is not a headcount, not a biography, not a national share, not a death total, and not all the slavery in the world.
- Fewer people disembarked than embarked, because people died. No further death figure is stated, and no ship is described.
- The shaded overlap of a local fact and a state story is rare. Most of each circle is not the other.
:::

:::exercise Build the missing argument {level=3}
You want a local fact to enter the shaded overlap and count as a state story. Choose the invented village Northmere, keep it labelled invented, and say what you would have to add. Then say why even a successful argument of that kind would still not describe Europe, and still not describe the Atlantic trade.
:::hint
Think of what Domesday added, for one kingdom, that a single fine did not. Then remember the edge of the kingdom and the edge of the trade.
:::
:::solution
Northmere's fine, inside the invention, is a local fact about one miller and one manor court. To enter the overlap you would have to add a real design of inquiry: many places, the same questions, a statement of what was not counted, and a rule for what variation would refute a kingdom sentence. That is the sort of thing the 1086 survey is, for England, and the example still cannot borrow Domesday's authority, because Northmere is invented and is not in the survey. Even if the argument succeeded inside a real kingdom, [[#thm-travel]] would stop it at that kingdom's edge. Europe is a further scale, and Domesday itself does not cross it. The Atlantic embarkation total is a different object again, an estimate of 12,521,354 people embarked from 1501 to 1866, not a manor fine and not a continental picture. A successful illustration has a stated size. It does not, by succeeding, illustrate everything else. Fewer people disembarked than embarked, because people died, and that sentence is about the trade, not about Northmere. The village remains invented. No testimony, no ship and no death total is added in order to make the overlap look larger than it is.
:::
