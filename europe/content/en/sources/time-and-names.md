Why is a date not a neutral label?

A date looks like the one part of a history that cannot be accused of taking a side. It is a number, a month, a day. [[sources/evidence]] has just insisted that a charter and a silence are made by someone. The calendar seems to sit underneath that trouble, as if the year were a shelf and the events were objects placed on it. They are not. The shelf has a shape. There is no year 0. Rome, Britain and Russia did not tear the same days out of the same October. A regnal year names a king before it names a time, and a nickname such as the Terror or the Middle Ages is a claim about what the time was worth. Someone benefits from the name. The public timeline of this atlas prints BC. The lesson says BCE for the same numbers. The difference is a printing convention, not two pasts.

The point is not that dates are optional. The point is that a date is already a small argument, and a reader who cannot see the argument will "correct" a true date into a false one. A day dated before a calendar reform is not an error. It is dated in the calendar then in use. Two labels for the October Revolution are one event. Inserting a year 0 to make a subtraction look like ordinary arithmetic does not repair the count. It invents a year.

## The year that is not there

:::definition The historical year count {#def-count}
The **historical year count** used here has no year 0. The year after 1 BCE is 1 CE. The elapsed count from year A BCE to year B CE is A plus B minus 1. From a year A CE to a later year B CE the elapsed count is B minus A, not an inclusive count of both ends. The same numbers, when this site's timeline prints them, appear as BC. BCE and BC are two names for one numeral.
:::

:::theorem The span across the divide drops the phantom zero {#thm-elapsed}
From 1 BCE to 1 CE is one year, not two. In general, the elapsed count from year A BCE to year B CE is A plus B minus 1. A subtraction that treats the BCE year as a negative integer and then forgets the missing zero counts a year that never occurred.
:::
:::proof
The sequence of years is the evidence. The year before 1 BCE is not a zero, and the year after 1 BCE is 1 CE. Nothing labelled 0 stands between them. The step from the close of 1 BCE to the close of 1 CE is therefore one year. The formula is that fact written for any pair. Add the two numerals and you have counted a zero that the sequence does not contain. Subtract one and the phantom drops out. Check the smallest case: 1 plus 1 minus 1 is 1. Check a case that does not cross the divide, so the formula must not be used by habit: from 800 to 843, both CE, the elapsed count is 843 minus 800, which is 43, not 44. Inclusive counting of both ends would inflate every span by one. This atlas does not do that.

What would count against [[#thm-elapsed]] is a year 0 in the historical count itself: a document, a reign, or an astronomical table in this era-system whose year is zero and which sits between 1 BCE and 1 CE. There is none. What would not count against it is the convenience of negative numbers in a modern calculation. Integers are a tool. They are allowed to contain a zero. The past, on this count, is not. A student who inserts the zero to make the subtraction easier has changed the calendar, not clarified it. What would also fail as a counter-example is the timeline's refusal to display a year 0. The figure below is built to show that refusal. It is not a second chronology in which zero secretly returns.
:::

The name attached to the missing zero, in the course record, is Dionysius Exiguus. We do not give him a year of his own. None is locked here, and a biography with an invented date would only add a false precision to a lesson about false precision. What we use is the count, not a life. The practical test is the one you can do without him. Take the years in order and refuse to jump from 1 BCE to a zero, or from a zero to 1 CE.

:::widget timeline
start: -5
end: 5
window: 1
events: -1,1 BCE; 1,1 CE
caption: Move the year onto 0. The readout should say there is no year 0, and that the distance from 1 BCE to 1 CE is one year.
:::

Move the year onto 0 before you trust a subtraction that looks tidy. The readout should say there is no year 0, and that the distance from 1 BCE to 1 CE is one year. The event labels say BCE because that is how the brief names them. Elsewhere on this site a negative year is printed BC. Read BC, in that readout, as the lesson's BCE. The window of one year is small on purpose. It will not let you hide the gap inside a century.

Two further spans keep the rule from being a trick that works only for the pair 1 and 1. Julius Caesar is killed on 15 March 44 BCE. Vesuvius erupts in 79 CE, and Pompeii is buried. The elapsed count from 44 BCE to 79 CE is 44 plus 79 minus 1, which is 122. You can see the same total in pieces. From 44 BCE to 1 BCE is 43 years. From 1 BCE to 1 CE is 1 year. From 1 CE to 79 CE is 78 years. Then 43 plus 1 plus 78 is 122. The integer shortcut that forgets the missing zero adds 44 and 79 and gets 123. The extra one is the phantom year. It is not a year of Pompeii, and it is not a year of the assassination. From 27 BCE, the settlement in which Octavian is called Augustus, to 117 CE, the death of Trajan, the count is 27 plus 117 minus 1, which is 143. The same wrong shortcut gives 144. Neither span is a story of decline or recovery. Each is a distance, and the distance is only as good as the rule.

:::example From 1 BCE to 1 CE {#ex-one}
How many years elapsed from 1 BCE to 1 CE, and which integer operation wrongly answers 2?
:::solution
Use [[#thm-elapsed]]. A is 1 and B is 1, so the elapsed count is 1 plus 1 minus 1, which is 1. The year after 1 BCE is 1 CE. There is no year 0 to cross. The operation that answers 2 treats the years as the integers 1 and minus 1 and subtracts, or simply adds the numerals and stops. Either way it counts a zero the historical sequence does not contain. The timeline is there so you can move onto the missing year and read the refusal. The answer is one year, not two, and not zero.
:::
:::

:::example A span that crosses, and one that does not {#ex-cross}
Compute the elapsed count from 44 BCE to 79 CE, and the elapsed count from 800 to 843. Explain why the two computations are not the same rule applied blindly.
:::solution
From 44 BCE to 79 CE the count crosses the divide, so [[#def-count]] gives 44 plus 79 minus 1, which is 122. In pieces: 44 minus 1 is 43, the step from 1 BCE to 1 CE is 1, and 79 minus 1 is 78. Then 43 plus 1 plus 78 is 122. Adding 44 and 79 to get 123 inserts a year 0. From 800 to 843 both years are CE, so the count is 843 minus 800, which is 43. You do not add, and you do not subtract an extra 1. Inclusive counting would make that span 44, and this atlas rejects it. The coronation of 800 and the partition of 843 are not the subject here. The subject is that a rule for crossing the divide must not be used on a span that never crosses it.
:::
:::

## Three reforms, three calendars

:::definition Old Style and New Style {#def-styles}
**Old Style** means the Julian civil calendar still in use in a state that has not adopted the reform. **New Style** means the Gregorian calendar. A date written before a state's own switch is a date in the calendar that state was using. It is not an error waiting to be translated out of existence.
:::

:::theorem A date before a reform is not an error {#thm-not-error}
Rome's switch, Britain's switch and Russia's switch are three acts, on three dates, omitting three different runs of day-names. A document dated on a day that one of those states had not yet skipped is dated correctly in the calendar then in force. Correcting it as if every state switched in 1582 replaces a true date with a false one.
:::
:::proof
The successions are the evidence, and they do not match. In Rome, 4 October 1582 was followed by 15 October 1582. The day-names 5 October through 14 October were not used there. That is ten names. The lived next day was one day later, called by a label ten names ahead. People did not mislay ten days of their lives. The labels jumped. In Britain, 2 September 1752 was followed by 14 September 1752. The unused names are 3 September through 13 September, eleven names. In Russia, the day after 31 January 1918, Julian, was 14 February 1918, Gregorian. The unused names are 1 February through 13 February, thirteen names. Not every country switched in 1582. Britain is the locked case of a state that waited until 1752, and Russia until 1918.

What would count against [[#thm-not-error]] is evidence that Britain or Russia did in fact skip the October days of 1582, so that a British or Russian date of 10 October 1582 was already impossible. The chronology says the opposite. Their switches are 1752 and 1918. A British date of 10 October 1582 is a Julian date, legal in Britain, and not a blunder for 20 October or for any other "corrected" day. What would also count against the claim is a showing that the skipped names were blank stretches of experience rather than renamed successive days. The sources give successions of dates, not missing weeks of consciousness. A reform can be real, local and easy to misread. The misreading is the myth this section stops.
:::

The Roman reform is Pope Gregory XIII's, and the course record dates it to 1582 for that reason. The British reform is an act of the Parliament of Great Britain. The Russian reform is an act of the Council of People's Commissars. Three authorities, three mornings. A letter dated 3 September 1752 in London is the problem case in the other direction: that day-name was not used in Britain, because 2 September was followed by 14 September. A letter dated 3 September 1751 is ordinary. The year matters, and the place matters. "September the third" is not an error in the abstract. It is an error only where the civil calendar had already abolished that name, and it is a good date where the calendar had not.

Russia's arithmetic has a trap of its own, and it is worth doing slowly so that the thirteen unused names are not "corrected" into fourteen. The day after 31 January 1918 was 14 February 1918. If you count the February labels from 1 to 14 inclusive you get fourteen names, and you have included 14 February, which was a real day, the first day of the new style. The names that did not occur are 1 through 13, which is thirteen. Lived time from the last Julian day to the first Gregorian day is one day. The label jumped. A student who announces that Russia lost fourteen days has counted the arrival. The same discipline protects Rome and Britain. Do not count the day you landed on as one of the days you skipped.

## One event, two dates, in 1917

The October Revolution is 25 October 1917 Old Style and 7 November 1917 New Style. Both dates name one event. Russia had not yet switched. The switch is the next year, 31 January followed by 14 February 1918, so a Russian civil date in 1917 is still Julian, and a Paris or London date for the same night is Gregorian. Neither label is the other label's mistake.

The conversion that joins them can be checked on the locked pair without adding new days. October has 31 days. Add thirteen days to 25 October: 25 plus 13 is 38, and 38 minus 31 is 7, so the New Style label is 7 November. Thirteen is the distance these two official styles still had in 1917, visible in the pair itself. It is not a third calendar. [[wars/revolutions-1917]] tells what the seizure did to the war and to the state. This chapter does not. A date lesson that turns into a political narrative has changed the subject. What belongs here is the double name, and the fact that the double name is older than the reform of 1918.

The February Revolution, so called, is the other locked pair of the same year, and it is a different event. On the Old Style calendar it runs from 23 February to 3 March 1917. On the New Style calendar those endpoints are 8 March and 16 March 1917. The word February records the Russian month-name. In Paris the same unrest is a March event. Calling either month a blunder confuses a name with an error, which is the failure [[#thm-not-error]] forbids. Nicholas II abdicated on 15 March 1917 New Style, which is 2 March Old Style. That day sits inside both versions of the range: 2 March falls between 23 February and 3 March, and 15 March falls between 8 March and 16 March. One abdication, two labels. We do not add any other day in 1917. The chronology locks these, and a further day would be a date we had decided to invent.

Check the February endpoints the same way, and then stop. 1917 is not a leap year in the civil count that matters here, so February has 28 days. Add thirteen days to 23 February: 23 plus 13 is 36, and 36 minus 28 is 8, so 8 March. Add thirteen days to 3 March and you land on 16 March. Add thirteen days to 2 March and you land on 15 March. The arithmetic uses only the days already named. It is not an invitation to fill the range with a diary.

:::example A British day in the Roman October {#ex-british-1582}
Rome followed 4 October 1582 with 15 October 1582. Britain followed 2 September 1752 with 14 September 1752. A London letter is dated 10 October 1582. Is the date an error, and what would a mistaken "correction" do?
:::solution
The date is not an error. [[#thm-not-error]] separates the switches. Britain did not adopt the reform in 1582. A London date of 10 October 1582 is a Julian date, written in the calendar Britain was using, and Britain went on using it until September 1752. In Rome, the names 5 October through 14 October 1582 were not civil days, so the same inscription in Rome would be a different problem. Place is part of the date. A correction that moved the London letter by ten days, or that declared it void because Gregory had already reformed Rome, would replace a true Old Style date with a fiction. The letter does not become New Style until a rule says it does, and Britain's rule is dated 1752.
:::
:::

:::example Two labels for October {#ex-october}
Show that 25 October 1917 Old Style and 7 November 1917 New Style can be one date, and say why the Russian switch of 1918 does not make one of them wrong.
:::solution
Add thirteen days to 25 October. October has 31 days, so 25 plus 13 is 38, and 38 minus 31 is 7. The New Style label is 7 November. The chronology states both labels for one revolution and places the calendar switch in 1918, when 31 January was followed by 14 February. October 1917 is before that morning. Russian civil dating and Gregorian dating therefore diverge, and both names are right in their own style. [[#def-styles]] is the distinction. What would split them into two revolutions is evidence of two seizures thirteen days apart. That evidence is not what the pair records. The political narrative is [[wars/revolutions-1917]]. The date lesson ends at the pair.
:::
:::

## Names that already take a side

:::definition A period name {#def-period}
A **period name** is a claim about a stretch of time, worn as if it were a joint in nature. A regnal year is the small version: it counts from an accession and therefore asserts whose accession counts. A nickname such as the Terror, or the Middle Ages, is the large version. It asserts what the stretch was like, and usually who was right. The dates, where we have them, are not the same thing as the nickname.
:::

A regnal year is easiest to see when it is still only a form. The clause that follows is invented for the explanation. It is not a charter. "Given in the year N of King N." Even as a toy, the clause has chosen a king. The count starts from that king's accession, and a writer who will not grant the accession will not grant the year. Two chanceries in a civil war can date the same afternoon by two reigns. Both dates can be sincere. They are not neutral labels for one shelf. They are claims about legitimacy, written in the form of a calendar. Promoting the toy into evidence of a real reign would require a real document, a real ruler and a year this chronology locks. The toy has none of those, and it must not be given them by atmosphere. [[#def-period]] is already visible in the formula. [[sources/evidence]] would call the unmarked toy an invented sentence, and would be right.

The Terror is a real convention with real dates, and the name is still a claim. As a named period it runs, conventionally, from September 1793 to 27 July 1794, the day also called 9 Thermidor, when Robespierre falls. The dates are a bracket the chronology allows. They are not a definition of the French Revolution, and they are not its opposite. [[revolutions/republic-and-terror]] teaches the bracket as a government at war, using exceptional tribunals, and it refuses a count of the dead. This chapter refuses that count as well, and it refuses any description of how people were killed. What we add is the question a date-as-label always raises. Who benefits from the name?

Someone who wants the whole Revolution judged by that bracket benefits. The nickname concentrates a long political transformation into its most frightening months and then lets the months stand for the rest. Someone who wants the bracket remembered as a sealed exception, with nothing to say about 1789 or about the years after Thermidor, also benefits. The name does the sealing. Both uses are arguments. Neither is given to you by the dates alone. September 1793 and 27 July 1794 can be stated with no nickname at all. The moment the nickname is treated as a natural object, like a mountain range, the argument has been hidden inside the word. A regnal year hides a king. The Terror hides a verdict.

The Middle Ages hide a geometry. "Middle" is only a middle if you have already chosen the two ends: a classical world that has finished, and a modern world that has begun. This atlas does not lock a first year or a last year for that nickname, and it will not invent them. Supplying a birthday for the Middle Ages would be exactly the false precision the missing dates forbid. Who benefits? Anyone who needs the centuries so named to be a trough between two peaks, and anyone who needs one European story with a dull centre so that a later rebirth, or a later state, can look like the awakening. The benefit is not a conspiracy. It is the ordinary advantage of a plot. [[crises/city-states]] refuses the plot in which a dark middle gives way to a renaissance light, and [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]] refuses "dark ages" as our own claim. Here the nickname is enough. It is a claim about a joint in time. Time does not come jointed in that way. We draw the joints, and then we forget that we drew them.

:::warning The corrected date, and the inserted zero
Two myths do most of the damage. The first says that any date before a state's calendar reform is wrong and should be shifted until it matches Rome in 1582. Britain's own switch is 1752, and Russia's is 1918. A London date of 10 October 1582 is not a Roman date that someone botched. The second myth inserts a year 0 so that BCE and CE subtract like ordinary integers. From 1 BCE to 1 CE the insertion produces two years. The count is one. A third, quieter myth treats the Terror or the Middle Ages as a joint nature made, rather than as a name from which someone benefits. The dates of the Terror, September 1793 to 27 July 1794, can be stated without the verdict. The Middle Ages cannot be given a birthday this chronology has not locked.
:::

In Rome, 4 October 1582 was followed by 15 October 1582. In Britain, 2 September 1752 was followed by 14 September 1752. In Russia, the day after 31 January 1918 was 14 February 1918. The three reforms are real, they are not the same morning, and a date written before the relevant reform is not an error.
:::

## What a neutral label was hiding

Once a date is allowed to be an argument, several famous corrections fall apart. Historians who translate every Russian day of 1917 into New Style, and then drop the Old Style name, have not removed politics from the page. They have chosen one chancellery's calendar as the real one. The choice may be convenient for a reader in a Gregorian country. Convenience is not neutrality. Keeping both names, and saying which style each belongs to, is the more accurate sentence. It is also the sentence that leaves the February Revolution in February for the people who named it, and in March for the people whose newspapers already used the new style. [[wars/revolutions-1917]] can then tell the politics without pretending the month-name was a mistake about the weather.

The regnal year and the nickname are the same lesson at two sizes. A clerk who dates by the conqueror's reign has already treated the conquest as holding. A teacher who uses the phrase "in the Middle Ages" has already committed to the claim that a classical world ended and a modern world began, and that the stretch between them is one thing. You can go on using the nickname if you show the claim. You cannot go on using it as a shelf. The Terror is the test, because the dates are locked and the temptation is strong. September 1793 to 27 July 1794 is a bracket. "The Terror" is what you have decided the bracket means, and the decision helps some stories and hurts others. Say so, or the date has quietly become a verdict.

The next chapter asks what a claim is, once dates and names are no longer allowed to do the claiming by themselves. A cause has to be something that could have been otherwise. A label such as Westphalia, or the Renaissance, bundles a claim and often hides it. The elapsed count you have just practised will not settle that question. It will only keep the years inside it honest. That question is [[sources/argument]].

## Where this leads

A date is now a small claim with a rule, not a neutral label. [[sources/argument]] separates a claim, a cause and a label, and it keeps counter-evidence inside the sentence. The double dates of 1917 are told as politics in [[wars/revolutions-1917]]. The bracket called the Terror is told as a government in [[revolutions/republic-and-terror]]. The nickname Middle Ages is under pressure wherever the atlas refuses a dark middle, beginning with [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]] and [[crises/city-states]].

:::summary
- There is no year 0. The year after 1 BCE is 1 CE, and the elapsed count from that year to that year is one.
- From year A BCE to year B CE the elapsed count is A plus B minus 1. From 800 to 843, both CE, it is 43, not an inclusive 44.
- The site timeline prints BC. The lesson says BCE for the same numbers.
- Rome followed 4 October 1582 with 15 October 1582. Britain followed 2 September 1752 with 14 September 1752. Russia followed 31 January 1918 with 14 February 1918.
- A date before the relevant reform is not an error. A British date in October 1582 is a Julian date, not a mistake.
- The October Revolution is 25 October 1917 Old Style and 7 November 1917 New Style, one event, before the switch of 1918.
- The February Revolution is 23 February to 3 March 1917 Old Style, or 8 to 16 March New Style. Nicholas II abdicated on 2 March Old Style, which is 15 March New Style.
- A regnal year, the Terror and the Middle Ages are claims. The Terror's conventional bracket is September 1793 to 27 July 1794. Someone benefits from the name.
:::
