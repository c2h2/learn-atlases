What does the name Europe pick out, and whom does a single outline leave out?

A coastline is not a people, and a people is not a treaty. The word Europe has been asked to do at least four jobs that do not coincide. It has been a classical geographical name, set beside other parts of the world and attached to no constitution. It has been a map of Latin Christendom, in which a shared Latin rite and a shared set of exclusions did the work of an edge. It has been a balance-of-power idea, a habit of diplomacy among crowned states that could, and did, erase a large polity in the name of equilibrium. Late in the story it has been a treaty organisation: a list of governments that have signed, and a list of governments that have not. These are not four sketches of one object, growing clearer until the present outline appears. They are four uses. A sentence that is true of one can be false of the others.

The temptation this chapter exists to refuse is to draw the European Union and walk backwards until the past agrees. On that walk, a coronation in Rome becomes an early Brussels, a religious peace becomes a draft constitution, and anyone who lived inside the geography but outside the later membership is treated as a delay. The walk flatters the present. It also makes the earlier centuries unreadable, because it forces them to answer a question they were not asking. Byzantium, al-Andalus, the Ottoman empire, Jewish communities and Roma were inside the geography. They have often been written as outside the story. The dates 711, 800, 1453 and 1957 are markers of different maps. They are not rungs on a single ascent, and none of them is the birth of a continent.

## A name is not the ground

:::definition The name Europe {#def-name}
**Europe**, in this chapter, is a name people have used to pick out a place, a community of the faithful, a diplomatic project, or a set of signatories. It is not a natural fact with one edge, one people, or one set of values. Mountains, seas and winters are real. They do not, by themselves, decide who belongs in the history.
:::

:::definition A map {#def-map}
A **map**, here, is a claim about an edge. It says who counts as inside, who is placed at the margin, and who is written out. The claim may be geographical, religious, diplomatic or legal. When the claim changes, the rocks do not move. Readers who treat the latest map as the ground will mis-describe every earlier one.
:::

:::theorem Four uses, four objects {#thm-four}
The classical geographical name, the map of Latin Christendom, the balance-of-power idea, and the treaty organisation established on 25 March 1957 are not the same object. A truth about one does not transfer to the others. None of them is the birth of a continent, and the four dates 711, 800, 1453 and 1957 do not form a single ascent towards a finished outline.
:::

:::proof
The four uses answer different questions, and they fail in different ways. A classical geographer could divide the inhabited world into parts and give one part this name without founding a church, a kingdom, or a customs union. There was no European state to be named. The coronation of Charlemagne by Pope Leo III on 25 December 800 made an imperial claim in the Latin west. It did not abolish the Roman state at Constantinople, and it did not describe al-Andalus. A balance among powers is a habit of diplomacy. The Treaty of Utrecht in 1713 and the Final Act of the Congress of Vienna on 9 June 1815 are settlements among states, not a creed and not a definition of a landmass. The partitions of Poland-Lithuania in 1772, 1793 and 1795 show the same habit deleting a large polity. The European Economic Community, established with Euratom by the Treaties of Rome on 25 March 1957, is a membership list. States inside the geography and outside that list were not thereby outside Europe.

What would count against [[#thm-four]] is a single institution, continuous from the classical name to 1957, whose own act fixed the edge for every century in between: one chancellery, one council, or one treaty that defined the continent rather than a membership. Shared vocabulary is not that institution. A later reader who can see all four dates on one timeline has not discovered that the people at the time were looking at that timeline. If such a continuous defining institution turned up in the record, the claim would have to be narrowed or dropped. Nothing this atlas treats as settled is that institution.
:::

The distinction is easy to say and easy to lose. We reach for one outline because atlases have covers, courses have titles, and a political argument in the present often wants a civilisational subject older than itself. [[#def-name]] blocks that move at the start. The name is something people did with a word. [[#def-map]] blocks the next move, which is to treat this year's edge as the edge that was always there, only badly drawn. [[#thm-four]] is the check. If a paragraph slides from a Greek geographical word to a medieval Latin church to a twentieth-century treaty without changing the object of the sentence, the paragraph has changed the subject and kept the noun.

Latin Christendom is a religious map, not a single set of values. A diplomatic balance is a project among states, and it could erase a state, as the partitions of Poland-Lithuania did. A true sentence about one use is not a definition of the land.
:::

## The classical name

Greek and Latin geographical teaching divided the world it knew into parts, and Europe was one of those parts, set beside Asia and beside Libya, the name later writers render as Africa. The division followed seas, shores and inherited stories. It did not follow a constitution, because there was no constitution of Europe to follow. The name could be taught to a student who had never asked which king was legitimate. That is already enough to separate it from every later political project that borrowed the same word.

What the name did not do is as important as what it did. It did not make the cities of the Aegean into a nation. A polis was a community of citizens and a territory, not a province of a Greek state, and there was no Greek state. Shared language, cult and games were real, and they were not a government. Persia, under rulers this atlas meets again in [[greece/persian-wars]], was a great empire next door, with its own politics. It was not a moral category called the East, and a Greek victory over a Persian force was not the rescue of a continent. To read those wars as the birth of European freedom is to take a classical geographical word and stuff it with a modern plot.

Rome makes the same point from the other shore. The republic and then the empire ruled lands that later maps would sort into Europe, Asia and Africa, and they described their own world in the language of the city and the empire, not as a European project. Citizenship, as [[rome/imperial-society]] will take it up, could be extended very widely without becoming a nationality in the modern sense. Enslaved people remained enslaved under that extension. Nothing in that legal history is a vote for a European spirit. When the western imperial line is given a conventional end in 476, the eastern empire continues, and the continuation is fatal to any story in which "Europe" simply inherits Rome and leaves Constantinople outside. We will use 476 as a worked claim in [[sources/argument]]. Here it is enough that a geographical name and an imperial state are already two different objects.

A classical name can be precise about shores and still be useless as a membership test. People moved across the named parts. Cults travelled. Armies recruited from more than one shore of the sea that the geographers treated as a boundary. The name picked out a teaching about the world. It did not pick out a closed club of the worthy. Later writers who speak of "European civilisation" as if Homer, a jurist in Beirut and a monk in Northumbria had enrolled in one tradition are using the classical word as a label for their own canon. The canon may be worth arguing about. It is not what the geographical name was.

## A religious map

Latin Christendom is the second use, and it is a map in the sense of [[#def-map]]. It picks out churches that recognised a Latin rite and, for much of this period, a papal claim, together with the people those churches were willing to count. It does not pick out every Christian, and it does not pick out everyone who lived between the Atlantic and the Black Sea.

On 25 December 800 Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne emperor in Rome. That act is a claim. It asserts an imperial title in the west, tied to the bishop of Rome. It is not a description of the Roman state that still ruled from Constantinople, and it is not a verdict on the societies of Iberia. The coronation has a day, a place and a beneficiary. Those are the marks of an event. They are not the marks of a continent being born. Readers who want a birthday often stop here, because a crown is a satisfying picture. [[#thm-four]] asks them to say which object was crowned. An emperor was. Europe was not.

The relationship between the Latin and Greek churches is a long process, not a switch. In 1054, mutual excommunications involving Humbert and Michael Cerularius give a marker that textbooks use for a schism. The marker is not the whole story. People did not go to bed in communion and wake up as two civilisations. Treating 1054 as the moment "Europe" chose the Latin side writes the Greek-speaking Roman world out of the continent by a clerical quarrel. The quarrel matters. It is not an edge in the rock.

Other markers show the religious map drawing an edge inside the population, not only against a rival church. The traditional date for the baptism of Vladimir of Kiev is 988. It is a traditional marker of the conversion of the Rus', not a census of belief. A baptism of a ruler is evidence about a court and a policy. It does not tell you what every inhabitant thought, any more than an official religion tells you that. In November 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council issued Canon 68, which requires Jews and Muslims in Latin Christendom to be marked by dress. That is a decree about exclusion. It is not a description of every town's practice. It is evidence that the religious map could put people who lived in Latin Christian lands formally at the edge of the visible community. A map that needs a badge for its neighbours is not a neutral outline of a continent. It is a rule about who may pass unmarked.

The crusading sequence shows the same map attacking people it also, on another description, ought to have counted as kin. Urban II preached at Clermont in November 1095. The First Crusade took Jerusalem on 15 July 1099. In 1187 Saladin took Jerusalem after Hattin. On 13 April 1204 the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople, a Christian city. Latin Christian armies destroyed life and authority in the capital of the eastern Roman state. Whatever else 1204 is — and [[christendom/crusades]] tells it as a crusade, not as an accident outside the story — it is fatal to the picture of Christendom as one side facing an outside. A religious map can turn on the Christians it has placed on the wrong side of its own line. That is a fact about the map. It is not a fact about a single European mind.

## Inside the geography, written outside the story

Byzantium is the usual western name for the Roman state that continued in the east. The name is itself a small map: it lets a Latin narrative treat that state as a neighbour of Europe rather than as the Roman empire still in place. Justinian, whose reign and law books belong to [[after-rome/byzantium]], ruled a Roman state with a Greek-speaking court. He was not a footnote to the Franks, and the Franks were not the whole of the Christian world when one of them was crowned in 800. Between that coronation and the fall of Constantinople lie centuries of an empire that minted, legislated, taxed and argued in its own voice. In 1204, as just noted, Latin crusaders sacked its capital. On 29 May 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II took Constantinople. The eastern Roman state ended. Ottoman rule is not "the end of history" in the Balkans. It is a change of sovereignty in a city that sits inside every honest geographical use of the name Europe. A history that stops the European story at the city walls in 1453 has changed the subject from the place to a religious qualification.

Al-Andalus belongs inside the same correction. In 711 Tariq ibn Ziyad's force crossed into Iberia. That crossing is the start of a long Islamic political history in the peninsula, which [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]] takes up, not an interruption of a Europe that was already complete. In 756 Abd al-Rahman I established the Umayyad emirate of Cordoba. In 929 Abd al-Rahman III took the title of caliph. The Caliphate of Cordoba is an Iberian fact and a Mediterranean fact. Writing it as a foreign episode, between a Visigothic prelude and a Christian restoration, uses the religious map as if it were the ground. The battle remembered as Tours or Poitiers has a traditional date of October 732. Some scholars, using the Chronicle of 754, prefer 733 or 734. The date is disputed, and the lesson has to say so rather than pick a numeral and hide the argument. Whichever year is preferred, the battle did not "save Europe". Raids continued. Charles Martel was not yet king. The story of a continent saved is a later legend. Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 and the surrender of Granada on 2 January 1492 are later events in the peninsula. They change rulers and, in 1492, they sit beside other events of the same year. They do not reach backwards and turn 711 into a mistake.

The meaning of 1492 is disputed, and it is disputed for a precise reason. The year holds several events, and collapsing them is how a birthday gets manufactured. On 2 January Granada surrendered. On 31 March the Alhambra Decree ordered the Jews of Castile and Aragon to convert or leave, with a deadline of 31 July. On 3 August Columbus sailed from Palos. On 12 October he made landfall in the Caribbean. A religious expulsion, the end of a Muslim polity in Iberia, and an Atlantic crossing are not one event called the birth of modern Europe. Jewish communities were already inside the story the decree tried to end: in al-Andalus, in the Latin kingdoms, and, under other rules, in the Ottoman world. The decree is evidence of a policy of exclusion. It is not evidence that a continent had at last become itself. This atlas states no number of people who left. A false precision would only decorate the policy with a costume of statistics.

Roma are inside the geography and have repeatedly been written as outside the story: as wanderers, as a policing problem, as a people whose history can be postponed until a footnote. This atlas does not lock a first year of arrival, and it will not invent one. The absence of a locked year is a limit of the chronology we are using here. It is not a claim that the people were absent, and it is not a permission to leave them out of the question this chapter asks. A single outline that has room for a treaty and no room for Roma has already announced that it is a canon, not a map of inhabitants. The same is true of Jewish communities in centuries when no decree happens to be dated. Presence does not begin at the moment a hostile law mentions you.

The Ottoman empire makes the point at the scale of a state. After 1453 the sultan ruled from a city that Latin maps had called imperial. Ottoman power in the Balkans and the eastern Mediterranean is European political history on any definition of Europe that follows the land rather than the rite. On 12 September 1683 the siege of Vienna was lifted at Kahlenberg. That relief is a military event with a day. It is not "Europe versus Asia" as a permanent fact. The chronology of this atlas says so because the slogan is a standing temptation: to take one battle in which a Habsburg city held and to rename it as the biography of a continent. The Ottoman empire was not Asia camped temporarily on the wrong side of a wall. It was a long political order ruling many of the lands the geographical name already covered. Readers who want the religious map and the geographical name to be the same object have to push that order out of Europe by definition. [[#def-map]] is the name of that push.

## A balance, not an outline

The third use of the name is a balance-of-power idea. It belongs to a world of several armed states, each worried that one of the others will become irresistible. The idea picks out players. It does not pick out inhabitants. A village, a minority, or a kingdom that the players agree to divide does not become "less European" by being available for the bargain. It becomes less protected.

In 1713 the Treaty of Utrecht settled a war fought over a Spanish succession and, with it, over the shape of several empires. The treaty is a bargain among powers. It is not a geographical discovery, and it is not the moment the continent acquired a constitution. On 9 June 1815 the Final Act of the Congress of Vienna closed a settlement that had been sitting since 1814. Vienna rearranged states after Napoleon's defeat. It did not find the natural borders of a civilisation. Both dates are useful precisely because they are late, specific and documentary. They show the balance as a practice with treaties, not as an instinct flowing from the classical name.

The partitions show what the practice costs. In 1772, 1793 and 1795 Russia, Prussia and Austria divided Poland-Lithuania until the commonwealth was erased as a state. It had been a large European polity, not a gap on the map. A history written only from the point of view of the partitioning capitals can call the result a restoration of balance. A history of the commonwealth has to call it a destruction. Both descriptions can cite the same years. They are not the same claim. The balance-of-power idea, if it is allowed to pretend it is simply "Europe defending its shape", hides the second claim inside the first. Who benefits from that hiding is not mysterious. The powers that gained territory benefit. The story also benefits anyone who wants European history to be a story of great powers only, with the erased polity as a blank that was always going to be filled.

This is not yet a verdict on the motives of each minister. Motives are where single-cause stories go to hide, and [[sources/argument]] will refuse them. The point here is narrower. A diplomatic idea and a landmass are different objects. You can describe Utrecht, Vienna and the partitions without ever deciding where "Europe" spiritually begins. The moment the diplomatic idea is promoted into the definition of the continent, the erased state is pushed out of the definition in the same breath as it is pushed off the political map. That is [[#def-map]] doing violent work with a polite vocabulary.

Nor does the balance inherit the religious map cleanly. The powers that partitioned Poland-Lithuania were not one church. The peace of 24 October 1648, which adjusted the Peace of Augsburg and admitted Calvinists in the empire, is sometimes recruited as the birth of a state system that then "becomes" Europe. That reading is later. Westphalia does not invent a finished theory of modern sovereignty. Using 1648 as the moment the balance became a continent is another birthday, smuggled in between the coronation and the treaty of Rome. We leave the mechanics of that peace to [[reformations/religious-war]] and the logic of labels to [[sources/argument]]. Here, 1648 is a warning against adding a fifth birthday while pretending not to.

## A membership list

The fourth use is late, and it is the one a present-day reader is most likely to treat as obvious. On 18 April 1951 the Treaty of Paris established the European Coal and Steel Community. It entered into force in 1952. On 25 March 1957 the Treaties of Rome established the European Economic Community and Euratom. These are treaties among states. A treaty has signatories. The signatories of 1957 are a small set of western European governments, not a roll of the peoples who live between the Atlantic and the Urals, and not a roll of the peoples who lived there in 800. The European Economic Community is a membership list. It is not a definition of the continent.

The list is allowed to change without the coastline moving, which is the practical test of [[#thm-four]]. On 1 January 1973 the United Kingdom, Ireland and Denmark joined the Community. They did not sail into Europe on that morning. They acceded to a treaty organisation they had been outside. States that did not accede remained where the geography had placed them. On 1 January 1995 Austria, Finland and Sweden joined the European Union, the later name of the same institutional family. On 1 May 2004 Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia joined. In 2007 Bulgaria and Romania joined. On 1 July 2013 Croatia joined. On 23 June 2016 the United Kingdom voted to leave. It left on 31 January 2020, and the transition ended on 31 December 2020. Each of these is an institutional fact about a list. None of them is a geographical discovery, and none of them reaches backwards to anoint 800 or to disqualify 711.

Several European states were outside the 1957 list for reasons that had nothing to do with being outside the place: neutrality, dictatorship, occupation, a different alliance, a domestic argument about whether to sign. The east of the continent in 1957 included states aligned with Moscow, and it included people those states ruled. Writing them out of "Europe" until a later accession is the walk backwards, run in reverse: the present list is treated as the goal, and earlier absence from the list is treated as absence from the history. The full institutional narrative belongs to [[postwar/welfare-and-treaties]] and [[postwar/union]]. This chapter only needs the category. A customs union is not a civilisation. A civilisation with one set of values is not a claim this atlas will make, because the record just surveyed will not bear it.

Move the window across the four dots before you trust a sentence that puts them in one plot. From 711 to 800 is eighty-nine years, counted as the later year minus the earlier, the same convention by which this atlas counts from 800 to 843 as forty-three years. From 800 to 1453 is six hundred and fifty-three years. From 1453 to 1957 is five hundred and four. The gaps are the point. A conquest in Iberia, a coronation in Rome, the taking of Constantinople, and a treaty signed in that city under a different sovereignty are not one process photographed at four moments. The figure will let you put them in one frame. The frame is a tool. It is not an argument.

## A worked case: one city, three maps

Take Constantinople, and refuse to let one sentence swallow it.

In 800 the city was the seat of the Roman empire in the east. The coronation in Rome did not move that seat. A map of Latin Christendom that treats 800 as the founding of Europe has to decide what the city on the Bosporus was, if not European and not quite foreign. The usual decision is to rename the state Byzantium and set it beside the story. The rename is a choice of map, not a report on what the court called itself.

In 1204 Latin Christian crusaders sacked the city. The religious map and the city were then not merely different. They were in combat. Any sentence of the form "medieval Europe defended Constantinople" is false of that April. The defenders and the attackers can both be called Christian, and the attackers can be called European on the geographical use of the name. The sentence survives only if "Europe" has been quietly redefined as "the Latin side".

In 1453 Mehmed II took the city, and the eastern Roman state ended. Ottoman Constantinople, later Istanbul in common speech, remained a capital inside the geography. Jewish communities lived under Ottoman rule, including people who had left Iberia after the decree of 1492. The religious map of Latin Christendom does not describe that city. The geographical name does. The balance-of-power idea meets the same city again whenever Habsburg, Venetian, Russian or other forces fight the sultan for position. The treaty organisation of 1957 does not have the city as a signatory; Turkey is not on the 1957 list. A reader who concludes that the city leaves Europe in 1453, or that it enters Europe only if a later treaty says so, has confused [[#def-name]] with a border post.

What would count against this worked case is evidence that the city moved, or that one of these dates really did define the continent rather than a sovereignty. The city did not move. The dates define sovereignties, a sack, and a non-membership. Three maps, one place. The place is the check on the maps.

:::example A span the atlas already uses {#ex-verdun}
The Treaty of Verdun is dated 843. This atlas counts the elapsed span from 800 to 843 as 43 years. Compute it from the two years, and say what Verdun did not found.
:::solution
843 minus 800 is 43. The subtraction is the rule for two years CE: later minus earlier, not an inclusive count of both ends, and not a story about progress. Verdun divided a Frankish inheritance. It did not found France and Germany. The number 43 is a distance between a coronation and a family partition. A reader who treats 43 as "the length of time Europe took to become two nations" has added a plot the treaty does not contain. The full political reading belongs to [[after-rome/carolingians]]. The arithmetic here is only the distance, plus a refusal.
:::
:::

:::example From the fall to the treaties {#ex-1453-1957}
How many years elapsed from 1453 to 1957, and why is the number not a measure of recovery?
:::solution
1957 minus 1453 is 504. On 29 May 1453 Mehmed II took Constantinople and the eastern Roman state ended. On 25 March 1957 the Treaties of Rome established the European Economic Community and Euratom. The 504 years are the gap between a change of sovereignty and a membership list. They are not a convalescence, a return of "Europe" to itself, or evidence that the city taken in 1453 had left the continent until a treaty invited it back. Ottoman rule in the Balkans continued inside the geography. The gap is an argument against a single ascent, which is what [[#thm-four]] claims. If the two events were stages of one birth, the kind of act would match and the counter-evidence would be empty. The kinds do not match.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented-birth}
The following sentence is invented for the example. It is not a quotation and not a source. "After the crossing of 711 the land was lost to Europe, and only a later Christian victory gave it back." What would it take to promote this sentence into evidence, and which locked facts already count against the plot even before that promotion?
:::solution
Promotion would require a surviving trace that says this, or from which this is a fair paraphrase; a maker; a date; and a purpose. Even then the trace would show that someone made this claim. It would not, by itself, show that the claim was true. The inference would still have to be separated from the sentence, and you would have to say what would count against the inference.

The plot is already in trouble against the chronology, with no need of a fake chronicle. The crossing of 711 is the start of a political history inside Iberia, not the loss of a finished continent. The emirate of 756 and the caliphate of 929 are governments in the peninsula, not an absence. Tours, whose traditional date is October 732 and whose alternative dates 733 and 734 are argued from the Chronicle of 754, did not save Europe. Granada's surrender on 2 January 1492 is one event in a year that also holds the Alhambra Decree, a sailing, and a landfall. Historians dispute what 1492 means, and this atlas refuses to collapse it into a restoration of the continent. The invented sentence is the religious map pretending to be a property register.
:::
:::

:::example Two treaties, not one birth {#ex-two-treaties}
Set 18 April 1951 beside 25 March 1957. What does each date establish, and what question is it a mistake to make them answer?
:::solution
On 18 April 1951 the Treaty of Paris established the European Coal and Steel Community, which entered into force in 1952. On 25 March 1957 the Treaties of Rome established the European Economic Community and Euratom. Both are membership arrangements among states. Neither answers the question "what is Europe?" or the question "when was the continent born?" A reader who asks those questions of a signature is using [[#def-map]] backwards: the legal edge is real as a list, and it is being asked to double as the ground. Later changes to the list, such as 1 January 1973 and 31 January 2020, are changes to membership. They would be unintelligible if 1951 or 1957 had defined the place rather than a set of signatories.
:::
:::

:::warning The outline walked backwards
The usual myth says that Europe is a civilisation with one set of values, and that a present union is that civilisation at last taking legal form. On this myth, 800, 1648 and 1957 are steps, 711 and 1453 are threats from outside, and Jewish communities and Roma are decoration. The myth fails because the four uses in [[#thm-four]] are not one object, because the date of Tours is disputed and the battle did not save a continent, because 1453 ended a Roman state without ending the history of the Balkans, and because a membership list can gain and lose states without the land gaining or losing them. A single set of values is not a source. It is a slogan wearing the name.
:::

:::history
On 25 December 800 Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne emperor in Rome. On 29 May 1453 Mehmed II took Constantinople, and the eastern Roman state ended. On 25 March 1957 the Treaties of Rome established the European Economic Community and Euratom. The three acts sit in one geographical neighbourhood only if the neighbourhood is drawn widely enough to include Rome, Constantinople and the states that signed. They do not sit in one project.
:::

## What a single outline costs

Put the excluded back in the sentence and watch the outline lose its cleanliness. That loss is the result, not a problem to be repaired.

A classical geographical sentence can include the Aegean, Italy, the Balkans and Iberia without deciding the true faith. The moment faith becomes the edge, Greek-speaking Christians and Muslims in al-Andalus have to be qualified, explained, or removed. A balance-of-power sentence can include the sultan as a power and the commonwealth of Poland-Lithuania as a power. The moment "Europe" means the Latin west plus its favoured heirs, the sultan becomes an encroachment and the commonwealth becomes a buffer that may be sacrificed. A membership-list sentence can be exact: these governments signed, those did not. It becomes false when exactness about signatures is promoted into a judgement about who has a history on the continent.

The people a Latin map leaves at the edge are not a miscellaneous remainder. They are the test. Byzantium is the Roman empire the Latin coronation did not replace. Al-Andalus is the Iberian order the "saved Europe" legend cannot see except as a danger. The Ottoman empire is the sovereignty in Constantinople after 1453 and the army before Vienna in 1683, and it is also a government under which Jews and others lived. Jewish communities are inside Latin cities, inside al-Andalus, and inside the Ottoman world, and they are the object of Canon 68 in 1215 and of the Alhambra Decree in 1492. Roma are inside the lands the name claims, and they are missing from the birthday stories because birthday stories are about crowns and treaties. An outline that is tidy has usually just made a cut. [[#def-map]] asks you to say where the cut was, who benefited, and which source would show the people on the far side of it.

None of this is a plea to empty the name of content so that nothing can be said. The classical name has a history of use. The religious map has decrees, coronations and sacks. The balance has treaties and partitions. The membership list has dates of signing, accession and departure. Each can be taught. What cannot be taught, because it is not there, is the single object of which these are supposed to be stages. The next chapter asks a prior question that this one has been using without stopping on it: what a source can support, and what a silence can support, when someone claims that a people was not really there because the birthday story forgot to mention them. That question is [[sources/evidence]].

## Where this leads

The name is now a set of uses rather than a birth. [[sources/evidence]] asks what a charter, a survey or a silence can actually witness, which is the next discipline this kind of argument needs. The heirs who were written out of the single outline are taught in their own right in [[after-rome/byzantium]], [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]] and [[after-rome/carolingians]]. Exclusion as a legal and religious practice returns in [[christendom/exclusion]]. The treaties that are not a definition of the continent return in [[postwar/welfare-and-treaties]] and [[postwar/union]].

:::summary
- Europe is a name with several uses, not a natural object with one edge and one set of values.
- The classical geographical name, the map of Latin Christendom, the balance-of-power idea and the treaty organisation of 25 March 1957 are four different objects.
- 711, 800, 1453 and 1957 are markers of different maps. The gaps between them are not a slow approach to a finished continent.
- Byzantium, al-Andalus, the Ottoman empire, Jewish communities and Roma sit inside the geography and have often been written outside the story.
- Tours did not save Europe, the date is disputed, and 1683 is not Europe versus Asia as a permanent fact.
- The partitions of 1772, 1793 and 1795 erased a large polity in the name of a balance. A balance is not an outline of the land.
- The European Economic Community is a membership list. Accession and departure change the list. They do not move the coast.
- A tidy single outline usually means a cut has already been made. The cut is the thing to explain.
:::

## Exercises

:::exercise From a crossing to a coronation {level=1 check="89"}
Using the rule that the elapsed span from year A CE to year B CE is B minus A, how many years elapsed from 711 to 800? (Enter the number of years.)
:::solution
The later year minus the earlier year is 800 minus 711. That is 89. The same convention makes the span from 800 to 843 equal 43, which is the atlas's check on the arithmetic. The answer is not an inclusive count of both end years, and it is not a claim that the two dates are one process.
:::
:::

:::exercise From a coronation to a fall {level=1 check="653"}
How many years elapsed from 800 to 1453, on the same rule? (Enter the number of years.)
:::solution
1453 minus 800 is 653. The coronation on 25 December 800 and the fall of Constantinople on 29 May 1453 are separated by that span. The number measures distance in the year-count. It does not measure progress towards a later treaty.
:::
:::

:::exercise What the treaty is {level=1 check="1957"}
In what year were the Treaties of Rome signed, establishing the European Economic Community and Euratom? (Enter the year.)
:::solution
The treaties were signed on 25 March 1957. The year is 1957. What they established is a pair of treaty organisations with members, not a definition of the continent. The Coal and Steel Community is earlier: the Treaty of Paris is 18 April 1951, in force in 1952. Do not collapse the two treaties into one birthday.
:::
:::

:::exercise Two dates, one city {level=2}
13 April 1204 and 29 May 1453 both concern Constantinople. Explain, in a few sentences, why they are different kinds of event, and why neither is a step towards 25 March 1957.
:::solution
On 13 April 1204 the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople, a Christian city. The attackers were Latin Christians. The map of Latin Christendom was, on that day, an instrument against the eastern Roman capital, not a description of a single Christian side. On 29 May 1453 Mehmed II took the city and the eastern Roman state ended. Ottoman rule followed. That is a change of sovereignty, and the chronology refuses the further claim that history in the Balkans ended with it. The Treaties of Rome on 25 March 1957 are a membership list among much later states. A sack, a conquest and a signature do not illustrate one another. Putting them in one ascent is the myth of the outline walked backwards: one civilisation, discovered early and completed by a treaty. The three events stay three events.
:::
:::

:::exercise A balance that deletes {level=2}
Poland-Lithuania was partitioned in 1772, 1793 and 1795, and erased as a state. Why can this not be told as the continent merely finding its natural outline?
:::solution
The commonwealth was a large European polity, not a gap on the map. The partitions were acts by Russia, Prussia and Austria. A balance-of-power story can describe the result as equilibrium among the partitioning powers. That description is a claim about the interests of those powers. It is not a geographical fact, and it is not evidence that the erased state had been outside Europe. [[#def-map]] treats the "natural outline" sentence as a map pretending to be the ground. Who benefits is visible: the states that took the territory, and any later narrative that wants European history to contain only the survivors. Utrecht in 1713 and Vienna on 9 June 1815 are other settlements among powers. They likewise do not discover a civilisation.
:::
:::

:::exercise An invented birthday {level=2}
The following sentence is invented for this exercise, and it is not a source: "In 800 Europe was born as one civilisation, and in 1453 it was saved from the east." Say which locked facts break it, including the dispute over Tours if you use that battle.
:::solution
The sentence is a label, not a report. The coronation of 25 December 800 gave Charlemagne an imperial title in the Latin west. It did not found a civilisation with one set of values, and it did not end the Roman state at Constantinople. The fall of the city on 29 May 1453 ended that eastern Roman state. It did not save Latin Christendom from "the east", and Ottoman rule is not the end of history in the Balkans. If the invented sentence is leaning on the older "saved Europe" legend, the battle of Tours has a disputed date, October 732 or else 733 or 734, and it did not save Europe either. Charles Martel was not yet king. Raids continued. The relief of Vienna on 12 September 1683 is a further lure towards the same legend, and it is not Europe versus Asia as a permanent fact. Promoting the invented sentence into evidence would require a source that said it, a maker, a purpose, and a separation between what the source shows and what a later reader infers. Even then it would witness a claim, not a birth. [[sources/evidence]] is where that separation is the whole subject.
:::
:::

:::exercise Outside the list, inside the place {level=3}
A reader says: "Britain was outside Europe until 1973, because it was outside the European Economic Community." Using [[#def-map]] and [[#thm-four]], explain the confusion. Say what 1 January 1973 changes and what it does not.
:::hint
Ask which of the four uses the reader has silently promoted into the definition. Then ask where Britain was, on the geographical use of the name, in 800, in 1453 and in 1957.
:::
:::solution
The reader has taken the fourth use, the treaty organisation, and treated it as the ground. That is the error [[#def-map]] names: a claim about an edge, here a membership edge, is being used as if it were the place itself. [[#thm-four]] says the membership list of 25 March 1957 is not the classical name, not Latin Christendom, and not the balance of power. Britain was outside the 1957 list. It was not outside the geography, and it was not outside European political history in the centuries when the list did not exist. On 1 January 1973 the United Kingdom, Ireland and Denmark joined the Community. What changed is the list. What did not change is the coastline, the earlier history, or the status of states that still did not join. The same test applies to later accessions and to the departure of 31 January 2020. A state can leave a treaty organisation. It does not thereby fall out of the continent, any more than it entered the continent by signing.
:::
:::

:::exercise Four dates, no staircase {level=3}
Write one sentence that contains 711, 800, 1453 and 1957 and does not make them a single ascent. Then say what evidence would count against your sentence.
:::hint
Give each year a different kind of object: a crossing, a coronation, a change of sovereignty, a signature. A counter-example would be one institution that actually defined the edge across all four.
:::
:::solution
One acceptable sentence is: in 711 a force crossed into Iberia and began a political history the religious map did not own; on 25 December 800 a pope crowned a Frankish king emperor in Rome without ending the Roman state in the east; on 29 May 1453 that eastern state fell to Mehmed II and Ottoman rule continued inside the geography; on 25 March 1957 a small set of governments signed the Treaties of Rome, which listed members and did not define the continent. The sentence uses [[#thm-four]]. It names different kinds of act, and it does not say that each prepared the next.

What would count against it is a source showing that one continuous institution defined a single European edge across these dates, so that the crossing, the coronation, the fall and the signature were really one subject's successive acts. A shared noun in later textbooks does not do that work. Nor does the timeline widget, which can show all four dots in one window and still, by its own caption, refuse them the status of a birth. If a reader can only connect the dates by adding the phrase "and so Europe emerged", the added phrase is the myth, not the evidence.
:::
:::
