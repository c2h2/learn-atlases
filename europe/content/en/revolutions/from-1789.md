What had the Revolution done by the end of the summer of 1789, and what had it not done?

By the beginning of October 1789 a National Assembly was legislating, the Bastille had fallen, decrees had struck at feudal forms, a Declaration had been dated, and the king was being brought from Versailles to Paris. That is a great deal. It is not a republic. The king had not been executed. The Terror had not begun. Colonial slavery had not been abolished. Women did not vote. Calling 14 July the start of modern democracy flattens a summer of different acts and borrows a regime France did not yet have.

The summer is not the answer for 1791 or for 1792. A constitution a king accepts, a flight, a war declared while he is still king, a suspension, and then a republic: these are later regimes. [[revolutions/old-regime]] stopped at 17 June. This chapter starts from that claim and refuses to let July swallow what follows.

## One summer, several acts

:::definition Revolutionary act {#def-act}
A **revolutionary act**, in this chapter, is a dated public act that changes a specified legal or military relation: who sits, who holds a fortress, which dues are attacked, which rights are declared, where the king sleeps, whether a king remains. It is not a mood called the Revolution, and it is not a later regime read backwards into an earlier day. Several acts in one summer do not become one act because they share a year.
:::

The sequence is short enough to memorise and sharp enough to argue with. On 5 May 1789 the Estates-General opened. On 17 June the National Assembly was declared. On 20 June, three days later, deputies found their usual hall closed, met in a tennis court, and bound themselves not to disperse until a constitution existed. We do not quote the oath. The political content is the claim: the assembly's life did not depend on the king's permission for that day's room. A lockout was a tactic. The oath was a reply. It still left Louis XVI on the throne.

On 14 July a crowd in Paris, with men of the city's new militia, took the Bastille, a fortress, a prison, a store of arms and a symbol of royal force. The governor was killed. We do not describe how, and we give no count of prisoners, because none is locked. What changed was command in Paris. A civic militia, soon the National Guard, held arms the court could not simply grant and withdraw. Ministers fell. The king came to the city and recognised what he could not undo. None of that is a national election or a text of rights. A capital can decide a ministry and still not be a democracy of adults.

The countryside was already moving. Through the summer, rumours of brigands and refusals of dues spread across many regions, a wave later called the Great Fear. Lords' houses were attacked. Not every village rose, and we will not invent a count of houses burned. The assembly in Versailles was answering peasants as well as Parisians. That is the setting for the first week of August, and it is a warning about scale. Paris on 14 July is not France. France in revolt against dues is not one peasant.

:::theorem By October the monarchy had been constrained, not replaced {#thm-summer}
By the end of the summer of 1789, taking the march of 5–6 October as the summer's last act, France had a National Assembly set on a constitution, a Paris that had taken a royal fortress, decrees attacking feudal legal forms, and a Declaration of rights dated 26 August. The king was in Paris and still king. The republic had not been declared. The king had not been executed. The Terror had not begun. Colonial slavery had not been abolished. The limit is the list itself: each item is an act in [[#def-act]], and none of the later absences is a small print underneath a glory.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the order of dates. 17 June creates the National Assembly. 20 June binds it to a constitution. 14 July changes force in Paris. The August decrees, 4 to 11 August, attack feudal dues and personal servitude: some rights abolished, many pecuniary rights declared redeemable, so a night of speeches is not a completed transfer of property. 26 August dates the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. We do not quote it. A declaration can set a standard and still not fix the electorate, the colonies or the status of women. On 5–6 October the king is brought to Paris, women of the city prominent in the march. Guards were killed. We do not describe that or count the dead. The result is location. King and assembly are in the city that took the Bastille. Kingship has not ended.

What would count against the claim? A decree, on or before 6 October 1789, proclaiming a republic, executing the king, abolishing colonial slavery, or giving votes to women and to the unpropertied. There is no such decree. The king remains, and in 1791 he will be offered a constitution. A metaphor that hides the republic inside August 1789 fails that later signature.
:::

From 4 August to 11 August is a span, not a flash. 11 August is 7 days after 4 August. Deputies renounced privileges in a fever of speeches, and later decrees had to say what the fever meant. Personal servitude was attacked outright. Many payments on land were, in the assembly's categories, to be bought out rather than cancelled. Peasants who heard that feudalism ended in a night were not fools, and lawyers who insisted on redemption were not inventing the text. Both the night and the small print are the act. A later village fight over a due shows the gap between a decree and a field. It does not make the decree nothing.

The Declaration is not the constitution, completed only on 3 September 1791, and it is not a census of who held the rights in the title. Men without the later tax qualification, women, and enslaved people were not carried into political rights by the date alone. The text still gave critics a measure. In 1791 Olympe de Gouges published on the rights of woman. We do not quote her. The summer's title was already being answered as an exclusion. She was put to death in 1793, in the period the next chapter dates. That death does not make her text the law of 1789, and it is not a reason to skip the exclusion.

:::example From the fortress to the Declaration {#ex-july-august}
The Bastille is taken on 14 July 1789. The Declaration is dated 26 August 1789. How many days after 14 July is 26 August? July has 31 days. Count the later date, not both endpoints. Then say which of the two acts is a text of rights, and which is a change of force in Paris.
:::solution
From 14 July to 14 August is 31 days, the length of July. From 14 August to 26 August is 12 days. 31 plus 12 is 43, so 26 August is 43 days after 14 July. The first date is a change of force. The second is a declaration of rights, which we do not quote and which is not yet a constitution. The 43 days also hold the decrees of 4–11 August, neither the storming nor the Declaration.
:::
:::

The execution is 21 January 1793. Colonial abolition as a French decree is 4 February 1794. Nothing in July or August 1789 enfranchises women or ends slavery. ## A constitution, and a flight

Between October and the flight this atlas does not lock a year-number for a day-by-day politics of the next season, and we do not invent one. What can be said is institutional. Local government was rebuilt. Festivals advertised unity. The church was brought under the state by a law that required an oath, and many clergy refused, splitting parishes. A revolution that reorganises a church has opened a second front beside tax and feudal dues, before the king tries to leave.

In June 1791 the king and his family left Paris in disguise and were stopped at Varennes. A king who flees towards the frontier does not accept the direction he is still formally heading. He was brought back and not deposed. That summer troops fired on petitioners in Paris who wanted a sharper break. We do not count the dead or describe the volley. The constitutional monarchy was already using force against republicans before it had a republic. The constitution was completed on 3 September 1791 and accepted on 14 September, 11 days later. The flight did not prevent the signature. Damage to trust and a signed constitution are both true. Readers who want Varennes to end the monarchy are a year early.

:::example Flight, then a signature {#ex-varennes}
The flight to Varennes is in June 1791. The constitution is completed on 3 September 1791 and accepted by the king on 14 September 1791. What may you infer from that order, and what may you not infer? There is no locked day inside June, so do not invent one.
:::solution
You may infer that the constitutional monarchy survived its king's attempt to leave. Completion on 3 September and acceptance on 14 September are acts of a regime that still has a king. The eleven days, 14 minus 3, are a formal interval, not a new revolution. Trust was damaged, because a flight is public reluctance. You may not infer that the republic or the Terror had begun, or that acceptance was enthusiastic, or date the flight to a day of June, or move the king's death of 21 January 1793 back to Varennes. A failed flight, a firing with no number attached, and a signed constitution can share a year without being one event.
:::
:::

The constitution of 1791 kept a king and a single legislature. It distinguished voters from the wider name of citizen in the Declaration. The criterion was a tax payment, and the sum is not locked, so we do not state it. A declaration and a franchise are different instruments, and the franchise was narrower. Women were not voters. Enslaved people were not freed. Calling this settlement democracy, without those limits, extends the Bastille myth into 1791. The new legislature sat that autumn. The war of the following spring is declared by this monarchy, not by the republic.

## The colony in the same year

:::proposition Saint-Domingue belongs to 1791 {#prop-colony}
The revolt of enslaved people in Saint-Domingue, beginning in August 1791, is part of the history of 1791 and not a colonial appendix. The constitution completed on 3 September 1791 does not abolish slavery. A metropolitan settlement and a colonial war occupy one year and do not illustrate each other for free.
:::
:::proof
The chronology places the revolt in August 1791, the year of Varennes and of the constitution. Saint-Domingue, the main French plantation colony, was organised by the Code Noir of 1685 and by the trade in [[states/atlantic]]. The revolt made slavery a war. The constitution frees nobody. That is the metropolitan fact beside the war. What would count against the proposition? A clause of 3 September 1791, or of 26 August 1789, that abolished slavery and was in force in the colony that August. There is none. The Convention's abolition is 4 February 1794, in [[revolutions/republic-and-terror]]. Restoration in 1802 and Haiti's declaration on 1 January 1804 belong to [[revolutions/napoleon]]. Emancipation with no restoration and no new state is not this sequence.

What would also count against us is the claim that the Declaration caused the revolt. Enslaved people did not need a Paris text in order to have purposes, and the Declaration did not free them. Rumour and news may have travelled. Authorship by a Paris decree is not established, and we will not invent a quotation from a text this atlas does not quote.
:::

We do not describe the violence of the revolt as a procedure, and we do not give a death toll. The political category is enough. A plantation society was at war with the people it enslaved. Free people of colour had their own claims on citizenship, already in dispute before August, and those claims are not the same as the revolt of the enslaved, even when the wars overlap. Two exclusions can be true in one colony. The summer of 1789 in Paris had done nothing that settled either of them.

## War, and then a republic

On 20 April 1792 France declared war on the King of Bohemia and Hungary, the title in the act, the Habsburg monarch the chronology also calls Austria. The formula matters. It is a declaration against a king, issued while France still had a king. Some deputies wanted a war to expose enemies and rally the country. Some at court hoped a defeat would restore royal authority. We do not need one motive to be the whole cause. The declaration is a choice under the constitution of 1791, not a reflex of 14 July 1789. Foreign threats and noble emigration are context. We do not lock a separate day for them.

:::theorem 1789, 1791 and 1792 are different regimes {#thm-regimes}
The summer of 1789 leaves a revolutionising monarchy. The constitution accepted on 14 September 1791 is a monarchy with a written settlement and a restricted franchise. From 21–22 September 1792 France is a republic at war. These are not three names for one regime. War is declared on 20 April 1792, before the republic exists. The limit: "different" does not mean unrelated. Each regime inherits the previous crisis. Inheritance is not identity.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the order. Through 6 October 1789 Louis XVI is constrained and not replaced, which is [[#thm-summer]]. 1791 records a flight in June, a constitution on 3 September and an acceptance on 14 September. 1792 records war on 20 April, suspension on 10 August, killings of prisoners in September, Valmy on 20 September, and the republic on 21–22 September. The republic comes after the war has begun. The king is suspended on 10 August and not killed that day. His death is 21 January 1793, the next chapter, and it is not the birth of the republic. The republic is a vote of the Convention after elections under the emergency of August.

What would count against the theorem? A text of summer 1789 that already abolishes the monarchy, a declaration of war dated after 22 September 1792, or a republic proclaimed at Varennes. None of those is the chronology. Suspension on 10 August and proclamation on 21 September are weeks apart, and the weeks contain the September massacres and Valmy. Collapsing them is how the chapter would fail.
:::

From 20 April to 10 August is 112 days, long enough to stop the story in which war and the fall of the king are one afternoon. The steps are in [[#ex-one-twelve]]: 30, then 31, then 30, then 21. On 10 August a Paris insurrection suspends the monarchy. The king becomes a prisoner of the new situation, and a Convention is called. This is no longer the constitution he accepted in September 1791. It is not yet a sentence of death.

In September, prisoners in Paris were killed in the September massacres. Historians disagree about the chain of command: who urged the killings, who tolerated them, and how far the city's authorities were the authors. The disagreement is part of the event. We take the fact of the killings and refuse a number of the dead, because none is locked, and we refuse a description of the method. The massacres are not the Terror, which conventionally runs from September 1793 to 27 July 1794. A year separates the two Septembers. Anyone who uses one for the other has lost the brackets the next chapter exists to teach.

On 20 September 1792 the battle of Valmy checked the immediate advance of the forces invading France. We give no size of either army. On 21–22 September the Convention abolished the monarchy and proclaimed the republic. Valmy is one day before the 21st. Adjacency is not causation. A battle is a military result. A republic is a constitutional vote. They can share a week, and the week can matter, without the cannon having voted.

:::example An invented line about Valmy {#ex-valmy}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a dispatch and not a decree. "Valmy created the republic." Using 20 September and 21–22 September 1792, say what is wrong with the verb, and what a careful sentence would say instead.
:::solution
Valmy is 20 September. The republic is 21–22 September. The battle is one day earlier, not the author of a decree. "Created" turns a military check into a constitution. A careful sentence says the battle checked an invasion and that the Convention then abolished the monarchy. Willingness among deputies is not creation. The careful sentence would fail if the republic had been proclaimed before the battle, or by commanders as an order. The invented sentence cannot tell a battle from a regime, which is what [[#thm-regimes]] stops.
:::
:::

:::example War and suspension are not one date {#ex-one-twelve}
France declares war on the King of Bohemia and Hungary on 20 April 1792. The monarchy is suspended on 10 August 1792. How many days after 20 April is 10 August? Use the month lengths April 30, May 31, June 30, and stop at 10 August rather than at 20 August. Then say what kind of regime declares the war.
:::solution
From 20 April to 20 May is 30 days. From 20 May to 20 June is 31 days. From 20 June to 20 July is 30 days. From 20 July to 10 August is the 11 days left in July after the 20th (31 minus 20) plus 10 days of August, which is 21. The total is 30 plus 31 plus 30 plus 21, which is 112. So 10 August is 112 days after 20 April. The war is declared by the constitutional monarchy of 1791, with Louis XVI still king. The republic does not yet exist. Starting the republic in April, or treating August as the same morning, drops 112 days and a change of regime.
:::
:::

The counter-argument says the summer of 1789 already contained everything later, because crowds killed and marched, so the Terror was present in germ. The summer was not gentle. Guards died in October. A governor was killed in July. The Great Fear frightened people who lived by dues. Germ is a metaphor. The Terror, as the next chapter defines it, is a government at war using exceptional tribunals from September 1793 to 27 July 1794. A crowd in 1789 is not that government. The distinction is what lets you ask what changed.

A second counter-argument says the colony is a distraction from the real revolution, which is Paris. [[#prop-colony]] is the answer. August 1791 sits in the same year as the constitution. Dating Varennes and missing Saint-Domingue decides in advance that a European capital is the only scale. The revolt does not explain the Tennis Court Oath, and the oath does not explain the revolt. Sharing an era is not a licence to illustrate one by the other without dates.

:::warning The Bastille as a birthday
People repeat that the Bastille begins modern democracy. 14 July 1789 changes force in Paris. Adult democracy is not what France had that evening, in October, or under the constitution accepted on 14 September 1791. Women did not vote. The franchise was qualified by tax. Enslaved people in Saint-Domingue were not freed, and in August 1791 they were at war. The republic is 21–22 September 1792. A birthday story keeps one fortress and throws away the dates that would check it.
:::

:::history
The Estates-General opened on 5 May 1789, with Louis XVI still the king who had called them. The Bastille was taken on 14 July. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen is dated 26 August. The republic is not one of these acts. It is a later regime, in a later year.
:::

## Where this leads

[[revolutions/republic-and-terror]] begins when this chapter stops: the king is executed on 21 January 1793, the levy of August 1793 follows, and the Terror has brackets that do not cover 1789. [[revolutions/napoleon]] takes the restoration of colonial slavery and Haiti's declaration, which are unintelligible if August 1791 is left out. The trade and the Code Noir are [[states/atlantic]]. The old regime's fiscal crisis, which this summer is no longer only a fiscal crisis, is [[revolutions/old-regime]].

:::summary
- A revolutionary act is a dated change in a specified relation, not a mood and not a whole regime squeezed into 14 July.
- By 6 October 1789 France had a National Assembly, the Bastille taken, the August decrees, the Declaration of 26 August, and a king brought to Paris. He was still king.
- The August decrees are a span from 4 to 11 August. Some dues were abolished and many were declared redeemable. A night of speeches is not a finished map of property.
- The Declaration is not quoted here, and it is not a constitution, not women's suffrage, and not the abolition of slavery. The constitution is completed on 3 September 1791 and accepted on 14 September.
- The flight to Varennes in June 1791 damaged the monarchy and did not prevent that acceptance. In August 1791 the revolt in Saint-Domingue began. It is part of the year.
- War is declared on 20 April 1792 against the King of Bohemia and Hungary, under a monarchy. The monarchy is suspended on 10 August. The republic dates from 21–22 September, one day after Valmy and not identical with Valmy.
- The September massacres of 1792 are not the Terror of September 1793. The king's execution has not happened by the end of this chapter.
:::

:::exercise What the summer is not {level=2}
A friend says: "Once the Bastille fell, France was a democratic republic and the colonies were free." Using only dates from this chapter, write four corrections. Each correction needs a date or a dated absence.
:::solution
First, 14 July 1789 takes a fortress. It does not proclaim a republic. That proclamation is 21–22 September 1792. Second, the king accepts a constitution on 14 September 1791, so the regime after the Bastille is still a monarchy. Third, that constitution restricts the franchise by a tax qualification, and women do not vote, so adult democracy is false even of 1791. Fourth, slavery is not abolished in 1789 or on 3 September 1791. The revolt in Saint-Domingue begins in August 1791, and the decree of abolition is 4 February 1794. The friend has kept one day and invented a regime.
:::

:::exercise Order inside 1792 {level=2}
Put these in chronological order and say which one is the start of the republic: the declaration of war on the King of Bohemia and Hungary, Valmy, the suspension of the monarchy, the September massacres, the proclamation of the republic. Give each a date.
:::solution
The war is declared on 20 April 1792. The monarchy is suspended on 10 August 1792. The September massacres follow, in September, before the battle. Valmy is 20 September 1792. The republic is proclaimed on 21–22 September 1792. Only the last of these is the start of the republic. The war is earlier, and it is declared by a monarchy. The massacres are killings of prisoners, not a constitutional founding. Valmy is a battle one day before the proclamation and is not the proclamation.

:::exercise Adjacency and cause {level=2}
Why is "Valmy created the republic" a worse sentence than "Valmy was fought the day before the republic was proclaimed"? Use [[#ex-valmy]] and [[#def-act]]. Do not invent a general's words.
:::solution
[[#def-act]] separates a military result from a change in the form of the state. Valmy, on 20 September 1792, checks an invasion. The republic, on 21–22 September, is an act of the Convention. The second sentence keeps the order and the kind. The first gives the battle a verb that belongs to a decree. One day is where stories smuggle causation. An argument about morale is allowed if it is offered as an argument. It may not replace the Convention with a cannon. No commander's words should be invented.
:::

:::exercise Would the Declaration settle the colony? {level=3}
:::hint
You may not quote the Declaration. You may use 26 August 1789, August 1791, 3 September 1791 and 4 February 1794. Ask what a text in Paris would have had to contain, and what the revolt shows about waiting for that text.
:::
A writer says: "The Declaration of 1789 made the revolt in Saint-Domingue unnecessary, because liberty had already been stated." Explain why that sentence fails, and what evidence would have made it succeed. Keep [[#prop-colony]] in view.
:::solution
The sentence fails because a declaration we do not treat as having abolished slavery cannot have made a revolt against slavery unnecessary. The revolt begins in August 1791. The constitution of 3 September 1791 still frees nobody. French legal abolition is 4 February 1794, and even that is reversed in 1802. The writer's "already" does the work of a quotation we will not invent and of a legal effect the later dates contradict.

The sentence would succeed only if a clause in force in Saint-Domingue from 26 August 1789 had ended slavery, and if August 1791 were not a revolt against enslavement. Rumour of rights can still be part of the colony's politics. Part of the politics is not a completed emancipation. [[#prop-colony]] keeps the constitution and the revolt in one year without letting either cancel the other.
:::

:::exercise Three regimes, one habit of reading {level=3}
:::hint
Take a window of zero on the widget seriously. A habit of reading can collapse three dots, or it can collapse several acts inside one dot. Your counter-example should do both jobs with dates.
:::
Someone says: "I only need the word Revolution. The differences among 1789, 1791 and 1792 are pedantry." Give one difference between each pair of years, with dates, and explain what political question becomes unaskable if the differences are dropped.
:::solution
Between 1789 and 1791: on 6 October 1789 there is no completed constitution; on 14 September 1791 the king accepts one, after the flight of June. Between 1791 and 1792: the accepted constitution is a monarchy, whereas on 21–22 September 1792 a republic is proclaimed, and the war declared on 20 April 1792 is already months old. Between 1789 and 1792 the gap is that whole arc. A constrained king is not a republic at war.

Drop the differences and you cannot ask whether the war preceded the republic, how a king could sign a constitution after trying to flee, or what the summer failed to settle in Saint-Domingue. Those questions are [[#thm-summer]] and [[#thm-regimes]]. The widget's window of zero will not let you stand on one year and pretend you can see the others.
