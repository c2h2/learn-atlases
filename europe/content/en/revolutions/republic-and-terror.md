What are the dates of the Terror, and what do they not explain?

The Terror is a name for a period, and the period has brackets. Conventionally it runs from September 1793 to 27 July 1794, 9 Thermidor, when Robespierre fell. Those brackets answer the first half of the question and almost none of the second. They do not explain 1789 or the September massacres of 1792. They do not include the death of Louis XVI on 21 January 1793. They do not make colonial abolition on 4 February 1794 into a killing, or the killing into an abolition. A name that tries to do all of that is doing political work. The dates are how you catch it.

[[revolutions/from-1789]] left France a republic at war, proclaimed on 21–22 September 1792, with a king suspended and not yet dead. This chapter is what that republic did next, and what it did not do. It did not restore the summer of 1789. It did not, on 9 Thermidor, restore that summer either. Thermidor ends a named period. It does not end the republic.

## Brackets, not a meaning

:::definition The Terror {#def-terror}
The **Terror** is the conventional name for the government of the French republic at war from September 1793 to 27 July 1794 (9 Thermidor), when Maximilien Robespierre fell. It is a period of exceptional tribunals and of concentrated emergency power. It is not a synonym for the Revolution, not the opposite of the Revolution, and not a method of killing. This chapter states no number of people put to death, because none is locked, and it describes no procedure.
:::

A nickname is a claim about where to put the violence. [[sources/time-and-names]] already says that a name such as the Terror benefits whoever draws the box. Enemies of the republic use it to make every earlier reform guilty. Defenders of 1789 use it to keep the summer clean. Later politicians use it as a standing warning against popular politics. All three uses need the dates to be fuzzy. Kept sharp, September 1793 to 27 July 1794 is a period of government, and a period can be described without becoming a philosophy of history.

Historians disagree about the start. Some begin with the September massacres of 1792, some with the king's death, some with a revolutionary tribunal earlier in 1793, before the conventional September. The disagreement is why "conventionally" belongs in [[#def-terror]]. This atlas uses those brackets because the texture of law changes in the autumn of 1793, when suspicion becomes a ground for arrest, and because political protection changes on 27 July 1794. If nothing institutional had changed at either end, the convention would be empty. The ends are not the birth and death of violence. Violence is already in [[revolutions/from-1789]], and repression does not vanish on the morning after Thermidor.

On 21 January 1793 the Convention put Louis XVI to death. We say that, and we stop. The trial was a political act of the republic against the king it had abolished as a king. The death is not inside the conventional Terror. January is not September. Readers who pack the execution into "the Terror" have usually learned the nickname first and the calendar second. The order in this chapter is the other way round. First the date, then the name, then what the name cannot swallow.

The republic that tried him was at war and splitting. Coalition armies pressed the frontiers. Inside the country there was civil war, including a large rising in the west and armed resistance in cities that rejected the Convention. Repression was violent. We do not describe it, and we give no number of the dead. Historians disagree about the scale of killing in these conflicts and in the tribunals. That disagreement is a reason to refuse a single figure. "Many" is available. A false total is not.

:::theorem The brackets do not explain the Revolution {#thm-brackets}
The Terror, dated from September 1793 to 27 July 1794, is a wartime government using exceptional tribunals. It is not the meaning of the Revolution and not a betrayal that leaves some other, pure revolution untouched. The king's death on 21 January 1793 lies outside the brackets. So does 1789. The limit: war is a condition of this government. War is not a sufficient explanation of these particular tribunals.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the fit between the name and the institutions, and the misfit with the longer sequence. From September 1793 the Convention governs through emergency laws, a committee, deputies with extraordinary powers, and tribunals whose business is political danger. On 27 July 1794 that protection breaks with Robespierre's fall. Before September 1793 the republic has already been proclaimed, gone to war, put its king to death, and seen prisoners massacred in September 1792. After 27 July 1794 the republic still exists. A meaning identical with the brackets cannot account for the years on either side. A betrayal story that puts the real revolution only in 1789 cannot account for a republic that continues to make law after Thermidor.

What would count against the claim? Evidence that these tribunals were already the ordinary constitution in the summer of 1789, or that 27 July 1794 changed nothing in who held emergency power. The constitution accepted on 14 September 1791 was a monarchy with a restricted franchise, not this committee. Thermidor changes the personnel and the cover even though it does not restore 1789. What would also count against us is a showing that every state at war with France used the same tribunals for the same interval. Britain was at war with the republic and did not. The French dates are French government, not the automatic face of war. The levy of August 1793 shows a desperate military crisis. It does not dictate a law about suspicion.
:::

August 1793 is easy to fold into September, and it should not be. The *levée en masse* calls on people, labour and material. We give no size of army. A mass levy says the state may mobilise inhabitants. A tribunal that treats suspicion as its ordinary docket says something else. The claims sit a month apart and share a rhetoric of emergency. Support is not identity. The levy is August. The conventional name starts in September. The rising in the west is a civil war, not a courtroom in Paris, even when deputies on mission carry the emergency into the departments.

The counter-argument from necessity says invasion plus civil war left no other government available. [[#thm-brackets]] already limits that reply: a condition is not a script. In the spring of 1794 some of the men who had led the republic were themselves put to death, before Robespierre fell. Factions destroyed each other under the emergency they had built. That does not make the war unreal. It makes "no other choice" too smooth. Necessity would require that this form of tribunal was the only form any republic in that war could use, and that Thermidor was impossible until the war ended. The war did not end in July 1794. The named period did.

:::example What sits outside the brackets {#ex-outside}
Place each of these relative to September 1793 and 27 July 1794: the September massacres, the execution of Louis XVI, the *levée en masse*, the fall of Robespierre. Give the date of each, and say which ones are inside the conventional Terror.
:::solution
The September massacres are in September 1792, a year before the conventional start. Louis XVI is put to death on 21 January 1793, also outside. The *levée en masse* is August 1793, immediately before the name and still outside it. Robespierre falls on 27 July 1794, 9 Thermidor, which ends the brackets. Only the government between September 1793 and that fall is the conventional Terror. Anything you cannot date against those two ends does not belong in the name.
:::

## What the government was

The Convention was the assembly of the republic. From it came the Committee of Public Safety, which in 1793 and 1794 concentrated the executive work of war and police. It was never a single man, even when Robespierre was its most famous member. Deputies on mission carried wide powers into armies and departments. Local clubs and officers used the emergency for their own enmities. A history that is only Robespierre is a biography in the costume of a regime. A history that is only a crowd lets the committee off the page. Paris passed laws, agents acted outside Paris, and people who were not agents denounced their neighbours. All three can be true without a number of the dead and without a description of how a sentence was carried out.

Political clubs of women were shut in the autumn of 1793, inside the brackets. The revolution criticised in 1791 for speaking of the rights of man and not of woman was not widening citizenship in these months. It was narrowing organised politics. Olympe de Gouges, who had published against that exclusion, was put to death in 1793. We do not describe the death. The fact blocks two cartoons: that the Terror was a festival of equality, and that 1789 had already settled who counted.

There was also a campaign against the church, often called dechristianisation, in these years. It is not the same act as a tribunal. Closing churches, pressing priests and staging civic festivals are cultural and coercive policies. They belong to the emergency's claim to remake public life. They do not explain the brackets by themselves, and the brackets do not make every festival into a trial. The revolutionary calendar, in which 27 July 1794 is 9 Thermidor, is itself a claim about time. We teach both names for one day. We do not pretend the calendar is a science that replaced the need for the Gregorian date. Anyone who benefits from fog prefers one name and a mood. You need both names and a year.

:::example A span inside one year of the brackets {#ex-span-1794}
4 February 1794 and 27 July 1794 fall in one year. February 1794 has 28 days, because 1794 is not a leap year. How many days after 4 February is 27 July? Show the steps from 4 February to 4 July, then on to 27 July. Say, before you interpret, only the number.
:::solution
From 4 February to 4 March is 28 days, the length of a non-leap February. From 4 March to 4 April is 31 days. From 4 April to 4 May is 30 days. From 4 May to 4 June is 31 days. From 4 June to 4 July is 30 days. The sum of those steps is 28 plus 31 plus 30 plus 31 plus 30, which is 150. From 4 July to 27 July is 23 days. 150 plus 23 is 173. So 27 July is 173 days after 4 February. The number is the distance between colonial abolition and 9 Thermidor. Interpretation comes next, in [[#prop-split]]. A widget that places both events on one dot called 1794 has not told you about these 173 days.
:::
:::

## One year, two acts

On 4 February 1794 the Convention abolished slavery in the French colonies. The date falls inside the brackets, which insults any story in which the brackets have only one kind of content. A government can emancipate and repress in the same season. The revolt in Saint-Domingue had begun in August 1791, and in 1793 abolition had already been proclaimed in part of the colony by agents on the spot. The February decree is the metropolitan law that generalises abolition. It does not make the revolt a Paris invention, and it does not make the tribunals into a colonial policy. [[revolutions/napoleon]] restores colonial slavery in 1802. The abolition of 1794 is a dated law, reversible by a later law, inseparable from a war the enslaved had already forced. It is not a permanent moral of the republic.

:::proposition February and Thermidor are different acts {#prop-split}
4 February 1794, the abolition of slavery in the French colonies, and 27 July 1794, the fall of Robespierre, are different acts that share a year. The first is a law of freedom in the colonies. The second ends the conventional Terror. Neither act is the meaning of the other. The widget that marks only "1794" cannot split them. The lesson must.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the content of the two dates and the 173 days between them, computed in [[#ex-span-1794]]. Abolition names a legal status in the colonies. Thermidor names a break in who was protected by the emergency in France. Nothing in the decree of 4 February says that Robespierre will fall, and nothing in the fall restores or abolishes slavery. Restoration is 1802. What would count against the proposition? One decree in which abolition and the fall were the same article, or a showing that 4 February had no effect on legal status. The decree's effect was contested and depended on force, a limit we accept. Dependence on force does not turn a law of status into a law about a Paris committee. If the two acts were one, Haiti's later declaration and the restoration of slavery would be reversals of Thermidor rather than of February.
:::

:::corollary Thermidor ends the name, not the republic {#cor-thermidor}
The fall of Robespierre on 27 July 1794 ends the period conventionally called the Terror. It does not restore the monarchy of 1789 and it does not end the republic. Whoever treats Thermidor as a return to the summer of the Bastille, or as the end of revolutionary government, has changed the subject.
:::
:::proof
1789, as [[revolutions/from-1789]] argued, left a king in place. That king is dead from 21 January 1793. No act of 27 July 1794 recalls the Estates-General. The republic proclaimed in September 1792 continues. In 1795 it is reorganised as the Directory, a divided executive, and 1795 is also the third partition of Poland-Lithuania. A French republic and a destroyed commonwealth can share a year, as February and Thermidor share 1794. What would count against the corollary? A restoration of the king's heir in July 1794, or a dissolution of the republic in the same week. Neither happens. The coup that ends this republican sequence is 9 November 1799, 18 Brumaire, in [[revolutions/napoleon]]. The gap from 1794 to 1799 is evidence. Thermidor is not Brumaire.
:::

The counter-argument says that abolition during the Terror taints the decree, or, in the opposite pamphlet, that the Terror is justified because it freed the colonies. Both need [[#prop-split]] to fail. Condemning the tribunals does not repeal 4 February, and celebrating 4 February does not make a tribunal into emancipation. Commissioners in Saint-Domingue had already moved in 1793. Paris generalised. Calling that either the Terror's gift or the Terror's crime puts one name where two acts are. The restoration in 1802 shows that emancipation was a policy, open to reversal, not a mystical consequence of emergency justice.

:::example An invented motto {#ex-motto}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a decree and not a speech. "The Terror is whatever the Revolution really means, and 4 February is a detail of that meaning." What does the sentence erase, and what would count against your criticism of it?
:::solution
It erases the brackets. If the Terror is the meaning of the whole Revolution, then 1789, the constitution of 1791, the republic of September 1792 and the Directory of 1795 have no content of their own. It also erases [[#prop-split]]. 4 February 1794 abolishes colonial slavery. 27 July 1794 ends an emergency's leadership. The motto makes the first a footnote to a mood. The criticism would fail if the brackets really covered the whole sequence, and if the decree had no bearing on legal status. They do not, and it does. The motto is what the nickname produces when nobody asks for a start and an end.
:::
:::

:::example January is not September {#ex-january}
Louis XVI is put to death on 21 January 1793. The conventional Terror begins in September 1793. The widget gives 1793 a single label, "King executed". Why is sharing the year not enough to put that death inside the brackets?
:::solution
January is not September. The death is a political act of the republic against its former king, dated before the conventional start in [[#def-terror]]. The brackets open when emergency law thickens, not when the king dies. A year-label cannot show the gap, which is why [[#ex-outside]] keeps January outside the name. Sharing 1793 is not one act.
:::

:::warning Terror as the meaning, or as the opposite
People repeat either that the Revolution was the Terror, or that the Terror betrayed the Revolution. The first makes September 1793 into 1789. The second makes 1789 innocent by parking every hard question in one later box. Both need the brackets to be vague, and both fail [[#thm-brackets]]. This chapter describes no method of killing. From September 1793 to 27 July 1794 a government at war used exceptional tribunals, and on 4 February 1794 that year also abolished colonial slavery. A sentence that cannot hold those facts apart is the myth.
:::

:::history
On 27 July 1794, 9 Thermidor, Maximilien Robespierre fell. The date ends the period conventionally called the Terror. It does not end the republic, and it does not restore the monarchy that had put Louis XVI on a throne in 1789. He had been put to death on 21 January 1793, outside those brackets.
:::

## Where this leads

[[revolutions/napoleon]] opens on 9 November 1799, when this republic's later form is overthrown, and it has to keep 4 February 1794 in mind because 1802 restores colonial slavery and 1 January 1804 is Haiti's declaration. The summer that Thermidor does not restore is [[revolutions/from-1789]]. The trade that made Saint-Domingue a plantation colony is [[states/atlantic]]. A nickname as a claim about time is [[sources/time-and-names]].

:::summary
- The Terror is conventionally September 1793 to 27 July 1794, 9 Thermidor, when Robespierre fell. The brackets are a claim about a government, not a definition of revolution.
- Louis XVI is put to death on 21 January 1793, before those brackets. The September massacres are in 1792. The *levée en masse* is August 1793. None of these is "the Terror" by the conventional name.
- Historians disagree about whether the name should start earlier, and they disagree about the scale of deaths. This atlas states no figure and describes no procedure.
- The government is the Convention's emergency: a committee, deputies on mission, exceptional tribunals, local denunciation. It is not a biography and not a crowd alone.
- War is a condition, not a sufficient cause. The named period ends in 1794 while the war does not.
- 4 February 1794 abolishes slavery in the French colonies, inside the year of Thermidor and 173 days before 27 July. The two acts are not one act.
- Thermidor does not restore 1789 and does not end the republic. In 1795 the republic is reorganised, and 1795 is also the third partition of Poland-Lithuania.
:::

:::solution

:::exercise A day in July {level=1 check="27"}
9 Thermidor is which day of July 1794? (Enter the day of the month.)
:::solution
The chronology equates 9 Thermidor with 27 July 1794. The day of the month is 27. The revolutionary calendar's name and the July date are one day, not two events.
:::

:::exercise Distance inside 1794 {level=1 check="173"}
4 February 1794 abolishes colonial slavery. 27 July 1794 is 9 Thermidor. February 1794 has 28 days. How many days after 4 February is 27 July? (Enter the number of days.)
:::solution
From 4 February to 4 July is 150 days: February contributes 28 to the step from the 4th to the 4th of March, then March 31, April 30, May 31 and June 30, and 28 plus 31 plus 30 plus 31 plus 30 is 150. From 4 July to 27 July is 23 days. 150 plus 23 is 173. The two acts are 173 days apart.

Sort these into chronological order: 9 Thermidor, the *levée en masse*, the republic proclaimed, the king's execution, the abolition of colonial slavery, the September massacres. Give each a date, and mark which one is inside the conventional Terror but is not an act of the tribunals' story.

:::exercise A condition is not a script {level=2}
A student writes: "France was invaded and there was civil war, so the Terror was necessary and the dates do not matter." Answer using Britain at war, the fact that the war outlasts 27 July 1794, and [[#thm-brackets]]. Do not offer a death toll as your evidence.
:::solution
Invasion and civil war explain why a government reaches for emergency powers. They do not choose September 1793 and 27 July 1794, and they do not write these tribunals. Britain was at war with the republic and did not adopt this form. The name ends on 9 Thermidor while the war continues. If necessity were enough, Thermidor would wait for peace, and every belligerent would look the same. [[#thm-brackets]] keeps the war as a condition and refuses it as a sufficient cause. No death toll is available, and a toll would not turn a condition into a script.
:::

:::exercise Who benefits from a fuzzy box? {level=2}
Name two opposite political uses of the word Terror, and say how the conventional dates obstruct both. Use the autumn of 1793 and 4 February 1794 in the answer.
:::solution
One use stuffs 1789 into the tribunals and makes the whole Revolution guilty. The Bastille is 14 July 1789 and the conventional start is September 1793, so the dates obstruct it. Another use keeps 1789 pure by calling everything painful a later betrayal, or justifies the tribunals because emancipation happened "under" them. The brackets are real, so the emergency is not a myth, and 4 February 1794 is inside the year without being a tribunal. In the autumn of 1793 women's organised politics was shut and exceptional laws thickened. That autumn is not a festival of equality and not the Declaration. A fuzzy box helps anyone who would rather win the argument than date the institution.
:::

:::exercise What would make the brackets empty? {level=3}
:::hint
A convention about dates is empty if nothing in government changes at the ends. Look at autumn 1793 and at 27 July 1794 separately. Do not answer with a number of executions, and do not describe a procedure.
:::
[[#def-terror]] admits that historians disagree about when the name should start. What evidence would make you drop the conventional brackets as useless, and what evidence would make you keep them while still recording the disagreement? Use no figure for the dead.
:::solution
You would drop the brackets if September 1793 marked no change in exceptional justice and 27 July 1794 marked no change in who held the emergency. The name would then be a mood spread from 1792 or from the king's death, and the convention would be a school habit. You would keep the brackets, and still record the disagreement, if the autumn of 1793 thickened emergency law and Thermidor broke the protection of the men identified with it, while earlier violence stayed real and outside the name. That is this chapter's position. Disagreement about a start is a reason to say "conventionally", not a reason to have no start. A figure for the dead would not decide it, and this atlas has none.
:::

:::exercise Hold February and Thermidor apart {level=3}
:::hint
Use the 173 days, the revolt of August 1791, the partial proclamation of abolition in the colony in 1793, and the restoration of slavery in 1802. The point is kind of act, not a moral score.
:::
A speaker says: "Either the Terror freed the colonies, or the abolition is disgraced because of the month it fell in. Choose." Refuse the choice with [[#prop-split]]. What would force you to accept it?
:::solution
The choice assumes that two acts in one bracket share one moral. 4 February 1794 generalises abolition. 27 July 1794 ends the conventional Terror. They are 173 days apart. The revolt began in August 1791, and abolition had already been proclaimed in part of Saint-Domingue in 1793, so February is not the sole author of freedom. The law is still a law of status. In 1802 a later regime restores slavery, which shows that February could be reversed and was not a property of the tribunals. You can condemn the emergency and record the decree. The speaker's "either" forbids that.

You would accept the choice only if the two dates were one act, or if the decree had no content except the tribunals. [[#prop-split]] says they are not one act. The 1794 dot is what the choice looks like when a figure cannot split a year. The lesson refuses that compression without calling the year innocent.
