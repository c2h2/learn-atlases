Why is a fiscal crisis not yet a revolution?

A crown that cannot pay is in political danger. It is not, by that fact alone, in a revolution. Here a revolution is a change in who may authorise law. France had a crisis of payment: wars financed by debt, and privilege that made a general tax hard to pass. That crisis becomes a crisis of representation only when the body called to repair it refuses to sit in the old way. The Estates-General opened on 5 May 1789. On 17 June the third estate took the name National Assembly. Those two dates are the hinge. A stormed prison, a republic, a dead king, the Terror: each belongs to a later lesson.

Two shortcuts are always on offer. One says that a known deficit must bring the regime down. The other says that a court, and especially a queen, spent the kingdom into collapse. Neither can be checked against the institutions. No deficit figure is locked, so none will be invented. The old regime will not be told as a household. Privilege, office and debt are the political facts. A published shortfall does not vote.

## A composite, not a cartoon

:::definition Old regime {#def-old-regime}
The **old regime** is the name, taken from the French *ancien régime*, for the order a revolution claims to have ended. In this chapter it means the French composite of privileges, venal offices, provincial laws and royal debt as that composite stood when the Estates-General opened in 1789. It does not mean "France before modernity", and it does not mean a court comedy. A great deal of it was recent war finance resting on older rights.
:::

France was a composite monarchy in the sense of [[states/composite-monarchy]]: one king, many laws. Provinces kept different bargains. Some voted taxes in their own estates. Others were run by royal officers. A town, a guild or a lord could hold an exemption, a closed trade, dues or a court a neighbour did not. Calling that absolutism hides the bargaining. Louis XIV's government, in [[states/fiscal-military]], advertised capacity and did not abolish the privileges it taxed around.

Three orders organised the estates: clergy, nobility, and everyone else the elections could see. That third is not "the people". Women were not deputies. Enslaved people in the colonies stood outside the classification. The Code Noir of 1685 is [[states/atlantic]]. The revolt that breaks plantation slavery open is [[revolutions/from-1789]]. The third-estate men of 1789 were largely lawyers, office-holders and owners. "The nation" was a claim, not a census.

:::definition Privilege {#def-privilege}
**Privilege** is a legal advantage attached to an order, a province, a corporation, a town or an office. It covers exemptions from taxes, seigneurial dues, special courts and the exclusive right to do a particular thing. It is not a synonym for snobbery. A privilege can be written, pleaded and inherited, and still be the reason a general tax fails.
:::

Office belonged with privilege. Many judicial and administrative posts were property, bought, and able in practice to pass to an heir. The owner of a high court office was often noble by that ownership. He lived on fees and status, and he had a reason to resist a reform that would end the sale of office or tax an exemption. "The nobility" is too smooth a word. Courtiers, poor provincial gentry and magistrates did not share one income. They did share a reason to hope a new tax would fall elsewhere.

The parlements were royal courts that registered laws, not a British parliament and not a modern supreme court. They could delay registration and protest. When ministers tried to make the privileged pay, these courts spoke of fundamental law. The language was a real constraint, and it was not a popular constitution. Magistrates defending fiscal immunity are an opposition, not democracy in waiting. Historians disagree whether that opposition is a constitutional tradition or a defence of status. Both readings have evidence. Neither turns a remonstrance into a revolution.

:::quiz
On the day the Estates-General opened, which description fits the old regime?
- [ ] A single machine called absolutism, in which privilege was only a costume
- [x] A composite of privileges, offices and debts, in which a general tax was politically hard
- [ ] A republic in all but name, waiting for a deficit to be published
- [ ] A court household whose spending is a measured cause we can cite
:::solution
[[#def-old-regime]] and [[#def-privilege]] are the description this chapter uses: orders, provinces, venal office and a tax system full of exemptions. Absolutism as a single machine is a label, not a machine you can point at. No deficit figure is locked, so a household's spending cannot be offered as the measured cause. On 5 May 1789 the state was still a monarchy calling its estates, not a republic.
:::
:::

The queen belongs in a warning, not in the definition. A foreign princess and a necklace scandal are the stuff of a cartoon, and a sentence about bread is not established as her words, so we will not repeat it. A cartoon of a woman feeds a misogynist story. It does not explain why a tax was easier to announce than to collect from the exempt, or why a magistrate could block a minister. We use no sum in livres. Personality is not a budget.

Tax names show the same structure without a yield. The *taille* fell on people who lacked privilege. The *gabelle* was a salt tax, hated and uneven. A *vingtième*, a twentieth by its name, still met exemptions and resistance. Naming the taxes is enough. A receipt we do not have would be false precision. The point is who could escape.

## War finance without a new authorisation

The war we can date is the Seven Years' War, from 1756 to 1763, fought in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa and India. France lost territory and spent heavily. 1763 minus 1756 is 7. 1789 minus 1756 is 33. A fiscal problem can sit for a generation. That span is already an argument against the idea that a war's bill simply is a revolution.

Further wars after 1776 added to the debt. The American Declaration of that year is context only, as [[states/enlightenment]] insists, and this atlas has not locked a year for the French crown's entry into that conflict, so we do not invent one. The dated background is war finance, including the global war of 1756 to 1763. A minister who needs credit in the 1780s is meeting a structure: debt contracted for war, and privileges that will not quietly become a uniform tax base.

:::theorem A deficit is not yet a change of who authorises law {#thm-not-yet}
A fiscal crisis becomes a revolution only when the means of authorising tax and debt are themselves refused and replaced. Knowledge of a shortfall is not that refusal. The French old regime in 1789 was a composite of privileges, offices and debt, and the debt was real. The revolution is not dated from the moment ministers knew they were short. Debt was a condition. It was not a sufficient cause, and it did not make the events of June necessary.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is institutional and comparative. War finance from 1756 to 1763 left a crown that needed tax and credit. Privilege, as in [[#def-privilege]], let orders and office-holders resist a general tax. Parlements could delay laws. Ministers could fall without the form of the monarchy changing. Britain funded debt through a parliament, with the Bank of England from 1694 and the Bill of Rights of 1689 after the landing of 1688. That settlement is not a democratic revolution, and the name Glorious Revolution is partisan, as [[states/fiscal-military]] says. Debt with a workable authorisation is a fiscal state. Debt with a blocked one is a political crisis. Only refusing the old authorisation is a revolution in this claim's sense.

What would count against the claim? A dated act, before 17 June 1789, by which a shortfall alone had already moved legislative authority, with no change in how the estates sat. Or a Europe in which every heavily indebted monarchy produced the same break. We have neither. A locked deficit figure would not help: a number is not an assembly, and this atlas has no such figure.
:::

The counter-argument deserves the same space. It says the Enlightenment public had already withdrawn legitimacy, so the fiscal crisis only lit a train that was laid. The *Encyclopédie* begins in 1751. The *Social Contract* and *Emile* are 1762. Beccaria on crimes and punishments is 1764. Smith's *Wealth of Nations* and the American Declaration are 1776. [[states/enlightenment]] refuses the sentence that the Enlightenment caused the French Revolution, and this chapter takes that refusal as a premise. A language of rights can justify a tax under a king, a remonstrance, or the third estate's claim to be the nation. Availability is not a destination. A cause, in [[sources/argument]], has to be something that could have been otherwise. The estates could still have voted a tax by order and gone home. That they did not is an event in 1789, not a deduction from 1762.

Historians disagree about the weights: debt, the parlements, the language of rights, the pressure of bread. The disagreement is about weight, not the dates. We will not invent a price or a harvest year. Bread explains pressure. It does not sign the name National Assembly.

The partition of Poland-Lithuania in 1772 is a different event on the same axis. Russia, Prussia and Austria cut into a commonwealth that had a parliament. The sequence through 1793 and 1795 is in [[states/east]]. A parliament did not make a state safe, and a fiscal crisis did not doom one to revolution. France was not partitioned in 1789. The comparison blocks a category mistake.

:::example Two spans on one axis {#ex-span}
The Seven Years' War begins in 1756. The first partition of Poland-Lithuania is in 1772. The Estates-General opens in 1789. Compute the difference of the years from 1756 to 1789, and from 1772 to 1789. Then say what a window of three years, set on 1789, does to the earlier dots.
:::solution
1789 minus 1756 is 33, and 1789 minus 1772 is 17. The war's own bracket, 1756 to 1763, differs by 7, so most of the 33 years is aftermath. A window of half-width 3 years shows a neighbourhood of 1789 and does not reach 1772 or 1756. Leaving the window there hides the fiscal background. That is why you move the year. The single dot for 1789 also cannot show that 5 May and a later July day are different acts.
:::
:::

:::example Days from the opening to the new name {#ex-days}
The estates opened on 5 May 1789. The National Assembly was declared on 17 June 1789. How many days after 5 May is 17 June? May has 31 days. Count the later date, not an inclusive total of both endpoints.
:::solution
Days after 5 May through 31 May: 31 minus 5 is 26. From the end of May to 17 June is 17 days. 26 plus 17 is 43, so 17 June is 43 days after 5 May. The interval is not a mood of suddenness. A body can change its claim in 43 days and still have opened as the old estates. The span is long enough for a tactic and short enough to forbid the story that "1789" is one blur.
:::
:::

## From a deficit to the estates

Calling the estates was a royal decision. The crown needed money and credit, and the ordinary paths — edicts, courts, an assembly of notables — had not produced a general settlement. We do not date the notables. That year is not locked, and the logic does not need it. When the usual bodies will not legitimise a tax, a king reaches for the body that, in old language, can speak for the realm. He calls the estates to save the fiscal monarchy, not to abolish himself. Intention is not result. It is evidence that June was not fixed in advance.

How the estates would vote was the crisis inside the crisis. Voting by order let clergy and nobility outvote the third even if its deputation matched the other two combined. The rule in 1789 did give the third that enlarged deputation. We state the rule and not a headcount, because none is locked. Numbers mattered only if the vote was by head. The privileged orders and the king preferred the old form. The third's deputies had reasons to refuse it. A disputed rule turned a fiscal question into a question of representation. That conversion is the heart of the chapter. It is not a crowd at a fortress, and not a republic.

On 5 May 1789 the estates opened. Treat that morning as the king's assembly still. The hall, the speeches, the verification of powers: none of that is yet a National Assembly. The dispute over whether credentials should be checked in common or by order occupied the weeks that followed. Procedure is the politics. A reader who wants a riot in the first week of May is reading a later day backwards.

:::proposition The hinge is 17 June, not the opening itself {#prop-hinge}
On 17 June 1789 the third estate, joined by some of the clergy, declared itself the National Assembly and claimed authority over taxation. That declaration, not the opening on 5 May, is the hinge at which a fiscal crisis becomes a crisis of representation. The limit is part of the claim. 17 June does not depose the king, does not write the August decrees, and does not take the Bastille. It changes who claims to authorise. It does not complete a new regime.
:::
:::proof
5 May opens the Estates-General. 17 June names the National Assembly. The days between were spent on verification of powers, which is whether the old form would survive. The declaration ties taxation to the assembly's consent. A body called in three orders has answered as one nation. That is the new authorisation [[#thm-not-yet]] required before the word revolution could be used without cheating.

What would count against the proposition? Evidence that 5 May had already transferred legislative power, so that 17 June was a formality, or that 17 June left tax legally untouched. The ceremony was the old regime in its own forms. The deputies' point was that taxation now needed the assembly they claimed to be. Whether they can make the claim stick is [[revolutions/from-1789]], not a reason to move the hinge back to 5 May.
:::

We stop at the hinge on purpose. On 20 June deputies will bind themselves not to disperse until a constitution exists. On 14 July a Paris crowd will take the Bastille. Decrees from 4 to 11 August will attack feudal rights. On 26 August the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen will be dated. On 5–6 October a march will bring the king from Versailles towards Paris. Each is a different act. Narrating them here would turn the hinge into the whole summer and smuggle in results 17 June had not produced. The dates exist so a naming is not mistaken for the end. The naming is not the storming.

A pamphlet of 1789, Sieyès on the third estate, belongs to this hinge and not to a later republic. We do not quote it. Paraphrased, it claimed that the third estate was already the nation and that the privileged orders were not a necessary part of political society. A paraphrase is not a source text. The direction is what matters: nation was being defined against privilege in the same season as the electoral dispute. The pamphlet does not open the estates and does not pass a decree. Print and the vote of 17 June are related, not identical. Collapsing them lets the counter-argument about 1762 back in.

:::quiz
What is true on the evening of 17 June 1789?
- [ ] The republic has been proclaimed and the king has been executed
- [ ] The Bastille has fallen and the August decrees have abolished feudal dues
- [x] A National Assembly has been declared, and the republic, the king's death and the Terror are still in the future
- [ ] Publishing the deficit has already completed the revolution
:::solution
[[#prop-hinge]] puts the hinge on 17 June. The republic is 21–22 September 1792. The king dies on 21 January 1793. The Terror, as a named period, runs from September 1793 to 27 July 1794. The Bastille is 14 July 1789 and the August decrees are 4 to 11 August, both later and both taught next. A deficit is not itself a revolution, which is [[#thm-not-yet]].
:::
:::

## What a grievance can support

The cahiers are the grievance lists drawn up for the elections. They are sources, not a national mind. A parish list and a noble list are different documents. A silence is evidence about who was asked to write, the distinction in [[sources/evidence]], not proof of an empty mind. Many ask for regular meetings, tax reform and an end to particular abuses, in loyal language about the king. Loyalty in a formula is not affection measured, and it is not a republican manifesto. A demand for a republic has to be found in the texts.

The limit runs the other way too. A request to lower a tax is a complaint, and a bundle of complaints is not yet the National Assembly, because a village does not declare a nation. Scale, as [[sources/scale]] insists, is not free. The deputies' act on 17 June is a state act. A parish text is a local act. They can point the same way and still be different evidence.

:::example An invented cahier sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a quotation from any cahier. "The village of Saint-Inventé asks only that the salt tax be lowered, blesses the king, and says nothing about a republic." The village is invented. If a real cahier had said this, what could the sentence support, and what could it not support?
:::solution
It could support one place's fiscal grievance and the loyal language of the genre, not a kingdom's will. It does not ask for a republic. A request to lower a tax is a complaint, so it cannot show contentment, and one village is not France. It states no deficit. A national claim would need many cahiers, set against noble and clerical ones. You would still not have the evening of 17 June, an act of deputies. The invented village is a scale trap.
:::
:::

:::example A debt that did not rename the legislature {#ex-britain}
Set two dated institutions side by side. In 1694 the Bank of England is founded. On 5 May 1789 the French Estates-General opens. What is the comparison allowed to prove, and what is a step too far?
:::solution
The comparison may show that public debt and political rupture are not the same event. Britain fought the century's wars with a bank from 1694 and a parliament restated in the Bill of Rights of 1689, after 1688. France opened the estates in 1789 because tax and credit were blocked by a different set of privileges. It may not show that Britain was calm or a model. The settlement of 1688 was not a democratic revolution, and its nickname is partisan. 1789 minus 1694 is 95. The gap is the point, and it is evidence for [[#thm-not-yet]], not one story called the rise of the modern state.
:::
:::

Move the year to 1789 and leave the window at 3. You see the estates, not 1756 and not 1772. Slide back and the estates disappear. The partition between them destroys a parliamentary commonwealth. It is not a French deficit. One dot for 1789 cannot be both 5 May and 14 July. This chapter reads it as 5 May and as the hinge of 17 June, which shares the year and is not its own point. The next chapter reads July, August and October as their own acts.

:::warning The deficit, or the queen, as fate
People repeat that the old regime fell because the deficit was known, or because a queen was frivolous. The first confuses a fiscal crisis with a change in who authorises law, which is [[#thm-not-yet]], and treats June as scripted. Ministers called the estates to save a monarchy. The second puts a woman in the place of a tax system. A scandal is not a budget, and no deficit is locked to compare with a household. The cartoon fails when you ask who could block a tax, and what changed on 17 June.
:::

:::history
On 5 May 1789 the Estates-General opened under Louis XVI, called because the crown needed money and a consent it could not otherwise get. On 17 June the third estate declared a National Assembly. The summer has later acts, and they are not this note. The republic is not among them, and it is not among these two dates either.
:::

Louis XVI remains king on 17 June. He called the estates and will try to manage what he called. He is not yet on trial before a convention. To narrate him as already dead is to read 21 January 1793 into a May morning. To narrate him as the sole cause returns to the cartoon. The institutions did the blocking. The deputies did the renaming. The debt made the calling look, to the ministry, like a way out.

## Where this leads

[[revolutions/from-1789]] starts again from 5 May and 17 June, then does the work this chapter refused: the oath, the Bastille, the August decrees, the Declaration, the October days, and the different regimes of 1791 and 1792, including the revolt in Saint-Domingue. The fiscal state that made a funded debt thinkable is [[states/fiscal-military]]. The publics that did not compel June are [[states/enlightenment]]. The commonwealth partitioned in 1772 is [[states/east]]. How a label differs from a cause is [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- The old regime is a composite of privileges, offices, provincial laws and debt, not a court cartoon and not a machine called absolutism.
- Privilege is a legal advantage. It made a general tax hard to pass. That is a political fact, not a story about manners.
- The Seven Years' War, from 1756 to 1763, is the dated war finance in the background. From 1756 to 1789 the difference of the years is 33.
- No deficit figure is locked. We do not invent one, and we do not let a queen stand in for one.
- The books of 1751, 1762 and 1764 are a public, not a sufficient cause. A cause has to be something that could have been otherwise.
- On 5 May 1789 the estates opened inside the old monarchy. On 17 June the National Assembly was declared. That hinge is a new claim to authorise tax. It is not the Bastille, and it is not a republic.
- Britain after 1688, with the Bill of Rights in 1689 and the Bank of England in 1694, shows that public debt need not rename a legislature. The comparison limits the French claim. It is not a moral ranking.
:::

:::exercise Could June have gone another way? {level=2}
Suppose the three orders had agreed, early in June 1789, to vote a tax in the old form and go home. The books of 1751, 1762 and 1764 would still have been printed. Would [[#thm-not-yet]] still stand? What does the supposition do to the idea that those books had made a revolution necessary?
:::solution
The supposition is the "otherwise" a cause needs. A tax voted by order, and a dispersal, would have been a remedy inside the old authorisation, with no National Assembly on 17 June. The books of 1751, 1762 and 1764 would still have existed, so they are insufficient. The deputies did not disperse. The supposition tests necessity. It does not describe June. [[#thm-not-yet]] stands: the refusal is the new authorisation, not the printing and not the bare debt. Those publics make the language easier to understand. That is not the same as bound to happen.

The king's death on 21 January 1793 and the Terror's conventional start in September 1793 are further off. Stopping at 5 May would leave a fiscal monarchy calling its estates. :::exercise What would defeat the claim? {level=3}
:::hint
Look for an act dated before any refusal of the old forms, and ask whether debt alone moved the authority to make law. Another indebted state counts only if you do not turn the comparison into advice.
:::
[[#thm-not-yet]] says a fiscal crisis is not yet a revolution. Describe one piece of evidence that would make you abandon the claim, and one piece that would only make you narrow it. Use no invented deficit, and do not treat an invented speech as evidence.
:::solution
You would abandon the claim if, before 17 June 1789 and before any dispute about how the estates sat, a shortfall alone had moved legislative authority. A ceremony that still asked for money is not that act. You would also abandon it if indebted monarchies routinely produced the same break with no political choice. They did not.

You would only narrow it if a locked account showed that one war's debt outweighed privilege in the decision to call the estates. That changes a weight. It does not turn the calling into 17 June. Britain already narrows the claim: debt can be carried when authorisation works. An invented ministerial speech would neither narrow nor defeat it.

:::exercise The queen and the scale of a cause {level=3}
:::hint
Separate a cultural fact, such as a pamphlet or a rumour, from a fiscal institution, such as an exemption or a venal office. Ask what a single household would still have failed to explain.

A reader says: "Privileges and 17 June are unnecessary. The court was extravagant, the queen was hated, and that hatred is the revolution." Answer in three steps. A hostile pamphlet can show that print attacked the queen, and that reputation can touch credit because lenders have opinions. That is the strongest version of the point.

It cannot show that a household caused the fiscal crisis. No deficit is locked, so none may be invented. A scandal does not repeal a privilege or a venal office. A mood is not an Estates-General.

Even with a future household total, 17 June would remain the hinge. A large bill explains pressure to call the estates. It does not force the deputies to refuse the old way of sitting. The reader's sentence skips that date.
