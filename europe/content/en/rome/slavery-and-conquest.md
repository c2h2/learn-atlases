How did conquest and slavery belong to one history?

The Republic of offices, already an Italian power before 264 BCE, then fought three wars with Carthage and, in the same broad generations, destroyed a great city in Greece. Conquest on that scale was not a foreign adventure beside an otherwise civic life. It was how the political class got commands, how the city got provinces, and how a great many people became property. Enslaved people are not a side chapter and not a private moral failing. Keith Bradley’s argument, in *Slavery and Society at Rome*, is used here in paraphrase and not as a quotation: slavery is an institution of Roman society. Law, sale, household, farm, mine and public service all assume it. A cruel master is not the explanation.

The dates are one line, and the line is the argument. The First Punic War runs from 264 BCE to 241 BCE. The Second runs from 218 BCE to 201 BCE, with Cannae in 216 BCE and Zama in 202 BCE. The Third runs from 149 BCE to 146 BCE. In 146 BCE Carthage is destroyed, and Corinth is destroyed in the same year. In 133 BCE Tiberius Gracchus holds the tribunate. The crisis in the city is about land and the consequences of empire. He is not a liberal reformer.

We will not give a number of people enslaved. The chronology does not lock one, and a round total would be false precision. We will not describe the mechanics of punishment. Enslavement, sale and forced labour can be named without a manual of violence. A governor is not a modern colonial officer. The extraction was still real.

## Three wars, and a year with two destructions

Carthage was a great city of the western Mediterranean, with its own empire and its own wars. It was not a blank against which Rome discovered that it was Europe. Two imperial cities fought. Rome won. The gaps in the chronology matter as much as the battles.

The first war, 264 BCE to 241 BCE, is fought above all for Sicily. It is largely naval. The elapsed count from start to end is 23 years, a count of numerals, not a casualty list and not a claim about how many seasons were fought in full. At the end, Carthage leaves Sicily. Roman rule there becomes a province: a sphere beyond the Italian alliance, governed by a Roman commander. Italy had been organised by settlement and treaty, as [[rome/republic]] argued. Sicily is organised as subject territory. That is a change in the kind of conquest, not the invention of conquest.

Then a gap. From 241 BCE to 218 BCE the elapsed count is again 23 years. The equality is an arithmetic accident, useful only as a warning. The same number does not make the gap and the war the same kind of event. During the gap this atlas locks no battle-year. The second war begins in 218 BCE, when a Carthaginian army campaigns into Italy. Hannibal’s invasion is the fact those years name. We do not quote him, and we do not give the size of his army.

Cannae, in 216 BCE, is a catastrophic Roman defeat, two years on the elapsed count from the start of the war. Roman armies are broken. The Republic does not surrender. The Italian alliance is strained, and some allies go over, but the system built before 264 BCE does not dissolve in one afternoon. A disaster is not a verdict on the whole war, and it is not a number of dead. No death-total is locked, so none is stated.

The war runs until 201 BCE. Zama, in 202 BCE, is the Roman victory in Africa that decides it, under a Roman commander against Hannibal’s army. From Cannae to Zama the elapsed count is 14 years. From Zama to the war’s end-date, 201 BCE, it is one year. The battle and the end of the war are not the same numeral. Carthage is defeated, confined and left in place. It is not yet destroyed. Between 201 BCE and 149 BCE the elapsed count is 52 years. This atlas does not lock a battle-year in the east inside that gap, so we do not insert Cynoscephalae or any other undated victory under a fake numeral. Roman war in the east is visible, in this chronology, at the other destruction of 146 BCE.

The third war is short. From 149 BCE to 146 BCE is an elapsed count of three years. It ends with the destruction of Carthage. In the same year Rome destroys Corinth. One year, two cities, two theatres. Carthage is the old enemy of the Punic wars. Corinth is a great Greek city. The Republic that entered this stretch as an Italian alliance leaves it able to abolish a western capital and a Greek capital in the same numbered year. We do not describe the killing or the demolition, and we do not number the dead or the enslaved. A city that had been a power was ended as a power, and a great many of its people were taken into slavery. That last clause is qualitative. It is not a census.

:::definition Province {#def-province}
A **province** is a sphere of Roman command outside the Italian alliance of citizens, Latins and allies. A magistrate or a former magistrate governs it, commands troops and presides over justice. A province is not a modern colony and not a colonial office of the later European kind. It is still a machine of extraction: tribute, grain, labour and the private profit of the men sent to rule it. The people of a province are, in this period, mostly not Roman citizens.
:::

:::theorem The three wars are one history of conquest, and 146 BCE is two destructions {#thm-two-cities}
The Punic wars from 264 BCE to 146 BCE, with Corinth destroyed in the same year as Carthage, are a single process of Roman conquest. They are not three isolated quarrels, and they are not a western war with an unrelated Greek footnote.
:::
:::proof
The chronology puts the wars on one line: 264–241, 218–201 with Cannae in 216 and Zama in 202, then 149–146, and it puts Corinth in 146 as well. The gaps are real — 23 years, then 52 — and they do not break the process into separate worlds. Sicily becomes a province after the first war. Italy is invaded in the second and holds. Carthage is destroyed in the third. Corinth’s destruction in the same year shows Roman power in Greece reaching the same kind of decision. What would count against the claim is evidence that Corinth was destroyed without Roman command, or that someone else destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE. The chronology does not say that. A missing battle-year is a limit on what we may date. It is not a peace of the whole Mediterranean.
:::

:::example Same elapsed count, different kind of event {#ex-twenty-three}
Compute the elapsed count from 264 BCE to 241 BCE, and from 241 BCE to 218 BCE. Then say what the equality does not mean.
:::solution
From 264 to 241 the elapsed count is 23 years. From 241 to 218 it is also 23 years. The first span is the First Punic War. The second is the gap before the Second. The equality is a property of these numerals. It does not mean the gap was a war, and it does not measure ships, deaths or seasons.
:::
:::

:::example Cannae and Zama are not the whole war {#ex-cannae-zama}
Place in order the start of the Second Punic War, Cannae and Zama. Compute the elapsed count from Cannae to Zama, and from Zama to 201 BCE. What mistake does a story make if it ends the war at Cannae, or if it treats Zama and 201 as the same fact?
:::solution
The order is 218 BCE, then 216 BCE, then 202 BCE. From Cannae to Zama the elapsed count is 14 years. From Zama to 201 BCE it is one year. Ending in 216 BCE ends on a Roman catastrophe and deletes the years in which the Republic continues and wins. Fusing 202 with 201 deletes the difference between a battle and the chronology’s close of the war. The war is the bracket 218–201, with two battles inside it that do different work.
:::

## Slavery as an institution

After these wars, enslaved people are a structural part of Roman society. Romans had enslaved captives before 264 BCE, and other ancient societies, including Athens and Carthage, practised slavery too. The claim is not that the First Punic War invented slavery. It is that Mediterranean conquest made enslaved labour a central institution of the Republic that won, not a private vice at the edge of a citizen story.

Roman law treats the enslaved person as property: bought, sold, inherited and used. Enslavement comes through capture, through birth to an enslaved mother, and through trade. The wars are mass capture. Sale turns captives into labour for Italy and for Roman households. Elite houses, many farms of the rich, mines, and parts of the city’s work — building, haulage, craft, the service of magistrates — assume that labour. Historians disagree about the share of Italian land worked by enslaved people, and about how far peasant farming remained ordinary. The disagreement is not a licence to invent a percentage. Bradley’s framing survives it. Even if the proportion is debated, the law, the market in people and the acceptance of enslaved labour are how this society is built.

Manumission is part of the institution, not a refutation of it. Romans freed some enslaved people, and a freed person could become a citizen, with limits on office. That shows an owner’s power to release. It does not show that slavery was mild. We will not invent a rate. Resistance belongs here and stays undated where the chronology is silent. Enslaved people fled and sometimes revolted. This atlas does not lock a year for the most famous Italian revolt, so we do not invent one, and we do not give a death-total. Revolt shows coercion. It is not a request to describe punishments.

A private-vice story says cruelty is the whole account. Cruelty occurred. It does not explain property law, the expectation of captives, or a public career that assumed an enslaved household. Change the master and the status remains.

The counter-argument is right that slavery is older than 264 BCE, in Rome and elsewhere, and wrong if it then treats the wars as irrelevant. We do not date a first enslaved person, and we do not call Athens or Carthage innocent. The scale after 264 BCE, the provinces, and the wealth fought over in 133 BCE are unintelligible if enslaved people are a footnote. What would count against that is evidence that after 146 BCE enslaved labour was marginal in law and in elite households, farms and mines. Historians arguing about the share of slave estates are arguing inside the institution. They do not licence a percentage.

:::definition Slavery as a Roman institution {#def-slavery}
**Slavery**, in this chapter, is the legal and social institution by which people are property: captured, born, bought, sold and compelled to labour in households, on estates, in mines and in the city’s work. It is not a private moral failing, and it is not an episode beside the political history. Manumission and freed people are parts of the institution. They are not evidence that it was peripheral.
:::

:::theorem Conquest and slavery are one process seen from two ends {#thm-one-process}
The wars that produced provinces and destroyed Carthage and Corinth also produced enslaved people as a structural supply of labour and status. A citizen epic with the enslaved as a domestic detail is one history broken in half.
:::
:::proof
Capture in war is a recognised source of slave status in Roman law, and the wars dated here are the large captures of this period. Elite and public life of the later Republic presupposes enslaved labour. We paraphrase that assumption and do not invent the sentences of the agricultural writers. What would count against the claim is a demonstration that people enslaved in these wars were a negligible trickle, unrelated to Italian estates and urban households. The legal category is older than 146 BCE and is fed by it. A locked total showing enslavement to have been rare would also count against it. There is no such total. The absence limits our precision. It does not licence a citizen-only history.
:::

:::example A governor and a captive, labelled as types {#ex-two-ends}
The pair is invented and labelled as invented. It is not a biography and not a count.

- A praetor is sent to a province in the generation after 146 BCE. He commands troops, hears cases and buys people for his household.
- A person captured at the destruction of a city is sold. The buyer is not the praetor. The sale is ordinary.

In what sense are these two ends of one history? What must you not add?
:::solution
The praetor is conquest as government: a province, a Roman office, extraction. The captive is conquest as status: a person made property by the wars the office exists to wage. They may never meet. Slave law, the market and the assumption that a governor’s house contains enslaved people are the connection. Bradley’s framing stops us calling the purchase a private hobby beside the “real” public work.

Do not add a number of people enslaved, a punishment, or a speech. The example illustrates a structure. It does not witness a particular sale.
:::
:::

## What a province was

A governor is a Roman politician with *imperium*, the power to command, used away from the Italian system of allies. He is often a praetor or a consul, or a man whose command has been extended after the year. He is not a professional civil servant of a colonial ministry, because there is no such ministry. The Republic sends the same kind of man who has stood for office in Rome. A command is a prize in the career, and a chance to pay debts and collect a fortune. The abuses are famous in later writing. We do not need a particular scandal, and we will not invent one. Ordinary extraction does the work: taxes and grain, requisitions, jurisdiction that outsiders cannot really appeal, and war on the edge of the province that produces more captives.

The governed are not citizens, apart from communities specially rewarded. Their own elites often collect what Rome demands and grow powerful by doing it. Local mediation does not make the extraction voluntary. It means Roman rule already works through men on the spot who are not sent from Rome. A province is not a void waiting for a governor’s genius.

Public contractors bid for certain revenues. They are citizens whose profit depends on the empire, not a modern fiscal service, and no tax rate is locked. Sicily after 241 BCE is the early case. Africa after 146 BCE, and Greece in the year of Corinth’s destruction, extend it. We do not date each provincial statute.

:::proposition A governor is not a modern colonial officer, and the extraction is still real {#thm-governor}
Provincial government in the Republic is a Roman magistracy extended overseas, not an office of a modern colonial state. Tribute, grain, jurisdiction and captives are still extraction.
:::
:::proof
The men who govern are magistrates and former magistrates of the city in [[rome/republic]]. There is no separate colonial service. The revenues and commands are attested by the political struggles of the later Republic, including 133 BCE, which is unintelligible if provinces do not pay. What would count against the first half is a permanent civil ministry, distinct from the magistracies, running the provinces on a modern plan. That ministry is not there. What would count against the second half is evidence that provinces cost Rome nothing and supplied neither surplus nor careers. The competition for commands shows the opposite.
:::

## The tribunate of 133 BCE

Tiberius Gracchus was a noble. In 133 BCE he was tribune of the plebs, the office later Romans connected with the struggle of the orders. A tribune can propose laws to the plebeian assembly and can block a magistrate. Gracchus used it for a land law. In paraphrase of the later accounts, and not as a quotation: public land had gathered in the hands of the rich, and long wars had used up citizen soldiers. Whether the diagnosis was fair is debated. We do not settle it with a figure for the land, because none is locked.

He took the proposal to the assembly against senatorial opposition and broke with the custom that a colleague’s veto should stop a tribune. He was killed in the political violence of that tribunate. We do not describe the killing. A disagreement about land inside the citizen body ends with the death of a tribune. That is new among the years this atlas locks. It is not yet the civil wars of the late Republic. It is the citizen body arguing, lethally, about the consequences of empire.

He is not a liberal reformer. A later programme of equal rights would include people the Republic excluded. His politics, as the year shows them, are about land for citizens and about the citizen army. They do not emancipate the enslaved, enfranchise the Italian allies, or bring women into the assemblies. The allies’ later war is not given a year here, so it is not narrated. Seriousness about a land bill is not liberalism, and the insult that he wanted to be a tyrant is not an analysis either. Collegiality did not contain the fight.

The elapsed count from 146 BCE to 133 BCE is 13 years. The closeness puts the tribunate on the same line as the destructions. It does not prove that he had Carthage in mind, and we do not invent a speech. It does rule out a history of “domestic reform” with the wars left out. Some historians draw a road from 133 BCE to the principate and call the revolution already begun. That road is a later drawing. This chapter does not walk it, and it does not fill the gap with battles the chronology does not lock. The next locked political death is Caesar’s, in [[rome/end-of-the-republic]]. What 133 BCE shows is that empire had become a question inside the citizen assemblies, and that the question could kill.

:::example A sentence that makes him a liberal {#ex-liberal}
The following sentence is invented. “In 133 BCE Tiberius Gracchus published a programme of equal rights for citizens, allies, women and enslaved people, and the senate killed him for that equality.”

What is wrong with it, given the real year and the real office?
:::solution
The year and the death in political violence echo a real event. The programme is invented, and it reverses the chapter’s warning. The tribunate of 133 BCE is about land and the citizen army. It is not a universal franchise and not an emancipation. The sentence turns a noble’s use of the tribunate into a later ideology, then uses the killing as a martyrdom for that ideology. Labelled as invented, it is a specimen of the liberal-reformer myth, not a source.
:::

:::warning Not a private vice, and not a number
Two refusals belong together. Slavery is not the personal cruelty of masters, separate from the wars, the provinces and the land law. The institution is public. And the chronology has not counted the people enslaved. A total would feel more serious than “many”. It would be less honest. The same warning covers punishment. Describing how pain was applied turns a legal status into a spectacle. Name capture, sale, property and labour. Stop there.
:::

The Third Punic War ended in 146 BCE with the destruction of Carthage, and Corinth was destroyed in the same year. In 133 BCE Tiberius Gracchus held the tribunate. The politics of land in the city were already the politics of empire, and enslaved people were already structural in the society those wars had made.
:::

## Where this leads

The conquest that staffed Roman society with enslaved people also strained the habits that kept magistrates inside a shared career. [[rome/end-of-the-republic]] takes the next locked dates: the Ides of March, Actium, and the name Augustus. The offices those dates inherit are the offices of [[rome/republic]]. Slavery does not end when Republican politics ends. It is still there when citizenship is widened in [[rome/imperial-society]].

:::summary
- The First Punic War is 264–241 BCE, the Second is 218–201 BCE, with Cannae in 216 and Zama in 202, and the Third is 149–146 BCE. Carthage and Corinth are both destroyed in 146 BCE.
- The gaps of 23 years and then 52 years are real. A missing battle-year must not be filled in. Conquest still joins the dates that are locked.
- A province is a Roman command outside the Italian alliance. A governor is a magistrate, not a modern colonial officer. Tribute, grain and captives are still extraction.
- Slavery is an institution: property, capture, birth, sale and labour. It is not a private vice. Manumission does not make it marginal. No total of people enslaved is stated.
- Historians disagree about how much Italian land enslaved labour worked. The disagreement does not return slavery to a footnote, and it does not justify a percentage.
- Tiberius Gracchus in 133 BCE is a crisis of land and of the citizen army, an elapsed count of 13 years after the two destructions. He is not a liberal reformer, and he did not free the enslaved.
- Conquest and slavery are one process. The citizen and the captive are not two stories.
:::
