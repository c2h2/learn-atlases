Why is 476 a convention?

In 476 CE Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus. Textbooks often close the Roman empire on that deposition. The closure is a convention: not the end of Roman life, not the end of the Roman state, and not a moral. The eastern empire continued. Julius Nepos, a rival claimant, died in 480 CE, an elapsed count of 4 years later. A story that needs the west to die on one cue has to ignore Nepos, ignore the east, and ignore the longer change around the year: the sack of 410 CE, generations of military and political breakdown, and a society that still used Roman law, landowning, cities and Christian institutions.

This chapter moves the stop. It starts from the historian’s bundle called the third-century crisis, 235 CE to 284 CE, then Diocletian from 284 CE, the Milvian Bridge on 28 October 312 CE, the toleration of 313 CE, the council of 325 CE and the dedication of Constantinople on 11 May 330 CE. It only starts an official Christian religion, in 380 CE and 391 CE. [[after-rome/christianisation]] continues that subject. Lead, Christianity and decadence are refused as explanations of 476 CE. Peter Heather’s *The Fall of the Roman Empire* treats the western military loss as real. It is one account, not an oracle. Other historians stress accommodation and what continued. Historians disagree. A real collapse of western government and a conventional date can both stand.

## A label for fifty years, not an event

From 235 CE to 284 CE the elapsed count is 49 years. The third-century crisis is a historian’s bundle, not one battle, reign or afternoon. Inside the brackets, without extra years, emperors are made and unmade often, and many die by violence. Frontiers on the Rhine, the Danube and the east are under pressure in overlapping generations. For a time, regions break with the centre. Coinage and tax come under strain, and cities find the old civic spending harder. The chronology locks 235 and 284, and the judgement that the label bundles them. It does not lock a civil-war year inside the brackets, so we do not invent one.

Historians disagree about the weight of the word “crisis”. Some see a break between the principate and what follows. Others see a severe run of wars that damaged some regions far more than others, while elites, languages and landholding carried on. What would count against using the label at all is ordinary continuity of government, army and tax from 235 CE to 284 CE. The decades do not look like that. What would count against calling the label one event is the length. Forty-nine years of different wars is a period.

:::definition Third-century crisis {#def-crisis}
The **third-century crisis** is the historian’s label for the period from 235 CE to 284 CE. It bundles frequent violent changes of ruler, pressure on several frontiers, fiscal strain and episodes of regional breakaway. It is not a single event, and it is not the fall of the Roman state. The empires of the next century are still Roman, rebuilt out of this period.
:::

:::theorem The crisis is a bundle, and Diocletian’s reign is not its last afternoon {#thm-bundle}
The years 235–284 CE are a historian’s bundle of crises, not one event. Diocletian, from 284 CE, marks a reconstruction of rule after the bundle, not the end of a collapse already finished.
:::
:::proof
The chronology gives the brackets and Diocletian’s year together. Variety of wars, usurpers and regions is why “bundle” is the right noun. His reign starts a reorganised monarchy: colleagues sharing imperial rank, a denser provincial and fiscal map, a court more openly monarchical than the principate’s republican dress. What would count against the claim is evidence that 235–284 was one battle, or that 284 CE changed nothing. Shared rule and fiscal reconstruction are the change. What would also count against it is one cause stated as the meaning of all 49 years. No such cause is locked.
:::

The reconstruction rebuilds the capacity to tax, to command armies and to present a ruler as more than a magistrate who refused the name of king. [[rome/end-of-the-republic]] already placed the change of supremacy in 27 BCE, with the forms kept. Diocletian’s court makes the supremacy harder to mistake. This atlas does not lock the year of each colleague’s appointment, so there is no timetable of the shared rule. Shared rank covered several frontiers and did not end civil war. The next dated facts are a battle between Roman claimants in 312 CE and an agreement in 313 CE. Shared rule is a technique, not a peace.

:::example Forty-nine years, and the error of one afternoon {#ex-forty-nine}
Compute the elapsed count from 235 CE to 284 CE. A summary says the crisis “lasted a moment, until Diocletian ended Rome’s troubles.” What does the count do to that sentence?
:::solution
The elapsed count is 284 minus 235, which is 49 years. A moment does not last 49 years. The year 284 CE ends the historians’ bracket. It does not end Roman troubles or civil war. The Milvian Bridge is still ahead, in 312 CE. [[#thm-bundle]] separates a reconstruction from the fantasy that the troubles were over.
:::
:::

## From the Milvian Bridge to a new capital

On 28 October 312 CE, on the usual dating, Constantine defeated Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge. The battle is a civil war about which Roman claimant holds the west. It does not decide the religion of the empire, and it does not found a church. Later Christian writers told that Constantine fought under a Christian sign. We do not quote that telling, and we do not treat the vision as fact. “Usual dating” means we teach this day and do not offer another numeral.

In 313 CE Constantine and Licinius agreed a policy of toleration, later called the Edict of Milan. The name is later. In paraphrase, not as their words, the policy allowed Christian worship beside other worship, ended persecution of Christians as the partnership’s policy, and restored property persecution had taken. Toleration is not establishment. From 313 CE to the council of 325 CE is an elapsed count of 12 years, and from 313 CE to the edict of 380 CE is 67. The partnership itself did not last. This atlas does not lock the year of the later war between Constantine and Licinius, so we do not invent it. The policy and the political pair are different facts.

The council at Nicaea in 325 CE is another kind of act. Constantine summoned bishops. They argued about the relation of the Son to the Father, in the dispute associated with Arius, and adopted a creed. In paraphrase, without quotation: the council treated the Son as fully divine with the Father, and rejected Arius’s teaching. Defining orthodoxy defines who is outside it. The emperor is in the room where doctrine is settled, and Christianity is not yet the official religion. The argument did not stop in 325 CE. Chalcedon in 451 CE is a later doctrinal division, and it belongs to [[after-rome/christianisation]]. Naming it only warns against treating Nicaea as the end of Christian disagreement.

On 11 May 330 CE, on the traditional date, Constantine dedicated Constantinople, the old Greek city of Byzantium rebuilt as an imperial capital. We teach the marker and do not pretend to have watched the ceremony. The dedication does not create a new civilisation in a day, and it does not end the eastern Roman state by renaming it. It gives that state a capital which outlasts the western office, which is why 476 CE cannot be the end of Rome as a state. The elapsed count from 330 CE to 476 CE is 146 years: the life of a capital, not a prologue. [[after-rome/byzantium]] tells the later history.

:::definition Toleration is not establishment {#def-toleration}
The agreement of 313 CE, later called the Edict of Milan, is a policy of **toleration**. The name is later. The policy allows Christian worship. It does not make Christianity the official religion, and it does not describe what every inhabitant believed. Official religion is dated here to 380 CE.
:::

:::theorem The dates 312, 313, 325 and 330 are different kinds of act {#thm-kinds}
The Milvian Bridge, the toleration, the council of Nicaea and the dedication of Constantinople do not form one conversion. A battle, a permission, a doctrinal line and a capital are four acts.
:::
:::proof
The chronology separates them: 28 October 312, then 313, then 325, then 11 May 330. From 312 to 313 the elapsed count is one year; from 313 to 325 it is twelve; from 325 to 330 it is five. A rival is defeated, a persecution is dropped, a creed is adopted, a city is dedicated. What would count against the claim is one document of 312 CE containing the toleration, the creed and the dedication. None exists. The story of a sign at the bridge pulls the battle towards a conversion. The separate numerals resist that pull. Calling 313 CE an established church would also defeat the claim. [[#def-toleration]] holds the permission apart from 380 CE.
:::

:::example From permission to official religion {#ex-sixty-seven}
Compute the elapsed count from 313 CE to 380 CE, and from 380 CE to 391 CE. What does each count forbid?
:::solution
From 313 to 380 the elapsed count is 67 years. From 380 to 391 it is 11 years. The first forbids treating toleration and official religion as one step. The second forbids treating the measures of 391 CE as the same act as the edict of 380. Those measures restrict public pagan cult. They are not a census of belief.
:::
:::

## An official religion, only begun

In 380 CE the Edict of Thessalonica made Christianity the official religion of the empire. Read the sentence as law. The emperors’ creed is the creed of the state, and other Christian positions can be treated as heresy. Enforcement is a process, not a switch. Bishops, courts and habit do not move together, and 380 CE is not a census of belief. No percentage of compliance is locked, so none is stated.

In 391 CE measures associated with Theodosius restricted public pagan cult. A ban on public sacrifice is not evidence that every private or rural practice stopped in that year. The 11 years from 380 to 391 keep official status and the ban on public cult apart.

These two acts only start Christianisation. [[after-rome/christianisation]] continues them, including what did not change: Roman law, landowning and tax. Christianisation is not a European spirit arriving, and not pagan rationality going out. Nothing here causes 476 CE. The east became officially Christian and continued. The western office ended, on the conventional dating, inside a Christian empire. A cause shared by the half that continues cannot be the difference. From 380 CE to 476 CE the elapsed count is 96 years.

## The sack, the deposition and the claimant

On 24 August 410 CE the Visigoths under Alaric sacked Rome. The sack shocks a city that had not been taken by a foreign army for a very long time. It is not the end of the western office. The court is no longer identical with the city. We do not date the move of the court, because that year is not locked. The office continues after the sack. Its conventional end is 476 CE, an elapsed count of 66 years later. Alaric did not install Odoacer, and he did not touch the capital dedicated in 330 CE.

Between 410 and 476, armies recruited largely from peoples the Romans called barbarians bargained with western emperors and sometimes disposed of them. “Barbarian” is a Roman insult that became a period name, not an explanation. Goths and others were already inside the military system. This chapter does not tell each reign. Unlocked usurpations would be the fake precision already refused inside the third-century bundle.

In 476 CE Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus. The name echoes an undated foundation legend, which is why the deposition makes a neat last page. The person deposed is a child emperor in Italy, not a founder. On the usual account Odoacer did not take the western imperial title and looked to the eastern emperor for recognition. The western office, as a separate court, stops being filled. That is a real change in the government of Italy. It becomes a convention when it is asked to end Roman civilisation, the Roman people, or the Roman state.

Julius Nepos died in 480 CE. He had been recognised in the east and he outlived the deposition by 4 years. The convention prefers the Italian deposition because it is the clearer break in the peninsula. The chronology locks both years so the choice stays visible.

The eastern empire continues from the capital of 330 CE, officially Christian since 380 CE, with Roman law, tax and armies. Odoacer’s coup does not annul it. Later centuries call that state Byzantine. [[after-rome/byzantium]] explains the name. Treating “Byzantine” as a new species born in 476 CE lets the convention define Romanness as whatever died in the west.

In the west, law, landowning, Latin, cities, churches and local elites continue under kings who often wanted the fiscal machinery. [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]] tells that history. Heather’s book insists the western military collapse was real. It is not a verdict against historians who stress continuity. You can hold that central government failed and still refuse “Roman civilisation ended in 476”.

:::theorem 476 CE is a convention, not the end of Roman life or of the Roman state {#thm-476}
Odoacer’s deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476 CE is the conventional end of the western imperial office. It is not the end of the Roman state, because the eastern empire continued, and not the end of Roman life in the west. Julius Nepos’s death in 480 CE shows that a western claim outlived the deposition.
:::
:::proof
The chronology states the convention and the limits together. The sack of 410 and the capital of 330 keep 476 from being a lonely climax. Heather’s book shows that a serious case for real western collapse exists, and that it is one account. What would count against the claim is an eastern empire ending in 476 CE, or law, cities and landholding in the west ceasing in that year as a body. They did not. What would count against the narrower content is evidence that Romulus was not deposed, or that a continuous western court carried on regardless. The deposition stands. The wholesale ending does not.
:::

:::example Four years and a claimant {#ex-four}
Compute the elapsed count from 476 CE to 480 CE, and from 410 CE to 476 CE. A timeline prints only 476 and captions it “the fall of Rome”. What has it deleted?
:::solution
From 476 to 480 the elapsed count is 4 years. From 410 to 476 it is 66 years. The timeline deletes Alaric’s sack and Julius Nepos’s death, and it has no place for an eastern empire that does not stop. The caption turns a deposition in Italy into the end of the Roman name. [[#thm-476]] keeps the deposition. The sack is 24 August 410 CE. Nepos dies in 480 CE. The east continues.
:::
:::

:::example One cause, shared by the east {#ex-shared-cause}
These sentences are not sources. “The west fell because of lead in the pipes.” “The west fell because it became Christian.” “The west fell because of decadence.” Using the eastern empire’s continuation, and the dates 380 CE and 476 CE, say why none can be the difference between east and west.
:::solution
A cause of the western office’s end has to be unshared with the east, or different in the two halves for a reason you can state. Lead, Christianity and elite luxury have been claimed for the Roman world as a whole. The east is Roman, officially Christian from 380 CE, and it continues after 476 CE. A shared feature cannot by itself pick out the west. The elapsed count from 380 to 476 is 96 years, so Christianity is not a prompt effect of the edict either. Do not replace the three sentences with another single noun. Military pressure, fiscal failure and bargains with armies are a cluster historians argue about, including Heather. They are not a motto.
:::

:::warning Not one sentence, and not the end of a civilisation
Lead, Christianity and decadence are each one sentence asked to explain a change that is not one event, and each is shared, in the usual telling, with an eastern empire that did not lose its office in 476 CE. Christianity here is a sequence of acts: toleration in 313, a council in 325, official religion in 380, a restriction on public pagan cult in 391. Those acts only start Christianisation. Decadence is an insult, not a checkable cause. Lead is a material story this chronology does not lock.

The deposition is real. Roman life in the west is not extinguished by it, and the eastern state is not extinguished by it. Nepos dies in 480 CE. Heather’s account of western collapse belongs beside that distinction. It does not license the sentence that Rome ended when the western court stopped being filled.

## Where this leads

The western office is a convention with a date. What continues is a set of societies, churches and an eastern Roman state. [[after-rome/christianisation]] takes 313, 325, 380, 391 and the council of 451 further than a start. [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]] takes the kingdoms that governed Roman provinces after the western court failed. [[after-rome/byzantium]] takes the state that did not stop. Keeping the east and the death of Nepos inside the claim is the same discipline as [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- From 235 CE to 284 CE is 49 years. The third-century crisis is a historian’s bundle, not one event. Diocletian, from 284 CE, reconstructs rule and does not end Roman history.
- On 28 October 312 CE Constantine defeated Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge, on the usual dating. In 313 CE he and Licinius agreed toleration, later called the Edict of Milan. The name is later. Toleration is not an official church.
- Nicaea in 325 CE draws a doctrinal line. Constantinople’s traditional dedication is 11 May 330 CE. An eastern capital is why 476 CE cannot end the Roman state.
- The Edict of Thessalonica in 380 CE makes Christianity official. Enforcement is not a census of belief. Measures against public pagan cult in 391 CE are a further act. These acts only start Christianisation.
- Alaric sacked Rome on 24 August 410 CE. Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus in 476 CE. Julius Nepos died in 480 CE. Historians disagree about the width of the western collapse.
- The eastern empire continued. Law, cities, land and churches in the west did not stop. Heather’s book is one account of a real military and political loss, not an oracle.
- Lead, Christianity and decadence fail as one-sentence causes. A cause shared with the east cannot distinguish the two halves.
:::
