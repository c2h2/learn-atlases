What changed when Augustus took that name, and what was kept?

The Republic of offices did not stop on one evening. It was captured, emptied and relabelled across a short series of dated markers, while the names of the offices stayed in use. Julius Caesar was assassinated on 15 March 44 BCE. The men who killed him did not restore a stable republic. Actium, on 2 September 31 BCE, left one commander standing. In 27 BCE that commander, Octavian, was called Augustus. Consuls, senate and assemblies continued. The monarchy kept the forms. “The Republic ended on one day” is a later simplification, and it is the simplification this chapter refuses.

The years between the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus and the Ides are full of civil violence and extraordinary commands. This atlas does not lock those battle-years. We do not invent them, and we do not narrate every civil war. What the locked dates can teach is already enough: a killing that failed to restore the old competition, a battle that ended the last war between Roman commanders of that generation, and a settlement that kept republican names inside a new kind of power.

Freedom, as a share in those offices, had never belonged to all adults. [[rome/republic]] made that limit the centre of the earlier story. [[rome/slavery-and-conquest]] made conquest and slavery the content of the empire the offices fought over. The monarchy does not take a universal liberty from people who had never held it. It takes the competition for supreme command away from the political class, and it leaves the excluded where they were. Enslaved people remain enslaved. That sentence is still true in 212 CE.

## The Ides of March

Caesar gathered in his own person the kind of power the annual magistracy was built to split: a long command, a civil war carried into Italy, a dictatorship that was no longer the old short emergency, and a supremacy his enemies called kingship. We can name that accumulation without dating each step. The years of the civil war before the assassination are not in the chronology, so they are not given numerals here. The dated fact is the death. On 15 March 44 BCE he was assassinated in Rome. The killers included men who had held office with him and men who said they were defending the Republic.

The defence failed. Killing the dominant man did not return Rome to a stable competition among equals. The political class split again, and the men who claimed to have saved the Republic were drawn into further wars. We do not name or date those fields. The result, which the next locked date records, is that the killers did not become the government. A republic is not restored by removing one man if provinces remain long commands, armies look to a general for their reward, and the city’s offices can be bundled by whoever wins the next round.

The assassination was not the end of Roman power, and not the emancipation of the enslaved, who do not enter the killers’ quarrel. It was not a return to 509 BCE, already a memory rather than a document. It was not, by itself, the principate. Octavian, Caesar’s heir, is not yet Augustus. Treating 15 March as the evening on which freedom ended collapses him, Antony and the settlement of 27 BCE into the same night. The elapsed count from 44 BCE to 27 BCE is 17 years. Those years are the mistake in the one-evening story.

:::definition The Ides, as a marker {#def-ides}
The **Ides of March** here means only the assassination of Julius Caesar on 15 March 44 BCE. It is a dated political killing. It is not a symbol of liberty restored, and it is not a symbol of liberty dying in one stroke. The Republic was not stable before that day, and it was not re-founded on that day.
:::

:::theorem The killers of Caesar did not restore a stable republic {#thm-ides}
The assassination on 15 March 44 BCE removed one dominant man. It did not restore a durable competition of annual magistrates. Civil war continued until one commander remained.
:::
:::proof
The chronology’s next marker is Actium, on 2 September 31 BCE, not a generation of ordinary consular government. The killers’ claim, that they acted for the Republic, is reported as theirs. The sequence that follows is the evidence against its success: renewed civil war, then a settlement in 27 BCE that concentrates power while keeping the old names. What would count against the theorem is evidence that, after 15 March 44 BCE and before Actium, the senate and the assemblies again assigned the great commands by the old competition and the armed rivals laid those commands down. That is not the path to Actium. A locked date on which the Republic was formally re-established and then functioned would also count against it. There is no such date.
:::

:::example Seventeen years, not one evening {#ex-seventeen}
Compute the elapsed count from 44 BCE to 31 BCE, and from 44 BCE to 27 BCE. Why does the one-evening story fail this arithmetic before anyone asks what the settlement contained?
:::solution
From 44 BCE to 31 BCE the elapsed count is 13 years. From 44 BCE to 27 BCE it is 17 years. From Actium to the settlement, 31 minus 27, it is 4 years. A story that ends freedom on the evening of 15 March 44 BCE has to pretend that Actium and the name Augustus are the same event as the assassination. They are not. Arithmetic does not say what kind of regime 27 BCE created. It says that “one evening” is the wrong shape for three dates.
:::
:::

## Actium

After the Ides, Roman commanders fought each other with the armies and provinces the conquest had created. A three-man coalition held power for a time and killed political enemies by proscription. We do not describe those killings, and we do not count the dead. The coalition did not last. One member was pushed aside. The war that remained was between Octavian and Antony. Antony’s alliance with Cleopatra let Octavian’s side speak of the war as a war against a foreign queen as well as against a Roman rival. That presentation was politics. The battle was a civil war.

Actium, on 2 September 31 BCE, is the naval battle that decided it. The chronology gives day, month and year. We do not narrate the formations, and we do not invent either commander’s words. Octavian’s forces won. He was left the last of the men who had commanded a side. The Hellenistic kingdom in Egypt, which Cleopatra ruled and which Antony had joined to his cause, did not survive as a rival of Rome. We do not date the deaths that followed the battle. Actium is the marker we are allowed: the civil war of Roman commanders had a winner.

Winning a civil war is not yet a constitution. In the days after Actium Octavian is master of the armies that mattered. He does not yet bear the name the settlement will give him. Stopping on 2 September 31 BCE and announcing that the Empire began at sea repeats the one-evening mistake, four years early. From Actium to the settlement the elapsed count is 4 years. The forms of the Republic are still the language in which power has to be described. There is still a senate, and there are still consuls. There is no longer a second Roman warlord at the head of an eastern half of the Roman world.

:::definition Actium as a result {#def-actium}
**Actium** is the naval battle of 2 September 31 BCE in which Octavian defeated Antony. It ended the last civil war between Roman commanders of that generation. It did not, on the day, confer the name Augustus, and it did not publish a new constitution. It produced a single winner.
:::

## The name, and the forms that stayed

In 27 BCE Octavian received the name Augustus. The name marks him out from every other magistrate without calling him king. Romans had built a traditional identity on the rejection of kings. A monarchy that intended to last in this city could not advertise itself in the royal word. It could advertise peace after civil war, the restoration of the public thing, and a man who was first among others. Historians call the regime the **principate**, from *princeps*, the first man. The word is a scholarly name for a monarchy that kept republican forms. It is not a judgement that the age was golden, and it is not a quotation from Augustus. He later published an account of his own acts. We treat that account as his case, not as a neutral chronicle, and we do not reproduce its sentences.

Some adjustments to his legal powers came afterwards. This atlas does not lock that later year, so we do not invent it, and the claim does not depend on it. In 27 BCE the shape is already visible. He is set apart by a name. He holds the military provinces, the commands that contain the armies, while other provinces are left to senatorial government. He can be consul, and others are consul too. The senate meets. The word *res publica* remains in use. The monarchy is not hidden by being unspoken. It is spoken in a vocabulary that denies the word “king”.

What was kept is not a painted screen with nothing behind it. Consuls continued to be elected. The senate remained the council of the political class. Provinces still had governors. Assemblies still existed. Careers were still a sequence of magistracies. A senator who never crossed the princeps could live a life that felt, office by office, like his grandfather’s. The difference is at the top, which is where civil war had been decided. The earlier generation could still hope that a rival would hold the great command next year. After the settlement the great commands are in one man’s gift. Election to the consulship remains an honour. It is no longer the road to supreme power.

Succession shows the same double face. Augustus did not take a hereditary royal title. He still had to arrange a successor, by adoption and shared powers. We do not narrate the family deaths. A monarchy that refuses the word “king” still has a monarch’s problem, the next monarch, which a republic of annual colleagues was not supposed to have.

:::theorem The monarchy kept the forms {#thm-forms}
From 27 BCE, when Octavian was called Augustus, one man held a supremacy the annual magistracy could not contain, and the offices, the senate and the name of the Republic continued. The regime was a monarchy in republican forms, not the end of those forms and not the Republic as it had been.
:::
:::proof
The chronology’s settlement is the one we are following: the institutions of the Republic were kept and emptied. Consuls, senate, provinces and the refusal of the royal name continue, set against the concentration of the military provinces and the end of civil war between rival commanders after Actium. What would count against the claim is evidence that after 27 BCE the assemblies and the senate ordinarily chose the great commands against Augustus, as the normal rule rather than as a dangerous exception. The ordinary pattern is the reverse. What would also count against it is evidence that he took the name of king and abolished the magistracies. He did not. Forms without supremacy would be the old Republic. Supremacy without forms would be the one-evening story. Neither is the settlement.
:::

One counter-argument calls 27 BCE a restoration and means it. Civil war had been terrible, and Augustus offered the old language together with a monopoly of the armies. We do not quote his preference. A restoration in which one man distributes the armies restores peace and titles, not the competition that made the Republic a republic of offices. Relief is not a constitution. The other counter-argument says the forms were a fraud and should be ignored. That fails too. Laws, provinces and careers still used the offices. An emptied institution is not an absent one. [[#thm-forms]] is deliberately double. Drop either half and the settlement becomes a cartoon: pure continuity, or pure rupture. Mary Beard’s survey is written against a single Roman lesson for later Europe. The lesson to refuse here is that 27 BCE either saved liberty or buried it in a night.

:::example An account that is not a chronicle {#ex-not-a-chronicle}
Augustus later published an account of his own acts. The following sentence is invented, and it is not taken from that account. “On the day I was named, I wrote that the Republic was fully restored and that every adult was free.”

What may you use the real account for, and what is wrong with the invented sentence?
:::solution
The real account is his case: evidence of how he wanted the settlement described, including the refusal of the royal name and the claim to have restored the public thing. It is not a neutral chronicle, and this chapter does not reproduce its sentences. The invented sentence adds two falsehoods, that the old competition had returned and that freedom extended to every adult, and it pretends to be his words. An invented quotation is refused even when labelled, except as a specimen of the mistake.
:::
:::

:::example What a consulship was, before and after {#ex-consul}
Both descriptions are schematic. Neither may be filled in with an unlocked battle-year.

- Before the civil wars, two consuls enter office. A great foreign command is a prize the senate and the election can still give to a rival next year.
- In a year after 27 BCE, two consuls enter office. The military provinces that hold the main armies are in Augustus’s hands. One consul may be Augustus himself, or a man who has his favour.

What changed, and what is false in “nothing changed, because there were still two consuls”?
:::solution
The number of consuls is what was kept. The relation of the consulship to supreme power is what changed. After the settlement the great command is not among the prizes the election distributes. “Nothing changed” is the pure-continuity cartoon. “Consuls ceased to exist” is the pure-rupture cartoon. [[#thm-forms]] requires both the surviving title and the supremacy it no longer contains. The schematic adds no battle the chronology has left out.
:::
:::

:::example The excluded, who did not lose a vote in 27 BCE {#ex-excluded}
Name three sorts of people for whom the settlement of 27 BCE was not the loss of a political equality they had previously had. Then name the group for whom it did close a competition.
:::solution
Adult citizen women did not vote or hold magistracies before 27 BCE. Enslaved people were property before that year and remained so. Most free people in the provinces were not Roman citizens and had no share in the Roman assemblies. For these three there is no republican equality to confiscate. The group that lost a real competition is the office-holding class of Roman men, the nobles who had fought over commands. Their loss is the political content of the monarchy. Calling it the end of freedom for all Romans exports a noble’s grievance onto people the Republic had already excluded.
:::

:::warning Not one evening
The usual myth picks a night — the Ides, or the night after Actium, or the sitting in 27 BCE — and says that freedom ended then. The dates will not sit on top of each other. From 44 BCE to 27 BCE is 17 years, and those years contain a further civil war and a settlement that is verbal as well as military. The other fault is the word “freedom”. The Republic had been a competition of male citizens, weighted by wealth and closed to women, the enslaved and most provincials. Ending that competition is a real political change. Calling it the fall of liberty as such lets a narrow constitution borrow a universal grief. We do not invent Augustus’s words, or the killers’ words. The markers are enough, and they are three.

## Where this leads

The forms that survived 27 BCE are the shell in which cities, armies and citizenship actually run. [[rome/imperial-society]] asks what imperial citizenship covered, from a buried town in 79 CE to the grant of 212 CE, and it refuses a golden age. The offices that were emptied are the ones defined in [[rome/republic]]. The people who remained property are the institution of [[rome/slavery-and-conquest]]. How a label can hide a claim is the question of [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- Caesar was killed on 15 March 44 BCE. The killers did not restore a stable republic. Civil war followed. We do not date the battles the chronology leaves out.
- Actium, on 2 September 31 BCE, left Octavian the winner. It was not yet the name Augustus. The elapsed count from the battle to the settlement is 4 years.
- In 27 BCE Octavian was called Augustus. He did not take the name of king. Consuls, senate and the republican name continued. Supreme military power did not continue as an open prize.
- The claim is double: the monarchy kept the forms, and the forms no longer organised the supremacy. Pure continuity and pure rupture are both cartoons.
- From 44 BCE to 27 BCE is 17 years. “Freedom ended on one evening” fails the arithmetic. Most adults had not held that freedom.
- The principate is a historian’s name for this monarchy in republican dress. It is not a golden age, and it is not a quotation from Augustus.
- A further adjustment of his powers is often discussed. This atlas does not lock that year. The settlement of 27 BCE already supports the claim.
:::
