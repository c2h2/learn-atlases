What did imperial citizenship cover?

After 27 BCE the monarchy kept the names of the Republic and held the supremacy those names no longer filled. This chapter does not ask whether Augustus had been sincere. It asks what that supremacy governed, and what a grant of citizenship did inside it. Cities and their local elites did much of the governing. The army was a profession and a political fact. A map of greatest extent is a choice of measure, not a natural outline, and the death of Trajan in 117 CE is the usual moment at which that choice is sprung. In 212 CE the Constitutio Antoniniana extended Roman citizenship very widely. It did not free enslaved people, and it did not make the free equal to one another.

None of this is a golden age. The word **principate** names a monarchy in republican dress. It is not a compliment. Peace in some provinces, for some decades, is a real pattern. It is not a reason to forget slavery, the frontier, or the narrowness of honour inside a citizen body. Greg Woolf’s *Rome: An Empire’s Story* treats the empire as institutions and local deals, not as a map that filled itself in. We will not give a population of the empire. No such figure is locked.

## Cities, elites and the army

Roman power in the provinces did not work as a clerk in a capital writing to every village. For most free people it worked as a city. A town had magistrates of its own, a council of local notables, festivals, quarrels about land and debt, and a relationship with the governor. The councillors, often called decurions in the west, were the local rich. They were expected to pay for buildings, games and grain in a shortage, and they were honoured for doing it. Scholarship calls that exchange euergetism: public spending by the wealthy in return for status. The name is a label for a pattern, not a quotation from an inscription. The people who sat on the council were not the people who paved the street, and the paved street did not make them equal.

Local elites collected, mediated and sometimes resisted. A governor who wanted taxes, order and recruits needed them. They needed Roman recognition, Roman law for their property, and protection against rivals. Woolf’s local deal is this bargain, repeated in cities that were not copies of one another. Greek cities in the east had long civic traditions. Western towns were often newer, sometimes planted under Roman influence, sometimes growing around a fort or a road. An empire of cities is a network of bargains, thicker in some regions than in others, and dependent on people who were not sent from Rome.

The army has to be described before citizenship can mean anything. Under the principate the legions are no longer the seasonal levy of a citizen militia. Service is long. It is paid. For those who survive, it ends with a discharge and a grant that may include citizenship for a man who did not already have it. No wage, no length of service in a fake precision, and no number of legions is stated. None of those quantities is locked. The army is a profession. Beside the citizen legions stand auxiliary units recruited from non-citizens, often given citizenship at discharge. The fighting force is both a citizen body and a machine for making citizens, one cohort at a time, long before 212 CE.

It is also a political fact. The supremacy of 27 BCE rests on the armies. When a princeps dies without a succession the armies will accept, the armies can make a princeps. Civil war in the principate is not a return to the Republic. It is a fight over who occupies the supremacy, conducted by professional soldiers whose pay and discharge depend on the winner. We do not narrate those wars, and we do not date the ones the chronology leaves out. A history of citizenship that talks only about laws, and never about the men with swords, has missed half the institution.

Frontiers belong with the army, and they are not a moral border. A line on a modern map, drawn where forts were thickest, is a later drawing. On the ground a frontier was a zone of forts, roads, allied kings, patrols and markets. Sometimes there was a wall. We do not date any particular wall, because this atlas does not lock the year. Roman power did not stop at a ditch, and the peoples beyond a ditch were not a single barbarism. For an auxiliary, citizenship was a legal reward at the end of service, not a seminar in identity.

:::definition Imperial citizenship before the general grant {#def-spread}
Before 212 CE, **Roman citizenship** is a legal status spreading by birth to citizen parents, by grant to individuals and communities, by manumission, and by discharge from auxiliary service. It is not a modern nationality, and it is not a description of every free inhabitant. A citizen man and a citizen woman do not have the same public life. A citizen and a free non-citizen do not have the same law. An enslaved person is not a citizen.
:::

:::theorem Cities and the army, not a map that filled itself, did the governing {#thm-deals}
Under the principate, most free people met Roman rule through a city and its elite, and met Roman force through a professional army. The empire is a network of those institutions. It is not the automatic painting-in of a map, and it is not the old assemblies of Rome writ large.
:::
:::proof
Local councils, local magistracies, benefactions and the governor’s dependence on them are the civic evidence, together with long-service legions and auxiliaries in frontier zones. Woolf’s account of local deals matches that evidence without requiring one civic culture. What would count against the claim is evidence that ordinary provincial government was done by Roman assemblies voting on each town’s business, or that the army remained a short-term militia of Italian citizens with no role in making emperors. Neither is the principate. A locked population showing that “most free people” is a fantasy of scale would also count against it. We have no population. The claim stays structural on purpose.
:::

:::example A councillor and a veteran, as types {#ex-two-careers}
The pair is invented and labelled as invented. It has no headcount.

- A decurion paves a street and expects a statue.
- A non-citizen soldier finishes auxiliary service and receives Roman citizenship on discharge, before 212 CE.

How are both inside [[#thm-deals]], and why is neither “the empire” on his own?
:::solution
The decurion is the local deal: civic honour, private wealth, public building, a town governing itself within Roman supremacy. The veteran is the army as a profession and as a gate into citizenship. Neither man is the empire. The decurion does not control the frontier, and the veteran does not govern the town he may never live in. A map shaded one colour would hide both, and a story that used either as a typical Roman would hide the enslaved people in neither honour. The schematic proves a distinction. It does not prove how many statues were set up, or how many auxiliaries lived to discharge.
:::

## A town buried

In 79 CE Vesuvius erupted. Pompeii and Herculaneum were buried. The event is a disaster with a date, not a moral with a date. The towns are evidence of a kind we rarely have: streets, houses, workshops, graffiti, and the remains of people and animals caught in the eruption. A Campanian town had elected local officers, a forum, temples, richer houses and poorer ones, and a labour force that included enslaved people. That is a city of the sort [[#thm-deals]] describes, frozen in an ordinary year that then stopped.

The uses we refuse matter as much as the uses we allow. Pompeii is not a moral. Some writers have treated the burial as a punishment of a wicked resort. A volcano does not select the wicked, and the chronology calls the event an eruption, not a judgement. Pompeii is also not a sample from which to compute the population of the empire. Two towns on the bay of Naples are not a frame for Spain, Egypt and the Danube. Any number offered as “how many died”, or as an imperial total extrapolated from the ruins, is a number this atlas will not use. Pompeii is one Italian municipality, unusually well preserved. A Greek city, a frontier settlement and a Nile village are not Pompeii with the ash removed.

What the burial can do is keep citizenship concrete. In 79 CE a municipality in Italy is full of people of different legal statuses in the same streets: town citizens, Roman citizens, enslaved people, and the recently freed. You cannot count them from the chronology. You can see that a shared catastrophe is not a shared status. The ash did not enfranchise anyone, and it did not make the principate a golden age that happened to stop, for two towns, in the middle of an ordinary year.

The elapsed count from 27 BCE to 79 CE crosses the change of era. From year A BCE to year B CE the count is A plus B minus 1, because there is no year 0. The year after 1 BCE is 1 CE. From the settlement to the eruption the count is 27 plus 79 minus 1, which is 105 years. Adding the numerals and stopping, at 106, invents a year 0. [[sources/time-and-names]] teaches the rule. A date in the principate is not allowed to float free of the settlement that opened it.

:::definition What Pompeii is evidence for {#def-pompeii}
**Pompeii**, with Herculaneum, is a town buried by the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE. It is evidence for the fabric and the statuses of one Italian municipality. It is not a moral example, not a census of the empire, and not a type of every provincial town.
:::

:::example The count across the missing year {#ex-no-zero}
Compute the elapsed count from 27 BCE to 79 CE. Show the wrong total that appears if a year 0 is inserted by adding the numerals and stopping. Which total does the rule require?
:::solution
The rule from year A BCE to year B CE is A plus B minus 1. Here that is 27 plus 79 minus 1, which is 105 years. The addition 27 plus 79 equals 106 only if a year 0 sits between 1 BCE and 1 CE and is counted. There is no such year. The elapsed count from 1 BCE to 1 CE is itself one year, which is 1 plus 1 minus 1. The chronology requires 105, not 106. Those years are the distance from Augustus’s name to a buried town. They are not a population and not a judgement.
:::

## The measure of an empire

Trajan died in 117 CE. In the years before that death, Roman armies had conquered in Dacia, north of the Danube, and had advanced far in the east. Some of the newest eastern positions were not kept. This atlas does not lock a separate year for the Dacian wars or the eastern campaign, so we do not invent those brackets. We use 117 CE as the chronology gives it: the death of an emperor, and the usual moment at which people draw a map and call it the greatest extent.

A map of greatest extent depends on what is counted. A province held for generations is not the same object as a territory occupied in a late campaign and soon abandoned. An allied kingdom, still ruled by its own king, is not a province. A desert patrol is not a civic territory. If “extent” means the furthest march under Trajan, the map is large and brief. If it means provinces administered through the second century, the map is smaller, and 117 CE is a poor summary of it, because the peak of the march and the shape of administration are different facts. Historians disagree about which measure deserves the phrase. The disagreement is a reason to state the measure, not a reason to print one outline as nature.

“Greatest” also slides towards “best”, and then the year becomes the climax of a golden age. The chronology does not say that. It says Trajan died in 117 CE, and that a map depends on the measure. Before and after that death the same institutions are in place: cities, a professional army, slavery, and a citizenship that is still a privilege. Extent is a geographical word. It is not a mark for happiness, and it is not a population.

From 79 CE to 117 CE the elapsed count is 38 years. A buried town and a dead emperor are different kinds of event. From 117 CE to 212 CE the elapsed count is 95 years. The grant is not Trajan’s policy, and it is not the meaning of his frontier. The three dates on one line refuse to explain each other.

:::theorem Greatest extent is a measure, not a fact of nature {#thm-extent}
The death of Trajan in 117 CE can be used to draw a large Roman map. Which map is the largest depends on what is counted as Roman. No one outline is the empire’s natural shape, and the outline is not a golden age.
:::
:::proof
Dacian conquest and an eastern advance are real, and so is the later abandonment of some eastern positions. “Furthest march” and “durable provinces” come apart. Allied kingdoms make “shaded on a map” come apart from “governed like a province”. The chronology states the dependence on the measure. What would count against the claim is a single official survey, locked to 117 CE, that later historians merely read off. There is no such survey here. A population or an index of welfare tied to that year would also count against it. None is locked. The death remains a death. The map remains an argument.
:::

## The grant of 212 CE

In 212 CE the Constitutio Antoniniana, associated with Caracalla, extended Roman citizenship very widely. The English “grant of citizenship” can sound like a statute of equality. It was an extension of a legal status. Before it, citizenship was already spreading by the routes in [[#def-spread]]. After it, the free population is, in the broad intention of the measure, a citizen population. The chronology’s care is the right care. The extension is very wide. It does not make every inhabitant equal in practice. Enslaved people remain enslaved.

The text is a fragment and a report, not a law we can quote. A Greek copy survives in pieces, and later jurists describe the effect. We paraphrase as the chronology does, and we do not invent the missing clauses. One argument among historians is that the grant had a fiscal motive: some citizen taxes, including taxes on inheritances, would then fall on more people. No rate is locked, so no rate is stated. A fiscal motive, if accepted, does not make the legal result fictitious. People used the status in court. Local rank did not disappear because the imperial status had widened.

What the grant did not do is the centre of the chapter. It did not abolish slavery. It did not equalise women and men: a free woman could be a citizen and still be shut out of office and of the army. It did not equalise rich and poor. A decurion and a citizen labourer did not share a council seat. It did not revive the Republican assemblies as the government of the empire, and it did not touch the army’s power to make emperors. It did not open a golden age. The bundle of crises in [[rome/later-empire]] begins in 235 CE, an elapsed count of 23 years later. The grant neither causes that bundle nor shields the empire against it.

Legal citizenship is not social equality. Sharing Roman legal forms is a real change for people who had been outside them. Social equality would have ended slavery, the decurions’ monopoly of honour, and the sexual limit on office. None of those ended in 212 CE.

One counter-argument calls the grant empty because practice stayed unequal, or because the motive was tax. Emptiness is the wrong word. A status that changes which law you use, which tax you owe and which status your children inherit is limited, not fictitious. Another counter-argument treats 212 CE as the birth of a homogeneous nation. Local languages, cults and hierarchies continue. Citizenship is a layer. What would count against the claim is evidence that the edict freed the enslaved, collapsed local rank, or handed the provinces back to Republican assemblies. The principate after 212 CE is still a monarchy, a slave society, a network of cities and an army.

:::theorem The grant of 212 CE widens citizenship and does not equalise {#thm-grant}
The Constitutio Antoniniana extended Roman citizenship very widely to free inhabitants. Enslaved people remained enslaved. Legal citizenship was not social equality, and it was not the restoration of republican government by assemblies.
:::
:::proof
The chronology states the extension and the limit together: citizenship is extended very widely, it does not make every inhabitant equal in practice, and enslaved people remain enslaved. Juristic description and the fragmentary copy support an effect of that width without a text we may quote. A fiscal motive may explain why a ruler widened the status. It does not show that the status was fictitious. What would count against the claim is a clause, or a practice immediately afterwards, that emancipated the enslaved as a class or equalised office, wealth and sex. No such clause is in the chronology. What would also count against it is evidence that the grant was never applied. The spread of citizen status in the legal record after 212 CE is the application.
:::

:::example Before and after, without a census {#ex-before-after}
The case is schematic. Before 212 CE one free man in a provincial city is a Roman citizen by birth and another is a free non-citizen. After the grant, both are citizens. An enslaved man in the same street is enslaved on both dates. A citizen woman is a citizen on both dates and holds no magistracy on either.

Which changes are real, and which sentence must you not write?
:::solution
The real change is the free non-citizen’s entry into Roman citizenship. That can matter in court, in tax and in his children’s status. The citizen by birth does not become more of a citizen. The enslaved man does not become free. The citizen woman does not gain a magistracy. The city does not gain a Roman assembly.

The forbidden sentence is that in 212 CE everyone became equal. It fails for the enslaved man, for the woman, for the decurion who still sits above both free men, and for the absence of any population against which “everyone” could be checked. [[#thm-grant]] allows a wide legal change and forbids equating it with equality.
:::
:::

:::example Three dots, three kinds of event {#ex-three-kinds}
Set 79 CE, 117 CE and 212 CE in order. Compute the elapsed count from the eruption to the death, and from the death to the grant. Why may none of these events be used as the meaning of the others?
:::solution
The order is 79, then 117, then 212. From the eruption to Trajan’s death the elapsed count is 38 years. From the death to the grant it is 95 years. The eruption buries two towns. The death ends a reign and tempts a map. The grant changes a legal status for free people and not for the enslaved. A buried town does not explain a frontier. A frontier does not free anyone. A citizenship law does not make the outline a fact of nature, and it does not make the principate golden. The counts keep the events apart.
:::

## Where this leads

A wide citizenship inside a monarchy is still a monarchy, and it is still exposed to the bundle that begins in 235 CE. [[rome/later-empire]] takes that bundle, Diocletian, Constantine and the convention of 476 CE. The slavery the grant did not touch is the institution of [[rome/slavery-and-conquest]]. The forms the principate inherited are the settlement of [[rome/end-of-the-republic]]. A single outline of “Rome at its height” hides a measure, which is why it fails in the same way a single outline of Europe fails in [[sources/naming-europe]].

:::summary
- Under the principate, cities and local elites do much of the governing. The empire is a network of deals, not a map that filled itself in.
- The army is a profession: long service, auxiliaries as well as citizen legions, discharge as one route into citizenship. Armies can also make a princeps. No wage and no headcount are stated.
- Frontiers are zones of forts, roads and allies. A line on a modern map is a later drawing, not a moral edge.
- In 79 CE Vesuvius buried Pompeii and Herculaneum. The towns are evidence of one municipality. They are not a moral and not a census.
- Trajan died in 117 CE. Greatest extent depends on the measure: furthest march, durable province and allied kingdom are different. The map is not a golden age.
- In 212 CE the Constitutio Antoniniana extended citizenship very widely. Enslaved people stayed enslaved. A woman could be a citizen without holding office. Legal status is not social equality.
- From 27 BCE to 79 CE the elapsed count is 105 years, not 106. There is no year 0.
:::

:::exercise A local deal is not an assembly {level=3}
A reader says: “After 212 CE every city was Rome in miniature, because everyone was a citizen and the local council was the Republic’s assembly continued.” Using [[#thm-deals]] and [[#thm-grant]], answer. Include one thing a local council did, and one thing it was not.
