What was the Republic, once its birthday story is set aside?

Later Romans told a clean story. Kings had ruled the city. The last of them was driven out, and the public thing, the *res publica*, began. They placed that change in 509 BCE. The year is a traditional date. It is not a minute we can read from a meeting held at the time, and it is not the birth of a modern republic. What we can study is a set of institutions: magistrates with short terms, a senate of men who had held those magistracies, and assemblies of citizens. Around those offices sat a much larger population that did not share them. Citizen women did not vote. Enslaved people were not citizens. Most free people in Italy were allies or subjects of other statuses, not voters in Rome.

From that traditional birthday to the first war with Carthage is a long stretch of Italian expansion. The elapsed count from 509 BCE to 264 BCE is 245 years. A birthday does not explain a system that has already spent that long making war, planting settlements and binding communities by treaty. The Republic, in the sense used here, is the republic of those offices and of that Italian power. The wars with Carthage begin in [[rome/slavery-and-conquest]]. They are not the start of Roman conquest.

A city that elects commanders is not, by that fact, a democracy of all adults. Athens, after the citizenship law of 451 BCE, already shows a narrow citizen body: adult men with two citizen parents, as [[greece/athenian-democracy]] sets out. Rome is not Athens under another name, and it is not a nation-state, any more than a polis was in [[greece/poleis]]. We do not derive modern republics from 509 BCE. We ask what the offices were, whom they left out, and what kind of power in Italy they already were.

## A date that is a tradition

The traditional start is part of a foundation legend. Later writers, working centuries afterwards, presented the end of kingship as the beginning of liberty. In paraphrase only: the king had become a tyrant, leading men expelled him, and two magistrates took the place of one life-long ruler. We do not quote those writers. A paraphrase of a moral story is not a document from the year it claims to describe. The first Olympic games are placed in 776 BCE by later tradition, and Solon’s archonship in 594 BCE by later tradition, as [[greece/poleis]] says. A traditional date can be taught. Repetition does not turn it into a contemporary record.

Why lock 509 BCE at all? Because the tradition is a fact about Roman memory. Later Romans organised their past so that liberty had a year. That organisation is evidence about them, not evidence that a constitution was promulgated in that year. [[sources/time-and-names]] is where a date is treated as a claim rather than as a neutral label. The sentence “the Republic began in 509 BCE” bundles a real contrast — historical Rome is not a monarchy of the old royal kind — with a precision the early evidence does not support.

The list of annual chief magistrates for the earliest generations is itself later. A name on that list is not the minutes of an election. Once the record is thicker, a familiar pattern appears: two senior magistrates, lesser magistrates with defined tasks, a senate, and assemblies. We describe that pattern for the Republic as it can be documented. We do not pretend it was photographed in 509 BCE. The stretch down to 264 BCE is Italian power, and it is also the later story of the struggle of the orders, which we will not decorate with statistics.

:::definition Republic {#def-republic}
The **Republic**, in this chapter, is the Roman system of limited magistracies, a senate and assemblies of citizens, as opposed to rule by a king. The Latin *res publica* means the public thing. It does not mean a democracy of all adults, and it does not mean a modern republic with a wide franchise and a written constitution of the later kind. The English word “republic” is a translation, not a claim that later republics were copied from this one.
:::

:::theorem The year 509 BCE is a tradition, not a documented foundation {#thm-tradition}
509 BCE is the traditional date for the start of the Republic. It records how later Romans placed the end of kingship. It is not a contemporary document of a change of constitution, and it does not found any modern republic.
:::
:::proof
The date comes from later Roman historical writing, not from a record made at the time. The contrast those writers cared about is real in a weaker form: in the period we can document, Rome is governed through magistrates, senate and assemblies, and Romans distinguished that arrangement from kingship. What would count against the claim is a contemporary record that these offices were established in 509 BCE as a constitution we can still read. We do not have one. What would also count against it is the use of that year, inside this chronology, as the legal source of a modern republic. It is not used so. The year remains a marker of memory.
:::

:::example The elapsed count from the tradition to the first Carthaginian war {#ex-elapsed-509}
The traditional start is 509 BCE. The First Punic War begins in 264 BCE. Take the elapsed count for two BCE years: subtract the later numeral from the earlier. Do not add a year to make the span inclusive. How many years is the elapsed count?
:::solution
From 509 BCE to 264 BCE the elapsed count is 509 minus 264, which is 245 years. An inclusive tally of year-names would be a different question. The chronology’s own example, from 800 CE to 843 CE, is 43 years, a difference and not an inclusive tally. The 245 years are why the birthday and the war are far apart. The institutions, not the birthday, fill the stretch.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a translation. “In 509 BCE the senate wrote a constitution of equal votes for every adult, and the kings departed because that text had been read aloud.”

What would it take to promote this sentence into evidence, and what can it be used for while it stays labelled as invented?
:::solution
Promotion would need a source created close enough to the events, identifiable as that source, and actually saying this. We would still ask what the source could know and what it wanted. The invented sentence fails those tests. It also smuggles in two claims this chapter rejects: that 509 BCE is a documented legislative moment, and that the Republic was a democracy of all adults. Left labelled as invented, it is only a specimen of a bad habit. A birthday story grows extra clauses — a written constitution, equal votes, a public reading — that the tradition does not authorise. It is not evidence about Roman opinions.
:::

## Magistrates, senate and assemblies

The institutions are the subject. They are knowable, in outline, for the middle and late Republic, from the way Romans sued, voted, commanded and argued about legality. They are not knowable as a single statute dated 509 BCE.

A **magistrate** is an office-holder. The senior pair are the consuls: two men, elected, holding power together for a year, each able to act and each a check on the other. One man’s command is limited by time and by a colleague. Beside them are other magistracies. Praetors often dealt with law and command, quaestors with money, aediles with the city’s fabric and markets, censors with the citizen lists and the senate’s membership. Duties shift. We will not freeze one chart and call it the original constitution. Power is carved into temporary offices, and having held office marks a man as part of the political class. A small number of rich families supplied consuls again and again. A command could be extended after the year. The idea of the office is limitation. The practice is a career for the few.

:::definition Magistrate {#def-magistrate}
A **magistrate** is a Roman office-holder chosen for a limited term, typically a year, often with a colleague who holds the same kind of power. The consuls are the senior pair. A magistrate is not a king, and a set of magistrates is not a modern separation of powers. The word does not cover the tribunes of the plebs in the strict sense. The tribune was an office of the plebeian organisation, powerful enough to block a magistrate.
:::

:::definition Senate {#def-senate}
The **senate** is a council, made up mainly of men who had held magistracies. Its advice on war, peace, finance and commands directed the Republic in the period we can document. It is not a parliament elected by all adults. Membership marked entry into the career of office, and the censors could revise the list. We do not pretend that the later senate was already sitting, with the same habits, in 509 BCE.
:::

Assemblies are where the word “democracy” is most often smuggled in. Citizens met to elect magistrates, to pass laws and to decide on war and peace. They did not meet as a crowd in which every person cast an equal vote counted head by head. They voted in groups, and the groups were not equal. In the assembly that elected the consuls, the grouping followed wealth, so that richer citizens’ groups carried the decision. A poor citizen could be a voter and still be a light one. The assemblies met in Rome. A citizen far from the city could not use a right he could not reach. We will not invent a figure for how many stayed home. There was more than one assembly. An assembly of citizens voting by unequal groups is not the rule of all adults, and it is not the rule of all free men in Italy.

The struggle of the orders is how later Romans told an earlier conflict. In that telling, citizens split between patricians, a hereditary group that claimed the senior offices and certain priesthoods, and plebeians, the rest. Plebeians included poor men and, as the story goes on, rich families still shut out of the patriciate. Later writers described withdrawals from the city, the creation of tribunes who could veto a magistrate, the writing down of law, and at last the opening of the consulship to plebeian families. Out of that, on their account, came a nobility of office: families, patrician and plebeian, that had held the senior magistracies and meant to keep them.

We will not retell those bargains as a documented social history. We have no statistics of wealth or votes for the early conflict, and no year this atlas locks, so we invent neither. We do not quote the Twelve Tables. Later Romans treated an early written code as a gain for people who had been subject to law they could not read. That is as much as we take. A modern sentence presented as one of its clauses would be an invented quotation.

What we carry forward is institutional. The tribune of the plebs can stop a magistrate. In 133 BCE Tiberius Gracchus uses that office in a crisis of land and empire, which belongs to [[rome/slavery-and-conquest]]. The political class we can see is a nobility of office, not a closed patriciate and not a democracy of all adults. Women, enslaved people and non-citizens were not the parties to the bargains.

:::theorem A republic of offices is not a democracy of all adults {#thm-not-democracy}
Election of magistrates by citizen assemblies does not make the Republic a democracy of all adults. Voters are male citizens, grouped so that wealth weighs heavily in the election of consuls, and they must be present in Rome. Women, enslaved people and the non-citizen population of Italy are outside those offices.
:::
:::proof
The assemblies, the qualifications for office and the legal status of women, enslaved people and Italian allies are the evidence, known from Roman public law in the documented Republic. Athens supplies a comparison: after 451 BCE an Athenian citizen in the democracy was an adult man with two citizen parents. Rome draws its own line and also weights citizen votes unequally. What would count against the claim is evidence that adult women, enslaved people, or the allied population at large voted and held the consulship. There is none. What would also count against it is a Roman law, dated in this chronology, that established equal adult suffrage. No such law is recorded here.
:::

:::example Three people, one household, before the Carthaginian war {#ex-household}
The case is a schematic, not a biography. Imagine a household in Italy in the generation before 264 BCE.

- Marcus is an adult male Roman citizen, registered among the richer classes, able to travel to Rome.
- Lucia, his sister, is a freeborn Roman citizen. She does not command the household’s votes.
- A woman enslaved in the house was captured in an Italian war.

Who, on [[#thm-not-democracy]], is inside the republic of offices as a voter and a potential magistrate? What does the sorting fail to tell you?
:::solution
Marcus is inside the circle of potential voters and, given property, of potential candidates. His consular vote is a group vote weighted towards wealth. Lucia is a citizen and is not a voter or a magistrate. The enslaved woman is not a citizen. Capture in war is already one route into slavery, the subject of the next chapter.

The sorting does not tell us what any of them thought. Silence in the public record shows who was asked to speak, not that a person had no mind. Three invented people are a diagram of a rule, not a sample, and not a count of such households.
:::
:::

## Italy before the war with Carthage

Expansion in Italy comes before the Punic wars. Skipping that fact makes Roman conquest look like a sudden overseas accident. From 509 BCE to 264 BCE is 245 years. At the end of that count Rome is already the leading power in peninsular Italy. The First Punic War, from 264 BCE to 241 BCE, is what that power does next, across the water.

The means were war, settlement and treaty, described without invented battle-years. Defeated communities lost land. Rome and the Latins planted settlements, new communities with a legal tie to Rome, sometimes of full citizens, sometimes of Latin status. A settlement of this kind is not a province and not a modern overseas colony. Provinces belong to the next chapter. Latin status was intermediate: not full Roman citizenship, not foreign. Allied communities, the *socii*, kept their own cities and owed troops. Some communities received Roman citizenship without the vote. Citizenship was a bundle — marriage, trade, appeal, and, for adult men who had the vote, the assemblies — and Rome could grant it in part.

The allies fought in Roman wars. A consul’s army is the city’s force plus the force of Italy as Rome had organised it. When Rome commits in 264 BCE to a war that lasts until 241 BCE, that alliance is the machine. We do not narrate the war here. The machine was not built by it.

A king from the Greek world after Alexander fought in southern Italy before that war. This atlas does not lock a year for his campaign, so we do not invent one. [[greece/alexander]] ends with the death in Babylon in 323 BCE and with Hellenistic kingdoms, not with one surviving empire. Rome enters the dated wars as an Italian power in that world. The struggle of the orders, told later, sits in the same undated stretch as the Italian wars. Domestic liberty and conquest are not on separate clocks. Enslavement of captives is already a Roman practice. We will not number those captives, and we will not pretend slavery arrives only in 264 BCE. The next chapter’s claim is that after the Carthaginian wars it is structural.

:::proposition Italian hegemony comes before the Punic wars {#thm-italy}
By 264 BCE Rome already directed an alliance of Italian communities, built by war, settlement and treaty. The First Punic War is not the beginning of Roman conquest.
:::
:::proof
The chronology places the traditional start in 509 BCE and the first war with Carthage in 264 BCE, and it gives no earlier locked battle-year to do the work of the Italian wars. The alliance is visible when that war begins: Latin and citizen settlements, allies owing troops, grades of status short of the full vote. Rome could not have fought the war the next chapter describes without that system. What would count against the claim is evidence that in 264 BCE Rome was still one Latin city among equals, with no hierarchy of allies and no resettled land. The war that then begins is not the war of such a city. Overseas provinces are new. Conquest is not.
:::

:::example Where 323 BCE sits between the two Roman dates {#ex-323}
Alexander dies in Babylon in 323 BCE. Using elapsed counts for BCE years, how many years separate 509 BCE from 323 BCE, and 323 BCE from 264 BCE? What does the pair show, and what does it not show?
:::solution
From 509 BCE to 323 BCE the elapsed count is 509 minus 323, which is 186 years. From 323 BCE to 264 BCE it is 323 minus 264, which is 59 years. The traditional Roman birthday lies well before Alexander’s death. The first war with Carthage lies after it. The Mediterranean world Rome is about to fight in has already passed through the breakup of his conquests into kingdoms. The counts do not date the southern Italian campaign of the later Greek king, and they do not make 323 BCE a Roman event. They stop a story in which one Roman birthday explains both the institutions and the later empire.
:::

:::warning Not the birthday of modern liberty
A common story takes 509 BCE as the start of republican freedom and draws a line to modern republics, as if a later franchise and a later written constitution were already in the Roman offices. The year is a later tradition, not a document. The offices excluded most adults, weighted the votes of those who had them, and belonged to a city already building power in Italy by war and alliance. Mary Beard’s survey of Rome is written against a single Roman lesson for later Europe. That refusal starts here, with the birthday.
:::

A tradition is not a fiction with no object. In the documented period Rome is governed through offices, and Romans treated kingship as what they had rejected. Some historians think a real change away from monarchy happened, even if the year and the heroic details are later shaping. That possibility does not damage [[#thm-tradition]]. The claim is not that kingship lasted until some secret year. It is that 509 BCE is not a documented foundation and not a source for modern republics. A contemporary text that fixed the offices in that year would count against the claim. We do not have one. The contrast with kingship can stand without a birthday, without statistics of the struggle of the orders, and without a quotation from the Twelve Tables.

:::history
Later tradition placed the start of the Republic in 509 BCE. The First Punic War began in 264 BCE. The offices this chapter describes fill the stretch between a birthday story and a war with Carthage, and they are not a democracy of all adults.
:::

## Where this leads

The Italian alliance is the machine the next war uses. [[rome/slavery-and-conquest]] puts it into the wars with Carthage, then into provinces and slavery, and then into the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus in 133 BCE. The world those wars meet is the one left by [[greece/alexander]]. The comparison that keeps “democracy” honest is [[greece/athenian-democracy]]. How a traditional year differs from a documented one is the work of [[sources/time-and-names]].

:::summary
- 509 BCE is the traditional start of the Republic. It is a later tradition, not a contemporary document, and not the origin of modern republics.
- The Republic we can describe is limited magistracies, a senate of former magistrates, and assemblies of citizens voting in unequal groups.
- The struggle of the orders is a later telling of an earlier conflict. It is not a social history with statistics, and it does not end in a democracy of all adults.
- Voters were adult men, present in Rome, with wealth weighing in the consular assembly. Women, enslaved people and most free people in Italy were outside the offices.
- By 264 BCE Rome directed an Italian alliance built by war, settlement and treaty. Conquest does not begin with the First Punic War.
- The elapsed count from 509 BCE to 264 BCE is 245 years. Alexander’s death in 323 BCE falls inside that stretch.
- The tribunate credited to the struggle of the orders returns in 133 BCE, in the next chapter, as an office used in a crisis of land and empire.
:::
