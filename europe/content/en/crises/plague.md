How should a death toll be stated when the evidence will not support one number?

The fourteenth century hands us two disasters that a short chapter likes to glue onto one slide. The Great Famine begins in 1315. Plague is reported at Messina in October 1347, and it is in much of western Europe in 1348 and 1349. Further waves follow, and this atlas does not date them. Neither disaster arrives with a census of the dead. The temptation is to borrow the upper end of a regional range and announce that half of Europe died. That sentence is not a fact this course will teach. It is the mistake the dates are here to stop.

A death toll is a claim about people. It needs a population before, a population after, and a reason to trust both figures. Europe in these years has none of those three as a single object. It has regions, some of them studied from tax lists, wills, manorial accounts and narrative reports, and many of them not studied in the same way. [[sources/evidence]] already treats the plague reports of 1347 as witnesses of a disaster rather than a census. This chapter stays with that rule and asks what kind of sentence the rule still allows. The allowed sentence is a range, tied to the places where the evidence has been worked, and openly unfinished everywhere else.

## Two dates, one generation

Use 1315 as the start of the Great Famine. The chronology locks the famine as 1315–1317, in much of northern Europe. It was not a single failed afternoon. It was a run of bad harvests, with hunger and disease among people and among animals, across more than one winter. People died. How many is not a number this atlas holds, so none is printed. "Many" is an honest word here. A false precision would be a second famine, this one of evidence.

The famine is not the plague. From 1315 to 1347 the elapsed count is 32 years, because this atlas counts the span from year A to year B as B minus A, the same way it counts from 800 to 843 as 43 years. Thirty-two years is a generation. A child born in the first hungry year could be an adult when a Sicilian port reports plague, and an old person in 1347 could remember 1315. Memory does not make the two events one event. A reader who says "the crisis of the fourteenth century" as if it were a single season has erased that generation to save a phrase.

What the famine does change is the background against which later losses are read. A population that has already been hit by hunger is not the same as a population in an ordinary year. That sentence is a caution, not a sum. We cannot subtract the unnamed dead of 1315–1317 from the unnamed dead of 1348 and call the remainder a result. Subtraction needs integers. We do not have the integers. The honest link is qualitative: scarcity was already a political and religious problem before plague was reported, and lordship already lived off rents and labour. [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]] is the chapter that says what a manor is. This one only borrows the term. A **manor** is a unit of lordship and rent, not a European system of identical peasants. Unfreedom varied by region and by century. The famine did not standardise it.

## A report is not a census

:::definition Report and regional estimate {#def-report}
A **report**, in this chapter, is a dated witness that plague is present, not a count of the dead. A **regional estimate** is a historian's range for the fall in numbers in a place where the evidence has been studied. It is not a European total, and it does not describe an unstudied place.
:::

In October 1347 plague is reported at Messina. Hold the verb. Reported means that news of the disaster is what the sources give us, at a port, in that month. It does not mean that Messina is the biological origin, and it does not mean that a clerk walked the island and wrote down every death. Earlier stages, along trade routes and in the Black Sea world, are argued in the scholarship. This atlas does not lock a year or a place for them before Messina, so this lesson gives none. The year 1346 is locked here for Crécy, in the next chapter, and must not be quietly reused as a plague date. The first plague year you may treat as settled is 1347, and only as a report.

In 1348 and 1349 the plague is in much of western Europe. The elapsed count from 1347 to 1349 is 2 years. That width is a spread in the sources, not a mortality rate. A disease can cross a lot of ground along roads and sea lanes and still kill very different fractions of the people who meet it. Density and routes matter, which is why [[christendom/towns-and-learning]] already connects towns and trade to the plagues of this course without a statistical claim it cannot source. Ports, river towns and fairs are why a report at Messina is news elsewhere in 1348 and 1349. "Soon" is those two years, not a claim that every village was struck in the same week.

Further waves follow. That sentence is part of the locked chronology, and it has no years attached. You will meet dates for later outbreaks in other books. Do not import them. A wave whose year is not locked cannot pad a total, and it cannot be narrated as a known fraction removed on schedule. Later waves are a reason to distrust any sentence that treats 1348 as the whole demographic history of the century, and a reason not to invent the missing dates in order to look complete.

An earlier plague is already in the atlas. In 541 plague arrives in Egypt and spreads, and that passage also refuses a death total. The refusal is a rule about evidence. It is not a special squeamishness about the fourteenth century. [[after-rome/byzantium]] sits nearer that earlier outbreak. What the two reports share is the shape of a careful sentence: arrival, spread, no continental integer.

:::theorem No single European death total {#thm-no-total}
The plague reported at Messina in October 1347, and present in much of western Europe in 1348 and 1349, cannot be given one European death total from the evidence this atlas uses. In many of the regions where the evidence is studied, estimates fall between about a third and about a half of the population, and some places differ.
:::
:::proof
The chronology locks the arrival, the two years of spread, and the shape of the range, and it forbids a single European total. That is the evidence the claim is allowed to lean on. Behind the range sit partial records: tax assessments that omit the exempt, court rolls that record the tenants a lord cared to list, wills that record the people who had something to bequeath, narrative reports that record what a writer found dreadful. Each of those is a real trace. None of them is Europe. Historians do not agree on a continental figure, and this lesson says so in the prose rather than in a footnote, because the disagreement is the result. What would count against [[#thm-no-total]] is a contemporaneous census of the population of Europe before and after the plague, with a known coverage and a known way of distinguishing plague deaths from other deaths. We do not have that census. A new regional study that falls outside a third to a half does not, by itself, refute the claim: the claim already says that some places differ, and it says "many", not "all". What would strain it is evidence that many of the studied regions fall outside that band, or any honest route to a single European integer. Until that route exists, "half of Europe died" is not a summary. It is a promotion of the upper end of a regional range into a continent.
:::

Read the quantifiers slowly. "Many" is not "every". "Studied" is not "inhabited". "About a third" and "about a half" are ends of a band, not two measurements of one body. "Some places differ" is a clause inside the claim, not a polite apology after it. A place whose best estimate sits near a third, a place whose best estimate sits near a half, and a place that sits outside both are all compatible with the sentence. They are not compatible with a European average dressed up as a finding. Averaging the ends of the band produces five twelfths, a precision no source recorded, and that fraction still pretends to be Europe. It is worse than either end, because it looks like a calculation.

The later name Black Death is a label. It tells a modern reader which disaster is meant. It does not add a count, and it does not turn regional estimates into a continental one. Prefer "plague" when you are stating what the reports say, and treat the later name as a label in the sense of [[sources/argument]]: a bundle that hides the claim if you let it.

## What changed, and what the deaths do not explain

Where numbers fell, labour could become scarcer and rents harder to collect on the old terms. That is a consequence worth stating, and it is not a wage rate. This atlas locks no price, no day-rate and no percentage rise in pay. The direction, in many of the places historians have been able to study, is that people who sold labour had more room to bargain, and people who lived from dues had to concede, coerce, or find new tenants. Which of those they did is local. A statute can try to freeze a wage. It cannot, by being passed, prove that the freeze worked, and it cannot speak for a kingdom whose rolls we have not read.

In England an ordinance of the plague years tried to hold wages down. A later statute is famous in English legal memory. Its year is not locked here, so it is not given. The attempt is the point: rulers answered a fall in numbers with a claim about whose bargaining was legitimate. That is evidence of anxiety at the top, not evidence that every labourer obeyed. The English Rising of 1381, also called the Peasants' Revolt, was not only peasants. From 1349 to 1381 is again 32 years. It belongs to [[crises/war-and-schism]], as a crisis of tax and lordship, not as a proof that plague caused revolt. "After" is not "because".

:::theorem Local bargains are not a later revolution {#thm-not-later}
Where the plague cut numbers, labour and rent could be renegotiated, and religious responses multiplied. Those consequences do not make the plague the cause of the Reformation or of the Renaissance, and they do not abolish the manor as a European fact.
:::
:::proof
The positive half rests on the kind of evidence manorial accounts and urban wage disputes can actually show: pressure on dues, attempts to compel labour, and a public religious life full of vows, processions and blame. The negative half rests on dates. The conventional start of the Hundred Years' War is 1337, and Crécy is 1346, both before Messina. A war that has already begun is not an effect of a plague that has not yet been reported. Humanism, as [[crises/city-states]] will define it, is a practice of Latin, schooling and chanceries, not a mood produced by death, and the chronology refuses to let the plague explain it. The theses are dated 31 October 1517. From 1349 to 1517 the elapsed count is 168 years. A gap of that size can hold influences. It cannot be skipped by a verb such as "led to". What would count against [[#thm-not-later]] is a demonstration that the renegotiation of labour took one legal form from Sicily to Scotland, or that 1517 is unintelligible unless a European death total is inserted as its cause. Neither demonstration is available. What would also count against it is evidence that unfreedom simply ended wherever numbers fell. The evidence runs the other way in some regions: lords reimposed dues. This atlas does not lock a year for those later impositions, so none is given. The scale lesson in [[sources/scale]] is the right test. A local bargain that is true does not license a national sentence, and it does not license a continental one.
:::

Religious responses need the same discipline. People processed, preached, made vows, and looked for someone to blame. In some towns, Jewish communities were attacked during the plague years. Blame is not a finding about the disease. A persecution is a fact about power and about neighbours, where it happened. It is not evidence that the victims caused the deaths. [[christendom/exclusion]] has already shown how Latin Christian law could mark Jews and Muslims as outside. The plague years did not invent that pattern. They gave it a panic to move in. This atlas locks no list of towns, no day and no count of the killed, so none is printed. Do not describe symptoms either. Clinical detail would not repair the missing census. It would only change the subject.

The counter-argument deserves a direct reply. A reader says: the range covers many studied regions, those regions include large kingdoms, and therefore Europe lost something in that band. "Include" is not "exhaust", and a band is not a point. A well-studied county can sit inside a badly known kingdom. Promoting the county to the kingdom, and the kingdom to the continent, is the scale error [[sources/scale]] is for. Refusing a total does not make the catastrophe smaller. "About a third to about a half, in many studied places, and different elsewhere" is already a terrible sentence. Adding a continental integer would not honour the dead. It would pretend to have counted them.

## A worked case, labelled invented

The brief asks for an arithmetic case so that the two ends of the range cannot be stacked by accident. Take an invented town of 600 people. It is not Messina, not a parish in the records, and not Europe. Call it invented every time you use it. If this town loses a third of its people, 200 die and 400 remain, because a third of 600 is 200. If it then loses a half of the remainder, 200 of those 400 die and 200 remain. The result after the first loss is 400. The result after the second is 200. They differ. A half of the original 600 would have been 300, which is neither 400 nor 200. The phrase "a third and then a half of the remainder" is a sequence of two different operations. It is not a translation of "about a third to about a half".

The historical range describes different studied regions, some nearer a third and some nearer a half. It does not describe one town struck by the low estimate and then by the high estimate. The remainder 200 is an answer only for this invented town. Anyone who carries 200 or 400 into a European sentence has forgotten the label. If you reverse the order, a half of 600 leaves 300, and a third of 300 leaves 200. The same finish is algebra, not a fact about successive waves. Show the steps, and then leave them in the toy.

:::example The gap from famine to report {#ex-gap}
The Great Famine starts in 1315. Plague is reported at Messina in 1347. What is the elapsed count of years from 1315 to 1347, and what may you not conclude from it?
:::solution
The elapsed count is 1347 minus 1315, which is 32. You may conclude that the famine and the report are a generation apart, so they are not one season and not one name. You may not conclude that the same people died, that one caused the other, or that 32 is a death toll. The famine's own dated span, from 1315 to 1317, is an elapsed count of 2 years, and that 2 is not a count of the dead either.
:::
:::

:::example An invented town, in two steps {#ex-town}
An invented town has 600 people. It is not a real place. It loses a third, and then it loses a half of the remainder. How many remain after each step, and why do the results differ?
:::solution
A third of 600 is 200, so 400 remain after the first step. A half of 400 is 200, so 200 remain after the second step. The results differ because 400 is not 200. A half of the original 600 would have been 300, a third figure again. None of 400, 300 or 200 is a European total. The historical phrase "about a third to about a half" is a range across studied regions, not a licence to apply both rates in sequence to one town and then to a continent.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-sentence}
The following sentence is invented. Treat it as a forgery written to be caught. "In the year 1348 half of Europe died, and from that day every lord lost his peasants and the new age began." What can you keep, and what must you refuse?
:::solution
You may keep 1348 as a real year in which plague is in much of western Europe. You must refuse "half of Europe died", because that promotes one end of a regional range to a continent. You must refuse "every lord", because a manor is local and unfreedom did not vanish on one day. You must refuse "the new age began", because [[#thm-not-later]] blocks the plague as the cause of the Renaissance or the Reformation. A chronicle's moral is not a census. Promoting this invented sentence into evidence would take a named source, a known author, and a reason to trust the count. The sentence has none of those, and it was labelled invented so that you would not go looking for them.
:::
:::

:::example Two real events, side by side {#ex-pair}
Set 1315 beside October 1347. One is the start of the Great Famine in much of northern Europe. The other is a report of plague at Messina. Which of them, if either, is a death toll?
:::solution
Neither. 1315 dates the start of a famine whose span is 1315–1317, without a locked number of deaths. October 1347 dates a report at a port, which is a witness of disaster, not a census. Putting them side by side shows a gap of 32 years and a difference of kind. The famine is a shortage across harvests. The report is news of plague. Collapsing both into "the death toll of the fourteenth century" answers a question the sources have not been asked in a form they can answer.
:::
:::

:::warning Half of Europe
The sentence people repeat is that half of Europe died. It fails because "about a half" is the upper end of a range for many studied regions, not a measurement of the continent, and because some places differ. Repeating the sentence does not make the missing census appear. It only makes a careful range sound like a cowardly version of a bold truth. The bold version is the one the evidence will not carry.
:::

:::history
In October 1347 plague is reported at Messina. In 1348 and 1349 it is in much of western Europe. The estimates historians publish for many studied regions lie between about a third and about a half of the population, and they are not a single European death total.
:::

Keep the word "about" on the fractions, and keep Europe out of the invented town. Witnesses, of the sort a collection of sources can gather, tell you what people said they saw. They do not add up to the integer the proverb wants. A place that differs from the band is already allowed by [[#thm-no-total]]. The claim would have to be rewritten only if many studied regions sat outside it, or if a real census replaced it. Hiding a difference to keep a smooth sentence would be the evasion. Using the clause that some places differ is not.

## Where this leads

The next chapter, [[crises/war-and-schism]], takes up a war whose conventional start in 1337 is already past when Messina reports plague, and a papal schism that begins in 1378. Those are crises of authority and taxation. They are not moods caused by a death toll. [[crises/city-states]] will refuse the further claim that mass death produced humanism. [[reformations/luther]] is the place for 1517, and it is not an effect you may read off a fraction. If you need the method again, [[sources/scale]] is where a true local fact is stopped from becoming a false continental one, and [[sources/evidence]] is where a report stays a report.

:::summary
- The Great Famine starts in 1315 and runs through 1317 in much of northern Europe. It has no locked death total.
- From 1315 to the report at Messina in October 1347 is 32 years. The two disasters are a generation apart.
- 1348 and 1349 are years of spread across much of western Europe. Further waves follow, without years this atlas will invent.
- In many studied regions, estimates fall between about a third and about a half, and some places differ. There is no European total.
- "Half of Europe died" promotes the top of that range to a continent. It is the sentence this chapter exists to stop.
- Labour, rents and religious blame, including attacks on some Jewish communities, are consequences. They are not the Reformation or the Renaissance, and they are not one European bargain.
- An invented town may be used to keep a third distinct from a half. Its remainders must not be carried out as a continental result.
:::

:::exercise Averaging the band {level=3}
A writer says: a third is one end, a half is the other, so Europe lost about five twelfths, the average of the two fractions. Explain why the average is not an improvement on the myth of a half. What would actually count against [[#thm-no-total]]?
:::hint
Ask whether the two fractions are two measurements of one population. Then ask what the word "many", and the clause about places that differ, already allow.
:::
:::solution
A third is 4/12 and a half is 6/12. Their average is 5/12, about five twelfths. That looks like care, and it is still a European total. The two fractions are not two readings of one continent. They are the ends of a band reported for many studied regions. Averaging the ends invents a precision no census recorded, and it erases the clause that some places differ. It is the myth of a single number in cleaner clothes. What would count against [[#thm-no-total]] is a real census of Europe before and after, or evidence that many studied regions fall outside the band. One place outside the band is already provided for. An average is not that evidence. It is arithmetic performed on the wording.
:::
:::

:::exercise A differing place {level=3}
Suppose a real region, not an invented town, is one day shown to have lost about a tenth, and another to have lost about two thirds. A critic says [[#thm-no-total]] has failed. Has it? Say what further result would force you to rewrite the claim.
:::hint
Look at the quantifier "many", and at the words "some places differ". Then separate a difference the claim already allows from a difference that would break it.
:::
:::solution
It has not failed on those two regions alone. [[#thm-no-total]] says that in many studied regions the estimates fall between about a third and about a half, and that some places differ. A tenth and two thirds are differences of the kind the second clause names. They would become a reason to rewrite the claim if they turned out to be typical, that is, if many of the studied regions sat outside the band, or if someone produced a genuine European total that replaced the range. Until then, citing one steep place and one milder place is a way of using the claim, not of breaking it. Invented towns cannot do even that much: an invented tenth is not a study. The critic's error is to treat a range with an explicit exception as if it had been a universal fraction.
:::
