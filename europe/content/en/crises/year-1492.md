How many events is 1492?

At least four, and they are not one birth of the modern world. On 2 January Granada surrenders. On 31 March the Alhambra Decree orders the Jews of Castile and Aragon to convert or leave, with a deadline of 31 July. On 3 August Columbus sails from Palos. On 12 October he makes landfall in the Caribbean. A conquest, an expulsion order, a sailing and a landfall share a court, the court of Isabella and Ferdinand. Sharing a court is why one calendar year can hold them. It is not a reason to give them one meaning. The chronology of this atlas marks the meaning of 1492 as disputed. Historians do not agree that the year is the beginning of modernity, and this chapter says so here, not in a footnote. The slogan is a label. The dates are the claims.

Two further dates keep the year from pretending to be the only dot on the line. On 1 November 1478 Sixtus IV authorises the Spanish Inquisition. On 7 June 1494 the Treaty of Tordesillas divides overseas claims between the crowns of Castile and Portugal. In May 1498 Vasco da Gama reaches Calicut, which matters here only as a maritime link and not as an Indian history. On 22 August 1485 Richard III is killed at Bosworth and Henry Tudor becomes Henry VII, an English dynastic fact on the figure so that 1492 is not the only dot, and not part of Granada, the decree or the voyage.

## Four dates, one court

:::definition Bundle year {#def-bundle}
A **bundle year** is a calendar year that later readers treat as one event. 1492 is a bundle year. The events inside it are a conquest, an order to convert or leave, and a voyage with a landfall. They share a court. They are not one birth of modernity.
:::

:::definition The Alhambra Decree {#def-decree}
The **Alhambra Decree** is the order dated 31 March 1492, commanding the Jews of Castile and Aragon to convert or leave, with a deadline of 31 July 1492. Expulsion is the outcome named for those who do not convert. The decree is not a count of people, and it is not an order aimed at the Muslim polity that surrendered on 2 January.
:::

Read the four dates as a sequence before you read them as a symbol. Granada surrenders on 2 January. The decree follows on 31 March, still in the same year, and the deadline of 31 July falls before anyone sails. Count the interval the decree itself creates. From 31 March to 30 April is 30 days, thence to 31 May is 31, thence to 30 June is 30, thence to 31 July is 31. The sum is 122 days. The deadline is not the decree. It is the end of a legal clock. From 31 July to 3 August is 3 days. The ships leave after the clock has run out. This atlas does not say who was aboard, and it forbids a number. It also forbids the quiet story in which the ships are the transport of the decree. The dates are different. The groups of people are different. From 3 August to 12 October, counted as 31 days from 3 August to 3 September, 30 days on to 3 October, and 9 days on to 12 October, is 70 days. Seventy days is a calendar interval between sailing and landfall. It is not a crew, not a fleet, and not a population waiting on a beach.

The court explains the crowding and does not cancel it. Isabella and Ferdinand hold the crowns of Castile and Aragon. This lesson does not date their marriage. The year needs the holding, not a wedding day. Two crowns are a composite monarchy, the kind of stack [[states/composite-monarchy]] will define, not a modern nation called Spain born on 2 January. A conquest can be completed, a decree sealed and a voyage licensed by the same princes and still be three policies. Contemporaries can connect them in their own minds. Historians argue about how far the court itself joined the projects. That argument is not settled by a slogan, and this lesson does not quote Columbus. A paraphrase you have not checked is not a source. Even a genuine link in a letter would be one writer's argument, not a definition of the year.

:::theorem Four dates are not one modernity {#thm-four}
Granada's surrender, the Alhambra Decree, Columbus's sailing and his landfall are four dated events in 1492. They share the court of Isabella and Ferdinand. They are not the birth of modern Europe. A reader who uses the year as that birth has replaced four claims with a label.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the four dates, kept apart, plus the deadline that sits between the decree and the sailing. A conquest on 2 January, an order on 31 March with a clock that ends on 31 July, a departure on 3 August and a landfall on 12 October cannot be one afternoon. Sharing a court explains how one year holds them. It does not give them one aim that contemporaries named modernity. The word is later. The chronology lists the meaning of 1492 among the things this atlas must teach as disputed, which is why the disagreement is stated in the prose. What would count against [[#thm-four]] is a source from 1492 that itself presents the four as one programme under a name we are entitled to translate as the birth of the modern world. A source that links a voyage to the recent conquest, if you meet a real one and do not invent it, would show a connection. A connection is not a programme, and a programme is not that later name. Tordesillas, on 7 June 1494, is two years later and is a treaty between crowns about claims. It is not a retrospective glue that turns 1492 into a single act.
:::

## A conquest, not a nation

On 2 January 1492 the last Muslim polity on the Iberian mainland surrenders. Granada's surrender is a conquest. It is not a sack this lesson will narrate, and it is not a set of terms this lesson will invent. The locked fact is the surrender. Behind it lie centuries that a single label of reconquest flattens. In 711 a force crosses into Iberia. In 756 Abd al-Rahman I establishes the Umayyad emirate of Cordoba. In 929 Abd al-Rahman III takes the title of caliph. In 1212 Las Navas de Tolosa is a Christian victory, and it is not the end. From 711 to 1492 the elapsed count is 781 years. From 1212 to 1492 it is 280. A campaign that needs 280 years after Las Navas, and 781 after the crossing, is not one battle with a delayed result. [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]] holds the earlier polities. [[christendom/crusades]] holds Las Navas in a different frame, as one dated battle among others, not as a preface waiting for Ferdinand.

The people conquered are the people of that polity and its territory. They are the first of three groups this chapter refuses to merge. Their history does not become Europe's gain by being finished as a polity. Ottoman rule after 1453 was a warning, in [[crises/city-states]], against calling a conquest the end of history. Granada is the same kind of warning in the west. A Muslim political order on the mainland ends. Muslim and converted lives do not thereby become a blank on which a modern state writes its birthday. This atlas does not lock a later expulsion of Muslims, so none is dated. Do not borrow a year from outside and attach it. The decree of 31 March is not that missing order. It addresses Jews of Castile and Aragon.

## An order to convert or leave

The decree is dated 31 March, after the surrender and before the sailing. It names two legal outcomes, conversion or departure, and a deadline, 31 July. Calling it only an expulsion, which is the brief's shortest word, is fair to those who left and unfair if it hides the other outcome. People who converted remained under Christian rule, in the sight of an institution already authorised. On 1 November 1478 Sixtus IV authorises the Spanish Inquisition. From 1478 to 1492 is 14 years. The Inquisition is not founded in order to carry out a decree that does not yet exist. It is the world in which conversion was not a private safety. This lesson does not say that every convert was tried, and it does not describe a procedure. [[christendom/exclusion]] has already separated the papal Inquisition of 1231, a procedure against heresy, from this later royal and papal institution. The two share a word. They are not the same office. Canon 68 of the Fourth Lateran Council, in November 1215, had already required distinctive dress for Jews and Muslims in Latin Christendom, as a decree rather than as a description of every town. 1492 does not invent exclusion. It dates one order, in two kingdoms, with a clock.

No number of the expelled is locked. None is printed. A round integer from another book would be the same false precision the plague chapter refused for a continent. "Many" is allowed if you cannot stop yourself, and silence is better. The decree is evidence of a legal will, not of a census of obedience. Some will have converted. Some will have left. Some histories of particular towns can show which, and those histories are not given a numeral here. Jewish communities in Iberia are older than the decree. This lesson does not date a first arrival. Their presence under Muslim rulers and under Christian rulers is a longer fact than 1492, and it is not a golden age this chapter will invent in order to make the decree look like a fall from paradise. The decree is harsh enough as a legal fact. It does not need a romance on either side.

The people named in the decree are the second group. They are not the conquered of Granada, even where a Jewish community lived in that city, because the order is aimed at Jews of the two kingdoms as Jews, and the surrender is aimed at a Muslim polity. Overlap of a person who could be described in both ways is not identity of the two acts. Folding them into "the people Spain expelled in 1492" commits both errors at once: it invents a single modern Spain, and it invents a single population.

## A voyage that is not a prize

On 3 August Columbus sails from Palos. On 12 October he makes landfall in the Caribbean. People lived there. The voyage is not the discovery of an empty land, and it is not a gift Europe gives itself. This lesson will not celebrate it. Celebration is a tone that has already decided the bundle year in favour of one of the four dates and against the people who were already on the other shore. No number of people on the ships is locked. No number of people in the Caribbean is locked. Both silences are required. The third group is the people who meet the voyage. They are not a prize that completes Granada, and they are not the Jews ordered to convert or leave.

:::theorem Three groups are not Europe's gain {#thm-groups}
The people conquered in the surrender of Granada, the Jews of Castile and Aragon ordered to convert or leave, and the people in the Caribbean are three groups. Folding them into Europe's gain erases two of the three and turns a bundle year into a victory lap.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the difference of the acts. A surrender on 2 January ends a polity in Iberia. A decree on 31 March threatens a religious community in Castile and Aragon with a legal choice. A landfall on 12 October begins a colonial encounter with people who did not share that court. The court can license all three and still not make the three populations one beneficiary. What would count against [[#thm-groups]] is evidence that these are the same people, or that a source from 1492 counts them as one European profit. No such count exists in this atlas, and the prohibition on numbers is part of the proof: an integer that put the expelled, the conquered and the islanders into one sum would be an invention, not a refutation. Tordesillas, on 7 June 1494, shows how quickly crowns moved from a landfall to a treaty about claims. A treaty between Castile and Portugal describes the claimants. It does not describe Caribbean societies, and it does not make the landfall a peaceful legal fact. Two years, the elapsed count from 1492 to 1494, is long enough for a treaty and far too short to be a history of the people claimed.
:::

The counter-argument from the shared court is the strongest one, and it should not be caricatured. One pair of monarchs, one chancery, one religious policy, one interest in the ocean: a reader can say the four dates are one policy seen from four angles. The reply is that a court is a place where several policies are decided, not a machine that gives them one essence. The deadline expiring before the sailing is the detail the one-policy reading finds awkward. If the ships were the means of the decree, the decree's clock and the sailing date would be the same act. They are three days apart, and the lesson still refuses to put the expelled on the ships. Awkwardness is not yet a refutation of every link. It is a reason to list the dates before you join them. Join them, if you have a source, as a hypothesis about a court. Do not join them as the birth of a world.

## What the label does to the neighbours

Print is already a workshop by 1492. From 1454 to 1492 is 38 years, and from 1455 is 37. [[crises/print]] has refused to let that workshop cause the Reformation, and this chapter refuses to let it cause the bundle year. Constantinople falls on 29 May 1453, 39 years before Granada, and [[crises/city-states]] has refused to let the fall cause humanism. A birth narrative that needs the press, the city and the landfall has to drag other years into this one. [[sources/argument]] separates a label from the claim it hides. The hidden claim is that modernity has a birthday, and that the birthday is good for Europe. [[#thm-four]] and [[#thm-groups]] unpack it.

:::example The decree's clock {#ex-clock}
Using 30 days from 31 March to 30 April, 31 days on to 31 May, 30 days on to 30 June and 31 days on to 31 July, how long is the decree's own interval, and how many days after 31 July does Columbus sail?
:::solution
30 plus 31 plus 30 plus 31 is 122. The decree's interval is 122 days. Columbus sails on 3 August, which is 3 days after 31 July. The number of days after the deadline is 3. The 122 is a legal clock, not a number of people expelled. The 3 is a gap between a deadline and a sailing, not a loading list. Neither integer may be carried out as a census.
:::
:::

:::example Seventy days is not a crew {#ex-seventy}
From 3 August to 3 September count 31 days, from 3 September to 3 October count 30, and from 3 October to 12 October count 9. What is the sum, and what is it forbidden to be?
:::solution
31 plus 30 plus 9 is 70. The sum is 70 days, the calendar interval from sailing at Palos to landfall in the Caribbean. It is forbidden to be a count of people on the ships, a count of ships, or a count of people already living in the Caribbean. No such count is locked. The interval also keeps the sailing and the landfall from collapsing into one afternoon inside the bundle year. They are the third and fourth events, not one event told twice.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chapter title {#ex-title}
This sentence is invented. "In 1492 Spain discovered America, expelled the Jews and the Muslims, and the modern world began." Strike out each false piece, and say which dates a careful sentence would have to list instead.
:::solution
"Spain" as one modern nation is not what the crowns of Castile and Aragon are. "Discovered America" celebrates a voyage and treats a landfall in the Caribbean as an empty find. "Expelled the Jews" hides the decree's other legal outcome, conversion, and invents a number by sounding like a total. "Expelled the Muslims" is not the locked event. The locked event is a surrender of Granada on 2 January, and this atlas does not date a later expulsion of Muslims. "The modern world began" is the label [[#thm-four]] refuses. A careful sentence lists 2 January, 31 March with a deadline of 31 July, 3 August and 12 October, names three groups, and stops. The invented sentence was built to show how a true year launders four distortions at once.
:::
:::

:::example Earlier and later than the bundle {#ex-neighbours}
Sixtus IV authorises the Spanish Inquisition in 1478. Tordesillas is dated 7 June 1494. What are the elapsed counts from 1478 to 1492 and from 1492 to 1494, and what does each count block?
:::solution
1492 minus 1478 is 14. 1494 minus 1492 is 2. Fourteen years blocks the story in which the Inquisition is founded to enforce the Alhambra Decree: the authorisation is earlier than the decree. Two years blocks the story in which the landfall and the treaty are one afternoon, and it also blocks any use of Tordesillas as a description of Caribbean societies. The treaty is between crowns. The people in the Caribbean are the third group in [[#thm-groups]], not a clause of the treaty.
:::

:::warning The birth of the modern world
The myth gives 1492 a title, the birth of the modern world, and asks one landfall to carry Granada, the decree, the press, humanism and the state. It fails because the four dates are a conquest, an order to convert or leave, and a voyage, and because the meaning of the year is disputed. The people conquered, the people ordered to convert or leave, and the people in the Caribbean are not Europe's profit. A birthday that needs their erasure is a label, not a finding.
:::

:::history
In 1492 Granada surrenders on 2 January, the Alhambra Decree is dated 31 March, with a deadline of 31 July, Columbus sails from Palos on 3 August and makes landfall in the Caribbean on 12 October. Isabella and Ferdinand's court holds all four. The year is not one birth of the modern world.
:::

The warning is the slogan and the history note is the list. Remember the list, and remember that the deadline falls before the sailing. That order stops the ships from becoming the secret mechanism of the decree, and stops the landfall from becoming the moral of the surrender. A busy court is not a philosophy of history. The disputed meaning of the year is an instruction to keep the events apart until a source, not a chapter title, joins them.

## Where this leads

[[reformations/luther]] is the next course's opening, dated 1517, not an effect of this bundle. [[states/composite-monarchy]] is where two crowns under one pair of princes are a form of state rather than a nation born in a year. [[states/atlantic]] is the long consequence of European voyages and law across the ocean, and it is not a tally you may hang on the ships of 3 August. [[christendom/exclusion]] is the older law of marking and the papal Inquisition of 1231, against which 1478 and 1492 should be read as later and distinct. [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]] is 711 and the polities Granada does not retroactively simplify. [[crises/print]] is the workshop already at work, which did not cause this year and was not caused by it.

:::summary
- 1492 holds four dates: Granada surrenders on 2 January, the Alhambra Decree is 31 March, Columbus sails on 3 August, and the landfall is 12 October.
- The decree orders Jews of Castile and Aragon to convert or leave by 31 July. There is no count of people. The deadline is 3 days before the sailing.
- The three groups are the conquered of Granada, the people named in the decree, and the people in the Caribbean. They are not Europe's gain.
- The events share Isabella and Ferdinand's court. They are not the birth of modern Europe, and historians do not agree on that slogan.
- The Spanish Inquisition is authorised on 1 November 1478, 14 years earlier, and it is not the papal Inquisition of 1231.
- Tordesillas, on 7 June 1494, divides claims between crowns. It does not describe the Caribbean.
- Granada ends a Muslim polity after a long Iberian history, from 711 and from Las Navas in 1212. It does not found a modern nation. The figure's other dots are not the four events.
:::
