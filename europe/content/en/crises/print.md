What did print change, and what did it not cause?

It changed the making of books. A text that had to be copied by hand could, once type was set, be multiplied on a press, sold at a fair, and finished, still, by a hand that added initials and colour. It did not, by itself, cause the Reformation. Gutenberg's printed Bible is usually dated about 1454–1455. There is no day. The theses are dated 31 October 1517, and that quarrel belongs to [[reformations/luther]]. From 1454 to 1517 the elapsed count is 63 years. From 1455 to 1517 it is 62. Either reading is about two generations, and neither is a neighbour of the other on a five-year window. Most of what the new trades printed was not new philosophy. Indulgences and schoolbooks matter. Manuscript culture continues. A technology can change speed and price and still not be the author of a later doctrine.

The previous chapter left the press beside the fall of Constantinople and refused to make them one cause. This chapter keeps the refusal and changes the question. [[crises/city-states]] defined humanism as a practice of Latin, schooling and chanceries, already under way before 29 May 1453. The press can multiply a humanist grammar and a liturgical book with the same screws. Multiplication is indifferent to the novelty of the text. That indifference is the fact the myths about public opinion cannot survive.

## A Bible without a day

:::definition Print {#def-print}
**Print**, in this lesson, is the making of texts with movable type and a press, in a workshop that also depends on paper, ink, correctors and buyers. It is not a synonym for new ideas, and it is not a single day in the life of one man.
:::

The Bible usually dated about 1454–1455 is a Latin Bible, a text the Church already held and already read. Printing it does not add a book to the canon. It adds copies. The usual dating is a range of about two years, not a colophon this atlas is willing to treat as settled. Do not invent a day. Do not split the difference and call 1 January a compromise. A compromise day is still an invention. Gutenberg's name is the name attached to that Bible. His birth and his death are not locked here, so they are not given. The workshop is the unit: type, a press, ink that will take on metal, and the money to buy paper before a sheet is sold. Partners and creditors belong to that unit. A lawsuit's year is not locked, so 1455 must not be quietly reused as the date of a quarrel in a court. In this lesson 1455 is only the end of the Bible's usual range.

Mainz can be named as the city of that work. Naming a city is not a claim that the invention has a birthday, or that no other workshop mattered. Paper is older than this Bible in any story the chronology will let us tell. The press does not invent the sheet. It fills more sheets than a copyist fills. That sentence is the whole of the technological claim this chapter needs. It does not require a count of sheets. No count of books is locked in the chronology. None is stated. A large-sounding integer copied from another book would be a false precision of the same family as a European plague total. Refuse it for the same reason.

:::theorem Multiplication is not the Reformation {#thm-not-cause}
Print multiplies texts, changes the speed at which copies can be made, and can change price. It builds trades. It does not, by itself, cause the Reformation. The theses are dated 31 October 1517, about two generations after the Bible of about 1454–1455.
:::
:::proof
The evidence for what print changes is the workshop itself. Once type is set, a further copy is a different kind of labour from a line written out by hand. Speed follows from that difference, as a direction, not as a timed record this atlas has locked. Price can fall as copies multiply, and again the direction is the claim. No sum in livres or pounds is locked, so none is written. Trades follow the machine: typefounders, pressmen, paper makers, correctors, carters, stallholders at fairs. The evidence against a cause of the Reformation is the gap and the contents of the output. From either end of the Bible's range to 1517 is 63 or 62 years, and the figure's window of five years does not hold both. In those decades the press multiplies a Latin Bible the Church already used, schoolbooks for the Latin [[#def-print]] did not invent, and indulgences. Most of what was printed was not a new philosophy. What would count against [[#thm-not-cause]] is evidence that the quarrel of 1517 was impossible without print and that the press's ordinary work was already that quarrel. The second clause fails on indulgences and schoolbooks. The first is a counterfactual. Manuscript culture had already carried texts for centuries, which [[christendom/towns-and-learning]] is there to recall, and it continues after 1455. A condition that later helps a text to travel is not, by itself, the cause of the text's argument.
:::

The counter-argument is the one a serious reader will make, and it deserves the serious reply. The theses circulated. Circulation on paper is easier when type is set. Therefore, the reader says, print caused the Reformation. The "therefore" is the fault. A means that makes copying faster can be important in a later quarrel and still not be the quarrel. If multiplication were itself reforming, the Bible of about 1454–1455 would already be the break with Rome. It is a Latin Bible. The press also multiplied indulgences, the form the later quarrel attacks. A machine that serves the form and the attack on the form is not on one side. [[reformations/luther]] will date the theses, say what is not established about a church door, and keep indulgences as the occasion rather than as a cartoon of a church with no other life. This chapter must not tell that story early, and it must not tell the door as a fact. The door is not established. The date 31 October 1517 is.

## What the trades actually made

:::definition Ordinary print {#def-ordinary}
**Ordinary print** is the bulk of the workshop's work: indulgences, schoolbooks, service books, grammars, calendars and the Latin texts institutions already wanted. An **indulgence**, here, is a printed form stating a spiritual benefit. It is not, in this chapter, the whole of the later critique, and it is not a count of sheets.
:::

Schoolbooks are the hinge with humanism. A grammar in classical Latin is a tool for the education [[crises/city-states]] described, tutors and boys and a credential for chanceries. The press can make that tool cheaper to obtain, in the directional sense already stated, without becoming the author of the curriculum. The curriculum is a practice of imitation. The press is a practice of multiplication. Confusing them is how a history of technology swallows a history of teaching. Women largely shut out of that schooling are not admitted to it by the existence of a press. A technology does not correct a social exclusion it did not design. No count of women readers is locked, so none is given.

Indulgences show the same split from another side. They were a pastoral and financial instrument before they were a printed form. Printing them makes them easier to multiply. It does not invent the theology, and it does not refute it. When, in 1517, indulgences become the occasion of a public quarrel, the press is in the background as a means of making forms and, later, of making arguments. Background is the right depth. A reader who starts the story at the Bible and ends it at the theses has skipped the ordinary decades in which the press was busy with the Church's own paperwork. Those decades are the evidence that print and reform are not one event.

Fairs belong with the trades. Cloth and other goods were already bought and sold at fairs. Books join what a fair can sell. This atlas does not lock a privilege year for a book fair, so none is given. The point of naming fairs is commercial, not civic. A printer who cannot buy paper cannot multiply a text, however urgent the text's ideas. Capital sits in the workshop before fame does. That is a constraint on the myth of the lone inventor as much as on the myth of the sudden public. The inventor, in so far as a name is warranted, is a workshop with a Bible to show. The public, in 1455, is a set of buyers and readers of that Bible and of humbler work. It is not yet an opinion about the Church organised as a cause.

Manuscript culture continues, and this is the fact that should make the word "revolution" stick in the throat. Choir books, presentation copies, charters and the ordinary scrawl of administration do not stop because type exists. Many printed books were still finished by hand. A page can be printed and then painted. The two techniques share a desk. Anyone who draws a wall across 1455, manuscript on one side and print on the other, has taken the end of a dating range for the end of a culture. What would count against the claim that manuscripts continue is a stop, dated and general, after which hand copying ceases. There is no such stop in this atlas. Survival of handwritten books after the Bible's range is ordinary, and it is counter-evidence to every sentence that makes 1455 the last medieval year.

## A worked list, and the window

Take an invented printer's list. It is invented, and it has no numbers on it, because a number would be the kind of count the chronology refuses. The list reads: a Latin Bible, a grammar, a pile of indulgence forms, a calendar. Put the list next to the claim that print created new philosophy. The Bible is old. The grammar teaches an old language, however newly imitated. The indulgence states a benefit the Church already offered. The calendar orders the year. None of the four items is a system of thought appearing for the first time. The list is a fair picture of ordinary print, and it is forbidden to promote it into the archive of a named shop in Mainz. A real inventory would have quantities and a date. This one is a teaching object. Its emptiness of integers is deliberate.

Now put the same list next to 1517. Nothing on it entails the theses. A workshop can print every item on the list and never meet a reformer. A reformer can, two generations later, use presses, including presses that still print indulgences. The elapsed counts, 62 and 63, are the quantitative form of "not by itself". The figure makes the same point without the subtraction, if you keep the window at five years and try to see the Bible and the theses together. You cannot. The caption says the window does not hold both. Believe the caption, and then do the subtraction so that you are not trusting a picture you have not checked.

:::example The gap the window cannot hold {#ex-gap}
The Bible is usually dated about 1454–1455. The theses are dated 1517. Compute the elapsed count from each end of the range to 1517. Does a window of five years hold the press and the theses?
:::solution
1517 minus 1454 is 63. 1517 minus 1455 is 62. Both counts are far larger than five, so a window of five years does not hold both, whichever end of the Bible's range you start from. The two counts differ by a year because the Bible's date is a range. That difference is a reason to say "about", not a reason to invent a day that would let you pick a single integer and hide the uncertainty. Neither 62 nor 63 is a count of books or a cause.
:::
:::

:::example An invented day {#ex-day}
This colophon is invented. "Finished at Mainz on 1 January 1455, being the day print created public opinion." What is wrong with it, given that 1455 is a real year in the Bible's range?
:::solution
1455 is inside the usual range, which is why the forgery is tempting. The day, 1 January, is not locked. A date inside a true range is still an invented day if the day itself was never established. The causal clause is worse. Print does not create public opinion in 1455, and [[#thm-not-cause]] refuses the press as the cause of a Reformation that is still 62 years away even from the end of the range. A real colophon, if one were locked, would date a book. It would not date an opinion. The sentence was labelled invented so that the true year would not launder the false day.
:::
:::

:::example Ordinary work {#ex-list}
The invented list in the chapter names a Latin Bible, a grammar, indulgence forms and a calendar, and it gives no quantities. Why is the absence of quantities part of the lesson, and which item would a reader wrongly call the Reformation?
:::solution
No count of books is locked in the chronology, so an invented quantity would teach a false precision. The list can show kinds of work without pretending to be an inventory. None of the items is the Reformation. The indulgence is the one a reader will wrongly promote, because indulgences are the occasion of the theses in 1517. An occasion later is not a cause now. Printing a form that states a spiritual benefit multiplies a practice the Church already had. It does not open [[reformations/luther]].
:::
:::

:::example City, press, theses {#ex-three}
Set three locked dates in order: 29 May 1453, the Bible's range about 1454–1455, and 31 October 1517. Which pairs are close, and which pair is the one the myths collapse?
:::solution
The fall of Constantinople and the start of the Bible's range are close: 1454 is the year after 1453. Close is not a cause, which is the previous chapter's point as well as this one. The Bible and the theses are not close. The elapsed count is 63 years from 1454, or 62 from 1455. The myth collapses the press into the theses, and sometimes collapses the city into the press as well, so that one decade gives birth to modernity. The order of the three dates is the correction. A conquest, a workshop's Bible, and a later quarrel about indulgences are three events. Manuscripts continue through all of them.
:::
:::

Move the year onto 1454 and then onto 1455. Both marks say "about", because the Bible is a range and the figure has to show both ends rather than invent the day the prose refuses. :::warning Public opinion in 1455
The sentence to refuse is that print created public opinion in 1455. It takes the end of a dating range for a Bible and makes it the birthday of modern politics. A public that argues in print about doctrine is a later and partial thing, and even then it is not everyone. In 1455 the press has a Latin Bible and the start of a set of trades. It does not have a parliament, a Reformation, or a census of readers. Manuscripts continue, which is enough to stop the wall across the year.
:::

:::history
Gutenberg's printed Bible is usually dated about 1454–1455, and no day is established. On 29 May 1453 Mehmed II takes Constantinople, the year before that range begins. The theses are dated 31 October 1517. The press multiplies texts. It does not, by itself, cause the Reformation.
:::

A last distinction keeps the chapter from becoming a sermon against technology. Print did change something real. A student who could buy a grammar, a parish that could buy a service book, a preacher who could carry a pile of forms: each of those is a change in the availability of a text. Availability is not agreement. Two readers of the same printed page can remain on opposite sides of a later quarrel, and the page will not have caused either of them. The worked list is there so that you can feel how ordinary the change was. Ordinary is not the same as small. It is the opposite of miraculous. The miracle story is the one in which a machine thinks. The workshop story is the one in which people set type, buy paper, correct a sheet and sell it beside cloth. Keep the second story, and the dates will do the rest.

## Where this leads

[[crises/year-1492]] is the dot the figure compresses into "Granada and the ocean". The press does not explain that year, and that year does not explain the press. [[reformations/luther]] is where 31 October 1517 is taught properly, including what is not established. [[crises/city-states]] is the practice of letters the press was able to multiply and did not invent. [[christendom/towns-and-learning]] is the manuscript world that does not end on the day this lesson refuses to invent. [[sources/argument]] is the method: a cause has to be the sort of thing that could have been otherwise, and a label such as "the print revolution" bundles a claim about public opinion that [[#thm-not-cause]] will not sign.

:::summary
- The Gutenberg Bible is usually dated about 1454–1455. There is no day, and there is no locked count of books.
- Print multiplies texts with type and a press, and it builds trades in paper, type, correcting and fairs. Price and speed can change. No sum of money is stated.
- The text of that Bible is a Latin Bible the Church already held. Multiplication is not a new canon and not a translation.
- Most of what was printed was ordinary: indulgences, schoolbooks, service books, calendars. New philosophy was not the bulk.
- Manuscript culture continues. A printed page can still be finished by hand. 1455 is not a wall.
- The theses are dated 31 October 1517, an elapsed count of 63 years from 1454 and of 62 from 1455. The press does not, by itself, cause the Reformation.
- Print does not create public opinion in 1455. A faster copy is not an opinion.
:::

:::exercise The serious counter-argument {level=3}
A reader grants every date in [[#thm-not-cause]] and still says: the theses circulated, print made circulation possible, and therefore print caused the Reformation. Where is the fault in the "therefore"? What would actually count against the claim?
:::hint
Separate a means that can help a text travel from the argument the text makes. Then look at what the press had already been multiplying, including indulgences, for about two generations.
:::
:::solution
The fault is the jump from a means to a cause. A press can make copying faster, and faster copying can matter in 1517, without the press being the author of the quarrel. If multiplication itself reformed the Church, the Latin Bible of about 1454–1455 would already be the break. It is not. The same trades multiplied indulgences, the form later under attack, which is an odd shape for a cause that is supposed to be on only one side. Manuscripts had carried arguments for centuries. What would count against [[#thm-not-cause]] is evidence that the quarrel was impossible without print and that the press's ordinary product was already that quarrel. Ordinary print, schoolbooks and indulgence forms, fails the second test. The elapsed count of 62 or 63 years fails the attempt to hide the gap inside the word "therefore".
:::

:::exercise Price without a sum {level=3}
The lesson says print can change the price of a book, and it states no sum. Why is the silence required, and how can a direction still be a claim? What extra sentence would turn the direction into the myth of public opinion?
:::hint
Ask what the chronology has locked about money. Then ask what a buyer is, as against a public that shares a doctrine.
:::
:::solution
No price is locked, in livres or in pounds, so a numeral would be an invention. A direction can still be a claim: once type is set, further copies are a different labour from handwriting, and a text can become easier to obtain. Easier to obtain is not free, and it is not universal. A buyer of a grammar is not a public opinion. The extra sentence that would turn the direction into the myth is any sentence that dates a shared modern politics to 1455, or that treats a cheaper book as agreement with a doctrine. [[#thm-not-cause]] allows the change in price and refuses that agreement. A locked account book with a real sum would let a future lesson state a price. It would still not state an opinion.
:::
