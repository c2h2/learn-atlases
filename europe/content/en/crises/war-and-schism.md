What do a long war and a papal schism do to authority?

They do not produce one mood called decline. From the conventional start in 1337 to Castillon on 17 July 1453, a series of conflicts later labelled the Hundred Years' War sets an English claim to the French crown against the French crown's refusal of that claim. Inside those brackets, and not as their cause, the Western Schism begins in 1378 and is ended by the election of Martin V in 1417. One crisis is about which king is owed tax and allegiance. The other is about which pope is owed obedience. They overlap on a timeline. Overlap is not identity. Authority here means the claim to be obeyed, and the means, especially taxation, by which obedience is paid for. It does not mean a spirit of the age.

The plague chapter has already refused to let 1347 explain everything that follows. The refusal matters at once. Crécy is fought in 1346, and the war's conventional start is 1337, both before plague is reported at Messina. A war already under way is not a consequence of a disaster not yet reported. [[crises/plague]] keeps the demographic range in its own terms. This chapter will not borrow a death toll, and it will not invent battle casualties to replace one. No army size is locked. None is given.

## A label for a series of conflicts

:::definition The Hundred Years' War {#def-war}
The **Hundred Years' War** is a later label. Its conventional brackets run from 1337 to Castillon on 17 July 1453. The label gathers a series of conflicts, truces and campaigns. It is not a claim that fighting was continuous, and it is not a casualty list.
:::

The English king claimed the French crown. The French crown treated the claim as an aggression. That dispute is political and dynastic, and it is fiscal, because war on this scale is paid for by taxes, loans and the seizure of resources. People who were not princes paid, and people who were not princes were told that the paying was a duty to a rightful king. Which king was rightful is the argument the battles advertise. The battles do not settle the argument by their fame, and this lesson will not pretend that a victory's reputation is a count of the dead.

Crécy, in 1346, and Agincourt, on 25 October 1415, are the two battles the chronology locks inside the brackets before the end. From 1346 to 1415 the elapsed count is 69 years. Sixty-nine years is not one afternoon of fighting described twice. It is time enough for a truce, a new reign, a new tax and a new grievance. You will meet, in other books, treaties that tried to fix the succession or the territory. This atlas does not lock their years, so this lesson does not date them and does not narrate them as if a missing year were a detail. What is locked is enough: the war is a series, the series has a conventional beginning and a conventional end, and the end is Castillon, not a proverb about national character.

The elapsed count from 1337 to 1453 is 116 years. The label says "hundred". The count says 116. The round name is a memory aid, not a measurement. A reader who treats the name as a duration has done the arithmetic backwards, starting from the slogan and hoping the years will agree. They agree only roughly. Rough agreement is what a later label is. It is not a reason to compress 116 years of politics into one battle, or to speak as if England and France were already the nation-states of a later century. They were kingdoms with claims, assemblies that could be asked for money, and subjects who sometimes refused.

On 17 July 1453 Castillon is the conventional end. In the same year, on 29 May 1453, Constantinople falls to the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II. The widget in this chapter marks 1453 as Castillon, because that is the end of the war's brackets. The year is not only that battle. The fall of the city is a different polity ending, and [[crises/city-states]] is where it is taught. Putting both dates in one year is a defence against the mood. Two endings in 1453 are not one European dusk.

## A person in the war, not a founder

Joan of Arc is burned at Rouen on 30 May 1431. She is a person inside the war: a commander on the side of the French claim, captured, tried, and killed. The campaigns she fought are not given separate years in this atlas. The date that is locked is her death. Do not fill the silence with a battle you happen to remember and a year the chronology has not locked. Do not invent a speech for her. Paraphrase is not available where this file has no words of hers to paraphrase.

:::theorem Joan does not found a nation {#thm-joan}
Joan of Arc's death at Rouen on 30 May 1431 does not found a French nation. She is a person in a dynastic and fiscal war that continues, on the conventional dating, until Castillon on 17 July 1453. Reading her as the founder of French nationalism moves a later political idea back into a trial.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the pair of dates and the kind of conflict the label describes. From 1431 to 1453 the elapsed count is 22 years. A nation already founded in the later sense would not still be waiting on a conventional end of a war about a crown. Allegiance in 1431 is argued in the language of rightful kingship, heresy and treason, which is why a woman who had led troops could be burned after a trial. Those categories are not the same as a nineteenth-century nation, and this atlas has not locked a year in which that later idea can be dropped backwards onto Rouen. What would count against [[#thm-joan]] is evidence that a French nation-state, with the institutions that word later names, exists because of her death, or that the war's fiscal and dynastic character stops on 30 May 1431. The brackets do not stop. Castillon is still ahead. Fame is not a constitution. A real quotation, if you meet one in a source you can check, would be her words or her judges' words. It would not be a theory of nationalism unless the source itself stated one. This lesson will not invent the quotation that would counterfeit that theory.
:::

Later politics wanted a founder who was not only a king. That want does not change the trial. She can be courageous in the sources, and the trial can be a killing dressed as justice, without either sentence making her the origin of a nation.

## Two popes, and then a third claim

:::definition The Western Schism {#def-schism}
The **Western Schism** is the split that begins in 1378, when Urban VI and Clement VII both claim the papacy. A further election, whose year this atlas does not lock, adds a third claim. The Council of Constance sits from 1414 to 1418. Jan Hus is burned in 1415. Martin V is elected in 1417, and that election is the end of the schism on the dating this atlas uses.
:::

The long papal residence at Avignon begins in 1309, with Clement V. It is not the schism. From 1309 to 1337 is 28 years, so the residence is already a fact when the war's label starts, and from 1309 to 1378 is 69 years, so the schism is a later break inside a papacy that had already left Rome for a long stretch. This lesson does not date the return from Avignon. The year is not locked, and inventing it would glue the residence to the schism as if they were one scandal. They are two facts about papal authority. [[christendom/popes-and-kings]] has already taught the earlier fight over office, including the bull Unam sanctam in 1302. The fourteenth century does not invent the conflict of popes and kings. It adds a double election, and then a triple claim, to a conflict that was already sharp.

In 1378 a Roman election is followed by a second election. Urban VI and Clement VII both claim to be pope. Kingdoms choose, and the choice is political as well as sacramental, because a pope confirms dispensations, takes revenue and judges appeals. Obeying one pope means rejecting the other. Subjects can find themselves under a king who has chosen the rival of the pope their neighbours recognise. Authority splits along a line that is not the same line as the war. France and England are already enemies in the dynastic war. They are not thereby assigned to opposite popes by a law of nature. Alliances of that sort are decisions. Decisions can be told only where the dates are locked. The locked fact is the split itself, not a map of every kingdom's obedience.

Before Constance there is a third claim. Say that, and stop. The year of the further election is not in the chronology, so it is not written here. What Constance does with the claims is dated. The council sits from 1414 to 1418. From 1414 to 1418 is 4 years. Inside those years it does two things that a mood called decline cannot hold in one gesture. In 1415 it burns Jan Hus. In 1417 it elects Martin V, which ends the schism. A council can restore a single papal office and still kill a man condemned as a heretic. Ending the schism is not a story of toleration. [[christendom/exclusion]] is where heresy is taught as a category authorities make usable. Hus is a case of that category at work in 1415, not a preview of 1517. From 1415 to 1517 the elapsed count is 102 years. A later reader who calls Hus the first Protestant has turned a condemnation into a trailer for the theses. [[reformations/luther]] is the chapter for the theses. The dates do not license the trailer.

The same year, 1415, also holds Agincourt, on 25 October. The widget says so in one dot, and the dot is a problem you are meant to notice. A battle in France and a burning at a council in the empire are not one event because they share a year. Anyone who remembers only "1415" has already lost half of the politics. The exercise later in the chapter forces the split. The figure's caption warns you before you get there.

## A rising, and one imperial paragraph

In 1381 the English Rising, also called the Peasants' Revolt, challenges tax and lordship in England. It was not only peasants. The second name is the myth: it shrinks a rising of mixed condition into a single rural type, and then invites you to dismiss it as a peasants' affair or to romanticise it as the voice of the countryside. Both readings obey the false name. The chronology's correction is social, not sentimental. Townsmen as well as rural people took part. This lesson does not invent a speech, a tax rate, or a list of the dead. It was put down. The putting down is a fact about authority: a crown at war could still face a refusal at home, and the refusal was about who paid. From 1378 to 1381 is 3 years, so the rising sits near the start of the schism and inside the war's brackets. Nearness is not merger. The rising does not elect a pope, and the schism does not abolish an English tax.

In 1356 Emperor Charles IV issues the Golden Bull, an imperial constitution of electors. It regulates how the emperor is chosen. It is not a French treaty and not a papal election. From 1337 to 1356 is 19 years, so the bull falls inside the war's conventional brackets. From 1356 to 1378 is 22 years, so it also falls before the schism. One empire tries to fix a procedure, by naming the princes who elect, while two kingdoms fight over a crown. That contrast is the whole use of this paragraph. The bull does not end imperial politics, and it is not a modern constitution. A numeral for the college of electors is not locked here, and the argument does not need one. Election, rather than a single hereditary capital, is the procedure. A reader who wants one crisis called decline has to flatten this document into the same mood as Crécy and, later, the double papacy. The dates do not cooperate.

:::theorem Two crises, not one decline {#thm-two}
The Hundred Years' War and the Western Schism are two crises of authority. Sharing a stretch of years does not make them one mood called decline, and ending one does not end the other.
:::
:::proof
The war's brackets run from 1337 to 17 July 1453. The schism runs from 1378 to the election of Martin V in 1417, while Constance continues to sit until 1418. The schism therefore sits inside the war's brackets, and it ends 36 years before Castillon, because 1453 minus 1417 is 36. The parties differ. The war is a conflict of crowns, taxes and allegiance in the kingdoms that accept or reject the English claim. The schism is a conflict of obediences owed to rival popes, later three claimants, settled by a council that also burns a heretic. The Golden Bull of 1356 is a third document, imperial and elective, which fits neither story and so blocks the mood from being the only story available. The English Rising of 1381 blocks it again, from below, without becoming either the war or the schism. What would count against [[#thm-two]] is a source showing one authority, one tax and one allegiance covering both the papal election and the French crown, or evidence that Castillon and the election of Martin V are the same act. They are not the same act. They are not the same year. A feeling that the century looks unhappy is not a counter-example. It is the mood, restated.
:::

## Worked cases

Set the ends side by side before you trust a mood. Martin V is elected in 1417. Castillon is fought on 17 July 1453. Anyone who stops the century at the healed papacy has left a war running. Anyone who tells only the war has missed 39 years, from 1378 to 1417, in which Latin Christians could not agree which bishop of Rome was real. People paid taxes and used church courts while two, and then three, men claimed the keys. Ordinary continuity is an argument against decline as a description of every life. It is not an argument that the split was small. A doubted papal office is a real crisis of authority, and it is a bounded one.

:::example Why the label says hundred {#ex-hundred}
The conventional brackets run from 1337 to Castillon in 1453. What is the elapsed count of years, and what does the count do to the name Hundred Years' War?
:::solution
1453 minus 1337 is 116. The elapsed count is 116 years. The name says hundred, which is a round later label, not the measurement. You may keep the name if you keep it as a label for a series of conflicts. You may not treat it as a stopwatch, as one continuous battle, or as a casualty total. No casualty total is locked, so none is stated.
:::
:::

:::example One year, two events {#ex-1415}
The year 1415 contains Agincourt on 25 October and the burning of Jan Hus. Why does the figure give them one dot, and why must the lesson refuse to leave them as one event?
:::solution
A timeline of this kind has one mark per year, so the dot labelled for both is a compression, not a claim that a battle and a burning are the same act. Agincourt is a battle in the dynastic war. Hus is burned by the Council of Constance, which is sitting in the schism. The parties, the places and the kind of authority are different. Remembering only the year drops one of the two. The widget's caption says that 1415 carries two events so that the compression stays visible.
:::
:::

:::example An invented founding {#ex-joan-sentence}
This sentence is invented. "On 30 May 1431 Joan of Arc founded the French nation in the fire at Rouen, and the Hundred Years' War ended with her." What, if anything, is dated correctly, and what does [[#thm-joan]] require you to strike out?
:::solution
The day and the place of her death are right: 30 May 1431, Rouen, burned. Strike out the founding of a nation. Strike out the claim that the war ended with her. The conventional end is Castillon on 17 July 1453, an elapsed count of 22 years later. The invented sentence uses a real date as bait. A real date does not authenticate the moral glued to it. Promoting the sentence into evidence would take a source from 1431 that itself states a nation in the later sense. This lesson has no such source, and the sentence was labelled invented so that you would not go looking for one.
:::
:::

:::example Two endings {#ex-ends}
Place the election of Martin V in 1417 beside Castillon on 17 July 1453. Does the first ending close the second crisis?
:::solution
No. Martin V's election ends the Western Schism. Castillon ends the conventional brackets of the war. From 1417 to 1453 is 36 years. Constance itself sits on until 1418, so even the council is not finished in the year of the election. Ending the papal split restores one line of papal authority. It does not decide the French crown, cancel a tax, or make Joan's death in 1431 into a national foundation. [[#thm-two]] is the claim this pair of dates supports.
:::

:::warning A mood called decline
The usual myth says that war, schism, rising and bull are one decline, a single failing of medieval authority on the way to something modern. The myth fails because the crises end at different times, bind different people, and include an imperial constitution that is trying to fix a procedure rather than to illustrate a mood. Joan of Arc is the other myth inside the same habit: a person burned at Rouen on 30 May 1431 is asked to found a nation that the war, still open until 1453, has not yet become. Decline and destiny are both ways of not looking at the brackets.
:::

:::history
The Western Schism begins in 1378, with Urban VI and Clement VII both claiming the papacy, and the election of Martin V in 1417 ends it after the Council of Constance has sat from 1414. Joan of Arc is burned at Rouen on 30 May 1431. On 17 July 1453 Castillon ends the conventional Hundred Years' War, whose label runs from 1337, and in the same year, on 29 May, Mehmed II takes Constantinople.
:::

A reader may call the distinction pedantic, because some writers at the time used the language of disaster, especially about the schism. Their anguish is evidence about the schism. It is not evidence that Crécy, the Golden Bull, the rising of 1381, Hus and Castillon are one process. [[sources/evidence]] stops a cleric's moral from becoming a census of the century. What would count against the separation is a mechanism, the same tax and the same ending, running through the war and the schism together. Point to it with locked dates, or keep the two names.

## Where this leads

[[crises/city-states]] takes the year 1453 as the fall of Constantinople and asks what humanists did, which is a practice of letters and office, not a mood that begins when a war ends. [[crises/plague]] is the demographic chapter you should not flatten into this one. [[christendom/popes-and-kings]] is the earlier history of the same papal office. [[reformations/luther]] is where 1517 belongs, a long elapsed count after Hus and not a sequel this chapter is allowed to announce. [[sources/argument]] is the method: a label such as Hundred Years' War or decline bundles a claim, and the claim has to be unpacked into dates.

:::summary
- The Hundred Years' War is a later label. The brackets run from 1337 to Castillon on 17 July 1453, an elapsed count of 116 years, not one battle and not a casualty list.
- Crécy is 1346 and Agincourt is 25 October 1415. From the first battle to the second is 69 years. No deaths are numbered.
- Joan of Arc is burned at Rouen on 30 May 1431. The war's conventional end is still 22 years ahead. She does not found a French nation.
- The Western Schism runs from 1378, Urban VI and Clement VII, to Martin V in 1417. Constance sits 1414–1418, burns Hus in 1415, and is not the same thing as the Avignon residence that begins in 1309.
- 1415 carries two events, Agincourt and Hus. Ending the schism does not end the war.
- The English Rising of 1381 was not only peasants. The Golden Bull of 1356 is an imperial rule for electors, not a chapter of the French war.
- Two crises of authority are not one mood called decline. The same year, 1453, also holds the fall of Constantinople, which is a third ending and not a proof of the mood.
:::
