What kind of war was the Peloponnesian War?

It was a war among Greek states. It begins in 431 BCE and ends with the surrender of Athens in 404 BCE. On one side is Athens and the empire it held in the Aegean, the league of [[greece/persian-wars]] after that league had become a power. On the other side is Sparta and her allies, Corinth and Thebes among them. Persia is involved, especially late and with money, and is not the meaning of the war. The citizen men who vote in Athens are the body defined in [[greece/athenian-democracy]]. Their defeat does not abolish the polis, and it does not reveal “the Greek view of life”. Socrates is tried and put to death in 399 BCE, five years after the surrender. That is an Athenian event. It is not what the war meant.

## Among Greeks

:::definition Peloponnesian War {#def-war}
The **Peloponnesian War**, on the usual dating, is the war that begins in 431 BCE between Athens and Sparta and their allies, and ends when Athens surrenders in 404 BCE. The name is a later convenience for a series of campaigns, not a single battle and not a civil war inside one Greek nation. There was no such nation to have a civil war.
:::

:::definition Athenian empire {#def-empire}
The **Athenian empire**, in this war, is the power Athens held over other poleis in the Aegean: contributions, coerced loyalty, and the capacity to harm allies and neutrals. It is not a separate country from the polis of Athens, and it is not a province-system of a later Roman kind. The allies remain poleis. They are poleis under pressure.
:::

Thucydides, an Athenian who was there for part of the war and who wrote its history, distinguishes disputes that were argued in public from what he presents as the truer cause: the growth of Athenian power and the fear it caused in Sparta. That is his argument, and it is a strong one, because the empire was real and Sparta did choose war. It is not a law that the outcome had to follow. Corinth had grievances. Allies wanted to leave. Athens chose to fight rather than dissolve the empire. A cause, as [[sources/argument]] uses the word, has to be the sort of thing that could have been otherwise. Several of these could have been otherwise. We will not quote Thucydides. A speech he puts in Pericles’ mouth near the start is evidence of what he wanted said, not minutes of the assembly. Pericles is named in the course of events at the outbreak. He does not live to see the surrender. We will not date his death. That year is not in the chronology.

A plague strikes Athens during the war. Many people die, including people the narrative treats as notable. We will not state a total. The chronology gives none, and a false total would only decorate a disaster. Contemporaries reached for religious explanations. A disease is not a verdict on a constitution. Sparta, which was not a democracy, was not spared the war’s other cruelties by having kings and ephors. The plague explains suffering and political strain inside the city. It does not explain why Greek states were fighting each other.

During the war Athens used force on Greek poleis that resisted or stayed out. Melos, a small polis, was destroyed. Thucydides stages a dialogue there. We will not quote the dialogue, and we will not date the destruction, because that year is not in the chronology. The episode is evidence of imperial violence by one polis against another. It is not a summary of Greek ethics. Other Greeks, including Sparta in other episodes, used massacre and enslavement when they won. Attributing the habit to democracy as such is a reading. It is not the only reading the wreckage allows.

:::theorem This was a war among Greek states {#thm-greek}
The Peloponnesian War was fought among Greek poleis, with an Athenian empire on one side and a Spartan alliance on the other. Persian help, especially later, is real and does not make the war a clash between Greece and Persia, or a rehearsal for a clash of civilisations. Defeat in 404 BCE is the defeat of an empire and of a city’s war policy, not the end of the polis.
:::
:::proof
The belligerents of 431 BCE are the evidence. Athens and the poleis it led or coerced fight Sparta and the poleis in her alliance. Syracuse, later, is a Greek polis in Sicily. The quarrels Thucydides puts at the start, including Corcyra and Potidaea, are quarrels among Greek states. Persia’s role grows when Sparta needs a fleet and the empire is willing to supply support. A satrap and a Persian prince pursuing their own interests are ordinary imperial politics. They do not change the identity of the armies that began the war, and they do not create a single Greek state on the other side.

What would count against the claim is a war whose main armies, from 431 BCE to the surrender, are Persian on one side and a united Greece on the other. That is the previous chapter’s shape, and it is not this one. What would count against the last sentence is the disappearance of Athens as a political community in 404 BCE. Athens surrenders, loses the empire, and gives up the fleet and the walls in the usual terms of the peace. An oligarchy backed by Sparta, remembered as the Thirty, then kills political enemies, citizens and metics, for a time. We will not number the dead. The oligarchy does not become the permanent form of the city. Democracy returns. We will not date the return. The return is enough to show that 404 BCE ended a war and an empire, not the polis.
:::

## Sicily, without a year

Athens decided, in the course of the war, to send an expedition to Sicily against Syracuse. Commanders associated with it include Alcibiades and Nicias. The expedition failed. Men and ships were lost, and the loss weakened the imperial city. We state no troop numbers, because the chronology states none. We state no year. A year sometimes printed elsewhere is not in this chronology, so it will not be printed here. The failure matters, and it does not finish the war. Fighting continues until the surrender in 404 BCE. A story that jumps from Sicily to the fall has skipped a stretch of war we are also refusing to date from a missing start. The honest sentence is shorter. A large Athenian effort against another Greek polis failed, and the war among Greek states went on.

That last fact is the one to hold. Sicily is not a side-show that stops being Greek because it is an island. Syracuse is a polis. The expedition is Greek power used against Greek power, which is what [[#thm-greek]] claims about the war as a whole. Leaving the year out is not vagueness for its own sake. It is the rule that a numeral has to be one we are allowed to treat as settled.

:::example Twenty-seven years {#ex-span}
The war begins in 431 BCE. Athens surrenders in 404 BCE. How many years is that span, and why is the surrender not “the next event after the outbreak” in any thick sense?
:::solution
431 minus 404 is 27. Check: 404 plus 27 is 431. Both dates are BCE. Twenty-seven years hold a plague, imperial violence, a failed expedition to Sicily that we do not date, Persian support for a Spartan fleet, and more than one phase of fighting. The two numerals are the brackets in [[#def-war]]. They are not a claim that nothing between them can be discussed. They are a claim that we discuss the middle without inventing years the chronology does not list. The answer is 27 years.
:::
:::

:::example What you may say about Sicily {#ex-sicily}
Write the strongest sentence this chapter allows about the Sicilian expedition. Then write two additions that are forbidden, one a year and one a number of soldiers, and say why each is forbidden.
:::solution
The allowed sentence: during the Peloponnesian War, Athens sent an expedition against Syracuse, a Greek polis in Sicily; the expedition failed, with loss of men and ships; the war continued until Athens surrendered in 404 BCE. A year is forbidden because the chronology does not date the expedition, and this lesson must not supply one. A number of soldiers is forbidden because no such figure is settled in the chronology, and a precise total would be false precision. The political point, Greek against Greek inside a war that ends in 404 BCE, does not need either addition.
:::
:::

The later fleet that beats Athens is Spartan, and it is built with Persian support. Then comes a naval defeat we will not date, and the surrender of 404 BCE. The irony is political, not literary. The empire the Delian League had been built to fight helps Sparta defeat the city that led the league. Persia is still a state with interests. It is not a symbol that left the stage in 479 BCE. Sparta wins the war and does not become a Greek nation either. She installs a favourable oligarchy and then finds, as hegemons find, that winning is not the same as ruling a world of poleis.

:::example Surrender, then a trial {#ex-pair}
Set 404 BCE beside 399 BCE. In the first year Athens surrenders. In the second, Socrates is tried and put to death. What kind of event is each, and what does the five-year gap stop you from saying?
:::solution
404 minus 399 is 5. The surrender is the military and political end of the Athenian empire and of this war. The trial is a legal event inside a polis that still has courts, after an oligarchy has come and democracy has returned. The gap stops the sentence “Socrates died with the war” and the sentence “the trial is why Athens fell”. The fall is earlier. The trial is later. They share a city. They do not share a meaning. Five years is enough time for the form of government to change more than once, which is already a reason not to treat 399 BCE as the moral of 431 BCE.
:::
:::

## Not the meaning of the war

Socrates’ trial is an Athenian jury of citizen men, in the restored democracy, condemning a citizen. The usual charges are impiety and corrupting the young. We will not quote the defence Plato later wrote, or the one Xenophon later wrote. Those texts take a side. They are not the minutes. The jury was using the institutions of [[greece/athenian-democracy]]: citizen men, a court, a vote. Philosophy is not the city’s policy. Most Athenians were not philosophers. Other Greek cities did not hold this trial. The death is about law, religion and politics in one polis after a lost war. It is not the Greek view of life, because there was no single Greek view waiting to be executed with him.

:::theorem The trial is not the meaning of the war {#thm-socrates}
Socrates’ trial and death in 399 BCE are an Athenian event, five years after the surrender of 404 BCE. They are not the meaning of the Peloponnesian War and not a summary of Greek thought. The war’s meaning, as far as this chapter claims one, is political: an empire of poleis lost a war against other poleis.
:::
:::proof
The dates are the first evidence. A trial in 399 BCE cannot be the cause of a surrender in 404 BCE, and it cannot be the content of campaigns that began in 431 BCE. The institutional evidence is the second. The court that condemns him is a democratic court of citizen men, not an assembly of the Hellenes and not the Thirty, whose oligarchic killings belong to the immediate aftermath of surrender and are a different crime. Plato and Xenophon write afterwards, in a quarrel about a man and a city. Their quarrel is part of Athenian intellectual history. Treating it as the war repeats the scale error of [[sources/scale]]: a true sentence about one trial becomes a false sentence about a generation of fighting.

What would count against the claim is evidence that the Greek states fought the war over Socrates, or that his death was decided by a common Greek authority, or that the surrender and the trial are the same act. The five years, the change of regime, and the ordinary subject of the war, empire and alliance, are the counter-evidence. What would count against a different mistake, the claim that the trial did not happen or did not matter in Athens, is the fact of 399 BCE itself. We are not making that mistake. The trial is real and local.
:::

- [ ] The second Persian invasion, continued without a gap from 479 BCE
- [x] A war among Greek states, with an Athenian empire on one side, from 431 to 404 BCE

:::solution

The third answer is the one the dates support.

One influential way of reading Thucydides takes the book as a warning that democratic Athens ruined itself by excess: too much daring, too little restraint after Pericles, a people that could not hold a consistent policy. You may report that as a reading of his narrative. You may not report it as the fact the war is. The assembly did make disastrous choices, Sicily among them, and Thucydides is harsh about later leaders. Sparta also chose a long war. Persian paymasters chose a fleet. Allies chose revolt. A tragedy about the excess of democracy is an argument about how to arrange those choices into a moral. [[#thm-greek]] is the checkable sentence. It does not need the moral, and the moral is not an upgrade of it.

Defeat does not close Greek political life. The polis of Athens continues, and so do the others. In the years after 404 BCE Greek states still fight, still make leagues, and still argue about constitutions. From the surrender to the battle of Chaeronea in 338 BCE, where Macedon defeats a Greek alliance, is 66 years. Those years are not a blank labelled decline. They are political life, and [[greece/alexander]] begins from the kingdom that wins in 338 BCE, not from a corpse of the polis. Philosophy continues in the same decades, including in a city that had executed one philosopher. The execution did not appoint philosophy as the city’s foreign policy, and it did not end either the philosophy or the policy.

:::example An invented moral {#ex-invented}
This paragraph is invented. It is not a source. “The Peloponnesian War, 431 to 404 BCE, was the tragic excess of democracy, and the death of Socrates in 399 BCE was its moral. Sicily, in a year we need not check, destroyed the city because the people were free.” Separate Thucydides’ kind of argument from the facts you can actually date.
:::solution
The brackets 431 and 404 BCE are real, and 399 BCE is a real trial. “Tragic excess of democracy” is a reading of Thucydides, or of readers of Thucydides, not a fact on the same level as the surrender. Sparta, Persia and the allies also acted. Sicily did not destroy the city by itself, and the war continued after the expedition failed. We do not add a year for it. “Because the people were free” forgets that the democracy was a body of adult citizen men, and that freedom in that sense included an empire. The death of Socrates is five years after the surrender, in a restored democracy, so it cannot be the moral that explains the defeat. The dated facts are a war among Greek states and a later trial. The tragedy is an optional plot.
:::
:::

## Objections, and a worked case

The objection from the eyewitness is the strongest. Thucydides saw the war, and he was serious. If he thought Athenian power and democratic unruliness were the heart of the story, a later reader who refuses the moral looks arrogant. The reply is not that he was a bad witness. The reply is that a witness can shape speeches, choose episodes, and still be the best continuous account we have. His judgement about Spartan fear is an argument we can accept as far as the empire was frightening. His portraits of the assembly are not a proof that democracy as a type caused the war. Oligarchic Sparta had to vote for it too, in whatever way Sparta voted. A book is evidence about a war and evidence about a writer. [[sources/evidence]] will not let the second replace the first.

A second objection says that leaving Sicily undated makes the lesson weaker than a textbook that prints a year. Weakness would be pretending the expedition did not happen. Printing a year the chronology does not list would be a different kind of weakness, a borrowed precision. You can teach a disaster without a numeral. You cannot teach a numeral you are not allowed to stand behind.

Worked case. An invented chronicle sentence says: “In 404 BCE the Greek nation surrendered to Sparta, philosophy died with Socrates, and the poleis ended.” Each clause has a true fragment and a false subject. Athens surrendered in 404 BCE, not a Greek nation. Socrates died in 399 BCE, not in the surrender, and philosophy did not die with him. The poleis continued, including Athens, through further wars down to Chaeronea in 338 BCE and beyond. The repair is almost entirely a change of nouns. The years, once put back in the right clauses, already do a large part of the work.

:::warning Not a tragedy of democracy, and not a clash of civilisations
Two myths meet in this war. One makes it a rehearsal for a later clash of civilisations, with Persia or “the East” as the real enemy. The belligerents of 431 BCE are Greek states. The other makes it a tragedy whose fact is the excess of democracy. That is a reading of Thucydides, and it should be labelled as his argument or as a later argument about him, not as the event. Athens was a democracy of some of the men, and it made fearful choices. Sparta was not that democracy, and it made a long war anyway. Neither myth is needed to say who fought, who surrendered, and who was tried five years later.
:::

:::history Brackets, then a trial
The Peloponnesian War begins in 431 BCE, with Pericles among the Athenian leaders named at the outbreak, and it ends with the surrender of Athens in 404 BCE. Socrates is tried and put to death in 399 BCE. The trial is an Athenian event five years after the surrender. It is not the meaning of the war.
:::

## Where this leads

The poleis survive the surrender, and a kingdom that is not a polis then defeats an alliance of them. [[greece/alexander]] starts from Chaeronea in 338 BCE and asks what Alexander’s death in 323 BCE ended. [[greece/athenian-democracy]] is the citizen body that fought on the Athenian side, exclusions included. [[greece/poleis]] is why a war among those bodies is not a national civil war. How to keep a historian’s moral from becoming a label is [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- The war begins in 431 BCE and Athens surrenders in 404 BCE. The span is 27 years. It is a war among Greek states.
- Athens fights as a polis that holds an empire. Sparta fights with allies. Some Greek poleis resist Athens and are destroyed. Syracuse is a Greek enemy in Sicily.
- The Sicilian expedition is part of the war. This chapter gives it no year and no troop numbers. The failure does not, by itself, end the war.
- Persian support helps Sparta later. That does not make the war a Persian war or a clash of civilisations.
- A plague strikes Athens during the war. No death total is stated. A disease is not a verdict on democracy.
- Socrates is tried and dies in 399 BCE, five years after the surrender, under the restored democracy. The trial is not the meaning of the war and not the Greek view of life.
- Defeat does not end the polis or Greek political life. From 404 BCE to Chaeronea in 338 BCE is 66 years of it.
:::

:::exercise Sicily in one sentence {level=2}
In one sentence, state what happened in the Sicilian expedition, using no year and no number of men. Then say what that sentence is still enough to prove about the kind of war this was.
:::solution
Athens sent a force against Syracuse, a Greek polis in Sicily, and the force was destroyed, without ending the war that continued until 404 BCE. That is enough to prove a piece of [[#thm-greek]]: the fighting was Greek against Greek, and a disaster inside it is not a war against Persia and not a single battle in 431 BCE. The missing year and the missing headcount are limits on precision. They are not limits on the identity of the enemy.

:::exercise Persian money {level=2}
Sparta builds a fleet with Persian support and then defeats Athens. A reader concludes that the Peloponnesian War was therefore a Persian war, like the war of 480 BCE. Where does that conclusion break?
:::solution
It breaks on who is fighting and when. In 480 BCE a Persian king campaigns against an alliance of poleis. In this war the armies and navies that open the conflict in 431 BCE are Greek, and the surrender in 404 BCE is a surrender of Athens to Sparta. Persian support is a state’s choice to fund one Greek side against another. It shows that the Achaemenid empire still has interests. It does not rename the belligerents. [[#thm-greek]] keeps the help and refuses the renaming.
:::
:::

:::exercise Not the Greek view of life {level=2}
Socrates is executed in Athens in 399 BCE. Why is it a mistake to call that execution the Greek view of life, or the meaning of the war that began in 431 BCE?
:::solution
The execution is one jury of Athenian citizen men, in one polis, on charges of impiety and corrupting the young, five years after that polis surrendered. Other Greek cities did not vote in the trial. Most Athenians were not philosophers, and philosophy is not the city’s policy. Plato and Xenophon write later defences that we do not quote and that are not minutes. A view of life shared by “the Greeks” is the national object [[greece/poleis]] already rejected. The war’s political content is empire and alliance, from 431 to 404 BCE. A trial after the end cannot be that content.
:::
:::

:::exercise A reading is not the fact {level=3}
A teacher says: “Thucydides shows that the war was a tragedy of democratic excess. Teach that as the fact, because he was contemporary.” Reply. You may give his argument a place. You must say what is still the fact if a reader rejects his moral.
:::hint
Keep the empire, the fear at Sparta, and the disastrous assembly decisions as discussable. Then name belligerents and choices that do not fit inside “excess of democracy” alone.
:::
:::solution
Thucydides’ argument can be taught as his. Athenian power was real, Sparta feared it, and the Athenian assembly made choices, Sicily among them, that he presents as a falling away from earlier restraint. That is a serious reading of one book. It is not the fact by itself, because a contemporary can shape speeches and still not convert a moral into a cause. Sparta chose a long war without being that democracy. Persian authorities chose to support a fleet. Allies chose to revolt. Those choices could have been otherwise. If a reader rejects the tragic moral, the fact that remains is [[#thm-greek]]: a war among Greek states from 431 to 404 BCE, an empire on one side, a surrender, and a polis that continues. The moral is optional. The brackets are not.
:::
:::

:::exercise The polis did not end {level=3}
Someone treats 404 BCE as the end of Greek political life, and 399 BCE as the funeral. Using the years 404, 399 and 338, explain what continued, and what kind of evidence would make the “end” sentence true.
:::hint
Order the three years first. Then ask what a continuing polis would look like, and what a real ending would have to remove.
:::
:::solution
The order is surrender in 404 BCE, a trial in a living city in 399 BCE, and a later battle, Chaeronea in 338 BCE, in which Macedon defeats an alliance of poleis that still exist to be defeated. From 404 to 338 is 66 years. Democracy returns in Athens after the oligarchy of the Thirty, though we do not date the return. Leagues and wars among Greek states continue. Socrates’ death is something the restored democracy does, which is possible only if political life is still there. The “end” sentence would be true if 404 BCE had abolished the poleis as communities, so that 338 BCE had nobody of that kind to fight, and if 399 BCE were a funeral of thought rather than a trial. The years show the opposite. What ended in 404 BCE was Athens’ empire and her war, not the Greek habit of politics.
:::
