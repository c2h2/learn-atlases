What was a polis, if it was not a Greek nation?

A later map of Europe likes to open with a people already united in everything but the paperwork. The Greek-speaking communities of the archaic age do not fit. They were many political bodies, not one body waiting to be named. They planted new communities across the sea, fought one another, and lived beside a power that was not Greek at all: the Persian empire, already the great state across the Aegean before any battle this atlas dates.

The question of this chapter is what to call one of those communities without smuggling in a nation. Shared speech, shared gods and shared games are part of the evidence. They are not a constitution. Colonies, leagues and tyrannies belong in the picture. A single Greek state does not. Two numerals are available as markers, and both have to be handled as traditions: 776 BCE for the Olympic games, and 594 BCE for Solon’s archonship. Neither numeral is a birth certificate.

The public timeline prints the same negative years as BC. Here the label is BCE. Both markers sit before the common era, so the gap between them is a plain subtraction, worked below.

## Citizens and a territory

:::definition Polis {#def-polis}
A **polis** (plural **poleis**) is a community of citizens together with a territory. The later phrase “city-state” points at the same unit. “Nation-state” does not. The territory commonly included a town and a countryside. Many people who lived and worked in that territory were not citizens: women of citizen families had a different standing from the men who held office, and outsiders, dependent labourers and enslaved people were not the citizen body.

A polis is not “Greece”, and it is not a stage on the way to a modern country. It could be small or large, on a coast or inland, democratic, oligarchic or under a tyrant. Size and the flavour of the constitution are not what make the unit. The citizen community and the territory are.
:::

Citizenship is local and inherited. A man who is a citizen in one polis is a foreigner in the next. There is no document that every Greek-speaking city has agreed to honour. What citizens typically share, and outsiders do not, is a bundle that varies by city: a claim on land, a place in the cult, access to courts, and a share in decisions where the constitution allows any share. Oligarchy is not a failed polis. It is one form a citizen body can take.

The countryside stops the picture from shrinking to temples. Citizens, outsiders and enslaved people all work the land, the households and the workshops. The town has magistrates, a place of assembly and shrines. None of that is a capital of the Hellenes. There is no such capital. Athens, Sparta, Corinth and Thebes are four poleis, not a stage set in which two of them stand for the rest.

Historians disagree about how far the polis type should be pushed. Some Greek-speaking communities were organised in looser regional ways, and Macedon, which the last chapter meets as a kingdom, is not a copy of Athens. That argument is about the edges of a category. It does not put a Greek state in the middle.

:::definition Colony {#def-colony}
A **colony** here is a new polis, founded from an older one, not a county of a Greek kingdom. The older city is a mother-city. The settlers become the citizen body of the new community. They may keep a founder’s cult, a story of origin and a tie of piety. They make their own laws and their own enemies. They are not an administrative district, because there is no Greek state for them to be a district of.
:::

Settlement ran to Sicily and southern Italy, the Black Sea, North Africa and trading posts in Egypt. Neighbours on the spot were often not Greek speakers, and the new polis dealt with them by trade, marriage, exploitation or war. A colony could also fight its own mother-city. Counties do not do that and remain counties. We will not date the foundations: the chronology does not list them. Syracuse can be named as a polis in Sicily without a foundation year. A precise year offered in another book stays a tradition until it meets the standard in [[sources/evidence]], a trace kept separate from the inference.

A **tyrant**, in the archaic usage, is a man who seized personal power in a polis. The word marks a route to power. It is not yet the later cartoon of a monster, and it is not a regular annual office. Some tyrants were remembered for building and festivals, some for violence against rivals. Both memories can be partisan, and later writers liked a morality play that ends in lawful government. We can use the category without the play. When a tyrant falls, the citizen community is still there. His career is not the biography of a nation.

Sparta shows how wide the definition is. It is a polis with two hereditary kings, a council of elders, annual magistrates called ephors, and an assembly. It also sits on the helots, an unfree population bound to the land of Laconia and Messenia. They are part of the structure, not a footnote to an ideal: a citizen minority living off unfree labour, often wary of campaigns that would leave home unsupervised. Sparta is not a democracy and not “the Dorian nation”. We will not number helots or full citizens. No such figure is settled in the chronology, and a vivid number would only counterfeit precision.

Athens, here, is the other worked polis, not the destination of the archaic age. The traditional date of Solon’s archonship is 594 BCE. The tradition credits him with a cancellation of debts and with ranking political rights by wealth, and later Athenians told that as the start of their own lawfulness. A city’s story about its lawgiver is evidence of memory and conflict. It is not the minutes of a session, and it is not a Hellenic assembly. [[sources/time-and-names]] is why a date can carry a claim. This one carries “Solon held an Athenian office then”, and even that is traditional.

The democracy associated with Cleisthenes is a different event, often dated 508/507 BCE, and it is the subject of [[greece/athenian-democracy]]. We name it here only to break a plot. Solon’s traditional year does not grow, by the mere passage of time, into a democratic constitution for the Greeks. Between a traditional archonship and a later reform of Athenian tribes there is a change of generation, a tyranny in between, and still no Greek state.

## What is not a state

:::definition Nation-state {#def-nation}
A **nation-state**, as this atlas uses the phrase, is a modern claim that one people and one state coincide: a single citizenship, and a government that speaks for that people. The phrase is ours, not an archaic title. We use it as a test. A polis fails the test because its citizen body is only a fragment of the Greek-speaking world. The Greek-speaking world fails the test because it has no single government.
:::

People did have a wider name. They could call themselves Hellenes, and the games could refuse competitors who did not count as Greek. That is a real boundary at a sanctuary, an ethnic and religious test for a festival. Who counted was sometimes disputed. A quarrel about eligibility is not a census, and it is not a ministry.

:::theorem There was no Greek state {#thm-no-state}
Shared language, cult and games did not amount to a Greek nation-state. There was no single citizenship, no single body of magistrates and no single peacetime army. An alliance made for a particular war is not that missing state. The claim does not say that Greek speakers had nothing in common. They had a great deal in common. Common culture is not a constitution.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the political map, as far as archaeology, inscriptions and later narratives let us draw it. Poleis make war on each other and peace in their own names. A citizen of Corinth is not thereby a citizen of Thebes. Sparta and Athens run different institutions in the same decades. Colonies act as separate states, including towards their mother-cities. When many poleis act together, as they will against Persia, they do it by alliance, with absentees and a command that has to be negotiated. That is an alliance, not a state.

Greek was a family of dialects, not a statute. A sanctuary can draw visitors from many cities because it is nobody’s capital. What would count against [[#thm-no-state]] is a near-contemporary trace of magistrates whose orders bind Greek-speaking communities as such, in peacetime, with one treasury and one citizenship those communities obey. A festival, a heroic poem or a modern map coloured one shade does not count. If that government appeared, the claim would have to be given up. It has not appeared.
:::

:::quiz
Which of these would be evidence of a Greek state, rather than evidence of a shared culture?
- [ ] A festival at Olympia that many cities attend
- [ ] A family of Greek dialects spoken around the Aegean
- [x] Magistrates whose orders bind the poleis as such, in peacetime, under one citizenship
- [ ] A myth in which Greek heroes fight on the same side
:::solution
A state, on the test in [[#def-nation]], needs a government and a citizenship, not only a likeness. Games, dialects and heroic myth are exactly the things [[#thm-no-state]] says are real and insufficient. The third option is the kind of trace the argument says is missing. If that trace turned up, the claim would fail.
:::
:::

The ethnic objection is serious and still insufficient. Hellenes could name themselves and keep others out of the games. That boundary is real, and it is a rule of a sanctuary, not a ministry. You can be a Hellene and a foreigner in the next polis, with no vote and no land. [[sources/scale]] is the warning against promoting a true sentence about a festival into a false sentence about a state.

:::theorem A league is still not a nation {#thm-league}
A league of poleis is an agreement among states, not a fusion of them into one people. Sparta’s Peloponnesian alliances are of this kind. So, later, is the league Athens will lead. Members keep their own citizenship. They can try to leave, and they can be forced to stay. Force shows power. It does not show that a nation has quietly come into existence.
:::
:::proof
Leagues here are built from oaths, leadership in war and contributions. They have a leading city, not a common citizen roll. A man does not stop being a Corinthian because his city follows Sparta. Leaving is argued as alliance and fear, not as treason to a nation. Coercion, where it happens, is a strong polis pressing a weaker one. An empire can grow from that. A nation is not the same growth.

What would count against the claim is a league that abolishes member citizenships and replaces their magistrates, so the poleis cease to be political communities. A common coin, a common shrine or one commander for a war is not enough. If a later chapter shows Athens squeezing allies, that is an empire inside a Greek-speaking world, not a nation waking up.
:::

## Two traditions and a long gap

The chronology allows two early numerals and tells us how to read them. The year 776 BCE is the traditional date of the first Olympic games, a later tradition, not a documented birth of a Greek nation. The year 594 BCE is the traditional date of Solon’s archonship. “Traditional” means a year Greeks later agreed to tell, from lists that were themselves compiled. It does not mean we have the minutes.

:::example The gap between the two traditions {#ex-gap}
The traditional Olympic date is 776 BCE. The traditional date of Solon’s archonship is 594 BCE. How many years lie between them? Both dates are BCE. There is no year 0 on this span. (Enter the reasoning in your notes; the result is a number of years.)
:::solution
Both years lie before the common era, so this is not a span from a BCE year to a CE year. The extra adjustment used when a span crosses the era boundary does not apply. There is no year 0 to insert or remove. Subtract the later year-number from the earlier: 776 minus 594 is 182. Check by addition: 594 plus 182 is 776. The traditions are 182 years apart.
:::
:::

That gap of 182 years is the point of the figure. Nothing in it fills the years with a nation. Colonies, tyrannies, wars between neighbours and a Persian empire already beside the eastern cities all sit in a gap the dots do not narrate. Two markers are not a claim that only two things happened.

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented for this lesson. It is not a source. “In 776 BCE the Greek nation held its first games, and in 594 BCE Solon wrote the constitution of that nation.” Read the sentence against the limits of the two dates. What, if anything, could be rescued from it, and what is doing the damage?
:::solution
Only the numerals can be rescued, and they are traditional. The year 776 BCE dates games at Olympia, not the birth of a nation. The year 594 BCE dates an Athenian archonship, not a Hellenic constitution. Solon is not a magistrate of all Greek speakers. The sentence keeps real years and changes the subject. Promoting it would take a near-contemporary trace that said these things, and [[sources/evidence]] would still separate that trace from the inference. A later chronicle’s pride would be a moral, not a fact it witnessed. We do not have the trace. The sentence stays invented.
:::
:::

:::example Two poleis, side by side {#ex-pair}
Set Sparta beside Athens in the archaic age. Sparta has kings, ephors and helots. Athens has, among its traditions, Solon’s archonship in 594 BCE. Both are poleis. Does that mean they share a constitution, or a Greek state?
:::solution
It means they are the same kind of unit, a citizen community with a territory, and not the same community. Their institutions differ. Solon’s year is an Athenian tradition, not a Spartan one, and not a national one. Sharing the label “polis” is like sharing the label “city-state”. It does not merge the citizenships. A reader who moves from “both are poleis” to “therefore one Greek state” has changed the scale, which is the mistake [[#thm-no-state]] blocks.
:::
:::

## Persia is already next door

A history of the polis that stays inside a sealed “Greek world”, and lets Persia enter only as a monster when the wars begin, has already decided that Greek politics were self-contained. They were not. The Achaemenid empire, the Persian royal power of which Darius and Xerxes will be the kings in the next chapter, is the great state on the eastern side of the Aegean. Greek-speaking cities in Ionia have to deal with it as subjects, neighbours or rebels before the first battle we date.

We will not invent the year in which that subjection began. It is not in the chronology, and this chapter does not need a substitute numeral. The fact we need is prior presence. Marathon, in 490 BCE, is a clash with an empire that already has politics of its own: a king, satraps, tribute, garrisons, and Greek subjects. It is not a first meeting between a finished nation and a formless East. [[greece/persian-wars]] tells the wars. Our only job here is to refuse to save the empire for a contrast.

The same refusal changes the colonies. A colony is not a piece of Europe planted in a void. It is a new polis in a crowded landscape: an empire to the east, and other peoples to the west and north who do not become a prelude because Greek speakers arrived. The polis is a local answer to that landscape, not a seed of a continent. [[sources/naming-europe]] is the longer argument against reading a civilisation back out of a modern name.

:::example Sort three objects {#ex-sort}
Sort these three into the categories of this chapter. First, Syracuse, a Greek foundation in Sicily. Second, Olympia, the sanctuary of the games whose traditional start is 776 BCE. Third, a satrapy of the Persian empire on the Asian side of the Aegean. Which, if any, is a polis? Which is a sanctuary rather than a capital? Which is a unit of a non-Greek empire?
:::solution
Syracuse is a colony and therefore a new polis, with its own citizens, not a county of a Greek nation and not a county of its mother-city. Olympia is a sanctuary and a festival, shared in the sense that many cities come there. It is not a capital, and the traditional date does not make it one. A Persian satrapy is a unit of the Achaemenid empire, a real state with its own chain of command. It is not a polis, and it is not “the East” as a mood. The sort fails if Syracuse is called a province, if Olympia is called a capital, or if the satrapy is called a blank space outside history until a Greek army arrives.
:::
:::

:::quiz
A textbook says: “The poleis were counties of the Greek nation, and Persia was outside the story until the wars.” Which correction matches this chapter?
- [ ] Both halves are right, because a shared language is a state and Persia was a distant symbol
- [ ] The poleis were counties, but Persia was already a great power next door
- [x] A polis was a citizen community with a territory; a colony was a new polis; Persia was already the neighbouring empire
- [ ] There were no poleis, only the Persian empire and its Greek servants
:::solution
The textbook sentence fails both tests this chapter sets. [[#def-polis]] and [[#def-colony]] refuse the county analogy, and [[#thm-no-state]] refuses the nation. The Persian empire is already the great power beside the Aegean, with Greek subjects, before the wars of the next chapter. The fourth option swings too far: poleis are real. They are simply not counties.
:::
:::

## Objections, and a worked case

Common action is a stronger objection than the ethnic name. Greek speakers did fight under shared banners, consult oracles and call other peoples barbarians. A critic says that if this is not nationhood, no ancient people could pass the test, so the test is only a modern trap.

The test is modern on purpose. We do not claim that an archaic Greek rejected the phrase “nation-state”. We claim that a modern drawing of one state around the Hellenes uses a later object, and that the evidence will not hold it. A shared campaign is an alliance. An oracle can be obeyed or ignored city by city. “Barbarian” marks speech and custom. It does not appoint a cabinet. The anachronism would be blaming them for failing a modern exam. [[#def-nation]] stops the exam being written backwards onto them.

Sparta’s leadership for more than one summer is the other objection. Duration is a power a leading polis can have. It is a nation only if the followers stop being their own citizen communities. They did not. The later war against Athens is a war among poleis, which is [[#thm-league]], not a civil war inside one realm.

Worked case. This paragraph is invented, not a source. “After the games began in 776 BCE, the Greeks matured as a nation. Solon, in 594 BCE, gave that nation constitutional form. The colonies were its counties overseas, and the tyrants were deviations from the national character. Persia can wait.”

Each clause fails a different check. The games are a festival tradition, and “matured” is a plot. Solon’s year is Athenian. Colonies are new poleis under [[#def-colony]]. “Deviations” assumes the national character it needs. Persia is already beside the story. Repair it by changing the subjects, not the numerals: a festival, an Athenian tradition, new citizen communities, local seizures of power, a neighbouring empire. [[#thm-no-state]] would reopen only if a contemporary inscription called those colonies districts of one Greek authority. A school map does not.

:::warning The rise from Homer to democracy
The usual myth is a single plot: poems, then games, then lawgivers, then a nation, then democracy as the point of the whole. It fails three ways at once. The poems are not dated in this chronology, and we will not invent a year for them; even if we could, a poem is not a constitution. The dates we do have, 776 BCE and 594 BCE, are traditions about a festival and an Athenian office, not chapters of one national life. Democracy is one later constitution of one polis, narrow in the ways [[greece/athenian-democracy]] will state, and it is not the purpose of Sparta, of a colony that never adopted it, or of the Persian empire next door. A plot that needs every earlier century to be a step towards Athens has chosen Athens first and arranged the evidence afterwards.
:::

:::history Traditions, not a birthday
The year 776 BCE is the traditional date of the first Olympic games, and it is a later tradition, not a documented birth of a Greek nation. The year 594 BCE is the traditional date of Solon’s archonship, an Athenian office in the later telling. The long gap between those traditions is full of poleis, colonies, tyrannies and a Persian empire already in place. It is not the childhood of a state.
:::

## Where this leads

The wars against that empire are next. [[greece/persian-wars]] keeps the poleis as poleis and asks what happens in 490 and 480 BCE once Persia is treated as a state with politics of its own. The method here is the method of [[sources/evidence]], [[sources/time-and-names]] and [[sources/scale]]: a tradition is not a witness, a date can carry a claim, and a true sentence about a festival is not a true sentence about a continent. Nothing in this chapter has produced a civilisation.

:::summary
- A polis is a citizen community plus a territory, not a nation-state and not “Greece”.
- Many people inside the territory were not citizens, and citizenship in one polis did not travel to the next.
- A colony is a new polis, not a county of a mother-city or of a Greek kingdom.
- Tyrannies and leagues sit inside this world of poleis; neither of them is a nation coming into being.
- The years 776 BCE and 594 BCE are traditions, 182 years apart, and neither founds a state.
- Shared language, cult and games are real, and they are not a government.
- Persia is already the great power beside the Aegean, not a symbol saved up for the next chapter.
:::

:::exercise Olympia is not a capital {level=2}
Someone says: “Olympia was the capital of Greece, because the games began there in 776 BCE and all Greeks came.” Using [[#def-polis]] and [[#thm-no-state]], explain what Olympia was, and what the traditional date does not prove.
:::solution
Olympia was a sanctuary with a festival. Many poleis had dealings with it, and a rule of admission could mark who counted as Greek for the games. A capital would be the seat of a government of the poleis. There is no such government. The year 776 BCE is a traditional date for the festival, not a record that a state opened for business. Attendance at games is the scale of a sanctuary. A capital is the scale of a state. The sentence promotes one into the other.
:::
:::

:::exercise Spartan kings {level=2}
Sparta has hereditary kings, a council of elders, ephors, an assembly and a large unfree population. A reader infers that Sparta was therefore the Greek nation, with the other cities as its dependents in a single realm. Where does that inference break?
:::solution
Sparta is a polis, one citizen community with a territory and a harsh order resting on helots. Kingship there is a Spartan institution, not a crown of the Hellenes. Other poleis keep their own citizenship and their own magistrates. Allies may follow Sparta in war and still be poleis, which is the distinction in [[#thm-league]]. Dependants who remain political communities are allies or subjects under pressure, not counties of a nation. The inference breaks by treating one city’s constitution as a continental state.
:::
:::

:::exercise Colonies at war {level=2}
Two mother-cities found colonies. A generation later the colonies fight each other, and one of them fights its own mother-city. A summary still calls the colonies “overseas counties of the Greeks”. What does the fighting show, and what would the summary need before the word “county” became accurate?
:::solution
The fighting shows separate political wills. A county does not wage war on the capital as a foreign power, and two counties of one state do not go to war as if they were states. Each colony fits [[#def-colony]]: a new citizen body. The summary would need evidence that a single Greek authority taxed them, appointed their magistrates and treated their wars as rebellion inside one jurisdiction. What we have instead is poleis using force in their own names. The word “county” adds a state the evidence does not contain.
:::
:::

:::exercise The childhood of the nation {level=3}
A writer accepts your subtraction, agrees that 182 years separate 776 BCE from 594 BCE, and still says: “That gap is the childhood of the Greek nation. Shared games prove a shared state, and Solon is the nation’s lawgiver, dated a little later.” Reply in a short argument. Separate what is true from what is a plot.
:::hint
You do not need a new year-number. Ask what a festival authorises, what an Athenian tradition authorises, and what evidence would make “nation” the right noun.
:::
:::solution
The arithmetic can be true and the plot false. The year 776 BCE authorises a traditional festival date. The year 594 BCE authorises a traditional Athenian archonship. A gap of 182 years authorises nothing about maturation, because “childhood” assumes the nation whose growth it claims to measure. Shared games prove a shared practice and a sanctuary that many cities used. They do not prove magistrates, a treasury or a citizenship, which is what [[#thm-no-state]] requires and does not find. Solon is a figure in one city’s memory. Calling him the nation’s lawgiver moves him from Athens to a state that is not there. The true remainder is: a festival tradition, a later Athenian tradition, and a long period of many poleis. The evidence that would rescue the writer is a peacetime government of the Hellenes attested between those dates. Traditions about games and about Solon are not that government.
:::
:::
