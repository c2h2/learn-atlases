Who was a citizen in democratic Athens, and who was not?

The question is the centre of the chapter, not an apology added after the glory. Athenian democracy was a real set of institutions, and it was narrow. Cleisthenes’ reforms are often dated 508/507 BCE. We use 508 BCE. Pericles’ citizenship law is often dated 451/450 BCE. We use 451 BCE. After that law, the person who votes, judges and holds the allotted offices is an adult man with two citizen parents. Women, enslaved people and metics do not. The assembly, the council, the courts and the use of the lot are real. They are not a written constitution, not a modern parliament, and not the birth of Western freedom. No population of Attica will be stated. The chronology does not give one, and a court speech is not a census.

## What the institutions were

:::definition Athenian democracy {#def-democracy}
**Athenian democracy** is the rule of assembled adult citizen men in Athens, through mass meetings, a council, large courts and offices filled mostly by lot. In the form associated with Cleisthenes it is often dated 508/507 BCE. In the form narrowed by descent it is marked by the citizenship law often dated 451/450 BCE. It is not a document that founds a modern state, and it is not a name for everyone who lived in Attica.
:::

The reforms sit after a tyranny, the Peisistratid house, and after a struggle among leading families in which Sparta intervened. We will not date the tyrant’s fall. It is not in the chronology. What 508 BCE marks, on the usual dating, is a new organisation of the citizen body. Local districts, the demes, became the units through which a man was identified as a citizen. New tribes cut across the older regional factions of coast, inland and city, so that a following built on one region was harder to assemble. A council, chosen by lot from those tribes, prepared business for the assembly. Ostracism, a vote with potsherds that sent a man into exile for a fixed term of years, belongs to the same political world: a device for removing a powerful citizen without a criminal trial. We will not count the votes required. The device matters as a kind of power the citizen men held over one another.

In the decades after 508 BCE the older council, the Areopagus, lost ground to the assembly and the courts. Parts of that shift have dates this chronology does not list, so we give none. The direction is enough. The democracy became, more fully, the rule of the men who turned up and of the juries drawn from them. That development is not a straight line from Solon’s traditional archonship in 594 BCE, which [[greece/poleis]] has already refused to treat as a national constitution. From 594 BCE to 508 BCE is a long gap with a tyranny in it. From 508 BCE to 451 BCE is another gap, of a different law.

The assembly was the citizen men present that day. They decided by majority. They were not representatives sent to speak for households, women or metics. Whoever stayed home had no proxy. The ordinary citizen man, if he comes, has a vote equal to a rich man’s on that motion. Most inhabitants of Attica are not in the room.

Most offices were filled by lot, for a short term, and the holder could be examined at the end. Ruling, inside the citizen body, was not reserved to experts or to families. The generals were elected and could be re-elected, because the city wanted to choose who led a campaign. Election is the exception. Pay for office and for attendance let poorer citizen men serve. We will not state a sum. Pay compensates people already inside the body. It does not address the people the law keeps outside.

There were laws, and many were inscribed. There were procedures for accusing a speaker of having proposed something contrary to the laws. That is a check inside the democracy. It is not a supreme court reading a single founding document, and the laws were not a written constitution in the modern sense. The assembly’s decisions are the city’s political acts. A stone is evidence. It is not a constitution waiting to be copied by later parliaments.

:::theorem These institutions are not a modern parliament and not the birth of Western freedom {#thm-not-parliament}
Assembly, council, courts and lot made a real democracy of adult citizen men. They did not make a parliament of representatives, a written constitution, or the origin of Western freedom. The claim limits itself twice: the institutions are not a myth, and the modern honour paid to them is not a description of 508 BCE or of 451 BCE.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the institutions themselves, as the Aristotelian *Constitution of the Athenians*, surviving decrees and the practice of the courts let us reconstruct them. Presence, lot, short terms and mass juries are a different machine from a chamber of elected representatives legislating under a superior constitutional text. Later Europeans admired Athens and built parliaments by other routes. Admiration is a fact about those Europeans. It does not move their institutions back into the fifth century.

“Western freedom” fails for a further reason. The West is a modern geographical moral, of the kind [[sources/naming-europe]] refuses to treat as an ancient actor. Freedom, in the Persian-war slogan, meant not being ruled by the Persian king. Inside Athens it meant the political life of a defined group of men, drawing on an empire over other poleis, as [[greece/persian-wars]] describes. The phrase changes the subject.

What would count against the claim is a text of the period in which Athenian offices are filled by representatives of all adult inhabitants, women and metics included, under a single constitutional document those inhabitants can invoke against the assembly. Decrees, jury pay and the lot do not become that text by being impressive. What would count against the reality of the democracy is evidence that the assembly and the courts did not in fact decide. Elite influence was real. It is not the same as the absence of the institutions.
:::

## The law of 451 BCE

:::definition The participant after 451 {#def-citizen}
After the citizenship law often dated 451/450 BCE, the **participant** in Athenian democracy is an adult man with two citizen parents. That is a rule of descent and of sex. It is not a count of persons. Women of citizen families can be required by the rule, because a son’s status depends on his mother, and still have no vote. Enslaved people and metics are outside the participating body whether or not they are adult and male.
:::

Before the law, the usual account makes an Athenian father the main descent test, and marriages with women of other cities were possible. The law tightens the test to two citizen parents. Historians disagree about the motive: those marriages, the privileges of an imperial city, or a moment in Pericles’ career. The effect is clearer than the motive, and the effect is what the diagram shows. We do not invent a single private reason and call it the law.

A **metic** is a free resident foreigner, often from another Greek polis, registered in Athens, liable to a tax we will not price, and typically in need of a citizen sponsor. Metics could be rich. They worked as traders and craftspeople. In the usual account they served in war. They did not sit in the assembly as metics. A grant of citizenship to a named foreigner is a decree, and it confirms the boundary: if the status had been ordinary, the city would not have had to confer it. An enslaved person is not a metic. Enslaved people were property in law. They worked in households, on farms, in workshops and in the silver mines of Attica. We will not describe that work, and we will not number them. Freedom, if it came, was not citizenship. A freed person did not thereby become a man with two citizen parents.

Women of citizen families are the hardest case, and the place where a glory-story most wants a footnote. They were not hidden from every public act. Priestesses held religious office. Festivals required the daughters and wives of citizen men. After 451 BCE a citizen mother is the condition of a citizen son. That is a political fact about her body and her parentage, and it gives her no vote, no lot-drawn office and no seat on a jury. Some historians reserve the word “citizen” for that descent status and then add that political rights were male. The vocabulary is a real argument. It does not move her into the shaded region of the figure. This lesson uses “participant” for the acts, and [[#def-citizen]] for the rule. Religious office is public. It is not the democracy.

The law reaches into households, which is why it is not a small electoral technicality. Court speeches return to whether someone’s mother was Athenian. That genre is accusation, not a register from which a population of Attica could be read. A silence about numbers is not a silence about the rule. The rule is in the law. The numbers are not in our chronology.

caption: The shaded region is the intersection the lesson treats as the participating body after 451 BCE. It is a diagram of a rule, not a count of people.

Read the labels in order. Adult, citizen parents, and a man: all three, or no participation. A citizen woman is adult and of citizen parents, and she is not a man, so she is outside the shading. A metic man is adult and male, and he lacks citizen parents. An enslaved man may share that unshaded corner with him. The figure cannot tell those two apart, because both fail the same condition of the rule. That limit is a virtue. The diagram was never a census of statuses. Children of citizen parents fail “adult” until they are enrolled. We will not invent an age of majority as a numeral. Enrolment in the deme is the city’s own gate, and it was a gate for sons.

:::example Fifty-seven years of change {#ex-span}
Cleisthenes’ reforms are often dated 508 BCE. The citizenship law is often dated 451 BCE. How many years separate them, and what does the gap forbid you to say about “the” Athenian citizen?
:::solution
Both dates are BCE, so subtract: 508 minus 451 is 57. Check: 451 plus 57 is 508. The double dates 508/507 and 451/450 are how the ancient year-pairs are often written. We still use 508 and 451 as this chronology does. The gap forbids a single birthday. The man who participates after 451 BCE has to satisfy a descent rule that is not identical with the tribal reform of 508 BCE. Fifty-seven years is time for a tyranny’s aftermath to become an imperial democracy, and for the city to decide that two parents, not one, guard the privileges.
:::
:::

:::example Three invented people {#ex-three}
These three people are invented for the example. They are not a census. First, an adult man enrolled in a deme, with an Athenian father and an Athenian mother, in the years after 451 BCE. Second, his adult sister, with the same parents. Third, an adult metic who has lived in Athens for years and serves when the city calls. Who is in the shaded intersection of the figure, and who is outside which circle?
:::solution
Only the first is in the intersection: adult, two citizen parents, and a man. The sister is adult and of citizen parents. She fails the condition labelled Man. Her status matters to the law, because a son of hers could inherit participation through her, and she still does not participate. The metic is adult and a man. He fails citizen parents. Service in war does not move him across. If a decree later names him a citizen, that decree is an exception granted by the body he is not yet in. None of the three is a population statistic. The sort is a reading of [[#def-citizen]].
:::
:::

:::example Two laws, side by side {#ex-pair}
Set 508 BCE beside 451 BCE. One date is associated with demes, tribes and a council chosen by lot. The other is a citizenship law about parentage. Are they the same reform, and is either the founding of Western freedom?
:::solution
They are two Athenian reforms, 57 years apart, answering different questions. Cleisthenes’ usual date reorganises an existing citizen body so that region and family followings work less easily. Pericles’ usual date decides who may enter that body in the next generation. Treating them as one founding merges a tribal reform with a descent test. Treating either as the birth of Western freedom adds a modern map and a rights-language the stones do not carry. Side by side, the dates show a democracy that changes its own boundary. A democracy that can tighten the boundary is not a timeless idea. It is a city using its assembly.
:::
:::

## Exclusions are the design

It is tempting to teach the assembly first and the exclusions afterwards, as a regret. That order is the myth of a glory with a footnote. The law of 451 BCE is about who the assembly is. Empire makes the question sharper, not softer. Allied contributions, described in [[greece/persian-wars]], fed the city’s political pay and its public building. Who shares a city that rich is a political fight. Closing the parentage rule is one way the citizen men fought it. You can admire the lot and still see that the lot was never offered to the metic who paid tax, the woman whose son the law scrutinised, or the enslaved worker in a mine. Admiration that requires those people to be blurred is not admiration of this city. It is admiration of a different one.

The courts are part of the same design. Large juries of citizen men heard cases that could ruin a politician or kill a defendant. In 399 BCE those courts will condemn Socrates. [[greece/peloponnesian-war]] treats that trial as an Athenian event after a defeat, not as the meaning of Greek thought. It belongs in this chapter only as a limit. A democracy of citizen men can destroy one of its own. That fact does not sum up the institution, and it does not widen the institution to the people who could not sit on the jury.

:::theorem The exclusions are the citizen body, not a footnote {#thm-exclusion}
Women, enslaved people and metics are outside participation after 451 BCE by the way the body is defined, not by an accident beside an otherwise universal democracy. Remove the exclusions and you are describing a city the law does not describe.
:::
:::proof
The law is the evidence: two citizen parents, and a political practice in which the assembly, the council, the juries and the allotted offices are male. Decrees that honour a foreign individual by granting citizenship show the same boundary from the other side. Religious offices held by women show a public role that systematically stops short of the vote. Slavery is legal and ordinary, in households and in the silver industry, which means the leisure and the revenue of citizen politics sit on unfree labour. None of this is an inference from silence alone. Where the sources are silent, they are silent about numbers and about the speech of the excluded, not about the rule. [[sources/evidence]] says a silence is not evidence that a person had no mind. It is evidence about who was asked. The excluded had minds. They did not have this franchise.

What would count against the claim is a decree or a credible narrative in which women as a class, or metics as a class, or enslaved people, sit in the assembly or on the juries after 451 BCE. A priestess does not count. A metic in the fleet does not count. A grant to one named man does not count as a class. If such a class inclusion turned up, [[#def-citizen]] would have to be rewritten. It has not turned up. What would also count against a cruder version of the claim, one that said women were socially invisible, is the religious record. We do not make that cruder claim. Exclusion from the democracy and presence in cult are both true.
:::

:::quiz
Which description of the law of 451 BCE is accurate?
- [ ] It gave the vote to every adult in Attica
- [ ] It is a written constitution of Western freedom
- [x] It requires two citizen parents, and participation remains with adult men
- [ ] It abolished slavery in the silver mines
:::solution
The law tightens descent. It does not open the assembly to women, metics or enslaved people, and it does not touch the legality of slavery. [[#thm-not-parliament]] blocks the constitutional and “Western” readings. The third option is the rule in [[#def-citizen]].

:::example An invented founding {#ex-invented}
This sentence is invented. It is not a source. “In 508 BCE Athens invented Western freedom by giving power to the people of Attica.” What, if anything, is dated correctly, and what has been added?
:::solution
The year 508 BCE is the usual date we use for Cleisthenes’ reforms, often written 508/507. That is the part that touches the chronology. “Invented Western freedom” adds a modern geography and a modern moral. “The people of Attica” adds the inhabitants who were not participant citizen men: women, metics, enslaved people, and, after 451 BCE, anyone who fails the two-parent rule. Even in 508 BCE the new tribes organise citizens, not the whole population. The sentence keeps a real year and replaces the citizen body with a later idea. A repair says: in 508 BCE Athenian citizen men were reorganised into a democracy that later, in 451 BCE, defined them by two citizen parents.
:::
:::

## Objections, and a worked case

The objection from vocabulary is the one to take seriously. If Athenian Greek can call a woman of citizen parents a member of the city, then a diagram labelled with “Man” looks like a modern insult projected backwards. The reply is in the acts, not in a refusal to hear the word. Descent-status and a vote are different. [[#def-citizen]] says she can be essential to her son’s status and absent from the assembly. Using “participant” for the second fact keeps the first fact visible. A diagram that shaded her in would be the anachronism, because it would award her a vote the courts and the decrees do not record.

A second objection says that lot and pay make the citizen men so equal that the exclusions are a small ancient habit, shared with every other city, and therefore not worth the centre of the lesson. Shared with other cities is not the same as small. Athens claimed, in its own political speech, a special freedom. The centre of the lesson is the gap between that claim and the body that enjoyed it. Other poleis were also narrow. That does not make this polis wide. And the habit was not only ancient custom sitting beside the democracy. The law of 451 BCE is a democratic decision to narrow the body further, taken in the assembly the citizen men already controlled.

Worked case. Three sentences, invented, are offered as a caption for the figure. “The shaded region is the people of Athens. Everyone outside it was almost a citizen. The diagram counts the population of Attica after 451 BCE.”

The shaded region is a rule, adult and two citizen parents and a man, not “the people” in the modern sense and not in the loose sense of inhabitants. People outside it were not almost citizens. A metic’s nearness is social and economic, and the law still excludes him. An enslaved person is not almost a participant. A citizen woman is close in a different way, through descent, and she is still outside participation. The diagram counts nobody. Any caption that ends in a population has left the evidence. The repair is the caption on the figure: a rule, not a count.

:::warning Not the birth of Western freedom
The usual myth says Athens invented Western freedom, and then adds, if pressed, that women and slaves were of course excluded, as if the exclusion were a footnote under a true sentence. The sentence is not true. Freedom from the Persian king was a real slogan in the previous chapter’s wars. Democratic institutions among citizen men were real. Neither slogan nor institutions included the inhabitants of Attica, and neither founded a Western political tradition sitting in 508 BCE or in 451 BCE. A footnote that apologises for a glory has already chosen the glory. This chapter chooses the boundary the law states.
:::

:::history A reform, then a tighter rule
Cleisthenes’ reforms are often dated 508/507 BCE. They reorganised Athenian citizen men into demes and tribes and opened the road to assembly, council and lot. Pericles’ citizenship law is often dated 451/450 BCE. After it, participation requires two citizen parents and is practised by adult men. Women, enslaved people and metics remain outside that practice.
:::

## Where this leads

The citizen men defined here are the Athenians who hold an empire and then fight a long war against other Greek states. [[greece/peloponnesian-war]] begins in 431 BCE and ends with surrender in 404 BCE. It is not a verdict on “the Greek mind”, and Socrates’ death in 399 BCE is one use of these courts, not a summary of them. [[greece/persian-wars]] is the victory and the league that made the question of sharing privileges urgent. How a source differs from the inference you draw, especially when the source is a speech in court, is [[sources/evidence]].

:::summary
- Athenian democracy is assembly, council, courts and lot among adult citizen men. It is not a parliament and not a written constitution.
- Cleisthenes’ reforms are often dated 508/507 BCE. We use 508 BCE. They reorganise citizen men. They do not enfranchise Attica.
- From 508 BCE to the citizenship law is 57 years. The law, often dated 451/450 and here 451, requires two citizen parents.
- After that law the participant is an adult man with two citizen parents. Historians disagree about the motive. The effect is the rule.
- Women of citizen families can transmit status and hold religious office, and they do not vote. Metics can be free, taxed and armed, and they do not vote. Enslaved people are property in law.
- The venn figure is that rule, not a population. No population of Attica is stated, because none is settled here.
- The institutions are not the birth of Western freedom. The exclusions are the design of the citizen body, not a footnote.
:::

:::exercise Not a parliament {level=2}
A reader says the Athenian assembly was a parliament, because the people debated and voted. Using [[#thm-not-parliament]], what is the difference that matters?
:::solution
A parliament in the later sense is a body of representatives, usually elected, speaking for a wider public, often under a written constitution that stands above a single meeting. The Athenian assembly was the citizen men who were present. They were not sent to represent women, metics or enslaved people, and they did not sit under one founding document. Most offices were allotted, not elected. The likeness is that both meet and vote. The difference is who is in the room and what kind of machine the vote belongs to. Calling it a parliament borrows a modern honour and spends it on the wrong object.
:::

:::exercise The metic in the fleet {level=2}
An invented metic serves in the Athenian fleet, pays the metic tax and never receives a grant of citizenship. A commentator says his service “makes him a citizen in practice”. Answer using the law of 451 BCE and the figure.
:::solution
Service and tax are obligations the city could impose on a free outsider. They are not the two-parent rule, and they are not enrolment as a citizen man. On the figure he can be adult and male and still fall outside citizen parents. “In practice” is doing the damage: it treats social usefulness as the franchise. A decree could have made him a citizen. The invented case says no decree did. He remains a metic, which is [[#def-citizen]] from the outside.
:::

:::exercise Why no population {level=2}
You are asked for the population of Attica under Pericles. The sources you have been offered are a citizenship law, jury speeches about parentage, and decrees. Why does this chapter refuse a figure?
:::solution
The chronology gives no population, and the sources named are not a census. The law states a rule. Speeches accuse particular people of failing it. Decrees grant status to named individuals. None of them counts inhabitants. A figure would be a false precision, and it would tempt a reader to treat the venn shading as a headcount. [[sources/evidence]] separates a trace from an inference. The allowed inference is the shape of the citizen body. The forbidden one is a number of people in Attica.

:::solution
What is real is a reform, often dated 508/507 BCE, that reorganised Athenian citizen men into demes and tribes and supported assembly, council and lot. That is Athenian democracy in an early shape, and [[#thm-not-parliament]] says it is real. “Western freedom” is not in that reform. The West is a later name, and freedom as a universal claim does not describe a citizen body. The exclusion of women, metics and enslaved people is how the body is defined, not a detail sitting under it. In 451 BCE the city tightens the definition further by parentage, which is [[#thm-exclusion]]. A detail of the age would be something the achievement did not need. This achievement, if the achievement is the democracy, needed a rule about who was in the room. The slide keeps a year and replaces the room with a slogan.

The writer has treated “necessary to someone else’s citizenship” as “holder of the same citizenship acts”. [[#def-citizen]] separates them on purpose.
