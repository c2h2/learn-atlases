What ended in 323 BCE, and what did not?

Alexander of Macedon died in Babylon in 323 BCE. What ended with him was a life, and the chance that the empire he had conquered would pass on as one kingdom. What did not end was Greek political life, the poleis, or the peoples of the lands he had taken. He did not unify Europe. He did not bring civilisation to societies that already had states, writing and kings. Macedon was a kingdom. It was not Athens, and it was not “the Greeks” as a state. The political result of the death is a set of Hellenistic kingdoms, which is the world [[rome/republic]] will later meet. That meeting is not told here.

## A kingdom, not a polis

:::definition Macedon {#def-macedon}
**Macedon** in this chapter is a kingdom on the northern edge of the older poleis, ruled by a royal house. It is not a democracy of adult citizen men, and it is not a name for the Greeks as one state. Its kings can lead Greek allies and still be at war with Greek poleis. Kingship is inherited, fought over and assassinated. It is not allotted.
:::

Philip II is the king who, in 338 BCE, defeats an alliance of Greek poleis at Chaeronea. Athens and Thebes are in that alliance in the usual account. The battle is a victory of a kingdom over citizen armies, not a reunion of a nation. Demosthenes is the Athenian speaker associated with resistance to Philip. We will not quote him. After the victory Philip organises a league of Greek states under his own leadership, a hegemony. We will not date the league separately from Chaeronea. Members remain poleis. Hegemony is the power [[greece/poleis]] already distinguished from a nation: followers who have not stopped being their own communities. Some Greeks had fought him. Some would serve his son. There is no unanimous Greek project hiding under the battle.

In 336 BCE Philip is assassinated. Alexander becomes king because he is the heir of that house, not because an Athenian assembly elects him. Early in the reign Thebes revolts and is destroyed. Many of its people are enslaved in the usual account. We will not date the destruction, and we will not number the dead or the enslaved. The fact is enough. A Macedonian king uses terror against a famous polis. That is not the behaviour of a unifier loved by “the Greeks”, and it is not a detail to be saved for a legend of generosity. It is the start of his power, before he ever crosses the sea.

:::theorem Chaeronea is not a Greek national act {#thm-chaeronea}
The defeat of a Greek alliance at Chaeronea in 338 BCE made Macedon the leading power among the poleis. It did not make Macedon “the Greeks”, and it did not make the poleis into one state under a national crown. Allies and enemies remained distinct.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the battle’s own sides. A kingdom fights an alliance. Alliances exist because the parties are still separate, which is the same reason a league was not a nation in [[greece/poleis]]. After the victory, hegemony is organised through the poleis that remain, not by abolishing them. Thebes’ later destruction shows the opposite of consent: a polis could still choose revolt, and the king answered with the city’s end. Greek soldiers in the later Asian campaign are recruits and allies of a Macedonian king, beside Macedonians, not a national levy of Hellas.

What would count against the claim is a single citizenship enacted at Chaeronea, or a source in which the defeated cities describe themselves as districts of a Greek nation rather than as losers to a king. A modern caption that says “Greece unites” is not that source. Philip’s death in 336 BCE is further counter-evidence to a stable national crown: the hegemony is personal enough that assassination is a political crisis, and the new king has to compel the poleis again.
:::

## The campaign, dated only where we may

In 334 BCE Alexander crosses into Asia. The enemy is the Achaemenid empire, the same imperial tradition that had remained a great power after Plataea in 479 BCE. The war is a Macedonian king’s invasion of that empire, with Greek allies and Greek opponents behind him, not a crusade of Europe. Between the crossing and the battle this chronology dates, there are further campaigns and battles in the usual narrative, along the coast and into Egypt. We do not date them one by one. The numerals we print are the crossing in 334 BCE and Gaugamela in 331 BCE.

Gaugamela is the decisive defeat of the Persian king in the field. After it, the imperial centres are open. Babylon is taken. The palace centre at Persepolis is burned in the usual account, and we will not date the fire. The Persian king does not keep his throne. He is killed in the course of the collapse, and we will not date that killing either. Darius’s line, as the ruling house of the imperial centre, is finished as a ruling house. The peoples of those lands are not finished. Egypt and Babylonia were old centres of kingship and writing before any Macedonian arrived. Iranian lands had been the empire’s own heart. Conquest is not a gift of history to people who lacked it.

The campaign goes on, through the Iranian plateau and as far as the Indus region. In the usual account the army eventually refuses to go further, and he turns back. We will not quote the refusal, and we will not number the army. The chronology does not settle a size, and [[#thm-death]] below does not need one. He dies in Babylon in 323 BCE. The sources do not agree on the cause. Fever is the ordinary report. Poison is a rumour. The political fact does not wait on the diagnosis. He leaves no heir able to hold what he had conquered.

:::example Fifteen years {#ex-fifteen}
Chaeronea is 338 BCE. Alexander dies in Babylon in 323 BCE. How many years separate the battle that made Macedon dominant in the Greek poleis from the death that broke the conquered empire into a succession crisis?
:::solution
338 minus 323 is 15. Check: 323 plus 15 is 338. Both dates are BCE, so there is no year 0 to insert. Fifteen years cover an assassination in 336 BCE, a crossing into Asia in 334 BCE, Gaugamela in 331 BCE, and the death. The span is short on the scale of empires and long enough for a hegemony and a conquest. It is not the completion of a nation. The answer is 15 years.
:::
:::

:::example A victory and a death {#ex-pair}
Set Gaugamela, 331 BCE, beside the death in Babylon, 323 BCE. One is a defeat of the Persian king. The other is the end of Alexander’s life. Why is the first not a completed settlement, even though it is a real victory?
:::solution
331 minus 323 is 8. Gaugamela wins a battle and opens the imperial centres. It does not write a constitution for a single successor state, and it does not keep the king alive. Eight years later the conqueror dies with no heir able to rule the whole. A victory is a result in a season. A settlement would be an inheritance that holds. [[#thm-death]] says that inheritance does not happen. Putting the dates side by side stops the habit of treating the greatest battle as the end of the story. The end, politically, is the death.
:::

From the crossing in 334 BCE to the death in 323 BCE is eleven years: 334 minus 323 is 11. From Philip’s assassination in 336 BCE to the crossing is two years. The figure’s dots are a compressed royal career. Compression is not completeness. A reader who slides from Chaeronea to the death and calls the slide “the unification of the Greeks” has ignored who was defeated in 338 BCE and what failed to stay united in 323 BCE.

## What the death broke

:::definition Hellenistic kingdoms {#def-hellenistic}
The **Hellenistic kingdoms** are the separate monarchies that came out of the commanders’ wars after Alexander’s death in 323 BCE. The word is a later label for that political world, not a Greek nation and not the Achaemenid empire under a new Greek sticker. The large pieces later ages name most often are a kingdom in Egypt, a kingdom in Asia, and a kingdom in Macedon. Borders moved. Poleis continued beside and inside these realms.
:::

In 323 BCE the tidy map is not yet drawn. There is a crisis. Commanders, the successors, fight over satrapies, armies and the remaining members of the royal house. Over the following decades the fights produce the kingdoms the label names. Ptolemy’s house rules in Egypt. Seleucus’s house rules a vast and changing Asian realm. The Antigonid house becomes the royal line associated with Macedon. None of these is a will read out at the funeral. Naming them is a way to see the result, not a way to pretend the result was instant. What is instant is the negative fact. The conquered empire does not remain one kingdom.

Greek becomes a language of government and of cities across a wide area. New cities are founded. Alexandria in Egypt becomes the most famous of them, and we will not date the foundation separately from the campaign. A language of rule is not a polity. [[greece/poleis]] already refused to turn shared speech into a state. The same refusal applies one scale up. Macedonian dynasties rule mixed populations, Egyptian, Babylonian, Iranian and others, and they need those populations’ harvests, priests and scribes. That is empire. It is not a fusion that makes one people, and it is not the end of the poleis, many of which keep their civic forms under and beside kings. Federal leagues of poleis also continue. We will not date their later prominence. Their existence is the point.

:::theorem The death ends a life and a single inheritance, not a world {#thm-death}
Alexander’s death in Babylon in 323 BCE ends his rule and the prospect that the conquered Achaemenid lands would pass on as one kingdom. It does not end the poleis, the political life of Greek speakers, or the societies that had been conquered. The result is a plurality of Hellenistic kingdoms, not a Greek nation completed.
:::
:::proof
The evidence for the break is the successors’ wars. Men who had been his commanders govern different regions and fight each other. No heir controls the satrapies as one administration through a generation. Babylonian chronicle material records the conquest and the death as events in that city’s political time. It is a check on a purely heroic Greek story, and we will not quote it. Greek and Roman narratives, written later and full of legend, still agree on the political outline: a death in Babylon, then division. Pierre Briant’s kind of reading, which sets the conquest against the Achaemenid empire it overthrew and against the legend, is the modern caution we follow. We do not need his page to see that Egypt and Babylonia were not empty before the crossing.

What would count against the claim is one successor actually holding the whole, from Babylon or from Pella, with the other commanders obeying, so that 323 BCE is a funeral and not a crisis. That is not what happened. What would count against the second half, the claim that a world did not end, is the disappearance of poleis and of non-Greek institutions after the death. Civic life, temples and local elites continue. A kingdom in Egypt that needs Egyptian wealth is not the abolition of Egypt. The plurality is the result [[#def-hellenistic]] names.
:::

:::theorem He was not a unifier of Europe or a bringer of civilisation {#thm-not-europe}
The campaign from 334 BCE to the death in 323 BCE was a conquest of the Achaemenid empire by the king of Macedon. It did not unify Europe, and it did not bring civilisation to the conquered. Those phrases praise a legend. They do not describe the opponents, the route or the result.
:::
:::proof
Europe is the wrong map. The route runs through Anatolia, the Levant, Egypt, Mesopotamia, Iran and on towards the Indus. Those are the lands of the empire and its neighbours, not a European peninsula being gathered into one state. [[sources/naming-europe]] is the argument that Europe has been several later ideas. None of them is Alexander’s army. Civilisation, used as a gift, requires the conquered to have been without political order. The evidence of Egyptian and Babylonian states, and of the Achaemenid kingship itself, is the counter-evidence. He overthrew a ruling house. He did not introduce history.

Chaeronea is the counter-evidence to “unifier of the Greeks”, and it is inside the Greek-speaking world rather than in Europe as a whole. He defeated Greeks before he led some of them. Thebes was destroyed. A unifier who begins by wrecking a polis has been mis-named. What would count against this rejection is a settlement in which the conquered lands lacked states before 334 BCE, or a European polity founded at his death and still there afterwards. The Hellenistic kingdoms are several, and they are not Europe.

What would not count is the later epithet “the Great”, or a classroom map that colours one empire as if it remained his family’s single state. The epithet is part of the legend. The colour is [[#thm-death]] drawn wrongly.
:::

:::example An invented completion {#ex-invented}
This sentence is invented. It is not a source. “In 323 BCE Alexander completed the unification of Greece and Europe and left one kingdom to his people.” Which words can be attached to the real year, and which words invent a result?
:::solution
The year 323 BCE is the death in Babylon. It can be attached to the end of his life and to the start of a succession crisis. “Completed” is the wrong verb for a death. “Unification of Greece” contradicts Chaeronea, where a kingdom defeated Greek poleis, and contradicts the survival of those poleis. “Europe” is not the theatre and not the result. “One kingdom” is what the commanders failed to maintain. “His people” pretends there was one people to inherit. The repair is the course’s own sentence: he died in Babylon, and the conquered empire was divided among his commanders rather than inherited as one Greek state.
:::

:::example Sort three powers {#ex-sort}
Sort these three as they stand in 334 BCE, the year of the crossing. First, Macedon. Second, Athens. Third, the Achaemenid imperial centre. Which is a kingdom about to invade, which is a polis that had been on the losing side at Chaeronea, and which is the empire being invaded?
:::solution
Macedon is the kingdom, ruled by Alexander since 336 BCE, and it is the invader of 334 BCE. Athens is a polis, a democracy of some of its men, defeated with other poleis at Chaeronea in 338 BCE and still a polis. The Achaemenid centre is the empire under attack, a state with its own king, not a blank and not a European frontier. The sort fails if Macedon is called a polis of the Athenian type, if Athens is called the nation, or if the empire is called a civilisation waiting to be brought.

## Objections, and a worked case

The serious objection is personal rule. For a few years one king was recognised across enormous distances. If that is not one empire, the objection says, nothing is. The reply separates conquest from inheritance. Personal rule of that breadth is real, and it is exactly what died with him. A state that cannot survive the king’s death without splitting has not become a durable single kingdom. The successors’ wars are not a detail under a completed project. They are the project’s political outcome. [[#thm-death]] is written that way on purpose. What would reopen it is obedience, not further conquest: one heir obeyed across the satrapies. Legend prefers the conquest.

A second objection says that refusing army sizes, and refusing dates for every battle between 334 and 331 BCE, leaves the campaign too thin to be a conquest. The thinness is a rule of evidence, not a doubt that the conquest happened. Gaugamela is dated. The imperial centres pass out of Achaemenid hands. The death is dated. Sizes in circulation are not in the chronology, and several famous battles have years this atlas will not print. You can teach a conquest as a change of rulers without a headcount and without a day-by-day route. Adding either would sound more exact and be less honest.

Worked case. An invented school paragraph says: “Philip united Greece at Chaeronea in 338 BCE. Alexander, a bringer of civilisation, crossed in 334 BCE, won the world at Gaugamela in 331 BCE, and in 323 BCE left that world as one kingdom. Rome later received the nation he had made.”

Philip defeated an alliance. He did not unite a nation. The crossing and Gaugamela are real and correctly dated, and they are a conquest of an empire that already had civilisation in the only sense the word can bear without insult, namely states and letters and gods of its own. The death in 323 BCE is the break, not the bequest of one kingdom. Rome does not receive a Greek nation. [[rome/republic]] starts a different institutional history, and the world that later wars will meet is the Hellenistic plurality. The paragraph’s dates are mostly the chronology’s dates. Its nouns are the legend. Repair the nouns.

:::warning Not a unifier, and not a gift of civilisation
The usual myth has two titles for the same man. One calls him the unifier of Greece, or of Europe. Chaeronea is a defeat of Greek poleis by a foreign kingdom’s king, and the campaign’s route is the Achaemenid empire, not a European union. The other calls him a bringer of civilisation. Egypt, Babylonia and Persia were not waiting in darkness for a Macedonian to begin their history. Both titles also hide the result the widget marks. The death in 323 BCE is a political break. It is not a completed project, and the kingdoms that follow are several.
:::

:::history A short royal span
Philip II defeated a Greek alliance at Chaeronea in 338 BCE and was assassinated in 336 BCE. Alexander of Macedon crossed into Asia in 334 BCE, defeated the Persian king at Gaugamela in 331 BCE, and died in Babylon in 323 BCE. The conquered empire was divided among his commanders rather than inherited as one Greek state.
:::

## Where this leads

Rome meets this world of kingdoms and poleis, not a Greek nation left finished in Babylon. [[rome/republic]] tells the republic’s own offices and its own traditional start. It should not be read backwards as the reason Alexander crossed, and it is not told in this chapter. Behind him, the war of [[greece/peloponnesian-war]] had already shown Greek states fighting one another, and [[greece/poleis]] is why neither Philip nor Alexander can be the missing nation. The habit of turning a conquest into a civilisation-story is the habit [[sources/naming-europe]] and [[sources/argument]] exist to slow down.

:::summary
- Macedon is a kingdom. It is not Athens and not the Greeks as a state. Chaeronea in 338 BCE is a defeat of a Greek alliance, not a national union.
- Philip II is assassinated in 336 BCE. Alexander becomes king as his heir. Thebes is destroyed in the early reign, without a year printed here and without a body count.
- He crosses into Asia in 334 BCE. Gaugamela, in 331 BCE, defeats the Persian king. Other battles of the campaign are not given years the chronology does not list. No army size is stated.
- From Chaeronea to the death is 15 years. From the crossing to the death is 11. The campaign is short. A short campaign is not a completed project.
- He dies in Babylon in 323 BCE. The cause is disputed and does not decide the politics. No heir holds the conquest as one kingdom.
- The Hellenistic kingdoms, in Egypt, in Asia and in Macedon, are the political result. Poleis and local societies continue. Greek as a language of rule is not a Greek nation.
- He is not a unifier of Europe or a bringer of civilisation. [[rome/republic]] is where Rome’s own story starts. It meets this plural world later.
:::

Athens is a democracy of some of its men. Chaeronea in 338 BCE was a battle between that kind of alliance and the kingdom. Larger does not turn a kingship into a lot-drawn democracy, and a king of Macedon is not the voice of poleis he has defeated. The reader has renamed a monarchy as a polis and then renamed the poleis as a nation.

:::exercise What did not end {level=2}
List two things that ended in 323 BCE and two that did not. Keep the conquered peoples and the poleis on the correct side of the line.
:::solution
What ended is Alexander’s life, and the prospect that the Achaemenid lands he had conquered would be inherited as one kingdom. What did not end is the poleis, which continue under and beside kings, and the societies of the conquered lands, which were states and populations before him and remain necessary to any king who wants their revenues. [[#thm-death]] is that split. A list that puts “the Greeks” or “Egypt” on the side of things that ended has repeated the legend.

:::exercise No army size {level=2}
A book offers a precise number of soldiers at Gaugamela and treats the number as the reason the battle mattered. Why does this chapter refuse the number, and what can you still say about 331 BCE?
:::solution
The chronology does not settle an army size, and a figure taken from a legendary tradition would be false precision. The political result does not need it. In 331 BCE the Persian king is defeated in the field, and the imperial centres then pass out of his house’s control. That is enough for the conquest to be real. It is not enough to make the conquest a single lasting kingdom, which is decided at the death in 323 BCE, not by the size of a line of battle. Refusing the number keeps [[#thm-death]] honest.

:::solution
The years can mark a real span: the crossing in 334 BCE and the death in 323 BCE, eleven years of campaigning. They cannot mark a European union or a civilising mission. The route is the Achaemenid empire and its neighbouring regions, including Egypt and Babylonia, which were old centres of states and writing, and the Iranian lands that were the empire’s heart. Civilisation as a gift fails on that evidence. Europe as the thing unified fails because Europe was not the theatre and no European state results. After 323 BCE the inheritance splits into Hellenistic kingdoms. The titles would be true only if the conquered lands had lacked political order before 334 BCE and if one European polity had been left standing after the death. The portrait’s dates are a career. The caption’s nouns are a later praise-poem. [[#thm-not-europe]] is the rejection.
