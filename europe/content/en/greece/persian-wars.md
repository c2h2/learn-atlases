What happened in 490 and 480 BCE, once Persia is treated as an empire?

The question refuses a picture in which a finished Greek nation meets a formless East and, by winning, rescues Europe. The poleis of [[greece/poleis]] were still poleis. Persia under Darius I and then Xerxes I was a great empire with a king, satraps, tribute and Greek-speaking subjects. The battles are real. Marathon is 490 BCE. Thermopylae and Salamis fall in 480 BCE, the same campaign season, and they are not the same outcome. Plataea is 479 BCE. The Delian League’s usual start is 478 BCE, the winter of 478/477. Greek victory on the mainland is real. It is not the birth of a continent, and it is not a lesson in which one side is freedom and the other is slavery.

## A state with politics

:::definition Achaemenid empire {#def-achaemenid}
The **Achaemenid empire** is the Persian royal state of this period, ruled by a king and divided among satraps. It held many peoples and languages, Greek among them, and it raised tribute and soldiers from its subjects. It is not a mood called “the East”, and it is not a mass of slaves set opposite a free Greece. Greek cities inside it had politics of their own, including the choice to rebel, to comply or to take the king’s side in a war.
:::

Darius I is the king under whom a Persian expedition is defeated at Marathon. The usual narrative does not put him on the field. He sends commanders. That is what an empire looks like: the centre decides, and agents carry a campaign to a distant shore. Between Marathon and the great invasion the kingship passes from Darius to Xerxes I. We will not date the death. It is not in the chronology, and the political point does not need a substitute year. Xerxes inherits an empire that has already fought Greek poleis and still has unfinished business with them.

Those poleis were not a single enemy. Greek-speaking cities in Ionia were already inside the imperial orbit, as subjects who had rebelled and been brought back, or as neighbours who had to choose. We will not date that rebellion. On the mainland, when the invasions came, some poleis fought the king, some tried to stay out, and some went over to him. The usual Greek word for that choice, medising, is itself evidence against a war of all Greeks against all Persians. A polis that medised was still a polis. It had made a calculation about power, not betrayed a nation that did not exist.

The empire’s own politics are not a blank. Satraps compete and obey. Succession matters. Subject peoples, including Greeks, serve in royal forces. Royal inscriptions, where we have them, speak the king’s language of lands and order. They do not narrate Marathon, and we will not pretend they do. Herodotus, writing later and from the Greek side of the sea, is the narrative we actually have for the campaigns. He is a source, not a mirror. [[sources/evidence]] keeps the trace and the inference apart. His taste for a story in which free men face a king is evidence of Greek political language. It is not a census of the empire, and it is not a warrant to call Persians slaves.

:::theorem The victory was real and it was not the rescue of Europe {#thm-europe}
An alliance of Greek poleis stopped Persian aims on the mainland in the campaigns of 490, 480 and 479 BCE. That victory is real. It did not rescue Europe, because Europe was not the belligerent, the empire did not collapse, and many Greek poleis were never in the alliance. “Freedom against slavery” is a slogan in Greek telling. It is not a description of Persian society, and it is not a description of the poleis, which held enslaved people and, at Sparta, helots.
:::
:::proof
The dated battles are the evidence for the victory, together with what did not follow them. Marathon in 490 BCE is a defeat of one expedition, and the war is not over. In 480 BCE a Greek force loses the pass at Thermopylae and a Greek fleet wins at Salamis. In 479 BCE a land battle at Plataea defeats the force left in Greece. After that, the king’s project of taking the mainland poleis is over. No further dated campaign in this chronology puts a Persian army back into that project. The victory is the failure of that project.

What the same evidence will not carry is a rescued continent. The alliance is a coalition of poleis, with a negotiated command and with absentees. Some Greek states fought for the king. The Achaemenid empire continues, with its satraps and its revenues, after Plataea. Nothing in the battles founds a European state or even a Greek one. [[sources/naming-europe]] is the argument that “Europe” has been several maps, not one actor waiting in 480 BCE to be saved.

What would count against the victory is a source, close to the events, in which the mainland campaign ends with the poleis of the alliance subject to the king. What would count against the second half of the claim is a contemporary text in which that alliance names itself Europe and in which the empire actually dissolves in 479 BCE. A modern textbook sentence does not do that work. Herodotus praising a Greek idea of freedom does not do it either. Praise is a claim. The empire’s survival is the counter-evidence to collapse, and Greek slavery and helotage are the counter-evidence to a photograph of a free society.
:::

## One season is not one outcome

The chronology puts Thermopylae and Salamis in the same campaign season of 480 BCE. That pairing is the fact a careless summary deletes. At Thermopylae a Greek force held a pass and was defeated. In the same season there was fighting at sea off Euboea. The decisive naval victory of the season, the one the chronology marks by name, is Salamis, where a Greek fleet defeated the Persian fleet. Themistocles is the Athenian associated with the choice to fight there. Xerxes I is the king. Athens had been left by many of its people before the battle. We will not quote an oracle, and we will not number ships or soldiers. The chronology gives no such figures, and the political result does not need them.

The land war was not finished at Salamis. The usual account has the king withdraw and leave a commander, Mardonius, with a force in Greece. Plataea, in 479 BCE, is the land battle that defeats that force. Sparta is prominent on the Greek side there, as Athens is prominent at sea. The alliance is still an alliance. A sentence that says “in 480 BCE the Greeks won” has deleted Thermopylae. A sentence that says “in 480 BCE the Greeks lost” has deleted Salamis. The year is one season with two outcomes, and the war’s land decision is the next year.

:::example Ten years, not one battle {#ex-ten}
Marathon is 490 BCE. Salamis is 480 BCE. How many years separate them, and why does that gap matter for a sentence that treats “the Persian war” as a single afternoon? Both dates are BCE. (The result is a number of years.)
:::solution
Subtract: 490 minus 480 is 10. Check by addition: 480 plus 10 is 490. Both years are BCE, so there is no year 0 to insert. The gap matters because Darius’s expedition and Xerxes’ campaign are different seasons, under a succession we do not date here, with a defeat and then a much larger war. Ten years is enough to stop the afternoon story. The answer is 10 years, and the war is still not over at Salamis.
:::
:::

:::example Two results in 480 BCE {#ex-season}
Set Thermopylae beside Salamis. Both belong to 480 BCE. One is a defeat of a Greek force at a pass. The other is a victory of a Greek fleet. A summary says, “480 BCE was the year Greece won its freedom.” What has the summary deleted, and what has it added?
:::solution
It has deleted Thermopylae, which is a loss in the same season, and it has deleted the fact that Plataea, the land decision, is 479 BCE. It has added “Greece” as if the alliance were a nation, and “freedom” as if the slogan were a description of the whole society. The honest pair is: one year, two results, an alliance of poleis, no army size, and a war that continues into the next year. Salamis is a real naval victory associated with Themistocles on the Greek side and with Xerxes’ fleet on the other. It is not the rescue of a continent.
:::
:::

:::example Eleven years, then a different object {#ex-eleven}
Compute the years from Marathon, 490 BCE, to Plataea, 479 BCE. Then say what kind of thing begins, on the usual dating, in 478 BCE, the winter of 478/477, and why it must not be called another name for the victory.
:::solution
490 minus 479 is 11. Check: 479 plus 11 is 490. Plataea is eleven years after Marathon and one year after Salamis. In 478 BCE, the winter of 478/477, the usual textbook date places the start of the Delian League. That is an alliance with a treasury and contributions, soon an instrument of Athenian power. A victory is a result in a season. A league is a structure that lasts and changes. Calling the league “the victory” collapses [[#def-league]] into the battles. The number 11 belongs to the war from Marathon to Plataea, not to the league.
:::

## The league is not the victory

:::definition Delian League {#def-league}
The **Delian League** is the alliance, usually dated from 478 BCE and the winter of 478/477, in which Athens and other poleis organised themselves against Persia after the victories. Its early centre of contributions is associated with Delos. Members owed ships or money. It is not a Greek nation, and it is not another name for Salamis or Plataea. It becomes a structure of Athenian power.
:::

The early league has a purpose that is still about the king: guarding the Aegean and carrying war towards Persian positions. Allies join for protection and for revenge, and also because Athens has the fleet. A leading city, contributions and oaths are the tools [[greece/poleis]] already named when it said a league is not a nation. Here those tools harden. Allies who try to leave are forced back. We will not date each revolt. The pattern is the evidence: membership stops being a free annual choice. Contributions that began as war finance become the means by which Athens maintains power, builds, and pays the political life of its own citizens. The citizenship law that later narrows who may share that life is taught in [[greece/athenian-democracy]], not here. The point now is only that the league has changed its practical meaning.

Historians disagree about whether the open war with Persia was closed by a formal peace in the middle of the fifth century. Any year proposed for that peace is absent from this chronology, so we print none. The disagreement does not put the league back into 479 BCE. With or without a treaty, the machinery stays, and it serves Athens. That turn prepares the next great war, the one that begins in 431 BCE between Athens and Sparta and their allies. [[greece/peloponnesian-war]] tells it. We do not. What belongs here is the cause of the fear: an Athenian empire in the Aegean, built in the name of a war against Persia, held together by force as well as by consent, and alarming to other poleis that were never members.

Persia does not leave the stage in order to become a symbol. The empire remains a great power. In the later stage of the Athenian war it will even support Sparta, which is a fact about imperial politics, not a paradox to be smoothed away. A state that loses a project in Greece can still have money, ships and interests a generation afterwards. That is ordinary for an empire. It is fatal to a story in which 479 BCE is the end of “the East”.

:::theorem The league’s later shape is not the victory {#thm-league}
The Delian League begins as an alliance against Persia and becomes an Athenian power. That second fact prepares a war among Greek states. It is not what was decided at Marathon, Salamis or Plataea, and it is not evidence that the victories were unreal.
:::
:::proof
The timing is the evidence. The battles are 490, 480 and 479 BCE. The league’s usual start is 478 BCE, the winter of 478/477, after the land victory. Institutions that coerce former allies are a further change, inside the league’s life, not a secret content of Salamis. Athenian use of allied resources is attested by the way the alliance actually ran: contributions, suppression of attempted exits, and an Aegean in which Athens could threaten other poleis. Sparta and her allies eventually treat that power as intolerable. The war of 431 BCE is the later proof that the league’s transformation mattered. It is not a proof that Plataea was a myth.

What would count against the claim is a record in which the league remains a voluntary and equal association down to 431 BCE, with no coercion and no Athenian control of common resources. What would count against the other half, the reality of the victories, is the counter-evidence already named in [[#thm-europe]]. Becoming an empire afterwards does not reach backwards and cancel a battle. It does mean that “Greek freedom” cannot be the moral of the whole sequence, because the leading free polis used the victory to dominate other Greek poleis.
:::

:::solution

:::example An invented moral {#ex-invented}
This sentence is invented. It is not a source. “In 480 BCE the free West defeated the slave East at Salamis and so saved Europe from Persia.” Using only the dates and the distinctions of this chapter, pull the sentence apart.
:::solution
The year 480 BCE contains Salamis, a real naval victory, and also Thermopylae, a defeat. The land decision is Plataea in 479 BCE. “The free West” and “the slave East” are not descriptions of the two societies: the poleis enslaved people, Sparta depended on helots, and the Persian empire was a state with subjects, not a slave mass. “Europe” was not the alliance and was not saved, because it was not a political actor in this war. What can be rescued is narrower. In 480 BCE a Greek fleet beat a Persian fleet at Salamis. The invented sentence keeps one true battle and wraps it in a myth this chapter exists to stop.
:::

## Objections, and a worked case

The serious objection is that we are scolding the sources. Greeks who fought said they fought so as not to be ruled by the king, and they called that freedom. Herodotus preserves the language. If the lesson then says the victory was not about freedom, it seems to prefer a modern scruple to the participants.

The reply is that we are grading the slogan, not banning it. Refusal to live under Persian royal rule is a real political aim of the alliance, and it is intelligible. It does not follow that Persian subjects were slaves in law, or that the alliance was a free society in any sense that includes women, metics, enslaved people and helots. It also does not follow that the aim stayed the meaning of the next thirty years of the league. A cause of a war and the use of a victory are different sentences. [[sources/argument]] separates a claim from a label. “Freedom” here is close to a label. The checkable claim is the one in [[#thm-europe]]: a project was stopped; a continent was not created; an empire continued.

A second objection says that without army sizes the battles are too vague to teach. The sizes in circulation are not in the chronology, and they are not stable in the scholarship in a form this atlas will print. What is stable is who fought, who won, and what political object did or did not result. A false precision about men on a beach would make the chapter sound more exact while adding nothing to [[#thm-europe]].

Worked case. This paragraph is invented. “Darius brought the slave empire of the East to Marathon in 490 BCE and was defeated by the Greek nation. Xerxes returned in 480 BCE, lost the war in a day at Thermopylae, and Europe was safe. Athens then led a league of equals, which is the same thing as the victory.”

Darius did not need to stand at Marathon for the expedition to be his, and the empire was not a slave empire. The victors were Athens and its allies, not a Greek nation. Xerxes’ campaign is 480 BCE, but Thermopylae is a Greek defeat in that season, not the loss of the war in a day. Salamis is the fleet victory; Plataea in 479 BCE is the land decision. Europe is the wrong object. The league, from 478 BCE, is not the victory and does not stay a league of equals, which is [[#thm-league]]. The repair keeps four dates and changes every noun that is doing mythological work.

:::warning Not the East, not a lesson in freedom
The usual myth has two faces. One calls Persians the East, or slaves, or a darkness against which Greece shines. The other calls the victories the rescue of Europe and the birth of Western freedom. Both fail the evidence. Persia is an empire with its own politics. The winners are an alliance of poleis, partial, quarrelsome and themselves slave-holding. The league that follows becomes an Athenian power over other Greeks. A story that needs the king to be a symbol, rather than a ruler, is not using the battles. It is using a later contrast, of the kind [[sources/naming-europe]] takes apart.
:::

:::history Four dates, not a founding
In 490 BCE a Persian expedition sent under Darius I was defeated at Marathon by Athens and its allies. The war was not over. In 480 BCE, one campaign season, a Greek force lost at Thermopylae and a Greek fleet won at Salamis, with Xerxes I the king and Themistocles associated with the Athenian plan. Plataea, in 479 BCE, was the land victory. None of these was the founding of Europe.
:::

## Where this leads

The league’s turn into Athenian power is the bridge. [[greece/athenian-democracy]] asks who, inside that imperial city, counted as a participant, and who was shut out of the freedom the slogans named. [[greece/peloponnesian-war]] is the war among Greek states that the league’s success made likely, and it is not told here. [[greece/poleis]] remains the reason a victory of several cities is not a victory of a nation. The method of separating a battle from a label is [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- The Achaemenid empire under Darius I and Xerxes I was a state with satraps, tribute and Greek subjects, not a symbol of the East.
- Marathon is 490 BCE, and it did not end the war. Thermopylae and Salamis share 480 BCE and have opposite results. Plataea is 479 BCE.
- From Marathon to Plataea is eleven years. The gap is the reason the war is not one afternoon.
- Greek victory on the mainland is real. It did not rescue Europe, dissolve the empire, or describe the poleis as a free society.
- Some Greek poleis medised. The war was not all Greeks against all Persians.
- The Delian League’s usual start is 478 BCE, the winter of 478/477. It becomes an Athenian power, and that turn is not the same thing as the victory.
- That power is the background of the later war among Greek states. It is not that war’s narrative.
:::

It shows that poleis made separate choices under threat. Going over to the king did not cancel the Greek speech, cult or citizenship of those cities. It cancelled the claim that the war was a national war of all Greeks. The alliance was a coalition, with absentees and with opponents who were themselves Greek states. What is allowed is the transformation in [[#thm-league]]. After 478 BCE an alliance against Persia becomes a structure Athens uses: contributions, forced loyalty, and power over other Greek poleis. The war that begins in 431 BCE is the later conflict those facts help to explain. Collapsing the league into “the cause” as a single label would also go too far. A cause has to be something that could have been otherwise. The empire in the Aegean made a Greek war likely. It did not make every later choice a formality.
