How did the European war widen, and when did Germany surrender? It widened by dated choices, not by a single battle unfolding of its own weight, and it ended in a surrender that carries three May dates in different senses. Germany invades Poland on 1 September 1939. Britain and France declare war on 3 September 1939. The large offensive in the west is not that week. It is 10 May 1940, and the French armistice is 22 June 1940. Germany invades the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941, one year later to the day. The United States is brought into the German war by a declaration on 11 December 1941, four days after Pearl Harbor on 7 December. None of these sentences is a technique of fighting, and none of them carries a casualty total. The chronology does not lock one. This chapter will not invent one.

The surrender is just as easy to flatten, and just as wrong to flatten. A document is signed at Reims on 7 May 1945. A document is signed at Karlshorst on 8 May 1945. The Soviet Union marked 9 May, because of the time difference. Teaching both 8 May and 9 May is the requirement. Teaching 7 May as well is what keeps the first signature from vanishing into the argument about the other two. Nine May is not a third signing that cancels the eighth, and the eighth is not a correction that cancels the ninth. They are different senses of one defeat. The previous chapter ended on the pact of 23 August 1939, nine days before the invasion. This chapter begins with that invasion and stops at the May dates. The genocide is [[wars/genocide]]. Mass murder was part of the war in the east. The estimate and the evidence are taught there, not anticipated here by a procedure.

## A sequence, not a technique

The declarations of 3 September 1939 are real and they are not the western offensive. From 1 September to 3 September is two days. From 3 September 1939 to 10 May 1940 is a much longer gap, worked below as 250 days. In that gap the war is not a blank, and it is not yet the campaign that produces the French armistice. Poland is invaded and occupied. The Molotov–Ribbentrop pact of 23 August is the immediate political setting. In the weeks after 1 September the Soviet Union occupies territory of eastern Poland. We do not lock a day for that occupation, because the chronology does not, so we do not invent one. Occupation is already a fact of 1939, before France has signed anything.

On 10 May 1940 the German offensive in the west begins. On 22 June 1940 France signs an armistice. From 10 May to 22 June is 43 days: May has 31 days, so 21 days remain after the 10th, and 22 June adds 22, but the cleaner count is from 10 May to 10 June as 31 days and then twelve days to 22 June, which is 43. We state the result and the span. We do not describe the campaign as a method. No lesson in this course is a manual of how an army broke another. The armistice of 22 June 1940 opens a period in which a French regime collaborates and in which people in France also resist. Both are true of one society. Neither is the character of France. The same refusal applies to every occupied country and to Germany itself.

On 22 June 1941 Germany invades the Soviet Union. The date shares its day and month with the French armistice and not its year. From 22 June 1940 to 22 June 1941 is 365 days, not 366. The leap day of 1940 fell in February, before the armistice, and 1941 is not a leap year, so it does not fall inside the twelve months. A reader who adds a day because "1940 was a leap year" has put February in the wrong place. The invasion opens the war in the east. Mass murder of civilians was part of that war. We say so once, and we send the evidence and the estimate to the next chapter rather than describe a procedure here. The Soviet Union, which had occupied eastern Poland in 1939, is now the invaded state. The pact of August 1939 was not a permanent alignment. It was a choice, broken by another choice on a stated day.

The war widens again in December 1941. Pearl Harbor is 7 December. On 11 December Germany declares war on the United States. The four days are the link between a Pacific attack and the European coalition. This chapter does not become a history of the Pacific. It records the declaration that makes the United States a belligerent against Germany, and it refuses to treat that declaration as the beginning of the European war. The beginning, in this course, is 1 September 1939. American entry in the earlier war was April 1917, taught in [[wars/first-world-war]]. The rhyme of "America comes in" is not an identity of dates.

:::definition Widening {#def-widening}
**Widening**, in this chapter, means a new state or a new front entering the European war on a locked date: the western offensive and the French armistice in 1940, the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, and Germany's declaration of war on the United States on 11 December 1941. Widening is not a metaphor for battles growing bloodier, and it is not a licence to state a casualty total.
:::

:::definition Occupation {#def-occupation}
**Occupation** is a system of rule over a defeated or invaded territory. It is compatible with collaboration and with resistance in the same society, and sometimes in the same life. It is not a moral type into which a nation can be sorted, and it is not a technique of combat.
:::

:::theorem The war widens by dated choices {#thm-widen}
The European war widens from the invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939 through the western offensive of 10 May 1940, the invasion of the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941, and Germany's declaration of war on the United States on 11 December 1941. These are choices with dates. They are not one battle, and they are not a manual.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the order of the locked dates and the gaps between them. Britain and France declare war on 3 September 1939, two days after the invasion, and the western offensive waits until 10 May 1940. France's armistice is 22 June 1940. The Soviet invasion is 22 June 1941, 365 days later. Pearl Harbor is 7 December 1941 and the German declaration is 11 December, four days later. What would count against the claim is a source in which these were one continuous action with no decisions in between, or a locked opening of the western offensive in the first week of September 1939. The chronology separates them. What would not count against it is a detailed account of how a unit fought on one of those days. Such an account might be true at its scale and still not be the widening. [[sources/scale]] applies. We also refuse casualty totals. A total is not one of the dates, and inventing it would not prove the order.
:::

:::theorem Teach 7 May, 8 May and 9 May in different senses {#thm-may}
Germany's surrender is signed at Reims on 7 May 1945 and at Karlshorst on 8 May 1945. The Soviet Union marked 9 May because of the time difference. Both 8 May and 9 May are to be taught. Nine May is not a third signing, and neither public date corrects the other out of existence.
:::
:::proof
The chronology distinguishes the acts. Reims is one signature, on 7 May. Karlshorst is another, on 8 May. The Soviet mark falls on 9 May because Moscow's date was already the next calendar day relative to the signing. We do not invent the hour. The time difference is the locked reason, and it is enough. What would count against the claim is evidence that only one of these dates occurred, or that 9 May was a separate ceremony contradicting the Karlshorst document rather than a calendar consequence of it. What would also count against a sloppy version is any claim that 7 May is a myth. It is the Reims signature. What would not settle the claim is a national habit of commemorating only one of the days. Habit is evidence of memory after the fact. The documents are the evidence of the surrender. Correcting a commemoration out of existence repeats, at one remove, the error this course refuses for the two calendars of 1917 in [[wars/revolutions-1917]]: a true second label is not a mistake.
:::

:::quiz
Which statement keeps the May dates in their different senses?
- [ ] Germany surrendered on 8 May, so 9 May is an error and 7 May did not happen.
- [ ] There were three unrelated surrenders, on 7, 8 and 9 May, to three different enemies.
- [x] Reims is 7 May 1945, Karlshorst is 8 May 1945, and the Soviet Union marked 9 May because of the time difference.
- [ ] The only surrender that counts is the French armistice of 22 June 1940.
:::solution
The third statement is [[#thm-may]]. The first deletes two locked dates, one of them a signature and one of them a commemoration with a stated cause. The second invents three unrelated surrenders. There are two signatures, and a third calendar label produced by the clock. The French armistice is 22 June 1940, early in the widening, not the German defeat.
:::
:::

## Gaps you can recompute

The widening is a set of spans. Each span blocks a story that welds the war into one week or one method.

:::example Two days, then two hundred and fifty {#ex-two-gaps}
Germany invades Poland on 1 September 1939. Britain and France declare war on 3 September 1939. The western offensive begins on 10 May 1940. How many days separate 1 September from 3 September? How many days separate 3 September 1939 from 10 May 1940? 1940 is a leap year. 1939 is not.
:::solution
From 1 September to 3 September is 2 days. For the longer gap, 3 September 1939 is day 246 of a non-leap year: January through August has 243 days, plus 3. Days from 4 September through 31 December are 365 − 246 = 119. In leap-year 1940, 10 May is day 131: January 31, February 29, March 31, April 30 and 10 more give 31 + 29 + 31 + 30 + 10 = 131. Then 119 + 131 = 250. The offensive is 250 days after the British and French declarations, and those declarations are 2 days after the invasion of Poland. The 250-day gap is not a phoney nothing. It is the distance between a declaration and a later offensive. We do not fill it with a casualty total.
:::
:::

:::example The same day and month, one year later {#ex-june-pair}
The French armistice is 22 June 1940. Germany invades the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941. How many days lie between them? Why does the leap day in 1940 not make the answer 366?
:::solution
The leap day is 29 February 1940, which is before 22 June 1940, so it is not inside the span. The next leap day is in 1944. From 22 June 1940 to 22 June 1941 is therefore an ordinary year, 365 days. The two dates share a day and a month and must not be merged. One is an armistice that takes France out of the fight as a belligerent of the sort that signed in 1940. The other is the opening of the war against the Soviet Union. A reader who remembers only "22 June" has lost the widening.
:::
:::

:::example Stalingrad's two dates, Warsaw's span {#ex-two-ends}
The chronology dates the German surrender at Stalingrad on 31 January 1943 and on 2 February 1943. How many days after 31 January is 2 February? The Warsaw uprising runs from 1 August 1944 to 2 October 1944. How many days after 1 August is 2 October, and what different number do you get if you count both endpoints?
:::solution
From 31 January to 2 February is 2 days. Both dates belong to the surrender at Stalingrad. Keeping only one of them is incomplete, and we do not assign a unit to either day beyond what the chronology says, which is the surrender itself. We also state no death total. From 1 August to 2 October: August has 31 days, so 30 days remain after 1 August, September has 30, and 2 October adds 2. 30 + 30 + 2 = 62. So 2 October is 62 days after 1 August. An inclusive count of both endpoints is 63. The chronology's phrase is a from-to range, and the difference of the dates is 62. The uprising is a rising in an occupied city. It is not the character of Poland, and it is not a manual of street fighting. It ends on 2 October without being the liberation of the city. The Red Army's later advance is a different fact. We do not invent a day for the city's liberation beyond this range.
:::
:::

:::example An invented single victory day {#ex-invented-may}
Treat this sentence as invented. "Germany surrendered once, on 8 May 1945, which makes 7 May a rumour and 9 May a political fiction, and the defeat was the result of one western landing." What has been deleted, and what would the landing's real date have to do before it could carry that conclusion?
:::solution
The sentence is invented. It deletes the Reims signature of 7 May and the Soviet mark of 9 May, against [[#thm-may]]. It also makes the Normandy landings of 6 June 1944 into the sole cause of a defeat that also includes the eastern war, Stalingrad in early 1943, and the failed plot of 20 July 1944. From 6 June to 20 July is 44 days: June has 30 days, so 24 remain after the 6th, and 20 July adds 20, or 30 days from 6 June to 6 July and then 14 days, which is 44. The plot fails. Its failure is not proof that no one in Germany opposed the regime, and its existence is not proof that the regime was about to fall. The landing is 6 June 1944,  not the surrender of May 1945, and not a technique. To carry the invented conclusion, Normandy would have to be the only front still fighting after 1943. It was not. The Soviet front continues. Yalta, in February 1945, is a conference of allies before the surrender, not the surrender.
:::
:::

Between Stalingrad and the May dates the war does not pause for the sake of a diagram. The surrender at Stalingrad is 31 January and 2 February 1943. The Normandy landings are 6 June 1944. The plot against Hitler fails on 20 July 1944. The Warsaw uprising runs from 1 August to 2 October 1944. Yalta meets in February 1945. Auschwitz is liberated by the Red Army on 27 January 1945. That last date is recorded here only as a date of the eastern advance. What the place was, and what the estimate of the murdered is, belongs to [[wars/genocide]]. We do not describe the place. The surrender documents of May are signed after Hitler's death. The chronology does not lock the day of that death, so we do not invent one. The locked end of the German war in this course is Reims, Karlshorst, and the Soviet mark.

Occupation is the system that sits under these dates, and it is where moral sorting does the most damage. A state can be occupied and still contain officials who collaborate, neighbours who hide the persecuted, and neighbours who denounce them. Sometimes one life does more than one of these things. The categories are historians' labels and, after the war, legal labels. They are not national characters. France after 22 June 1940 is the worked case, not because France is uniquely mixed, but because the armistice, a collaborating regime, and resistance are all documented and none of them cancels the others. Poland under occupation produces the Warsaw uprising and also every other conduct the uprising does not exhaust. Germany produces the regime, the plot of 20 July that fails, and conduct that fits neither the plot nor the leadership. The Soviet Union is invaded in 1941, having occupied eastern Poland in 1939. Britain declares war on 3 September 1939 and is not thereby innocent of empire. The course does not sort nations into the guilty and the innocent. What would count against that refusal is a record in which every person in a country fell into one category for the whole war. Court files, administrative papers and memoirs do not show that. A memoir is still not a census. [[sources/evidence]] limits what one life can prove, which is why [[#def-occupation]] speaks of a system and a society, not of a soul of a nation.

:::quiz
What is wrong with calling 22 June the date of the French armistice and of the invasion of the Soviet Union, without a year?
- [ ] Nothing. The two events are the same widening.
- [x] The armistice is 22 June 1940 and the invasion is 22 June 1941, 365 days later. The shared day and month hide two events if the year is dropped.
- [ ] Both events are in 1939, before the declarations.
- [ ] 22 June 1941 is the Karlshorst signing.
:::solution
The years are the difference. [[#ex-june-pair]] shows a 365-day span and explains why the leap day of February 1940 does not fall inside it. Karlshorst is 8 May 1945. The declarations of war on Germany by Britain and France are 3 September 1939. Dropping the year is how a contents-page habit erases the widening [[#thm-widen]] exists to protect.
:::

:::warning One victory day, and one moral type per nation
Two myths reinforce each other. The first picks a single day in May 1945 and calls the other dates mistakes. Reims on 7 May and Karlshorst on 8 May are signatures. The Soviet mark on 9 May is the time difference, not a fiction and not a third war. The second myth sorts each nation into the guilty or the innocent: a nation of resisters, a nation of collaborators, a nation whose army was the whole of its conduct. Occupation was a system of rule. Collaboration and resistance both happened, including inside one society. The failed plot of 20 July 1944 does not acquit Germany, and it does prove that opposition was not imaginary. The Warsaw uprising does not exhaust Poland. No casualty total is available in this chapter to award the moral prize. A total would not have done that work even if it had been locked.
:::

:::history
Germany invades Poland on 1 September 1939. Britain and France declare war on 3 September. The western offensive begins on 10 May 1940, and the French armistice is 22 June 1940. Germany invades the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941. The German surrender is signed at Reims on 7 May 1945 and at Karlshorst on 8 May 1945. The Soviet Union marked 9 May because of the time difference.
:::

The historical note is an order, and the order is the argument. Read it against the widget and the gaps open. Two days from invasion to western declarations. Two hundred and fifty days from those declarations to 10 May 1940. Forty-three days to the French armistice. Three hundred and sixty-five days to the invasion of the Soviet Union. Then Stalingrad's two dates in 1943, Normandy and a failed plot and Warsaw in 1944, Yalta in February 1945, and the May signatures. What would count against [[#thm-widen]] is a locked date that placed the western offensive in the first week of September 1939, or that placed the American declaration before Pearl Harbor. What would count against [[#thm-may]] is a single document that was the only signature and the only commemoration. What would not count against either claim is a film that shows one landing and one flag. A film is a scale. The dates are another.

The eastern war's mass murder is the hinge this chapter will not swing open. We have said that it was part of the war after 22 June 1941, and that the next lesson holds the estimate of the Jews murdered, the reason Wannsee is not a beginning, and the rule against a second invented total. Anticipating that lesson with a procedure would break its first rule. So would a casualty total for soldiers, slipped in while the reader is thinking about something else. "Many died" remains available. A precise integer does not.

## Where this leads

The estimate, the evidence and the word genocide are [[wars/genocide]]. The political division that follows the surrender, including Potsdam in July and August 1945, is [[postwar/division]]. The pact and the appointment that preceded the invasion are [[wars/interwar]]. The double dating of 1917, which this chapter has used as a parallel for May 1945, is [[wars/revolutions-1917]].

:::summary
- Germany invades Poland on 1 September 1939. Britain and France declare war on 3 September, two days later. The western offensive is 10 May 1940, 250 days after those declarations, and the French armistice is 22 June 1940, 43 days after the offensive.
- The Soviet Union occupies eastern Poland in the weeks after 1 September 1939. No day for that step is locked. Germany invades the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941, 365 days after the French armistice. The leap day of 1940 does not fall inside that year.
- Pearl Harbor is 7 December 1941. Germany declares war on the United States on 11 December. That widens the coalition. It does not start the European war.
- Stalingrad's surrender is dated 31 January and 2 February 1943. Normandy is 6 June 1944. A plot against Hitler fails on 20 July 1944, 44 days after the landings. The Warsaw uprising runs from 1 August to 2 October 1944.
- Occupation, collaboration and resistance coexist in single societies. This course does not sort a nation into one moral type, and it states no casualty total.
- Surrender: Reims on 7 May 1945, Karlshorst on 8 May 1945, marked in the Soviet Union on 9 May because of the time difference. Teach both 8 May and 9 May. Mass murder in the east is acknowledged here and taught in the next chapter.
:::

:::exercise Occupation is not a character {level=2}
Using France after 22 June 1940, explain [[#def-occupation]] in a way that blocks both "a nation of collaborators" and "a nation of resisters".

Calling France a nation of collaborators erases the resistance. Calling France a nation of resisters erases the collaboration. The same block applies elsewhere: the Warsaw uprising does not exhaust Poland, and the failed plot of 20 July 1944 does not exhaust Germany. :::exercise What the 365-day span refuses {level=2}
Why is it an error to add one day to the year between 22 June 1940 and 22 June 1941 on the ground that 1940 was a leap year? What historical merger does the error encourage?
:::solution
29 February 1940 falls before 22 June 1940, so it is outside the span. 1941 is not a leap year. The span is 365 days, as in [[#ex-june-pair]]. Adding a day misplaces February. The historical merger is to treat "22 June" as one event. The armistice and the invasion of the Soviet Union are a year apart and do different work in [[#thm-widen]]. The pact of 1939 had aligned Germany and the Soviet Union. The invasion breaks that choice. A leap-day mistake is small. The merger it rides with is not.

The reader who says 62 has answered "how many days after 1 August is 2 October?" The reader who says 63 has included both endpoints, counting every calendar date the range touches. Both arithmetic habits are real. The chronology's from-to phrase is a range, and this chapter uses 62 for the difference, as in [[#ex-two-ends]]. Neither figure is a death total, and neither makes the uprising the character of Poland. The rising ends on 2 October 1944. It is not the German surrender of May 1945.
