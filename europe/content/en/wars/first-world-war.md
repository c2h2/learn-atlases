What kind of war was fought between 1914 and the armistice? The question is about the war itself. It is not an invitation to smuggle back a single cause for July, and it is not a request for a death total. By 4 August 1914 Germany had invaded Belgium and Britain had declared war on Germany. Austria-Hungary had declared war on Serbia on 28 July. Germany had declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August. The choices inside that fortnight are the subject of [[nations/toward-1914]]. This chapter begins where that one stops. It runs from the invasion to the armistice of 11 November 1918, and it asks what sort of fight filled the years between: which fronts, whose labour, whose soldiers, and which sentences can be checked without a number this atlas does not lock.

A war can be European in its capitals and imperial in its armies at the same time. The decisions to declare war were taken in Vienna, Berlin, St Petersburg, Paris and London. The people who then fought and worked included conscripts and volunteers from those states, and also soldiers and labourers raised in colonies and dominions. The home front was not a metaphor standing behind a real war. Rationing, censorship, war industry and mourning were part of how the war was carried on. A history that keeps only a trench in France and Belgium has chosen a scale and called it the whole. [[sources/scale]] is the warning against that move. A true local story, even a terrible one, is not yet the story of the war.

Nor is the war explained by repeating the July crisis until it becomes a destiny. A mobilisation plan constrained a German timetable in 1914. A constraint is not the absence of later choices. Once the war was under way, governments still decided whether to open another front, whether to seek a separate peace, whether to conscript, whether to tighten a blockade, and whether to stop. The armistice is one of those decisions, dated 11 November 1918. It is not the Treaty of Versailles, which is 28 June 1919, and it is not a verdict on which July choice "really" caused the rest. We refuse that verdict here because the previous course already refused it, and because the dates of 1916, 1917 and 1918 are evidence of a war that continued, widened and at last stopped, not evidence of a script written in advance.

## More than one front

The Western Front is real. It is not the war's outline. Fighting against Austria-Hungary continued in the east and in the Balkans. The Ottoman empire was a belligerent, so the war's geography included Ottoman fronts. One of those was the fighting at the Dardanelles. The chronology does not lock a year for that campaign, so this lesson does not invent one. A real campaign can be named and still refused a numeral. Italy, which the alliance graph in [[nations/toward-1914]] left off the edges drawn for 1879, 1894, 1904 and 1907, did fight in the war. The chronology does not lock the day Italy entered, so we do not state one. Participation was a further choice. It was not a line already drawn by the Dual Alliance.

Outside Europe the empires fought one another as well. Campaigns in Africa and the Pacific were not a footnote added by a later moral taste. They followed from the fact that the belligerents were imperial states. The same fact brought colonial troops to European battlefields. French forces drew on North and West Africa and on Indochina. British forces drew on India, on the dominions, on the Caribbean and on African colonies. Labourers recruited in Asia worked behind British and French lines. We do not give a count of any of these men. The chronology does not lock one, and a round number copied from a textbook would be a false precision. The claim we do make is categorical. A war whose armies included those soldiers was an imperial war, even when the capital that recruited them was a European one.

That claim has a limit, and the limit is the point of [[#thm-imperial]]. Presence is not consent. A colonial subject in a metropolitan uniform is not evidence that the colony chose the war, and not evidence that the war was fought for that colony's freedom. Some soldiers' own motives are poorly recorded. A silence in the archive is evidence about who was asked to speak, not evidence that the person had no mind. That rule is taught in [[sources/evidence]]. It applies here with particular force. The empire's gazette is not the soldier's biography.

The home front belongs in the same argument. Belligerent states directed labour, censored news, borrowed money and rationed food. Women entered industrial work in large numbers in more than one country. We say "large numbers" because we will not invent a percentage. Several states conscripted men who had not volunteered. France and Germany entered the war with conscription already in their institutions. Britain moved from voluntary recruiting to conscription during the war. The chronology does not lock the day of that British change, so we do not supply one. Food shortage was a political fact, sharpest where a blockade cut imports. We give no death total for hunger. A ministry poster is evidence of what a government wanted believed. It is not evidence that people believed it. A village that leaves no protest in the record is not thereby shown to have been untouched, and not shown to have been enthusiastic.

Mutiny, desertion and strikes occurred, so the war was not one mood of obedient sacrifice. This chapter does not describe weapons or wounds. Naming the war as industrial is a claim about scale and supply, not a manual.

## Two battles in 1916, and no death total

Verdun begins on 21 February 1916. The Somme begins on 1 July 1916. Both are on the Western Front. Both are long battles rather than a single day's encounter, and both are often taught through a casualty figure. This atlas forbids that figure. The prohibition is not a hint that the battles were small. It is a rule about what may be treated as settled. A number that is not in the chronology is not available for emphasis, for comparison, or for the climax of a paragraph.

The two dates show that 1916 is not a blank between the outbreak and the American entry, and they let us compute a span in [[#ex-verdun-somme]] without a body count. Other fronts and home fronts continued in the same year. The widget later collapses 1916 to one dot. The caption says the dot holds two battles. It is a contents page, not a map.

:::definition Armistice {#def-armistice}
An **armistice** is an agreement to stop fighting. It is not a peace treaty. It does not, by itself, draw a new map, fix a sum of reparations, or found a new state. The armistice in this chapter is the one dated 11 November 1918.
:::

:::definition Home front {#def-home}
The **home front** is the mobilisation of labour, food, law, credit and information inside a belligerent society. It is part of the war. It is not a softer synonym for civilian life in peacetime, and it is not evidence that every household experienced the same war.
:::

:::definition Soldiers of empire {#def-empire-soldiers}
**Soldiers of empire**, in this chapter, means troops and military labourers raised in colonies and dominions and used in a war whose declarations were issued by European imperial states. The phrase is a category of recruitment. It is not a claim about loyalty, consent, or a shared political aim.
:::

:::theorem The war was imperial as well as European {#thm-imperial}
From 1914 to the armistice, the war was fought by European states and by the soldiers and labourers of their empires, on European fronts and outside Europe. Home fronts were part of that war. The claim does not assert that every colony experienced the war in the same way, and it does not include a count of men.
:::
:::proof
The declarations of late July and early August 1914 were acts of European states that already ruled empires, and those empires raised soldiers and labourers for the war, including on European fronts. Ottoman belligerency, the Dardanelles, and campaigns outside Europe put the fighting beyond France and Belgium, even where we refuse a year the chronology does not lock. Conscription, rationing, censorship and war industry show home fronts doing war work. What would count against the claim is a record in which the armies of 1914–1918 were only metropolitan volunteers on a single front, with no imperial recruitment and no mobilisation of production. That is not the record. What would not count against it is one colony that sent few soldiers, or one soldier who did not share his officers' politics. The claim is about the kind of war, not a uniform experience. A single village cannot cancel it, and cannot prove a national mood. That is the scale rule from [[sources/scale]].
:::

:::theorem The armistice is not the peace {#thm-not-peace}
The armistice of 11 November 1918 stopped the fighting on agreed terms. It did not write the peace settlement. The Treaty of Versailles is a later document, signed on 28 June 1919. No death total for Verdun or the Somme is required to date either the battles or the armistice, and none is stated here.
:::
:::proof
An armistice, as [[#def-armistice]] sets it, stops fighting. Versailles is a treaty signed in the Hall of Mirrors, where the German Empire had been proclaimed on 18 January 1871. The year numerals differ by 48, since 1919 minus 1871 is 48. The chronology dates Verdun from 21 February 1916 and the Somme from 1 July 1916 and withholds a death total. What would count against the claim is a treaty of borders and reparations dated 11 November 1918, or a locked casualty total for either 1916 battle. There is neither. Stopping the fighting did not freeze politics: governments fell, and borders were already in dispute. The claim is only that the stop and the settlement are different documents. Brest-Litovsk, on 3 March 1918, is a third document, an eastern peace before the western armistice, taught in [[wars/revolutions-1917]]. It is a further reason not to treat 11 November as the end of every front.
:::

Which statement matches the dates locked for this chapter?

- [ ] Verdun and the Somme can be taught only if a death total is attached.
- [x] Verdun begins on 21 February 1916, the Somme begins on 1 July 1916, and the armistice is 11 November 1918, with no death total attached to either battle.

The first option confuses two documents. Versailles is 28 June 1919. The second option is the habit this chapter exists to stop: the chronology forbids a death total for either battle. The fourth option compresses the war. The invasion of Belgium is 4 August 1914. The United States enters in April 1917. The third option is the locked sequence, and it is enough to identify the battles and the armistice without an invented count of the dead.

## A span you can check

Dates earn their place when a reader can recompute them. The next examples use only locked days. None of them produces a casualty figure, and none of them needs one.

:::example From Verdun's first day to the Somme's first day {#ex-verdun-somme}
Verdun begins on 21 February 1916. The Somme begins on 1 July 1916. How many days after 21 February is 1 July? 1916 is a leap year, so February has 29 days. Give the difference of the dates, not an inclusive count of both endpoints.
:::solution
Take 21 February as day zero and count forward. In a leap year, February has 29 days, so the days after 21 February still inside February are 22 to 29: eight days. March has 31 days, April 30, May 31 and June 30. Then 1 July adds one day. The sum is 8 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 1 = 131. So 1 July 1916 is 131 days after 21 February 1916. The same result follows from day-of-year numbers: 21 February is day 52 of a leap year (31 days in January plus 21), and 1 July is day 183 (31 + 29 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 1). Then 183 − 52 = 131. An inclusive count of every calendar date from 21 February through 1 July would be 132. That is a different question. The question asked here is the difference.
:::
:::

:::example A step inside the armistice year {#ex-span-war}
Germany invades Belgium on 4 August 1914. The armistice is 11 November 1918. In a non-leap year, how many days after 4 August is 11 November? 1918 is not a leap year. Give the difference of the dates, not an inclusive count. Also say how many leap days fall between 4 August 1914 and 4 August 1918.
:::solution
January through July in a non-leap year has 31 + 28 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 31 = 212 days, so 4 August is day 216. January through October has 212 + 31 + 30 + 31 = 304 days, so 11 November is day 315. Then 315 − 216 = 99. In 1918, 11 November is 99 days after 4 August. Between 4 August 1914 and 4 August 1918 the only leap day is 29 February 1916. We stop at these two checks rather than collapsing them into one grand total that could be misread as a year. The armistice remains 11 November 1918. The integer 99 is a step inside that year. It is not a count of the dead.
:::
:::

:::example An invented sentence, split in half {#ex-invented-somme}
Treat the following sentence as invented, and say which clause can be kept. "On 1 July 1916 the Somme began, and by nightfall a settled death total was already known, which this atlas ought to print."
:::solution
The sentence is invented, and it has to be labelled as such before it is used. The first clause states a locked date: the Somme begins on 1 July 1916. That clause can be kept, because it matches the chronology. The second clause invents a death total and then pretends the atlas is obliged to repeat it. The chronology says the opposite: do not state a death total for the Somme or for Verdun. To promote the second clause into evidence you would need a quantity the chronology had locked, together with the warning printed beside it. There is no such quantity. A vivid integer does not become a source by being precise. The same test rejects any other total smuggled into a sentence about 21 February or 1 July 1916. The date stands. The number does not.
:::
:::

:::example Two documents that share a mood and not a date {#ex-armistice-treaty}
A reader writes, as an invented note in the margin of a textbook: "11 November 1918, Versailles signed, the war's meaning settled." Identify each error, and replace the note with a sentence that uses only locked dates.
:::solution
The note is invented. It makes three mistakes. It calls the armistice a signing of Versailles. It places Versailles in 1918 rather than on 28 June 1919. And it treats a meaning as if it were a date. A replacement that stays inside the chronology is: the armistice of 11 November 1918 stopped the fighting; the Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors, the same hall in which the German Empire had been proclaimed on 18 January 1871. The year gap from 1871 to 1919 is 48. None of those sentences needs a casualty total, and none of them says that the treaty was the unavoidable result of the armistice. Between the two dates lay politics, including the revolutions taught in the next chapter.
:::
:::

## Widening, and an eastern peace mentioned only as a date

In April 1917 the United States enters the war. The month is locked. A single motive is not. Historians point to the war at sea, to finance, to prior sympathy and to immediate crises, and they do not all rank those reasons in the same order. This chapter locks the entry and refuses the ranking. April 1917 is not the armistice. From April 1917 to November 1918 is one year and seven months: twelve months take the count to April 1918, and May through November add seven more. American entry changed the coalition. It did not, on the day it happened, stop the fighting. A cause that arrives in April cannot be the explanation of battles already begun in February and July of the previous year. Verdun and the Somme are 1916. The American entry is evidence about how the war widened, not evidence about why it started. That is the same discipline [[sources/argument]] asks of any cause: it has to be the sort of thing that could have been otherwise, and it has to be placed in time.

On 3 March 1918 the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk takes Russia out of the war. The full argument, including the two calendars of 1917, belongs to [[wars/revolutions-1917]]. Here the treaty has one job. It is the eastern peace that comes before the western armistice. From 3 March to 11 November 1918 is 253 days. In that stretch Germany was no longer fighting the Russian state that had been at war with it since 1 August 1914. The armistice still falls on 11 November, and it is not a German victory. The eastern peace therefore cannot be narrated as the move that decided the war. It is a real change of fronts. It is not the script's last line. We do not describe the fighting of 1918 as a technique, and we do not invent an offensive's opening day. The chronology locks Brest-Litovsk and the armistice. Those two dates already refute the idea that leaving the eastern war was the same thing as winning the western one.

:::quiz
What is Brest-Litovsk doing in a chapter whose question stops at the armistice?
- [ ] It replaces the armistice, because the war really ended in the east.
- [x] It is the eastern peace of 3 March 1918, mentioned so that the armistice of 11 November is not mistaken for the end of every front. The revolutions are the next chapter.
- [ ] It is the treaty signed in the Hall of Mirrors on 28 June 1919.
- [ ] It is a death total for the Somme under another name.
:::solution
Brest-Litovsk is dated 3 March 1918. It is how revolutionary Russia leaves the war. The armistice on the west is still 11 November 1918, 253 days later, and it is not a German victory. Versailles is a different treaty on 28 June 1919. No casualty total is hiding under the name. The next chapter takes the revolutions, the calendars and this treaty as its own subject.
:::
:::

The chronology's refusal of a death total is itself a piece of evidence about method. It tells you what this course will not pretend to know. It does not tell you that the dead were few, and it does not tell you that numbers are always illegitimate in history. Census figures elsewhere in the atlas are locked because a stated count, with its warning, can be checked. A battlefield total that historians still reconstruct in incompatible ways is not given that status here. If you meet a precise Somme total in another book, you have met that book's choice. You have not met a figure you may quietly import.

:::warning The single trench and the borrowed total
A familiar picture shows one muddy trench, calls it the war, and pins a large round death total to Verdun or the Somme. Both parts fail. The war was fought on more than one front, by the soldiers of empires as well as by metropolitan armies, and it was carried by home fronts. The chronology does not lock a death total for either battle, so a borrowed integer is not a fact of this course. Refusing the integer is not a claim that the battles were minor. It is a claim about evidence. A second version of the myth says the armistice "was Versailles" or that the war's meaning was signed on 11 November 1918. The treaty is 28 June 1919. A stop to fighting is not a settlement, and a settlement is not a cause you may run backwards until July had no choices left.
:::

:::history
On 21 February 1916 the battle of Verdun begins, and on 1 July 1916 the battle of the Somme begins. In April 1917 the United States enters the war. The armistice is dated 11 November 1918. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, 3 March 1918, is the eastern peace before that armistice. None of these dates is a death total.
:::

If a sentence about 1916 cannot be said without a death total, it was depending on a number the course has withheld. If a sentence about 1918 cannot distinguish 11 November from 28 June 1919, it has merged two documents. What would count against the shape of the chapter is evidence of no imperial recruitment, no home-front mobilisation, or a treaty of settlement dated 11 November 1918. A memoir of one trench would not. It is evidence at the scale of a person. [[#thm-imperial]] stands or falls on recruitment, other fronts and ministries.

A war memorial that names only one village is evidence of who was carved in stone, not the order of battle. This chapter states no national death total and no European total for 1914–1918. "Many died" is as far as an unlocked quantity allows. The kind of war is still describable without that integer: a coalition war of empires, more than one front, home fronts, Verdun, the Somme, the American entry, Brest-Litovsk as a prior eastern peace, and the armistice.

## Where this leads

The armistice leaves Russia already out, by a treaty this chapter has only dated. [[wars/revolutions-1917]] takes up both calendars of 1917, the abdication, October, and Brest-Litovsk as an exit rather than as a mention. The peace treaties, and the claim that the years after 1918 were a corridor to one man, are [[wars/interwar]]. The July crisis remains [[nations/toward-1914]]. How a date differs from a label is [[sources/time-and-names]], and how a cause differs from a label is [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- The war from the invasion of Belgium on 4 August 1914 to the armistice of 11 November 1918 was a multi-front war of empires, not a single trench and not a single cause carried forward from July.
- Soldiers and labourers of the colonies and dominions were part of the war. Their presence is not consent, and this chapter states no headcount.
- Home fronts — labour, food, censorship, conscription — were war work. A poster is not a belief, and a silence is not a mood.
- Verdun begins on 21 February 1916 and the Somme on 1 July 1916, 131 days later. Neither battle is given a death total.
- The United States enters in April 1917. That widens the coalition. It does not start the war and it does not end it.
- Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918 is the eastern peace before the armistice. The revolutions are the next chapter. The eastern exit is not a German victory: the armistice is still 11 November 1918.
- The armistice stops the fighting. Versailles, 28 June 1919, is a later treaty, signed in the hall where the German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871.
:::
