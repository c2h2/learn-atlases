What changed when Christianity became the official religion of the Roman empire, and what did not?

The tempting answer is a mood: pagan light, then Christian darkness, or pagan confusion and then a Christian dawn. Neither curtain fits the dates. Toleration, a creed, an official religion, a restriction on public cult, and a council that split churches are different acts. They change what the state will honour in public and what it will punish. They do not describe every conscience, and they do not switch off Roman law, Roman landowning or Roman tax.

A law is a source. A sentence about what everyone then believed is an inference, and a much larger one. That difference is the subject of [[sources/evidence]]. Here the sources are dated public acts. The inference we refuse is a conversion statistic nobody counted, and a European spirit nobody could have minuted.

## Toleration is not an established church

On 28 October 312 CE, on the usual dating, Constantine wins at the Milvian Bridge. That battle is told in [[rome/later-empire]]. It is not, by itself, a church settlement. The next year Constantine and Licinius agree a policy of toleration, later called the Edict of Milan. The name is later. The policy is not yet an established church. Christians may practise in public without the old penalties; confiscated meeting places are to be restored. Traditional cults are not abolished in the same act. A government that stops persecuting one cult has not yet made that cult the religion of the empire.

Later stories let the battle of 312 CE do the work of the law of 380 CE. We will not. From 312 CE to 313 CE the year numerals differ by 1, and from 313 CE to 380 CE they differ by 67. The house rule for a span, the same rule by which this atlas counts from 800 CE to 843 CE as 43 years, is the difference of the year numerals. Adding both endpoints would turn 67 into 68 and 43 into 44. We do not do that.

:::definition Official religion {#def-official}
**Official religion** is a public and legal status: the cult the emperors name as the empire's own, with consequences for office, property and the right to worship in public. It is not a report of what every inhabitant believed, and it is not a claim that older rites vanished on the day of the decree.
:::

:::definition Heresy {#def-heresy}
**Heresy**, in this chapter, is the category a council or a law produces when it defines a Christian teaching as outside orthodoxy. The word does not mean "anything a modern reader dislikes", and it does not mean a private doubt that no court ever heard. Orthodoxy and heresy are defined together. You do not get one without the other.
:::

## What a council decides

In 325 CE the Council of Nicaea meets under an emperor. Bishops argue about the relation of the Son to the Father and write a definition that later generations treat as a standard. Teachings associated with Arius are placed outside that standard. In our words, not as a pasted creed: the council holds that the Son is of the same substance as the Father, and it marks the contrary teaching as unacceptable. That sentence is a description of a decision. It is not a quotation, and it is not a survey of what Christians in every city already thought.

A council under an emperor is a new kind of public fact. Nicaea does not yet make Christianity the official religion. It draws a line inside Christianity, so that people who call themselves Christians can be told that their Christianity is the wrong one. [[#def-heresy]] starts here as a working category. Later Latin law builds a machinery of exclusion on categories of this kind; that belongs to [[christendom/exclusion]], and we do not narrate it now.

What Nicaea cannot support is a map of belief. No one at the council counted the empire, and this atlas will not invent a share of Nicene and non-Nicene Christians. What would count against the narrow claim, that the council defined a line and not a census, would be minutes of a systematic inquiry into every province's private practice. What we have is a definition agreed by bishops with imperial backing. That is large. It is not a survey of minds.

:::quiz
What did the agreement of 313 CE do?
- [ ] It made Christianity the official religion and closed the temples
- [x] It set out toleration, and the later name Edict of Milan is not the same thing as establishment
- [ ] It was the Council of Nicaea, meeting twelve years early
- [ ] It abolished the land tax because the empire had become Christian
:::solution
The agreement of Constantine and Licinius in 313 CE is a policy of toleration. The familiar name is later, and the chronology is explicit that this is not yet an established church. Official religion is the Edict of Thessalonica in 380 CE. Nicaea, in 325 CE, is a council. The land tax is still there after all three.
:::
:::

## Official religion, then a restriction on public cult

In 380 CE the Edict of Thessalonica, associated with Theodosius I, makes Christianity the official religion of the empire. In paraphrase, and not as a quotation from the law: the emperors take one definition of Christianity, the definition they tie to the sees of Rome and Alexandria, as the public religion, and they treat other Christian definitions as illicit. The paraphrase is only a guide to the legal effect. The wording of the edict is a source a specialist reads in Latin. Our claim uses the effect the chronology states. Christianity becomes the official religion. Enforcement is a process, not a switch.

A decree can change the risks of public life and still leave households and old festivals only partly touched. Bishops gain a standing they did not have under a persecuting government. People who hold a non-Nicene theology, or who keep a traditional civic cult, face a state that has chosen a side. Neither fact is a census. [[#def-official]] is the right size of claim. "The empire became Christian in its heart in 380 CE" is the wrong size.

The year numerals of 391 CE and 380 CE differ by 11. In 391 CE Theodosian measures restrict public pagan cult: sacrifices and the old civic rites, not a second copy of the edict. One act chooses an official Christianity and disables other Christian teachings. The other restricts traditional cult in public. Merging them into a single ban invites the further mistake of treating a law as a window into private conviction. People comply, resist, reinterpret and ignore. A law shows what authority commanded. It does not show who obeyed from belief and who obeyed from prudence.

:::theorem Official religion is not a census of belief {#thm-census}
The edict of 380 CE and the measures of 391 CE are public and legal facts. They do not describe what every inhabitant of the empire believed, and no conversion statistic can be read out of them.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the kind of source. An edict names a public religion and attaches legal consequences. Measures against public cult restrict rites in the open. Neither document enumerates consciences, and the chronology is explicit that enforcement is a process, not a switch. The gap from 380 CE to 391 CE is itself a reason not to merge the acts: if 380 CE had already ended the old public cults, 391 CE would have had nothing public left to restrict. What would count against [[#thm-census]] is a contemporary count of belief, or evidence that public pagan cult had already ceased everywhere before 391 CE as ordinary civic practice. A closed temple is not that count. A surviving local rite does not erase the law. Neither is a percentage.
:::

## What did not stop

Roman government after 380 CE is still a tax-collecting, law-using, land-based state. Christianisation is a change in public religion and in the standing of bishops. It is not the arrival of a different kind of world, and it is not the arrival of a European spirit. The empire that issued these laws was Mediterranean. Its councils met in the east, at Nicaea in 325 CE and at Chalcedon in 451 CE. North Africa and Asia were inside the same imperial church. A spirit of Europe, understood as a later continent with one set of values, is an idea projected backwards. [[sources/naming-europe]] is where that projection is taken apart. Here it is enough to say that a Latin Christian western Europe is not what 380 CE founded.

Landowning does not stop. Great estates remain the backbone of local power, and the church becomes a great landowner in its turn. That changes some holders. It does not end the fact of holding land, and we will not invent a share of soil in ecclesiastical hands. Tax does not stop. The state still needs the land tax to pay soldiers. A change of public cult is not a fiscal reform.

Law does not stop. Justinian's Codex, Digest and Institutes, in [[after-rome/byzantium]], are dated 529 CE, 533 CE and 534 CE. They collect Roman jurisprudence. They do not replace it with a new Christian code. Enslavement does not stop either. The citizenship grant of 212 CE, taught in [[rome/imperial-society]], had left enslaved people enslaved, and official Christianity does not reverse that. No figure for the enslaved population is available, and none will be invented. A public religion can praise mercy and still leave the legal category in place.

:::theorem Law, land and tax outlast the change of public religion {#thm-continuity}
Christianisation altered official cult and the public role of bishops. It did not abolish Roman law, landowning or taxation, and it did not end enslavement.
:::
:::proof
The land tax and the private law of property are still how the state and the elite work after 380 CE and after 391 CE. Justinian's later codification collects Roman jurisprudence rather than discarding it. Enslaved people remain in the law, as they had after 212 CE. Bishops gaining courts and a voice in the city is a real change of local authority, and it sits inside that fiscal and legal order. What would count against [[#thm-continuity]] is evidence that assessment stopped because the public cult had changed, or that a law of 380 CE or 391 CE freed enslaved people as a class. A sermon against greed is not that evidence. One manumission is not the end of the institution.
:::

The sack of Rome on 24 August 410 CE is a negative test. Alaric's Visigoths sack the city after Christianity is already official: 410 minus 380 is 30. The sack did not unmake the church, and the church had not unmade Roman law. Many of the Goths were themselves Christians, though not of the Nicene definition. The assault is a military shock inside an officially Christian empire, not the start of the official religion and not a proof that the religion had failed as a law.

## Chalcedon divides

In 451 CE the Council of Chalcedon is both a definition and a split. In our description, not as a quotation: the council teaches one Christ in two natures, divine and human, without confusion. Churches that did not accept that formula, particularly in Egypt and Syria, parted from the imperial line. The division is doctrinal and political, because the emperor's church backed the council. Later names for the sides are labels. This chapter will not narrate every later church. A council can define orthodoxy and, in the same work, produce a lasting division.

From 325 CE to 451 CE the year numerals differ by 126. Nicaea draws a line about the Son and the Father. Chalcedon draws a line about person and natures. Official religion in 380 CE had already picked a Nicene reference and had not ended argument. [[#thm-census]] applies inside the church as well as at its edge. A law that names one Christianity does not produce one belief.

:::quiz
What can the Council of Chalcedon in 451 CE be used to show?
- [ ] That every Christian in the empire accepted one doctrine from that year on
- [ ] That public pagan cult was restricted for the first time
- [x] That a council can divide churches in the act of defining doctrine
- [ ] That Roman taxation and landowning came to an end
:::solution
Chalcedon is a doctrinal definition that large churches, especially in Egypt and Syria, did not accept. The restriction on public pagan cult is 391 CE, sixty year-numerals earlier, since 451 minus 391 is 60. Law, land and tax continue. A council is evidence of a decision and of a split, not of universal assent and not of a new economy.
:::
:::

## A worked sequence

Read the five dates as permissions, not as a slope. From 313 CE infer a change in legal risk, not an official religion. From 325 CE infer a line between orthodoxy and heresy, not an interview with the provinces. From 380 CE infer a legal status under Theodosius I, not a conversion rate. From 391 CE infer that traditional civic cult was still a public question. From 451 CE infer a split, not the cancellation of the edict. The dissenters argue about the right Christianity inside a world the edict has already reshaped.

Put a person beside the file as a limit, and label her as a type, not as a named source: a city-dweller in Egypt after 451 CE, under Roman tax, beside Roman landowning, and Christian in a church that rejects Chalcedon. The type breaks two slogans. She is not evidence that Rome had fallen, and she is not evidence that the empire was now of one faith. She would become evidence only if a source documented her. Until then she is a reminder, invented as a type and useless as a statistic.

Constantinople's clock blocks another slogan, that the official religion founded the Christian capital. The traditional dedication is 11 May 330 CE. Since 380 minus 330 is 50, the capital exists before the edict, and the edict does not found the city. One place can hold a palace, a tax office and a crowd that does not match the creed.

## Argument and counter-argument

The strongest objection to [[#thm-census]] is practical. If emperors restrict public pagan cult, favour one Christianity, put bishops in the centre of city life, and penalise rival Christian teachings, then for practical purposes the empire has become Christian. To insist on a gap between law and belief looks like pedantry. People follow power. After a generation, public religion and private habit converge. The objection deserves a straight answer.

Convergence in public is real, and it is not the whole of the objection. Closed temples, cancelled civic festivals, bishops as urban patrons, and heresy as a legal risk are changes the laws were built to produce. The objection then slides to a second sentence: that inner conviction followed, uniformly, and can be stated as a number. That sentence needs other evidence. Sermons that complain of unfinished work are not a census either. They do show that clergy did not think the job was done. What we refuse is false precision, not the direction of public change.

A second objection comes from the other side. Talk of transformation, of the sort associated with Peter Brown and named among the course references, can sand the coercion off the story. If nothing "fell", then 391 CE was a gentle change of taste. That reading fails too. A restriction on public cult is an act of power. Classifying Christians as heretics is an act of power. The right use of a transformation argument is to block a single year of darkness, not to deny the force of the dated laws. Catastrophe in 380 CE and gentle continuity across 380 CE are both too smooth. The file of acts is uneven on purpose.

What would settle the rejected claim, that the edict describes everyone's belief, is a count we do not have. What would unsettle the legal claim is evidence that the edict was not issued or did not name Christianity as the public religion. The legal claim stands. The census does not.

:::example The span from toleration to official religion {#ex-span}
The agreement of toleration is 313 CE. The Edict of Thessalonica is 380 CE. How many years is that span, on this atlas's rule? Then say, in one sentence, what the number is not allowed to mean. (Use the difference of the year numerals.)
:::solution
Subtract the year numerals: 380 minus 313 is 67. The span is 67 years, the same kind of count by which 843 minus 800 is 43, not a count of inclusive endpoints, which would give 68.

The number 67 measures the distance between a policy of toleration and a law of official religion. It does not measure a conversion, and it does not mean that nothing of religious importance happened in between. Nicaea, in 325 CE, sits inside the span and is a different kind of act.
:::
:::

:::example Two councils, side by side {#ex-councils}
Set 325 CE beside 451 CE. What can you say they share, and what must you not collapse?
:::solution
Each defines doctrine and thereby marks another teaching as outside it. Nicaea's argument is about the Son and the Father. Chalcedon's is about one person and two natures, and churches in Egypt and Syria reject it.

Do not collapse them into "the church decided". The year numerals differ by 126, because 451 minus 325 is 126. Official religion in 380 CE sits between them. A definition is evidence about that decision, not about all later assent.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a source.

"In the year the emperors made Christianity official, every inhabitant of the empire abandoned the old cults and accepted the creed."

What would it take to promote this sentence into evidence, and which dated acts already tell against it?
:::solution
Promotion would take a contemporary count of cult across the empire. An edict shows a command. A census of rites, which we do not have and will not invent, would be needed for "every inhabitant".

The measures of 391 CE still restrict public pagan cult, and 391 minus 380 is 11, which is pointless if everyone had already abandoned that cult. Chalcedon in 451 CE shows Christians divided about doctrine: 451 minus 380 is 71. The invented sentence turns a legal act into a universal change of heart.
:::
:::

:::example What 391 CE can support {#ex-391}
A reader says: "391 CE is the year paganism ended." Rewrite the claim at the size the evidence will bear, and name one thing the date cannot do.
:::solution
The size that fits is: in 391 CE Theodosian measures restricted public pagan cult. That is a legal and public act, aimed at civic rites, sacrifices and the old public worship. It belongs with the official religion of 380 CE but is not the same act. The year numerals differ by 11.

The date cannot end "paganism" as a word for every old habit, private memory or local practice, and it cannot yield a share of the population. It also cannot be moved back to 313 CE, which was toleration, or forward as if Chalcedon were about the same subject. Public cult is not belief in general, and a restriction is not a census.
:::
:::

Move the year and refuse a slope. A window of 10 years will not make 313 CE and 451 CE neighbours: the year numerals differ by 138, because 451 minus 313 is 138. Nothing on the line measures belief. A rising quantity of "Christianisation" would be your addition, not the figure's.

:::warning Darkness, or a dawn
The usual myth runs in two directions and fails in both. One version says that an official church put the lights out on a tolerant pagan reason. The other says that Christianity switched on a moral Europe. Traditional civic cult was not a liberal settlement, and the edicts were not a night of pure coercion or a sunrise of freedom. They were laws about public religion, councils that defined heresy as well as orthodoxy, and a state that went on taxing and holding people in slavery. A European spirit is not one of the dots. A conversion statistic is not either.
:::

:::history
In 380 CE the Edict of Thessalonica, under Theodosius I, made Christianity the official religion of the empire. Practice and enforcement took longer than a decree. The agreement of 313 CE had been toleration, later called the Edict of Milan, and the measures of 391 CE against public pagan cult were a further act, not a synonym for the edict.
:::

## Where this leads

The western provinces, already under this official religion, are the setting for the kingdoms in [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]]. A Frankish king's baptism will raise the same problem as 380 CE, a public act that is not a census, with the added problem that the year itself is disputed. The state that issued the edicts does not vanish when a western usurper is deposed: [[after-rome/byzantium]] follows that Roman government through Justinian's law and beyond. Why 476 CE is a convention, not the end of Roman life, is argued in [[rome/later-empire]] and, as a lesson in counter-evidence, in [[sources/argument]].

:::summary
- The agreement of 313 CE is toleration. The name Edict of Milan is later. It is not an established church, and the Milvian Bridge in 312 CE is not a substitute for it.
- Nicaea in 325 CE defines a doctrine and thereby defines heresy. A council is not a census of belief, and no conversion rate belongs in the story.
- The Edict of Thessalonica in 380 CE makes Christianity the official religion. Enforcement is a process. The measures of 391 CE restrict public pagan cult and are a different act.
- Official religion is a legal and public fact under Theodosius I. It does not say what every inhabitant believed.
- Chalcedon in 451 CE defines doctrine and splits churches, especially in Egypt and Syria. It does not end Roman law, landowning, tax or enslavement.
- Christianisation is not a European spirit, not a curtain of darkness, and not a dawn. The sack of Rome on 24 August 410 CE neither begins nor cancels the official religion.
:::

They agreed a policy of toleration. Christians could assemble and recover property without the agreement amounting to an official imperial religion. The familiar name, Edict of Milan, is later, so using it as if the participants had published a modern statute under that title overstates the document and misreads the legal result. A museum panel says: "380 CE: Europe becomes Christian." Keep whatever is defensible in the date, and replace the rest with a claim you could check. Name the limit in the same answer.

The defensible core is the Edict of Thessalonica in 380 CE, associated with Theodosius I, making Christianity the official religion of the Roman empire. A checkable replacement is: in 380 CE the emperors named a Nicene Christianity as the empire's public religion, and enforcement was a process rather than a switch.

"Europe becomes Christian" fails because the unit is the empire, not a later idea of Europe, and because the change is legal, not a census. :::exercise Two objections to the sack {level=2}
A reader says the sack of Rome on 24 August 410 CE proves that the official religion had already replaced the Roman state. Another says the sack proves the official religion had failed. Answer both, using the order of the dates and nothing about a European spirit.
:::solution
Both readings overload one assault. Official religion dates from 380 CE, and the measures against public pagan cult from 391 CE. The sack is later: 410 minus 380 is 30. It does not start that legal status and does not repeal it. Law, tax and landowning are not abolished by the entry of a Gothic army, nor were they abolished by the edict.

The second reading treats a military event as a verdict on doctrine. Many of the Goths were Christians of a non-Nicene kind, so the attack is not a pagan restoration and not a test of whether a creed was true. [[#thm-continuity]] survives both objections: cult and law run on different clocks.
:::

:::exercise Bishops and the tax {level=2}
Explain how bishops can become more important in city government while [[#thm-continuity]] remains true. Use no population figure and no share of land.
:::solution
A bishop can gain a court, a building and a say in urban quarrels. That is a real change in who mediates local power, and it is one of the things Christianisation changed. It happens in a world that still assesses land, recognises property and distinguishes free and enslaved people. Church estates change the holder of some land. They do not end landowning.

[[#thm-continuity]] would fail if bishops replaced the tax with gifts, or if only creed mattered in court. The sermon and the assessment exist side by side. Importance in the city is not the abolition of the fiscal state.
:::

A friend accepts that 380 CE is an edict, then says: "After 391 CE, public cult was restricted, so paganism ended. Asking for a census is a way to avoid the obvious." :::hint
Separate two claims that the friend has glued together: a command about public rites, and a result about every person's religion. Then ask what kind of source each claim needs. The year 391 CE is not the year 380 CE.

:::solution
The friend's argument is that restricting public cult ends "paganism", and that a demand for numbers is evasion. The practical half has force. Penalising public sacrifice changes the civic square, and public habit can shift. Historians should say so.

The counter-argument is that "ended" is a universal word. Public cult is not private habit or every rite a law did not see. The acts of 380 CE and 391 CE are eleven year-numerals apart and have different targets. Neither counts persons. Refusing an invented percentage is not a refusal to describe coercion.

The friend is right if the evidence shows public pagan cult gone wherever the law claimed to reach, and if the sentence is rewritten to that public scope. Give up the legal claim if the measures of 391 CE were not issued or did not aim at public cult. Give up [[#thm-census]] only if a contemporary census appears. Until then the law stands and the universal sentence does not.
:::
