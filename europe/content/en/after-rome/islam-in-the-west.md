How does Islamic political history belong inside a European course?

It belongs because of the map, not because of a moral score. Iberia is in Europe. From 711 CE, when a force led by Tariq ibn Ziyad crosses into the peninsula, a large part of its political history is the history of Muslim rulers, Muslim law and the people who lived under them. A course that falls silent until Latin Christian kings are back in every city has edited the geography. The edit is the same kind of choice [[sources/naming-europe]] names when it puts al-Andalus inside the map and refuses to write it as an interruption.

The polity did not begin at the straits. The Hijra is 622 CE, the start of the Islamic calendar's year count. Muhammad dies in 632 CE. Yarmouk, on the usual dating, is 636 CE, a defeat of an eastern Roman army that helps explain how a new state could hold Syria. None of those is an Iberian event. They are why 711 CE is intelligible. Tours, or Poitiers, is one battle with three candidate years, October 732 CE on the traditional dating and 733 or 734 CE for scholars who follow the Chronicle of 754. It did not save Europe. Charles Martel was not king. Raids continued. The Umayyad emirate of Cordoba is established in 756 CE, after every proposed date of the battle, and a caliphal title follows in 929 CE. A legend that treats the battle as an ending cannot survive its own timeline.

## A new polity, before Iberia

:::definition Hijra {#def-hijra}
The **Hijra** is the emigration, in 622 CE, from which the Islamic calendar's year count later begins. It is a date in Arabia. It is not an Iberian event, and a year in that calendar is not the same length as the Julian year this atlas uses for its own dates. This chapter does not convert freely between the two counts. It uses the Christian-era years the chronology has locked.
:::

:::definition Al-Andalus {#def-andalus}
**Al-Andalus** is the Muslim polity in Iberia, from the crossing of 711 CE through the emirate of Cordoba in 756 CE and the caliphate proclaimed in 929 CE, and beyond those titles for as long as Muslim rule in the peninsula lasts. It is a political history of rulers, tax, armies and cities. It is not a symbol of tolerance, and it is not a symbol of menace.
:::

From 622 CE to 632 CE the year numerals differ by 10. That decade is the life of the community under Muhammad after the Hijra, and this chapter is not a biography. The locked facts are the emigration that starts the calendar and the death. After the death, authority in the new community is a political question. This atlas has not locked a list of early caliphs' years, so none will be supplied. The next locked battle is Yarmouk in 636 CE, on the usual dating: four year-numerals after the death, since 636 minus 632 is 4. "Usual" here means what it means for the Lombard entry in [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]]. The date is the standard one to teach, and it is still a dating, not a charter we have copied out.

Yarmouk is an eastern Roman defeat. Syria passes out of Roman control in its aftermath. The Roman state itself, the subject of [[after-rome/byzantium]], does not end in 636 CE. A battle can remove a field army and a region without removing an empire. What the battle does for this chapter is explanatory. A new polity is already large, and already victorious against the emperor at Constantinople, decades before anyone crosses into Iberia. From 636 CE to 711 CE the year numerals differ by 75. From the Hijra to the crossing they differ by 89, because 711 minus 622 is 89. Iberia is a later western frontier of a history that has already begun. Telling Islamic politics as if it started at Tours is how a European course loses the explanation and keeps only the scare.

:::theorem Iberia after 711 belongs in the course {#thm-inside}
From 711 CE the political history of Iberia is not only a Latin history. A European course that omits the Muslim polities has chosen a map, not followed the evidence, and the events of 622, 632 and 636 CE explain the polity without yet being Iberian.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is geographical and dated. Tariq ibn Ziyad's force crosses into Iberia in 711 CE. The peninsula is inside the geography this atlas calls Europe. What follows, the emirate of 756 CE and the caliphate of 929 CE, is government in that peninsula, not a foreign episode parked offshore. The Hijra, the death of Muhammad and Yarmouk, on the usual dating, are earlier and elsewhere. They explain a new state. They do not happen in Iberia, which is why a chapter that began at Cordoba would be as misleading as a chapter that began at Tours. What would count against [[#thm-inside]] is a showing that 711 CE did not place Muslim rule inside Iberia, or that "Europe" in this atlas was defined in advance to exclude the peninsula's Muslim centuries. [[sources/naming-europe]] refuses that definition. What would not count is a reader's dislike of the rulers, or a later Latin conquest. Granada's surrender in 1492 CE, taught in [[crises/year-1492]], is a different event. It does not reach backwards and make 711 CE a footnote.
:::

## 711, then Cordoba

The crossing of 711 CE can be stated in one sentence, which is all the chronology locks and all this chapter will use. A force led by Tariq ibn Ziyad crosses into Iberia. There is no ship-count here, no speech from a rock, and no battle narrative. Adding them would be invention. The course record's sentence is the claim: after this year the peninsula's political history is not only a Latin history.

In 750 CE the Abbasid revolution overthrows the Umayyad caliphate in the east. In 756 CE Abd al-Rahman I, an Umayyad, establishes an emirate at Cordoba. The year numerals differ by 6. This chapter will not fill those six years with a travelogue it cannot date. The political result is enough. Al-Andalus becomes a separate Umayyad polity, not a loyal province of the new Abbasid caliph. An emir is a commander and a prince. He is not yet claiming the supreme title of caliph. Brian Catlos's history, named among the course references, is useful exactly here: the subject is politics, not a symbol of tolerance and not a symbol of threat.

In 929 CE Abd al-Rahman III takes the title of caliph. From 756 CE to 929 CE the year numerals differ by 173. A change of title is a claim about legitimacy, that the ruler in Cordoba is not a deputy of someone else, made while an Abbasid caliph still exists in the east. It is a fact of political language. It is not a census of how many people changed their worship in that year, and it is not the same institution as the Roman imperial title taken in Rome in 800 CE. The formal parallel is only this: a new supreme claim set beside an older one. The offices are different, the religions are different, and [[after-rome/carolingians]] must not be collapsed into Cordoba or the reverse.

This atlas has not locked a year for the end of the caliphate of Cordoba. The title taken in 929 CE is not a freeze-frame of the peninsula forever. Granada in 1492 CE is centuries later and is not the meaning of 711 CE. Between those poles the honest sentence is political. Rulers, tax, armies and the city of Cordoba are the subject. Muslims, Christians and Jews live under rules that change by reign. A protected status for Jews and Christians is still a lesser legal status: protection is not equality, and a tax attached to it is not a detail we can price, because no figure is locked. Cultural exchange, including books, is also real. Neither fact is the whole story, and neither is a score for a modern argument.

- [ ] Because Tours had already saved the continent, and 711 CE is only the sequel
- [x] Because a force led by Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed into Iberia, so the peninsula's politics are not only Latin after that year
- [ ] Because the Hijra of 622 CE happened in Iberia

:::solution
The Hijra is an Arabian date in 622 CE, and the caliphal title at Cordoba is 929 CE, not 622 CE. Tours is later than 711 CE and did not end Muslim rule. The crossing is the reason a European geography cannot tell Iberia as an uninterrupted Latin story. [[#thm-inside]] is that claim, with the earlier dates kept outside the peninsula.

## A battle that was not an ending

Tours and Poitiers are two names for one battle, not two battles. The traditional date is October 732 CE. Some scholars, using the Chronicle of 754, prefer 733 CE or 734 CE. All three have to be taught. This atlas will not pick in silence, any more than it picked a single year for Clovis. The figure below plots the traditional year and one alternative, 733 CE. It does not mark 734 CE. The missing dot is not a decision against that year. It is a limit of the figure, and the prose has to carry the third candidate.

Charles Martel commanded the Frankish force. He was mayor of the palace, the leading military power in the kingdom, and he was not king. He was not the man crowned on 25 December 800 CE. Charlemagne is a later member of the same family, Martel's grandson, and the imperial date is [[after-rome/carolingians]]. Merging the two men lets a battle do the work of a coronation. Whichever Tours year you use, the distance to 800 CE is still a matter of decades: 800 minus 732 is 68, 800 minus 733 is 67, and 800 minus 734 is 66. A mayor in the 730s is not an emperor in 800 CE.

The battle did not save Europe. That legend is a later story, Frankish in its first pride and continental in its modern form. It wants one afternoon in which "Europe" defeated "Islam". The afternoon, on any of the three dates, is a Frankish defeat of a raiding army. The strategic result was limited. Raids continued. The chronology states the continuation and does not lock a year for a later raid, so this chapter will not invent one. What it can lock is the politics after the battle. In 756 CE the emirate of Cordoba is established. From October 732 CE to 756 CE the year numerals differ by 24; from 733 CE they differ by 23; from 734 CE they differ by 22. On every reading, Cordoba comes afterwards. A legend that treats the battle as an ending has to explain a government founded after the ending. It cannot.

:::theorem Tours did not end Muslim rule, and it did not save Europe {#thm-tours}
The battle called Tours or Poitiers, traditionally dated October 732 CE and by some scholars 733 or 734 CE, did not save Europe and did not end Islamic political power in the west. Charles Martel was not king, raids continued, and the emirate of Cordoba was established in 756 CE.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the disputed date plus the later locked politics. Three candidate years mean that even the when of the legend is not settled. The Chronicle of 754 is why 733 and 734 CE are live, not because this chapter has a private preference. Martel held the mayoralty, not the crown; the imperial coronation of his grandson is 25 December 800 CE. The chronology states that raids continued. It also dates the emirate of Cordoba to 756 CE and the caliphate to 929 CE, both after the battle. What would count against [[#thm-tours]] is evidence that Muslim polities left Iberia in the 730s, or that no further raids occurred, or that Martel was already king. None of those is the record. What the battle can support is narrower and still worth having: a Frankish force defeated a raiding army, under a commander who was not king, in one of three years. Limited is not the same as imaginary.
:::

- [ ] The only date scholars use is October 732 CE
- [x] The traditional date is October 732 CE, some scholars prefer 733 or 734 CE, the battle did not save Europe, and Charles Martel was not king

:::solution
October 732 CE is traditional, not exclusive. The Chronicle of 754 is why 733 and 734 CE remain in the argument. Martel was mayor of the palace, not king, and he was not Charlemagne. The emirate begins in 756 CE, after the battle, and raids continued. "Saved Europe" is the legend [[#thm-tours]] refuses.
:::

## Argument and counter-argument

One objection says that putting al-Andalus inside a European course is a gesture, a way of being kind, and that the real Europe is the Latin Christian one the Franks were defending at Tours. On this view, 711 CE is an invasion from outside the subject, and the chapter is special pleading. The dates answer the objection without a sermon. [[#thm-inside]] does not ask anyone to like the rulers. It asks where the peninsula is, and who governed it. A course may still choose to study only Latin Christendom, but it should say that it has chosen. It should not say that the omitted centuries happened somewhere other than Europe. Yarmouk makes the same point from the other shore. The new polity is already a Mediterranean power in 636 CE. Iberia in 711 CE is a further reach of that power, not the sudden appearance of a topic that exists only to threaten the Franks.

The opposite objection is just as flattening. If the legend of a saved continent is false, then al-Andalus must have been a golden age of tolerance, and saying otherwise is a return of the menace story. [[#def-andalus]] blocks both symbols. Legal inequality for Jews and Christians is real. So is a learned culture in Arabic that later Latin scholars drew on, in ways [[christendom/towns-and-learning]] can take up without a foundation year this atlas has not locked. Catlos's point, used here as a rule rather than as a quotation, is that a political history survives both posters. What would make the tolerance poster true as a description of the whole period is a single legal order of equality, which the protected-and-taxed status is not. What would make the menace poster true as a description of Tours is an end to Muslim rule in the 730s, which 756 CE and 929 CE contradict.

A third pressure is to tell 711 CE properly, with ships, a rock and a speech. The pressure feels like completeness. It is invention. The chronology gives a commander and a crossing. [[sources/evidence]] is the general form of the refusal: do not promote a vivid sentence into a source. An invented detail would be labelled as invented, and it would still not be evidence.

:::example From the Hijra to Iberia {#ex-span}
The Hijra is 622 CE. Tariq's force crosses into Iberia in 711 CE. What is the difference of the year numerals, and what is the number not allowed to mean?
:::solution
711 minus 622 is 89. The span is 89 years.

It is not the length of a war, and it is not a claim that nothing happened in between. Muhammad dies in 632 CE. Yarmouk, on the usual dating, is 636 CE. Both sit inside the span, and neither is in Iberia. The number only stops a story that makes 711 CE the opening of Islamic political history.
:::
:::

:::example Three years, then an emirate {#ex-tours-years}
Set October 732 CE, 733 CE and 734 CE beside 756 CE. What remains true on every choice of the battle's year?
:::solution
On every choice Charles Martel is not king, the battle is not the coronation of 800 CE, and raids are not said to have stopped. The emirate of Cordoba is established in 756 CE, after the battle: 756 minus 732 is 24, 756 minus 733 is 23, and 756 minus 734 is 22.

So the legend of an ending fails whichever year the Chronicle of 754, or the traditional dating, is given. The figure in this chapter marks 732 and 733 CE only. 734 CE is still a candidate, and the arithmetic does not need a dot in order to count.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a source.

"King Charles saved Europe at Tours in 732, and no Muslim ruler remained in the west."

List the clauses that fail, and the locked fact that kills each one.
:::solution
"King" fails because Charles Martel was not king. He was mayor of the palace. "Saved Europe" fails because that is the later legend, and the battle's result was limited. "In 732" stated as the only year fails because 733 and 734 CE are also argued, from the Chronicle of 754, though October 732 CE remains the traditional date. "No Muslim ruler remained" fails because Abd al-Rahman I establishes the emirate of Cordoba in 756 CE, and Abd al-Rahman III takes a caliphal title in 929 CE.

Raids continued, with no year invented here for a later raid. The sentence is a legend compressed into one line. Promoting it would require evidence this chronology explicitly withholds.
:::
:::

:::example Yarmouk is not Iberia {#ex-yarmouk}
Why must Yarmouk, usually dated 636 CE, be taught in this chapter, and why must it not be told as an Iberian battle? Use the distance to 711 CE.
:::solution
Yarmouk explains the new polity. An eastern Roman field army is defeated, and Roman control of Syria fails in the aftermath, while the Roman state centred on Constantinople continues. Without that context, 711 CE looks like a bolt from an empty sky, which is how the Tours legend prefers to start the story.

It is not Iberian. 711 minus 636 is 75, and the places are different. Calling Yarmouk a Spanish battle, or calling 711 CE a second Yarmouk, merges a Syrian campaign with a crossing into Iberia. [[#thm-inside]] needs both facts, kept apart: a polity already in existence, and a later entry into a European peninsula.
:::

## Where this leads

Charles Martel's family, and the imperial claim of 25 December 800 CE that must not be confused with his battle, are [[after-rome/carolingians]]. The Roman state defeated at Yarmouk and still standing is [[after-rome/byzantium]]. Granada in 1492 CE is [[crises/year-1492]], not a gloss on 711 CE. Latin learning that later used Arabic books, without a foundation year invented here, meets [[christendom/towns-and-learning]]. The map that keeps al-Andalus inside Europe is [[sources/naming-europe]].

:::summary
- The Hijra is 622 CE. Muhammad dies in 632 CE. Yarmouk, on the usual dating, is 636 CE. These explain a new polity. They are not yet Iberian events.
- In 711 CE a force led by Tariq ibn Ziyad crosses into Iberia. The peninsula's political history is not only Latin after that year. This chapter adds no ships, no speech and no battle-piece.
- In 750 CE the Abbasids overthrow the eastern Umayyad caliphate. In 756 CE Abd al-Rahman I founds the Umayyad emirate of Cordoba. In 929 CE Abd al-Rahman III takes the title of caliph.
