Why is "barbarian invasions" a bad summary of the kingdoms that followed the western empire?

The phrase puts every army, every settlement and every new king on one slope, from outsiders to an empty Roman west. The dates will not sit on it. In 476 CE Odoacer deposes Romulus Augustulus, and the eastern empire continues; Julius Nepos dies in 480 CE. Goths and Franks build kingdoms in Roman provinces, on Roman land, with Roman tax and often with Roman administrators. The baptism of a Frankish king is disputed: both about 496 CE and about 508 CE have to be taught. The Lombard entry into Italy is usually dated 568 CE, after Justinian's reign of 527 to 565 CE, and it is not the same event as a fifth-century settlement. A summary that makes all of this one invasion has already chosen its answer.

This chapter refuses two extras the phrase smuggles in. One is racial replacement, a new people emptying a province. The other is a moral darkness this chapter will not adopt as its own claim. War and dispossession stay in the political history. They do not turn a Roman insult into a race, and they do not make 476 CE a blank page.

## An insult that became a period name

Latin *barbari* marked people as outside a Roman picture of civilised speech and manners. It was an insult and a category. It was not a nation, and it was not a race in any later biological sense. Goths and Franks were not one stock wearing one name. Roman writers reached for a single sneer because it made armed neighbours look illegitimate. Much later, national histories reused the sneer. Some wanted barbarian ancestors as founders. Some wanted barbarian destroyers so that a pure Rome could die and a pure nation could be born afterwards. Both uses treat the insult as if it had been an ethnic census.

:::definition Barbarian {#def-barbarian}
**Barbarian**, in this chapter, is a Roman insult for peoples outside a Roman civilised self-image. Used as a period name, "the barbarian invasions", it pretends that many kingdoms were one racial wave. It does not name a documented people, and it does not by itself describe how those kingdoms taxed and judged.
:::

:::definition Successor kingdom {#def-successor}
A **successor kingdom** is a monarchy built in a Roman province, drawing on Roman land, Roman tax and, often, Roman-trained administrators, under a king whose power did not come from the old imperial succession alone. It is not a county of a vanished empire, and it is not evidence that the provincial population had been replaced.
:::

Roman authors benefit from the period name, because the word keeps the new kings outside the only legitimacy they respect. Schoolbooks benefit, because one phrase covers a century and skips the difference between a sack, a deposition, a baptism and a later entry into Italy. The checkable history is political. Armed groups, many already inside or beside the empire, fought and bargained for land, status and revenue, then ruled provincials with tools the provincials knew.

[[#def-barbarian]] does not say that nobody crossed a frontier. People moved, and armies moved. The objection to the summary is the bundle attached to the movement: one race, an emptied west, and a stop to Roman institutions. Drop the bundle and a plainer sentence remains. Armed groups took and shared power inside Roman provinces, often by war, and then taxed and judged through Roman means.

## Goths, Franks, and Roman machinery

The sack of Rome on 24 August 410 CE, under Alaric, is a Gothic army in the city. It is taught from the other side in [[rome/later-empire]] and [[after-rome/christianisation]]. It is not yet a map of the kingdoms. Gothic groups did go on to rule in Roman provinces, in Gaul, in Iberia and in Italy. This atlas has not locked a separate foundation year for each of those kingdoms, so none will be invented here. What is locked is the pattern [[#def-successor]] names, and the Italian political dates that frame it: the deposition of 476 CE, the death of Nepos in 480 CE, and, much later, the Lombard entry usually dated 568 CE.

Between 476 CE and 568 CE a Gothic kingship in Italy is part of the political history, and this atlas has not locked a year for its beginning or its end. The pattern, rather than a reign-length, is what the sources support: a king in a Roman landscape, using men who could write Roman law and Roman letters about land and tax. Roman-trained officials drafting for kings is the opposite of a forest people inventing government from nothing. It is inheritance, in the sense Chris Wickham's comparative history, named among the course references, argues for the post-Roman west: old fiscal and legal habits under new rulers. A book is not a substitute for a charter. It is a reason not to start the story at zero.

The Franks are the other case the dates will actually hold. Their power in Gaul is the setting for Clovis, and for a baptism into the Nicene Christianity that had been the empire's official religion since 380 CE. That official religion is [[after-rome/christianisation]]. Many Gothic kings were already Christian in a form that did not match the Nicene line drawn at Nicaea in 325 CE. A Frankish king's acceptance of the official definition was therefore a political alignment with Nicene bishops and with a large Gallo-Roman public, not a first contact between "the Germans" and religion. It was also not a census of what every Frank believed. The same limit that blocks a conversion statistic in 380 CE blocks one here.

:::solution
The armies and the dispossession are real, which is why the first option fails. The phrase fails for a different reason. It bundles distinct kingdoms into one outsider race and skips the Roman machinery those kingdoms used. Goths and Franks were not one people, and 476 CE is a deposition, not a restored Republic. The eastern empire continues past that year.

## Two proposed years, and no silent choice

Historians do not have a contemporary register that says in which year Clovis was baptised. The main Latin narrative was written later by Gregory of Tours. This atlas has not locked a year for his history. It is not a baptismal register, and nothing in it will be presented here as a quotation. He orders wars and a baptism relative to each other. Historians who reorder those wars do not agree on the calendar year. One placing gives about 496 CE. Another gives about 508 CE, closer to a time when his power in Gaul was wider and his dealings with bishops in the south belong. Both have been argued. Choosing one in a textbook and hiding the other is what this atlas will not do.

The dispute is about the year. It is not, on the argument this chronology asks us to teach, a dispute about whether a Frankish king aligned himself with Nicene Christianity. What would count as settling the year is a contemporary document that bears one. What would count against the baptism as an event at all is a showing that the later narrative invented the alignment itself. Until either test is met, the sentence we can check is the narrow one. There was a public Nicene baptism of a Frankish king, and the year is not agreed. From about 496 CE to about 508 CE the year numerals differ by 12. Twelve years is enough to wreck any story that makes the baptism the precise cause of one campaign, because the campaign and the baptism cannot be locked in order. We can use the baptism as a public religious alignment. We cannot use it as a stopwatch.

:::theorem The baptism of Clovis has no single year {#thm-clovis}
Both about 496 CE and about 508 CE have been argued for the baptism of Clovis. Neither year may be taught as the certain date, and the baptism is not a census of Frankish belief.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the state of the argument, which the chronology marks as disputed, together with the kind of source. The narrative is later. It is not a charter with a year on it. Scholars who reconstruct the order of the wars it describes arrive at both proposed years, and this atlas has not locked the years of those wars, so it will not smuggle in a third date to break the tie. A public baptism into the official, Nicene form of Christianity fits the politics of bishops and Gallo-Roman cities after 380 CE. It does not record what every person in the kingdom thought. What would count against [[#thm-clovis]] is a contemporary document that fixes one year, or a consensus that one of the two reconstructions fails a test the other survives. Popularity in a textbook is not that test. What would count against the wider claim, that the alignment happened at all, is evidence that the later history invented the baptism. Disagreement about the year is not that evidence.
:::

A reader who wants a single year is usually asking for a cause. If the baptism is "early", it can be told as the secret of his rise. If it is "late", it can be told as the decoration of a victory already won. Both stories are available only if we pretend the year is known. [[#thm-clovis]] blocks the pretence. The political fact that survives either year is alignment with the official church. The military narrative that needs a particular year does not survive.

## 476 did not empty the west

Odoacer's deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476 CE is a real change of ruler in Italy. It became, much later, the conventional "fall of the Western Roman empire". The convention is not the event. The eastern empire continues. Nepos dies in 480 CE, so a rival western claim outlives the famous afternoon: 480 minus 476 is 4. Nothing in that pair of years describes an emptied countryside, a replaced population, or the end of tax. [[rome/later-empire]] and [[sources/argument]] set 476 CE beside the survival of the east on purpose. This chapter adds the kingdoms. The political history of the next generations is not a footnote to a fall. It is the history.

:::theorem The deposition of 476 did not empty the western provinces {#thm-empty}
Odoacer's deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476 CE did not empty the west of Romans, of tax, or of political life. Successor kingdoms are that political life, and the eastern empire continued.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is what follows the deposition, and what the deposition itself is. It is the removal of one claimant in Italy, not a reported evacuation of the provinces. Nepos dies only in 480 CE. Gothic and Frankish rule continues on Roman land, using Roman fiscal and legal habits. The east still has an emperor, which is why a western coronation in 800 CE will be contested from Constantinople, a later fact taught in [[after-rome/carolingians]]. What would count against [[#thm-empty]] is evidence that provincial populations were replaced as peoples in 476 CE, or that Roman tax and Roman legal practice stopped at that deposition as a general fact. A burned city, a confiscated estate, or a frightened chronicle is evidence of violence. It is not evidence of an empty continent. Regional variation is large. This atlas has not locked a year for the end of Roman government in Britain, and the sack of Rome on 24 August 410 CE is not that year. Britain is not Italy. The claim is about the pattern the locked dates support, not about every valley.
:::

The Lombard entry is the later case that stops anyone pasting the fifth century onto the sixth. Justinian reigns from 527 to 565 CE. Whatever his western wars achieved, and [[after-rome/byzantium]] is where his law and his capital belong, his reign ends in 565 CE. The usual date for the Lombard entry into Italy is 568 CE. Usual is not the same as a baptismal dispute with two live candidates. It means the scholarly convention is stable enough to teach as the standard date, while still being a convention rather than a charter in our hands. It is not a licence to treat 568 CE as the same wave that deposited Romulus. The year numerals differ by 92, because 568 minus 476 is 92. An eastern Roman reign sits in the gap. A summary called "the barbarian invasions" that runs from 476 CE through 568 CE as one event has ignored that gap.

- [ ] He was baptised in 508 CE, and the earlier proposal has been refuted
- [x] Both about 496 CE and about 508 CE have been argued, and this atlas does not choose
- [ ] A contemporary register gives the year, so there is nothing to dispute
:::solution
The chronology marks the baptism as disputed and names both years. There is no contemporary register in the evidence this chapter uses, and Gregory of Tours is a later narrator, not a charter. Teaching only one year would be the silent choice the chapter exists to stop. The dispute taught here is about the year of a Nicene baptism, not a denial that Frankish kingship aligned itself with the official church.

## Argument and counter-argument

The strongest objection to this chapter is that "invasion" is the honest word. Armies did cross into Roman provinces. People were killed and dispossessed. A soft vocabulary of settlement and transformation hides blood behind a clerk's desk. If the choice is between a racial epic and a story in which nothing hurt, the epic at least admits the war.

The objection is right about the war and wrong about the race. Refusing "barbarian invasions" as a summary is not a claim that the takeovers were peaceful, and it is not a claim that every provincial family kept its land. [[#thm-empty]] says the west was not emptied and that Roman fiscal life did not stop as a general fact. It does not say a peasant in a fought-over district was safe. The better sentence keeps the violence inside a political history: armed groups took power, often by war, and then ran Roman machines. What would make the racial summary true is evidence of replacement of the population as a people. What would make the "empty west" true is a stop to tax, cultivation and law at 476 CE. Chronicles of fear do not supply either. They supply fear, which we should not edit out.

A second objection says that a disputed baptism should be dropped. That is too neat. [[#thm-clovis]] keeps the alignment at the size the sources will bear and refuses a false year. Dropping it would hide the religious politics we can teach. Inventing a year between the two proposals would be a third invention, not a source.

Peter Brown's case for a long transformation, named among the course references, blocks a single year of darkness. It is not a permit to sand the wars flat. The file of dates stays uneven: a deposition, two proposals for one baptism, a later entry into Italy.

:::example Twelve years, and no winner {#ex-twelve}
About 496 CE and about 508 CE are both argued for Clovis's baptism. What is the difference of the year numerals, and what must you still not conclude? (Use this atlas's rule for a span.)
:::solution
508 minus 496 is 12. The difference is 12 years. It is not a count that adds one for each endpoint.

The number does not choose a year. It only shows how far apart the proposals are. A story that needs the baptism to cause one particular campaign cannot be locked, because twelve years is enough to reorder the campaign and the rite. The number is also not a census of Frankish belief.
:::
:::

:::example 476 beside 568 {#ex-pair}
Set the deposition of Romulus Augustulus beside the Lombard entry into Italy. What can stand in one sentence, and what cannot?
:::solution
In 476 CE Odoacer deposes Romulus Augustulus in Italy, and the eastern empire continues. The Lombard entry is usually dated 568 CE, after Justinian's reign of 527 to 565 CE. The year numerals differ by 92.

Those can stand together as two political breaks, not as one invasion. What cannot stand is a sentence in which 568 CE is the last ripple of 476 CE, or in which either year empties the province. Usual dating for the Lombards is firmer than the Clovis dispute, and it is still not a charter we are holding. Gothic rule in Italy between the two dates is real, and this atlas has not locked its bounds, so those bounds stay out of the sentence.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a source.

"In 476 the barbarian races replaced the Romans, the tax ceased, and the dark age began."

What would it take to promote any clause of it into evidence, and which locked facts already tell against it?
:::solution
Each clause needs its own kind of source. Replacement of a population would need evidence of peoples, not a list of kings. The end of tax would need fiscal records that stop, not a moral. A "dark age" would need a definition this chapter refuses to adopt as its own claim. The invented sentence has none of those sources behind it.

Against it: the eastern empire continues after 476 CE, and Nepos dies in 480 CE. Successor kingdoms use Roman land, tax and often Roman administrators. The Frankish baptism is argued at about 496 CE and about 508 CE, which is a religious politics, not an empty dark. The Lombard entry, usually dated 568 CE, is a later break, not the dawn of a darkness that began on one afternoon in Italy.
:::
:::

:::example What an administrator shows {#ex-admin}
A king in Italy employs Roman-trained men to write laws and letters about land and tax. What does that support, and what does it not support? Do not invent a reign-year for him.
:::solution
It supports [[#def-successor]]: a kingdom in a Roman province using Roman legal and fiscal habits. The men are evidence of continuity in the machinery, not a curiosity. Wickham's sort of comparative claim, that these kingdoms inherit Roman practices, has a concrete shape here.

It does not support a year this atlas has not locked. Gothic kingship in Italy after Odoacer is real and undated in this chapter. It also does not support racial replacement, and it does not show that every provincial peasant consented. A clerk's letter is evidence of government. It is not a census of loyalty, and it is not a proof that no one was dispossessed.
:::
:::

:::warning Replacement, and a dark age
Two myths travel as one sentence. The first says that barbarian races replaced the Romans. The second says that a dark age then began, as if the lights went out with Romulus. The word barbarian is an insult, not a race. Kingdoms on Roman land, using Roman tax and often Roman administrators, are not a replacement of the population and not an empty continent. Uneven records in some regions are a real problem for historians. They are not a moral darkness, and this chapter will not use "dark ages" as its own claim. War and dispossession stay in the story. A racial map does not.
:::

In 476 CE Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus. Julius Nepos, a rival claimant, died in 480 CE, and the eastern empire continued. The baptism of Clovis is a different kind of date: both about 496 CE and about 508 CE have been argued, and the Lombard entry into Italy is usually dated 568 CE.
:::

## Where this leads

The Roman state that did not stop in 476 CE is the subject of [[after-rome/byzantium]]: Justinian's law, the Nika riot, Hagia Sophia and a plague for which this atlas gives no death total. The official religion these kings inherited, and the limit of a public rite that is not a census, is [[after-rome/christianisation]]. A later Frankish family will turn kingship into an imperial claim in [[after-rome/carolingians]]. The deposition itself, as a convention rather than an end of Roman life, is argued in [[rome/later-empire]].

:::summary
- "Barbarian" is a Roman insult that became a period name. It does not name one race, and "barbarian invasions" hides distinct political histories.
- Goths and Franks built kingdoms in Roman provinces on Roman land, with Roman tax and often Roman administrators. This is not a racial replacement, and it is not a claim that the takeovers were peaceful.
- The baptism of Clovis is disputed. Both about 496 CE and about 508 CE have been argued. Do not choose in silence, and do not treat either year as a census of belief.
- 476 CE deposes Romulus Augustulus. It does not empty the west. The eastern empire continues, and Julius Nepos dies in 480 CE.
- The Lombard entry into Italy is usually dated 568 CE, after Justinian's reign. It is a later case, not the same event as the fifth-century settlements.
- This chapter does not use "dark ages" as its own claim. Uneven evidence is not a moral night.
:::
