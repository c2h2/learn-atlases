What did the coronation of 25 December 800 CE claim, and what did the Treaty of Verdun in 843 CE divide?

The coronation claimed an empire in the west. Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne emperor in Rome. Constantinople already had an emperor, and it contested the new title. Nothing in that afternoon ended the eastern Roman state, founded a nation, or made one man emperor of all Europe. Forty-three years later, by the difference of the year numerals, Verdun divided a Frankish inheritance among three grandsons, Lothar I, Louis the German and Charles the Bald. It did not found France and Germany. A later coronation, Otto I's in 962 CE, is another imperial claim, not the same ceremony. A traditional baptism at Kiev in 988 CE is a marker, not a census. Mutual excommunications in 1054 CE are a marker in a long argument between churches, not the birth certificate of two nations and not a divorce completed in a day.

The family was powerful before it was imperial. Charles Martel, Charlemagne's grandfather, was mayor of the palace, not king, and the battle associated with him is the disputed Tours of [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]]: traditionally October 732 CE, with 733 and 734 CE also argued. This atlas has not locked the year in which a Carolingian first took the royal title. What it has locked is the imperial date. Keeping Martel, the kingship and the emperor apart is how the coronation stays a claim instead of becoming a legend's last scene.

## A title, contested

:::definition Western imperial claim {#def-claim}
The **western imperial claim** is the title of emperor taken by Charlemagne when Leo III crowned him in Rome on 25 December 800 CE, and contested from Constantinople. It is not a transfer of the eastern Roman office, not a vacancy being filled, and not a statement that the emperor ruled every people in Europe.
:::

:::definition Inheritance division {#def-division}
An **inheritance division** is a split of lands, rights and titles among heirs of one family. The Treaty of Verdun in 843 CE is such a split. It is not the founding law of a nation-state, and it does not create the modern borders or the modern peoples of France and Germany.
:::

On 25 December 800 CE the pope and the Frankish king make a public fact. Leo III places an imperial crown. Charlemagne bears the title. A later Latin life tells the day in a shape that suits a court, and this atlas has not locked a year for that life. It will not be treated as a transcript, and a story in which the king is surprised will not be repeated as a fact. We also will not invent the text of any oath. The checkable sentence is [[#def-claim]]. After that Christmas there is a western emperor. The eastern court does not treat the title as its own.

Why the contestation matters: the Roman state at Constantinople, taught in [[after-rome/byzantium]], is still there. Justinian's law books are centuries behind, and the state has not become a fossil. A coronation in Rome is a challenge and a novelty in the west. It is not the rescue of an empty throne. This chapter will not date the eastern rulers of 800 CE, because those years are not locked, and it will not explain the contest by a vacancy. Two claims exist. One is old and eastern. One is new and western. Calling the second "the" empire, as if the first had resigned, is how a Frankish ceremony is asked to do too much.

Leo III is not a neutral master of ceremonies. He is a political actor, with enemies in Rome and a reason to want a protector. The year of his crisis is not locked here, so it will not be supplied. The locked date is the coronation itself. Protection and legitimacy run in both directions: a king gets a title the popes can give in Rome, and a pope gets a Frankish sword. That is politics. It is not yet the investiture conflict of [[christendom/popes-and-kings]], which has its own dates. It is enough to stop a picture of the crown descending from theology alone.

:::theorem The coronation of 800 was a new claim, not a transfer {#thm-claim}
On 25 December 800 CE Leo III's crowning of Charlemagne created a western imperial title that Constantinople contested. It did not end the eastern Roman empire, and it did not make Charlemagne emperor of all Europe.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the ceremony's place and the eastern response the chronology records. The crown is given in Rome, to a Frankish king, by a pope. An emperor already reigns in Constantinople, the Roman state that did not end in 476 CE. Contestation is what a living rival claim looks like. It would be unnecessary if the eastern office had lapsed. What would count against [[#thm-claim]] is evidence that Constantinople recognised the title as its own transferred office, or that the eastern imperial line had already stopped. A western story of vacancy is not that evidence, and this chapter will not date one. What the coronation can support is the new title, the papal role, and the quarrel. It cannot support a map of Europe with one emperor over every region, and it cannot support the end of the state in [[after-rome/byzantium]].
:::

## What 43 years later actually divided

From 800 CE to 843 CE is 43 years. The chronology states that count on purpose: 843 minus 800, not 44. In 843 CE the Treaty of Verdun divides the Frankish inheritance. Lothar I, Louis the German and Charles the Bald are grandsons of the man crowned in 800 CE. Lothar, the eldest, keeps an imperial title and a middle share of the lands and rights. Louis takes an eastern share. Charles takes a western share. The shares are bundles of rule, not nation-states. People in them are Franks and neighbours under kings. They are not citizens of the modern French or German republics, and the treaty does not say that they are.

:::theorem Verdun divided an inheritance and did not found two nations {#thm-verdun}
The Treaty of Verdun in 843 CE divided a Frankish inheritance among Lothar I, Louis the German and Charles the Bald. It did not found France and Germany.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the kind of act and the parties. Three grandsons split lands, rights and an imperial title that one of them keeps. [[#def-division]] is the class of document. A founding of two nations would be a different class: a people, a continuous legal person, and borders that match the later states. Verdun supplies none of that. The middle share does not correspond to one modern country, which is a clue that the treaty was not in the business of drawing those two. Later divisions inside the family followed; this atlas has not locked their years, and it will not invent them to make a national story smoother. What would count against [[#thm-verdun]] is a text of 843 CE that founds states under the modern names with the modern borders. There is no such text. What would not count is a later textbook that needs a birthday, or a line of kings in the west and the east that a historian can trace. Descent is not foundation. A western kingdom can have later heirs without Verdun having been their birth certificate.
:::

Who benefits from the birthday? Curricula that want France and Germany to begin on a date pupils can memorise. The date is useful for a national story and useless as a description of 843 CE. [[sources/time-and-names]] is the general warning: a nickname and a regnal arrangement are claims, and someone gains when the claim is treated as a label that no longer needs evidence. Here the gain is a simple map. The cost is the middle share, the imperial title that is not a nation, and the brothers who are a family, not delegates of two peoples.

Otto I is crowned emperor in 962 CE. That is another imperial claim, not a continuation of the same ceremony as 800 CE and not a codicil to Verdun. From 800 CE to 962 CE the year numerals differ by 162. From 843 CE to 962 CE they differ by 119. This atlas has not locked the kings between Verdun and Otto, so their reigns will not be filled in. The comparison is the type of act. A man is crowned emperor. He is not Charlemagne, and the year is not 800 CE. Otto's dot is the last event on the figure. The traditional baptism of 988 CE and the excommunications of 1054 CE fall beyond it, and they have to be taught in prose or they will be forgotten.

## A baptism that is not a census, and a schism that is not a day

In 988 CE, on the traditional dating, Vladimir of Kiev is baptised. The chronology says to treat the year as a traditional marker of the conversion of the Rus', not as a census of belief. Traditional is weaker than a charter and stronger than a guess: it is the date the tradition hands us, and we do not pretend a register survives in this book. The public act is a ruler's adoption of eastern Christianity. It is political and ecclesiastical. It does not say what every person in Kiev believed in 988 CE, and it does not interview the people a chronicle did not ask. The same limit governed the edict of 380 CE in [[after-rome/christianisation]] and the disputed baptism of Clovis in [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]]. Clovis has two proposed years. Vladimir has one traditional year. Neither is a count of souls.

One paragraph is enough for 1054 CE, and this is that paragraph. Mutual excommunications involve Humbert, a papal legate, and Michael Cerularius, patriarch of Constantinople. The acts are real, and they sit inside a longer argument about authority, rite and doctrine. They are not the day the Latin and Greek churches became sealed worlds, and they are not the founding of nations. Dispute and contact both exist before and after. The Latin story of popes and royal office continues in [[christendom/popes-and-kings]]. The eastern Roman state is still the state of [[after-rome/byzantium]]: centuries after Justinian, and centuries before the sack of 13 April 1204 CE and the fall of 29 May 1453 CE. A marker is not a divorce decree.

:::quiz
What did Verdun do in 843 CE?
- [ ] It founded the nations of France and Germany
- [ ] It repeated the ceremony of 25 December 800 CE with the same emperor
- [x] It divided a Frankish inheritance among three grandsons
- [ ] It was the traditional baptism of Vladimir of Kiev
:::solution
The three grandsons are Lothar I, Louis the German and Charles the Bald. They divide lands, rights and, for Lothar, an imperial title. Charlemagne had been crowned in 800 CE and is not the man in the treaty. Vladimir's traditional baptism is 988 CE. Founding two modern nations is the reading [[#thm-verdun]] rejects. The middle share is one reason the reading fails: it does not match one later nation.
:::
:::

## Argument and counter-argument

The defence of the national reading is that names and kingdoms have to start somewhere, and Verdun is where the western and eastern Frankish shares pull apart. Later France and later Germany are not inventions of the nineteenth century with no roots. To say the treaty "did not found" them can sound like a denial that anything continuous followed. The defence deserves the distinction it is owed.

Continuity of a kind is real. There is a western share and an eastern share, and later politics can be narrated forward from them. [[#thm-verdun]] does not forbid that narration. It forbids a particular sentence: that the treaty founded the modern states, as a birth certificate founds a legal person. The parties are brothers, not nations. The borders are not the modern borders. The middle share has no single modern successor that would make the treaty a machine for producing exactly two countries. What would make the national sentence true is the text we do not have. What keeps a modest continuity true is a chain of rule and name that a later chapter can study without backdating the passport.

A second objection says that if Constantinople contested the title, the coronation was a failure and should be taught as a papal fantasy. Contestation is not failure. [[#thm-claim]] says the title was new and disputed, which is what a real claim looks like when another emperor exists. A fantasy that nobody noticed would not have been contested. The eastern objection is evidence that both courts understood the title to matter. It is not evidence that Charlemagne stayed a king in fact while the pope played. He bore the western title. They refused to treat it as theirs.

Otto's coronation is the control. If 800 CE had founded "the" western empire as a single continuous ceremony, 962 CE would be an anniversary. It is a different man, 162 year-numerals later, taking the title again. The gap, which we will not fill with unlocked reigns, is the proof that imperial claims in this period are acts, repeated and rival, not one flame passed without a break.

:::example Forty-three years {#ex-forty-three}
The coronation is 25 December 800 CE. Verdun is 843 CE. How many years is the span, and why does this atlas refuse 44?
:::solution
843 minus 800 is 43. The span is 43 years. The chronology states that count directly. Adding both endpoints would give 44, and that is not the rule. The same difference of year numerals was used from 313 CE to 380 CE in [[after-rome/christianisation]].

The number is a distance between a coronation and a division of inheritance. It is not the length of a nation, and it does not make the three grandsons the same man as the emperor of 800 CE.
:::
:::

:::example Two coronations {#ex-two-crowns}
Set 25 December 800 CE beside 962 CE. What kind of act do they share, and what must you not call the second?
:::solution
Each is an imperial coronation: Charlemagne's by Leo III, and Otto I's in 962 CE. They share a type of claim. The year numerals differ by 162, because 962 minus 800 is 162. From Verdun in 843 CE to Otto the difference is 119.

You must not call Otto's coronation a continuation of the same ceremony, an anniversary, or the moment France and Germany begin. The figure in this chapter draws both dots and Verdun. It does not draw the kings in between, because this atlas has not locked them, and the edge of the axis is not itself an event.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a source.

"At Verdun in 843 the kings founded France and Germany, and their oath declared the nations born."

What is wrong with each half, and why must you not repair the second half by writing the oath?
:::solution
The first half makes an inheritance division into a founding of two modern nations. The parties are Lothar I, Louis the German and Charles the Bald, grandsons dividing lands and rights. [[#thm-verdun]] is the correction. The middle share does not become a nation in that sentence, which is one of the ways the sentence fails.

The second half invents an oath and then invents its contents. This chapter does not supply the text of any oath, even to refute one. Repair would be a second invention. The sentence can be refused without a substitute speech: no such declaration is in the evidence, and a national birth is not what the treaty does.
:::
:::

:::example A traditional baptism {#ex-vladimir}
Vladimir of Kiev is baptised, on the traditional date, in 988 CE. What may you infer, and how is the limit different from the limit on Clovis?
:::solution
You may infer a traditional marker of a ruler's public adoption of eastern Christianity, tied to the conversion of the Rus' as a political and ecclesiastical story. You may not infer a census of belief in Kiev, and you may not treat the year as a charter this book has reproduced. "Traditional" is the strength of the date.

Clovis, in [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]], has two argued years, about 496 and about 508 CE, and no silent choice. Vladimir has one traditional year, not a pair. Both limits refuse a count of souls. The difference is only how the calendar dispute is shaped, not a permission to read minds in 988 CE.
:::

Move the year. A window of 15 years will not hold 800 and 843 CE together: the distance is 43 years, and the window is less than half of that. Otto in 962 CE is further off still, 119 year-numerals after Verdun. The traditional baptism of 988 CE and the excommunications of 1054 CE have no dots, because they fall beyond the axis. Absence from the figure is not absence from the history. Do not let the three dots become a single imperial ceremony, and do not let Verdun's dot become a national birthday.

:::warning The birth of two nations
The usual sentence says that Verdun founded France and Germany. It did not. Three grandsons divided a Frankish inheritance in 843 CE. The shares are not the modern states, the middle share is not one of those two nations, and the people were not those nations' citizens. A later kingdom can descend from a share without the treaty being a birth certificate. The same habit of over-reading puts Charles Martel, a mayor who was not king, into Charlemagne's crown, and puts the coronation of 800 CE into a vacant eastern throne. Each of those sentences is larger than its date.
:::

## Where this leads

Lordship, rent and the variety of unfreedom in the Latin world that inherits these kingdoms are [[christendom/lords-and-peasants]]. The quarrel of popes and kings over office, which is not the same quarrel as Leo III's coronation, is [[christendom/popes-and-kings]]. The eastern empire that outlived 800 CE and contested the title is [[after-rome/byzantium]]. The Iberian polity that the legend of a saved continent tries to end before Charlemagne is even crowned is [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]].

:::summary
- On 25 December 800 CE Leo III crowns Charlemagne emperor in Rome. The claim is a new western title. Constantinople contests it. The eastern empire does not end.
- Charles Martel was not that emperor and was not king. This atlas has not locked the year a Carolingian first took the royal title. The locked imperial date is 800 CE.
- From 800 CE to 843 CE is 43 years. Verdun divides a Frankish inheritance among Lothar I, Louis the German and Charles the Bald. It does not found France and Germany.
- Otto I is crowned emperor in 962 CE. That is another claim, not the same ceremony as 800 CE and not a codicil to Verdun.
- 988 CE is the traditional date of Vladimir of Kiev's baptism. A marker is not a census of belief.
- 1054 CE, the mutual excommunications of Humbert and Michael Cerularius, is a marker in a longer schism, not a divorce completed in a day and not a national founding.
:::
