In what sense did the Roman empire continue after the deposition of 476 CE?

It continued as a state. The emperors at Constantinople did not treat Odoacer's removal of Romulus Augustulus as the end of the Roman empire, and the year did not make them resign. Julius Nepos dies in 480 CE, still in the west, while the east simply goes on. Modern books call that state Byzantium, from the older name of the city. The word is a shelf-mark. It is not the name the state used for itself, and it is not evidence that Rome had quietly become something else. Under Justinian, who reigns from 527 to 565 CE, the shelf-mark covers a Roman law in Latin, a Greek-speaking capital, a church dedicated on 27 December 537 CE, a riot in 532 CE, and a plague that arrives in 541 CE. Those are one reign. They are not one cause, and they are not a footnote to the Franks.

What the sense of "continue" cannot mean is that nothing changed, or that the court was a fossil, or that palace gossip was the constitution. Language shifted. Frontiers moved. A plague hurt the capacity to tax and to recruit, on a scale this atlas will not number. The claim is institutional. There is still an emperor, still Roman law, still a fiscal and military state, still a church the emperor treats as his business. [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]] is the western pattern of kings on Roman land. This chapter is the state those kings did not replace.

## A Roman name, a Greek capital

:::definition Byzantine {#def-byzantine}
**Byzantine** is a modern name for the Roman state ruled from Constantinople. It is not the state's own legal name, which remained Roman, and it is not a judgement that the state was a Greek deviation from a Latin original. Greek is a language of the capital and, increasingly, of government. It is not a resignation from Rome.
:::

:::definition The Justinianic codification {#def-codex}
The **Justinianic codification** is the set of law books issued under Justinian: a first Codex in 529 CE, the Digest and the Institutes in 533 CE, and a second Codex in 534 CE. The Codex gathers imperial constitutions, the Digest excerpts jurists and gives those excerpts authority, and the Institutes is a book for students. The set is Roman jurisprudence collected, not a new religion and not a Frankish code.
:::

By Justinian's reign the streets of Constantinople, and much of the court, speak Greek. The great law books are still issued in Latin. That combination is the political fact [[#def-byzantine]] is meant to protect. A western reader who meets only the Franks can think that "Roman" has already moved to Gaul, and that the east is a leftover speaking the wrong language. The law books say otherwise. They are the tools of a Roman emperor who can command jurists to file an enormous tradition into books students and judges can use. Judith Herrin's account, named among the course references, puts the same point as a long life rather than a prelude: this state is not waiting to become a chapter in someone else's history.

Who benefits from taking the name Roman away? After 25 December 800 CE a pope will crown a Frankish king emperor in Rome, and Constantinople will contest the claim. That coronation is [[after-rome/carolingians]]. It is easier to tell as a rescue of a vacant title if the eastern empire has already been renamed out of Rome. The renaming is a later convenience. In 529 CE nobody in the palace needs it. The emperor is making Roman law, in the Roman capital Constantine's city had been since the traditional dedication of 11 May 330 CE.

Court narrative is not the system. Writers near the palace, Procopius among them, describe factions, fear and the emperor's choices. They are evidence of what a literate observer chose to write. They are not a constitution. The constitution, in the only sense this chapter will use, is the set of institutions that outlast a rumour: tax, army, law, and the church as a public institution. Reducing the reign to intrigue is the mistake the warning below names. A riot can show that the capital was able to threaten the emperor. It cannot show that gossip was the government.

## One reign, in order

The order is the argument. In 529 CE the first Codex appears. In 532 CE the Nika riot breaks in Constantinople. In 533 CE the Digest and the Institutes appear. In 534 CE a second Codex revises the collection. On 27 December 537 CE Hagia Sophia is dedicated. In 541 CE the Justinianic plague arrives in Egypt and spreads. Justinian is still reigning in 565 CE. Anyone who makes the plague the cause of the riot, or the church an apology for the plague, has reversed the years.

The Nika riot is a political crisis in the capital, not a soap episode. Circus factions, whose chant gives the riot its name, turn a protest into a challenge to Justinian. A large part of the city burns, including the great church that stood there before. Many people are killed when the emperor's forces restore control. This atlas gives no death total, because none is locked and a guessed crowd is a false precision. Narrative sources describe a moment in which his hold on the city was in doubt, and then a violent restoration. We will not take one court author's drama as the political system. We will say what the riot shows: the capital could frighten an emperor, and the restored regime had the resources to build again. The law project does not stop for the ashes. Digest and Institutes follow in 533 CE, the second Codex in 534 CE.

Hagia Sophia, dedicated on 27 December 537 CE, is the rebuilt church. The year numerals of 537 CE and 532 CE differ by 5. A building on that scale is evidence of labour, materials and an imperial religious policy. It is not evidence that every resident was pious, and it is not a symbol that the west had gone dark. The Franks are not the audience the dome was built to shame. The audience is the Roman capital.

:::theorem The eastern state remained Roman {#thm-roman}
After 476 CE the empire continued as a Roman state at Constantinople: an emperor, Roman law, taxation and a public church. Greek speech did not make it a non-Roman fossil, and it was not a dependency of the Franks.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is institutional and dated. Justinian reigns from 527 to 565 CE and issues the codification of [[#def-codex]] in Latin in 529, 533 and 534 CE. The capital is the city dedicated, on the traditional date, on 11 May 330 CE. The state calls itself Roman. A modern label, Byzantine, records our shelf, not their abdication. Western kingdoms in [[after-rome/successor-kingdoms]] are heirs on Roman land; they are not the government of this capital. What would count against [[#thm-roman]] is a formal end of the imperial office in the east in 476 CE, or a new state that discarded Roman law for a different kind of authority. The codification is evidence in the other direction. What would not count is the presence of Greek, or a western writer's preference for calling these Romans Greeks. That preference is a polemic. It is evidence of rivalry, especially once a second imperial claim exists in 800 CE, and it is not a definition.
:::

:::theorem Law, riot, church and plague are not one cause {#thm-order}
The first Codex in 529 CE, the Nika riot in 532 CE, the Digest and Institutes in 533 CE, the second Codex in 534 CE, the dedication of Hagia Sophia on 27 December 537 CE, and the plague of 541 CE belong to one reign. None of them is the cause of the others, and the reign does not end in 541 CE.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the order of the locked years. The first law book precedes the riot. The Digest, the Institutes and the second Codex follow the riot, so the legal project runs through the crisis rather than being cancelled or caused by it. The dedication of 537 CE follows the burning of the earlier church; the year numerals differ by 5, and the riot has no locked month, so we do not pretend to count days. The plague arrives in 541 CE, after the dedication, and Justinian reigns on until 565 CE, a difference of 24 year-numerals from the plague's arrival to the end of the reign. What would count against [[#thm-order]] is a source that dated the plague before the riot, or that ended the imperial office in 541 CE. A sermon that blames the disaster on the sins of the city is not that source. A single cause is what the line of years refuses.
:::

- [ ] Only as a Greek fossil, with no law and no tax, until the Franks inherited it
- [x] As a state at Constantinople that kept the Roman name, issued Roman law under Justinian, and outlived 476 CE by centuries
- [ ] It ended when Hagia Sophia was dedicated on 27 December 537 CE
- [ ] It moved to Italy with Odoacer and ceased to have an eastern court
:::solution
Odoacer's deposition did not close the eastern court, and Nepos's death in 480 CE is still a western story. Justinian's Latin law books, from 529 CE to 534 CE, are Roman jurisprudence in a living state. The dedication of Hagia Sophia is a building, not an extinction. "Fossil" and "a footnote to the Franks" are the readings [[#thm-roman]] rejects.

## Plague without a total

In 541 CE the Justinianic plague arrives in Egypt and spreads through the empire and beyond. You may say that it was a disaster, and that in many places labour and tax became harder to sustain. You may not say how many died. No total is locked, and a fraction of the world, or of the empire, would be an invention. The same rule that stops a conversion statistic in [[after-rome/christianisation]] stops a body count here. "Many" is allowed. A precise toll is not.

The reign supplies the limit on causation. Justinian dies, as a reign-end, in 565 CE, not in 541 CE. The state is still there to be argued with in 800 CE, when a western coronation has to be contested from Constantinople. It is still there, as a state this chapter will not narrate further, until the dates other courses teach. A claim that the plague ended the empire is false on those later years. A claim that the plague changed nothing is also too strong: recruitment, revenue and settlement were not obviously untouched, and historians argue about scale precisely because the evidence will not support one number. We stop at the arrival, the spread, and the absence of a total. [[#thm-order]] already forbids making 541 CE the explanation of 532 CE.

## Two later sentences, and no more

The Roman state at Constantinople lasts for centuries after 476 CE. On 13 April 1204 CE a crusading army sacks Constantinople, a Christian city, a later break taught in [[christendom/crusades]] and not the end of the Roman imperial claim. On 29 May 1453 CE the city falls to the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II and the eastern Roman state ends, which [[crises/city-states]] takes up, and which is not a quiet extinction already finished in 476 CE.

Those two sentences are the whole of the later narrative this chapter is allowed. The sack of 1204 CE is a real blow to the city's government. It is not Justinian's crisis, and it is not a fossil finally cracking. The fall of 1453 CE is an end, dated and violent, not the last page of a story that had already closed in the fifth century. From 1204 CE to 1453 CE the year numerals differ by 249. From 565 CE, the end of Justinian's reign, to 1204 CE they differ by 639. The distances are the reason a lesson on the sixth century must not absorb either year into Nika, the law books or the plague.

:::quiz

- [ ] It has a death total stated in the chronology, and the lesson should quote it
- [x] It arrived in 541 CE and spread, and this atlas gives no death total
- [ ] It ended the eastern Roman state in the year it arrived
:::solution
The plague arrives in Egypt in 541 CE and spreads. The chronology forbids a death total, so any fraction is an invention. The Nika riot is 532 CE, before the plague, and Justinian reigns until 565 CE. The state's end, on the dating this atlas uses, is 29 May 1453 CE, not 541 CE.
:::

## Argument and counter-argument

The sharpest objection to [[#thm-roman]] is linguistic. If the court speaks Greek, and if later writers say Byzantine, then calling the state Roman is nostalgia, a compliment paid by modern historians who like continuity. The real break, on this objection, is cultural. A Greek court is a different civilisation, and the Franks, or the papacy, are the proper heirs of Rome.

The objection confuses a language with a constitution. [[#def-byzantine]] separates them on purpose. Latin law in 529 to 534 CE is not nostalgia. It is a government filing its own legal tradition while the capital speaks Greek. The state's name for itself stays Roman. Western writers who prefer "Greeks" are often defending a rival claim, above all after 800 CE. Their adjective is a source for the rivalry. It is a bad definition of the sixth-century state. What would make the objection stick is evidence that the imperial office in the east ended, or that Justinian's books presented themselves as the law of a new non-Roman people. They do not.

A second objection runs the other way. If the state continues, the riot, the plague and the sack of 1204 CE are details, and continuity is a smooth ribbon from Constantine to 1453 CE. [[#thm-order]] is the answer inside the reign: the ribbon is a sequence of different kinds of event, and the figure's line does not make them one cause. The two later sentences are the answer outside the reign. Continuity of the Roman claim is compatible with a burned capital, a plague we cannot number, a crusader sack and a final conquest. Smoothness is not what "the state lasts" means.

:::example The reign in order {#ex-order}
Put these in chronological order and say which causal sentence the order forbids: first Codex; Nika riot; Digest and Institutes; second Codex; dedication of Hagia Sophia; plague. Use the years 529, 532, 533, 534, 537 and 541 CE.
:::solution
The order is the first Codex in 529 CE, the Nika riot in 532 CE, the Digest and the Institutes in 533 CE, the second Codex in 534 CE, the dedication of Hagia Sophia on 27 December 537 CE, and the plague in 541 CE.

The order forbids "the plague caused the riot" and "the new church was an apology for the plague". It also forbids treating the law as a single book issued in a quiet year. The project runs from 529 CE through 534 CE, with a riot in the middle, and the church follows the riot because the earlier church had burned. One reign, not one cause.
:::
:::

:::example How long is the reign {#ex-reign}
Justinian reigns from 527 to 565 CE. What is the difference of the year numerals, and where inside that span do 532 CE and 541 CE fall?
:::solution
565 minus 527 is 38. The span is 38 years, by the difference of the year numerals, not an inclusive count.

532 minus 527 is 5, so the Nika riot falls five year-numerals after the reign begins. 541 minus 527 is 14, and 565 minus 541 is 24, so the plague arrives well inside the reign and the reign continues after it. Neither event is the edge of the reign, and neither may be told as its definition.
:::
:::

:::example An invented chronicle sentence {#ex-invented}
The following sentence is invented. It is not a source.

"After 476 the Roman empire survived only as a Greek fossil, irrelevant to Europe, and Justinian's plague, riot and new church were a single punishment."

Which clauses fail, and which locked facts do the failing?
:::solution
"Only as a Greek fossil" fails because the state issues Roman law in Latin in 529, 533 and 534 CE and still calls itself Roman. "Irrelevant to Europe" fails because the geography includes this capital, and because a western imperial claim in 800 CE has to be contested from it. Greek speech is not a resignation from Rome.

"A single punishment" fails the order in [[#thm-order]]. The riot is 532 CE, the dedication is 27 December 537 CE, and the plague is 541 CE. A punishment cannot cause events that precede it. No death total is available to thicken the sentence, and none should be added.
:::
:::

:::example Two sentences you must not expand {#ex-later}
Why does this chapter allow only one sentence each for 1204 CE and 1453 CE, and what would a third sentence about either year be likely to do wrong?
:::solution
Both years are later breaks, taught in other courses: the crusader sack of 13 April 1204 CE in [[christendom/crusades]], and the Ottoman conquest of 29 May 1453 CE in [[crises/city-states]]. The brief of this chapter is Justinian's state, which lasts long after 476 CE and is not defined by those later blows. One sentence each records that the blows are real and that they are not this reign.

A third sentence would start a narrative this chapter has not locked in detail: the government of the city after the sack, or the campaigns of 1453 CE. It would also tempt a moral, fossil or rescue, that [[#thm-roman]] exists to block. The distances alone, 1204 minus 565 and 1453 minus 1204, are enough to keep the years off Justinian's line.
:::

Move the year. The dots are different kinds of event, and a highlight is not a cause. The Digest is on the figure; the Institutes, issued in the same 533 CE, have no second dot, and the second Codex of 534 CE is not on the line at all. :::warning Fossil, or a palace serial
Two habits of telling this state both miss the institutions. One calls it a fossil, a Greek deviation from the West, already dead in spirit after 476 CE and interesting only as a relic the Franks or the Turks will supersede. The other tells the reign as court intrigue, a serial of whispers in which the law books and the tax are scenery. Justinian's codification, the army, the fiscal system and Hagia Sophia are the content. A riot shows that the capital could threaten an emperor. It does not show that gossip was the constitution. Greek is the capital's language, not a fall from Rome.
:::

:::history
Justinian reigned from 527 to 565 CE. His first Codex is 529 CE, the Digest and the Institutes are 533 CE, and the second Codex is 534 CE. Hagia Sophia was dedicated on 27 December 537 CE, after the Nika riot of 532 CE, and the Justinianic plague arrived in 541 CE. No death total is part of that record.
:::

## Where this leads

A Roman defeat in the east, at Yarmouk in 636 CE on the usual dating, is part of how a new polity reaches the Mediterranean and, later, Iberia. That question is [[after-rome/islam-in-the-west]]. The western imperial title of 25 December 800 CE, which this capital contests, is [[after-rome/carolingians]]. The sack of 13 April 1204 CE is [[christendom/crusades]]. The end of the state on 29 May 1453 CE is [[crises/city-states]]. None of those chapters makes Justinian a prelude.

:::summary
- After 476 CE the Roman empire continues as a state at Constantinople. "Byzantine" is our name, not theirs. Greek speech does not cancel the Roman claim.
- Justinian reigns from 527 to 565 CE. The first Codex is 529 CE, the Digest and the Institutes are 533 CE, and the second Codex is 534 CE. This is Roman law, not a footnote to the Franks.
- The Nika riot of 532 CE is a crisis in the capital. Many died, and this atlas states no death total. Hagia Sophia is dedicated on 27 December 537 CE. Law, riot and church are not one cause.
- The plague arrives in 541 CE and spreads. There is no death total. The reign, and the state, continue after that year.
- On 13 April 1204 CE a crusading army sacks the city; on 29 May 1453 CE the state ends under Mehmed II. Each fact is one sentence here. Neither is a fossil's quiet death in 476 CE.
- Court intrigue is not the political system. Tax, army, law and the public church are.
:::

No total is locked in the chronology. A number, or a fraction of the empire, would be an invention, the same kind of false precision a conversion statistic would be. "Many" and "a disaster" stay inside the evidence. A clinical description of the disease does not.

What can be said from the years is the place, not the toll. The plague arrives after the dedication of 537 CE and before the end of the reign in 565 CE. It does not cause the riot of 532 CE, and it does not end the state. Historians dispute the scale because the sources will not yield one figure. The dispute is a reason to stop, not a gap to fill.

:::exercise Not one cause {level=2}
Using only the order of 529, 532, 533, 534, 537 and 541 CE, refute the sentence "Justinian built Hagia Sophia to atone for the plague that had provoked the Nika riot."
:::solution
The riot is 532 CE. The dedication is 27 December 537 CE, five year-numerals later, after a fire. The plague is 541 CE, after both. A plague cannot provoke a riot that has already happened, and a church dedicated before the plague cannot be an atonement for it. The law books make the same point from the other side: the first Codex is 529 CE, before the riot, and the Digest, Institutes and second Codex follow the riot, so the legal work is not a mood produced by either disaster.

[[#thm-order]] is the general form of this reply. One reign can hold a law, a riot, a church and a plague without any of them being the cause of the rest.
