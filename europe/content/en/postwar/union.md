What did the Union become, and what is still not a closed past?

The Economic Community of 25 March 1957 was a small membership list, not a continent. The Union is a later treaty, signed at Maastricht on 7 February 1992 and in force on 1 November 1993. It acquires a currency in two steps, book money on 1 January 1999 and notes and coins on 1 January 2002. It acquires members in further steps, three states on 1 January 1995, ten states on 1 May 2004, Bulgaria and Romania in 2007, Croatia on 1 July 2013, and it loses a member: the United Kingdom votes to leave on 23 June 2016, leaves on 31 January 2020, and ends a transition on 31 December 2020. In the same years a military campaign is fought over Kosovo, a bankruptcy in the United States is not the same day as Greek loan programmes, Russia occupies and annexes Crimea in 2014, and on 24 February 2022 Russia begins a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. As of 2026 that war has not ended. This lesson states the invasion as an invasion. It gives no casualty total, it recommends no policy, and it does not tell you whom to support. The Union is not the happy ending of the atlas. A membership list that grows and shrinks, beside a war that is still open, is the opposite of an ending.

## From communities to a union

The road to Maastricht is three different acts, not a single birth. On 1 August 1975 the Helsinki Final Act is signed, a security document of states on both sides of the division, not a market. In 1979 the European Parliament is directly elected for the first time. An elected parliament is not a sovereign, and an election is not a currency. In 1986 the Single European Act revises the treaties towards a single market. It is not the Union's founding instrument. Maastricht is.

:::definition The Union {#def-union}
The **Union** here means the treaty organisation established when the Maastricht treaty enters into force on 1 November 1993, with a membership list that later changes. It is not a civilisation, not a synonym for the continent, and not the happy ending of European history.
:::

The treaty is signed on 7 February 1992 and enters into force on 1 November 1993. The signature is not the entry into force. From 7 February 1992 to 7 February 1993 is 366 days, because 1992 is a leap year and 29 February 1992 falls after 7 February, inside that span. From 7 February 1993 to 1 November 1993 is 267 days. The sum is 633 days. In those 633 days the treaty is a signed text that has not yet bound its members as a union in force. Collapsing the two dates makes a negotiation look like a switch.

What the treaty does, in the narrow sense this lesson needs, is give the Union its legal name and set a path that includes a single currency. It does not, on the day it is signed, put notes in a pocket. The euro as book money begins on 1 January 1999. Notes and coins begin on 1 January 2002. From the first of those days to the second is three years, and one 29 February falls inside the span, so the span is three years plus one day, not three bare years. We do not treat that February as a separate historical event, and we do not need its year as a political date. The three dates are the claim.

Not every member state adopted the notes and coins. The United Kingdom did not. Others also stayed outside the cash. A reader who says "Europe changed its money in 2002" has renamed a list as a continent, which [[sources/naming-europe]] already forbids for 1957, and has erased the states that kept their own notes. [[#def-union]] is the same ban, applied to a later treaty.

:::theorem Three dates, not one finished act {#thm-three}
The Maastricht signature of 7 February 1992, the entry into force on 1 November 1993, the euro as book money on 1 January 1999 and the notes and coins of 1 January 2002 are different dates. Collapsing them makes the Union look like a single finished act.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the gap between the dates and the difference of what each date does. A signature creates a text. Entry into force makes the Union a legal fact for its members. Book money is a unit of account. Notes and coins are a different day, three years and one day after the book money, and not every member takes them. None of these days is the enlargement of 1 May 2004, and none is the departure of 31 January 2020.

What would count against the claim is a single instrument that signed, entered into force, and issued notes on one day, with every European state inside it. There is no such instrument. What would not count against it is the political hope, real among many of the signatories, that a union and a currency were steps towards a more settled continent. A hope is not a date. [[postwar/from-1989]] has already refused to let 1989 be an ending. This claim refuses to let Maastricht be a second ending wearing a treaty's name.
:::

The counter-argument says that for practical purposes the Union "happens" at Maastricht, and the later dates are administration. Administration is where the practical purposes live. A firm that cannot yet pay in notes, a state that has signed and is not yet bound, a member that never takes the cash: each of these is invisible if you keep only 7 February 1992. The 633 days from signature to entry into force are the simplest check. If administration did not matter, those days would not exist.

:::quiz
Which sequence matches the currency and the treaty?
- [ ] Notes and coins begin on 7 February 1992, the day Maastricht is signed, in every European state.
- [ ] The euro as book money and the notes are both 1 January 1999, and Britain adopts both.
- [x] Maastricht is signed on 7 February 1992, enters into force on 1 November 1993, becomes book money on 1 January 1999, and becomes notes and coins on 1 January 2002, not in every member state.
- [ ] The Single European Act of 1986 is the day the notes are issued.
:::solution
[[#thm-three]] keeps four dates apart. The United Kingdom does not adopt the notes and coins. The Single European Act is 1986, a market revision, not the cash and not the Union's entry into force. Helsinki in 1975 and the direct elections of 1979 are earlier and different again.
:::
:::

:::example Signature is not entry into force {#ex-maastricht}
Find the date difference from 7 February 1992 to 1 November 1993, and say what the two dates do.
:::solution
From 7 February 1992 to 7 February 1993 is 366 days, including 29 February 1992. From 7 February 1993 to 7 November 1993 runs through the months as 28 plus 31 plus 30 plus 31 plus 30 plus 31 plus 31 plus 30 plus 31, that is 273 days, landing on 7 November. 1 November is 6 days earlier, so 273 minus 6 is 267. Then 366 plus 267 is 633. The treaty is signed on the first date and enters into force on the second. For 633 days it is not yet the Union in force. [[#thm-three]] starts from that gap and then refuses to fold in the currency dates as well.
:::
:::

## A list is exact

:::definition An exact enlargement {#def-ten}
An **exact enlargement** is a dated list of states that join, neither a region nor a mood. The enlargement of 1 May 2004 is exactly ten states: Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia. Adding or dropping a name changes the fact.
:::

Before that ten, three states join on 1 January 1995: Austria, Finland and Sweden. They are not a rehearsal of 2004, and they are not "the East". Sweden's accession is a treaty fact about a state that had stayed outside the Rome treaties. It does not make 1957 into a definition of Europe.

On 1 May 2004 the ten join. Count them as you read [[#def-ten]], because the count is the check. Malta and Cyprus are in the ten, and they are not former communist states of the eastern bloc. The Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland and Slovakia come out of that world by different paths, and Slovenia's path runs through the end of a Yugoslav republic as well as through accession. The lesson does not need those paths to police the list. It needs you not to say "Eastern Europe joined" as if that phrase were the ten. A nickname is not a list. Bulgaria and Romania join in 2007. They are not part of the ten. Croatia joins on 1 July 2013. A third date is not a footnote to the first. Someone who adds Croatia to the 2004 list, or who adds Bulgaria and then drops Malta to keep the number ten, has changed the fact in order to save a story.

The same discipline applies to a war that shares the euro's first year. On 1 January 1999 the euro begins as book money. On 24 March 1999 NATO begins a military campaign over Kosovo. It runs to 10 June 1999. From 24 March to 10 June is 78 days. From 1 January to 24 March is 82 days. A currency and a campaign share a year and not a kind of act. The campaign is the chronology's fact. Governments and historians disagree about its legal basis and about its results. This lesson records the dates and the existence of that argument. It does not decide the argument, and it gives no death total. Deciding would be a policy. The atlas does not offer one.

:::example Book money beside a campaign {#ex-kosovo}
Set 1 January 1999 beside 24 March 1999 and 10 June 1999. What may a single dot for 1999 hide?
:::solution
From 1 January to 24 March 1999 is 82 days: January has 31 and 1999 is not a leap year, so 1 January to 1 March is 31 plus 28, that is 59, and 1 March to 24 March is 23. The campaign then runs from 24 March to 10 June, which is 78 days: 24 March to 24 June is 31 plus 30 plus 31, that is 92, and 10 June is 14 days earlier, so 92 minus 14 is 78. A dot labelled 1999 can hide a currency, a military campaign of 78 days, and the fact that the two are not one policy. [[#thm-three]] has already separated the currency from Maastricht. This example separates the currency from the campaign. The argument about the campaign's legality is real. The lesson does not settle it.
:::
:::

:::example An invented completion {#ex-happy}
The following sentence is invented. "Once the ten joined in 2004, the Union had completed the work of 1989 and could close the atlas." What does the sentence do to the list, and which later dates does it have to pretend not to see?
:::solution
The ten of 1 May 2004 are exactly Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia. "The work of 1989" treats a membership list as the end of history, which [[postwar/from-1989]] already refuses, and it hides Malta and Cyprus inside a story about the old eastern bloc. The sentence cannot see 2007, when Bulgaria and Romania join, or 1 July 2013, when Croatia joins, or 23 June 2016, when the United Kingdom votes to leave, or 31 January 2020, when it leaves. It also cannot see 2014, when Russia occupies and annexes Crimea, or 24 February 2022, when Russia begins a full-scale invasion of Ukraine that as of 2026 has not ended. An invented sentence of completion is false because the list is exact and because the years after the list are not empty.
:::
:::

## Crisis, departure, invasion

On 15 September 2008 Lehman Brothers files for bankruptcy. That is a United States event. The European debt crisis that follows is not the same day. Greek loan programmes begin in 2010. We give no sum, in any currency, because the chronology gives none and a figure would only look like precision. The crisis tested a shared money whose members did not share one fiscal state of the same age. That is a description of an arrangement, not a recommendation that the arrangement be kept, broken or reformed. Several states were drawn in. Greece is the one this chronology names for the start of the programmes. We do not invent the other programmes' days. What the dates do support is the separation: 15 September 2008 is not 2010. A bankruptcy is not a loan programme. A lesson that starts the European crisis on the American filing day has skipped the only start the record gives.

The United Kingdom's leaving is three dates, and the three are the whole point. On 23 June 2016 the United Kingdom votes to leave the Union. The chronology does not give the size of the majority, so we do not invent a percentage. The result is a decision to leave. It is not the leaving. On 31 January 2020 the United Kingdom leaves. On 31 December 2020 the transition ends. From the vote to the departure is 1317 days. No 29 February falls inside that span: 29 February 2016 is before 23 June 2016, and 29 February 2020 is after 31 January 2020. Three years on from 23 June 2016 is 1095 days, three times 365. From that anniversary to 31 January 2020 is 222 days, and 1095 plus 222 is 1317. The transition, from 31 January 2020 to 31 December 2020, is 335 days, not 334, because 2020 is a leap year and 29 February falls inside those months. A person who says "Britain left in 2016" has the vote and has missed both later dates. A person who says "Britain left on 31 January, so that was the end of the law" has missed a transition of 335 days. We do not say whether the vote was wise. Wisdom would be a side. The dates are the lesson.

:::theorem Not a closed past {#thm-open}
The Union's treaties, currency, enlargements and the departure of the United Kingdom are on one timeline with Russia's occupation and annexation of Crimea in 2014 and with Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine beginning on 24 February 2022. They are not one story with a happy ending. As of 2026 the invasion has not ended, and it is not a closed past.
:::
:::proof
The evidence is the order of settled facts, and the chronology's own limit. Most states do not recognise the annexation of Crimea. That non-recognition is part of the fact, not a speech for a policy. The full-scale invasion begins on 24 February 2022. It is an invasion. Words that avoid the word, or that treat 2014 and 2022 as the same act, fail the dates: an occupation and annexation of Crimea is not the full-scale invasion, and the invasion is not a euphemism's "crisis" or "operation". The chronology states that as of 2026 the war has not ended. It states no casualty total. This lesson copies that limit and adds nothing about whom a reader should support.

What would count against the claim is a peace dated in this chronology that ended the war, or a showing that the Union's institutional history stopped before 2014 and so cannot sit on the same timeline. Neither is the record. Enlargement, a departure, a currency and an invasion are all dated. What would not count against the claim is the true list of institutional gains: a parliament directly elected from 1979, a union in force from 1993, a currency from 1999 and 2002, a larger membership from 1995, 2004, 2007 and 2013. Grant every one of those gains. None of them is an ending, because membership also shrinks on 31 January 2020, and because a full-scale invasion is under way in 2026. A gain is not a curtain.
:::

The counter-argument says that a textbook of institutions should stop at the institutions, and that a live war will date the page and drag it into a quarrel. The reply is the chronology itself, which does not stop before the invasion and which forbids the quarrel. Stopping early would make the Union the happy ending the claim denies. Going on into advice, a casualty figure, or a side, would make the lesson a speech. [[#thm-open]] is the narrow path between those failures. You can say that an argument exists, among states and among citizens, about recognition, about aid, about negotiations. The existence of the argument is a fact. The lesson does not join it.

Crimea in 2014 and the invasion of 24 February 2022 also show why a membership list is not a security guarantee told as destiny. The Union's list changes by treaty. The recognition of a border is a different kind of fact: most states do not recognise the annexation. We do not convert that sentence into a prediction about how the war ends. There is no ending in the record. The widget stops at 2022 because the chronology stops at a fact, not at a result. A figure that ran on to a peace it does not have would be a fiction.

:::example Three dates for one departure {#ex-brexit}
Separate 23 June 2016, 31 January 2020 and 31 December 2020. Give the date difference from the vote to the departure, and from the departure to the end of the transition.
:::solution
The vote to leave is 23 June 2016. The United Kingdom leaves on 31 January 2020. The transition ends on 31 December 2020. Three years on from 23 June 2016 is 1095 days, and no 29 February falls in the whole span through 31 January 2020, because the leap day of 2016 is already past on 23 June and the leap day of 2020 has not yet come on 31 January. From that anniversary to 31 January 2020 is 222 days: the month-steps from 23 June are 30, 31, 31, 30, 31, 30 and 31, which reach 23 January, and eight further days reach 31 January. Then 1095 plus 222 is 1317, the difference from the vote to the departure. From 31 January 2020 to 31 December 2020 is 335 days, because 2020 is a leap year and 29 February falls inside. The vote is not the leaving. The leaving is not the end of the transition. No percentage is stated, and none should be invented.
:::
:::

:::warning A happy ending, and a softer word
Two habits spoil this chapter. The first treats the Union as the happy ending of the atlas: 1989 completed, 2004 completed, nothing left to date. It fails on the departure of the United Kingdom, on the debt programmes of 2010, and on a war that is open in 2026. The second habit softens 24 February 2022 into a crisis, a conflict or an operation, or folds it into the annexation of 2014. The chronology's word is invasion, and the full-scale invasion has its own day. A third habit, smaller but constant, edits the ten of 1 May 2004, adding Croatia or Bulgaria, or dropping Malta and Cyprus because they spoil a regional nickname. The list is exact. The past is not closed.
:::

The Union's own dates, from Maastricht's signature on 7 February 1992 to the United Kingdom's departure on 31 January 2020, sit on the same timeline and do not close it.
:::

Nothing after it is drawn, because nothing after it is a result this chronology possesses. If you want the line to finish, you are asking the figure for a peace it does not have.

## Where this leads

There is no later course in this atlas. The open claim is the method of [[sources/argument]]: a dated invasion, a refusal of a casualty total, a refusal of a side. The reason the Union is still a membership list, and not the continent, is [[sources/naming-europe]]. What 1989 did not finish is [[postwar/from-1989]]. The institutions of division that were not permanent, the GDR among them, are [[postwar/division]]. The atlas uses the word invasion for 1 September 1939 in [[wars/second-world-war]] and for 24 February 2022 here because that is what the chronology says. The wars are not the same war, and the shared word is not advice about what anyone should do.

:::summary
- Maastricht is signed on 7 February 1992 and enters into force on 1 November 1993, 633 days later. Helsinki in 1975, direct elections in 1979 and the Single European Act in 1986 are earlier and different.
- The euro is book money on 1 January 1999 and notes and coins on 1 January 2002. Not every member state adopts the cash. The United Kingdom does not.
- Austria, Finland and Sweden join on 1 January 1995. On 1 May 2004 the ten are Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia.
- Bulgaria and Romania join in 2007. Croatia joins on 1 July 2013. Later joiners are not the ten.
- NATO's campaign over Kosovo runs from 24 March to 10 June 1999. The lesson records the argument about it and does not decide it.
- Lehman Brothers files for bankruptcy on 15 September 2008. Greek loan programmes begin in 2010. Those are not the same day.
- The United Kingdom votes to leave on 23 June 2016, leaves on 31 January 2020, and ends the transition on 31 December 2020.
- In 2014 Russia occupies and annexes Crimea. Most states do not recognise it. On 24 February 2022 Russia begins a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. As of 2026 the war has not ended. No casualty total, and no policy, is stated.
:::
