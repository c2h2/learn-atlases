# Chronology locked for this atlas

A year numeral in a lesson must appear in this file. A quantity (a population, a count of people, a percentage given as a number) must appear here, with the warning written beside it. If a date is marked disputed, the lesson says so and does not replace it with a different numeral.

Lessons say **BCE** and **CE**. The public timeline prints the same negative years as **BC**. There is **no year 0**: 1 BCE is followed by 1 CE. The elapsed count from year A BCE to year B CE is A + B − 1. From 800 CE to 843 CE is 43 years.

Do not invent quotations. Paraphrase.

## Calendars

- Gregorian reform, 1582: in Rome, 4 October 1582 was followed by 15 October 1582. Not every country switched then.
- Britain and the British empire switched in 1752: 2 September 1752 was followed by 14 September 1752.
- Russia: the day after 31 January 1918 (Julian) was 14 February 1918 (Gregorian).
- February Revolution: 8–16 March 1917 New Style (23 February–3 March Old Style). Nicholas II abdicated on 15 March 1917 New Style (2 March Old Style).
- October Revolution: 7 November 1917 New Style (25 October 1917 Old Style). This was before the 1918 calendar switch.

## Greece

- 776 BCE: the traditional date of the first Olympic games. It is a later tradition, not a documented birth of a Greek nation.
- 594 BCE: the traditional date of Solon’s archonship.
- 508 BCE: Cleisthenes’ reforms, often dated 508/507.
- 490 BCE: Marathon.
- 480 BCE: Thermopylae and Salamis, the same campaign season.
- 479 BCE: Plataea.
- 478 BCE: the usual textbook year for the start of the Delian League (winter 478/477).
- 451 BCE: Pericles’ citizenship law, often dated 451/450. An Athenian citizen in the democracy that followed was an adult man with citizen parents. Women, enslaved people and metics were excluded.
- 431 BCE: the Peloponnesian War begins, on the usual dating.
- 404 BCE: Athens surrenders.
- 399 BCE: trial and death of Socrates.
- 338 BCE: Chaeronea.
- 336 BCE: Philip II of Macedon is assassinated.
- 334 BCE: Alexander crosses into Asia.
- 331 BCE: Gaugamela.
- 323 BCE: Alexander dies in Babylon. The empire does not stay one kingdom.

A **polis** is a city-state, a community of citizens and a territory, not a nation-state. There was no single Greek state. Persia under Darius and Xerxes was a great empire. It is not a cartoon of “the East”.

## Rome

- 509 BCE: the traditional date for the start of the Republic. It is a later tradition.
- 264–241 BCE: First Punic War.
- 218–201 BCE: Second Punic War. Cannae 216 BCE. Zama 202 BCE.
- 149–146 BCE: Third Punic War. Carthage destroyed in 146 BCE. Corinth destroyed in 146 BCE.
- 133 BCE: tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus.
- 15 March 44 BCE: assassination of Julius Caesar.
- 2 September 31 BCE: Actium.
- 27 BCE: the settlement in which Octavian is called Augustus. “The Republic ended on one day” is a later simplification. The institutions of the Republic were kept and emptied.
- 79 CE: eruption of Vesuvius; Pompeii and Herculaneum are buried.
- 117 CE: death of Trajan. A map of “greatest extent” depends on what is counted.
- 212 CE: Constitutio Antoniniana. Citizenship is extended very widely. It does not make every inhabitant equal in practice, and enslaved people remain enslaved.
- 235–284 CE: the period often called the third-century crisis. The label is a historian’s bundle, not a single event.
- 284 CE: Diocletian.
- 28 October 312 CE: the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, on the usual dating.
- 313 CE: Constantine and Licinius agree a policy of toleration, later called the Edict of Milan. The name is later. This is not yet an established church.
- 325 CE: Council of Nicaea.
- 11 May 330 CE: the traditional date of the dedication of Constantinople.
- 380 CE: the Edict of Thessalonica. Christianity becomes the official religion of the empire. Enforcement is a process, not a switch.
- 391 CE: Theodosian measures against public pagan cult.
- 24 August 410 CE: the Visigoths under Alaric sack Rome.
- 451 CE: Council of Chalcedon.
- 476 CE: Odoacer deposes Romulus Augustulus. This is the conventional “fall of the Western Roman empire”. The eastern empire continues. Julius Nepos, a rival claimant, dies in 480 CE. 476 is a convention, not the end of Roman life.

## After Rome

- Baptism of Clovis: the date is disputed. Both about 496 and about 508 have been argued. Do not present either as certain.
- 527–565: Justinian.
- 529: Justinian’s first Codex. Digest and Institutes 533. Second edition of the Codex 534.
- 532: the Nika riot in Constantinople.
- 27 December 537: Hagia Sophia dedicated.
- 541: the Justinianic plague arrives in Egypt and spreads. Do not give a death total.
- 568: the usual date for the Lombard entry into Italy.
- 622: the Hijra, the start of the Islamic calendar’s year count.
- 632: death of Muhammad.
- 636: Yarmouk, on the usual dating.
- 711: Tariq ibn Ziyad’s force crosses into Iberia.
- Tours / Poitiers: the traditional date is October 732. Some scholars, using the Chronicle of 754, prefer 733 or 734. The battle did not “save Europe”. Raids continued, and the story of a continent saved is a later legend. Charles Martel was not yet king.
- 750: Abbasid revolution; the Umayyad caliphate in the east is overthrown.
- 756: Abd al-Rahman I establishes the Umayyad emirate of Cordoba.
- 25 December 800: Pope Leo III crowns Charlemagne emperor in Rome.
- 843: Treaty of Verdun. It divides a Frankish inheritance. It does not found France and Germany.
- 929: Abd al-Rahman III takes the title of caliph. The Caliphate of Cordoba.
- 962: Otto I is crowned emperor.
- 988: the traditional date for the baptism of Vladimir of Kiev. Treat it as a traditional marker of the conversion of the Rus’, not as a census of belief.
- 1054: mutual excommunications involving Humbert and Michael Cerularius. The schism between Latin and Greek churches is a long process. 1054 is a marker, not the whole story.

## Latin Christendom, about 1000–1300

- 910: the usual date for the foundation of Cluny (909 is also met in the scholarship; use 910 and do not fuss).
- 14 October 1066: Hastings.
- 1073: Gregory VII becomes pope.
- 1077: Canossa, January. Henry IV does penance. The conflict is not over.
- 1086: Domesday survey.
- November 1095: Urban II at Clermont.
- 1098: Cîteaux, the Cistercian start.
- 15 July 1099: the First Crusade takes Jerusalem.
- 1122: Concordat of Worms.
- 1187: Hattin, and Saladin takes Jerusalem.
- 13 April 1204: the Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople, a Christian city.
- 1212: Las Navas de Tolosa.
- June 1215: Magna Carta. It is a peace between a king and his barons, reissued, and later reinterpreted. It is not a constitution of modern liberty.
- November 1215: Fourth Lateran Council. Canon 68 requires Jews and Muslims in Latin Christendom to be marked by dress. This is a decree about exclusion, not a description of every town’s practice.
- 1225: a reissue of Magna Carta.
- 1231: Gregory IX and the papal Inquisition (the bull often cited is Excommunicamus). Inquisition here is a procedure. It is not yet the Spanish Inquisition.
- 1241: Mongol campaigns reach Hungary and Legnica. They are not “the end of Europe”, and the Mongol polity is not a footnote.
- 1265: the parliament summoned by Simon de Montfort, often cited in a later story of “the mother of parliaments”. It was a baronial parliament in a civil war.
- 1274: death of Thomas Aquinas.
- 1291: Acre falls. The crusader principalities on the mainland end.
- 1295: Edward I’s parliament, later called the Model Parliament.
- 1302: the bull Unam sanctam.
- 1309: Clement V and the start of the long papal residence at Avignon.

Serfdom was not one European system. Say what a **manor** is (a unit of lordship and rent), and that unfreedom varied by region and century.

## Crisis and the fifteenth century

- 1315–1317: the Great Famine in much of northern Europe. Use 1315 as the start.
- 1337: the conventional start of the Hundred Years’ War. The brackets 1337–1453 are a later label for a series of conflicts.
- 1346: Crécy.
- October 1347: plague is reported at Messina. 1348 and 1349: it is in much of western Europe. Further waves follow. Do not state a single European death total. Say that in many of the regions where the evidence is studied, estimates fall between about a third and about a half of the population, and that some places differ.
- 1356: the Golden Bull of Emperor Charles IV.
- 1378: the Western Schism begins. It ends with the election of Martin V in 1417. The Council of Constance sits 1414–1418. Jan Hus is burned in 1415.
- 1381: the English Rising, also called the Peasants’ Revolt. It was not only peasants.
- 25 October 1415: Agincourt.
- 30 May 1431: Joan of Arc is burned at Rouen.
- Gutenberg’s printed Bible is usually dated about 1454–1455. Do not invent a day. Print multiplies texts. It does not, by itself, cause the Reformation.
- 29 May 1453: Constantinople falls to the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II. The eastern Roman state ends. Ottoman rule is not “the end of history” in the Balkans.
- 17 July 1453: Castillon. The conventional end of the Hundred Years’ War.
- 1 November 1478: Sixtus IV authorises the Spanish Inquisition.
- 22 August 1485: Bosworth. Richard III is killed. Henry Tudor becomes Henry VII.
- 1492 is several events. 2 January: Granada surrenders. 31 March: the Alhambra Decree orders the Jews of Castile and Aragon to convert or leave, with a deadline of 31 July. 3 August: Columbus sails from Palos. 12 October: his landfall in the Caribbean. Do not collapse these into “the birth of modern Europe”.
- 7 June 1494: Treaty of Tordesillas.
- May 1498: Vasco da Gama at Calicut. Mention only as the maritime link, not as an Indian history.

## Reformations

- 31 October 1517: the Ninety-five Theses are dated. They were real, and they circulated. The story that Luther nailed them to the Wittenberg church door comes later (Melanchthon) and is not established. Do not tell it as fact.
- April 1521: Luther at the Diet of Worms. The Edict of Worms is May 1521.
- 1524–1525: the German Peasants’ War. Luther condemns the rebels. The war is part of the story, not an embarrassment to skip.
- 1527: the sack of Rome by imperial troops.
- 1529: the protest at Speyer from which “Protestant” comes; the Marburg colloquy, where Luther and Zwingli do not agree on the eucharist.
- 1531: Zwingli is killed at Kappel.
- 1534: the Act of Supremacy under Henry VIII.
- 1536: the first edition of Calvin’s Institutes; also a marker for the dissolution of English monasteries, which is a process, not one day.
- 1541: Calvin returns to Geneva.
- 1545–1563: the Council of Trent, with a long gap between sessions. “Counter-Reformation” hides the Catholic reform that is not only a reaction. Prefer “Catholic reform” and say what the older label leaves out.
- 25 September 1555: the Peace of Augsburg. Cuius regio, eius religio is a formula for Lutheran and Catholic princes in the empire. It does not include Calvinists. It is not modern toleration.
- 1559: Elizabeth’s Act of Supremacy and Act of Uniformity.
- 1562–1598: the usual brackets for the French wars of religion.
- 24 August 1572: the massacre of St Bartholomew’s Day in Paris, which spreads.
- 1588: the Spanish Armada. It does not, by itself, make England a great power.
- April 1598: the Edict of Nantes. A limited toleration for Huguenots, not equality.
- 23 May 1618: the Defenestration of Prague. The Thirty Years’ War is dated 1618–1648. It is not only a war of religion.
- 24 October 1648: the Peace of Westphalia (Münster and Osnabrück). Spain had recognised the United Provinces at Münster in January 1648. Westphalia adjusts Augsburg and admits Calvinists in the empire. It does not invent a finished theory of modern sovereignty. That reading is later.

## States, commerce, enlightenment

- 1556: the abdications of Charles V.
- 1579: the Union of Utrecht. 1581: the Act of Abjuration.
- 1602: the charter of the Dutch East India Company (VOC).
- 1609: the Twelve Years’ Truce begins (to 1621).
- 30 January 1649: execution of Charles I.
- 1651: the English Navigation Act.
- 1660: the Restoration in England.
- 12 September 1683: the relief of Vienna (Kahlenberg). The Ottoman siege is lifted. This is not “Europe versus Asia” as a permanent fact.
- 1685: Louis XIV revokes the Edict of Nantes (Fontainebleau, 18 October 1685). The same year, the Code Noir is issued for French colonial slavery.
- 1687: Newton’s Principia is published.
- 1688: William of Orange lands in England. The Bill of Rights is 1689. “Glorious Revolution” is a later, partisan name. Say what changed: a Catholic king is replaced, and parliament’s place is restated. It is not a democratic revolution.
- 1694: the Bank of England.
- 1701: the Act of Settlement; the elector of Brandenburg is crowned king in Prussia.
- 1 May 1707: the Acts of Union of England and Scotland.
- 1713: the Treaty of Utrecht.
- 1740–1748: the War of the Austrian Succession. Frederick II invades Silesia in 1740.
- 1751: the first volume of the Encyclopédie.
- 1756–1763: the Seven Years’ War. It is fought in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa and India. It is not only a European cabinet war.
- 1762: Rousseau’s Social Contract and Emile.
- 1764: Beccaria, On Crimes and Punishments.
- 1769: Watt’s patent for the separate condenser.
- 1772, 1793 and 1795: the three partitions of Poland-Lithuania. The commonwealth is erased as a state. It had been a large European polity, not a gap on the map.
- 1776: Smith’s Wealth of Nations; the American Declaration of Independence. Use the second only as context.
- 1791: the slave revolt in Saint-Domingue begins in August.
- 4 February 1794: the French revolutionary abolition of slavery in the colonies. Napoleon restores colonial slavery in 1802. The second French abolition is 1848.
- 1807: the British Act against the slave trade (the trade, not slavery itself). The 1833 Act abolishes slavery in most of the British empire, with apprenticeship continuing to 1838.
- Slave Voyages estimates, the embarkation-region table covering 1501–1866: **12,521,354 people embarked**. This is an estimate from that project’s published table, not a headcount taken at the time, and not a count of every slave trade in world history. Do not invent a disembarkation total, a national share, or a death total on the crossing. Say that fewer people disembarked than embarked, because people died on the voyage.

Enlightenment: publics, print, salons, academies and censors. It was not one programme, and it did not include everyone. Many of its writers accepted empire. Women hosted and wrote and were shut out of most academies. Do not write “the Enlightenment caused the French Revolution” as a single cause.

## Revolutions, 1789–1848

- 5 May 1789: the Estates-General opens. 17 June: the National Assembly. 20 June: the Tennis Court Oath. 14 July: the Bastille. 4–11 August: the August decrees. 26 August: the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. 5–6 October: the march to Versailles.
- June 1791: the flight to Varennes. 3 September 1791: the constitution is completed (the king accepts it on 14 September).
- 20 April 1792: France declares war on Austria (the King of Bohemia and Hungary). 10 August 1792: the monarchy is suspended. September massacres. 20 September: Valmy. 21–22 September: the republic.
- 21 January 1793: Louis XVI is executed. August 1793: the levée en masse. The Terror as a named period is conventionally September 1793 to 27 July 1794 (9 Thermidor), when Robespierre falls. Do not treat the Terror as the whole of the Revolution, or as its opposite.
- 9 November 1799: 18 Brumaire.
- 1 January 1804: Haiti declares independence. Toussaint Louverture was captured in 1802 and died in 1803. The Haitian Revolution is part of this story.
- 21 March 1804: the French Civil Code. 2 December 1804: Napoleon is crowned emperor.
- 21 October 1805: Trafalgar. 2 December 1805: Austerlitz.
- 24 June 1812: the Grande Armée crosses the Niemen.
- 6 April 1814: Napoleon abdicates the first time (the date often given; the abdication act is 6 April). 18 June 1815: Waterloo. 22 June 1815: the second abdication.
- 9 June 1815: the Final Act of the Congress of Vienna. The congress had been sitting since 1814.
- 1830: the July Revolution in France. The Belgian revolution begins in 1830.
- 1832: the British Reform Act. The franchise remains narrow.
- 1846: repeal of the Corn Laws.
- 1848: revolutions in many European cities, and not one revolution. The Communist Manifesto is published in London in February 1848. The Frankfurt parliament meets. Most of the revolutionary regimes are defeated. Do not say the year failed everywhere: serfdom in the Habsburg empire is abolished in 1848, and the Swiss settlement of 1848 lasts. Use 1848 for those, and do not invent a day for the Manifesto beyond February.

## Nations and industry, 1815–1914

Census of England and Wales. Two figures exist for 1801, and the lesson must show both:

- Enumeration Abstract, ordered to be printed 21 December 1801: England 8,331,434; Wales 541,546; Scotland 1,599,068. England and Wales in that summary sum to 8,872,980.
- The later official series, cited by the 1901 census report when it compares the century, gives England and Wales in 1801 as 8,892,536. The difference is a revision, not two different countries.

Later enumerated totals for England and Wales, as in the 1901 general report and the 1911 retrospective table:

- 1851: 17,927,609
- 1901: 32,527,843, enumerated as living at midnight on 31 March 1901

Ireland, from the same 1901 report’s comparative table:

- 1841: 8,175,124
- 1851: 6,552,385

The fall, 1,622,739, mixes deaths, emigration and fewer births. It is not a count of famine deaths. Do not state a famine death total.

- 27 September 1825: the Stockton and Darlington railway opens.
- 15 September 1830: the Liverpool and Manchester railway.
- 1833: the British Factory Act.
- 17 March 1861: the Kingdom of Italy is proclaimed.
- 3 July 1866: Königgrätz (Sadowa).
- 1867: the Ausgleich. Franz Joseph is crowned king of Hungary on 8 June 1867.
- 1–2 September 1870: Sedan.
- 20 September 1870: Italian troops take Rome.
- 18 January 1871: the German Empire is proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles.
- 10 May 1871: the Treaty of Frankfurt.
- March–May 1871: the Paris Commune.
- 1878: the Congress of Berlin.
- 13 June–13 July 1878 is the sitting; use 1878.
- 1884–1885: the Berlin Conference, 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885. It regulates European claims in Africa. It does not, in one meeting, give the continent away. Conquest takes longer and is violent.
- 1899–1902: the South African War.
- 1904: Roger Casement’s Congo report. Leopold II’s Congo Free State is his personal rule from 1885 to 1908, then the Belgian Congo. Atrocities are documented. Do not state a death total. The large round numbers in circulation are not settled enough for this atlas.
- 2 October 1904: von Trotha’s extermination order against the Herero in German South-West Africa. Historians classify the campaign against the Herero and the Nama (to 1908) as genocide. Do not state a death total.
- 1830: the French invasion of Algiers begins.
- 1857: the Indian rebellion. “Mutiny” and “First War of Independence” are both names in use. Say that the name is an argument.
- 1894: Dreyfus is arrested. 13 January 1898: Zola’s “J’Accuse” is published. 1906: Dreyfus is exonerated. You may name the title of Zola’s article. Do not invent the rest of the text.
- 22 January 1905: Bloody Sunday in St Petersburg, New Style (9 January Old Style).
- 1906: in the Grand Duchy of Finland, women gain the parliamentary vote and the right to stand. Use this as a European parliamentary first, and do not call it the first women’s suffrage anywhere in the world.
- 1867 and 1884: the second and third British Reform Acts. Women are not included.
- 1905: the union of Sweden and Norway is dissolved.
- Alliances: 1879 Dual Alliance (Germany and Austria-Hungary). 1894 Franco-Russian alliance. 8 April 1904: the Entente Cordiale. 1907: the Anglo-Russian convention. These are not one armed camp with no choices left.
- 1908: the Bosnian annexation crisis.
- 1912–1913: the Balkan Wars.
- 28 June 1914: Franz Ferdinand is assassinated in Sarajevo.
- 23 July 1914: the Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to Serbia.
- 28 July 1914: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
- 1 August 1914: Germany declares war on Russia.
- 3 August 1914: Germany declares war on France.
- 4 August 1914: Germany invades Belgium; Britain declares war on Germany.
- The July crisis is full of choices. A staff plan, including the German mobilisation plan associated with Schlieffen and revised by Moltke, constrained the German timetable. A constraint is not the same thing as a continent with no choices. Christopher Clark’s Sleepwalkers is one interpretation, not a verdict.

The classic claim about industry is that mechanised growth in cotton, coal and iron in Britain in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries was early by European standards. Historians argue about how early, how complete, and how much it depended on coal, wages, slavery and empire. Do not state it as a law that Britain had to be first.

## The world wars

- 21 February 1916: Verdun begins. 1 July 1916: the Somme begins. Do not state a death total for either.
- April 1917: the United States enters the war.
- 3 March 1918: the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk.
- 11 November 1918: the armistice.
- 28 June 1919: the Treaty of Versailles, signed in the Hall of Mirrors.
- 1919: Saint-Germain and Neuilly. 4 June 1920: Trianon. 1920: Sèvres, later revised.
- 28 October 1922: the March on Rome.
- 30 December 1922: the USSR is formed.
- 8–9 November 1923: the Munich putsch fails.
- 24 October 1929 and 29 October 1929: the Wall Street crash (Black Thursday and Black Tuesday). European slumps are connected and are not the same event on the same day.
- 30 January 1933: Hitler is appointed chancellor. 27 February 1933: the Reichstag fire. 23 March 1933: the Enabling Act.
- 15 September 1935: the Nuremberg Laws.
- 17–18 July 1936: the Spanish Civil War begins. It ends on 1 April 1939.
- 9–10 November 1938: the November pogrom (Kristallnacht).
- March 1938: the Anschluss. 30 September 1938: the Munich Agreement.
- 23 August 1939: the Molotov–Ribbentrop pact.
- 1 September 1939: Germany invades Poland.
- 3 September 1939: Britain and France declare war on Germany.
- 10 May 1940: the German offensive in the west. 22 June 1940: the French armistice.
- 22 June 1941: Germany invades the Soviet Union.
- 7 December 1941: Pearl Harbor. 11 December 1941: Germany declares war on the United States.
- 20 January 1942: the Wannsee conference. It coordinates a murder that has already begun. It is not the start. Mass shooting in the occupied Soviet Union begins in the summer of 1941. Chełmno begins killing in December 1941.
- 31 January and 2 February 1943: the German surrender at Stalingrad.
- 6 June 1944: the Normandy landings. 20 July 1944: the plot against Hitler fails.
- 1 August–2 October 1944: the Warsaw uprising.
- 27 January 1945: Auschwitz is liberated by the Red Army.
- February 1945: the Yalta conference.
- German surrender: signed at Reims on 7 May 1945, and at Karlshorst on 8 May 1945. The Soviet Union marked 9 May because of the time difference. Teach both 8 May and 9 May rather than correcting one of them out of existence.

The Holocaust: the figure used by historians and by memorial institutions (Yad Vashem, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum) is **about six million Jews murdered**. It is an estimate, not a list the perpetrators completed. Roma, disabled people murdered in the so-called euthanasia programme, many Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, and others were also murdered. Do not invent a second total. Do not describe killing procedures. Name killing centres as places of murder, and say that the evidence is documentary, testimonial and material.

- Raphael Lemkin’s book Axis Rule in Occupied Europe (1944) uses the word genocide.
- 9 December 1948: the UN General Assembly adopts the Genocide Convention.

Collaboration and resistance are messy categories. Both happened, often in the same society, and sometimes in the same life. Do not sort a nation into a single moral type.

Decolonisation, as European history because the empires were European:

- 14 August 1947: Pakistan. 15 August 1947: India. Partition is accompanied by very large violence. Do not state a death total.
- 7 May 1954: Dien Bien Phu.
- 1 November 1954: the Algerian war begins. 18 March 1962: the Évian accords. 5 July 1962: Algerian independence.
- 1960: a label, “the year of Africa”, for many independences. Use it as a label.
- 1975: independence of Angola and Mozambique as the Portuguese empire ends. The Carnation Revolution is 1974.

Welfare states are not one system. Britain, Sweden, West Germany and France built different settlements. Esping-Andersen’s Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism (1990) is a later typology to argue with, not a fact of 1950.

## How to use this file

Settled: treaty dates, battle dates marked as usual, the census integers, the Slave Voyages embarked total, the figure of about six million Jews murdered, the calendar switches.

Disputed, and must be taught as disputed: the date of Tours, the baptism of Clovis, the door of the Wittenberg church, the meaning of 476, the meaning of Westphalia, the meaning of 1492, whether Britain’s industrial lead was a law of history, whether the July crisis was a set of choices or a timetable.

Forbidden: a quotation you cannot find in this file, a casualty total not printed here, a population not printed here, a procedure of killing or of the slave ship.
