An **international monetary system** is the set of rules, conventions and institutions that govern how national monies relate to one another: what is a reserve asset, how $E$ is set, who lends in a crisis, and which country's money the rest of the world is willing to hold. The gold standard, the interwar chaos, Bretton Woods, and the post-1973 mix of floating rates, dollar reserves and the IMF are successive answers to those questions. None of them was a theorem. Each was a political settlement with an economic constraint — Hume's specie flow, Triffin's dilemma, the trilemma — attached. This chapter is that history and those constraints. It is the last chapter of the course because it uses every previous one: the balance of payments, parity conditions, regimes, unions and crises. Dates are verified; illustrative arithmetic is labelled. This is education, not a forecast of the dollar's share and not advice to hold any reserve asset.

## The classical gold standard, 1870–1914

Under a **gold standard** a country defines its money as a weight of gold and stands ready to convert, so $E$ between two gold countries is a **mint parity**, with **gold points** — shipping and insurance costs — as a narrow band.

::: definition Mint parity and gold points {#def-gold}
If a palm is defined as $1/20$ of an ounce of gold and a dollar as $1/20$ of an ounce, the mint parity is $E=1$ palm per dollar. If shipping a dollar's gold costs $0.01$ in palm units, the **gold points** are $0.99$ and $1.01$. Outside that band, specie flows: it is cheaper to ship gold than to buy the expensive currency. Inside the band, $E$ can move with bills of exchange and with interest rates.
:::

Hume's 1752 **price–specie-flow** is the adjustment: a current-account surplus brings gold, raises $M$ and $P$, and chokes off the surplus. The "rules of the game" of the late nineteenth century added an interest-rate rule: a gold outflow was to be met by a higher bank rate, attracting a capital inflow and cutting $Y$. In the trilemma, the gold standard is corner (1): a peg (to gold, hence to other gold countries) plus open capital, with $i$ set to defend $R$. Britain's bank rate was the system's central $i^*$ (Eichengreen, *Globalizing Capital*, 3rd ed., 2019).

The system was a gold *standard* for a core (Britain, France, Germany, the United States after 1879) and a gold *exchange* standard, with sterling or franc reserves, for much of the periphery. It delivered long-run price stability on average and a lot of short-run $Y$ volatility. Whether the volatility was the price of the peg or the price of an undeveloped lender of last resort is a dispute as old as Bagehot.

::: example Gold points {#ex-points}
Mint parity is $E=4.87$ palms per pound. Shipping and insurance cost $0.03$ palms per pound. (a) What are the gold points? (b) If $E=4.92$, what happens? (c) Why might a rise in Palmia's discount rate pull $E$ back without a gold shipment?
::: solution
(a) $4.84$ and $4.90$. (b) $E=4.92$ is above the upper gold point: it is cheaper to ship gold from Palmia (buy pounds with gold) than to buy pounds in the bill market. Palmia loses specie, $M$ falls. (c) A higher Palmia $i$ attracts a capital inflow (sterling bills, deposits), which supplies pounds without shipping gold, and may move $E$ back inside the band. That is the "rules of the game" as an interest-rate defence, the ancestor of UIP under a peg.
:::
:::

## Interwar failure

The First World War broke convertibility. The 1920s attempt to return — Britain at the pre-war sterling–gold parity in 1925 — produced a real appreciation, a difficult $Y$, and a 1931 exit. France accumulated gold; the United States sterilised inflows; Germany was bound by reparations and then by Brüning's deflation. Eichengreen and Temin, and Eichengreen (*Golden Fetters*, 1992), argue that the gold standard transmitted and deepened the Great Depression by forcing $i$ up and $M$ down in countries that were already shrinking. The competing account (Friedman and Schwartz on the US; some modern DSGE gold-standard papers) locates more of the US catastrophe in domestic monetary errors, with gold as a constraint that a more aggressive Fed could still have tested. Both accounts agree that 1931–33 was a run on the system's remaining convertibility, a first-generation-plus-banking crisis at global scale. This course does not adjudicate 1929; it records that the interwar gold standard failed as a system, and that the failure is part of why Bretton Woods was built with *adjustable* pegs and *capital controls*.

## Bretton Woods, 1944–1971

The United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944 created the IMF and the IBRD (World Bank) and a system of dollar pegs, the dollar itself convertible into gold for official holders at \$35 an ounce. Keynes's *bancor* and Clearing Union were not adopted; Harry Dexter White's dollar-centred design was. Pegs were adjustable, in principle, in the event of "fundamental disequilibrium". Capital controls were expected. In the trilemma, Bretton Woods is corner (3): peg plus controls, with some room for $i$.

::: definition Triffin's dilemma {#def-triffin}
**Triffin's dilemma** (Robert Triffin, *Gold and the Dollar Crisis*, 1960) is the claim that a gold-exchange system whose reserve asset is a national money cannot simultaneously (i) supply the growing world with enough reserves and (ii) keep the reserve asset credibly convertible into gold. Enough dollars in official hands to grease trade means more dollar claims than gold at \$35; scarce dollars to protect the \$35 pledge means a reserve shortage.
:::

The dilemma is a first-generation clock for the *system*: $D$ is US official-liability growth, $R$ is Fort Knox. The 1960s built palliatives — the Gold Pool, swap lines, **Special Drawing Rights** (SDRs), created in 1969 — and did not stop the arithmetic. On 15 August 1971 the United States suspended official gold convertibility (the Nixon shock). The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 tried new dollar pegs; they failed. By March 1973 the major currencies were floating.

::: proposition A schematic Triffin ratio {#prop-triffin}
Let $G$ be US gold, valued at the official price, and $L$ official dollar claims on the United States. Convertibility at the announced price is feasible in a run only if $G/L \ge 1$ (or $\ge$ some haircut). World reserves needed for trade grow with $Y_{\text{world}}$. If that growth is to be met by $L$ (gold being nearly fixed), $L$ rises and $G/L$ falls. There is no path on which $L$ grows with $Y_{\text{world}}$ *and* $G/L$ stays above the run-feasible threshold, unless $G$ is revalued or a non-national reserve asset (the SDR) takes the place of $L$.
:::

::: proof
$G$ is predetermined at the official price (gold production is small relative to stocks). $dL > 0$ is required if $d(L+G) = d(\text{world reserves}) > 0$ and $dG \approx 0$. Then $d(G/L) < 0$. A threshold $G/L \ge \kappa$ is eventually violated. Revaluing gold (raising the dollar price) raises $G$ in dollar units and resets the clock; it also redistributes to gold holders and was politically rejected in the 1960s. The SDR is a non-national $dG$.
:::

::: example A Triffin ratio {#ex-triffin}
US gold is \$12 billion at \$35 an ounce; official dollar claims are \$40 billion. (a) What is $G/L$? (b) If official claims grow 8 per cent and gold is unchanged, what is $G/L$ after five years? (The levels are schematic, of the order of late-1960s magnitudes, not a substitute for the Federal Reserve's historical tables.)
::: solution
(a) $12/40 = 0.30$. A run that tried to convert all official dollars would not be fully met at \$35. (b) $L_5 = 40 \times (1.08)^5 \approx 58.8$, $G/L \approx 12/58.8 \approx 0.20$. The ratio falls. That is [[#prop-triffin]] as compound interest. The actual 1960s ratio of US gold to official liabilities moved in this direction (Federal Reserve *Historical Statistics*; Bordo, *NBER*, various). The example is the mechanism, not a reproduction of a single year's table.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Bretton Woods, in the trilemma, is closest to
- [ ] a float with open capital
- [x] a peg with capital controls
- [ ] a gold coin standard with no central banks
- [ ] a monetary union among all members
::: solution
Pegs to the dollar, controls expected, some room for $i$: corner (3). It was not a monetary union (each country had its own $M$ and, in principle, an adjustable $E$). It was not a coin standard of the 1880s.

## Floating, the dollar, and the IMF after 1973

The Jamaica Accords of 1976 amended the IMF's Articles to legalise floating. The Plaza Accord (1985) and the Louvre Accord (1987) were coordinated attempts to manage the dollar. The EMS (1979) and then EMU (1999) rebuilt a peg system *inside* Europe while Europe floated against the dollar. Emerging markets moved, in waves, from pegs to more flexible arrangements after the 1990s crises, with the fear-of-floating caveat of [[international-finance/exchange-rate-regimes]].

The **dollar** remained the vehicle currency in FX (89.2 per cent of BIS 2025 turnover on one side of the trade), the main invoicing currency in trade, and the main official reserve asset (about 58 per cent of allocated COFER reserves in 2024). Eichengreen's *exorbitant privilege* (the phrase is Giscard d'Estaing's, the analysis is Kindelberger's, Triffin's and later Gourinchas and Rey's) is the claim that the United States earns a return differential on its external portfolio *because* the world wants dollar safe assets. Whether that differential is a privilege, a burden (Triffin, again, in a float), or a market-clearing price of safety (Caballero, Farhi, Gourinchas) is a live research question. This course uses the BIS and COFER *shares* as facts and does not treat any share as a recommendation to hold dollars, euros or renminbi.

**SDRs** are an IMF-issued reserve asset, valued on a basket (the dollar, euro, yuan, yen and sterling; the yuan was added in 2016). Allocations in 2009 and 2021 were crisis-period injections of official liquidity. They are not a world currency; they are a claim on the basket, usable among official holders.

The **IMF** has three functions in the Articles: surveillance, technical assistance, and lending to members with a balance-of-payments need. Quota-based resources, New Arrangements to Borrow, and bilateral borrowing agreements size the balance sheet. Conditionality is the political price of lending; the debate over whether programmes are too tight or too loose is the solvency-versus-liquidity dispute of [[international-finance/crises]], institutionalised. Regional arrangements — the ESM in Europe, Chiang Mai in East Asia, the Latin American Reserve Fund — are partial substitutes and complements.

::: example An SDR allocation {#ex-sdr}
The IMF allocates $100$ of SDRs to members in proportion to quotas. Palmia's quota share is $0.5$ per cent. (a) How many SDRs does Palmia receive? (b) Does Palmia's NIIP change at the moment of allocation? (c) When would Palmia's current account subsequently move because of the SDR?
::: solution
(a) $0.5$ of SDRs. (b) Palmia gains an official asset (the SDR holding) and a matching long-term liability to the SDR Department of the same amount, so the NIIP is roughly unchanged at allocation — an SDR is both a claim and an allocation-liability. (c) If Palmia *sells* SDRs for dollars and spends the dollars, or if it earns net interest on a net SDR position, the current account and the NIIP then move. An allocation is not a transfer of goods; it is a reserve asset. The 2021 general allocation of SDR 456 billion is the real-world counterpart (IMF, 2021), of which this example is the arithmetic of a share, not a reproduction of Palmia's actual quota.
:::
:::

::: example Vehicle-currency arithmetic {#ex-vehicle}
A Korean importer of Brazilian goods can pay in BRL, KRW or USD. Dealers' bid–ask in BRL/KRW is $0.80$ per cent of the mid, in USD/KRW $0.10$ per cent, and in USD/BRL $0.10$ per cent. (a) What is the round-trip cost of going via the dollar, versus the cross? (b) Why might the importer still invoice in dollars even if the cross were cheaper on this day?
::: solution
(a) Via the dollar: $0.10+0.10=0.20$ per cent, against $0.80$ per cent on the cross, so the dollar route is cheaper by $0.60$ percentage points. (b) Invoice currency is a network: the Korean firm's other trades, its bank's dollar credit line, and the Brazilian firm's dollar costs may all be in USD. A one-day cross quote does not rebuild that network. The spreads are invented; the inequality is the BIS fact that BRL/KRW is still often dealt via the dollar.
:::
:::

::: application COFER shares, not a forecast
IMF COFER (Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves) is the standard source for the dollar's reserve share. The dollar's share of *allocated* reserves has declined from above 70 per cent in the early 2000s to about 58 per cent in 2024, with the euro second, and the renminbi still in the low single digits of allocated reserves (IMF COFER releases, 2024–2025). Unallocated reserves and non-COFER holdings (gold, SDRs) complicate the picture. A declining *share* is compatible with a rising *level* of dollar reserves, because the pie grew. None of these sentences is a prediction of 2030, and none is advice to a reserve manager.
:::

## The trilemma as history

Obstfeld and Taylor (*Global Capital Markets*, 2004) read the twentieth century as a walk around the trilemma:

- 1870–1914: peg (gold) + open capital, $i$ subordinated.
- 1914–1945: breakdown, controls, inconvertible currencies, then a failed return to gold.
- 1944–1971: peg + controls, some $i$ room.
- 1973– : core floats, capital opened, $i$ aimed at domestic inflation; periphery a mix, with a 1990s attempt at corner (1) that often ended in [[international-finance/crises]], and a 2000s reserve-accumulation strategy that looks like self-insurance plus a managed $E$.

The walk is a history, not a cycle that must repeat. China's management of $E$, the capital account and $i$ since 1994 is the large current case of an interior point, with the trilemma's warning attached: interior points spend $R$ or tighten controls when tested (2015–16 was a test; the PBOC's own reports record reserve loss and a tighter CFM stance). The euro area is a union that floats as a bloc: internally a hard peg, externally corner (2).

::: warning The system is not a gold standard with a different metal
Calling the post-1973 arrangement a "dollar standard" is useful as a description of vehicle-currency and reserve-currency facts. It is misleading if it imports gold-standard *rules* (convertibility, gold points, a specie-flow $P$) that no longer bind. The United States does not convert dollars into gold at a fixed price. Adjustment of $E$ is allowed. Triffin's dilemma in a float is a *demand-for-safe-assets* problem, not a convertibility clock. Using 1960s language for 2020s institutions produces 1960s policy conclusions that the institutions cannot implement.
:::

## Evidence

**BIS turnover.** April 2025: \$9.6 trillion a day, dollar on one side of 89.2 per cent, euro 28.9 per cent, yen 16.8 per cent, sterling 10.2 per cent, renminbi 8.5 per cent (BIS Triennial Survey, 30 September 2025). Vehicle-currency status is more concentrated than reserve-currency status.

**COFER.** Dollar share of allocated reserves about 58 per cent in 2024; euro about 20 per cent; yen, sterling, renminbi, Canadian and Australian dollars the rest of the named pie (IMF COFER). Gold is held but not in COFER's foreign-exchange total.

**Current-account configuration.** The US deficit, and the surplus of China, Germany, Japan and oil exporters, is the post-Bretton Woods counterpart of the 1960s official-dollar problem: the reserve issuer is a net debtor in NIIP terms (IMF ESR 2026: US NIIP about $-88$ per cent of GDP). Valuation has kept the NIIP from tracking the cumulative CA one-for-one ([[international-finance/balance-of-payments]]).

**Programme history.** The IMF's large programmes — Mexico 1995, Asia 1997–98, Brazil 1998–99, Argentina 2000–01, Greece 2010, and many low-income facilities — are the system's lender-of-last-resort technology, imperfectly, in a world without a world central bank. Regional funds and swap lines (Fed swap lines in 2008 and 2020; PBOC swaps) are the other technology.

**Africa, the Middle East, Latin America.** The CFA zone's euro peg, the GCC's dollar pegs, and Latin America's mix of floats and administered rates (AREAER) are the system's geography, not a footnote. Oil exporters' $CA$ and reserve swings (Saudi Arabia, Nigeria, Norway's different institutional choice of a float-plus-fund) are how a commodity price enters the IMS.

::: intuition A reserve currency is a network
Dealers quote the dollar against everything; invoicing in dollars matches the quotes; official reserves in dollars match the invoices and the intervention need. That loop is why shares move slowly (network externalities) and why they can move (the network is not a law of nature). The euro is a second network, smaller; the renminbi is a growing onshore network with a still-gated capital account. The trilemma applies to each issuer: the United States has picked float + open capital + $i$ for the Fed, and the world has picked the dollar anyway. That combination is the post-1973 surprise that Triffin, in 1960, did not have to explain.
:::

## Where this course ends

The identities of [[international-finance/balance-of-payments]] still add up. Parity conditions of [[international-finance/exchange-rates]] and [[international-finance/ppp]] still price $E$ and $Q$. The current account is still $S-I$ and still an intertemporal choice ([[international-finance/current-account]]). Mundell–Fleming and the trilemma still organise short-run policy and regime choice ([[international-finance/mundell-fleming]], [[international-finance/exchange-rate-regimes]]). Unions and crises are still the stress tests ([[international-finance/monetary-unions]], [[international-finance/crises]]). The international monetary system is those pieces, plus the political fact of which money the rest of the world treats as a reserve. A later history will add whatever comes next; it will not repeal the identities or the trilemma.

::: history
The classical gold standard is a late-nineteenth-century construction, not a medieval survival; Britain's 1821 resumption and the 1870s spread of gold are the dating (Eichengreen, *Globalizing Capital*). Bretton Woods was negotiated in July 1944; Keynes and White are the named architects. Triffin's *Gold and the Dollar Crisis* is 1960; SDRs 1969; the Nixon shock 15 August 1971; generalised floating 1973; Jamaica 1976. The euro is 1999. The IMF's Articles have been amended seven times; the 1976 amendment is the legalisation of the float. COFER publication in something like its modern form is a 1990s–2000s product; the BIS triennial survey began in 1989 in its present coordination.
:::

::: summary
- A gold standard pegs $E$ to mint parity inside gold points and subordinates $i$ to $R$. Hume's specie flow is the goods-market adjustment; bank rate is the financial one.
- Bretton Woods was an adjustable dollar peg with controls and a gold-dollar official convertibility that Triffin showed could not both provision the world and stay credible.
- The Nixon shock ended that convertibility. Since 1973 the core floats, the dollar remains the vehicle and leading reserve currency, and the IMF is a surveillance-and-lending institution, not a world central bank.
- Triffin in a float is a safe-asset problem, not a gold-convertibility clock. Calling the system a "dollar standard" is a share-description, not a rule-description.
- The trilemma organises the history: gold as corner (1), Bretton Woods as corner (3), the core after 1973 as corner (2), the periphery as a tested mix.
- BIS 2025 turnover and IMF COFER shares are the current market-structure facts. They are not forecasts.
:::

(a) State Triffin's dilemma for 1960. (b) What is the analogue, if any, for a floating-dollar system in which the US does not convert dollars into gold? (c) Why is a falling COFER dollar *share* not by itself the 1971 clock?

(a) The world wants more dollar reserves as trade grows; more dollar claims threaten the \$35 gold pledge; protecting the pledge starves the world of reserves.
