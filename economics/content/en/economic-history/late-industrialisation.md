In 1960, South Korea’s GDP per capita sat in the same neighbourhood as Ghana’s, on Maddison’s figures. In 2018, in the Maddison Project Database 2023, Korea was at about 37,900 international dollars (2011 prices) and Ghana at a small fraction of that. China, which had a GDP per capita of about 1,510 in 1973 in the same database, was at about 13,100 in 2018. India accelerated after 1991. A handful of small open economies — Singapore, Hong Kong, Taiwan — had already joined the rich world. Sub-Saharan Africa, after a long stagnation, grew faster after about 1995. The United States and Western Europe, the golden-age catch-up having closed much of the gap, grew more slowly, then hit a financial crisis in 2008 whose world output loss was the largest since the 1930s (IMF WEO; World Bank).

This chapter is about that second great wave of catch-up — **late industrialisation** — and about the world economy it produced: a second globalisation of trade and capital, a China-sized shock to manufacturing prices and to commodity demand, and a crisis that was born in rich-country finance rather than in a gold standard. The explanations on the table are the course’s usual set: policy (export orientation versus import substitution), institutions, human capital, demography, and luck of location and of the Cold War. They will not be reduced to a single East Asian recipe.

## East Asia’s first wave

Japan’s post-war growth, already in [[economic-history/golden-age]], is the precedent. South Korea and Taiwan, from the 1960s, are the copies that were not copies. Both were Japanese colonies until 1945; both had land reforms, rising literacy, and, in the 1960s, a shift from import-substituting beginnings toward **export-oriented industrialisation**. Korea’s GDP per capita in the Maddison Project Database 2023 rises from about 1,550 in 1960 to 3,820 in 1973, 19,090 in 1995 and 37,930 in 2018. Official Korean statistics and the Penn World Table 10.01 (2017 prices, 1950–2019) tell the same qualitative story: a poor country became a high-income industrial economy in two generations.

The policy debate is old and unfinished. Amsden (1989) and Wade (1990) emphasised discipline-and-subsidy: Korean and Taiwanese states allocated cheap credit and protection *in return for* export performance, and they got steel, shipbuilding, electronics. The World Bank’s *East Asian Miracle* (1993) emphasised macroeconomic stability, education, and export orientation, and was more cautious about industrial policy’s contribution. Rodrik (1995) argued that an investment boom, coordinated by policy, came first, and that exports were how the boom paid for itself in foreign exchange. Young (1995), in a growth-accounting challenge, found that much of the tigers’ growth was factor accumulation — more capital, more labour, more education — rather than a TFP miracle. Subsequent work (Hsieh 2002; later PWT productivity) has qualified Young without returning to a pure residual miracle.

Singapore and Hong Kong are smaller, more entrepôt, and, in Hong Kong’s case, less of a developmental-state story. They matter as existence proofs of very high income in East Asia without Korea’s chaebol-and-MITI kit, and they matter as ports in the second globalisation.

::: definition Export-oriented industrialisation {#def-eoi}
**Export-oriented industrialisation** (EOI) is a strategy that uses world markets as the demand source for a growing manufacturing sector, typically with a relatively competitive exchange rate, duty-drawback or export-processing schemes, and — in the Korean and Taiwanese cases — state allocation of credit tied to export targets. It is distinct from **import-substituting industrialisation** (ISI), which uses the home market and tariffs as the demand source. The distinction is a spectrum: Korea protected the domestic market while forcing firms to export.
:::

::: proposition Accumulation can look like a miracle {#prop-young}
If $g_Y = \alpha g_K + (1-\alpha)(g_L + g_h) + g_A$, where $g_h$ is the growth of human capital per worker, a country that raises $s$, labour-force participation and schooling can have a high $g_Y$ with a modest $g_A$. Young’s (1995) claim about the tigers is that $g_A$ was not out of line with the OECD, once $g_K$ and $g_h$ were counted. The claim is about the residual, not about whether living standards rose — they did.
:::
::: proof
The identity is growth accounting. If $g_K$ is 10 per cent, $g_L + g_h$ is 3 per cent, $\alpha = 0.35$, and $g_Y$ is 8 per cent, then $g_A = 8 - 0.35\times 10 - 0.65\times 3 = 8 - 3.5 - 1.95 = 2.55$ per cent, high but not a 8 per cent residual. Raising $s$ in a Solow model raises $g_Y$ in the transition. A “miracle” that is mostly transition is still a miracle for the people who live it; it is not a permanent 8 per cent on $g_A$.
:::

::: example Korea’s compounding {#ex-korea}
Take Korean GDP per capita at 1,550 in 1960 and 37,930 in 2018 (Maddison Project Database 2023, 2011 international dollars). (a) What is the factor? (b) What constant annual rate produces that factor in 58 years? (c) At Ghana’s illustrative 1.5 per cent a year from the same start, what would Ghana have reached in 2018?
::: solution
(a) $37930/1550 \approx 24.5$. (b) $g = \ln(24.5)/58 \approx 3.20/58 \approx 0.055$, about 5.5 per cent a year per capita. (c) $1550 \times 1.015^{58} \approx 1550 \times 2.37 \approx 3,670$. The 2018 Korean figure is an order of magnitude larger. Compounding at 5.5 versus 1.5 per cent for two generations *is* the gap; the chapter’s question is why the rates differed.
:::
:::

## China, India and a second globalisation

China’s post-1978 path was the previous chapter’s close. It becomes, after 1990, a world event. Accession to the WTO in 2001, a manufacturing share of world exports that rose into the teens of per cent, and a current-account surplus that peaked above 9 per cent of Chinese GDP in 2007 (IMF WEO, 2008 vintage and later) rewired relative prices: manufactures cheaper, commodities dearer, and a “China shock” to manufacturing employment in the United States and Europe that Autor, Dorn and Hanson (2013, 2016) estimated as large and geographically concentrated. World Bank WDI (accessed 2026) put Chinese GDP per capita at about 25,200 current international dollars (PPP) in 2023, against about 74,000–82,000 for the United States depending on the PPP vintage — a remaining gap, a closed gulf relative to 1978.

India’s 1991 liberalisation — a balance-of-payments crisis, a devaluation, and a reduction of the licence raj under Rao and Singh — is a smaller, later opening. Growth of GDP per capita rose from the “Hindu rate” of about 1.5 per cent to of the order of 4–6 per cent in the 2000s (Indian national accounts; World Bank WDI). IT services, not only manufacturing, were the distinctive sector. Poverty fell; a large informal sector and a still-heavy agricultural employment share remain the structural difference from Korea at a similar income (Rodrik’s “premature deindustrialisation” is one name for the pattern).

The **second globalisation** is the post-1980, and especially post-1990, rise in trade/GDP, capital flows, and global value chains. Obstfeld and Taylor’s U-shape ([[economic-history/first-globalisation]]) has a second peak. Unlike 1870–1914, this peak has little mass migration into the rich countries as a share of their labour forces (there is migration, but not 1913-scale Atlantic settlement), floating rates rather than gold, and China as a manufacturing centre rather than a periphery. Baldwin (2016) called the new feature the unbundling of production: tasks, not only goods, trade.

::: definition China shock {#def-china-shock}
The **China shock**, in Autor, Dorn and Hanson, is the rise in import competition from China after the 1990s, identified in US local labour markets by a shift-share of industry exposure. Their estimates find substantial, persistent job losses in exposed commuting zones, with limited offsetting gains nearby. The paper is a labour-market geography, not a claim that US GDP fell by the same amount: consumers gained from cheaper goods, and exporters to China gained. The distribution of the shock is the finding.
:::

::: example A shift-share sketch {#ex-shift}
A town’s employment is 40 per cent in furniture, 10 per cent in software, 50 per cent in non-tradables. China’s share of US furniture absorption rises by 20 percentage points; software is unexposed. (a) What is the town’s exposure? (b) If each percentage point of exposure cuts local manufacturing employment by 0.3 per cent (an illustrative local elasticity, not Autor et al.’s estimate), what is the predicted manufacturing-employment effect? (c) Why might non-tradable employment fall too?
::: solution
(a) $0.40 \times 20 = 8$ percentage points of exposure. (b) $8 \times 0.3 = 2.4$ per cent of manufacturing employment. (c) Because lost manufacturing wages cut local demand for haircuts and retail — a local multiplier. Autor, Dorn and Hanson find such spillovers. National employment need not fall by the same amount if other regions expand; the local persistence is the puzzle for a spatial-equilibrium model with perfect mobility.
:::
:::

- [ ] a statistical artefact of PPP
- [ ] caused by the China shock
::: solution
Young’s growth accounting put the tigers’ residual in a normal range once accumulation was counted. Living standards still multiplied. The China shock is a 1990s–2000s US-and-Europe event, not Young’s subject.

## 2008, and the world after the golden age of catch-up

The 2008 financial crisis began in US housing and in securitised debt, spread through interbank markets, and produced a world GDP contraction in 2009 (IMF WEO: world output fell, advanced economies more than emerging). It was not a gold-standard crisis: the Fed, the ECB and others expanded their balance sheets, swapped dollars, and, in the US, the NBER dated the recession June 2009 as the trough. It *was* an international crisis: capital flows reversed, trade collapsed in 2008–09 by more than GDP (the “trade collapse”), and several European sovereigns then lost market access (Greece, Ireland, Portugal, later near-misses). Eichengreen’s 1930s gold fetters are a comparison, not a repeat: this time the large central banks were not defending a gold par.

Emerging Asia, led by China, recovered fast, in part on a 2009 Chinese fiscal-and-credit stimulus of a size that has no 1930s analogue. Latin America and Africa imported the crisis as a commodity-price and capital-flow shock and, in many cases, recovered with the 2010s commodity cycle — then struggled when it ended. The crisis is a reminder that late industrialisation does not abolish rich-country financial shocks; it changes who is at the other end of them.

World inequality of *countries’* GDP per capita, after widening from 1820 to about 1950 and remaining wide, has narrowed since the 1990s because of China and India (Milanovic; Bourguignon; the Maddison Project). Inequality *within* many countries has risen. Both statements are true; they use different distributions. The global extreme-poverty headcount, on the World Bank’s \$2.15-a-day line (2017 PPP), has fallen sharply since 1990, overwhelmingly because of China and then India and parts of East Asia (World Bank WDI, accessed 2026). Sub-Saharan Africa’s headcount remains high.

::: example 2008 is not 1929 {#ex-2008}
US real GDP fell about 30 per cent from 1929 to 1933 and of the order of 4 per cent from the 2007–08 peak to the 2009 trough (BEA; NBER). (a) What is the ratio of the two proportional falls? (b) If a Keynesian multiplier of 1.5 applied to a 4 per cent autonomous fall in spending, would you predict a 30 per cent GDP collapse? (c) What does (b) tell you about 1929–33?
::: solution
(a) $30/4 = 7.5$: the Depression’s proportional fall was seven to eight times larger. (b) No: $1.5 \times 4 = 6$ per cent, not 30. (c) That 1929–33 was not an ordinary demand shock times an ordinary multiplier. Banking collapse, the money stock, debt deflation and gold — the previous chapter — are the extra mechanisms. Calling 2008 “another Depression” is a statement about fear, not about the GDP path.
:::
:::

::: warning Convergence is not a law, and 2008 is not 1929
Conditional convergence in a Solow regression is not a promise that every poor country grows at Korean rates. Africa’s post-1995 growth, India’s post-1991 growth, and China’s post-1978 growth are three different episodes, not three draws from one $\lambda$. And 2008, terrible as it was for world output, was not a 30 per cent US GDP collapse: US real GDP fell of the order of 4 per cent peak to trough (BEA; NBER). Do not use “the Great Recession” and “the Great Depression” as synonyms.
:::

::: application Botswana, Mauritius, and the African mean {#app-africa}
Botswana (diamonds, relatively stable institutions) and Mauritius (sugar, then garments and services, a multi-ethnic parliamentary state) are African high-growth cases that the mean of sub-Saharan Africa conceals. The post-1995 African growth acceleration (Ndulu et al.; Rodrik; later IMF WEO) was real, commodity-heavy, and uneven. Treating “Africa” as a single late-industrialiser is the same mistake as treating “Asia” as Korea. The slave-trade and colonial persistence estimates of [[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]] are the background; they are not a ceiling.
:::

::: example World poverty arithmetic {#ex-poverty}
Suppose China’s population is 1.4 billion and 400 million people there move above a poverty line between 1990 and 2015, while the rest of the world sees a net fall of 200 million. (Orders of magnitude in the neighbourhood of World Bank poverty estimates, rounded for the exercise.) (a) What is the world headcount fall? (b) What share of the fall is China? (c) Why does this not imply that the World Bank line is the right welfare measure?
::: solution
(a) $400 + 200 = 600$ million. (b) $400/600 \approx 67$ per cent. (c) Because the line is an extreme-poverty threshold, not a median living standard; because PPP and survey data have errors; and because a person at 2.16 dollars a day is not well off. The arithmetic is why China dominates the *global headcount* story, not why Chinese GDP per capita is the whole of world welfare.
:::

## Where this course has arrived

The Malthusian world pinned living standards to demography. Britain escaped, slowly, then the North Sea, then the settler offshoots; the great divergence opened a gap that the first globalisation and empire reshaped but did not close. Two world wars and a depression broke the 1914 system. The golden age closed part of the OECD gap; planning industrialised at a terrible cost and then, in China, was exited into the largest catch-up on record. Late industrialisation has made the world distribution of income less bimodal than it was in 1970, without making it equal, and without repealing financial crises.

The next courses take the models this history has used — Solow, catch-up, trade, institutions — and do them properly: [[economic-growth/solow-model]], [[economic-growth/convergence]], [[economic-growth/institutions-and-growth]], [[development-economics/measuring-development]], [[international-trade/trade-facts]]. The history does not end in 2008 or in 2023. It ends, for this course, when the student can read a Maddison Project table, a PWT growth-accounting line, or a World Bank poverty headcount and say what question it answers.

::: history
**1960s, Korean and Taiwanese export take-off.** Land reform and literacy were already in place; EOI and high investment follow. **1978–2001, China.** Reform begins; WTO accession in 2001 completes a long opening. **1991, Indian liberalisation.** The licence raj is thinned after a payments crisis. **1993, World Bank *East Asian Miracle*.** The official attempt to extract a replicable recipe. **1995, Alwyn Young.** “The tyranny of numbers” in the *NBER Macroeconomics Annual* restated tiger growth as accumulation. **2008–09, global financial crisis.** World output contracts; it is not 1929. **2013, Autor, Dorn and Hanson.** The China shock as a local labour-market event.
:::

::: summary
- Korea’s GDP per capita rose by a factor of about 24 between 1960 and 2018 on Maddison Project Database 2023 figures, a constant-rate equivalent of about 5.5 per cent a year. Ghana, starting in the same neighbourhood, did not.
- Young (1995) found tiger growth to be mostly accumulation; Amsden, Wade and Rodrik found a role for industrial policy. Living standards rose either way.
- China’s post-1978 catch-up and WTO accession produced a world relative-price shock (Autor, Dorn and Hanson) and dominate the fall in global extreme poverty (World Bank).
- India’s 1991 opening raised per-capita growth without a Korean manufacturing share. Premature deindustrialisation is a live description, not a law.
- 2008 was the worst peacetime world output shock since the 1930s and was not a 30 per cent US GDP collapse. Do not conflate the two.
- Country-level incomes have converged since the 1990s because of Asia; within-country inequality has often risen. Use the distribution you mean.
:::

::: exercise EOI versus ISI {level=2}
In two paragraphs, contrast Korean EOI with Brazilian ISI in the 1960s–70s. Why might both produce fast growth for a time, and why might only one sustain it?
::: solution
Korea protected home markets but allocated credit and licences to firms that met export targets, so that the world price disciplined costs. Brazil’s ISI used the home market, high tariffs, and foreign-exchange rationing; it built auto and steel industries and ran into a foreign-exchange and debt constraint that bound in 1982. Both can raise $g_Y$ in a transition by raising $s$ and moving labour into industry.

EOI earns the dollars to import capital goods without an unsustainable current-account deficit; ISI, unless exports rise later, hits a payments ceiling. Korea’s exports rose as a share of GDP; Brazil’s debt rose. The contrast is a tendency, not a law: ISI countries that later opened (India, to a degree) are not Korea, and Korea’s 1997 crisis is a reminder that EOI does not abolish financial fragility.
:::
:::

::: exercise Autor et al. {level=2}
Why is a large local employment effect of the China shock consistent with small *national* US GDP effects of the same trade?
::: solution
Local losses can be offset, in the national accounts, by cheaper consumption goods, by profits of retailers and of exporters to China, and by employment growth in unexposed regions. If workers do not move, commuting zones bear a concentrated loss while the gains are diffuse. GDP is a national aggregate; the China shock paper is a geography of adjustment costs. Both can be right, and a student who quotes only one is answering a different question.
:::
:::

::: exercise 2008 versus 1929 {level=2}
Give three institutional differences that made 2008 less deep, in US GDP, than 1929–33, using this course.
::: solution
(a) No gold standard: the Fed could expand the base without a convertibility ceiling ([[economic-history/interwar-and-depression]]). (b) Deposit insurance and a (flawed but existing) lender of last resort, so that $c$ and $\rho$ did not explode as in 1930–33. (c) A larger government share and automatic stabilisers, plus a 2009 fiscal stimulus, so that a fall in $I$ was not allowed to multiply through to a 30 per cent GDP collapse. None of these made 2008 mild for employment in construction or for euro-area sovereigns; they made it not 1933.
:::
:::

::: exercise Derive Young’s point {level=3}
Suppose two countries have the same $g_A$ and the same $\alpha$, but country K raises $s$ so that $g_K = 10$ per cent in a transition while country G has $g_K = 4$ per cent. Labour and human capital grow at 2 per cent in both. (a) What is the gap in $g_Y$? (b) After K reaches its new Solow steady state, what happens to the gap in *growth rates*? (c) What happens to the gap in *levels*?
::: hint
Use [[#prop-young]] and the Solow distinction between transition and steady state.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\Delta g_Y = \alpha \Delta g_K = 0.35 \times 6 = 2.1$ percentage points, at the same $g_A$. (b) In the Solow steady state $g_Y$ returns to $n + g_A$ (or $n + g_h + g_A$ if human capital is included), so the growth-rate gap vanishes if $g_A$ is the same. (c) The *level* of $y$ remains higher in K because $s$ is higher. Young’s tigers can therefore look miraculous in a 30-year window (transition) without having a permanently higher residual. Whether Korea’s $g_A$ *was* the same as Ghana’s is a separate measurement; the algebra says accumulation is enough to produce a large temporary growth gap.
:::
:::

::: exercise A fair East Asian recipe {level=3}
In 400–600 words, extract what *can* be exported from Korea, Taiwan and China as policy, and what cannot (Cold War, geography, size, timing of the second globalisation). Take Young, Amsden/Wade, and the China shock as constraints on the recipe. Do not write a manifesto.
::: solution
A fair essay looks like this. What looks portable: a high investment rate, a competitive real exchange rate, education that raises $g_h$, and a state that can tax and that, if it subsidises, disciplines recipients against world prices. That package is consistent with Young (accumulation matters) and with Amsden/Wade (discipline matters). What does not look portable: the Cold War security umbrella and US market access that Korea and Taiwan enjoyed; Hong Kong’s entrepôt geography; China’s size, which makes its catch-up a world relative-price event rather than a small-open-economy event; and the 1990s–2000s window of WTO accession and global value chains, which is not 1965. A country that copies Korean credit targeting in 2020 without Korean schooling, without a competitive exchange rate, and without a market that will buy the exports, is not running the Korean experiment. The China shock, meanwhile, means that late industrialisers now compete with Chinese wages and logistics, a harder environment than Korea faced in 1970. The recipe, if any, is accumulation plus export discipline plus human capital, under a state that can implement it; it is not a five-year plan, and it is not laissez-faire. Ghana’s post-1995 growth and India’s services path are reminders that there are other ways to raise $g_Y$, none of which has yet produced a 24-fold rise in GDP per capita in 58 years.
:::
