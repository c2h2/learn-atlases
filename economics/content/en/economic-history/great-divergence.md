In 1700, the richest large economies in the world — the Dutch Republic, Britain, the Yangzi delta, parts of Japan and, on some measures, the Mughal heartland — differed in GDP per capita by a factor of perhaps two to three, not by a factor of twenty. By 1913 the United States, Britain and the settler offshoots were an order of magnitude ahead of China and India, and the gap with much of Africa and with the poorer parts of Latin America was of the same order. That opening is the **great divergence**. This chapter is about when it happened, how large it was, and which of the main explanations — institutions, geography and resources, culture, and the coal-and-wages complex of the previous chapter — can bear the weight of the numbers.

The phrase is Pomeranz’s (2000). The fact is older. Adam Smith, Malthus and Marx all took it for granted that Europe was already richer than Asia; a revisionist literature in the 1990s, associated with Pomeranz, Roy Bin Wong, Andre Gunder Frank and others, replied that the most advanced parts of China and Japan looked, in 1750, more like the most advanced parts of Europe than the older literature had allowed. Quantitative reconstructions since 2000 have narrowed the argument: they have not ended it. Levels in 1700 remain fragile ([[economic-history/malthusian-world]]); rankings in 1913 do not.

## When, and by how much

A useful distinction, due to Allen (2001) and to van Zanden and others, is between a **little divergence** inside Europe and a **great divergence** between Western Europe (plus its offshoots) and the large agrarian economies of Asia.

The little divergence is a North Sea story. Between 1500 and 1750, real wages in London and Amsterdam held up; real wages in Florence, Vienna, Gdańsk and other inland and Mediterranean cities fell (Allen 2001). Dutch GDP per capita in the Maddison Project Database 2023 is about 4,270 international dollars (2011 prices) in 1600 for Holland, against English figures of about 1,690 in 1600 and 2,410 in 1700. Italy, the richest region of Europe around 1300–1400, did not keep that lead. The little divergence is *before* the spinning jenny. It is the commercial, urban, high-wage economy that Allen then uses as a cause of the Industrial Revolution.

The great divergence is later and larger. Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) and the Maddison Project Database 2023 put Chinese GDP per capita a little above England’s around 1000 and still comparable in the fifteenth century. By 1700 the English figure is about 2,410 and the Chinese about 1,540; by 1800, 3,340 against 930; by 1913, Britain about 8,210 and China about 990, all in 2011 international dollars. India’s path, in Broadberry, Custodis and Gupta (2015), is from about 60 per cent of the British level around 1600 to about 15 per cent by 1871. Japan sits in between: about 1,070 in 1700, 1,580 in 1870, 2,430 in 1913 (Maddison Project Database 2023), already pulling away from China before the Meiji Restoration and then accelerating after it.

The settler offshoots — the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and, more ambiguously, Argentina and Uruguay — are part of the same story. US GDP per capita in the Maddison Project Database 2023 is about 2,540 in 1800, 4,800 in 1870 and 10,110 in 1913, overtaking Britain. Argentina in 1913 was among the richer economies in the same database, of the order of 6,000 international dollars, before a twentieth-century relative decline that belongs in a later chapter.

Africa and the rest of Latin America enter the comparison with thinner data. The Maddison Project’s 1820 and 1913 coverage for sub-Saharan Africa is sparse; South Africa in 1820 is about 1,190 international dollars in the 2023 database, a small settler-and-slave economy, not a continental average. Coatsworth (2008) and Prados de la Escosura (2009) put most of Spanish America well below the United States by 1820, with a gap that does not close over the nineteenth century. These are not footnotes. Any explanation of the great divergence that only talks about Britain and China is answering a smaller question than the one the map poses.

::: definition Great divergence {#def-divergence}
The **great divergence** is the persistent widening, from some point in the early modern period until the mid-twentieth century, of GDP per capita and of real wages between north-western Europe together with its settler offshoots, on the one hand, and the large agrarian economies of East and South Asia, together with much of Africa and Latin America, on the other. It is distinct from the **little divergence** inside Europe, in which the North Sea region pulled away from the Mediterranean and central Europe between about 1500 and 1750.
:::

::: example The little-divergence wage gap {#ex-little}
Allen (2001) finds that a London labourer’s welfare ratio holds up between 1500 and 1750 while a Florentine labourer’s falls. Suppose London stays at 1.6 baskets and Florence falls from 1.6 to 0.9. (a) What is the 1750 ratio of real wages? (b) In a common-$\bar w$ Malthusian model, what would you have expected? (c) Name two forces, other than measurement error, that can produce (a).
::: solution
(a) $1.6/0.9 \approx 1.78$. (b) Both cities returning toward a common $\bar w$, so a ratio near 1 in the long run. (c) A higher North Sea $\bar w$ (marriage pattern, urban demand); and a still-unfinished transition as Dutch and English $A$ and trade rose — the little divergence as a high-wage commercial economy, not as steam. Both belong in [[economic-history/industrial-revolution]] as background to Allen’s relative prices.
:::
:::

::: example A ratio over two centuries {#ex-ratio}
Take the Maddison Project Database 2023 figures cited above, in 2011 international dollars: Britain 2,410 in 1700 and 8,210 in 1913; China 1,540 in 1700 and 990 in 1913. (a) What is the Britain/China ratio in each year? (b) By what factor did British GDP per capita grow over the 213 years? (c) China’s *level* falls on these numbers. Give two reasons, other than “the numbers are wrong”, why a falling Chinese level can coexist with some technical change in Qing China.
::: solution
(a) $2410/1540 \approx 1.57$ in 1700; $8210/990 \approx 8.3$ in 1913. The ratio rises by a factor of about 5.3. (b) $8210/2410 \approx 3.4$. (c) Population grew faster than output, which is Malthusian arithmetic ([[economic-history/malthusian-world]]); and the composition of output can shift toward lower-value goods as high-value handicrafts lose out to imports, so that measured GDP per capita falls even if some new crops and some new crafts appear. A falling series is not a claim that nothing was invented in Qing China.
:::
:::

The widget’s constant rates are a sketch. The historical British path is slow then faster; the Chinese path is contested in the eighteenth century and grim in the mid-nineteenth (the Taiping war, 1850–64, was a demographic and economic catastrophe). The point of the sketch is the arithmetic of ratios: modest differences in growth rates, compounded for two centuries, produce the world of 1913.

## Four families of explanation

Four families of explanation dominate the literature. They are not mutually exclusive. They differ in what they take as exogenous, in the date at which they say the die was cast, and in how much of the 1913 gap they claim to explain.

**Institutions.** Property rights, constraints on the executive, and the state’s capacity to tax and to supply law are, in this family, the fundamental cause of long-run growth. North (1981, 1990) stated the programme. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson (2001), “The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development”, gave it its most cited empirical form: in a sample of former colonies, settler mortality is used as an instrument for colonial institutions, and institutions so instrumented explain a large share of today’s income gap. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson (2002) added a “reversal of fortune”: places that were relatively urbanised in 1500 are relatively poor today, among former colonies, which they read as evidence that extractive institutions were imposed where there were people to extract from, and inclusive institutions where Europeans settled. Engerman and Sokoloff (1997, 2002) told a related Latin American story: factor endowments (sugar, silver, a dense indigenous population) favoured inequality and extractive institutions, which then persisted.

**Geography and resources.** Diamond (1997) located the deep cause in the east–west axis of Eurasia, the available suite of domesticable plants and animals, and the disease environment. Pomeranz (2000) offered a more local geographic argument: Britain had coal at the right place, and the New World gave Western Europe “ghost acres” of land for food, fibre and timber that the Yangzi delta could not match. Sachs and others have documented a tropical penalty in late-twentieth-century data (malaria, soil, distance from markets), which may or may not have been as important in 1700. The resource argument is not “coal therefore industry” as a slogan; it is a claim about relative prices of energy and land at the moment when coal-using techniques existed.

**Culture.** Landes (1998), drawing on a long tradition that includes Weber’s Protestant ethic, emphasised work, thrift, curiosity and the status of merchants. Clark (2007), in *A Farewell to Alms*, argued that centuries of Malthusian selection in England — the rich having more surviving children — diffused “bourgeois” traits downward. Mokyr’s useful knowledge, already met in [[economic-history/industrial-revolution]], is a cultural-institutional hybrid: beliefs about nature, stored in communities of artisans and scientists. Culture is the hardest of the four families to measure, and the easiest to smuggle national stereotypes into. It is not therefore empty. Literacy, numeracy (age heaping), book production and the European marriage pattern are measurable, and they do differ across Eurasia in the centuries before 1800 (Baten and van Zanden 2008; A’Hearn, Baten and Crayen 2009).

**Wages, coal and the location of industry.** Allen’s argument, developed in the previous chapter, is a theory of *why the machines paid in Britain*. Combined with Pomeranz’s coal-and-ghost-acres geography, it becomes a theory of divergence: once Britain had the techniques, falling transport costs and further technical change spread them to other high-wage coal economies, and not, until much later, to low-wage agrarian ones. The great divergence, on this view, is the Industrial Revolution plus slow diffusion, not a deep institutional gap dating from 1500.

::: definition Inclusive and extractive institutions {#def-ajr}
Acemoglu and Robinson (2012), popularising the 2001–02 papers, call **inclusive institutions** those that secure property for a broad set of people and allow creative destruction, and **extractive institutions** those that concentrate power and rents. The labels are a classification of outcomes as well as of rules; critics (among them Austin 2008, Albouy 2012, and a large literature on African colonial history) argue that the classification is too coarse for the colonial record and that the settler-mortality instrument is fragile. The chapter uses the terms as AJR use them, and treats the debate as open.
:::

::: proposition Compounding and the required growth gap {#prop-compound}
If two economies start at a ratio $R_0$ of GDP per capita and the growth-rate gap is $\Delta g$ (in decimal terms) for $T$ years, the terminal ratio is $R_T = R_0 e^{\Delta g\, T}$. A rise in the Britain/China ratio from 1.6 to 8.3 over 213 years requires $\Delta g = \ln(8.3/1.6)/213 \approx 0.0077$, or about 0.8 percentage points a year.
:::
::: proof
$R_T / R_0 = e^{\Delta g\, T}$, so $\Delta g = \ln(R_T/R_0)/T$. Substitute $R_T/R_0 = 8.3/1.6 = 5.1875$, $T = 213$, $\ln 5.1875 \approx 1.646$, $1.646/213 \approx 0.00773$.
:::

[[#prop-compound]] is a discipline on stories. An explanation that creates a 0.2 percentage-point growth gap will not deliver 1913 from 1700 in these numbers. An explanation that creates a 2 percentage-point gap will overshoot unless it starts later or turns off. The Industrial Revolution’s measured British per-capita rate of 0.48 per cent a year from 1700 to 1870, against a Chinese rate that is near zero or negative, is in the right neighbourhood for the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries; the late nineteenth century, when British and American rates were higher and China was in political crisis, does more of the work.

## Institutions versus the rest: what the tests can show

AJR (2001) is the most influential statistical test in this literature, and it is also the most fought over. The identifying idea is that European colonisers built different institutions in different places, that the disease environment shaped settlement, and that settlement shaped institutions which then persisted. Settler mortality is not a direct cause of income in 1995, they argue, except through institutions. The second-stage coefficient on expropriation risk is large: a one-point improvement on their protection-against-expropriation index (which runs from about 4 to 10 in the sample) is associated with a large rise in 1995 GDP per capita.

The criticisms are of several kinds. Albouy (2012) argued that the mortality data are noisy and that the result is sensitive to how mortality is coded. Austin (2008), writing as an African economic historian, argued that the “compression of history” — treating 1500–2000 as one institutional regime — erases the slave trades, colonial policy changes, post-colonial states and commodity booms. Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes and Shleifer (2004) argued that the instrument may pick up human capital (settlers brought literacy) rather than institutions. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson have replied at each step; the exchange is ongoing. A student who cites “AJR 2001” as if it were a measurement of the great divergence of 1700–1913 is citing a paper about *former colonies in 1995*. China and Japan were never in the AJR colonial sample in the same way; India was, and India’s 1995 income is not 1913.

A different institutional test looks at *pre-colonial* states. Bockstette, Chanda and Putterman (2002) and Putterman and Weil (2010) find that a long history of statehood correlates with later income. That cuts against a naive “Europe had states, Asia did not” story: China had a state for two millennia. It also cuts against a naive “institutions in 1500 determine everything” story, because the content of the state — fiscal capacity, law, the treatment of merchants — varies. The European fiscal-military state of the eighteenth century, which could tax and borrow at scale (Brewer 1989; Dincecco 2011), is a more specific candidate than “having a state”.

Geography’s tests are also incomplete. Coal is where it is, and Britain’s coalfield-plus-water geography is a genuine advantage for *coal-using* techniques. It does not explain the little divergence of 1500–1750, which predates steam. Ghost acres are a genuine addition to Europe’s land endowment after 1492; they are also a story about the Caribbean, Brazil and the southern United States, which means they are a story about enslaved labour as well as about soil (we take this up in [[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]]). A tropical penalty in 1995 data does not, by itself, explain why the Yangzi delta — not tropical — fell behind Lancashire.

Culture’s tests are the most incomplete. Numeracy and literacy gaps are real. Clark’s selection argument is hard to distinguish from the rise of incomes that *caused* the rich to have more surviving children in a Malthusian world — which is the model, not a genetic extra. Weber’s Protestant ethic faces the Dutch and English lead, which is consistent, and the later industrialisation of Catholic Belgium, France and northern Italy, which is a problem if the ethic is doing the work. Mokyr’s useful knowledge is a research programme, not a completed measurement.

::: warning Do not treat 1995 as 1750
A regression of 1995 GDP per capita on colonial institutions is a statement about the late twentieth century. The great divergence is a statement about 1700–1913. Former colonies’ 1995 incomes mix the colonial period, the world wars, the golden age, planning, oil, and post-1960 policy. Japan, never colonised in the AJR sense, is rich; Spain, a coloniser, was poor by north-west European standards in 1820. Use each body of evidence for the question it was built to answer.
:::

::: example Settler mortality as an instrument {#ex-iv}
A stylised AJR regression is $\ln y = \beta I + u$, with $I$ instrumented by settler mortality $M$. Suppose the first stage is $I = 9 - 0.8 \ln M$ and the second stage is $\ln y = 6 + 0.5 I$. (Coefficients are illustrative.) (a) What is the implied reduced form $\ln y$ as a function of $\ln M$? (b) If country A had $\ln M = 2$ and country B had $\ln M = 5$, what is the predicted gap in $\ln y$? (c) Name two reasons this gap need not equal the great-divergence gap of 1700–1913.
::: solution
(a) $\ln y = 6 + 0.5(9 - 0.8\ln M) = 10.5 - 0.4\ln M$. (b) $\Delta \ln y = -0.4(2-5) = 1.2$, a factor of $e^{1.2} \approx 3.3$ in income. (c) The sample is former colonies in the late twentieth century, not all of Eurasia in 1913; and $I$ is a 1980s expropriation index, not a measure of Qing, Mughal or Tokugawa institutions. The calculation shows how an IV estimate maps into an income ratio, not that the mapping *is* the great divergence.
:::
:::

## China, India, Japan, Africa, Latin America

A fair account has to look at the large poor (or later-rich) economies on their own terms, not as a residual of British history.

**China.** The Song economy (tenth–thirteenth centuries) had extensive markets, paper money, coke-based iron in some regions, and a GDP per capita that Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) put in the same range as the richer parts of medieval Europe. The Ming and Qing empires ran a large agrarian fiscal state, not a commercial-military one on the British pattern. Pomeranz’s claim is that the Yangzi delta in 1750 was a land of dense markets, high agricultural productivity and proto-industry, comparable to England, and that the *break* was coal and the New World. Broadberry’s claim is that the GDP-per-capita lead had already moved to Britain by 1700, so that 1750 is too late a starting gun. Both can be partly right: a rich Chinese region can sit in a poorer Chinese empire, and a British lead in 1700 can still be a little-divergence lead rather than a steam lead. The nineteenth century is less ambiguous. The Opium War (1839–42), the Taiping war, and a fiscal crisis left China poorer in 1870, on the Maddison Project numbers, than in 1700. That is not a Malthusian steady state; it is a collapse.

**India.** The Mughal empire in 1600 was a large, urban, commercial economy. The eighteenth-century successor states, then Company rule (from the 1760s in Bengal, from 1858 as the Raj), coincide with the fall in India’s relative GDP per capita in Broadberry, Custodis and Gupta (2015). How much of that fall is colonial extraction, how much is the decline of the Mughal order, and how much is the world-price shock of British cotton, is the subject of a long debate (Naoroji’s drain theory; Morris D. Morris; more recently Roy, Parthasarathi, Clingingsmith and Williamson). Parthasarathi (2011) argued for competitive Indian textiles and a late, politically assisted British overtaking. Roy (2011) argued for a more gradual, market-driven change and for colonial public goods (railways, law) alongside colonial constraints. The honest summary is that the relative decline is in the data and that no single mechanism has exclusive claim to it.

**Japan.** Tokugawa Japan was closed, urbanised, and, on Bassino et al. (2019), slowly rising in GDP per capita. The Meiji Restoration of 1868 is the canonical “institutions can change” episode: a new state imported law, education, railways and mills, and GDP per capita roughly doubled between 1870 and 1913 on the Maddison Project Database 2023 figures (1,580 to 2,430). Japan is a problem for any theory in which geography or a 1500s institutional endowment is destiny. It is less of a problem for Allen: Japan industrialised with *already cheap* techniques, not with the 1770 jenny.

**Africa.** The slave trades (Atlantic, trans-Saharan, Red Sea, Indian Ocean) removed millions of people over several centuries; Nunn (2008) finds that African countries that exported more slaves, on the best available counts, have lower GDP per capita in the late twentieth century. That is a long-run institutional and demographic channel, not a 1913 national-accounts series. Colonial rule, mostly after 1880, is late in the great-divergence chronology: the divergence was already wide before the Scramble. Colonial institutions then shaped the twentieth century, for better and worse, in ways [[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]] takes up. A geography of tsetse, malaria and low population density (Austin, Hopkins, Iliffe) is part of the pre-colonial setting; it is not a verdict that “Africa had no history of markets”, which is false.

**Latin America.** Independence in the 1810s–20s did not produce a United States-style growth path. Engerman and Sokoloff emphasised inequality and extractive colonial institutions; Coatsworth and Haber, Razo and Maurer emphasised political instability and the slow build-up of fiscal and financial capacity after independence. Argentina’s 1913 richness and later relative decline show that “Latin America” is not one path. The comparison with British settler colonies — Canada, Australia — is the natural AJR experiment, and it is also a comparison of who settled, what they grew, and how indigenous populations were dispossessed. The last of these is not a side issue; it is the land endowment.

::: application The reversal of fortune {#app-reversal}
AJR (2002) report that, among former colonies, urbanisation in 1500 is *negatively* correlated with GDP per capita in 1995. The reading is that Europeans imposed extractive institutions on rich, densely peopled places (Mexico, Peru, India) and inclusive institutions on poor, sparsely peopled places (New England, Canada, Australia). Alternative readings: the 1500 urbanisation measure is better for Eurasia than for the Americas; the 1995 outcome mixes oil, the golden age and post-independence policy; and New England’s “inclusiveness” coexisted with the dispossession of indigenous peoples and, in the wider United States, with slavery. The correlation is a fact in their sample. The exclusive causal reading is a hypothesis.
:::

::: quiz
Japan’s rise from the Meiji Restoration to 1913 is most awkward for which family of explanations?
- [ ] Allen’s, because Japan used coal
- [x] theories in which a 1500s institutional or geographic endowment is destiny, because Meiji institutions and imported techniques changed the path within two generations
- [ ] Mokyr’s, because Japan had no useful knowledge

::: solution
Meiji Japan changed law, education and industrial policy and imported techniques whose costs had already fallen. That is a problem for destiny-from-1500 stories. It is consistent with Allen on *first* adoption (Britain, not Japan, in 1770) and with a knowledge story in which useful knowledge can be imported. Japan did not have New World ghost acres.

:::

::: example The required Chinese growth shortfall {#ex-shortfall}
Britain’s GDP per capita grows at 0.48 per cent a year from 1700 to 1870 (Broadberry et al. 2015). China’s GDP per capita in the Maddison Project Database 2023 is 1,540 in 1700 and 945 in 1870. (a) What is the implied annual Chinese rate? (b) What is $\Delta g$ relative to Britain? (c) Compare $\Delta g$ with the 0.8 percentage points of [[#prop-compound]] for 1700–1913, and say what must have happened after 1870 to complete the 1913 ratio.
::: solution
(a) $945/1540 \approx 0.614$ over 170 years, so $g = \ln(0.614)/170 \approx -0.0029$, about $-0.29$ per cent a year. (b) $\Delta g \approx 0.48 - (-0.29) = 0.77$ percentage points. (c) That already matches the 0.8 percentage-point gap of [[#prop-compound]] for the longer period. After 1870 Britain (and still more the United States) grew faster, while China remained near stagnation until the mid-twentieth century (1913 Chinese GDP per capita about 990). The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries *maintain* a wide ratio; they do not have to create it from scratch if the 1700–1870 gap is of this size. Uncertainty about the 1700 Chinese *level* still matters: a higher 1700 China would mean a steeper subsequent fall.
:::
:::

## Where this leads

The great divergence is the global counterpart of the Industrial Revolution. The next chapter asks how the late-nineteenth-century fall in transport costs, the gold standard and mass migration spread — and sometimes narrowed — the gap: [[economic-history/first-globalisation]]. Empire is not a separate planet; it is one of the mechanisms, and it has a chapter of its own ([[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]]). The institutional research programme continues in [[economic-growth/institutions-and-growth]] and in [[development-economics/institutions-and-governance]]. Convergence and catch-up after 1950, which reverse parts of the divergence, are the subject of [[economic-history/late-industrialisation]] and of [[economic-growth/convergence]].

::: history
**2000, Kenneth Pomeranz.** *The Great Divergence* argued that the Yangzi delta and Britain were comparable in 1750 and that coal and the New World, not a deep institutional unique, explain Britain’s lead. **2001, Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson.** “The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development” (*American Economic Review*) used settler mortality to instrument colonial institutions. **2001, Robert C. Allen.** “The great divergence in European wages and prices” (*Explorations in Economic History*) documented the little divergence inside Europe from the fourteenth century to 1914. **2015–18, Stephen Broadberry and co-authors.** Reconstructions of British, Indian and Chinese GDP placed the British lead earlier than Pomeranz’s 1750 and later than a purely medieval story.
:::

::: summary
- The little divergence (North Sea versus the rest of Europe, 1500–1750) precedes the great divergence (Western Europe and offshoots versus Asia, Africa and much of Latin America, visible by 1800 and wide by 1913).
- Maddison Project Database 2023 and Broadberry’s national accounts put the Britain/China GDP-per-capita ratio at about 1.6 in 1700 and about 8 in 1913, in 2011 international dollars. Levels are more fragile than the direction of the ratio.
- Four families of explanation — institutions, geography and resources, culture, and wages-and-coal — each fit some facts. None has exclusive claim to 1700–1913.
- AJR (2001) is a paper about former colonies in the late twentieth century, not a measurement of Qing China. Use it for the question it asks.
- Japan’s Meiji path, China’s mid-nineteenth-century collapse, India’s relative decline, African slave-trade effects and Latin American post-independence paths are part of the same problem, not residuals.
- Compounding discipline: a growth-rate gap of about 0.8 percentage points a year over two centuries is what the Britain/China ratio requires on these numbers.
:::

::: exercise Ghost acres {level=2}
Pomeranz argues that New World land supplied Europe with food, fibre and timber that China would have had to grow at home. Suppose Britain imports cotton that, grown at British yields, would have required $A$ acres, and that British agricultural land is $T$ acres. (a) In the Malthusian production function $Y = A_{\text{tech}} T^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}$, what does an effective land endowment $T+A$ do to $L^{*}$ at given $\bar w$? (b) Why is this not, by itself, an Industrial Revolution? (c) Why might it still matter for location?
::: solution
(a) $L^{*}$ scales with $T$, so ghost acres raise steady-state population in proportion to $(T+A)/T$, with no long-run rise in $\bar w$ ([[economic-history/malthusian-world#eq-Lstar]]). (b) A larger land endowment in a Malthusian model produces more people, not modern growth. (c) It can still matter if it relaxes a land constraint *during* a transition, cheapens cotton and timber for industry, or (in Pomeranz’s full argument) complements coal at a historical moment when coal-using techniques exist. Ghost acres are a candidate cause of *British* location, not a substitute for a theory of sustained TFP growth.
:::

::: exercise AJR’s sample {level=2}
Which of the following economies is *not* a good fit for the AJR (2001) colonial-settler design, and why: India, Nigeria, Argentina, China, Australia?
::: solution
China. It was never a European settler colony of the AJR type (a few ports were leased; the empire was not replaced by a colonial administration on the Indian pattern). India and Nigeria are colonies in the sample’s sense; Argentina and Australia are settler economies, with very different indigenous and slave histories. AJR’s design can speak to India versus Australia; it cannot, without extra assumptions, speak to Qing China versus Britain.
:::

::: exercise Recover $\Delta g$ from two paths {level=3}
In 1700, economy B has GDP per capita $y_B$ and economy C has $y_C = y_B / 1.6$. From 1700 to 1913, B grows at a constant $g_B = 0.011$ and C at $g_C = 0.003$. (a) What is the 1913 ratio $y_B/y_C$? (b) How does it compare with the 8.3 in the Maddison Project figures for Britain/China? (c) What $g_C$ would be needed to hit a ratio of 8.3, given $g_B = 0.011$?
::: hint
$R_T = R_0 e^{(g_B-g_C)T}$ with $T = 213$ and $R_0 = 1.6$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $R_{1913} = 1.6 \times e^{0.008 \times 213} = 1.6 \times e^{1.704} \approx 1.6 \times 5.50 \approx 8.8$. (b) A little above 8.3; the constant-rate sketch slightly overshoots the 1913 figures used in the chapter. (c) Need $\Delta g = \ln(8.3/1.6)/213 \approx 0.00773$, so $g_C = 0.011 - 0.00773 \approx 0.0033$, about 0.33 per cent a year. A China that grew at one-third of one per cent, not at a negative rate, would still have produced a ratio near 8 if Britain grew at 1.1 per cent. The 1913 gap is not, by itself, proof of Chinese collapse; the 1870 level in the Maddison Project Database 2023 is what makes the collapse reading harder to avoid.
:::

::: exercise Write a fair paragraph {level=3}
In 250–400 words, compare Pomeranz (2000) and Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) on China versus Britain. State each side’s claim about *levels* around 1700–1750, name the series each relies on, and identify one fact that would count against each side. Do not declare a winner.
::: solution
A fair paragraph looks like this. Pomeranz claims that the richest parts of China, especially the Yangzi delta, were comparable to Britain in living standards, land productivity and market development around 1750, so that the subsequent divergence cannot be attributed to a deep European institutional unique; coal at the right place and New World resources explain why Britain, not the delta, industrialised. He relies on comparative real-wage fragments, agricultural yields, and qualitative evidence on markets and proto-industry. A fact that would count against him is a well-measured unskilled welfare ratio of 1.5–2 in London against about 1 in the delta (Allen et al. 2011), or a national GDP-per-capita lead for Britain already in 1700 that survives re-basing. Broadberry, Guan and Li claim that Chinese GDP per capita was already below England’s by 1700 and that the gap widened through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; they rely on reconstructed national accounts, not on a single rich region. A fact that would count against them is a substantial upward revision of Qing output, or evidence that their English series is too high in 1700 relative to a purchasing-power benchmark. Both sides agree that 1913 is not close. The dispute is about 1700–1750 levels and about whether the cause of what follows is coal-and-land or a lead that had already been built in the little-divergence centuries. Wages, regional GDP and national GDP need not tell the same story, and a fair comparison says so.
:::
