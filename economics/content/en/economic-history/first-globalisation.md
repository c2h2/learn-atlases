Between 1870 and 1914 the world economy became, by several measures, more integrated than it had ever been and more integrated than it would be again until the 1990s. Grain prices in Liverpool and Chicago moved together; real wages in Sweden and the United States moved together; a British saver could buy a railway bond in Argentina, an Australian mortgage and a share in a gold mine on the Rand in a single afternoon in the City. The gold standard, steamships, railways, the telegraph and the Suez Canal (opened 1869) were the hardware. Mass migration — some 55 million Europeans left for the Americas and Oceania between 1840 and 1914, with the peak after 1870 — was the labour-market hardware. This chapter is about that integration, about the prices and wages it equalised, and about the politics that it produced.

O’Rourke and Williamson (1999), *Globalization and History*, is the organising text. Their claim is not that trade was new in 1870. The claim is that *transport costs fell far enough* that commodity prices and, through trade and migration, factor prices began to converge across the Atlantic in a way that the eighteenth century had not seen. The first globalisation is a relative-price event, not a patriotic one. It created winners (European workers, New World landowners) and losers (European landowners, New World workers who competed with immigrants), and those groups voted.

## Transport costs, steam and the grain invasion

The fall in transport costs had two legs: ocean and land. On the ocean, iron and then steel hulls, steam turbines, and the Suez shortcut cut the time and the real freight on bulky goods. Harley (1988) and later work compiled in Mohammed and Williamson (2004) show real ocean freight rates falling by the order of 70 per cent between the 1840s and 1913 on many Atlantic routes. On land, railways did what no canal could do for the interiors of North America, the Argentine pampas, the Ukrainian steppe and the Indian subcontinent. US railroad mileage rose from about 30,000 miles in 1860 to more than 250,000 by 1913 (Historical Statistics of the United States). The Indian railway network, built from the 1850s under British rule, exceeded 30,000 miles by 1910. Refrigeration, from the 1870s, added meat and dairy to the list of goods that could travel.

The **grain invasion** is the European name for the result. Wheat from the American Midwest, the pampas and the Black Sea arrived in Liverpool and Antwerp at prices that undercut Prussian and English landlords. O’Rourke and Williamson document the Liverpool–Chicago wheat-price gap collapsing from tens of per cent in the 1870s toward a few per cent by 1913. The same collapse appears in meat, hides and wool. In the other direction, European manufactures — Lancashire cottons, Ruhr steel, French silks — became cheaper in Buenos Aires, Melbourne and Bombay.

::: definition First globalisation {#def-first}
The **first globalisation** is the period, conventionally 1870–1914, in which a sharp fall in transport and communication costs, the gold standard, and mass migration of labour and capital produced large convergences of commodity prices and, across the Atlantic, of real wages, together with a political reaction in the form of tariffs and, eventually, immigration restrictions.
:::

::: definition Factor-price convergence {#def-fpc}
In the Heckscher–Ohlin model, trade in goods is a substitute for trade in factors: a labour-scarce, land-rich country exports land-intensive goods, which raises the real return to land and lowers the real wage, while the labour-rich partner sees the opposite. **Factor-price convergence** is the resulting tendency of wages and land rents to move toward each other across countries. Migration of labour and capital reinforces the same pattern. The theorem’s assumptions (identical technologies, two factors, no specialisation at a corner) are not literally true of 1870–1914; the *direction* of the wage and rent changes is the test.
:::

::: proposition A transport-cost bound on the price gap {#prop-gap}
If a homogeneous good can be shipped from A to B at a real cost $\tau$ per unit, then in the absence of tariffs the price gap satisfies $\abs{p_B - p_A} \le \tau$. A fall in $\tau$ shrinks the maximum gap. If both markets are competitive and the good is traded, the bound is tight up to measurement error: $p_B = p_A + \tau$ when A exports.
:::
::: proof
Suppose $p_B > p_A + \tau$. Then an arbitrager buys in A, ships, and sells in B for a sure profit, which cannot be an equilibrium. The symmetric argument rules out $p_A > p_B + \tau$. When A is the exporter, $p_B = p_A + \tau$ in a competitive equilibrium with positive trade.
:::

::: example A 70 per cent freight fall {#ex-freight}
Mohammed and Williamson (2004) find real ocean freight rates on many Atlantic routes falling by of the order of 70 per cent from the 1840s to 1913. If a 1870s shipping cost is 0.40 per bushel, what is the 1913 cost at a 70 per cent fall, and what does [[#prop-gap]] then say about the maximum price gap?
::: solution
$0.40 \times 0.30 = 0.12$ per bushel. The law of one price then binds the import–export price gap to at most 0.12, plus tariffs. That is the grain-invasion arithmetic: European landlords lose protection-by-distance.
:::
:::

::: example The Liverpool–Chicago wheat gap {#ex-wheat}
Suppose wheat is \$1.00 a bushel in Chicago and \$1.50 in Liverpool, and shipping costs \$0.40 a bushel, all in real terms. (Numbers are illustrative of a 1870s gap, not a quoted day.) (a) Is there an arbitrage? (b) After freight falls to \$0.10, what is the new bound on the Liverpool price if Chicago remains at \$1.00 and Chicago is the exporter? (c) Why might the Liverpool price fall by *less* than \$0.30?
::: solution
(a) Yes: $1.50 - 1.00 = 0.50 > 0.40$. Shipping a bushel earns \$0.10. (b) $p_{\text{Liv}} = 1.00 + 0.10 = 1.10$. (c) Chicago is not a small supplier. Extra European demand bids up the Chicago price as the gap closes, so the European price falls by less than the fall in $\tau$ and the American price rises. Both sides of the Atlantic share the gains from cheaper shipping; the incidence depends on elasticities of excess supply and demand, exactly as in the tax-incidence diagram of a principles course.
:::

## Migration, wages and the Atlantic labour market

People moved in numbers that still dwarf most later episodes as a share of the sending populations. Hatton and Williamson (1998, 2005) put European emigration at about 55 million between 1840 and 1914, with the United Kingdom, Ireland, Italy, Germany and Scandinavia as the main senders and the United States, Argentina, Canada, Brazil and Australia as the main receivers. At the peak, in the decade before 1914, more than a million people a year entered the United States. Ireland after the famine and Norway and Sweden in the 1880s lost a large share of their young adult populations. Return migration was substantial, especially among Italians; the 55 million is gross, not net.

The wage consequences are the Heckscher–Ohlin-plus-migration prediction. In the sending countries, a smaller labour force relative to land should raise real wages; in the receiving countries, the opposite. O’Rourke and Williamson find real-wage *convergence* across the Atlantic: Swedish, Irish and Norwegian real wages rose toward the American level, and the American–British gap narrowed. Taylor and Williamson (1997) attribute a large share of that convergence to migration itself, as against trade or capital flows. The result is not a claim that American wages fell in absolute terms — they did not, over the long run — but that they rose more slowly than they would have without immigration, while European wages rose faster.

Latin America was a receiving region, not a footnote. Argentina and Uruguay had some of the highest GDP-per-capita figures in the Maddison Project Database 2023 by 1913 (Argentina about 6,050 international dollars, 2011 prices), built on wheat, beef and European immigrants. Brazil’s coffee economy drew Italian and Portuguese labour after the abolition of slavery in 1888. These are Atlantic labour markets, not just a United States story.

Asia’s labour markets were linked by a different migration: Indian and Chinese indentured and contract labour to the Caribbean, South Africa, Malaya, Ceylon and the Pacific, in smaller numbers than the transatlantic European flow but with large local effects (Northrup 1995). The Atlantic and the Indian Ocean were not one labour market; they were two, with different legal regimes and different wage consequences.

::: example A simple migration arithmetic {#ex-migration}
Two regions, Old and New, have labour demand $w = a - b L$. Old starts with $L_O = 80$, New with $L_N = 20$, and $a = 10$, $b = 0.05$ in both (illustrative). (a) What are the autarky wages? (b) If 10 workers move from Old to New, what are the new wages? (c) Who gains and who loses among workers and among landowners, if land is the residual claimant in a specific-factors sense?
::: solution
(a) $w_O = 10 - 0.05\times 80 = 6$, $w_N = 10 - 0.05\times 20 = 9$. (b) $L_O = 70$, $L_N = 30$, so $w_O = 6.5$, $w_N = 8.5$. The gap falls from 3 to 2. (c) Old World workers gain, New World workers lose; Old World landowners (whose residual falls as wages rise) lose, New World landowners gain. That is the political map of 1870–1914: European labour and New World land on one side, European land and New World labour on the other.
:::
:::

The New World is land-rich and labour-scarce. Exports of grain and meat, and immigration, both lower the New World wage relative to the Old World wage (or slow its rise) and raise New World rents. European landlords faced the grain invasion; European workers were the beneficiaries.

## Capital flows and the gold standard

Capital moved with people, and in some decades more spectacularly. British net foreign assets by 1913 were of the order of 150 per cent of British GDP (estimates in Feinstein, Edelstein, and in Obstfeld and Taylor 2004). The destinations were not, in the main, poor tropical colonies. They were the settler economies and the railways of the New World and of Europe: the United States, Argentina, Australia, Canada, and also India, where railways and government bonds absorbed British savings. Edelstein (1982) and later work found that the *ex post* return on British overseas assets was a little higher than on domestic assets, not a colonial giveaway and not a free lunch.

The monetary frame was the **classical gold standard**. Britain had been on gold, in effect, since the 1820s; Germany joined in 1871–73, France and the United States (de facto, then the Gold Standard Act of 1900) in the same decades; by 1914 much of the trading world had a gold or gold-exchange standard. A par rate, convertibility, and free gold shipment produced a tight band on exchange rates — the gold points — and, in the textbook version, an automatic adjustment of prices and interest rates via Hume’s specie-flow mechanism.

::: definition Gold points {#def-goldpoints}
Under a gold standard, the **gold points** are the exchange rates at which it pays to ship gold rather than to buy a bill of exchange. If the mint par is $\bar e$ (domestic currency per foreign currency) and the cost of shipping gold is $c$ per unit, the spot rate $e$ must lie in $[\bar e - c, \bar e + c]$ or gold flows. The band is the monetary counterpart of [[#prop-gap]].
:::

The textbook adjustment is smoother than the history. Bordo and Kydland (1995) treated the gold standard as a contingent rule: convertibility could be suspended in a war, as Britain did in 1797–1821 and as everyone would do in 1914, and restored later. Obstfeld and Taylor (2004) emphasised the **trilemma**: a country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility and an independent monetary policy at once. The gold-standard world chose the first two. Interest rates in the periphery tracked London, sometimes painfully: the 1890 Baring crisis, Argentine default and the Australian depression of the 1890s are the open-economy cost of the rule. The benefit, for countries that stayed on gold, was a lower borrowing rate in London — the “good housekeeping seal of approval” of Bordo and Rockoff (1996), a claim that later work has qualified but not discarded.

China and India sat in this system as silver or gold-exchange economies, not as full members on the Bank of England’s terms. India’s shift toward a gold-exchange standard around 1898, and the rupee’s link to sterling, is a colonial monetary history as well as a globalisation history. China’s silver standard meant that the late-nineteenth-century fall in the gold price of silver was a real depreciation, which some authors (including Friedman and later monetary historians of China) treat as a stimulus to Chinese exports. The first globalisation was not a single currency club; it was a nested set of metallic and colonial pegs.

::: example Hume’s flow in a small open economy {#ex-hume}
A small country on gold has a price level $P$ and a gold stock $G$, with $P = v G$ in a quantity-theory shorthand (velocity and output fixed). World prices are $P^{*}$. Net exports are $NX = \phi(P^{*}/P - 1)$. Gold flows as $\dot G = NX$. (a) What is the steady-state $P$? (b) If $P^{*}$ falls 10 per cent (a world deflation), what happens to $G$ and to $P$ in the new steady state? (c) Why might the transition hurt more than this model says?
::: solution
(a) $\dot G = 0$ requires $P = P^{*}$. (b) $P$ must fall 10 per cent; with $P = vG$, $G$ falls 10 per cent. Gold leaves until prices have matched the world. (c) Because output and employment are not fixed in the short run, because banks contract when gold leaves, and because wages and mortgage debts are sticky in money terms. The 1873–96 “Great Deflation” in gold-standard countries is the historical counterpart: falling prices, rising real debt burdens, and a political fight over silver (the United States) and protection (Germany, France). Hume’s flow delivers the end point; it does not invoice the unemployment along the way.
:::
:::

## Winners, losers and the political reaction

Cheap grain was a gift to European workers and a blow to European landlords. The political map of the 1870s–90s is the map of that blow. Germany’s 1879 tariff, France’s Méline tariff of 1892, and the slow retreat of British landlords from the Corn Laws they had already lost in 1846 are the standard exhibits. O’Rourke (1997) estimated large losses for European landowners from the grain invasion. Sweden and Denmark moved toward processed agricultural exports (butter, bacon) rather than toward autarky in grain; the response is not unique.

In the New World the losers were workers who competed with immigrants, and the political response, with a lag, was immigration restriction: the US Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the “White Australia” policy, and, after the war, the US quota acts of 1921 and 1924. Timmer and Williamson (1998) document the association between the immigrant wage gap and restriction. The first globalisation, on this reading, produced its own brakes.

Latin America and the periphery did not vote in European parliaments, and their terms of trade are a separate series. The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis, a mid-twentieth-century claim that primary-product prices trend down against manufactures, is not a fact about every decade of 1870–1914. Blattman, Hwang and Williamson (2007) and others find that *volatility* of commodity prices, more than a common trend, is the penalty of specialisation. Argentina’s 1913 richness was real; so were its 1890s crash and its exposure to a handful of export prices.

Africa’s incorporation into the first globalisation was late and violent: the Scramble after 1880, cash crops, mines, and colonial tax systems. Hopkins (1973) and Law (several essays) described West African trade before the Scramble; the first globalisation did not create African markets from nothing. It did redirect them toward European manufactures and toward a small set of export crops, with consequences that the next chapter takes up.

East Asia’s incorporation was also political. Japan chose, after 1868, to join on Meiji terms: tariffs were constrained by the “unequal treaties” until the 1890s, then used; industrialisation was a state project as well as a market one. China was opened by war and by treaty ports, not by a Meiji Restoration, and its 1870–1913 GDP-per-capita path in the Maddison Project Database 2023 is roughly flat (about 945 in 1870, 990 in 1913). The first globalisation was an opportunity for some states and a constraint for others.

::: warning Price convergence is not income convergence
The Liverpool–Chicago wheat price can equalise while GDP per capita in Britain and India, or in the United States and Brazil, remains far apart. Factor-price convergence is a theorem about wages and rents *under shared technology*. When technologies differ — the whole point of the great divergence — goods-price equalisation does not deliver income equalisation. Do not read a chart of wheat prices as a chart of living standards.
:::

::: application The trilemma in 1907 {#app-trilemma}
The 1907 financial crisis in New York was a domestic bank run with an international gold constraint. US interest rates spiked; gold was imported; the Bank of England raised Bank Rate to keep its reserve. A country on gold that wants to stem an outflow must raise rates, even if domestic activity is already weakening. That is the trilemma, fifty years before Mundell named it. The Federal Reserve was created in 1913 in the aftermath; the gold standard was not. The next chapter’s interwar story begins when the same constraint meets a much larger shock.
:::

## Where this leads

The first globalisation is the open-economy counterpart of the great divergence: it spread cheap goods, people and capital along the gap, and it created the political coalitions that would close the borders after 1914. Empire was one of the legal frames in which this happened ([[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]]). The First World War ended the gold-standard, free-migration world, and the interwar attempt to rebuild it failed ([[economic-history/interwar-and-depression]]). The second globalisation after 1950, and especially after 1990, is a different legal and monetary animal; it appears in [[economic-history/late-industrialisation]] and in [[international-trade/trade-facts]]. The trilemma and the gold points are the history of [[international-finance]] before that course’s models.

::: history
**1869, the Suez Canal.** The canal cut the Europe–Asia sea route and is a convenient marker for the steam-and-steel transport revolution; the Atlantic freight collapse was already under way. **1879, Bismarck’s tariff.** Germany’s turn to protection on grain and iron is the political twin of the grain invasion. **1999, Kevin O’Rourke and Jeffrey Williamson.** *Globalization and History* documented Atlantic price and wage convergence in 1870–1914 and restated Heckscher–Ohlin as a historical claim. **2004, Maurice Obstfeld and Alan Taylor.** *Global Capital Markets* measured the U-shape of capital mobility over the twentieth century: high before 1914, low in mid-century, high again at the end.
:::

::: summary
- Real ocean freight rates on many Atlantic routes fell by of the order of 70 per cent from the 1840s to 1913; railways opened interiors. The law of one price then bound more tightly ([[#prop-gap]]).
- About 55 million Europeans emigrated between 1840 and 1914. Real wages converged across the Atlantic; European workers and New World landowners gained, European landowners and New World labour-competitors lost.
- British overseas assets were of the order of 150 per cent of GDP by 1913, directed mostly to settler economies and railways, not to a tropical drain.
- The classical gold standard fixed exchange rates and, with capital mobility, surrendered monetary independence (the trilemma). Adjustment was not costless.
- Price convergence is not income convergence when technologies differ. Argentina in 1913 was rich; China was not; both traded.
- Tariffs and, later, immigration restrictions were the political reaction. 1914 ended the regime.
:::

The mint par is 1 and the cost of shipping gold is 0.01 each way. How wide is the full band between the gold points, from the lower to the upper point? (Enter the width, for example 0.02.)
::: solution
The band is $[0.99, 1.01]$, width $0.02$.

::: exercise Tightening $\tau$ {level=1 check="1.15"}
Wheat in the exporting market is 1.00. Shipping costs fall from 0.40 to 0.15. If the exporter’s price is unchanged, what is the new importing-market price?
::: solution
$p_B = 1.00 + 0.15 = 1.15$.

::: exercise Migration and the wage gap {level=1}
In [[#ex-migration]], the autarky wage gap is 3 and the post-migration gap is 2. By what fraction does the gap close? Why is this not a prediction that American wages fall in absolute terms between 1870 and 1914?
::: solution
The gap closes by $1/3$. Labour demand curves can shift out with capital accumulation, technical change and land settlement, so both wages can rise while the *gap* falls. That is the Atlantic pattern: American real wages rose, European real wages rose faster.
:::

::: exercise Incidence of cheaper freight {level=2}
Exporter excess supply is $Q = 2(p_A - 1)$ and importer excess demand is $Q = 3(2 - p_B)$, with $p_B = p_A + \tau$. (a) Find $p_A$ and $p_B$ when $\tau = 0.5$. (b) Repeat for $\tau = 0.1$. (c) Who captures more of the fall in $\tau$, the exporter or the importer?
::: solution
(a) $2(p_A-1) = 3(2 - p_A - 0.5) = 3(1.5 - p_A)$. Then $2p_A - 2 = 4.5 - 3p_A$, so $5p_A = 6.5$, $p_A = 1.30$, $p_B = 1.80$. (b) $2(p_A-1) = 3(2-p_A-0.1) = 3(1.9-p_A)$. Then $2p_A-2 = 5.7-3p_A$, $5p_A = 7.7$, $p_A = 1.54$, $p_B = 1.64$. (c) The importer’s price fell from 1.80 to 1.64 ($\Delta = -0.16$); the exporter’s rose from 1.30 to 1.54 ($\Delta = +0.24$). Of the 0.40 fall in $\tau$, the exporter captured 0.24 and the importer 0.16. The more elastic side of the market captures less of the incidence — here importer demand is more elastic (slope 3 versus 2), so the importer’s price moves less. (Signs: exporter price *rises* as $\tau$ falls, which is a gain to New World landowners.)
:::

::: exercise The trilemma {level=2}
A gold-standard periphery country faces a sudden stop of capital inflows. Using the trilemma, explain in a paragraph why it cannot use a monetary expansion to offset the shock without leaving gold, and name a historical episode from the chapter.
::: solution
With the exchange rate fixed to gold and capital mobile, the domestic interest rate is tied to London’s. A sudden stop is an outward shift of the capital account; to keep gold convertibility the central bank must raise rates (or otherwise contract) to retain or attract gold, which deepens the domestic slump. Expanding the money supply would lower rates, gold would leave, and the peg would break. Argentina in 1890 (the Baring crisis) and Australia in the 1890s are the chapter’s episodes. The only way to expand is to leave gold, which the classical-era members were extremely reluctant to do.
:::
:::

::: exercise India and China in 1870–1913 {level=2}
The Maddison Project Database 2023 gives China about 945 international dollars in 1870 and 990 in 1913, and Japan 1,580 and 2,430. The first globalisation is the same world market. Why might two Asian economies have such different 1870–1913 paths? Give two mechanisms, one “opportunity” and one “constraint”.
::: solution
Opportunity: Japan’s Meiji state used the world market to import techniques, machinery and ideas, and after the 1890s used tariff autonomy to protect selected industries; China did not have a comparable fiscal-military reform. Constraint: China’s silver standard, war indemnities, treaty-port regime and political fragmentation (and the Taiping aftermath) limited the state’s ability to invest in railways and education on the Japanese scale; the same world prices that rewarded Japanese silk and cotton mills met a Chinese economy in fiscal and political crisis. Other fair pairs: human capital (Japanese numeracy and schooling), and the unequal treaties’ longer grip on Chinese tariff policy.
:::

::: exercise Derive Hume’s steady state {level=3}
In the model of [[#ex-hume]], $P = vG$ and $\dot G = \phi(P^{*}/P - 1)$. (a) Show that $P = P^{*}$ is the unique steady state for $P > 0$. (b) Linearise $\dot G$ near the steady state and show that the gold stock converges. (c) What happens to the speed of adjustment if $\phi$ is small (inelastic trade)?
::: hint
Let $G^{*} = P^{*}/v$. Then $\dot G = \phi(G^{*}/G - 1)$. Linearise in $G - G^{*}$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\dot G = 0$ iff $P^{*}/P = 1$ iff $P = P^{*}$. (b) $\dot G = \phi(G^{*}/G - 1)$. Near $G^{*}$, $G^{*}/G - 1 \approx -(G-G^{*})/G^{*}$, so $\dot G \approx -(\phi/G^{*})(G-G^{*})$. The eigenvalue $-\phi/G^{*}$ is negative, so $G$ converges. (c) Small $\phi$ means a slow eigenvalue: gold and prices adjust slowly. A less open economy, or one whose goods are imperfect substitutes for world goods, pays a longer transition for a world price shock. That is one reading of why large gold-standard countries could have long deflations in 1873–96.
:::
:::

::: exercise Wages versus rents {level=3}
A New World economy produces grain with $Y = T^{1/2} L^{1/2}$. Land $T$ is fixed at 100; labour rises from 25 to 49 because of immigration. Grain is sold at a world price $p$ that rises from 1 to 1.2 as freight falls. Labour is paid its marginal product in grain units, then valued at $p$. (a) Compute the real wage in grain units, $w/p$, at both points. (b) Compute land rents (the residual) in grain units. (c) Now value wages and rents at the world price $p$. Who gains in terms of the numeraire good (manufactures, whose price is 1)?
::: hint
$w/p = \mathrm{MPL} = (1/2)(T/L)^{1/2}$. Residual rent in grain is $Y - (\mathrm{MPL})L = (1/2)Y$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\mathrm{MPL} = 0.5 \sqrt{T/L}$. At $L=25$, $\mathrm{MPL} = 0.5 \times 2 = 1$; at $L=49$, $\mathrm{MPL} = 0.5 \times \sqrt{100/49} = 0.5 \times 10/7 \approx 0.714$. Real wages in grain fall. (b) $Y = \sqrt{T L}$. At $L=25$, $Y=50$, rent in grain $=25$; at $L=49$, $Y=70$, rent in grain $=35$. Rents in grain rise. (c) Wage bill in manufactures: $p \times w_{\text{grain}} \times L = p \times \mathrm{MPL} \times L = p \times (Y/2)$. At the first point, $1\times 25 = 25$; at the second, $1.2 \times 35 = 42$. The *wage bill* rises because $p$ and $L$ rise, even though $w/p$ falls. Rent in manufactures: $p \times 25 = 25$ then $p \times 35 = 42$. Landowners gain unambiguously in the numeraire. Workers’ *real wage in grain* falls; their real wage in manufactures is $p \times \mathrm{MPL}$, which goes from $1\times 1 = 1$ to $1.2 \times 0.714 \approx 0.86$ — also down. Immigration plus a milder rise in $p$ can leave New World workers worse off in both goods, which is why unions and restrictionist politics appear even in a booming settler economy.
:::
:::
