Between 1950 and 1973, Western Europe and Japan grew at rates that would have been fantasy in 1913 and that have not been matched, in those countries, since. Maddison’s tables, now in the Maddison Project Database 2023, put Western European GDP per capita growth at of the order of 4 per cent a year in this window, Japan’s at of the order of 8 per cent, and the United States — already the frontier — at of the order of 2.5 per cent. The period is the **golden age** of catch-up: reconstruction, the Marshall Plan, Bretton Woods, trade liberalisation inside Europe, a backlog of American techniques, and a labour force that moved out of agriculture. It ended in the 1970s, with oil shocks, inflation, and a slowdown in productivity that has a literature of its own.

This chapter is about the numbers, the institutions that framed them, and the competing accounts of why growth was so fast and why it slowed. It is not a claim that 1950–73 was golden everywhere. China’s Great Leap Forward (1958–62) and famine, India’s Hindu rate of growth, much of sub-Saharan Africa after late-colonial and early-independence commodity booms, and Latin America’s mix of fast growth and instability are part of the same calendar and a different experience. Those paths occupy this chapter’s last sections and the two chapters that follow.

## Reconstruction, Bretton Woods and catch-up

Europe in 1945 was a capital-scarce, labour-abundant, politically fragile set of economies, with a backlog of American techniques that the 1930s and the war had not fully absorbed. The Marshall Plan (1948–52) transferred about USD 13 billion (of the order of USD 150 billion in today’s prices, depending on the deflator) to Western Europe. Eichengreen (2007), *The European Economy since 1945*, treats the Plan as important less for the dollars than for the political bargain: US aid in return for intra-European trade, the OEEC, and a commitment not to return to 1930s autarky. The quantitative literature (Eichengreen and Uzan 1992; Ritschl; De Long and Eichengreen) finds a positive but not miraculous GDP effect of the transfers themselves. West Germany’s *Wirtschaftswunder*, France’s *Trente Glorieuses*, Italy’s industrial triangle, and the Scandinavian social democracies are regional versions of the same catch-up.

Bretton Woods (1944) created the IMF and the World Bank and a system of adjustable pegs to the dollar, itself convertible into gold at USD 35 an ounce for official holders. Capital controls were permitted — a different corner of the trilemma from 1914 ([[economic-history/first-globalisation]], [[economic-history/interwar-and-depression]]). Trade was liberalised on a most-favoured-nation track under the GATT (1947), with Europe going further inside the European Payments Union and then the EEC (Treaty of Rome, 1957). Japan was occupied until 1952, then grew under a US security umbrella, MITI, and a high investment rate.

::: definition Golden age (1950–73) {#def-golden}
The **golden age** is the period of unusually rapid GDP-per-capita growth in Western Europe and Japan from about 1950 to the 1973 oil shock, characterised by catch-up to the US frontier, high investment, a shift out of agriculture, stable (by later standards) inflation under Bretton Woods, and a slowdown after 1973 that was common to the OECD.
:::

::: definition Catch-up growth {#def-catchup}
**Catch-up growth** is the tendency of a country below the technological frontier to grow faster than the frontier country by adopting existing techniques, provided it has the complementary capital, skills and markets. In a simple form, $g_i = g^{*} + \lambda \ln(y^{*}/y_i)$, so that the growth gap is proportional to the log income gap. The golden age is a high-$\lambda$ episode for Western Europe and Japan; $\lambda$ is not a world constant (see [[economic-growth/convergence]]).
:::

::: proposition A backlog can raise $\lambda$ {#prop-backlog}
If war and depression prevent a follower from adopting frontier techniques for $T$ years, the log gap $\ln(y^{*}/y)$ is larger, at given $g^{*}$, than it would have been on an uninterrupted catch-up path. When adoption resumes, $g_i = g^{*} + \lambda \ln(y^{*}/y_i)$ is therefore faster. The golden age can be *partly* a delayed adoption of 1920s–40s American techniques, not a permanent rise in $\lambda$.
:::
::: proof
On an uninterrupted path the gap shrinks at rate $\lambda$. A freeze of $T$ years leaves the gap larger by about $(g^{*} - g_{\text{frozen}})T$. Restarting catch-up at that larger gap produces a temporarily higher $g_i$. As the gap closes, $g_i$ falls back toward $g^{*}$. That is a prediction of a slowdown even without oil shocks.
:::

::: example Compounding at golden-age rates {#ex-compound-ga}
Take 4.1 per cent a year for a Western European economy and 2.4 per cent for the United States, 1950–73 (23 years; rates in the neighbourhood of Maddison’s OECD figures). (a) By what factor does European GDP per capita rise? (b) US GDP per capita? (c) If Europe starts at 50 per cent of the US level in 1950, where is it in 1973?
::: solution
(a) $1.041^{23} \approx 2.52$. (b) $1.024^{23} \approx 1.73$. (c) $0.50 \times 2.52 / 1.73 \approx 0.73$. Europe closes from half the US level to about three-quarters, in this sketch. The arithmetic is catch-up: faster growth, closing gap, not overtaking.
:::
:::

## Growth accounting: capital, labour, TFP

The Solow decomposition $g_Y = \alpha g_K + (1-\alpha)g_L + g_A$ ([[economic-history/industrial-revolution]]) has been applied to the golden age by Denison, Maddison, Crafts and Toniolo, and the later Penn World Table productivity module (Feenstra, Inklaar and Timmer 2015; PWT 10.01 covers 1950–2019). Two regularities survive the revisions.

First, **TFP growth was high** in Europe and Japan, not only capital accumulation. A pure investment boom with diminishing returns would have shown falling TFP; the data show the opposite. Abramovitz and David, and later Crafts, read this as the absorption of American mass-production techniques, plus the reallocation of labour out of low-productivity agriculture. Second, **the slowdown after 1973 is in TFP as well as in capital**. Oil is a relative-price shock; it is not, on standard accounting, the whole of a TFP slowdown that lasted.

Japan’s investment rate was extraordinary: gross capital formation of the order of 30 per cent of GDP in the 1960s (historical national accounts; Maddison; PWT). The labour force moved from farms to factories; the educational attainment of the young cohorts rose. Ohkawa and Rosovsky (1973) and later Jorgenson and Nomura documented the dual: a modern sector absorbing techniques, a traditional sector shrinking. South Korea and Taiwan, still poor in 1950, were not yet in Japan’s class; their miracles are the next generation ([[economic-history/late-industrialisation]]).

::: example A European decomposition {#ex-denison}
A stylised golden-age economy has $g_Y = 5.0$ per cent, $g_K = 5.5$ per cent, $g_L = 0.8$ per cent, $\alpha = 0.35$. (a) What is $g_A$? (b) What is growth of output per worker? (c) How much of (b) is TFP?
::: solution
(a) $g_A = 5.0 - 0.35\times 5.5 - 0.65\times 0.8 = 5.0 - 1.925 - 0.52 = 2.555$, about 2.6 per cent a year. (b) $g_Y - g_L = 4.2$ per cent. (c) TFP is 2.6 percentage points of the 4.2, about 60 per cent; the rest is capital deepening $\alpha(g_K - g_L) = 0.35\times 4.7 \approx 1.6$ percentage points. High TFP, not just “they invested”, is the golden-age signature.
:::
:::

::: example A four-year Marshall transfer {#ex-marshall}
A recipient economy has GDP of 100 each year. For four years it receives 2 a year in aid, all invested, on top of its own saving. In a closed Solow arithmetic with $\alpha = 1/3$, a 2 per cent of GDP extra in $K$ raises $Y$ by about $(1/3)\times 2$ per cent if $K/Y$ is near 3 (so 2 of GDP is about 0.7 per cent of $K$). (a) Why is the level effect of the dollars small? (b) Why might the political effect still be large?
::: solution
(a) Even if every dollar is invested, four years of 2 per cent of GDP is 8 per cent of a year’s GDP added to $K$. With $K/Y \approx 3$, that is about 2.7 per cent of $K$, and with $\alpha = 1/3$ output rises of the order of 1 per cent — a level blip, not a 4 per cent growth-rate miracle. (b) Because aid was conditional on intra-European trade and on not repeating 1930s autarky, and because it eased a foreign-exchange constraint that could have cut *private* investment by more than 2 per cent of GDP. Eichengreen’s emphasis on the bargain, rather than on the dollars as capital, is this arithmetic plus politics.
:::
:::

## Why so fast? Competing accounts

Several accounts, again not exclusive.

**Catch-up and the backlog.** [[#prop-backlog]] plus a large US–Europe gap in 1950. The US was the frontier in mass production, management and energy-using techniques. Europe and Japan had the skills to copy. This account predicts a slowdown as the gap closes, which is what happened, and it predicts slower growth in the US itself, which is also what happened.

**Institutions of restraint.** Eichengreen (1996, 2007) argued that wage moderation, in return for high investment, was a cooperative equilibrium in post-war Europe: unions accepted wage restraint, firms invested, and welfare states and works councils locked in the bargain. The equilibrium unravelled in the late 1960s as tightness in labour markets raised militancy. The account fits Germany, the Nordics and parts of the Low Countries better than it fits France’s more conflictual path, and it is a complement to catch-up rather than a substitute.

**Trade and integration.** The EEC, EFTA, and GATT rounds raised intra-European trade. A larger market supports scale and a finer division of labour (the Smithian channel of [[economic-history/industrial-revolution]], now inside Europe). Frankel and Romer (1999) is a later cross-country argument that trade raises income; the historical European experiment is the 1950s–60s liberalisation after 1930s autarky. Britain, outside the EEC until 1973, grew more slowly than the Six on some measures, which is consistent with the trade story and also with Britain’s smaller gap to the frontier and its older industrial structure (Crafts 2018).

**Macropolicy and Bretton Woods.** Demand was managed, unemployment was low by later OECD standards, and the exchange-rate system avoided 1930s competitive deflation. Whether Keynesian policy *caused* the golden age, or merely did not prevent it, is disputed. The US inflation of the late 1960s, the Vietnam-and-Great-Society fiscal impulse, and the breakdown of Bretton Woods in 1971–73 (Nixon’s closing of the gold window, then generalised floating) are the same system coming apart.

::: quiz
The catch-up account of the golden age predicts that, as Europe and Japan close the gap to the United States,
- [ ] their growth rates should remain 4–8 per cent indefinitely
- [x] their growth rates should fall toward the US rate
- [ ] the United States should start growing at 8 per cent
- [ ] TFP growth in the followers should be zero, because they only accumulate capital
::: solution
$g_i = g^{*} + \lambda \ln(y^{*}/y_i)$ falls as $y_i$ rises toward $y^{*}$. High TFP in the followers is *how* catch-up happens (copying techniques raises $A$). The slowdown after 1973 is, on this view, partly a success: the gap had shrunk.
:::
:::

## The slowdown, oil and the rest of the world

Oil prices quadrupled in 1973–74 (OPEC embargo) and rose again in 1979–80 (Iranian revolution). For oil importers this was a terms-of-trade loss, a relative-price shock to energy-using capital, and a burst of inflation that, combined with the end of Bretton Woods, produced the Great Inflation and then the Volcker disinflation. Bruno and Sachs (1985) is the standard account of the supply shock. TFP slowdown, however, predates 1973 in some series and continues after oil prices fell in the 1980s, which is why a pure oil story is incomplete. Information-technology measurement, a shift toward services, and the exhaustion of catch-up are the other candidates (Griliches; Jorgenson; Crafts).

**Latin America** grew, in several countries, at respectable rates in the 1950s and 1960s under import-substituting industrialisation (ISI): Brazil, Mexico, and, more unstably, Argentina. Prebisch at ECLA supplied the intellectual case. The 1970s debt boom and the 1982 debt crisis ended that phase; the “lost decade” of the 1980s is a growth collapse, not a golden age (Edwards; Díaz Alejandro). ISI is a policy, not a synonym for failure: the same decades produced a steel and auto industry in Brazil and a fiscal-external constraint that later bound.

**India** after 1947 ran a planned, licence-raj economy with a “Hindu rate of growth” of about 3.5 per cent a year in GDP, or about 1.5 per cent per capita, until the 1980s (Indian national accounts; Maddison Project). That is not 1950s Japan. It is faster than the colonial late nineteenth century on Broadberry, Custodis and Gupta’s path, and it is slow relative to East Asia. The 1991 liberalisation belongs in [[economic-history/late-industrialisation]].

**Africa.** Some colonies and new states had fast growth in the 1950s–60s on commodity booms (cocoa, copper, coffee). From the 1970s, a long stagnation in much of sub-Saharan Africa — the subject of a large literature on institutions, commodity prices, conflict and policy (Ndulu et al.; Easterly and Levine; the later recovery after 1995) — means that 1950–73 was not a golden age on a continental average. Ghana versus South Korea, both poor around 1960, is the cliché comparison and still a useful one: similar starting GDP per capita on Maddison’s figures, incomparable 1990s outcomes.

**China** in this period is a planned economy, with a famine of extraordinary scale in 1959–61 (see [[economic-history/planned-economies]]). It is not a golden-age observation.

::: warning Do not average the OECD and call it the world
A 4 per cent Western European rate is a fact about Western Europe. World GDP per capita grew more slowly, because China, India and much of Africa and Latin America were large in population and slower in growth. The golden age is a regional episode with a name that has stuck. Use the regional series.
:::

::: example Ghana and Korea around 1960 {#ex-ghana}
Maddison’s 1990-Geary–Khamis figures, still widely quoted for this comparison, put South Korea and Ghana in the same neighbourhood of GDP per capita around 1960 (of the order of 1,000–1,300 1990 dollars). By 2018, in the Maddison Project Database 2023 (2011 international dollars), South Korea is about 37,900 and a representative West African figure is an order of magnitude lower (Ghana is several thousand). (a) If Korea grew at 6 per cent a year per capita and Ghana at 1.5 per cent for 50 years, what is the ratio of their incomes after 50 years, starting from equality? (b) What does the arithmetic *not* explain?
::: solution
(a) $(1.06/1.015)^{50} \approx 1.044^{50} \approx 8.7$. A factor of about nine from growth-rate gaps alone. (b) Why the rates differed: policy (Korea’s export-oriented industrialisation versus Ghana’s post-Nkrumah instability and later recovery), institutions, education, the Cold War security umbrella, commodity dependence, and the rest of this course. The arithmetic is compounding, not a theory of Ghana or of Korea.
:::
:::

::: application Bretton Woods as a trilemma choice {#app-bw}
Unlike the classical gold standard, Bretton Woods allowed capital controls. Countries could peg to the dollar and still run a domestic monetary policy, up to a point. The point was reached when US inflation and the US current-account position made the gold-dollar peg incredible. The 1971–73 collapse is the trilemma reasserting itself once capital became harder to bottle up. The golden age’s monetary frame was a political construction, not a return to 1914.
:::

## Where this leads

The golden age is catch-up under a particular international order. Planned economies tried a different order, with a different growth path and a different failure ([[economic-history/planned-economies]]). Late industrialisers in East Asia, and then China and India, are a second wave of catch-up, in a floating-rate, high-capital-mobility world ([[economic-history/late-industrialisation]]). The Solow model and convergence regressions are the theory in [[economic-growth/solow-model]] and [[economic-growth/convergence]].

::: history
**1944, Bretton Woods.** The IMF, the World Bank, and dollar-gold pegs. **1947, GATT.** A multilateral track for tariff cuts, distinct from the EEC’s deeper integration. **1948–52, Marshall Plan.** About USD 13 billion in official aid to Western Europe, tied to intra-European trade. **1957, Treaty of Rome.** The EEC Six. **1971–73, end of Bretton Woods.** Nixon closes the gold window in August 1971; generalised floating follows. **1973, first oil shock.** The conventional end of the golden age.
:::

::: summary
- Western Europe grew at of the order of 4 per cent a year per capita from 1950 to 1973, Japan at of the order of 8 per cent, the US at of the order of 2.5 per cent (Maddison Project / Maddison). The gap closed; it did not reverse.
- High TFP, not only investment, is the growth-accounting signature. Reallocation out of agriculture and a backlog of American techniques are the leading interpretations.
- Catch-up arithmetic ([[#prop-backlog]]) predicts a slowdown as the gap closes. Oil, inflation and the end of Bretton Woods are additional 1970s shocks.
- Bretton Woods chose pegs plus capital controls, a different trilemma corner from 1914, and collapsed when the dollar-gold peg became incredible.
- 1950–73 was not golden in China (famine), India (slow per-capita growth), or much of Africa. Latin American ISI produced growth and a later debt crisis.
- Ghana versus Korea around 1960 is compounding plus policy, not a geography joke.
:::

At 4.1 per cent a year, by what factor does GDP per capita rise in 23 years? (Enter $1.041^{23}$ rounded to two decimal places.)

::: exercise TFP residual {level=1 check="1.8"}
$g_Y = 4.0$, $g_K = 5.0$, $g_L = 1.0$, $\alpha = 0.3$. What is $g_A$ in per cent per year? (Enter one decimal place.)
::: solution
$4.0 - 0.3\times 5.0 - 0.7\times 1.0 = 4.0 - 1.5 - 0.7 = 1.8$.

::: exercise Closing a gap {level=1}
Europe starts at 50 per cent of US GDP per capita, grows at 4.1 per cent, the US at 2.4 per cent, for 23 years. What share of the US level does Europe reach? (Use [[#ex-compound-ga]].)
::: solution
$0.50 \times 1.041^{23} / 1.024^{23} \approx 0.50 \times 2.52 / 1.73 \approx 0.73$, about 73 per cent.
:::

::: exercise Why $\lambda$ might fall {level=2}
Give two reasons, one mechanical and one institutional, why $\lambda$ in $g = g^{*} + \lambda \ln(y^{*}/y)$ might have been higher in 1955 than in 1985 for France or Japan.
::: solution
Mechanical: the 1955 gap was larger (war destruction, delayed adoption), so even a constant $\lambda$ produces faster growth; after 1973 the remaining gap was smaller. (If the question is $\lambda$ itself, the mechanical point is that a *backlog* of discrete techniques, once copied, is gone — $\lambda$ falls because the easy copies have been made.) Institutional: Eichengreen’s wage-moderation bargain was intact in 1955 and under strain by the late 1960s; Bretton Woods provided a stable relative-price frame that floating and inflation removed. Either is a reason the golden-age coefficient need not apply to 1985.
:::
:::

::: exercise Marshall Plan {level=2}
The Marshall Plan was about 2 per cent of recipients’ GDP a year for a few years, in round numbers. In a Solow model with a saving rate raised by 2 percentage points for four years, would you expect a permanent 2 per cent rise in the *growth rate*? Why or why not?
::: solution
No. A transfer raises the level of $K$ (or allows more $C$ without cutting $I$). In Solow, a higher $s$ raises the *level* of $k$ and $y$ on the balanced path, and raises growth only in the transition. Four years of 2 per cent of GDP is a level effect of a few per cent of $K$, not a permanent 2 per cent on $g_Y$. The Plan’s advocates (and Eichengreen) therefore stress the political-economy and trade effects, not a Solow miracle from the dollars alone.
:::
:::

::: exercise Oil and TFP {level=2}
Why is a 1973–74 oil-price jump not, by itself, a complete explanation of OECD TFP growth remaining lower in the 1980s, after real oil prices had fallen?
::: solution
A relative-price shock can scrap energy-intensive capital and lower measured TFP for a time. Once oil prices fall, that mechanism should reverse or fade. A TFP slowdown that lasts into the 1980s and 1990s (in Europe more than in the US, on some measures) needs something else: exhaustion of catch-up, a shift to services, measurement of IT, labour-market institutions, or a slower rate of frontier innovation. Bruno and Sachs explain the 1970s; they do not, without help, explain 1990.
:::
:::

::: exercise Derive the catch-up path {level=3}
Let $g_t = g^{*} + \lambda \ln(y^{*}/y_t)$ and $g_t = \mathrm{d}\ln y_t / \mathrm{d}t$, with $y^{*}$ growing at $g^{*}$. (a) Show that the log gap $d_t = \ln(y^{*}/y_t)$ obeys $\dot d = -\lambda d$. (b) Solve for $d_t$. (c) What does this imply for the half-life of the gap if $\lambda = 0.02$ per year?
::: hint
$\dot d = g^{*} - g_t$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\dot d = g^{*} - g_t = g^{*} - \bigl(g^{*} + \lambda d\bigr) = -\lambda d$. (b) $d_t = d_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. (c) Half-life $t$ satisfies $e^{-\lambda t} = 1/2$, so $t = \ln 2 / \lambda \approx 0.69/0.02 \approx 35$ years. A $\lambda$ of 2 per cent a year, a ballpark Barro-style rate, closes half the gap in a generation. Golden-age Europe closed faster than that, which is a high-$\lambda$ (or a backlog) observation.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write the rest of the world in {level=3}
In 300–500 words, explain why a student who knows only the West German *Wirtschaftswunder* has not understood 1950–73 as world economic history. Use India, China, Ghana or Brazil, and the Maddison Project, and distinguish level, growth rate and population weight.
::: solution
A fair answer looks like this. West German growth of several per cent a year per capita, from a 1950 level already far above India’s or China’s, is a catch-up to the US with a large TFP residual. In the same years India grew at about 1.5 per cent per capita — a positive rate, a low one — and China ran a planned industrialisation interrupted by a famine that killed tens of millions and shows up as a hole in GDP. Sub-Saharan Africa’s average is pulled by commodity cycles and, later, a long stagnation; Ghana’s comparison with Korea is the cautionary compounding tale. Brazil grew fast under ISI and then hit a debt wall in 1982. World *average* GDP per capita is a population-weighted mean: China and India together were (and are) more than a third of world population, so their slow per-capita growth in 1950–73 drags the world average far below the OECD golden-age rate. A student who quotes “the world grew at 4 per cent” is quoting Europe. The golden age is real, regional, and named by the countries that had it. The rest of the world is not a residual of the Marshall Plan; it is most of the people.
:::
