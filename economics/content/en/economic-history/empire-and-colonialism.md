Empire is not a side chapter in the history of the modern world economy. The Atlantic slave trades, the colonisation of the Americas, Company and then Crown rule in India, the Scramble for Africa, and the treaty-port regime in China were among the ways labour, land and fiscal capacity were reallocated between 1500 and 1960. This chapter asks what those reallocations did — to the colonies, to the metropoles, and to the long-run distribution of income — and treats the answers as empirical, not as slogans.

Three distinctions keep the subject from collapsing into a single story. First, **settler colonies** (Canada, Australia, New Zealand, the northern United States) are not the same object as **extractive or plantation colonies** (the Caribbean sugar islands, the Belgian Congo, much of Spanish America) or as **peasant-export colonies** (much of West Africa, Burma, parts of India). Engerman and Sokoloff, AJR, and Africanists such as Hopkins and Austin have all insisted on the distinction; they disagree about what follows from it. Second, **the slave trades** are not the same as nineteenth-century colonial rule: they overlap in the Caribbean and in the American South, and they do not overlap for most of the Scramble, which came after the Atlantic trade had been made illegal. Third, **costs and benefits to the metropole** are not the same as costs and benefits to the colony. A drain from India can be small as a share of British GDP and large as a share of Indian tax revenue. Both statements can be true.

The chapter is for education, not for a verdict on the present politics of empire. The numbers are often worse than the slogans, in both directions.

## The slave trades

The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (Eltis, Richardson and collaborators; estimates current as of the 2010s releases) puts about 12.5 million Africans embarked on Atlantic slaving voyages between the sixteenth century and the 1860s, and about 10.7 million disembarked in the Americas. The difference is the middle passage. Brazil and the Caribbean took the majority; the mainland North American colonies took a minority of the disembarked, and their slave population then grew by natural increase, unlike the sugar islands, which for long periods did not. Trans-Saharan, Red Sea and Indian Ocean trades, over a longer span, added further millions; the totals are more uncertain (Lovejoy; Austen).

The American side of the trade is a labour-supply shock to plantation agriculture: sugar, then coffee, cotton and rice. Cotton from the US South fed Lancashire ([[economic-history/industrial-revolution]]); sugar from the Caribbean fed European calories and a complex of shipping, insurance and processing in Bristol, Bordeaux, Nantes and Liverpool. Williams (1944), *Capitalism and Slavery*, argued that slave-based profits financed British industry. The quantitative literature since (Eltis and Engerman 2000; Harley; the Crafts tradition) has found that the slave-related Atlantic was important to particular ports and sectors and a small share of British GDP and of industrial investment. A small share can still matter at the margin for a leading sector; it is not a demonstration that the Industrial Revolution “was” slavery. The honest position is the conjunction: the cotton economy of 1800 was entangled with enslaved labour, and the aggregate growth-accounting residual of [[economic-history/industrial-revolution]] is not a slave residual.

The African side is a demographic and institutional shock. Nunn (2008) matched shipping records to ethnic homelands, constructed estimates of slaves exported by modern African country, and found a negative correlation with late-twentieth-century GDP per capita, robust to various controls and to an instrument based on distance to slave-demand centres. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011) added a trust channel: descendants of more heavily raided groups report lower trust in survey data. The papers are not a complete history of African poverty — colonial rule, post-colonial policy, geography and the commodity lottery all sit in the residual — and they are the leading quantitative claim that the slave trades still show up in incomes.

::: definition Slave-trade intensity {#def-nunn}
**Slave-trade intensity**, in Nunn’s usage, is the estimated number of people exported in the Atlantic, trans-Saharan, Red Sea and Indian Ocean trades, scaled by land area or by historical population, for a modern African country. The research hypothesis is that higher intensity caused worse subsequent institutions and lower income, through depopulation, political fragmentation and a rise in raiding.
:::

::: example A demographic hole {#ex-hole}
Suppose a region of 4 million people loses 20,000 people a year to slave exports for a century, and that natural increase would otherwise have been 0.5 per cent a year. (Numbers are illustrative of an order of magnitude, not a particular kingdom.) (a) In a closed Malthusian model with no exports, what is the population after 100 years from a start of 4 million? (b) With the export drain, treat net growth as $0.005 - 20000/P$ and compute, roughly, the population at which net growth is zero. (c) Why is this *not* Nunn’s regression?
::: solution
(a) $4 \times 10^{6} \times e^{0.5} \approx 6.6$ million. (b) $0.005 P = 20000$ gives $P = 4$ million. The drain can pin population at the starting level while a neighbour grows. (c) Nunn’s outcome is GDP per capita in the late twentieth century, not population in 1800, and his channel runs through institutions and trust as well as through missing people. A Malthusian drain that lowered population *could* have *raised* wages in the short run ([[economic-history/malthusian-world]]); the finding that former heavy-export regions are poorer today is therefore not Malthusian arithmetic, and needs the institutional channel (or a human-capital channel, or both).
:::
:::

Abolition is part of the same history. Britain’s slave trade was made illegal in 1807, slavery in the empire in 1833 (with a transition and compensation to owners); the United States abolished the trade in 1808 and slavery in 1865; Brazil abolished slavery in 1888; Cuba in the 1880s. Compensation to British owners, financed by a gilt issue, has been estimated at £20 million, about 5 per cent of British GDP at the time (Draper and the Legacies of British Slave-Ownership project). The freed people received no equivalent transfer. Sugar production shifted toward Cuba, Brazil and, later, beet in Europe. The end of the Atlantic trade did not end coerced labour: indenture from India and China, and colonial forced-labour regimes in parts of Africa, continued into the twentieth century.

## What empire did to the metropole

Davis and Huttenback (1986), *Mammon and the Pursuit of Empire*, tried to write a balance sheet of the British empire for the late nineteenth century. Their finding, contested in detail and robust in direction, is that the empire was not a spectacular profit centre for Britain as a whole: defence and administration were costly, the extra returns on colonial capital were modest, and the distribution of any surplus favoured some City interests and some manufacturers against the British taxpayer. Offer (1993) and others have argued that the strategic and food-security value of empire does not show up in a peacetime rate of return. O’Brien (1988) concluded that the extra profit from empire was small relative to British GDP. None of this is a claim that empire was “free” for the colonised. It is a claim about British national accounts.

India is the hardest case, because it was large. Dadabhai Naoroji’s **drain theory** (1870s–1900s) counted Home Charges, private remittances and unequal trade as a transfer from India to Britain. Modern restatements (Mukherjee; a critical survey in Roy 2019) put the measurable fiscal drain in the low percentages of Indian national income — not 0, not 20. Railways, the telegraph, a unified legal and postal system, and the canal colonies of the Punjab are the public-goods side of the same ledger (Donaldson 2018 estimates large welfare gains from Indian railways as a goods-market integration). The two sides do not cancel in a single number. A railway that raises Indian GDP can coexist with a fiscal transfer and with a tariff policy that favoured Lancashire. Clingingsmith and Williamson’s deindustrialisation arithmetic ([[economic-history/industrial-revolution]]) is a world-price shock as well as a colonial-policy shock.

France, the Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, Belgium, Germany, Italy, Japan and Russia ran empires of different sizes and types. The Dutch Cultivation System in Java (1830–1870) extracted a large share of Javanese agricultural surplus (Fasseur; van Zanden and Marks). King Leopold’s Congo is an extreme of private extractive violence, not a typical colony; it is also not a myth. Japanese rule in Korea and Taiwan built factories, schools and a state, and did so in a coercive frame; later Korean and Taiwanese growth cannot be read as a simple colonial gift or a simple colonial curse (Kim; Booth; the later literature on East Asian developmental states belongs in [[economic-history/late-industrialisation]]).

::: proposition A small metropolitan gain can be a large colonial loss {#prop-scale}
Let $Y_M$ and $Y_C$ be metropolitan and colonial GDP, and let $D$ be a transfer from colony to metropole. Then $D/Y_M$ can be small while $D/Y_C$ is large, whenever $Y_C \ll Y_M$. A drain that is 1 per cent of British GDP in 1913 is several per cent of Indian GDP if Indian GDP is a fraction of British.
:::
::: proof
$D/Y_C = (D/Y_M)\times(Y_M/Y_C)$. Maddison Project Database 2023 levels imply a UK/India GDP-per-capita ratio of several to one in 1913, and India’s population was several times Britain’s, so the two GDP totals are closer than the per-capita figures; even so, a transfer that is a rounding error in London can be a visible share of the Indian central budget. The algebra is an accounting identity, not a measurement of $D$.
:::

::: example Home Charges in a stylised budget {#ex-drain}
Suppose Indian national income is 100, the colonial government raises 8 in taxes, and Home Charges plus sterling remittances of officials are 1. (Illustrative shares, in the neighbourhood of orders of magnitude discussed by Roy and by the drain literature, not a particular year.) (a) What share of national income is the transfer? (b) What share of tax revenue? (c) If the same 1 is 0.4 per cent of British GDP, what does [[#prop-scale]] say about the two political conversations?
::: solution
(a) 1 per cent of Indian national income. (b) $1/8 = 12.5$ per cent of tax revenue. (c) In Britain the item is lost in the national accounts and visible, if at all, as a line in the Indian budget debate; in India it is a large slice of the state’s resources. Both percentages are true of the same 1. Arguing from only $D/Y_M$ or only $D/Y_C$ is the mistake [[#prop-scale]] is there to prevent.
:::
:::

## Colonial institutions, from the Andes to the Gold Coast

Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson (2001, 2002), already met in [[economic-history/great-divergence]], classified colonial institutions as inclusive or extractive and traced them to settlement. Engerman and Sokoloff (1997, 2002) classified New World economies by factor endowments: sugar and a slave labour force (the Caribbean, Brazil) produced inequality and restricted political institutions; small-scale grain and a settler labour force (the northern United States, Canada) produced the opposite; mineral-and-*encomienda* Spanish America sat in between. Banerjee and Iyer (2005) compared Indian districts under landlord (*zamindari*) and non-landlord colonial land systems and found lower agricultural productivity and public goods, later, in the landlord districts. Dell (2010) used a regression discontinuity at the boundary of the colonial *mita* in Peru and found persistent negative effects on living standards.

African colonial rule is late (mostly after 1880) and internally diverse. The Gold Coast’s cocoa farmers (Hill 1963) are a peasant-export story: African producers responded to prices, and the colonial state taxed trade. The Belgian Congo’s rubber, and later copper, are an extractive-enclave story. Settler colonies in Algeria, Kenya and Southern Rhodesia reserved land and wrote labour laws for a white minority. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012) constructed real-wage series for British African cities in the colonial period and found levels that, at some dates and places, compared with the poorer parts of Asia — a reminder that “colonial Africa” is not a single wage.

Latin America’s colonial period is earlier and longer. Independence in the 1810s–20s did not reset factor endowments. The *mita*, the silver of Potosí, sugar in the northeast of Brazil, and the hacienda are the usual exhibits of extractive origins; the recent literature (Coatsworth; Haber; Bruhn and Gallego 2012) has added political instability after independence and the slow build-up of fiscal capacity as proximate causes of the nineteenth-century gap with the United States. Argentina’s 1913 richness ([[economic-history/first-globalisation]]) sits awkwardly in any story that treats “Latin America” as uniformly extractive from 1530 to 1913.

::: definition Extractive colonial institution {#def-extractive}
An **extractive colonial institution**, in the AJR–Engerman–Sokoloff sense, is a set of rules that concentrates political power and land (or mineral) rents in a small elite, typically a colonial or creole elite, and that limits the labour and schooling of the rest of the population. Plantation slavery, the *mita*, and concessionary companies are examples. The term is a type, not a measurement of every colony in every decade.
:::

::: warning Persistence is not destiny
A significant coefficient on a 1700s institution in a 2000s income regression is evidence of *average* persistence in a sample. It is not a claim that no colony can change (Meiji Japan was not a colony, but South Korea and Taiwan were), nor a claim that every poor country is poor “because of” a particular colonial statute. Post-colonial policy, world prices, education and public health move incomes, as [[economic-history/late-industrialisation]] documents. Treat persistence estimates as one input, not as a closed history.
:::

::: quiz
Banerjee and Iyer (2005) compared Indian districts with different colonial land-revenue systems. Their finding is evidence for
- [ ] a uniform British drain from all of India
- [x] lasting effects of *particular* colonial institutions (landlord versus non-landlord) *within* India
- [ ] the irrelevance of geography, because all Indian districts share a monsoon
- [ ] AJR’s settler-mortality instrument, which they use
::: solution
The paper is a within-India comparison of land systems. It supports the idea that the *type* of colonial institution matters, and it does not measure a drain, does not eliminate geography, and does not use settler mortality.
:::
:::

## Empires as trade policy and as violence

Colonies were in the metropolitan tariff system, the shipping system and the monetary system. Imperial preference, navigation acts (before the mid-nineteenth century), and the rupee’s link to sterling are trade and monetary policy. The counterfactual — would India have industrialised on Japanese lines with tariff autonomy in 1870? — is not identified. Japan had tariff autonomy only after the 1890s and had a Meiji state; British India had railways, a customs service and no infant-industry policy on the Japanese pattern. Roy’s scepticism and Parthasarathi’s more political account are the two poles; the chapter in [[economic-history/industrial-revolution]] already refused to pick one as the whole story.

Violence is not an externality to be mentioned and dropped. The Herero and Nama genocide (1904–08), the Maji Maji war, the Amritsar massacre (1919), the Putumayo rubber atrocities, and the famines of British India (the 1870s, 1890s, 1943) are part of the economic history because they destroyed labour, livestock and fiscal capacity, and because the *response* to famine is a test of the colonial state’s priorities (Sen 1981 on entitlements; the debate on 1943 in Sen, O’Gráda, and later work). A growth rate that averages through a famine is a statement about survivors and about later years. It is not a humanitarian audit.

Decolonisation after 1945, and the earlier independence of Latin America, close the formal empires. The economic paths that follow — import-substituting industrialisation in Latin America and India, African post-colonial growth and stagnation, East Asian catch-up — are the subject of later chapters. Formal independence is a change of fiscal and trade regime, not an automatic change of GDP per capita.

::: example The metropolitan rate of return {#ex-return}
A metropolitan saver can earn 4 per cent at home and 5 per cent on a colonial railway bond, on an illustrative £100. Empire defence attributed to that colony costs the metropolitan taxpayer £0.30 a year per £100 of colonial capital. (Numbers are illustrative of the Davis–Huttenback question, not a particular issue.) (a) What is the private extra return? (b) What is the social extra return after the defence cost? (c) Why might the City still lobby for empire?
::: solution
(a) 1 percentage point, or £1 a year. (b) $1 - 0.30 = £0.70$, still positive in this sketch, but much smaller; if defence costs were attributed more heavily, the social return could turn negative while the private return stayed positive. (c) Because the extra return is concentrated among bondholders and contractors, while the defence cost is spread across taxpayers. A small (or negative) social return is compatible with a strong political demand for empire. That is the distributional content of the metropolitan balance sheet.
:::
:::

::: application The Scramble as a late shock {#app-scramble}
The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 and the subsequent partition of Africa come *after* the great divergence is already wide and *after* the Atlantic slave trade has been made illegal. Explanations of African poverty that begin in 1880 miss the slave trades; explanations that end in 1880 miss colonial borders, cash-crop taxation, missions and mines. Nunn’s slave-export intensity and Michalopoulos and Papaioannou’s (2016) work on partitioned ethnic groups are attempts to keep both shocks in view. Neither paper claims to have the whole of the twentieth century in one coefficient.
:::

::: example A plantation staple and inequality {#ex-staple}
A small Caribbean island produces sugar with a Leontief technology: one unit of sugar requires 1 unit of land and 4 units of labour. World sugar prices double. Land is owned by 5 per cent of households; labour is supplied by the rest, with a reservation wage in subsistence plots. (a) Who captures the windfall if extra labour is elastically available at the reservation wage? (b) How does the answer change if the labour force is enslaved and the reservation wage does not apply? (c) Why do Engerman and Sokoloff care about (a) and (b) for later institutions?
::: solution
(a) The land rent absorbs the windfall; wages stay at the reservation level. Inequality rises. (b) The owner of the enslaved labour force captures both the labour residual and the land rent; the enslaved do not have a reservation wage in the market sense. Inequality is still more extreme. (c) Because a sugar boom that concentrates rents in a small elite produces a politics of restricted franchise, limited schooling and cheap labour, which can persist after emancipation as a low-education, high-inequality equilibrium. That is their New World hypothesis, not a proof that every sugar island followed it in every decade.
:::
:::

## Where this leads

Empire shaped the first globalisation ([[economic-history/first-globalisation]]) and the great divergence ([[economic-history/great-divergence]]); it is not a substitute for either. The interwar breakdown of the open economy, and the Second World War, end several empires and exhaust others ([[economic-history/interwar-and-depression]], [[economic-history/golden-age]]). Development economics inherits the colonial map of institutions, borders and human capital ([[development-economics/institutions-and-governance]]). Late industrialisers that were colonies (Korea, Taiwan, India, Ghana) and those that were not (Japan, China, Thailand) are the natural comparison in [[economic-history/late-industrialisation]].

::: history
**1807, abolition of the British slave trade.** Parliament prohibited British subjects from participating in the Atlantic slave trade; enforcement and other countries’ abolition stretched through the nineteenth century. **1884–85, Berlin Conference.** European powers set rules for the partition of Africa; the Scramble was already under way. **1944, Eric Williams.** *Capitalism and Slavery* tied the Atlantic slave economy to British industrialisation; the quantitative debate with it is still the starting point for the metropolitan-benefit question. **2008, Nathan Nunn.** “The Long-Term Effects of Africa’s Slave Trades” (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*) gave the African-side question its standard statistical form.
:::

::: summary
- About 12.5 million people were embarked in the Atlantic slave trade (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database). Nunn (2008) finds that African countries that exported more slaves are poorer in the late twentieth century; the channel is institutional and demographic, not a Malthusian wage boom.
- Metropolitan profits from empire were, on Davis and Huttenback and O’Brien, small as a share of British GDP; the same transfer can be large as a share of colonial tax revenue ([[#prop-scale]]).
- Settler, plantation and peasant-export colonies are different objects. AJR, Engerman–Sokoloff, Banerjee–Iyer and Dell identify particular institutions, not “colonialism” as a single treatment.
- Indian railways raised welfare as market integration (Donaldson 2018); the drain, Lancashire-oriented trade policy and famines belong on the same ledger. The ledger does not sum to one slogan.
- Persistence estimates are averages. Korea, Taiwan and Botswana are reminders that colonial origins do not freeze 2000s incomes.
- Violence, famine and forced labour are part of the economic record, not an ethical appendix.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Scale of a transfer {level=1 check="5"}
A transfer $D$ is 1 per cent of metropolitan GDP. Colonial GDP is 20 per cent of metropolitan GDP. What per cent of colonial GDP is the transfer? (Enter the number, without a per-cent sign.)
::: solution
By [[#prop-scale]], $D/Y_C = 0.01 / 0.20 = 0.05$, or 5 per cent.

::: exercise Atlantic numbers {level=1}
The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database estimates about 12.5 million embarked and 10.7 million disembarked. What is the implied middle-passage death toll in millions, and why is this *not* the total demographic cost to Africa?
::: solution
$12.5 - 10.7 = 1.8$ million deaths on the middle passage. The total demographic cost also includes deaths in capture and on the African coast, lost births, the other slave trades, and the political fragmentation that Nunn emphasises. The 1.8 million is a shipping residual, not a full counterfactual population.
:::
:::

::: exercise Who is in AJR’s sample {level=1}
Which of these is a settler colony in the AJR–Engerman–Sokoloff sense, and which is a plantation colony: Barbados, Canada, the Gold Coast, Massachusetts Bay?
::: solution
Canada and Massachusetts Bay are settler (grain, European labour, temperate). Barbados is plantation (sugar, enslaved African labour). The Gold Coast is a peasant-export colony in the later literature, not a settler colony and not a sugar island; cocoa was grown by African farmers.
:::
:::

::: exercise Williams and the residual {level=2}
Suppose slave-related Atlantic activities are 4 per cent of British GDP in 1800 and have the same TFP growth as the rest of the economy. (a) What do they contribute to *aggregate* TFP growth? (b) Why might Williams still be right about *cotton* even if (a) is small? (c) What evidence would you want next?
::: solution
(a) Their contribution to aggregate TFP growth equals 4 per cent of whatever the common TFP rate is — small if the 4 per cent share is small. (b) Cotton’s *share* was small but its TFP growth was not ([[economic-history/industrial-revolution]]); if enslaved labour made raw cotton cheap, it lowered a key input price for the leading sector, which is a relative-price channel, not a GDP-share channel. (c) The price of raw cotton with and without the slave South (a tough counterfactual), the share of cotton in British exports and in the leading-sector contribution, and the use of slave-trade profits in industrial investment (the original Williams claim, which Eltis and Engerman find is small).
:::
:::

::: exercise Banerjee and Iyer {level=2}
Landlord districts in colonial India had, in Banerjee and Iyer (2005), worse later public goods. Give one institutional channel and one identification worry.
::: solution
Channel: landlord (zamindari) systems concentrated land rights and political power, weakening the demand for mass schooling and irrigation that peasant-proprietor districts supplied. Identification worry: the British may have imposed landlord systems in already more stratified or more densely settled areas, so that pre-colonial inequality, not the land law, explains later public goods. The authors use the fact that the system often followed the date of conquest (an administrative rule of thumb) as a quasi-experiment; whether that rule is as good as random is the debate.
:::
:::

::: exercise Railways versus the drain {level=2}
Donaldson (2018) finds large welfare gains from Indian railways. Naoroji counted a drain. In a paragraph, explain how both can be true, using [[#prop-scale]] and the distinction between level and growth.
::: solution
Railways raise the *level* of real income by cutting transport costs and integrating goods markets — a social saving, in Fogel’s sense, that Donaldson estimates as large. A drain is a *transfer* of part of that income (or of tax revenue) to the metropole. A bigger pie from which a slice is remitted can leave Indians better off than with neither railways nor drain, worse off than with railways and no drain, and still exploited in Naoroji’s sense. Growth rates compound the level effect of railways; they do not make the transfer disappear. The two calculations answer different questions, and adding them into a single “was the Raj good?” index is a category error.
:::
:::

::: exercise Nunn’s regression and a confound {level=3}
Nunn (2008) regresses late-twentieth-century log GDP per capita on slave-export intensity. (a) Why is OLS vulnerable to reverse causation? (b) Nunn instruments with distance to slave-demand centres. What must be true of distance for the instrument to be valid? (c) Name a channel other than “institutions” through which historical slave exports could still affect 2000s income, even if the instrument is valid.
::: hint
For (a), think about which African societies were most involved in selling slaves. For (b), the exclusion restriction.
:::
::: solution
(a) Societies that were already more fragmented, or more engaged in raiding, may have supplied more slaves *and* had worse later institutions; OLS then blames the trade for what predates it. (b) Distance to the demand centres (the Atlantic coast, the Sahara routes, the Red Sea) must affect 2000s income only through slave exports, not through, for example, distance to world markets today, climate, or coastal location. Nunn includes controls for these; the exclusion restriction remains the controversial part. (c) Human capital: the trades selected on age and sex, and destroyed schooling and skills; ethnic fractionalisation; the location of later colonial borders. A valid IV still leaves the *mechanism* open; it does not, by itself, prove the trust channel of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011).
:::
:::

::: exercise A counterfactual India {level=3}
In 400–600 words, sketch a counterfactual India that had tariff autonomy and a Meiji-style state from 1870, and say which of the following you would expect to change a lot, a little, or not at all: (i) the world price of Lancashire cotton, (ii) Indian handloom employment in 1900, (iii) Indian GDP per capita in 1913, (iv) the 1943 Bengal famine. Ground the sketch in Clingingsmith and Williamson, Roy, and the Meiji comparison. Do not claim certainty.
::: solution
A fair sketch looks like this. A Meiji-style Indian state with tariff autonomy would have been able to tax Lancashire cloth and to subsidise Bombay mills, as Japan did after the 1890s. Clingingsmith and Williamson’s world-price shock would not disappear: British TFP growth in cotton was real, and ocean freight was falling anyway, so (i) the world price of Lancashire cotton changes little (India is not a large enough importer, in this period, to set that price). (ii) Handloom employment in 1900 would still have fallen, because the price shock is a productivity-and-freight shock, but mills in Bombay and Ahmedabad might have taken a larger share earlier, so the *composition* of Indian textile employment changes more than the fact of a handloom decline. Roy’s continuity of crafts would still be in the picture. (iii) Indian GDP per capita in 1913 is the hard one. Japan went from 1,580 to 2,430 international dollars (Maddison Project Database 2023) between 1870 and 1913; India did not. A Meiji India might have closed part of that gap — railways were already being built; the missing pieces are tariff policy, education, and a fiscal state oriented to industry — but Indian agriculture, caste, and regional fragmentation are not Japanese, and a student who writes “India would have been Japan” is not using the comparison. A modest upward revision of 1913 Indian GDP per capita is a defensible guess; a doubling is not. (iv) The 1943 famine is a wartime entitlements disaster (Sen) in a still-colonial fiscal and shipping system. A sovereign Indian state in 1943 might have managed grain movements and priorities differently; it might also have been at war. The famine is not a simple corollary of 1870 tariff policy. The point of the exercise is to separate world prices (hard to change) from domestic industrial policy (changeable) from wartime food policy (a different instrument).
:::
:::
