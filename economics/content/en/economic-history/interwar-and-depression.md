The First World War ended the first globalisation as a going concern. Four years of industrialised killing destroyed capital, killed and maimed millions of workers, turned empires into successor states, and replaced the gold standard with a tangle of debts, reparations and paper currencies. An attempt to put the pre-1914 world back together — Genoa 1922, Britain’s return to gold in 1925, the Dawes and Young plans — produced a brief, uneven boom and then, from 1929, the deepest peacetime collapse of output, prices and trade in the history of the modern economy. This chapter is about that sequence, and about the three families of explanation of the Great Depression that still organise the literature: spending (Keynes), money (Friedman and Schwartz), and the gold standard as an international constraint (Eichengreen, Temin).

The facts are not in serious dispute. US real GDP fell by about 30 per cent between 1929 and 1933; US unemployment reached a quarter of the labour force in 1933 (Lebergott; later revisions by Darby and by Romer do not make the episode ordinary). US industrial production fell by nearly half. World trade collapsed: the volume fell of the order of 25 per cent, the value by more, as prices fell and as tariffs rose (Kindleberger 1973; League of Nations yearbooks). Germany, which had already had a hyperinflation in 1923, went through a second catastrophe of unemployment and politics. Britain, which left gold in 1931, had a shallower slump. Countries that stayed on gold longer did worse (Eichengreen and Sachs 1985). Recovery, when it came, tracked the leaving of gold more closely than it tracked any single domestic spending programme.

## War, debts and the 1920s

The war’s economic legacy had three pieces. First, **destruction and demobilisation**: France’s northern departments, Belgium, Poland, and the Italian front had lost capital; every belligerent had to reabsorb soldiers. Second, **public debt**: Britain’s national debt rose from about 25 per cent of GDP in 1914 to about 130 per cent in the early 1920s (Reinhart and Rogoff’s historical series; UK debt statistics). Third, **reparations and inter-allied debts**: the Treaty of Versailles (1919) levied reparations on Germany; Britain and France owed the United States. Keynes’s *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) argued that the reparations were unpayable in real goods without a German export surplus that the recipients would not accept. The 1920s were an attempt to recycle those claims: American lending to Germany, German reparations to France and Britain, allied debt service to the United States. When US lending stopped, the circuit broke.

Germany’s hyperinflation of 1922–23 is the extreme monetary counterpart. Prices rose by a factor of $10^{12}$ in the last year of the inflation (Bresciani-Turroni 1931; Cagan 1956; later reconstructions by Webb and by Ferguson). The inflation destroyed mark-denominated debts, including much of the domestic public debt, and destroyed the savings of the German middle class. The Rentenmark and then the Reichsmark (1923–24) restored a unit of account. The episode is a quantity-theory laboratory and a political scar. It is not the Great Depression; 1929–33 in Germany was a deflationary collapse, not a second hyperinflation.

Britain returned to gold in April 1925 at the pre-war par of \$4.86 to the pound. Keynes, in *The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill* (1925), argued that the pound was overvalued by of the order of 10 per cent, which would require deflation of British wages and prices. Unemployment in Britain remained high through the 1920s, especially in the old export trades. Whether 1925 “caused” that unemployment is still disputed (a real overvaluation versus a more stubborn labour-market and structural story in coal, cotton and shipbuilding). The gold constraint is not disputed: once at \$4.86, the Bank of England had to keep rates high enough to hold gold.

::: definition Debt deflation {#def-fisher}
Fisher (1933) called **debt deflation** the rise in the real burden of nominally fixed debts when the price level falls. Borrowers cut spending, some default, banks’ assets sour, and the money multiplier contracts. The mechanism turns a fall in $P$ into a fall in $Y$, rather than into a restoring rise in real balances.
:::

::: definition Gold-standard trilemma (interwar) {#def-trilemma}
The **trilemma** of open-economy macroeconomics says that a country cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility and an independent monetary policy. Interwar gold-standard members chose the peg and, to a large extent, capital mobility, and therefore could not expand money when domestic activity collapsed — unless they left gold.
:::

::: example An overvalued par {#ex-par}
Suppose British prices have risen 10 per cent relative to US prices since 1914, and the government restores the 1914 gold par. (a) What must happen to British prices, if US prices are given, to restore purchasing-power parity? (b) If money wages are sticky, what happens to British unemployment in the meantime? (c) Why is this not a full account of 1920s Britain?
::: solution
(a) British prices must fall about 10 per cent. (b) Real wages rise, employment in tradables falls, unemployment rises. (c) Because British unemployment was already high before 1925, because coal and cotton had structural problems, and because the 10 per cent figure is a Keynes estimate, not a consensus measurement. The exercise is the logic of an overvalued peg, not a complete labour-market history.
:::
:::

## The slump, 1929–33

The US peak is conventionally 1929. Industrial production and real GDP turned down; the stock market crashed in October (the crash is a symptom and a wealth shock, not a sufficient cause of a 30 per cent GDP collapse). Banking panics followed in waves, 1930–33. Friedman and Schwartz (1963), *A Monetary History of the United States*, documented a fall in the US money stock of about one-third, and argued that the Federal Reserve failed as a lender of last resort: it allowed banks to fail, the deposit-currency ratio to collapse, and the money multiplier to contract. The 1930 failure of the Bank of United States (a New York bank, not the central bank) is their exhibit of a preventable shock.

Keynes’s *General Theory* (1936) recast the slump as a failure of aggregate demand: a collapse of investment, a rise in liquidity preference, and a wage-and-price mechanism that did not restore full employment. In the IS–LM translation (Hicks 1937), the economy sat in a region where monetary expansion might be weak (a liquidity trap) and fiscal expansion would work. The New Deal mixed relief, public works, the NRA’s cartelisation, and, in 1933, the leaving of gold. Which pieces did the work is a live dispute (Cole and Ohanian 2004 on the NRA; Romer 1992 on monetary expansion after gold; Fishback on relief).

Eichengreen (1992), *Golden Fetters*, and Temin (1989) put the gold standard at the centre of the *international* slump. Gold-standard countries imported US deflation; they raised rates to defend gold when they should have cut them; they transmitted the shock through the gold points and through capital flight. The evidence is the cross-country pattern: countries that left gold earlier (Britain 1931, the sterling area, then the US 1933, then the gold bloc in 1936) recovered earlier (Eichengreen and Sachs 1985; Campa 1990 for Latin America). The gold-standard account does not deny that US money and US spending collapsed; it says that the international monetary system turned a US shock into a world depression and blocked the obvious policy response.

::: proposition Leaving gold and recovery {#prop-gold}
If two gold-standard economies are identical except that one leaves gold at date $t_1$ and the other at $t_2 > t_1$, and if leaving gold allows a depreciation and a monetary expansion, then industrial production in the early leaver should recover sooner. The prediction is about *timing*, not about the size of any one public-works programme.
:::
::: proof
Under [[#def-trilemma]], the peg-and-capital-mobility pair rules out monetary expansion. Leaving gold relaxes the constraint. A depreciation raises the relative price of tradables and, with sticky wages, raises employment in those sectors; a monetary expansion, once gold no longer binds, can raise demand. The country that relaxes first gets those channels first. Eichengreen and Sachs (1985) is the empirical counterpart: a cross-section of 1930s depreciations and industrial-production recoveries.
:::

::: example A money-multiplier collapse {#ex-multiplier}
The money stock is $M = m B$, where $B$ is the monetary base and $m = (1+c)/(c+\rho)$ is the multiplier, with $c$ the public’s currency-deposit ratio and $\rho$ the banks’ reserve-deposit ratio. (a) If $c$ rises from 0.1 to 0.3 and $\rho$ rises from 0.1 to 0.2, by what factor does $m$ fall? (b) If the central bank keeps $B$ constant, what happens to $M$? (c) What would a Friedman–Schwartz lender of last resort have done?
::: solution
(a) Initially $m = 1.1/0.2 = 5.5$. Then $m = 1.3/0.5 = 2.6$. The multiplier falls by a factor $2.6/5.5 \approx 0.47$, more than half. (b) $M$ falls by the same factor. (c) Raised $B$ enough to offset the fall in $m$, and lent to solvent but illiquid banks so that $\rho$ and $c$ did not rise as far. Whether the 1930s Fed *could* have done this under gold is the Eichengreen qualification: a large rise in $B$ might have threatened convertibility until gold was abandoned.
:::
:::

Smoot–Hawley (1930) raised US tariffs; retaliation followed. Irwin (1998, 2011) finds that the tariff was a contractionary shock to trade but not, on its own, large enough to explain a 30 per cent GDP collapse. Kindleberger’s (1973) hegemonic-stability reading — that Britain could no longer lend as a last resort and the United States would not yet — is a story about the missing public good of international liquidity. It sits comfortably with Eichengreen’s gold-standard mechanics.

Outside the North Atlantic the slump was a commodity-price crash. Latin American primary exporters (Chilean nitrates and copper, Brazilian coffee, Argentine wheat and beef) lost export earnings; several defaulted on sovereign debt and left gold early, then recovered earlier (Campa 1990; Díaz Alejandro). Japan left gold in 1931, depreciated, and expanded militarily and industrially under Takahashi until 1936. China, on silver, experienced the slump as a US silver-purchase shock in 1934–35 as much as a gold-standard shock. India, in the sterling area after 1931, imported Britain’s depreciation. Africa’s colonial cash-crop economies saw price collapses; the political consequences include the 1930s labour and tax protests that African historiography treats as a turning point.

- [ ] had deeper slumps, because depreciation imported inflation

- [ ] all left in 1929
::: solution
The cross-country pattern is early leavers, earlier recovery. Britain 1931, the US 1933, the gold bloc 1936 is the ordering. It is the central fact of the gold-standard account.
:::

## Competing accounts, fairly stated

A fair chapter does not pick a winner by adjective. The three accounts emphasise different constraints, and they are partly nested.

Friedman and Schwartz are strongest on the US money stock, the bank failures, and the Fed’s passivity. They are weaker, without an international amendment, on why the slump was worldwide and why recovery lined up with leaving gold. Keynes is strongest on the failure of wage cuts to restore employment, on investment collapse, and on the case for demand. He is weaker as a *cross-country* prediction unless one adds why some countries could expand demand and others could not. Eichengreen is strongest on that cross-country prediction and on the trilemma. He is not a denial of US bank failures or of a collapse in US investment; gold is the constraint on the *response* and the mechanism of *transmission*.

Cole and Ohanian (2004), in a real-business-cycle reading, argued that New Deal cartelisation (the NRA, later labour policy) delayed US recovery after 1933 by keeping real wages too high. Romer (1992) argued that monetary expansion, once gold no longer bound, explains most of the US recovery. The two papers can both be partly true: output can be below a counterfactual because of cartelisation *and* rising because of money. The 1937–38 US recession, which followed a monetary and fiscal tightening, is a piece of evidence for the demand side.

::: warning The stock-market crash is not the Depression
The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell by a large multiple from 1929 to 1932. Households’ direct stock holdings were concentrated, and the wealth effect on consumption, though real, is not 30 per cent of GDP. Banking failures, the money stock, debt deflation (Fisher 1933), and the gold constraint are the mechanisms that turn a crash and a downturn into a depression. A lecture that stops in October 1929 has not explained 1933.
:::

::: example Debt deflation in a stylised balance sheet {#ex-fisher}
A farmer owes 100 in a mortgage, grows output worth 100 at 1929 prices, and faces a 20 per cent fall in the price of wheat. The mortgage is fixed in money. (a) What is the real debt burden after the price fall? (b) Why might the farmer cut spending by more than the fall in current income? (c) How does a gold-standard deflation make Fisher’s mechanism international?
::: solution
(a) Real debt rises by a factor $1/0.8 = 1.25$, a 25 per cent increase. (b) Because net worth has fallen, the farmer may be near default, credit is rationed, and precautionary saving rises. (c) Gold-standard countries import the deflation of gold prices; their debtors, in money terms, are in the farmer’s position at once. Fisher’s 1933 paper is a closed-economy mechanism; the gold standard is how it travels.
:::
:::

::: application Germany 1930–32 {#app-bruning}
Chancellor Brüning’s deflationary policy — wage cuts, fiscal tightening, defence of gold — is the political extreme of the trilemma. German unemployment rose to about 30 per cent of the labour force (official figures; the exact rate depends on the denominator). Whether an earlier leaving of gold was politically available, given the 1923 memory, is a historical question; that staying on gold was contractionary is the economic one. The Nazi vote rose with unemployment (a large literature, including King, Rosen, Tanner and Wagner 2008). The chapter records the correlation; it does not treat it as a sufficient political history.
:::

## Where this leads

The Depression ended the first globalisation’s monetary and commercial regime. Reconstruction, Bretton Woods and the post-war golden age are an attempt to keep trade open without gold’s trilemma in its 1930s form ([[economic-history/golden-age]]). Planned economies drew part of their political licence from the slump ([[economic-history/planned-economies]]). The same trilemma and the same lender-of-last-resort problem reappear in 2008, in a different monetary system ([[economic-history/late-industrialisation]]). Money, inflation and IS–LM are the theory in [[macroeconomics/money-and-inflation]] and [[macroeconomics/is-lm]]; the open-economy version is [[international-finance]].

::: history
**1925, Britain’s return to gold.** Winston Churchill, as Chancellor, restored the pre-war par; Keynes’s pamphlet against the decision is *The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill*. **1929–33, the US slump.** Real GDP fell about 30 per cent; the money stock fell about one-third (Friedman and Schwartz 1963). **1931, Britain leaves gold.** Sterling depreciated; the sterling area followed. **1933, the United States leaves gold.** Roosevelt suspended convertibility in April; the dollar was devalued to \$35 an ounce in 1934. **1936, Keynes’s *General Theory*.** The slump’s demand-side book, published as recovery was already under way in the early leavers of gold.
:::

::: summary
- US real GDP fell about 30 per cent from 1929 to 1933; unemployment reached about a quarter of the labour force. World trade collapsed in volume and still more in value.
- Three accounts — Keynes (spending), Friedman and Schwartz (money and the Fed), Eichengreen and Temin (gold as constraint and transmission) — are partly nested, not mutually exclusive.
- The cross-country pattern of recovery tracks the date of leaving gold (Eichengreen and Sachs 1985). That is the gold account’s strongest fact.
- Hyperinflation in Germany in 1923 and deflation in 1929–33 are different catastrophes. The first destroyed a currency; the second was a gold-standard collapse of demand.
- Smoot–Hawley was contractionary and not large enough, on Irwin’s estimates, to be the main cause. Commodity exporters in Latin America, Asia and Africa imported the slump as a terms-of-trade crash.
- The stock-market crash is a chapter of the story, not the story.
:::

::: exercise Nested explanations {level=2}
In two paragraphs, explain how Friedman–Schwartz and Eichengreen can both be right about 1929–33, and what fact would count against each.
::: solution
Friedman and Schwartz document a US money-stock collapse and a Fed that did not offset a falling multiplier. That can be the US shock. Eichengreen adds that the gold standard transmitted the shock and prevented the Fed (and other central banks) from expanding $B$ as a last resort until gold was abandoned. The two are nested: gold is the constraint on the Friedman–Schwartz remedy, and US money is the shock that gold then spreads.

A fact against Friedman–Schwartz as a *complete* international account would be a country that kept its money stock up, stayed on gold, and still collapsed as far as the US — or a recovery that lined up with gold-leaving rather than with $M$. A fact against Eichengreen as a complete US account would be a US recovery that began while the US was still on gold, or a money-stock path that did not collapse. The 1933 date, when the US left gold and recovery accelerated, is the overlap of the two stories.
:::
:::

::: exercise Latin America {level=2}
Why might a Chilean copper exporter have left gold earlier than France, and recovered earlier, even without a Keynesian public-works programme? Use Campa (1990) and the trilemma.
::: solution
A copper-price collapse produces a current-account deficit and gold outflow. Under the trilemma the central bank must contract to stay on gold, which deepens the slump. Leaving gold allows a depreciation that raises the local-currency price of copper, cushions the fiscal accounts, and frees monetary policy. Campa (1990) finds that Latin American leavers recovered earlier. Public works are not required for this channel; the exchange rate is the instrument. France, in the gold bloc until 1936, chose the other corner of the trilemma and recovered later.
:::
:::

::: exercise Smoot–Hawley {level=2}
Irwin argues that Smoot–Hawley did not cause the Depression. What *would* a tariff of that size have to do, in a simple Keynesian or gold-standard accounting, to explain a 30 per cent fall in US GDP? Why is that implausible?
::: solution
US trade was a small share of GDP (imports of the order of 5 per cent). Even a large percentage fall in import volumes, and a similar fall in exports via retaliation, is a few percentage points of GDP in a Keynesian arithmetic with a modest multiplier — not 30 per cent. In a gold-standard accounting, a tariff can attract gold and *raise* the domestic money stock, which is the wrong sign for a slump (the beggar-thy-neighbour effect is on foreigners). The tariff was a shock to trade and to partners; it was not the 30 per cent. Banking, money, debt deflation and gold remain the large mechanisms.
:::
:::

::: exercise Offset the multiplier {level=3}
In [[#ex-multiplier]], $m$ falls from 5.5 to 2.6. (a) By what factor must the base $B$ rise to keep $M$ constant? (b) If gold cover requires $B \le G/\theta$ with $\theta = 0.4$ and $G$ fixed, can the Fed always do (a)? (c) Relate the answer to “golden fetters”.
::: hint
$M = mB$. Gold cover is a ceiling on $B$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $B$ must rise by $5.5/2.6 \approx 2.12$, a doubling plus a bit. (b) Not if $B$ is already near $G/\theta$. A doubling of $B$ at fixed $G$ would breach a 40 per cent cover. (c) That is Eichengreen’s fetter: the last-resort expansion Friedman and Schwartz wanted was, at some dates, incompatible with convertibility. Leaving gold removes the ceiling. The 1933 US suspension is, on this arithmetic, a licence to raise $B$.
:::
:::

::: example A 1931 choice {#ex-1931}
Britain leaves gold in September 1931; France stays until 1936. Sketch, in three sentences, the trilemma for each in 1932.
::: solution
Britain has a floating (managed) pound, so it can cut Bank Rate without an immediate gold drain; capital mobility is imperfect but the peg no longer binds. France has a gold peg and, with mobile capital, must keep rates high enough to hold gold as the sterling area depreciates against the franc. French tradables lose competitiveness; British tradables gain. The 1932 pair is [[#prop-gold]] in real time.
:::
:::

::: exercise Timing versus depth {level=3}
Eichengreen and Sachs (1985) correlate *depreciation* with *recovery* of industrial production. (a) Why is this not the same as correlating “having a Keynesian Treasury” with recovery? (b) What confound would worry you? (c) Why is the British 1931–33 recovery, which was not a New Deal, a useful data point?
::: solution
(a) Leaving gold is a monetary-and-exchange-rate event; a Keynesian Treasury is a fiscal event. Some leavers had no New Deal. (b) Countries that were going to recover for other reasons (better terms of trade, smaller bank failures) might have been more willing to leave gold; reverse causation and omitted shocks. (c) Britain left gold, did not run a US-scale New Deal, and recovered earlier than the gold bloc. That observation fits gold-leaving as a sufficient instrument for recovery *relative to staying on gold*, without requiring the NRA. It does not prove that fiscal policy never matters; it proves that gold was a binding constraint.
:::
