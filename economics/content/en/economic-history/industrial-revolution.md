Sometime between the 1760s and the 1830s, in a medium-sized island already rich by world standards, a cluster of industries — cotton spinning, iron smelting, steam power — began to raise output per worker in a way that did not peter out. Contemporaries noticed smoke, mills and unrest. Historians named the episode the **Industrial Revolution**, a phrase popularised by Arnold Toynbee’s lectures, published in 1884. Quantitative work since the 1980s has made the episode both smaller and more interesting: smaller, because Crafts and Harley showed that the whole-economy growth rate was modest; more interesting, because a modest rate, sustained, is exactly what the Malthusian world had not delivered.

This chapter asks three questions. What, in the numbers, actually happened to British output, productivity and living standards between about 1700 and 1870? Why did the new techniques pay in Britain and not, in the first instance, in France, the Yangzi delta or Gujarat? And how should we weigh coal and high wages, institutions, science and “useful knowledge”, and demand, against one another? The answers are not a verdict on national character. They are a set of measured facts and a set of competing mechanisms, each of which fits some of the facts.

## What grew, and how fast

The textbook image is a take-off: cotton, coal, iron and steam, and a nation transformed in a generation. The national accounts say otherwise.

Crafts (1985) and Crafts and Harley (1992) revised the older Deane and Cole estimates downward. Broadberry, Campbell, Klein, Overton and van Leeuwen (2015), working from the output side, find British GDP growth of the order of 0.5 per cent a year in the early eighteenth century, rising to about 1.5 per cent a year by the early nineteenth, with GDP per capita growth of about 0.48 per cent a year over 1700–1870 as a whole. Antras and Voth (2003), using dual (price-based) productivity measures, and Crafts and Harley agree that **total factor productivity** in the whole economy grew slowly until the second quarter of the nineteenth century. Cotton spinning and iron were spectacular; they were not, in 1800, a large enough share of GDP to haul the aggregate along at modern rates.

::: definition Industrial Revolution as a growth episode {#def-ir}
In this course the **Industrial Revolution** is the onset, in Britain between about 1760 and 1830, of *sustained* rises in GDP per capita and in total factor productivity that were not reversed by population growth. It is not a claim that most people worked in factories by 1830, nor that the growth rate of GDP was already 2 per cent a year. Factory employment, steam horsepower and modern growth rates came later; the break with the Malthusian regime came here.
:::

Two sectoral facts organise the rest of the chapter.

First, **cotton**. Hargreaves’s spinning jenny (patented 1770, invented in the 1760s), Arkwright’s water frame (1769) and Crompton’s mule (1779) raised labour productivity in spinning by a large multiple. Harley (1998) and Chapman and Butt in the Crafts–Harley tradition show cotton’s share of British GDP rising from a small cottage industry to several per cent of output by the 1830s. The raw cotton came from the American South after Whitney’s gin (1793) and, before that, from the West Indies and the Levant. The finished cloth sold in Europe, West Africa and India. Cotton is therefore a global commodity as well as a British technique; we return to the enslaved labour behind the raw fibre in [[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]].

Second, **energy**. Wrigley (1988, 2010) contrasted an **organic economy**, in which the energy budget is the annual product of photosynthesis — wood, muscle, wind, water — with a **mineral economy**, in which a stock of coal is drawn down. English coal output rose from a few million tonnes a year in the seventeenth century to tens of millions by 1830 and more than 100 million by the 1870s (Mitchell, *British Historical Statistics*, 1988). Steam engines, from Newcomen (1712) to Watt (separate condenser patented 1769) to high-pressure engines after 1800, converted that coal into rotary motion. The share of steam in industrial power was still limited in 1800; water wheels remained important. The direction of change is not.

::: example Compounding at Industrial Revolution rates {#ex-compound}
Take the Broadberry et al. (2015) average of 0.48 per cent a year for British GDP per capita over 1700–1870. (a) By what factor does income per person rise in 170 years at that rate? (b) At 0.20 per cent a year — their English rate for 1270–1700 — what would the factor have been over the same 170 years? (c) At 2.0 per cent a year, a representative rich-country rate after 1950, what is the factor over 170 years?
::: solution
(a) $1.0048^{170} \approx 2.26$. Income a little more than doubles. (b) $1.0020^{170} \approx 1.40$. (c) $1.020^{170} \approx 29$. The Industrial Revolution is a change of regime relative to the previous four centuries, and it is not yet the golden age of [[economic-history/golden-age]]. A student who pictures 1760–1830 as a 2-per-cent take-off is picturing the wrong century.
:::
:::

Growth accounting makes the same point in a different language. Write $Y = A K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}$, now with $K$ a produced capital stock rather than a fixed land endowment. Then

$$
g_Y = \alpha g_K + (1-\alpha) g_L + g_A,
$$ {#eq-accounting}

where $g_A$ is the Solow residual, **total factor productivity** (TFP). Crafts (2004, 2018) surveys estimates in which $g_A$ for the British economy as a whole is of the order of 0.1 to 0.4 per cent a year before 1830, and higher thereafter. Capital deepening — more mills, more houses, more ships — accounts for much of the rest of GDP growth. Population growth of about 1 per cent a year by the early nineteenth century (Wrigley and Schofield 1981) ate a large share of GDP growth, which is why GDP *per capita* grew more slowly than GDP.

::: proposition Slow aggregate TFP is consistent with fast sectoral TFP {#prop-slow}
If a leading sector with TFP growth $g_A^{s}$ has share $\theta$ of value added, and the rest of the economy has TFP growth zero, aggregate TFP growth is $\theta g_A^{s}$. A cotton industry that is 5 per cent of GDP and whose TFP grows at 4 per cent a year contributes 0.2 percentage points to aggregate TFP growth.
:::
::: proof
Aggregate TFP growth is the value-added-weighted sum of sectoral TFP growth rates (Domar 1961; the statement here ignores intermediate inputs). With two sectors, $g_A = \theta g_A^{s} + (1-\theta)\cdot 0 = \theta g_A^{s}$. Substitute $\theta = 0.05$ and $g_A^{s} = 0.04$.
:::

[[#prop-slow]] is why the Crafts–Harley revision and the cotton miracle can both be true. It is also why, as cotton’s share grew and as steam spread into transport after 1830, aggregate growth accelerated. The “revolution” is a rising share of revolutionary techniques, not a step change in the whole-economy residual in 1760.

## Why Britain? High wages and cheap coal

The techniques of the British Industrial Revolution were not, in the first instance, adopted in France, the Low Countries, China or India. That is the fact that a theory of *location* must explain. Several theories are on the table. This section states Allen’s, which has the virtue of being a relative-price argument, and the next section states the institutional and knowledge arguments that compete with it.

Allen (2009), *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective*, starts from two relative prices. British labour was expensive, and British coal was cheap. In the eighteenth century, Allen’s welfare ratios for building labourers were high in London and Amsterdam and low in Florence, Vienna, Beijing, and the Indian cities in his comparison (Allen 2001; Allen, Bassino, Ma, Moll-Murata and van Zanden 2011). At the same time, the pithead price of coal on the Tyne and in the West Midlands was a fraction of the price of energy in equivalent units in most of Europe and Asia, where the relevant energy was charcoal or muscle. A spinning jenny, a water frame or a coal-using blast furnace saved expensive labour and burned cheap energy. In a low-wage, expensive-energy economy the same machine did not pay.

::: definition Induced innovation (Allen) {#def-allen}
**Induced innovation** in Allen’s sense is the claim that the *direction* of technical change responds to relative factor prices. When the wage–energy ratio is high, inventors and adopters search for labour-saving, energy-using techniques. Britain’s high-wage, cheap-coal economy made the Industrial Revolution’s canonical machines profitable at British prices; the same machines were not profitable at French, Chinese or Indian prices until wages rose or energy cheapened.
:::

The argument is local, not global. It does not say that Britain was more inventive in some cultural sense. It says that a French or Chinese cotton spinner, facing low wages and dear fuel, would have been right *not* to install a jenny. As British machines improved, their costs fell, and they eventually paid everywhere: that is why the techniques spread in the nineteenth century. The location of the *first* adoption is a matter of 1760s relative prices.

::: example When does a jenny pay? {#ex-jenny}
A stylised hand spinner produces 1 unit of yarn per year. A jenny, costing $c$ units of capital (a rental of $r c$ per year), lets one spinner produce $q > 1$ units. Labour costs $w$ per spinner-year. The jenny is worth installing if the average cost of jenny-spun yarn is below the cost of hand yarn:

$$
\frac{w + r c}{q} < w \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad w > \frac{r c}{q-1}.
$$ {#eq-jenny}

Take illustrative numbers: $q = 3$, $r c = 16$ (the annual user cost of the machine). (a) What is the threshold wage? (b) If the British wage is 10 and the Indian wage is 3, in the same units, who installs the jenny? (c) If later improvements cut $r c$ to 4, what is the new threshold, and who installs?
::: solution
(a) The jenny pays if $w > rc/(q-1) = 16/2 = 8$. (b) Britain at $w = 10$ installs; India at $w = 3$ does not. (c) The threshold falls to $4/2 = 2$. Now both install. The numbers are illustrative; the shape is Allen’s: early machines pay only where wages are high, and cheaper later machines pay everywhere.
:::
:::

Wrigley’s energy argument and Allen’s wage argument are complements. Coal in Britain was not just cheap; it was accessible by water, so that the energy-using techniques of the Midlands and the North did not have to pay overland freight on every tonne. Pomeranz (2000) adds a geographic rider: Britain sat on coal, and the Yangzi delta did not sit on coal that could be brought to its cities at a comparable cost. We will meet Pomeranz again in [[economic-history/great-divergence]]. The coal argument is a claim about *British* location; it is not, by itself, a claim about why France, which had coal in the Nord and the Loire, industrialised later.

## Institutions, knowledge and demand

A second family of explanations locates the Industrial Revolution in Britain’s institutions. North and Weingast (1989) argued that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 committed the English state to honour its debt and to respect property, lowering the cost of capital and raising the return to investment. Parliament, the common law, and a patent statute of 1624 (with a more usable practice by the eighteenth century) are the usual exhibits. Mokyr, McCloskey and others add a cultural layer: an **industrial Enlightenment**, in Mokyr’s (2002, 2009, 2016) phrase, in which “useful knowledge” — the belief that nature can be understood and tinkered with — became respectable, and in which the Royal Society, the Lunar Society of Birmingham, and a dense craft culture linked scientists to millwrights.

::: definition Useful knowledge (Mokyr) {#def-mokyr}
**Useful knowledge**, in Mokyr’s usage, is propositional and prescriptive knowledge about nature that can be applied to production: why a pump works, how to measure a steam engine’s efficiency, which ore smelts at which temperature. The Industrial Enlightenment is the social system that produced, stored and shared that knowledge in eighteenth-century Europe, with Britain as a particularly thick node.
:::

A third family emphasises demand. The British home market was integrated, urban and, by 1750, already consuming tropical groceries and cottons. Trade with the Atlantic world, including markets that rested on enslaved labour, widened the market for British manufactures (Williams 1944 is the classic statement; the quantitative literature since is mixed, and is taken up in [[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]]). In a Smithian story, a larger market supports a finer division of labour and a larger return to a fixed cost of invention.

These families are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence does not award a single prize.

Against a pure 1688-institutions story: the Dutch Republic had secure property, deep capital markets and a sophisticated state in the seventeenth century, and did not have an industrial revolution on the British pattern. France after 1789 had a revolution in property rights and still industrialised later. Britain’s patents were expensive and often circumvented; many canonical inventions were not patented, or were patented after they had spread. Institutions may have been a background condition — a reason Britain was already a high-wage commercial economy by 1700 — without being the trigger in 1760.

Against a pure useful-knowledge story: the scientific revolution was European, not British. France had a larger scientific establishment. The early cotton machines were the work of craftsmen, not of Fellows of the Royal Society. Mokyr’s reply is that Britain had the *combination* of craft skill and access to science, and that the steam engine, unlike the jenny, really did draw on atmospheric science and on systematic measurement (Watt, Boulton, and the Birmingham network). The reply is plausible and hard to test. Allen’s reply is that the knowledge was more widely available than the relative prices that made it worth using.

Against a pure demand story: India and China had larger domestic markets, in people if not in purchasing power, and did not industrialise. Atlantic trade was important to particular sectors (cotton, sugar, shipping) and a small share of British GDP. O’Brien and Engerman (1991) and later work put the extra demand from the slave-based Atlantic at a few percentage points of British industry, not at the whole of the Industrial Revolution. A few percentage points can still matter at the margin; they are not a sufficient cause.

Allen’s induced-innovation argument predicts that the spinning jenny was first adopted in Britain because
- [ ] British inventors were uniquely scientific
- [x] British wages were high relative to the cost of machines and energy, so a labour-saving machine paid there first
- [ ] the Glorious Revolution of 1688 lowered British interest rates
- [ ] India lacked cotton
::: solution
Allen’s claim is a relative-price claim. India had cotton; France had scientists; 1688 is a different argument (North and Weingast). The jenny paid where labour was expensive.
:::

## Living standards, labour and the pessimist–optimist debate

Did the people who worked in the new industries become better off? The question split historians into **optimists** and **pessimists** for a century, and the numbers have not ended the argument because “better off” has several dimensions.

Real wages of English building labourers, in Clark (2005) and in Allen’s series, are roughly flat from 1760 to about 1810–20 and then rise. Feinstein (1998) constructed a more pessimistic real-wage series for the average worker, with little net gain until the 1820s and a clearer rise after 1840. Broadberry et al. (2015) GDP per capita rises through the period, but GDP per capita is not the labourer’s wage: rents, profits and a shift in the mix of goods sit in the gap. Heights of British soldiers and convicts, compiled by Floud, Wachter and Gregory (1990) and revisited by Cinnirella (2008) and others, show a dip in the early industrial decades — a health and nutrition marker that can move against real wages if disease environments worsen in cities.

Urbanisation is part of the dip. The share of the English population living in towns of 5,000 or more rose sharply (Wrigley). Cities had higher money wages and higher mortality. The Malthusian urban death penalty of the previous chapter was still at work, now in Manchester and Liverpool rather than in medieval London. Sanitation, diet and smaller families eventually reversed the urban penalty; that reversal is a nineteenth-century public-health story as much as an industrial one.

Factory labour, including the labour of women and children, is another dimension. The cotton mill used child piecers and female spinners; parliamentary inquiries of the 1830s and 1840s documented hours, accidents and ages. The Factory Act of 1833 restricted children’s hours in textile mills; later acts extended the principle. Whether the mill was worse than the cottage or the farm it replaced is a comparison that depends on the alternative: cottage spinning was poorly paid, and rural poverty in the South of England in the Speenhamland years was real. The comparison is not an excuse. It is a reminder that the baseline is the Malthusian world, not a twentieth-century welfare state.

::: warning Growth of GDP is not growth of wages
A rise in GDP per capita can coexist with a flat real wage for decades if the labour share falls, if rents and profits absorb the gain, or if the GDP deflator and the labourer’s consumer price index diverge. Feinstein’s wage series and Broadberry’s GDP series can both be right. Do not read a GDP-per-capita chart as a history of working-class consumption. Do not read a flat wage series as a history of stagnation in every component of income.
:::

::: example A labour-share gap {#ex-share}
Suppose GDP per worker rises 30 per cent between 1780 and 1840, while the real wage rises 5 per cent. In a one-good Cobb–Douglas world the labour share $wL/Y$ is constant at $1-\alpha$, so the wage and GDP per worker should move together. (a) What happens to the labour share in this example? (b) Give two historical reasons, other than a falling labour share, why the two series might diverge even if the labour share were constant.
::: solution
(a) The labour share moves with $w / y$. It falls by a factor $1.05/1.30 \approx 0.81$, a 19 per cent decline. (b) The wage series is for a particular occupation (building labourers, cotton spinners) and may miss changing occupational mix; the consumer price index for wage-earners (bread, coal, rent) can rise faster than the GDP deflator, which includes investment goods and exports whose prices fell. Either breaks the one-good link between $w$ and $y$.
:::
:::

## Britain in a European and Asian mirror

Belgium and France industrialised in the first half of the nineteenth century, Germany from the 1840s, with railways, coal of the Ruhr, and a customs union (the Zollverein, 1834) as the usual markers. The United States, already a high-wage economy in Allen’s tables, adopted British techniques and then, in small arms, clocks and later cars, developed interchangeable parts and a “American system of manufactures.” None of these is a copy of 1760s Lancashire. Each is a data point for the theories above.

If Allen is right, industrialisation should appear where wages are high and coal is cheap, once the techniques exist. Belgium (coal, relatively high wages, close to Britain) fits. The Yangzi delta (low wages, expensive coal at the mill) fits the *non*-adoption. The United States (high wages, wood and then coal) fits adoption and a labour-saving bias. France is the awkward case: not poor, not without coal, and slower. Crafts (1984) and later work treat French industrialisation as later and more gradual, not as a failure of property rights after 1789.

If Mokyr is right, industrialisation should appear where useful knowledge is thick. That is a European, and especially a north-western European, prediction, and it fits the eventual spread of industry on the Continent better than it fits the British *lead*. If North and Weingast are right, the prediction is about secure property and limited government, which the Dutch had early and which several industrialisers (Germany, Meiji Japan, late-nineteenth-century Russia) had in qualified forms at best.

Meiji Japan, which we take up in [[economic-history/late-industrialisation]], is a useful out-of-sample check. Japan in 1868 was not a high-wage coal economy on Allen’s British pattern, and it was not a Glorious-Revolution state. It industrialised, from the 1880s, by importing techniques whose costs had already fallen, by mobilising a state around railways, textiles and later steel, and by raising human capital. That is consistent with Allen’s claim about *first* adoption — the jenny paid in Britain in 1770, not in Japan — and with a later, cheaper, more imitative path. It is also consistent with a knowledge story in which the Meiji state set out to import the useful knowledge itself.

::: application Cotton in India {#app-india}
India was the world’s leading cotton-textile producer in the eighteenth century. By 1900 it was a large importer of Lancashire yarn and cloth, and a mill industry of its own was only beginning around Bombay. Clingingsmith and Williamson (2008) attribute much of the nineteenth-century decline of Indian handicrafts to a combination of declining agricultural productivity in India (raising the relative cost of food, hence of Indian wages in terms of cloth) and declining transport costs and British productivity (lowering the price of Lancashire cloth). Roy (2000, 2011) emphasises continuity of Indian craft production and the rise of mills, and is less willing to call the episode “deindustrialisation.” Both agree that the story is prices and productivity, not a single colonial decree that closed the handlooms. Colonial policy still belongs in the account — tariffs, railways, and the absence of an infant-industry policy on the Japanese pattern — and is discussed in [[economic-history/empire-and-colonialism]].
:::

::: example Growth accounting in a stylised mill town {#ex-accounting}
A mill town’s real output grows at 4 per cent a year, its capital stock at 5 per cent and its labour force at 1 per cent. The capital share is $\alpha = 0.3$. (a) What is TFP growth? (b) What share of output growth is accounted for by TFP? (c) If population and the labour force grow at the same rate, what is the growth of output per worker, and how much of that is TFP?
::: solution
(a) From [[#eq-accounting]], $g_A = 0.04 - 0.3\times 0.05 - 0.7\times 0.01 = 0.04 - 0.015 - 0.007 = 0.018$, or 1.8 per cent a year. (b) $1.8/4.0 = 0.45$, so 45 per cent of output growth. (c) $g_Y - g_L = 3$ per cent a year. TFP is still 1.8 percentage points of that; the rest is capital deepening, $\alpha(g_K - g_L) = 0.3\times 4 = 1.2$ percentage points. Even a “revolutionary” town gets a large fraction of its extra output per worker from accumulation rather than from the residual.
:::
:::

## Where this leads

The Industrial Revolution is the first escape from the Malthusian regime of [[economic-history/malthusian-world]]. The next chapter asks why the escape was so geographically concentrated, and why China, India and much of the rest of the world remained on the old path for another century: [[economic-history/great-divergence]]. The first globalisation then spread cheap transport, capital and people, and with them both convergence and new inequalities ([[economic-history/first-globalisation]]). Growth accounting, TFP and the Solow model are developed as theory in [[economic-growth/technology-and-growth-accounting]]. The living-standards debate — wages, heights, mortality — is a historical counterpart of the measurement problems in [[development-economics/measuring-development]].

::: history
**1769, James Watt and Richard Arkwright.** Watt patented the separate condenser for the steam engine; Arkwright patented the water frame. The two patents are convenient markers, not the whole of the technical story: Newcomen engines had been pumping mines since 1712, and spinning was transformed by a sequence of machines. **1884, Arnold Toynbee.** His posthumous *Lectures on the Industrial Revolution of the Eighteenth Century in England* named the period and pictured a sharp break. **1985, N. F. R. Crafts.** *British Economic Growth during the Industrial Revolution* put the aggregate growth rate on a lower path and forced the field to distinguish leading sectors from the whole economy. **2009, Robert C. Allen.** *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* stated the high-wage, cheap-coal argument in a form that can be compared with relative prices in France, India and China.
:::

::: summary
- British GDP per capita grew at about 0.48 per cent a year from 1700 to 1870 (Broadberry et al. 2015). That is a break with the previous four centuries and is not yet modern growth.
- Cotton, iron and steam had high TFP growth and, at first, small shares of GDP, so aggregate TFP grew slowly ([[#prop-slow]]).
- Allen’s explanation of location is relative prices: high British wages and cheap coal made labour-saving, energy-using machines pay in Britain first.
- Institutional (North and Weingast) and useful-knowledge (Mokyr) arguments explain a European commercial background better than they explain a British trigger in 1760; they are not therefore empty.
- Real wages lagged GDP per capita; heights and urban mortality paint a darker picture of 1780–1840 than GDP does. Optimists and pessimists are often describing different series.
- Later industrialisers adopted cheaper, already-improved techniques. That is consistent with Allen on first adoption and with a broader story of diffusion.
:::

A leading sector has TFP growth of 5 per cent a year and a value-added share of 6 per cent. The rest of the economy has TFP growth of zero. What is aggregate TFP growth, in per cent per year? (Enter the number, for example 0.3 for three-tenths of one per cent.)
::: solution
By [[#prop-slow]], $g_A = 0.06 \times 5 = 0.3$ per cent a year.

::: exercise Doubling at 0.48 per cent {level=1 check="146"}
Using the rule of 70, how many years does it take GDP per capita to double at 0.48 per cent a year? (Enter $70 / 0.48$ rounded to the nearest year.)
::: solution
$70 / 0.48 \approx 145.8$, which rounds to 146 years. The exact doubling time is $\ln 2 / 0.0048 \approx 144$ years.
:::

In [[#eq-jenny]] with $q = 3$ and $rc = 16$, what is the lowest wage at which the machine is just worth installing? (Enter the threshold wage.)
::: solution
$w = rc/(q-1) = 16/2 = 8$.

::: exercise Two TFP rates {level=2}
Britain’s whole-economy TFP grows at 0.3 per cent a year, cotton’s TFP at 3 per cent, and cotton is 8 per cent of GDP. (a) What TFP growth in the non-cotton economy is implied? (b) If cotton’s share rises to 16 per cent and the two TFP rates are unchanged, what happens to aggregate TFP growth?
::: solution
(a) $0.3 = 0.08\times 3 + 0.92\times g_A^{n}$, so $0.3 - 0.24 = 0.06 = 0.92 g_A^{n}$, hence $g_A^{n} \approx 0.065$ per cent a year — essentially zero given the precision of the data. (b) $g_A = 0.16\times 3 + 0.84\times 0.065 \approx 0.48 + 0.055 = 0.535$ per cent a year. A rising share of a fast sector raises aggregate TFP even if no sector speeds up. That is the Crafts–Harley arithmetic of the early nineteenth century.
:::
:::

::: exercise Relative prices, not culture {level=2}
A French mill and a British mill have access to the same jenny. British wages are 10, French wages are 6, and $rc = 16$, $q = 3$ as in [[#ex-jenny]]. (a) Does either mill install? (b) A fall in transport costs on coal cuts British $rc$ to 10 but leaves French $rc$ at 16, because French mills burn dearer fuel. What happens? (c) In two sentences, why this pattern is evidence for Allen rather than for a story in which the French “lack enterprise”?
::: solution
(a) Threshold $8$. Britain installs ($10>8$), France does not ($6<8$). (b) British threshold falls to $10/2 = 5$; Britain still installs. France is unchanged and still does not. (c) The same machine is available in both countries; the difference is factor prices, including energy. A culture of enterprise would have to explain why French mills adopted the cheaper later machines, which they did, once relative prices moved.
:::
:::

::: exercise Wages, GDP and heights {level=2}
Between 1780 and 1840, suppose GDP per capita rises 25 per cent, Feinstein’s real wage is roughly flat, and mean adult male height falls by 1 cm. Is this pattern a contradiction? What would you need to know about urbanisation, disease and the labour share to interpret it?
::: solution
It is not a contradiction. GDP per capita includes profits, rents and a changing mix of goods; the real wage is a particular price of labour deflated by a working-class basket; height is a net nutrition residual after work, disease and childhood conditions. Urbanisation can raise money GDP and money wages while worsening the disease environment enough to cut heights. A falling labour share can raise GDP per capita with a flat wage. To interpret the pattern you need the urban share, cause-specific mortality, and a labour-share or occupational-mix series — not a single “living standards” index.
:::
:::

::: exercise Derive the jenny condition {level=3}
A hand spinner produces 1 unit of yarn per year at cost $w$. A machine of user cost $rc$ lets one worker produce $q$ units. (a) Derive [[#eq-jenny]]. (b) Show that a fall in $r$ (cheaper capital) has the same qualitative effect as a fall in $c$ or a rise in $q$. (c) Allen also emphasises cheap *energy*. How would you extend the inequality if the machine requires $e$ units of coal at price $p_E$?
::: hint
Average cost of machine yarn is $(w + rc + p_E e)/q$. Compare with $w$.
:::
::: solution
(a) Hand yarn costs $w$ per unit. Machine yarn costs $(w+rc)/q$ per unit. The machine pays if $(w+rc)/q < w$, so $w + rc < qw$, so $rc < (q-1)w$, so $w > rc/(q-1)$. (b) $r$, $c$ and $1/(q-1)$ all enter the threshold linearly; cutting the interest rate, cutting the price of the machine, or raising its productivity all lower the wage at which adoption begins. (c) The condition becomes $w > (rc + p_E e)/(q-1)$. Cheap coal (low $p_E$) lowers the threshold, which is why Allen’s argument is a wage-and-energy argument, not a wage argument alone.
:::

::: exercise Could cotton haul the aggregate? {level=3}
Suppose cotton TFP grows at 5 per cent a year from 1780 to 1830. Whole-economy TFP grows at 0.3 per cent a year. (a) If the rest of the economy has zero TFP growth, what average share of GDP must cotton have had over the period? (b) Historical estimates put cotton’s share well below 10 per cent for most of these years. What does that imply for TFP growth outside cotton? (c) Why might Domar aggregation, which weights by gross output rather than value added, raise cotton’s contribution?
::: hint
For (a) use $g_A = \theta g_A^{s}$. For (c), think of cotton’s demand for machinery, coal and shipping.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\theta = 0.3/5 = 0.06$, a 6 per cent average share. (b) If the true share averaged 4 per cent, say, then $0.3 = 0.04\times 5 + 0.96 g_A^{n}$ gives $g_A^{n} \approx 0.10$ per cent a year. Some TFP growth outside cotton is required, or the 0.3 per cent aggregate figure is a little high, or both. Crafts and Harley’s reading is that there *was* TFP growth in a handful of other sectors (iron, some transport) and very little in the rest of a still-agrarian economy. (c) Domar weights a sector by the ratio of its gross output to GDP, which exceeds its value-added share when the sector uses intermediate inputs. Cotton’s contribution to aggregate TFP then includes productivity gains that show up as cheaper intermediates for other industries. That raises cotton’s measured contribution without contradicting a small value-added share.
:::
