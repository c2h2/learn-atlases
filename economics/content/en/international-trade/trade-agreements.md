Twenty-three countries signed the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade in Geneva on 30 October 1947. The International Trade Organisation that was supposed to house it never came into being: the Havana Charter of 1948 failed in the US Congress. The GATT survived as a contract, hosted eight rounds of tariff cuts, and was absorbed, on 1 January 1995, into the World Trade Organization created by the Uruguay Round's Marrakesh Agreement. As of 2024 the WTO had 166 members, including China (acceded 2001), Russia (2012), and, most recently, Comoros and Timor-Leste (2024). Alongside it, and sometimes in tension with it, sit hundreds of **regional trade agreements** (RTAs): the European Union, USMCA, CPTPP, RCEP, AfCFTA, Mercosur, ASEAN's FTAs, and many bilateral deals. This chapter describes those institutions as they are, and the economic logic — Bagwell and Staiger's terms-of-trade theory, the commitment theory of the last chapter — that makes sense of them. It does not score "the WTO" or "regionalism" as good or bad. It says what the rules do, where they bind, and what a trade war is in the models of [[international-trade/tariffs-and-quotas#thm-opt-tariff]].

Gains from trade remain the reason a government might *want* to lower its own and its partners' tariffs. Distributional costs remain the reason it might not want to do so alone. An agreement is a technology for exchanging market access, for committing against future lobbies, and for handling the disputes that follow. Whether any particular agreement, in any particular year, achieved those things is an empirical question with a mixed record, and the chapter will cite the record as such.

## The GATT and the WTO

The GATT 1947 is a contract among "contracting parties" on trade in goods. The WTO 1995 is an organisation: it houses the GATT 1994, the General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS), the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS), a Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU), and a sheaf of other agreements (subsidies, agriculture, sanitary measures, technical barriers, rules of origin, trade facilitation). Membership is of states and of separate customs territories (Hong Kong, China; Macao, China; Chinese Taipei). Decisions are by consensus in practice.

Three principles organise the goods rules.

::: definition MFN, national treatment and bindings {#def-gatt}
**Most-favoured-nation (MFN)** treatment (GATT Article I) requires that a concession granted to one member be granted immediately and unconditionally to all members. **National treatment** (Article III) requires that, once a good has cleared customs, it be treated no less favourably than a like domestic good in internal taxes and regulations. A **binding** (Article II) is a legal ceiling on an MFN tariff line: the applied tariff may be lower (the gap is **binding overhang**), it may not be higher without compensation or a dispute. Exceptions include Article XX (general exceptions: health, conservation, public morals, with a chapeau against disguised restriction), Article XXI (security), Article XIX (safeguards), and Article XXIV (RTAs).
:::

MFN is a non-discrimination rule among foreign sources; national treatment is a non-discrimination rule between foreign and domestic goods *inside* the border. Bindings are the commitment. The Kennedy Round (1964–67) and Tokyo Round (1973–79) cut industrial tariffs among participants by formulae; the Uruguay Round (1986–94) extended bindings to developing members' industrial tariffs, brought agriculture into the system in a limited way, created GATS and TRIPS, and replaced GATT's diplomacy-flavoured disputes with the DSU.

::: proposition Reciprocity and the terms-of-trade theory of agreements {#prop-bagwell-staiger}
Bagwell and Staiger (1999, 2002) model a two-country tariff game. Each government maximises a politically weighted welfare that includes a terms-of-trade gain from its own tariff ([[international-trade/tariffs-and-quotas#thm-opt-tariff]] plus [[international-trade/political-economy-of-trade#def-pfs]]). The Nash tariffs are too high: each ignores the terms-of-trade loss it inflicts on the other. A reciprocal agreement that cuts tariffs so as to *neutralise terms-of-trade effects* (local prices move, world prices do not, or world prices are restored) raises both governments' politically weighted welfare. MFN and reciprocity together prevent the bilateral opportunistic deals that would otherwise shift the terms of trade onto third parties. Assumptions: governments care about local prices and terms of trade in the way the model specifies; there is no other international externality (or it is absorbed into the same logic).
:::

::: proof Sketch.
Home's tariff $t$ lowers $p_w$ and raises Home's local price $p_d$. Foreign is hurt by the fall in $p_w$ on its export. In Nash, Home sets $\partial G/\partial t = 0$ counting the ToT gain and not counting Foreign's $G^*$. Reciprocal cuts that hold $p_w$ constant remove the international externality and leave each government choosing local prices as if it were small — which, given its political weights, is the politically efficient choice. MFN stops Home from cutting $t$ only for a preferred partner and thereby pushing the ToT loss onto a third country. Bagwell and Staiger (2002), *The Economics of the World Trading System*, is the monograph.
:::

The theory is a positive account of *why the rules have the shape they have* — MFN, reciprocity, bindings, and a dispute process that authorises *withdrawal of equivalent concessions* rather than fines. It is not a finding that every WTO outcome is efficient, nor that terms of trade are the only motive. Ossa (2011) adds a "new trade" political-economy externality (market-access motives with monopolistic competition); Maggi and Rodríguez-Clare (1998, 2007) add the commitment motive of the last chapter. The legal texts are consistent with more than one of these, and the empirical work (Broda, Limão and Weinstein 2008 on ToT motives in unbound tariffs; Limao 2006, 2007 on preferences and MFN) finds traces of several.

::: example Binding overhang {#ex-overhang}
A member's bound tariff on a line is 20 per cent; its applied MFN rate is 8 per cent. (a) How much can it raise the applied rate without breaching the binding? (b) Why might applied rates sit below bindings (overhang), especially in developing members? (c) What does overhang do to the value of the commitment in [[#prop-bagwell-staiger]]?
::: solution
(a) 12 percentage points. (b) Applied rates track political-economy and macroeconomic conditions; bindings were often set high in the Uruguay Round as a price of bringing developing members' industrial tariffs into the system. Overhang is large in much of South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Latin America (WTO Tariff Profiles). (c) A binding with overhang is a weaker commitment: the government can raise applied rates, hurting partners' terms of trade, without a legal breach, up to the bound. During crises, applied rates do sometimes rise inside the overhang. The commitment theory still applies *at the bound*; the ToT theory applies to applied rates.
:::
:::

::: example Reciprocity as a joint move {#ex-reciprocity}
Home and Foreign are large. Each has a Nash tariff of 20 per cent. A bargain cuts both to 8 per cent. Explain, using [[#prop-bagwell-staiger]], why each government can prefer the bargain to Nash even though each, *holding the other's tariff fixed*, would like to raise its own tariff back toward 20 per cent. What is the role of the binding?
::: solution
At Nash, each is already on its best-response: a unilateral cut would *lose* ToT and be politically costly. A *joint* cut improves each country's ToT (the partner's cut) by more than it costs in the own-tariff dimension, once political weights are those of $G$. That is why the agreement is sustainable only if defection is punished — by the partner's withdrawal of concessions, which is what the DSU authorises. The binding is the legal expression of "do not go back to 20". Without it, the bargain is cheap talk in a one-shot game.
:::
:::

::: warning The WTO is not a free-trade organisation
The WTO does not require zero tariffs, does not prohibit all subsidies, and does not abolish anti-dumping. It requires non-discrimination (with listed exceptions), bindings, and a process. Agricultural tariffs and subsidies remain high relative to industrial goods in many members; trade in services is far more restricted than trade in goods, GATS notwithstanding; TRIPS is an intellectual-property agreement, not a trade-liberalisation agreement. Describing the WTO as "free trade" is a slogan. Describing it as a weak organisation because tariffs are not zero is the opposite slogan. The legal object is the one in [[#def-gatt]].
:::

## Dispute settlement

The DSU is the WTO's distinctive enforcement technology. A member that considers another member to have breached an obligation requests consultations; a panel may then be composed; its report can be appealed to the Appellate Body; the Dispute Settlement Body adopts reports by reverse consensus (they go through unless every member objects). If the losing member does not comply, the winner may be authorised to **retaliate** — to suspend equivalent concessions, typically tariffs on a politically chosen list of the loser's exports. There is no damages award in money.

From 1995 through the late 2010s the system handled several hundred disputes. High-profile cases included bananas (EU preferences), beef hormones, Boeing–Airbus subsidies (DS316, DS353), rare earths, and a series of US–China cases after 2001. The Appellate Body ceased to hear new appeals in December 2019, after the United States blocked new appointments, citing concerns about judicial over-reach and about the treatment of China's protocol of accession. Some members have used a multi-party interim appeal arrangement; others have "appealed into the void", leaving panel reports unadopted. The facts of the blockage are not in dispute; the merits of the US critique, and of the Appellate Body's case law, are a legal and political controversy this chapter does not adjudicate.

Retaliation as the remedy is the Bagwell–Staiger logic in operation: the threat that restores the terms of trade (or the political market access) that was taken. It is a blunt threat. Small members cannot credibly retaliate against large ones; large members can, which is why a good deal of compliance is reputational and reciprocal rather than a small-on-large punishment.

## Regional trade agreements

GATT Article XXIV permits a **customs union** (free trade internally, a common external tariff) or a **free-trade area** (free trade internally, members keep their own external tariffs) provided the RTA covers "substantially all trade" and does not, on the whole, raise barriers to outsiders. GATS Article V is the services analogue. In practice the WTO has never blocked an RTA; hundreds are in force (WTO RTA database).

::: definition FTA, customs union and trade diversion {#def-rta}
A **free-trade area (FTA)** eliminates (most) tariffs among members and leaves each member's external tariff in its own hands; **rules of origin** then determine which goods count as originating, to prevent deflection (shipping into the low-tariff member and on to the high-tariff member). A **customs union (CU)** adds a common external tariff and can, in principle, do without rules of origin on internal trade. Viner (1950) distinguished **trade creation** (a member's supply replacing less efficient domestic supply) from **trade diversion** (a member's supply replacing a more efficient outsider's). An RTA can raise or lower world welfare depending on the balance; it can raise or lower a *member's* welfare for the same reason, and because of the terms-of-trade effects on outsiders.
:::

::: proposition Kemp–Wan and the ambiguity of FTAs {#prop-kemp-wan}
Kemp and Wan (1976) showed that a customs union can always be designed, with a common external tariff and internal lump-sum transfers, so that outsiders are no worse off and members are better off. There is no equivalent general theorem for FTAs with rules of origin. An FTA may divert trade, may worsen members' external terms of trade, and may, through rules of origin, raise production costs (Krueger 1993; Krishna 2005). Assumptions of Kemp–Wan: a CU, choice of the external tariff, and lump-sum transfers among members.
:::

::: proof
Kemp–Wan: set the CU's external tariff so that the vector of extra-union trade is unchanged (outsiders face the same residual demand); internal free trade then delivers a standard gains-from-trade improvement among members, which can be distributed by lumpsum. An FTA cannot generally freeze extra-union trade with a single instrument per member, and rules of origin add a new distortion. Hence the ambiguity.
:::

A short, factual list of large RTAs, with dates of entry into force as notified or as commonly dated:

- **European Union.** A customs union (completed for goods in 1968) and a single market (1993) with free movement of goods, services, capital and labour among members; a common commercial policy. The United Kingdom left on 31 January 2020; the EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement applied from 1 January 2021.
- **USMCA** (United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement), in force 1 July 2020, successor to NAFTA (1994). Tighter auto rules of origin than NAFTA.
- **CPTPP** (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership), in force 30 December 2018 among the first ratifiers; the United Kingdom acceded in 2023–24. The United States signed the predecessor TPP and withdrew in 2017.
- **RCEP** (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership): ASEAN plus China, Japan, Korea, Australia and New Zealand; signed 15 November 2020, in force 1 January 2022 for the first group of ratifiers. India withdrew from the negotiations in 2019.
- **AfCFTA** (African Continental Free Trade Area): legal instruments entered into force 30 May 2019; tariff phase-downs and the protocol agenda are ongoing. Intra-African merchandise trade remains a smaller share of Africa's trade than intra-regional shares in Europe or Asia (UNCTAD and African Union reporting).
- **Mercosur** (Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay, with later associates) is a customs union on paper with incomplete internal liberalisation and a common external tariff with many exceptions.
- **ASEAN** Free Trade Area and ASEAN's plus-one FTAs with China, Japan, Korea, India, Australia and New Zealand, which RCEP in part overlays.

Rules of origin are not a technicality. In North American autos, USMCA's higher regional-content requirements are a stated industrial-policy tool as well as an anti-deflection device. In African and ASEAN agreements, restrictive origin rules can eat the preference margin, especially for countries whose exports are GVC slices rather than wholly obtained goods (World Bank WDR 2020). An FTA's applied preference is the MFN tariff minus the preferential rate, *if* origin is satisfied; utilisation rates are often well below 100 per cent.

::: example Trade creation and diversion {#ex-viner}
Home has a \$10 unit cost of producing a good; Partner has a \$6 cost; Outsider has a \$4 cost. Home's MFN tariff is \$5, so Outsider's duty-paid price is \$9 and Partner's is \$11. Home therefore imports from Outsider in MFN. An FTA with Partner zeros Partner's tariff, so Partner's price becomes \$6, Outsider remains at \$9, and Home switches to Partner. Identify creation, diversion, and the change in Home's welfare ignoring consumer-surplus triangles (compare unit costs paid).
::: solution
Before: Home imports from Outsider at a duty-paid \$9, of which \$4 is the resource cost and \$5 is Home revenue. After: Home imports from Partner at \$6, with no revenue. The resource cost has risen from \$4 to \$6 (diversion: \$2 per unit). Revenue of \$5 is lost. Consumers pay \$6 instead of \$9 (a \$3 gain per unit). Net of the lost revenue, Home is down \$2 per unit on the resource-cost comparison — Viner's diversion. There is no creation here because Home was already importing. If instead Home had been producing at \$10 and the FTA replaced *that* with Partner at \$6, the resource-cost saving of \$4 would be creation. Real FTAs mix both; Viner's point is that the sign is not given by "FTA" as a label.
:::
:::

::: example Three large RTAs, factually {#ex-three-rtas}
For each of RCEP (in force 2022), AfCFTA (legal entry into force 2019) and CPTPP (in force 2018), name the membership type (FTA or CU) and one feature that [[#def-rta]] says will matter for whether trade is created or diverted: a common external tariff, rules of origin, or the size of the external MFN barrier being jumped.
::: solution
All three are FTAs, not customs unions: members keep their own external tariffs. Rules of origin therefore matter in each. RCEP overlays existing ASEAN-plus-one FTAs, so the *incremental* preference margin (the MFN barrier jumped) is in many lines already small, which limits both creation and diversion on those lines. AfCFTA's external MFN barriers are higher on average in several members (WTO Tariff Profiles), so a completed internal cut *could* create more, but origin rules and non-tariff barriers are the practical constraint. CPTPP combines already-open members (Singapore, New Zealand, Chile) with higher-MFN members; the preference margin, and so Viner's stakes, varies by pair. None of the three is Kemp–Wan by construction: they do not choose a common external tariff to freeze outsider trade.
:::
:::

## Trade wars

A **trade war** in the Johnson (1953–54) and Bagwell–Staiger sense is the Nash equilibrium of the tariff game: each country sets [[international-trade/tariffs-and-quotas#eq-opt-t]] given the other, and both end up with higher tariffs and lower (politically weighted) welfare than under a reciprocal agreement. Retaliation is not a failure of the model; it is the model.

Historical episodes that are commonly described as trade wars include the tariff escalations of the early 1930s (Smoot–Hawley in the United States, 1930, and the replies) and, in a narrower set of goods, the US–China tariff increases that began in 2018. Fajgelbaum, Goldberg, Kennedy and Khandelwal (2020) estimate that the 2018–19 US tariffs were passed through to US prices of the affected goods, with a national real-income loss relative to the previous applied rates; Amiti, Redding and Weinstein (2019) reach a similar pass-through conclusion. China's retaliatory tariffs hit US agriculture in particular, and were followed by US fiscal payments to some of the affected producers. Subsequent rounds of measures, including after 2024, are still being assessed; this chapter records the 2018–19 estimates and does not forecast later ones. Whether those tariffs were a successful terms-of-trade play, a political-economy play, a national-security play, or some mix is a dispute the estimates of pass-through inform but do not settle. The small-country diagrams say that if pass-through to domestic prices is complete, there was no ToT gain on those goods; the large-country formula says a ToT gain requires a fall in the *foreign* pre-tariff price. Both can be checked, good by good; they have been, with a range of findings across products.

The chapter's job is not to pick a winner. It is to say that the instruments of [[international-trade/tariffs-and-quotas]], used simultaneously by more than one large country, are the object Johnson named, that the GATT/WTO is a (partial, incomplete, currently strained) contract to stay off that equilibrium, and that RTAs are a (partial, preferential, Viner-ambiguous) alternative contract.

::: application From Smoot–Hawley to bindings
The Smoot–Hawley Act of 1930 raised US tariffs on a broad range of imports; Irwin (1998, 2011) discusses the magnitudes and the retaliation. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 authorised bilateral bargains, a precursor of GATT reciprocity. The GATT's subsequent rounds cut, and bound, industrial tariffs among participants to low single digits on average, with peaks remaining in agriculture, textiles (until the Multifibre Arrangement's expiry in 2005) and some consumer goods (WTO Tariff Profiles). The 2018–19 measures, and later industrial-policy tariffs, are in part a test of how much of that bound structure still constrains applied rates. Bindings that are not enforced, or that sit below applied rates only because of unused overhang, are a weaker constraint. That is a description of the legal technology, not a prediction of the next decade.
:::

::: history
**1947, GATT** signed in Geneva, 23 parties. **1948, Havana Charter** of the ITO, not ratified by the United States. **1964–67, Kennedy Round**; **1973–79, Tokyo Round**. **1986–94, Uruguay Round**; **1995, WTO** begins. **1999, Seattle** ministerial failure; **2001, Doha Round** launched, later stalled. **1999–2002, Bagwell and Staiger**, the terms-of-trade theory of the GATT. **2019, Appellate Body** ceases to hear new appeals. **2018–20**, US–China tariff increases; **2020, USMCA**; **2022, RCEP** in force; **2019, AfCFTA** legal entry into force. The chronology is institutional history; it is not a welfare ranking of the rounds.
:::

## Where this leads

This is the last chapter of the trade course. International *finance* — the current account, exchange rates, capital flows, crises — is [[international-finance]], and it is not a substitute: a tariff still has the real effects computed here whatever the exchange-rate regime, though the Mundell–Fleming short run will mix them with expenditure-switching. Development policy, industrialisation and poverty are [[development-economics]], where several of this course's models (HO, specific factors, Melitz, GVCs) reappear with different evidence. The gains from trade, the distributional costs, the instruments, the politics and the contracts are the toolkit. They do not, on their own, dictate a commercial policy.

::: summary
- The GATT (1947) and the WTO (1995) are a contract of MFN, national treatment and bindings, with listed exceptions, not a free-trade mandate ([[#def-gatt]]).
- Bagwell and Staiger: reciprocal, MFN cuts internalise a terms-of-trade externality in a politically weighted tariff game ([[#prop-bagwell-staiger]]). Commitment against lobbies is a complementary motive.
- Dispute settlement authorises withdrawal of concessions, not money damages. The Appellate Body has not heard new appeals since December 2019.
- RTAs (FTAs and CUs) are an Article XXIV exception. Viner's creation versus diversion, Kemp–Wan for CUs, and rules of origin for FTAs, govern their welfare economics ([[#def-rta]], [[#prop-kemp-wan]]).
- A trade war is Nash in the Johnson–Bagwell–Staiger game. Estimates of the 2018–19 US–China tariffs find substantial pass-through to US prices of affected goods; they do not settle the political or security motives.
- Large current RTAs include the EU, USMCA, CPTPP, RCEP and AfCFTA, with the dates and memberships recorded above.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise MFN {level=1}
Home cuts its tariff on Partner's widgets to zero and leaves the tariff on Outsider's widgets at 10 per cent. Which GATT article is in play, and what exception might Home invoke if Partner is in an FTA with Home?
::: solution
Article I (MFN) requires the zero rate to be extended to Outsider. Article XXIV is the exception if the cut is part of a qualifying FTA or CU.
:::
:::

::: exercise A binding {level=1 check="12"}
The bound rate is 25 per cent, the applied MFN rate is 13 per cent. By how many percentage points can the applied rate rise without breaching the binding?
::: solution
$25-13=12$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reciprocity in words {level=1}
In one sentence, state the Bagwell–Staiger idea of a reciprocal cut that "neutralises terms-of-trade effects".
::: solution
Each country cuts its tariff so that world prices (the terms of trade) are restored, while local prices move toward the politically efficient level; the international ToT externality is removed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Viner arithmetic {level=2 check="2"}
In [[#ex-viner]], the outsider's resource cost is 4 and the partner's is 6. What is the per-unit resource-cost increase from diversion (the number Viner would call the efficiency loss per unit, ignoring revenue and consumer surplus)?
::: solution
$6-4=2$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Kemp–Wan {level=2}
Why does Kemp–Wan require a *customs union* (a common external tariff) rather than an FTA, and what role do lump-sum transfers play?
::: solution
A single external tariff vector is the instrument that freezes extra-union trade, leaving outsiders whole. An FTA has as many external tariffs as members and cannot generally hit that target. Transfers distribute the internal gains so that every member agrees; without them, some members might lose even if the CU as a whole gains.
:::
:::

::: exercise Rules of origin {level=2}
Why does an FTA need rules of origin while a complete customs union does not, and how can those rules reduce the uptake of preferences in a GVC-intensive export (a Vietnamese electronic assembly, a Mexican auto part)?
::: solution
Without origin rules, goods would enter the FTA through the low-external-tariff member and move duty-free to the high-tariff member (deflection). A CU's common external tariff removes that arbitrage. Origin rules that require a high regional value content, or that disallow cumulation, can make a GVC slice — whose local value added is a fraction of the customs value, as in [[international-trade/firms-in-trade#ex-tiva]] — fail origin, so the exporter pays MFN after all. Utilisation rates then sit below 100 per cent.
:::
:::

::: exercise Nash versus agreement {level=3}
Two large countries set tariffs simultaneously. Using [[international-trade/tariffs-and-quotas#thm-opt-tariff]] and [[#prop-bagwell-staiger]], explain why the Nash equilibrium is not politically efficient for the two governments, and what reciprocity does to world prices. What happens if a third country is left out of the bargain and MFN is *not* applied?
::: hint
Each government counts its ToT gain and not the other's ToT loss.
:::
::: solution
Each sets $t = 1/\varepsilon^*$ given the other, counting the ToT gain on its imports and ignoring the ToT loss inflicted on the partner. Both tariffs are too high from the pair's joint political-welfare standpoint. Reciprocal cuts that hold world prices constant remove that externality. If a third country is left out and MFN is not applied, the pair can cut only on each other and push the ToT loss onto the third country — a preferential deal that may harm the outsider, which is why MFN is part of the Bagwell–Staiger package and why Article XXIV tries (imperfectly) to discipline the exception.
:::
:::

::: exercise Appellate Body {level=2}
State, as a fact, what happened to the WTO Appellate Body in December 2019, what a member can still do with a panel report, and why "appealing into the void" is a description of a legal tactic rather than of a welfare result.
::: solution
The Appellate Body lost quorum and ceased to hear new appeals after the United States blocked appointments. A panel report can still be issued; if the loser appeals and there is no Appellate Body, the report is not adopted (appeal into the void). Some members use a multi-party interim arrangement. The tactic is a fact about DSU procedure; it does not, by itself, say whether the underlying measure raised or lowered welfare.
:::
:::

::: exercise AfCFTA and intra-African trade {level=3}
Intra-African merchandise trade is a smaller share of Africa's total trade than intra-regional shares in Europe or East Asia. Using gravity ([[international-trade/trade-facts#def-gravity]]), Viner ([[#def-rta]]), and rules of origin, give three distinct reasons an AfCFTA *on paper* might not raise that share one-for-one with the legal text, and one reason it still might.
::: solution
Gravity: African GDPs are smaller and distances (and landlocked trade costs) are large, so even zero tariffs leave a smaller predicted intra-share than the EU's. Viner: if members' comparative advantages are similar and outsiders (EU, China, India) remain the efficient source of manufactures, diversion is limited and creation may be modest. Rules of origin and non-tariff barriers (the protocol agenda) can eat preferences, as in other South–South FTAs. It still might raise the share if the binding constraint was the tariff (or the uncertainty of the tariff) on goods that African members *do* produce at low cost — food staples, simple manufactures, and some services — and if origin rules are lenient enough for those goods. The legal entry into force in 2019 is the start of that test, not the result.
:::
:::

::: exercise Pass-through and the ToT {level=3}
Fajgelbaum, Goldberg, Kennedy and Khandelwal (2020) find that 2018–19 US tariffs passed through to US import prices of the affected goods. In the language of [[international-trade/tariffs-and-quotas]], what does complete pass-through to the *domestic* price, with no fall in the *foreign* pre-tariff price, say about the terms-of-trade gain on those goods? Why is that not, by itself, a complete welfare or political evaluation of the measures?
::: hint
A ToT gain requires $p_w$ to fall.
:::
::: solution
Complete pass-through with a stable foreign pre-tariff price means $p_d$ rose by (about) the full tariff and $p_w$ did not fall: there was no ToT gain on those goods, and the small-country DWL-plus-transfer diagram applies. Incomplete pass-through, or a documented fall in the foreign pre-tariff price, would be the large-country ToT channel. The finding is about one channel in one episode. A complete evaluation would add: other goods, retaliation, fiscal transfers to affected producers, any GVC incidence ([[international-trade/firms-in-trade#ex-tiva]]), and the political and security motives that [[international-trade/political-economy-of-trade]] treats as arguments of $G$ rather than of $W$. Pass-through does not score those.
:::
:::
