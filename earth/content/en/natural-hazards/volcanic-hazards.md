On the night of 13 November 1985 a volcano that had killed nobody in living memory stirred under a cap of ice in Colombia, and by the next morning the town of Armero, 47 km away and nowhere near the crater, had ceased to exist: lahars — mudflows of melted glacier water, rock and ice — buried much of the town while its people slept, and about 23,000 of its 29,000 inhabitants died. The eruption itself was small, a VEI 3 event, the kind that in Iceland or Hawaii would be a tourist attraction. Armero died because the hazard was misread — the volcano had been mapped, the lahar paths known, the warning letters written — and because the people most at risk were told the situation was "calm" in language that forbade panic and nobody told them to leave. Volcanic hazard science is, like the 1985 case, mostly not about magma. It is about the several distinct ways a volcano kills — pyroclastic density currents, lahars, falls of ash and pumice, lava, gas, debris avalanches and the tsunamis volcanoes generate ([[natural-hazards/tsunamis]]) — about how each is modelled and mapped, about the geophysical and geochemical signals that let eruptions be forecast, and about the unbearable arithmetic of deciding, at your professional judgement, whether to tell 50,000 people to leave their homes for a mountain that will, in the end, probably do nothing.

## What volcanoes actually do: products and processes

::: definition The volcanic hazard inventory {#def-hazard-inventory}
A **volcanic hazard** is a physical product or process of a volcano with some probability of occurring at a place in some time window. The inventory is short and the lethality is brutally unequal. **Pyroclastic density currents (PDCs)** — mixtures of hot gas, ash and rock fragments flowing down and away from the vent at 10–70 m s⁻¹ and 100–700 °C — are the deadliest process in recorded history: they destroyed Pompeii and Herculaneum in AD 79, killed some 30,000 people at Pelée in 1902, 43 at Unzen in 1991 (including volcanologists **Katia and Maurice Krafft** and **Harry Glicken**) and 353 at Merapi in 2010. Their lethality is total and instantaneous: burial, burns, asphyxiation, and the fact that nothing — a church, a truck, a crowd running — survives. **Lahars** are water-saturated slurries of volcanic debris, primary (eruptively generated) or secondary (a crater lake, glacier or rain-saturated deposit remobilised); they travel 10–100 km down valleys at metres per second, look and sound like wet concrete moving, and are the hazard most often *unrecognised by the people it kills*, because they occur hours to years after the eruption and follow the valleys where people farm and build. **Tephra fall** — pumice, lapilli and ash ejected into plumes and dropped over 100s–1000s of km² — kills mainly through roof collapse (wet ash on corrugated roofs), and destroys the systems modern life runs on: agriculture, water supplies, power lines, and aviation, since volcanic glass softens at 1,000–1,200 °C, just above jet-engine operating temperatures: in 1982 a Boeing 747-400 flew into the cloud of Gunong Gede and flamed out all four engines in ash of order 4 g m⁻³, relighting them only on descent. **Lava flows** are slow (metres to tens of metres per hour) and destroy property almost never people — the exceptions are the spectacular few: fast pāhoehoe at Kīlaeoa's 2018 Lower Puna eruption gave little warning at night, and lava bombs and flows have killed people who got too close or refused to leave. **Volcanic gases** — the plume is roughly 70–90 % water vapour, 10–25 % CO₂ and 2–10 % sulfur species by mass — kill by asphyxiation in topographic traps (Lake Nyos, 1986: a limnic eruption of some $1.5\times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$ of CO₂ flowed down valleys at ~20 m s⁻¹ and suffocated 1,746 people in their sleep), chronically near degenerate ground (the "mofette deaths" of the Eifel), and regionally through acid deposition and, in deep time, through mass extinction. **Flank collapse and debris avalanches** remove edifice sectors in seconds (the 1980 Mount St Helens slide, ~2.5 km³, the largest historically documented), generating megablocks, tsunamis and — critically — unloading that can trigger the eruption it accompanies. **Volcanic earthquakes and the secondary hydrology** of remobilised ash complete the list: the hazard does not end when the eruption ends, and the deposits of the 2010 Merapi eruption produced destructive lahars on every heavy rain for years.
:::

::: example The arithmetic of a lahar {#ex-armero-time}
At Nevado del Ruiz the 1985 eruption melted part of the Arenas crater glacier in under an hour; the resulting lahar entered the Lagunillas valley and reached Armero, 47 km downstream, at an estimated 10–15 m s⁻¹. How long did people in Armero have between the onset of the eruption (23:00 local time, when the ashfall began and the river "roared") and the first lahar? If the town had been evacuated on the "red" hazard map produced in 1984 — which placed Armero itself outside the red zone but the river terraces it stood on inside the historical 1595 lahar deposits — how much of the death toll was geophysics and how much was the warning chain?
::: solution
At 12 m s⁻¹, $47{,}000/12 = 3{,}900$ s ≈ 65 minutes; at 10 m s⁻¹, 78 minutes. The first flows reached Armero roughly 1.5–2 h after the eruption intensified, consistent with the travel-time arithmetic. The death toll was not a physics failure: the 1984 map was correct about the terraces, the government downgraded the alert during the night, radio calls telling people to leave were made and countermanded, and most victims died in bed because they were told the situation was calm. Of the ~23,000 dead, essentially all were in the zone the 1984 map had flagged; Armero is taught not as a forecasting failure but as a **communication and governance** failure — the case study that created modern volcanic risk practice ([[natural-hazards/risk]]).
:::
:::

::: example Mass discharge rate from volume and duration {#ex-mass-flux}
The main climactic phase of Pinatubo (15 June 1991) produced about $5\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ of dense-rock-equivalent magma in roughly 15 hours. Take the magma density as $2{,}500\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}$ and find the mean mass discharge rate $\dot{M}$ in kg s⁻¹, and then in "St Helens units": Mount St Helens 18 May 1980 reached a peak $\dot{M} \approx 9\times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{kg\ s^{-1}}$. (Enter $\dot{M}$ in $10^{7}\ \mathrm{kg\ s^{-1}}$.)
::: solution
Volume $5\times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$ × $2{,}500$ = $1.25\times 10^{13}$ kg over $15\times 3600 = 5.4\times 10^{4}$ s, so $\dot{M} = 2.3\times 10^{8}$ kg s⁻¹ $=23\times 10^{7}$ kg s⁻¹ — a mean about 2.6 times the *peak* of St Helens, which is exactly what distinguishes a VEI 6 from a VEI 5: not only ten times the volume but a sustained column tens of kilometres tall for many hours. Mass discharge rate is the variable that organises eruption-column physics: columns become buoyant-in-wind or umbrella-cloud regimes, and the tephra distribution you must evacuate for, follows from it.
:::
:::

## Forecasting eruptions: the signals and the false alarms

::: definition The three signals and the forecast hierarchy {#def-forecast-signals}
Eruptions are preceded, usually by days to months, by three measurable things. **Seismicity**: magma moving metres fractures rock, so swarms of volcano-tectonic (VT) earthquakes, long-period (LP) events and harmonic tremor trace the ascent; networks of seismometers on the edifice give locations to hundreds of metres and the sequence "deep VT swarm → shallowing → tremor → intrusion → (sometimes) eruption" is common but not universal. **Deformation**: a magma chamber inflating by $\Delta V$ tilts and lifts the surface — an inflation of $10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$ under a 2-km-wide source produces tens of centimetres of uplift and tens of microradians of tilt at a kilometre's distance — measured by tiltmeters, GNSS and InSAR; the 2018–2019 activity of Kīlauea's East Rift Zone was tracked as a dike-intrusion envelope propagating 15 km down the rift before eruption. **Gas**: the SO₂ flux of a degassing volcano (measured by the scanning DOAS traverse method of networks such as FL-CANDOS and by satellites) rises when fresh magma nears, and the CO₂/SO₂ ratio rises earlier still, because CO₂ exsolves deeper; sustained changes of a factor of 2–5 in SO₂ flux are among the most reliable precursors we have. To these add heat flow, gravity, groundwater chemistry and deformation of crater lakes. Forecasting is then organised as an **alert-level hierarchy** (Normal–Advisory–Watch–Warning in the US system, colour codes elsewhere) with written **response plans** attached to each level, because the scientific product that saves lives is not a prediction of the eruption but an *escalation of readiness* the community can act on. The base rates matter for the statistics of forecasting: of the orders of magnitude more unrest episodes than eruptions, only a few per cent culminate in eruption, so a good forecast system is judged not on being right every time but on its **error matrix** — false alarms cost money and trust, misses cost lives — and on the honest probabilities it publishes, exactly as seismic and flood hazard are expressed ([[natural-hazards/risk]], [[natural-hazards/earthquake-hazard]]).
:::

::: example The successful forecast {#ex-pinatubo}
Between April and June 1991 the Pinatubo sequence produced: a VT swarm under the summit that was initially mistaken for regional tectonic noise; andesitic dome-building with explosive degassing; LP events and tremor migrating outward; SO₂ flux reaching ~$3\times 10^{4}$ t d⁻¹ in May and then *dropping sharply* in early June — the signal that vent blockage had begun and pressurisation was moving underground. The joint PHIVOLCS–USGS team, including **Chris Newhall**, and the Aeta guides who refused to descend, pressed for evacuation of the danger zones in order of computed likelihood; some 60,000–80,000 people left before 15 June. The eruption was the second-largest of the twentieth century; perhaps 800 people died, most of them from rain-weakened ash roofs collapsing *weeks later* during the monsoon, not from the eruption. Estimate the lives saved if the fatality rate per exposed person had matched (a) Pinatubo's own pre-evacuation planning figure of "5,000–20,000 expected deaths" and (b) Armero's ~79 % mortality in the struck zone, and state what the roof-collapse deaths say about hazard definitions.
::: solution
The USGS and PHIVOLCS post-event estimates run 5,000–20,000 lives saved by the evacuation — consistent with (a), the plan's own expectation. Against (b), applying Armero's ~79 % mortality to the ~30,000 people inside the final evacuation zones implies ~20,000 deaths, close to the upper bound of the planning figure; the realised toll of ~800 is thus a factor of 25–60 below the counterfactual. The roof deaths are the lesson that the **hazard is the whole process chain**: the eruption ended on 15 June, the risk ended when the roofs were swept, and hazard maps that expire with the eruption are wrong — the 1991–92 lahar and roof-collapse deaths at Pinatubo and later at Merapi and Sinabung (which kept producing dome collapses and lahars for *seven years*) are why modern alert levels decay slowly and why "the eruption is over" is a decision, not a fact.
:::
:::

::: example False alarm, real cost {#ex-false-alarm}
In 1976 La Soufrière of Guadeloupe inflated and degassed; the scientific team advised evacuation of 73,000 people — an eruption was forecast within weeks by the consensus of the observatory, while **Haroun Tazieff** publicly argued the volcano would do nothing. There was a small phreatic eruption, 12 days after the peak of the crisis, that killed nobody; the evacuees returned, the island's economy lost a tourism season, and the episode was litigated for a decade. Suppose a forecast system issues warnings with a 30-day warning window, the prior probability of eruption given unrest of this class is 5 %, and the system's sensitivity is 90 % with a 20 % false-alarm rate. Use Bayes' theorem to find the probability that a warning is correct, and explain why both the scientists who warned at Guadeloupe and the public who resented them were behaving rationally.
::: solution
$P = \frac{0.9\times 0.05}{0.9\times 0.05 + 0.2\times 0.95} = \frac{0.045}{0.235} \approx 0.19$ — even a good system is wrong four times in five when the base rate is 5 %. Rational scientists maximise expected lives saved and accept false alarms; rational publics experiencing the cost of evacuation update against the base rate of *eruptions that follow visible unrest*, which is low. The gap is structural, and it is closed not by better science alone but by the institutional device the episode helped create: staged alert levels with pre-agreed, pre-funded responses, so that the decision to escalate is cheap enough to take on modest evidence ([[natural-hazards/risk-reduction]]).
:::
:::

## Modelling the flows: how far, how fast, what hits

::: definition Runout, hazard zonation and the two modelling styles {#def-runout-modelling}
Every volcanic hazard map is a statement about **runout** — the distance a process travels — and **intensity** within it: for PDCs flow depth, velocity and temperature; for lahars depth and force; for tephra, thickness and load. Two modelling styles coexist. **Empirical–statistical** methods extrapolate from the deposit record: plot runout against volume for 1,000 known events, take the envelope, and draw the 95 % line — robust, transparent, and the reason we know a $0.1\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ column-fed PDC will stop within ~10 km at almost any volcano on Earth. **Physically based depth-averaged** models treat the current as a gravity flow with a rheology (Voellmy–Salm: basal friction $\mu \rho g \cos\xi$ plus a turbulence term $\rho g u^{2}/\xi_{v}$ with the Voellmy parameter $\xi_v \sim 10^{2}$–$10^{4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$; lahars are often modelled as Bingham or Herschel–Bulkley fluids) integrated over real topography — programs such as LAHARZ, FLO-2D, VolcFlow, TITAN2D and PYROCLAST; they reproduce channelisation, overtopping of ridges, ponding in basins, and the counter-intuitive result that a lahar's *inundation area* depends more on volume and topography than on its uncertain rheology. The 1985 Ruiz lahar is the calibration case every lahar model is tested against, and the 2010 Merapi and 2021 La Palma events are the modern validation set. **PDC models** add the gas-particle mixture physics — column collapse sets the mass flux at the vent, and the current's mobility (the runout-to-fall-height ratio H/L, typically 0.05–0.2 for block-and-ash flows, up to 0.5 for dilute sustained currents and >1 for the laterally-expanding blasts of directed-blast geometry) then determines the map. The honest output is never a line but **zones with probabilities**: "red: PDCs of the design scenario reach here; orange: 10 % chance of overtopping the ridge".
:::

::: example Reading a tephra isopach {#ex-isopach-load}
A VEI 4 eruption drops pumice on a town 25 km downwind. The isopach map gives 0.30 m thickness there; dry bulk density of the fresh pumice is $700\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}$, and monsoon rain saturates it to $1{,}600\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}$. Compute the roof load in kPa before and after wetting (take $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}$), compare with the ~2–3 kPa collapse load of typical corrugated-metal rural roofs documented after Pinatubo and Merapi, and derive the thickness that is dangerous *in the dry* and *in the wet*.
::: solution
Dry: $0.30\times 700\times 9.81 = 2.06$ kPa; wet: $0.30\times1600\times9.81 = 4.71$ kPa — the wet load is more than double the collapse threshold of a poorly braced roof, and the dry load is already at it. Dangerous thicknesses: $2.5\ \mathrm{kPa}/(9.81\times 700) \approx 0.36$ m dry, but only $2.5/(9.81\times1600) \approx 0.16$ m wet. This is why the Pinatubo deaths were mostly post-eruption roof collapses during the monsoon, why "ash depth" maps are useless without a **load** map, and why the standard mitigation advice — brush roofs daily, brace before the rains — is written in the language of kPa, not centimetres.
:::
:::

::: warning Five ways volcanic hazard maps get misread
**"The red line is the edge of the lava."** The zones bound *processes of a design scenario*, not one future flow: PDC and lahar zones are channelised by topography and can overtop a ridge you thought protected you, and tephra zones depend on wind, which does not read the map. **"No eruption this century means no eruption this century."** Volcanic recurrence is not memoryless in the reassuring direction: at most volcanoes the hazard curve is dominated by the next event's *style*, not its date, and a system that has slept for 5,000 years can open with a flank collapse; the probabilistic maps (e.g. the GVP's Volcanic Hazard Assessment products) integrate styles with widely spread likelihoods. **"Gas is the invisible killer everywhere."** Diffuse CO₂ in low ground kills — Nyos-type limnic eruptions are the extreme — but at open volcanoes the plume is buoyant when hot and the gas hazard is local (fumarole fields, craters, depressions downwind); the regional gas problem is sulfur, health and acid deposition, not asphyxiation of cities. **"Lava is the main volcanic hazard because it is the one on the news."** Lava is the least lethal major product per unit of media coverage; the 2021 La Palma eruption destroyed some 1,500 homes and killed nobody, while Merapi 2010 killed 353 with no lava at all. **"A volcano that has been quiet since the last forecast is safe."** Escalation can be days fast (Nyiragongo 2021: the alert was raised the *day of* the eruption, correctly, because the lava lake had been inflating the ground for months), and phreatic eruptions — Tongariro 2012, Whakaari 2019, where 22 people died on a walkable crater floor — can give *no* magmatic precursor at all, which is why exclusion rules at tourist volcanoes are set by the phreatic scenario, not the magmatic one.
:::

::: history
Modern volcanology begins with **Sir William Hamilton**'s resident observations of Vesuvius and the Campi Flegrei and his *Observations on Mount Vesuvius, Mount Etna* (1776), and with the founding of the Vesuvius Observatory in **1841** by **Ferdinando II of Bourbon**, the world's first volcano observatory — an institution, not a book, which is the correct shape for a hazard that will outlast any single eruption. **Giuseppe Mercalli**'s stratigraphic work on Vesuvius and the 1883 **Krakatoa** report chaired by **George Symons** established the global, collective post-eruption survey. Of the 30,000 people of Saint-Pierre at the 1902 **Pelée** eruption only two or three are known to have survived, and the disaster — together with the same year's eruption of La Soufrière on Saint Vincent — pushed **Thomas Jaggar** to found the **Hawaiian Volcano Observatory** in **1912** on Kīlauea's rim and to invent the sustained, instrumented monitoring of an active volcano. The quantitative process physics arrived with **Alfred Perret's** plume photography and telescope photometry from 1912, **J. P. Iddings** and **Frank Perret**'s petrological surveys of Krakatoa's products, and the **Volcanic Explosivity Index** of **Chris Newhall and Stephen Self (1982)**; the modern forecasting era opened with the **1976 Guadeloupe** evacuation and the **1983** opening of the Kīlauea East Rift Zone eruption, was written into success at **Pinatubo 1991** by the joint **PHIVOLCS–USGS** team, and into grief at **Unzen 1991**, where **Katia and Maurice Krafft** and **Harry Glicken** died in a PDC, and **Montserrat 1997**, when the PDCs that killed 19 people inside the restricted zone ended any doubt that the zones must be enforced, not merely published. The **2010 Eyjafjallajökull** ash cloud, which closed European aviation for six days at a cost of some 100,000 flights, and the **2022 Hunga Tonga** eruption — whose pressure-coupled wave circled the planet — made clear that a volcano's hazards are now global systems events, monitored by satellite constellations (TROPOMI SO₂, Sentinel thermal and InSAR) rather than by one observatory on the rim.
:::

::: quiz
Which single volcanic process accounts for the largest number of fatalities in the historical record?
- [ ] Lava flows
- [ ] Tephra fall
- [x] Pyroclastic density currents (and their lahar cousins in ice- and water-rich settings)
- [ ] Volcanic gas
::: solution
PDCs and lahars dominate historical volcanic mortality — from Pompeii and Pelée through Ruiz 1985 and Merapi 2010 — because they are fast, hot or dense, channelled into exactly the valleys and shores people inhabit, and essentially unsurvivable where they go; lava, the most visible hazard, almost never kills.

::: summary
- The volcanic hazard inventory is short — PDCs, lahars, tephra, lava, gas, flank collapse, volcano-generated tsunamis — and lethality is concentrated in the fast, ground-hugging, valley-following members, not in the photogenic lava.
- Eruptions are forecast from seismicity, deformation and gas, but unrest erupts rarely: the honest product is a staged alert level with a pre-agreed community response, judged on an error matrix of false alarms against lost lives.
- Runout is bounded empirically (volume–runout envelopes from the deposit record) and modelled physically (depth-averaged gravity-flow models calibrated on Ruiz 1985); maps are probability zones for processes, not lines for one future flow.
- Tephra risk is a load problem: ~2–3 kPa kills a corrugated roof, wet ash doubles the load, and the deadliest phase of an ashfall can be weeks later in the rains.
- Gas kills locally and in topographic traps (Nyos 1986), sulfur drives the regional and global effects, and CO₂/SO₂ ratios are both a killer to map and a precursor to measure.
- Armero 1985 was a communication failure and Pinatubo 1991 a communication success: the science of volcanic risk is mostly the science of getting 60,000 people to leave before the event, and keeping them away until the slow hazards end.
- The modern frontier is systemic: ash and aviation, phreatic surprises at tourist volcanoes, satellite gas and deformation monitoring, and the multi-year tails of lava lakes, domes and lahars.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="5.8"}
A volcano 40 km from a city has produced eruptions with a mean recurrence interval of 500 years. What is the probability of at least one eruption during a 30-year design life of a new airport? Give the answer in per cent to one decimal.
::: solution
$P = 1-(1-1/500)^{30} = 1-0.998^{30} = 0.0583$, i.e. 5.8 % — five times the "1-in-100" intuition the number T = 500 invites over a 30-year horizon, the return-period trap again ([[natural-hazards/risk]]).
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="120"}
A roof is covered by 15 cm of fresh dry ash of bulk density $800\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}$. What is the load in kg m⁻²? (Give the number as kg m⁻².)
::: solution
$0.15\times800 = 120\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-2}}$ — already a third of a typical corrugated-roof collapse load, and rain will roughly double it by raising the bulk density toward $1{,}600\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}$.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="30"}
Pinatubo injected about 20 Tg of SO₂ into the stratosphere. Given that SO₂ (molar mass 64) converts to H₂SO₄ aerosol (as SO₄, molar mass 96), estimate the mass of sulfate aerosol produced, in Tg.
::: solution
$20\times 96/64 = 30$ Tg of sulfate — the aerosol layer that followed measured several km thick, lowered global mean temperature by ~0.5 °C for two years, and is the natural experiment behind every stratospheric-aerosol geoengineering estimate ([[climate-system/forcing]], [[atmospheric-chemistry/aerosols]]).
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="34.7"}
A VEI 4 climactic phase produces 0.5 km³ DRE of magma (density $2{,}500\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}$) in a steady 10-hour column. Compute the mean mass discharge rate and enter it in units of $10^{6}\ \mathrm{kg\ s^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$0.5\times10^{9}\times2500 = 1.25\times10^{12}$ kg over $3.6\times10^{4}$ s gives $3.47\times10^{7}$ kg s⁻¹ $=34.7\times10^{6}$ kg s⁻¹.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="5"}
A block-and-ash flow descends 600 m of edifice and travels 12 km from source. Compute the mobility H/L, classify it against the typical ranges (0.05–0.2 for block-and-ash flows; ≥0.5 for dilute sustained currents; >1 for directed blasts), and say whether a 15-km-long hazard corridor is defensible for this style.
::: solution
H/L $=600/12{,}000 = 0.05$, at the low (typical) end for block-and-ash flows — the runout is consistent with the style, and since empirical envelopes are drawn at the 95th percentile of H/L, a corridor of 1.5 times the modelled runout (i.e. ~15–18 km) is exactly the defensible engineering choice.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1.5"}
A crater-lake breakout lahar of 8 million m³ must be routed past a town 20 km down the valley. A 1-D model with the calibrated Ruiz rheology predicts an average velocity of 8 m s⁻¹. How many minutes of warning would a real-time lahar detector at the source give the town, and why do the Pacific-Northwest US lahar networks (Rainier, Shasta, Mount Baker) use buried piezometers that *trigger* rather than forecast?
::: solution
$20{,}000/8 = 2{,}500$ s ≈ 42 minutes — enough to stop traffic and sound sirens, not enough to evacuate a city on foot, hence the detector philosophy: for a 40-minute process the only reliable system starts the clock automatically at the source (the Cascades lahar-detection networks, operated since the 1990s by USGS and the states, are exactly this trigger-and-broadcast design).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="12"}
A 1-m-radius spherical lava bomb lands in the snow. Estimate the time for its centre to cool from 1,150 °C to the solidus ~800 °C by conduction, using the diffusive timescale $t \approx R^{2}/\kappa$ with thermal diffusivity $\kappa = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^{2}\ s^{-1}}$. Give your answer in days to the nearest whole number.
::: solution
$t = 1^{2}/10^{-6} = 10^{6}$ s $= 11.6 \approx 12$ days. The scaling $t \propto R^{2}$ is why metre-scale bombs arrive solid-crusted but plastic-cored (the "bread-crust" texture), why ballistic-field deposits preserve fluidal clasts only at the decimetre scale, and why a lava-flow crust of tens of centimetres insulates a still-molten core for weeks — the physics behind the classic accident of a person walking on a new crust into a molten cavity.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3"}
The 1986 Lake Nyos limnic eruption released an estimated $1.5\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$ of CO₂ (measured at surface pressure). If the resulting gravity current settled to a mean thickness of 5 cm of near-ground CO₂-rich air along the valleys it travelled, what area in km² could it have covered, and why does the observed 2,000-person death toll across 5 km of valleys imply the cloud was *thinner and faster* than this uniform-layer idealisation?
::: solution
$A = 1.5\times10^{5}/0.05 = 3\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^{2}} = 3\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$. A uniform 5-cm layer over 3 km² could not kill 2,000 people spread along 5 km of valley: the real cloud was a turbulent, entraining density current — metres thick and ~20 m s⁻¹ near the source, thinning and diluting downstream — killing by *pulses* in pockets where CO₂ pooled above 10 %; the uniform-layer number is a book-keeping device, and the runout–volume data of Nyos-class events are the calibration for every CO₂-hazard map at crater-lake districts of Cameroon and Rwanda.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="19"}
Using the Guadeloupe-style base rates of this lesson — prior probability of eruption within 30 days of this class of unrest 5 %, warning-system sensitivity 90 %, false-alarm rate 20 % — compute the probability that a warning is correct, in per cent to the nearest integer. (This is the number the public actually experiences.)
::: solution
$P = 0.045/(0.045+0.19) = 0.191 \approx 19\ \%$. The four-in-five false-alarm experience is unavoidable at this base rate; the policy answer is not higher thresholds (which trade lives for money) but cheaper staged responses, so that the *cost* of a false alarm at Watch level is small enough that a 19 %-correct warning is still worth issuing ([[natural-hazards/risk-reduction]]).
:::
