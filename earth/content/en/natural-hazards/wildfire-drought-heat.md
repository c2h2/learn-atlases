On 8 August 2022 the weather station at Mortagne-au-Perche in Normandy recorded 42.9 °C, breaking a record that had stood since the 2003 heatwave that killed some 70,000 people across Europe — and three weeks later the Loire River, France's longest, fell to levels where navigation closed and nuclear power plants that draw its water for cooling throttled back production. The same summer's drought browned lawns from Lisbon to Berlin, cut maize yields a fifth, and left salt water 8 km up the Garonne. France is a temperate, wet, rich country: the 2022 trio of heat, drought and the fires that followed (66,000 ha burned, the Gironde alone evacuating 36,000 people from the largest fire in modern French history) was not an anomaly to be explained away but a preview of the Mediterranean climate that southern France is acquiring. The three hazards in this lesson are the *slow-onset* trio: each is a warning-free, ramp-shaped emergency — heat that kills on the third day, drought that declares itself when the reservoir gauge is already embarrassing, fire that arrives in the hours a heatwave and a drought intersect — and each punishes the forecasting culture built for sudden events, because the disaster is not the day the thermometer peaks but the month the body, the soil or the forest cannot recover.

## The heat: how a weather maximum becomes a mortality curve

::: definition The heat–health relationship, wet-bulb limits, and the urban island {#def-heat}
Human beings are cooling machines with a 37 °C set point: metabolism produces ~100 W at rest and 300–1,000 W during work, and disposal requires evaporating roughly a litre of sweat per hour of hard outdoor labour — which fails when humidity is high, and fails absolutely at a **wet-bulb temperature** of ~35 °C, the theoretical limit of evaporative cooling (the highest reliably observed values are ~34–35 °C, briefly, in the Persian Gulf and the Indus valley; values near the limit now occur a handful of days a year on three continents and will, on high-emission pathways, become periodic in the Indo-Gangetic plain and the Gulf coast before 2100). The epidemiology is a **J (or U) curve** of mortality against temperature with a minimum around the local seasonal average — the "optimum" — and the excess-death risk at a given absolute temperature is *lower in hot climates than temperate ones*, because acclimatisation (physiological, and behavioural: fans, siestas, architecture) shifts the curve. The three amplifiers that turn a maximum into a disaster are: **humidity** (the 2003 European and 2022 Pakistani events were deadly at 40–45 °C dry-bulb partly because nights never cooled — a **night-time minimum** above ~25–28 °C denies the body its recovery, which is why heat mortality tracks the *number of consecutive days and nights* more than the peak); the **urban heat island** (cities 2–8 °C warmer than surrounding countryside at night, from stored daytime heat in masonry, reduced evapotranspiration, canyon geometry that traps longwave radiation, and waste heat — the 1995 Chicago heatwave's 700-plus deaths concentrated in the poorest, least-treeed, least-air-conditioned neighbourhoods, a pattern of **inequality of exposure** now documented for every continent); and **lag** (heat's mortality effect is concentrated in days 0–3, but the 2003 European excess of ~70,000 deaths accumulated over two weeks, and the 2021 Pacific Northwest "heat dome" — 49.6 °C at Lytton, BC, a town then 99 % destroyed by fire two days later, 600-plus excess deaths in British Columbia alone — killed people in wooden houses with no air conditioning anywhere in the province). **Heat early-warning systems** (the US National Heat Risk Tool, India's heat action plan piloted in Ahmedabad since 2013 and now in a dozen states, Europe's various national plans built after 2003) work by shifting the trigger from absolute temperature to *forecast excess mortality*, and the measured effect of the Ahmedabad model is on the order of a 30 % reduction in heat mortality for its city population — the cheapest large mortality dividend in all of hazard management.
:::

::: definition Drought: the four droughts, the indices, and the walking disaster {#def-drought}
Drought is the only major hazard with no agreed definition, because it is defined by *what it fails to do*: **meteorological drought** (precipitation below a threshold, usually the 20th percentile of a calendar period), **agricultural drought** (soil moisture insufficient for crops — which lags rainfall by weeks and is worsened by the evaporative demand a heatwave adds, so that "hot droughts" are now the norm), **hydrological drought** (streamflow and groundwater below normal, lagging by months, which is why "the drought is over" is never true the day it rains), and **socioeconomic drought** (demand exceeds supply at some cost — a 1-in-50-year hydrological drought in Singapore, with its reservoir-and-desalination portfolio, is a smaller event than a 1-in-5-year one in a city with one reservoir). The standard index is the **SPI** (McKee et al., 1993): the precipitation total over a chosen accumulation period (1, 3, 12 months) standardised against the local historical distribution, so that −1.0 is "moderate" and −2.0 "extreme" drought — its virtue is simplicity and comparability, its vice is that it ignores temperature entirely, which is why the **SPEI** and soil-moisture indices matter in a warming world, where the *demand* side of the drought balance grows ~1–2 % per decade of potential evapotranspiration even where rainfall is unchanged. The **evolution of a drought** is a three-month story: a rain deficit → soil moisture exhaustion → crop stress and fire danger → streamflow decline → reservoir and groundwater drawdown → restrictions and emergency aid — which is why drought is called the "walking disaster" or "creeping disaster": by the time it is official, the damage is done, and the 2012 US midwest drought (the "thousand-year drought" of American agriculture, ~$30 billion of damage, the largest crop-insurance payout in US history) was forecastable at the soil-moisture stage in April and was not acted upon until August. Drought's true record is in *scope*, not suddenness: the 1982–83 and 1997–98 El Niño droughts each affected tens of millions of people (the 1997–98 Indonesian fires alone released an estimated 13–26 Gt CO₂ from peat and forest fires, a large fraction of then-annual fossil emissions from China), and the 2006–10, 2012–16 and 2020–23 California events, the 2015–2020 Cape Town near-zero-day sequence (day-zero projected for April 2018; avoided by 50 % demand cuts), and the 2022–24 Horn of Africa's five failed rainy seasons (the longest such streak in 40 years of records, ~24 million people food-insecure) are the current textbook cases of a hazard whose economics are enormous and whose body counts, thanks to aid logistics, are mostly *avoided* — which is exactly how droughts become famines the next time.
:::

::: example The heat dome of 2021 and the blocked-atmosphere geometry {#example-heatdome}
In late June 2021 a quasi-stationary high-pressure system (an "omega block") sat over western North America for six days while an onshore flow pumped tropical moisture upslope. Portland hit 46.7 °C, Vancouver 49.6 °C — 16–20 °C above the June normal, values that in the local climate distribution are a 1-in-500-to-1-in-1,000-year event *in the historical record*, and which attribution analysis (World Weather Attribution) found would have been "nearly impossible" without anthropogenic warming. Explain the two dynamical ingredients (ridge amplification + soil-moisture/latent-heat feedback + orographic downslope warming) and why the *night-time* minima of 25–30 °C, not the afternoon maxima, drove the 600-plus deaths in a province where domestic air conditioning was rarer than 10 %.
::: solution
The ridge compressed and warmed descending air (adiabatic heating), clear skies added insolation, and the dry, snow-free western slopes offered no evaporative cooling — sensible heat partitioning amplified the anomaly. At night the dome acted as a lid and the urban island kept interiors above the 30 °C at which sleep and cardiovascular recovery fail; deaths clustered in attics and top floors of uncooled homes. The event's lesson for temperate coasts: the hazard is the *unprepared distribution*, not the isotherm.
:::
:::

::: example Cape Town's day-zero arithmetic {#example-capetown}
Cape Town's dam storage fell from 76 % (June 2015) to 13.5 % (February 2018), and the city published a "Day Zero" — the projected date the dams would drop below 13.5 % usable storage and 22,000 people per day would begin queueing for water — that crept from April 2018 to July 2018 and never arrived, because consumption fell from 1,000 to ~500 million litres per day. Model the system as a bucket: storage $S$ (400 GL usable at the start of 2018), inflow $I$ (winter rainfall, ~350 GL/yr in a normal year, ~150 GL in the drought's third year, arriving May–August), demand $Q$ (summer 1,000 ML/d). Why did the 2018 winter rains and the demand cut each matter more than the other, and why is "Day Zero" a risk-communication device rather than a forecast?
::: solution
In bucket terms, $dS/dt = I(t) - Q(t)$: with $Q = 1{,}000$ ML/d year-round and drought-year inflows, $S$ crosses the trigger in early 2018 — the published trajectory. Halving $Q$ saved ~180 GL over the critical year, comparable to the entire winter inflow anomaly; the two levers were of the same order, and the city pulled both. Day Zero was a *conditional* projection (given constant demand) designed to change the condition — a rare case of a forecast whose success was its own falsification, and the template now copied by other cities (São Paulo's 2014–15 crisis had no such campaign and lost a third of its reservoirs).
:::

## Fire: the ignition, the fuels, the wind

::: definition The fire triangle at landscape scale: fire weather, fuels, and ignitions {#def-fire}
A wildfire needs ignition, fuel and weather, and each term has become a policy variable. **Fire weather** is captured by the **Canadian Fire Weather Index** (Daily Severity Index, the component of the burn index) and the US **National Fire Danger Rating System**: low humidity (dead-fuel moisture below ~10 %), high temperature, and — the multiplier — **wind**, which supplies oxygen, throws embers kilometres ahead (spot fires), and drives the fire's rate of spread $R$ in the classic Rothermel (1972) formulation, itself the core of the operational **FARSITE** and WRF-SF models. The **wind fields** of catastrophe are specific: the Santa Ana winds of southern California (autumn, hot dry downslope flow of 20–40 m s⁻¹ channelled through passes — the 2018 Camp Fire, 85 deaths and 19,000 structures, spread 6,000 ha in its first five hours under such winds), the **bora** and **maestrale** of the Adriatic, the **pamperos** of the Argentine pampas, Australia's **southerly busters** and the dry change-fronts that drove Black Saturday (7 February 2009: 173 deaths, 450,000 ha, the 1,000 °C firestorm of the Kinglake corridor), and the **foehn** winds of the Mediterranean. **Fuels** are the second term: fire regimes co-evolved with vegetation, and the current disasters are fuel-structure problems — Australia's eucalypt forests are fire-adapted (eucalypt oil loads the air with volatiles; the 2019–20 Black Summer's pyrocumulonimbus storms generated their own weather, injecting smoke into the stratosphere and burning inside temperate rainforests that had never burned in human memory, charring 10 million hectares and killing or displacing an estimated 3 billion animals by the WWF's widely-cited estimate), while the US west's 100-plus years of fire suppression left forests 30–100× denser in small-diameter trees than before Indigenous burning, so that fires now consume the seed-trees and convert forest to grassland **type-conversion** territory (Colorado's 2012 High Park fire; the 2020 Oregon fires that converted 40 % of some stands). **Ignitions** are the human term: ~85 % of US and Australian fires are human-caused (powerlines — the Camp and Dixie fires both started from equipment; the 2022 Bohemian Grove and the 2018 Camp Fire powerline cases are the documented ones), lightning the rest but the lightning ones the fastest-growing under climate drying of fuels. The **fire–vegetation feedback** and the **fire–carbon feedback** close the loop: boreal peat and permafrost-adjacent fires (the 2014–24 Siberian fire season, 2023's Canadian season of 18.5 million ha burned — seven times the 1983–2022 average, with smoke crossing the Atlantic to New York in June 2023) release carbon that no forest management will re-sequester this century.
:::

::: example Black Saturday: the 3.5-hour fire {#example-blacksaturday}
On 7 February 2009 the Australian state of Victoria experienced its hottest day on record (Melbourne 46.4 °C) after a long drought, with a cool change and northerly winds of 120–180 km h⁻¹ ahead of the front. The Kinglake firestorm moved 30 km in 3.5 hours, with crown fires 60 m tall, spotting 5 km ahead, and temperatures that melted glass and steel; 173 people died, most in the open or in cars, most within 5 km of the road they were trying to drive. The Royal Commission's answer was a new "Catastrophic" fire-danger category and total-fire-ban powers. Explain why the *hour of the day* (afternoon), the *direction* (ahead of the front, winds northerly at 10 m s⁻¹), and the *fuel* (curured grass and drought-stressed eucalypt understory) made this a fire no suppression force on earth could have fought, and what fraction of the deaths were in the open or in vehicles.
::: solution
Rate of spread under those winds exceeded 100 m/min with flame lengths >50 m: radiant pre-heating 100 m ahead, embers igniting structures 5 km ahead of the front — the fire created its own wind system (fire-induced convective inflow), so "fighting" it was physically impossible; ~70 % of the deaths were people caught outdoors or in vehicles, which is why the Commission's core recommendation was *leave early or don't go* — the only effective survival strategy for a fire that outruns cars.
:::
:::

::: example The fire–drought–heat triangle: 2019–20 Black Summer {#example-blacksummer}
Australia's 2019–20 fire season burned ~18.6 million hectares, released ~900 Mt CO₂ (about 1.8× the nation's annual fossil emissions), and followed the **most severe drought on record** for the Murray–Darling basin (2017–19, the "Millennium Drought's" sequel) and the hottest spring and December on record (national December mean +2.7 °C above the 1961–90 average). The season's pyrocumulonimbus (pyroCb) storms numbered 20–30, one injecting smoke 15+ km into the stratosphere — the largest such injection measured — and generating dry lightning ignitions 300 km ahead of the fires. Quantify the triangle: given that the drought reduced live-fuel moisture to record lows, the heat extended the fire-weather season (now starting ~3 weeks earlier than the 1980s in southeastern Australia), and the pyroCb behaviour is itself wind- and smoke-coupled, explain why the three hazards of this lesson must be modelled as one system, and what the 2019–20 attribution analyses concluded about the *probability* of such a season.
::: solution
The chain is: low rainfall → dead- and live-fuel moisture at ignition thresholds → heat raising vapour-pressure deficit and day-count of fire weather → wind-driven spread → smoke and aerosol feeding pyroCb → dry-lightning ignitions and unpredictable winds. Attribution (World Weather Attribution) found climate change had made the heat and dryness of the fire season at least 30 % more likely and ~40 % more intense in the affected regions — not "causing" the fires (ignitions are human and lightning) but loading every term of the fire triangle at once.
:::
:::

::: warning The three lies of slow-onset hazards: "it's just a hot day", "the reservoirs are fine", "we've never had fire here"
**"It's just a hot day."** Heat's disaster variable is the *night*, not the day, and its victims are the un-air-conditioned, the socially isolated, the outdoor worker and the very old: the 1995 Chicago and 2003 European events were both record-temperature events *and* social-structure autopsies (the Chicago dead were disproportionately poor, Black, elderly and alone — Klinenberg's *Heat Wave* remains the definitive study). **The reservoirs are fine.** Hydrological drought lags meteorological by months and groundwater by years: the 2022 European drought revealed itself in low rivers and warm rivers before anyone's reservoir gauge was embarrassing, and the 2012 US drought was visible in soil moisture in April. **We've never had fire here.** The 2019–20 Australian fires burned temperate rainforests and alpine peat with no fire history; Greece's 2021 fires (3,000+ ignitions in August, 1.2 million ha across southern Europe's worst fire month), Siberia's 2020–24 Arctic-circle fires, and Hawaii's 2023 Lahaina fire (100 deaths, the deadliest US fire since 1918, driven by a hurricane-drought combo and invasive grass) all broke the "never here" defence. The general form of the error is treating a *stationary* distribution as the design basis: every one of these events was outside the historical envelope *of that place* and inside the envelope of the new climate.
:::

::: history From Smokey Bear to the age of fire {#history-fire}
The 20th century's fire story is a policy arc with three acts. **Act I — suppression (1910–1970s):** the "Big Burn" of 1910 (3 million ha, 87 deaths, in Montana–Idaho) made the US Forest Service a fire-fighting agency; the 1935 "10 AM policy" (all fires out by 10 a.m. the next day) and Australia's post-WWII fire-suppression doctrine built forests denser than any Indigenous fire regime had maintained for millennia — the Aboriginal cultural-burning traditions that had shaped the Australian continent were displaced after colonisation and are only now being reinstated (West Arnhem's savanna-burning programs abate ~3 % of Australia's annual emissions). **Act II — the reckoning (1988–2009):** the 1988 Yellowstone fires (320,000 ha, the end of "suppression" as a philosophy for wilderness), the 2003 Canberra firestorm (4 deaths, 500 homes, in the *national capital*), and Victoria's Black Saturday (2009, 173 deaths) forced the "prepare, act, survive" reform of Australian warning policy and the "fire-adapted community" doctrine. **Act III — the age of fire (2015– ):** the 2015–16 Canadian Fort McMurray fire (the costliest Canadian disaster, 2,400 km², 90,000 evacuated), the 2017–19 Californian megafire era (the 2018 Camp Fire; the 2020 August Lightning Complex fires), the 2019–20 Black Summer, Siberia and Canada 2023 (18.5 million ha), Hawaii's Lahaina 2023, and the 2023–24 Canadian and Greek seasons — with the result that the Western US now spends more than half its federal wildfire budget on *suppression* (a reversal of the 1995 ratio), and the scientific community's shorthand "pyrogeography" or "Pyrocene" for the new regime. The policy frontier is now the one the 1910 generation refused to consider: living with fire — prescribed burning at scale (US target 10 million ha/yr, currently ~3), utility hardening, and building codes in the wildland–urban interface (Australia's AS 3959, California's Chapter 7A) — because the fire-weather season is lengthening ~20 % per decade in the fire-prone west, and no budget can suppress a climate.
:::

Why do heat-action plans trigger on *forecast excess mortality* (or on heat that is anomalous for the locality) rather than on a fixed temperature like 40 °C?

**Because the mortality–temperature curve's minimum sits at the local normal: 32 °C kills in Manchester (2022's 40 °C killed ~3,000 excess deaths in England) while 42 °C is a routine summer day in Marrakesh.** Triggering on local anomaly (or on forecast deaths, as the US Heat Risk Tool and India's heat action plans do) fires the alarm when the *population*, not the thermometer, is at risk — and adds the two crucial lead days that acclimatisation and school-closure decisions need.

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="2.3"}
A worker at 30 °C air temperature performs 250 W of external work at 20 % efficiency, so 1,250 W of heat must be disposed of. If 100 % is removed by evaporation (no dry convection because air is near skin temperature), how many litres of sweat per hour must evaporate? (Latent heat of vaporisation ≈ 2.4 MJ/kg.)
::: solution
Heat to remove = 1,250 W = 4.5 MJ/h; ÷ 2.4 MJ/kg = 1.875 kg/h ≈ 2 L/h — the practical guidance figure quoted for hot-weather work ("up to 2 L per hour") falls straight out of the energy balance, and it is why heat-stroke cases arrive already dehydrated: no one drinks 2 L/h voluntarily.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="2.3"}
Using the inverted result that the urban heat island adds 3 °C to night-time minima, and the finding that heat-mortality risk doubles for every 5 °C of anomaly above the local optimum, by what factor does a 3-day heatwave with 6 °C anomalous nights (3 °C anomaly + 3 °C UHI) raise mortality risk?
::: solution
Doubling per 5 °C: factor = 2^(6/5) = 2.30 — the UHI's 3 °C alone contributes 2^(3/5) = 1.5×; the answer to one decimal is 2.3.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="6.9"}
A city's reservoir holds 60 GL usable in January with demand of 1,000 ML/day year-round and a winter inflow of 150 GL arriving May–August. How many months of demand in total does the available water (60 + 150 GL) cover, and what does that say about the projected Day Zero?
::: solution
Demand = 1,000 ML/d ≈ 30.4 GL/month; 210 ÷ 30.4 = 6.9 months — the stored water runs out around July–August, before the next winter refills, which is precisely how Cape Town's Day Zero was projected; the demand cut to 500 ML/d doubled the covered span to ~13.8 months, past the next rains, and that is the whole arithmetic of the averted disaster.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="250"}
A fire's rate of spread in grass under 10 m s⁻¹ wind is 60 m/min. A village lies 15 km downwind. How many minutes does the population have, and why did Black Saturday's Royal Commission conclude that this number, not the fire's size, is the design quantity for evacuation?
::: solution
15,000/60 = 250 minutes ≈ 4 hours of *fire travel* time — but minus the time to detect, confirm, decide, and the traffic throughput of the village's single road, which is why the Commission's finding was that leaving early is the only strategy that works: fires accelerate, spot ahead, and change wind, so nominal travel time is the *maximum* available, not the planning one.
:::

Canada's 2023 fire season burned 18.5 million ha against the 1983–2022 average of 3 million ha. Express the 2023 area as a multiple of the average, and if the average season released 200 Mt CO₂, estimate 2023's release assuming proportionality (the true estimate was higher, ~600 Mt, because peat and boreal fuels burn deeper per ha).
::: solution
18.5/3 = 6.2×; proportional release 6.2 × 200 = 1,240 Mt vs the ~600–1,000 Mt range published — the discrepancy is the peat/deep-burn term. The multiple to one decimal is 6.2.

::: exercise {level=2 check="360"}
An SPI-12 (12-month standardised precipitation index) of −2.0 means 12-month rainfall lies 2 standard deviations below the mean. If mean annual precipitation is 600 mm with σ = 120 mm, what was the year's precipitation, and what fraction of years does SPI ≤ −2.0 represent for a normal distribution?
::: solution
600 − 2×120 = 360 mm; P(z ≤ −2) = 2.3 % — "extreme drought" is by construction a 1-in-44-year event *in the base period*, which is the SPI's hidden assumption: if the base period warms and dries, the index silently redefines the normal.

::: exercise {level=3 check="1180"}
The 2021 Pacific Northwest heat anomaly was +16 °C over 3 days. Assume mortality follows the J-curve with baseline 20 deaths/day at the optimum and risk doubling per 4 °C of anomaly (temperate populations), and that the effect lasts 3 days with a 2-day decay tail at half risk. Estimate the excess deaths.
::: solution
Risk factor 2^(16/4) = 16× → 320 deaths/day during the 3-day peak, 160/day for the 2-day tail: total = 3×320 + 2×160 = 1,280 vs baseline 5×20 = 100 → excess ≈ 1,180 — the published BC + US excess was ~600–1,000, so the simple model over-predicts by ~20–50 % (acclimatisation and healthy-user effects); the order of magnitude is the point.

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.8"}
A pyrocumulonimbus injected smoke to 15 km in 2019–20 with an estimated emission of 900 Mt CO₂. Compare this with Australia's annual fossil CO₂ emissions (~500 Mt) as a ratio, and explain why the smoke's climate effect was *net cooling* for months despite the carbon.
::: solution
900/500 = 1.8×; stratospheric aerosol and organic carbon particles scatter sunlight (the 2019–20 pyroCb layer measurably perturbed radiative forcing for months, like a small Pinatubo), while the CO₂ warms for centuries — the timing of the two effects differs by two orders of magnitude, which is why "the fires released more carbon than the country emits" is both true and not the climate story of the season.

::: exercise {level=3 check="50"}
In a 2022 European "hot drought", potential evapotranspiration (demand) was 20 % above the 1981–2010 normal while precipitation was 20 % below. Treating drought severity as demand/precipitation, by what percentage did severity increase relative to normal?
::: solution
Normal ratio 1.0; new ratio 1.2/0.8 = 1.5 → +50 % severity from a "20 % dry year" — the demand side doubles the signal, which is why hot droughts under climate change arrive more often than precipitation-only indices (the SPI's blind spot) predict.

::: quiz
The single most cost-effective heat-protection intervention documented across the 2003-Europe and Ahmedabad experiences is:
- [ ] Spraying roads with water
- [ ] Painting all roofs white
- [x] Targeted outreach to known vulnerable people plus cooling centres and night recovery
- [ ] Banning outdoor work whenever the forecast exceeds 35 °C

**None of the glamorous options: it is *targeted outreach to known vulnerable individuals* (check-in registers, opening cooling centres, moving work to mornings) combined with night-time recovery — the Ahmedabad model, which cut heat mortality ~30 % at near-zero infrastructure cost.** The engineering answers (district cooling, reflective roofs) matter for exposure over decades; the mortality curve of a heatwave is bent in the 48 hours before it by phone calls, not by paint.

::: summary
- Heat kills on the *night* and the *anomaly*: mortality follows consecutive hot days and nights above the local optimum, with the urban island adding 2–8 °C to minima and lag of 0–3 days; wet-bulb ~35 °C is the physiological design limit, briefly reached today, periodic this century in the Indo-Gangetic plain and Gulf coasts.
- Drought is four droughts (meteorological → agricultural → hydrological → socioeconomic) with lags of weeks to years; SPI standardises the supply side, but hot droughts are demand-driven, and the "walking disaster" is only ever official after the damage — Cape Town 2018 is the model of *aversion* (demand halved), the Horn of Africa 2020–23 the model of *scope* (five failed rainy seasons).
- Fire = weather × fuels × ignitions: fire weather lengthens ~20 %/decade, fuel structures are the inheritance of a century of suppression (US forests 30–100× denser in small stems), ~85 % of ignitions are human; Black Saturday and the Black Summer show fires that create their own weather (pyroCb) and burn "unburnable" places.
- The trio is one system: heat + drought make fire, fire destroys the vegetation that buffers the next drought, and boreal/peat fire emissions (Siberia, Canada 2023: 18.5 M ha) feed the carbon loop back into all three.
- The policy frontier: forecast-based triggers (heat action plans, seasonal drought outlooks), prescribed burning at scale, utility hardening, WUI building codes, and reservoir-demand management — the slow-onset trio is won or lost in the months before the disaster, which is precisely where election cycles and news cycles do not look.
:::
