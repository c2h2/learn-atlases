On 16 January 2020, three weeks before the world stopped for a virus, the city of Jakarta was already stopping for water: the Ciliwung river had flooded a fifth of Indonesia's capital, 60,000 people left their homes, and the discussion — again — was whether to move the capital. Jakarta is the perfect opening case for this lesson because it contains every element of the risk equation in one place: a hazard (subsidence of up to 25 cm/yr in places, sea-level rise, heavy tropical rain), exposure (34 million people in the metro region, the world's largest concentration of people below sea-level-protection standards, much of it informal settlement on the river floodplains that the poor cannot leave), vulnerability (the flood deaths are concentrated in the kampungs with the weakest houses and the least warning access), and disaster risk *governance* (a flood-canal legacy from colonial times, a sea-wall mega-project called the Great Garuda now competing with the simpler option of managed retreat, and a national disaster agency, BNPB, created only in 2007 after the Indian Ocean tsunami exposed the absence of one). The 2015 Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction — the successor to the Hyogo Framework and the current global treaty — defines the field in one formula taught everywhere from Jakarta to Christchurch: risk is not the hazard. Risk is what happens when a hazard meets something exposed and vulnerable, and the only reason any rich city has a low death toll from a 2017 hurricane (Ireland, 2023 Storm Éowyn, no deaths) while a poor city has thousands (Port-au-Prince, 2010, 100,000-plus) at similar or smaller hazard intensity is the entire, unglamorous machinery of this lesson: mapping, building codes, land-use planning, insurance, early warning, and the political economy of who pays for all of it.

## The risk equation and the map

::: definition Risk, hazard, exposure, vulnerability: the quantities and the debates {#def-risk-equation}
The professional formulation (the IRDR and UNDRR lexicons) writes **risk = f(hazard, exposure, vulnerability)** and, for engineered systems, decomposes further: **risk = hazard × probability of failure given the hazard × consequences of failure**. Each term is a discipline. **Hazard** is the physical event: its intensity, frequency, duration, spatial extent — the output of the previous eight lessons of this course, expressed as return-period maps (the 1-in-100-year flood extent), PSHA ground-motion maps, or pyroclastic-density maps. **Exposure** is *what is in the way*: people, buildings, infrastructure, ecosystem services, counted by location and value — a purely accounting quantity that changes yearly and is the fastest-growing term in the risk equation worldwide (the value of assets in the US coastal 3-metre zone alone exceeds $1 trillion). **Vulnerability** is the fraction of exposed value (or people) lost given the hazard — a *function*, not a constant: the same Category 3 hurricane produces a 0.1 % and a 30 % loss rate depending on building stock, and the vulnerability function is exactly where the rich-poor difference lives (the 2010 Haiti earthquake: M7.0, the same size as the 2010 Christchurch event that killed zero — 100,000-plus deaths and 1.5 million homes because of unreinforced concrete-block construction, no code enforcement, and a health system that the quake itself removed). The **risk triangle** of insurance — hazard, exposure, vulnerability — becomes a **risk assessment** when multiplied out into a loss-exceedance curve: the probability that losses exceed $X, for all X, whose most famous failure is the 2005 Katrina flood-insurance gap (only ~15 % of Louisiana's homeowners carried flood cover before the storm, the National Flood Insurance Program having been reformed and then not enforced) and whose most famous success is the Dutch "room for the river" and flood-risk modelling regime, which since 1996 has required every flood defence to be designed against a *probability* of failure rather than a height. The great conceptual debate of the field is between the **"event" approach** (risk as expected annual loss, the engineering-economic standard) and the **"pressure and release" approach** (Blaikie and Wisner's PAR model: disasters are the point where unsafe conditions — dynamic pressures, root causes — meet a hazard; the 1985 Mexico City earthquake killed 10,000 in the collapsed colonial-district buildings and the lakebed-amplified zone while modern towers on the volcanic ground survived, a distribution of damage that is a *social* fact as much as a seismological one). Modern practice keeps both: expected-loss curves for insurance and defence design, vulnerability analysis for everything that decides *who* is unsafe.
:::

::: definition Risk mapping, zoning and land-use: the first line of defence {#def-risk-mapping}
Every serious hazard programme starts with a map, and every map is a negotiation. The workflow is fixed: (1) **hazard modelling** — deterministic scenarios (the design earthquake, the PMF flood, the maximum credible lava path) and probabilistic ones (PSHA; the 100-year flood with climate uplift); (2) **exposure inventory** — every structure, road, pipeline and person, increasingly from satellite and cadastral data; (3) **vulnerability functions** — damage matrices by construction type (the EMS-98 macroseismic classes; the HAZUS/FEMA flood-depth damage curves); (4) **risk computation** into loss-exceedance curves and spatial risk layers; and (5) **the political step: turning the map into a rule**. The rules come in a hierarchy of strength: **disclosure** (US flood-hazard disclosure at sale, the Community Rating System's insurance discounts), **zoning** (the EU Floods Directive's flood-risk maps and management plans, required every six years since 2007; Japan's disaster-prevention law zones, France's PPRT and PPR natural-risk plans that can prohibit building outright), **relocation** (the US FEMA buy-outs — 4,000-plus properties acquired in Missouri alone after repeated 1993 and 2015 floods; the 2010–19 relocation of 18,000 people out of the Colombian La Pintada danger zone), and **critical-infrastructure siting** (the post-2011 Japanese tsunami-zoning reform that moved homes and kept 20,000-plus people out of the run-up zone of the 2024 Noto event). The map's credibility is everything and its politics are brutal: the 2008 ICZC maps for New Orleans were accused of downgrading risk to keep insurance premiums and development flowing; the 2011 Christchurch land-damage zonation (red, orange, green) was litigated to the Supreme Court over whether "land damage" included future earthquake risk; and the 2023 Italian debate over the 22,000-plus municipalities with flood and landslide risk maps showed the pattern everywhere: mapping runs ahead of *enforcement* by a decade, and 40–60 % of new construction in Italian flood zones since 2018 sits inside mapped hazard areas. The honest summary from three decades of practice: **mapping works when it changes a price (insurance), a permit (zoning), or a purchase (buy-out)** — and does almost nothing when it only changes a colour.
:::

::: example The two earthquakes: Haiti 2010 and Christchurch 2010/2011 {#example-two-quakes}
On 12 January 2010 a M7.0 fault passed within 25 km of Port-au-Prince (population then ~2.8 million in the metro). On 4 September 2010 a M7.1 passed within 30 km of Christchurch (metro 380,000), and its M6.3 aftershock on 22 February 2011 passed within 5 km of the CBD. The Haitian death toll: 100,000–316,000 (the range itself a scandal of counting), 1.5 million homeless, $8 billion direct loss (58 % of GDP), and a cholera epidemic introduced by peacekeepers that killed 10,000-plus more. The Christchurch February event: 185 deaths, $40 billion loss (the costliest in OECD southern-hemisphere history), zero cholera, and a rebuilt city. Both were the same hazard class; the difference is the entire content of this lesson: construction (Haiti's unreinforced masonry and "kolé" concrete-block schools vs Christchurch's post-1976 code stock, itself only partly compliant — the 2011 deaths clustered in a handful of pre-code and non-compliant buildings, CTV and the PGC being the two towers whose failures were prosecuted), the *secondary* vulnerabilities (Christchurch's liquefaction and the CBD's demolition of 1,000-plus buildings; Haiti's hillside shanties and the near-total destruction of government and hospital capacity, which killed the survivors of the first hours), and the risk *governance* (New Zealand's Earthquake Commission insurance and the Royal Commission's building-code inquiries vs Haiti's absence of a building code since 1955 and the post-donation coordination chaos of the "Republic of NGOs"). The one number that summarises it: the same-size earthquake's death toll differs by three orders of magnitude between the two cities — which is why the WHO and IRDR define disaster risk as a property of the *society-hazard system*, not of the event.
::: solution
The comparison's analytic value is that it holds the hazard nearly constant and varies everything else: exposure (both cities were on the hazard), vulnerability functions (unreinforced vs code construction), and governance (insurance, code enforcement, emergency response, and the post-event "build back better" phase that the 2015 Sendai Framework made a formal priority). It also shows the *second* failure mode: Christchurch's loss was enormous because the exposed capital was enormous — risk can be made "rich-country" (few deaths, huge insured losses) or "poor-country" (many deaths, small GDP losses), and the Sendai target structure deliberately counts both.
:::
:::

::: example The Netherlands: designing against a probability instead of a height {#example-dutch}
For seven centuries the Dutch defended against the sea with dikes of a height decided by experience ("the highest water plus a bit"). After the 1953 North Sea flood (1,836 deaths in Zeeland, the delta works that followed being the largest coastal-engineering project in history), the 1996 and 2009 Flood Protection Acts went further: every dike ring must meet a **failure probability** — 1-in-10,000 per year for the western lowlands — assessed by the national "Toetsingskader" with modelled water levels, wave overtopping, pipe erosion and (since 2014) climate scenarios, and rings failing the test enter the national reinforcement programme with ~€7.3 billion budgeted. Explain the conceptual revolution (from design height to reliability), the "room for the river" complement (29 projects, 800 million m³ of extra floodplain given to the rivers between 2006 and 2019 after the 1993 and 1995 near-evacuations of 250,000 people), and the 2021 policy step that admits the terminal problem: what does a probability standard do when the distribution itself moves?
::: solution
Reliability-based design forces every link — crest height, wave run-up, seepage, piping, and the models' own uncertainties — into one exceedance-probability budget, and makes the standard a *policy choice* (1-in-10,000 is a judgement about acceptable risk, not a measurement). Room for the River attacks the other side of the equation (conveyance and floodplain storage instead of only containment). The terminal problem is answered by the 2021 Delta Programme's "adaptive pathways": the standard is now paired with pre-agreed triggers for future upgrades, because a fixed probability against a non-stationary climate is a slowly widening lie — the same lesson the US FEMA 500-year mapping and the Swiss hydrological design standards are learning.
:::
:::

::: example Tohoku's warning and the land-use end of the chain {#example-tohoku-chain}
The 2011 Tohoku earthquake generated the world's best early warning: the JMA issued the emergency earthquake alert 3 seconds after the P-wave arrived (the 73 seconds of P–S lead time reaching Tokyo's shinkansen, which stopped without a derailment), and the tsunami warning — "Major tsunami, 3 m and above" — within 3 minutes of the rupture, radio and TV cutting in nationwide. The run-up exceeded every design: 14.1 m at Miyako, and the wave overtopped or undermined 100-plus of the ~400 coastal defence systems built to the pre-2011 standards, including the 13-m Kamaishi seawall whose notches were designed to split waves (it worked; the harbour town behind it was still flooded). Roughly 90 % of the ~19,500 dead and missing were tsunami victims, and studies of the affected prefectures found mortality in the *designated safe zones* and behind the tall seawalls was not lower than elsewhere — the vertical-evacuation buildings were there, but the evacuation *behaviour* and the land use behind the wall had not kept pace. Post-2011, Japan passed the 2013 tsunami-countermeasure zones law: 514 municipalities zoned by 2020, with building restrictions in the deepest run-up zones and 10-m-plus elevation projects at Rikuzentakata's pine belt. Trace the four-phase chain through this event and identify which link the deaths actually concentrated in.
::: solution
Detection and dissemination performed at world-best levels; the failure was the fourth link, *response capability* — evacuation completion (the wave arrived 18–30 minutes after the quake in the worst villages, and the designated routes and safe zones were wrong) and the land-use decision that let thousands live *behind* a seawall whose design height the event doubled. This is why the chain's endpoint is zoning: the warning tells people to leave; only the map decides whether their house was ever allowed to be in the run-up.
:::

## The warning chain, the code, and the money

::: definition Early warning: the four-phase chain and the last mile {#def-early-warning}
The World Meteorological Organization's four-phase chain — **risk knowledge → detection and monitoring → warning dissemination → response capability** — is a statement that a forecast is not a warning: a perfect cyclone track kills no one if the fisherfolk on the sandbars never hear it or don't believe it or have nowhere to go. The success story that defines the chain is **Bangladesh**: the 1970 Bhola cyclone killed 300,000–500,000; the 1991 cyclone killed 138,000; the 2007 Sidr cyclone killed ~3,500; the 2017 "out-of-season" cyclone and the 2020 Amphan cyclone (10 million evacuated, ~100 deaths from a Category 4 landfall) completed the curve. The intervention was the chain, not any link: India and Bangladesh's cyclone-warning multiplication (one to six warnings per event, in local dialects, through volunteers, mosque loudspeakers, cell-broadcast), 12,000-plus cyclone shelters and raised embankments, and the civil-defence volunteer network — a 3,000-fold reduction in deaths-per-landfall-unit-exposure at trivial cost per life, and the empirical foundation of the UN's "Early Warnings for All" initiative declared in 2022 (target: every person on Earth covered by early warning by 2027, with only ~50 % of the world's WMO members currently able to issue multi-hazard warnings and only ~40 % of the least-developed countries having multi-hazard systems). The failure modes are equally instructive and now formally named: the **warning-behaviour problem** (evacuation rates for US hurricane mandatory orders run 0–80 % depending on storm perception, past experience and order level — the 2005 Katrina evacuation occurred despite orders because the storm was forecast to miss New Orleans, and the 2017 Irma "mis-forecast" that made Florida's Governor's evacuation order look overcautious is now understood as a reason to *keep* ordering early), the **lead-time problem** (flash floods and tornadoes leave minutes; the 2021 European floods saw cell-broadcast failures in Germany's warning app NINA and the total collapse of the Arnsberg district's siren network, the public learning from WhatsApp groups instead), the **false-alarm budget** (the 2018 Hawaii missile-attack false alarm cost measurable trust in 383 seconds), and the **last-mile asymmetry** (the 2011 Tohoku warning arrived within 3 minutes of the quake and evacuated most of the coast — and over 4,000 people still died, many in the designated safe zones that the run-up reached: the warning chain ends only where the *land-use decision* ends, which is why Japan re-zoned after 2011). The frontier is **impact-based warning** — saying "this flood will reach 1.2 m in this neighbourhood" rather than "30 mm of rain" — now operational in the UK (the joint Met Office–Environment Agency flood warnings name the affected streets), Australia's Fenner school, and the EU's EFAS-to-national-service chain.
:::

::: definition Codes, insurance and finance: the political economy of prevention {#def-codes-finance}
**Building codes** are the single largest mortality-reducer in the hazard toolkit when enforced: Turkey's 2000 code (post-1999 İzmit, 17,000 deaths) was in force for the 2023 earthquakes — and the 50,000-plus deaths nonetheless concentrated in pre-2000 stock and in *new* buildings whose collapse was traced to under-reinforced detailing and non-enforcement, the prosecution files running to thousands of contractors; the lesson the field states bluntly is that **a code is a construction-inspection-and-licensing system, and without enforcement it is a document**. New Zealand's post-Christchurch unit-title litigation, Italy's post-L'Aquila (2009, 309 deaths) criminal convictions of risk-commission scientists *and then* of builders (the 2012 appeal acquittal of the scientists being the famous "great commission case" on the impossible legal position of communicating scientific uncertainty), and California's progressive Chapter 7A wildland-urban-interface glazing and venting code (the only US state-level code element proven to cut structure loss in the 2017–21 fire siege) show the three code battlegrounds: existing stock, enforcement, and liability. **Insurance** prices risk if allowed to, and is suppressed by politics nearly everywhere: the US National Flood Insurance Program (created 1968, now ~$20 billion federally indebted, its pre-2012 "grandfathered" premiums covering ~20 % of true risk, its 2012 and 2019 reforms toward full-risk pricing producing exactly the predicted political backlash over Louisiana and Texas maps) is the canonical case of **subsidised exposure growth**; Germany's ~46 % household flood-insurance take-up (mandatory in the south, near-zero in the north, and the 2021 Ahr-valley losses of ~€30–40 billion uninsured being converted into a live federal mandate debate) shows the same experiment run at the state level. The **risk transfer** stack above insurance — catastrophe bonds (a ~$20 billion market by 2024), the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility's parametric payouts (triggered by wind-speed at the station, paid in 14 days, and criticised for basis risk when the 2017 storms' payouts were a fraction of losses), and the African Risk Capacity — is genuinely fast for fiscal liquidity and genuinely thin for the actual loss. The **resilience-investment** case is now quantified by the multi-country studies the field relies on: the US NIST Multi-Hazard Mitigation Council's 2019 update finding ~$6 of avoided loss per $1 of mitigation (the original 2005 "1:4" figure, the one used by US grant programmes), the World Bank's lifecycle analyses of new buildings in earthquake countries (1:5 to 1:10 for code-compliant construction), and the standing finding that **prevention is chronically underfunded relative to response** — the global humanitarian-to-reduction ratio running roughly 7:1 in the opposite direction of every cost-benefit study, which is a governance fact, not an economics one.
:::

::: example The warning chain that worked: Bangladesh's cyclone programme {#example-bangladesh}
Trace the 2020 Amphan cyclone through the four-phase chain: India's IMD issued the first alert 72 hours out and the "extreme severe" warning 24 hours before landfall (peak gusts 185–195 km/h, a 2–4 m surge forecast for the coast); Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme mobilised 75,000-plus volunteers with loudspeakers and flags through the night; 2.4 million people moved into 14,000-plus shelters in one of the largest peacetime evacuations in history; landfall was at 16:00 on 20 May. Deaths in Bangladesh: ~20–100 (against 300,000–500,000 for the comparable 1970 event); in India, ~100 (against 10,000 for the 1999 Odisha super cyclone). Identify the two chain links that did most of the work, the residual vulnerability that remained (the housing stock: most shelter deaths and nearly all of the ~$13 billion loss were damage to *homes*, not drownings), and why the programme's cost (~$0.5 per person covered per year for the volunteer network) makes it the highest return-on-investment intervention in this entire course.
::: solution
The detection-forecast link (IMD's intensity and surge skill, now 5-day tracks) and the dissemination link (the CPP's human network, which converts a scientific warning into a household decision) did the work; the response-capability link (shelters and raised platforms) caught the failures of the first two in 1991 and 2007. The residual is exposure+vulnerability in housing: the shelters saved the people while the surge took their houses — which is exactly the Sendai Framework's argument that warning without resilience leaves the loss curve untouched even as the death curve collapses.
:::
:::

::: warning The four failure modes of risk reduction: the map nobody enforces, the code nobody inspects, the insurance that hides the risk, and the warning nobody heeds
Every intervention in this lesson has a documented failure mode, and pretending otherwise is how programmes fail twice. **The unenforced map**: Italy's 22,000 flood/landslide-mapped municipalities and continued building in mapped hazard areas; the US NFIP's mapped Special Flood Hazard Areas with ~70 % of at-risk homes uninsured and building continuing. **The uninspected code**: Turkey 2023 (code on the books, collapses in the same city — the failure was inspection and the "zoning amnesty" purchases of irregular construction in 2018); Haiti's 1955– codelessness being the terminal case. **The suppressed insurance price**: the NFIP's debt and the resulting development in its subsidised maps; the ~$14 trillion of global uninsured natural-catastrophe exposure (the Swiss Re and Marsh market reports) being both a protection gap and a hidden subsidy to exposure growth. **The ignored warning**: the 2021 Ahr valley (sirens down, app overloaded, the flood arriving overnight in a region that "doesn't flood"), the 2017 Las Vegas shooting-adjacent mass-notification failures, and the general rule from evacuation research that a warning competes with the recipient's mental model of the world and often loses — which is why the field now designs for *trust, specificity, and source-consistency* (the risk-personification and impact-based-warning research programmes) rather than for more decibels. The meta-failure is **the prevention discount**: every one of these instruments pays off in avoided events — invisible by construction — while response pays off in televised rescue, which is why the global risk-finance ratio runs backwards from every cost-benefit study and why the Sendai Framework's monitoring exists at all.
:::

::: history From disaster response to disaster risk reduction: the fifty-year arc {#history-rr}
The institutional history is short and steep. **1970s–80s — the response era:** the 1971 IDNDR predecessors, the UNDRO (1971) as a coordination office, and the US creation of FEMA (1979) — disaster management as *response logistics*. The pivot came from the developing world: the 1982–85 African famine response and the 1985 Mexico City earthquake (which produced both the urban-vulnerability research agenda and the discovery that *volunteers* do the first-day rescue everywhere) fed the UN General Assembly's 1984 Convention on the Safety of Nuclear Installations-adjacent debates and, decisively, the **1987 World Commission on Environment and Development** linked disasters to (un)sustainable development. **1990s — the framework era:** the **1989 IDNDR** (International Decade for Natural Disaster Reduction, resolution 44/236) made "mitigation" and "preparedness" legitimate UN business; the 1994 Yokohama Strategy added vulnerability; the 1995 Decade's mid-term review produced the **FEMA-to-DRR vocabulary** now standard. The 1990s were also the decade of the wake-up calls — the 1991 Bangladesh cyclone (138,000 deaths, which *started* the warning-chain programme), the 1995 Kobe earthquake (6,400 deaths in Japan, ending the myth of the fully protected rich country), the 1998 China Yangtze floods (3,000–4,000 deaths, 18 million homes) and Hurricane Mitch (11,000 deaths in Central America, which the World Bank used to argue the debt-for-nature and prevention case). **2000–2015 — the Hyogo era:** the 2000 disbanding of the IDNDR and creation of UNISDR; the **2005 Hyogo Framework for Action** ("The Better Build Back" clause after the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, 168 countries endorsing "national strategies for DRR"); the 2005–2015 decade that produced national disaster agencies in 130-plus countries (Indonesia's BNPB 2007, the Philippines' NDRRMC 2010 after Ondoy), the first global loss databases (EM-DAT, DesInventar), and the quantified prevention case (the 2005 NIST Multi-Hazard Mitigation Council 1:4 study). **2015– — the Sendai era:** the March 2015 **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030** (adopted in Sendai four weeks before the Paris Agreement, its four priorities and seven global targets explicitly *outcome* targets — "substantially reduce disaster deaths" and "number of affected people" rather than "build more shelters"), the 2015 Paris Agreement's Warsaw International Mechanism on Loss and Damage (disasters and slow-onset events formally entering climate politics), the 2015 Kathmandu "Build Back Better" (the phrase coined after the 2010 Haiti experience and formalised in the 2015 Sendai framework), the 2017–2022 war-in-disasters and pandemic years that exposed the "disaster-DRR-climate" silos, and the 2022 UN **Early Warnings for All** initiative — the 2025 mid-review finding that the world is *not on track* for the mortality and loss targets even as the warning-coverage line rises steeply, the gap being exactly the last-mile and vulnerability terms of this lesson.
:::

::: quiz
A city doubles its river's levee standard from the 20-year to the 100-year flood and, over the following decades, its floodplain population triples. In risk terms, this is the textbook case of:
- [ ] Residual-risk elimination
- [x] The levee effect: protected-area development raising exposure until total risk exceeds the pre-defence level
- [ ] Vulnerability displacement
- [ ] The Sendai target-achievement pathway
::: solution
**The levee effect (or "safe development paradox")**: protection suppresses the *perceived* risk, which raises the exposure the next exceedance must cash — documented for the US NFIP-mapped floodplains (protected floodplain development grows faster than unprotected), the French dyke systems whose 2003 and 2021 failures hit villages that grew behind them, and the 1927 Great Mississippi Flood following a century of levee-boosted delta development. The defence-era answer is the Dutch one: pair every defence with the *residual-risk* plan (evacuation, land-use, insurance) so the protected area keeps a memory of the water.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="63"}
A levee is designed for the 1-in-100-year flood. Over a 100-year century, what is the probability (in %, nearest integer) of at least one overtopping?
::: solution
$1 - 0.99^{100} = 1 - 0.366 = 0.634$ — 63 %: "designed for the 1-in-100-year event" means roughly even odds of being exceeded within the design century itself, which is why the return period is a statement about acceptable annual risk, not a promise.

::: exercise {level=1 check="26"}
Using the same 1-in-100-year levee: what is the probability of at least one overtopping in 30 years, as a percentage to the nearest integer?
::: solution
$1-0.99^{30} = 26\%$ — the point taught with this number is that "1-in-100" means a 26 % chance within a single 30-year mortgage, which is why the design return period is a *policy* about acceptable annual risk (1 %/yr here), not a promise.

::: exercise {level=1 check="3000"}
Bangladesh's cyclone deaths fell from 300,000 (1970) to 100 (2020 Amphan) while exposure grew several-fold. Compute the crude deaths-per-event ratio 1970:2020 and state why the ratio *understates* the achievement.
::: solution
300,000/100 = 3,000-fold; it understates because the 2020 coastal population, density and warning-era exposure are several times 1970's — the deaths-per-capita-exposure reduction is larger still, and the 1991→2007→2020 sequence (138,000 → 3,500 → ~100) tracks the chain's construction phases, not luck.

::: exercise {level=2 check="4"}
The NIST Multi-Hazard Mitigation Council's 2019 update found $6 avoided for $1 spent (the 2005 study found $4). For a national mitigation budget of $2 billion/yr, compute the avoided-loss difference between the two ratios over a decade, and identify the two reasons the ratio is *higher* in the 2019 update.
::: solution
$(6-4)\times2\times10 = \$40$ billion of avoided loss over the decade; the 2019 update's higher figure reflects (1) inclusion of risk-reduction co-benefits (energy, amenity, emissions) and (2) better loss-avoidance attribution using post-event counterfactuals (e.g. the 2011–17 US hazard-mitigation grant programme data). The honest caveat: the ratio varies from ~1:1 (some floodplain buy-outs) to >1:10 (earthquake code retrofits in dense poor cities), and the *budget* decision is about the portfolio, not the average.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.01"}
A city's flood risk: annual exceedance probability 1 % for the design flood, failure probability of the defence given the design flood 0.2, consequence $5 billion. Compute the expected annual loss, and explain why the *product* structure means a defence upgrade from 0.2 to 0.05 failure probability saves more than a levee height increase that moves the exceedance probability from 1 % to 0.8 %.
::: solution
EAL = 0.01 × 0.2 × 5G = $10 million/yr. The upgrade to 0.05 cuts EAL to $2.5M (saving $7.5M/yr); the height increase to 0.8 % cuts it to $8M (saving $2M/yr) — because the reliability term is the one the defence actually controls, and the 1 % is climate-shifting anyway. The ratio of savings is 3:1, and the expected annual loss asked for is 0.01 × 0.2 × 5 = 0.01 — $10 million/yr, or 0.01 in $bn.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="100"}
In the 2023 Turkey earthquakes, the vast majority of the 50,000-plus deaths occurred in buildings built before the 2000 code. If the affected provinces held 2.1 million dwellings, 70 % pre-2000, and the collapse/death concentration was ~100× the post-2000 stock's rate, estimate the share of deaths that fell on the pre-2000 stock (assume equal per-building exposure to shaking for the two stocks and a post-2000 death share of ~1 %).
::: solution
Pre-2000 share of deaths ≈ (0.7×100)/(0.7×100 + 0.3×1) = 70/70.3 = 99.6 % — the arithmetic of why "the code works but enforcement failed" is the accepted reading: the code-era stock performed, and the prosecutions concentrated on pre-code and fraudulently-built stock. The check value is the pre-2000 death share in %, rounded: 100.

::: exercise {level=3 check="2000"}
Germany's post-2021 flood uninsured loss was ~€30–40 billion against ~46 % household take-up. If mandatory flood insurance had covered the whole country at actuarially fair rates and the fair premium is 0.5 % of insured value per year, what insured value does the loss imply (midpoint €35 billion, assume the insured stock would have suffered the same physical loss), and what does the 0.5 % premium mean for a €400,000 house?
::: solution
The fair premium on a €400,000 house is 0.005 × 400,000 = €2,000/yr — the price signal that the current political economy avoids (and with a 1-in-100 event losing ~20 % of value, the exposed stock implied by the €35G loss is ~€175G in the affected areas). The 2021 losses converted into a mandate debate rather than a premium debate, which is precisely the NFIP's lesson re-run in Germany.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.9"}
A warning system covers 1 million people at $0.5/person/yr and reduces cyclone mortality by 95 %. A cost-effectiveness study values a statistical life at $50,000 (the World Bank's DRR-appropriate value for low-income countries) and expects 200 deaths per decade without the system. Compute the system's decade cost, the decade deaths averted, and the benefit-cost ratio.
::: solution
Cost = $0.5M/yr × 10 = $5M; deaths averted = 190; benefit = 190 × $50k = $9.5M; BCR = 1.9 — and the number is deliberately modest: the same study with high-income VSL ($10M) gives BCR ~380, which is the ethical fault line the field argues about openly (the VSL choice, not the epidemiology, determines whether saving poor lives "pays").

::: exercise {level=3 check="7"}
The 2015 Sendai monitoring reports "affected persons" as a target. If a country's disaster deaths fall 40 % over a decade while its "affected persons" count rises 30 %, give two legitimate interpretations (one about counting, one about residual risk) that the field actually debates.
::: solution
Counting: improved reporting and expanded definitions (small events now counted; the DesInventar effect) raise the count while the hazard's true toll falls. Residual risk: mortality reduction is the *easiest* target (warning chain), while exposure growth means more people are damaged without dying — exactly the Dutch "rich-country risk profile" (few deaths, rising losses) and the reason Sendai counts deaths, people affected, economic loss and disaster frequency as four separate targets. The countable answer: the Sendai Framework has 7 global targets.

::: summary
- Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability: the Haiti–Christchurch comparison holds the hazard constant and shows three orders of magnitude in outcome — risk is a property of the society-hazard system, and the vulnerability term is where the deaths live.
- Risk maps work only through instruments: disclosure, zoning (EU Floods Directive), buy-outs, and infrastructure siting; mapping runs a decade ahead of enforcement, and the "levee effect" shows protection without residual-risk planning can raise total risk.
- The warning chain (knowledge → detection → dissemination → response) is the best-documented success in the field: Bangladesh's 3,000-fold cyclone-death reduction at ~$0.5/person/yr; its failure modes (trust, lead time, last mile) are why impact-based warning and post-2011 Japanese re-zoning now close the loop into land use.
- Codes are enforcement systems, not documents (Turkey 2023, Haiti's codelessness); insurance prices risk or hides it (the NFIP's $20bn debt, Germany's 46 % take-up and €30–40bn uninsured loss); prevention's benefit-cost ratios (1:4 to 1:10) coexist with a 7:1 funding bias toward response — a governance failure, not an analytical one.
- The arc from 1970s response logistics through the 1989 IDNDR, Hyogo 2005 and Sendai 2015 (seven outcome targets, Build Back Better) to Early Warnings for All 2022 is a half-century of learning that disasters are designed — by what we build, where we allow it, who is warned, and what we insure — which is also the honest statement that they can be un-designed.
:::
