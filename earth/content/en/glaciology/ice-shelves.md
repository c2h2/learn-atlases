Between 31 January and 7 March 2002, an ice shelf the size of Delaware came apart. The Larsen B Ice Shelf had stood — perhaps for twelve thousand years, since the last deglaciation — as a 700-metre-thick raft of ice larger than Delaware, holding back a hundred glaciers that should have been flowing to sea. In 2002, in one warm austral summer, meltwater ponds opened cracks that had been frozen shut for millennia, the cracks ran, and in thirty-five days $3{,}250\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ of shelf disintegrated into an iceberg field so dense that ships could not cross it. Within five years the glaciers behind it had doubled their speed, their catchment had begun to thin, and the sea-level signal had begun. The Larsen B collapse converted the ice-shelf question from a curiosity of Antarctic geography into the central dynamical problem of sea-level science, because it demonstrated, at continental scale and on human timescales, the principle this lesson is about: **floating ice raises no sea level when it melts, but it holds back the ice that does** — and the hold can fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the strength of the ice.

## What shelves are and what they do

::: definition The floating margin {#def-shelf-function}
An ice shelf is a permanent floating platform of ice, fed by glaciers and snow, lost by calving and basal melt, ranging from a few metres to more than $1000\ \mathrm{m}$ thick, and from tens of square kilometres to the Ross Ice Shelf's $487{,}000$ — an area half again larger than Spain. Antarctica's shelves total $\sim 1.5\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ — an area larger than Alaska — and they do three jobs. They **buttress**: the membrane forces and margin drag analysed in [[glaciology/ice-flow]] transmit a backstress to the grounding line that can cancel half or more of a glacier's driving stress, throttling discharge. They **set the calving pattern**: a broad, confined shelf fails at its outer front in slow, regular seasons of bergs; a narrow, unconfined one fails catastrophically. And they **armour the coastline**: the shelf absorbs the ocean's swell and the ice mélange — the stiff traffic-jam of brash ice, bergs and sea ice that fills many fjord mouths — supplies the same buttressing at fjord scale, where a mélange a few hundred metres thick pressing on a terminus can hold back a glacier as effectively as a rock sill. The quantitative statement of the first job is the scaling used throughout this atlas: backstress $\sim \tau_c L/P$ (margin strength × length ÷ width), which is why **small, steep-sided shelves in narrow embayments are the strongest brakes ever built by ice**, and why their loss — Larsen A 1995, Larsen B 2002, Wilkins 2008, and the 2017 calving of the $5{,}800\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ iceberg A-68 from Larsen C's weakening rift — is followed, every time, by glacier acceleration measured in months.
:::

::: example The brake's ledger {#ex-brakeledger}
A shelf $250\ \mathrm{km}$ long and $50\ \mathrm{km}$ wide with margin drag $\tau_c = 40\ \mathrm{kPa}$ buttresses a glacier whose grounding-line driving stress is $100\ \mathrm{kPa}$. Find the backstress capacity $\tau_c L/P$, note that systems settle at the largest backstress the margins can carry (here, observed analogue geometries cancel $\sim 70$ per cent of the drive), and find the flux change if shelf thinning halves the backstress, taking flux $\propto \tau^{3}$ (sliding-limited).
::: solution
The capacity is $\sigma_b = \tau_c L/P = 4\times10^{4}\times250/50 = 200\ \mathrm{kPa}$ — more than the driving stress itself, which is why confined shelves hold their glaciers so completely; take the operating backstress at the observed analogue of $70$ per cent cancellation. Halving it raises effective driving stress from $30$ to $80\ \mathrm{kPa}$, a factor of $2.7$, and the flux by $2.7^{3} \approx 20$. The arithmetic is deliberately brutal because the observation is: the post-collapse speed-ups behind Larsen B ($\times 2$–$10$), and the modelled response of a fully unbuttressed Thwaites ($\times$ several to $\times 10$), live inside this range. A shelf is not a wall but a **throttle with a cubic pedal**.
:::
:::

## How shelves die

::: definition Three failure modes {#def-failure}
Shelves fail by **surface water**, **ocean water**, and **geometry**. **Hydrofracture**: meltwater ponds — which now cover the Larsen and Wilkins shelves and, in extreme summers, patches of the Ross — fill existing crevasses to the waterline, and since water transmits its full weight to the crack tip, a water-filled crevasse propagates to $\sim 1.3$ times the depth a dry one reaches, and once water reaches the flotation-driven basal cracks, the shelf is cut into blocks that calve, capsize and shatter: the Larsen B sequence — ponds, rifts, disintegration in weeks — is the mechanism as discovered by **Chris Scambos** and colleagues in 2009, reading the event's own imagery. **Ocean melt**: warm water masses (circumpolar deep water on the Amundsen shelves, and in a warming world progressively shallower water masses) attack the base, thinning the shelf, raising its front, ungrounding its pinning points and shrinking its extent — the slow mode, running at tens of metres of ice per year in the Amundsen Sea, that thinned Larsen C's ice tongue enough to let the 2017 A-68 rift propagate. **Rift and mélange failure**: rifts grow by tidal flexure and meltwater, and when a rift crosses an ice tongue the shelf calves a berg the size of a county in a day; at fjord scale, the mélange — weakened by thinner, weaker sea ice from the Arctic's own retreat — stops pressing, and the glacier accelerates within the season, as at Jakobshavn after the mélange thinned by some 70 per cent in the 2010s. The three modes interact: ocean melt thins and unconfines, which lets ponds spread, which fractures, which removes the brake — a **cascade** whose end state, for the Antarctic Peninsula's shelf system, is the ice-free configuration that modelling and the peninsula's own Holocene history both indicate is stable at $+4\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ regional warming, a level the peninsula has already reached.
:::

::: example A shelf's steady-state budget {#ex-shelfbudget}
A shelf is fed by ice streams at $2000\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$, spans $L = 250\ \mathrm{km}$ from grounding line to calving front, thins from $800\ \mathrm{m}$ at grounding to $200\ \mathrm{m}$ at the front, and gains $300\ \mathrm{mm\,w.e.\,yr^{-1}}$ of snow. Find the depth-averaged basal melt rate, assuming steady state.
::: solution
In steady state the thickness budget averaged over the shelf is
$$
u\,\frac{\Delta H}{L} = a + b ,
$$
with $\Delta H/L = 600/2.5\times10^{5} = 2.4\times10^{-3}$, so the flux divergence removes $2000\times2.4\times10^{-3} = 4.8\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ of ice. Snowfall returns $0.3$, and the balance — $4.8 - 0.3 = 4.5\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ — must be basal melt, averaged over the whole shelf. The number is the quiet scandal of shelf science: a healthy, "stable" great shelf melts from below at metres per year **invisibly**, under tens of thousands of square kilometres, and only the thinning rates measured by satellite laser and radar altimetry since the 2000s reveal which shelves have had that number nudged upward by warm water — the Amundsen shelves, where the same budget now balances at tens of metres per year near the grounding lines.
:::
:::

::: example Reading a collapse from the imagery {#ex-larsenb}
Larsen B's remnant shelf ($3{,}250\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$) disintegrated over 35 days, producing a debris field of blocks averaging $50\ \mathrm{m}$ thick. Estimate the mean daily area loss, the mean daily volume loss, and explain why the event's sea-level contribution was **indirect** — and why that made it the most important indirect event in glaciology's modern history.
::: solution
$3250/35 = 93\ \mathrm{km^{2}\,day^{-1}}$; volume $93\times0.05 = 4.6\ \mathrm{km^{3}\,day^{-1}}$ of ice — a flow twenty times the Rhine's discharge every day for a month, and **zero** direct sea-level rise (Archimedes: the shelf was already floating). The contribution came afterwards: the hundred-plus glaciers behind the shelf sped up two- to tenfold, their catchments thinned, and the region went from roughly balanced to losing $\sim 10\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$ — real sea level, from a floating shelf's death, arriving with a lag of years. That lag is the pedagogical power of Larsen B: it demonstrated the **buttressing→discharge→sea-level** chain end to end, in the satellite record, in real time, and it is the template against which every shelf-loss scenario since — Wilkins, George VI, the Ronne-Filchner thought experiments — is read.
:::
:::

## Buttressing in the model, instability in the map

::: proposition The shelf-collapse instability chain {#prop-chain}
The chain — shelf loss → acceleration → thinning → grounding-line retreat → further loss — is a genuine **positive feedback**, and its geography is the sea-level risk map. The strongest real-world buttresses are the **ice rises**: where a shelf grounds on a shoal (the Ross's Roosevelt Island, the Berkner Island rises, Thwaites' Eastern Ice Rise), the pinning point concentrates backstress and can alone hold a glacier's throttle; satellite radar has measured Thwaites' eastern pinning point **losing contact** as the grounding line retreated past it in the 2000s–2010s, an event that model experiments say precedes multi-decadal acceleration. The feedback is bounded by physics, however, and the bounds matter for projections: a shelf that is **wide relative to its feeding glacier** regenerates buttressing as it thins (the membrane stresses scale with thickness squared and the embayment walls remain), whereas a **narrow, short** tongue — the peninsula type — thins to failure. The **DeConto–Pollard** experiments that produced multi-metre 2100 projections do so by assuming the peninsula-style cascade propagates to the whole Amundsen Sea and then to the clifftop marine margins of East Antarctica; the same models, run without cliff failure, return to the mainstream half-to-two-metre range. The disagreement is not about data but about **which shelf geometry the century's oceans leave standing** — which is why the shelf-basal-melt community now instruments the cavities (hot-water boreholes, underwater gliders and thermistor arrays through 600 m of ice, the UK–US programmes at Pine Island and Thwaites since 2012–2019) as the most direct sea-level early-warning system ever built.
:::

## The shelfs' future

::: definition Projections and the early-warning geometry {#def-future}
Coupled climate–ice models disagree about shelf **basal melt** far more than about surface melt, because the ocean terms depend on regional circulation that coarse models get wrong in sign as often as magnitude; the consensus structure is nonetheless clear. Under high emissions, the Amundsen Sea shelves (Pine Island, Thwaites) lose most of their buttressing by 2100 in most models, with the multi-century commitment of the full Amundsen collapse ($\sim 1.2\ \mathrm{m}$) effectively booked; the Ronne-Filchner — the largest remaining brake, holding back the Support Force and Foundation ice streams, together another $\sim 1.7\ \mathrm{m}$ — sits on a bed that deepens to $3\ \mathrm{km}$ and is projected by half the models to begin retreating this century; and the peninsula's remnant shelves (Larsen C, George VI, Bach) are in the failure envelope of $+4\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ regional warming, which the high paths reach by 2060. The **early-warning observables** are not air temperature but geometry: shelf area and front position (satellite optical), surface-pond extent (optical, the signature of hydrofracture readiness), ice thickness (ICESat-2 and BedMachine reconstructions), rift propagation (the 175-km crack across Larsen C, watched since 2014), grounding-line position at pinning points (radar), and cavity water temperature (the moored arrays). Every one of these is now an operational, publicly reported quantity — the first time in geological history that the failure of a geological structure has been instrumented in advance — and the honest summary is that the shelves will not fail like glass but like a **scheduling problem**: which geometry the ocean reaches first, and whether the surface water arrives to finish the job.
:::

::: example The mélange switch {#ex-melange}
Jakobshavn's fjord mélange thinned by 70 per cent between 2000 and 2016 while the glacier's velocity rose about 50 per cent. Using the mélange backstress scaling $\sigma_m \propto H_m^{0.7}$ (the observed relation), find the change in backstress, and assess whether its loss can explain the speed-up if the mélange had been supplying $25\ \mathrm{kPa}$ against a $100\ \mathrm{kPa}$ driving stress.
::: solution
$\sigma_m$ falls by $1 - 0.3^{0.7} = 1 - 0.43 = 57$ per cent. On $25\ \mathrm{kPa}$ of mélange backstress that is a loss of $14\ \mathrm{kPa}$, raising effective driving stress from $75$ to $89\ \mathrm{kPa}$ — 19 per cent — and, with the cubic, roughly doubling the flux: consistent with the observed speed-up. The mélange lesson generalises to every fjord glacier with a seasonally stiff mélange — most of southeast Greenland — and links two Arctic changes that look unrelated: **sea-ice retreat removes the fjord's second brake**, which is why Greenland's outlet glaciers now respond to winter sea-ice extent as well as to summer melt.
:::
:::

::: warning "Ice shelves don't matter because they float" is the most expensive sentence in climate science — and the five caveats that follow it
Five errors. **The Archimedean truism is true and useless**: yes, a melting shelf raises no sea level, and yes, that fact has been used for thirty years to argue shelves are irrelevant — while their entire function is to hold back the **grounded** ice that does. **Not all shelves buttress equally**: the Ross, largest of all, buttresses little of the EAIS interior (its glaciers are mostly shallow-based and its own flow is nearly unconfined), which is why the "but the Ross is stable" reassurance misreads the risk map — the brakes that matter are the small, confined, Amundsen and Ronne-side shelves. **Larsen-type collapse is not proven for thick shelves**: the 200 m-deep water and 700 m thickness of the Ronne-Filchner front put it outside every observed failure case, and the honest statement is that we have **no observational precedent** for how a deep, wide shelf dies — only models, and they disagree. **Basal-melt rates are the least constrained number in ice-sheet science**: cavity temperatures vary by degrees on tidal and eddy timescales, and a model that gets the 2010s Pine Island melt right may be right for the wrong water mass — projections of shelf survival inherit that uncertainty directly. **And mélange loss is not yet a proven climate trend**: Jakobshavn's speed-up correlates with mélange thinning and with a warming ocean, and the two causes are not cleanly separated — the fjord is an experiment whose controls we do not fully set, and the field's caution there is method, not confusion.
:::

::: history
The buttressing principle was stated by **Thomas Hughes** (1973) alongside the instability itself, and the first observational demonstration of post-collapse acceleration came from **Eric Rignot** and colleagues on the Larsen B remnant glaciers (2004), followed by **Roland Warner** and the Wilkins analyses (2010) and the peninsula-wide speed-up synthesis of **Rignot, Bindschadler and colleagues** (2019). **Chris Scambos** and colleagues' 2009 hydrofracture paper made the meltwater mechanism the standard explanation for the peninsula collapses; the 175-km Larsen C rift was tracked by **Adrian Luckman**'s and **Daniela Jansen**'s groups through the 2010s, and the 2017 A-68 calving was predicted weeks ahead from rift-propagation rates. **Lena Bunge, Heinz Miller** and **H. Fritsch**'s 2008 radar-imaging analysis of the Larsen C rift system established the flexure-driven fracture mechanics now used for rift forecasting. The cavity-observation era began with **Keith Nicholls** and the British Antarctic Survey's hot-water drill and sensor programmes at Pine Island (2012) and the US West Antarctic Ice Sheet Observatory campaigns (2019, 2022–23) at Thwaites, which put the first moored thermistor arrays beneath the most consequential shelf on Earth.
:::

::: quiz
When an ice shelf melts away entirely:
- [ ] sea level rises by the shelf's melt volume.
- [x] sea level is unchanged by the melting itself, but the glaciers behind it accelerate and their grounded ice raises it.
- [ ] sea level falls because the seabed is unloaded.
- [ ] nothing changes for decades.
::: solution
Floating ice is already displaced water — its melt adds nothing directly. The sea-level signal is **dynamical, not thermometric**: removing the brake lets the grounded catchment drain, and that arrives with a lag of years to decades (Larsen B: acceleration within 2 years, catchment thinning within 5, measurable sea-level contribution within the decade).
:::

::: summary
- Shelves are floating brakes: membrane forces, margin drag (σ_b ~ τ_c L/P) and pinning-point backstress can cancel half or more of a glacier's driving stress; small confined shelves are the strongest brakes.
- They fail by hydrofracture (ponds driving water-filled rifts), ocean melt (thinning, unpinning, unconfining) and rift/mélange failure — and the modes cascade.
- Larsen B 2002 is the demonstrated chain: collapse → 2–10× glacier speed-up → catchment thinning → real sea-level rise, with a years-long lag.
- Buttressing depends on geometry: wide shelves regenerate it as they thin, narrow tongues do not; ice rises and pinning points concentrate it, and their loss precedes acceleration.
- The risk map is geometric: Amundsen shelves (Thwaites, Pine Island) first, Ronne-Filchner uncertain and unprecedented, peninsula remnants inside the +4 °C failure envelope.
- Shelf survival is now an instrumented, observable question — area, ponds, rifts, grounding lines, cavity temperature — the first advance warning system for the failure of a geological structure.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Ross volume {level=1 check="219000"}
The Ross Ice Shelf covers $487{,}000\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ with mean thickness $450\ \mathrm{m}$. Find its volume in $\mathrm{km^{3}}$ to the nearest thousand. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
$4.87\times10^{5}\times0.45 = 2.19\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ — $219{,}000$. As sea level the same arithmetic gives $219{,}000\times0.917/3.61\times10^{8}\times10^{3} = 0.56\ \mathrm{m}$: the Ross itself is half a metre of sea level **if it were grounded**, which it is not — the shelf's sea-level importance is entirely the ice it holds, which is several metres more.
:::
:::

::: exercise A-68 {level=1 check="1160"}
Iceberg A-68 (Larsen C, 2017) covered $5{,}800\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ at a mean thickness of $200\ \mathrm{m}$. Find its volume in $\mathrm{km^{3}}$.
::: solution
$5800\times0.2 = 1160\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ — three times Lake Geneva, calved in days, and raising sea level by exactly nothing. The berg's real significance is the 10 per cent loss of Larsen C's area and the modelling consensus that the remaining shelf is now below the stability threshold for the peninsula's warm-path future.
:::
:::

::: exercise Calving flux {level=1 check="7.5"}
A shelf front $50\ \mathrm{km}$ long, $300\ \mathrm{m}$ thick, advances at $500\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ and calves at steady state. Find the calving flux in $\mathrm{km^{3}\,yr^{-1}}$. (Enter to one decimal.)
::: solution
$Q = 5\times10^{4}\times300\times500 = 7.5\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,yr^{-1}} = 7.5\ \mathrm{km^{3}\,yr^{-1}}$. Steady state means the flux equals the upstream supply — which is why a **speed-up** at the front (thinning, unpinning) is read immediately as an imbalance: the front is the glacier's cash register, and velocity × thickness × width is the only audit that matters for sea level.
:::
:::

::: exercise Backstress scaling {level=2 check="150"}
Find the backstress of a shelf $150\ \mathrm{km}$ long in a $40\ \mathrm{km}$-wide embayment with margin drag $40\ \mathrm{kPa}$ ($\sigma_b = \tau_c L/P$), in kPa.
::: solution
$\sigma_b = 40\times150/40 = 150\ \mathrm{kPa}$ — comfortably above typical driving stresses, which is why narrow-embayment shelves hold their glaciers so tenaciously, and why the Larsen C remnant, despite being a fragment, still matters: the geometry that makes a brake is the **embayment**, not the area.
:::
:::

::: exercise Ponded crevasse {level=2 check="1.12"}
By what factor does a water-filled crevasse deepen relative to a dry one at the same driving stress (use the density ratio $\rho_w/\rho_i$)? (Enter to two decimals.)
::: solution
$1025/917 = 1.12$ for the simple water-matches-ice-pressure balance; with the crevasse filled to the **surface** the driving pressure at depth includes the full water column and the propagation depth can exceed the dry depth by 30 per cent or more — enough, in a 200 m-thick shelf, to cross from "crevasse" to "through-cutting fracture." This is the whole Larsen-B meltwater mechanism in one ratio.
:::
:::

::: exercise Mélange backstress {level=2 check="57"}
Mélange thickness falls 70 per cent and backstress scales as $H_m^{0.7}$. Find the percentage drop in backstress. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
$1 - 0.3^{0.7} = 1 - 0.43 = 57$ per cent. For fjord glaciers whose mélange supplies tens of kilopascals, that is a change in effective driving stress of order 10–20 per cent — and with the cubic sliding law, a flux change of 30–70 per cent: the reason Arctic sea-ice retreat is now treated as an outlet-glacier boundary condition.
:::
:::

::: exercise Post-collapse lag {level=3 check="100"}
After Larsen B's removal its glaciers lost mass at an initial rate of $10\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$, decaying exponentially with a catchment drainage timescale of $\tau = 120$ years. Find the total mass lost over the first decade, in Gt to the nearest ten. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
$M = 10\times120\times(1 - e^{-10/120}) = 1200\times0.080 = 96\ \mathrm{Gt}$, about $100\ \mathrm{Gt}$, i.e. $0.27\ \mathrm{mm}$ of sea level from one shelf's death in a single decade. The exponential bookkeeping is how the buttressing-loss term enters sea-level budgets: small shelves, small numbers; multiply by the peninsula's ten remnants and the Amundsen giants, and the same arithmetic becomes the difference between half a metre and two.
:::
:::

::: exercise The Ronne question {level=3 check="2.7"}
The Ronne-Filchner system holds back grounded ice worth $\sim 1.7\ \mathrm{m}$ of sea level behind a shelf whose backstress is estimated at $60$ per cent of the driving stress. If ocean warming removes half the backstress, and flux scales as $\tau^{7/4}$ (deformation-limited), find the flux multiplier to one decimal. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
Effective driving stress rises from $0.40$ to $0.70$ of the full drive, a factor of $1.75$; flux rises by $1.75^{7/4} = 2.7$. A 2.4× discharge from the Support Force and Foundation ice streams would commit the system to metres over centuries — the calculation behind the "unprecedented, unprecedentable" language the literature uses for the Ronne, and the reason its front is now surveyed annually even though no observed shelf has ever failed at its scale.
:::
:::
