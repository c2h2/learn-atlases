In September 2020, a diesel storage tank at the Norilsk-Taimyr energy complex in Siberia — supported, as it had been for thirty years, on piles driven into ground that was supposed to stay frozen — dropped off its supports and ruptured, releasing 21 000 tonnes of diesel into the Ambarnaya River: the worst Arctic oil spill in history, and the first whose root cause was written in the language of soil physics. The same ground is moving the village of Newtok, Alaska, whose residents voted in 2019 to relocate their entire community as bank-slumping permafrost ate the site on the meandering river's eroding bank; it buckles the Qinghai–Tibet Railway, whose engineers threaded the roadbed with thousands of ammonia heat-pipes, crushed-rock embankments and ventilated culverts to keep the ground frozen through a warming decade; and it stores, in the form of dead plants that never finished rotting, roughly **twice as much carbon as the modern atmosphere**. Permafrost — the permanently frozen 25 per cent of the Northern Hemisphere's land — is the cryosphere's slowest component and its most asymmetric threat: it thaws at metres per century, but what it releases has a century-scale and a millennial tail, and its failure modes — thermokarst collapse, slumps, methane ebullition — are discontinuous, local, and wildly under-represented in the global budgets that policy relies on.

## Frozen ground and its ice

::: definition What permafrost is {#def-permafrost}
Permafrost is ground — soil or rock — that has remained at or below $0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for **two consecutive years or more**: a definition of temperature, not of ice, though ice is usually what makes it matter. It underlies $\sim 22.8\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ of the Northern Hemisphere (a quarter of the land, half of Russia, a fifth of Canada), ranges from a metre thick in sub-Arctic discontinuous zones to $1500\ \mathrm{m}$ in Siberia's continuous cold, and carries a surface veneer — the **active layer**, $0.5$–$2\ \mathrm{m}$ thick — that freezes and thaws annually, isolating the frozen world below from the weather above. The ice is the engineering and ecological fact: freezing segregates ice into lenses, wedges (the vertical ice veins of **ice-wedge polygons**, each cracking open in winter cold to $-40\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and re-closing in summer, growing millimetre by millimetre for millennia), and the intrusive cores of **pingos** — domed hills, up to $70\ \mathrm{m}$ high, that are literally inflated by freezing groundwater and that collapse to leave ring-shaped lakes when they melt. Ground ice can occupy 10–50 per cent of the volume of ice-rich permafrost, and because ice turns to water and water drains, **thaw subsidence** is volumetric: ground that thaws with 30 per cent ice content sinks 30 per cent of the thawed depth — the mechanism of every slumping riverbank, every collapsed "drunken forest" of toppled larch, every crater-like pond that appears in a previously flat field of tundra overnight.
:::

::: example The arithmetic of sinking {#ex-subsidence}
A $10\ \mathrm{m}$ column of ice-rich permafrost with 35 per cent volumetric ice content thaws completely and the drainage is free. Find the subsidence, and explain why the resulting surface water accelerates further thaw by an order of magnitude.
::: solution
Subsidence $= 0.35\times10 = 3.5\ \mathrm{m}$. The new surface sits at or below the summer water table, so the site becomes a pond — and water short-circuits the insulation that protects permafrost: snowmelt pond water conducts heat downward at fluxes that open ground, insulated by dry soil and (in winter) snow, never delivers, while the dark water surface absorbs solar energy directly and the pond's edges **retrogressively** collapse as the thaw undercuts the bank. This is **thermokarst**: thaw not as a slow downward front but as a landscape-scale failure that self-amplifies, and it is why the field's central modellers' worry is that thermokarst lakes — which already cover about a tenth of the Alaskan Arctic Coastal Plain and rising fractions of the West Siberian Lowland — deepen and coalesce faster than any conduction-based projection of "gradual permafrost loss" admits.
:::
:::

## The thawed landscape

::: definition Thermokarst, Yedoma and the thaw vocabulary {#def-thermokarst}
Permafrost terrain is a **relict landscape in active decay**, and its vocabulary is a catalogue of failure modes. **Thermokarst** — the general collapse-and-pond process — builds thaw lakes whose shores advance as fast as $1\ \mathrm{m\,day^{-1}}$ during storm-driven wave erosion of ice-rich banks (the Alaskan Arctic Coastal Plain's lakes have coalesced into drainage networks over decades). **Retrogressive thaw slumps** — amphitheatres tens of hectares across, with walls retreating metres per summer after a wet year triggers them — are the permafrost equivalent of a landslide and the most efficient carbon exporters per unit area known. **Yedoma**: the ice-rich, syngenetic silts of unglaciated Siberia, Alaska and the Yukon — $10^{5}$–$10^{6}$-year-old grassland-mammoth-steppe sediment, hundreds of metres thick in places, holding perhaps $500\ \mathrm{GtC}$ in a form that is **labile** (grass-root carbon decomposes far faster than the woody peat of low-Arctic tundra) and interlayered with 20–50 per cent ice by volume, i.e. maximally vulnerable to the arithmetic of the previous section. **Talik** — the unfrozen window that opens beneath lakes, rivers and eventually the whole active layer as warming penetrates — disconnects the frozen ground from the atmosphere's mercy and commits the deep ice to centuries of eventual thaw regardless of future emissions. And **cryoturbation** — the frost-churn of the active layer, which mixes, buries and re-exposes carbon at centimetres per year — is the slow mixer that keeps feeding fresh substrate to the thawing front. The lesson of the vocabulary is that permafrost thaw is **not a diffusion problem with a smooth answer**: it is a landscape failure with thresholds, and the carbon release follows the failure geometry, not the temperature curve.
:::

::: example The lake that eats tundra {#ex-slump}
A retrogressive thaw slump on Herschel Island (Beaufort Sea coast) — the type example studied since the 1990s — initiates after an exceptionally wet summer, exposes Yedoma, and retreats $12\ \mathrm{m}$ per year across a $200\ \mathrm{m}$-wide headwall, thawing a $40\ \mathrm{m}$-deep ice-rich column. Estimate the annual volume of permafrost destroyed and the carbon released if Yedoma holds $25\ \mathrm{kg\,C\,m^{-3}}$.
::: solution
Volume $= 12\times200\times40 = 9.6\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,yr^{-1}}$, carbon $9.6\times10^{4}\times25 = 2.4\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{kg} = 2400\ \mathrm{tC\,yr^{-1}}$ from one headwall — a few thousand tonnes per year per slump, against a global slump population now mapped in the **thousands** by satellite and aerial survey (the Canadian Arctic, Svalbard, Siberia), each with a lifetime of decades. The arithmetic is why the permafrost community's honest statement on carbon release is not a smooth flux but a **population problem**: the release is set by how many slumps, lakes and collapsing coastlines initiate per decade, and initiation is threshold-driven — wet summers, storm waves, fire — events whose frequency is rising faster than the mean temperature that models use.
:::
:::

## The carbon vault and its feedback

::: proposition The permafrost carbon feedback {#prop-carbon-feedback}
Northern permafrost soils hold $\sim 1500\ \mathrm{GtC}$ in the top three metres (Tarnocai's inventory, still the standard), of which perhaps $1000$–$1400\ \mathrm{Gt}$ is **vulnerable** to thaw this millennium — against $870\ \mathrm{GtC}$ in the atmosphere and the $\sim 2500\ \mathrm{GtC}$ already emitted since 1870. The release is aerobic-dominant: most thawed ground drains, oxidises its carbon to CO₂, and does so at rates that laboratory incubations show are fast for young, labile carbon and slow-but-irreversible for the old; a few per cent of the release is **methane** from waterlogged thaw lakes, ponds and the anoxic bottoms of coalescing lakes — released not by diffusion but by **ebullition**, bubble streams whose fluxes satellite and tower campaigns show are concentrated in the outflow channels of thaw lakes and, in the Siberian Arctic, in the shallow **subsea permafrost** of the East Siberian Arctic Shelf, where Holocene-thawed offshore ground vents methane in strings along palaeo-river beds. The climate consequence is a **positive feedback with a long fuse**: model intercomparisons place the permafrost carbon addition at tens of ppm CO₂-equivalent by 2100 and hundreds by 2300, worth roughly $+0.2\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ this century and $+1\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ or more beyond — small enough that it does not change the century's headline, large enough that **it is not in most emissions budgets and cannot be stopped once initiated**: the deep thaw, once the active layer breaches the ice-rich layers, commits its own future. The feedback's asymmetry is the policy fact: a third or more of the eventual release (in model estimates) is already committed by warming to date, and the permafrost vault, unlike fossil carbon, has no switch — only the general one.
:::

::: example The vault's budget {#ex-vaultbudget}
Assume $1400\ \mathrm{GtC}$ vulnerable, 10 per cent released over the 21st century, 4 per cent of it as methane. Find the mean annual CO₂ and CH₄ carbon fluxes, compare the methane term's warming with the CO₂ term's using a 100-year GWP of 28, and state the two reasons the real 21st-century release is likely **front-loaded relative to temperature** and **back-loaded relative to emissions**.
::: solution
Total release $140\ \mathrm{GtC}$ over 100 years: $134\ \mathrm{GtC}$ as CO₂ ($1.34\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$) and $5.6\ \mathrm{GtC}$ as CH₄ ($0.056\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$). In CO₂-equivalent the methane term is $5.6\times28 = 157\ \mathrm{GtCO_2e}$ against the CO₂ term's $134\times(44/12) = 491$ — the methane is a quarter of the total forcing from 4 per cent of the carbon. **Front-loaded relative to temperature** because near-surface, labile, ice-rich carbon thaws first (the easy carbon goes early); **back-loaded relative to emissions** because the deep thaw is conduction-limited and continues for centuries after CO₂ stops — the permafrost feedback is the textbook example of a climate process that turns "net zero" into "net zero *emissions*," not net zero *warming*, and whose bill arrives after the negotiations end.
:::
:::

## Frozen foundations: the built environment

::: definition Hazards and engineering {#def-hazards}
Permafrost is a **foundation material** whose strength is its temperature, and the engineering literature now reads like a medical chart: the Trans-Alaska Pipeline's 100 km of thermosyphon-cooled supports, the Qinghai–Tibet Railway's heat-pipes, crushed-rock embankments and ventilated culverts (built 2006 to a 50-year permafrost assumption already being tested), the 3.5 million people and the airports, roads and tailings dams of northern Russia's permafrost cities — where the 2020 Norilsk failure was the first disaster officially attributed to permafrost subsidence. The assessment numbers: roughly **half of northern infrastructure is in areas where ground warming by 2050 will impose significant additional maintenance or failure risk**, with the highest exposure in the discontinuous permafrost zone — the thin, warm, near-melting ground that is first to go and least represented in design codes. The ecological hazards run alongside: **drunken forests** (waterlogged, toppled stands that switch from sink to source), the release of legacy pollutants (fallout radionuclides, mercury, and — the headline-grabbing but quantitatively minor risk — ancient pathogens, as with the 2016 anthrax outbreak in Yamal linked to a thawed reindeer carcass), and the methane-explosion craters of the Yamal Peninsula, where over a dozen blowouts since 2014 punctured the surface and, in 2014, killed grazing reindeer by the thousand. The adaptation toolkit is real but local: thermosyphons, crushed-rock basements, pile foundations with ventilated cavities, and — where the ground is already gone — **managed retreat**, of which Newtok/Mertarvik is the first US federal case and will not be the last.
:::

::: example Design against a moving baseline {#ex-engineering}
A railway embankment on ice-rich permafrost at $-2\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ mean ground temperature is designed for a 50-year life with a $+1\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ warming allowance. If the permafrost table lies $8\ \mathrm{m}$ down and the ice content below is 25 per cent, find the subsidence if warming thaws the top $4\ \mathrm{m}$ of ice-rich ground, and evaluate whether the warming allowance was the right variable to protect.
::: solution
Subsidence $= 0.25\times4 = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ — catastrophic for a railway graded to centimetres. The allowance protected **air temperature**, the wrong variable: what matters is the **thaw depth**, which responds nonlinearly (ponding, loss of the organic mat, drainage change, the zero-curtain's stored latent heat) and can jump by metres when a site crosses the ice-rich threshold. Modern northern engineering therefore designs against **ground temperature and ice content profiles** — thermistor arrays as the primary instrumentation, heat-pipes as the primary repair — and the honest planning statement is that in the discontinuous zone, no engineering fixes a site whose ground is gone: there, the design question becomes the Newtok question, where to move.
:::
:::

::: warning Permafrost carbon is not a "time bomb" with a date, thaw is not uniform, and the methane numbers are not what the headlines say
Five errors. **"Time bomb" implies a schedule; the vault leaks on a conduction clock** — most of the vulnerable carbon releases over centuries to millennia, and the century-scale release is a multiplier on the trajectory, not a cliff. **The methane fraction is small and its GWP is contested**: 3–5 per cent of the release as CH₄ is the model consensus, and the 100-year GWP of 28 understates near-term (20-year) impact while overstating the millennial — quoting a single number either way is a rhetorical choice, not a measurement. **"Permafrost is thawing everywhere uniformly" confuses the map**: the continuous zone will lose only its top tens of metres this century; the **discontinuous** zone loses most of its permafrost — and it is the discontinuous zone that holds most of the people, the roads and the design-code gap. **Fire, not temperature, is often the proximal trigger**: burn removes the insulating organic layer and drops the summer thaw depth by half again, so post-fire permafrost loss runs decades ahead of the climate-only projection — and boreal burn area is rising; the carbon feedback and the fire feedback are the same feedback wearing two hats. **And subsea methane is real but bounded**: the East Siberian Shelf's vents are concentrated, tide-modulated, and mostly re-oxidised in the water column — the "sleeping giant" framing that made 2010s headlines overstates the shallow-hydrate doomsday scenario by an order of magnitude, a correction the permafrost community itself published and the headlines did not carry.
:::

::: history
The science grew from Russian **merzlotovedenie** (frozen-ground studies) — **Mikhail Ivanovich Sumgin**'s 1927 treatise systematised the field and built the geotechnical basis of Siberian infrastructure, and the term **permafrost** itself entered English through the mid-century US–Soviet cooperation on frozen-ground research (1952). The **International Permafrost Association** (1983), the **CALM** (Circumpolar Active Layer Monitoring, 1995–) and **GTN-P** (Global Terrestrial Network for Permafrost) borehole networks turned a geotechnical specialty into a monitoring discipline, and the carbon-vault numbers are the work of **Charles Tarnocai** and colleagues' 2009 inventory (1035 GtC in the top 3 m), the vulnerability and vulnerability-modelling school of **David McGuire**, **Gustaf Hugelius** and **Kevin Schaefer**, and the feedback intercomparisons of **Christian Schneider von Deimling** and colleagues (whose "emission efficiency per unit of vulnerable carbon" framing reorganised the debate) and **Eleanor Burke** and colleagues' permafrost-baseline projections. The engineering classics are the Trans-Alaska Pipeline's thermosyphon era (1977) and the Qinghai–Tibet Railway's permafrost programme (2006), and the 2020 Norilsk failure — the first disaster officially attributed to permafrost subsidence — has begun the slow work of writing the hazard into insurance tariffs and building codes.
:::

::: quiz
The "zero curtain" in a freezing soil profile is:
- [ ] the winter snow layer that insulates the ground.
- [x] the months-long plateau at 0 °C while latent heat of fusion is released.
- [ ] the active layer's summer thaw depth.
- [ ] the depth of ice-wedge cracking.
::: solution
As pore water freezes it releases $334\ \mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}}$, holding the freezing front at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for weeks to months while heat drains away — the "curtain" visible in every borehole temperature record. The same latent heat, in reverse, buffers a warming permafrost table for years after the air has crossed the threshold: the zero curtain is why permafrost temperature records show such stubborn flat-lines, and why the ground's response to warming arrives as a step, not a slope.
:::

::: summary
- Permafrost — ground frozen ≥2 years — underlies a quarter of the Northern Hemisphere; its ice (lenses, wedges, pingo cores, Yedoma) can be 10–50 per cent of volume, making thaw a volumetric collapse, not a softening.
- Thaw is landscape failure: thermokarst lakes, retrogressive slumps, Yedoma collapse and talik formation are threshold processes that amplify themselves by ponding water.
- The vault holds ~1500 GtC — twice the atmosphere — and its release this millennium is committed; the feedback is worth ~+0.2 °C by 2100 and ~+1 °C beyond, mostly as CO₂, with a methane term that is a quarter of the forcing from 4 per cent of the carbon.
- The release is front-loaded in easy carbon and back-loaded in deep conduction — the process that makes "net zero emissions" not "net zero warming."
- Permafrost is a foundation material: half of northern infrastructure sits on ground that will warm significantly by 2050, and the discontinuous zone — most people, thinnest ice — fails first.
- Fire, drainage change and storms, not mean temperature alone, set the pace; the honest budget is a population problem (slumps, lakes, coasts), not a smooth flux.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Active-layer velocity {level=1 check="15"}
A $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ active layer thaws over a 100-day summer. Find the mean thaw-front advance in $\mathrm{mm\,day^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$1500/100 = 15\ \mathrm{mm\,day^{-1}}$ — the active layer's summer race against the winter freeze, and the reason the **net** of the two (the long-term permafrost table) is one of the most sensitive indicators of high-latitude climate: a centimetre-per-year imbalance, invisible in any single season, destroys or builds a landscape in a century.
:::
:::

::: exercise Vault versus atmosphere {level=1 check="1.7"}
Find the ratio of permafrost soil carbon ($1500\ \mathrm{GtC}$) to atmospheric carbon ($870\ \mathrm{GtC}$). (Enter to one decimal.)
::: solution
$1500/870 = 1.7$. The number belongs on every emissions chart: the Northern Hemisphere's frozen soils are a second, unpriced, threshold-laden carbon reservoir of the same order as the atmosphere itself, and every permafrost paper's "twice the atmosphere" headline is this division.
:::
:::

::: exercise Subsidence {level=1 check="2.4"}
$8\ \mathrm{m}$ of permafrost with 30 per cent volumetric ice thaws with free drainage. Find the subsidence in metres to one decimal.
::: solution
$0.30\times8 = 2.4\ \mathrm{m}$. The ground surface drops by the ice's volume, not its mass — which is why ice-rich sites pond, why "the ponds appeared overnight" is a reliable local testimony, and why InSAR subsidence maps of the tundra read like a map of future lakes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Slump carbon {level=2 check="1125"}
A thaw slump retreats $10\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ across a $150\ \mathrm{m}$ headwall, thawing a $30\ \mathrm{m}$ column at $25\ \mathrm{kg\,C\,m^{-3}}$. Find the annual carbon release in tonnes of carbon. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
$10\times150\times30\times25 = 1.125\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{kg} = 1125\ \mathrm{tC\,yr^{-1}}$. One slump, a thousand-tonne river of carbon per year; thousands of slumps, and the population becomes a gigatonne-scale term in the budget.
:::
:::

::: exercise Methane equivalence {level=2 check="157"}
$5.6\ \mathrm{GtC}$ released as methane over a century. Find the CO₂-equivalent forcing sum in $\mathrm{GtCO_2e}$ using GWP-100 $= 28$. (Enter to the nearest integer.)
::: solution
$5.6\times28 = 157\ \mathrm{GtCO_2e}$ — comparable to two decades of modern fossil emissions, from 4 per cent of the permafrost release. The exercise is the reason the community argues about GWPs: on a 20-year horizon the same methane is worth $84\times5.6 = 470$, and the policy weight of a feedback depends on which century you are pricing.
:::
:::

::: exercise Heat-pipe duty {level=2 check="630"}
A thermosyphon cools a railway embankment by moving $20\ \mathrm{W}$ of heat continuously. Find the heat removed per year in MJ, and state the thickness of ice (per $\mathrm{m^{2}}$) whose melting that heat could drive (latent heat $3.06\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$). (Enter the MJ to the nearest ten.)
::: solution
$20\times3.15\times10^{7} = 6.3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J} = 630\ \mathrm{MJ}$, equivalent to melting $6.3\times10^{8}/3.06\times10^{8} = 2.1\ \mathrm{m}$ of ice per square metre per year — enough to keep a metre-scale active layer frozen through a warming summer, which is why a few hundred thousand dollars of passive steel per kilometre outlives a million dollars of gravel ballast.
:::
:::

::: exercise Stefan thaw depth {level=3 check="23"}
If a $+5\ \mathrm{K}$ thermal disturbance propagated into permafrost purely by conduction (soil $k = 1.5\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, volumetric latent heat of thaw $9.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$), find the Stefan-law thaw depth after 100 years, in metres to the nearest metre. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
$d = \sqrt{2k\Delta T\,t/\rho_w L} = \sqrt{2\times1.5\times5\times3.15\times10^{9}/9.2\times10^{7}} = \sqrt{513} = 23\ \mathrm{m}$. Observed climate-driven thaw over the same century is **metres**, not tens of metres — the gap is the point: the surface energy budget, the refrozen winter lid, the zero curtain and drainage all throttle the front, and the 23 m is the **upper bound that committed deep thaw** represents once thermokarst ponding bypasses those brakes. The difference between the two numbers is the difference between a managed century and an unmanaged one.
:::
:::

::: exercise Hydrate depth {level=3 check="130"}
Methane hydrate is stable at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ above $2.6\ \mathrm{MPa}$. With overburden density $2000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, find the minimum depth of subsea-hydrate stability below the seabed, in metres to the nearest ten. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
$h = 2.6\times10^{6}/(2000\times9.81) = 133\ \mathrm{m}$, i.e. $130\ \mathrm{m}$ to the nearest ten. The shallow subsea hydrates of the East Siberian Shelf — emplaced when the shelf was permafrost at glacial sea level, now barely at the stability edge under metres of water — are the reservoir behind the "sleeping giant" scare; the same arithmetic shows why they are **thin, patchy and mostly re-dissolving in the water column rather than venting**, and why the community's corrected estimate of their century-scale contribution is two orders below the headline numbers.
:::
