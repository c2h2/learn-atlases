Stand at the South Pole and you stand on nearly three kilometres of ice; stand at the geographic centre of West Antarctica and you would stand — if the ice were lifted away — on an island archipelago, its sea channels deeper than the fjords of Norway, its bedrock hundreds of metres **below** today's sea level. That contrast is the most important fact in ice-sheet science. East Antarctica is a single vast ice mass resting mostly on high, cold, subaerial rock, stable for millions of years and holding enough ice — some 52 metres of sea level — to end coastal civilisation as a concept. West Antarctica is a mosaic of marine-terminating glaciers assembled on flooded shelves, young in geological terms, moving through a handful of ice streams, and holding only a few metres of sea level — but holding them behind a system whose stability rests on buttressing ice shelves and seafloor pinning points that ocean warming can attack **from below**, at the one place the climate system can deliver heat without touching the atmosphere. The two ice sheets are not one problem with two names; they are a slow problem measured in millions of years and a fast problem measured in centuries, and the history of ice-sheet science is the discovery that the fast one exists.

## Anatomy: domes, beds and the marine half

::: definition The two ice sheets {#def-anatomy}
Antarctica's ice — $26.5\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$, $90$ per cent of terrestrial fresh ice — divides into the **East Antarctic Ice Sheet** (EAIS: ~52 m of sea-level equivalent, up to $4.1\ \mathrm{km}$ thick, a cold-based dome over a high bed) and the **West Antarctic Ice Sheet** (WAIS: several metres of sea-level equivalent, warm-based, its marine-based sectors — the Amundsen Sea glaciers draining Pine Island and Thwaites, and the Ross Sea's Antarctic Ice Stream system — resting on beds up to $2.5\ \mathrm{km}$ below sea level). Greenland adds $2.9\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ (7.4 m), mostly above sea level, melting from the surface. Ice sheets are shaped by their own flow: **divides** and **domes** where ice radiates, **troughs and trough-mouth glaciers** where bedrock lows focus the drainage, and **ice shelves** — the Ross and Ronne-Filchner, kilometres thick at their grounding lines — where the ice floats and the ocean does the work. The bed is the destiny: an ice sheet grounded **above** sea level can only be eroded from above (surface melt, which is slow in cold climates), while one grounded **below** sea level is attacked by the ocean at its thickest, warmest, most mobile place — the grounding line, where ice goes afloat and where, as the next section shows, the geometry can turn retreat into a self-driving machine. The Antarctic ice sheet as a whole is old (its East Antarctic core dates to the mid-Miocene cooling, $\sim 14\ \mathrm{Ma}$); the West Antarctic Ice Sheet is a Pliocene-Pleistocene assembly whose marine sectors have probably collapsed and rebuilt repeatedly through the warm intervals of the last 5 million years.
:::

::: example Sea-level arithmetic {#ex-sle}
Convert the Greenland ice sheet ($2.9\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ of ice) and the marine-based sectors of West Antarctica ($\sim 1.4\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$) to sea-level equivalent (ocean area $3.61\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$), and find the sea-level rise if Greenland lost mass at its 2010s rate ($200\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$) for a century.
::: solution
Greenland: $2.9\times10^{6}\times917/1000 = 2.66\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ of water, $2.66\times10^{6}/3.61\times10^{8} = 7.4\ \mathrm{m}$; the WAIS marine sectors: $1.4\times10^{6}\times0.917/3.61\times10^{8} = 3.6\ \mathrm{m}$. A century at $200\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$ removes $2\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{Gt} = 2\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{kg}$; since $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ of sea level is $3.7\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{kg}$, that is $54\ \mathrm{mm} = 5.4\ \mathrm{cm}$ per century from that term alone. The arithmetic is the scale of the problem in one line: the **observed** 2010s contributions (Greenland $\sim 0.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, Antarctica $\sim 0.4$, glaciers $\sim 0.7$) already sum to over half of the $\sim 3.3\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ total rise, and the ice sheets' century-scale reservoirs are measured in **metres** — a reminder that the present rate is not the ceiling but the ramp.
:::
:::

## The marine instability

::: proposition Marine ice-sheet instability (MISI) {#prop-misi}
On a **retrograde** bed — one that deepens inland — the grounding line is in unstable equilibrium. The flux of ice across a grounding line increases steeply with the ice thickness there ($q \propto H^{m}$ with $m \approx 3/2$–$7/4$ in the standard boundary-layer theories of **Schoof** and **Schoenfeld**), and on a retrograde bed, **retreat thickens the ice at the grounding line**: retreat increases $H$, which increases discharge, which causes further retreat. The feedback has no climate trigger and needs no warming to continue once initiated — it is geometry, not weather. Three qualifications keep the statement honest. Grounding lines can be **stabilised** by pinning points — ridges on the bed, or the wedge of sediment at a grounding zone — and, more importantly, by **shelf buttressing**: a floating shelf pressed against its embayment transmits backstress to the grounding line, and the stability of the whole system is the stability of the shelf. The **ice-cliff failure** hypothesis extends the logic: if a shelf is lost, a marine-terminating ice face taller than $\sim 200$–$250\ \mathrm{m}$ above the waterline (a ratio of cliff height to buoyancy that failure experiments and the 2021 Thwaites eastern-side geometry both approach) may fracture under its own weight, letting retreat proceed by calving rather than melting — the "MICI" mechanism whose observational test is the single most consequential open question in sea-level science. The Amundsen Sea sector — Pine Island and Thwaites glaciers, draining a catchment holding $\sim 1.2\ \mathrm{m}$ of sea level, grounded $1.5\ \mathrm{km}$ below sea level on a retrograde bed, with a shelf thinned by $\sim 1.7\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ of ocean-driven basal melt — is the natural experiment now running, its grounding line retreating up to $1.2\ \mathrm{km\,yr^{-1}}$ since the 1990s, its speed doubling since the 1970s.
:::

The map of retrograde beds is, more than any temperature projection, the map of where the metres of sea level live.

## The modern budget

::: definition IMBIE and the sources of loss {#def-budget}
The **IMBIE** (Ice Sheet Mass Balance Inter-comparison Exercise) assessments reconcile the three methods of the previous lesson — glaciological, geodetic, and the flux-discharge method — into one number per ice sheet per decade, and the 2010s numbers are: Greenland losing $\sim 200\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$ (about half from surface mass-balance loss, the melt-runoff of a darkening surface, half from dynamical discharge), Antarctica losing $\sim 120\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$ with **80 per cent of it from West Antarctica**, dominated by the Amundsen Sea glaciers whose discharge has tripled since the 1970s. The attribution follows the physics: Greenland's loss is **atmosphere-driven** (warm, humid air masses, darkening albedo from dust, black carbon and snow algae, and the hydrofracture of its 2019 and 2021 melt seasons), West Antarctica's is **ocean-driven** (circumpolar deep water diverted onto the shelves by wind-driven changes and subsurface warming, melting the shelves from below at rates that reach tens of metres of ice per year near the grounding lines), and East Antarctica is, on the decadal record, **roughly in balance** with a few marine-based sectors (Totten, Denman, the Wilkes subglacial basins) losing and the interior gaining snow — a zero that is a coincidence of opposing fluxes, not stability. The projection consequence is the **bimodality of sea-level risk**: under low emissions the century's rise is dominated by thermal expansion and glaciers and stays near half a metre; under high emissions the ice-sheet terms — surface melt on Greenland scaling with warmth, marine-instability dynamics in the Amundsen Sea — take over, and the spread of published 2100 projections for the high path spans from well under a metre to several, the tail set not by the confidence in any model but by whether Thwaites' shelf survives the century.
:::

::: example How warm water carves a cavity {#ex-cavitymelt}
Circumpolar deep water $1.5\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ above the local freezing point floods the cavity under Pine Island's shelf. With a turbulent exchange velocity $\gamma = 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, seawater $\rho c_p = 4.1\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$, find the basal melt heat flux and the melt rate in $\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ of ice ($\rho L = 3.06\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$), and explain why the melt concentrates at the grounding line rather than the front.
::: solution
The turbulent heat flux is
$$
q = \rho c_p\,\gamma\,\Delta T = 4.1\times10^{6}\times10^{-4}\times1.5 = 615\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}},
$$
melting $615/3.06\times10^{8} = 2\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 63\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ of ice — the observed order for the deepest cavity zones. The melt concentrates where the water is warmest **relative to its freezing point**, and the freezing point of water rises with pressure at only $-7.5\times10^{-4}\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per metre of depth: at $700\ \mathrm{m}$ below the surface, water at $1.2\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is $0.3\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ above its (depressed) freezing point and melts vigorously, while the same water at the front, near the surface, is barely above freezing and melts little. This is why warm deep water is a **grounding-line weapon** — it melts hardest exactly where melting unpins the ice — and why the depth of the bathymetry leading to a glacier is as predictive of its future as its latitude.
:::
:::

::: example The Amundsen Sea experiment {#ex-amundsen}
Pine Island Glacier's grounding line retreated $31\ \mathrm{km}$ between 1992 and 2017, and its velocity roughly doubled since the 1970s. If flux across the grounding line scales as $q \propto H^{7/4}$ and the ice thickness at the grounding line has increased by 20 per cent as it retreated over the retrograde bed, find the flux increase attributable to geometry alone, and explain why observed flux growth exceeded it.
::: solution
$(1.20)^{7/4} = 1.37$ — a 37 per cent flux increase from the MISI geometry alone. Observed discharge rose far more because the **shelf throttle weakened in parallel**: Pine Island's shelf thinned by tens of metres between the 1990s and 2010s, reducing backstress, and the two effects compound (less buttressing raises the effective driving stress at the grounding line, and the thicker ice supplies it). This is the operational reality of MISI: the geometric instability is real but slow on its own, and the fast decades come when **ocean melt attacks the buttress and the bed geometry simultaneously** — which is exactly the condition under warm deep-water intrusion, and exactly why the Amundsen Sea, not the cold dry continent's interior, is the sea-level question of the century.
:::
:::

## Deep time: the ice sheets' history and future

::: definition Cenozoic ice and the Pliocene test {#def-deeptime}
The present icehouse began with the **mid-Miocene cooling** ($\sim 14\ \mathrm{Ma}$), when East Antarctica's expansion coincided with the closure of the Central American seaway? — the causation is still debated; what is dated is the ice: East Antarctic ice was substantial by $12$–$10\ \mathrm{Ma}$, the continent reached near-modern extent by the Pliocene, and the **Pliocene warm period** ($3.3$–$3.0\ \mathrm{Ma}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ near $400\ \mathrm{ppm}$, sea level $10$–$25\ \mathrm{m}$ above present) is the geological test of marine-instability hypotheses: proxy records (diatoms from the Wilkes Subglacial Basin, foraminiferal isotope excursions, New Zealand's raised-shoreline microtidal records) indicate East Antarctic marine sectors retreated inland of their present positions and West Antarctica was substantially reduced — a warm-world configuration in which the **marine-based sectors of both ice sheets were unstable at only a few degrees of ocean warming**. The Pleistocene then imposed the 100-kyr rhythm: the Northern Hemisphere's great Late Cenozoic ice sheets (Laurentide, Fennoscandian, Barents-Kara) grew on emerged, retrograde-bedded continental shelves and collapsed in deglaciations that raised sea level $120$–$130\ \mathrm{m}$, with meltwater pulse events — Meltwater Pulse 1A at $14.6$–$14.2\ \mathrm{ka}$, $14$–$18\ \mathrm{m}$ in $\sim 500$ years, rates of $\sim 3$–$4\ \mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$ — proving from the sedimentary record that ice systems **can** deliver centimetre-per-year sea-level rise when geometry and ocean cooperate. The youngest lesson is the **2.7-million-year-old ice** recovered near Little Dome C: Antarctica's glacial history is longer than the Quaternary, its CO₂ sensitivity written across a million years of it, and its future — on our emissions trajectory — most closely resembles the Pliocene, the epoch when today's coastline did not exist.
:::

::: example Residence time of an ice sheet {#ex-residence}
Antarctica's mean surface accumulation is $170\ \mathrm{mm\,w.e.\,yr^{-1}}$ over $1.37\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$. Find the total accumulation in $\mathrm{km^{3}\,yr^{-1}}$ and the mean residence time of ice (volume $26.5\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$), and compare with the oldest dated ice ($2.7\ \mathrm{Ma}$).
::: solution
Accumulation $= 0.17\times1.37\times10^{13} = 2.3\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,yr^{-1}} = 2330\ \mathrm{km^{3}\,yr^{-1}}$, giving a mean residence time of $26.5\times10^{6}/2330 = 11{,}400$ years. The mean is a flow-through average — ice parcels follow wildly different paths, some recycled in millennia along the fast coastal routes, some sequestered for a million years in the cold plateau's slow interior — and the $2.7\ \mathrm{Ma}$ basal ice is the extreme tail of that distribution, old because it sits above the divide where flow is slowest and the bed is cold enough that the ice survived rather than melted. The contrast between the 11 000-year mean and the 2.7-million-year tail is the difference between an ice sheet's **turnover** and its **memory**: the first sets how fast it responds to forcing, the second is what it remembers of past climates — and what it will remember of ours.
:::
:::

::: warning The ice sheets are not "melting" in the way the sentence implies, West Antarctica is not "collapsing" yet, and East Antarctica's zero is not stability
Five errors. **Greenland melts; West Antarctica flows**: the former's loss is half surface melt and runoff, the latter's is almost entirely **discharge** — ice calved and melted at the bed by ocean heat — and the difference dictates which projections, attributions and (frankly) which emissions levers apply. **"West Antarctica is collapsing" is a headline, not an observation**: what is observed is a marine sector losing mass at 3–5 times its 1970s rate with grounding lines retreating at well-measured kilometres per year — an instability **initiated in one sector**, not a continent-wide collapse, and the difference matters because the timescales differ by an order of magnitude. **East Antarctica's near-zero balance is a flux coincidence**: interior snow gain (itself a wetter-climate signal) currently offsets marine-sector loss, and every model projects the sign to flip as ocean heat reaches the Wilkes and Totten basins — the "stable East" is a statement about the 2000s, not the 2100s. **Ice-sheet projections are not model failures when they disagree**: the 2100 spread under high emissions reflects genuine structural uncertainty about **shelf survival and cliff failure**, and a projection set that excludes the tail is not conservative but wrong — the honest framing is risk-based, like earthquake and flood statistics. **And isostasy matters locally**: the crust still rebounding from the last deglaciation (centimetres per year around Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Bothnia) and the gravitational fingerprint of past ice mean that **local** sea level can differ from the global mean by tens of per cent — tide-gauge trends near formerly ice-loaded coasts are not global sea-level data.
:::

::: history
The marine principle was named by **Thomas Hughes** in 1973 — his "cluster of ice streams" papers identified marine ice-sheet instability and the buttressing role of shelves two decades before anyone measured them — and the modern grounding-line physics, the boundary-layer flux asymptotics that every ice-sheet model now uses, was developed by **Frank Pattyn** (2002) and **Johannes Schoof** (2007). The West Antarctic marine-instability projections that reshaped the sea-level literature came from **Robert DeConto** and **David Pollard** (2016), whose inclusion of cliff-failure and hydrofracture mechanics produced multi-metre twenty-first-century tails and a fierce, productive debate about whether the mechanism operates at full scale — the question now aimed at Thwaites Glacier by the international drilling and observatory programmes of the 2020s. The observational spine is the satellite record: **ICESat** altimetry (2003–09), **GRACE** gravimetry (2002–17), InSAR velocity mapping by **Helmut Rott**, **Ian Joughin** and **Eric Rignot**, and the **IMBIE** intercomparisons (2012–) that turned a hundred contradictory mass balances into one number; the deep-time spine is the ice-core programme, from **Vostok** (1980s, with **Claude Lorius**'s French–Soviet forecast of the greenhouse effect in 1976) through **EPICA Dome C** (800 ka, 2004) to the current search for million-year-old ice around Little Dome C.
:::

Marine ice-sheet instability requires:

::: summary
- Antarctica is two problems: the EAIS (52 m of sea level, cold, mostly above sea level, million-year stability) and the WAIS (a few metres, marine-based, warm-based, shelf-buttressed), plus Greenland (7.4 m, surface-melt-dominated).
- Marine ice-sheet instability is geometry: on retrograde beds, retreat thickens grounding-line ice, flux rises, retreat continues — self-sustaining, ocean-triggered, shelf-dependent.
- The 2010s budget (IMBIE): Greenland ~200 Gt yr⁻¹ (half surface, half discharge), Antarctica ~120 Gt yr⁻¹ (80 per cent West, ocean-driven), East Antarctica near zero by coincidence.
- The Pliocene (CO₂ ~400 ppm, sea level 10–25 m high) is the geological test case: marine sectors of both ice sheets retreated at a few degrees of ocean warming.
- Meltwater Pulse 1A proves centimetre-per-year sea-level rise is physically available when geometry and ocean cooperate; ice-cliff failure is the unresolved extension.
- Ice-sheet sea-level risk is bimodal by emissions and structural by mechanism — the honest projection is a distribution whose tail is written in the bedrock maps.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Greenland's century {level=1 check="5.4"}
At the 2010s rate of $200\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$, find Greenland's sea-level contribution over 100 years in cm ($1\ \mathrm{mm}$ of sea level $= 3.7\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{kg}$). (Enter to one decimal.)
::: solution
$2\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{kg}/3.7\times10^{14} = 54\ \mathrm{mm} = 5.4\ \mathrm{cm}$. The number is deliberately sober — a century at the **current** Greenland rate is half the century's likely total rise — and deliberately misleading if read as a ceiling: the rate is rising, and the surface-melt term scales with warming, not with 2019.
:::

::: exercise IMBIE to millimetres {level=1 check="0.33"}
Antarctica loses $120\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$. Find the sea-level contribution in mm yr⁻¹ to two decimals.
::: solution
$1.2\times10^{11}/3.7\times10^{14} = 0.33\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ — about a tenth of total observed rise, but with 80 per cent of it coming from one ocean-exposed corner of one ice sheet: the **concentration** of the signal, not its size, is the warning.

::: exercise Flotation thickness {level=1 check="1120"}
A glacier grounds where the bed lies $1000\ \mathrm{m}$ below sea level. Find the ice thickness at the grounding line (flotation: $H = (\rho_w/\rho_i)\,b$), in metres to the nearest ten. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
$H = (1025/917)\times1000 = 1118\ \mathrm{m}$, to the nearest ten $1120\ \mathrm{m}$. Grounding lines are flotation lines, which is why they migrate kilometres in response to metres of thinning: an ice stream grounded at $-1500\ \mathrm{m}$ needs $1.7\ \mathrm{km}$ of ice to stay put, and thin it by a tenth and the grounding line walks inland until the bed shoals — or the ice thickens again, if the bed allows.
:::

::: exercise MISI flux gain {level=2 check="2.03"}
With grounding-line flux $q \propto H^{7/4}$, find the flux factor if retrograde-bed retreat thickens grounding-line ice from $1000$ to $1500\ \mathrm{m}$. (Enter to two decimals.)
::: solution
$(1500/1000)^{7/4} = 1.5^{1.75} = 2.03$ — a doubling from geometry alone. Compare the Amundsen Sea glaciers' observed flux changes since 1970 (roughly doubled): the geometric instability, once the shelf throttle weakens, is sufficient to explain the bulk of what has happened without invoking any new climate sensitivity.
:::
:::

::: exercise Cliff-height limit {level=2 check="24"}
Structural failure experiments and modelling suggest ice cliffs taller than $\sim 250\ \mathrm{m}$ **above the waterline** tend to fail under their own weight. Using flotation, find the corresponding **total** ice thickness in hundreds of metres (e.g. enter 27 for 2700 m).
::: solution
Freeboard is $1 - \rho_i/\rho_w = 0.105$ of total thickness, so total thickness $= 250/0.105 = 2380\ \mathrm{m}$, i.e. about $24$ hundred metres. The implication is stark: an ice column of $2.4\ \mathrm{km}$ grounded at $-2.1\ \mathrm{km}$ water depth cannot stand as a cliff face, and much of the Wilkes and Aurora basins' geometry sits in exactly that range — which is why the ice-cliff-failure hypothesis, though untested at full scale, cannot be dismissed for East Antarctica's marine sectors.
:::
:::

::: exercise Meltwater pulse rate {level=2 check="3.2"}
Meltwater Pulse 1A raised sea level $16\ \mathrm{m}$ in $500$ years. Find the mean rate in mm yr⁻¹ to one decimal.
::: solution
$16\,000/5000 = 3.2\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ — more than ten times the present rate, sustained for five centuries, and the geological existence proof that the climate system's ice-drainage machinery can run at rates that no 20th-century tide-gauge record contains. Modern sea-level projections are bounded below by the tide gauges and above by the corals of MWP-1A; the honest question is which century we are closer to resembling.
:::

::: exercise Ice-sheet turnover {level=3 check="11400"}
Find the mean residence time of Antarctic ice (volume $26.5\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$; accumulation $170\ \mathrm{mm\,w.e.\,yr^{-1}}$ over $1.37\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$), in years to the nearest hundred. (Enter the number.)
::: solution
Accumulation $= 0.17\times1.37\times10^{13} = 2.33\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,yr^{-1}} = 2330\ \mathrm{km^{3}\,yr^{-1}}$; residence time $= 26.5\times10^{6}/2330 = 11{,}400$ years. The turnover number sets the **response time of the whole system** to sustained forcing (tens of millennia for the EAIS to rebuild what it loses), while the 2.7-million-year basal ice records its memory — and the asymmetry between the two is why an ice sheet, once lost, is not recoverable on any political or even civilisational timescale.

::: exercise The buttressing switch {level=3 check="16"}
A glacier's grounding-line flux scales as $q \propto H^{7/4}$ and its shelf backstress cancels $60$ per cent of the driving stress (as in [[glaciology/ice-flow]]'s exercise). If the shelf thins until it cancels nothing, by what factor does flux rise? (Enter to the nearest integer.)
::: solution
Driving stress rises by $1/0.4 = 2.5$; with flux scaling roughly as the cube of driving stress in the sliding-limited case (or the $7/4$ power of thickness in the deformation-limited case), the flux factor is $2.5^{3} \approx 16$ in the sliding limit and $2.5^{7/4} \approx 4$ in the deformation limit — the truth for real Antarctic glaciers lies between, and even the conservative end **quadruples** discharge. This is the single calculation behind every "1970s × 3" headline from the Amundsen Sea, and it explains why shelf survival, not surface temperature, is the variable to watch.
:::
