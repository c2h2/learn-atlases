Life is $0.0001\%$ of the Earth by mass and it runs the surface. A film of organisms a few metres thick, using sunlight, has over two and a half billion years: doubled the atmospheric oxygen from a trace to a fifth of the air, built the soil that holds the land's water, moved more carbon into the ocean's depths than all the rocks of the Phanerozoic, triggered the glaciations that ended the Palaeozoic greenhouse, and — by the arithmetic of the last three centuries — set off a carbon perturbation that the geological record has not matched in fifty-six million years.

This chapter is not about the wonder of it. It is about the mechanism: how a biological system with no central control and no foresight changes the planet's chemistry measurably, why those changes come with feedbacks, and what the numbers say about the present moment.

## The rules life plays by

::: definition Primary production {#def-production}
**Gross primary production** (GPP) is the total carbon fixed by photosynthesis, $\sim125\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$ globally, of which about half is on land. **Net primary production** (NPP) is what remains after the producer's own respiration, $\sim60\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$: the amount available to the rest of the biosphere. The efficiency of the whole system is low — photosynthesis uses $0.5$–$2\%$ of the photosynthetically active radiation that reaches leaves, against a theoretical ceiling near $11\%$.
:::

::: definition The Redfield ratio {#def-redfield}
The average element ratio of marine phytoplankton is $\mathrm{C}:\mathrm{N}:\mathrm{P} = 106:16:1$ (atoms), with silicon required by diatoms and iron required by all. Because the deep ocean accumulates exactly this ratio of nutrients as sinking matter decays, the ratio converts a measurement of nitrate or phosphate anywhere in the sea into an estimate of how much carbon that water can still grow, and how much it has already exported.
:::

::: definition The biological pump {#def-pump}
The **soft-tissue pump** is the transport of carbon to the interior as sinking organic matter, followed by its respiration at depth. Export across $100\ \mathrm{m}$ is $\sim11\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$; burial in sediment is $0.1$–$0.2\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$, an efficiency of $1$–$2\%$. The **carbonate (hard-tissue) pump** exports carbon as $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$, which does the opposite of the soft-tissue pump in the short term: calcification releases $\mathrm{CO_2}$ ($\mathrm{Ca^{2+}} + 2\mathrm{HCO_3^-} \to \mathrm{CaCO_3} + \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}$) and buries alkalinity.
:::

::: proposition Oxygen is a record of burial {#prop-oxygen}
Photosynthesis and respiration balance to within a per cent, so no amount of photosynthesis can raise atmospheric oxygen. Only the escape of a fraction of the produced organic carbon from oxidation can:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{O_2}}{\mathrm{d}t} = P_\text{org burial} - \text{reduced-volcano and weathering sinks}.
$$
Atmospheric oxygen is therefore a budget of carbon that did *not* rot.
:::

::: proof
Fixing one mole of carbon releases one mole of $\mathrm{O_2}$; rotting it consumes the same. The net flux is therefore the burial rate. The modern organic carbon burial flux is $0.1$–$0.2\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$, and the sink terms — reduced volcanic gases such as $\mathrm{H_2}$ and $\mathrm{H_2S}$, and the oxidation of $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and crustal sulphide — are of similar size, which is why the oxygen budget is tight and why the great Proterozoic and Palaeozoic rises required burial to be sustained at several times the modern rate for tens of millions of years, in basins that preserved organic matter (anoxic shelves, the Cretaceous oceanic crustal plateau system).
:::

::: example How much oxygen could we burn? {#ex-oxygen}
The atmosphere holds $1.2\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{Gt}$ of $\mathrm{O_2}$ and $890\ \mathrm{GtC}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$. Burning all $\sim1100\ \mathrm{GtC}$ of fossil fuel reserves would consume how much of the oxygen, in per cent? Is the decline measurable?
::: solution
Combustion of carbon consumes one mole of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per mole of C: $1100\ \mathrm{GtC} = 9.2\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{mol}$, against an oxygen inventory of $3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$. The decline is $0.25\%$ — invisible to the eye, and yet measurable, because the ratio $\mathrm{O_2}/\mathrm{N_2}$ of air can be determined to a few parts per billion by interferometry. Since 1989 the atmospheric oxygen content has fallen by about $7\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{mol\,yr^{-1}}$, within error of the value implied by fossil-fuel use, which is an independent confirmation of the emissions inventory and of the fact that the land and ocean sinks do not return the oxygen.
:::
:::

## The great experiments life has run on the atmosphere

::: definition Mass-independent sulfur isotope fractionation {#def-mifs}
Ultraviolet photolysis of $\mathrm{SO_2}$ in an anoxic atmosphere produces sulfur isotope anomalies ($\Delta^{33}\mathrm{S} \ne 0$) that are preserved in sediments. Once an ozone layer exists, ultraviolet is absorbed high up, the pathway shuts off, and $\Delta^{33}\mathrm{S}$ returns to zero. A $\Delta^{33}\mathrm{S}$ signal in a rock is therefore a direct, mechanism-specific test for an oxygen-free atmosphere.
:::

The signal is present in rocks older than about $2.45$ billion years and absent from all younger ones. That single observation dates the Great Oxidation Event, ties it to the irreversible oxidation of the surface environment, and explains why banded iron formations — which require dissolved $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ in sea water — disappear from the record at the same time. The intervening 900 million years, the "boring billion", held oxygen at perhaps a hundredth to a tenth of modern levels; animals, which need oxygen to build large bodies at low ambient concentrations, waited.

::: example Oxygen, fire and the colonisation of the land {#ex-fire}
Charcoal appears in the rock record only after land plants do, in the mid-Silurian, about $430$ million years ago. Fire requires an oxygen threshold: surface fires do not spread below roughly $15\%$ $\mathrm{O_2}$ by volume. The Phanerozoic record of oxygen, reconstructed from carbon-cycle models and from the fossil-plant and charcoal records, ranges from about $15\%$ in the mid-Palaeozoic to $30$–$35\%$ in the latest Carboniferous and earliest Permian.
::: solution
The correlation between the oxygen curve and the charcoal abundance is one of the tighter couplings in the geological record: more oxygen, more fire, more charcoal. The late Palaeozoic peak coincides with the great coal swamps — vast peat accumulations, which are exactly the incomplete rotting that the argument of [[#prop-oxygen]] requires — with the first extensive arid belts, and with the largest insects that ever lived, whose tracheal systems scale favourably with oxygen partial pressure. It also coincides with a global cooling into the late Palaeozoic ice age, because the same burial of organic carbon that released the oxygen also removed $\mathrm{CO_2}$.

The lesson generalises. Life did not merely respond to the Palaeozoic climate; it changed the flammability, the erosion rate, the weathering rate and the carbon dioxide of the planet, and the climate answered.
:::
:::

::: definition Bioturbation and the agronomic revolution {#def-bioturbation}
Burrowing animals mix sediment with an effective diffusivity $K \sim 1$–$10\ \mathrm{cm^2\,yr^{-1}}$, so the depth of the mixed layer grows as $\sqrt{Kt}$: a few centimetres in a decade, tens of centimetres in a millennium. Bioturbation destroys laminated lamination, oxygenates the sea floor, changes erosion rates by binding or loosening grains, and — before it evolved — did none of these. The appearance of deep burrows in the early Cambrian is called the **agronomic revolution** because it turned a two-dimensional seafloor into a three-dimensional habitat and ended the reign of microbial mats.
:::

::: example Reading a sediment with a diffusion equation {#ex-bioturb}
A tephra layer is deposited on a seabed where $K = 4\ \mathrm{cm^2\,yr^{-1}}$. After how long is the layer's peak concentration reduced to half its original height, and how thick has the mixed horizon become?
::: solution
A thin instantaneous source spreads as $c(z,t) \propto \exp(-z^2/4Kt)$, whose peak falls as $1/\sqrt{4\pi Kt}$ and whose $1/e$ width is $2\sqrt{Kt}$. Halving the peak requires $4\pi Kt$ to grow fourfold, i.e. four times the reference time; taking the reference as one year gives $t = 4\ \mathrm{yr}$ for a factor of two relative to one year, and in general the peak halves again for each factor of four in time. The mixed thickness after $1000$ years is
$$
2\sqrt{Kt} = 2\sqrt{4\times1000} = 126\ \mathrm{cm},
$$
which is deeper than most real mixed layers because burrow depth is limited by the energy cost of digging and by oxygen; observed mixed layers are $5$–$30\ \mathrm{cm}$.

The practical consequence is that bioturbation sets the time resolution of the sedimentary record. A storm bed can be recorded in hours, but its sharp upper contact is smeared by biology within decades, so the highest-frequency climate information in marine sections is destroyed by animals. The Precambrian, before burrowers, preserves laminations that the Phanerozoic cannot ([[sedimentology/continental-environments]]).
:::
:::

## Regulation without a regulator

::: definition Daisyworld {#def-daisyworld}
The Gaia hypothesis, in its testable form, says that life tends to regulate the surface environment because organisms that alter their environment in ways that suit themselves leave more descendants. The **Daisyworld** model makes the point in algebra: a planet with two species, dark daisies (albedo $0.2$) and light daisies (albedo $1.0$), on a bare-rock planet of albedo $0.5$. As sunlight rises, the daisies that keep the planet cool take over, and the planetary temperature stays nearly flat — until the daisies can no longer survive, and the temperature jumps.
:::

::: proposition Regulation has a range and then it fails {#prop-daisy}
In Daisyworld the equilibrium temperature is buffered against solar forcing while the daisies can grow, because the cover fraction $f$ adjusts so that the absorbed stellar flux is nearly constant. The buffering is a property of the feedback, not of any intention, and it fails abruptly at the edge of the daisies' tolerance.
:::

::: proof
Write the planetary albedo as the cover-weighted mean $\alpha = \alpha_\text{rock}(1 - f_\text{d} - f_\text{l}) + \alpha_\text{d}f_\text{d} + \alpha_\text{l}f_\text{l}$, and let each cover grow at a rate that is a bell-shaped function of temperature. The equilibrium is the fixed point of the coupled system (energy balance, cover fraction). Where the daisies' growth is temperature-limited and their cover is not saturated, $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}S \ll \mathrm{d}T_\text{bare}/\mathrm{d}S$: the derivative is reduced by the factor $1 + \beta$, where $\beta$ measures how strongly albedo responds to temperature through the biology. When $f$ reaches $0$ or $1$, or when the tolerance curve is left, $\beta$ collapses and the buffered regime ends. This is the mathematical content of every "stabilising feedback with a limited range" argument in the Earth system — including the silicate weathering thermostat and, on shorter timescales, the carbon-cycle sinks of [[earth-system/carbon-cycle]].
:::

Daisyworld is not a claim about the Earth; it is a demonstration that regulation does not require foresight, which was the substance of the twenty-year argument between its critics and its authors. The interesting empirical question is how much regulation the actual Earth shows, and the honest answer is: some, in specific places (ocean albedo and sulphur, soil moisture and vegetation, wetland methane), and much less at the planetary scale than the 1970s statements implied.

::: example The ocean fertilises itself, perhaps {#ex-dms}
Marine phytoplankton release dimethylsulphoniopropionate, which bacteria convert to dimethyl sulphide (DMS); $\sim15$–$25\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}$ reaches the atmosphere, where it oxidises to sulphate and methanesulphonate and provides cloud-condensation nuclei over the ocean. Take $10\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}$ of that flux, and ask whether it is large enough to matter for the aerosol.
::: solution
$10\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}$ is $10^{13}\ \mathrm{g\,yr^{-1}}$, which as sulphate is $3\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{g\,SO_4\,yr^{-1}}$. The atmospheric lifetime against wet deposition is $1$–$3$ days, so the standing burden this flux can support is
$$
\frac{10\ \mathrm{Tg\,S\,yr^{-1}}}{365/2.5\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}} \approx 0.07\ \mathrm{Tg\,S},
$$
the same order as the measured non-sea-salt sulphate burden of the remote marine atmosphere. The biological source is therefore large enough to dominate the remote-ocean sulphate aerosol, and measurements confirm that it does: over the Southern Ocean most non-sea-salt sulphate is biogenic.

Whether the feedback closes — whether warmer water means less DMS and hence more sunlight and more warming — is the CLAW hypothesis, and it remains unresolved. Laboratory and field studies disagree on the sign of the temperature sensitivity of DMS production, and cloud responses to aerosol are the least constrained term in climate sensitivity ([[atmospheric-chemistry/aerosols]], [[climate-system/feedbacks-sensitivity]]).
:::

::: warning The Amazon is not the lungs of the Earth, and Gaia is not a deity
Two slogans do a great deal of harm by being half right. "The lungs of the Earth" implies that the rainforest supplies our oxygen: it does not, because a mature forest respires almost exactly what it fixes, and the atmosphere's oxygen surplus comes from burial in sediments and from the ocean's net community production over geological time. What the Amazon does supply is water — it recycles two thirds of its own rainfall — and carbon storage, and both are being lost. "Gaia" implies the Earth is a self-regulating organism: the useful content of the idea is that biological feedbacks exist and can stabilise or destabilise, and the misleading content is the implication of purpose or of robustness. Daisyworld buffers until it doesn't, and then it breaks.
:::

::: quiz
Why can a doubling of global photosynthesis, on its own, leave atmospheric oxygen unchanged?
- [ ] Because oxygen reacts with ozone faster than it is produced.
- [x] Because respiration and decomposition consume essentially all the oxygen produced; only buried organic carbon leaves a net surplus.
- [ ] Because the ocean absorbs the extra oxygen.
- [ ] Because photosynthesis releases oxygen only at night.
::: solution
Photosynthesis and respiration are near-perfect opposites and are roughly balanced globally, so the fluxes cancel in the oxygen budget. The net source of oxygen is the fraction of primary production that escapes oxidation — today $0.1$–$0.2\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$ of organic carbon and the pyrite sulphur that accompanies it, a few per cent of one per cent of production. This is why the Great Oxidation Event required a change in *burial*, not in productivity: the evidence points to the stabilisation of organic carbon in newly created continental shelves and the exhaustion of reduced sinks such as dissolved $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and volcanic $\mathrm{H_2}$ ([[earth-history/oxygen-proterozoic]]).
:::
:::

::: warning The current extinction rate is the least precise and most consequential number in this chapter
Background extinction, measured from marine invertebrate genera through the Phanerozoic, is about $0.1$–$1$ extinctions per million species-years. Observed vertebrate extinctions since 1900 run at $50$–$100$ times that; projected extinctions from habitat loss, using the species–area relation, run at $100$–$1000$ times; and the IPBES assessment of 2019 concluded that around a million species are threatened. Three honest caveats. Species–area projections assume equilibrium and over-predict near-term losses. Taxonomic knowledge is so uneven that most threatened species are undescribed. And the number that matters for ecosystem function is not species count but the loss of functions, interactions and biogeographic spread, which no index captures. The conclusion survives all three caveats; the precision does not.
:::

::: history
The idea that life is a geological force older than geology itself: Jean-Baptiste Robinet (Robinet) and later Georges-Louis Leclerc de Buffon (Buffon) both wrote of living matter as an active agent in the eighteenth century. Vladimir Vernadsky (Vernadsky) made it quantitative and planetary in *La Biosphère* (1926), arguing that living organisms are "a geological force" and that the biosphere's chemistry is dominated by biological transfer of gases — the book that made the concept respectable and that was ignored in the West for forty years. The Russian soil tradition (Dokuchaev, Sukachev) supplied the coevolution of soils and life. Alfred Redfield (Redfield) in 1934 explained the fixed nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio of sea water as the stoichiometry of the organisms themselves, turning ocean chemistry into biology. G. Evelyn Hutchinson (Hutchinson) in the 1940s and 1950s founded the ecology of biogeochemical cycles. In 1965 James Lovelock (Lovelock), consulting for NASA on how to detect life on Mars, argued that a living planet's atmosphere is in chemical disequilibrium and would be recognisable from orbit; with Lynn Margulis (Margulis), who supplied the microbial metabolism, this became Gaia (1972–1974). Andrew Watson (Watson) and Lovelock's Daisyworld (1983) turned the argument from a quarrel into a modelling exercise. Meanwhile the empirical base accumulated: Newell (Newell) in 1965 computed the background extinction rate from the fossil record, Raup (Raup) and Sepkoski (Sepkoski) in the 1980s found the periodicity that provoked the impact debate, Luis and Walter Alvarez (Alvarez) found iridium in the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary clay in 1980, and Farquhar (Farquhar) and colleagues in 2000 showed that mass-independent sulfur isotope anomalies in Archaean rocks proved an anoxic early atmosphere, giving the field its cleanest single datum.
:::

## Where this leads

Life is the boundary condition that the physical climate does not have: it sets the surface albedo, the roughness, the evapotranspiration, the soil depth and the weathering rate, and it sets the composition of the air. Its chemistry is developed in [[biogeochemistry/principles]] and [[biogeochemistry/production-decomposition]], its deep history in [[earth-history/early-earth]], [[earth-history/oxygen-proterozoic]] and [[earth-history/cenozoic]], its ocean machinery in [[oceanography/productivity]], its soils in [[geomorphology/weathering]] and [[geochemistry/weathering-chemistry]], and its present crisis in [[climate-change/impacts]].

::: summary
- Photosynthesis and respiration cancel; only burial changes the air. Oxygen is a ledger of carbon that did not rot.
- Mass-independent sulfur isotopes date the Great Oxidation Event at $2.45$ Ga and prove the earlier atmosphere was anoxic; banded iron formations end at the same horizon.
- Global NPP is $\sim60\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$; humans use or appropriate roughly a third of the terrestrial part, and human-made mass now exceeds the mass of all living biomass.
- The biological pump exports $11\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$ and buries $1$–$2\%$ of it; its efficiency declines with warming.
- Bioturbation, with $K \sim 1$–$10\ \mathrm{cm^2\,yr^{-1}}$, sets the mixing depth and therefore the time resolution of the sedimentary record.
- Regulation without a regulator is possible (Daisyworld), but it has a range and fails abruptly — the correct frame for every biological feedback.
- Extinction rates are $50$–$1000$ times background depending on how they are measured; the loss of functions is less well measured than the loss of species.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Photosynthetic efficiency {level=1 check="0.063"}
Land GPP is $60\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$ over $1.3\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ of vegetated surface. Incoming photosynthetically active radiation over land averages $110\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and $1\ \mathrm{g\,C}$ fixed requires $470\ \mathrm{kJ}$. Find the fraction of PAR fixed.
::: solution
Carbon fixed per unit area: $60\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{g\,yr^{-1}}/1.3\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^2} = 4.6\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$. Energy: $4.6\times10^{5}\times470 = 2.2\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$, or $6.9\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Against $110\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ this is $6.3\%$ of PAR — but note that this is *gross* fixation per unit leaf area averaged over the whole land surface including deserts; per unit leaf area in growing season the efficiency is a few per cent, consistent with the $0.5$–$2\%$ range quoted for canopies.
:::
:::

::: exercise Redfield arithmetic {level=1 check="2120"}
A litre of sea water contains $20\ \mu\mathrm{mol}$ of phosphate and, by Redfield, the corresponding carbon. How many micromoles of carbon can be produced from it?
::: solution
$20\times106 = 2120\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,C}$. Conversely, a measured phosphate drawdown of $2\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ in the surface layer implies $212\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ of carbon fixed and exported — the arithmetic behind every estimate of the biological pump.
:::
:::

::: exercise Pump efficiency {level=1 check="1.4"}
Export across $100\ \mathrm{m}$ is $11\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$ and burial is $0.15\ \mathrm{GtC\,yr^{-1}}$. Find the burial efficiency.
::: solution
$0.15/11 = 1.4\%$. The remaining $98.6\%$ is respired inside the ocean, which is exactly what keeps the deep ocean's carbon and nutrient inventory high and its surface waters poor.
:::
:::

::: exercise The species–area relation {level=2 check="0.44"}
With $S/S_0 = (A/A_0)^{0.25}$, what fraction of species is lost at equilibrium when habitat is reduced to $10\%$ of its extent?
::: solution
$1 - 0.1^{0.25} = 1 - 0.56 = 0.44$, so $44\%$. Halving habitat costs $16\%$. The exponent $z$ ranges from $0.15$ (continuous, well-sampled habitats) to $0.4$ (islands, islands-in-the-sky), and the equilibrium assumption means the prediction is a commitment for centuries, not for next year.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bioturbation {level=2 check="40"}
With $K = 4\ \mathrm{cm^2\,yr^{-1}}$, find the $1/e$ mixing thickness $2\sqrt{Kt}$ after $100$ years.
::: solution
$2\sqrt{4\times100} = 40\ \mathrm{cm}$; the half-amplitude thickness is smaller by a factor of about two, $\sim20\ \mathrm{cm}$, which is the observed range for pelagic sediments.
:::
:::

::: exercise Oxygen and the carbon cycle {level=2}
If all $1100\ \mathrm{GtC}$ of fossil reserves are burned, by how many parts per million does the atmospheric oxygen volume mixing ratio fall, and is that detectable?
::: solution
$9.2\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{mol}$ of $\mathrm{O_2}$ out of $3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$, i.e. $2.5\times10^{-3}$ relative. Against $209{,}500\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ this is a fall of $520\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ — five hundred parts per million of the air's oxygen. Modern interferometric methods reach $10$–$20\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$, so the signal is two orders of magnitude above the noise, and it has been measured. The oxygen measurement is thus an independent check on the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ measurement: the two must decline and rise in the ratio set by combustion chemistry, and they do.
:::
:::

::: exercise The oxygen threshold for fire {level=3}
Flammability experiments on vegetation give a critical oxygen concentration near $15\%$ for sustained spread and $26\%$ for crown fires. If the late Carboniferous reached $32\%$, by what factor does the burnable area increase if fire spread scales as $(p\mathrm{O_2} - 0.15)^{2}$?
::: solution
$[(0.32 - 0.15)/(0.21 - 0.15)]^{2} = (0.17/0.06)^{2} = 8.0$ relative to the present, and $[(0.32-0.15)/(0.16-0.15)]^2 = 289$ relative to a low-oxygen mid-Palaeozoic atmosphere. The scaling is invented for the exercise — the real relationship is a percolation threshold, sharper than any power law — but the message is robust: a few per cent of oxygen swings the fire regime by an order of magnitude, which is why the charcoal record, the first arid dune fields and the first large wildfires all appear together in the Silurian–Devonian, and why the Carboniferous coal swamps are interleaved with charcoal layers.
:::
:::

::: exercise Nutrient trapping and the pump {level=3}
The deep ocean has $2.2\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ of phosphate against near zero at the surface. Estimate the carbon that the soft-tissue pump holds out of the atmosphere–surface ocean system, and compare it with the assessed effect of the pump on pre-industrial $\mathrm{CO_2}$.
::: solution
If the surface layer (take $100\ \mathrm{m}$) had deep-water nutrients, it would hold $2.2\times106 = 233\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ more carbon. Spreading that through the whole ocean ($100/3800$ of its depth) gives $\sim6\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$, or
$$
6\times10^{-6}\times1.4\times10^{21}\times12\ \mathrm{g} = 100\ \mathrm{GtC}
$$
of carbon that the pump keeps out of the surface layer. Because the surface ocean resists changes in $p\mathrm{CO_2}$ only weakly compared with the atmosphere, the atmospheric effect is much larger than this inventory: model experiments that remove the soft-tissue pump raise pre-industrial atmospheric $\mathrm{CO_2}$ by roughly $100$–$200\ \mathrm{ppm}$, and experiments that also remove the temperature-driven solubility contrast raise it by several hundred more. The pump is not a rounding error in the carbon cycle ([[climate-system/carbon-climate]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Biomass accounting {level=3}
Global living biomass is $550\ \mathrm{Gt\,C}$, of which plants are $450$. Humans are $0.06\ \mathrm{Gt\,C}$, domesticated animals $0.1$, wild mammals $0.2$. Anthropogenic (built) mass is $1100\ \mathrm{Gt}$ dry. Compute the ratios: humans to plants, humans and livestock to wild mammals, built mass to living mass.
::: solution
Humans are $0.06/450 = 1.3\times10^{-4}$ of plant biomass, and $0.06/550 = 1.1\times10^{-4}$ of all life. Humans plus livestock are $0.16/0.2 = 0.8$, i.e. $45\%$ of mammal biomass by these particular estimates (published values range up to $96\%$ depending on how wild mammal biomass is counted). Built mass is $1100/550 = 2$ times the mass of all living things.

The arithmetic is worth doing slowly, because it is the honest version of "humans are a small part of nature". By number of individuals we are not; by biomass we are negligible; by the mass of what we build and by the fraction of primary production we appropriate we are a geological agent, and by the rate at which we are changing the carbon and nitrogen cycles we are, on the relevant timescales, the dominant one.
:::
:::

::: exercise Regulation and its failure {level=3}
In a Daisyworld with bare-rock albedo $0.5$, dark-daisy albedo $0.2$ and light-daisy albedo $1.0$, the surface is entirely covered by dark daisies. By how much does the absorbed stellar flux change if the daisies die out, and what does that say about the hysteresis of a vegetated planet?
::: solution
Absorbed fraction goes from $1 - 0.2 = 0.8$ to $1 - 0.5 = 0.5$: a $37.5\%$ reduction in absorbed energy, which on the global mean insolation of $341\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ is $102\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ — comparable in magnitude to the largest radiative forcings the Earth has experienced in its recent history. A vegetated surface is therefore strongly bistable near the desert threshold, and the transition back requires the climate to become considerably cooler than the one that killed the vegetation. The same asymmetry appears in real systems: the Sahel's greening and browning thresholds differ, permafrost-carbon and boreal-forest-albedo feedbacks are hysteretic, and it is why "tipping point" is the right vocabulary for the biological regulation of the planet ([[climate-change/tipping-points]]).
:::
:::
