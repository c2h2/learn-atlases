A sensor is a chain of trade-offs and an orbit is a schedule, and the two together — not the brand name on the mission patch — determine what a dataset can and cannot say. The trade-offs are the four resolutions: spatial (what size object), spectral (what kind of material), radiometric (what contrast) and temporal (how often), of which the first three are coupled by photon statistics through the pixel's signal-to-noise ratio, and all four are coupled to the fourth by the orbit's geometry. The schedule is celestial mechanics plus politics: sun-synchronous dawn-and-dusk orbits for illumination-consistent imaging, geostationary orbits for continuous watches, repeat tracks for change detection and interferometry, and constellations that buy revisit time with hundreds of small satellites. This lesson makes the chain explicit — detector designs from whiskbroom scanners to push-broom arrays and synthetic apertures, the radiometry of signal-to-noise and quantization, the orbital mechanics a mission designer actually uses, and the institutional architecture of the Landsat, Sentinel, MODIS-VIIRS and weather-satellite records that the rest of this course, and most of modern Earth-system science, consumes. The prerequisite is the previous lesson's radiometry; the reward is the ability to open any mission's product page and read, in ten minutes, exactly what it can measure to what accuracy how often — a skill that matters more than any single technique in the lessons that follow.
::: definition The four resolutions and their couplings {#def-resolutions}
**Spatial resolution** is the size of the ground area a pixel integrates — formally the convolution of the detector's instantaneous field of view with the platform's altitude and pointing, practically the ground sample distance (altitude times IFOV, or swath width divided by across-track pixels: a 185-km swath over 7517 detectors is 24.6 m, rounded to the "30-m" Landsat label for its resampled products). The relevant quantities are the **point spread function** (a pixel is not a box; it bleeds) and the **modulation transfer function** (the contrast loss at fine spatial scales that decides whether a 30-m pixel can resolve a 40-m road at all, let alone measure it). **Spectral resolution** is band count and width: panchromatic (one broad band), multispectral (4–15 bands, Landsat-and-Sentinel-2 scale), hyperspectral (100–300 bands, PRISMA-and-EnMAP-and-EMIT scale, with the photon penalty and the data-volume penalty that the classification lesson's dimensionality problem inherits), and the few-hundred-channel hyperspectral imagers of the 2020s that are finally paying EMIT's debt of unmixing accuracy. **Radiometric resolution** is the smallest detectable radiance difference: the detector's noise-equivalent delta radiance, the quantization of the digitizer (Landsat-1's 6 bits, 64 levels; Landsat-8-and-Sentinel-2's 12 bits, 4096 levels), and the signal-to-noise ratio that ties all three resolutions together — for a photon-noise-limited detector, SNR grows as the square root of (pixel area × band width × dwell time), so halving pixel size costs a factor of two in SNR unless band width, dwell or aperture pays it back, which is the one equation behind every "why can't it be high-resolution, hyperspectral, daily and cheap?" conversation. **Temporal resolution** is revisit, bought three ways: a wide swath (MODIS's 2330 km buys two days at the cost of 250–1000-m pixels), a fleet (Sentinel-2's two satellites halve the five-day design revisit; Planet's hundreds of Dove cubesats buy daily 3-m at the cost of three bands and modest calibration), or a high orbit (geostationary: the whole disk, hourly-or-ten-minutely, at kilometre pixels). The couplings are the lesson: resolution is not a spec sheet, it is a constrained optimization, and choosing a dataset for a task is writing down which resolution the science needs and which it can pay away.
:::

::: definition Sensor designs: whiskbroom, push-broom, arrays, apertures {#def-designs}
**Whiskbroom scanners** (the 1972–2013 Landsat MSS-and-TM-and-ETM+ lineage, NOAA's AVHRR) sweep a pointing mirror across track and sample along track with one-or-a-few detectors: proven, calibratable, and limited by dwell time — the mirror must spend its integration time on every pixel, so finer pixels demand faster mirrors or lower SNR. **Push-broom imagers** (SPOT-1's 1986 HRV heritage; Landsat-8's OLI, Sentinel-2's MSI, the whole modern optical fleet) stare at one across-track line with a detector array and let the platform's motion build the second dimension: every pixel gets the full line-integration time, so push-broom buys SNR and geometric stability (no moving mirror jitter) at the cost of needing long, good detector arrays — the reason spectral-band count and array length became the currency of optical mission design, and why CTM-and-HyspIRI-style designs add along-track staging to escape the line-length limit. **Focal-plane arrays** couple the detector story to cooling and drift: silicon sees 0.4–1.0 µm; InGaAs shortwave-infrared arrays (the 2020s H4RG-and-LWIR-MCT generations) must be cooled to ~90 K or their dark current eats the signal, which is why SWIR-and-thermal instruments carry radiators-and-Stirling coolers and why the shortwave-infrared bands of Sentinel-2-and-Landsat-8 age measurably (the 2016–2024 SWIR-degradation-and-anomaly literature is a sensor-health paper in serial form). **Synthetic-aperture radar inverts the design logic**: along-track resolution comes not from the antenna but from coherent processing of the echoes as the platform flies — a physical 10-m antenna synthesizes a 10-km aperture and metre-scale pixels independent of altitude and weather — so the "detector" is a signal processor and the trade shifts from photons to pulse bandwidth (range resolution, c/(2B)) and processed bandwidth (azimuth resolution, half the real antenna length in stripmap). **Lidar designs** count photons and time their flight (the microlidar-and-ICESat-and-GEDI-and-ATLAS lineage: spot, linear array, and sector-flashing geometries, each a different purchase of footprint, coverage and vertical accuracy), and **radiometers** (microwave and thermal) trade spatial for radiometric resolution in the opposite direction from imagers, because the targets emit weakly and the science is in the parts-of-a-kelvin. The design class fixes a dataset's failure modes as much as its strengths: whiskbrooms die of mirror wear and timing jitter, push-brooms of dead detector columns (the Landsat-7 path-stripes after its 2003 slice failure, Sentinel-2A's 2023 column anomalies), SARs of processor-and-orbit drift, lidars of laser lifetime — and a careful user knows the failure modes of the dataset before the statistics of the science.
:::

::: definition Orbits: sun-synchronous, geostationary, repeat tracks, constellations {#def-orbits}
Low Earth imaging orbits sit at 500–800 km where drag is tolerable and the field of view is fine, and they exploit two perturbations the textbook mechanics ignores. The **J2 oblateness torque** precesses the orbital plane at a rate proportional to cos(i)/(a(1−e²))²·⁵, and at ~705-km altitude a near-polar inclination of ~98.2° precesses exactly 360° per year — the **sun-synchronous orbit**, constant local solar time of day, illumination-consistent time series, and the geometric reason a snowmelt or crop phenology record from a sun-synchronous fleet is interpretable at all (the 1984–2025 Landsat-5-to-Landsat-9-and-Sentinel-2 succession is designed around it, with the dawn-dusk variant near 06:00-and-18:00 for thermal-and-radiation-budget missions like the 2000 Terra-and-Aqua pairing). **Repeat tracks** add a second resonance: choosing the period so that an integer number of orbits slips an integer number of Earth-rotations-worth of ground tracks over an integer number of days (Sentinel-1's 175 orbits in 25 days, Landsat's 233 in 16) makes the satellite revisit the same ground geometry — the precondition for change detection, for along-track interferometry, and for the altimetry ground tracks whose 10-to-35-day revisits built the sea-level record of the [[oceanography/sea-level]] lesson. **Geostationary orbit** (35786 km, 24-hour period, equatorial) buys a fixed view of a hemisphere at the cost of coarse pixels and a grazing viewing geometry at high latitudes — the meteorological-imager architecture (the 1966–2025 from-SMS-to-GOES-R-and-Meteosat-Third-Generation-and-Himawari-8-and-9 lineage of the [[meteorology/forecasting]] course), ten-minute mesoscale imaging, lightning mapping and, since the 2010s, geostationary hyperspectral sounders whose spectra probe the boundary layer the polar orbiters sample only twice a day. **Medium orbits** serve positioning (the 20200-km GNSS constellations of the [[remote-sensing/maps-projections]] lesson) and altimetry's intermediate references, while **constellations** — Planet's optical swarms, Spire's radio-occultation fleet, the 2020s radar-satellite startups — replace the single-satellite revisit schedule with a statistical one: N spacecraft, random-ish phases, an expected revisit that is a distribution rather than a clock, and calibration-and-vicarious-validation programs (the 2018–2025 Planet radiometric-characterization literature) struggling to hold the network's photometry to the few-per-cent that scientific products quietly require. The institutional orbit matters as much as the physical one: the free, sustained, decades-long data policies (Landsat's 2008 free release, Copernicus's open regime) are what converted orbits into records, and every longitudinal study in the lessons ahead is a study of whichever orbital schedules some agency agreed to keep flying.
:::

::: example Landsat and Sentinel-2: two resolutions, one record {#ex-landsat-sentinel}
Landsat (1972–present, nine successful satellites, 2900-plus scenes per path-and-row, 30-m multispectral, 185-km swath, 16-day repeat, 12-bit since 2013) and Sentinel-2 (2015–present, A-and-B-and-C, 10–20–60-m bands, 290-km swath, five-day combined revisit, 13 bands including the 0.7-µm red-edge trio Landsat never carried) are the two halves of the land-imaging backbone, and their differences are the resolutions definition in practice: Sentinel-2's red-edge bands serve the vegetation-physiology products of the image-processing lesson, Landsat's 15-µm-thermal-equivalent-and-SWIR band set (and its 1982-start record) serve the temperature-and-fire-and-lake-ice products of the thermal lesson, and the USGS-and-EUMETSAT harmonization (the 2016–2024 L2A-to-Landsat-surface-reflectance intercomparison literature, residuals of one-to-three per cent in most bands) is what lets a single time series cross the 2013–2015 sensor boundary. The joint record — five decades of 30-m-equivalent, illumination-consistent, free-availability surface reflectance — is the reason land-change science can date a deforestation front, a reservoir or a housing tract to the month, and it exists because two agencies kept flying the same trade-off for fifty years: the institutional resolution, as the orbits definition insists, is a fifth resolution.
:::

::: example MODIS and VIIRS: the wide-swath workhorses {#ex-modis-viirs}
MODIS (Terra 1999, Aqua 2002; 36 bands, 250–1000-m pixels, a 2330-km swath swept at 1.0 Hz, one-to-two-day global return) and its successor VIIRS (Suomi-NPP 2011, NOAA-20 2017, NOAA-21 2022; 22 bands, 750-m and 375-m imaging, 3060-km swath, and — after the early-banding-artifact literature of 2012–2018 — a mirror design finally quiet enough for calibration) are the reason global products exist at daily cadence: the MODIS land-surface-temperature, NDVI, burned-area, leaf-area, snow-cover, aerosol-and-fire products (the 2002–2025 collection-and-reprocessing lineage, collection 6.1 in 2021) and their VIIRS successors define the observational layer under the [[climate-change/observed-change]] lesson's greening-and-browning and fire-activity statistics and under the [[glaciology/observing-ice]] lesson's melt-season records. The design's price list is equally instructive: coarse pixels mean mixed scenes and the fractional-cover literature of the previous lesson; a five-year-plus mirror-and-detector degradation history means the entire vicarious-calibration apparatus (the desert-study sites, the moon-as-lamp technique of the 2003–2016 Sun–Moon calibration literature) exists to hold the trending instrument to its one-per-cent design; and the 1.38-µm cirrus band, the 1.6-µm snow band and the 3.7-µm fire band are each a window-and-target trade bought with a detector channel — twenty-two to thirty-six channels for the whole planet every day, versus thirteen for a fifth of it every five days, the two regimes the fusion lesson later learns to combine.
:::

::: example Geostationary imagers: watching the same storm for twelve hours {#ex-geo}
A polar-orbiting imager sees a cyclone twice a day; Himawari-8-and-9 (2015-and-2016, AHI with 16 bands and a ten-minute full-disk scan) and the GOES-R series GOES-16-and-17 (2016–2018, ABI, the same band plan, five-minute mesoscale scans) watch it continuously — and the difference is not convenience but physics: diurnal cloud-and-convection cycles, rapid intensification, lightning, volcanic ash and smoke-plume transport are all processes whose timescale is shorter than a polar revisit, and the geostationary records (the 2015–2025 Himawari-and-GOES-R convection-and-eye-and-rapid-intensification literature, the 2022 Hunga Tonga ash-and-wave tracking that made the [[natural-hazards/volcanic-hazards]] lesson's nowcast case) are the payoff of the orbits definition's hemisphere-fixed geometry. The costs are the definition's too: kilometre-scale pixels (two kilometres at best), grazing high-latitude viewing that smears the geometry the maps lesson will formalize, and a thermal band set designed around the 4-and-11-and-13-µm windows where the split-window logic of the thermal lesson must fight a slantier path. The 2020s generation — MTG-I-with-its-FCI-and-IRS sounders, the 2025-and-beyond geostationary-sounder launches — adds hyperspectral infrared sounding from 36000 km, closing the atmospheric-chemistry-sounding gap that the [[atmospheric-chemistry/trace-gases]] lessons sample only in swaths, and making the four-orbits-in-one architecture (geostationary imager, polar imager, polar sounder, constellation) the honest description of how the atmosphere is actually observed.
:::

::: example CubeSat constellations: resolution as a statistics problem {#ex-constellations}
Planet's Dove fleet (hundreds of 5–10-kg cubesats since 2016, three-or-four-band 3–5-m cameras, a daily global image stitched from tens of thousands of scenes) and the 2020s radar-and-infrared startups (Capella's 25-cm-spotlight radars, Umbra's, and the methane-sensing constellations of the 2022–2025 MethaneSAT-and-CarbonMapper generation) test the orbits definition's statistical-revisit claim against the radiometry of the first lesson: a 5-kg satellite has a 7-cm aperture where Landsat-class photometry wants 20, so the photon budget is paid for with band width, with SNR, and with calibration discipline — and the 2018–2025 vicarious-calibration literature (the PICS-desert-and-ROCC-retrieval intercomparisons, the two-to-five-per-cent absolute-reflectance results, the per-camera relative-stability numbers) reads like a court transcript on exactly that question. The honest summary from the validation papers: constellation data are excellent for detection-and-location at daily cadence (the 2020–2025 ship-detection, construction-tracking, flood-mapping and dark-vessel literature), adequate for fractional-cover trends when the fleet's relative stability is exploited and its absolute offsets are avoided, and unreliable for the sub-per-cent radiometric claims that only calibrated single instruments with desert sites can support. The constellation era's real innovation is not small satellites but the product model — tasking, daily global basemaps, analysis-ready tiles — which moves the trade-off from the sensor to the user's question, and which is why the classification-and-change lessons ahead treat "which dataset" as a hypothesis about timescale, not about prestige.
:::

::: history From rocket cameras to open constellations {#hist-sensors}
**1946–1972.** A V-2 camera at White Sands in October 1946 photographs 200 km of the Earth from 105 km up, the first image taken from space; the 1960 TIROS-1 television (the Verner Suomi photopolarimeter's contribution to its successor line) turns clouds into a global synoptic object; and the 23 July 1972 launch of Landsat-1, the William Pecora-designed Earth Resources Technology Satellite, begins the continuous land-imaging record — a whiskbroom, four bands, 80-m pixels, and a data policy, finalized in 2008 as free release, that becomes the field's most consequential hardware decision. **1978–2002.** The sensor families diversify: Seasat's 1978 radar (ten months that founded the radar lineage of the [[remote-sensing/radar-lidar]] lesson), the 1982–2013 meteorological-AVHRR and 1986 SPOT-1 push-broom generations that fixed today's detector architecture, the 1991–1995 ERS-1-and-RadarSat calibration heritages, and the 1999–2002 Terra-and-Aqua EOS platforms, whose MODIS instruments first deliver the daily global, 36-band, calibrated-products regime. **2014–2024.** Copernicus opens systematic 10-m optical-and-radar data (Sentinel-1A in April 2014, Sentinel-2A in June 2015, the constellation and free-and-open policies that the 2016–2024 analysis-ready-data literature converts into continental-scale science by anyone), Planet's Doves normalize daily 3-m commercial imagery from 2016, Landsat-8-and-9 carry the record forward at 12 bits, and the 2022–2024 NISAR-and-SWOT-and-EMIT launches close the decade that made interferometry, surface hydrology and hyperspectral data open by default — sensor history ending, for now, with the orbit as infrastructure and the data policy as the instrument.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-sunsync}
Why do land-imaging satellites fly sun-synchronous orbits rather than arbitrary polar orbits?

- [x] Constant local solar time keeps illumination geometry nearly fixed between revisits, so reflectance changes mean surface changes
- [ ] Because sun-synchronous orbits require less launch energy
- [ ] Because the J2 perturbation improves radiometric calibration
- [ ] Because geostationary satellites cannot see polar regions

::: solution :::
Sun synchronism is the J2-driven precession matched to one year, and its purpose is photometric: the same scene is imaged at nearly the same sun angle every revisit, so the BRDF-and-illumination term of the previous lesson is held constant and time series are interpretable. Launch energy (B) is essentially indifferent at fixed inclination; J2 (C) is the mechanism, not a calibration benefit; and (D) is true but irrelevant to the choice.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-constellation}
A cubesat constellation achieves daily global coverage at 3-m resolution with three spectral bands and a few-per-cent absolute radiometric uncertainty. For which task is it least suited?

- [ ] Detecting and locating new construction within a week
- [ ] Mapping the extent of a flood the day after it happens
- [x] Detecting a 0.5-per-cent decadal surface-reflectance trend for climate records
- [ ] Counting ships in a busy shipping lane

::: solution :::
Sub-percent absolute radiometry demands stable, vicariously calibrated single instruments with long time-overlap records — the Landsat-and-Sentinel cross-calibration regime, not a fleet with per-camera offsets of a few per cent (the constellation example's validation literature). Daily-change detection-and-location (A, B, D) is exactly what the statistical-revisit model buys; the trade the fleet makes is absolute radiometry for cadence, and the honest uses follow from the trade.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="30"}
A push-broom imager has a 180-km swath imaged by 6000 detector elements across track. What is the ground sample distance in metres?
::: solution :::
GSD = 180000/6000 = **30 m** — the swath-over-detectors arithmetic behind every "30-m" label, and a reminder that the true resolution is the point-spread-function width, typically 1.1–1.3 times the nominal GSD, which is why a 30-m Landsat pixel resolves a 40-m road only as a partial-brightness ramp.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="7.5"}
Compute the orbital speed at 705-km altitude: v = sqrt(GM/r) with GM = 3.986e14 m³/s² and r = 6371 + 705 = 7076 km. Give the answer in km/s to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
v = sqrt(3.986e14/7.076e6) = sqrt(5.633e7) = 7506 m/s, about **7.5 km/s** — 7.5 km every second, which is why a 185-km along-track scene is imaged in 25 seconds, why attitude jitter at the one-hertz level is a real geometric error, and why the along-track dimension of every push-broom image is the platform's flight written in metres.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="99"}
Using the same numbers (r = 7.076e6 m, v = 7506 m/s), compute the orbital period T = 2πr/v in minutes, to the nearest minute.
::: solution :::
T = 2π × 7.076e6/7506 = 5923 s = 98.7 min, about **99 minutes** — the low-orbit metronome: about fifteen orbits a day, which is why a 16-day repeat needs 233 orbits and a 12-day repeat 175, and why "daily revisit" from one narrow-swath satellite is physically impossible without either a wide swath or a fleet.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="4096"}
Landsat-8's Operational Land Imager digitizes each band at 12 bits. How many quantization levels does that give?
::: solution :::
2¹² = **4096** levels (Landsat-1's 6 bits gave 64) — the radiometric-resolution revolution in one exponent, and the reason the 2013-and-later records can resolve dark-water and dense-vegetation gradients that saturated-or-flattened in the older archives, at the cost of the file sizes the data-handling literature lives on.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="2"}
Radar and radiometer processing averages n independent looks to reduce speckle-and-noise; the noise standard deviation falls as sqrt(n). By what factor does 4-looking reduce the noise?
::: solution :::
sqrt(4) = **2** — the averaging trade that runs through the whole course (multi-looking in radar, the twelve-month compositing of MODIS surface reflectance, the ensemble means of the climate-modelling course), always paying resolution or latency for noise: 4-looking halves the noise and doubles the effective pixel, the resolution-versus-precision exchange rate in its purest form.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="6"}
Sentinel-1A has a 12-day repeat orbit. With an identical Sentinel-1B in the same track phase (the 2016–2022 pairing), what is the combined revisit time in days?
::: solution :::
12/2 = **6 days** — the fleet arithmetic of the orbits definition (and with Sentinel-1C since 2024 the design intent is 3-satellite, 4-day coverage), and the reason "radar every week, everywhere, free" — the flood-and-ice-and-forest-change backbone of the 2015–2025 literature — is a statement about orbital schedules, not about any single instrument's capability.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3.4"}
Sentinel-1's 175-orbit-in-25-days repeat ground track means the equator-crossing time of each successive orbit drifts before the pattern closes. The drift per orbit is 25 days divided by 175 orbits. Give the answer in hours to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
25 × 24/175 = 600/175 = 3.43, about **3.4 hours** per orbit — the ground track walks westward-and-southward by that much local time each pass until day 25 closes the pattern, and the same drift, deliberately mis-tuned, gives the alternative-ascending-and-descending-and-tandem geometries (the SWOT kaRIn wide-swath altimetry of 2023, the ICESat-2-and-ECOSTRESS formations) that turn one orbit's sparsity into another's coverage.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="5"}
In stripmap mode, a synthetic-aperture radar's azimuth resolution equals half the real antenna length, independent of altitude. For a 10-m antenna, what is the azimuth resolution in metres?
::: solution :::
10/2 = **5 m** — the synthetic-aperture paradox of the designs definition (a longer real antenna gives finer azimuth resolution but, at fixed altitude, a narrower antenna beam and hence finer Doppler bandwidth — the factor of two is the coherent-processing result), and the reason radar pixels are rectangular design choices (Sentinel-1's 5.4 × 20 m burst geometry) rather than diffraction limits from orbit.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3"}
Two cameras are otherwise identical, but one has a 30-cm aperture and the other a 10-cm aperture. Photon-limited signal-to-noise scales with aperture area, so by what factor is the larger aperture's SNR better?
::: solution :::
(30/10)² = **3** — the aperture is the one resolution a cubesat cannot buy with software (the constellation example's photon-budget sentence), and the square law is why the flagship radiometers grow rather than multiply: a 20-cm-to-30-cm step triples the noise budget of every band, which is why Planet's photometry stays a few per cent and Landsat's stays below one.
:::

::: warning Four sensor-and-orbit fallacies
1. **"Twice the pixels, twice the information."** Halving pixel size quadruples data volume and quarters photon flux, so unless aperture, band width or dwell pays the SNR bill, the finer image carries the same information in more, noisier samples — the widget's square law — and the 2010s-and-2020s super-resolution literature's sobering result is that learned downscaling-and-fusion recovers correlation structure, not photons that were never collected. The honest question before any "higher-resolution" upgrade is which scientific claim the current pixels cannot support: if the answer is aesthetic, the cost is pure loss, and the classification lesson's dimensionality-and-mixture arguments get worse, not better, with every finer, wider, noisier cube.
2. **"Same date, same orbit, so the images are comparable."** Same orbit is not same geometry: off-nadir angles vary within and between fleets (Sentinel-2's ±50° full range, Landsat's ±7.5° plus the post-2003 SLC-off gaps), bidirectional effects move canopy reflectance by tens of per cent with view angle, and adjacent-scene BRDF anisotropy is exactly the signal the MODIS-and-Sentinel-2 normalization products exist to remove; the change-detection literature's most retracted-looking results (apparent deforestation along track edges, "phenology" that is a mirror-angle step function) are geometry mislabelled as surface. Comparable means co-registered, co-normalized and atmospherically matched — three operations, none automatic, all reportable.
3. **"Free data means free knowledge."** The open-record argument of this lesson is about institutional resolution, but the products themselves carry processing-baseline versions, known anomalies (the Landsat-7 band-5-and-6 degradation and its 2022 end, Sentinel-2A's 2023 detector-column anomalies, VIIRS's early banding), and reprocessing discontinuities (the 2016 Landsat geometric-and-radiometric reprocessing, collection 6-to-6.1 MODIS changes) that break naive long time series in exactly the places the longest records are most tempting. The 2019–2024 analysis-ready-data movement (the CEOS-OpenDataCube-and-STAC machinery) exists because the data are free but the metadata discipline is not; a trend fitted across an unflagged baseline change is an artifact with a p-value.
4. **"The constellation will replace the flagship."** Cadence and aperture are different currencies: the constellation example's daily 3-m fleet cannot hold a sub-per-cent radiometric trend, cannot carry the thermal-and-SWIR bands the flagship missions carry, and cannot task-and-stare the way a 705-km observatory can, and the 2020s record (Landsat-9-and-Sentinel-2-and-Sentinel-1 all flying while the fleets proliferate) shows complementarity winning, not replacement — the correct mental model is an observing system with tiers (calibrated reference instruments, systematic mid-resolution fleets, taskable very-high-resolution and specialized sensors), whose weakest link is always the cross-calibration between tiers. Mission-architecture papers say this in a decade of planning; users should say it in the first paragraph of every methods section that splices datasets, because the splice is where the error lives.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
A remote-sensing dataset is four resolutions and a schedule: **spatial** (ground sample distance and the point-spread function behind it), **spectral** (band count against the photon-and-data-volume penalty), **radiometric** (bits, noise-equivalent radiance, and the square-root photon law that couples the first three), and **temporal** (revisit bought with swath, fleet or high orbit) — and choosing a dataset is writing down which resolution the science needs and which it will pay away. The detector architectures (whiskbroom's calibratable dwell, push-broom's line-integration SNR, focal-plane arrays' cooling-and-aging realities, synthetic aperture's coherent inversion, lidar's photon counting) each fix a dataset's failure modes before its statistics matter, and the orbital architecture (sun-synchronous for photometric consistency, repeat tracks for change and interferometry, geostationary for process timescales, constellations for statistical cadence) fixes what can ever be measured about a changing planet. The record — Landsat's five decades of 30-m continuity, Sentinel's open 10-m-and-radar regime, MODIS-and-VIIRS's daily global products, the geostationary storm watches, the cubesat daily-3-m layer — is institutional infrastructure as much as hardware, its free-data policies the decisive design decision and its calibration-and-cross-calibration literature the load-bearing wall under every trend, classification and reanalysis in the lessons ahead. The fallacies (pixels for information, orbits for geometry, free for known, fleets for flagships) all trade the dataset's spec sheet for its error budget, and this course's answer is the same every time: **name the quantity, name the resolution it needs, name the calibration that ties the numbers together, and put the metadata version in the methods section** — because in observation-system design, as in the previous lesson's radiometry, the measured number and the wanted number are not the same thing until someone does the work of connecting them.
:::
