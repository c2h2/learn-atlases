Classification is where the course's calibrated pixels become answers: a label, a fraction, a change map — and therefore where the course's errors become conclusions. The statistical problem is deceptively simple: assign each pixel's reflectance vector to a class (forest, corn, asphalt, water) or to a proportion of classes, using training data and a model, and then say honestly how good the resulting map is. The difficulty is that the pixels are not independent samples of clean class-conditional distributions — they are spatially autocorrelated mixtures with correlated errors, classified by models whose assumptions (Gaussian, linear, independent, stationary) the landscape violates in order — and that the assessment problem is at least as hard as the classification problem, because reference data cost money and field seasons while classified pixels cost nothing. This lesson builds the classical machinery (the maximum-likelihood statistical classifier, support-vector machines, the random-forest-and-boosted-tree default of the 2010s, the convolutional-and-foundation-network wave of the 2020s), the accuracy-assessment canon (error matrices, producer's-and-user's accuracy, kappa and its modern critics, the sample-design literature, the area-adjusted estimators that fixed "accuracy" for mapped-area claims), and change detection in its three families (post-classification comparison, image differencing-and-radiation-normalization, and the 2015–2025 deep-learning direct-change architectures), with their shared nemesis: distinguishing change from the un-removed differences of the previous lesson's correction chain. The prerequisites are the image-processing lesson's surface reflectance and the statistics of conditional probability; the reward is the ability to read a land-cover product's accuracy report — and to write one that survives review.
::: definition Classifiers: from likelihoods to forests to networks {#def-classifiers}
**The statistical (likelihood) family** treats classification as density estimation: estimate each class's spectral probability density from training pixels and assign by maximum posterior (the 1970s maximum-likelihood classifier, Gaussian by assumption — which is why its textbook behavior, and its known failures with multimodal classes and small training sets, still structure the literature's baselines); its practical descendants are the spectral-mixture models of the em-radiation lesson (probabilistic unmixing is classification's soft cousin) and the Gaussian-process-and-bayesian classifiers that keep the uncertainty the field keeps needing. **The margin-and-kernel family** — support-vector machines (the 1995–2010s maximal-margin classifiers whose kernel trick handles non-linear boundaries with few training pixels, the 2005–2015 default for high-dimensional-and-hyperspectral problems, and whose sensitivity to the C-and-gamma hyperparameters the 2010s intercomparison literature documented as enough to silently swing a few per cent of overall accuracy) — gave way, in practice, to **the tree-ensemble family**: random forests (bagged trees with random feature subsets, the 2010s-and-2020s workhorse for land cover, its out-of-bag error a free-and-honest validation, its variable-importance plots the most-cited-and-most-abused diagnostic in applied remote sensing) and gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost-and-LightGBM, the competition-winning refinement that dominates the 2020s national-and-global product benchmarks); the ensembles' real gifts are tolerance of non-Gaussian mixtures, native handling of the texture-and-topography-and-phenology covariates that single-date spectra cannot exploit, and — crucially for time-series classification — clean handling of hundreds of features from the dense Sentinel-and-Landsat stacks (the 2015–2025 continuous-algorithms-and-continuous-platforms-and-random-forest-phenology studies). **The deep-learning family** enters at every stage: convolutional networks for spatial context (the 2015–2020 segmentation era, U-Net architectures for buildings-and-roads-and-fields), recurrent-and-transformer models over image time series (the 2020–2025 sPLINK-and-DeepTime-and-TTAE lineage benchmarked by the Massive-Time-series-Benchmark effort, competitive with gradient boosting at higher cost), and the 2021–2025 foundation models (Prithvi-and-SatMAE-and-DINO-style pretrained encoders promising few-shot classification where labels are scarce — the promise real, the 2024–2025 evaluations sober: pretrained helps most when training data are genuinely few and domains genuinely similar). Three cross-cutting truths: the classifier is rarely the bottleneck (the 2019–2024 intercomparisons consistently find training-data quality-and-design dominating model choice by margins larger than any algorithm's edge); features-and-temperatures beat architectures (adding the radar-and-DEM-and-phenology covariates moves accuracy more than swapping random forest for a network); and every family's output is a probability the accuracy assessment must then treat honestly, because a calibrated 70-per-cent probability and an overconfident 99-per-cent one are not the same map wearing different clothes.
:::

::: definition Accuracy assessment: the matrix, the statistics, the design {#def-accuracy}
The **error (confusion) matrix** is the field's primary document: rows are reference classes, columns are mapped classes, and from it every honest statistic is a ratio — **overall accuracy** (the trace over n, the most-reported and least-informative number in remote sensing), **producer's accuracy** (a row's diagonal share: the probability a real forest pixel is mapped forest; omission's complement) and **user's accuracy** (a column's diagonal share: the probability a mapped forest pixel is real forest; commission's complement) — producer's-and-user's being the class-specific pair that makes "95 per cent accurate" meaningless whenever classes differ in size or in the direction of their errors. **Kappa** (the chance-corrected agreement (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e)) earned decades of dominance and lost it for good reasons the 2013-and-2020s critiques (the Stehly–Foody-and-Russell-Goodman line) made standard: it depends on the classification's own marginals (so two equally good maps with different class balances get different kappas), it is not comparable across studies, and it answers a question (agreement with reference beyond chance) narrower than the question users ask (are my mapped areas right?); it survives as a legacy-comparability statistic, reported alongside the matrix, never instead of it. **Area-adjusted accuracy** is the modern fix for mapped-area claims: because reference samples are drawn from the map (or from stratified designs), naive per-class accuracies must be reweighted by mapped area (the 2014–2016 McRoberts-and-Frank-Kodal-and-Olofsson post-stratification-and-hierarchical-assessment framework, now the global-products standard: the ESA-WorldCover-and-ESRI-LC-and-Dynamic-World accuracy reports are written in it), and confidence intervals on accuracies-and-on-area must carry the sampling design (the misweighted-binomial variance of stratified reference data, not the naive binomial, or the intervals lie). **Sample design is the budget that decides everything else**: probability sampling (simple, stratified, systematic-random) for assessment versus convenience sampling (the photo-interpreter's favorite sites) that silently biases every downstream number; the per-stratum sample-size arithmetic (the 30-to-50-per-minority-class floor for stable producer's accuracies, the Olofsson hierarchical design's allocation rules); and the reference-data honesty problem the 2020s crowdsourcing-and-VHR-imagery literature keeps rediscovering — a reference label is itself a classification with its own error, and when reference-and-map errors correlate (both wrong about the same shadow), the matrix flatters the map. The final canon entry is **spatial structure**: classification errors cluster (the misclassified field is next to its correctly classified twin), so accuracy statistics computed assuming independent samples understate uncertainty (the 2010s-and-2020s effective-sample-size literature), and maps should be assessed with error-maps-and-local-accuracy surfaces, not just global scalars — the same spatial-dependence argument the GIS lessons will make for every statistic in geography.
:::

::: definition Change detection: three families and their confusions {#def-change}
**Post-classification comparison** (classify both dates, overlay the maps) is the oldest and most robust family: it is insensitive to the radiometric differences that plague direct comparison (each date's atmosphere-and-illumination are absorbed by its own classifier), it yields interpretable from-to matrices, and its costs are the compounding of two classification errors (a change-detection accuracy is roughly the product of the two maps' accuracies for rare changes — the base-rate arithmetic of the exercises) and its blindness to subtle continuous change. **Direct image comparison** works on the images themselves: band-by-band differencing-and-ratioing (the 1970s river-and-bare-soil changes), index differencing (the dNBR burned-severity convention, the NDVI anomaly literature), the **radiation-normalization** methods (the 1990s–2020s histogram-matching-and-pseudo-invariant-target regressions that make two dates comparable when correction alone cannot), and the thresholded-difference family whose threshold choice is an ROC decision the base-rate fallacy will punish; its virtue is sensitivity to continuous-and-sub-pixel change, its vice is that any uncorrected atmospheric, phenological, BRDF-or-registration difference enters the same difference image as the target change — the image-processing lesson's registration example being the canonical demonstration. **Feature-and-object comparison** extracts comparable geometric objects per date (waterlines from the radar-and-optical water indices of the coastline literature, glacier margins, road graphs, building footprints) and compares the objects, not the pixels — the family behind the long historical records (the 1973–2020 Landsat waterline-and-shoreline lineages of the geomorphology-and-coast literature) because objects survive five decades of sensor changes that no pixel statistic does. **The direct deep-learning family** (the 2018–2025 Siamese-and-fully-convolutional change architectures — the BIT-and-ChangeFormer-and-SiamCDN lineage benchmarked on the LEVIR-CD-and-S2Looking-and-OpenChangeCD suites) learns the difference end-to-end, dominates the building-change benchmarks, and inherits every pre-2018 confusion unchanged: trained-and-evaluated on clean co-registered pairs, it is quietly excellent at registration artifacts and quietly wrong on phenology, which is why the 2023–2025 operational-deployment papers spend more pages on augmentation-and-domain-transfer than on architecture. Across all families the interpretive canon is one sentence, and the warnings section repeats it with teeth: **a difference is not a change** — change requires the difference to be attributed to the surface process of interest against the alternatives (illumination, atmosphere, phenology, geometry, sensor), and the study design (the season pairing, the harmonization, the ablations) is the attribution.
:::

::: example Global land-cover products: accuracy reports at planetary scale {#ex-global-products}
The 2020–2025 global land-cover wave — ESA WorldCover 2020-and-2021 (10 m, random forest on Sentinel-1-and-2 stacks, the area-adjusted assessment reporting ~73–76 per cent overall with the class-by-class table published), Google's 10 m annual land change (the 2023-and-2024 Science-paper-era assessment, ~74 per cent), the ESRI-and-Carnegie-and-ETH 10 m annual land cover (the 2021 release and its ~75-per-cent independent assessment), and Dynamic World's near-real-time 10 m probabilities (the 2021–2025 operational lineage) — is best read as an accuracy-assessment masterclass: all four are built by tree ensembles or their near cousins on radar-plus-optical-plus-phenology features (the features-beat-architectures finding, at scale); all four publish error matrices computed by stratified probability sampling with area-adjusted estimators (the definition's framework as house style); and all four show the same structure in their errors — wetlands-and-mangroves confused with trees and grasslands, cropland mosaics confused everywhere, the accuracy surface tracking the reference-data economy (Europe-and-North-America assessments rest on dense photo-interpretation infrastructure, central-Africa-and-Amazonia on sparser-and-more-uncertain reference). The comparison users actually need is in the class rows, not the headline: a flood study needing water-versus-vegetation separation is choosing among products that are all ~90 per cent on that pair, while a peatland-or-mangrove study is choosing among products that are 40–60 per cent and disagreeing with each other by more than they agree with the ground — the 2022–2025 blue-carbon intercomparisons finding mangrove-area differences of tens of per cent between products whose headline accuracies differ by one point. The lesson generalizes to every product paper: read the class rows, read the confidence intervals, read where the reference data came from, and treat the overall accuracy as the least-informative number in the table.
:::

::: example Burned area and dNBR: one index, two careers {#ex-burned}
The fire program's product chain shows classification-and-change in one pipeline: the MODIS-and-VIIRS burned-area products (MCD64A1-and-its-successors, the 2006–2025 lineage) detect burn by an abrupt shortwave-infrared reflectance drop and near-infrared-and-NDVI fall — a change-detection classifier whose active-fire-and-hotspot co-inputs and whose small-patch omission (the 2010s-and-2020s assessments: Mediterranean-and-boreal small burns undercounted by tens of per cent, the 2020 Australian-and-Siberian-event intercomparisons) are documented class rows of the definition's kind; and the Landsat-based dNBR (the differenced normalized-burn-ratio, the USGS-and-MTBS convention: pre-fire NDVI-minus-NIR composite minus post-fire, scaled, thresholded by the burn-severity classes) converts the same spectral physics into a severity map whose accuracy is a separate, humbler claim — the 2010s–2025 field-plot validation literature finding dNBR-to-field-severity correlations of 0.5–0.8 that weaken in the highest-severity tail (saturation), in mixed-canopy understorey burns, and wherever the post-fire scene is cloud-or-shadow-contaminated within the sensitive first months (the post-fire-erosion studies' registration-and-brightness confounds). The two careers are the lesson: as a detector, the index is operational and global, its errors known-and-mapped; as a severity measurement, the same index is a proxy with a site-dependent calibration, and the 2020s management literature's rule (calibrate dNBR to local plots before using it to allocate restoration money) is the index-fallacy discipline of the image-processing lesson applied to change. The third career is inference: burn-severity maps feeding the carbon-accounting of the [[climate-change/impacts]] lesson inherit, un-err-barred, exactly the validation correlations above — the propagation question the spatial-analysis lesson will make explicit.
:::

::: example Urban mapping with deep networks: buildings, base rates, and the accuracy that fooled a continent {#ex-urban-deep}
Automated building extraction from 0.5-m imagery (the 2018–2025 U-Net-and-mask-R-CNN era, the WHU-and-inDRA-and-DFC benchmarks, the 2023–2025 foundation-model transfers) is the field's most seductive classifier and its most instructive accuracy story. The seduction: intersection-over-union scores above 0.8 on benchmark cities, visually perfect roof outlines, continental-scale products (the Google-and-Microsoft-and-OpenAerialMap building layers of 2022–2025, billions of footprints) that look like solved infrastructure data. The instructive part is what happens off-benchmark: transfer to informal settlements-and-rural-compounds (the training domains' blind spots — the 2021–2025 Africa-city evaluations finding user's accuracies 15–30 points below the benchmark numbers), to mountainous-and-forested terrain where shadows-and-canopy mimic roofs, and above all to **rare-event statistics**: when buildings are 2 per cent of pixels, a detector with 95-per-cent specificity still floods the map with false positives (the base-rate arithmetic of the exercises, the same math the flood-mapping validation literature learned in 2015–2020), which is why the serious 2023–2025 products publish precision-recall curves at multiple thresholds and per-context accuracy tables (the WorldBank-and-GlobalHumanitySet-era assessments) instead of overall accuracy, and why the population-and-exposure models that consume building layers now routinely run sensitivity analyses on the detector's false-positive rate — the downstream-uncertainty chain the spatial-analysis lesson generalizes. The deep-learning wave changed classification's ceiling on instrumented benchmarks; the accuracy-assessment canon is what keeps the wave's outputs honest where the training data never went.
:::

::: example Deforestation alerts: three systems, three error budgets {#ex-deforestation}
Tropical forest-loss monitoring now runs three operational change systems whose differences are the change-detection definition's families in production: the Hansen Global Forest Change loss-year record (2000–2024, Landsat-based, 30-m, annual — a post-classification-and-trajectory hybrid whose accuracy varies from 90-plus per cent in homogeneous boreal-and-temperate forest to the 60s in dry-forest-and-mosaic savanna, and whose loss signal is sensitive to selective logging-and-drought-and-fire as well as clearance), RADARSAT-2-and-Sentinel-1-based RADD (the 2021–2025 radar alerts, cloud-proof by design, weeks-latent, tuned for the Amazon's wet-season clarity), and GLAD-and-RADD-L (Landsat-and-Sentinel-2 optical alerts, days-latent, cloud-limited), with the 2022–2025 intercomparison literature (the Congo-and-Amazon alert-fusion studies) quantifying what the families predict: radar alerts catch clearance in wet seasons optical systems miss, optical alerts catch small-and-partial disturbance radar thresholds smooth away, and every system's false positives concentrate in the same places — regenerating-secondary-forest edges, shade-coffee-and-agroforestry mosaics, and the post-flooded-forest-and-logging-scar confusion that the accuracy definition's base-rate arithmetic makes inevitable at 1–2-per-cent-annual-loss prevalence. The institutional payoff is the triage workflow the warnings section prescribes: alerts (high sensitivity, modest precision) filtered by persistence-and-context (hydrology, prior loss, concession boundaries) before field verification (independent reference at the definition's design standards) before attribution (the supply-chain-and-governance analyses that the [[earth-resources]] and [[climate-change/impacts]] lessons consume) — remote sensing's change product functioning exactly as an observing system should: not a verdict, a well-characterized prior feeding an honest decision.
:::
::: history Maximum likelihood to foundation models {#hist-classification}
**1965–1985.** Classification begins as pattern recognition imported wholesale: the 1960s–1970s maximum-likelihood-and-Bayes classifiers (the Landsat-era Gaussian assumption and its covariance-pathologies literature), the 1973–1979 error-matrix-and-accuracy statistics settling into the canon (the 1976 Rosenfeld-and-Kaufley and 1979–1981 Horn-and-Kirby-and-Cardille critiques of overall accuracy, the producer's-and-user's pair standardized by the 1983–1985 forest-inventory work), and the change-detection triad appearing in the 1974–1984 literature (the 1984 Singhakowalski-and-Cohen band-differencing and 1986 post-classification comparisons of the Landsat-I period). **1987–2012.** The model carousel turns: neural networks' first wave (the 1989–1994 multilayer-perceptron experiments), support-vector machines' 1995–2010s dominance (the 2005–2012 hyperspectral-SVM literature), the 1990s–2000s classification-trees-and-ensembles lineage (random forests' 2001 method paper entering remote sensing mid-2000s), and the accuracy-critique era beginning (the 2002–2012 kappa-dependence analyses that the 2013 Stehly–Foody paper and the 2014–2016 area-adjusted-estimator papers convert into the modern assessment framework). **2012–2025.** Deep learning arrives with the data that make it work (the 2012 ImageNet year, the 2014–2018 convolutional land-cover wave, the 2015–2020 dense-Sentinel time-series classifiers and the 2018–2024 Siamese-change architectures), the global-product era forces open accuracy reports (the 2019–2024 WorldCover-and-Google-and-ESRI assessments written in the area-adjusted canon), and the 2021–2025 foundation-model-and-few-shot literature — pretrained encoders, prompt-and-adapt transfer, the benchmark-reform debates — reopens the question the 2010s random-forest consensus had closed: whether the model, not the data, can be the bottleneck, with the 2025 answer still the field's oldest: it depends on where the labels came from.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-producer-user}
A wetland class has producer's accuracy 55 per cent and user's accuracy 90 per cent. The map:

- [x] Omits many real wetlands but the wetlands it does map are almost always real
- [ ] Maps all real wetlands but adds many false ones
- [ ] Has balanced errors and moderate overall accuracy
- [ ] Cannot be assessed without the kappa value

::: solution :::
Producer's accuracy is the row share (real wetlands correctly mapped — 55 per cent means 45 per cent omitted), user's is the column share (mapped wetlands that are real — 90 per cent means few commission errors): the classic conservative-detector profile of the global products' wetland rows. Option B swaps the two accuracies; option D mistakes a legacy summary statistic for the matrix it summarizes.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-change}
A dNBR burned-area map validated against field plots shows 92 per cent overall accuracy in a boreal region and 61 per cent in a Mediterranean mosaic. The most likely explanation is:

- [x] Class prevalence and mixture: sparse, high-contrast burns in homogeneous boreal forest versus small, mixed, low-contrast burns in fragmented Mediterranean landscapes
- [ ] The Mediterranean sensor having worse radiometric calibration
- [ ] Field plots being impossible in Mediterranean terrain
- [ ] Boreal regions having fewer cloud-free scenes
::: solution :::
Accuracy tracks the landscape's contrast-and-scale, not the sensor: the same algorithm on the same data regime performs as well as its rarest important class allows (the global-products example's class-rows rule, the base-rate arithmetic of the exercises). Calibration (B) is region-independent by design; plots exist throughout the Mediterranean (C); clouds (D) affect data availability, not validation design when scenes were acquired.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="90"}
An error matrix has 1000 reference pixels and 900 of them are mapped correctly. What is the overall accuracy in per cent?
::: solution :::
900/1000 = **90 per cent** — and the accuracy definition's warning in one number: if 850 of the 900 correct pixels are the dominant class, the map's rare classes could still be disasters, which is why the trace is the starting point of the report and never the conclusion.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="80"}
Of 50 reference forest pixels, 40 are mapped as forest. What is forest's producer's accuracy in per cent?
::: solution :::
40/50 = **80 per cent** — the row share, meaning 20 per cent of real forest is omitted somewhere in the matrix's off-diagonal cells; read the column shares next, because a producer's 80 hiding a user's 50 is a map that finds forest everywhere but misses much of it, the opposite error budget from the wetland quiz's conservative detector.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="75"}
Of 60 pixels mapped as cropland, 45 are cropland in the reference. What is cropland's user's accuracy in per cent?
::: solution :::
45/60 = **75 per cent** — the column share: a quarter of the mapped cropland is something else (the 2020s global-products literature's cropland rows, the most commission-prone class in every intercomparison), and the number a carbon-or-food-security model consuming the map should carry as its input uncertainty, not the overall accuracy three rows away.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.8"}
A classification has overall accuracy 0.90 and chance agreement 0.50. Compute kappa = (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e) to two decimal places.
::: solution :::
kappa = (0.90 − 0.50)/(1 − 0.50) = 0.40/0.50 = **0.80** — and the critique in one comparison: the same 0.90 overall accuracy against a chance agreement of 0.94 (a dominant-class map) yields kappa 0.17 (the level-3 exercise), which is why kappa's defenders-and-critics agree on its arithmetic and disagree on whether one number should carry the verdict the error matrix states in full.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="180"}
A stratified accuracy design allocates at least 30 reference samples to each of 6 classes. What is the minimum total sample size?
::: solution :::
6 × 30 = **180** — the sample-design floor (30–50 per minority class for stable producer's accuracies), and the reason accuracy budgets are field-season budgets: the 2020s global-product assessments spend hundreds of thousands of photo-interpreted points precisely because the rare-class rows, not the headline, are where the confidence intervals live.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="600"}
Post-classification comparison maps forest as 5000 hectares at date 1 and 4400 hectares at date 2. What is the apparent forest loss in hectares?
::: solution :::
5000 − 4400 = **600 hectares** — the apparent change, which the definition's compounding-error argument immediately qualifies: with per-date forest user's-and-producer's accuracies of 90 per cent, the two maps' errors alone can shift the estimate by a couple of hundred hectares, so the honest statement is 600 ± (the error-propagated interval), and the 2010s-and-2020s deforestation-accounting literature's confidence intervals on area change are exactly that propagation made explicit.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.17"}
A dominant-class map has overall accuracy 0.95 but chance agreement 0.94. Compute kappa to two decimal places.
::: solution :::
kappa = (0.95 − 0.94)/(1 − 0.94) = 0.01/0.06 = 0.167, about **0.17** — a map that is 95 per cent correct and nearly worthless, the base-rate fallacy's accuracy-report counterpart: whenever prevalence is high, overall accuracy must be read against chance (or, better, against the rare-class rows that are this map's real report card).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.6"}
A balanced 2-class matrix: 100 reference forest pixels (80 mapped forest) and 100 reference non-forest pixels (80 mapped non-forest). With p_o = 0.80 and p_e = 0.50, compute kappa.
::: solution :::
kappa = (0.80 − 0.50)/(1 − 0.50) = **0.60** — the same 80-per-cent per-class performance that scored 90 overall in the level-1 exercise's unbalanced matrix now scores 0.60 kappa against balanced chance: the marginals-dependence the accuracy definition's kappa critique calls fatal to cross-study comparison, computed twice to make the point unforgivable to forget.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.16"}
Change occupies 2 per cent of a scene. A detector has true-positive rate 0.90 and false-positive rate 0.10. Using Bayes' theorem, what is the precision (positive predictive value) of mapped change, to two decimal places?
::: solution :::
Precision = (0.90 × 0.02)/(0.90 × 0.02 + 0.10 × 0.98) = 0.018/0.116 = 0.155, about **0.16** — a "90-per-cent-sensitive" detector whose change map is five-sixths false positives, because the 98 per cent of unchanged pixels outnumber the target fifty to one: the base-rate arithmetic of the urban-deep-learning example, the reason change-detection papers report precision-recall (not sensitivity alone) and why operational flood-and-deforestation alerts triage detections with context filters (persistence, hydrology, prior maps) before anyone believes a pixel.
:::

::: warning Four classification fallacies
1. **"The map is 95 per cent accurate."** Accurate for whom, and about what? The global-products example's structure repeats in every regional study: a headline overall accuracy carried by the dominant class, the study's actual subject (mangrove, peatland, informal settlement) sitting at 40–60 per cent in the class rows, and the consumer — a carbon accountant, a conservation planner, an epidemiologist — whose error budget is the rare class's, not the headline's; the 2022–2025 blue-carbon-and-cropland intercomparisons finding products with one-point-apart headline accuracies disagreeing by tens of per cent on the mapped area of interest is the fallacy's cost, invoice included. The rule: no product is cited without its error matrix, its area-adjusted class accuracies, and the reference-data provenance, and no model consumes a class without propagating that class's user's-and-producer's pair.
2. **"Training and test sets are independent samples."** They are neighbours: random pixel-splitting of a single scene puts training pixels and test pixels of the same field, same illumination, same sensor artifacts on both sides of the line, and the resulting accuracy is inflated by spatial autocorrelation (the 2018–2024 block-cross-validation-and-object-based-split literature measures the inflation at several points to — sometimes tens of points — for spatially structured classes), while the deep-learning benchmarks' same-city training-and-testing compounds the error across study sites (the transfer evaluations' 15–30-point drops). The same autocorrelation argument recurs in the accuracy definition's effective-sample-size paragraph and the spatial-analysis lesson's statistics: **independence is an assumption you design, not a property pixels have**, and any accuracy claim that has not said how its samples were separated is a claim about interpolation, not prediction.
3. **"More classes, more training data, better map."** The taxonomy and the data must co-grow: adding a class the sensor cannot separate (two crop varieties at 10 m, two forest associations without lidar) adds confusion, not information — the separability statistics of the definitions' likelihood era still govern, and the 2020s global-products literature's honest finding is that coarser, well-separated taxonomies at 10 m beat ambitious ones on every accuracy metric; meanwhile adding training pixels of the same convenience sites adds variance-reduced bias (the reference-data-provenance paragraph again), and the 2010s-and-2020s sample-selection literature's result is that ten well-designed stratified points teach more than a hundred photo-interpreter favorites. The fallacy's institutional form — product roadmaps driven by class counts and pixel counts because they are fundable and accuracy is not — is why the assessment canon exists and why this lesson keeps returning to it.
4. **"Change was detected, therefore change happened."** The difference image is an equal-opportunity messenger: real change, phenological shift, atmospheric residual, BRDF-and-illumination step, registration edge, sensor degradation and label noise all arrive in the same array (the image-processing lesson's registration arithmetic, the em-radiation lesson's atmosphere), and the direct-change deep networks' benchmark excellence — trained on clean co-registered pairs — is itself a demonstration that the models learn to ignore the confounds only where the benchmark removed them. The canon's answer is design, not statistics: season-matched pairs, harmonized baselines, ablations against pseudo-invariant targets, persistence-and-context filters on detections, and post-classification comparison as an independent witness; and the reporting answer is one sentence, always: **which alternative explanations were tested, with what, and what residual confidence remains** — because in change detection, as in the classification of a single date, the map is a hypothesis about the ground, and the accuracy report is the experiment.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Classification converts calibrated reflectance into labels and change, and the whole discipline lives in keeping two ledgers. The **model ledger**: likelihood classifiers' Gaussian ideals, support-vector margins, random-forest-and-boosted-tree ensembles (the 2010s–2020s workhorses, out-of-bag error honest by construction), deep convolutional-and-temporal networks and 2020s foundation models — and the intercomparisons' consistent verdict that training-data design and covariates (radar, DEM, phenology) move accuracy more than architectures, so the model is chosen last and audited first. The **assessment ledger**: the error matrix as primary document, producer's-and-user's accuracies as the class-specific pair that makes headlines meaningless in the best way, kappa as a marginals-dependent legacy statistic, area-adjusted estimators and stratified designs as the modern canon for mapped-area claims, spatial autocorrelation as the effective-sample-size tax on every independence assumption, and the base-rate arithmetic (the exercises' 0.16-precision detector) as the standing punishment for ignoring prevalence. Change detection inherits both ledgers in three families — post-classification comparison (robust, compounded), direct differencing (sensitive, confounded), feature-and-object comparison (the long-record workhorse) — plus the deep-learning direct family whose benchmark excellence is a statement about benchmark hygiene, and all four obey the interpretive canon: **a difference is not a change until illumination, atmosphere, phenology, geometry and sensor are eliminated as witnesses**, and a map is not a fact until its error matrix, its sample design and its reference-data provenance are published. The examples — global products read by their class rows, dNBR's two careers, the building-extraction base-rate story — are the same argument at planetary, fire-front and rooftop scale, and the fallacies (headline accuracy, fake independence, class-count ambition, difference-as-change) are the ledgers unkept.
