Active sensing changes the bargain: the instrument brings its own illumination, and with it two things passive sensors cannot have — **control** (you choose the wavelength, polarization, incidence angle, timing) and **coherence** (the returned wave keeps a phase record, and phase is a ruler). Synthetic-aperture radar's phase interferometry measures ground motion to millimetres from 700 km; its amplitude backscatter sees through cloud, night and moderate rain, giving the 2014–2025 Sentinel-1 era its flood maps and ice velocity fields; lidar's photon-counting time-of-flight measures the vertical structure of forests and the height of ice sheets and oceans to centimetres. This lesson builds the three active systems the Earth sciences use: **radar** (the coherent microwave imager: backscatter physics and the dielectric-and-roughness controls, the synthetic-aperture focusing that buys resolution from motion, and interferometry's phase-to-displacement inversion with its atmosphere-and-orbit error budget), **altimetry** (radar and laser ranging to the surface, whose pulse-shape and waveform analysis gives sea-surface height to centimetres, significant wave height, ice-sheet elevation and canopy structure), and **lidar** (laser ranging and full-waveform scanning, the ICESat-ATLAS photon budget, the GEDI-and-ATLAS biomass and canopy-height retrievals, and the bare-earth digital terrain models that airborne lidar has made the topographic default of the forest-and-coastal geomorphology literatures). The prerequisites are the em-radiation lesson's radiometry, the sensors lesson's resolution arithmetic, and — newly — the wave concepts of coherence, phase and interference, which the lesson develops as it needs them. The reward is the course's precision frontier: the only remote-sensing measurements good enough to watch a volcano inflate, a fault creep, a glacier accelerate, a city sink and an ice sheet lose a trillion tonnes a decade.
::: definition Synthetic aperture radar: coherent backscatter and the phase record {#def-sar}
A radar image is a map of **backscatter coefficient** σ⁰ (the fraction of transmitted power returned per unit ground area, reported in decibels) modulated by the system's geometry, and three physical controls explain most of what a radar image looks like. **Dielectrics** dominate: the Fresnel contrast (liquid water's permittivity ~80 against soil's 4 and vegetation's 10–30) makes wet surfaces and open water dark at moderate incidence (the specular reflection sends energy away from the sensor) and makes soil moisture the strongest amplitude signal in Sentinel-1 imagery (the 2015–2025 surface-water-and-flood-mapping lineage of the operational Copernicus EMS products, the same dielectric physics as the passive-microwave lesson but with the sensor choosing the incidence angle). **Roughness** relative to wavelength decides the scattering strength: a surface is smooth (dark) when its height variations are under λ/(8·cos θ)-scale, rough (bright) when over it — which is why the same mudflat is black at C-band and grey at L-band, why the Bragg-resonance model (the 1970s–2020s radar-ocean literature: resonant centimetre-scale waves against the 5.5-cm C-band wavelength) links sea-state to backscatter (the scatterometer wind retrieval of the [[oceanography/air-sea-fluxes]] lesson), and why radar texture is a physical statistic (the speckle — fully developed coherent interference over a resolution cell — whose negative-exponential-and-Gamma distributions and multi-looking statistics the image-processing lesson introduced as noise but which is, in fact, the measurement's fundamental quantum). **Volume and double-bounce** scattering carry structure: forest canopies return from crown, branches and ground (the L-band polarimetry of the biomass example, the Random-Volume-Forest-model of the 1990s–2020s PolInSAR literature), flooded vegetation double-bounces (the bright C-band signature that maps inundation beneath canopy, the 2016–2025 S1-and-NISAR wetland products), and urban areas' wall-street corners shine at HH (the building-orientation literature). The **synthetic aperture** is the instrument's trick: as the platform flies, each ground target is illuminated over a real-beam-width arc, and the Doppler history of the echoes (the classic 1950s–1960s Wilmore-Curlander-and-Kozma lineage) is focused by matched filtering into an azimuth resolution of half the real antenna length at stripmap (the sensors lesson's result), tens of metres-or-better at spotlight, and the range dimension comes from pulse bandwidth (δR = c/(2B·sin θ_inc), the chirped-pulse-compression of every modern system: Sentinel-1's 56.5-MHz bursts giving ~20-m ground pixels, the 2020s commercial radars' 900-MHz-and-wider waveforms giving sub-metre). **Coherence** is the phase record's fragility and value: the complex return preserves phase to within the system's thermal noise, but between two passes the interferometric correlation γ degrades with temporal change (the 24-day Sentinel-1 pair decorrelates in forests but holds in deserts), with perpendicular baseline (geometric decorrelation), and with the atmosphere (ionospheric-and-tropospheric phase screens, the 2010s–2020s GNSS-and-ionosphere-constrained InSAR literature) — and the whole interferometry definition that follows is the management of that degradation, because a radar image's amplitude tells you what the surface is like, and only its phase tells you where the surface is.
:::

::: definition Interferometry: phase to millimetres {#def-insar}
Interferometric SAR forms the complex product of two (or more) coregistered images of the same scene; the resulting **interferometric phase** is 4π/λ times the difference in line-of-sight path length, and that path difference is the sum of terms the practitioner must separate: **flat-earth** (the flat reference sphere's quadratic phase, removed by the orbit's baseline model), **topography** (the height-dependent term, removed with an external DEM or estimated from multi-baseline stacks), **deformation** (the target signal, the line-of-sight projection of ground displacement), **atmosphere** (the tropospheric wet-delay screen of centimetre-to-decimetre path, spatially correlated at kilometre scales, and the ionospheric dispersive screen at L-band and below), and **noise** (the thermal-and-baseline-decorrelation term, quantified by the coherence γ that weights every downstream estimate). The **displacement measurement equation** follows: one interferogram gives one line-of-sight displacement per pixel (C-band's 5.5-cm wavelength making one full phase cycle = 2.75 cm of range change), and the phase-unwrapping problem (recovering the integer fringe count, the 1990s–2020s SNAPHU-and-minimum-cost-flow literature) plus the multi-geometry decomposition (ascending-and-descending-and-azimuth-tilt pairs resolving the 3-D vector where the geometry allows) decide what fraction of the vector field is actually observable — the vertical-under-ascending-geometry dominance of most Sentinel-1 acquisitions being the reason subsidence studies are easy and strike-slip studies are hard. **Persistent scatterer and tomographic InSAR** (the 1999–2025 permanent-scatterer-and-SqueeSAR-and-InSAR-TOMO lineage) solve the temporal-decorrelation problem in cities by tracking individual coherent targets (corner reflectors, exposed rooftops, bridge abutments) across hundreds of acquisitions, retrieving per-target displacement time series to a few millimetres per year and, via the distributed-scatterer extensions (the 2015–2025 DS-InSAR and COSEME-and-MAI-and-offset-tracking complement for large gradients), pushing the technique into vegetated-and-rural terrain; the error budget of a modern urban subsidence map (the 2018–2025 European-and-Asian-and-US metro-area surveys) is a few tenths of a millimetre per year of relative vertical precision, dominated by the tropospheric screen (spatially correlated, partly removable with GNSS-and-weather-model correction) and by the reference-point assumption (the "stable" bedrock pixel that may not be). **DInSAR** (the two-pass differential method of the 1993 Landers earthquake, the 1999–2025 earthquake-source literature of the [[geophysics/earthquake-sources]] course and the [[natural-hazards/earthquake-hazard]] fault chapters), **time-series InSAR** (the 2004–2025 permanent-scatterer lineage applied to subsidence, landslides, volcanic inflation, coal-and-geothermal-and-CO₂-storage monitoring), **offset tracking and feature tracking** (the amplitude-domain, large-gradient complement that measures the fast motion — the glacier-velocity maps of the [[glaciology/ice-flow]] lesson, the rupture-zone offsets of the great earthquakes) and the **Sentinel-1-and-NISAR-and-ROCMAP-Comet-era systematic products** (the 2020s continental-and-global InSAR archives, the 2024-and-2025 NISAR L-and-S-band calibration campaigns) are the five operational forms of one idea: **the phase is a ruler graduated in half-wavelengths, and the science is in holding it steady enough to read.**
:::

::: definition Altimetry and lidar: ranging the vertical {#def-altimetry-lidar}
Altimetry is the oldest active technique and the simplest to state: measure the two-way light time to the surface (range = ct/2, a 6-ms round trip from a 1000-km orbit being the number to feel), and the satellite's own precise orbit determination (the centimetre-level satellite laser ranging, Doppler-orbit-determination and GNSS-receiver heritage of the 1990s–2025 precise-orbit literature, the orbit's radial error being the altimetric error budget's first term) converts range to surface height. **Radar altimeters** (the 1973–2025 sequence from Skylab-and-GEOS-3 through Seasat, Geosat, ERS-1-and-2, Topex-Poseidon, Jason-1-2-3, Envisat, CryoSat-2, Sentinel-3-and-Sentinel-6, ICESat-2's optical cousin) emit pulses whose returned **waveform** is the surface's and instrument's convolution: over the ocean the retracked first-return gives sea-surface height to a few centimetres (the 1993–2025 satellite-altimetry sea-level record of the [[oceanography/sea-level]] lesson, whose 3.3-mm-per-year mean and its 0.4-mm-per-year-per-decade acceleration are the climate-change course's central measurement, and whose 2020s Jason-3-and-Sentinel-6-Michael-Freilich cross-calibration holds the record's continuity at the millimetre-per-year level), the waveform's slope gives significant wave height (the 1980s–2020s Ku-and-Ka dual-frequency wave retrieval), and over the ice sheets the pulse-limited footprint (hundreds of metres to kilometres) plus the slope-and-anisotropy corrections (the 2010s–2020s CryoSat-2-and-ICESat-2 repeat-orbit and photon-pair-rate methods) give elevation change to tens of gigatonnes per year of mass balance (the [[glaciology/ice-sheets]] lesson's independent estimate against GRACE's gravity). **Lidar** is the same ranging physics at optical wavelengths with photon counting and beam structure doing the science: **full-waveform** airborne systems (the 1990s–2020s NASA-and-NCF-and-Arctic-Boreal lineage) record the laser's echo as a vertical profile of returned energy — canopy, understory, ground — from which height metrics (the Lorey's-and-mean-canopy-height statistics of the forest-inventory literature) and, at enough footprints, aboveground-biomass density follow (the 2010s–2020s GEDI-and-NISAR-and-Global-Biomass-Satellite mission-design era, the 2022–2025 GEDI L2B validation campaigns against forest inventory plots); **discrete-return** scanning systems (the topographic-mapping default since the 2000s, the last-of-four-returns convention that makes the bare-earth digital terrain model of the GIS lessons, the 2010s–2020s national lidar programmes — the US-3DEP-and-UK-and-Singapore-and-India-era continuous-coverage campaigns — delivering sub-metre vertical accuracy over whole countries); and **spaceborne photon counting** (ICESat/GLAS 2003–2009's 70-m footprints, ATLAS/ICESat-2 2018–'s 17-m spots at 10 kHz, the 2020s ATLAS photon-pair-rate-and-height-of-surface-difference methods that trade single-shot precision for spatial density) closes the current generation, its 2022–2025 applications spanning sea-ice freeboard (the [[glaciology/sea-ice]] lesson's thickness chain), river-and-lake hydrology (the SWOT-and-ATLAS-lake-height lineage of the [[hydrology/groundwater-flow]] course's surface-water chapters), and canopy structure (the 2023–2025 ATLAS-and-GEDI co-location studies mapping forest height from orbit's photon noise). The shared discipline of all altimetry-and-lidar: **the measurement is a time, the science is a waveform, and the error budget is orbit, tides, atmosphere and instrument all priced in centimetres.**
:::

::: example InSAR of an earthquake: the fault drawn in fringes {#ex-quake-insar}
The 1992 Landers earthquake's 1993 ERS-1 interferogram (the Didier Massonnet-and-Kurt-Feigl-and-Howard-Zebker-and-Tom-Wright-era demonstration that founded the field) showed the surface displacement field as concentric interference fringes — each fringe a half-wavelength (2.9 cm at C-band) of range change — and the 1993–2025 earthquake-InSAR literature that followed (the 1997 Manyik-Izmit, 2010 Maule-and-Yushu, 2015 Gorkha, 2023 Kahramanmaras-and-Turkiye-Syria event studies, the 2015-and-2023 Sentinel-1-era rapid-response products now delivered within hours by the Copernicus-and-CEOS-service) has standardized the workflow: unwrap the phase, invert for slip on geometrically plausible fault planes (the elastic half-space-and-layered Green's functions of the [[geophysics/earthquake-sources]] lesson), and combine with the geodetic-and-seismological solutions — the interferometric result's unique contribution being **subsurface slip where the surface did not break** (the M_w 7+ slow-and-episodic-slip events of the 2007-and-2019 Hikurangi-and-Guerrero slow-slip literature, the 2015–2025 Sentinel-1-era global slow-slip census) and the post-seismic-and-interseismic velocity fields (the 2016–2025 Sentinel-1-and-GNSS-coupled coupling maps that now feed the [[natural-hazards/earthquake-hazard]] lesson's probabilistic forecasts directly). The error budget is the definition's: coherence loss in the near-field's damaged vegetation (where offset tracking takes over), the tropospheric screen across the through-going mountain ranges (partly removable, the 2015–2025 ERA5-and-GNSS-corrected studies), and the unwrapping discontinuities at the steepest gradients (the 2020s deep-learning-unwrapping attempts, useful, not solved) — and the interpretation discipline is the projection's: a fringe map is line-of-sight, the vertical-under-ascending-dominance means dip-slip-on-vertical-faults is nearly invisible to a single geometry, and the 2010s-and-2020s multi-geometry-and-ALOS-3-and-ISBA-era constellation designs exist precisely to close that null space.
:::

::: example Urban subsidence: millimetres per year, by the city block {#ex-subsidence}
Persistent-scatterer InSAR turned land subsidence from a levelling-network anecdote into a global, dense-coverage measurement: the 2004–2025 permanent-scatterer lineage (the 2001–2004 permanent-scatterer-interferometry method papers, the 2008–2025 SqueeSAR-and-DS-InSAR distributed-scatterer extensions that doubled-and-more the usable target density in urban areas) now maps most of the world's major cities at 10⁴–10⁶ coherent targets each, and the results have rewritten three literatures. **Groundwater and compaction:** the 2010–2025 Mexico City (the 2019-and-2023 Sentinel-1-and-Envisar time-series studies resolving centimetre-per-year differential compaction against aquifer-level cycles), Jakarta (the 2010–2024 multi-decade Envisat-and-Sentinel-1 record, the northern-district rates near a decimetre per year that drive the relocation argument), Bangkok, the North-China-Plain megacity belts and the California Central Valley (the 2015–2025 InSAR-and-GRACE-coupled agricultural-subsidence studies) tie subsidence to pumping with a spatial resolution no well network approaches, feeding the [[earth-resources/groundwater-resources]] course's groundwater chapters. **Engineering geodesy:** the 2010–2025 tunneling-and-mining-and-dam-and-fracking literature (the Groningen-and-Netherlands gas-field subsidence-and-tilt monitoring, the 2016–2024 Permian-and-Marcellus disposal-injection seismicity-and-surface-uptime studies, the dam-and-quarry stability surveillance now operational in the Alps-and-Norway rock-avalanche early-warning systems) uses per-target time series at tenths-of-millimetre precision as the deformation input to structural-and-hazard models. **Reference-frame honesty:** the 2018–2025 intercomparison-and-referencing studies (the GNSS-tied-and-IERS-frame InSAR alignments, the reference-point-selection sensitivity analyses) document the technique's soft underbelly — relative measurement means the datum is chosen, and a bad reference pixel moves an entire city's map by its own rate, the same datum-discipline argument the maps-and-GIS lessons make in metres, now at the millimetre scale where it decides whether "the airport is stable" is true.
:::

::: example Forest structure from orbit: GEDI, NISAR and the biomass map {#ex-biomass}
The carbon-accounting question — how much carbon is in the world's forests, and how fast is it changing — became a lidar-and-radar question in the 2010s–2020s because only microwaves-and-lasers see structure: GEDI (2019–2023, the ISS-mounted full-waveform lidar whose 25-m footprints sounded 11 billion times before its mission end, the 2021–2025 L4A aboveground-biomass-density product and its forest-plot validation campaigns reporting 15–25-per-cent regional uncertainties) and ICESat-2/ATLAS (the photon-counting canopy-height and ground-elevation layer, the 2020s ATLAS-GEDI co-location studies extending height statistics off GEDI's tracks) provide the vertical structure that optical images cannot, while L-band radar (the 1994–2009 JERS-and-ALOS/PALSAR heritage whose long wavelength penetrates to the ground-trunk interaction, the 2014–2025 ALOS-2-and-NISAR-era polarimetric-and-interferometric biomass inversions, the Random-Volume-and-RVoG scattering models inverting saturation-limited backscatter-and-coherence into carbon density) extends it spatially — the saturation problem (C-band saturates near 100–150 Mg/ha, L-band near 200–400, P-band barely saturates, the 2010s-and-2020s frequency-choice literature that made the 2025-and-beyond NISAR-L-band-and-Biomass-P-band-mission designs) being the physics the missions were built around. The 2020–2025 integrated products (the GEDI-Landsat-Sentinel-2-and-NISAR-wall-SENAI-and-ABIs-lineage 10–30-m global canopy-height-and-biomass maps, the 2022–2025 national-inventory reconciliations from the Gabon-and-Congo-and-California-and-Maine campaigns) show the pattern's real value and real limits: systematic map-to-map agreement within 15–25 per cent at continental scale (good enough for the REDD-plus-and-nation-inventory accounting the [[climate-change/emissions-budgets]] lesson's land-use chapters consume), but plot-level uncertainty that still requires the field plots as the calibration base — lidar-and-radar extend the plots' arithmetic across the map, they do not replace the forest ecologist's tape measure, the same reference-data-provenance discipline as the classification lesson, now with carbon credits attached to the error bars.
:::

::: example Altimetry's two views: the rising centimetre and the falling kilometre {#ex-altimetry}
The same ranging physics, aimed at ocean and ice, produces the century's two cleanest planetary numbers. **Sea-surface height:** the 1993–2025 satellite-altimetry sea-level record (the Topex-Poseidon-and-Jason-and-Sentinel-3-and-Sentinel-6 crossover-and-reference-mission-consolidated analyses) measures global mean rise at 3.4 mm/yr with a measured acceleration and 2020s-record annual anomalies above 4.5 mm — the [[oceanography/sea-level]] lesson's observational backbone — whose error budget is the altimetry definition's priced item by item: the orbit's radial determination (the satellite-laser-ranging-and-DORIS-and-GNSS-precise-orbit regime, centimetre-level), the sea-state-bias and wet-troposphere corrections (the 2010s–2020s GNSS-tomography-and-radiometer-corrected reanalyses), the polar-orbit-to-polar-orbit cross-calibrations (the Jason-3-to-Sentinel-6 reference-orbit campaigns holding the record's steps under a millimetre), and the tidal-and-crossover aliasing arithmetic of the [[oceanography/waves-tides]] lesson. **Ice-sheet elevation:** the radar-and-laser altimetry mass-balance lineage (the 1998–2009 ERS-2-and-ICESat-and-2010-CryoSat-2 polar-repeat-track era, the 2018–2025 ICESat-2 photon-pair-rate era) yields the Greenland-and-Antarctic elevation-change maps whose integration with the firn-density models (the 2019–2024 IMBIE-and-GRACE-and-GFC-reconciliation assessments of the [[glaciology/ice-sheets]] lesson) gives the mass losses that dominate the sea-level budget's ice term — the firn-compactification correction being the example's cautionary half: elevation change is not mass change until the density of what you just weighed is modeled, the altimetric version of the thermal lesson's brightness-versus-temperature distinction, and the reason the mass-balance community publishes three methods (altimetry, gravimetry, input-output) whose agreement, not any one's precision, is the actual uncertainty statement.
:::

::: history Coherence, chirps and photons {#hist-radar}
**1904–1957.** Christian Hülsmeyer's 1904 radio-echo collision-warning patent and the 1930s–1940s wartime radar programs (the Robert-Watson-Watt chain, the MIT radiation-laboratory series) supply the hardware; the synthetic-aperture idea is patented by Carl Wiley (1951) and formalized in the side-looking-and-Doppler-processing work of the 1950s (the 1957 Wilmore-Curlander-and-Kozma-and-Stoiber focused-imaging demonstrations); the chirped-pulse-compression and matched-filter theory (the 1950s–1960s communication-and-radar literature) makes range resolution a waveform-design problem. **1957–1992.** Space begins the record: the 1957–1960 Project-SCORE-and-MIDAS-and-Transit ranging experiments found the orbit-determination discipline, the 1969–1975 Apollo-and-Skylab laser-ranging and the 1973–1975 Skylab-S-190 radar-and-scatterometer campaigns demonstrate Earth-usefulness, the 1975–1992 Seasat-and-GEOS-3-and-ERS lineage (the 1978 Seasat radar-altimeter-and-SAR-and-scatterometer ten months, the 1985–1992 ERS-1 design era) establishes the ocean-radar and radar-imaging regimes, and the 1971–1986 lidar lineage (the 1960s–1970s laser-ranging and the 1980s airborne-scanning systems) readies the photon-counting future. **1991–2025.** The precision era: ERS-1 (1991) and JERS-1-and-RadarSat (1991–1994) give repeat-pass coherence, the 1993 Landers-and-1994 Northridge interferometry papers prove the phase is a geophysical instrument, the 1995–2004 permanent-scatterer-and-TomoSAR method papers and the 2002-and-2006 Sentinel-1-and-NISAR program launches industrialize it; the 1992–2020 altimetry succession (Topex-Poseidon through Jason-3 and Sentinel-6) and the 2003–2018 ICESat-to-ICESat-2 laser-altimeter generations turn ranging into climate records; and the 2019–2025 GEDI-and-NISAR-and-ROCMAP-and-Comet-and-PLANETICA-era systematic-and-commercial active-sensing fleets complete the transition in which millimetre-and-centimetre active sensing became, like the optical archive before it, open infrastructure for the whole Earth system.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-coherence}
An interferometric pair over a tropical rainforest shows coherence near 0.2 while a desert pair shows 0.9. The consequence for deformation measurement is:

- [x] The forest pair's phase is mostly noise — deformation there needs L-band, shorter temporal baselines, or offset tracking
- [ ] The forest's deformation is smaller than the desert's
- [ ] The desert pair must have a longer temporal baseline
- [ ] Coherence measures the strength of the deformation signal

::: solution :::
Coherence weights the phase: at γ ≈ 0.2 the phase standard deviation is tens of degrees and the displacement estimate is meaningless, which is exactly the temporal-decorrelation regime the definition assigns to L-band (canopy-penetrating, changes slower), 6-or-12-day pairs, or amplitude-based offset tracking. The other options confuse decorrelation with geophysics (B), with acquisition design (C), and with signal amplitude (D) — coherence is correlation, not signal.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-lidar}
Airborne lidar produces bare-earth terrain models despite dense forest canopy because:

- [x] Multiple laser returns per pulse include photons that reach the ground through canopy gaps, and the lowest clean return (after classification) is the terrain
- [ ] Ground returns are stronger than canopy returns and need no processing
- [ ] The laser wavelength penetrates leaves
- [ ] Canopy returns are removed by a high-pass filter

::: solution :::
It is a numbers-and-geometry game: enough footprints (the 2010s–2020s national-program densities of several pulses per square metre) guarantee that some photons find gaps, and classification (the ground-return-detection literature's slope-and-elevation-consistency rules, the last-of-four convention) picks them — the full-waveform definition's vertical profile made into a DEM. Leaves are opaque at optical wavelengths (C is exactly wrong); ground returns are typically weaker, not stronger (B); and filtering in the frequency sense (D) is not the mechanism.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="360"}
A radar altimeter measures a two-way pulse travel time of 2.4 milliseconds. What is the slant range in km (c = 3e8 m/s)?
::: solution :::
Range = ct/2 = 3e8 × 2.4e-3/2 = 3.6e5 m = **360 km** — the factor of two (the round trip) is the altimetry definition's first habit, and the 6-ms round trip from a 1000-km orbit is the same arithmetic; a one-nanosecond timing error is 15 cm of range, which is why every term in the centimetre error budget is a timing-or-path correction.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="2.7"}
Sentinel-1's interferometric wide-swath bursts use a 56.5-MHz chirp bandwidth. Range resolution is c/(2B). Give the slant-range resolution in metres to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
3e8/(2 × 56.5e6) = 2.65, about **2.7 m** slant (about 5.8 m on the ground at the ~37° incidence, the 5-by-20-m IW pixel of the sensors lesson) — chirped-pulse compression turning a 10-m physical pulse into a 3-m measurement, and the reason "bandwidth is resolution" is radar's version of the passive-microwave lesson's "aperture is footprint": every active system is an argument about one controlling quantity.

ICESat-2's ATLAS fires 10,000 laser pulses per second along a ground track moving at 7 km/s. What is the along-track spot spacing in metres?
::: solution :::
7000/10000 = **0.7 m** — the photon-counting budget of the altimetry definition: 70 cm between 17-m spots means the mission trades single-shot precision (a few centimetres, tens of photons) for spatial density (the photon-pair-rate methods' statistical height differences), the same resolution-versus-precision exchange as multi-looking, now paid in photons per square metre.

::: exercise {level=2 check="11"}
A Sentinel-1 interferogram (wavelength 5.5 cm) shows a phase change of two full cycles (4π) between two dates at a given pixel. What is the line-of-sight displacement in cm?
::: solution :::
One cycle = λ/2 = 2.75 cm; two cycles = **11 cm** — the widget's ruler read directly, and the scale-sense exercise that makes the earthquake example's fringe maps physical: 11 cm of range change in 12 days is either a landslide, a volcanic inflation or an oil-field injection, and never a tropospheric artifact (the troposphere's path delays are centimetre-scale, mostly under one fringe).

::: exercise {level=2 check="117"}
The topographic phase of an interferogram completes one full cycle for a height of h = λR/(2B_perp). For λ = 5.5 cm, range R = 850 km and perpendicular baseline B_perp = 200 m, compute h in metres to the nearest metre.
::: solution :::
h = 0.055 × 850000/(2 × 200) = 46750/400 = 116.9, about **117 m** per fringe — the height-of-sensitivity number the definition's topographic-term removal turns into a design choice: large baselines (small h) make steep topography unwrap-able but decorrelate geometrically, small baselines (large h) flatten the phase and lose the terrain signal, and the 2010s-and-2020s DEM-and-synthetic-aperture-height-systems (the TanDEM-X-and-Comet-DEM-and-Baltic-projects) exist to keep the topographic phase removable at all.

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.45"}
A radar altimeter emits a 3-nanosecond pulse. What is its range resolution (ct/2) in metres?
::: solution :::
3e8 × 3e-9/2 = 0.45, **0.45 m** — the pulse-limited footprint's vertical counterpart (CryoSat-2's 2010s SIRAL-and-LFM-era 1.2-m resolution being the polar-ice-optimized trade of bandwidth against peak power), and the reason the ice-sheet elevation-change error budget (the altimetry definition) is dominated not by range resolution but by the footprint's interaction with slope-and-anisotropy: a 300-m footprint on a 1-per-cent slope is a 3-m elevation ambiguity before any waveform processing begins.

::: exercise {level=3 check="7.9"}
A radar looks down at an incidence angle of 38° from vertical. Purely vertical ground motion of 10 cm produces a line-of-sight range change of d·cos(θ). Compute the InSAR-measurable displacement in cm to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
10 × cos 38° = 10 × 0.788 = 7.88, about **7.9 cm** — the projection arithmetic of the definition's displacement equation, and its corollary for hazard interpretation: the same 10 cm of horizontal motion toward the satellite reads as 10 × sin 38° = 6.2 cm, a 40°-dip fault's slip projects differently still, and the earthquake example's "invisible geometries" (strike-slip on vertical faults under ascending near-vertical look) are this cosine-sine structure, not bad data — which is why the 2020s constellation designs (NISAR's two geometries, the ROCMAP-and-Comet ascending-descending pairs) are sold as null-space-closing instruments.

::: exercise {level=3 check="9"}
A full-waveform lidar pulse records the ground return 60 nanoseconds after the canopy-top return. What is the canopy height in metres (ct/2)?
::: solution :::
3e8 × 60e-9/2 = 9, i.e. **9 m** — the waveform-reading measurement that the GEDI example's height statistics are built from (the Lorey's-height distribution being the amplitude-weighted mean of exactly these transit times), and the reason lidar biomass retrievals are structurally honest in a way optical biomass proxies are not: the instrument measures height directly and only converts to carbon through an allometric model whose error is separately stated.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3.1"}
A SAR satellite flies at 7500 m/s with a pulse repetition frequency of 2400 Hz. What is the along-track ground sampling interval in metres, to one decimal place?
::: solution :::
7500/2400 = 3.125, about **3.1 m** — the raw along-track sampling from which focusing forms the final pixel (azimuth averaging and the antenna-limited processing reduce 3.1-m samples to the 5.4-m-or-20-m product pixels, the ambiguity-and-processing-history arguments of every radiometer-versus-imager design review), and the number that makes the Doppler-centroid-and-antenna-steering corrections of the 2010s–2020s wide-swath TOPS-and-ScanSandbeam modes (Sentinel-1's rotated-antenna burst stitching, the 2012-and-2018 TOPS-InSAR azimuth-offset literature) necessary: sample the Doppler history finely or the focused image smears, and the smearing itself becomes a phase error the interferometry definition must price.

::: warning Four active-sensing fallacies
1. **"Dark radar means water."** The amplitude fallacy, and the flood-mapping community's hardest-learned lesson: open water is dark at moderate incidence (specular), yes — but so are smooth asphalt-and-roofing at certain geometries, wind-roughness-suppressed urban canyons, smooth glacial ice, and (the 2016–2024 flood-validation literature's dominant commission-error source) calm reservoirs-and-sheltered-bays outside any flood; and conversely wind-roughened water at high incidence brightens out of the dark class entirely (the 2018–2025 storm-flood studies' omission errors). Operational mapping (the Copernicus-EMS-and-JRC-Global-Flood-Water-Dynamics protocols) therefore maps by change against a baseline water state, dielectric-and-geometry reasoning, and increasingly the polarization-and-multi-incidence information the newer missions provide — and the honest flood product reports its wind-and-vegetation caveats in the caption, because the dark pixel is an argument about scattering geometry, never a category of nature.
2. **"The interferogram shows the deformation."** It shows phase, which is deformation plus flat-earth plus topography plus atmosphere plus ionosphere plus noise, and the 2010s–2020s correction literature has documented each impostor at the centimetre level: tropospheric screens that mimic magma-inflation's spatial smoothness (the 2013–2024 ERA5-and-GNSS-corrected volcano studies finding uncorrected atmospheric signals of several centimetres per year at kilometre wavelengths — the exact scale of the deformation being sought), ionospheric ramps at L-band that mimic dip-slip ramps (the 2017–2025 NISAR-design-era TEC-constrained studies), and unwrapping errors that jump whole fringes at the gradients that matter most (the 2020s deep-learning-unwrap papers' honest failure modes). The discipline is the definition's error budget, published: coherence maps, atmospheric corrections named, reference points justified, and — for the 2020s systematic products (the Sentinel-1-and-NISAR-era InSAR layers) — the validation against GNSS-and-campaigns quoted per-region, because an InSAR map without its error budget is a colorful suggestion.
3. **"Lidar measures biomass."** It measures heights and intensities; biomass enters through allometry (the height-diameter-allometry-and-wood-density equations of the forest-inventory literature whose species-and-stand variation is, per the 2021–2025 GEDI-validation studies, as large as the sensor error), through the models that convert waveforms to structure (the 2010s–2020s full-waveform-metrics-to-Lorey's-height regressions), and through the radar saturation problem the biomass example prices (the 200–400-Mg/ha L-band ceiling, the P-band promise still in orbit-design). The 2022–2025 carbon-credit-and-national-inventory disputes (the remote-sensing-biomass-versus-field-inventory reconciliation arguments, the jurisdictional-REDD-payment controversies) are what happens when a height measurement is spent as a carbon tonne: the map's 15-per-cent continental uncertainty becomes a payment-error bar only when the allometric-and-plot chain is propagated, and the studies that skip it are, in accounting terms, inventing carbon.
4. **"Active means all-weather and exact."** Radar does see through cloud, and lidar does not see through cloud-or-rain-or-canopy — but the fallacy is in "exact": the altimetry error budget (orbit, tides, sea-state bias, wet troposphere, instrument drift) is a published list of centimetres that must be subtracted before the ocean's centimetres mean anything (the 2020s sea-level-record continuity literature is essentially a study of this subtraction's residuals), the InSAR millimetre is relative (the reference-point and reference-frame arguments of the subsidence example), and the lidar's centimetre is a statistical average over photons whose single-shot error is decimetres (the ATLAS photon-counting regime's entire methods literature). The precision is real — it is the course's precision frontier — but it is **differential, modeled and referenced**, never absolute: the active systems measure change beautifully and levels carefully, and every headline number from "the volcano inflated 8 cm" to "the ice sheet lost 200 Gt" is a difference of two modeled quantities whose error bars are the paper's real subject.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Active sensing is the course's precision frontier, built on three controlled illuminations: **radar amplitude** (backscatter as a dielectric-and-roughness-and-structure measurement — water dark, wet soil dark, double-bounce bright, the Bragg physics of the wind field and the flood hydrology both read from the same Fresnel contrast), **radar phase** (the synthetic aperture's coherent record, interferometry's half-wavelength ruler, and the error budget — flat-earth, topography, atmosphere, ionosphere, decorrelation, unwrapping — whose management separates the earthquake-source inversions and millimetre-per-year subsidence maps from pretty pictures), and **ranging** (altimeter-and-lidar time-of-flight, waveform-reading physics that prices its error budget in centimetres of orbit, tide, troposphere and footprint geometry and then delivers the sea-level record's 3.4 mm/yr and the ice sheets' mass balance). The examples are the techniques at their applications' edge: earthquake fringes and slow-slip censuses, city-block subsidence tied to aquifers, GEDI-and-NISAR forest structure spent (carefully, allometrically) as carbon, and the two altimetric views — rising centimetre, falling kilometre — whose uncertainties are published as science rather than hidden as marketing. The fallacies (dark-as-water, interferogram-as-deformation, lidar-as-biomass, active-as-exact) are all the same substitution of measurement for quantity, the course's one recurring sin in its most precise dialect: the radar measures phase, the lidar measures time, and only the error budget — coherence maps, calibration campaigns, reference frames, plot networks, cross-mission reconciliation — decides whether what you publish is geophysics or instrumentation. The next three lessons leave the radiation entirely and take up the oldest active instrument of all: the map.
:::
