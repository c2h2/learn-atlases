Every pixel of every satellite image is a radiometric measurement: a quantity of electromagnetic energy, collected over a defined band of wavelengths, from a defined solid angle, attributed to a defined patch of the surface — and the entire discipline of remote sensing rests on reasoning backward from that number to the surface that produced it. The backward step is never unique without physics: the same radiance leaving a pixel could come from a bright small object or a dim large one, from reflected sunlight or emitted heat, from the ground or from the atmosphere above it. This lesson builds the physics that makes the inversion defensible — the radiometric quantities and their units, the solar and thermal sources described by Planck, Wien and Stefan-Boltzmann, the atmospheric absorption and scattering that filter the signal and define the spectral windows every sensor is built around, and the spectral signatures of vegetation, water, soil, snow and rock whose mixtures fill the pixels of every land image. The prerequisite is the radiation bookkeeping of the [[earth-system/energy-balance]] lesson; the payoff is that from here on, no satellite image is a picture — it is a stack of physical measurements, each with a source, a path, an interaction and a sensor, and every interpretation is a claim about all four.
::: definition Radiometry: the quantities, the sources, the windows {#def-radiometry}
Four quantities are confused more often than every other error in this course combined, and the confusion is unforgivable because the units differ. **Radiant flux** (watts) is power; **radiance** (W m⁻² sr⁻¹ per unit wavelength or wavenumber) is flux per unit projected area per unit solid angle — the quantity a sensor measures, because it is conserved along a ray in a lossless medium, which is why "brightness temperature" and "at-sensor radiance" are meaningful across distances; **irradiance** (W m⁻²) is flux arriving at (or leaving) a surface per unit area, the quantity that drives energy balance; and **reflectance** and **emissivity** (dimensionless, zero to one) are the surface properties that convert one into the other. The sources are two and only two: the Sun, a 5772-K blackbody whose spectral irradiance above the atmosphere peaks at 0.48 µm with a total of 1361 W m⁻² (the solar term, dominant from the visible to the shortwave infrared), and the Earth, a ~288-K blackbody peaking at 9.9 µm (the thermal term, the only game in town past 3 µm except for reflected sunlight in the daytime shortwave-infrared). Planck's law gives the spectral shape, Wien's displacement law (λ_max = 2898/T µm) the peak, and the Stefan-Boltzmann law (F = σT⁴) the integral; Kirchhoff's law (emissivity equals absorptivity at each wavelength, in equilibrium) licenses the use of one emissivity per band per surface type. Between source and sensor stands the atmosphere, and its opacity is not uniform: molecular absorption by water vapour, carbon dioxide, ozone, oxygen and methane carves the spectrum into **atmospheric windows** — the visible-near-infrared (0.4–0.9 µm), the shortwave-infrared windows near 1.2, 1.6, 2.1 and 3.7 µm, the 3.4–4.2 and 4.7–5.0 µm thermal bands, the great 8–14 µm thermal infrared window, the millimetre-submillimetre microwave windows (with the 22-GHz water and 60-GHz oxygen lines between them), and the 1-cm-to-1-m radar window that is nearly perfect — while scattering (Rayleigh, λ⁻⁴, molecular; Mie, aerosol-sized and haze-producing; geometric, cloud-droplet and white) attenuates and adds path radiance to everything shorter than ~1 µm. Every sensor in this course is an engineering answer to the question "which window, and how wide?" — and every atmospheric correction is the arithmetic of undoing what the window let through.
:::

::: definition BRDF, bidirectionality, and what a "reflectance" is {#def-brdf}
A surface is not a number: the fraction of light it reflects depends on the direction the light comes from and the direction it leaves, and the complete statement of that dependence is the **bidirectional reflectance distribution function** (BRDF), f_r(θ_i, φ_i, θ_v, φ_v), with units sr⁻¹, defined so that the reflected radiance equals the integral of f_r times the incident irradiance over the hemisphere. Three consequences organize a surprising amount of practice. First, the same field measured at two sun angles or by two satellites gives different radiances — the off-nadir view geometry of push-broom instruments, the hot-spot effect (the brightening when the view direction aligns with the sun direction, hiding shadows within the canopy), and the forward-scattering haze of aerosols at high airmass are all BRDF and path effects, not surface change, and the 16-day MODIS and Sentinel-2 time series must be normalized with a BRDF model (the RossThick-LiSparse kernel models of the MODIS land products, the Sentinel-2 surface-reflectance processing baseline) before "change" means anything. Second, most practical quantities are directional integrals or samples of the BRDF: the **bi-hemispherical reflectance** (albedo, the hemispheric integral, the energy-balance number), the **nadir reflectance** (what a vertical-looking sensor sees), and the **directional-hemispherical reflectance** used in radiative-transfer models. Third, the BRDF is where structure shows: a row-crop field, a coniferous forest and a flat salt pan have recognizably different shape functions, and the shadowing term of the LiSparse model is literally a canopy-architecture retrieval. For diffuse illumination (overcast, or the strongly scattering blue bands) the directional bookkeeping collapses toward the bi-hemispherical case, which is why blue-band surface types are harder to separate than red-edge ones under haze. The single most common quantitative error in applied remote sensing is treating a directional reflectance sampled at one geometry as if it were an albedo; the single most consequential is treating two reflectances from two geometries as a measurement of change.
:::

::: definition Spectral signatures: vegetation, water, soil, snow, rock {#def-signatures}
Five surface classes carry most of the information in optical images, and each has a signature recognizable to a trained eye. **Green vegetation** is dominated by pigment and structure: strong absorption by chlorophyll a and b at 0.45 and 0.67 µm and by carotenoids in the blue gives the 0.55-µm green peak; the spongy mesophyll's internal scattering lifts the near-infrared plateau to 40–50 per cent reflectance between 0.75 and 1.3 µm (a "red edge" transition from 0.67 to 0.75 µm so steep it is the most reliable life-detection signature ever instrumented); and liquid-water absorption bands at 1.45, 1.94 and 2.7 µm carve the shortwave infrared, so that leaf water content, and then canopy water and senescence, read out as band-depth ratios (the NDVI of near-infrared over red, the photochemical and structure-insensitive index, the water index — all rationalized in the [[remote-sensing/image-processing]] lesson). **Water** is the opposite: clear open water reflects a few per cent and absorbs exponentially toward the near-infrared, so its signature falls from ~5–10 per cent in the blue to near zero past 0.7 µm, and the water-leaving signal (turbidity, chlorophyll, coloured dissolved organic matter) lives only in the blue-green and, turbid-case, in a modest near-infrared shoulder — the entire discipline of ocean colour exists inside that statement (the [[oceanography/productivity]] lesson's satellite chlorophyll). **Soil and rock** are comparatively featureless, rising gently and monotonically from visible to shortwave-infrared, with diagnostic iron-oxide absorptions near 0.48 and 0.86 µm, ferrous and ferric iron and hydroxyl-and-carbonate absorptions at 2.2–2.35 µm (the alteration mineralogy of the [[remote-sensing/classification]] lesson's mining maps), clay Al-OH at 2.2 µm, and moisture bands at 1.4 and 1.9 µm superimposed on the vegetation-like water features; soil brightness increases with dryness and decreases with organic matter, so the soil line in red-near-infrared space is a straight, bright, low-NDVI line the vegetation points grow away from. **Snow** is bright and flat in the visible-near-infrared, then crashes through the 1.5 and 2.0 µm ice absorptions, which is why the normalized difference snow index pairs green against shortwave-infrared; grain size darkens the shortwave-infrared (aging, wetness, pollution) and makes snow age datable. **Built materials** — concrete, asphalt, metal, painted surfaces, roofing — are chemically dull but geometrically loud: low shortwave-infrared contrast, high specular sensitivity, and the urban heat and brightness signatures of the [[remote-sensing/thermal-microwave]] lesson. Real pixels are mixtures: a suburban pixel is the linear (approximately) sum of these endmember signatures weighted by sub-pixel cover, and spectral mixture analysis — solving the linear system for the fractions — is the honest reading of any coarse pixel, the sub-pixel foundation of the impervious-surface, fractional-cover and burned-area products this course will use.
:::

::: example Why the path matters: blue skies, hazy bands, path radiance {#ex-path}
Take a clear-sky Landsat scene over a dark lake and a hazy scene over the same lake a week later. The lake's true reflectance has not changed, yet the second scene's blue-band digital number is far brighter: molecular (Rayleigh) scattering, with its λ⁻⁴ dependence, sends sunlight into the sensor's line of sight that never touched the ground — **path radiance**, additive and blue-heavy — while simultaneously attenuating the surface signal by the band's transmittance. The correction for it (the dark-object subtraction of the image-processing lesson, or a radiative-transfer inversion with measured aerosol optical depth) is a subtraction of a scattering calculation, not a fudge: subtract the path term, divide by the transmittance, and the lake returns to its physical reflectance. The same physics explains why the blue band is the least reliable band over land (largest path term, strongest aerosol sensitivity), why the coastal-aerosol band at 0.44 µm exists on Landsat-8 and Sentinel-2 at all (it is dedicated to measuring the thing the other bands must remove), why sunsets are red (long path, blue scattered away twice), and why the same surface looks different from two satellites at different airmasses. The lesson generalizes: in the visible-near-infrared the atmosphere is not a window with a smudge, it is an optical element with a calculable point-spread function, and a radiance image that has not been corrected is a convolution of surface and sky.
:::

::: example The red edge: physiology you can see from orbit {#ex-rededge}
A farmer's irrigated field and an identical stressed field differ, hours before any visual symptom, in two numbers a satellite reads: the 0.67-µm chlorophyll absorption weakens as chlorophyll degrades or thins, and the 0.74-µm near-infrared plateau shifts in position as the mesophyll structure collapses. The ratio of the two effects is the NDVI, and its sensitivity is not a laboratory curiosity: the 2000-and-2001-and-2012-and-2022 drought-and-heat anomalies of the Cropland Data Layer and MODIS feed and yield-forecast systems are red-edge-and-red-band anomalies first, harvest estimates second, and the whole early-warning chain for the [[earth-resources]] course's food-and-water chapters rests on the quantum biology of the red edge scaled to orbit. The quantitative chain is instructive: leaf-scale absorption coefficients measured in spectrometers, a canopy radiative-transfer model (PROSAIL and its relatives) linking leaf biochemistry to canopy reflectance, a bandpass integration over the sensor's spectral response functions, and an atmospheric correction to surface reflectance — four physical steps between a leaf and a global index, each with published error terms, which is why NDVI intercomparison between sensors is a calibration project (the surface-reflectance harmonization of the 2016–2020 CEOS-and-USGS work) and not a formula.
:::

::: example Ocean colour: the hardest radiance in the business {#ex-ocean-colour}
Water-leaving radiance over the open ocean is about 0.1 mW cm⁻² µm⁻¹ sr⁻¹ at 550 nm — under ten per cent of the total radiance reaching the satellite, the rest being atmospheric path radiance and sun-glint off the surface. Retrieving it means removing 90-plus per cent of the signal with models (the 1980s–2020 Gordon–Clark–Antoine aerosol and glint algorithms), and the residual error budget is why ocean colour's history is a history of calibration: the 1978 CZCS, the 1997 SeaWiFS (a decade of vicarious calibration against the Marine Optical Buoys), the 2002–2025 MODIS-and-MERIS-and-Sentinel-3-and-PACE lineage, with the 2024 PACE hyperspectral data finally testing the unresolved-mixture assumptions of twenty-five years of chlorophyll algorithms. The payoff, the global map of surface chlorophyll and the [[oceanography/productivity]] lesson's net-primary-production estimates, is the clearest case in this course that a physical quantity measured to one-per-cent radiometric accuracy (the SeaWiFS requirement) is what turns a picture into a global carbon-cycle constraint.
:::

::: example Shortwave-infrared mineralogy and the urban mixture {#ex-minerals}
Two demonstrations of the shortwave infrared's double life. First, alteration mapping: hydrothermal systems deposit clays, micas, carbonates and sulphates whose Al-OH, Mg-OH, C-O and sulfate absorptions fall between 2.0 and 2.5 µm, and the 1999–2025 ASTER-and-HyMap-and-PRISMA-and-EnMAP lineaments-and-mineral-maps of porphyry and epithermal districts (the [[earth-resources]] course's exploration chapters) are band-depth and matched-filter readings of exactly the absorptions of the signatures definition — iron oxides at 0.86 µm for oxidation, white micas at 2.20 µm for acidity, carbonates at 2.33 µm for neutralization, a whole petrology readable in reflectance because the photons involved are vibrational overtones of the bonds themselves. Second, the urban mixture: a 30-m Sentinel-2 pixel over a suburb contains roof, road, wall, tree and lawn, and its spectrum is a linear mixture of the five signatures of the definition; constrained non-negative least squares against endmember libraries (bright concrete, dark asphalt, green vegetation, dry vegetation, shade) recovers fractional cover to a few per cent of truth on validation sites, and those fractions — not the raw pixels — are what the imperviousness trends, urban-heat and flood-modelling literatures actually consume. The example's cautionary half: endmember variability (two concrete roofs differ; a shaded roof differs more) is the dominant error term, and any mixture result whose error bars do not include it is overfit.
:::

::: history From two dark spots to calibrated photons {#hist-radiometry}
**1800–1900.** William Herschel (1800) thermometers beyond the red end of the spectrum and finds "calorific rays"; Johann Ritter (1801) finds the chemical equivalent at the violet end; the Stefan-Boltzmann law (1879–1884, Josef Stefan empirically, Ludwig Boltzmann by thermodynamics), Wilhelm Wien's displacement law (1893) and Max Planck's radiation law (1900) — the ultraviolet catastrophe's resolution and quantum theory's birth certificate — supply, within a century, the complete physics of the source term that every thermal-infrared and microwave mission still uses band by band. **1871–1930s.** Lord Rayleigh's scattering theory (1871, λ⁻⁴, with Gustav Mie's 1908 general solution for aerosols) explains the blue sky and builds the first term of every atmospheric correction; the photographic and then panchromatic aerial-photography programs of the World-War-I era make radiometry an operational art. **1960s–1972.** Radiometric calibration goes orbital: the 1960s radiometers on TIROS and Nimbus measure the Earth's radiation budget and test the blackbody approximations, the 1969–1972 Landsat multispectral scanner is designed around the atmospheric windows of the signatures definition with laboratory spectral libraries as its requirement documents, and the 1972 launch begins the continuous calibration record whose vicarious-reconciliation updates (the 2016–2024 Landsat-and-Sentinel-2 cross-calibration literature) tie the 1986 Landsat-5 radiances to present ones within a per cent per decade — the longest photon-trust chain in the business.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-windows}
Why does the 8–14 µm atmospheric window exist at all, given that the atmosphere is opaque over most of the thermal infrared?

- [ ] Because the Earth's emission is too weak outside that band to be detected
- [x] Because the rotational-vibrational absorption lines of water vapour, carbon dioxide and ozone leave a gap between the 6.3-µm water band and the 15-µm carbon dioxide band where absorption is weak
- [ ] Because aerosols do not scatter at those wavelengths
- [ ] Because satellite detectors are blind outside that band

::: solution :::
Window structure is molecular quantum mechanics: 6.3 and 12–18 µm belong to H₂O, 15 µm to CO₂, 9.6 µm to O₃, and the 8–14 µm gap between the water and carbon-dioxide complexes is where the atmosphere is comparatively transparent — the window every thermal-infrared instrument (ASTER TIR, MODIS bands 20–36, Landsat TIRS, ECOSTRESS) is built inside, with the split-window 10.8/12 µm pair straddling the residual water-vapour gradient precisely so the correction can be estimated. The other options confuse detectability, aerosols and instrument design with the molecular opacity that defines the window.
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-rededge}
A crop is suffering nitrogen deficiency that reduces chlorophyll but leaves canopy structure intact. Which spectral change is expected first?

- [x] Reflectance increases in the red (0.67 µm) absorption while the near-infrared plateau stays roughly the same, lowering NDVI
- [ ] Reflectance increases equally in the red and the near-infrared, leaving NDVI unchanged
- [ ] Reflectance decreases in the red and increases in the shortwave-infrared water bands
- [ ] Reflectance decreases everywhere because the canopy darkens

::: solution :::
Chlorophyll controls the 0.67-µm absorption; structure controls the near-infrared plateau. Chlorophyll loss with intact structure therefore raises red reflectance alone — the red-edge shift of the physiology example — so the red band rises, the near-infrared does not, and NDVI (near-infrared minus red over their sum) falls. Structure damage (lodging, drying, senescence) is what moves the near-infrared; water stress moves the 1.94-µm band. The quiz's point: NDVI is a ratio of two different diagnoses, and reading it without saying which term moved is the interpretation error the red-edge example warns against.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="10.1"}
Using Wien's displacement law λ_max = 2898/T (µm, K), what is the peak wavelength of emission from a land surface at 288 K? Give the value in µm to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
λ_max = 2898/288 = 10.06, about **10.1 µm** — inside the 8–14 µm window, which is the design coincidence that makes passive thermal remote sensing possible from orbit; the same law puts the Sun's peak at 0.50 µm and fire at 3–4 µm, explaining the mid-wave bands of the widget.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="4.3"}
Rayleigh scattering cross-section varies as λ⁻⁴. By what factor is scattering at 0.45 µm (blue) stronger than at 0.65 µm (red)? Give the ratio to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
(0.65/0.45)⁴ = (1.444)⁴ = 4.34, about **4.3** times — the quantitative reason the blue band carries the largest path radiance of the visible bands (the path example), why historical aerial film was hazy in blue and clear through a red filter, and why aerosol retrievals are built on blue-band measurements.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="2.48"}
Photon energy is E = hc/λ, with hc = 1240 eV·nm. What is the energy of a 500-nm photon, in eV, to two decimal places?
::: solution :::
E = 1240/500 = **2.48 eV** — enough to drive chlorophyll photochemistry (the ~1.8-eV threshold), which is why the visible window is simultaneously the photosynthetically active window and the window life made visible; a 10-µm thermal photon carries only 0.124 eV, vibrational-scale energy, which is why thermal infrared reads temperature and bonds, not chemistry.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.67"}
A pixel has surface reflectance 0.50 in the near-infrared and 0.10 in the red. Compute NDVI = (NIR − red)/(NIR + red) to two decimal places.
::: solution :::
NDVI = (0.50 − 0.10)/(0.50 + 0.10) = 0.40/0.60 = 0.667, about **0.67** — healthy mid-season cropland or grassland; note that the same value can come from many (NIR, red) pairs (0.34/0.068 gives it too), which is the ratio-index ambiguity the red-edge quiz's closing caution generalizes, and why the two-band literature reports both bands when physiology matters.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1.15"}
By what factor does the total blackbody emitted flux change between a surface at 300 K and the same surface at 290 K (Stefan-Boltzmann, F = σT⁴)? Give the ratio F(300)/F(290) to two decimal places.
::: solution :::
(300/290)⁴ = (1.0345)⁴ = 1.149, about **1.15** — a 3.4 per cent temperature change moves the emitted flux by 15 per cent, the sensitivity that makes thermal infrared a precise thermometer (a kelvin of land-surface temperature is a ~1.4 per cent radiance signal at 11 µm, comfortably above the 0.3-K noise of modern instruments) and the reason urban-heat-island retrievals work at all.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="31.8"}
A Lambertian surface with reflectance 0.10 is illuminated by a downwelling irradiance of 1000 W m⁻². Its radiance is L = E·ρ/π. Compute L in W m⁻² sr⁻¹ to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
L = 1000 × 0.10/π = 100/3.1416 = 31.83, about **31.8 W m⁻² sr⁻¹** — the π is the bi-hemispherical-to-directional bookkeeping of the BRDF definition (a Lambertian surface distributes ρE over π steradians of exitance), and this single conversion underlies every reflectance-to-radiance calibration in the course; forgetting it is a 33-per-cent error, larger than most atmospheric corrections.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.81"}
An atmospheric band has one-way transmittance 0.90 between the surface and the sensor. For a nadir (vertical) path the path length is one scale height; for a view at 60° from nadir the path is twice as long. What is the transmittance along the slanted path, assuming Beer's law (T_slant = T^n where n is the path multiple)? Give to two decimal places.
::: solution :::
T_slant = 0.90² = **0.81** — Beer's law is exponential in path, so transmittances multiply along path multiples, and a sensor's whole off-nadir angular range (Sentinel-2 observes to ±50°, path multiple up to ~1.6) must be transmittance-corrected band by band; this is one of the two view-angle effects the BRDF definition says must be separated before change is claimed, the other being the surface's own directional response.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="64"}
A mixed pixel's red-band reflectance is 0.14. Endmembers: bare soil 0.30, dense vegetation 0.05. Using the linear mixture ρ_pixel = f·ρ_veg + (1−f)·ρ_soil, what is the vegetation fraction f in per cent?
::: solution :::
0.14 = 0.05f + 0.30(1−f) → 0.14 = 0.30 − 0.25f → f = 0.16/0.25 = 0.64, i.e. **64 per cent** vegetation — the spectral-mixture arithmetic of the urban-mixture example, and the reason one band suffices only for two known endmembers: real pixels need as many bands as endmembers plus one, non-negativity and sum-to-one constraints, and error bars dominated by endmember variability, not by this solvable algebra.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="71.4"}
A sensor measures total radiance 80 units over a dark target; the modeled path radiance for that band and geometry is 30 units and the transmittance is 0.70. Surface-reflected radiance obeys L_measured = L_path + T·L_surface. Recover L_surface, in units to one decimal place.
::: solution :::
L_surface = (80 − 30)/0.70 = 50/0.70 = 71.43, about **71.4 units** — subtract the path, divide by the transmittance, the two-step dark-object correction of the path example in arithmetic form; and note the error amplification built into the division: a 10-per-cent error in T becomes a 10-per-cent error in every surface radiance, which is why atmospheric correction quality, not sensor noise, sets the accuracy floor of most optical remote sensing.

::: warning Four radiometry fallacies
1. **"The pixel value is the reflectance."** Raw digital numbers are radiometric intermediates — gain-and-offset versions of at-sensor radiance that contain path radiance, illumination geometry, adjacency, detector non-linearity and (for older missions) degrading calibration; the 2016–2024 Landsat radiometric-saturation-and-degradation reprocessing literature exists because millions of archived scenes failed exactly this step. The chain radiance → reflectance requires solar irradiance, sun angle, transmittance and path subtraction, and skipping it silently weights every downstream statistic by airmass, aerosol and date — the reason the classification and change-detection lessons of this course insist on surface reflectance as the starting material, and why "we used the scene as delivered" belongs in a methods section's confessions, not its methods.
2. **"NDVI measures vegetation."** It measures a ratio of one absorption depth to one scattering plateau, which correlates with greenness, cover, chlorophyll, water, structure, phenology and soil background in proportions that vary by biome, sensor and season — the ambiguity exercise's two-band degeneracy in daily practice. The 2000s-and-2020s greening-and-browning literature's hardest arguments are not about NDVI's computation but about which term moved: China-and-India's greening resolved into irrigation-and-intensification (the 2019-and-2020 Cheng–Jiao analyses versus the 2019 Breckle–Ghosh global models) precisely because the ratio alone cannot say, and every headline from "the Earth is getting greener" to "the Amazon is browning" inherits the same underdetermination until the red edge, the shortwave-infrared water bands and the microwave vegetation optical depth are brought in as independent witnesses.
3. **"A brighter thermal-infrared pixel is a warmer pixel."** Thermal radiance is emissivity times the blackbody function, and emissivity varies by material and roughness: dry soils and rocks sit near 0.95, vegetation near 0.98–0.99, water at 0.96–0.99 with spectral structure, and polished or rough surfaces depart further still, so a quartzite outcrop at 305 K can out-radiate a wet soil at 310 K in one band and under-radiate it in another (the temperature-emissivity separation problem of the thermal lesson, the split-window's answer for water-vapour error but not for emissivity itself). The fallacy's practical form — calling Landsat land-surface-temperature maps "surface temperature" without the ±1–2 K emissivity term, or reading a daytime urban-rural contrast as pure heat when roof-and-wall emissivities and cavity geometry drive much of it — is the most cited error in urban-climate remote sensing.
4. **"The atmosphere is a fixed correction."** Its optical depth varies by orders of magnitude (clean marine boundary layers to dust-and-smoke events with visible-band optical depths above one), its windows shift with water vapour (the 8–14-µm window's transmission ranges from near unity in dry polar air to a fraction in humid tropics), and its path radiance is scene- and angle-dependent; a single global correction, or worse, a correction applied from a different date than the scene, leaves residuals structured exactly like the features being mapped — dust plumes mimicking soil-type boundaries, smoke mimicking burn scars, precipitable-water gradients mimicking soil-moisture gradients. The honest practice is per-pixel-or-per-scene atmospheric characterization with reported uncertainty (the MODIS aerosol-and-water-vapour products, Sentinel-2's Sen2Cor-and-MACCS pairing, the 2020s harmonization literature), and the honest statement of result is "surface reflectance to X per cent given aerosol optical depth of Y," never "surface reflectance," full stop.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Remote sensing is inverse physics with an error budget: a sensor records **radiance** (the only distance-conserving radiometric quantity, and the one every mission calibration report is written in), which is the sum of an attenuated surface term and an additive atmospheric path term, and the entire interpretive chain of this course — reflectance, classification, temperature, backscatter — begins by undoing that sum with Planck, Kirchhoff, Beer and a window structure written by molecular rotation-vibration spectra (the visible-near-infrared to 0.9 µm, the shortwave-infrared windows to 2.5, the 3–5 and 8–14 µm thermal bands, the millimetre and centimetre microwave windows). Surfaces answer in five dialects — vegetation's chlorophyll-and-structure red edge and water bands, water's exponential absorption and the ocean-colour ten-per-cent signal, soil-and-rock's iron-and-hydroxyl absorptions, snow's crystalline crash at 1.5 and 2.0 µm, built materials' chemical dullness and geometric loudness — and pixels are linear mixtures of them, so the honest product is a fraction with an error bar, not a class with a colour. The BRDF discipline binds it all to geometry: reflectance is a function of sun-and-view directions, time series must be normalized before change is claimed, and the two great confusions (directional reflectance for albedo, two geometries for change) are the same error wearing two hats. The fallacies — pixel-as-reflectance, NDVI-as-vegetation, brightness-as-temperature, atmosphere-as-constant — are all one fallacy, **the measured quantity mistaken for the quantity wanted**, and every technique in the lessons ahead (the calibration and correction of the [[remote-sensing/sensors-orbits]] and [[remote-sensing/image-processing]] lessons, the separability statistics of the [[remote-sensing/classification]] lesson, the temperature-emissivity separation of the [[remote-sensing/thermal-microwave]] lesson, the dielectric physics of the [[remote-sensing/radar-lidar]] lesson) is a machine for keeping the two apart. Get this lesson's bookkeeping right and a satellite image becomes a stack of calibrated physical measurements; get it wrong and it remains what the untrained call it: a picture.
:::
