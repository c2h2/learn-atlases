A petroleum system tells you where the hydrocarbons are; this lesson is about getting them out, counting them honestly, and understanding what their extraction has done to the world. The distance between oil in place and oil in a tanker's hold is the distance between geology and engineering: pressure drives the first barrels, water and gas drive the next third, steam and carbon dioxide chase the last, and economics decides which fraction ever moves at all. Coal is the same story on land — a carbonised swamp graded by burial into lignite or anthracite, mined by dragline or longwall, and now the largest single source of energy-related emissions in the world. Natural gas, the most mobile and most transitional of the three, turns every one of its advantages (clean burning, cheap molecules) into a logistics problem (it will not cross an ocean without becoming a liquid one-six-hundredth of its volume). This lesson covers reservoir drive and recovery factors, the well and platform technology that made deepwater and shale possible, the coalification ladder and its mining systems, the gas value chain from pipeline to LNG train, and the Hubbert-curve debate about what happens when production peaks — a debate that has been wrong about dates and right about shapes for seventy years. It ends where the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] lesson began: with the distinction between what is in the ground, what can be produced at a price, and what the climate allows.

## Getting It Out: Drive, Sweep, and Recovery Factors

::: definition Drive mechanisms and recovery: primary, secondary, tertiary {#def-drive}
A reservoir produces because something pushes. Four natural drives, often in sequence, set a field's first-decade behaviour: **solution-gas drive** (pressure falls below the bubble point, exsolved gas bubbles through the pore space and lifts oil along — efficient for light oils, catastrophic for heavy ones, which simply stop flowing as the gas goes immobile); **gas-cap drive** (a free gas cap expands like a piston as pressure drops, sweeping oil down-dip — the classic mechanism of structural crests, and the reason gas caps are never flared if they can be re-pressured); **water drive** (edge or bottom water expands into the void as pressure falls, maintaining reservoir pressure and giving the best primary recoveries, 40–60 % of STOIIP in strong aquifer cases like some Middle East carbonates); and **compaction and gravity drainage** (the porosity itself collapses in unconsolidated Tertiary sands — the Ekofisk subsidence story, 13 m of seabed settlement and platform-leg shortening in two decades — while steep dips let oil simply drain downward). Primary recovery under natural drive is typically 10–25 % of STOIIP; the other three-quarters is why the industry invents. **Secondary recovery** — waterflooding, invented at Bradford, Pennsylvania in the 1860s and industrialised in the 1940s, now the method behind roughly half of world production — re-pressures the reservoir and sweeps oil toward producers, lifting recovery to 30–45 %. **Tertiary recovery (EOR)** attacks what water cannot: viscosity (steam — the Kern River cyclic steaming since 1960 and the Cold Lake/SAGD in-situ oil-sands operations), mobility and miscibility (CO2 flooding, which became the Permian Basin's second industry, and nitrogen), and residual-trapping forces (polymer and surfactant floods, deployed at giant scale on Daqing since the 1990s). EOR adds perhaps 5 % of global supply today and is the hinge on which most "deep-decade" oil-supply scenarios turn.
:::

## Wells, Rigs, and the Technology That Moved the Curve

::: definition The well as a machine; the offshore as an industrial system {#def-wells}
A producing well is a pressure-containing tube engineered against the earth: steel casing string after string (conductor, surface, production, liner) cemented into the borehole, drilling mud whose density is the primary blowout barrier (the Macondo lesson restated), and a wellhead "christmas tree" of valves that meters the flow. The two technologies that re-shaped global supply in 25 years are both old ideas executed newly. **Directional and horizontal drilling**: from the 1929 Schultze well and 1948 patent-era experiments to the 1980–90s MWD/LWD tools that let a driller steer a 2 km lateral inside a 3 m coal bed or a 30 m shale layer, so that one surface slot drains a section a kilometre across. **Multistage hydraulic fracturing**: isolation sleeves and bridge-plug techniques (the 1998–2002 Mitchell Energy and later refinements of the 1947–49 Stanolind fracturing patents) let one horizontal well place 30–40 fractures along its length, turning tight rock with matrix permeability measured in *nano*-darcies into an economic producer by giving it artificial highways to the wellbore. Offshore, the rig fleet is a ladder of buoyancy and weather tolerance: jack-ups in shelf water, semisubmersibles and drillships with dynamic positioning to 3 km water and beyond, tension-leg and spar platforms in the Gulf of Mexico since the 1990s, and FPSOs (floating production, storage and offloading units — pioneered on the North Sea's Argyll field in 1976 and now the development standard from Guyana to Brazil) that decouple production from the subsea trees, manifolds and risers of a field 100 km away. The economics follow the engineering: a US onshore conventional well is a few million dollars, a shale well with 60 stages five to eight times that, a deepwater well a hundred million, and a frontier exploration well — the only kind that ever finds the next Guyana — 50 to 200 million, with a four-in-five chance of being dry.
:::

::: example The shale revolution: an industry rebuilt as a factory {#ex-shale}
Between 2007 and 2019 the United States added about 8 million barrels per day of oil — more growth than any country in history — almost all of it from three shale plays: the Bakken (Williston Basin), the Eagle Ford (south Texas) and the Permian Basin's Delaware and Midland sectors. The enabling stack was geological (the 1980s–90s realisation, argued for the Barnett by USGS's Kent Newell in 1999 and practised by George Mitchell's team after 1991, that the shale was a *continuous* accumulation — source and reservoir together, with no spill points and no edge water), directional (km-scale laterals), mechanical (60-stage completions by 2015), and chemical (fracking fluids: 99 % water and sand plus a percent of friction reducers and biocides, the 2011–2012 "99.5 % water" disclosure fight notwithstanding — the disclosed additive lists of the 2010s showed dozens of named compounds at ppm levels). The economic signature is the **hyperbolic decline**: a shale well loses 60–70 % of its first-year output in year two and needs continuous drilling just to hold a basin flat — which converts the industry's objective function from "maximise recovery per field, live 30 years" to "maximise capital efficiency per well, repeat monthly." The 2014–16 and 2020 price collapses killed the growth-at-any-price model and produced the return-on-capital discipline of the 2020s, while the 2013 **Zenith well** (Eagle Ford: 3,000 m lateral, 40 stages, 30 % production uplift for 15 % cost) set the template everyone copied. The strategic consequences are still unfolding: US crude output of 13.2 million bbl/d by 2024 (the world's largest producer), LNG exports that made the US the world's biggest gas exporter by 2023, a structural collapse in seaborne competition from Brent that capped Russia and OPEC's pricing power, and — the other side of the ledger — methane-leakage debates (the 2018–2020 EPA inventory revisions and the 2018 Brandt PNAS study on the 1–12 % leakage range that decides whether gas beats coal on CO2-equivalent) and the groundwater-contamination litigation (Dimock 2010 and the 2016–2023 Pennsylvania and Oklahoma cases) that made disclosure of fracking chemistry the central political fight of the 2010s.
:::

::: example The Athabasca oil sands: the upgrade economy {#ex-oilsands}
The Alberta oil sands hold the world's third-largest oil reserves — about 167 billion barrels, behind Venezuela and Saudi Arabia — and almost none of it behaves like oil. The Athabasca, Cold Lake and Peace River deposits are Cretaceous sands flushed by biodegradation to 8–14 °API heavy oil (the [[earth-resources/petroleum-systems]] preservation story at continental scale: a seep-and-microbe system that ate the light ends of an entire basin's charge). Two mining geometries follow from depth: the **open-pit mines** of the Athabasca near Fort McMurray (Suncor's 1967 Great Canadian Oil Sands works — the first commercial operation, built on Karl Clark's hot-water separation process, patented in 1928, which made extraction feasible: mine, hot-water separate, upgrade by coking and hydrocracking), and the **in-situ steam** operations (Cyclic Steam Stimulation, piloted at Cold Lake from the 1960s and commercial from the 1980s, and — after Roger Butler's 1978 process concept and the 1987 Cold Lake field test — steam-assisted gravity drainage in paired horizontal wells from the mid-1990s) that now produce two-thirds of Canada's 3.3+ million bbl/d. The economics are a spread trade: Canadian heavy sells at a discount to WTI (the Western Select Differential, which blew out to 40 dollars a barrel when the Enbridge Line 3 bottleneck and 2014–15 shale light-sweet glut coincided), the discount is the reward for the upgrader's capital and the natural-gas consumed to make steam, and both collapse or expand with pipeline access — the 2010–2015 "bitumen boondoggle" literature, the 2015 Fort McMurray wildfire that shut a third of the sands' output for two months, and the 2024 Trans Mountain expansion completion are the three acts. The emissions ledger is equally structural: upgraders plus mining put oil-sands barrels at roughly 3–5 times the upstream intensity of conventional barrels, which made them the target of the 2015–2025 "just transition" debates in a province where the sector is a third of the economy.
:::

## Natural Gas: Molecules Without Logistics Are Worth Nothing

::: definition The gas value chain: pipelines, LNG, storage, and the molecule's two markets {#def-gas-chain}
Natural gas is the most energy-dense clean-burning fossil fuel per carbon emitted (about half the CO2 of coal per unit of heat when burned in a modern combined-cycle turbine at ~60 % efficiency, versus ~35–40 % for subcritical coal) and the hardest to move. Its value chain has three links, each with its own economics. **Production**: associated gas (the companion of oil, flared by the tens of billions of cubic metres per year until Qatar, Norway and — haltingly — Nigeria and Iraq captured it: global routine flaring still ran ~150 billion m³/yr in the early 2020s, the World Bank's Zero Routine Flaring count), dry gas from non-associated fields, and unconventional gas — coalbed methane (the San Juan and Queensland wells that created Australia's east-coast gas market), tight gas, and shale gas (the 2010s game-changer: by 2010 shale was 20 % and by 2024 three-quarters of US gas output). **Transport**: pipelines (the workhorse — the 1958 Trans-Canada system and the 1980s Soviet Urengoy–Europe trunklines built the continental gas map) versus **LNG**: cooling to −162 °C shrinks gas to 1/600 of its volume, at an energy cost of 8–10 % of the cargo and a capital cost that kept LNG regional (Australia–Japan, Qatar–Europe) until the 2016-onward US Gulf Coast wave (Sabine Pass first cargo 2016, then Corpus Christi, Freeport, Plaquemines) made the Atlantic basin global and price-linked; regasification terminals receive the cargo at the far end. **Storage and flexibility**: gas's unique trick is that the reservoir itself is the storage — depleted fields, aquifers and salt caverns (the UK's Rough caverns, the US's 400+ underground gas storage reservoirs holding ~30 % of annual consumption as winter insurance) — which is what lets a molecule produced in April heat a home in January, and what makes gas the grid's swing partner for wind and solar. The 2022 European energy crisis was a lesson in all three links at once: pipeline dependence, LNG competition with Asia, and storage-fill policy (the EU's 90 % pre-winter storage rule of 2022) as instruments of state.
:::

::: example North Field: how one structure became the world's gas market {#ex-qatar-lng}
Qatar's North Field — the offshore carbonate reservoir that is the eastern half of a single structure whose western half, the North Dome, feeds Iran's South Pars (the two nations share a gas field the size of Lebanon) — holds something like 10 % of the world's conventional gas in place, and Qatar has turned it into the most reliable supply contract in the market. The sequence is a masterclass in chain-building: 1991 first LNG cargo (the Qatargas-1 train, a then-unprecedented 3 million tonnes/year "mega-train" design that halved unit costs), 2000s Train expansions and the 2004 Ras Laffan build-out, the 2018–2025 capacity race toward 126 million tonnes/year with the North Field East and South projects, and the 2022–2024 TotalEnergies 27-year contracts with Pakistan and China that locked acreage decades out. The strategic logic is the inverse of Russia's: Qatar chose LNG (flexible destination, higher cost, no transit-state risk) over pipelines (cheap, captive, dependent on neighbours) precisely because a small Gulf state selling into Europe and Japan cannot trust a pipe. The market consequences: Qatari cargo pricing anchored the 2004–2014 era of **oil-indexed LNG contracts** (the formula that tied gas prices to the destination market's oil cost for a decade), the 2022 crisis broke the index toward spot gas-on-gas competition, and the US Gulf Coast response — from importer-of-last-resort to the world's largest exporter by 2023, 85+ million tonnes/year — is the two-decade feedback loop from shale molecule to TTF price that European energy ministers discovered, blessing or blaming, in one winter. The environmental counterweight is real: Qatar's LNG carries upstream methane-leakage and flaring legacies (the satellite flaring surveys of the 2010s–2020s), and the 2021–2024 OGMP 2.0 reporting regime and the 2021 Global Methane Pledge (100+ countries, 30 % methane cut by 2030, launched at COP26) are the industry's first serious attempt to audit the chain end to end.
:::

## Coal: Stratigraphy Turned Into Fuel

::: definition The coalification ladder and the two minings {#def-coal}
Coal is plant matter graded by time, temperature and pressure along the **coalification ladder**: peat → lignite (15–20 MJ/kg dry, the North Dakota lignites and the Rhineland brown coal, soft, crumbly, wet, mined only within ~100 km of the power station because it spontaneously combusts and loses value in transport) → sub-bituminous (the Powder River Basin's low-sulfur coal, 24–30 MJ/kg, the largest US basin by tonnage) → bituminous (24–35 MJ/kg, the caking coals that make metallurgical coke for steel — the caking property, the free-swelling index, is what makes a coal "steelmaking" and why met coal trades at a premium and is traded globally while most thermal coal moves regionally) → anthracite (30–35 MJ/kg, the hard, near-carbon fuel of Pennsylvania's anthracite region, in decline since the 1960s). The geochemical dial is the same vitrinite reflectance that reads oil maturity — coals pass from oil-window to gas-window coals at Ro ~1.3–2.5 — which is why the world's anthracites are also its coalbed-methane prospects: the gas window generates methane that desorbs onto the coal's micropore surfaces (coal stores gas by weight, not in pores — the Langmuir isotherm of the CBM well-log suite), and dewatering a coalbed is what releases it. Mining comes in two geometries: **surface (strip and dragline) mining**, where overburden is removed and replaced — 200-tonne shovels and 10,000-tonne draglines in the Powder River Basin and Germany's Garzweiler pit (a single German excavator, the Bagger 288 lineage, moves more earth per year than the Panama Canal) — which yields ~85 % of US and ~65 % of world coal at a fraction of deep-mining cost and fatality rates; and **underground mining**, dominated since the 1950s by the British-invented **longwall** face (a 300-m shearer traversing the coal, the roof collapsing behind the powered roof supports — the strata-control geometry that makes UK and Australian underground coal competitive) and room-and-pillar (the US method, now increasingly mechanised and, in gassy deep seams, a methane-hazard business: the 2010 Upper Big Branch explosion, 29 deaths, and the 2006 Sago mine disaster that reset US escapement-refuge standards). The human and geological ledger of coal is the densest in energy: world production of ~8.8 billion tonnes (2023, China ~53 %, India ~11 %, the US ~6 %), the 2021–2022 price crisis that briefly made "coal is back" a headline, and the 2021-onward China–EU "dual carbon" programmes that for the first time put national coal-power growth caps (not just intensity targets) into policy — the policy architecture that the [[climate-change/attribution]] and [[climate-change/emissions-budgets]] lessons treat from the other side.
:::

::: example Britain's coal: the rise, the politics, and the last kilowatt {#ex-uk-coal}
No single commodity has done more modern history than British coal, and no country illustrates the full arc — resource, empire, labour politics, Thatcher-era rupture, and outright phase-out — better. The Carboniferous coal measures of Wales, Yorkshire, Nottingham and South Wales made the first industrial economy: output rose from ~2.5 million tonnes in 1700 to 287 million tonnes in 1913 (the world's largest industry, employing a million miners, exporting steam coal worldwide), and the 1947 nationalisation into the National Coal Board — Europe's largest single employer — made the industry a state within the state. Its end came in three acts. The **1984–85 miners' strike** (the Orgreave confrontation, the flying pickets, the NUM's defeat) broke the industrial politics that had made coal ungovernable for both parties; colliery closures, which had run continuously since 1958 under every government, accelerated from 170 pits to the last deep mine, Kellingley, closed in December 2015. The **2015–2024 climate policy acts** then finished the market: the carbon price floor (2013) and the Emissions Performance Standard made new coal power uneconomic, the 2015–2019 fight over the Winchfield open-cast mine and the 2022–2023 "don't pay for Ratcliffe" campaign kept the last plants in the headlines, and on 30 September 2024 Ratcliffe-on-Soar — the last coal-fired plant in the country that invented coal power — burned its last shift, a century and a half after the first. The third act is the global mirror: China now produces and burns more coal than the rest of the world combined (~4.7 of ~8.8 billion tonnes in 2023), its coal fleet is the median-age 14-year-old system that its 2021–2025 "clean and efficient use" programme and 2025 peak-coal pledge are designed to age out, and India's 1 billion tonnes of proposed capacity is the single largest uncertainty in every emissions pathway in the [[climate-change/projections]] lesson. Coal's story is the proof that resources are political: the same molecules that built the Royal Navy were written off by a carbon price and a strikebreaker's victory, and the next coal boom — Vietnam, Indonesia, the 2023–2025 Southeast Asia build-out — is being financed on Chinese terms under the Belt and Road's coal exit (pledged 2021, only partially honoured).
:::

## Peaks: Hubbert's Curve and the Politics of Exhaustion

::: definition The Hubbert curve: what it predicts and what it cannot {#def-hubbert}
M. King Hubbert's 1956 paper "Nuclear Energy and the Fossil Fuels" made a single statistical observation do enormous work: if discoveries of a finite resource follow a bell-shaped sequence, and if production lags discovery by a roughly constant interval, then production itself traces a bell curve — symmetric about a peak whose height and date depend on how much of the ultimately recoverable endowment is produced early. Hubbert's method (the discovery-vs-production "bell within a bell" area-matching, later formalised as the **Hubbert linearisation** of cumulative production against production rate) nailed the 1970 lower-48 US oil peak (9.6 million bbl/d, predicted 1965–71, actual 1970) and was so discredited when OPEC's price power and then the shale revolution reopened the curve that the debate became methodological. The honest statement of the art: Hubbert curves are **right about shapes and wrong about dates**, because (1) the endowment is not fixed — reserves grow with price and technology (the North Slope, deepwater Gulf and Bakken were all "not recoverable" in 1956), (2) the curve is the sum of many overlapping sub-curves (conventional onshore, deepwater, unconventional) whose sequence is set by capital cycles, not geology, and (3) demand-side shocks (1973, 1979, 2008, 2020) reset the production path in ways no supply asymptote anticipates. What survives is the core insight, which no counterexample has touched: **production follows discovery by about a decade, and a system whose annual consumption is three times its annual discovery is living on inventory** — world oil discoveries peaked near 55 billion barrels/year in the 1960s against current consumption of ~36 billion barrels/year, and the conventional world's decline since 2005 (roughly 3–5 %/yr on the legacy base, the "death march" of mature fields) is the reason the marginal barrel since 2010 has been unconventional, deepwater, or Canadian. The transition question — whether oil demand can peak before supply, which is what the 2015–2025 EV and policy wave implies — is a different, demand-side peak that Hubbert's machinery cannot address; it belongs to the [[climate-change/responses]] lesson's carbon-budget arithmetic.
:::

::: history From Drake's salt-drill to the methane audit: production's 165 years {#hist-production}
**1859–1901**: Colonel Drake's salt-well steam engine at Titusville strikes oil at 21 m in August 1859, and the 1870s Standard Oil trust turns a flammable curiosity into the world's first global commodity (kerosene for lamps, then — after the 1886 Benz patent and Texas's 1901 Spindletop gusher — gasoline for engines; Standard's 1911 breakup creates Exxon, Mobil and the seven sisters' lineage). **1910s–1940s**: search for oil for the Royal Navy's fuel (Churchill's 1914 oil-of-the-Navy decision, the 1914 Anglo-Persian 51 % purchase) creates the Middle East concessions — Mesopotamia's 1927 Baba Gurgur well, Iran's 1908 Masjed Soleyman precedent, and the 1928 Red Line agreement that cartellised the market; the 1933 Ibn Saud concession, the 1938 Dammam No. 7 discovery, and the 1944 founding of Aramco's predecessor transfer the centre of gravity to the Gulf. **1960–1973**: the founding of OPEC (September 1960, Baghdad — Venezuela's and Iran's price-defiance idea, as documented in Daniel Yergin's 1991 *The Prize*, which won the 1992 Pulitzer) and the 1970–71 Tehran and Tripoli agreements end the concession era; the 1973 embargo quadruples prices, creates the International Energy Agency (1974) and its 90-day stocks rule, and begins the North Sea and Alaska race (Prudhoe found 1968, Trans-Alaska line 1977; Ekofisk 1969, Forties 1970, Brent 1971). **1979–2005**: the second shock (1979) and the 1986 price collapse (down to $10) teach the industry its cost curve; 3D seismic (1990s), horizontal drilling and deepwater (Troll 1995, the Gulf's compliant towers of the 1980s, then the 2000s spars and TLPs) open the deep; OPEC's 2000s spare-capacity swing and China's demand surge (its 2004 net-import crossover) re-make the geography. **2006–2020**: the unconventional era — US gas from shale by 2008, oil by 2012, the 2015–16 collapse that killed 100+ North American independents, OPEC+'s 2016–2020 managed-decline partnership, and the 2020 negative-price April (WTI May futures at −$37, the one week the storage curve went backwards). **2021–present**: the post-pandemic whiplash (2022's $139 Brent after the February invasion of Ukraine, Europe's emergency LNG re-plumbing, the EU's REPowerEU gas-demand cuts), the US returning to record output, and the arrival of the **methane audit** (the 2021 Global Methane Pledge, the 2024 International Methane Emissions Observatory reporting, the 2024 COP28 "transitioning away from fossil fuels" paragraph) — the first regime that prices not the carbon in the molecule but the leakage of the chain that delivers it.
:::

A field's reservoir pressure begins at 35 MPa, above the oil's bubble point of 25 MPa. Once pressure falls to 24 MPa, the producing gas-oil ratio measured at surface will:

- [x] Rise above its solution value as free gas forms in the pores and flows
- [ ] Stay exactly at the solution ratio because gas cannot flow as a separate phase

Below the bubble point, exsolved gas forms a mobile (or, in heavy oil, immobile) free phase; where it can flow, the producing gas-oil ratio climbs above the original solution ratio — the surface signature that solution-gas drive has begun, and the reason primary GOR curves are the field's first clue to its own drive mechanism.

The main reason Hubbert's 1956 method failed to foresee the 2010s US production record was that:
- [ ] His arithmetic was wrong about the 1970 peak
- [x] He treated the recoverable endowment as fixed, while technology and price repeatedly enlarged it (deepwater, subsalt, shale) and shifted the bell
- [ ] OPEC deliberately overproduced to break his curve

### Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="115"}
A field has STOIIP 500 million barrels. Primary (solution-gas) recovery yields 12 % of STOIIP; converting to a waterflood raises the recovery factor to 35 %. How many additional million barrels does the waterflood add?
::: solution
Primary = 500 × 0.12 = 60 million barrels; waterflood total = 500 × 0.35 = 175 million; the increment is 175 − 60 = 115 million barrels — which is why secondary recovery, not exploration, has historically been the largest single "new field" in any mature province.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="122.5"}
A shale well flows an average of 1,000 barrels per day in its first year and declines 65 % per year thereafter. What is its rate, in barrels per day, at the end of year two?
::: solution
1000 × 0.35 × 0.35 = 122.5 bbl/day. Steep hyperbolic decline is the signature of the unconventional factory model: two-thirds of a well's first-year output is gone by its second anniversary, so sustaining a basin's production is a drilling-rate problem, not a reserve problem.

An LNG carrier's cargo is 165,000 m³ of liquid. Using the 600:1 expansion ratio, how many million cubic metres of gas does one cargo represent?
::: solution
165,000 × 600 = 99e6 m³ — about a month of UK winter peak-day demand in one ship, which is why a single blocked pipeline (the 2022 Nord Stream shutdown) or a single frozen US Gulf feed gas outage moves the whole Atlantic gas price.

::: exercise {level=2 check="36.5"}
World oil consumption is about 100 million barrels per day. How many billion barrels does that take per year?
::: solution
100e6 × 365 = 36.5e9, so 36.5 billion barrels per year — against peak annual discoveries of ~55 billion barrels in the 1960s and roughly 10 billion in the 2010s: the discovery-versus-consumption gap that makes every "new normal" supply story a story about decline rates.

::: exercise {level=2 check="3.5"}
A 1 GW power station runs at 38 % net efficiency on coal of 24 MJ/kg. How many million tonnes of coal does it burn per year (1 year = 3.154e16 J per GW)?
::: solution
Fuel energy = 3.154e16 / 0.38 = 8.3e16 J; mass = 8.3e16 / 24e6 = 3.46e9 kg ≈ 3.5 million tonnes — one unit of one percent of world coal output per gigawatt, and the arithmetic behind why coal plants are the largest point sources of CO2 per unit of electricity ever built.

North Field holds about 9,700 trillion cubic feet of gas. Taking 1 standard cubic foot of gas as 1.085 MJ, convert the field's gas to exajoules.

::: exercise {level=3 check="139"}
The oil sands hold about 167 billion barrels of reserves and produce about 3.3 million barrels per day. At a flat rate, how many years of production is that? Round to the nearest year.
::: solution
167e9 / (3.3e6 × 365) ≈ 139 years — the reserve-life number that ends every oil-sands debate, and the reason the binding constraint is carbon policy and water and gas for steam, not geology: the resource is effectively infinite relative to any climate budget.

US lower-48 conventional oil peaked at 9.6 million bbl/d in 1970; the shale-era peak was 12.3 million bbl/d in 2018. By what factor did the second bell exceed the first? Give to two decimal places.
::: solution
12.3 / 9.6 = 1.28 — a 28 % higher peak, 48 years later, from rock that was considered non-reservoir in 1970: the single cleanest number against fixed-endowment peak theories, and the reason the next binding constraint is demand, not supply.

::: warning Four misconceptions about fossil-fuel production
1. **"Reserves are a physical inventory."** Reserves are a price-and-technology claim: the same North Slope crude was "resource" in 1968 and "reserve" in 1977 at a different oil price and pipeline state. Reserve-life ratios (the R/P numbers quoted for oil, gas and coal) therefore drift upward for producing countries and downward for embargoed ones, and comparing them across regimes is meaningless — the discipline for reading them is in the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] lesson.
2. **"Peak oil was disproved."** Conventional-oil *supply* peaking was not disproved — lower-48 oil did peak in 1970 and the conventional global base has been flat-to-declining since 2005 — but the *total* curve kept re-belling because unconventional supply arrived faster than conventional decline. The honest claim is that technology, price and policy, not geology alone, set the date — and that a demand peak driven by EVs, efficiency and renewables is now the operative peak, which no resource can prevent.
3. **"Coal can be phased out quickly because it is declining in the West."** OECD coal demand has fallen ~30 % since 2012, but non-OECD (China, India, Southeast Asia) holds ~75 % of the operating fleet, the median Asian plant is under 15 years old against a 35–40 year life, and the 2021–2022 power-crash winter showed how fast coal share rebounds when gas fails. A coal phase-out is a *fleet-age and grid-flexibility* problem, not a sentiment problem.
4. **"Methane doesn't matter next to CO2."** Over 20 years methane traps ~80 times the heat of CO2 (the IPCC AR6 GWP-20 of 82.5), and the chain's leakage — the 2018–2024 US EPA inventory revisions and the IEA's tracking of 70+ million tonnes/year of energy-sector methane — is the difference between calling gas a "bridge fuel" and a peer of coal. The good news is arithmetic too: leakage is the cheapest emissions cut available (capturing vented gas often pays for itself), which is why the 2021 Global Methane Pledge and the 2024 methane rules made it the first fossil-regulation that targets the chain rather than the molecule.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Getting hydrocarbons out is a story of pressure and sweep: natural drives (solution gas, gas cap, water, compaction) give up 10–25 % of the oil in place, waterflooding — the quiet technology behind half the world's barrels — lifts that to 30–45 %, and steam, CO2 and chemicals fight for the remainder, so that "recovery factor" is the honest measure of how much engineering a barrel carries. The technology of the last quarter-century (steerable laterals, 60-stage completions, subsea systems and FPSOs) did not find new basins so much as it redefined rock and water depth that capital could work, producing the two re-bellings of the supply curve — deepwater and shale — and moving the marginal barrel from the Persian Gulf to the Permian, the North Atlantic and the Athabasca. Coal, still 27 % of primary energy and the largest single emissions source, runs the same physics at the surface: a coalification ladder read by vitrinite reflectance, two mining geometries with wildly different labour and landscape footprints, and a political economy now splitting into an OECD phase-out (Britain's 287 million tonnes of 1913 to zero megawatts in 2024) and an Asian fleet-age reality. Gas, the cleanest-burning and most logistical of the fuels, is the swing partner of the transition — and its leakage accounting, not its combustion, is the open question. Through all of it runs Hubbert's honest ghost: production follows discovery, inventories are finite, and the only durable forecast is that the curve is a stack of bells whose next rung is set by what technology and policy make recoverable — or allowable. The next lesson turns the pore space these fuels vacated into the storage resource; the one after that turns to the water that shares the aquifers.
:::
