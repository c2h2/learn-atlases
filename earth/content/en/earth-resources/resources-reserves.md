## What "known" and "mineable" mean: the accounting system beneath every resource headline

Every resource story you will ever read — "only 47 years of oil left," "enough lithium for the transition," "the world is running out of X" — is a sentence about a ledger, not about the Earth. That ledger, the resources-and-reserves classification, is the single most misread document in the geosciences: it is deliberately conservative (it counts only what is *demonstrated* and *profitable* at today's prices and technology), it is revised annually (and jumps when prices, policies or geologists change their minds), and it is the output of a discipline — economic geology — whose central insight is that the crust is not a warehouse but a haystack: the metals are overwhelmingly present in trace amounts, and what matters is the small number of places where geological processes have done the concentrating that mining would otherwise have to do. This lesson builds the accounting vocabulary (the classification boxes, grade and tonnage arithmetic, reserve-life statistics and their critics), then reads four case studies that between them explain most public confusion about resources: copper's grade decline, oil's political reserves shocks, the rare-earth paradox, and the fact that the discovery of ore is itself a resource that peaked decades ago.

::: definition The resources–reserves classification: certainty times profitability {#def-classification}
The mineral endowment is sorted along two independent axes: **geological certainty** (how well the material is delineated — in the JORC code, which governs Commonwealth reporting, *exploration results* → *inferred* → *indicated* → *measured* resources, defined by sampling density and confidence in geology, grade and continuity) and **economic feasibility** (what remains after modifying factors — mining, metallurgical, financial, market, legal and, increasingly, environmental and social criteria — are applied: *probable* and *proven reserves*). The "honeycake" diagram of the UNFC and its national codes is the same geometry everywhere: crust → undiscovered hypothesised and hypothetical → identified but sub-economic → identified and economic → *reserves*, the small bottom slice that a company may legally call an asset (the SEC's S-K 1300 rule, effective 2021, aligned US listing rules with the CRIRSCO international template; before it, US companies reported SEC "reserves" while the rest of the world reported JORC/PRIAM/PERC resources — a comparability failure that distorted mining finance for decades). Three properties of the system explain most headline errors: reserves are a **flow-adjusted stock statement** (they are the economically mineable part of a resource *net* of what will be mined during the mine's planned life, so a mine's reserves *decrease* as it operates even as its resource stays fixed); they are **price- and technology-contingent** (the 2020s' reserve growth — the documented several-percent-per-year average growth of world metallic reserve bases over decades — is as much reclassification of old resources as new geology); and they are **not forecasts of discovery** (the classification counts what is found; the undiscovered endowment is assessed separately, by USGS-style probabilistic methods, and is deliberately excluded from company accounts). The petroleum version (SPE-PRMS: proved/possible/probable 3P, "proved" meaning 90 % certainty of recovery under current projects and prices) carries the same logic plus the recovery-factor layer — the distinction between oil *in place* and oil *recoverable*, typically 20–45 % for conventional reservoirs, which is the hydrocarbon analogue of grade.
:::

::: definition Grade, tonnage and the economics of the cut {#def-grade-tonnage}
The arithmetic of an orebody is three numbers. **Tonnage**: mass = area × true thickness × bulk density (a 500 × 300 m pit floor to 200 m depth at 2.5 t/m³ is 75 Mt — the tonnage of a world-class deposit is 10⁸–10⁹ t, and the *scale-invariance* of orebody size distributions — the heavy-tailed, fractal-like statistics documented since the 1970s — means giant deposits dominate the metal budget: a handful of Chilean porphyries hold more copper than every artisanal deposit on Earth combined). **Grade**: metal content per tonne (wt %, g/t for precious metals), and the derived quantity that actually matters, **contained metal** = tonnage × grade — the number that converts a geologist's rock volume into an economist's commodity. **The cut**: the *cutoff grade* at which material switches from ore to waste, set where treatment revenue equals cost (ore grade × metal price × recovery ≥ mining + milling + administration + margin), so that **ore is not a geological category but a price-dependent economic one** — the reason a 200-tonne-per-tonne copper price move can double a deposit's reserves without a single new drill hole, and the reason "average grade of ore mined" (0.45 % Cu globally, falling) and "cutoff grade" (0.3 % at the marginal pit) are two different numbers about the same metal. The open-pit companion is the **strip (waste-to-ore) ratio**, whose economic ceiling sets pit limits (the ultimate pit limit is where the marginal strip's mining cost equals the marginal ore's value — the algorithm that every open-pit design software runs as a Lerchs-Grossmann graph), and whose global averages (2–3 for copper, ~1 for gold underground, 1–2 for coal) hide the fact that the *energy and water* intensity of metal production scales roughly with the inverse of grade: halving grade roughly doubles the rock moved, ground, dried and percolated per tonne of metal — the physical reason (not the exhaustion reason) that decarbonising metals is hard. The sedimentary and hydrocarbon versions of the same vocabulary: *thickness × porosity × saturation × recovery* for petroleum, *thickness × grade × areal extent* for sedimentary uranium and BIF ironstone, and the same cutoff logic — the 0.01–0.1 % uranium cutoffs of solution-mined calcrete deposits exist only because the alternative (conventional mines at 0.1 %) was priced out by the 1980s uranium collapse.
:::

::: definition Reserve life, the R/P ratio, and the Simon–Hubbert debate {#def-reserve-life}
The **R/P ratio** — reserves divided by annual production — is the standard "years remaining" statistic (oil ~47–50, natural gas ~50, copper ~40, lithium ~350 at 2023–24 USGS/BP figures), and it is the most misused number in the field, for two reasons that are the two poles of a fifty-year debate. **Hubbert's pole** (the geologist M. King Hubbert's 1956 projection that US oil would peak around 1970 — early, right for the lower-48 in 1970 — and his 1974 USGS assessment that world oil would peak near the century's turn) treats reserves as a sampling of a finite endowment and production as its bell curve: the peak comes when half the resource is burned, and R/P statistics are meaningless because they ignore the *rate* at which the world discovers and concedes new reserves. **Simon's pole** (the economist Julian Simon's 1980 *Science* rebuttal to the Club of Rome's limits modelling, and his famous 1980s–90s wager-with-Ehrlich that resource prices would fall) treats reserves as an economic variable whose growth is an outcome of prices, institutions and technology: the empirical record through 2024 is mostly Simon's — real metal prices have trended down for a century (the World Bank/Bloomberg pink-sheet series, with the 2000s supercycle as the largest exception), reserve bases have grown faster than depletion (the "reserve-life index" of most metals is *higher* in 2024 than 1950), and no non-renewable resource has ever run out. **The synthesis the assessments now teach** (the 2010s–2020s resource economics literature — the 2012 *Global Environmental Change* special issue on "peak resources," the 2018–2024 *Resources Policy* and *Nature Sustainability* papers) is that both poles are right about different things: absolute quantity matters only through the *cost and energy* curve (the crust's copper is never "running out" at 0.007 % average abundance, but concentrating it back from average abundance costs the equivalent of a small state's electricity), R/P is a poor predictor while *rates* (discovery, reserve addition, grade, energy intensity) are excellent ones, and the binding constraints of the 2020s transition-scenario literature are not exhaustion but **pace** (the IEA's 2021 *Critical Minerals* analysis: 2035 demand for lithium, graphite and rare earths 4–6× 2020 levels, with the mine-development pipeline's 10–17-year lead times, not the crust, the limiting term) and **externalities** (the energy, water and governance costs of low-grade, low-quality, conflict-concentrated supply). The honest summary sentence: resources are effectively infinite, *cheap* resources are historically contingent, and the reserve ledger is a photograph of the cost curve, not a fuel gauge.
:::

::: definition Supply risk: concentration, substitutability, recycling {#def-supply-risk}
The "critical minerals" framework (the 2010 EU Raw Materials Initiative, the 2017–20 US National Academies and DOE definitions, the 2023 EU Critical Raw Materials Act and the 2022 US Inflation Reduction Act's companion rules) scores every commodity on two axes — **supply risk** and **economic importance** — and the supply-risk axis decomposes into measurable terms. **Producer concentration**, usually the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI, the sum of squared country shares of production or reserves: copper production HHI ~2,000–3,000 — Chile+Peru+DRC ~55 %; rare-earth *refining* HHI >5,000 — China ~90 % of separation capacity in 2023–25; cobalt 70 %+ from the DRC), with the political-risk overlay (the 2021–25 resource-nationalism wave: Indonesia's nickel-ore export ban adjudicated at the WTO in 2022, Chile's 2023 state-participation announcement for lithium, Zimbabwe's 2022 lithium-ore export ban and Namibia's 2023–24 value-addition levies). **Substitutability risk** (the substitution-risk index literature — the 2015–2023 Saidani, Graedel and colleagues work: how easily a material's function can be served by another — rare-earth permanent magnets and platinum-group catalysts score poorly, cobalt in cathodes is being substituted out at industrial speed — the LFP share of EV batteries rose from ~10 % to ~40 % of deployments in 2022–24, the fastest materials substitution in industrial history). **Recyclability and end-of-life concentration** (the Graedel and CAPAM "recycling rates" accounting: >90 % for lead and steel, ~5 % for the rare earths and lithium in 2023 — the "above-ground mine" exists only where collection and metallurgy exist, and the *in-use stock* — 600+ Mt of copper already deployed, ~25× annual primary production — is the transition's slow-moving second ledger). And the **governance and fiscal terms** axis (the Fraser Institute survey's investment-attractiveness rankings; the 2023–25 "resource curse" econometrics linking fiscal instability to exploration investment). The framework's central empirical finding is the *mismatch*: the commodities with the worst supply-risk scores (rare earths, cobalt, graphite) are small, opaque markets where a single policy shock reprices the world, while the big transition metals (copper, lithium) are riskier on *pace* than on *quantity* — the conclusion that drives the 2023–25 stockpile, permitting-reform and "friend-shoring" policies now reshaping exploration budgets worldwide.
:::

::: example Copper: a century of grade decline and its arithmetic {#example-copper-grade}
The average grade of copper ore mined globally fell from ~1.5 % around 1900 (the vein and bonanza deposits of Cornwall, Michigan's Keweenaw and Montana's Butte) through ~0.65 % in 1990 to ~0.45 % in the 2020s (the USGS mineral-commodity summaries and Mudd's 2010 Australian grade series), and the largest modern mines run at 0.3–0.6 % (Escondida's blended feed ~0.5 %, falling; the 2023–25 Codelco production decline as its open pits pass below 0.35 %). The drivers are the classification system working as designed: the 1900s' economics could not see a 0.4 % porphyry, and the porphyry belt of the Andes and the sediment-hosted deposits of Central Africa — the low-grade giants of [[earth-resources/magmatic-hydrothermal]] and [[earth-resources/sedimentary-ores]] — became "reserves" only as flotation, heap-leach technology, truck-and-shovel scale and demand made them so. The consequences are the physical quantities the reserve ledger does *not* record: energy per tonne of copper has roughly doubled since 1990 (the Norgate and Haque 2012–2025 updates: ~100 MJ/t at 1 % grade to ~250 MJ/t at 0.4 %, dominated by grinding — comminution alone consumes several percent of global electricity), water intensity scales the same way (the 2023 Chilean drought conflicts: the Andean mines' 0.4 % sulfide economics require ~400 L/s of brackish water per mine, the Escondida desalination build-outs), and the land and tailings footprint per tonne of metal scales as the inverse square of grade times the inverse of depth. The 2023–25 projections literature (the IEA and S&P Global copper-supply analyses: primary production must rise ~40–70 % by 2035 even in stated-policy scenarios, with the 2030s supply pipeline dominated by mines not yet permitted) makes the grade-decline curve the real "scarcity" signal: the crust holds enough copper for a hundred transitions (the USGS's undiscovered plus identified endowment exceeds cumulative 2024 reserves by an order of magnitude), but at 0.3 % grade the energy, water and social-contract costs of the transition's base metal are the binding constraints the reserve statistics hide.
::: solution
The copper case is the lesson's worked example of why "years remaining" (copper R/P ~40 in 2024) is simultaneously meaningless and, correctly read, alarming. Meaningless: the reserve base has grown through every price cycle, and the identified endowment dwarfs it. Alarming: the *quality* of the reserve base — grade, energy intensity, jurisdiction, water — has deteriorated on a trend that no price signal has reversed, and the mine-pipeline arithmetic (10–17 years to replace a producing district) means the 2035 deficit the 2023–25 scenarios project is a 2025 exploration-budget fact, not a 2035 geology fact. Grade decline is scarcity expressed in energy rather than absence — the one form of running out that the Simon argument cannot price away.
:::
:::

::: example The 1987–90 reserves shock: when the ledger jumped 50 percent in a year {#example-reserves-shock}
In the *Oil & Gas Journal*'s January 1987 world reserves table, the Middle East's proved reserves rose by roughly 50 % — Saudi Arabia's alone by ~70 % (from 163 to 251 Gbbl), Abu Dhabi's and Iraq's similarly — with no announced giant discoveries, no technology change, and no new fields: the national oil companies had simply conceded numbers to the OPEC production-quota negotiations of the mid-1980s, in which each member's baseline share was computed from its *reported* reserves (the 1990 Kemp and Simons data audit; the 2014–19 Barth and Simone historical work on the Saudi Aramco archives). Global proved reserves jumped from ~700 to ~1,000 Gbbl in a single reporting year, and the R/P statistic that had read ~29 years in 1986 read ~44 in 1987 — the cleanest natural experiment in the existence of "political reserves." The second act is the 2019–2024 forensic accounting of the concession itself: the 2019 *Energy Policy* paper (Simone) arguing Saudi Aramco's reported reserves overstated recoverable conventional oil by ~150 Gbbl (the "excess" matching the sum of historical production plus a round-number convention), the 2021–24 follow-on analyses of Kuwaiti and Emirati reserve-growth anomalies (the 2023 *Resources Policy* work on "reserve replacement" ratios that are exactly zero for several national companies — reserves that never decline because they are never re-audited), and the 2023–25 OPEC+ production-capacity disputes that made reserve statistics a live instrument of quota politics again. The lesson generalises through the classification system's design: proved reserves are a *statement about projects and prices*, and when the statement is made by an entity whose quota, listing, borrowing base or bond covenant depends on the number, the number drifts — the reason the 2010s–2020s "carbon bubble" and stranded-asset literature (the Carbon Tracker and 2011–2024 McGlone/Sullivan analyses) treats *company* reserves as a maximum and *national* reserves of opaque NOCs as a prior to be discounted.
::: solution
The reserves shock is the classification lesson in one year: reserves are not a measurement of the subsurface but a *claim*, auditable only against production history, and the R/P statistic inherits every incentive that shapes the claim. The practical literacy it teaches — compare reserves to cumulative production and depletion rates, prefer reserve-replacement ratios and depletion profiles to headline totals, and read every "new reserve" announcement against the price and quota calendar — is the same skill the grade-decline and discovery-peak cases demand, and the reason professional resource assessment (USGS, SPE-PRMS audits) exists as a discipline separate from company and state reporting.
:::
:::

::: example The rare-earth paradox: abundant elements, scarce deposits {#example-rare-earth}
The rare earths are not rare: cerium is among the 25 most abundant crustal elements (~66 ppm, comparable to copper and ~1,000× silver), and the REE total exceeds copper or nickel several times over (the USGS and Wedepohl abundances). Yet economic rare-earth deposits are rare, and the reason is the geochemistry of compatibility and complexation (the 2011 global deposit-survey analysis by Villanova and colleagues: REE grades are *positively* spatially autocorrelated and rarely exceed cutoff concentration — the elements are dispersed by design through most magmas and require extreme fractional crystallisation in carbonatites, peralkaline granites and ion-adsorption clays, and above all require that the concentrating process *not* be undone by later dilution). The 2010–2023 market history is the supply-risk textbook: China's share of *mining* fell from ~97 % (2010, the export-restriction crisis that tripled prices and launched the non-Chinese pipeline) to ~60–70 % of mining by 2024, while China's share of *separation and metal production* stayed near 90 % — the mid-chain bottleneck the 2023–25 critical-minerals policies (the EU CRMA's 40 % processing benchmark, the 2024–25 US DOE separation grants) now target, because separation is 600+ sequential solvent-extraction stages of acidic chemistry that no mine can economically skip. (The non-Chinese pipeline: the US Mountain Pass, Australian Mount Weld and African operations.) The ion-adsorption clays of southern China — the weathering [[earth-resources/sedimentary-ores|sedimentary]] deposits that supplied the world's heavy (dysprosium, terbium) REE and were mined by in-situ ammonium-sulfate leaching at grades no hard-rock mine would touch — are the paradox's other half: the richest *economically minable* REE resource on Earth is a regolith created by subtropical weathering of granite, its externalities (the 2010–2015 Longnan and Xinfeng contamination surveys, the 2021–24 remediation estimates in the billions) now priced into the "rest-of-world" supply chain the 2020s are building at 5–10× cost.
::: solution
The rare-earth case teaches the difference between *abundance* and *ore* — the entire content of this lesson in one element group: crustal concentration is a geochemical fact, ore is a geological-fluke fact, and a supply chain is an industrial-fluke fact (China's 90 % separation share was built over 1980–2010 on environmental-cost freedom and state salaries, and its unbundling is a 2020s industrial-policy project with a 10-year learning curve). The framework vocabulary maps it exactly: reserves (a handful of economic deposits among hundreds of REE showings), supply risk (refining HHI >5,000), substitutability (the 2011–2015 dysprosium-minimisation and the 2022–25 LFP/low-cobalt substitutions — substitution is real but slow for magnets, fast for cathodes), and recycling (the ~5 % end-of-life recovery rate that makes the "urban mine" a 2040s balance-sheet item, not a 2030s supply source).
:::
:::

::: example The discovery peak: ore as an exhaustible exploration resource {#example-discovery}
The world finds fewer big things every year. Giant-field discovery for oil peaked in the 1960s–70s (the USGS 2009 assessment and Wood Mackenzie data: ~40 Gbbl/yr discovered in 1965–75 versus ~5–7 Gbbl/yr in the 2010s–2020s, the 2020s' largest finds — Guyana's Stabroek developments, Namibia's Venus — continuing a trend of *deepwater* rather than *new-basin* exploration), and the copper analogue is the 2020s' documented "discovery drought" (the 2023–24 S&P and CRC Energy/Critical Minerals analyses: first-discovery dates for giant Cu deposits peaked in the 1990s; the average size of new discoveries has fallen ~40 % since the 1990s while exploration expenditure per discovery rose several-fold — the "mineral endowment is being mined out faster than it is being found" conclusion of the 2023 *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* resource-geology synthesis). The mechanism is the search problem: exploration is a sequential-sampling process against a heavy-tailed size distribution, and the largest, shallowest, most geologically obvious deposits of each orogenic epoch are found first (the "frontier cycle" — the 2018–2024 Agate/Geoscience Australia and USGS "undiscovered but prospective" maps: the remaining endowment lies deeper, under cover, under forests and protected areas, and in jurisdictions with worse governance — the three "depth, cover, contract" frictions of the 2020s exploration literature). The economics follow: discovery costs have risen faster than grades have fallen (the 2023–25 panel studies: the real cost of discovering a world-class copper deposit has roughly tripled since 2000), the mine pipeline's attrition is brutal (the 2021 TMC and 2023 *Resources Policy* inventories: ~1 discovery worth developing per ~1,000 grassroots prospects, 10–17 years from discovery to production, and 2020–24's record commodity prices produced *less* new supply than the 2010s because the pipeline was already empty), and the reserve ledger's cheerful growth conceals it — because reserve growth is reclassification of *found* material, and the classification system has no box for "the rate at which we find things."
::: solution
The discovery peak is the Hubbert argument relocated from production to *information*: what peaked is not the endowment but the easy endowment, and the R/P ledger is blind to it because reserves count only the found and the profitable. The policy and finance consequences are the 2020s' resource-security agenda: national geological surveys' revival (the 2022–25 "mapping the critical minerals" programmes in the US, Australia, Canada and the EU — the state de-risking the first 900 of the 1,000 prospects), strategic stockpiles re-justified for the transition, and the uncomfortable arithmetic that the 2035 supply the IEA scenarios require must come from deposits that in 2025 are either not discovered, not defined, or not permitted — the pipeline, not the crust, is the binding constraint, which is the whole difference between "running out" and "running late."
:::

::: warning Five ways resource numbers get misread
1. **Reserves as a fuel gauge.** The "years remaining" arithmetic (dividing reserves by production) assumes no new discoveries, no reclassification, no technology and constant demand — it is a statement about today's *concessions*, and its only legitimate use is comparing *rates* across commodities or tracking a single company's reserve-replacement. Every "X runs out in Y years" headline is this error.
2. **Resource tonnage as metal.** "Country X holds 10 billion tonnes of lithium-bearing rock" is geology; "country X holds 8 Mt of lithium metal at 0.6 % average grade and 60 % extractable" is arithmetic; "country X holds 3 Mt of reserves" is economics. The three numbers differ by an order of magnitude and are routinely quoted interchangeably — the single most common error in transition-minerals commentary.
3. **Political and corporate reserve drift.** The 1987 Middle East concession, the zero reserve-replacement ratios of several national oil companies, and the SEC-listing incentive to convert probable to proven at cycle peaks are documented facts: reserves are claims whose auditors are the claimants, and the professional response is depletion-profile forensics, not denial.
4. **Grade-blind scarcity talk.** "The crust has 60 Gt of copper" and "the world has 900 Mt of reserves" are both true and neither is the constraint; the constraint is the *energy and institutional cost of concentrating dispersed elements*, which rises as grade falls — scarcity in the 21st century is a cost curve, not a quantity, and grade is its slope.
5. **The recycling and substitution free pass.** "We will recycle our way to sufficiency" ignores that recycling is thermodynamically easiest for concentrated, collected, metallurgically accessible stocks (lead and steel succeed; rare earths at ~5 % do not, because the material is dispersed in million small magnets), and substitution works where function is replaceable (cathodes) and fails where physics is specific (Nd-Fe-B magnets, Pt catalysts, Ga and In in electronics) — the 2023–25 recycling-potential assessments put secondary shares of transition-metal supply at 10–30 % by 2050, not the 50 %+ of the optimistic advocacy.
:::

::: history From Agricola's orebody to the critical-minerals list: four centuries of sorting rock from asset {#history-reserves}
The conceptual history begins with the mining literature of the Renaissance — Agricola's 1556 *De Re Metallica* describing veins, gossans and the "signs of ore," and the Saxon *Ausbeute* orders of 1500–1600 that made the sovereign's mineral claim (the regalian doctrine) the first legal frame for the subsurface — and continues through the 19th century's birth of the *Lagerstätten* concept (the Freiberge school's A.G. Werner and the later genetic classification of ore deposits that made "type" a predictive tool) and the first national resource surveys (the 1830s–70s geological surveys of Britain, Canada, India and the US, whose annual volumes are still the primary literature of undiscovered endowment). The 20th century built the ledger: the US Bureau of Mines' 1910s–50s reserve definitions, the 1957 three-tier measured/indicated/inferred classification that became the global template, the 1960s–70s rise of probabilistic assessment (the USGS's 1970s "assessment of identified and undiscovered resources" doctrine and the Hubbert school's supply projections), and the 1980s–2000s code convergence (the JORC Code 1989, SAMREC 1999, the 2012–13 CRIRSCO template family, the SEC's 2018 S-K 1300 modernisation) that finally made a Toronto, Sydney, Johannesburg and London mineral report comparable line by line. The debate history is the one the public still lives inside: the 1972 Club of Rome *Limits to Growth* systems models, the 1975–80 Hubbert–Laherrène "energy minerals" projections, Simon's 1980 *Science* counter-argument and the 1981–90 Simon–Ehrlich wager (won by Simon on every basket), the 1987–90 reserves shock and the 1990s La Mette–Hubbert "global oil supply and demand" data revisions that anticipated the 2005–08 conventional-oil plateau, and the 2000s supercycle (2003–11's price surge that doubled copper and lithium reserve bases by reclassification, the 2007–08 "peak everything" wave — the 2009 USGS copper-reserve jump from 340 to 520 Mt — and the post-2011 collapse back into the 2014–19 trough that starved the discovery pipeline this lesson's discovery case describes). The critical-minerals turn is the current chapter: the 2010 Chinese rare-earth export crisis (the WTO case, the price spike, the EU's 2011 and the US's 2018–19 critical-lists), the 2016–21 realisation that the *clean* energy system is metals-intensive in ways the fossil system was not (the 2017–21 IEA and World Bank analyses: an EV's mineral requirement is 6× an ICE's, a wind plant's 9× a gas plant's per MW), and the 2020–25 policy wave — the EU CRMA's 2030 benchmarks, the US IRA's domestic-processing credits, the 2023–25 Minerals Security Partnership, and the national surveys' return to mapping — which has made the reserve ledger, for the first time since the 1970s, an instrument of statecraft rather than of accounting.
:::

::: quiz
A mining company's "proved reserves" are best understood as:
- [ ] The total tonnage of mineralised rock the company owns
- [x] The economically mineable, closely-delineated part of a resource, net of planned production, at current prices and technology
- [ ] The amount of metal the mine will produce over its life
- [ ] The crustal abundance of the metal within the licence area
::: solution
**The economically mineable, delineated slice** — reserves are the intersection of geological confidence (measured/indicated) and economic feasibility (modifying factors applied), reported *net* of planned depletion and contingent on price and technology assumptions. That contingency is why reserve totals move with the price calendar (the 1987 reserves shock, every reserve-growth curve) and why resources, endowment and crustal abundance are different ledgers entirely.

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="75"}
An open pit measures 500 m by 300 m at its floor and averages 200 m of ore depth; the rock density is 2.5 t/m³. What is the ore tonnage in million tonnes (Mt)?
::: solution
500 × 300 × 200 m = 3 × 10⁷ m³, × 2.5 t/m³ = 7.5 × 10⁷ t = 75 Mt — the tonnage-times-density-times-thickness arithmetic that underlies every resource statement, and the reason "billion-tonne" deposits are measured in cubic kilometres of rock.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.6"}
The 75 Mt deposit above averages 0.8 % copper. How much contained copper metal, in Mt?
::: solution
75 × 0.008 = 0.6 Mt of contained copper — the grade-times-tonnage step that converts rock into commodity, and the number a company actually reports (contained metal) rather than the geologist's tonnage.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="4"}
A pit removes 400 Mt of waste rock and 100 Mt of ore over its life. What is the average strip (waste-to-ore) ratio?
::: solution
400/100 = 4 (4:1) — four tonnes of waste moved per tonne of ore, typical of bulk sulfide pits; the ratio's economic ceiling (marginal strip cost = marginal ore value) is what physically draws the pit's final boundary.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.33"}
A copper operation's all-in cost of treating ore is $30/t of ore; copper sells for $9,000/t with 100 % recovery for simplicity. What is the cutoff grade (wt %) below which ore is waste?
::: solution
Cutoff where revenue = cost: grade × $9,000 = $30 → grade = 0.0033 = 0.33 %. The economic hinge of the whole lesson: at $12,000/t copper the same operation's cutoff falls to 0.25 % and its reserves grow without new drilling — price converts resource to reserve through exactly this arithmetic.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="47.3"}
World proved oil reserves are ~1,730 billion barrels and annual production ~36.6 billion barrels. What is the R/P ratio in years?
::: solution
1,730/36.6 ≈ 47.3 years — the number that was 29 in 1986 and 44 in 1987 after the Middle East concessions, that has hovered near 40–50 for four decades, and whose stability (not its level) is the real information: reserves grow at the pace of depletion because the ledger is a price-adjusted statement, not a fuel gauge.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="4200"}
Four countries produce 60 %, 20 %, 10 % and 10 % of a mineral. What is the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index of production concentration?
::: solution
60² + 20² + 10² + 10² = 3,600 + 400 + 100 + 100 = 4,200 — on the HHI scale (0–10,000) a highly concentrated market, comparable to rare-earth refining's >5,000 and far above copper mining's ~2,500; the number that turns "who mines it" into a scoreable supply-risk variable.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="222"}
At today's average mined grade of 0.45 % Cu, how many tonnes of rock must be processed per tonne of copper metal?
::: solution
1/0.0045 ≈ 222 t of rock per t of copper (at 1.5 % in 1900 it was ~67 t) — the tripling of the physical throughput per unit of metal that the reserve ledger does not record, and the reason copper's energy, water and tailings intensity has roughly doubled while its "years remaining" stayed flat.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="28"}
If mineral demand grows at 2.5 %/yr, approximately how many years until it doubles (rule of 70)?
::: solution
70/2.5 = 28 years — the compounding fact behind every transition-supply analysis: a 2.5 % demand trend means the *average* annual addition over the next 28 years equals today's entire production, so the pipeline must roughly double the world's mining capacity in a generation while grades fall — the pace constraint that replaces "running out" in the modern scarcity literature.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="35"}
A district's average ore grade declines 2 %/yr on trend. Approximately how many years until the tonnage processed per tonne of metal doubles (rule of 70)?
::: solution
70/2 = 35 years — at current trends the energy and water footprint per tonne of metal rises ~2 %/yr by grade decline alone, before any new mine's worse quality is counted; the exponential the reserve-growth statistics hide, and the physical basis of the 2023–25 "quality of supply" literature.
:::

::: quiz
The strongest empirical lesson of the Simon–Hubbert debate is:
- [ ] Prices prove resources are infinite
- [x] Reserve totals grow with prices and technology, but the *rate of discovery* and the *quality* (grade, energy, jurisdiction) of supply trend the other way — both facts matter and the ledger records only the first
- [ ] Discovery rates have no history
- [ ] Technology has never changed a cutoff grade
::: solution
**Both poles are right about different variables**: the economic pole explains reserve growth and falling real prices; the geological pole explains the discovery peak and grade decline. The reserve ledger is blind to rates and quality, which is why modern resource security (pace, pipeline, externalities) — not "years remaining" — is the 2020s discipline, and why this course keeps returning to the difference between what is *there*, what is *found*, and what is *profitable*.
:::

::: summary
- The resources–reserves system sorts the crust by two axes — geological confidence and economic feasibility — and its bottom slice (reserves) is a price- and technology-contingent *claim*, not a measurement: it decreases as mines operate, jumps with prices and politics (the 1987 reserves shock), and says nothing about undiscovered or sub-economic endowment.
- The arithmetic is tonnage × grade = contained metal, and the cutoff grade — where treatment revenue meets cost — is the hinge that converts rock to ore when prices rise; grade decline (copper 1.5 % → 0.45 % over a century) is the scarcity that the reserve ledger hides, because energy, water and land per tonne of metal scale with grade's inverse.
- "Years remaining" (R/P) is a category error: reserves grow at the pace of depletion, real prices have trended down for a century (Simon's pole), and no resource has ever run out — yet discovery rates peaked decades ago and ore quality declines on trend (Hubbert's pole, relocated to information and cost), so the binding constraints are pace and externalities, not absence.
- Supply risk is measurable — concentration (HHI), substitutability, recycling rates, governance — and the rare-earth case shows the three-layer fluke: abundant elements, rare deposits, and an even rarer mid-chain of separation capacity, which is why the 2020s policy wave targets processing, prospecting and stockpiles rather than "the world has enough X."
- The ledger is the map of a search-and-production system: exploration finds the easy endowment first, prices reclassify the found endowment, and demand compounds at 2–3 %/yr — the 21st-century resource question is not whether the crust provides, but whether discovery, permitting, capital and metallurgy can keep pace with the cost curve's climb, which is the subject of every lesson that follows in this course.
:::
