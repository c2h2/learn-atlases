Every resource lesson in this course has described an extraction; none has kept the ledger. This is that ledger: the surface and subsurface legacies of getting minerals, coal, oil and gas out — the tens of thousands of active mines and hundreds of thousands of wells whose waste rock, tailings, brines, subsidence craters and orphaned casings define whole regions' water chemistry and land economics — and the young engineering and legal science (1970s onward) that tries to price, prevent and clean the account. The discipline rests on a brutal asymmetry: extraction revenues arrive in decades, while the liabilities — acid-generating waste rock, tailings dams, plumes in aquifers, wells with no owner — persist for centuries and outlive the companies that created them. The lesson runs through the four great impact classes (landscape and waste; acid mine drainage; spills and produced water; subsidence, methane and the orphaned-well inheritance), each paired with its regulatory arc and its signature disasters: Brumadinho and Fundão's tailings flows, Butte's Berkeley Pit and Tar Creek's lead, Exxon Valdez to Deepwater Horizon's $20-billion reckoning, and Germany's and Britain's coal-legacy waters. The prerequisites are the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] lesson (liability is the other side of the reserve ledger) and the [[earth-resources/groundwater-resources]] lesson (most extraction impacts are, in the end, groundwater impacts).

## Landscape, Waste Rock, Tailings

::: definition The mass balance of mining: disturbance, waste, and the tailings problem {#def-waste}
Mining is defined by its waste. The **strip ratio** (waste moved per unit product) runs from under 1 for thick coal seams to about 10:1 in porphyry copper and 100:1-plus in open-pit gold — meaning a 2020s copper mine moving 500 Mt/yr of rock yields a few tens of megatonnes of mill feed and leaves the rest to be placed, watered and stabilised; open pits end as landforms of cubic-kilometre scale (Bingham Canyon's roughly 2.7 km³, whose 2013 and 2021 wall failures rewrote its slope design), and underground mines leave subsidence troughs at the surface — the metre-scale depressions of coal longwalling that made the Ruhr's and Yorkshire's compensation ledgers a permanent branch of property law. The two permanent legacies are **waste rock** — barren rock dumped in engineered hills whose slopes and geochemistry must hold for millennia, the design philosophy of the 1980s–90s "landform reclamation" school (Bell and colleagues in Australia's Hunter Valley, who rebuilt stable landforms from the geometry of natural slopes) — and **tailings**, the ground-to-sand-and-silt fraction left after milling, mixed with process water and pumped into impoundments. The 2019 EarthEye radar survey counted some 3,600 tailings facilities worldwide, and the failure record is why they are now board-level risk: the 1966 Aberfan coal-slip in Wales killed 116 children and founded spoil-tip law; the 2015 Fundão collapse (Samarco) sent 40 million m³ of iron-ore tailings down the Doce River to the Atlantic, killing 19 and terminating a fishing economy; and the 2019 Brumadinho collapse (Vale's Córrego do Feijão dam) killed 270, buried a workforce cafeteria and a tailings run, and made what the 2020 Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (ICMM, UNEP and PRI, March 2020) and the investors' insurance repricing made commercially decisive: the end of **upstream-raise** construction — building the dam upward on the liquefiable mud itself, the cheapest and deadliest method — with ICMM members committing in 2020 to phase it out entirely. The design answers are simple to state and expensive to build: downstream-raise dams on sound bedrock, filtered **dry stacking** (pressing the free water out before placement, removing the liquefaction fuel), and reprocessing legacy tailings for residual metal while re-depositing the residue drier and denser than the original dam.
:::

::: definition Acid mine drainage: the chemistry that outlives the mine {#def-amd}
Excavation is a redox experiment. Pyrite (FeS₂), stable for millions of years below the water table, oxidises when mining exposes it to air and water: the net reaction converts one tonne of pure pyrite into roughly 1.6 tonnes of sulphuric acid plus dissolved iron, and the iron's further oxidation — catalysed up to a thousand-fold by the acidophile *Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans*, the 1947 Colmer-and-Hinkle discovery that made acid mine drainage a microbiology as much as a geochemistry problem — autocatalyses the cascade until pH falls below 2 and every heavy metal the rock holds (copper, zinc, cadmium, aluminium, and radionuclides where the host is granitic) dissolves into the seepage. The result, **acid mine drainage (AMD)**, is the largest long-term water-quality problem in the old mining world: the 2001 US National Research Council assessment put abandoned-mine drainage on more than 200,000 km of US streams, and Europe's coal and uranium fields run perpetual treatment works with no completion date. The hierarchy is prevention first: **encapsulation and water covers** (the 1990s–2020s practice of isolating acid-generating waste below the water table or under oxygen-barrier caps and wetlands, the same logic that made Greater Sudbury's 100,000 acidified lakes — the 1980s-onward "Sudbury Model" of liming, replanting and smelter-stack controls — the world's largest and most successful metallurgical reclamation) beat **active treatment** (lime and caustic dosing, the 1970s-onward high-density-sludge plants) whose perpetual operating cost outlives every corporate balance sheet, and both beat the 2015 **Gold King** demonstration that remediation is itself a hazard — an EPA breach of the ageing tailings impoundment above Silverton, Colorado, that released some 3 million gallons of orange, metal-laden sludge down the Animas and San Juan rivers and made "do not disturb without characterisation" the closure planner's first commandment. **Passive treatment** — anoxic limestone drains, constructed wetlands and sulfate-reducing bioreactors that run the microbial chemistry in reverse — is cheap to run and honest about its limits: it buys time and beauty, not immunity, at high acidity loads. The governance point is the course's core: AMD is a liability with a 500-year horizon attached to a 30-year corporation, which is why financial-assurance bonds, closure plans filed before the first truck (the 1977 Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act's requirements in US coal, the 2020 tailings standard's closure provisions) and funded abandoned-land programmes (the US Abandoned Mine Land fee, whose 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act renewal carried more than $12 billion for legacy coal country) are the only instruments that survive bankruptcy.
:::

::: definition Spills, produced water, and the offshore risk budget {#def-spills}
Petroleum's impact ledger has two very different columns. **Acute spills** are the photographed ones: the tanker era began with the 1967 Torrey Canyon's 120,000 tonnes on the Cornish coast (and the first international liability regime, the 1969 Civil Liability Convention), passed through the 1978 Amoco Cadiz's 223,000 tonnes off Brittany, and was refounded by the 1989 Exxon Valdez — 41 million litres in Prince William Sound, whose legal aftermath (the 1990 Oil Pollution Act's double-hull and response-planning architecture) made "no more Valdezs" the design brief of a tanker generation: by the 2020s per-tonne spill frequency had fallen two orders of magnitude from the 1970s peak. The offshore column is different: the 2010 Deepwater Horizon blowout — 4.9 million barrels (about 650,000 tonnes) blown out over 87 days, the largest marine spill in history, with the 2015–2016 settlements approaching $21 billion in US penalties and natural-resource damages alone — proved that the tail of the risk distribution is set not by tanker discipline but by blowout probability in frontier water, and its response — the 2011–2012 well-control compacts and the regulator's rule cycles on blowout preventers and well integrity — is the textbook of industrial safety-after-failure. The chronic column is larger than either: **produced water** — the formation water that comes up with every barrel (several billion litres per day in the 2020s US, the Permian's ratio approaching ten water barrels per oil barrel in mature fields) — is re-injected into deep disposal wells, and that disposal is the induced-seismicity story of the 2010s central United States: the 2011 Youngstown, Ohio injections behind the first clearly-attributed injection earthquakes, Oklahoma's earthquake hundredfold of 2009–2015 (answered by the 2015–2016 disposal-volume cuts and the USGS's 2016 synthesis), and Texas's 2019 permit cancellations are the template now written into shale regulation: traffic-light protocols on disposal, not on production.
:::

::: definition The quiet legacies: subsidence, orphan wells, and fugitive methane {#def-legacy}
Between the photographed disasters and the acid chemistry lies the largest line of the ledger: time. **Subsidence** deforms the surface wherever rock is mined out or pore pressure drops — the coal-trough villages of the Ruhr and Yorkshire with their century of compensation law; the Wilmington oil field of Los Angeles, whose 1932–1960s metre-scale troughs and pioneering geodetic monitoring made subsidence a reservoir-management variable; and the Netherlands' Groningen gas field, where the 2012 magnitude-3.6 Huizinge earthquake turned a nuisance-level induced-seismicity debate into a national referendum on extraction, forced the production cuts of 2014–2018, and ended in the 2023 final shutdown — the largest induced-seismicity policy reversal on record, with remediation of 30,000-plus damaged houses still running. **Orphan and abandoned wells** are the industry's oldest liability with the newest data: the United States holds hundreds of thousands of wells drilled before modern regulation, tens of thousands already legally "orphaned" (no owner, no fund, some leaking), and a 2024 peer-reviewed inventory put the total historical well count above 3.8 million, most of them never mapped — which is why the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act's $1.15 billion orphan-well plugging programme, the first federal appropriation for the task whose state contracts ran 2022–2025, is best read as a century of deferred maintenance arriving as a stimulus line. The **fugitive methane** from those wells, from abandoned coal mines and from live but unmeasured operations is the legacy that joins the climate ledger: the 2018 Alvarez-and-Colomb satellite-and-aircraft synthesis that found US oil-and-gas emissions ~60 % above inventory, the super-emitter surveys of the 2019–2023 Alberta, Permian and Turkmenistan campaigns, and the 2023 MethaneSAT and Carbon Mapper flights that began naming operators rather than averages, together showing that a few per cent of assets emit most of the gas and that a single unplugged well can leak tonnes per year for decades. The 2020s policy synthesis — the Global Methane Pledge's 2021 30 %-by-2030 target, the US EPA's 2024 super-emitter regulations, the EU methane regulation's 2025–2027 reporting and import benchmarks — is in substance legacy-industry regulation: the cheapest methane abatement on Earth is gas already leaking, and finding it is a survey problem, not a science problem.
:::

::: definition Cleaning up: the legal science of liability, closure and circularity {#def-remediation}
Remediation law is younger than mining by four thousand years and rests on three inventions. **Liability**: the polluter-pays principle (OECD 1972, EU Treaty Article 191) made cleanup the operator's bill rather than the taxpayer's — implemented in the US as CERCLA/Superfund (1980, the ~1,300 National Priorities List sites of the 2020s, the retroactive joint-and-several liability that made environmental insurance an industry), the EU Environmental Liability Directive (2004), and mining states' financial-assurance requirements (bonds and guarantees sized to closure cost, whose chronic under-bonding — the 2016 US Government Accountability Office finding that hard-rock bonds cover only a minority of cleanup cost, and the bond re-forms of 2020s Nevada, Arizona and Australia — is the field's central enforcement failure). **Closure planning**: the requirement, standard since the 1990s (South Africa's 1997 policy, the EU Mining Waste Directive 2006, ICMM's 2019 closure guidance and the 2020 tailings standard) that a mine file its death plan before its birth certificate — landform, water treatment horizon, monitoring end-date and the money to reach it. **Circularity**: the recognition that legacy waste is inventory — the "urban mine" arithmetic (the Global E-waste Monitor's 62 Mt generated in 2022 with under a quarter formally collected, the 2010s–2020s e-waste hubs of Agbogbloshie and Guiyu as the cautionary photo-essay, and the 2024 EU battery regulation's recycled-content mandates as the first industrial attempt to close the loop) and the tailings-reprocessing industry (the 2020s South African plants re-milling gold slimes at 50-year-old grades richer than many live mines, and the EU Critical Raw Materials Act's 2024 secondary-source benchmarks) make yesterday's liability tomorrow's orebody wherever grade, volume and location allow. The honest synthesis from three decades of Superfund and mine-closure practice: cleanup works when money is secured before the need, when the end-state is written as a number (a pH, a concentration, a land use) rather than an adjective, and when the aquifer, not the parcel, defines the project boundary — the three conditions that the 2020s just-transition funds (the EU Just Transition Fund's coal-region programmes 2021–2027, Germany's €40+ billion structural package for the coal regions agreed 2020, the US Abandoned Mine Land bonuses) are now attempting to scale from sites to regions.
:::

::: example Brumadinho and Fundão: forty months that rewrote tailings law {#ex-brumadinho}
Brazil produced the tailings industry's before-and-after in a single mining state. On 5 November 2015 the Fundão barrier (Samarco, the Vale–BHP joint venture) failed above the village of Bento Rodrigues in Minas Gerais: 40 million cubic metres of iron-ore tailings — a slurry the consistency of wet concrete — travelled 600 km down the Doce River to the Atlantic, killing 19 people, burying the village, terminating the estuary fishery, and contaminating drinking water for half a million; the 2017 Renova foundation's 20-year, 24-billion-reais remediation agreement, the federal and state actions that pushed total Samarco liabilities past 170 billion reais, and the criminal trial that convicted the plant's Australian-Brazilian management in 2021 (the convictions later appealed, the case's unresolved accounting a lesson in itself) made Fundão the most expensive environmental disaster in Brazilian history. Forty months later, on 25 January 2019, Vale's own Córrego do Feijão dam at Brumadinho, 120 km away and audited as stable, liquefied in under a minute: the wave overran the mine's offices and cafeteria and a downstream community, killing 270 people, and its run-out mapped the exact population that the Fundão inquiry had said would never be hit again. The institutional speed of response is the case study: within fourteen months the 2020 Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management was drafted by ICMM, UNEP and PRI with the families of Brumadinho on the drafting committee; within five years the upstream-raise method that built both dams was being retired by major miners and Brazil's 2019 state law banned it outright, while Vale's February 2021 reparation framework with Minas Gerais — 170 billion reais, the largest environmental settlement anywhere at the time — set the price of a tailings run in the currency of decades. The engineering residue is the transferable part: tailings are now classified by failure physics (static liquefaction under load, the Seed and Idriss soil-mechanics tradition that the two failures validated on full scale), dam-break inundation maps are drawn in metres and minutes, and the escape-route drills that Brumadinho's survivors described as "the thing that should have existed" are standard at GISTM-compliant sites — the cheapest safety technology in mining, and the one that took 289 lives across two disasters to mandate.
:::

::: example Butte and Tar Creek: two American legacy geometries {#ex-butte-tarcreek}
The two founding Superfund landscapes show why legacy cleanup is one science with two very different engineering problems. **Butte, Montana** is a point-source monster: a century of copper mining (the "Richest Hill on Earth", 1864–1983, under Anaconda and then ARCO/BP) left the Berkeley Pit — a cut roughly 3 km around and hundreds of metres deep, flooding uncontrolled since dewatering stopped in 1983 into a lake of pH ~2.5 ferric-sulphate water carrying well over 100,000 mg/L of dissolved copper and iron, the acid-sulphide weathering column of every geochemistry textbook run at city scale, with warm arsenic-bearing groundwater rising through the adjacent "Yankee Freedom" acid seep into the Clark Fork headwaters. The remedy is a perpetual machine of water-treatment plants and liability ledgers — from BP's 2002 $105 million Superfund settlement to the EPA's 2023 consolidation of the work under a single Butte Priority Office — projected to run for centuries at tens of millions of dollars a year, the acid-mine-drainage horizon made literal, and a live test of whether general funds, not corporate bonds, can hold a century-scale obligation. **Tar Creek, Oklahoma** is the diffuse version: seventy years of lead-zinc mining (1917–1967, the Tri-State district's zinc capital) left some 9,000 hectares of "chat" — white, wind-mobile, lead- and cadmium-rich tailings — spread through the fields, yards and playgrounds of Picher and its neighbours, with the 1990s studies finding a third of local children above the intervention blood-lead level. Tar Creek was among the first Superfund sites listed in 1983, and its remedy was not chemistry but land use: the EPA's 2000s–2011 acquisition and demolition of hundreds of homes over chat and collapsing mine voids, and Picher's 2013 de-incorporation with fewer than two dozen residents left. Tar Creek's transferable truth: when the liable owner is defunct, the waste is unengineered and residential, and the exposure pathway is the town itself, cleanup becomes relocation — the policy instrument of last resort that no 20th-century mining law contained, and the reason the 2020s debate over Superfund's reformed funding is really a debate over who lives where.
:::

::: example The coal-legacy turn: the Ruhr, Lusatia and the British coalfields {#ex-coal-legacy}
The richest mining-legacy programmes on Earth belong to regions that are closing, not opening, coal: Europe's coal phase — Germany's 2020 coal-exit law with its €40-plus-billion structural package for the Ruhr, Lusatia, central Germany and the Helmstedt regions, and Britain's post-1994 pit closures that left the state-owned Coal Authority holding the inheritance — is the first industrial retirement in history negotiated as a landscape conversion. **Water is the hidden engineering**: when the last pumps stop, coalfields flood along their own gradients, and two public trusts now run the largest mine-water utilities on the planet — the UK Coal Authority's ~70 perpetual treatment schemes (dosing and polishing hundreds of millions of litres a day of acidic, metal-rich mine water, whose 2019 Cragg Vale zinc kill and 2022–2023 Environment Agency prosecutions made legacy discharge a national headline), and the German RAG Foundation, endowed in 2007–2008 with some 2.2 billion euros to run post-mining water management for two centuries — pre-funded perpetual care whose 2020s cost reports already show bills rising as the fields flood past the old pumps' design horizon. **Landscape conversion is the visible half**: the Ruhr's Emscher river daylighting and the re-use of coking plants and pit-heads as parks and museums (Zollverein's 2001 UNESCO listing); and Lusatia's International Building Exhibition Fürst-Pückler-Land (2000–2010), which flooded open-cast pits into a chain of twenty lakes — Germany's newest tourism region, where the 2020s arguments over lake water budgets show that even "finished" conversions need hydrological management in perpetuity. Germany's 2024 agreements with RWE and Leag pulled parts of the western exit forward toward 2030, making Lusatia the live experiment in whether mine closure can become a region rather than a site. The comparative moral is in the sequencing: the German and British programmes were funded *before* the last pit closed, while Appalachia's abandoned-coal-mine backlog — tens of thousands of unaddressed sites, many from operators long dissolved — was left to the 1977 AML fee's starved fund until the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act's $12-billion renewal and the 2023–2025 state bonus payments began clearing it. America is discovering the same arithmetic one generation late, at compound interest.
:::

::: example Orphan wells and super-emitters: mapping the invisible inventory {#ex-orphan-methane}
The 2010s–2020s methane surveys accomplished what two decades of rulemaking had not: they made the industry's smallest legacies visible, quantified and attributable. The measurement arc is worth memorising as a method: the 2013–2014 aircraft campaigns over the Denver-Julesburg and San Juan basins (the Brandt and colleagues 2014 *Science* synthesis finding basin methane double inventory) opened the era; the Alvarez and colleagues 2018 *Science* Albuquerque flyover put national oil-and-gas methane ~60 % above EPA estimates; and the super-emitter catalogues that followed (Carbon Mapper's PRISMA detections from 2021, MethaneSAT launched in March 2023, the IEA's Global Methane Tracker from 2021) converged on one Pareto law — a few per cent of sites produce most of the leaks, findable from orbit for pennies per tonne abated. The orphan-well half of the inventory is older and messier: state records (Pennsylvania's 100,000-plus documented legacy wells, the 2023 Texas legislative reform that finally created a funded orphan-well programme, California's CalGEM well-inventory digitisation), the 2014 Cornell and 2024 multi-university estimates that put the US historical well count in the millions with most drilled before any regulation existed, and the 2021 federal $1.15 billion plugging programme — the first sustained national attack on the backlog, whose 2022–2025 state contracts plugged wells at tens of thousands of dollars each, thousands completed, backlog measured in tens of thousands more. The policy synthesis arrived 2021–2024: the Global Methane Pledge (September 2021, 150-plus countries, a 30 %-by-2030 cut), the US EPA's December 2024 methane rules whose super-emitter response programme makes satellite detection a regulatory trigger with a fee schedule, the EU methane regulation in force from 2024 with 2025–2027 reporting and import-intensity benchmarks, and Canada's 2024 tightening proposals — all of them, in substance, legacy-infrastructure rules. The quiet revolution is epistemic: for the first time a rusted casing and an abandoned coal seam are line items in public, satellite-verifiable accounts — the accounting that the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] lesson always said was missing.
:::

::: history From Torrey Canyon to the satellite ledger: sixty years of extraction law {#hist-impacts}
**1966–1973**: the founding disasters — Aberfan's coal-slip (October 1966, 116 children, the UK's first spoil-tip legislation and a compensation tribunal that invented "disaster law"), the Torrey Canyon (March 1967, the first supertanker wreck and the first international liability convention, 1969), the Cuyahoga River fire (1969, the image that catalysed the US Clean Water Act of 1972) and the Amoco Cadiz (1978, 223,000 tonnes off Brittany, the tanker-design and port-state-control revolution). **1977–1980**: the liability architecture — SMCRA's reclamation bonds and AML fee (1977), the Love Canal hearings (1978) and CERCLA/Superfund (1980), the first general retroactive cleanup law anywhere, whose 2020s record (thousands of assessed sites, ~400 delisted) is the honest measure of what liability law can and cannot do. **1983–1989**: the legacy sites surface — Tar Creek's emergency Superfund listing (1983), the Berkeley Pit's dewatering stop (1983, the start of the century-scale treatment machine), and the Exxon Valdez (March 1989, 41 million litres, the 1990 Oil Pollution Act and the double-hull fleet). **1994–2010**: the closure-profession era — the Mine Closure Colloquia series from 1994, the EU Mining Waste Directive (2006) and financial-assurance reforms, the Sudbury Model's two-decade demonstration that regional acidification can be reversed, and the Deepwater Horizon blowout (April–July 2010, 4.9 million barrels, the ~$21 billion reckoning and the well-control compacts). **2015–2019**: tailings' terrible education — Fundão (2015), Gold King (2015), Brumadinho (2019), and the 270 + 19 deaths that ended the upstream-raise era. **2020–2025**: the synthesis — the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (March 2020), the Global Methane Pledge (September 2021), the US orphan-well appropriation and $12-billion AML renewal (2021), Germany's coal-exit structural package (2020 law, 2024 acceleration), the EU methane regulation (2024) and EPA super-emitter rules (2024), and the 2024 Global Stocktake's first-ever call to transition away from fossil fuels — the first decade in which the ledger of extraction, not its geology or economics, sets the regulatory frontier.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-upstream}
Why did the 2020 Global Industry Standard make upstream-raise tailings dams effectively unfinanceable?

- [ ] They cost more to build than downstream designs
- [x] Their failure mode is liquefaction of the impounded slurry itself, so a stable reservoir is a structural precondition of the dam — the inversion that Fundão and Brumadinho proved fatal
- [ ] They cannot be instrumented with piezometers
- [ ] They require more maintenance than downstream designs

::: solution :::
In an upstream raise, each new lift of the dam is built on top of the deposited tailings — the "dam" and the "waste" share a body, so any liquefaction of the slurry *is* a dam breach, with the run-out travelling through the deposition area where workers and offices sit. Downstream designs build on foundation soil and keep the slurry behind a conventional embankment; they cost more earthwork but cannot fail by the impounded material becoming the failure. The financing change followed the physics: insurers and lenders repriced upstream facilities after 2019, and the standard's requirement of no-new-upstream plus emergency plans and inspection made the method commercially dead.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-amd-microbe}
Microbes accelerate acid mine drainage primarily by:

- [ ] Secreting acids that directly dissolve pyrite
- [x] Catalysing the oxidation of Fe(II) to Fe(III), the fast oxidant that attacks pyrite — closing an inorganic loop a thousand times faster
- [ ] Fixing sulphur into biomass, releasing heat
- [ ] Consuming oxygen faster than it diffuses into the waste rock

::: solution :::
Pyrite oxidation by oxygen alone is slow; Fe(III) oxidises pyrite much faster, and the acidophilic iron-oxidisers (Acidithiobacillus and relatives) regenerate Fe(III) from the Fe(II) produced, at the low pH that the reaction itself creates — a positive feedback in which biology catalyses geochemistry. That is why AMD systems self-sustain below pH 2, why the 1947 Colmer-Hinkle discovery changed the field, and why "sterilising" waste is never an option: the microbes are environmental, not introduced.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="90"}
**Strip ratio.** A surface coal mine with a strip ratio of 6:1 removes 15 Mt of coal in a year. How many megatonnes of waste rock must it place?
::: solution :::
15 × 6 = **90 Mt** of overburden — six times the product, which is why the mine's earthmoving fleet, not its coal plant, sets the cost curve, and why the shape of the dumped hills is the mine's longest-lived engineering artifact.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="14.6"}
**Tailings tonnage.** A copper mill processes 40,000 tonnes of ore per day, and essentially all the ore mass reports to tailings after a 25 % concentrate yield. What is the annual tailings tonnage in Mt?
::: solution :::
Tailings ≈ 40,000 × 365 = 1.46 × 10⁷ t = **14.6 Mt/yr** — the mass-balance reason a mid-sized copper mine is first a rock-handling company that sells copper, and why the tailings facility, not the pit, is usually the largest structure on the site.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="41600"}
**Spill volume.** The Exxon Valdez released about 11 million US gallons of crude. Convert to cubic metres (1 US gallon = 3.785 litres).
::: solution :::
11 × 10⁶ × 0.003785 = 41,635 ≈ **41,600 m³** — and the comparative sense-check: Deepwater Horizon's 4.9 million barrels is 780,000 m³, nineteen Exxon Valdezes blown out over three months, which is why the two spills produced two different regulatory regimes (tanker design versus well control).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1633"}
**Acid potential.** Oxidation of pyrite (FeS₂, molecular weight 120) yields two molecules of sulphuric acid (MW 98) per molecule of pyrite. How many tonnes of pure sulphuric acid can 1,000 t of pure pyrite generate?
::: solution :::
Acid per tonne pyrite = 196/120 = 1.63. For 1,000 t: **1,633 t** of H₂SO₄ — the number that makes waste-rock geochemistry (the acid-generation test on every drill core) the most consequential analysis in mine design, and that turns "just a little pyrite" into a perpetual treatment plant.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1233"}
**Lime requirement.** Slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂, MW 74) neutralises sulphuric acid (MW 98) mole for mole. How many tonnes of lime are needed to neutralise the 1,633 t of acid from the previous problem (ignore inefficiency)?
::: solution :::
1,633 × 74/98 = 1,233 ≈ **1,233 t** — and the real number is 20–50 % higher because of incomplete mixing and the hydrolysis of dissolved aluminium and iron: the operating budget of every active AMD treatment plant in the world is this arithmetic multiplied by perpetual inflow.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="2.4"}
**Subsidence.** A longwall panel extracts coal 2.5 m thick with a mining recovery of 80 %; the overburden transmits 120 % of the extracted volume to the surface as subsidence. What is the central surface subsidence?
::: solution :::
Subsidence = 2.5 × 0.8 × 1.2 = **2.4 m** — the reason longwall regions keep aerial levelling archives, why the UK's compensation law exists, and why the Groningen decision-makers said the gas was not worth the earthquakes: subsidence and fault-slip are the same accounting of removed volume, one slow, one sudden.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="650000"}
**Blowout mass.** Deepwater Horizon released 4.9 million barrels of oil of density ~830 kg/m³. Estimate the released mass in tonnes (1 barrel = 0.159 m³).
::: solution :::
Volume = 4.9 × 10⁶ × 0.159 = 7.8 × 10⁵ m³. Mass = 7.8 × 10⁵ × 0.83 = 6.5 × 10⁵ ≈ **650,000 t** — larger than the entire annual production of several North Sea fields, released uncontrolled, and the mass that the $21 billion settlement, 11 wrongful-death counts and the natural-resource damage assessment had to price.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="29"}
**Orphan-well warming.** An unplugged well leaks methane at 2 g/min. What is its warming impact over one year in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent (GWP-100 = 28; 1 year = 525,600 min)?
::: solution :::
Mass = 2 × 525,600 g = 1.05 t CH₄/yr. CO₂-e = 1.05 × 28 ≈ **29 t** — one well equals a small car's lifetime emissions every year it stays unplugged; multiply by the tens of thousands of orphans in the 2024 US inventory and the plugging programme's $1.15 billion is, climate-wise, the cheapest mitigation line in the Infrastructure Act.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3400"}
**Urban mine grade.** The 2022 Global E-waste Monitor counts 62 Mt of electronics discarded, 22 % formally collected. At an average gold content of 250 g per tonne of collected devices, how many tonnes of gold sit in the formally-collected stream?
::: solution :::
Collected = 62 × 0.22 = 13.6 Mt. Gold = 13.6 × 10⁶ × 0.00025 = **3,400 t** — more than a decade of primary gold mining's output (annual mine supply ~3,000 t) flowing past waste bins every year at a grade 50 times a natural orebody, which is the whole circular-economy argument in one division — and the whole reason the 78 % informally-handled remainder (Agbogbloshie's open burning) is simultaneously the resource and the crime scene.

::: warning Four misconceptions about extraction impacts
1. **"The big spill is the big impact."** Photographed disasters drive the law, but the chronic ledger is larger: produced water dwarfs spills in volume, acid mine drainage outlasts every spill recovery by two centuries (see the widget's two curves), and Europe's coal-water treatment bills recur forever. Impact budgets, like carbon budgets, should be kept in tonnes-times-years — and on that metric the quiet items win.
2. **"Cleanup restores the baseline."** Remediation engineering returns *function and risk*, not origins: treated water that meets a number, landforms that don't erode, exposure pathways removed — but flooded coalfields cannot be re-drained without restarting the acid, chat piles can only be moved or buried, and the Lusatian lakes are a hydrological commissioning project, not a landscape restored. The honest end-state language is "safe and monitored," and the 2020s tailings standard's insistence on *predictable* long-term behaviour is the law finally admitting restoration is a metaphor.
3. **"Regulation follows disasters."** Partly true — Aberfan, Valdez, Brumadinho each bought a regime — but the deeper pattern is that regulation follows *measurement*: Darcy-era hydrogeology made plume law possible, InSAR made subsidence attributable, satellite methane made operators namable. Every tightening of the last decade (super-emitter rules, tailings inventories, orphan-well funds) traces to an instrument that made an invisible liability visible and quantifiable.
4. **"Circularity ends extraction impacts."** Secondary supply is real and growing — e-waste gold at fifty times ore grade, reprocessed tailings richer than live mines — but the 2020s scale of secondary metals covers a fraction of demand growth, and circularity has its own residues (the informal e-waste burning of Agbogbloshie is an extraction impact wearing a recycling label). Circular flows shift the impact ledger's columns; they do not close the book, and the book is this lesson's subject.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Extraction's ledger balances two masses: the product, sold in decades, and the residue, engineered for centuries. The mass balance is brutal (strip ratios of ten, tailings tonnage equal to ore, one tonne of pyrite yielding 1.6 tonnes of acid), the liabilities are asymmetric in time (a spill heals on a decade clock, acid mine drainage decays on a drainage clock, a chat pile endures until moved), and the law has responded in three inventions — liability (Superfund's retroactive polluter-pays, financial assurance sized to closure cost), closure planning filed before extraction begins (SMCRA to the 2020 tailings standard), and now circularity (legacy waste as tomorrow's orebody). The disasters that wrote the regimes are a roll-call with a physics behind each name: Aberfan's tip and Torrey Canyon's tanker founded tip law and liability conventions; Exxon Valdez rebuilt tankers and Deepwater Horizon rebuilt well control; Fundão and Brumadinho ended the upstream-raise era in forty months; Groningen traded a gas field for a shake-map and a shutdown; Butte and Tar Creek showed perpetual treatment and managed retreat as the two endpoints of legacy engineering; and the satellite methane surveys of 2013–2024 made the smallest legacies — orphan wells, super-emitters — publicly countable for the first time. The comparative lesson for resource economics is the one the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] lesson taught from the other side: a reserve is only as secure as its balance sheet, and a mine is only as closed as its assurance fund. The 2024 Global Stocktake's transition-away-from-fossilfuels language makes this ledger a live discipline — hundreds of operating sites, tens of thousands of legacy ones, and a decade in which the accounting, not the geology, sets the frontier.
:::
