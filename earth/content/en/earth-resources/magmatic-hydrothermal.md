## From melt to ore: how magmatism and hydrothermal fluids build deposits

Ninety per cent of the metals in the modern economy sit in deposits whose genesis involves one of two processes: a silicate melt that concentrated the metal on its way to crystallising, or a hot aqueous fluid that leached, transported and dropped the metal somewhere it could be mined. The discipline that reads these processes — economic geology — organises the world's orebodies into *deposit models*, archetypes (porphyry, VMS, MVT, orogenic lode, IOCG, layered intrusion) that are simultaneously genetic hypotheses and exploration checklists, and the models' success is the strongest argument in geology for thinking in systems: the 1960s porphyry-copper model turned a mining curiosity (the low-grade disseminated stocks of the American Southwest) into the exploration template that found Escondida, Grasberg and every other giant of the Andean and Pacific metallogenic epochs. This lesson walks the magmatic and hydrothermal families in the order a geologist reads them — source process, transport, trap — and uses four deposits (Bushveld, Olympic Dam, Solwara 1, Kalgoorlie) to show how the same fluid-and-melt physics builds a stratiform chromite, a breccia pipe, a seafloor mound and an orogenic gold camp.

::: definition Magmatic ore-forming processes: compatibility, fractionation and immiscibility {#def-magmatic-processes}
Magmatic deposits form because elements partition unequally during melting and crystallisation (the partition-coefficient and compatibility logic of [[geochemistry/trace-elements]] applied to ore). The four industrial applications: **partial-melting concentration** (the mantle's incompatible-element signature makes carbonatites the planet's REE, Nb and P concentrators — Bayan Obo's REE-Fe-Nb, Palabora's Cu-apatite-magnetite, the Phoscorites' P; and high-degree melting of the mantle makes komatiites the Ni workhorse — Kambalda's 33 Mt at 3–5 % Ni sit in 2.7 Ga komatiite flows because 40 % melting of a depleted mantle produced a melt already carrying 10× normal-mantle Ni). **Fractional crystallisation cumulates** (the layered intrusions: crystals settling in a long-lived magma chamber build monomineralic layers of economic grade — Bushveld's chromitite seams (0.5–2 m of 40–50 % Cr₂O₃ in a 100 km-thick intrusion pile), the Stillwater and Norilsk PGE-bearing chromitite and sulfide horizons, the apatite layers of Phalaborwa, the Fe-Ti-V magnetite layers of Tellnes and the White Mountain anorthosite suite). **Sulfide immiscibility** — the single most important concentration mechanism for the chalcophile elements: at sulfide saturation a separate Fe-Ni-Cu-rich liquid (a *sulfide melt*, 900–1,200 °C, density ~5) forms and, with partition coefficients of 10²–10⁴ for Cu, Ni, PGE, Re, Os, strips them from thousands of cubic kilometres of magma into a few cubic kilometres of liquid — the Norilsk-Talnakh 450 Mt at 1.8 Ni+Cu+PGE and 120 Moz associated Pd-Pt, the Kambalda nickel, the PGE reefs of Bushveld's Merensky and UG2 (whose origin — settled crystal-liquid mushes versus later remobilisation — remains the field's liveliest debate after a century of study). **Fluid exsolution and late-magmatic brines** (the water that a hydrous granite or carbonatite exsolves at 2–5 km depth concentrates Sn, W, Li, F, Be and Nb into greisen and pegmatite (the Pilbara and Zhejiang LCT pegmatites, the Cinovec and Zinnwald W-Sn greisens), and the immiscible salt-rich brines of the peralkaline and topaz-bearing granites make the Yichun and Kangarkun Rb-Cs-Li fields). The tectonic control is the exploration map: arc and post-collisional settings make porphyry and epithermal; cratonic and LIP settings make magmatic sulfide and layered intrusion; rift and craton-margin settings make carbonatite and alkaline complexes — the reason the global endowment is *clustered in time and space* (the metallogenic-epoch concept of the 1970s–80s: 2.7, 1.9, 0.5 Ga komatiite-Ni and greenstone-gold epochs, the 2.06 Ga Bushveld event, the 55–25 Ma Andean porphyry pulse) rather than uniformly distributed.
:::

::: definition Porphyry systems: the planet's copper, molybdenum and (some) gold factories {#def-porphyry}
The porphyry copper system is the best-quantified ore system on Earth and the workhorse of the industrial-metal supply: ~60 % of the world's copper and ~90 % of its molybdenum come from porphyry and porphyry-related deposits (the USGS and Sillitoe syntheses), including every top-ten copper mine. The archetype (Bingham, Butte, El Teniente, Escondida): a 1–3 km-diameter cupola of hydrous, oxidised, intermediate-to-felsic magma (the "porphyry" stock, emplaced at 2–6 km depth beneath a stratovolcano) crystallises and exsolves a Cl- and S-bearing brine (200–600 °C, 1–20 wt % salt, the halite-bearing fluid inclusions of early quartz veins); metal is carried as chloride complexes (CuCl₂⁻, MoO₄²⁻) and dropped where the fluid cools, boils or is diluted by meteoric water, producing the diagnostic **alteration zonation** read on every exploration project — potassic core (K-feldspar-biotite, the Cu core), intermediate argillic and phyllic (sericite-quartz, the "shell" where much of the ore sits), advanced argillic and propylitic outward (the zonation is a fluid-flow map, and the 1960s–70s discovery that the alteration shells *precede and outsize* the ore made it the vector to the buried stock). The genetic requirements explain the tectonic clustering: slab-derived, water-rich, *oxidised* magmas (high fO₂ keeps sulfur as sulfate so that Cu stays mobile; reduced magmas pre-saturate sulfide and lose Cu early — the 2010–2020s "slab oxidation" and arc-magma-fertility literature), thick-but-not-too-thick crust (the 2019–2023 "porphyry fertility" work on arc crustal thickness: 30–50 km favours adakite-rich, slab-melting regimes), and first-order crustal structures (the fault systems that focus both magma and fluid — the Atacama fault zone's role in the Escondida district). The family's economics are the resources lesson's grade-tonnage trade made physical: 0.3–0.6 % Cu over 0.5–5 Gt (a single Escondida orebody contains more copper than all of Australia's historical production), mined by open pit or block cave at throughputs of 400,000 t/day. The related families: **epithermal** veins and breccias (the 100–300 °C, <1.5 km distal expression — low-sulphidation adularia-sericite Au-Ag deposits like Tonopah and the Hauraki Plains, made by boiling and fluid mixing in the shallow crust; high-sulphidation advanced-argillic deposits like La Coipa), **porphyry gold** (the high-fO₂, high-Cl, lower-Cu/Au systems of Lihir, Cadia and the Mocoa belt), and the **IOCG** family (below) whose boundary with porphyry and with epithermal systems is the field's most productive argument.
:::

::: definition Hydrothermal ore systems: fluids, sources and traps {#def-hydrothermal}
Hydrothermal deposits are made by aqueous fluids (100–600 °C, from a few wt % to >40 wt % salt) that leach metal from rock, carry it as chloride, bisulfide or fluoride complexes, and precipitate it at a trap — and the discipline's core method is *fluid forensics*: fluid-inclusion microthermometry (homogenisation temperature, ice- and halite-hydrohalite melting give T and salinity), stable isotopes (δD and δ¹⁸O of vein quartz and sulfates distinguish magmatic water (δD ~ −20 to −60, δ¹⁸O ~ +5 to +10) from meteoric (δD < −80 in most terranes), metamorphic dewatering (δD ~ −20 to −60, δ¹⁸O shifted by the host) and seawater (δ¹⁸O ~ 0, radiogenic Sr)), and radiogenic tracers (Sr-Nd-Pb-S-Hg of the sulfides themselves). The fluid-source taxonomy organises the families: **magmatic-hydrothermal** (porphyry, greisen, tin skarn, the high-sulphidation end — fluids whose water and sulfur are dominantly magma-derived); **metamorphic** (the orogenic gold and lode systems: dewatering of greenschist-facies metasediments and greenstones at 300–450 °C, Au-As-Sb-W fluids focused by deep crustal faults (the 2019 Hagemann and colleagues review; the 2019 *Nature Geoscience* core-source counterpoint)); **basinal brine** (the Mississippi-Valley-type Pb-Zn of the Ozark and Appalachian mines and the Irish carbonate-hosted field: compaction- and topography-driven migration of basin brines at 50–200 °C, ore dropped where basement faults meet oxidised carbonate, the reduced sulfur supplied by thermochemical sulfate reduction by basin hydrocarbons — the "MVT machine" that made the Ozark and Appalachian camps, with the 2020s Irish carbonate-hosted revisions); **metasomatic and replacement** (skarns at granite-carbonate contacts — the Cu-W-Mo skarns of the Great Basin and the Altun; the carbonate-replacement Pb-Zn of Biseberg and the Upper Silesian field); and the **submarine** families (below). The trap physics is a short list with infinite combinations: boiling (the epithermal and orogenic-gold precipitation mechanism — phase separation strips H₂S and CO₂ and drops gold as a sulfide or native metal), fluid mixing (ore-grade barite and fluorite mark the mixing fronts of MVT and epithermal systems), redox fronts (the uranium roll fronts of the Wyoming Basin and Athabasca — the redox trap of [[earth-resources/sedimentary-ores]] in a hydrothermal costume), wall-rock reaction (the desilicification and sulfidation that make the fault rocks of the Betze-Post and Plutonic mines), and simple permeability contrast (the ore shelled beneath a seal, the same stratigraphy that makes a petroleum trap — the reason "geology is a trap science" is the exploration slogan).
:::

::: definition Submarine and iron-oxide families: VMS, SEDEX and IOCG {#def-submarine-iocg}
**VMS (volcanogenic massive sulfide)**: seafloor hydrothermal circulation around active and extinct spreading- and arc-segment volcanics leaches Cu-Zn-(Au-Ag) from fresh basalt and drops it as polymetallic massive pyrite-chalcopyrite-sphalerite mounds and stockworks at the vent (the modern analogue: the 350–400 °C black smokers of TAG and Endeavour, whose chimneys are 10⁴–10⁵-year features) and as distal sedimentary exhalites (barite, jasper, manganese). The ancient record — Kuroko (Japan's 1970s type example), Kidd Creek's 1.5 Gt zinc-rich pile, the Flin Flon and Cyprus ophiolite camps — supplies ~15 % of Cu and a major share of Zn, and the exploration logic is basin-analysis: volcanic lithofacies, footwall alteration pipes, exhalite plumes, and the 2000s "VMS-fertility" work on arc extension rates. **SEDEX (sedimentary exhalite)**: the same vent machine run on basin brines rather than magmatic heat, dropping Pb-Zn-Ag(-Ba-F) into closed or starved basins as laminated sulfide shales and mound breccias — the giant Paleoproterozoic Sullivan (165 Mt at 9 % Zn-Pb), the Broken Hill (whose 1917 discovery and 25 Mt of high-grade Pb-Zn-Ag remain the family's prize and whose metamorphic transposition still resists a consensus model), the Century and Mount Isa systems, and Alaska's Red Dog (60+ Mt at ~16 % Zn, discovered 1968 and mined since 1989 as the world's largest single zinc producer). **IOCG (iron oxide-copper-gold)**: the 1990s-named family (the 1992 Hitzman proposal and the 1995 Barton iron-oxide(-Au-U) synthesis) of breccia-hosted and replacement Cu-Au(-U-REE) ores with magnetite-hematite gangue, no obvious magmatic heat source required, and metals probably from mixed basinal, metamorphic and magmatic-brine sources — Olympic Dam, Candelaria, the Carajás iron-oxide belt, and the Prominent Hill and Carré-domain prospects of Australia's Gawler craton. IOCG is simultaneously the field's most successful exploration model (it found Prominent Hill and the Kintyre district) and its most contested concept (the 2010s–2020s "is IOCG a real family or a repository?" debate), and its 2020s importance is cobalt and rare earths: the iron-oxide systems of the Great Bear and the Mesoproterozoic belts are now on every critical-minerals prospective map.
:::

::: example Bushveld: the largest ore system in history {#example-bushveld}
The 2.06 Ga Bushveld Large Igneous Province (the 2.06 Ga event, the 370,000 km² of eroded intrusion and overlying Transvaal Supergroup) is the planet's reference layered intrusion and the case study in magmatic concentration at planetary scale: a ~9 km stack of mafic cumulates (the Critical, Main and Merensky zones) that hosts ~70 % of the world's platinum-group metal endowment (the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries: the UG2 and Merensky chromitite-peggmatoid reefs, mined at 0.5–2 m thickness and 4–10 g/t PGE grade, plus the Platreef contact-style mineralisation), the world's largest chromium resource (the 2023 USGS: Bushveld holds the majority of the global ~570 Mt chromite reserve base), and the world's largest zirconium, vanadium (the magnetite layers: 100+ Mt V₂O₅) and one of its largest hafnium and scandium concentrations. The genetic debates are the science: the Merensky and UG2 "reefs" (settled crystal-liquid slurries at sulfide-saturation pulses, the 1970s–2020s literature, versus later fluid remobilisation of PGE within the chromitite — the 2011–2023 platinum-group mineralogy studies), the source of the anomalously high PGE and Cr fluxes (the 2013–2024 isotopic work on deep-mantle plume versus recycled-arc components), and the 2020s "Bushveld as a critical-minerals province" reassessment (the 2023 *Ore Geology Reviews* surveys of the intrusion's Sc, Hf, V and rare-earth potential, and the debate over deep-level mining's water impacts on the dolomite aquifers that supply Gauteng). The mining reality is equally instructive: 120 years of mining the shallow reefs has driven depths past 2 km (the 2010s rockburst seismicity, the 2020s mechanisation-versus-narrow-reef stoping argument), and South Africa's PGE production decline since 2015 (the 2022–25 strikes, power crisis and shaft closures) is the supply-risk case study for a transition metal (PGEs are ~90 % used in catalytic converters — the 2020s EV-share erosion of that demand is simultaneously the province's biggest price risk and the reason the 2023–25 PGE exploration revival targets the *underexplored eastern limb and northern limb* rather than the known reefs).
::: solution
Bushveld is the magmatic-processes lesson at full scale: a single plume event whose main intrusion stage 2021–23 high-precision geochronology constrains to ~1 Myr of pulses — built a crystal-settling factory that delivered Cr, V, PGE, Zr and Hf in mineable layers, and the same event's erosion and cover explain why 70 % of the PGE sits in one 100 × 300 km ellipse. The deposit's 2020s story — deep mines, water conflicts, strike-prone labour, and a demand base (diesel catalysts) that the EV transition is eroding even as hydrogen-economy electrolyser and fuel-cell PGE demand is born — is the resources lesson's "quality and pace, not quantity" thesis in one province: the endowment is effectively untouchable in scale, and the binding constraints are depth, water, power and politics.
:::
:::

::: example Olympic Dam and the IOCG question {#example-olympic-dam}
Olympic Dam (South Australia, discovered 1975 from a geophysical anomaly over 150 m of cover — the Gawler craton's exploration geophysics case study) is the world's largest uranium and silver resource and among its largest copper resources: 3.8 Gt at 1.1 % Cu, 0.3 % U₃O₈ and 13 g/t Ag (the 2024 Artemis and BHP mineral-resource statements), hosted in a ~10 km hematite-rich breccia complex at the top of a 1.6 Ga granite. The deposit is the IOCG family's type example and its central puzzle: the metals are in hematite-magnetite-chalcocite-chalcopyrite breccia and replacement bodies with no porphyry at depth (the 2010s–2020s deep drilling and magnetotellurics: no fertile magma body within several kilometres), the ore-forming fluids were a mixed brine (the isotope work: basinal + magmatic + metamorphic components at 250–400 °C), and the event is now dated to ~1.45 Ga (the 2007–2024 geochronology: the breccia and mineralisation postdate the 1.59 Ga granite by 150 Myr — the "long-lived hydrothermal system" versus "short event" debate). The mining history is the resources lesson's development-pipeline case: the 2012 abandonment of the proposed $30 bn underground expansion (500 kt/yr copper and 3,500 t/yr uranium), the 2016–19 uranium-only interim plan, and the 2021–25 studies and approvals process for the smaller underground design that will succeed the 1988 open pit in the 2030s, the 2019–2024 nuclear-demand revival that re-rated its uranium, and the 2023–25 realisation that its silver (the world's largest single silver resource) and its cobalt and tellurium credits make it a critical-minerals asset in four commodities at once.
::: solution
Olympic Dam's lesson is the deposit-model one: the IOCG concept (1990s) was built *around* it, and the concept then found the next ones (Prominent Hill, Carra, the Kintyre district) — models are exploration technologies, and their power is that they predict what to look for *and where* (cover, geophysics, breccia, hematite). The unresolved fluid source is the honest caveat the field lives with: a deposit can be economically and structurally well-understood and genetically contested after fifty years, which is why the warning below treats deposit models as hypotheses with exploration value, not as settled truths.
:::
:::

::: example Solwara 1 and the deep-sea ore test {#example-solwara}
In 2011–2016 the seafloor 1,600 m below the Bismarck Sea (Papua New Guinea's exclusive economic zone) was mapped, drilled and sampled as the world's first deep-sea mine candidate: Solwara 1, a *modern* active hydrothermal mound (the 2011 pilot ore collection — the first-ever deep-sea retrieval of massive sulfide — and the 2012–16 sampling programme) holding ~1.86 Gt at 1.9 % Cu and 8 g/t Au (the 2016 technical report: 35 Mt Cu and 470 t Au at grades 4–5× the best land porphyries), one of ~25 known massive-sulfide mounds on the Manus back-arc spreading centre, where 100,000-year vent lifetimes (the 2017–2023 Endeavour and TAG age studies) build high-grade chalcopyrite-rich chimneys and talus that are, in VMS terms, "fresh ore" — unoxidised, undeformed, and 100× richer in copper per tonne than anything on land. The 2017–2025 controversy is the resources lesson's externalities case: the 2011 and 2014 environmental-impact disputes (the 2017 *Nature Geoscience* and 2021 *Nature* commentaries on sediment-plume and mid-water ecology, and the 2023–25 biology syntheses showing endemic vent-fauna communities and multi-year recovery timescales at mining scales), the 2021 Nauru "two-year rule" trigger (the 2025 exploitation-regulations crisis at the ISA, the 2023–25 moratorium declarations by 20+ states and the 2024–25 EU and corporate sourcing exclusions), and the 2023–25 legal architecture (the 2024–25 ITLOS advisory-opinion proceedings on state-sponsorship liability and the stalled 2024 negotiation of the ISA draft mining code). The geology is not in dispute: the VMS model (this lesson's submarine family) works on the modern seafloor exactly as it does in the 2.7 Ga greenstones, and the 2020s ROV and drill-ship technology can mine it; what is unresolved is whether the environmental and governance costs of the first mine can be bounded — the same "externalities as the binding constraint" verdict the onshore mines reached a generation earlier, arriving at the seafloor before the seabed's own regulatory system exists.
::: solution
Solwara 1 is the lesson's controlled experiment in the difference between *ore* and *permissible*: the highest-grade copper-gold resource accessible to 2020s technology, in water where no one lives, owned by no one, and still blocked — because the classification system's "economic feasibility" now includes environmental and social criteria whose institutions (the ISA code, the ITLOS liability regime, the corporate sourcing rules) are being written in real time. It also closes the loop on the VMS model: the same fluid system that made Kidd Creek 2.45 Ga ago is making ore *now*, at 350 °C and 250 atm, which is the strongest possible demonstration that deposit models are descriptions of an operating process, not just of a fossil.
:::
:::

::: example Kalgoorlie: orogenic gold and the metamorphic fluid {#example-kalgoorlie}
The Kalgoorlie terrane (Western Australia, the 1893 Paddy Hannan discovery, the Super Pit open cut — a single pit with a reserve base of ~10 Moz at 2–3 g/t (the 2023–25 Mineral Resources statements)) is the type example of the orogenic gold family that supplies ~30 % of mined gold (the 2019 Hagemann and colleagues *Ore Geology Reviews* review: orogenic lode systems from LaRonde and Timmins to Obuasi and the Ashanti belt): gold (with As, Sb, W, Ag, base-metal sulfides in quartz-tourmaline-carbonate veins and replacement bodies) in 1.9–2.7 Ga greenstone belts and adjacent metasediments, deposited at 3–12 km depth from low-salinity, CO₂-rich, metamorphic dewatering fluids (the fluid-inclusion and isotope record: 250–400 °C, δD and δ¹⁸O pointing to devolatilised greenstone and sediment sources) focused by deep crustal-scale faults (the 2019–2024 "fault-system architecture" exploration work: the Kalgoorlie and Kurnalpi structural corridors, the bend-and-jog controls on the Boorara and Hannan's Select orebodies). The genetic history is the field's best-documented paradigm shift: the early-20th-century "magmatic" and "syenite-related" models (the district's own geology service), the 1980s–90s metamorphic-fluid revolution (the 1980s Goldfarb and Groves fluid studies and the 1992 Groves and colleagues "orogenic gold" synthesis and its 2013 and 2019 refinements), and the 2019–2025 core-source controversy (the 2019 *Nature Geoscience* core-partitioning paper and the 2020–2024 isotope rebuttals: whether the metal's ultimate source is the core, the greenstones, or both, with the 2023–25 consensus settling on greenstone-leaching with possible deep components). The 2020s exploration frontier is the "under 500 metres cover" problem (the 2023 *Nature* commentary on the greenfield cover-exploration gap and the 2024–25 deep-drill results below the Kalgoorlie Formation): the district's 120 years of mining found every outcrop expression, and the next 20 Moz, if it exists, is under transported cover that the 1990s–2020s geophysics and regolith-gas surveys are only now learning to see through.
::: solution
Kalgoorlie teaches the fluid-source lesson with a century of hindsight: the ore is a *metamorphic* product (dewatering of a deforming greenstone terrane at 400 °C, focused by the same faults that deformed it), not a magmatic one — the isotope and inclusion data that overturned the district's own founding models — and the same physics now guides exploration from Ghana to Victoria. It also demonstrates the deposit-model life cycle: the orogenic-gold model (1990s) organised the family, drove the 1990s–2010s discoveries (the 2014–24 West African and Victorian discoveries), and is now being stress-tested by the core-source debate and by the cover problem that defines the 2020s exploration frontier.
:::

::: warning Five misconceptions about ore-system science
1. **"Anomaly equals ore."** Geochemical and geophysical anomalies are ubiquitous; ore requires the *conjunction* of source, pathway, trap and preservation, and most anomalous districts never make a mine (the 2020s "provincial fertility" work: a handful of terranes supply most of each metal — enrichment is clustered, and the anomaly is the beginning of the question, not the end).
2. **"Deposit models are proven genetics."** The porphyry, IOCG and orogenic-gold models are *exploration tools* with contested edges (the IOCG-family debate, the Olympic Dam fluid-source argument, the gold-source controversy): they tell you what measurements to make and where analogous systems occur, not what *must* be present, and the field's history is full of models that organised discovery while misdescribing mechanism.
3. **"Hydrothermal means magmatic water."** The isotope record repeatedly finds meteoric, metamorphic-formation and basin-brine waters doing the transport even where magmatism provides heat or structure (the MVT Pb-Zn systems have no igneous rocks at all; the orogenic-gold fluids are metamorphic dewatering; the Olympic Dam brine is mixed) — "hydrothermal" names the fluid's temperature, not its parent.
4. **"Giant deposits are typical."** Deposit-size distributions are heavy-tailed (the Pareto statistics of the 1970s–2020s: the largest 1 % of copper deposits hold ~80 % of the metal), and the giants cluster in specific tectonic windows (the Andean 55–25 Ma pulse, the 2.7 Ga greenstones, the 1.8–1.6 Ga Gawler) — resource assessment that ignores the clustering systematically misprices both exploration potential and depletion.
5. **"High grade means easy."** Grade is one term in a feasibility equation that includes depth, strip, water, power, infrastructure, metallurgy and social licence: Solwara 1 is the highest-grade copper asset in the western world and cannot be built; Bushveld's reefs are 100× cutoff and mined at 2 km with rockburst support; and the 2020s' "difficult" deposits (Arctic, deep, water-poor, conflict-adjacent) are exactly the ones the transition scenarios require — the definition of "ore" is a mine plan, not a grade.
:::

::: history From lode mining to fluid systems: three centuries of ore-deposit science {#history-ore-deposits}
The questions are older than the science: Agricola's 1556 *De Re Metallica* already argued the veins of the Harz for a hydrothermal (spring-water) origin against the volcanic and in-situ-transformation schools, and the 18th–19th-century Neptunist-Plutonist and later "metasomatic versus replacement" debates (the 1880s–1910s Van Hise and Emmons campaign around the Bingham and Leadville systems) established that ore minerals replace wall rock in zonal paragenetic sequences. The 20th century supplied the mechanisms: the 1900s–1930s paragenetic and temperature-zoning tradition (the 1933 Lindgren classification of hypothermal-mesothermal-epithermal deposits, the Becke and Lindgren vein zonation studies), the 1920s–50s experimental sulfide solubility chemistry (the 1960s Barton and Toulmin sulfide-stability work) that made hydrothermal transport thermodynamically quantitative, and the 1960s–70s plate-tectonic revolution of metallogeny (the 1968–72 Sillitoe and the 1973 Mitchell-Garson models tying porphyry copper and VMS to arcs and spreading centres — the moment economic geology became a process science; the 1967 USGS porphyry-copper symposium). The discovery record drove the models: the 1900s–10s bulk-tonnage transition at Bingham and Butte, the 1966 Kambalda nickel (Riddell's discovery in the komatiite terrane), the 1960 Ertsberg and 1988 Grasberg strike-area discoveries (the largest single copper resource), the 1975 Olympic Dam geophysical-anomaly discovery, the 1970s–80s Super Pit consolidation, and the 1968 Red Dog discovery under shale, developed from 1989. The fluid-inclusion and isotope revolution (the 1960s–70s Roedder inclusion microthermometry, the 1970s–80s Taylor-Sheppard-O'Neil stable-isotope mapping of ore fluids) converted the 19th-century fluid intuitions into measurement, and the 1990s–2020s era has been synthetic and industrial: the 1992 orogenic-gold synthesis, the 1990s IOCG debates, the 2000s–2020s numerical modelling of porphyry and basin-fluid systems (the 2010s–2020s coupled thermal-hydrological-mechanical models of ore formation), the 2010s–2020s "mineral systems" prospectivity-mapping and machine-learning exploration (the 2019–2025 BHP and CSIRO "discovery engines" programmes), and the 2016–2025 deep-sea test in which the oldest model in the book — the VMS system — became the newest frontier and its governance the field's open question.
:::

::: quiz
The best evidence that an ore fluid was metamorphic rather than magmatic in origin is:
- [ ] The presence of quartz and sulfide minerals
- [x] Fluid inclusions of low salinity and CO₂-rich composition with H- and O-isotope signatures matching devolatilised host rocks, in a terrane with no nearby intrusion
- [ ] High homogenisation temperatures above 500 °C
- [ ] Ore veins oriented along faults
::: solution
**The isotope-and-inclusion fingerprint**: metamorphic dewatering fluids are typically low-salinity, CO₂-bearing, 250–450 °C, and carry the H-O-Sr isotopic memory of the rocks being metamorphosed — the orogenic-gold signature. Fault orientation and quartz-sulfide mineralogy are necessary but not diagnostic (magmatic fluids use faults too), and >500 °C points toward magmatic inputs.

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="4"}
A porphyry copper deposit contains 800 Mt of ore averaging 0.5 % Cu. How much contained copper metal, in Mt?
::: solution
800 × 0.005 = 4 Mt of contained copper — the tonnage-times-grade arithmetic of the bulk-tonnage families, and roughly the contained-copper size of a top-ten global deposit.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="10"}
A quartz vein system averages 5 g/t gold over 2 Mt of ore. How many tonnes of contained gold?
::: solution
5 g/t × 2 × 10⁶ t = 10 × 10⁶ g = 10 t of gold (≈ 320 koz) — the vein-system arithmetic: grades 100× a porphyry's, tonnages 1,000× smaller, which is why the two families are mined by different industries.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="100"}
Copper's crustal abundance is ~50 ppm. What enrichment factor does a 0.5 % (5,000 ppm) porphyry ore represent?
::: solution
5,000/50 = 100× — and the enrichment must be *even larger* relative to the rock's leachable fraction; ore is what geology concentrates, and the concentration process (melt, fluid, or both) is what the geologist maps.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="50"}
A komatiite-hosted nickel ore grades 2 % Ni. How many tonnes of ore are processed per tonne of nickel metal?
::: solution
1/0.02 = 50 t of rock per t of Ni — the physical advantage of magmatic concentration: the sulfide-liquid process did in the magma chamber what 50 tonnes of milling would otherwise have to undo.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="3.2"}
A 1 Gt porphyry orebody averages 0.4 % Cu and the mill recovers 80 %. How many Mt of copper metal are produced from it?
::: solution
1,000 Mt × 0.004 × 0.80 = 3.2 Mt — the recovery factor matters as much as grade, and it is metallurgy (oxide vs. sulfide, chalcopyrite's leach recalcitrance) that decides which half of the contained metal becomes a saleable concentrate.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="41.8"}
Olympic Dam's mineral resource is 3.8 Gt at 1.1 % Cu (2024 statement). How much contained copper, in Mt?
::: solution
3,800 × 0.011 = 41.8 Mt of contained copper — nearly twice the world's 2024 annual mine supply of copper (23 Mt) in one hematite breccia; the scale that makes "resource" and "reserve" diverge over decades of mine-building.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="10"}
Copper's partition coefficient between an immiscible sulfide liquid and a basaltic melt is ~1,000. If the melt contains 100 ppm Cu, what Cu concentration (in %) does the sulfide liquid reach?
::: solution
100 ppm × 1,000 = 100,000 ppm = 10 % Cu in the sulfide liquid — the single calculation behind every magmatic sulfide deposit, from Norilsk to Kambalda: a thousandfold extraction into a few cubic kilometres of liquid.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="400"}
Gold's crustal abundance is ~4 ppb. An orogenic gold camp contains 1,550 t (50 Moz) of gold. If the metal were stripped from crust averaging 4 ppb, how many gigatonnes of crust must have been dewatered and leached?
::: solution
1,550 t ÷ (4 × 10⁻⁹ t/t) = 3.9 × 10¹¹ t ≈ 400 Gt of crust — the "paleoplumbing" arithmetic: a few cubic kilometres of ore required circulating fluid through a small mountain range's worth of rock, which is why the faults, not just the veins, are the exploration target.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="35.3"}
Solwara 1 hosts 1.86 Gt of massive sulfide at 1.9 % Cu. How much contained copper, in Mt?
::: solution
1,860 × 0.019 = 35.3 Mt — comparable to a mid-ranked porphyry at four times the grade, in an active vent system; the number that launched a decade of deep-sea mining controversy and the 2023–25 governance freeze.

::: summary
- Magmatic deposits record concentration during melting and crystallisation: komatiites and mantle plumes deliver Ni and PGE (Kambalda, Norilsk, Bushveld), layered intrusions build settleable layers of chromite, magnetite and apatite, and immiscible sulfide liquids extract chalcophile elements a thousandfold — all tightly controlled by tectonic setting and terrane age.
- Porphyry systems — hydrous, oxidised arc magmas exsolving brines — supply most of the world's copper and molybdenum at 0.3–0.6 % grades over gigatonnes, with alteration zonation as the exploration vector; their epithermal and IOCG relatives extend the family into gold, silver and uranium.
- Hydrothermal deposits are fluid systems read through inclusions and isotopes, and the sources are plural: magmatic water, metamorphic dewatering (orogenic gold — Kalgoorlie), basin brines (MVT and SEDEX Pb-Zn), and seawater (VMS mounds, living on the modern seafloor at Solwara 1) — the trap physics (boiling, mixing, redox, wall-rock reaction) is shared across all of them.
- Deposit models are exploration technologies with contested genetics: the porphyry, IOCG and orogenic-gold concepts have organised a century of discovery (Olympic Dam, Red Dog, the West African camps) while their fluid-source debates remain open — models predict where to look, not what must be there.
- The 2020s frontier is the conjunction of the physical and the permissible: giants under cover and under water (the cover problem, deep-sea vents, 2 km reefs) are limited by depth, water, power, governance and social licence rather than geology — the same lesson the reserves ledger taught, now applied to the ore systems that supply the transition.
:::
