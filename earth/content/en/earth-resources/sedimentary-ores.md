## The surface as a mine: oxygen, water and time as ore-forming agents

Deep geology concentrates metals by melting and boiling; the surface concentrates them by dissolving, oxidising, winnowing and precipitating — and the industrial ledger gives the surface credit for most of it: the aluminium (bauxite), the bulk of the steel's iron (banded-iron-formation-derived ore), much of the nickel (laterites), the manganese, the industrial minerals (potash, phosphate, salt, sulfur), a third of the lithium (brines), and the largest gold deposit ever mined (the Witwatersrand paleoplacers). Sedimentary and surface ore systems are also the ones whose formation records the planet's chemical history most directly: the banded iron formations *are* the Great Oxidation Event written in centimetre couplets, the black shales are the trace-metal sink of the oxygenated ocean, and the uranium unconformity deposits exist only because the atmosphere and the biosphere together made groundwater oxidising. This lesson reads the four surface machines — flowing water (placers), deep weathering (laterites, bauxites, supergene blankets), chemical precipitation (iron formations, manganese, exhalites, evaporites, redox-trapped uranium) — and the oxygen history that switched them on and off, with four case studies (Witwatersrand, Hamersley-Simandou, the Atacama salars, the Athabasca Basin) that between them cover gold, iron, lithium and uranium.

::: definition Placers: hydraulics as a density separator {#def-placers}
A placer is what a river, beach or wind does when it runs the ore-dresser's first step — gravity separation — on kilometres of sediment: dense, hard, chemically durable minerals survive transport and concentrate against a density-and-hydraulic-equivalence law (the same settling logic as [[sedimentology/sediment-transport]]: equal-settling grains scale with the square root of the density ratio, so a quartz grain about three times the diameter of a gold grain behaves like it hydraulically — fine gold travels with sand and drops where the flow drops), while everything else is removed by repeated abrasion and chemical attack (the durability filter: gold, cassiterite, chromite, rutile, zircon, ilmenite, monazite and diamond survive; most else does not). The two families: **terrane placers** (paleo- and active: the Witwatersrand's 2.9–2.8 Ga fluvial-lacustrine conglomerates — the "reefs" — which have yielded ~2.7 billion ounces of gold, roughly a third of all gold ever mined on Earth, from a deposit whose origin is still contested (the 2015–2016 Barnicoat and McDonald *Nature Geoscience* exchange: a hydrothermal source for the gold and the iconic "ball-and-bar" pyrite nodules versus the classic fluvial-biochemical model; the 2020s syntheses settle on a hybrid: a fluvial placer of Au- and pyrite-rich sediment, later modified by basin fluids); the 2020s placer exploration frontier is the "buried channel" geometry — paleo-drainage mapping under sand and basalt). **Beach and offshore placers** (the modern heavy-mineral-sand industry: the Australian and South African strandlines supplying the world's ilmenite, rutile, leucoxene and zircon — the 2023–25 Australian dominance of Ti-mineral feedstock (ilmenite plus rutile, ~90 % of the titanium chain's first step), the Indonesian and Chinese tin from offshore dredging (the 2019–2023 Bangka-Belitung environmental audit literature), the Namibian and Namib diamond gravels, and the monazite rare-earth placers of the Kerala and Brazilian coasts). The economics follow the geometry: placers are the only mines that process *unlithified* sediment (dredges and jigs, no crushing or grinding — the energy per unit metal is the lowest in mining, which is why tin and titanium concentrate where placers exist), and their grades are the resources lesson's inverse grade-tonnage rule in its most extreme form: a 2 % ilmenite beach sand is economic because the "mill" is a floating spiral classifier, while a 0.2 % paleoplacer reef justified 4 km of shaft.
:::

::: definition Weathering deposits: laterite, bauxite, supergene blankets {#def-weathering}
Deep chemical weathering in tropical, humid, slowly-eroding terrains strips silica and bases from rock and leaves the insoluble residue concentrated — a natural beneficiation operating for millions of years at ambient temperature. The three industrial products: **bauxite** (the Al residue of intensely weathered basalt or karstified limestone: gibbsite-boehmite-diaspore with iron oxides, the tropics' gift — 90 % of the world's aluminium starts as bauxite mined at strip ratios near zero in Jamaica, Guinea (the 2019–2024 Boke district surge that made Guinea the second producer), and Queensland; the 2023–25 "laterite-aluminium" frontier is the Amazon and Guiana bauxites' deforestation conflicts and the 2024–25 "bauxite residue" (red mud) utilisation research), **nickel laterite** (the weathered mantle over komatiite and peridotite: a limonite-haematite cap, a smectite "clay silicate" horizon and a saprolite core carrying the Ni-Mg silicate ore (0.8–2.5 % Ni), now ~40 % of world nickel production and the fastest-growing supply source (the 2020s Indonesian laterite-HPAL and RKEF boom that made Indonesia the top producer and triggered the 2023–25 nickel price collapse; the 2023–25 Philippine ore-export and laterite-mining moratorium debates; the high-pressure acid leach (HPAL) plants' 2020s record — half of the first decade's plants struggled, the 2021–24 Indonesian wave succeeded — the engineering lesson that laterite's cheap mining hides a brutal pyrometallurgical or acid cost), and **supergene enrichment** (the descending water table's oxidation front that strips copper from primary chalcopyrite and re-deposits it as chalcocite-covellite-blanket grades 2–10× the primary ore — the source of the "oxide and secondary-sulfide" ores that made the Southwest US, Zambia and Atacama mines profitable for a century, now largely mined out (the 2020s "supergene famine": the primary-sulfide leach-recalcitrance problem behind copper's 2023–25 declining recoveries), and the 2020s exploration twist: the "blind" and "relic" supergene blankets under desert cover, the 2020s work on arid-landscape preservation windows that make the Atacama's late-Miocene wet event the reason the giant northern-Chile blankets exist at all). The residual family's close relatives: the supergene manganese caps over carbonates (the Groote Eylandt and Gabon high-grade ores), the residual kaolin and refractory clays, the lateritic gold and diamond caps (the "iron cap" indicators), and the 2020s "critical-metal laterites" (the Sc-Co-Ni laterites of Cuba and the Philippines, the 2023–25 New Caledonia and Madagascar cobalt-laterite proposals).
:::

::: definition Chemical sedimentary deposits: iron formations, manganese, evaporites, redox-trapped metals {#def-chemical-sedimentary}
When lake and sea water react with the atmosphere, rivers, hydrothermal vents or the salt itself, the water column precipitates ore layers that can be traced for hundreds of kilometres. **Banded iron formation (BIF)**: alternating iron silicate/oxide (greenalite-magnetite-haematite) and silica layers deposited 3.8–0.7 Ga with a 2.7–1.8 Ga peak — the source of ~90 % of mined iron (the Hamersley, Pilbara, Superior, Labrador and Krivoy Rog ranges; the "natural" 25–35 % Fe ores are beneficiated or, where metamorphosed and decarbonated, mined as 60–67 % direct-shipping ore, the 2020s "green-steel premium" product). The depositional mechanism is the Precambrian oxygen pump: anoxic, iron-rich deep waters (the "ferruginous ocean") upwelled onto shallow shelves where local oxygen production or evaporation dropped the iron as chemical sediment, and the great BIF pulse ended when the Proterozoic ocean's dissolved-iron reservoir collapsed (the 2019–2024 "oceanic iron reservoir and shuttle" synthesis — the 2019 Konhauser and 2023 Swindlehurst and colleagues *Nature Geoscience* and *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* papers — which replaced the simple "oxygen appeared, iron precipitated" story with a redox-shuttle mass-balance in which BIFs are the sink term of an anoxic ocean's iron budget, and their end is the proof the reservoir ran down). **Manganese formations and caps**: the same redox logic at higher Eh (Mn²⁺ oxidises only where oxygen or microbes push the water column above +0.4 V): the 2.2 Ga Kalahari field (the world's land Mn reserve, ~80 %), the 1.9 Ga Griqualand West and the 2020s' deep-sea nodule and crust resource (the polymetallic-nodule question the Solwara 1 lesson met from the copper side). **Evaporites**: seawater or lacustrine brine evaporated past halite saturation in restricted basins, precipitating the solubility sequence (carbonate → gypsum → halite → potash salts: the Devonian Prairie Evaporite's 200+ m sylvite-kainite potash fairway that makes Saskatchewan ~30 % of world K₂O, the Permian Delaware and Zechstein salts, the 2022 potash price shock (the Belarus-Russia sanctions and the 2022–25 food-fertiliser geopolitics), the Searles Lake and Turkish-Karoo soda-borate deposits, and the Atacama and Puna salars' lithium-potash-caesium-boron brines (below). **Redox-trapped metals in permeable sandstones**: oxidised ("roll-front") uranium, vanadium-selenium-molybdenum in carbonaceous shales (the Muskox and Petrol-type deposits), and copper-cobalt in the reductant-rich basal conglomerates (the Central African Copperbelt's sedimentary-hosted stratabound ores, source of about half the world's cobalt and a rising share of its copper, whose ore shoots sit exactly at the redox front between the oxidised aquifer above and the reductant facies below — the same trap geometry as the uranium rolls, 2 Gyr apart). **Unconformity-related uranium**: the highest-grade uranium deposits on Earth (the Athabasca Basin below), made when oxidised, chloride-rich basin brines were reduced at graphitic basement unconformities ~1.7–0.9 Ga.
:::

::: definition Oxygen history as metallogeny: the GOE, the NOE and the redox stack {#def-oxygen-metallogeny}
The distribution of sedimentary ores through deep time is a record of the atmosphere-ocean redox ladder (the paleoredox proxies literature of [[earth-history/oxygen-proterozoic]] applied to ore): **the Archean (>2.45 Ga)** — anoxic atmosphere, ferruginous oceans, no oxidative weathering (so no roll-front uranium, no supergene copper, no bauxite), but the BIF pulse, the Witwatersrand placers and the black-shale Mo-Zn sinks; **the Great Oxidation Event (2.5–2.1 Ga)** — the sulfur-isotope and chromium-isotope evidence for a permanent atmospheric-oxygen threshold (the 2011–2024 S-MIF and Cr-isotope records: the 2011 Luo and colleagues sulfur-isotope synthesis and the 2023–25 high-precision geochronology placing the GOE's main step at ~2.43–2.40 Ga), which switched on oxidative weathering (the first red-beds, the first roll-type uranium precursors, the 2.2 Ga Kalahari manganese — the first large Mn formation, right at the step), and switched off the BIF pump over the following ~300 Myr; **the "boring billion" (1.8–0.8 Ga)** — a partially oxygenated ocean with a persistent ferruginous deep reservoir (the 2010s–2020s Fe-speciation and Mo-isotope records), slow weathering, and the great Cu (Copperbelt and the Mesoproterozoic belts) and uranium (Athabasca, 1.7–0.9 Ga events) deposits whose formation needed the *intermediate* redox states that only a partially oxygenated world provides; **the Neoproterozoic-Cambrian step-up (0.8–0.5 Ga)** — the "second oxygenation" (the 2010–2024 Ce-anomaly, Mo and U isotope records and the 2023–25 "oxygen and animal origins" debate), which produced the first oxidative weathering profile of the Cambrian (the first "modern" paleosols), the first major Mn platform deposits, and the redox-window metallogeny of the Paleozoic (the 2022–24 "black shale critical metals" wave: the Ordovician and Devonian shales' V-Ni-Mo-Zn-U enrichments as the sink facies of the newly oxygenated deep shelves — the 2023 *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* assessment that black shales are a genuine multi-metal resource now that surface mines are uneconomic). The practical consequence for resources: **ore systems are time-structured** — you cannot search for a roll-front uranium deposit in an Archean basin or a banded iron formation in a Devonian one, and the exploration target list for each metal is a map of Earth's oxygen history.
:::

::: example The Witwatersrand: the largest gold deposit ever mined and its unresolved origin {#example-witwatersrand}
The 2.9–2.8 Ga Central Rand Group conglomerates of the Witwatersrand basin (the 1886 discovery on the Main Reef, the 2024 production still exceeding 2 Moz/yr from mines 3–4.5 km deep — the 2023–25 "6000" deep-drilling project's 5 km rock-temperature and seismicity engineering) have yielded ~2.7 billion ounces of gold — more than any other district, from a sedimentary rock. The classic model (a fluvial-lacustrine delta system, the "reefs" as paleoplacers, the iconic pyrite "balls" as syngenetic lake chemistry) was challenged by the 2015 *Nature Geoscience* paper (Barnicoat and colleagues) arguing the gold, uraninite and pyrite nodules were hydrothermal and the conglomerates merely the permeable host — a claim the 2016 McDonald and colleagues reply and the 2019–2024 mineralogical work (the 2023–25 *Mineralium Deposita* and *Economic Geology* studies of the reef pyrite's sedimentary zoning) largely rebalanced toward a hybrid: a sedimentary concentration of Au- and pyrite-rich material from the Archean hinterland, later remobilised and reprecipitated by basinal fluids during the 2.0 Ga Ventersdorp and 1.0 Ga Kalahari events. The mining present is the resources lesson's "quality, not quantity" theme: the district's ore grade has fallen from ~15 g/t in the 1930s to ~8 g/t (the 2023–25 average of the deep level and the 2024 Sibanye-Stillwater and Harmony reserve statements), the mines are the deepest on Earth (4.5 km, 60 °C rock, 200-person-minute ventilation logistics, the 2022–24 rockburst fatalities and the mechanisation-versus-shaft-reef debate), and the 2020s "tailings re-treatment" industry (the 2023–25 estimates of the South African tailings (the Mine Waste Solutions and BCRS re-treatment programmes): the dumps hold 40–60 Moz of gold and an acid-mine-drainage liability that haunts the basin) is literally a second mining of the first mine's waste — the circular-economy case study the critical-minerals lesson keeps citing.
::: solution
The Witwatersrand is the sedimentary-ores lesson in one basin: a placer (the hydraulics), a chemical sink (the pyrite), a hydrothermal overprint (the fluids), a 3.5-billion-year preservation miracle (the basin's burial and the Vredefort and Ventersdorp events that did *not* destroy it), and a modern resource whose remaining value depends on depth, labour, electricity and the re-mining of waste. The unresolved origin debate is not academic: the placer model predicts gold along paleochannels (the 2020s "West Witbank deep" exploration), the hydrothermal model predicts it along basin-scale faults — and the district's next 100 Moz depends on which prediction the drill follows.
:::
:::

::: example Hamersley to Simandou: iron ore's quality economy {#example-iron-ore}
The Hamersley Range's 2.7–2.4 Ga BIF (the Marra Mamba and Brockman iron formations, the Pilbara and Midwest regions — the 2023–25 BHP, Rio Tinto and Fortescue operations: ~900 Mt/yr of the world's ~2.6 Gt seaborne iron ore) is the metamorphosed, high-grade end of the BIF story: the tectonite-hematite "pinnacle" ores (the 2020s mining of the "hard-brock" and "channel iron" ores at 60–67 % Fe with near-zero alumina) are the steel industry's premium feed, and the 2021–2024 price history (the 2021 $200/t spike, the 2022–23 China-property slump to $80–100, the 2024–25 "green premium" for high-grade ore as the DRI-hydrogen-steel pilots — the 2023–25 H2 Green Steel and HYBRIT feedstock specifications — require ≥65 % Fe that most low-grade Pilbara and Chinese blends cannot meet) re-opened the quality question the 2010s bulk-supercycle had buried. Simandou (Guinea — the 2010–2024 Rio Tinto–Chalco–Simfer saga, the 2024-11 first shipment from Blocks 1/2 (the Winning Consortium) and the 2025–26 ramp: the "world's best untapped iron ore district" at 55–67 % natural hematite (the 2024 statements: ~2.4 Gt at 65 % for the developed blocks, and the 2025 analyses projecting Simandou adds 10 % of seaborne supply)) is the quality play for the hydrogen-steel transition, and its 670 km rail and port build (the 2019–2024 Simandou infrastructure consortium, the Guinea corridor's "resource-for-infrastructure" finance model) is the 2020s case study in whether a new giant can be built at all in the decade of steel's demand peak (the 2024 "China steel demand peaked in 2020 at 2.1 Gt" consensus, against the 2024–25 India build-out). The low-grade end is the other half of the story: the 25–35 % "blue dust" and taconite ores (the Minnesota Mesabi's 2020s pellet plants, the 2023–25 "concentrate oversupply" as China's DRI build-outs out-pelletise) require wet grinding, magnetic separation and sintering — 5–10× the processing energy per tonne of Fe — and the 2024–25 "green steel premium" is the first market mechanism that prices the difference, which is the quality lesson the resources lesson's grade-decline curve predicted.
::: solution
Iron ore teaches that "abundance" and "ore" diverge by redox history and by market quality: the BIF event delivered 10¹³ t of iron, but only the structurally upgraded, weathered, hematite-rich windows are mineable at 65 % without beneficiation, and the 2020s steel transition re-prices exactly that difference. Simandou's simultaneous "largest iron project in history" and "bet on a shrinking market" framing is the resources lesson's pace-versus-quantity paradox in mineral form: the world does not need more iron; it needs *high-grade* iron at the moment its hydrogen-steel fleet arrives — a timing and quality problem that the tonnage statistics cannot express.
:::
:::

::: example The Atacama salars: lithium, evaporation and water politics {#example-salars}
The Puna-Atacama salar belt (the "lithium triangle" of Chile, Argentina and Bolivia) supplies ~30 % of the world's lithium (the 2023–25 USGS and IEA summaries: the Salar de Atacama's SQM and Albemarle operations at 0.35 % Li brine — the highest-grade exploited lithium resource on Earth — and the 2021–2022 price spike that took battery-grade carbonate from $7/kg to $80/kg and the 2023–24 collapse back through −80 %), and its production process is the chemical-sedimentary lesson in industrial form: Holocene brines, concentrated by evaporation in the closed basins of the hyperarid Altiplano (the lithium's ultimate source: the 10–40 Ma volcanic fields (the 2023–25 isotope work tracing the lithium to the young volcanic fields)), are pumped into 100–3,000 ha solar ponds where 18 months of evaporation (the concentration-factor arithmetic: 25× for Li, 100× before salt precipitation forces the split) drop halite, then potassium salts, then the lithium concentrate that travels to coastal converters. The 2023–25 controversies are the sustainability case study: the hydrology question (the 2021–2024 studies of the Atacama's brine-table decline — tens of metres in the production areas, the 2023–24 hydrological papers on the brine-surface interaction and the flamingo-lagoon salinity shifts), the "extraction-to-resource ratio" debate (the 2024 CORFO technical audits: the salars' brine resources are enormous (the Salar de Uyuni's 9+ Mt Li — the world's largest single lithium resource) but their *extraction ratios* (Mg/Li ratios from ~6 in Atacama to >1,000 in many salars, the evaporation losses, the 30–50 % recoveries) make resource size a poor guide to supply), the 2023 Chile National Lithium Strategy (the 2024 SQM-Codelco joint venture and the 2024–25 "lithium socialism" litigation), and the 2024–25 direct-lithium-extraction (DLE) wave (the 2023–25 pilot-to-plant scale-ups in Argentina and Nevada: the claim that DLE doubles recoveries and ends pond evaporation — the 2025 verdict still pending on whether it is a revolution or a costlier flowsheet).
::: solution
The salars are the lesson's chemical-sedimentary system under a microscope: the ore was made by climate (evaporation exceeding recharge by 100 mm/yr for 10 Myr), is mined at a rate the aquifer's recharge cannot match (the "fossil brine" question — the 2023–25 isotope dating of Atacama brines: residence times of 1,000–10,000 years, i.e. mining is groundwater *depletion*, not renewable pumping), and its economics are set by chemistry (Mg/Li ratio, boron and beryllium impurities) more than by tonnage. The 2021–24 price cycle proved the point twice: the 2022 spike was a *conversion-capacity* shortage (the mines and ponds were fine; the refineries were not), and the 2023–24 collapse was a supply response to a demand forecast — the volatility that makes "the salars hold 50 million tonnes" (they do) irrelevant to whether a given battery plant gets its carbonate in 2027.
:::
:::

::: example The Athabasca Basin: the world's highest-grade uranium, twice made {#example-athabasca}
The Athabasca Basin (Saskatchewan — the 1968 "Ansell" occurrence and the 1988 "Palaeochannel" discovery that opened the Cigar Lake and McArthur River district) hosts the world's highest-grade uranium deposits (Cigar Lake: 2.4 % U₃O₈ average — ~10,000× crustal abundance; McArthur River: the highest-grade uranium mine ever built, at ~10 % average grade), formed over two superposed events: the 1.7 Ga Athabasca Group sandstone deposited on 2.0–1.8 Ga metamorphic basement, and the ore stage itself (the 2023–25 high-precision geochronology: early REE-metasomatism at ~1.6–1.55 Ga and the main ore event at ~1.05–0.95 Ga) when oxidised, chloride-rich brines (the 2020s fluid-inclusion work: basin brines, not metamorphic waters) migrated along basement faults, were reduced at graphitic shear zones, and dropped pitchblende and coffinite in breccias a few metres across. The mining history is the resources lesson's boom-bust-engineering trilogy: the 2006–2007 discovery-driven price spike (the $140/lb 2007 peak that licensed the mines), the 2008 Cigar Lake water inrush (the 2008–2015 freeze-wall engineering — the largest artificial ground-freezing project ever attempted — and the 2024–25 production ramp to the 18 Mlb/yr nameplate), the 2011 Fukushima and 2018–2020 price trough (the 2020 sub-$25/lb bottom that idled McArthur River and Elliot Lake), and the 2021–2025 "uranium is back" restart wave (the 2024 term prices above $80/lb, the 2024 Cameco restart approvals, the 2025 "uranium supply deficit" analyses and the SMR-demand literature) that makes the basin, again, the West's uranium anchor (the 2024: Athabasca supplies ~20 % of world uranium from two mines).
::: solution
The Athabasca is the redox-trap lesson at its purest: uranium is mobile only in oxidised, chloride- or carbonate-rich water and precipitates only when something (graphite and hydrocarbons) reduces it — so the ore sits exactly at the unconformity where basin brines met basement reductants, and the exploration target is a *geochemical boundary surface*, not a rock type. The district's twice-told grade (10,000× crustal) and twice-told history (1.6 Ga alteration, 1.05 Ga ore) also carry the preservation message: the basin's cover is why the ore survived, and the cover is why it took 120 years of drilling under 500 m of sandstone to find it — the "under-cover" exploration frontier the magmatic lesson met at Kalgoorlie, arriving in uranium.
:::

::: warning Five misconceptions about surface and sedimentary ores
1. **"Sedimentary means shallow and cheap."** The Witwatersrand is mined deeper than any mine on Earth; HPAL laterite plants are among the hardest chemical-engineering challenges in mining; and the Athabasca's grades are worthless without freeze-wall and cover-drilling technology. The surface made the ore; the depth to it and the chemistry of it decide the cost.
2. **"BIFs record when iron was abundant."** They record when iron could *precipitate* — the 2019–2024 redox-shuttle synthesis shows the Archean ocean's iron reservoir, not its supply, set the BIF pulse, and the formations ended because the reservoir ran down, not because iron became scarce. Earth-history reading of ore facies is a mass-balance exercise, not a presence-absence one.
3. **"Brine lithium is free because it is dissolved."** Dissolved means *dilute*: Atacama brine is 0.35 % Li, and the 25–100× concentration is done by solar evaporation that takes 18 months, consumes water the desert does not have, and fails outright where Mg/Li exceeds ~6. The hard-rock and brine routes are chemistry trades, not a free lunch.
4. **"Placers are a prospector's art."** The modern heavy-mineral-sand industry is a dredge-and-spiral logistics business supplying most of the titanium chain and a third of the zircon, and the 2020s Indonesian and Namibian cases show its environmental and governance costs are the binding constraints — placer economics is about *processing cheapness* and *extraction politics*, not gold pans.
5. **"Phosphate and potash are just rocks."** They are the planet's nutrient stores with 70 %+ of reserves in single jurisdictions (Morocco-Western Sahara for phosphate; Canada, Belarus, Russia for potash), no substitutable supply, and demand fixed by human food (the 2022 price shocks: potash +200 %, phosphate +300 % at the sanctions-and-food crisis peak) — the strategic-minerals literature's purest case that "criticality" is a function of who owns the deposit, not of crustal abundance.
:::

::: history From iron ranges to oxygen curves: the sedimentary-ore science timeline {#history-sedimentary-ores}
The industrial history begins with the great sedimentary discoveries themselves: the 1844 Marquette and 1848 Mesabi iron ranges (the Mesabi's open-pit transition and the 1900s consolidation that made Minnesota the world's iron district), the 1886 Witwatersrand gold discovery (the 1890s deep-reef cyanidation and the 1900s "Witwatersrand the gold bank" literature), the 1886 Hall-Héroult process and the early-20th-century bauxite industry of Jamaica, Suriname and the Guianas that made aluminium an industry, the 1941 Postmasburg start of the Kalahari manganese field, and the 1925 Hamersley discovery and the 1960s BHP and Hamersley Iron development that built the Pilbara. The science followed the mines in three waves. The 19th–early-20th-century stratigraphic era read the orebodies as formations (the 1896 Van Hise Lake Superior iron-formation memoir, the 1910s "deposition vs replacement" debates over the Mesabi and the 1920s Lindgren-era classifications of the sedimentary-hosted ores). The mid-century geochemical era made the surface a reactor: the 1950s–70s Holland and Garrels redox-precipitation models of the iron formations, the 1966–72 Hardie and Eugster evaporite-chemistry experiments, the 1970s "unconformity-related uranium" models of the Elliot Lake and Athabasca schools, and the 1973–75 Garrels-Holland oxygen-debate papers that framed the BIF problem as an atmospheric-history problem. The isotope and paleoredox era (1990s–2020s) turned the framing into measurement: the 1996 "2.45 Ga GOE" stratigraphic consensus, the 2000–2011 sulfur-isotope MIF revolution (the 2000–2011 Farquhar, Johnston and Luo records of the oxygen steps), the 2004 Anbar and 2009 Kendall molybdenum-isotope records of ocean oxygenation, the 2010s–2020s Fe-speciation and Cr-isotope syntheses (the 2015 Planavsky, the 2017 Konhauser and 2023–25 high-precision GOE geochronology), and the 2019–2024 "oceanic iron reservoir" BIF synthesis. The applied wave is current: the 2020s "critical metals in sedimentary systems" assessments (the 2021–2025 *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* and USGS work on black-shale V-Ni-Mo-Zn, the 2023–25 "lithium brine sustainability" and DLE literature, the 2024 Simandou first shipment, and the 2023–25 "phosphorus and potash security" reports of the food-fertiliser crisis), which have made sedimentary ore geology, for the second time in a century, a strategic science.
:::

- [ ] Life stopped precipitating iron

::: solution
**The reservoir ran down**: in the 2019–2024 shuttle synthesis, BIFs are the sink of a vast anoxic ferruginous ocean reservoir; once oxygenated deep waters and sulfide burial closed the iron cycle (the sulfidic deep ocean), the dissolved-iron supply collapsed and the BIF machine stopped — a mass-balance end, not a supply or biology end.

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="30000"}
Atacama brine containing 300 mg/L Li is concentrated 100-fold by evaporation. What is the resulting lithium concentration in mg/L?
::: solution
300 × 100 = 30000 mg/L (3 % Li) — the concentration factor that turns a dilute natural brine into a lithium concentrate, and the reason evaporation time (and water rights), not rock tonnage, is the salar's production constraint.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.4"}
A BIF-derived ore averaging 25 % Fe is beneficiated to a 62 % Fe concentrate with no loss of iron. What fraction of the run-of-mine tonnage becomes product?
::: solution
25/62 ≈ 0.40 — 60 % of the mined rock becomes tailings, the beneficiation tax that the 62 % "standard" hides, and the reason 65 % natural ores (Hamersley, Simandou) command a quality premium that low-grade blends cannot process their way out of.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.2"}
A beach sand contains 2 % ilmenite and 10 Mt of it is dredged. How much ilmenite concentrate is produced?
::: solution
10 × 0.02 = 0.2 Mt — the heavy-mineral-sand arithmetic: no crushing, no grinding, just density — the lowest energy per tonne of concentrate in the minerals industry, and the reason Ti feedstock is a placer-and-coastline business.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="83.3"}
A nickel laterite ore grades 1.2 % Ni. How many tonnes of ore are processed per tonne of nickel metal?
::: solution
1/0.012 ≈ 83 t of ore per t of Ni — comparable to a porphyry copper's rock-per-metal ratio, which is why laterite's "cheap mining" claim dies at the smelter or acid plant: the ore is mined at 1 % and *metallurgically* treated like 1 %.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="17.5"}
Lithium's crustal abundance is ~20 ppm. Atacama brine contains 350 ppm Li. What is the concentration factor of the brine relative to average crust?
::: solution
350/20 = 17.5× — the first concentration step was done by the volcanic source rocks and the basin; the solar ponds add another 100×; the full chain from crust to battery-grade carbonate spans three orders of magnitude, most of it paid for in energy, water and time.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="7500"}
Cigar Lake ore averages 2.4 % U₃O₈ (24,000 ppm). Average crustal U₃O₈-equivalent abundance is ~3.2 ppm. What is the enrichment factor?
::: solution
24,000/3.2 ≈ 7500× — the highest grade of any uranium mine in history (a truckload of Cigar Lake ore carries the uranium of a thousand truckloads of Canadian conventional ore), the payoff of focusing basin-brine uranium into metre-scale basement breccias.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="20000"}
If BIF deposition sequestered ~100 Tg of iron per year over a 200 Myr peak interval, how much total iron (in Gt) does that represent?
::: solution
100 Tg/yr = 10¹⁴ g/yr; × 2 × 10⁸ yr = 2 × 10²² g = 20000 Gt of iron — the flux-scale proof that BIFs are an ocean-reservoir drain, not a local deposit: the number that makes the "reservoir ran down" model the only one that closes the mass balance.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="21000"}
The Witwatersrand has produced ~2.7 billion ounces of gold ≈ 84,000 t. At the crustal abundance of 4 ppb, how many gigatonnes of crust would have to be averaged to obtain that gold?
::: solution
84,000 t ÷ (4 × 10⁻⁹) = 2.1 × 10¹³ t = 21000 Gt — twenty trillion tonnes of average crust, the scale of the Archean sediment system that the placer machine winnowed, and the reason the district is a *system* to be mapped rather than a deposit to be hit.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.26"}
A sylvinite seam (30 % KCl) 2 m thick and 1 km² in area is mined at bulk density 2.1 t/m³. How much KCl, in Mt, does it contain?
::: solution
10⁶ m² × 2 m × 2.1 t/m³ = 4.2 Mt of ore; × 0.30 = 1.26 Mt KCl — the evaporite arithmetic: a two-metre seam under Saskatchewan is a world-class potash district, and the Prairie Evaporite's 200 m of salt is why Canada holds ~a third of the world's K₂O reserves.
:::

::: quiz
The reason roll-front uranium deposits are absent from the Archean rock record is:
- [ ] Archean sandstones do not exist
- [x] Roll-fronts require oxidised, uranium-carrying groundwaters, which require an oxygenated atmosphere and oxidative weathering — both post-GOE
- [ ] Uranium was rarer in the Archean crust
- [ ] Archean rivers were too fast for redox fronts
::: solution
**They need an oxygenated surface**: the roll-front machine runs on O₂-oxidised meteoric water leaching U from basement and carrying it to a reductant — a post-2.45 Ga process, like supergene copper and bauxite. The Archean's ore menu (BIF, placers, black shales) and the Phanerozoic's (rolls, Mn platforms, supergene blankets) are the redox history written in deposit types.
:::

::: summary
- The surface concentrates metals by four machines: flowing water (placers — density plus durability plus hydraulics, from the Witwatersrand to the ilmenite beaches), deep weathering (bauxite, Ni laterite, supergene blankets — natural beneficiation by rainfall), chemical precipitation (BIF, manganese, evaporites — the water column as reactor), and redox trapping in permeable rocks (roll-front uranium, Copperbelt Cu-Co, black-shale Mo-V-U).
- Each machine is switched on or off by Earth's oxygen history: BIFs and placers are Archean-anoxic products; rolls, supergene ores, bauxites and great Mn formations require the GOE; the highest-value manganese platforms and modern nutrient cycles follow the Neoproterozoic step — ore-system exploration is applied deep-time redox stratigraphy.
- The case studies share one structure: enormous tonnage, quality-graded, and now governed by pace and permission — Witwatersrand at 4.5 km depth and re-mined tailings, Hamersley-Simandou's green-steel quality premium, the Atacama's fossil-brine water politics, the Athabasca's freeze-walled 10,000× ore.
- The 2020s re-rate sedimentary ores twice over: as the transition's quality inputs (high-grade Fe, laterite Ni, brine Li, black-shale critical metals) and as the strategic-nutrient stores (phosphate, potash) whose jurisdictional concentration makes them the purest "criticality-by-ownership" commodities in the book.
- The lesson's method is the redox mass balance: read an ore system as a source, a pathway, a trap and a preservation window in a specific ocean-atmosphere state — the same four questions the magmatic lesson asked of melts and fluids, now asked of air, water and time.
:::
