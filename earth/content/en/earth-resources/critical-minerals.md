Criticality is not a property of an element and not a property of a market — it is a property of the *coupling* between the two. A material becomes critical when something society cannot easily do without (economic importance) runs through a supply chain that society cannot easily re-route (supply risk), and the two axes multiply: a scarce material with many substitutes is a price problem, a common material with a single supplier is a vulnerability problem, and the combination — a material that is moderately abundant in the crust but concentrated in one country's refineries and one country's factories — is the modern problem. That is why "critical minerals" is a geology lesson and a political one at once: the geology put the lithium in salars and pegmatites, the cobalt in copper veins and laterites, the rare earths in carbonatites and ion-absorption clays; the last thirty years of industrial strategy put the *processing* in one place, and the last fifteen years of export controls made the map a weapon. This lesson defines criticality and its metrics, walks through the concentration data chain by chain, reads the demand stack of the energy transition, surveys the policy toolkit (lists, stockpiles, benchmarks, alliances, and the new export-control arms race), and closes with the four cases that between them define the field: the 2010 rare-earth shock and its fifteen-year aftermath, cobalt's substitution cycle, graphite and the anode choke point, and the 2021–2025 price roller-coaster that taught everyone that price shocks are also policy.

## What "Critical" Means: Two Axes, Many Lists

::: definition Criticality: the two-axis definition {#def-criticality}
A material is **critical** when (i) its **economic importance** is high — it is an essential input to sectors that cannot readily substitute it (defence, electricity, transport, computing) — and (ii) its **supply risk** is high — the probability and consequence of a supply disruption (geographic concentration of production or processing, political instability, export restrictions, trade barriers, resource scarcity) are material. The definition, standardised by the 2008 US National Research Council report *Minerals, Critical Minerals, and the U.S. Economy* and the 2010 European Raw Materials Initiative, deliberately separates criticality from *scarcity*: crustal abundance is only one input to supply risk, and often not the largest. The consequence is that criticality lists are re-drawable at zero change in geology — the 2010 China rare-earth export suspension moved cerium from "abundant" to "strategic" overnight, and the 2023–2025 control waves did the same for gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite — and that the most critical *step* in a chain is usually not the mine but the factory: the 2023–25 assessments (IEA *Critical Minerals at a Glance*, the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries) find refining and manufacturing shares in China of 60–90 % for most transition metals even where mining is global.
:::

::: definition Measuring criticality: HHI, import reliance, and consequence {#def-criticality-metrics}
The metrics are unglamorous and decisive. The **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)** sums the squared percentage shares of producers (or exporting countries): 10,000 for a monopoly, 2,500 is the antitrust threshold for "highly concentrated", and the 2023 IEA supply-chain assessments score rare-earth *separation* near 9,000, cobalt *mining* above 5,000, and iron ore mining near 1,500. **Import reliance** (net imports as a share of apparent consumption) is the exposure metric: the 2024 US assessment found the United States net-import-dependent for 49 of 50 assessed commodities, and fully import-dependent (100 %) for gallium, graphite and manganese. **Nationally-determined criticality** lists then weight the two axes with judgement: the 2023 European CRMA list runs 41 "critical raw materials" (27 "strategic"), the 2022 US list 50 commodities, Japan's and Korea's lists prioritise by industry structure — and the lists overlap on roughly fifteen materials (the rare earths, cobalt, lithium, graphite, gallium, rare-earth-adjacent scandium) with disagreement driven by who buys, who processes and who stocks. The metrics' shared weakness is that they measure *today's* trade flows, not the elasticity of tomorrow: substitution, re-shoring and recycling are all invisible in an HHI computed from 2024 shipments, which is exactly why the 2010 rare-earth episode (below) both validated and embarrassed the indices.
:::

::: history From one embargo to an arms race of lists {#hist-critical-lists}
The term "strategic minerals" dates to the 1970s — the 1973–74 oil embargo and the Zaire/Angola copper-cobalt scares produced the 1975 founding of the International Energy Agency (oil-only, to the lasting regret of minerals policymakers) and the post-1948 US National Defense Stockpile debates — but the modern era begins on **15 September 2010**, when China suspended rare-earth shipments to Japan after a fishing-boat collision near the Senkaku/Diaoyu islands. Praseodymium-neodymium oxide went from ~$4/kg to ~$130/kg within a year, Western media discovered that every F-35, Prius and wind turbine contains rare-earth magnets, and the policy scramble began: the 2010 EU Critical Raw Materials report, the 2011 EU list and Raw Materials Initiative, the 2012–2014 WTO case (US, EU, Japan vs China) that killed the export quotas by 2015, Japan's 2010 JOGMEC risk-bureau and stockpiling programme, and the 2017–2018 US executive order and USGS 50-commodity list. Then, after a decade of détente while prices slumped, the controls returned as industrial policy: China's **July 2023** export licensing on gallium and germanium, **December 2023** on graphite, **September 2024** an outright embargo on antimony to the United States, **April 2025** licensing on seven medium and heavy rare earths, and the **9 October 2025** measures — licensing of all US-bound rare-earth exports, of the mining, refining and magnet *technologies* themselves, and an extraterritorial "0.1 % content" rule reaching any foreign-made magnet containing Chinese rare earths. The **30 October 2025 Busan meeting and November truce** suspended the October rules for a year — leaving every capital on earth to conclude that the suspension, not the rule, is the temporary thing. The countermeasures are now doctrine: the 2022 US Inflation Reduction Act's sourcing conditions, the 2023 EU Critical Raw Materials Act, the Minerals Security Partnership (2022, fourteen members plus the EU), the 2025 US Section 232 investigations into processed critical minerals, and the October 2025 "Project Vault" — a 12-billion-dollar federal-plus-private critical-minerals reserve, the largest stockpile initiative since 1948.
:::

## The Concentration Map: Mine, Refinery, Factory

::: definition The three-stage choke point: mining, processing, manufacturing {#def-chokepoints}
Every critical-mineral chain has three stages with *different* maps, and criticality lives in the narrowest. **Mining** is the most globalised stage — lithium comes from Australia, Chile, China and a dozen African states; cobalt from the Democratic Republic of Congo (73 % of 2023 world mine supply) but also Indonesia and the Philippines; rare earths from China (~70 % of the 2024 mine output, quota 270 kt of oxides), the United States, Myanmar and Australia. **Processing** is where the map collapses: the 2023–24 IEA assessments put China at ~65 % of lithium refining (up from 36 % in 2018), ~44 % of cobalt refining, ~90 % of rare-earth separation, ~68 % of germanium and most gallium refining (China makes >90 % of primary gallium, a by-product of its alumina empire), and ~half the world's antimony smelting. **Manufacturing** concentrates further still: China makes roughly 90 % of the world's sintered rare-earth magnets, ~80–90 % of battery-grade graphite products, and the majority of cells and cathode — so a mine in Nevada can pass through a refinery in Malaysia into a magnet plant in Zhejiang and count, under rules of origin, as exactly the dependency the mine was drilled to break. The strategic concept here is the **choke point**: the stage with the highest HHI and the longest lead-time to new capacity — for rare earths it is separation and magnet sintering (5–7 year build and qualification cycles), for graphite it is spheroidisation and purification (a handful of plants, all in China), for cobalt it is the DRC's logistics corridors rather than its pits. The 2021–2025 Western response — Mountain Pass separation, Lynas Texas, anode plants in Ontario and Bavaria — is the first deliberate re-routing of a metal-processing map since the 1940s, and its cost is the subject of the warnings below.
:::

::: example The 2010 rare-earth shock and its fifteen-year aftermath {#ex-rare-earths-2010}
The control case for everything in this lesson. Before 2010 China supplied ~95 % of the world's rare-earth oxides at prices that had fallen for two decades; the September 2010 export suspension to Japan, the 2011–2014 quota regime, and the smuggling revelations (2011–2015 US investigations found Chinese material entering via third countries to evade quotas) produced a 3–30× price spike, a WTO victory (2014 ruling, quotas abolished 2015) and — the instructive part — almost no lasting de-risking. The price spike funded three things: the restart of Mountain Pass (Molycorp's 2012–2015 venture went bankrupt when prices collapsed back toward the Chinese domestic level — the canonical "price-umbrella" story: a low-cost incumbent can bankrupt high-cost entry by simply matching price), a decade of Japanese and European *demand* engineering (JOGMEC-backed equity in Lynas's Mount Weld; magnet reformulation cutting dysprosium by half; recycling from hard-drives and motors; the 2011–2020 reduction of Western rare-earth *consumption* per unit of magnet by roughly a third), and one surviving mine-plus-refinery chain: Lynas's Kuantan plant, protected by an unusual 2020 Australian statutory covenant when Malaysia threatened its licence. By 2023 the ledger read: China's *mining* share down from 95 % to ~70 %, its *separation* share unchanged at ~90 %, magnet manufacturing ~90 %. The 2021–2025 round reversed the direction of travel: Lynas's 2022 DoD contract for a Texas heavy-rare-earth facility, MP Materials' 2023–2024 Mountain Pass separation start, the July 2025 DoD–MP Materials contract with a 110-dollar-per-kilogram price floor for separated neodymium-praseodymium (the first US production subsidy for a rare earth since the 1960s, and a DoD equity stake), and — within days, October 2025 — China's technology-export controls aimed precisely at cloning that chain outside China. Fifteen years, and the lesson stands: price spikes cure mines, not refineries, because refineries are cheap to build where the incumbent is and ruinous to build anywhere the incumbent can follow you.
:::

## The Demand Stack: What the Transition Asks of the Crust

::: definition Mineral intensity of clean-energy technology {#def-mineral-intensity}
The demand side of criticality is a materials-science fact with a policy face: low-carbon technologies are *more* material-intensive per unit of energy delivered than the fossil systems they replace, because wind and sun disperse energy that coal and gas concentrate. The 2021 IEA *The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions* quantified it: an electric car requires roughly **6×** the mineral inputs of a combustion car, an onshore wind plant ~9× the minerals of a gas plant per MW-equivalent, offshore wind and "utility-scale" storage higher still, solar PV ~2.5× — and the intensity is *concentrated* in a short element list: lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, the rare earths neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium and terbium, copper, plus silicon, manganese and platinum-group metals in smaller tonnages. The demand multipliers to 2040 under the stated-policies scenario: lithium ~40×, cobalt ~20–30×, graphite and rare earths ~7–9×, nickel ~20× — tonnages that, if delivered as projected, make the energy transition the largest metals-buildout in history, comparable to a second electrification. Two caveats the 2021 report under-weighted and the 2023–2025 *Critical Minerals Market Reviews* added: demand is *elastic in chemistry* (the LFP and sodium-ion substitutions cut cobalt and nickel intensity far faster than any mine plan assumed — see the cobalt example below), and demand is *elastic in price* in the worst possible timing (a price spike that arrives during a buildout decimates the marginal buyer — the 2022 lithium spike destroyed a third of projected spodumene demand response by 2024, and the collapse that followed bankrupted the mines that the spike had licensed, the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] discovery-lag cycle running at eleven-year tempo instead of its usual thirty).
:::

::: example Cobalt: the price cycle, the substitution, and the DRC's quota card {#ex-cobalt-cycle}
Cobalt is the case study in how fast a "critical" demand can evaporate. The 2016–2018 EV panic (cobalt-intensive NMC chemistries, a 2× price spike to ~$95,000/t in February 2018) triggered exactly the responses criticality theory predicts and, in hindsight, under-appreciated: battery makers reformulated. NMC cathode cobalt fell from 20 kg to under 5 kg per tonne-of-cathode-equivalent across product generations, LFP — no nickel, no cobalt, no lithium hydroxide requirement — went from ~5 % of the EV battery market in 2018 to over 40 % by 2024 (and roughly half of Chinese installations), and sodium-ion cells (CATL's Naxtra line, 2023; the 2024–2025 Chinese two-wheeler and storage markets) opened a cobalt-free, lithium-optional flank. Supply meanwhile exploded where nobody had priced it: Indonesian nickel HPAL plants produce cobalt sulphate as a by-product of battery-grade nickel, and by 2023 Indonesia was the world's second cobalt producer. The result: cobalt hit its lowest real price in recorded history in early 2025 (~$22,000/t), DRC export revenues halved, and Kinshasa played its own card — a February 2025 export suspension, replaced by a quota system (the announced 2026 cap near 97,000 t against 220,000 t of 2024 output) that doubled the price within months. Every actor's lesson: the buyer learned that chemistry substitutes faster than mines build; the seller learned that the by-product mine does not respond to price at all (Indonesian cobalt keeps coming while nickel economics hold), which is what made the *quota* the only lever left; and the analyst learned that cobalt's 2030 "shortage" forecasts of 2018 and its 2025 "structural surplus" of 2024 were both confident and both wrong, because both held chemistry constant. The residue is a genuine strategic exposure — the DRC still refines almost none of its cobalt (China does ~44 % and processes the rest into Chinese precursor and cathode), and cobalt remains on every criticality list — but as a lesson in how *substitution risk* belongs on the supply-risk axis of any list drawn by the buyers.
:::

::: quiz
The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index is used in criticality assessment rather than a simple "top-3 share" because:
- [ ] It counts the number of producing countries, penalising small producers
- [x] It weights large shares by their square, so it captures the *leverage* a dominant producer holds, not just the count of suppliers
- [ ] It adjusts for transport costs, making chains comparable across oceans
- [ ] It measures price volatility directly, which simple shares cannot
::: solution
HHI sums *squared* shares: a chain with one 90 % producer scores ~8,100+ while a chain with four 25 % producers scores 2,500, even though both have "top-3 shares" that a naive count might treat similarly. Squaring is what encodes dominance — the leverage that makes disruption credible — which is why the rare-earth separation chain (top producer ~90 %) reads as critical while chains with similar top-3 sums but flatter distributions do not.
:::
:::

## The Policy Toolkit: Stockpiles, Benchmarks, Alliances, Controls

::: definition The four generations of critical-minerals policy {#def-policy-toolkit}
Policies against supply risk come in four generations with escalating ambition and cost. **First: stocks.** The 1939 US Stock Piling Act and its 1948–1955 Truman-era build created the National Defense Stockpile (defence-only, 75 commodities at its 1950s peak, sold down through the 1990s); Japan's JOGMEC metal stocks (2010) and the 2023–2025 EU stockpiling plan and the 2025 US Project Vault (12 billion dollars, 60-day emergency response for automotive and battery supply chains, deliberately *industry-managed* rather than government-warehoused) are the modern form — cheaper than mines, useless against a decade-long denial. **Second: intelligence and lists** — the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries (1918 lineage), the 2011-onward EU lists, the 2018 US list — which change nothing directly but re-price risk for insurers, lenders and procurement. **Third: capacity policy** — the 2020s frontier: the EU CRMA's 2030 benchmarks (at least 10 % of EU annual consumption mined, 40 % processed, 25 % recycled *within* the EU, and no more than 65 % of any strategic raw material from a single third country at any stage) and its 2025 list of thirteen "strategic projects"; US Department of Defense Title III and Defense Production Act instruments (the 2020–2025 rare-earth and magnet awards, the 2025 MP Materials price floor and equity); the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act's sourcing conditions and the 2024–2025 "foreign entity of concern" rules that deny credits to cells touching listed companies — the sharpest tool in the box, since it lets subsidy conditionality do what tariffs could not. **Fourth: alliance and countermeasure policy** — the Minerals Security Partnership's project diplomacy (2022), G7 and EU–Japan–India corridors, reciprocal market-access deals, and the new negative instrument, *export controls*, now wielded by both sides (China's 2023–2025 licensing and embargo chain; the 2025 US Section 232 tariffs on copper and processed minerals). The toolkit's history is a pendulum between stockpiling (insure against disruption) and reshaping (prevent it), and the 2020s verdict is that insurance is too cheap to skip and reshaping too slow to rely on: the CRMA's own 2025 review conceded the 2030 processing benchmark will be missed.
:::

::: example Graphite and the anode choke point {#ex-graphite}
Graphite is the most under-loved and most tightly choked link in the battery chain, and its 2023–2025 story is a masterclass in stage-specific criticality. The facts: China mines ~65 % of the world's ~350 kt of flake graphite and, far more importantly, owns almost all the *value-adding* steps — purification (chemical, energy- and water-intensive, sited in Shandong and Heilongjiang next to the mines) and **spheroidisation**, the rounding of flake into the ball-shaped anode powder that cells actually use, at ~90 % Chinese share. Anodes are where the West's battery buildout was quietly planned on Chinese sand: by 2022 the United States was importing ~70 % of its anode material from China, and the entire non-Chinese cell industry bought spheroidised graphite on Chinese terms. Beijing's December 2023 export-licensing requirement on anode-grade graphite (nominally "dual-use" for defence, arriving one month after the US Section 301 decision to raise anode-graphite tariffs to 25 % effective 2026) produced a two-year scramble: licence queues in 2024, Japanese and Korean anode makers diversifying flake sourcing to Mozambique, Madagascar and Brazil, the US Department of Defense and Canadian and EU subsidy programs funding spheroidisation plants in Québec, Ontario and Bavaria (first non-Chinese spherical-anode capacity at meaningful scale, 2025–2026), and synthetic (petroleum-coke-derived) anode makers in China simply *raising prices* as domestic feedstock costs fell — the by-product trap again, since synthetic graphite rides on the margarine-and-alumina-adjacent coke market. The 9 October 2025 controls added graphite to the US-bound licensing web, and the November truce lifted it for a year. The structural lesson sits in the [[earth-resources/sedimentary-ores]] frame: graphite is not scarce — flake graphite is a common metamorphic and synthetic product, and the USGS lists it as "critical" purely on import reliance — and the choke point is a *shape*, literally: the geometry of a ball of carbon, and the forty years of process know-how it took to make it cheaply. Criticality, in the end, is usually a factory, not a rock.
:::

::: warning Five misconceptions about critical minerals
1. **"Critical means scarce."** The opposite: gallium is more abundant than copper, graphite is common carbon, and the rare earths are not rare — cerium is about as abundant as copper. What makes them critical is *concentrated processing* (gallium rides China's alumina refineries), *by-product rigidity* (germanium comes out of zinc and coal ash; you cannot "mine more" without mining more of something else), or *manufacturing geometry* (magnets, spheroidised anode). Crustal abundance is the least useful number on the page.
2. **"Lists measure risk; they don't create it."** Lists are instruments: the 2018 US list conditioned procurement, the CRMA benchmarks condition permitting priority, the IRA's foreign-entity rules condition 370-billion-dollar-scale tax credits — and the 2025 Chinese controls responded to the rules by targeting the *ownership* of foreign mines through the 0.1 % content rule. Every list is now a move in a game, which is why list methodology (who counts as "processing"?) has become a legal battleground.
3. **"Price spikes solve scarcity."** Sometimes they deepen it. The 2021–2022 spike licensed mines that the 2024–2025 collapse stranded, and the incumbent's response to high prices is to *flood* (the 2015–2020 and 2024–2025 Chinese price cuts that killed three separate Western lithium and rare-earth ventures — the "price umbrella" that makes single-supplier markets unentranceable at any price). Stocks and price floors exist precisely because the spike's cure is temporary.
4. **"Recycling closes the loop soon."** Recycling is real and growing (2024–2025: battery black-mass plants in Nevada, Ontario and Germany; the 2025 EU battery-regulation recycled-content rules) but the arithmetic is slow: with today's fleet, end-of-life scrap cannot supply 2040 demand before ~2050, because the fleet itself is the ore body; and for cobalt and nickel it already supplies ~10–30 % of feedstock while for rare earths in magnets it remains ~1 % of use. Recycling is a *second* supply chain, not a substitute for the first.
5. **"The transition's mineral demand is destiny."** The 2021–2025 record is the counterexample: chemistry substitution (LFP, sodium-ion, ferrite-assisted and rare-earth-free motors) removed more cobalt, nickel and dysprosium demand by 2025 than every mine plan added supply security — demand is a design variable, and the most under-used policy in the toolkit is funding the substitution, which is what Japan actually did after 2010 and what everyone cited and nobody funded at scale.
:::

::: example The 2021–2025 price roller-coaster and the policy boomerang {#ex-price-cycle}
The five-year cycle that re-taught the classics. **2021–2022, the spike:** battery-grade lithium carbonate rose from ~$7/kg to ~$80/kg (late 2022), spodumene from ~$450 to over $7,000/t, nickel briefly tripled on the March 2022 Tsingshan LME squeeze, and real prices for most transition metals hit or approached supercycle highs — the collision of the demand stack (EV sales doubling yearly), post-pandemic capex starvation (the 2015–2020 exploration drought, [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]]), and panic inventory-building. **2023–2025, the collapse:** carbonate fell ~90 % to ~$9–10/kg, spodumene to ~$800, cobalt to record lows, rare-earth oxide prices ~40 % off — driven by Chinese oversupply (the 2023–2025 African shale and South American brine buildouts, the Indonesian nickel-cobalt wave), demand deceleration in the West, and the substitution wave. **The policy boomerang:** every government that had just legislated "supply security" responded to the collapse, not the spike — the 2025 US price floor for separated rare earths (buying *against* cheap Chinese material), the EU's strategic-project subsidies, Project Vault's reserve, the 2025 US Section 232 tariffs arriving as US smelting was already shrinking. The economics are the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] cycle with a geopolitical overlay: spikes over-license, collapses under-maintain, and the strategic reserve plus price floor is an attempt to *flatten the cycle itself* — the first serious attempt since the abandoned 1970s–80s international commodity-buffer-agreement era (the failed International Tin Council 1985 and the UN Integrated Programme for Commodities' buffer stocks, whose collapse taught the West thirty years of minerals laissez-faire). Whether a democratic industrial policy can outlast a price cycle — the test the 2025–2030 magnet and lithium plants will run — is the open question of this entire lesson.
:::

::: history From the Defense Stockpile to Project Vault: seventy years of minerals policy {#hist-policy-pendulum}
**1939–1955:** the Stock Piling Act and the Truman build — 75 commodities, 8 billion dollars of chrome, manganese, cobalt and tungsten, the explicit lesson of the 1940 Japanese tin-and-rubber embargo; the stockpile becomes the Cold War's silent mine (the 1950s "surplus" debates begin immediately and never end). **1970s:** the "nonfuel minerals" moment — the 1973 Arab oil embargo, the 1973–74 Zaire/Angola cobalt and copper scares (the Ford Operation and the 1975 IEA founding, oil-only), the 1975–1989 International Tin Council buffer-stock experiment, and the 1980s Reagan-era turn to market orthodoxy and stockpile sell-downs. **1990s:** the sell-down era — the 1993 National Defense Stockpile Management Office sales fund the very African and Latin American mines they undercut, and "supply security" becomes a phrase only environmental and mining historians use. **2010–2019:** the rare-earth shock's institutional residue — the EU Raw Materials Initiative (2010, lists plus innovation partnerships plus governance diplomacy), Japan's JOGMEC risk bureau, stockpiling and overseas-equity program (2010–), the US 2017 executive order and the 2018 list, and China's 2012–2015 WTO defence of its quota regime (which it lost and obeyed — a detail the 2023–2025 controls quietly abandon). **2020–2025:** the return of everything: the EU Critical Raw Materials Act (2023) with its 10/40/25/65 benchmarks and strategic projects, the IRA and foreign-entity rules, the Minerals Security Partnership (2022), the Defense Production Act and Title III magnet awards, the 2025 Section 232 processed-minerals investigation and tariffs, and **Project Vault** (October 2025): 10 billion dollars of EXIM lending plus 2 billion private, an industry-managed reserve of "the minerals that matter for EVs and batteries" — the stockpile idea reborn not as a warehouse of ingots but as a balance-sheet instrument, and the clearest signal yet that the 1990s interregnum is over. The pendulum's arc — insure (1940s), insure (1970s), ignore (1990s), insure *and* reshape (2020s) — is the institutional history of this lesson's two axes.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="3750"}
A supply chain is served by three countries with shares of 50 %, 25 % and 25 %. Compute the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index of this chain.
::: solution
HHI = 50² + 25² + 25² = 2500 + 625 + 625 = 3750 — above the 2,500 "highly concentrated" threshold, even though the chain has three suppliers and a "top-3 share" of 100 % that sounds diversified.

::: exercise {level=1 check="80000"}
Direct-drive EV traction motors use about 8 kg of neodymium-praseodymium magnets each. If the world builds 10 million such EVs in one year, how many tonnes of NdPr magnets does that require?
::: solution
10 million × 8 kg = 8 × 10⁷ kg = 80000 tonnes of magnets — for scale, world mine production of all rare-earth oxides is ~350,000 t/yr, so EVs alone would claim a quarter of it at current magnet loadings.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="210"}
A recycler processes 500 kt of battery black mass per year with a 60 % collection-and-delivery rate and a 70 % metal recovery rate. How many kilotonnes of recovered metals does the plant output?
::: solution
500 × 0.60 × 0.70 = 210 kt of recovered metals — the two multiplicative losses (logistics and chemistry) are why "recycling will supply the transition" claims need both collection systems and refineries, not just furnaces.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="28000"}
World mine production of rare-earth oxides is 350 kt/yr, and China performs ~92 % of the world's separation. How many tonnes of rare-earth oxides are separated *outside* China each year?
::: solution
350,000 × 0.08 = 28000 t/yr — the entire non-Chinese separation industry is smaller than a single Chinese provincial cluster, which is why "we mine at Mountain Pass" and "we separate at Mountain Pass" are two different sentences with two different dates (2018 and 2024).
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="9"}
Spodumene concentrate peaked near $7,650/t in November 2022 and bottomed near $850/t in 2025. By what factor did the price fall from peak to bottom?
::: solution
7650/850 = 9 — a nine-fold round trip in under three years, on a commodity whose mine development takes 7–10 years: the arithmetic of the discovery-lag cycle applied to a policy-driven demand shock.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="12"}
The EU CRMA benchmark requires at least 10 % of EU annual consumption of a strategic raw material to be mined within the EU by 2030. If EU consumption of lithium is 120 kt (LCE) in 2030, what is the minimum EU mined output in kt (LCE) needed to meet the benchmark?
::: solution
0.10 × 120 = 12 kt LCE mined in the EU — roughly the current output of the whole European lithium industry, which is why the benchmark's real content is the permitting reform and strategic-project list attached to it.

::: exercise {level=3 check="15000"}
Direct-drive offshore turbines use roughly 100 kg of rare-earth magnets per MW of rated capacity. If the world installs 150 GW of new direct-drive wind capacity in one year, how many tonnes of rare-earth magnets does that require?
::: solution
150 GW = 150,000 MW; 150,000 MW × 100 kg/MW = 1.5 × 10⁷ kg = 15000 t of magnets — against world mine production of ~350,000 t of all rare-earth oxides, so wind at this build rate alone would claim over 4 % of oxide output for magnets, before EV motors.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3200"}
A 1,000 GWh EV battery market uses NMC cells containing 8 kg of cobalt per MWh. If the LFP share (zero cobalt) rises from 10 % to 50 % of the market, by how many tonnes does cobalt demand fall?
::: solution
1,000 GWh = 10⁶ MWh. At 10 % LFP: 0.90 × 10⁶ × 8 kg = 7,200 t. At 50 %: 4,000 t. The fall is 3200 t — 44 % of the cobalt demand, removed by a chemistry choice in under five years and no new mines.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="120"}
The United States holds 2,000 t of stockpiled cobalt bought at an average $30/kg. If the 2025 quota shock lifts cobalt to $60/kg, what is the stockpile's market value in millions of dollars?
::: solution
2,000 t = 2 × 10⁶ kg; at $60/kg the value is 1.2 × 10⁸ dollars = 120 million dollars — trivially small against a 12-billion-dollar reserve for a 30-billion-dollar annual market, which is the scale lesson of Project Vault's sizing debate.

## Summary

::: summary
- **Criticality = economic importance × supply risk**, not scarcity: the lists (EU CRMA 41/27, US 50, Japan, Korea) are methodology documents, and the most useful metric is the HHI of the *processing* stage, not the mine.
- **The map collapses downstream**: mining is broad (lithium four continents, cobalt one-and-a-half, rare earths four), processing is narrow (China ~65 % Li refining, ~90 % REE separation, >90 % gallium, ~90 % magnets and spherical anode), and the choke point is always the stage with the longest build-and-qualify time — a factory, not a rock.
- **Demand is a design variable**: the transition is 6× more mineral-intensive per car than the system it replaces, but chemistry substitution (LFP 5 % → 40 %, sodium-ion, Dy reduction) removed more critical-metal demand in 2021–2025 than every mine plan added security — the most under-used policy is funding substitution.
- **The policy toolkit has four generations** — stocks (1948 stockpile, 2025 Project Vault), lists and intelligence, capacity policy (CRMA 10/40/25/65 benchmarks, IRA sourcing and foreign-entity rules, DoD price floors and equity), and alliance-plus-countermeasure policy (MSP, Section 232, export controls) — and the 2023–2025 control wave shows every instrument is now also a weapon.
- **The price cycle is the deepest force**: spikes over-license, collapses under-maintain, and the incumbent's price umbrella deters entry at any price — which is why the 2025 policy frontier is *cycle-flattening* (floors, reserves, strategic procurement), the first serious attempt since the buffer-agreement era that ended with the 1985 tin-collapse.
:::
