Beneath every barrel of oil the planet has produced lies a far larger, slower reservoir: the heat that radioactivity and primordial energy conduct upward at an average of 0.065 watts per square metre — ten thousand times less than sunlight, but unceasing, weather-proof, and at drillable depths in half the world's crust hot enough to make steam. This lesson is about the two ways the subsurface serves energy: taking heat *out* (hydrothermal power, enhanced systems, and the direct-use district heating that is actually the world's largest geothermal application by energy) and putting things *in* (carbon dioxide, hydrogen, compressed air and natural gas, stored in the same pore systems that held petroleum for fifty million years). The two directions share one geology — permeability, seal, pressure and the mechanics of rock — and one political economy: the subsurface is the only large-scale, long-duration storage technology the energy system has, and the only carbon-removal partner the hard-to-abate sectors have. The lesson opens with the heat engine itself (heat flow, resource classes, power-plant thermodynamics), moves through the four cases that define the field — Iceland's direct-heating economy, Larderello and The Geysers' 170-year steam history, Kenya's rift-valley electricity miracle, and the enhanced-geothermal frontier shadowed by the Pohang earthquake — and then turns to storage: the four CO2 trapping mechanisms and the Sleipner-to-Northern-Lights regulatory arc, hydrogen in salt, and compressed-air electricity. The prerequisite is the [[earth-resources/petroleum-systems]] lesson: every storage site is a petroleum system read backwards, with the seal doing the work and the time-scales inverted.

## The Heat Engine: Heat Flow, Resource Classes, and Plants

::: definition Earth's heat budget and the geothermal resource ladder {#def-heatflow}
The surface heat flow — mean 0.065 W/m² over continents, 0.10 W/m² over oceanic crust, measured since the 19th-century mine thermometers and mapped globally since the 1960s heat-flow compilations of Birch, Lee and Clark — is two-thirds radiogenic (decay of uranium-238, thorium-232 and potassium-40 in crustal rocks) and one-third primordial heat left from accretion and core formation. Wherever the gradient departs from the conductive background of 25–30 °C/km — at ridges, hotspots, back-arc basins, rifts, and beneath granite batholiths with high heat production — the resource ladder runs from easiest to hardest: **hydrothermal convection systems** (permeable rock carrying water that meteoric circulation has already heated: liquid-dominated fields below 200 °C, vapour-dominated like The Geysers and Larderello where a low-permeability cap lets the reservoir boil; ~13 GW of global power capacity in 2023, concentrated on the Pacific ring and the East African Rift), **hot sedimentary and geopressured basins** (warm saline brines and overpressured gas-bearing zones at 2–5 km, the heating and now the lithium-extraction play of the Rhine graben, the Paris basin and the Permian), **hot dry rock and enhanced geothermal systems** (low-permeability hot rock — granites and basalts — stimulated into engineered reservoirs, the resource base that is technically the largest and commercially the smallest), and **ground-source heat pumps** (shallow loops and boreholes that use the 10–15 °C annual-mean ground temperature as a heat sink, the quiet technology behind Sweden's and China's building-heating booms and, by thermal output, the world's largest geothermal application — over 100 GWth installed globally by 2023 against 13 GWe of power). The exploration logic is petroleum's inverted: instead of finding traps for buoyant fluid, geothermal drillers hunt *heat plus permeability plus water* — and where two of three exist, the third is bought with stimulation or reinjection.
:::

::: definition Geothermal power plants: steam, flash, binary — and the capacity-factor advantage {#def-plants}
The plant follows from the fluid. **Dry-steam plants** (Larderello's 1913 generators and The Geysers since 1960) pipe vapour straight to the turbine — possible only in the rare vapour-dominated fields, and the simplest machine in the business. **Flash plants** (Wairakei's 1958 invention, the workhorse of liquid-dominated fields) drop pressure at the wellhead so a fraction of the hot brine "flashes" to steam; double-flash designs extract a second stage from the residual brine and lift efficiency toward 12–15 % of the heat — modest by design, because Carnot's law is unforgiving at 200 °C against a 30 °C sink. **Binary (organic Rankine cycle) plants** (the 1980s Ormat patents that made low-temperature resources bankable) run the brine through a heat exchanger that vaporises isopentane or ammonia at 85–150 °C, fully closed-loop, which is why every reinjected field since the 1990s (the 2008 consent-era reforms in Iceland and Germany's Munich geothermal district heating) pairs binary units with 100 % reinjection: the reservoir is the working fluid, and wasting it is both thermodynamic and regulatory suicide. The decisive economic number is the **capacity factor**: 90–95 % for a good geothermal plant against 25–45 % for wind and solar, because the resource is a baseload heat flow, not a weather event — geothermal is the only renewable that runs like a coal plant, at full output through winter nights and droughts, which is why its levelised cost (70–120 dollars/MWh for good resources, higher for EGS) understates its system value in high-renewables grids. The offsetting weakness is the front end: exploration drilling is 30–50 % of project cost with a genuine dry-hole risk — geothermal's exploration risk is *shallower and cheaper* than oil's but its per-MW capital intensity is far higher, and the 2016–2019 wave of EGS setbacks (the funding collapses of 2010s EGS startups, from AltaRock's 2013 suspension of its Newberry well to the ownership churn at Desert Peak) is the ledger of that risk.
:::

::: example Iceland: a country that piped its volcanoes {#ex-iceland}
Iceland is geothermal's existence proof at national scale. The island sits on a ridge and a hotspot at once, its 33 active volcanic systems leak heat at ten times the global mean gradient, and Reykjavik's engineers spent a century learning to ask them politely. The sequence: 1907, the first direct-use heating at Reykholt's farmhouse; 1930, Reykjavik's first district-heating pipeline, drilled by hand in the hot springs outside town; the 1970s oil-price-era national drilling programme that converted 90 % of Icelandic homes to piped geothermal water (today's ~55 °C district loops, pumped from 1–3 km boreholes, with the used water cascaded through the greenhouse belts that grow the country's tomatoes and, since 2010s, its algae and data-centre heat contracts); and the power stations — Hengill's Svartsengi (1976, the plant whose surplus discharge built the Blue Lagoon by accident) and Nesjavellir (1990), plus the high-temperature Krafla field where 2012's mid-depth (IDDP) research well hit 427 °C supercritical water at 4.5 km, the first time anyone drilled into a magma-adjacent supercritical reservoir and the seed of the 2016–2025 Iceland Deep Drilling Project's commercial pilot. The 2023–2025 Sundhnúkur eruption series near Grindavík — which has threatened Svartsengi's pipelines repeatedly, forced the Blue Lagoon's closures, and prompted cooled-lava and reinforcement works to protect plant infrastructure — are the counter-lesson: on a living volcano, the resource and the hazard are the same system, and critical infrastructure must be designed for eruption as well as for temperature. The national ledger: about 30 % of Iceland's electricity from geothermal (70 % hydro), space heating for 99 % of homes at among the lowest district-heating costs in Europe, and a 2024 government pivot to geothermal-driven green industry (data centres, green hydrogen pilot at Svartsengi) now colliding with the seismicity of injection — the 2009–2012 Hengill swarm (magnitude 5.1, attributed to Nesjavellir reinjection by the 2020s seismological analyses) ended the "geothermal is earthquake-free" marketing permanently.
:::

::: example Larderello and The Geysers: 170 years of steam, and the bust cycles that taught reservoir management {#ex-larderello-geysers}
The world's two vapour-dominated fields wrote the industry's first and hardest lessons. **Larderello**, in Tuscany's boraciferous hills, is the oldest: Francesco Zakelli's 1827 process for extracting boric acid from the soffioni's steam created the industry, Prince Piero Ginori Conti's 1904 dynamo lit five lamps from the vapour, the 1913 industrial plant began generating for the grid, and by 1939 Larderello was Europe's largest non-hydroelectric power station — 130 MW from a field whose pressure, drilled too hard, had begun to decline by 1940s and fell 60 % before the 1950s–60s discovery of the deeper carbonate reservoir and the discipline of regulated make-up reinjection (the 1990s–2010s reinjection programme, now operated by Enel Green Power at ~800 MW with near-total fluid return, is the field's second life). **The Geysers**, in California's Mayacama Mountains, is the mirror: steam discovered at the surface in 1846, the first shallow wells in the 1920s, commercial power from 1960, a 1980s tax-credit boom (the 1980 ERTA windfall that built 1,400 MW in six years) followed by the 1987 tax-law repeal and the 2008–2009 financial crisis that together produced the industry's two great busts; and then the reservoir-management masterwork — the field's steam is meteoric, its natural recharge slow, and by the 1990s output was falling despite the drilling, until the 1997–2003 Northern California Water Agency pipeline began injecting 16–20 million cubic metres per year of **treated municipal wastewater** from the Bay Area into the field: today two-thirds of The Geysers' steam is recycled sewage, the 2008 drought year when treated-water deliveries were cut showed the dependency in real time, and the field — still the world's largest geothermal complex at ~1,200 MW — is the standing proof that a vapour field is a managed water budget, not a mine. The 2020s drilling of new fault-block step-outs on the field's north end is the current chapter: even giants need inventory.
:::

::: definition Enhanced geothermal systems and induced seismicity {#def-egs}
An **enhanced geothermal system (EGS)** makes its own reservoir: drill into hot but tight rock, stimulate it — hydro-shearing along natural fractures rather than the massive new-crack fracturing of shale, since the target is shear slip on existing joints, not tensile wings — proppant or sustain the conductivity, and loop water through the engineered permeability. The canon: the 1977–1984 Fenton Hill (New Mexico) hot-dry-rock experiments that proved the concept and defeated the economics; Soultz-sous-Forêts (France, 1987 onward, the European testbed for stimulation seismology); Cooper Basin's 2003–2012 Habanero attempts at 4–5 km and 250 °C; and the 2018–2025 second wave — the Utah FORGE National Laboratory demonstration site (open-source stimulation data, 2018–2022), and Fervo Energy's horizontal-well, dense-stage, fibre-optic-monitored "geothermal-as-a-factory" wells that cut drilling times toward shale economics and by 2023–2025 had private capital and an ExxonMobil partnership testing geopressured brines (the 2023 Fervo–Exxon deal in the Permian, where the same wells produce lithium-bearing brine and power). The industry's shadow is **induced seismicity**: injection raises pore pressure, unclamps faults, and the traffic-light protocol (stop at magnitude thresholds — the 2006 Basel 3.4 event that shut a Swiss EGS, the 2017 magnitude-5.4 Pohang quake in South Korea — the 2019 government-commissioned investigations attributing the damaging quake to the stimulation well, and the indictments and trials that followed — is the field's cautionary tale) is the governance answer. The physics is honest: any injection that re-pressures rock by tens of MPa at kilometre scale can reach a critically stressed fault; the mitigation is distance, pressure management (soft-start rates, pressure ceilings below fault-reactivation thresholds — the 2020s "injection-induced seismicity rate" models of McGarr and the Cornell group, whose 2019 *Science* synthesis set the traffic-light physics), and transparency via public microseismic catalogues. Geothermal's social licence rests on being the *good* injection story — and Pohang is why regulators now treat it as a managed industrial risk, not a benign one.
:::

::: example Olkaria and the East African Rift: geothermal as development policy {#ex-kenya}
Kenya's arm of the East African Rift — the same tectonic tearing that opened the Red Sea — hosts a technical geothermal potential the Geothermal Development Company puts near 10,000 MW, sitting under a country that in 2004 had ~1,200 MW of mostly drought-exposed hydro. The build-out since is the Global South's flagship. Olkaria, explored since 1967 under UNDP sponsorship with the first 5 MW unit in 1981, grew to roughly 800 MW by the mid-2020s under KenGen, while Ormat's Olkaria III became the template for independent power production under the 2010 Energy Act's feed-in tariff. Three policy moves did the work that subsidies alone never do: the Geothermal Development Company's state-funded exploration drilling, which hands private builders a de-risked resource instead of a lottery ticket; fixed-price power-purchase agreements that made commercial debt available to a resource class banks had never financed; and the explicit strategic choice of baseload heat over variable imports, made in the two drought winters (2009 and 2017) when hydro's collapse blacked out the factories. The ledger by 2024: about 1,000 MW installed, Africa's largest geothermal fleet by a factor of five, roughly 40–45 % of national generation, and the Menengai and Tulu Moye pilots extending the model toward Ethiopia and across the border. The frictions are the standard ones: pipeline and well-pad compensation disputes with rift-valley communities (the 2011–2013 Olkaria resettlement cases and the 2020s benefit-sharing legislation), the transmission bottleneck that strands new capacity in the north, and the honest arithmetic that a megawatt of Kenyan geothermal costs about what a megawatt of California geothermal costs while serving a grid with 6 %/yr demand growth and no industrial-rate base. Olkaria's verdict, in the rhetoric of the 2023 Nairobi climate summit, was the sober one: geothermal is not cheap — it is *sovereign*, a baseload fuel that no drought, shipping lane, or neighbour can switch off.
:::

::: example The 2020s geothermal boom: Wall Street discovers the subsurface {#ex-egs-boom}
Between 2019 and 2025 private capital poured into geothermal with a ferocity unseen since the 1980s tax credits, and the reason is a change of metaphor: geothermal stopped being mining and became manufacturing. Fervo Energy's Cape and Beacon pilots in Nevada and Utah applied the shale playbook — kilometre horizontal laterals, 40-plus stimulation stages, permanent fibre-optic strain sensing, closed-loop brine circulation — and reported drilling-cost curves falling toward 10 million dollars per well, the threshold at which EGS power competes with storage-subsidised solar; the 2023 Fervo–ExxonMobil joint venture then folded the same wells into the Permian's geopressured trend, where 300 °F brines carry oil, gas and lithium-bearing salinity in one package, and the 2023–2025 scramble for Smackover and Permian brine acreage (Exxon's 2023 Arkansas lithium-acquisition entry the most prominent) made the geothermal-driller's side business of critical-minerals extraction a headline strategy. On the conventional side, Turkey's 2007–2023 feed-in tariff built the world's third-largest binary fleet (from zero to ~1,700 MW, nearly all low-temperature ORC units in the Gediz and Büyük Menderes grabens — and a cautionary tale of reservoir overbuild, since the 2023–2025 production declines at several fields arrived before contract expiry, the classic make-up-injection lesson ignored). And in the heating transition that gets no venture capital, the quiet giant is the ground-source heat pump: Sweden's Stockholm archipelago (seawater-source since the 1980s, now the world's largest urban heat-pump district system), China's Xi'an and Beijing plains (the 2017–2021 "coal-to-clean" programmes that installed hundreds of GWth of shallow geothermal and, in the 2021 gas squeeze, showed both the buffer and the limits), and the 2021–2025 European building-code wave (Germany's 2024 heating-law heat-pump defaults, France's and the Netherlands' geothermal district tenders) all rest on the same 15 °C constant: the ground as the planet's largest battery of low-grade heat, moved by electricity whose carbon intensity decides the verdict.
:::

## Putting Things Back: Carbon, Hydrogen, Air

::: definition Geological carbon storage: four traps, one permit {#def-co2-storage}
Storing carbon dioxide underground is the petroleum industry's discipline run in reverse: inject a buoyant supercritical fluid — above its critical point of 31 °C and 74 bar, CO2 is a single phase about half as dense as water and thinner than most oils — into a permeable saline formation or depleted reservoir, and let the same five-element logic that held oil for fifty million years hold it indefinitely. The four trapping mechanisms operate on escalating time-scales and descending reversibility. **Structural and stratigraphic trapping**: the seal — mudstone, salt, the very caprocks of the [[earth-resources/petroleum-systems]] lesson — mechanically holds the buoyant plume; it is the workhorse for the first centuries and the reason storage sites are found with petroleum exploration tools. **Residual trapping**: as the plume migrates, capillary forces strand ganglia of immobile CO2 in the pore space — potentially 10–30 % of an injected bank in a heterogeneous saline aquifer, and effectively permanent on injection time-scales. **Solubility trapping**: CO2 dissolves into formation water until the dense, CO2-charged brine sinks in convective fingers; the 2009–2015 imaging of the Sleipner plume and the 2008–2016 monitoring at Ketzin, Germany, showed dissolution becoming the dominant long-term mechanism in water-rich systems. **Mineral trapping**: CO2-charged brine precipitates carbonate minerals over thousands of years — except where basaltic glass shortcuts the geology, as the CarbFix pilot at Iceland's Hellisheiði plant demonstrated in the 2016 *Science* reports, with dissolved CO2 mineralising in under two years. The site requirements are a regional seal, adequate thickness and injectivity (pressure buildup, not pore volume, is the usual capacity constraint — the 2020s literature on brine co-extraction and pressure management), and a monitoring, verification and remediation plan: time-lapse seismic and gravimetry to image the plume (Sleipner's 4D surveys since 1997 are the reference data set), groundwater and soil-gas surveillance, and satellite or eddy-flux checks at surface sites. The regulatory skeleton arrived between 2005 and 2024: the 2006 London Protocol amendment legalising sub-seabed disposal and OSPAR's 2007 decision for the north-east Atlantic; the EU Storage Directive (2009/31/EC), which made storage an ETS-credited abatement cost; the US EPA's 2010 UIC Class VI deep-injection rules; the 2018 overhaul and 2022 Inflation Reduction Act expansion of the 45Q tax credit (up to 180 dollars per tonne for direct air capture); and the Paris Agreement Article 6 negotiations (rules agreed 2021–2024) that decide whose emission inventory a stored tonne leaves.
:::

::: definition Storing molecules and electrons: hydrogen, air, and natural gas {#def-h2-caes}
The same caverns and pores store the rest of the energy system's inventory. **Hydrogen** has been stored industrially in salt since 1971, when ICI's Billingham complex began cycling hydrogen through a salt mine cavern — the technology is proven at the 500,000-plus barrel scale (the 1980 Spindletop H2 storage facility near Beaumont, Texas, still the US's only large H2 cavern) and the 2020s European pilots (France's HyPSTER and the Cavernest salt-cavern programme, Germany's long-running HyStock aquifer experiments and the 2020s northern-German cavern tenders, the Netherlands' 2023 Drenthe salt-cavern hydrogen decision) are the first steps toward seasonal wind-to-hydrogen storage. Salt is the easy host — impermeable, self-healing, 99 % cushion-gas purity — but the fleet of the future must also use **depleted gas fields and saline aquifers**, and that frontier carries real physics: hydrogen embrittles wellbore steel, feeds underground methanogens that eat H2 into methane (the 2010s–2020s laboratory and field-tracer studies behind the UK and German H2-storage pilots), and requires cushion-gas chemistry (nitrogen blending) that keeps the mix pipeline-spec. **Compressed-air energy storage (CAES)** stores electricity as pressure: the 1978 Huntorf plant in Germany (290 MW for two hours from salt caverns, running on gas trim since 1978) and the 1991 McIntosh, Alabama plant (110 MW, 27 hours, the only US installation) proved the grid service, and the 2017–2025 advanced-adiabatic generation (China's 2021 Hubei 60-MW/8-hour plant and the 300-MW-class units at Yingkou and Qian'an commissioned 2022–2025) pursues the no-combustion dream by storing compression heat in rock caverns or ceramic bricks; round-trip efficiency has climbed from Huntorf's 42 % toward 60–70 %. **Natural gas** itself is the grandfather: the US's 400+ underground gas storage reservoirs (depleted fields and aquifers, ~30 % of national annual throughput in winter-ready inventory), Europe's 1,300-plus storage operations and the 2022 EU regulation mandating 90 % fill before winter after a decade of under-fill, and the post-2022 argument that gas storage is now also a hydrogen-blending and strategic-reserve asset. The common thread is time: batteries do hours, subsurface storage does seasons, and a grid at 80 % wind and solar needs both.
:::

::: example Sleipner to Northern Lights: thirty years of paying for pressure {#ex-sleipner}
The world's carbon storage industry began as a gas-processing nuisance. Norway's Sleipner West field produces gas with 9 % CO2 — too much for the European pipeline specification of 2.5 % — and in 1992 the Norwegian government gave Statoil a choice: vent the CO2 or pay a carbon tax (the 1991 CO2 levy of roughly 50 dollars per tonne in today's money). Statoil chose reinjection: since 1996, a dedicated well has been putting about 1 million tonnes per year of separated CO2 into the Utsira Sand, a 200–300 m-thick clean saline formation 800–1,000 m below the seabed with no drinking-water value and a 200 m shale cap. The accident of necessity became the science: Norway required monitoring, and the resulting **time-lapse (4D) seismic catalogue** — 30 surveys by 2024, the plume imaged as a bright 200-hectare layer growing laterally and thinning, with no leak signature and a geomechanical uplift of a few centimetres measured at the seabed — is the single most important field data set in storage science, the reason the IPCC's 2005 special report could call geological storage a mature option, and the reason "Sleipner" appears in every regulatory submission. The followers built the modern market: Snøhvit (2008, offshore Arctic, captured at the liquefaction plant), In Salah in Algeria (2004–11, the depleting-gas case whose documented caprock uplift and new fractures (the 2011–2015 IEAGHG and academic analyses) rewrote the industry's injection-pressure design rules), Canada's Weyburn-Midale (2000 onward, CO2-enhanced oil recovery with 20+ million tonnes monitored under the IEAGHG mandate), the US Gulf Coast's Grand Gulf and the 2017 Petra Nova and 2014 Boundary Dam retrofits (the first coal-capture-to-storage plants, and by 2023–2025 the template for the 45Q-funded pipeline hubs — Summit USA's Midwest Project and the Texas Circle cluster), and Europe's answer: the EU Innovation Fund's 2020–2024 round (35+ projects, 10 Mt/yr of planned capacity), the Netherlands' Porthos system and the 2020–2024 Rotterdam and Antwerp port cluster decisions), and Norway's Longship programme with **Northern Lights**, the 2020 open tender (first commercial CO2 transport-and-storage contract signed 2020, first cargo received 2025 at a terminal outside Øygarden) that finally made cross-border CO2 shipping — the London-Protocol-amended, OSPAR-ratified, DNV-certified shipping of a waste gas — a commercial service with a price list. Thirty years after the tax that started it, storage's binding constraint is no longer geology or law but the same one as always: someone must pay per tonne, and only a dozen jurisdictions do.
:::

::: history From Larderello's lamps to the storage permit: two centuries of using the subsurface {#hist-geo-storage}
**1827–1913**: Francesco Zakelli's boric-acid works put Tuscany's steam vents to industrial use, and Prince Piero Ginori Conti's 1904 dynamo and 1913 plant at Larderello make the world's first geothermal electricity — a technology that will stay confined to volcanic steam for half a century. **1947–1960**: the experiment era — New Zealand's 1949 Wairakei borehole discovery of a giant liquid-dominated system (first power 1958, the flash-station prototype that became the world's standard), Italy's post-war Larderello rebuild, and the United States' 1960 Geysers commissioning. **1970–1987**: the first boom and bust — the US Geothermal Steam Act amendments of 1970 and 1975 and the DOE laboratory programmes that followed the 1973 oil shock, the 1980 Energy Tax Act credits that built The Geysers to 1,400 MW in six years, and the 1987 repeal that ended the era. **1971–1978**: the storage seed corn — the 1971 Frio Sand injection tests in Texas (the first deliberate CO2 injection into a saline aquifer, run as an enhanced-recovery analogue by the Texas Bureau of Economic Geology), the 1977–78 parallel US and European aquifer-injection pilots, and Germany's 1978 Huntorf compressed-air plant — three experiments that define the modern fields of storage and its monitoring science. **1991–1996**: pricing carbon into the rock — Norway's 1991 CO2 levy forces Statoil's Sleipner reinjection decision (approved 1992, first injection 1996), and the London Protocol of 1996 becomes the first treaty framework to contemplate sub-seabed disposal (amended 2006 to legalise it). **2004–2014**: the demonstration decade — the 2005 IPCC special report on carbon capture and storage (the assessment that made storage a household policy option), In Salah and Snøhvit, the CarbFix basalt experiments from 2007, the EU Storage Directive (2009), the US EPA Class VI rules (2010), and the 2014 Boundary Dam retrofit that proved capture on a working coal plant. **2015–2025**: the policy build-out — the Paris Agreement's Article 6 rulebook (negotiated 2016–2024) that inventories stored tonnes, the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act's 45Q expansion, the Global Methane Pledge (2021) that turns the subsurface into a monitoring target, the EU Innovation Fund's 35-project 2020–2024 CCS round, the Net-Zero Industry Act's 2024 benchmark of 50 Mt/yr of EU storage by 2030, and the 2025 first Northern Lights cargo — storage's passage from single projects to a priced service industry.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-heatflow}
A field area of 10 km² loses heat at 0.08 W/m². What is the total conductive heat power through that area?

- [ ] 8 kW
- [x] 800 kW
- [ ] 8 MW
- [ ] 80 MW

::: solution :::
0.08 W/m² × 10 km² = 0.08 × 10,000,000 m² = 800,000 W = 800 kW. Note the scale gap that defines geothermal engineering: a whole field area leaks less heat than a 1 MW engine, which is why hydrothermal *convection* (moving stored heat, not conducting it) and borehole fields of hundreds of loops are needed to make useful power.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-trapping}
Which CO2 trapping mechanism becomes dominant over the longest time-scales in a water-rich saline aquifer, and why is it effectively irreversible on human time-scales?

- [ ] Structural trapping, because the seal heals over time
- [ ] Residual trapping, because buoyant CO2 redissolves and rises again
- [x] Solubility and mineral trapping, because dissolved CO2 makes dense brine that sinks and precipitates carbonates
- [ ] All trapping is equally reversible, which is why storage is unproven

::: solution :::
Once CO2 dissolves, the brine is denser than its surroundings, so it migrates downward (convective fingering) away from any pathway back, and over longer periods precipitates carbonate minerals. Structural and residual trapping can in principle be undone by seal failure or re-pressurisation; dissolved and mineralised CO2 cannot rise. The Sleipner, Ketzin and CarbFix records are the evidence base for the rates.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.8"}
**Heat flow through a field.** A geothermal prospect covers 10 km² and measures a surface heat flow of 0.08 W/m². What is the total conductive heat loss through the area, in megawatts?
::: solution :::
0.08 W/m² × 10 × 10⁶ m² = 8 × 10⁵ W = **0.8 MW** — less than a single house's heating demand spread over ten square kilometres, which is why economic geothermal needs convection, depth, or borehole fields, not conductive heat flow.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="100"}
**Target temperature.** A sedimentary basin has a surface temperature of 10 °C and a mean gradient of 30 °C/km. What temperature (°C) should a 3 km well expect?
::: solution :::
10 + 30 × 3 = **100 °C** — binary-plant territory (85–150 °C), the class of resource behind Turkey's 1,700 MW of ORC capacity and most direct-heating schemes.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="416"}
**The capacity-factor dividend.** A 50 MW geothermal plant runs at a 95 % capacity factor for a year. How much electricity does it generate, in GWh?
::: solution :::
50 MW × 8,760 h × 0.95 = 416,100 MWh ≈ **416 GWh** — the same nameplate solar would deliver ~150 GWh at 35 %: the baseload premium that makes geothermal's higher capital cost defensible in a decarbonising grid.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="120"}
**Aquifer capacity.** A saline storage target covers 40 km², is 100 m thick, and has 20 % porosity. If CO2 occupies 25 % of the pore space at a reservoir density of 600 kg/m³, what is the storage capacity in megatonnes?
::: solution :::
Pore volume = 40 × 10⁶ × 100 × 0.20 = 8 × 10⁸ m³. CO2 volume = 0.25 × 8 × 10⁸ = 2 × 10⁸ m³. Mass = 2 × 10⁸ × 600 = 1.2 × 10¹¹ kg = **120 Mt** — two decades of a single coal plant's emissions in one modest formation, before any of the capacity limits that pressure (not volume) usually impose.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="25"}
**Sleipner's ledger.** The Sleipner project has injected about 1 million tonnes per year of CO2 since 1996. Roughly how many megatonnes had been stored by 2021, and what fraction of a 120 Mt aquifer capacity does that represent?
::: solution :::
1 Mt/yr × 25 years = **25 Mt** — about one-fifth of the 120 Mt aquifer capacity computed in the previous problem, injected through a single well: the point about how small industrial point sources are relative to sedimentary storage volumes.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="53"}
**Hydrogen cavern energy.** A salt cavern holds gas at an average density of 8 kg/m³ of hydrogen, and 200,000 m³ of working gas is withdrawable. With a lower heating value of 120 MJ/kg, how much energy (GWh) can be recovered?
::: solution :::
Mass = 200,000 × 8 = 1.6 × 10⁶ kg. Energy = 1.6 × 10⁶ × 120 = 1.92 × 10⁸ MJ = 1.92 × 10¹⁴ J ÷ 3.6 × 10¹² J/GWh ≈ **53 GWh** — enough for a small city's winter week, or a 1 GW hydrogen turbine for two days at full output: seasonal storage is a volume business, not a power business.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3.94"}
**Direct-use economics.** A 10 MWth geothermal district-heating system runs 7,884 hours per year (90 % capacity), displacing pipeline gas at 0.05 euros/kWh_th. What are the annual fuel-cost savings in millions of euros?
::: solution :::
7,884 h × 10 MW = 78,840 MWh = 7.884 × 10⁷ kWh × 0.05 €/kWh = 3.94 × 10⁶ € ≈ **3.94 million euros per year** — at this rate the ~100-million-euro capital cost of a heating doublet field pays back in about 25 years *without* counting the carbon price, the air-quality benefit, or the gas-import substitution that made Iceland's and Munich's programmes policy choices rather than investments.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="7.5"}
**Capture scale.** A 1 GW coal plant (38 % net efficiency) burns the 3.46 million tonnes of bituminous coal computed in the petroleum-systems lesson. At 2.42 t CO₂ per tonne of coal and 90 % capture, how many megatonnes of CO₂ per year must be stored?
::: solution :::
Coal: 3.46 Mt. CO₂: 3.46 × 2.42 = 8.37 Mt. Captured: 8.37 × 0.90 = 7.53 ≈ **7.5 Mt/yr** — which is 7.5 Sleipners, or the whole 120 Mt capacity of the example aquifer gone in 16 years: one power plant is a *decadal* storage commitment, and this arithmetic — tonnes per plant-year versus cubic kilometres per basin — is why storage advocates push for hub pipelines serving many emitters rather than one-well-per-plant.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1381"}
**CAES round trip.** The Huntorf compressed-air plant delivers 290 MW for 2 hours at a round-trip efficiency of 42 %. How much electricity (MWh) was used to charge the caverns?
::: solution :::
Output = 290 × 2 = 580 MWh. Input = 580 / 0.42 = 1,381 MWh ≈ **1381 MWh** — the 581 MWh difference is the physics tax of 1978; advanced-adiabatic plants that store compression heat in rock or brick target 60–70 %, and the whole research programme is about spending that tax on thermodynamics instead of on natural gas, as Huntorf's gas trim still does.

::: warning Four misconceptions about geothermal and storage
1. **"Geothermal is everywhere."** Conventional hydrothermal power genuinely is site-specific (volcanic and rift settings, hot granites), and EGS does not repeal geology so much as widen the menu: you still need drillable heat within 3–6 km, water to circulate, and stress states that shear rather than seal. The honest framing from the 2019 MIT Future of Geothermal Energy study is that EGS could scale the resource base by an order of magnitude *at higher cost per kilowatt* — an economic problem dressed as a geological one, and still an economic problem in 2025.
2. **"Injection causes earthquakes, therefore storage is unsafe — or, injection never causes earthquakes, therefore no monitoring is needed."** Both halves are wrong. Induced seismicity is a function of stress, not of fluid type: the same physics that makes Pohang possible makes most soft-start, pressure-capped projects uneventful, and the traffic-light-plus-catalogue regime now standard from Basel to Oklahoma — including Oklahoma's 2015–2017 disposal-volume reduction rules following the USGS's 2016 induced-seismicity synthesis — is the evidence that monitoring can work. The post-2011 US injection-well rulemaking wave (EPA Class VI for CO2, expanded Class II rules after the 2011 Youngstown, Ohio injections) is the regulatory answer, not a ban.
3. **"CO2 storage is unproven."** The counterfactual is geology itself: petroleum systems prove that buoyant fluids stay sealed in sedimentary basins for tens of millions of years wherever seals exist — the industry's task is to find seals, not to invent containment. Operationally, Sleipner's 30 surveys and 25+ million tonnes with no leak, Weyburn's two decades of monitored EOR co-optimisation, and the 2025 Northern Lights service contracts make storage the *least* speculative of the negative-emissions toolkit. What remains genuinely unproven at scale is not the rock but the economics: only carbon prices, 45Q-style credits, and Article 6 markets make stored tonnes worth paying for, and every one of them is a policy variable that policy can revoke.
4. **"Stored CO2 is a liability that outlives the company."** It is — which is the point. The liability framing is why the EU directive transfers long-term responsibility to the state after closure and validation (the member-state transfer practice the directive mandates, codified nationally by the UK's 2019 storage-liability regime and Norway's 2020 rules), why the US Class VI rules require financial responsibility for a century of post-injection site care, and why the accounting question — whose tonne, whose tonnes, whose leak — consumed a decade of Paris negotiations. Storage is a 1,000-year promise; the honest industry is the one that prices the promise before the plume arrives.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
The subsurface is a two-way technology. Taking heat out runs on the gradient ladder — 0.065 W/m² of conductive background, tenfold anomalies where ridges, hotspots and rifts thin the crust, and a resource hierarchy from vapour-dominated steam (Larderello's 1913 generators, The Geysers' 1,400-MW tax-boom and the recycled-wastewater reinjection that now makes two-thirds of its steam) through flash and binary plants to the 100 GWth of ground-source heat pumps that are geothermal's true mass market. Its economics are baseload economics (90–95 % capacity factors, high capital, exploration risk that state-funded de-risking drilling — Kenya's GDC model — is designed to socialise), and its social licence is a seismicity-management problem: Pohang's magnitude-5.4 lesson, traffic-light protocols, and public microseismic catalogues are the new normal for stimulation. Putting things in runs on the same five-element logic read backwards: supercritical CO2 held by seal, capillary, dissolution and mineralisation (the Sleipner–Ketzin–CarbFix evidence chain), hydrogen and compressed air in salt, natural gas in depleted fields — each a storage service whose price is set not by geology but by carbon levies, capacity markets and seasonal spreads. The regulatory arc from the 1996 London Protocol through the EU directive, Class VI and 45Q to Article 6 has turned a 1971 aquifer experiment into a service industry with a first commercial cargo in 2025, and the EU's 50 Mt/yr benchmark makes storage declared infrastructure. What binds is the same constraint as every lesson in this course: the subsurface is finite, contested, and governed — a commons of pore space whose leases are written in tonnes, bars and decades, and whose most valuable property, once again, is the seal. The next lesson turns to the shallowest and most contested subsurface resource of all: the groundwater in the same sedimentary basins.
:::
