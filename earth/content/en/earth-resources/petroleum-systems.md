A petroleum system is a single story told by a sedimentary basin: organic matter buried fast enough to survive, cooked to exactly the right temperature for long enough, expelled from its source rock at the moment the migration pathways and the traps existed, and preserved ever since. Every oil field in the world — the Permian Khuff carbonates of Ghawar, the Jurassic salt-dome flanks of the Gulf Coast, the Paleogene turbidites of Guyana's Stabroek block, the Bakken's own source rock — is one sentence in such a story, and the discipline of petroleum geology is reading the whole grammar at once. This lesson builds the system element by element (source, migration, reservoir, seal, trap, timing), follows hydrocarbons from the kitchen to the trap, treats exploration as portfolio risk under uncertainty, walks through the basin families that structure the world's endowment, and closes with four cases — the Gulf of Mexico, the Middle East giant-oil province, the South Atlantic salt and turbidite frontier, and the North Sea's mature-basin second act — that between them cover most of the oil and gas produced in human history. The same grammar, applied in reverse, is what makes the subsurface a place to *put things back* — carbon dioxide, hydrogen, air — which is why this lesson is the prerequisite for the storage lesson that follows.

## The Petroleum System: Source, Migration, Reservoir, Seal, Trap, and Time

::: definition The petroleum system: elements and processes {#def-petroleum-system}
A **petroleum system** — the 1980s Shell concept formalised by Magoon and the USGS as "a system comprising the total set of generated and migrated hydrocarbons occurring in a basin and related to a single effective source rock" — requires **five elements**: an organic-rich **source rock**, a permeable **reservoir**, an impermeable **seal**, at least one **trap**, and migration **pathways** (faults, carrier beds, fractures); and **four processes**: generation, migration, accumulation and preservation — with **timing** governing all nine, because the trap must exist before or during peak expulsion, the seal before accumulation, and the burial path must have carried the source through its kitchen at the right moment in the structural history. The concept's power is *falsifiability*: a basin can be condemned by the absence of a single element (no mature source condemned most of onshore Southeast Asia for decades; no reservoir quality condemned the Southern North Sea's Carboniferous to gas-only development), and the USGS World Petroleum Assessment (1995–2000) made the concept industrial by assessing *undiscovered* resource play-by-play with probability models rather than field-by-field with decline curves. The corollary that organises this lesson: **oil is found where it was generated**, and the map of the world's giant fields is a map of the world's best source rocks, their burial histories, and the traps that stood ready when they began to charge.
:::

::: definition Source rocks and kerogen: TOC, type, maturity, and the Van Krevelen diagram {#def-source-rocks}
Source rocks are fine-grained sedimentary rocks — marine black shales, lacustrine shales, organic carbonates, coals — assessed by three numbers. **Total organic carbon (TOC)**: fair above 0.5 %, good 1–3 %, excellent above 3 % (the Kimmeridge Clay averages ~2 %, the Jurassic Kazhdumi and Cretaceous Mahanet marls of the Gulf 4–8 %, the Bakken 4–12 %). **Kerogen type**, read from hydrogen-to-carbon and oxygen-to-carbon ratios on the **Van Krevelen diagram** of the 1960s–1970s Tissot synthesis: **type I** (algal, lacustrine — the most oil-prone, the Green River formation and the Barremian–Aptian lacustrine source of the South Atlantic pre-salt), **type II** (marine algal — the workhorse oil-to-gas kerogen of the Kimmeridge Clay, the Mahanet, the Smackover), **type III** (woody terrestrial — gas-prone, the Carboniferous coals that charge the Southern North Sea, the Paleogene Wilcox Group of Texas). **Thermal maturity**, measured by vitrinite reflectance (Ro) and Rock-Eval Tmax, decides what the source is making *now*: immature below Ro ~0.5, the **oil window** at Ro ~0.6–1.0, wet gas to ~1.3, dry gas to ~2.0, dead graphite beyond. Tissot & Welte's *Petroleum Formation and Occurrence* (English edition 1984) codified the kinetics that make maturity a *time–temperature* property, not a temperature property: the same oil can be generated at 60 °C over 100 million years or 150 °C over 1 million, which is why rapid Tertiary-delta burial (the Gulf of Mexico, the Niger delta) makes oil at depths that would have cooked a slow basin's source to gas — and why the "kitchen" is a moving, time-specific place rather than a depth contour.
:::

::: history From Barnard's shales to the USGS assessment: 150 years of system thinking {#hist-petroleum-system}
The pieces are older than the concept. **1861**: Sophus Barnard's "On the origin of petroleum" argues that the bitumens of the Ohio oil rocks are the altered remains of Ordovician algae — the source-rock idea sixty years before anyone could prove it, while the inorganic carbide school of Mendeleev held the floor. **1885–1901**: the anticlinal theory (White's 1885 paper on the petroleum regions of Pennsylvania) turns oil hunting from digging beneath seeps into drilling structure, and the 1901 Spindletop gusher near Beaumont — roughly 100,000 barrels per day for nine days from a salt-cored dome read as *geometry* — announces the petroleum age and halves US oil prices within a decade. **1920s–1930s**: reflection seismology makes traps visible before drilling; the 1930 East Texas field, the largest onshore US field and found geophysically, ends the seep-hunting era. **1940s–1960s**: biostratigraphy and sequence mapping build subsurface stratigraphy; Wallace Pratt's 1942 work on the geographic concentration of giants anticipates province thinking, and M. King Hubbert's 1956 bell-curve paper gives the same systems a production destiny (the subject of the [[earth-resources/hydrocarbons-coal]] lesson). **1968–1988**: plate tectonics explains the map — the 1970s Atlantic-margin models predicted West Africa's oil before it was found, and the North Sea's 1969 Ekofisk and 1971 Forties discoveries vindicated the passive-margin source-rock analogy to Middle America — while Tissot & Welte and the 1984–1988 Shell petroleum-system papers (Magoon, Dow) make the *system*, not the trap, the unit of exploration. **1990s–2000s**: 3D seismic, sequence stratigraphy and the play-based USGS World Petroleum Assessment industrialise the concept; **2000s–2020s**: the frontier moves under salt (Brazil's 2006–2010 pre-salt), under mudlines (the Guyana fan, 2015) and into the source rocks themselves (the 2000s shale revolution) — three times that the petroleum-system concept predicted oil where trap-thinking said there was none.
:::

## From the Kitchen to the Trap: Expulsion, Migration, Accumulation

::: definition Expulsion, migration and the charge of a trap {#def-migration}
Oil leaves its source rock only when generation creates overpressure. **Primary migration** (expulsion) begins at oil-window onset, when generated hydrocarbons and microfracturing push bitumen into carrier beds, typically after the source has generated 50–100 mg of hydrocarbon per gram of organic carbon. **Secondary migration** then does two things at once: *buoyancy* drives the light phase up along carrier beds and fault conduits against the capillary **entry pressure** of seals (a good mudstone seal holds a column of tens to hundreds of metres — entry pressures of 0.1–1 MPa correspond to roughly 30–300 m of oil — while halite and anhydrite seal kilometres), and *water flow* focuses that buoyant flow along the regional spill-point slope of the carrier network, so migration runs basin-ward from kitchens and fills traps in spill-point order — the **fill-and-spill cascade** that makes a mature fairway a chain of progressively smaller, progressively younger fields upstream of the kitchen. **Charge-source correlation** — the biomarker, carbon-isotope and natural-gas (C1–C5, wetness index) fingerprinting practised since the 1970s, and the tool that distinguishes thermogenic from microbial gas — is how explorationists answer the only question that matters about a dry hole: *was the system absent, or did the trap miss the timing?* The answer decides whether a basin is written off or re-opened with a different structural map; the 2010s Namibia re-opening turned on kitchen-and-pathway arguments, not new traps.
:::

::: definition Traps and seals: structural, stratigraphic, and combination {#def-traps}
A **trap** is any geometry in which a permeable bed or body is closed updip by seal, and the taxonomy is short even though its combinations built the industry. **Structural traps**: anticlines (the Middle East fold belts, the North Sea's Forties and Brent structures, Spindletop), fault blocks and rollovers (the Gulf of Mexico and North Sea extensional blocks, where the fault itself is the seal — the 1980s–90s fault-seal revolution turned shale-gouge ratio and gauge-zone analysis from structural geology into risk assessors), salt-related traps (domes, welds, canopies and overhangs — Gulf Coast and South Atlantic salt created hundreds of traps, each with its own seal-integrity puzzle), and inversion structures. **Stratigraphic traps**: pinchouts against shale (the deepwater Gulf's channel sands, the North Sea's Brent deltas), reefs and buildups (the Devonian Leduc reefs of Alberta, found in 1947 and the decade's greatest exploration prize), carbonate platforms and their flank reefs (the Middle East platform interiors, Brazil's pre-salt microbialite mounds), and unconformities beneath shales. **Combination traps** mix the two: giant Elk Hills, and the North Slope's Prudhoe — a faulted and tilted anticline beneath an unconformity, charged from the Shublik source below. **Seals** do the actual keeping: mudstones (entry pressure scales inversely with throat radius — the 1990s seal-capacity literature), evaporites (the Jurassic Hith and the Aptian salt — the seals of the two greatest petroleum provinces on earth), carbonates, and shale smears along faults. And every seal is finally a *mechanical* object, since fault reactivation, salt withdrawal, uplift-driven gas expansion and simple overpressure can break a trap that was full for fifty million years — which is why most of the petroleum ever generated, perhaps nine-tenths, leaked out (the 2003 US National Research Council *Oil in the Sea* survey reckoned natural seepage at 0.2–2.7 million tonnes per year, visible as the Gulf's "oil slicks from below"), and the fields we produce are the survivors of a fifty-million-year integrity test.
:::

## Source-Rock Families and the World's Petroleum Provinces

::: definition The short list: a handful of source rocks made most of the oil {#def-source-families}
Roughly 90 % of the world's oil comes from a handful of source intervals, almost all marine anoxic-event deposits. The **Cretaceous Mahanet/Mundii equivalents** (Oceanic Anoxic Events 1a–2, ~120–90 Ma) charge nearly all of coastal West Africa from Angola to the Niger Delta, and the Java Sea (the Mundii shale): type II marine shales carrying the distinctive Cretaceous biomarker assemblage — isoprenoids and gammacerane — that lets geochemists date a crude to its kitchen. The **Jurassic Kimmeridge Clay and Kazhdumi equivalents** charge the North Sea and much of the Middle East's oil respectively (the Kazhdumi also charges the giant fields of onshore Iran through Zagros migration). The **Paleogene lacustrine shales of the South Atlantic** (the Aptian "Laje C" source of the Potiguar Basin and the Gondwana lake shales of the Tucano and Gabon basins) are type I algal sources of extraordinary oil quality — up to 20 % TOC. The **Carboniferous coal-measure source** (the "Coal Measures" and Silesian clastics) is the gas-prone type III engine of the Southern North Sea, the Netherlands, Poland's gas fields and much of onshore China. And the **Devonian Shublik source of the Alaska North Slope and the Cretaceous–Jurassic marine shales of the Williston Basin** — Prudhoe Bay's 13-billion-barrel charge and the self-charging Bakken — shows the two ends of the family: long-distance charge from a marine shelf source, and tight-oil self-sourcing in which the Bakken shale is source and reservoir at once (the subject of the [[earth-resources/hydrocarbons-coal]] lesson's unconventional section). The pattern for exploration is simple and brutal: provinces built on Cretaceous marine and lacustrine sources are oil-dominated and time-critical (short-lived traps, Tertiary structures); provinces built on Jurassic and Paleozoic marine sources are giant-field country (long-lived, stable cratonic cover, huge fairways); provinces built on coals are gas provinces.
:::

## Plays, Prospects, and Exploration Risk

::: definition Plays, prospects, and probability of success {#def-plays}
A **play** is a set of prospects with the same combination of source, reservoir, seal, trap and timing — "the faulted rollover in the Miocene turbidite beneath the regional mudstone" is a play; so is "the reef flank against the Permian anhydrite." A **prospect** is a specific drilled-able trap within a play; exploration proceeds play-first, because one successful well de-risks every prospect that shares its system. The industry quantifies the hunt with the **probability of success**: the product of independent chances for source, reservoir, seal, trap, surface and timing (POS = Ps × Pr × Pse × Pt × Pti, with surface and timing often folded into "risk of failure" modifiers), typically 0.1–0.25 for frontier wildcats and 0.3–0.5 for step-outs inside a fairway. Multiplying five factors of 0.7 already gives 0.17 — which is why exploration is a **portfolio** business of many small tickets rather than a search for sure things, and why expected-value economics (POS × mean recovery × price, versus the cost of the well) governs what gets drilled when oil is cheap. The **prospect inventory** of a basin — ranked by POS, mean size and, crucially, *differentiation value* (how much a dry hole would learn about the play) — is the strategic asset of an exploration company; the 2010s shale revolution was, in portfolio terms, the discovery that some "abandoned plays" had continuous, undrillable-by-ticket-size gas and oil in them all along, converting exploration risk into development density.
:::

## Basin Families and the Global Map

::: definition Four basin families, four exploration styles {#def-basin-families}
**Passive continental margins** (the Atlantic rims, Australia's northwest shelf, the North Sea central graben) hold the largest deepwater systems: wide shelf-edge deltas, world-class marine source rocks emplaced during rifting and the oceanic anoxic events, and reservoirs in deltaic sands, carbonates and basin-floor fans; salt (Brazil, West Africa, Gulf of Mexico) decorates the traps. **Intra-cratonic sag basins** (the Middle East's Arab–Permian megabasin, West Siberia, the Williston) are shallow, stable, giant-field factories: modest heat flow makes kitchens late but gentle, structures are broad and long-lived, and the fields are huge, cheap and shallow — the reason the Gulf region's oil costs single-digit dollars to lift. **Foreland basins** (the Zagros fold belt, the Rocky Mountain foothills, the Western Desert of Egypt's flexural margin) are flexed by thrust loading, so structure and migration are young and spectacular — Zagros traps grew mostly in the last 10 Myr, right on time for the Miocene–Pliocene charge. **Rift and rift-drift basins** (the East African lakes, the Viking Graben, the South Atlantic's pre-salt) are narrow, hot and fast: kitchens come early, and in the South Atlantic the salt that drapes the rift sequence created both the seal and the structural theatre for everything after. The exploration style follows the family: margins reward seismic imaging and play diversification; sag basins reward stratigraphic subtlety and scale; forelands reward structural sophistication and timing; rifts reward bold first-well geometry — and each family's source-rock age sets the *age of the oil* on the world market: Cretaceous for West Africa, Jurassic for the North Sea and Middle East, Paleogene for the Gulf of Mexico and Southeast Asia.
:::

::: example The Gulf of Mexico: salt, speed, and the deepwater laboratory {#ex-gom}
The Gulf of Mexico is the petroleum-system textbook written in salt. Rifting ~160–155 Ma laid down the Louann Formation evaporites over the basin's own immature source; the salt's later flow — diapirs, canopies, turtle-structure accommodation space — created the trap catalogue, while the real source rocks came later: Jurassic Smackover carbonates in the deep and, above the salt, the rapid Cretaceous–Paleogene marine shales whose huge burial beneath the Miocene delta made the modern Gulf a high-heat-flow, fast-cooking machine. The consequences are visible from space: the Gulf leaks. The 2003 National Research Council survey attributed the bulk of the ocean's natural seepage estimate to basins like this one, and satellite surveys since 2010 have counted thousands of seep-driven "marine oil slicks" over the deepwater — the surface expression of a system that charged, and continues to leak from, traps for 20 million years. The exploration history is a masterclass in playing above and then beneath the salt: 1938–1950s salt-dome flanks on the shelf; 1961 Petronius and the 1980s–90s sub-canopy plays in 300–2,000 m water; 2000s subsalt imaging (long-offset seismic, then full-azimuth 3D) cracked the salt curtain and delivered Thunder Horse (discovered 1999, one of the largest US fields), Mars-Bakhit, and Blind Faith; 2010s–2020s exploration stepped into new plays such as Shell's Cretaceous "Atlauts" fairway announced in 2014. The counter-lesson is **Macondo** (April 20, 2010): a pressure-prediction failure in a Pliocene prospect above a salt weld killed eleven workers and released an estimated 3.19 million barrels (the USCG/BSEE final estimate, ~780 million litres) over 87 days — the industry's starkest demonstration that the petroleum system's *fluid pressure* is as real a hazard as its geology, and the reason well-integrity and blowout-preventer regulation was rewritten worldwide.
:::

::: example The Middle East: the giant-oil province and Ghawar {#ex-middle-east}
The Arab–Permian megabasin of the Gondwana craton is the largest petroleum system ever assembled: the Jurassic Kazhdumi and Cretaceous Mahanet-equivalent marine shales of the Zagros foreland generated oil that migrated along a gentle regional dip — in places more than 1,000 km — into the carbonate factory of the platform to the east, where the Permian Khuff carbonates and the Jurassic Arab–Hith play hold the giant fields. Ghawar, 240 km long and up to 25 km wide along the structural grain of the Hasa arch, is the largest conventional oil field ever found: original oil in place measured in the hundreds of billions of barrels, cumulative production by 2024 of some 60–65 billion barrels (roughly 7 % of all oil ever burned), and still producing around 3.8 million barrels per day in the mid-2020s from the Arab-D carbonate drained along its crest by water injection below the water-oil contact. The system's gifts are the explorer's dream: source on the scale of a continental margin, reservoir a 2,500-km carbonate platform, seal 1,000 m of evaporite, structure that began growing in the Paleogene and kept growing gently, and a cratonic roof that kept traps intact for tens of millions of years. Its frustrations are the mirror image: giant fields mean giant *water cuts* (Ghawar's injection programme handles some 9–10 million barrels of water per day), thin oil columns in flanks, and carbonate heterogeneity that makes flow units — the layered dolomite and grainstone beds of Arab-D described in the Saudi Aramco geobooks of the 1990s–2000s — the difference between economic and marginal. The province is the empirical basis for the resource-conservatism argument in the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] lesson: giants of this kind are found in *provinces*, not in ones and twos, and the provinces are all found.
:::

::: example Guyana and the South Atlantic: the frontier that a play concept found {#ex-guyana}
Stabroek block, 150 km off Guyana, is the clearest vindication of play-based exploration in the industry's modern era. ExxonMobil's team entered the frontier in 2009 on a source-rock analogy — that the same Aptian–Cenomanian marine shales that charge West Africa (the conjugate margin rifted away 120–100 Ma) would charge the Guyana coast — and on a reservoir play: Paleocene–Eocene turbidite fans shed off the paleo-Amazon fan complex into the basin. When no seismic amplitude "bright spot" worked in the mud-poor area, the team bet on *dry* fans imaged in rock-physics detail rather than hydrocarbon indicators. Liza, drilled on the first attempt in 2015, was a 1-billion-barrel discovery in 1,700 m of water; by 2024 the block held more than 11 billion barrels of recoverable resource across Payara, Whiptail, Hammerhead and successors, first oil came in December 2019 on an FPSO-per-phase plan that became the template for deepwater development worldwide, and by 2025 production exceeded 600,000 barrels per day — making Guyana the fastest-growing oil country on earth and the largest per-capita producer outside the Gulf. The petroleum-system reading: a mature Cretaceous source at the right maturity in the right place, sourced from the world's largest sediment-discharge system of the Paleogene (the proto-Amazon), reservoir in basin-floor fans with 20–25 % porosity at 5–6 km depth, seal in the deep-sea mudstones, and trap in faulted and capping stratigraphic geometry — a play now being tested on the conjugate margins of Suriname and the equatorial Atlantic, where the same source charges but the reservoir fairway must be re-found.
:::

::: example The North Sea: a mature province's second act {#ex-north-sea}
The North Sea is what a petroleum system looks like after forty years of production: 27 billion barrels produced by 2024 from the UK sector alone, more than 400 fields, and a resource still reckoned at 5–10 billion barrels of undiscovered, mostly small, mostly near-existing infrastructure. The system itself is a single elegant story: Middle Jurassic Kimmeridge Clay source, oil in the central graben (Ekofisk 1969, Brent 1971, Forties 1970, Statfjord 1971), gas in the Southern Basin's Carboniferous and Permian reservoirs sealed by Zechstein salt, Cretaceous chalk reservoirs in the central graben and Paleocene Forties sandstones and Middle Jurassic Brent deltas in the north — the Brent and Forties fields themselves being named after the birds whose flight paths crossed the platforms. The exploration history is the world's best data set on *prospect depletion*: the 1970s giants gave way to 1980s–90s structure-and-stratigraphy hybrids (of the 1980s' discoveries, most were partly stratigraphic), then to 2000s–2020s "near-field" plays — intra-Delfi in the Brent delta, Clair's swell-structured Paleocene fans west of Shetland (Europe's largest field by OOIP, discovered 1993, production only from 2001 because the heavy oil needed pressure support), and the 2010s–2020s sub-salt and Cretaceous plays of the Faroe–Shetland margin. The sector's second act is now also the storage story: the same depleted fields and saline aquifers that made Britain's gas are being repurposed for carbon storage (the Acorn project in the Southern Gas Basin, the Northern Endurance Partnership under the North Sea for Edinburgh and Teesside), which makes the seal-capacity and pressure-history discipline of this lesson directly load-bearing for the [[earth-resources/geothermal-storage]] lesson that follows.
:::

## Preservation: Biodegradation, Water Washing, and Thermochemical Sulfate Reduction

::: definition How accumulations die: the preservation processes {#def-preservation}
A trap that charges is not automatically a field; the accumulation must survive its own geological future. **Biodegradation**: meteoric water carrying sulfate and microbes enters along regional aquifers from the outcrop and eats the crude in a predictable order — alkanes, then iso-alkanes, then aromatics, then resins (the 1993–1995 Peters–Stow and later Aepcks/Volkman bio-marker ladders; the 1993 Heinrich framework scaled the severity from 1 to 10) — leaving the heavy, sulfur-rich, high-viscosity oils of the Alberta tar sands (the biodegraded rim of the Western Canada sedimentary basin, the largest surface expression of the process, ~1.6 trillion barrels OOIP rendered heavy by Cretaceous-Paleogene seepage and microbial attack), the Orinoco belt, and the heavy rims of many Gulf of Mexico and Java fields. **Water washing** by meteoric or flushed connate water strips the light ends and salts; **gas-cap loss** by fault leakage shrinks a field's solution-gas and can leave a ring of asphalt. **Thermochemical sulfate reduction (TSR)** — sulfate in carbonates reacting with oil above ~140 °C to generate H2S and CO2 (the 1995–2000s Ohmoto–Krouse and Worden work; the giant sour fields of the Appalachian Clinton, the Kazhdumi-sourced sour oils of the Zagros, and the North Sea's Ekofisk, whose H2S arrived with Cretaceous charge) — destroys oil quality, spoils reservoirs with pyrite and anhydrite cement, and caps how deep a sour trend stays economic. The practical exploration consequence is a *preservation map*: outcrop distance of the seal, paleogeography of meteoric recharge, fault reactivation history and the burial-temperature curve together predict where an otherwise-charged basin keeps its oil light — and where it leaves only tar mats, sour gas, and seeps.
:::

::: warning The system can be right and the well still dry {#warn-system-right}
A perfect petroleum-system map does not guarantee a discovery, because three of the four processes are *time-critical in ways the elements are not*: charge can precede trap formation (the trap was a kitchen then — dry hole in a proven basin), seal can break after accumulation (a full trap that leaked — a dry hole with tar mats in the capillary fringe), and water can flush a shallow accumulation while the deep one survives. This is why modern exploration risk is dominated by *timing and preservation* probabilities rather than element-existence probabilities: in mature provinces, source, reservoir and seal are usually known, and the two factors that separate the 2015 Guyana success from the 1970s dry holes in the same water were imaging that resolved the fan geometry and a rock-physics argument that the fans were dry-charged — geometry and charge timing, not missing elements.
:::

::: quiz
A basin has mature source, good reservoir and seal, and a simple four-way dip closure on structure. The most likely remaining reason a first wildcat goes dry is:
- [ ] The reservoir has never been sampled, so its porosity is uncertain
- [x] Timing: the trap formed after peak charge, so the kitchen had already stopped expelling when the closure existed
- [ ] The seal is mudstone rather than salt, and mudstone cannot hold hydrocarbons
- [ ] Biodegradation has destroyed the entire column before drilling
::: solution
In a proven system with known elements, timing is the dominant residual risk: a closure that grew after the kitchen stopped charging (or was destroyed and re-formed after charge) is an empty trap in a working petroleum system. Mudstone seals hold most of the world's oil (entry pressure, not lithology, sets capacity); porosity uncertainty changes volumes, not presence; biodegradation rims a column, it does not erase a deep, freshly charged accumulation.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Two fields in the same basin produce oils of identical biomarker fingerprint, but Field A's oil is 14 °API, sulfur-rich and asphaltic while Field B's is 38 °API and sweet. The most consistent explanation is:
- [ ] Field A charges from a coaly source and Field B from an algal source
- [x] Field A's accumulation is being — or was — biodegraded by meteoric-water flush, while Field B was charged late and sealed deep
- [ ] Field B's oil is thermogenic and Field A's is abiotic
- [ ] Field A has a younger, less mature source rock
::: solution
Identical biomarker fingerprints mean the same source and kitchen; the difference is preservation. Severe biodegradation removes alkanes and light ends in a fixed order, raising density and sulfur concentration and leaving asphaltenes — exactly Field A's signature — while a late-charged, deeply buried, meteorically isolated accumulation (Field B) keeps its original quality. Source type would change the fingerprint; abiotic oil is not a commercial reality.
:::

### Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="220"}
Estimate STOIIP for a faulted rollover: gross 30 m, net-to-gross 0.8, area 10 km², porosity 0.25, water saturation 0.30, oil formation volume factor 1.2 res m³ per stock-tank m³ (use 6.29 bbl per stock-tank m³). Give the answer in million stock-tank barrels, rounded to the nearest 10 million.
::: solution
Bulk volume = 10e6 m² × 30 m = 300e6 m³. Net pore volume = 300e6 × 0.8 × 0.25 = 60e6 m³. Hydrocarbon pore volume = 60e6 × (1 − 0.30) = 42e6 res m³. Surface volume = 42e6 / 1.2 = 35e6 stock-tank m³ = 35e6 × 6.29 ≈ 220e6 bbl, so about 220 million STB.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.27"}
A wildcat's independent chances are: source 0.8, reservoir 0.7, seal 0.8, trap 0.75, timing 0.8. What is the overall probability of success, to two decimal places?
::: solution
POS = 0.8 × 0.7 × 0.8 × 0.75 × 0.8 = 0.2688, so about 0.27 — a one-in-four well, which is typical of a mature-province exploration prospect and already above frontier wildcat odds.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="204"}
A mudstone seal has a capillary entry pressure of 0.5 MPa. With oil density 800 kg/m³ and formation water 1050 kg/m³ (g = 9.81 m/s²), what is the maximum oil column the seal can hold, in metres? Round to the nearest metre.
::: solution
h = Pc / ((ρw − ρo) g) = 500000 / (250 × 9.81) = 500000 / 2452.5 ≈ 204 m of oil column — enough for most structural traps, but a reminder of why salt (entry pressures in the MPa range) underlies the giant fields.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="340"}
A prospect with POS 0.2 and mean recoverable 100 million barrels costs 60 million dollars to drill. If the explorer values recoverable oil at 20 dollars per barrel in the ground, what is the prospect's expected net value in millions of dollars?
::: solution
Expected gross value = 0.2 × 100e6 × 20 = 400 million dollars; net of the 60-million-dollar well, expected value = 340 million dollars. Positive expected value with 80 % failure odds is the normal state of exploration portfolios — variance, not certainty, is what you are buying.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="175"}
A stacked-sand field has STOIIP 500 million barrels and a recovery factor of 0.35 under waterflood. How many million barrels are recoverable?
::: solution
Recoverable = 500 × 0.35 = 175 million barrels. Recovery factors of 0.3–0.4 are typical of good waterflooded sandstones; the gap between 175 and 500 is the "energy penalty" that primary depletion leaves behind and that gas injection, WAG and EOR fight over.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="3.6"}
A basin's surface temperature is 10 °C and its burial geothermal gradient is 25 °C/km. At what depth (km, to one decimal) does the 100 °C isotherm — mid-oil-window by kinetics — lie?
::: solution
Depth = (100 − 10) / 25 = 3.6 km. At a hotter 35 °C/km gradient the same isotherm sits at 2.6 km, which is why volcanic-margin and rifted basins mature their sources early and shallow — and why the same source rock can be oil-prone in one half of a basin and gas-dead in the other.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3.8"}
Ghawar produced about 3.8 million barrels per day in the mid-2020s against world consumption of about 100 million barrels per day. What percentage of world supply did this single field provide? Give the number to one decimal place.
::: solution
3.8 / 100 = 0.038, so 3.8 % — a scale of concentration with no parallel outside national oil companies: one field, one structure on one arch, supplied about one twenty-sixth of everything burned, which is the empirical content of the "giants are found in provinces, and the provinces are all found" argument.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="36667"}
The 2010 Macondo blowout released an estimated 3.19 million barrels over 87 days. What was the average release rate in barrels per day? Round to the nearest thousand.
::: solution
3190000 / 87 ≈ 36667 bbl/day — roughly 1.5 % of US output at the time, sustained for three months, and the number that made blowout frequency, not well cost, a board-level metric worldwide.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3"}
A kitchen charges a spill-point cascade with 500 million barrels of oil. Five traps lie up-dip in spill order, each with capacity 150 million barrels; spills from each fill the next. How many traps fill completely?
::: solution
500 / 150 = 3.33, so three traps fill and spill (3 × 150 = 450) and the fourth holds the remaining 50 million barrels — the fill-and-spill arithmetic that makes mature fairways a downstream-to-upstream sequence of full giants, one half-full trap, and empty upslope structures.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
A petroleum system is nine things at once — source, reservoir, seal, trap, pathways, and the generation, migration, accumulation and preservation that must interlock in time — and the concept's power is that any single missing element falsifies the whole basin while every present element merely raises the odds. Generation is kinetics, not depth: kerogen type sets what is made, time–temperature dose sets when, and the kitchen is a moving place that traps must anticipate rather than a contour to drill to. Migration is buoyancy focused by water flow along spill-point cascades, and it concentrates an entire basin's output into a handful of fairways — which is why 90 % of the oil sits in a dozen source-rock families and two dozen provinces, from the Cretaceous marine shales of West Africa and the Kazhdumi–Mahanet pair of the Middle East to the Kimmeridge Clay, the lacustrine pre-salt lakes, and the coal-measure gas kitchens. Exploration is portfolio arithmetic under POS multiplication, won by play concepts (Guyana's dry-fan bet on a source-rock analogy) and lost by timing and preservation (charge before trap, seal break, microbial and thermochemical destruction of what was found). The system's hazards are real — Macondo's 36,667 barrels per day for 87 days, seepage that leaks most of what is generated — and its gifts are finite: the giant-oil province era is over, the frontier is now deep, sub-salt, sub-basalt and unconventional-continuous, and the same seals, aquifers and depleted reservoirs that held hydrocarbons for fifty million years are the assets now being re-permitted for carbon, hydrogen and air. The next lesson turns the production side of this story into its resource and reserve arithmetic; the one after that turns the pore space itself into the resource.
:::
