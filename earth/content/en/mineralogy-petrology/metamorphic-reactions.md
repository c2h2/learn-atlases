A metamorphic rock is a record of an environment that no longer exists. Its minerals grew in the solid state, at depth, under pressures and temperatures that the rock has since travelled back through, and they preserve both the conditions of their growth and, if they are lucky, the path between them. The whole discipline rests on a single fact established in the last chapter: for a given bulk composition, a given pressure and temperature permit exactly one equilibrium assemblage, and that assemblage can be read from the rock. Metamorphic petrology is therefore a decoding exercise, and the reactions that make it possible are of two kinds — those that depend on pressure and temperature in opposite ways, which give the axes, and those that depend on one alone, which give the calibration.

## Facies, isograds and index minerals

::: definition Metamorphism, protolith and facies {#def-metamorphism}
**Metamorphism** is the mineralogical and textural change of a rock in the solid state, driven by changes of pressure, temperature and fluid composition, at conditions below those at which it melts and below the range of diagenesis. The original rock is the **protolith**. A **metamorphic facies** is a set of assemblages that can form from protoliths of any composition within a given range of pressure and temperature; the boundaries between facies are therefore reaction surfaces in $P$–$T$ space, not arbitrary lines. A **facies series** is the sequence of facies a terrain passes through along a prograde path, and it names the geodynamic setting: a Buchan series is hot and low-pressure, an Abukuma series is very hot and very low-pressure, a Barrovian series is intermediate, and a Sanbagawa series is cold and high-pressure.
:::

::: definition Isograd, index mineral and zone {#def-isograd}
An **isograd** is the surface in a metamorphic terrain at which a particular mineral first appears in a particular bulk composition; the region between two isograds is a **zone**, and the mineral that defines it is the **index mineral**. Isograds are reaction surfaces, so they are three-dimensional, they are folded with their host, and — critically — they occur at different positions in rocks of different bulk composition. The Barrovian sequence in pelitic rock, from low to high grade, is **chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite**, and it remains the standard field subdivision of any Barrovian terrain.
:::

::: warning An isograd is not a contour of temperature
The temptation is to read the first appearance of garnet as a $500^\circ\mathrm{C}$ isotherm. It is not. An isograd is the trace of a reaction, and a reaction depends on pressure, on the activity of water, on the bulk composition of the rock, and on whether the rock actually reached equilibrium. In practice the garnet isograd in a pelite and the actinolite–hornblende isograd in an adjacent metabasite do not coincide, because they are different reactions with different slopes; and in a terrain with rapid uplift the isograds are systematically lower in temperature than the facies would suggest, because the rock did not have time to catch up. Isograds map the *rock's* history, not the field's temperature.
:::

## The reactions that matter

::: example The aluminium silicate triple point {#ex-alsiv}
The three polymorphs of $\mathrm{Al_2SiO_5}$ — andalusite, kyanite and sillimanite — define the most famous univariant diagram in geology. The triple point lies at about $501^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $3.83\ \mathrm{kbar}$, and three univariant curves radiate from it, each with a slope of order ten bar per kelvin. Sketch the diagram and read off the three paths.
::: solution
$$
\text{andalusite} \rightleftharpoons \text{kyanite} \rightleftharpoons \text{sillimanite}
$$
Andalusite occupies the low-pressure corner, kyanite the high-pressure corner, sillimanite the high-temperature corner. Kyanite is the densest polymorph, with a molar volume of $45.0\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ against andalusite's $51.5$ and sillimanite's $50.2$, and the phase rule allows only one of them to be stable at any pressure and temperature.

Three paths, three settings. A **low-pressure, high-temperature** path — contact aureole, extensional terrain, Buchan or Abukuma facies — crosses the andalusite–sillimanite boundary and gives andalusite then sillimanite, with kyanite never appearing. A **Barrovian** path starts in the andalusite field and crosses into sillimanite at higher grade, again without kyanite. A **subduction-zone** path enters the kyanite field, and if it is warm enough it may then cross into sillimanite at the top of the eclogite facies. Finding kyanite in a metamorphic terrain therefore means the rock reached several kilobar more pressure than the andalusite field allowed — above roughly $6\ \mathrm{kbar}$ at $500^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and above $10\ \mathrm{kbar}$ at blueschist-facies temperatures — whatever its later history. Finding andalusite and sillimanite together with no kyanite, and no relict of any kind, means it never did.

Because all three boundaries have slopes of only about ten bar per kelvin, a boundary crossed on a heating path records a temperature far more sharply than a pressure: a $50\ \mathrm{K}$ uncertainty moves the pressure estimate by half a kilobar, whereas the same half-kilobar uncertainty moves the temperature estimate by only $50\ \mathrm{K}$.
:::
:::

::: example The three isograds that proved isograds are reactions {#ex-three-isograds}
In the Sanbagawa and Shuksan belts, three isograds in metabasic rock — the lawsonite–clinozoisite isograd (LCC), the albite–epidote–quartz isograd (AZO) and the anorthite–epidote isograd (AO) — lie within a few hundred metres of one another over tens of kilometres, in rock types as different as greenschist and amphibolite, and they occur at the same structural level in terrains that experienced pressures from 4 to 12 kbar. What does that require of the reactions?
::: solution
It requires that all three reactions have a **very large $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T$**, so that on a pressure–temperature diagram they are nearly vertical lines and they occur at essentially the same temperature whatever the pressure. And that is what they have: LCC, AZO and AO are net-transfer reactions in which the volume change $\Delta V$ and the entropy change $\Delta S$ are both very small and happen to give a ratio $\Delta S/\Delta V$ that is tiny — a slope of hundreds of bar per kelvin. All three therefore lie within a few tens of degrees of one another, near $450$–$500^\circ\mathrm{C}$, over the whole pressure range of the blueschist and amphibolite facies.

The observation was decisive historically. If isograds were merely the contours along which a mineral became stable in some general sense, they would not coincide in three dimensions across changing lithology and changing pressure. That they do is direct field proof that they are equilibrium reaction surfaces, and that metamorphic rocks really do record equilibrium conditions. The corollary is a useful tool: a metabasite that contains lawsonite has been below about $500^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at whatever pressure it reached, and one that contains clinozoisite without lawsonite has been above it. The pair is a thermometer that works in rocks too fine-grained for any mineral chemistry.
:::
:::

::: example Decarbonation and the fluid buffer {#ex-dqf}
In impure dolomitic limestone at low pressure the reaction
$$
2\,\mathrm{dolomite} + \mathrm{quartz} \longrightarrow \mathrm{forsterite} + 2\,\mathrm{calcite} + 2\,\mathrm{CO_2}
$$
(the DQF reaction) has a strong dependence on the composition of the fluid. At $700^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and 1 kbar it is in equilibrium with a fluid containing about 60 % $\mathrm{CO_2}$. What does a rock containing forsterite + calcite + CO₂-free fluid tell you, and why are marbles such good recorders of fluid flow?
::: solution
The reaction is a **buffer on fluid composition**. If the fluid passing through the rock is more $\mathrm{CO_2}$-rich than the equilibrium value, the reaction runs to the left and consumes carbon dioxide by making dolomite; if it is more water-rich, the reaction runs to the right and releases carbon dioxide until the equilibrium ratio is restored. A rock of this bulk composition therefore cannot record the fluid's original composition, only the buffered one — and that is exactly why it records something better: the fact that fluid flowed, and in what direction.

Marbles are the best natural recorders of channelised fluid flow because decarbonation reactions release $\mathrm{CO_2}$ in large amounts and are extremely sensitive to the fluid's $\mathrm{CO_2}$ activity. A forsterite-bearing marble a few metres wide, surrounded by dolomite + quartz marble that has not reacted, means that a water-rich fluid was focused through that channel and reacted to completion; the width of the reacted zone, divided by the total flux, gives the permeability of the rock to that fluid. Where the same stratigraphy is unreacted over kilometres, the permeability was negligible and the metamorphic fluid moved somewhere else entirely.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A pelite contains andalusite overgrown by sillimanite, and no kyanite at any stage. What does that record?
- [ ] Burial to more than 10 kbar followed by exhumation.
- [x] Heating at low pressure, below about 4 kbar throughout.
- [ ] Heating and simultaneous pressurisation along a subduction path.
- [ ] The rock never reached equilibrium, so nothing can be inferred.
::: solution
Andalusite then sillimanite with no kyanite at all — not even as a relict core — means the rock stayed on the low-pressure side of the kyanite field throughout, that is below about 4 kbar, while its temperature rose through the andalusite–sillimanite boundary. This is the classic low-pressure, high-temperature path of a Buchan or Abukuma terrain, and it is the opposite of a subduction or collisional-burial path.
:::
:::

## Thermometers and barometers

::: definition Exchange reactions and the distribution coefficient {#def-exchange}
An **exchange reaction** swaps one cation for another between two phases without changing the number of moles of any component:
$$
\mathrm{Mg_3Al_2Si_3O_{12}} + \mathrm{Fe\text{-}biotite} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Fe_3Al_2Si_3O_{12}} + \mathrm{Mg\text{-}biotite}.
$$
Because the reaction involves no change in the number of gas molecules and only a small volume change, its equilibrium constant depends on temperature and only weakly on pressure — which is what makes it a thermometer. The measured quantity is the **distribution coefficient**
$$
K_D^{\mathrm{Fe\text{-}Mg}} = \frac{(\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{Mg})^{\text{garnet}}}{(\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{Mg})^{\text{biotite}}} .
$$
:::

::: example The garnet–biotite thermometer {#ex-gtbi}
A simplified calibration of the garnet–biotite exchange thermometer, fitted to the experimental and natural data for the reaction above, is
$$
\ln K_D^{\mathrm{Fe\text{-}Mg}} = \frac{1166}{T} - 0.410 ,
$$
with $T$ in kelvin. Garnet from a pelitic gneiss has $X_{\mathrm{Mg}} = 0.25$ and coexisting biotite has $X_{\mathrm{Mg}} = 0.42$. Find the peak-metamorphism temperature, and estimate the effect of a $0.02$ error in each analysis.
::: solution
$$
(\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{Mg})^{\text{gt}} = \frac{0.75}{0.25} = 3.00, \qquad
(\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{Mg})^{\text{bi}} = \frac{0.58}{0.42} = 1.381, \qquad
K_D = \frac{3.00}{1.381} = 2.17 .
$$
Then
$$
\frac{1166}{T} = \ln 2.17 + 0.410 = 0.776 + 0.410 = 1.186
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T = 983\ \mathrm{K} = 710^\circ\mathrm{C},
$$
which is upper-amphibolite-facies, consistent with a sillimanite-bearing pelite.

For the error, differentiate: $\mathrm{d}T/T = -\mathrm{d}(\ln K_D)\,T/1166$, so a $\pm 0.02$ error in $X_{\mathrm{Mg}}$ of the garnet changes $\ln K_D$ by about $0.02/0.25 = 0.08$ and the temperature by
$$
\Delta T \approx \frac{0.08\times983}{1166} = 67\ \mathrm{K}.
$$
Two such errors, in the same direction, give $130\ \mathrm{K}$. That is the honest precision of a single-pair garnet–biotite temperature, and it is why published metamorphic temperatures are quoted to the nearest $10\ \mathrm{K}$ and should be read to the nearest $50\ \mathrm{K}$. The systematic errors — ferric iron in biotite, the calcium content of garnet, and re-equilibration during cooling — are larger still, which is why the best studies report a grid of garnet core-to-rim profiles against biotite rather than a single number.
:::
:::

::: proposition Net-transfer reactions as barometers {#prop-barometer}
A **net-transfer reaction** changes the number of moles of each phase, and typically has a large $\Delta V$. By the Clausius–Clapeyron relation
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta V},
$$
such a reaction has a steep positive slope when $\Delta V$ is large and $\Delta S$ modest, and its equilibrium therefore depends strongly on pressure. Two examples anchor the pressure scale of metamorphism:
$$
\mathrm{albite} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{jadeite} + \mathrm{quartz}, \qquad
\mathrm{plagioclase} + \mathrm{olivine} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{spinel} + \mathrm{clinopyroxene}.
$$
The first lies near $20\ \mathrm{kbar}$ at blueschist-facies temperatures and is the reason jadeite and aragonite in a metabasite mean subduction to depths greater than 40 km. The second lies near $10\ \mathrm{kbar}$ and is the reason a garnet peridotite with no plagioclase came from below 30 km.
:::

::: example Reading a facies series {#ex-facies-series}
Two terrains both reach sillimanite–K-feldspar grade in pelitic rock. Terrain A preserves kyanite in its lowest zone and staurolite nowhere. Terrain B preserves andalusite throughout, with cordierite and spinel at the highest grade, and its metabasites contain two-pyroxene hornfels. Give the facies series, the approximate pressure range and the setting of each.
::: solution
Both terrains reached about $700$–$750^\circ\mathrm{C}$, since both crossed the muscovite + quartz = K-feldspar + sillimanite + water reaction. The pressure separates them.

**Terrain A** is **amphibolite to granulite facies at 8–11 kbar**: a classic **Barrovian** series, generated by crustal thickening during collision, with the kyanite recording the high-pressure peak and the absence of staurolite recording either a higher peak temperature or a staurolite-consuming reaction crossed on the way up. The heat arrives slowly, by thickening and by the diffusion of heat from below, so the terrain takes tens of millions of years to warm and the isograds are smooth.

**Terrain B** is **hornblende hornfels to sanidinite facies at 1–3 kbar**: an **Abukuma** or contact-metamorphic series. Cordierite and spinel at low pressure and high temperature, two-pyroxene hornfels in the metabasite, and andalusite rather than kyanite are all low-pressure indicators. The heat came from an intrusion, the aureole is narrow, and the duration is $10^4$–$10^5$ years rather than $10^7$.

The two terrains are distinguished in the field in an afternoon, and they record opposite geodynamic regimes: crust that was buried and crust that was cooked.
:::

## Time, and the width of an aureole

::: example How wide can a contact aureole be? {#ex-aureole}
A granite at $800^\circ\mathrm{C}$ intrudes country rock at $200^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Take the thermal diffusivity of the rock as $\kappa = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. How wide is the contact aureole after $10^4$ years, and after $10^6$ years? What does the answer imply about the sillimanite aureoles of the Barrovian type, which are tens of kilometres wide?
::: solution
Heat spreads by conduction over a diffusion length
$$
L = \sqrt{\kappa t}.
$$
After $10^4$ years ($3.2\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{s}$), $L = \sqrt{3.2\times10^{5}} = 560\ \mathrm{m}$. After $10^6$ years ($3.2\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{s}$), $L = \sqrt{3.2\times10^{7}} = 5.7\ \mathrm{km}$.

Conduction therefore makes aureoles that are hundreds of metres to a few kilometres wide, which is exactly what is observed around most granites. It cannot make an aureole fifty kilometres wide in the time available: that would require $t = L^2/\kappa = (5\times10^{4})^2/10^{-6} = 2.5\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s}$, some eighty million years of perfectly steady heating by a single pluton. Wide regional metamorphic zones are therefore **not** contact aureoles, and were never plausibly explained by plutonic heat. They record the thickening of the crust itself, with heat arriving by the slow upward conduction of the geothermal gradient through a crust that has been doubled, plus the advection of heat by partial melts and by fluids. This was the argument that turned regional metamorphism into a tectonic problem, and it is a clean example of a back-of-the-envelope diffusion calculation settling a hundred-year controversy.
:::
:::

::: history
The systematic study of metamorphic zones began with the Alpine geologists reading their own mountains: the Barrovian sequence was established by George Barrow (Barrow) in the Scottish Highlands between 1893 and 1912, purely from field occurrence of index minerals and without any thermometry at all, and Pentecarl's parallel work in the Pyrenees and Becke's in the Alps gave the same result independently. The phase-rule interpretation came from the petrographic schools of the 1920s and 1930s, and the facies concept itself from Pentti Eskola (Eskola) in 1920, who defined facies from the rock rather than from the conditions and thereby made the whole subject field-mappable. The decisive turn was experimental: the reversal of the $\mathrm{Al_2SiO_5}$ equilibria in a pressure vessel, the calculation of the first metamorphic reactions from thermodynamic data by Ramberg, and then, from the 1960s, the programme of measuring equilibrium constants in piston-cylinder apparatus and combining them with solution models. The modern era of quantitative thermobarometry dates from the 1970s and 1980s — the garnet–biotite thermometer of Ferry and Chacko, the garnet–aluminium silicate–quartz barometer, the development of internally consistent thermodynamic datasets by Berman, Holland and Powell, and the software that turns a bulk composition into a calculated phase diagram. The last of these changed the practice of the subject more than any single reaction: a petrologist now computes a pressure–temperature–composition section for the exact rock in hand rather than borrowing a diagram from a textbook.
:::

The thermodynamics behind these reactions is in [[geochemistry/reactions]]; the use of the resulting pressure–temperature paths to reconstruct orogeny is the next chapter, [[mineralogy-petrology/pt-paths]]; the settings that generate hot and cold metamorphism are in [[mineralogy-petrology/igneous-settings]] and [[structural-geology/orogens]]; and the granulites and eclogites of the deep crust are exposed for study in the terrains described in [[geomorphology/landscape-evolution]].

::: summary
- A metamorphic facies is a set of assemblages stable over a range of pressure and temperature, and its boundaries are reaction surfaces, not conventions.
- An isograd is the trace of one reaction in one rock type; it is not an isotherm, and different isograds in different lithologies do not have to coincide.
- Exchange reactions have small $\Delta V$ and are thermometers; net-transfer reactions have large $\Delta V$ and are barometers.
- The aluminium silicate polymorphs define the pressure scale of metamorphism, with kyanite requiring more than about 11 kbar.
- The lawsonite, albite–epidote–quartz and anorthite–epidote isograds in metabasites occur at nearly the same temperature at all pressures, which is the field proof that isograds are reactions.
- Decarbonation reactions buffer fluid composition, which makes marbles recorders of fluid flow rather than of fluid provenance.
- Conduction limits contact aureoles to a few kilometres; regional metamorphic zones require crustal thickening.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Facies and assemblage {level=1}
A pelite contains garnet + staurolite + muscovite + quartz, and a metabasite from the same outcrop contains actinolite + chlorite + albite + epidote. Are these consistent with one another, and what facies are they?
::: solution
Yes — both are **amphibolite facies** (the metabasite at its lower boundary, in the epidote–amphibolite subfacies). Garnet + staurolite in a pelite is upper greenschist to amphibolite facies, and actinolite + epidote + albite in a metabasite is the same conditions. The point of the exercise is that facies are defined so that rocks of completely different composition, in the same place, give the same answer; if they do not, either the rocks are not in equilibrium or they are not the same age.
:::
:::

::: exercise Polymorph density {level=1 check="kyanite"}
Which $\mathrm{Al_2SiO_5}$ polymorph is the densest, and why is it the diagnostic mineral of subduction zones?
::: solution
**Kyanite**, at $45.0\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$ against andalusite's $51.5$ and sillimanite's $50.2$. The densest polymorph is stable at highest pressure, and the pressures required — above about $6\ \mathrm{kbar}$ at $500^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and above $10\ \mathrm{kbar}$ in the colder parts of a subduction zone — are reached at 25–40 km depth beneath a cold, fast-subducting slab. Kyanite therefore marks a rock that was cold *and* deep, which is the definition of a subduction-zone environment.
:::
:::

::: exercise Balanced reactions {level=1 check="2"}
Balance the reaction $\mathrm{Al_2Si_2O_5(OH)_4} \to \mathrm{Al_2SiO_5} + \mathrm{SiO_2} + n\,\mathrm{H_2O}$. What is $n$?
::: solution
$n = 2$. Kaolinite $\mathrm{Al_2Si_2O_5(OH)_4}$ has four hydroxyls, which make two waters; the remaining $\mathrm{Al_2SiO_5}$ and $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ account for the two aluminium, two silicon and nine oxygen. The reaction is the key isograd in high-alumina pelites and meta-arkoses, occurring near $380^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at 2 kbar, and it is nearly pressure-independent in the same way as the metabasite isograds.
:::
:::

::: exercise Garnet–biotite {level=2 check="565"}
Garnet with $X_{\mathrm{Mg}} = 0.20$ coexists with biotite of $X_{\mathrm{Mg}} = 0.40$. Using the calibration of [[#ex-gtbi]], find the temperature in $^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
::: solution
$K_D = (0.80/0.20)/(0.60/0.40) = 4.00/1.50 = 2.67$. Then $1166/T = \ln 2.67 + 0.410 = 0.981 + 0.410 = 1.391$, so $T = 838\ \mathrm{K} = 565^\circ\mathrm{C}$. This is the garnet-isograd grade, and the answer is sensibly just above the $500$–$550^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at which the biotite-in and garnet-in reactions occur in pelites at moderate pressure.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which reaction is which {level=2}
Classify as thermometer or barometer, and say why: (a) $\mathrm{Mg}$–$\mathrm{Fe}$ exchange between garnet and clinopyroxene; (b) $\mathrm{albite} = \mathrm{jadeite} + \mathrm{quartz}$; (c) the anorthite content of plagioclase in equilibrium with garnet, quartz and aluminosilicate (GASP).
::: solution
(a) **Thermometer.** An exchange reaction has almost no volume change, so its equilibrium is nearly pressure-independent and dominated by the entropy term. (b) **Barometer.** One mole of solid becomes two, with a large positive $\Delta V$; the equilibrium moves strongly with pressure and is the standard high-pressure indicator in metabasites. (c) **Barometer**, in principle: GASP is a net-transfer reaction involving four phases and a large $\Delta V$. In practice it is very sensitive to the anorthite content of plagioclase, which is hard to measure and easily changed by later diffusion, which is why GASP pressures carry larger errors than the garnet–biotite temperature does.
:::
:::

::: exercise Aureole width {level=2 check="5"}
Using $\kappa = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, how long does conduction take to heat rock $4\ \mathrm{km}$ from a pluton? Give your answer in units of $10^5$ years.
::: solution
$t = L^2/\kappa = (4000)^2/10^{-6} = 1.6\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{s}$, which is $1.6\times10^{13}/3.16\times10^{7} = 5.1\times10^{5}$ years. Half a million years is long enough for a pluton to remain hot, so kilometre-scale aureoles are conduction-consistent — but note the square. Doubling the width requires four times the time, which is why aureole width is such a poor thermometer and such a good clock.
:::
:::

::: exercise Retrograde textures {level=2}
A granulite-facies garnet is surrounded by a symplectite of clinopyroxene + plagioclase, and the rock's peak assemblage included orthopyroxene + sillimanite + K-feldspar + quartz. What happened, and what does the symplectite record?
::: solution
The rock was brought up from granulite-facies conditions (about 9 kbar, $800^\circ\mathrm{C}$) fast enough that the peak assemblage was preserved in the cores of grains but the garnet reacted at its rim on decompression. The symplectite is the product of a reaction of the type
$$
\mathrm{garnet} + \mathrm{quartz} \longrightarrow \mathrm{clinopyroxene} + \mathrm{plagioclase},
$$
which becomes stable at lower pressure, and its intergrown texture records growth by simultaneous nucleation of two phases at a moving boundary — a texture that forms only when the reaction is far from equilibrium, that is, when the rock was cooling or decompressing quickly. Symplectites are therefore not merely a curiosity: they are the field evidence for **exhumation rate**, and the composition of the symplectite phases gives the pressure at which decompression was recorded, which is one end of the pressure–temperature path of [[mineralogy-petrology/pt-paths]].
:::
:::

::: exercise The pressure of a facies {level=3}
Blueschist-facies rocks contain glaucophane, lawsonite, aragonite and jadeite. Estimate the pressure and temperature range, explain why the facies is confined to a narrow band in the Earth, and explain why it is so rarely older than the Mesozoic.
::: solution
The assemblage requires roughly **12–25 kbar and 300–500 °C**: high pressure and low temperature. Jadeite + quartz in place of albite fixes the lower bound near 10–12 kbar; aragonite in place of calcite requires above about 3 kbar at these temperatures and is preserved metastably at the surface; lawsonite survives only below about 500 °C; and glaucophane stability defines the field.

The facies is confined because it requires a geothermal gradient of $3$–$7\ ^\circ\mathrm{C\,km^{-1}}$, against a global average of about $25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C\,km^{-1}}$ in the continental crust. Only a subducting slab, cold in its core and carried down faster than it can warm, provides that. Blueschists are therefore a diagnostic and exclusive product of cold subduction, and their distribution in time is a measurement of when the Earth began to subduct cold lithosphere: the oldest reported blueschists are Palaeoproterozoic, about 1.9 billion years old and much debated, and the rock becomes common only in the last 700 million years. That scarcity in the older record is one of the principal lines of evidence that modern-style, cold plate tectonics is a late phenomenon in a progressively cooling Earth.
:::
:::

::: exercise Derive the Clausius–Clapeyron slope {level=3}
Starting from the equality of Gibbs free energy between two phases along an equilibrium boundary, derive $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T = \Delta S/\Delta V$, and explain why a reaction with $\Delta V > 0$ and $\Delta S > 0$ has a positive slope and why most dehydration reactions have steep positive slopes.
::: solution
Along an equilibrium boundary the reaction is at equilibrium, so $\Delta G = 0$ at every point on it. Moving from one point to a neighbouring point,
$$
\mathrm{d}(\Delta G) = 0 = -\Delta S\,\mathrm{d}T + \Delta V\,\mathrm{d}P,
$$
using $\mathrm{d}G = -S\,\mathrm{d}T + V\,\mathrm{d}P$ for each phase and subtracting. Rearranging gives $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T = \Delta S/\Delta V$.

A reaction with both positive has a positive slope: heating favours the high-entropy phase, and pressure must rise to compensate by favouring the low-volume phase. Dehydration reactions have steep positive slopes because $\Delta V$ is dominated by the large molar volume of the released water — hundreds of $\mathrm{cm^3}$ — while $\Delta S$ is dominated by the entropy of that same water, which at 5 kbar and $600^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is large but not enormous. The ratio comes out at $5$–$15\ \mathrm{bar\,K^{-1}}$, so a $100\ \mathrm{K}$ change in the estimated temperature of a dehydration isograd moves the pressure by only $0.5$–$1.5\ \mathrm{kbar}$. That is why dehydration isograds are good thermometers and poor barometers, and why the pressure of a metamorphic terrain must come from a net-transfer reaction instead.
:::

::: exercise Metamorphism and time {level=3}
A terrain shows a prograde Barrovian sequence overprinted by a low-pressure, high-temperature assemblage, and the second event is dated 25 million years after the first. Propose a tectonic history and list the four observations that would test it.
::: solution
The classic reading is **collision, then collapse**. Crustal thickening during convergence buries the terrain to 9–11 kbar and produces the Barrovian assemblages over 30–40 million years. Thickening becomes gravitational instability: the lithosphere delaminates or the root is convectively removed, the crust extends, and upwelling hot rock and syn- to post-tectonic granites cook the already-thickened section from below, producing andalusite, cordierite and migmatite at 2–4 kbar while the rock is being exhumed. The 25-million-year gap is the time for thickening to reach its maximum and for the lithospheric instability to develop.

The four tests. **(i) Pressure–temperature paths** from garnet zoning and thermobarometry: the second event must be clockwise in $P$–$T$ space — pressure falling while temperature rises — which is the signature of exhumation with heating, as opposed to the counter-clockwise path of simple burial and erosion. **(ii) Structure:** the low-pressure assemblages must be associated with extensional shear zones and syn-tectonic plutons of the right age. **(iii) Geophysics and sedimentology:** the foreland basin must show a pulse of rapid subsidence and then uplift, and the geochronology of the detritus must record the exhumation. **(iv) Magmatism:** a flare-up of potassic to peraluminous granites at the age of the second event, with the isotopic signature of lower-crustal melting. Any one of these is suggestive; all four together are a demonstration.
:::
