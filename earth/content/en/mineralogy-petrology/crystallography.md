In 1669 Nicolaus Steno, a Danish anatomy student in Florence, noticed that a crystal of quartz may be large or small, fat or thin, perfect or broken, but that the angle between any two of its corresponding faces is always the same. In 1783 René-Just Haüy, a schoolmaster with a mineral specimen he had dropped on the floor of the Cabinet du Roi, noticed that a crystal of Iceland spar breaks only into rhombohedra, however awkwardly you strike it, and that every rhombohedron has the same angles as every other. Between those two observations lies the discovery that the crystal has an internal structure, and that the structure is a **lattice**: a pattern repeated in three dimensions, whose possible geometries can be enumerated as a matter of logic, before anyone looks at a crystal at all. There are fourteen lattices, thirty-two point groups and two hundred and thirty space groups, and there will never be a fifteenth, a thirty-third or a two-hundred-and-thirty-first.

::: definition Lattices, cells and systems {#def-lattice}
A **space lattice** is an infinite array of points with identical surroundings, generated by three non-coplanar translation vectors $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{c}$. The **unit cell** is the parallelepiped they span, and its geometry is specified by the edge lengths $a$, $b$, $c$ and the interaxial angles $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$. Seven metric systems exhaust the possibilities:

| system | metric | lattice types | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| cubic | $a=b=c$, all $90^\circ$ | P, I, F | halite, garnet, diamond |
| tetragonal | $a=a\ne c$, all $90^\circ$ | P, I | zircon, rutile |
| hexagonal | $a=a\ne c$, $\gamma=120^\circ$ | P | apatite, beryl, graphite |
| trigonal | $a=b=c$, $\alpha=\beta=\gamma\ne90^\circ$ | P (rhombohedral) | calcite, dolomite, cinnabar |
| orthorhombic | $a\ne b\ne c$, all $90^\circ$ | P, C, I, F | olivine, topaz, aragonite |
| monoclinic | $a\ne b\ne c$, $\beta\ne90^\circ$ | P, C | gypsum, orthoclase, augite, mica |
| triclinic | all unequal, all oblique | P | albite, microcline, kyanite |

Adding the possible centring and symmetry gives $14$ Bravais lattices, $32$ crystal classes (point groups), and $230$ space groups. Of the roughly six thousand known mineral species, something like a third are monoclinic and a fifth are orthorhombic; only a handful of common minerals are cubic, and none is known to be quasicrystalline except a natural quasicrystal found in the Khatyrka meteorite in 2009, with fifty-fold diffraction symmetry and no periodic lattice at all.
:::

::: proposition The crystallographic restriction {#prop-restriction}
A rotation axis in a periodic lattice can have order only $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$ or $6$. Suppose an $n$-fold axis of rotation through a lattice point $A$, and an identical axis through a neighbouring point $B$ at the shortest lattice distance $t$. Rotating $B$ about $A$ through $\theta = 2\pi/n$ and $A$ about $B$ through $-\theta$ generates two lattice points whose separation $t'$ along the line $AB$ must be an integral multiple of $t$:
$$
t' = t - 2t\cos\theta = m\,t \Longrightarrow 2\cos\theta = 1-m,
$$
an integer. Since $|2\cos\theta| \le 2$, the only possibilities are $2\cos\theta = 2, 1, 0, -1, -2$, giving $\theta = 0, 60, 90, 120, 180$ degrees and $n = 1, 6, 4, 3, 2$.
:::

::: proof
The argument is due to Frankel (1831) and is reproduced in every crystallography text since. Read it the other way: a five-fold axis would require $2\cos 72^\circ = 0.618$ to be an integer, which it is not, so **no periodic arrangement of points can have five-fold symmetry**. This is why a snowflake has six arms and never five, why the rose window of a cathedral and the rosette of a mineral differ in kind and not merely in craftsmanship, and why the discovery in 1982 by Dan Shechtman of an alloy with sharp diffraction spots and icosahedral symmetry — five-fold axes and all — was at first unpublishable, was ridiculed for two years, and won the Nobel Prize of 2011. Quasicrystals are not periodic; they are ordered, and their diffraction requires six indices rather than three. In minerals they are so far known from one meteorite, but their existence closes an argument that ran for a century. ∎
:::

::: example Miller indices, and the angle between faces {#ex-miller}
A face of a cubic crystal cuts the axes at $1a$, $-2b$ and $2c$. Index it. Then find the angle between $(111)$ and $(1\bar1 1)$ in a cubic cell, and between the same indices in a tetragonal cell with $c/a = 2$.
::: solution
The reciprocals of the intercepts are $1$, $-\tfrac12$, $\tfrac12$; clearing fractions gives $(2\bar1 1)$. Faces are written in round brackets, a form or set of symmetry-equivalent faces in curly, directions in square, and a family of directions in angle brackets; a bar over a digit means a negative index, and in the hexagonal system a fourth index $i = -(h+k)$ is added so that the three equivalent horizontal axes are treated alike.

For a cubic cell the angle between two faces follows from the dot product of the normals,
$$
\cos\phi = \frac{h_1h_2 + k_1k_2 + l_1l_2}{\sqrt{h_1^2+k_1^2+l_1^2}\sqrt{h_2^2+k_2^2+l_2^2}} = \frac{1-1+1}{\sqrt3\sqrt3} = \frac{1}{3},
$$
so $\phi = 70.53^\circ$ — the angle between the faces of an octahedron, and the reason an octahedral crystal of magnetite or spinel looks the way it does. In a tetragonal cell the normals are not simply $(h,k,l)$; the correct expression is
$$
\cos\phi = \frac{h_1h_2+k_1k_2 + c^2l_1l_2/a^2}{\sqrt{h_1^2+k_1^2+c^2l_1^2/a^2}\sqrt{h_2^2+k_2^2+c^2l_2^2/a^2}},
$$
and with $c/a = 2$ the numerator is $-1+1+4 = 4$ and the denominator $\sqrt6\sqrt6 = 6$, so $\cos\phi = 0.667$ and $\phi = 48.19^\circ$. The same three numbers, in a different lattice, give a different angle: which is why indexing a crystal means measuring its cell first, and why the optical goniometer of the nineteenth century and the four-circle diffractometer of the twentieth do the same job with different light.
:::

## Crystal faces, and what they tell you

::: definition Forms, twins and growth {#def-forms}
A **form** is a set of faces related by the crystal's symmetry: the cube is $\{100\}$, the octahedron $\{111\}$, the dodecahedron $\{110\}$ in the cubic system, and the hexagonal prism $\{10\bar10\}$ with the two pyramids $\{10\bar11\}$ and $\{10\bar1\bar1\}$ makes up the familiar "quartz crystal". A real crystal is the intersection of several forms, and the relative size of the faces records the conditions of growth, not the symmetry. A crystal is **euhedral** when bounded by its own faces, **subhedral** when partly, and **anhedral** when it has filled a space left by others — a texture that in a thin section reads directly as the order of crystallisation.

**Twinning** is the intergrowth of two or more crystals of the same species in a symmetry-related orientation that is not a symmetry operation of the single crystal. The laws are named for the species or the discoverer and are diagnostic: the **albite law** on $\{010\}$ gives the polysynthetic twins that identify plagioclase under crossed polars; the **pericline** law gives the cross-hatch of gridiron albite; the **Carlsbad** twin is common in orthoclase; **tartan twinning** on the albite and pericline laws together is diagnostic of microcline; the **spinel law** on $\{111\}$ twins magnetite and hercynite; quartz twins on the **Dauphiné** and **Brazil** laws, the latter so common in deformed quartz that it is used as a strain indicator; and gypsum's "swallowtail" contact twin is the first twin anyone sees in a hand specimen.
:::

::: example Nucleation, and why glass is not a mineral {#ex-nucleation}
For quartz crystallising from a silicate melt take an interfacial energy $\sigma = 0.4\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$, a melting temperature $T_m = 1713\ \mathrm{K}$, a latent heat of fusion $L_f = 1.8\times10^6\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ and a density $2650\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Find the critical radius of a nucleus at a undercooling of $200\ \mathrm{K}$ and at $20\ \mathrm{K}$, and explain why a basalt can be glassy and a rhyolite rarely is.
::: solution
The volume free energy of the transformation is $\Delta G_v = \rho L_f\Delta T/T_m$, and the critical radius at which a spherical nucleus stops dissolving is
$$
r^* = \frac{2\sigma}{\Delta G_v} = \frac{2\sigma T_m}{\rho L_f\,\Delta T} = \frac{2\times0.4\times1713}{2650\times1.8\times10^6\times\Delta T} = \frac{2.86\times10^{-7}}{\Delta T}\ \mathrm{m}.
$$
At $\Delta T = 200\ \mathrm{K}$ this is $1.4\ \mathrm{nm}$, a handful of unit cells, and nucleation is easy; at $\Delta T = 20\ \mathrm{K}$ it is $14\ \mathrm{nm}$ and the nucleation rate, which goes as $\exp[-16\pi\sigma^3/(3\Delta G_v^2kT)]$, falls by some twenty orders of magnitude. Growth, on the other hand, is controlled by diffusion and slows as the melt becomes polymerised and viscous.

This is the whole of the difference between obsidian and granite in terms of rate, and between glass and rock in terms of kind. A basalt is a simple, low-viscosity melt that can be quenched through its glass transition before the nuclei reach $r^*$, and so it forms glass — obsidian, palagonite, the rinds of pillow lavas — wherever it is cooled fast enough. A rhyolite is a polymerised, water-poor, ferociously viscous melt in which nothing diffuses and nothing nucleates, and it therefore cools to a glass (pitchstone, pumice, the black bands of an obsidian flow) at rates that would leave a basalt wholly crystalline; but where it crystallises at all it does so slowly and coarsely, because the few nuclei that form grow as fast as the ions can reach them. The texture of an igneous rock is a record of this competition between nucleation and growth, and both are functions of undercooling and viscosity.
:::
:::

::: example From the cell to the density {#ex-density}
Forsterite, $\mathrm{Mg_2SiO_4}$, is orthorhombic with $a = 4.756$, $b = 10.195$, $c = 5.923\ \text{angstroms}$ and $Z = 4$ formula units per cell. Find the cell volume and the theoretical density, and compare it with the measured $3.22\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.
::: solution
For an orthorhombic cell the volume is simply the product of the edges,
$$
V = 4.756\times10.195\times5.923 = 287.4\ \text{angstroms}^3 = 2.874\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{cm^3},
$$
and with a molar mass of $140.69\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$,
$$
\rho = \frac{ZM}{N_A V} = \frac{4\times140.69}{6.022\times10^{23}\times2.874\times10^{-22}} = \frac{562.8}{173.1} = 3.25\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$
The agreement with the measured value to one per cent is not a coincidence to be taken lightly: it means that the structure determined from the diffraction pattern, the composition determined chemically, and the density determined by weighing a crystal in liquid are three independent measurements of the same thing, and that the model of the crystal as a periodic array of whole-number formula units is right. Where they disagree, one of them is wrong, and historically the disagreement has been the starting point of three quarters of the corrections to mineral formulae — the water of the hydrous minerals, the iron in the olivine, the vacancy in the garnet.

The calculation also explains why the heavy minerals sink in the dense liquids used to separate them, why a garnet porphyroblast grows downward into its own mud, and why the density contrast between an eclogite at $3.4$ and its parent gabbro at $2.9\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ is enough to make a subducting slab sink.
:::
:::

::: example Why halite is a cube and sometimes an octahedron {#ex-wulff}
The surface free energies of halite are $\gamma_{\{100\}} = 0.20$ and $\gamma_{\{111\}} = 0.19\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$ in pure water, and $\gamma_{\{100\}} = 0.20$, $\gamma_{\{111\}} = 0.25$ in a solution containing borate. Use the Wulff construction to find the equilibrium shape in each case.
::: solution
The Wulff construction says that the equilibrium crystal exposes each face at a distance from the centre proportional to its surface free energy, so that the total surface energy $\sum\gamma_i A_i$ is a minimum for a given volume. The faces with the lowest energy therefore stand furthest out and dominate the shape. In pure water the $\{111\}$ faces are the cheaper and the crystal tends to the octahedron, bounded by eight $\{111\}$ faces with the $\{100\}$ faces reduced to small squares at the corners; in borate the order reverses and the cube wins.

The real control on a natural crystal is rarely the equilibrium shape but the growth rate, which is not the same thing: a face that grows fast disappears, and the crystal is bounded by its slowest faces, which are usually the ones with the highest density of atoms and the lowest density of kink sites — the same faces, in most cases, which is why the equilibrium and growth arguments so often agree. Where they disagree is where the chemistry interferes: the octahedral halite of the salinas and of the Zechstein evaporites, the cubic magnetite that becomes an octahedron in a boron-rich pegmatite, the tabular hematite that is a hexagonal prism in hand specimen and a plate in the ore, and the quartz of a geode whose prism faces have been eaten away by etching until only the pyramids remain. Reading a crystal's shape is reading its environment, and the discipline of doing so carefully is what separates a mineralogist's description from a collector's.
:::

::: warning Three confusions about crystals
**A crystal is not a crystal shape.** Ice in a glacier is a crystal aggregate with no faces whatever and is as crystalline as a diamond; a glass has beautiful curved surfaces and is not a crystal at all. The defining property is the internal periodicity, which is what the diffractometer reads and what the microscope sees. **A face is not a cleavage.** Halite's cubic faces are growth faces and its cleavage is also cubic, but calcite's cleavage rhombohedron is rarely seen as a growth form, and mica's perfect basal cleavage has nothing to do with the shape of its crystals: the cleavage is the trace of the structure's weakest planes and the face is the slowest-growing one, and they coincide only by accident. **Twinning is not a defect to be ignored.** The polysynthetic twins of plagioclase are the fastest way to identify it, the deformation twins of quartz and olivine are among the best stress indicators in the rock record, and the twin boundaries of a perovskite solar cell are where the current leaks.
:::

::: quiz
A crystal is found to have a single four-fold rotation axis and no other symmetry except a mirror plane perpendicular to it. Its system is:
- [ ] Cubic, because it has a four-fold axis
- [x] Tetragonal
- [ ] Hexagonal, because four does not divide six
- [ ] Triclinic, because it has so little symmetry
::: solution
A single four-fold axis requires the $a$ and $b$ axes to be equal and the $c$ axis distinct, with all angles at ninety degrees: the tetragonal system. The cubic system requires four separate three-fold axes along the body diagonals, which is a much stronger condition; the presence of a four-fold axis alone does not make a crystal cubic, and this is the most common error in the identification of an unknown crystal's system.
:::

::: history
Steno's observation of 1669 was followed by a century of accumulation: the law of constancy confirmed by Romé de l'Isle in 1783, and the law of rational indices — that the intercepts of any face are in ratios of small whole numbers — implicit in Haüy's "integral molecules" of 1784 and stated explicitly by Whewell in 1821 after Ewald's German formulation. Haüy's theory was wrong in detail, since his "molecule" was a rhombohedron of calcite rather than a unit cell of nine atoms, but it was structurally correct and explained everything then known. The enumeration of the fourteen lattices by Bravais in 1848 and of the thirty-two classes by Hess in 1830 turned crystallography into mathematics; the two-hundred-and-thirty space groups, found independently by Fedorov and Schönflies in 1891, were announced in a paper that took three years to publish and was received with indifference. In February 1912 Walter Friedrich and Paul Knipping, at Max von Laue's suggestion, passed X-rays from a Coolidge tube through copper sulphate crystal and photographed spots on a photographic plate, and the structure of the crystal became measurable rather than inferable. The Braggs built the spectrometer in Cambridge in 1913, solved NaCl in 1914 — the structure that showed there is no such thing as a sodium chloride molecule — and diamond in the same year. William Lawrence Bragg was twenty-five and remains the youngest science Nobel laureate. The silicate structures waited for the electron count: W. L. Bragg's 1913 paper proposed the tetrahedral arrangement in silicates, and Pauling's rules of 1929 made the whole of mineral chemistry a matter of electrostatics and geometry.
:::

::: summary Key takeaways
- The crystal is defined by its periodic internal structure, not by its shape; seven metric systems, fourteen lattices, thirty-two classes, two hundred and thirty space groups.
- The crystallographic restriction forbids five- and seven-fold axes because 2 cos theta must be an integer; quasicrystals escape by not being periodic.
- Miller indices are cleared reciprocals of the axial intercepts, and the angle between faces follows from the cell metric, not from the indices alone.
- Forms record symmetry, face sizes record growth, and twinning records both symmetry and deformation: the twin law is an identification tool.
- Nucleation needs undercooling and goes as an exponential of minus the interfacial energy cubed; growth needs diffusion. Their competition writes the texture of every igneous rock.
- Bragg's law turns the crystal into a set of peak positions, and the powder diffractometer is the instrument that says what a mineral is when the microscope cannot.
:::

Find the angle between $(100)$ and $(111)$ in a cubic crystal, in degrees.
::: solution
$\cos\phi = 1/\sqrt3$, so $\phi = 54.74^\circ$ — the angle between a cube face and an octahedron face, and the complement of the angle between the two $\{111\}$ planes along which diamond cleaves.

::: exercise Counting lattices {level=1 check="4"}
How many Bravais lattices belong to the orthorhombic system?
::: solution
Four: primitive, base-centred, body-centred and face-centred. The cubic system has three, the tetragonal two, and the triclinic, hexagonal and rhombohedral one each, giving fourteen in all.

::: exercise Radius ratio {level=1 check="0.29"}
Given $r(\mathrm{Si}^{4+}) = 0.40\ \text{angstroms}$ and $r(\mathrm{O}^{2-}) = 1.38\ \text{angstroms}$, find the radius ratio and the predicted coordination.
::: solution
$0.40/1.38 = 0.29$, which lies between $0.225$ and $0.414$: four-fold, tetrahedral coordination. The same rule gives $0.72/1.38 = 0.52$ for magnesium, six-fold, and $1.38/1.38 = 1.0$ for potassium, eight- to twelve-fold — which is why silicon is in tetrahedra, magnesium in octahedra, and potassium only in the large sites of feldspar and mica.
:::

::: exercise Bragg's law {level=1 check="29.4"}
Find $2\theta$, in degrees, for the calcite $(10\bar4)$ reflection with $d = 3.035\ \text{angstroms}$ using copper radiation of $\lambda = 1.5418\ \text{angstroms}$.
::: solution
$\sin\theta = 1.5418/(2\times3.035) = 0.2541$, $\theta = 14.72^\circ$, $2\theta = 29.45^\circ$. This is the strongest peak of calcite and the reason a diffractometer run at low angles distinguishes limestone from dolomite in minutes.
:::

::: exercise Critical nucleus {level=2 check="7.2"}
For the constants of the worked example, find the critical radius in nanometres at an undercooling of $40\ \mathrm{K}$.
::: solution
$r^* = 2.86\times10^{-7}/40 = 7.2\ \mathrm{nm}$. Between $200\ \mathrm{K}$ and $20\ \mathrm{K}$ of undercooling the critical radius grows tenfold and the nucleation rate falls by something like twenty orders of magnitude, which is the difference between a rock full of small crystals and a rock of glass.

::: exercise Pauling's second rule {level=2 check="3"}
In forsterite $\mathrm{Mg_2SiO_4}$ each oxygen is bonded to one silicon in tetrahedral coordination and to some number of magnesiums in octahedral coordination. How many?
::: solution
Pauling's second rule requires the sum of the electrostatic bond strengths reaching an anion to equal its charge. The Si-O strength is $4/4 = 1$ and the Mg-O strength $2/6 = \tfrac13$, so the oxygen needs $(2-1)/(1/3) = 3$ magnesium neighbours, and forsterite's structure has exactly that: each oxygen is at the centre of three $\mathrm{MgO_6}$ octahedra and one $\mathrm{SiO_4}$ tetrahedron. The same arithmetic applied to enstatite, $\mathrm{MgSiO_3}$, where the oxygens are of two kinds — those shared between two tetrahedra (bridging, with a Si-O contribution of 2 and needing no Mg) and those bonded to one tetrahedron (needing three Mg) — predicts the pyroxene structure's two distinct oxygen sites, which the X-ray determination later found.
:::

::: exercise Deriving the restriction for two dimensions {level=3}
In two dimensions, show by considering the rotation of a lattice vector that only 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold axes are possible, and enumerate the five two-dimensional lattices by adding centring. Why is there no "centred square" lattice distinct from the square lattice?
::: solution
Rotating a lattice by $\theta$ about two lattice points separated by $t$ must give a lattice vector along the original line, so $t(1-2\cos\theta) = mt$ and $2\cos\theta$ is an integer, as in three dimensions: the allowed rotations are $180$, $120$, $90$ and $60$ degrees. The five plane lattices follow from the metrics: oblique, rectangular, centred rectangular, square and hexagonal.

A "centred square" lattice is not a sixth, because the centred square cell contains a smaller square cell rotated by forty-five degrees with one-quarter the area: centring a square gives nothing new. The same argument, applied to the rectangular lattice, gives a genuinely new lattice, because the centred rectangle cannot be reduced to a smaller primitive rectangle with right angles. This is the whole of the enumeration, and it is why the fourteen three-dimensional lattices are fourteen and not more: every apparent new centring either reduces to an existing lattice in a smaller cell or is excluded by the point symmetry it would require.
:::

::: exercise The stereographic projection {level=3}
Two faces of a crystal of unknown symmetry give a pole-pole angle of $60^\circ$ on the goniometer, and the crystal has a horizontal mirror plane. Plot the possible symmetry and decide what systems remain open.
::: solution
A pole at $60^\circ$ from another, with a horizontal mirror, generates by reflection a pair of poles at $\pm 60^\circ$ from the vertical, and the angle between the two reflected poles is $2\times60 = 120^\circ$ if they lie in the same vertical plane, or less if they do not. The possibilities are a single two-fold axis (monoclinic or orthorhombic), a three-fold axis with the poles at $70.53^\circ$ from the vertical (trigonal or hexagonal), a four-fold at $60^\circ$ (tetragonal) or a six-fold at $30^\circ$.

The measurement that decides is the number of equivalent faces: count them. Six equivalent faces related by a horizontal six-fold axis and a vertical mirror is the hexagonal dipyramid; three is trigonal; four with a horizontal mirror is tetragonal or orthorhombic depending on the presence of a vertical mirror. This is exactly what a reflecting goniometer and a two-circle table did between 1870 and 1920, and it is why the classical mineral descriptions in Dana's give the measured angles to the minute of arc: the angles are the symmetry, and the symmetry is the class.
