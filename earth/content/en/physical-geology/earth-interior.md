The deepest hole ever dug reached $12{,}262\ \mathrm{m}$ on the Kola Peninsula in 1989, after twenty years of drilling, and stopped at $180\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ rather than at any limit of technology. That is $0.19\%$ of the way to the centre of the Earth — less, in proportion, than the thickness of the skin on an apple. Everything in this chapter is known without drilling: the radius of the core to within a few kilometres, its density, the speed of sound through it, whether it is solid or liquid, what minerals the mantle is made of at $600\ \mathrm{km}$ depth, and how fast the whole planet is cooling.

The method is that of a physician with a stethoscope. We cannot see the interior, but the interior rings when struck, and the ringing tells us what it is made of. In 1909 Andrija Mohorovičić noticed that seismic waves from the Zagreb earthquake arrived in two sets, a fast set and a slow set, and that the only explanation was a boundary in the Earth's crust at about $54\ \mathrm{km}$ depth. In 1926 Beno Gutenberg used the disappearance of P waves beyond $103^\circ$ to fix the radius of the core at $3480\ \mathrm{km}$, a number that has moved by less than $10\ \mathrm{km}$ since. In 1936 Inge Lehmann, sorting through the seismograms of a Dutch earthquake, found a faint arrival that could only have come from a solid ball inside the liquid core. All three deductions were made from stopwatches and paper records, and all three have survived.

## The layers, and how each was found

::: definition The structure in one paragraph {#def-layers}
The **crust** is $5$–$10\ \mathrm{km}$ of basaltic oceanic rock or $30$–$70\ \mathrm{km}$ of granitic continental rock, with $V_P = 6$–$7\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, resting on the **Moho** at $V_P = 8.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The **mantle** extends to $2891\ \mathrm{km}$ depth: olivine and its polymorphs down to $660\ \mathrm{km}$, then bridgmanite plus ferropericlase. The **outer core** is liquid, $2200\ \mathrm{km}$ thick, excludes S waves entirely, and is mostly iron. The **inner core** is solid, radius $1221\ \mathrm{km}$, and deforms as if it were a single crystal or nearly so. Between the crust and the mantle's transition zone lies the **asthenosphere**, a low-velocity layer $50$–$250\ \mathrm{km}$ deep beneath the oceans, in which the rock is close enough to its solidus that it flows easily.
:::

::: proposition The liquid outer core follows from S waves alone {#prop-liquid-core}
No direct S wave is recorded at epicentral distances greater than $103^\circ$, while P waves return beyond $142^\circ$. Since S waves cannot propagate in a fluid, and since the geometry requires that any ray reaching beyond $103^\circ$ must cross the region inside $r = 3480\ \mathrm{km}$, the outer core is liquid. The P-wave shadow zone between $103^\circ$ and $142^\circ$ then follows from refraction: the drop in P velocity at the core–mantle boundary bends the transmitted ray towards the normal, so no P energy emerges in the band between.
:::

::: proof
Take a ray that just grazes the core–mantle boundary at radius $r_c$. In the mantle just above the boundary $V_P = 13.7\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$; just below it $V_P = 8.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Snell's law at the boundary gives the angle of transmission $\theta_t$ from
$$
\frac{\sin\theta_t}{\sin\theta_i} = \frac{8.0}{13.7} = 0.584 .
$$
For the grazing ray $\theta_i = 90^\circ$, so $\theta_t = 35.7^\circ$: the ray is bent sharply towards the radius, and the ray that would have continued to the surface at $103^\circ$ is deflected into the core. Tracing the family of rays that turn above the core and those that enter it leaves an angular gap at the surface — the shadow zone — whose observed inner edge of $103^\circ$ fixes $r_c/R = 0.546$, and whose outer edge of $142^\circ$ fixes the velocity jump. Both numbers are measured, and both are consistent with $r_c = 3480\ \mathrm{km}$.

That the outer core is liquid is confirmed independently three times over: S waves do not cross it; the normal modes of the Earth (its free ringing after a great earthquake) include modes that only exist if a fluid layer is present; and the tidal Love number $k_2 = 0.30$ requires a planet that is rigid in shear near its centre. ∎
:::

::: example The core radius from the mean density {#ex-core-radius}
The Earth's mass is $5.974\times10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and its mean radius $6371\ \mathrm{km}$, giving a mean density of $5515\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Model the Earth as two layers, a core of density $11{,}000$ and a mantle of $4500\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and find the core radius. Then test the model against the observed moment of inertia factor $I/MR^2 = 0.3308$.
::: solution
With $k = r_c/R$ and volumes proportional to $k^3$,
$$
5515 = 11{,}000\,k^3 + 4500(1-k^3) \Longrightarrow 1015 = 6500\,k^3 \Longrightarrow k = 0.156^{1/3} = 0.538 ,
$$
so $r_c = 3430\ \mathrm{km}$, within $1.5\%$ of the seismological value of $3480\ \mathrm{km}$. Two numbers as different as a stopwatch and a balance scale give the same radius, which is the kind of agreement that makes a model believable.

For the moment of inertia of two uniform spheres,
$$
\frac{I}{MR^2} = \frac{2}{5}\cdot\frac{11{,}000\,k^5 + 4500(1-k^5)}{11{,}000\,k^3 + 4500(1-k^3)} = \frac{2}{5}\cdot\frac{4818}{5563} = 0.346 ,
$$
against an observed $0.3308$. The model is too mass-convex, so the real Earth must be *denser still at the centre* than a uniform core: compression raises the core's density from $9.9$ at the top to $12.8\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ at the centre. The discrepancy is not a failure but a measurement of the density gradient.
:::
:::

## What the mantle is made of: mineral physics

Seismology gives velocities; mineral physics tells you what velocities mean. The argument runs: measure the seismic velocities and densities of candidate minerals at the pressures and temperatures of the mantle in a diamond-anvil cell or a multi-anvil press; find the assemblage that matches; and if no assemblage of the right *composition* matches, the composition is wrong.

::: definition The olivine spinel transition and the transition zone {#def-olivine}
Olivine, $(\mathrm{Mg,Fe})_2\mathrm{SiO_4}$, is the dominant mineral of the upper mantle. At $13.9\ \mathrm{GPa}$ ($410\ \mathrm{km}$) it transforms to **wadsleyite**, at $17.5\ \mathrm{GPa}$ ($520\ \mathrm{km}$) to **ringwoodite**, and at $23.8\ \mathrm{GPa}$ ($660\ \mathrm{km}$) it breaks down to **bridgmanite** $(\mathrm{Mg,Fe})\mathrm{SiO_3}$ perovskite plus **ferropericlase** $(\mathrm{Mg,Fe})\mathrm{O}$. The three discontinuities at $410$, $520$ and $660\ \mathrm{km}$ are not compositional boundaries but *phase transitions in the same rock*, and they are the reason the mantle's "layers" between $410$ and $660\ \mathrm{km}$ have the same chemistry and different physics.
:::

::: theorem Clapeyron slopes make a discontinuity a thermometer {#thm-clapeyron}
A phase boundary in $P$–$T$ space has slope
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta V},
$$
so a discontinuity with a positive slope sits *deeper* where the mantle is cold and *shallower* where it is hot, and a discontinuity with a negative slope does the opposite. The $410$-km discontinuity has $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T \approx +3.3\ \mathrm{MPa\,K^{-1}}$ and the $660$-km one $\approx -2.0\ \mathrm{MPa\,K^{-1}}$, and their opposing signs are the reason the pair is used to test whether mantle convection is layered or whole-mantle.
:::

::: proof
Along a phase boundary the two phases have equal Gibbs free energy, $G_1 = G_2$. Differentiating, $-S_1\mathrm{d}T + V_1\mathrm{d}P = -S_2\mathrm{d}T + V_2\mathrm{d}P$, which rearranges to the Clausius–Clapeyron relation above. The sign of the slope is the sign of $\Delta S/\Delta V$: since the denser phase is always the more ordered one for these silicate transitions, $\Delta S$ and $\Delta V$ have the same sign and the slope is positive — except for the ringwoodite $\rightarrow$ bridgmanite transition, where the volume collapse is unusually large and the slope is negative.

Convert a slope into a depth shift by dividing by the lithostatic gradient $\rho g \approx 3500\times9.8 = 34.3\ \mathrm{kPa\,m^{-1}}$:
$$
\Delta z = \frac{(\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T)\,\Delta T}{\rho g}.
$$
For the $410$ beneath a slab $200\ \mathrm{K}$ colder than average, $\Delta P = 3.3\times10^{6}\times(-200) = -6.6\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, so $\Delta z = -19\ \mathrm{km}$: the boundary stands $19\ \mathrm{km}$ *shallower*. For the $660$, whose slope is negative, the same cold anomaly gives $+12\ \mathrm{km}$ deeper. ∎

The observation is that beneath cold ocean and subduction zones the $410$ rises and the $660$ deepens, exactly as the two slopes predict, and that the amplitudes match the temperature anomalies measured from tomography. Where the two discontinuities do *not* behave this way — where the $660$ appears flattened or absent — there is either a compositional anomaly or partial melt.
:::

::: example How much does a slab pull the $660$ down? {#ex-660}
Beneath a subduction zone the slab is $400\ \mathrm{K}$ colder than the surrounding mantle at $660\ \mathrm{km}$ depth. Take $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T = -2.0\ \mathrm{MPa\,K^{-1}}$ and $\rho g = 34.3\ \mathrm{kPa\,m^{-1}}$. Find the depth shift of the $660$, and say what happens to a slab that reaches a discontinuity whose slope makes it *harder* to enter the lower mantle.
::: solution
$$
\Delta P = (-2.0\times10^{6})\times(-400) = +8.0\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{Pa} = 800\ \mathrm{MPa},
\qquad
\Delta z = \frac{8.0\times10^{8}}{3.43\times10^{7}} = +23\ \mathrm{km}.
$$
The $660$ is depressed by about $23\ \mathrm{km}$ beneath cold slabs, which is observed in global stacks of seismic profiles. Because the slope is negative, cold rock transforms to the denser lower-mantle assemblage *deeper* than warm rock does, so the phase change that would help the slab sink is delayed where the slab is coldest and densest. This is the origin of the "660 as a slab barrier" hypothesis, and of the observation that some slabs flatten at $660\ \mathrm{km}$ (the Tonga slab is the classic case) while others — beneath the Pacific and beneath old, cold, fast-subducting margins — are imaged by tomography in the lower mantle all the way to the $D''$ layer. The present consensus is that the $660$ is a partial, transient barrier rather than an absolute one, which means mantle convection is *mostly* whole-mantle with sluggish exchange across the transition zone.
:::
:::

## The core, and the problem of its density

::: proposition The outer core is too light to be pure iron {#prop-core-light}
At the pressure of the core–mantle boundary ($136\ \mathrm{GPa}$) pure iron has a density of about $13.0\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$; the outer core there is $9.9$–$10.0\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, and the deficit grows to about $10\%$ at the top of the core. The core therefore contains several per cent of light element. The candidates, ranked by the experiments, are silicon, oxygen, sulphur, carbon and hydrogen — and the mixture matters, because it determines the core's density, its sound speed, its viscosity, how much heat it can give up, and therefore how long the geodynamo can run.
:::

::: example The density deficit of the core {#ex-deficit}
Pure liquid iron at the top of the outer core ($136\ \mathrm{GPa}$, $4000\ \mathrm{K}$) has $\rho = 12.4\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $V_P = 8.9\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$; the observed outer core there is $\rho = 9.9\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $V_P = 8.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Shock and diamond-anvil experiments give a density deficit of roughly $1.5$ per cent per atomic per cent of oxygen, so that $x$ atomic per cent of light element gives $\rho = 12.4(1 - 0.015x)$. Estimate $x$, and the mass of light element in the core if it is all oxygen.
::: solution
$$
\frac{9.9}{12.4} = 0.798 = 1 - 0.015x \Longrightarrow x = 13.5\ \mathrm{at\%}.
$$
Converting to weight per cent for an Fe–O alloy, $0.135\times16/(0.865\times55.85 + 0.135\times16) = 4.3\ \mathrm{wt\%}$ — in the accepted range of $5$–$10\ \mathrm{wt\%}$, though oxygen alone makes the sound speed too low, which is why the modern view is a mixture of oxygen, silicon, sulphur, carbon and hydrogen. The mass of light element is then $0.06\times1.85\times10^{24} \approx 1.1\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{kg}$, some $20$ times the mass of the whole crust, and its chemical potential is one of the two or three things driving the geodynamo: as the inner core grows, the light element is excluded from the crystallising iron and rises, and that compositional convection is at least as important as thermal convection.
:::
:::

::: warning "The mantle is rock like the crust, only deeper"
Below about $200\ \mathrm{km}$ the minerals are not the ones in any hand specimen: ringwoodite, bridgmanite and ferropericlase are high-pressure phases that exist only above $14\ \mathrm{GPa}$ and turn back to olivine and enstatite when brought to the surface — which is why the mantle has never been *seen*, only sampled in xenoliths that are almost always shallow. Conversely, the mantle is not molten: the low-velocity zone is a rock within a per cent or two of melt, and it flows not because it is liquid but because it is hot, wet and slowly deforming. The most persistent error in this subject is to confuse "ductile" with "molten", and the second most persistent is to imagine the core as molten lava — it is liquid *iron*, at $4000$–$6000\ \mathrm{K}$, under $135$–$330\ \mathrm{GPa}$.
:::

## Heat: the Earth is still cooling

::: example The Earth's cooling rate {#ex-cooling}
The surface heat flow totals $47\ \mathrm{TW}$ ($4.7\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{W}$), of which about $20\ \mathrm{TW}$ is radiogenic heat produced in the crust and mantle and the rest comes from the core and from secular cooling. Take the mantle's heat capacity as $M c_p = 4.0\times10^{24}\times1200\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Find the implied cooling rate of the mantle, and the temperature drop over $4.5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ if that rate were steady.
::: solution
The non-radiogenic heat loss is $27\ \mathrm{TW}$, of which perhaps $10$–$12\ \mathrm{TW}$ leaves the core; take $15\ \mathrm{TW}$ as the secular cooling of the mantle:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1.5\times10^{13}}{4.8\times10^{27}} = 3.1\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{K\,s^{-1}} = 9.8\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{K\,yr^{-1}},
$$
which is $0.1\ \mathrm{K}$ per million years and $440\ \mathrm{K}$ over the age of the Earth. The Earth is cooling, but slowly: the mantle has probably cooled by a few hundred degrees since $3\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ ago, which is enough to have changed everything about the crust it produces — the komatiites of the Archaean require mantle potential temperatures $200$–$300\ \mathrm{K}$ above today's, and they disappear from the record when the continents became large ([[earth-history/early-earth]]).

The heat budget is the reason the subject is contentious: $20\ \mathrm{TW}$ of radiogenic heat in a mantle containing $20$ parts per billion of the incompatible elements is the "Urey ratio" problem, and the difference between a high and a low value decides whether the early Earth was much hotter than today or only slightly.
:::
:::

::: quiz
The $410$-km discontinuity is observed to be about $20\ \mathrm{km}$ *shallower* beneath a cold subduction zone than the global average. This is because
- [ ] cold rock is denser, so the boundary rises
- [x] the transition has a positive Clapeyron slope, so at lower temperature the transition occurs at lower pressure
- [ ] the slab mechanically pushes the boundary up
- [ ] the $410$ is a compositional boundary and cold slabs are more buoyant
::: solution
With $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T > 0$, a negative temperature anomaly requires a lower pressure to reach the transition, so the boundary shallows; the $660$, with a negative slope, deepens in the same place. The two opposite responses to the same cold anomaly are what make the pair a thermometer, and the observed amplitudes match the temperature anomalies from tomography.
:::

## How else we know: the other probes

Seismology is not the only channel. The **normal modes** of the Earth — the whole planet ringing with a $54$-minute period after the 1960 Chilean earthquake, first observed on a gravimeter and then on long-period seismographs — gave the deep structure independently of any travel-time assumption, and are the reason modern models like PREM are smooth in the deep mantle rather than artefacts of ray geometry. **Gravity and the moment of inertia** constrain the density distribution, as in [[#ex-core-radius]]. **Free-air and Bouguer anomalies** map the crust, and **geoid** data map mantle convection at wavelengths of thousands of kilometres. **Mantle xenoliths** brought up by kimberlite sample the mantle to $200\ \mathrm{km}$, and are the only direct samples of the upper mantle we have; **transition-zone diamonds** containing ringwoodite inclusions are the only direct samples of the mantle transition zone, and they carry enough water to have made the headlines of 2014 correctly: the transition zone may hold as much water as the oceans, in a rock that is not wet.

**Geoneutrinos** — antineutrinos from the decay chains of uranium and thorium in the rock — have been counted at the KamLAND and Borexino detectors, and give a direct, if statistically thin, measurement of the radiogenic heat production: about $20\ \mathrm{TW}$, consistent with the geochemical budget. **Geomagnetic secular variation** and the **palaeomagnetic record** constrain the core's motion and the age of the inner core, and **high-pressure experiments** close the loop by measuring what the waves should do.

::: history
The interior was built from almost nothing. Oldovius in 1773 and then Poisson and Green showed how elastic waves travel through a sphere; the practical start was the invention of the seismograph in the 1890s. Andrija Mohorovičić used the 1909 Zagreb event to find the crust–mantle boundary, which still carries his name. Richard Dixon Oldham identified the core in 1906 from the distorted seismograms of the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, and Beno Gutenberg gave its depth in 1912 and refined it in his 1926 monograph. Harold Jeffreys's *The Earth* (1924, fourth edition 1970) assembled the whole subject — seismology, gravity, isostasy, tides, heat — into a single quantitative model, and set the standard for the field. Inge Lehmann's 1936 note on the inner core was written while she was a 48-year-old woman in a Copenhagen office, and reversed a conclusion of Jeffreys's that had stood for a decade. The mineral-physics era begins with Percy Bridgman's high-pressure work of the 1940s and, for the mantle specifically, with the synthesis of the garnet and spinel transformations in the 1960s; the reference Earth model PREM (Dziewonski and Anderson, 1981) is still in daily use, and the current frontier is not the location of the layers but their mineralogy, their water content and the flow between them.
:::

::: summary Key takeaways
- The crust is a rind: $0.19\%$ of the radius has been drilled, and everything below is inferred from waves, gravity, rotation and experiments.
- The layers are found by velocity jumps: the Moho at $8.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, the 410, 520 and 660 km discontinuities, the core–mantle boundary at $2891\ \mathrm{km}$ and the inner-core boundary at $5150\ \mathrm{km}$.
- The outer core is liquid because no direct S wave crosses it; the P-wave shadow zone of $103^\circ$–$142^\circ$ fixes its radius at $3480\ \mathrm{km}$.
- Between 410 and 660 km the rock is the same and the minerals are not: olivine transforms to wadsleyite, ringwoodite and then to bridgmanite plus ferropericlase.
- Clapeyron slopes turn discontinuities into thermometers: the 410 shallows and the 660 deepens beneath cold slabs, by about $20\ \mathrm{km}$ per $200$–$400\ \mathrm{K}$.
- The core is $5$–$10\ \mathrm{wt\%}$ light element; its exclusion at the growing inner core is a principal driver of the geodynamo.
- The moment of inertia factor $0.3308$ requires density to increase towards the centre; a uniform-core model gives $0.346$.
- The Earth loses $47\ \mathrm{TW}$, about $40\%$ of it radiogenic, and cools at about $0.1\ \mathrm{K}$ per million years.
:::

At what epicentral distance does the direct S wave cease to be recorded, and what does that prove?

$103^\circ$, the angular distance to the tangent of the core. Any S ray reaching further must cross the core, and none does: the outer core has zero shear strength. The P-wave shadow runs from $103^\circ$ to $142^\circ$, and P returns beyond it because P *is* transmitted into the liquid.

What fraction of the Earth's volume is in the core (radius $3480\ \mathrm{km}$, $R = 6371\ \mathrm{km}$)?
::: solution
$(3480/6371)^3 = 0.546^3 = 0.163$, so $16\%$ of the volume and $32\%$ of the mass — the core is twice as dense as the mean planet, and the mantle is correspondingly lighter than the mean.
:::

::: exercise Snell at the core {level=2 check="36"}
Take $V_P = 13.7\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ above the core–mantle boundary and $8.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ below. For a ray incident at $90^\circ$ (grazing), find the angle of transmission into the core.
::: solution
$\sin\theta_t = 8.0/13.7 = 0.584$, so $\theta_t = 35.7^\circ$, about $36^\circ$. The ray is bent $54^\circ$ towards the radius in a single step, which is the whole reason there is a shadow zone.

::: exercise Clapeyron topography {level=2 check="34"}
Beneath a hot mantle plume the mantle at $410\ \mathrm{km}$ is $350\ \mathrm{K}$ hotter than average. With $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T = +3.3\ \mathrm{MPa\,K^{-1}}$ and $\rho g = 34.3\ \mathrm{kPa\,m^{-1}}$, find the depth shift of the $410$.
::: solution
$\Delta P = 3.3\times10^{6}\times350 = 1.16\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, so $\Delta z = 1.16\times10^{9}/3.43\times10^{7} = 34\ \mathrm{km}$ deeper. Hot mantle *deepens* a positive-slope discontinuity and *shallows* the $660$, so a plume produces a characteristic deep 410 and shallow 660 pair, which is what is observed beneath Hawaii and Iceland.
:::

::: exercise Moment of inertia {level=2 check="0.33"}
Compute $I/MR^2$ for a uniform-density Earth, and explain why the real value of $0.3308$ rules that model out.
::: solution
For a uniform sphere $I = \tfrac{2}{5}MR^2$, so $I/MR^2 = 0.40$. The Earth's $0.33$ means mass is concentrated towards the centre; a two-layer model with a $3480\ \mathrm{km}$ core of $11{,}000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ gives $0.346$, and only a core whose density rises to $13{,}000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ at the centre reproduces $0.33$. Rotation, measured from the precession of the equinoxes, is thus a probe of the deepest $1000\ \mathrm{km}$.
:::

::: exercise The heat budget {level=3}
The surface heat flow is $47\ \mathrm{TW}$; radiogenic production in the crust is $8\ \mathrm{TW}$ and in the mantle $12\ \mathrm{TW}$; heat flowing out of the core is estimated at $10\ \mathrm{TW}$, of which $3\ \mathrm{TW}$ is released by inner-core growth and $2\ \mathrm{TW}$ by the sinking of light element. Close the budget and state what the residual must be.
::: solution
Sources: $8 + 12 = 20\ \mathrm{TW}$ radiogenic, plus $10\ \mathrm{TW}$ from the core, of which $5\ \mathrm{TW}$ is primordial and latent heat of the core. Total accounted: $30\ \mathrm{TW}$. The residual, $17\ \mathrm{TW}$, must be secular cooling of the mantle plus primordial heat still being released.

The residual is the interesting number. If it is all mantle cooling, the mantle cools at $0.11\ \mathrm{K\,Myr^{-1}}$ and would have been $500\ \mathrm{K}$ hotter $4.5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ ago — too hot, because that would make the Archaean crust mafic and thick in a way the rock record does not entirely support. The usual reconciliations are a lower core heat flow (a thermally insulating layer at the base of the mantle, or a stably stratified outer-core layer), a higher radiogenic budget (rejected by the geoneutrino measurements), or a mantle whose viscosity rises as it cools, so that heat loss slows down as the planet ages. All three are active research, and all three are testable with the same data: the cooling rate of the core, measured from the palaeomagnetic record of the field's intensity through the Proterozoic.
:::

::: exercise Deriving the shadow zone {level=3}
For a sphere of radius $R$ with a sharp velocity decrease at radius $r_c$ and constant velocity above it, show that a ray grazing the core reaches an angular distance $\Delta_0 = 2\arcsin(r_c/R)$, and evaluate $\Delta_0$ for $r_c/R = 0.546$. Why is the observed inner edge of the shadow zone $103^\circ$ rather than this value, and how is the core radius actually obtained from it?
::: solution
For a ray grazing the core the geometry of the sphere gives an angular distance from the source of $\arcsin(r_c/R)$ to the tangent point on either side, so the innermost distance at which a mantle-only ray can arrive is $\Delta = 2\arcsin(r_c/R)$ measured along the refracted path. With $r_c/R = 0.546$ this gives $\Delta = 2\times33.1^\circ = 66^\circ$ for a ray that never enters the core, and the observed $103^\circ$ is larger because the mantle's velocity gradient bends the rays downward before they reach the core; the correct treatment integrates the spherical Snell invariant $p = r\sin\theta/V(r)$ through the mantle's velocity profile.

The clean way to see what the observation constrains is to invert the problem: for a given mantle velocity profile, compute $\Delta_\text{shadow}(r_c)$ and find the $r_c$ that reproduces $103^\circ$. Gutenberg did exactly this in 1926 and obtained $3470\ \mathrm{km}$; modern free-oscillation data give $3480.0 \pm 0.9\ \mathrm{km}$. The exercise's real lesson is that a shadow-zone angle is not a direct measurement of a radius but of a radius *and* a velocity profile together — which is why the interior was only pinned down once the whole velocity profile, not one discontinuity, had been inverted.
:::

::: exercise The age of the inner core {level=3}
The core must have cooled by of order $1000\ \mathrm{K}$ from its temperature when it segregated for the inner core to begin to freeze. Take the core's heat capacity as $Mc_p = 1.85\times10^{24}\times800\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and find the cooling time for core heat flows of $10$, $15$ and $50\ \mathrm{TW}$. Compare with the age of the Earth and with the $3.5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ antiquity of the dynamo, and discuss.
::: solution
The cooling time is $\tau = Mc_p\Delta T/\dot Q$:
$$
\tau = \frac{1.48\times10^{27}\times1000}{\dot Q} =
\begin{cases}
4.7\ \mathrm{Gyr} & \dot Q = 10\ \mathrm{TW}\\
3.1\ \mathrm{Gyr} & \dot Q = 15\ \mathrm{TW}\\
0.9\ \mathrm{Gyr} & \dot Q = 50\ \mathrm{TW}.
\end{cases}
$$
At the low end the core can barely have cooled enough for an inner core to exist at all before the present day, which makes the inner core younger than the dynamo by a factor of several; at the high end the inner core is as old as the Earth and the core should have frozen solid by now unless the light element and latent heat have supplied a compensating source. The paradox is real and is called the "age of the inner core problem": the core must have cooled by roughly $1000\ \mathrm{K}$, but a conductive lid at the base of the mantle limits the heat flow to values that make this awkward.

Three resolutions are current. The core may have been much hotter, and the liquidus correspondingly higher, so that the inner core is young — perhaps only $0.5$–$1.5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$, which is consistent with the appearance of a strong, modern-style dynamo in the Proterozoic record after a long interval of weak, enigmatic fields. The core's thermal conductivity may have been lower in the past, or the light-element inventory larger, so that compositional convection began early and thermal convection late. Or the "lid" at the base of the mantle may be much more insulating than assumed, in which case the mantle cooled slowly and the core is still hot. The uncertainty is a factor of several, and it matters beyond the core: the inner core's age sets when the geodynamo's energy budget changed, which is recorded in the palaeosecular variation and in the true polar wander of the continents.
:::
