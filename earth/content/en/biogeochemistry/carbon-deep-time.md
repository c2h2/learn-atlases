The fast cycle keeps the planet habitable on human timescales; the **slow cycle** keeps it habitable on geological ones. Over tens of millions of years, the carbon budget of the surface Earth is run by two engines pointed at each other across the rock record — **volcanic and metamorphic degassing**, which feeds CO₂ to the atmosphere-ocean system at ~0.1 GtC yr⁻¹, and **silicate weathering**, which draws it back at the same average rate, leaving the atmosphere as the small, contested buffer between them. This lesson is the slow carbon cycle and its pathological record: the Urey thermostat that held Phanerozoic climate inside the habitable window through supercontinents, snowballs and superplumes; the **carbon isotope excursions** that catalogue what happens when the balance is broken by large igneous provinces and methane; and the PETM — the Eocene's 200,000-year fever, geology's closest experiment to our own, whose most useful number is a rate: **today's emissions are roughly ten times faster than the PETM's carbon release**, and that difference — not the total — is the difference between a perturbation the Earth system absorbed and one it will spend a half-million years digesting.

## The thermostat: weathering versus degassing

::: definition The Urey reactions and the silicate weathering thermostat {#def-thermostat}
The slow cycle's bookkeeping is the **Urey reactions** (Harold Urey, 1952): weathering of calcium silicate, $\mathrm{CaSiO_3 + 2CO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow Ca^{2+} + 2HCO_3^- + SiO_2}$, followed by marine carbonate precipitation, $\mathrm{Ca^{2+} + 2HCO_3^- \rightarrow CaCO_3 + CO_2 + H_2O}$, netting $\mathrm{CaSiO_3 + CO_2 \rightarrow CaCO_3 + SiO_2}$ — one mole of atmospheric carbon buried per mole of anorthite dissolved, the weathering flux (~0.1 GtC yr⁻¹, balanced against degassing at steady state) and the reason the atmosphere's CO₂ inventory is processed by this pathway on a timescale of 10⁵ years. The **thermostat** (the **Robert Berner, James Kasting, Kirk Gregory and Lee** modelling lineage, 1980s–2010s, and the GEOCARL palaeo-CO₂ synthesis) is the negative feedback that closes the loop: **weathering rates rise with temperature and rainfall** (the Arrhenius kinetics of feldspar dissolution, the rock-mass balance of orogeny and relief), so a warmer world weathers faster, draws down more CO₂, and cools itself back — and the reverse for a snowballed world, where ice-limited weathering lets degassed CO₂ pile up for millions of years until the greenhouse breaks the ice (the Neoproterozoic snowball escapes, the same mechanism in reverse). The thermostat's three caveats are the lesson's frontiers. **It is slow**: its response time is 10⁴–10⁶ years (the ocean and soil chemistry buffer first, the weathering feedback second), which is precisely why it cannot help the anthropocene perturbation — the system's fastest stabiliser needs a hundred human generations to register one doubling. **Its set-point moves with biology and geography**: the Devonian rise of roots and burrows accelerated weathering (the post-vascular-plant spike in marine lithium and strontium isotopes, the "Greensphere" argument that biotic weathering helped trigger the Late Palaeozoic ice age), and supercontinents parked over the equator weather away their greenhouse (the ice ages cluster with tropical continents and mountain belts, the Himalayan question — Cenozoic cooling engine or steady-state bystander? — the **Katz, Miller, Raymo and Ruddiman** debate of the 1990s and its 2020s river-geochemistry reconciliation). **Its gain is debated**: the rainfall–weathering sensitivity that makes the feedback strong or weak is exactly what the snowball and "faint young Sun" episodes stress-test, and the Proterozoic is where the thermostat's stability is argued. The slow cycle's moral for the present: the planet *will* fix the anthropogenic perturbation, on the weathering clock, by adding alkalinity to the ocean — a service that will take ~100,000 years to complete and whose intermediate steps (the acidification, the warming, the anoxia) are the chapters of every excursion in the record below.
:::

::: example The thermostat's clock {#ex-urey}
Weathering currently buries ~0.1 GtC yr⁻¹ as carbonate via the Urey pathway, and the atmosphere holds 875 GtC. Find the weathering turnover time of atmospheric CO₂, and explain in two sentences why the recovery time of a 400 GtC perturbation is *longer* than this number suggests, and what process actually controls it.
::: solution
Turnover $= 875/0.1 = 8{,}750$ years — already four centuries of human civilisation per turnover, and already the wrong number for recovery: the fast sinks (ocean, biosphere) will draw the atmosphere down within centuries, but the system's **alkalinity** — the carbonate inventory that sets how much CO₂ the ocean can hold and at what pH — is restored only by deep-sea carbonate dissolution and weathering, the **carbonate compensation** process, on 10⁴–10⁵ years; and the climate returns to baseline only when the drawn-down CO₂ has been balanced by the alkalinity rebuild. That is why the PETM's excursion tail runs ~100,000–200,000 years, and why the honest half-life of the anthropocene carbon problem is the sediment record's, not the air's.
:::
:::

## The excursion catalogue: when the balance breaks

::: definition Carbon isotope excursions and their meanings {#def-cie}
A **carbon isotope excursion** (CIE) is the sediment record's smoke signal: a global, synchronous shift in the δ¹³C of marine carbonates, organic matter or soil carbonates, recording an injection of carbon with an isotopic flavour unlike the normal cycle's. The logic is stoichiometric: organic matter and methane are **light** (δ¹³C ≈ −25 ‰ for organic carbon, −60 to −100 ‰ for microbial methane, versus 0 ‰ for mantle CO₂ and the marine carbonate standard), so a negative excursion means **light carbon added to the ocean-atmosphere system**, and the excursion's size and shape, run through an isotope mass balance, are the geologist's estimate of the carbon released. The Phanerozoic catalogue reads as a list of large igneous provinces and their consequences: the **Hirnantian** (445 Ma, a +4 to +5 ‰ *positive* excursion — the Ordovician glaciation's signature, evidence that the first great ice age was accompanied by enhanced organic-carbon burial, a possible drawdown trigger); the **Devonian Kellwasser** (372 Ma, positive, anoxia- and extinction-coupled); the **Permian–Triassic** (252 Ma, −3 to −8 ‰, the Siberian Traps, the largest mass extinction, its excursion's shape arguing for a two-stage release — rapid volcanism plus a methane or coal-fired second pulse, with the most acidified oceans and the most sterilised continents of the last 540 million years); the **Toarcian** (183 Ma, −6 to −8 ‰, the Karoo-Ferrar large igneous province's volcanism, the seabed-methane hypothesis of **Andy Kemp and colleagues** (2005–2015), and the one CIE whose onset rate was measured at seasonal resolution by ammonite oxygen isotopes: the excursion sped the climate **~10× faster than the background**, the same rate comparison that convicts the present); the **PETM** (56 Ma, the lesson's centrepiece); the **smaller early-Eocene hyperthermals** (ETM2, ETM3 and their cousins — each a carbon release, their orbital periodicity a live argument about methane reservoirs primed by eccentricity); and the **Cretaceous oceanic anoxic events** (the mid-Cretaceous thermal maximum's positive excursions and black shales, the greenhouse world at its hottest, ~1,000–2,000 ppm CO₂ and crocodiles on Antarctica). The Proterozoic adds the tectonic-scale events: the **Lomagundi-Jatuli** positive excursion (2.22–2.06 Ga, the largest δ¹³C departure in Earth history — +5 to +12 ‰ sustained for ~160 million years, the isotopic fingerprint of a global organic-carbon or methane burial event, its cause and its link to the Great Oxidation still unresolved), and the **Tonian-Cryogenian "Shuram/Denino" negative excursion** (to −12 ‰, the largest negative in the record, its interpretation a live argument between methane-release, upwelling and basin-scale hypotheses, its stratigraphy the backbone of the snowball-timing debate). The excursion record's deep lesson for the present is **rate, not amount**: the PETM released a fraction of the fossil carbon now held in reserves, and its biological damage (benthic extinction, reef crisis, dwarfed foraminifera) was severe — and the 2010s boron-isotope pH reconstructions (**Caitlin Penman and colleagues** 2016, **Marcus Gutjahr and colleagues** 2017) showed the PETM ocean acidified by ~0.3 units — about what we are on track for this century — while doing it over ~5,000–20,000 years instead of two. The mass extinctions of the record cluster where **rate, magnitude and environmental state** coincide; the PETM, the one event whose rate we can compare quantitatively, was survived — and the present perturbation is the PETM's rate multiplied by ten, on a biosphere already carrying the Holocene's load.
:::

::: example Reading an excursion's release {#ex-cie-release}
A CIE shifts marine carbonate δ¹³C by −5 ‰ globally. The surface ocean-atmosphere DIC reservoir is ≈ 2,300,000 GtC, and the injected carbon has δ¹³C = −60 ‰ (methane). Estimate the released carbon with the two-endmember approximation $\Delta\delta \approx f\,(\delta_{in} - \delta_{bg})$, and explain why the naive estimate is far too large.
::: solution
$-5 = f(-60-0)$ gives $f = 0.083$, so release $\approx 0.083\times2.3\times10^{6} \approx 190{,}000$ GtC — absurdly large, and the absurdity is the point: the simple box ignores **burial of the excursion's own carbon** (part of the light carbon is buried as organic matter or methane-derived carbonate, and the isotope signal is diluted by the huge carbonate-platform reservoir), so full mass-balance models with burial terms give the PETM ~4,500–12,000 GtC instead; every published CIE release carries a factor-of-three error bar and a footnote about the missing sediment record.
:::
:::

## The PETM: geology's own experiment

::: definition The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum, read as a modern analogue {#def-petm}
The PETM (55.9–55.6 Ma) is the best-sampled abrupt climate change in the rock record, discovered in the deep-sea cores of the early 1990s (**Bains and colleagues**, 1999's isotope type-section work, and the 1990s' landmark interpretations by **Koch, Zachos, Gingerich, Kelly, Thomas, Wing, Behrensmeyer, Lohmann and Schrag**, synthesised by the PETRNM network, 2007–2013). Its inventory: a −3 to −6 ‰ carbon isotope excursion; a deep-sea temperature rise of **4–7 °C**; an ocean acidification and dissolution horizon with the extinction of ~35–50 per cent of deep-sea benthic foraminifera; a terrestrial revolution (the first appearance of modern primate, perissodactyl and rodent faunas in the Bighorn and Polecat basins, mammalian dwarfing, an aridification and wildfire spike in the pollen and charcoal records); and a carbon release of **~4,500–12,000 GtC** whose source is the field's longest-running argument: the classic **methane-clathrate hypothesis** (hydrate destabilisation on a warm baseline — the light isotope signature argues for a biological source), the **sill-combustion hypothesis** (the North Atlantic Igneous Province's sills baking the East Greenland and Rockall coal and evaporite measures — the thermogenic-carbon modelling of **Henrik Svensen and colleagues** (2004–2009), the osmium-isotope evidence for a volcanic pulse at onset, the 2017–2023 mercury-anomaly studies tying the release to the volcanism), and the hybrid positions (volcanism tipped, methane amplified). The PETM's numbers for the present: the onset release rate was **~0.6–1.1 GtC yr⁻¹** versus **~10 GtC yr⁻¹ today — an order of magnitude faster**; the ocean acidified ~0.3 units (boron isotopes) versus our projected 0.3–0.4 this century; the warming was 4–7 °C versus our 2–5 for the century's high cases; the recovery took **>100,000 years** (the excursion's carbonate-dilution and weathering tail) versus the slow cycle's ~100,000-year service contract on our own perturbation. The PETM is thus the **lower bound on the damage of a fast release**: mammals radiated, the oceans deoxygenated and acidified and recovered, the climate reset to a hothouse state — and the 2020s' planktonic foraminiferal biogeography even argues the tropical surface ocean was partly *emptied* of its resident species, with migration from the subtropics refilling it after. The present experiment differs in the two parameters that matter — **rate** (10×) and **starting state** (we begin at 424 ppm on an icehouse planet with 8 billion people and a Holocene-shaped coast; the PETM began ice-free and unpeopled) — and the honest reading of the analogue is that the PETM is the *mild* case: the events in the record with comparable rates — the Permian–Triassic, the Toarcian — are the ones with the mass extinctions.
:::

::: example The rate comparison, done carefully {#ex-rate}
The PETM released ~10,000 GtC over a ~10,000-year onset; humanity has released ~400 GtC of fossil carbon since 1850 in 175 years. Compute both mean rates (GtC yr⁻¹) and their ratio, and state what the PETM's slower rate bought the Earth system that ours does not.
::: solution
PETM: $10{,}000/10{,}000 = 1$ GtC yr⁻¹; modern: $400/175 \approx 2.3$ GtC yr⁻¹ (and ~10 at the current annual rate); ratio **2.3–10×**. The slower rate bought **buffering time**: at 1 GtC yr⁻¹ the carbonate chemistry, the biological pump and (over millennia) the weathering feedback partially absorb the pulse, the surface ocean stays further from corrosive, and deep-sea and soil communities retain refugia and adaptation time; at 10 GtC yr⁻¹ every buffer is outrun and the saturation horizon shoals faster than organisms can migrate — the whole physics of why emissions *rates* and cumulative *totals* are both policy variables.
:::
:::

::: warning Analogues are analogies, the thermostat is not a safety net, "the CO₂ was higher then" is not an argument, excursions are not all the same story, and the deep future is not an argument for the present
Five errors. **"The PETM proves we'll be fine"**: the PETM was survived by a biosphere with no people, no agriculture, no ice-sheet commitments and a third of the rate — and its own survivors paid with a mass extinction of deep-sea life and a reorganised tropics; the analogue's value is the **processes** (acidification, deoxygenation, migration, extinction) that recur, not a promise of a benign outcome at ten times the rate. **"Weathering will fix it, so the slow cycle is our backstop"**: it will fix it — in 100,000 years, through the acidification and warming intervals that the excursion record shows are the *unavoidable middle* of the recovery; the thermostat protects the deep future from the fast world, never the other way round, and civilisation's timescale is the fast one. **"Eocene CO₂ was 1,000 ppm and there were palms on Antarctica"**: true, and the sentence conceals the argument — the Eocene had no ice sheets, no modern monsoon deserts, no 75 cm of inherited Holocene sea-level commitment, and no eight billion people in the productive coastal zone; "the climate was different then" describes *a different planet's equilibrium*, not a safety rating for the transition path between equilibria, and the transition path is where everything alive is located. **"Every CIE is volcanism plus methane"**: the excursions differ in sign (the Hirnantian and Kellwasser are *positive* — burial events), in source (mantle, methane, organic burial, evaporite sulfur), in duration (the Lomagundi's 160 million years versus the PETM's 200,000) and in the biosphere's starting state, and the sloppy "volcanism did it" generalisation has repeatedly mis-dated and mis-attributed events (the Toarcian's source argument, the Shuram's origin debate) — excursion science is a source-apportionment discipline, and its uncertainty is its honest feature. **And "the rock record shows CO₂ and temperature correlate, so everything is settled"**: the slow cycle's correlations constrain **equilibrium** sensitivity across tens of millions of years (and the PETM and Eocene do support the IPCC's range), but they cannot constrain the **rate** of ice-sheet loss, monsoon reorganisation, or the compound extremes that decide the century — the palaeoclimate of the next 200 years is not in the rock record, because the rate is not in the rock record.
:::

::: history
The slow cycle's theory is mid-century physics: **Harold Urey**'s 1952 *The Planets* setting the carbonate–silicate equilibria; **Robert Garrels and Henry Holland**'s 1960s ocean-buffering geochemistry; **Robert Berner**'s GEOCARB series (1991–2009, the CO₂ curve read from the rock record) and the GEOCARL synthesis; **Kirk Gregory and colleagues**' and **Lee and Kasting**'s thermostat and snowball-escape modelling; **James Kasting**'s *How to Find a Habitable Planet* (2010, the thermostat's textbook); **Paul Hoffman and Daniel Schrag**'s CO₂-weathering snowball arguments (1998–2000); and **Andy Ridgwell and Lee Kump**'s excursion and "faint young Earth" modelling. The excursion catalogue is an ocean-drilling and land-sections discipline: the PETM's discovery in the early 1990s (**Bains and colleagues** and the Deep Sea Drilling Program's legacy cores), the 1990s isotope-and-fossil revolution (**Koch, Zachos, Gingerich, Kelly, Thomas, Wing, DiMichele, Behrensmeyer, Ivany, Curtis, Lohmann, Schrag**), the PETRNM synthesis (2007–2013), **Henrik Svensen and colleagues**' Greenland sill and thermogenic-carbon work (2004–), the mercury-anomaly era (2017–), and the boron-isotope pH revolution (the method of **Spivack and Edmond**, 1987, applied to the PETM by **Penman and colleagues** (2016) and **Gutjahr and colleagues** (2017)) — with **Andy Kemp's** ammonite-rate work on the Toarcian (2005–2015) supplying the one Mesozoic event with a seasonally resolved onset. The 2020s' era is model intercomparison (**DeepMIP**'s PETM and Eocene protocols) and rate apportionment — the field's long argument that the PETM was a methane event, a volcanic event, or both, now converging on "both, in that order," and the older argument about what the next excursion will read like in 50 million years now conducted with instruments running.
:::

::: summary
The slow carbon cycle is the planet's long-run climate control: volcanic degassing feeds the atmosphere ~0.1 GtC yr⁻¹, silicate weathering (the Urey reactions) takes it back at the same mean, and the temperature–rainfall dependence of weathering makes the pair a thermostat with a 10⁴–10⁶-year response time — the reason the Phanerozoic stayed habitable and the reason it cannot save us on ours. The excursion catalogue — Hirnantian, Kellwasser, Permian–Triassic, Toarcian, PETM, the oceanic anoxic events, the Lomagundi and the Shuram — is the record's evidence that when the balance breaks (usually with a large igneous province's help), climate response, acidification, anoxia and extinction follow the **rate** of release as much as the amount. The PETM is the experiment to learn from: ~10,000 GtC at ~1 GtC yr⁻¹, 4–7 °C, 0.3 pH units, a deep-sea extinction, a 100,000-year recovery — and a modern release running at ten times its pace on a colder, ice-capped, crowded planet. The slow cycle will fix what we break, on the weathering clock; the entire content of the next few centuries is what the middle of that fix looks like for the things that cannot wait 100,000 years.
:::
