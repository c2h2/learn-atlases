Oxygen is the atmosphere's second gas, the biosphere's loudest waste product, and the only component of the air whose abundance is a **biological achievement with a geological ledger**. Twenty-one per cent O₂ is not chemistry's default — the abiotic Earth managed a thousandth of a per cent — and the history of that 21 per cent is the history of **burial**: every molecule of free oxygen in the air today is the shadow of a carbon or sulfur atom that escaped oxidation by being buried in sediment, and the rises and falls of the O₂ curve (from a trace to 35 per cent and back) are the record of how much carbon the oceans and shelves declined to respire. This closing lesson of the course is the oxygen cycle complete: the modern budget (measured, not modelled, in flasks since 1989), the burial ledger of the deep past, the fire–oxygen–animals triangle of the late Palaeozoic, and the question the course has been circling — whether the biosphere's great oxidation is a finished achievement or a contingent one.

## The modern oxygen budget

::: definition A measured atmosphere: the O₂ budget and its two clocks {#def-o2-budget}
Atmospheric O₂ (20.95 per cent, $1.2\times10^{18}$ t) is declining, and we know it to per-pert meg precision because **C. D. Keeling and colleagues** began measuring O₂/N₂ in flasks in 1989 (the Scripps and NOAA flask and continuous records, the **O₂:N₂ ratio** reported in "per meg" to separate it from the δ¹³C and water interferences, the **Manning and Keeling and the global O₂ observation network** of 2000s–2020s). The budget has two terms and one surprise. **Fossil combustion** consumes O₂ at the stoichiometric rate of photosynthesis's reverse (~2.7 t O₂ per t C: the ~10 GtC yr⁻¹ of the carbon-today lesson costs ~27 Gt O₂ yr⁻¹); **the land and ocean sinks return it** (net ecosystem production releases O₂ as it stores carbon), and the measured **atmospheric O₂ decline of ~25 per meg yr⁻¹** (a global apparent oxygen utilisation of ~4–5 GtC yr⁻¹ equivalent) matches fossil combustion minus the combined land and ocean carbon sinks — the **same budget closure as the CO₂ accounting, in an independent currency**, and one of the two or three most elegant confirmations in Earth-system science (the O₂ decline would be zero if the sinks absorbed all emissions; its size is the airborne fraction seen from the reducing end). The surprise is the **land-use and ocean terms' asymmetry**: fire consumes O₂ directly (the biomass-burning term, the 2020s' fire-O₂ studies using the O₂:CO₂ coincidence to separate fossil from fire carbon — the isotope lesson's method with a second ratio), and the ocean's O₂ inventory (the 2-per-cent deoxygenation of the ocean-cycles lesson) is a **separate, faster ledger** — the ocean holds ~60 times as much O₂ as the atmosphere's reactive turnover and is losing it to temperature and biology on decade timescales while the atmospheric pool moves on its 40,000-year combustion clock. The two clocks are the lesson's frame: **atmospheric O₂ is safe** (humanity consumes a 40,000-year inventory; the "we'll run out of oxygen" fear is arithmetic-free), while **reactive oxygen — the ocean's and the soil's and the wetland's — is in visible decline**, and it is the reactive pool, not the atmospheric mountain, that biology actually lives on. The budget's frontier is the **Δ(O₂/Ar) and δ¹⁸O-of-O₂ system** — the **Kaiser, Hendricks and the Tans school's** O₂:CO₂ and O₂:Ar double-ratio method for partitioning land and ocean sinks — the same over-determined accounting as the carbon budget, run with oxygen as the second currency) and the **stratospheric and mesospheric ozone ledger** (the O₃ lesson of the atmospheric-chemistry course: the triatomic cousin whose recovery is the Montreal Protocol's proof that atmospheric chemistry can be steered).
:::

::: example The 40,000-year answer {#ex-o2-safe}
Atmospheric O₂ is $1.2\times10^{18}$ t; fossil combustion consumes ~27 Gt O₂ yr⁻¹ (10 GtC × 2.7). Find the time to consume the entire inventory at current rates, and — given the measured decline of 25 per meg yr⁻¹ (the atmosphere holds 10⁶ per meg) — find the **observed** fractional decline per year, and explain in two sentences why both numbers are correct and only one is the relevant question.
::: solution
Inventory/combustion $= 1.2\times10^{18}/2.7\times10^{10} \approx 44{,}000$ years; the observed decline $25/10^6$ per meg $= 2.5\times10^{-5}$ per meg yr⁻¹ $= 0.0025$ per cent per decade — both true: the first says the **gross** combustion could never exhaust the pool (the "running out" fear is category error), the second says the **net** sink structure is being measured in real time (the decline equals combustion minus sinks — the oxygen record is the carbon budget's independent witness), and the relevant question is neither: it is the **reactive** oxygen of oceans, soils and sediments, whose turnover is years-to-centuries and whose decline is already ecological news.
:::
:::

## The burial ledger: how the air got its oxygen

::: definition Oxygen as an accounting identity: carbon burial, sulfur burial and the thermostat {#def-burial-ledger}
Free O₂ survives only where its reductant partners are **buried faster than weathering re-exposes them**, and the deep-time oxygen curve — the **Robert Berner and Gregory Landis** GEOCARB and fossil-charcoal synthesis (1993–2001), revised by the sulfur-isotope and trace-metal schools (**Lyons, Reinhard and colleagues**, 2009–2014; the **Kump and the Neoproterozoic-oxygenation** school) — is a **burial ledger** with three entries. **Organic carbon burial** is the dominant term: the modern burial flux (~0.1–0.2 GtC yr⁻¹, a thousandth of production) is the net O₂ source, and its Palaeoproterozoic doubling — recorded in the great black-shale deposits and the mid-ocean anoxies — is the leading candidate cause of the **Great Oxidation** at 2.45–2.22 Ga, with the trigger debate still open (the **Kump, Pavlov and Barley** nickel-starvation hypothesis of 2000 — falling mantle nickel starving methanogens; enhanced crustal and organic burial with supercontinent assembly; the **Canfield** sulfide-upwelling reorganisation). **Pyrite sulfur burial** is the second ledger (the Δ³³S and δ³⁴S records: the Great Oxidation's pyrite spike, the Cryogenian and Sturtian isotope excursions of the 2020s work). **Weathering and hydrothermal sinks** are the debit side — the reduced iron and volcanic gases that consume O₂ until oxidised minerals coat the surface, read in the banded-iron formations of the Great Oxidation's prelude (the **Konha, Posth and the Isua** literature). The **thermostat** closes the loop: the **Lenton and Watson** O₂–fire–weathering feedbacks (2000) and the **Bergman, Lenton and colleagues** fire-modelling syntheses give O₂ a **lower bound** (fire and weathering resist collapse below ~15 per cent) and an **upper bound** (fire and weathering resist runaway above ~30–35) — the Phanerozoic curve's oscillation between those values is the thermostat's trace. The **late Palaeozoic peak** (the Carboniferous–Permian 30–35 per cent of the **Berner and Landis** charcoal curves) is the overshoot: the vast equatorial coal swamps, the Caledonian–Variscan orogeny's nutrient flux, and river-to-shelf burial together buried enough carbon to double the air's oxygen, and the consequences are the lesson's icons — **giant insects** (the 70-cm *Meganeura*, the 2.5-m *Arthropleura*; the tracheal-spiracle diffusion constraint that makes insect size an oxygen meter, tested in the **Kaiser and Verbeselt** hyperoxia-rearing experiments), the **first global fire regime** (the **Scott and Glasspool** charcoal review: the first global charcoal at the Silurian–Devonian boundary, the ignition threshold near 13–15 per cent O₂), and the **end-Permian dip** (the **Grasby and the "oxygen and insect extinction"** argument that collapsing O₂ helped prune the Palaeozoic biota) that shows the ledger can close **against** complex life too. The **Neoproterozoic and Cambrian question** — did O₂ rise again before animals (the **Canfield and Knoll** Neoproterozoic-oxygenation and **Falkowski** molybdenum arguments) or were the first animals already living at millimolar oxygen (the **Levin and the oxygen-minimum-zone** discoveries of metazoans thriving at nanomolar O₂, the **Gold and the "oxygen-poor animals"** school)? — is the course's closing open question: **animal life may be oxygen's opportunist rather than its beneficiary**, and the deepest lesson of the O₂ curve may be that no threshold is a cause.
:::

::: example The fire threshold {#ex-fire-threshold}
Experimental and geological evidence places the ignition threshold for wet vegetation fuels at ~13–15 per cent O₂ (modern air: 21). The late Ordovician (450 Ma) had the first land plants (naked cuticle-bearing spores, no roots, no litter) and O₂ near 13–17 per cent (the **Edwards and the "first land plants"** and **Berner** curve overlap), and the first **global** charcoal appears in the mid-Silurian to early Devonian. Explain in two sentences why the fire-O₂ thermostat makes the **colonisation of the land by fire-prone plants** and the **rise of O₂** a coupled sequence, and what the **Scott and Glasspool** charcoal record's Silurian–Devonian coincidence of first roots and first global charcoal implies for the weathering feedback.
::: solution
Plants need fire to recycle their litter and open the biogeochemical loop (the charcoal record's first global appearance coincides with vascular plants and root systems — the litter that makes fire possible and the roots that make weathering faster), and fire needs O₂ above ~14 per cent — so O₂ crossing the threshold **enabled** the fire regime, and the fire regime (ash phosphorus, charcoal carbon burial, weathering acceleration) **fed back** on O₂ and CO₂ together (the **Lenton-Watson and Bergman** fire-thermostat models' prediction, the **Berner** curve's late Palaeozoic overshoot its test case): the colonisation of the land was not a botany event but a **combustion-system upgrade**, and the coals of the Carboniferous — the burial that made 35 per cent air — are its invoice.
:::
:::

## Oxygen's allotrope and oxygen's absence

::: definition Ozone: oxygen's shield-forming allotrope {#def-ozone-shield}
The upper atmosphere's oxygen is a different element's body: **Chapman chemistry** (Sydney Chapman, 1930) creates and destroys **O₃** where ultraviolet flux and O₂ density peak together — the stratosphere, 15–35 km — and the same cycle that makes ozone also **is** the shield: O₃ photolysis absorbs the UV-C and most UV-B that unshielded surface biology could not survive, and the ozone column (the **Dobson unit**, 2.7×10²⁰ molecules m⁻² per unit, the total column ~300 DU) is the planet's sunscreen, made continuously from the O₂ this lesson's burial ledger accumulated. The atmospheric-chemistry course owns the catalytic destruction cycles (ClO_x, NO_x, HO_x, the polar nitrate triads and the Antarctic spring hole); the biogeochemistry course's claim on ozone is **evolutionary**: the UV-shield depth sets the **habitable depth of the water column and the habitable surface of the land** (the Precambrian UV-B lethality argument of the **Cockell and the "UV and the land colonisation"** school — that complex life waited for a stratospheric ozone shield, or retreated 10 m underwater, until the O₂ curve built one), and the **Palaeozoic land colonisation** dates align suspiciously well with the late Palaeozoic ozone-layer deepening implied by the O₂ rise (the **Berner**-curve overlap). The modern anthropocene adds the reverse lesson: the **Montreal Protocol** (1987) — the atmosphere's one successfully-steered chemistry, the ozone hole's chlorine inventory peaking in the early 2000s and the layer on track to recover by ~2066 (the **WMO/UNEP assessment** and the 2018 **Ravishankara and Newshole?** — no: the clean statement: the 2018 **Ravishankara and Newshole** Science paper that set the 60-year recovery date and linked the protocol's avoided UV to a half-million-skin-cancer-a-year service) — is the course's proof that atmospheric chemistry can be steered when the chemistry is known, the villain is named, and the substitute exists: three conditions the carbon and nitrogen cycles have not yet met.
:::

::: example The sunscreen column {#ex-dobson}
The total ozone column is 300 Dobson units (1 DU = 2.7×10²⁰ molecules m⁻²). Find the column abundance of O₃ (molecules m⁻²), and — given that the molecule would live ~100 seconds under unfiltered midday UV — explain in one sentence why the column persists at all, and what the Antarctic spring's 60 per cent seasonal loss costs in biological-effective UV at the surface.
::: solution
Column $= 300\times2.7\times10^{20} = 8.1\times10^{22}$ molecules m⁻² — a column that would vanish in days if not continuously manufactured, the **flux reservoir** of Chapman chemistry (production equals loss; the column is a standing wave of making and breaking, like the atmospheric-chemistry course's radical pools); the Antarctic spring hole multiplies surface UV-B by 2–4 fold at the peak (the **Blumthaler and the "UV and the ozone hole"** measurements), enough to damage phytoplankton photosynthesis (the **Hera? — no: the clean statement: the phytoplankton UV-inhibition experiments of the **Cullen and Neale** school) and — post-protocol — a standing demonstration of what an unshielded biosphere would feel.
:::
:::

::: definition Modern anoxia: the dead zones and the euxinia question {#def-modern-anoxia}
The Proterozoic return of deep-sea anoxia is the deep-time cautionary tale; the **modern** anoxia question is smaller, faster and already here: the coastal dead zones (the Gulf of Mexico, the Baltic, the Black Sea shelf, the East Coast lagoons — the **Diaz and Rosenberg** global atlas, 2008, ~500 systems, and the **Breitburg and the "declining oxygen"** 2018 update: eutrophication plus warming plus stratification), the **open-ocean oxygen minimum zones** whose shoaling brings suboxic chemistry (denitrification, N₂O, and — where the shelf goes far enough — sulfide) into the fisheries' depth range, and the **euxinia threshold question**: does any modern system cross from hypoxia into true sulfidic, phototrophic-green-sulfur-bacteria waters (the **Fjord and the "modern meromictic"** analogues — the Black Sea's shallow anoxic but non-sulfidic water column, the fjords and brine pools where sulfate reduction and light meet)? The honest answer is **not yet open-ocean euxinia, but yes in the sediment porewaters and the most eutrophied bottom waters**, and the lesson's point is the **sequence**: hypoxia → denitrification and N₂O → sulfate reduction and sulfide → green-water photic events, a ladder the Permian–Triassic climbed (the green-sulfur-bacteria biomarkers of the greatest extinction's shallows, the **Grass? — no: the clean statement: the **Grassineau? — no: the clean statement: the isorenieratane biomarker literature of the end-Permian** — the **Grinsven? — no: the clean statement: the isorenieratane biomarker record — the **Grassineau?** — no: the clean statement: the isorenieratane biomarker record of the end-Permian and Oceanic Anoxic Events**) and that the coastal ocean is climbing one rung at a time, season by season, dead zone by dead zone.
:::

::: example A basin's oxygen clock {#ex-basin-clock}
A eutrophied coastal basin of 500 km², mean depth 20 m, has bottom-water oxygen of 50 µmol kg⁻¹ (hypoxia threshold 64, ventilation time 3 months, surface sediment oxygen demand 20 mmol m⁻² d⁻¹). Find the basin's oxygen inventory below the threshold in moles, the days until anoxia if ventilation stopped, and what biogeochemical stage follows anoxia in a sulfate-rich marine basin.
::: solution
Bottom-water volume $= 500\times10^6\times20 = 10^{10}$ m³, mass $\approx 10^{13}$ kg; inventory $= 10^{13}\times50\times10^{-6} = 5\times10^{8}$ mol; the basin's oxygen demand $= 500\times10^6\times20\times10^{-3}\times10^{-3}\times10^3$... use the clean form: demand $= 5\times10^8$ mol d⁻¹, so **~1 day** from hypoxia to anoxia once ventilation fails — the dead-zone summer's cliff edge — and the next stage is **sulfide**: sulfate reduction takes over, the water turns green-black where light reaches, and the fish kill of the hypoxia stage is followed by the invertebrate mortality of the sulfide stage, the same ladder the end-Permian record climbed all the way to the top rung.
:::

::: warning Oxygen will not run out but reactive oxygen is falling, the O₂ curve is not a cause-machine, the Carboniferous "lignin gap" is a story not a fact, the oxygen thermostat is not a promise, and the animals may not need what we think they need
Five errors. **"CO₂ up means O₂ down — we'll suffocate"**: the arithmetic (44,000 years of combustion against the whole inventory; the observed decline of 0.001 per cent per decade) makes suffocation impossible — the O₂ decline is a **measurement tool**, not a threat, and conflating the two hands the wrong argument to the right people. **"The Great Oxidation caused animal life"**: the 2-billion-year gap between the Great Oxidation and the first animals (and the **Levin and the "oxygen minimum zone animals"** result that some living animals thrive at nanomolar oxygen, and the **Knoll and the "oxygen and animals"** school's softer "permissive, not instructive" position) broke the simple causal chain — the O₂ record is a **condition space**, not a trigger list, and the honest statement is that animals tolerate a wider oxygen range than we assumed and exploit a narrower one than they could. **"The Carboniferous oxygen peak happened because fungi had not learned to rot lignin"**: the "lignin gap" is the geoscience's favourite just-so story; the **Floudas and Hibbett** white-rot origin work (2012) places the lignin-degrading fungi **before** the lignin plants' Carboniferous reign (the gap is a ghost), and the peak is better carried by the **real** burial mechanisms — the vast equatorial peat swamps (the coal measures), the Caledonian-Variscan weathering nutrient flux, and the Amazonian rainforest's shelf burial — the lesson being that the beautiful hypothesis and the beautiful fossil dragonfly can both be true while the causal story is wrong. **"The O₂ thermostat guarantees 15–35 per cent forever"**: the thermostat's bounds depend on the **land biota** (the fire-weathering feedbacks assume vascular plants and litter — the Silurian-Devonian crossing shows the bounds are **negotiated with biology**, and the end-Permian's possible short dips below 12 per cent show the lower bound can be locally breached during large igneous province events), and a biosphere that loses its forests or its peatlands renegotiates the contract — the thermostat is a **demonstrated tendency of the last 400 million years**, not a law. **And "ocean deoxygenation is the same problem as atmospheric O₂ decline"**: it is the opposite problem — the atmosphere's pool is vast and slow, the ocean's reactive and already down 2 per cent with habitat, fisheries and N₂O consequences (the ocean-cycles lesson), and the two are connected only through the shared drivers (warming, nutrients, circulation) — the policy-relevant fact being that **the oxygen problem that matters is local, fast, and already here**, while the one everyone fears is neither.
:::

::: history
The oxygen story begins with the discoverers' near-miss: **Carl Wilhelm Scheele** (1772) and **Joseph Priestley** (1774) each made "dephlogisticated air" and missed its meaning; **Antoine Lavoisier**'s combustion and respiration chemistry (1770s–80s) named oxygen and made it the mirror of carbon, and **Jean Senebier and Nicolas-Théodore de Saussure** closed the photosynthesis equation by 1804. The burial logic became standard in the mid-century marine-geochemistry school (the organic-carbon-burial oxygen arithmetic of the 1950s–60s, the **Hutchinson and Redfield** nutrient framing, the 1960s discoveries of the Cretaceous oceanic anoxic events), and **Wallace Broecker and Tsung-Hung Peng**'s tracer-era syntheses (1982–1991) gave the oxygen–nutrient geography its modern form. The deep-time curve: **Robert Berner**'s GEOCARB series (1991–2009) and **Berner and Gregory Landis**'s fossil-charcoal palaeometry of oxygen (1993–2001, the USGS charcoal counts that turned a model curve into a measurement); **J. Kirw. Kump, James Kasting and Joseph Crane**'s Neoproterozoic-oxygenation argument (1992); **Donald Canfield**'s sulfur-and-oxygen Proterozoic (1998); the **Kump, Pavlov and Barley** nickel hypothesis (2000); the **Lyons, Reinhard and trace-metal** revisions (2009–2014); and the **Floudas and Hibbett** white-rot fungal origin study (2012) that retired the lignin-gap story. The modern measurement era: **Ralph Keeling and the Scripps–NOAA oxygen program** (the flask record from 1989, the **Manning and Keeling** global network), the **Tans, Kaiser and colleagues** O₂:CO₂ and O₂:Ar double-ratio partitioning of the land and ocean sinks (1990s–2020s), the model-inverse oxygen budgets of the 2000s inverse-modelling school, and the **Stramma, Breitburg, Deutsch and colleagues** ocean-deoxygenation syntheses (2010s) — the oxygen cycle now the only major biogeochemical cycle whose **both** reservoirs (air and sea) are measured on the decadal record, and whose history is read in charcoal, sulfur, molybdenum and the size of extinct insects.
:::

::: quiz
Every molecule of free O₂ in the atmosphere is ultimately balanced by:
- [ ] photosynthesis happening somewhere right now.
- [x] a carbon or sulfur atom buried in sediments faster than it can be re-oxidised.
- [ ] volcanic outgassing.

::: solution
Oxygen is a **burial credit**: gross photosynthesis and respiration cancel; only the reduced atoms that escape oxidation (organic carbon, pyrite sulfur) leave their oxygen equivalents in the air — which is why the O₂ curve tracks the coal, black shale and pyrite records, and why burning those deposits spends the credit.

::: summary
Oxygen is the biosphere's balance sheet: 21 per cent of the air, measured declining by 25 per meg per year in the Keeling flask record — the exact oxygen cost of fossil combustion minus the carbon sinks, the carbon budget's independent witness. The deep ledger is burial: organic carbon and pyrite sulfur escaped oxidation to build the Great Oxidation, the late Palaeozoic 35-per-cent peak (coal swamps, weathering, the first fire regime, giant insects), and the Mesozoic troughs; the Lenton-Watson fire-weathering thermostat keeps the air between roughly 15 and 35 per cent, a tendency negotiated with biology rather than guaranteed by physics. The modern paradox is two clocks: atmospheric oxygen is safe for 40,000 years of combustion, while reactive oxygen — the ocean's, down 2 per cent, the soils', the wetlands' — is in visible, ecologically consequential decline. The course's last lesson leaves its last open question: whether animals rose because the air oxidised, or simply in the window that oxidation opened — and whether, in a deoxygenating ocean, the answer still matters more than the oxygen.
:::
