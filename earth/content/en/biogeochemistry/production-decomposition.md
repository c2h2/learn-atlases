The biosphere runs on two engines pointed in opposite directions: one that fixes a quarter of the sunlight's usable energy into carbon and builds bodies out of it, and one that takes the bodies apart and gives the carbon back, faster or slower depending on temperature, water, and the chemistry of the litter. Between them — in the gap between production and decomposition — sits every unit of wood, peat, soil and fossil fuel on Earth, and the entire question of whether the living surface is a sink or a source. **Primary production** and **decomposition** are therefore the two largest biogeochemical fluxes on the planet (each moving ~60 gigatonnes of carbon a year through land, the ocean's engine moving nearly as much again), and their balance — not either alone — sets the growth of the biosphere, the inventory of soils, the oxygen of the air and the size of the atmospheric CO₂ reservoir on all timescales shorter than geology. This lesson is the pair of engines: what runs them, what limits them, and what happens when a warming world speeds the second more than the first.

## Production: where light meets nutrient

::: definition The light-use economy and its limits {#def-production}
Photosynthesis is a **light-use** business with a **nutrient** constraint, and the global pattern falls out of the two. Gross primary production (GPP) totals ~123 GtC yr⁻¹ on land and ~110 in the ocean; half is respired by the producers themselves, leaving **net primary production** (NPP) of ~56–60 on land and ~45–50 in the sea — the biomass budget available to everything else. On land, production follows the **Monteith light-use-efficiency** formulation (John Monteith, 1972): GPP = (absorbed photosynthetically-active radiation) × (efficiency, 0.5–2 g C per MJ, downgraded by cold, drought and nutrient poverty), which is why terrestrial NPP maps as a gradient from tropical rainforest (~2,000 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹) through temperate forest and savanna (~600–1,200) to grassland, boreal forest, tundra and desert (<100), and why the satellite era's solar-induced fluorescence and flux-tower networks (FLUXNET's 500+ sites) can watch the biosphere's photosynthesis **fainting in marine heatwaves and droughts** — the 2015 and 2023 Amazon anomalies visible from orbit. In the ocean, light penetrates only the top ~100 m of a 3,700-m world, and the surface is stripped of nutrients by its own fertility: production is therefore confined where physics **reunion**s light and nutrient — the high-latitude and coastal upwelling zones, the equatorial divergence, the Southern Ocean's iron-stunted margins — while the stratified subtropical gyres (70 per cent of the ocean surface) are blue deserts at desert NPP rates, and the **shelf seas** (8 per cent of the area) punch far above their weight (a quarter to a third of marine production on a fraction of the water, the fishery consequence obvious). The limiting-nutrient geography follows Redfield's ledger: iron in the high-nitrate low-chlorophyll seas (the Martin iron hypothesis and its six mixed fertilisation experiments), nitrogen where diazotrophs can relieve it (nitrogen where diazotrophs can relieve it — *Trichodesmium* and its kin, whose blooms track warm, stratified, phosphorus-rich water — while), phosphorus in the ancient, N-replete oceans of deep time (and perhaps the future), and light itself in the polar spring bloom and the deep chlorophyll maximum. The human imprint on production is now the largest single one in the biosphere: fertilizer and irrigation have **doubled** the NPP of cropland relative to its natural state (the "land-sparing by intensification" argument), CO₂ fertilisation has lifted global GPP by ~12 per cent since 1900 (the satellite greening, half of it in the drylands where water rather than nitrogen binds the response), and the same CO₂ and warming that green the land are **browning** the ocean (stratification cuts nutrient supply; the global marine NPP trend of the satellite record is flat-to-declining, its magnitude a standing sensor-record debate).
:::

::: example A forest's light budget {#ex-lue}
A temperate forest absorbs 500 W m⁻² of photosynthetically-active radiation over the growing season at light-use efficiency 1.0 g C per MJ, with no stress downgrades. Find daily GPP (C m⁻² d⁻¹) for a 12-hour day, and the annual NPP if respiration takes half and the season runs 200 days.
::: solution
PAR energy $= 500\times43{,}200 = 2.16\times10^{7}$ J $= 21.6$ MJ m⁻² d⁻¹; GPP $= 21.6\times1.0 = 21.6$ g C m⁻² d⁻¹; annual GPP $= 21.6\times200 = 4{,}320$; NPP $= 2{,}160$ g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ — a productive forest, and the exercise is the Monteith engine entire: sunlight × efficiency × season, with the climate writing checks (drought, cold, vapour pressure) that the efficiency has to cash.
:::
:::

## Decomposition: the engine of rot

::: definition Litter quality, climate, and the anaerobic alternative {#def-decomposition}
Decomposition returns the fixed carbon through the **heterotrophic engine** — bacteria and fungi mining litter and soil with extracellular enzymes — at ~55–60 GtC yr⁻¹ on land and ~50 in the ocean's water column and sediments, and its rate is set by three factors in tension. **Substrate quality**: labile compounds (sugars, proteins) vanish in days, while **lignin, suberin, waxes and tannins** resist enzymatic attack — the lignin:N ratio and the "chemical lability" of litter (the **Yasso** decay-sequence hypothesis: quality governs the fast fraction and the molecular memory of what survives into soil) explain why needle litter accumulates and manure vanishes; the modern twist is the **microbial-efficiency-matrix** framework of the **Mark Bradford and Joshua Schimel** school): microbial carbon-use efficiency (CUE, the fraction of consumed carbon built into biomass rather than respired, ~0.3–0.6) determines whether carbon is **lost or transferred** down the soil profile, and microbial necromass — dead bugs, not dead plants — now accounts for **the majority of stable soil organic matter** (the "necromass shuffle" that rewrote soil science's textbook in the 2010s). **Climate**: decomposition's temperature sensitivity (the van 't Hoff **Q₁₀**, typically ~1.5–2.5 — rates rise 50–150 per cent per 10 °C) exceeds photosynthesis's, which is why **soils are the climate system's positive feedback**: a warming that speeds rot more than growth drains the largest terrestrial carbon pool; and moisture gates everything (aeration controls whether the endpoint is CO₂ or the wetter world's **methane**, waterlogged soils and wetlands emitting ~150–200 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹, rice paddies and ruminants adding the human half of the modern budget). **Depth and protection**: the old textbook idea that chemical recalcitrance builds long-lived soil carbon has been dethroned by **physico-chemical protection** (carbon adsorbed to clays and occluded in aggregates escapes enzymes regardless of its chemistry — which is why deep soil carbon, old enough to be radiocarbon-depleted, turns over in millennia while surface humus turns over in years), and by the recognition that warming may free protected carbon too (the **priming effect**: fresh root exudates stimulating microbes to mine old carbon — a mechanism that makes the soil feedback **nonlinear** and the models' soil modules the weakest link in every land carbon-cycle projection). The decomposition engine, in short, is where the carbon cycle's fastest and largest uncertainties live: the same degree of warming that grows a forest may empty its soil, and the difference is written in enzyme kinetics and clay mineralogy, not in the CO₂ price.
:::

::: example The Q₁₀ wager {#ex-q10}
Soil respiration has Q₁₀ = 2. Find the rate increase for +3 °C, and the carbon lost from a 1,200 GtC active-layer soil pool (turnover 30 yr at baseline) over a century if warming speeds heterotrophic respiration by that factor with no compensating input increase.
::: solution
$2^{0.3} = 1.23$ — a 23 per cent speed-up; baseline respiration $= 1{,}200/30 = 40$ GtC yr⁻¹, warmed to 49, so the extra loss over 100 yr is $9\times100 = 900$ GtC in the no-offset limit (the true number is far smaller because inputs also rise and substrate depletes — the point of the exercise is the **scale of the wager**: even a third of this limit is a multi-decade airborne-fraction catastrophe, which is why every CMIP land model's soil module is audited before its politics is).
:::
:::

## The soil library and its permafrost annex

::: definition The soil carbon inventory and its vulnerable annexes {#def-soil-library}
Soil holds **1,500–2,400 GtC** (the range is the depth and rock-carbon argument of the 2010s: 2 m versus 3 m, inorganic carbon included or not) — more than the vegetation and the atmosphere combined, and the great majority of it **not** in the fast surface layer: the mean-age distribution of soil carbon is bimodal, with a third-to-half turning over in years-to-decades and the deep, mineral-protected fraction in centuries-to-millennia. The inventory's geography is decomposition's autobiography: the peatlands (3 per cent of land, ~550 GtC — a quarter of the soil pool in an area the size of Australia, built by **waterlogging's arrest of rot**, the Holocene's slowest and most reliable carbon sink), the permafrost region's **~1,500 GtC** of frozen organic matter (soil plus the yedoma ice-complex, twice the atmospheric inventory, frozen by a climate that is now gone), and the grassland and cropland profiles whose fate (ploughing the steppe has released **half to two-thirds** of their carbon wherever agriculture arrived — the Siberian, North American and Ukrainian granary histories readable as a carbon-loss stratigraphy). The **permafrost** annex is the century's great carbon-cycle uncertainty: current projections (the **Schuur, McGuire and colleagues** ground-truthed assessments) place the permafrost-region release at **tens of GtC yr⁻¹ by 2100 under high emissions** (soil CO₂ and methane in a ratio the models still disagree on by a factor of two), with the **abrupt thaw** (thermokarst collapse, subsea methane) as the tail risk the models under-represent because they resolve metres, not slumps; the **global peat and dryland** annexes add their own wildcards (dryland "biogeochemical hot pulses" after rain events, the Amazon dieback's transition from sink to source documented across 2010–2020 fire and drought years). The bookkeeping consequence is the course's central warning: the **land sink of the last 60 years (~3 GtC yr⁻¹) is not a service the biosphere owes** — it is the incidental surplus of CO₂ fertilisation on a nitrogen-fertilised, partly reforesting planet, and the same biosphere's decomposition engines, droughts and fires are already **reducing** its margin (the 2010s–2020s airborne-fraction trend, the 2023 Northern Hemisphere fire and anomaly years). A soil-carbon strategy for climate (the "4 per 1000" initiative's arithmetic: +0.4 per cent per year in soil carbon would offset a quarter of emissions) is therefore both the most attractive and the most over-sold idea in carbon management — attractive because soils are vast and agriculture touches them daily, over-sold because saturation, nitrogen limits and the reversal risk (ploughed, burned, drained: the carbon leaves faster and more permanently than it came) bound the promise to a fraction of the slogan.
:::

::: example The permafrost arithmetic {#ex-permafrost}
The permafrost region holds ~1,500 GtC in the top 3 m; models project ~10–20 per cent thawed and a third of that decomposed to CO₂/CH₄ by 2100 under high emissions. Find the carbon release range, its CO₂-equivalent in ppm (as CO₂), and why the methane fraction makes the range wider than it looks.
::: solution
$1{,}500\times0.10\times\tfrac{1}{3} = 50$ to $1{,}500\times0.20\times\tfrac{1}{3} = 100$ GtC; as CO₂ that is $50/2.12 \approx 24$ to $100/2.12 \approx 47$ ppm. The methane fraction (a few to ~20 per cent of the anaerobic thaw, each gram worth ~80 CO₂-years over a century) **widens the upper bound by decades of warming-equivalent** and arrives front-loaded in the first 50 years — which is why the permafrost question is not "how much carbon" but "how fast, in what gas, and through which landscape: gradual drying or abrupt collapse."
:::
:::

## The aquatic middle: the microbial loop

::: definition The microbial loop and the fate of marine production {#def-microbial-loop}
In the sea, most of what photosynthesis fixes never reaches a mouth that matters to fisheries or to the carbon sink: it is intercepted by the **microbial loop** (**Farooq Azam and Tom Fenchel and colleagues**, 1983) — the pathway in which dissolved organic carbon (a third to a half of marine GPP, leaked by healthy cells and torn from them by viral lysis, the "viral shunt" that recycles a quarter to a half of daily production before it is eaten) is taken up by free-living bacteria, built into bacterial biomass, and grazed by flagellates and ciliates that are in turn eaten by copepod larvae. The loop's two numbers organise marine biogeochemistry: **bacterial growth efficiency** (the marine CUE, ~0.1–0.4 in warm oligotrophic water, higher in cold, nutrient-rich, diatom-rich systems — the reason warm water respires carbon closer to the surface), and the **export ratio** (the fraction of NPP that sinks below the mixed layer, ~0.1–0.4, the quantity the biological pump — and hence the atmospheric CO₂ it holds down — actually depends on). The loop's lesson generalises to every decomposition system: **the size structure of the decomposer community, not just the temperature, sets whether carbon leaves the system or circles it** — a diatom bloom sinking in a week delivers carbon to depth; the same carbon leaked as dissolved matter in a warm gyre is breathed back within days, and the ocean's response to warming (smaller cells, lower export efficiency, shallower respiration — the "shallowing of the ocean's metabolism" documented by the BATS and HOT time-series, running since 1988 and 1989) is a restructuring of the loop more than a change in its rate.
:::

::: example The export ratio decides {#ex-export}
A province has NPP of 300 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹. Compare the carbon exported below the mixed layer (per m² per year) at an export ratio of 0.1 (warm, microbial-loop-dominated) versus 0.35 (diatom bloom), and explain in one sentence why the ocean's carbon sink is a **food-web** question.
::: solution
$300\times0.1 = 30$ versus $300\times0.35 = 105$ g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ exported — a 3.5-fold difference from **community structure alone**, with the same photosynthesis. The carbon sink depends on whether production leaves the surface as fast-sinking aggregates and faecal pellets or dissolves into the loop and is respired in situ: which is why "more CO₂, more algae, more sink" fails as arithmetic, and why the century's marine carbon projections hinge on who eats what, how fast, and how deep.
:::

::: warning NPP is not the carbon story alone, Q₁₀ is not a promise, necromass rewrote the textbook, the ocean's engine is not exempt, and "fertilise the biosphere" is bounded by nitrogen and water
Five errors. **"Protect forests = protect carbon"**: forests store carbon and **cycle** carbon — a mature forest's NPP is large but its net ecosystem production is near zero, and the wood product and soil accounting (plus the albedo trade-off of boreal afforestation, which can warm more than the carbon cools) decides whether a hectare of trees helps; the carbon that matters is the **net** of the two engines, not the gross photosynthesis the posters celebrate. **"Warming will speed decomposition by Q₁₀, full stop"**: the acclimation literature (microbial communities down-regulate enzymes, substrate pools deplete, and the apparent Q₁₀ falls as warming proceeds — the "priming paradox" and the 2010s warming experiments showing smaller responses than the seasonal Q₁₀ predicts) says the **effective** feedback is smaller than the naive one — and the deep-soil and permafrost literature says the naive one may still be right where substrate is not limiting; the honest range spans a factor of three. **"Soil organic matter is humus, chemically stubborn"**: the humus-model century is over — stable soil carbon is mostly **mineral-associated microbial necromass and plant-derived polymers protected by surfaces and aggregates**, and "humification" is a process of death and attachment, not of molecular distillation; the practical consequence (that **physical disturbance — ploughing, drainage — breaks protection and releases old carbon regardless of chemistry**) is why no-till and rewetting work and "add compost to build humus" half-works. **"The ocean's decomposition engine is separate"**: marine heterotrophic respiration consumes ~90 per cent of the ocean's NPP **inside the mixed layer** (the microbial loop returning carbon before it can be exported), and the export efficiency that determines the biological pump's strength — and its weakening under warming (larger cells, warmer-adapted communities with lower CUE, more respiration per unit export) — is the ocean carbon-cycle question of the century. **And "CO₂ fertilisation will keep greening the land"**: the fertilisation effect is real (~12 per cent GPP lift) and already **decaying** as nitrogen and water bind it (the FACE experiments' progressive nitrogen limitation, the dryland greening's sensitivity to the next drought, the Amazon's 2015/2023 reversals); the land sink is a **transient subsidy from the future**, and the decomposition engines, fires and harvests are writing the bill.
:::

::: history
Production science begins with **Joseph Priestley and Jan Ingenhousz**'s plant-air experiments (1770s) and **Julius von Liebig**'s mineral and minimum-law doctrine (1840); the carbon-measurement era with **Frederick Blackman**'s limiting-factor physiology (1905) and **Daniel Arnon, Robert Emerson and William Arnold**'s photosynthesis quantum work (1930s–40s, the 1961 Nobel to the latter two with **Melvin Calvin**'s cycle). **Monteith**'s light-use efficiency (1972–79) turned physiology into remote sensing, and the **BIOMASS/UMD and MODIS** production models (Running and colleagues, 1990s–2000s) plus the FLUXNET tower network (1997–) and OCO-2 and solar-induced-fluorescence satellites (2010s) made the terrestrial engine a daily observable. Decomposition science runs from **Marcelin Boullanger and the Rothamsted school**'s long-term manure and plot experiments (the 1843– plots that first measured humus decay), through **Waksman and Starkey**'s soil-microbiology textbook (1924), the **Jenny and Schlesinger** biogeochemistry syntheses, and the **Q₁₀/Chen-Widlund** microclimate debates, to the 2010s rewrites: the microbial-efficiency-matrix framework of **Bradford and Schimel**, the warming-experiment syntheses of **Reichstein, Janssens, Peñuelas and colleagues**, and the permafrost inventories of **Tarnocai, Hugelius, Schuur and McGuire** — the last two decades' lesson that the soil's bookkeeping had to be rewritten before the century's could be trusted. The ocean's engine is the **Redfield–Krogh–Steemann Nielsen** lineage (the 1930s–50s: **August Krogh**'s respiration measurements, **Marcus Steemann Nielsen**'s ¹⁴C method of 1952 that made marine production measurable), the biological-pump theory of **Volk and Hoffert** (1985) and **Jorge Sarmiento and Denis Gruber**'s ocean biogeochemistry synthesis (2006), and the global ocean colour program (CZCS 1978 through Sentinel-3 and PACE 2024) that watches the blue deserts and the blooms from orbit.
:::

::: summary
The biosphere's two engines — photosynthesis (~230 GtC yr⁻¹ gross, half respired on the spot) and decomposition (~110 GtC yr⁻¹ returned) — bracket every carbon question on human timescales: production is a light-use economy limited by water, nutrients and (at sea) the reunion of light with deep nutrients; decomposition is an enzyme economy governed by litter quality, temperature (Q₁₀ ~2), moisture (CO₂ versus CH₄) and, above all, **protection** — the mineral and aggregate custody of necromass-derived carbon that makes soils the largest land pool. The vulnerable annexes (peat, permafrost's 1,500 GtC, the deep mineral soil) are the century's positive-feedback watchlist, and the land sink's future — the transient surplus of fertilisation minus the accelerating engines of rot — is the difference between a biosphere that helps and one that bills us. The engines are now observable daily from towers and orbit; the science's frontier is the part still hidden in soil, where the carbon is old, protected, and thawing.
:::
