The carbon cycle of the present is an experiment conducted on a single planet by a single species, with the measurement record as the lab notebook. Since 1850 the surface system has taken in roughly **690 gigatonnes of fossil and land-use carbon**, and about **46 per cent of it** remains in the sky, where the excess has lifted CO₂ from 278 to 424 ppm, warmed the surface ~1.45 °C, and set the ocean's chemistry back 0.11 pH units — all while the system's sinks, running at full speed, have done their best to keep up. This lesson is the **contemporary carbon budget**: the numbers, who measured them, how they close, where the sinks are failing, and what the remaining carbon budget for a temperature target actually means. It is the biogeochemistry course's balance sheet, and the arithmetic that every climate-policy number in the next two courses inherits.

## The ledger: emissions, sinks, airborne fraction

::: definition The modern carbon budget {#def-modern-budget}
The budget's two sides, in the **Global Carbon Project's** annual accounting (the 2023 edition's central values, ±1σ): **sources** — fossil and industry emissions **9.3 GtC yr⁻¹** (36.8 GtCO₂, of which cement's ~0.6 and flaring's ~0.4 are the non-combustion remainder), land-use change **1.1 GtC yr⁻¹** (deforestation ~1.3 minus the regrowth and woody-encroachment sink ~0.3, the sign of the residue still contested region by region), total **~10.4 GtC yr⁻¹** or ~38 GtCO₂; and **fates** — atmospheric growth **4.7 GtC yr⁻¹** (the Keeling curve's 2.5 ppm yr⁻¹ climb, itself accelerating at ~0.03 ppm yr⁻² — the second derivative that makes "the growth rate is fine" a category error), ocean sink **2.9 GtC yr⁻¹** (the physically driven solubility-and-pump uptake of anthropogenic carbon, measured by shipboard DIC repeats and the GLODAP inventory, whose cumulative total since 1850 is **175 ± 35 GtC**, ~26 per cent of all emissions), land sink **2.9–3.2 GtC yr⁻¹** (the residual, but corroborated by inventory, flux-tower, and atmospheric-O₂ and Δ¹⁴C accounting: CO₂ fertilisation plus nitrogen deposition plus temperate reforestation, minus the burning and harvesting). The **airborne fraction** is ~46 per cent of the decade, with no significant trend once the interannual noise (El Niño's land-respiration and fire years: 2015–16's record 3.4 ppm jump) is filtered — the budget's most reassuring and most treacherous number, reassuring because the sinks have so far scaled with emissions, treacherous because they scale as a **fraction of a disequilibrium** (the ocean absorbs what the surface warming and stratification let it, the land what nutrients and moisture allow) and not as a promise. The budget's **uncertainties are asymmetric in consequence**: the fossil number is known to a few per cent (combustion chemistry and fuel ledgers), the land-use number ±0.5 with regional arguments behind it (the "Amazon source, African sink" re-scaling of the 2020s), and the **land sink's variance** — the ±0.5–1 GtC El Niño and fire swings — is what will decide whether the airborne fraction stays at 0.45 or climbs, and it is the term the observing system (the GEOSS/GCOS carbon-pilot satellite constellation, the OCO-2/3 and GOSAT footprints, the GEDI and NISAR forest-structure radars) is being built to pin down this decade.
:::

::: example The budget closes, with a warning label {#ex-gcp}
Using 9.3 fossil + 1.1 land-use = 10.4 emitted, 4.7 atmospheric growth, 2.9 ocean: find the land sink as a residual, its uncertainty if each term carries ±0.5 (root-sum), and the year CO₂ crosses 450 ppm if growth stays at 2.5 ppm yr⁻¹ from today's 424.
::: solution
Land $=10.4-4.7-2.9 = 2.8$ GtC yr⁻¹, $\pm\sqrt{4\times0.5^2} = \pm1.0$ as a residual of four terms — the land sink is the budget's **least measured and most subtracted** term, the accounting warning in one error bar; the crossing is $(450-424)/2.5 \approx 10$ years, the arithmetic that makes "2030s" the current language of 1.5 °C (the climate-change course's projections, not this lesson's, own the uncertainty in the growth rate itself).
:::
:::

## The ocean's other ledger: acidification

::: definition The carbonate chemistry and the Ω ledger {#def-acidification}
The ocean's carbon uptake is not chemically free. Dissolved CO₂ sets the **carbonate system** — $pCO_2 \propto [H^+][DIC]/[CO_3^{2-}]$ in its essential proportionality — and adding 30 per cent more DIC to a system buffered at pH 8.1 has three measured consequences: surface pH has fallen **0.11 units** (a 30 per cent rise in hydrogen-ion concentration, the largest chemical change the surface ocean has known in ~55 million years, since the PETM), the **Revelle factor** (the buffer ratio, ~10–15 and rising as carbonate is consumed) means each added tonne of CO₂ raises surface $pCO_2$ about ten times more than dilution alone would — the ocean's willingness to keep absorbing **decays as it absorbs** — and the carbonate ion, the raw material of shells, has fallen ~8 per cent globally, deepest fastest in the cold, high-latitude waters where solubility loads carbon most heavily. The operational metric is the **saturation state** $\Omega = [Ca^{2+}][CO_3^{2-}]/K_{sp}$: above 1, shells do not dissolve; below, seawater is corrosive. Surface $\Omega_{aragonite}$ has fallen from ~3.4 to ~3.0 globally and to **near 1 in parts of the Arctic and the Southern Ocean**, where winter waters already undersaturate; the **pteropod** field record (the "sea butterflies" whose shells pit in undersaturated water, documented off the US west coast's upwelling since 2007 and in the Arctic's summer undersaturation) is the visible-in-a-microscope end of the ledger, and the **oyster hatchery collapses** of the mid-2000s Pacific Northwest (the acidified upwelling water that killed larval crops, now managed with on-site chemistry monitoring — acidification's first documented economic loss, and a preview of the tropical coral and foraminiferal ledgers to come). The deep-sea ledger is slower and larger: the **lysocline and carbonate compensation depth have risen ~400–500 m** since 1850 (the deep Pacific's and Atlantic's calcite dissolution response to the young anthropogenic carbon pulse, measurable in the sediment record against the preindustrial baseline), and the mass of carbon the sediments will dissolve in compensation (the "neutralisation" of the acidification by alkalinity addition from below) is a **10⁴–10⁵-year** service the ocean will perform for us whether or not we stop emitting — the same slow chemistry that saved the PETM climate, on the same clock that makes today's surface-chemistry change **geologically instantaneous** (the rate of DIC addition is an order of magnitude faster than any known natural analogue in the Cenozoic record, the foraminiferal δ¹¹B and B/Ca evidence that the PETM's carbon release, at its fastest, still spread its pH minimum over millennia rather than decades).
:::

::: example The Revelle arithmetic {#ex-revelle}
The buffer (Revelle) factor is defined $RF = (\Delta pCO_2/pCO_2)/(\Delta DIC/DIC)$. With surface DIC 2,000 µmol kg⁻¹, preindustrial pCO₂ 280 µatm and RF = 12, find the pCO₂ rise from adding 100 µmol kg⁻¹ of anthropogenic DIC to a closed surface parcel, and explain in one sentence why RF rises as DIC is added.
::: solution
$\Delta DIC/DIC = 100/2000 = 0.05$; $\Delta pCO_2/pCO_2 = 12\times0.05 = 0.60$, so pCO₂ rises $0.60\times280 = 168$ µatm — to ~448, the reason the surface ocean is a **willing but quickly-stiffening** partner: as carbonate ions are consumed, the buffer's denominator falls and each future tonne raises pCO₂ (and lowers pH and Ω) **more** than the last — the sink's fractional efficiency decays with use, the opposite of a bank and the same as a depleting one.
:::
:::

## The land's ledger, the budget's future, and removal

::: definition Land sinks, the remaining budget, and the removal reality {#def-rcb}
The land sink of the last half-century is a **four-part coalition with two defections in progress**: CO₂ fertilisation (the FACE and chamber evidence, ~0.5–1 GtC yr⁻¹ of extra uptake at current CO₂, declining per ppm as nitrogen and phosphorus bind it), nitrogen deposition from the human cycle (the fertilisation of the temperate forests and the boreal, ~0.4–0.6, and the same flux's acidification and eutrophication debts), temperate and boreal **revegetation** (the China-and-Europe greening, the US Southeast's abandoned-farmland forest, ~0.5–1), and the **old-growth carbon retention** that fires and logging now attack — against the defections: the **Amazon's transition** (the 2010–2020 inventory and atmospheric evidence that the southeastern arc has become a source of ~0.3–0.5 GtC yr⁻¹, the biomass decline documented by the long-term plot network's mortality rise and the 2015/2019/2023 fire and drought years), and the **boreal fire-and-bark-beetle regime** (the 2014–2023 Canadian fire seasons that turned whole-year continental inventories positive, the 2023 fires alone emitting ~0.4–0.7 GtC of biomass carbon — comparable to the entire annual land-use flux of a large emitting country). The **remaining carbon budget** (the RCB of the carbon-budget literature and the IPCC's special report: the cumulative CO₂ emissions compatible with a temperature limit, derived from the near-linear **transient climate response to cumulative emissions**, ~0.45 °C per 1,000 GtCO₂, plus the non-CO₂ forcing and the Earth-system already-committed): for 1.5 °C at 50 per cent probability, **~250 GtCO₂ from the start of 2024** — a decade of current emissions; for 2 °C, ~1,200 — a generation and a half — and the budget's honest annotation is that it is **not a quota but a probability statement**, spent at 400 GtCO₂ per decade, and already partly consumed by the warming we have locked in. **Carbon dioxide removal** (CDR) enters the budget as the arithmetic of closure: every 1.5 °C pathway in the IPCC's assessed scenarios uses it (median ~5 GtCO₂ yr⁻¹ by 2050, ~600 GtCO₂ cumulative by 2100), while the **deployed reality** of the mid-2020s is ~0.002 GtCO₂ yr⁻¹ of engineered removal (the direct-air-capture and bioenergy-with-capture plants, a factor of ~2,000 short of the modelled 2050 median) against ~2–3 GtCO₂ yr⁻¹ of conventional land-based storage (forests and soils, with the permanence and reversal risk of fire and markets attached) — the gap between the models' assumption and the deployment curve being the single most consequential number in climate policy, and the reason the honest reading of the budget is "**emissions now, removal sized to what physics and land can actually deliver, and no accounting that assumes the third option**."
:::

::: example The budget's clock {#ex-rcb}
Remaining budget for 1.5 °C (50 per cent): 250 GtCO₂; current emissions 40 GtCO₂ yr⁻¹ with non-CO₂ forcing fixed. Find the exhaustion year at flat emissions, and the year if emissions fall 7 per cent per year (the Paris-aligned slope), and state why the first number is not the one policy uses.
::: solution
Flat: $250/40 \approx 6$ years (the mid-2030s); at −7 per cent yr⁻¹ the cumulative sum $40\,(1-e^{-0.07T})/0.07 = 250$ gives $1-e^{-0.07T} = 0.438$, $T \approx 8.6$ years — the **modest** extension that decay buys, because the budget is cumulative and even fast declines keep spending. The first number is not policy's because budgets are **probabilistic and renewable in principle** (negative emissions can give budget back — the removal question's whole weight in one asterisk), but the arithmetic's lesson stands: the budget is measured in **years of current emissions**, not decades of planned ones.
:::
:::

::: definition The biosphere's breath: CO₂'s seasonal cycle {#def-seasonal}
Mauna Loa's curve is a sawtooth drawn by the northern biosphere: CO₂ falls ~6 ppm each boreal spring and summer as photosynthesis outruns respiration across three continents, and rebounds each autumn and winter as rot and dark respiration outrun growth — an amplitude of ~3 ppm at the summit and **15 ppm or more at high-northern stations** (Barrow/Utqiaġvik, Alert), where the winter air mass originates. The record's two slow changes are climate findings in their own right: the amplitude has **grown ~10–20 per cent since the 1960s** (the enhanced high-latitude growth signature — greening, lengthening growing seasons, the **Myneni, Running, Nemani and colleagues** 2002 detection and the 2020s inverse-model confirmations), and the **spring turnaround has advanced by days-to-weeks** at northern stations (the phenology shift readable directly in the curve's inflection, corroborated by satellite greenness and bloom records, and the one climate signal latent in Keeling's own 1960s data). The seasonal cycle is also the variance lesson in miniature: the same photosynthetic engine that draws the sawtooth drives the land sink, its amplitude is the **gross flux** the budget's small imbalance lives inside (a ~3 GtC yr⁻¹ sink riding on ~120 GtC yr⁻¹ of seasonal breathing — which is why the sink is hard to measure and easy to disrupt), and El Niño years raise the rebound's size (2015–16's 3.4 ppm growth: ~7 GtC of extra tropical respiration and fire carbon, the largest land-sink failure yet recorded in the curve). The frontier is the cycle's **asymmetry under future climate**: if warm springs advance growth but warm autumns, droughts and fires add more respiration than the longer season fixes, the amplitude grows while the **annual mean rises anyway** — a greener-looking planet with a faster-breathing and weaker-sequestering land.
:::

::: example An El Niño written in ppm {#ex-elnino}
The 2015–2016 El Niño left an extra ~3.4 ppm of CO₂ growth above the trend. Convert the anomaly to gigatonnes of carbon, and explain in one sentence why the event is read as a **land** signal rather than an ocean one.
::: solution
$3.4\times2.12 \approx 7$ GtC of extra carbon in the atmosphere in ~18 months — the size of the entire annual land-use flux, released by tropical drought and fire and reduced land uptake (the ocean's El Niño response is small and opposite: stronger winds and equatorial cooling slightly boost its uptake). The δ¹³C and Δ¹⁴C anomalies of the same years — fossil-depleted, biomass-burning-flavoured air — convict the land with an isotope signature, the tracer method catching a hemisphere-scale flux failure in the act.
:::
:::

::: warning The budget is a probability not a quota, the sinks are fractions not promises, acidification is not "just pH", removal is not a plan B already in the drawer, and the land sink's variance is the system's main character
Five errors. **"We have 250 GtCO₂ so we can keep going till the 2030s"**: the remaining budget is a **50-per-cent-probability** statement around a central estimate whose own uncertainty (the climate sensitivity spread, the non-CO₂ forcing, the committed warming) is ±250 GtCO₂ — the budget could be half or double, and the precautionary reading is smaller, not larger; "exhaustion" is also not a cliff but a probability that rises with every tonne. **"The ocean and land will keep absorbing ~half"**: they absorb half because of **two disequilibria** (the surface-to-interior mixing gradient, the fertilisation-minus-disturbance balance), both of which weaken as the system adjusts (the Revelle factor rising, the stratification strengthening, the Amazon turning, the fires eating the boreal) — the airborne fraction's gentle upward trend is the first measurement of the weakening, and the CMIP6 ensemble's land-sink spread (a factor of two by 2100) is the honest statement of the future. **"The ocean is going to pH 7"**: the end-of-century projections at high emissions are **0.3–0.4 units**, not 1.4 — and the point is not the round number but the **saturation states and the organisms**: Ω_aragonite crossing 1 in the polar summer, the pteropod's pitting shell, the coral calcification decline documented across the Great Barrier Reef's 1980s–2020s skeletal records, the larval oysters of Washington — acidification is an **organism-by-organism, region-by-region** ledger, not a global pH headline. **"We'll capture it later"**: the models' removal medians (5 GtCO₂ yr⁻¹ by 2050) exceed deployed reality by three orders of magnitude, the land-based half competes with food and biodiversity for the same land the appropriation ledger already overspends, and the permanence question (a forest is a loan, a reservoir is a lease, a saline aquifer is the only product with a geological term) means removal's **storage** is a separate engineering problem from its **capture**; the budget arithmetic is kinder to "cut now" than to "cut later plus remove," and every year of delay raises the second option's price. **And "the interannual wiggle is noise"**: the El Niño land-respiration swing (±1 GtC yr⁻¹) and the fire regime are **the signal of the future climate's land sink**, not a perturbation around it — the 2015, 2023 and future fire years are the land sink's variance arriving on schedule, and a carbon policy built on the mean without the variance is a policy built on the part of the cycle that is ending.
:::

::: history
The budget's measurement lineage is the atlas's longest continuous experiment: **John Tyndall**'s greenhouse spectroscopy (1859–61) and **Svante Arrhenius**'s first CO₂-climate calculation (1896, his 3 °C sensitivity within a factor of today's range); **Thomas Chrowder Chamberlin**'s rival carbon-cycle reasoning (1899, including the ocean-uptake caveat); **Roger Revelle and Hans Suess**'s 1957 demonstration that the surface ocean could not absorb the fossil pulse (the Revelle factor's discovery, the prediction that made the Keeling measurement necessary and sufficient); **Charles David Keeling**'s Mauna Loa and Antarctic curves from 1958 (the preindustrial-intercomparison with the ice cores, the sawtooth's seasonal reading as the northern biosphere's breath, the curve's 65-year continuation by his son **Ralph Keeling** and the Scripps/NOAA program); the **WOCE/CLIVAR repeat-section program and the GLODAP inventories** (the 1990s–2020s shipboard inventories that counted the ocean's anthropogenic carbon directly, **Sabine, Gruber, Khatiwala, Key** and colleagues' independent-inventory methods); the **FLUXNET** towers (1997–), the **Global Carbon Project**'s annual budgets from 2006 (**Pierre Friedlingstein, Corinne Le Quéré, Pep Canadell, Robbie Andrew** and a hundred-author team — the closest thing planetary science has to a quarterly earnings release); the **satellite era** (GOSAT 2009, OCO-2 2014, the carbon-delta-plus and anthropogenic-NO₂ emission-verification studies of the 2020s that can now **detect a country's emissions change from orbit** — the transparency architecture the Paris Agreement's enhanced transparency framework was written to use); and the **remaining-budget literature** (**Myles Allen**'s carbon-climate mathematics and the 2013–2018 carbon-budget synthesis, the IPCC Special Report 1.5's 2018 formalisation, and the Global Carbon Budget's annual updates). The acidification ledger runs from the nineteenth-century carbonate chemistry of the Challenger expedition's analyses (1880s) through **Uwe Riebesell, Ken Caldeira, Scott Doney, Victoria Fabry, Jean-Pierre Gattuso and Richard Feely**'s 1990s–2000s discovery that the surface pH decline was already measurable, the pteropod and hatchery reports of 2007–2009, and the **SOCEP/OSNAP and BATS/HOT** chemistry time-series (1980s–) that make the pH decline as observed a fact as the CO₂ rise.
:::

::: quiz
The Revelle factor (~12) describes:
- [ ] how fast the ocean mixes anthropogenic carbon to depth.
- [x] how strongly a given fractional DIC addition raises surface pCO₂ — the buffer's stiffness.
- [ ] the airborne fraction's decade trend.
- [ ] the ratio of land to ocean sink.
::: solution
It is the carbonate buffer's leverage ratio, $(\Delta pCO_2/pCO_2)/(\Delta DIC/DIC)$: high RF means the ocean resists absorbing more CO₂ per unit surface pCO₂ difference — and it **rises** as carbonate is consumed, so the sink's efficiency decays with use.

::: summary
The contemporary carbon budget is measured, not modelled: ~10.4 GtC yr⁻¹ emitted (9.3 fossil, 1.1 land-use), 46 per cent airborne (the Keeling curve's 2.5 ppm yr⁻¹), ~26 per cent ocean (175 GtC since 1850, counted on ships), ~30 per cent land (the residual, the fertilisation coalition, the weakening Amazon and fire years its defections). The ocean's chemical bill — pH down 0.11, Revelle factor rising, aragonite saturation approaching unity in polar water, the lysocline 500 m shallower than it was — is the other, quieter crisis, and the land's bill is a sink built from transient subsidies. The remaining carbon budget (250 GtCO₂ for a coin-flip at 1.5 °C, six years of current emissions) is a probability with a slope, and the removal capacity every pathway assumes is three orders of magnitude from deployment. The budget closes today; the question of the century is whether it closes tomorrow, and that question lives in the sinks' variance, the fires' calendar, and the difference between the models' removal and the world's.
:::
