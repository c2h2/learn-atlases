In April 1815 a volcano the size of a mountain range ended its argument with the sky, and the following year — 1816, the "Year Without a Summer" — gave Europe thirteen months of rain, frost in June at Albany, harvest failure and the last subsistence famine of the Western world, while in a Geneva villa a shivering Lord Byron's houseparty invented Frankenstein by candlelight because the sun would not come out. Tambora's winter was the last great climate event that happened **to** the historical record; everything since has happened **in** it, because the two centuries after Tambora built the two things that make the last two thousand years a laboratory rather than a chronicle: a global thermometer network (and before it, national series like the Central England Temperature, running unbroken to 1659) and a proxy science fine enough — annual-resolution trees, cave drip-water, ice chemistry, historical documents — to reconstruct the centuries before instruments and test them against the instruments where they overlap. The result is the field's most politically contested and best-evidenced chapter: the discovery that the Medieval and Little Ice Age "climates" of popular memory were regional patterns, not global states; that the twentieth century's warmth left the natural range of the last two millennia within centuries of its start; and that the "hockey stick" controversy of 2003–2006 — the most attacked figure in the history of environmental science — ended not in doubt but in **consilience**: a dozen independent proxy families agreeing on the same shape. This lesson is what the last two thousand years say, and why saying it was so hard.

## The evidence base

::: definition Instruments, documents, and high-resolution proxies {#def-ltm-evidence}
The last two millennia are the only climate interval read with **four archives at once**. The **instrumental record** (HadCRUT, Berkeley Earth, the ERSST ocean series) begins sparsely around 1850 and becomes near-global by 1900; the **Central England Temperature** (1659–) is the world's longest national series, and the **long instrumental series** (Uppsala, Vienna, Padua, De Bilt, the Greenland Danish meteorological stations) anchor the 1700s and 1800s; ship logs and, now, rescued digitised logbooks (the Old Weather and International Comprehensive Ocean-Atmosphere Data Set projects) push the oceans back to the 1750s. **Historical documents** — Chinese dynastic histories' freeze and harvest records (the Lake Taihu and Bohai Sea ice dates running two thousand years), European chronicles, parish registers of famine, Icelandic annals, the "price and harvest" series that Le Roy Ladurie turned into climate history in the 1960s — are qualitative-to-semiquantitative, richly dated, and read with the source-criticism of the discipline that invented them. **High-resolution proxies** supply the global coverage the documents lack: annually-resolved tree-ring width and density networks (thousands of series, the densest in the Northern Hemisphere, absolute-dated by cross-dating), speleothem δ¹⁸O at annual-to-decadal resolution, ice-core chemistry (the sulphate layer catalogue of every eruption back to 500 BCE and beyond, the lead and now the industrial black-carbon curves), corals (the only tropical annual archive, ENSO's diary), and varved and annually-banded lake sediments. The **overlap test** — reconstructing the 1900s from the proxies alone and comparing to the gauges — is the field's recurring exam, passed with the lesson that tree-ring **density** and networks of everything do well at decadal-to-multidecadal warmth and poorly at the daily variance and spatial detail the instruments measure; and the **dating precision** (dendrochronology's absolute years, ice-core layer counts, speleothem uranium-thorium) is what lets the last two millennia ask the causal questions — what followed the 536 CE dust veil, what followed each eruption, what the sun's minima did — with event-attribution rather than correlation.
:::

::: example The overlap test {#ex-overlap}
A tree-ring density network reconstructs the 1970–2000 mean Northern Hemisphere summer temperature at 0.35 °C above its 1961–1990 baseline with an independent-instrument value of 0.42 °C. Find the bias, and explain why the "divergence problem" (ring widths in some high-latitude networks ceasing to track post-1950 temperature) makes the **calibration design** — not the correlation coefficient — the honest quality metric.
::: solution
Bias $= 0.42-0.35 = 0.07$ °C — small, but systematically **cold-biased**, the direction that matters: if widths diverge downward while density holds, a network built on widths under-reads the modern warmth, and a network built on density must be checked for the opposite drift. The honest metric is out-of-sample skill (calibrate on 1900–60, verify on 1960–2000, and report both), because a high in-sample $R^{2}$ on data the calibration already saw measures fitting, not seeing — the statistical rule the hockey-stick debates taught a generation of policy-adjacent statisticians to demand.
:::
:::

## The canonical episodes

::: definition Roman Climate Optimum, Late Antique Little Ice Age, MCA, LIA {#def-episodes}
**Roman Climate Optimum** (~200 BCE–150 CE, the "Roman Warm Period" in its older, vaguer naming): stable, warm, wet-in-the-right-places Mediterranean conditions — the grain base of the empire's density — read from speleothems, lake cores, Alpine ice and documents; its end (the transition to the more variable, wetter-later regime of Late Antiquity) coincides with, and may partly reflect, the shift in orbital and volcanic forcing that the **536 and 540 CE double eruption** — the largest climate perturbation of the Common Era before 1815, whose dust veil "gave forth its light without brightness like the moon giving forth its light" (Procopius, an actual eyewitness sentence), whose cooling the tree rings and ice cores now place at 1.5–2.5 °C for a decade, and whose 2024 source identifications (an Icelandic eruption for 536, Salvador's Ilopango for 540) completed the catalogue — and whose human sequel, the **Justinianic Plague** (541 CE, the first *Yersinia pestis* pandemic, whose demography the pollen-based "agriculture collapse" curves of the 2020s trace), opened the **Late Antique Little Ice Age** (536–660 CE, Büntgen and colleagues' synthesis: a 125-year cold spell sustained by the eruptions, the plague's land abandonment, and the sun's own quiet century). The **Medieval Climate Anomaly** (~950–1250 CE) is the field's great correction: real and dramatic in the North Atlantic (Norse Iceland 870, Greenland 985 and its pastures, the Vinland voyages, European expansion and the medieval cultivation of Alpine valleys and wheat to 62°N), but **not a global state** — the tropics' and Southern Hemisphere's medieval centuries are asynchronous, and the "Medieval Warm Period" of popular memory is a North Atlantic pattern wearing a global name. The **Little Ice Age** (~1300–1850, the term **Gordon Manley**'s 1939 coinage, the evidence base **Le Roy Ladurie**'s 1967 synthesis first assembled) is equally a pattern-with-a-forcing: the Thames frosts of 1683–84 (the frostfairs), the 1693–97 Nordic famine that killed a tenth of Finland and Sweden's population, the glacier advances that destroyed Swiss villages (the document archive that made the LIA the first "reconstructed" climate change), and a forcing budget the 2010s largely closed: **a train of tropical eruptions in the 1250s–1500s** (Samalas 1257, the 1450s Kuwae, the 1600 Huaynaputina, the 1815 Tambora finale), **solar minima** (the Wolf and the **Maunder Minimum**, 1645–1715, the sunspot-less decades named for **Edward Maunder** and resurrected by **John Eddy** in 1976, whose climate effect — real but small, a watt or so — is now dwarfed by the volcanic term in most budgets), and the slow orbital cooling of the neoglacial, with **volcanism as the trigger** (the Miller and colleagues 2012 demonstration that a sequence of large eruptions, amplified by sea-ice and ocean feedbacks, can initiate LIA conditions from a warm state — the mechanism that makes the LIA the abrupt-change literature's Holocene case study). Between the LIA and us sits the **twentieth century**: warming that the 2019 **PAGES2k "consilience"** synthesis — 717 proxy series, six families, all continents — placed **outside the range of the last two thousand years by mid-century and beyond everything in the record by 2000**, with the Medieval's regional maxima never approaching the twentieth-century global mean after the 1940s: the conclusion the next section's fight was about.
:::

::: example Volcano versus sun, in one budget {#ex-forcing-budget}
The 1815 Tambora aerosol layer forced roughly −2 W m⁻² for two years (λ = 0.8 K per W m⁻²); the Maunder Minimum's total-solar-irradiance anomaly was ~−1 W m⁻² sustained for ~70 years. Find the peak volcanic temperature response, the equilibrium solar response, and explain why the LIA's cold centuries track the **eruption train** better than the sunspot record.
::: solution
Tambora: $0.8\times2 = 1.6$ °C of transient response (observed Northern Hemisphere summers ~1–1.5 °C cooler in 1816 — the calibration of "volcanoes work" at the historical scale); solar: $0.8\times1 = 0.8$ °C equilibrium, but the real TSI anomaly is better estimated at −0.1 W m⁻² (the old "1 W" figures came from over-reading the sun–climate correlation), giving ~0.1 °C — a century's ripple. The eruption train, by contrast, delivers −0.2 to −0.5 W m⁻² **decade after decade** (the ice cores record ~70 eruptions of ≥Laki size between 1450 and 1700), and each eruption's sea-ice and ocean-coupling memory overlaps the next: the LIA is a **volcanically paced, ocean-buffered** cold state with the sun as supporting actor — the 2010s attribution result, and the reason "the sun caused the Little Ice Age" survives only in the literature of climate scepticism, not of climate physics.
:::
:::

## The hockey stick and the consilience

::: definition MBH, the critics, and the 2019 settlement {#def-hockey-stick}
In 1998–99 **Michael Mann, Raymond Bradley and Malcolm Hughes** reconstructed Northern Hemisphere temperature for 1,000 (then 600) years from a then-unusual network of 358 proxy series and produced the figure — a flat-ish "shaft" of medieval-to-nineteenth-century variation, a twentieth-century "blade" rising out of it — that became the icon of the climate debate and the target of the most sustained attack in the history of science: the **NAS panel** (the 2000 North committee, whose report "reconstructing past climate" endorsed the approach while demanding more transparency), the **Wegman Report** (2006, the US House energy-committee review by the statistician Edward Wegman, whose own errors were then documented by the statistical community), the climate-research-unit email archive episode of 2009 (which produced eight parallel investigations and no finding of scientific misconduct, while establishing that scientists, like all scientists, write unguarded emails), and Mann's two decades of defamation litigation (ending 2024 in a Canadian Supreme Court refusal to hear the case and a settlement with the source of the most serious allegations). The methodological disputes — whether principal-component-based proxy selection suppressed low-frequency variance ("spatial PCA flattens the medieval," the Mann–Bradley–Hughes critics were right about for their 1999 estimator, and the field's answer was to rebuild the reconstructions with variance-preserving methods and get the same shape), whether tree rings over-represent the 20th century, whether the medieval was warm — were real, publishable, and **resolved by doing the work**: the **PAGES2k** project's 2013 hemispheric synthesis (the warmest 30 years of the last 1,400 are the last 30; the MCA never approaches 20th-century warmth in the global mean), the 2019 **consilience** study (independent proxy families — trees, corals, ice, sediments, documents, model-simulated "virtual proxies" — agreeing on the same global-to-regional temperature history within errors), and the **model-paleo** fusion (the last-two-millennia CMIP ensembles reproducing the forced evolution: volcanic cooling episodes, the Maunder dip, the twentieth-century takeoff) that now anchors attribution: **the pre-1900 variability is explicable as volcanoes plus sun plus internal noise; the post-1950 warmth is not explicable without greenhouse forcing, and the 2010s–2020s exceed every reconstruction's maximum**. The settlement is not that the hockey stick was right in every pixel — the 1999 version was too cold in the medieval and too smooth, and Mann's own later reconstructions moved accordingly — but that the **conclusion** (the 20th century left the two-millennium range; the MCA was regional; the LIA was forced) survived every attack because it was, and is, what the data say from a dozen directions at once.
:::

::: example How far outside? {#ex-outside}
A two-millennium reconstruction gives a pre-industrial natural range of ±0.2 °C around its 1–1800 mean (95 per cent envelope) and a 2010s anomaly of +1.1 °C. Find how many envelope-half-widths the present exceeds the pre-industrial mean, and explain why the correct statement is "outside the range of the last two thousand years" rather than "the warmest day in recorded history."
::: solution
$1.1/0.2 = 5.5$ half-widths — the present sits far outside the two-millennium natural envelope, which is the defensible claim. The slogan version fails on three counts: reconstructions are **hemispheric-to-global annual means** (the claim is about the mean state, not daily weather), the envelope is a **reconstruction's** range (with its own uncertainty, which is why the careful statements say "likely" and cite the proxy families), and "warmest in 2,000 years" **understates** the deeper result — the Pliocene and Eocene were warmer, and the point is not the superlative but the **discontinuity**: the present leaves the range of the epoch civilisation belongs to, which is exactly what the Holocene-comparison framing of every planetary boundary means.
:::
:::

## Beyond temperature: the event catalogue

::: definition Radiocarbon excursions, the sun's diary, and nature's experiments {#def-miyake}
The last two millennia's highest-resolution chronology runs on cosmogenic nuclides — and its strangest discoveries are their **spikes**. Radiocarbon in annually-ringed trees shows two abrupt ~1.5–2 per cent jumps, in 774/775 CE and 993/994 CE (the **Miyake events**, discovered by Miyake and colleagues in 2012 when they read Japanese cedars to annual resolution looking for supernova signals): global, single-year ¹⁴C production pulses whose energy source is most plausibly **extreme solar particle events** — superflares from a star we thought tame — with supernova and gamma-ray alternatives constrained and largely excluded by the isotope ratios (¹⁰Be, ³⁶Cl in the ice mirrors of the events are present but small, favouring the solar interpretation that the 2020s modelling consensus now favours). Their significance is dual: for chronology, the spikes are **time markers of unprecedented precision** (a single year, globally synchronous — the 775 layer has dated Japanese and European timbers and even refined the Santorini-eruption debate's floating chronologies by proxy), and for civilisation, a standing reminder that the solar engine can deliver events a thousand times the 1859 Carrington event's particle flux — an **existential infrastructure risk** (the modern grid and satellite layer has never been tested at 774 scale, and the event's occurrence twice in the last three millennia is the recurrence-rate estimate) that palaeoclimate contributes to space-weather policy. The same radiocarbon series, smoothed, gives the sun's **secular diary** — the medieval and modern maxima, the Maunder and the ~1450s minima (the radiocarbon production curve, ground-truthed by the sunspot record where they overlap and by the ¹⁰Be ice record where they do not) — and the **solar-climate correlation's** honest scale: irradiance changes of a few tenths of a W m⁻², a tenth of a degree at most, real but supporting-actor forcing (the 2010s PMIP last-millennium ensembles' verdict). The category's companion is nature's forced experiments: the **eruption catalogue** (the ice-core sulphate series now dates ~5,000 eruptions over 2,500 years, with the Sigl and colleagues 2015 and 2021 master chronologies supplying the forcing for every last-millennium model run — and resolving the field's favorite whodunits: the 1257 Samalas candidate for the "mystery eruption" that opened the LIA's cooling, confirmed by 2013 source work on Lombok; the 1452/3 Kuwae; the 1600 Huaynaputina, whose drought signature reaches Korean and Chinese records), each eruption a **replicated experiment in radiative forcing** whose climate response — the ~0.4–0.8 °C Northern Hemisphere coolings the CMIP ensembles now reproduce — calibrates the fast feedbacks the projections depend on. The last two millennia are not only a temperature story; they are the **forced-response laboratory** for the whole atlas.
:::

::: example Reading a Miyake year {#ex-miyake}
Tree-ring ¹⁴C jumps 1.9 per cent (≈1.2× the annual production added in one year, given the fast exchange with the atmosphere's small inventory) in 774/775 and then relaxes by ~30 per cent of the excess within a decade. Find the atmospheric ¹⁴C inventory's approximate residence-time behaviour implied, and why the spike's **single-year** width requires a production mechanism rather than a carbon-cycle one.
::: solution
The fast initial relaxation (~30 per cent in ~10 yr) is the atmosphere–ocean surface and biosphere exchange (the decadal carbon-cycle clock seen before the deep ocean takes over on its thousand-year one — the same exchange rates the fossil-CO₂ uptake literature measures, read from the other direction). The single-year rise requires a **production** pulse: carbon-cycle perturbations (volcanoes, fires, even the 536 dust veil's reduced photosynthesis) move carbon between reservoirs on decade-plus timescales and cannot stack a global isotope spike into one ring-year — only cosmic-ray production, instantaneous at tree-ring resolution, can. The exercise is the isotope end of the last-two-millennia method: **shape in time is mechanism**, the same logic that separates a volcanic cooling (years, follows sulphate) from a greenhouse warming (centuries, follows emissions) in every chapter of this atlas.
:::
:::

::: warning The MCA was real and not global, the LIA was forced and not solar-only, the hockey stick was wrong in details and right in conclusion, documents are data with a source-criticism, and "it has varied before" is still the wrong inference
Five errors. **"The Vikings grew wine in Greenland, so the Medieval was warmer than today"**: Norse Greenland (985–~1450) is real and climate-relevant — and was a **regional** anomaly that ended, via the Little Ice Age's sea ice and storms, in the colonies' extinction; no two-millennium reconstruction puts the MCA's global mean near the late-20th-century value, and the PAGES2k generation has said so with every proxy family available. **"The Little Ice Age was caused by the sun"**: the Maunder Minimum's radiative effect is ~0.1 °C; the eruption train plus ocean–ice memory does the work (the 2012 Miller et al. demonstration) — the sun is a real but minor player, and the "sun did it" narrative persists because it is the only version with a policy audience, not the only version with a budget. **"The hockey stick was debunked"**: what was debunked were specific 1999 estimator choices (the PCA low-frequency issue, real and fixed) and specific over-claims (the smoothing); the reconstruction's **conclusion** was independently reproduced by every method and archive family that tried, including the critics' own preferred approaches — the episode is the textbook case of self-correction working and the public being told the opposite. **"Historical documents are just stories"**: the Chinese freeze records, the Icelandic annals and the parish famine series are **dated, spatially fixed, and cross-checkable against proxies** (the 1600 Huaynaputina's drought signals appear in Chinese harvest records and tree rings in the same two years) — they are data with a source-criticism discipline, which is harder than gauges, not softer. **And "climate changed before, so today is natural"**: the last two millennia are precisely the experiment that refutes it — the pre-industrial excursions are **attributable** (eruptions, sun, internal), their global amplitudes are ≤0.2 °C, their rates are a fraction of the present, and the post-1950 signal fails every natural-forcing reconstruction while matching the greenhouse one: the historical record is not the escape hatch from attribution; it is attribution's calibration set.
:::

::: history
The field's deep roots are in **Le Roy Ladurie**'s 1967 *Histoire du climat depuis l'an mil* (the first modern climate history of the last millennium, from French wine-harvest and peasant records), **Gordon Manley**'s 1974 synthesis of the Little Ice Age (the name he popularised), and **Huntington?** — no: the earlier natural-history tradition (**Laville, and the seventeenth-century Instrumental Revolution's** weather diaries — the Royal Society's 1660s correspondence network that first proposed a global instrument grid). The proxy era: **Douglass**'s tree rings (1900s–20s), **Charles Brooks** and then **Hubert Lamb**'s paleoclimate syntheses (Lamb's 1977 *Climate: Present, Historical and Future* is the field's bible, his 1965 medieval-climate paper its manifesto), **Emiliani**'s and the isotope school's marine context, and the **LaMarche–Stommel** radiocarbon-sunspot synthesis (1968, the solar-climate program's modern start). The modern evidence base: the **International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme**'s and then **PAGES**'s coordinated reconstructions (the 2000s "2k" network), the **Old Weather** and logbook digitisation projects, the **ice-core volcanic sulphate catalogue** (Zielinski, **Sigl** and colleagues' 2015 and 2021 master chronologies — the eruption forcing series now used by every model), and the **Maunder–Eddy** solar episode (1852/1976). The controversy literature — **Mann, Bradley, Hughes** (1998, 1999), the **North** (2000) and **Wegman** (2006) reviews, the **NAS** 2006 "Reconciling observations" report, the **climate research unit** inquiries (2009–10), the **PAGES2k** hemispheric (2013) and **consilience** (2019) syntheses, and the **last-millennium CMIP** ensembles — is itself now a taught case study in how science argues with itself in public and, on this evidence, wins.
:::

::: quiz
The modern consensus on the Medieval Climate Anomaly is that it was:
- [ ] a globally synchronous warm state warmer than the late 20th century.
- [x] a real but regionally asynchronous pattern, warmest in the North Atlantic, never globally matching 20th-century warmth.
- [ ] entirely a fabrication of 19th-century historians.
- [ ] caused by a solar supermaximum.
::: solution
Regional, asynchronous, North-Atlantic-emphasised, and driven by a mix of modest solar gain, low volcanic activity and internal variability (the Atlantic multidecadal mode) — warm where the Norse farmed and the Alps were grazed, not warm everywhere, and nowhere near the modern global mean: the textbook case of "a pattern, not a state."
:::

::: summary
The last two thousand years are climate history read with four archives — instruments back to 1659 (England) and 1850 (world), documents with source-criticism, high-resolution proxies, and the ice's eruption catalogue — and they deliver three settlements: the medieval and Little Ice Age "climates" were **patterns** (regional, forced — volcanoes for the LIA, mostly — and never global states matching the present), the twentieth century left the two-millennium natural envelope by mid-century and every reconstruction's maximum by 2000 (the PAGES2k consilience), and the hockey-stick controversy, for all its genuine methodological lessons, ended with the conclusion standing because a dozen independent archives bend the same way. The epoch is the attribution calibration set: pre-1900 variability is explicable without greenhouse gas, post-1950 warmth is not explicable with it excluded. The Year Without a Summer was the last climate catastrophe of the historical record that was purely natural; the record since is the evidence that we stopped being able to say that — and the reconstruction science that proved it is the atlas's best demonstration that the paleo- and the present are one continuous, auditable experiment.
:::
