Beneath the sediment-water interface lies the climate record the planet keeps for itself: 70 per cent of the Earth's surface is ocean, its floor is a sink for everything the water column and the continents deliver, and where the supply is steady and the water deep enough, the ooze accumulates like a slow metronome — a centimetre or two per millennium, each one a page of shells, dust, and chemistry laid down by seasons no one wrote down. The recovery of that archive is one of the twentieth century's great engineering-and-science collaborations: from the Challenger's first dredgings in 1872 to the drilling fleets of the Deep Sea Drilling Project and its successors, which since 1968 have pushed cores through the sediment pile and, with them, an entire philosophy of Earth history — that the way to read the planet is to **sample it on a grid and date what you find**. This lesson is the sediment itself: what it is made of, what it can be made to say, and the events — anoxic episodes, sapropels, dust storms frozen in calcareous ooze — that it preserves in slow motion.

## The sedimentary setting

::: definition Four sediments, one chemistry, and the dissolution filter {#def-sediment-types}
Marine sediment is sorted by source into four families, and the map of them is a map of climate and circulation. **Calcareous ooze** (foraminifera and coccoliths — the white chalk of the Cretaceous is its extreme) dominates the fast-accumulating, well-flushed low-latitude seafloor; **siliceous ooze** (diatoms and radiolarians) rules the upwelling edges (eastern boundaries, the Southern Ocean's diatom belt) where nutrients feed glass instead of chalk; **terrigenous (lithogenous) mud and clay** records the continents — rivers, icebergs (the "ice-rafted debris" of Heinrich events, dropped where icebergs melted), and wind (the red clays of the North Pacific are Asian dust, their accumulation-rate curves one of the Cenozoic's monsoon-and-uplift chronicles); and **hydrogenous and cosmogenic** material (manganese crusts, extraterrestrial spherules, the rare iron meteorites recovered from the Pacific mud) supplies the geochemical levers. The filter that makes the system treacherous is **carbonate chemistry**: the calcite compensation depth (CCD, ~4–5 km, shallower in the glacial century's CO₂-poorer oceans) dissolves below its lysocline every shell, so a basin deeper than the CCD records **no carbonate** — and therefore no δ¹⁸O stratigraphy — precisely in the basins that best preserve anoxic and excursion records; the opal compensation depth plays the same trick on the siliceous archive. Accumulation rates span six orders of magnitude (open-pelagic ~1 cm/kyr; turbidite channels metres per event-year; abyssal red clay a millimetre per millennium), and every core is therefore a low-pass filter whose cutoff must be stated. The final setting is **bioturbation**: burrowers and deposit-feeders churn the top 5–20 cm — at typical accumulation rates, the last few thousand years — blurring event layers, recycling young shells downward and old ones upward, and setting the resolution floor that every "millennial-scale stability" claim must clear.
:::

::: example Mass accumulation and the dust ledger {#ex-mar}
A North Pacific core accumulates at 4 cm kyr⁻¹ with dry bulk density 0.5 g cm⁻³. Find the total mass accumulation rate in g m⁻² yr⁻¹, and the non-carbonate (dust) flux if 60 per cent is terrigenous — noting that modern aeolian dust to this region is ~10 g m⁻² yr⁻¹.
::: solution
$\mathrm{MAR} = 4\ \mathrm{cm\,kyr^{-1}}\times0.5\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 2\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}\,kyr^{-1}} = 20\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,kyr^{-1}} = 20\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$; the dust fraction is $0.6\times20 = 12$ g m⁻² yr⁻¹ — a factor of several above today's, the standard form of the glacial-dust statement (arid expanded source regions, stronger winds, and a shallower CCD all at once), and the arithmetic by which "the ice age was dustier" became a number rather than an adjective.
:::
:::

## Reading the forams

::: definition Planktonic and benthic, assemblage and chemistry {#def-forams}
Foraminifera — single-celled protists with calcareous tests, some surface-dwelling, some bottom-crawling — are the deep sea's workhorse because they are abundant, species-rich, temperature-honest, and preservable, and they are read in two registers. The **assemblage register** counts species: planktonic assemblages are converted to sea-surface temperature, salinity and seasonality by transfer functions (CLIMAP's 1981 modern-analogue machinery, refined into modern MAT and PFT methods with ~1–2 °C skill), benthic assemblages to bottom-water temperature and oxygen; the coiling direction of *Neogloboquadrina pachyderma* (left-coiling polar, right-coiling subpolar) is the classic single-species thermometer, and the first rule of the register — **species ecology is assumed constant** — is the same uniformitarian bet as everywhere else. The **chemistry register** measures the tests themselves: δ¹⁸O (ice volume plus bottom-water temperature for benthics, the global ice-volume curve's backbone — the benthic record is dominated by the glacial–interglacial water signal, which is why the stack reads ice sheets, not local climate); Mg/Ca, Li/Ca and the boron and clumped-isotope systems for temperature and pH; Cd/Ca and the rare earths for nutrient water-mass mixing (the glacial reorganisation of Atlantic nutrients, the Pacific's carbon reservoir); and the carbon isotopes δ¹³C, whose deep-water gradient maps the balance between the Atlantic's surface-to-deep overturning and the Pacific's sluggish stratification — the tracer behind every "did the conveyor stop?" claim. The two registers are cross-checked against each other and against the sedimentology (sorting, ice-rafted debris, rare-earth patterns), because the forams' one standing weakness is **dissolution and selective preservation**: the record is written by what survived, and the preservation index (fragment abundance, test etching) must be plotted beside every isotope curve — the discipline's version of checking whether the thermometer melted.
:::

::: example The stack as an ice meter {#ex-stack}
The benthic δ¹⁸O stack LR04 runs from ~3.3 ‰ at the Holocene to ~5.3 ‰ at glacial maxima. Of that 2.0 ‰ amplitude, roughly 1.0 ‰ is the **water** (ice-volume) signal, and 0.1 ‰ of water-isotope change corresponds to ~11 m of sea level. Estimate the glacial–interglacial sea-level drop and compare with the coral and shelf estimate (~120 m).
::: solution
The water half-amplitude of 1.0 ‰ implies $1.0/0.1\times11 = 110$ m — within a hair of the 120 m read from raised coral terraces, which is the cross-check that validates both archives. The remaining ~1.0 ‰ of benthic amplitude is deep-water cooling, and separating the two (with Mg/Ca or clumped isotopes on the same cores) is the IMBIE programme's standing task: benthic δ¹⁸O is an ice-meter **after calibration**, not directly.
:::
:::

## Events in slow motion

::: definition Anoxic episodes, sapropels, and the Mediterranean school {#def-events}
Not all of the record is gradual rain: the seafloor archive's dramatic chapters are **events**. The **oceanic anoxic events** (OAEs) of the Cretaceous — Aptian OAE 1a (~120 Ma) and Cenomanian–Turonian OAE 2 (~93.5 Ma), whose black shales drape every ocean basin, carry massive δ¹³C excursions, and record the mid-Cretaceous greenhouse's productivity–anoxia–mercury (the Caribbean Large Igneous Province for OAE 2) feedbacks — are the deep-time precedent for every "rapid carbon addition" argument in this atlas, and the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum's marine chapter (benthic extinction, carbonate dissolution, a −3 ‰ carbon isotope excursion) is its best-instrumented smaller sibling, covered in the deep-time lesson. The **sapropels** are the Mediterranean's varves: tens of dark, organic-rich layers (the Holocene S1, deposited ~9–6 ka, is the thickest and best dated) interleaved in the sea's usual pale marl, laid down when African monsoon rains turned the Nile into a sediment and nutrient river, freshened the surface, stratified the basin and starved its bottom of oxygen — the African Humid Period written in the sea, and the type case of **regional hydrological change recorded by a basin-wide archive**. The Mediterranean is in general the discipline's classroom: the **Messinian salinity crisis** (5.96–5.33 Ma), when the basin evaporated to a deep halite-filled desert whose evaporites now host hydrocarbon reservoirs, is the endmember of basin isolation; the **Younger Dryas sapropel** and the Holocene millennial-scale salinity records are its abrupt-change laboratory; and the Black Sea's "sapropel" — once the exhibit for a catastrophic Holocene Mediterranean-inflow flood, now read as a more gradual freshening — is the cautionary companion, a textbook example of a dramatic hypothesis correctly demoted by better coring and dating. The modern additions are the **slow events**: the sediment-trap and time-series mooring records that watch the seafloor's biology respond to the ocean's seasonal and decadal forcing in real time, and the **subseafloor biosphere** — the kilometre-deep, energy-starved microbial province discovered by the drilling programmes, whose metabolism alters the very geochemistry the palaeoclimate reader uses, and whose existence rewrote the global carbon cycle's budget at the same moment the isotope stratigraphy was maturing.
:::

::: example Sapropel S1's ledger {#ex-sapropel}
Sapropel S1 in an eastern Mediterranean core is 42 cm thick, spans 9.0–6.0 ka, and contains 4 per cent organic carbon (dry density 0.8 g cm⁻³). Find its organic-carbon mass accumulation rate in g m⁻² yr⁻¹ and compare with the marl above (~0.1 g m⁻² yr⁻¹).
::: solution
Accumulation $= 42\ \mathrm{cm}/3000\ \mathrm{yr} = 0.14\ \mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$; organic MAR $= 0.14\times0.8\times0.04\times10^{4} = 4.5$... working units: $0.14\ \mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}} \times 0.8\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 0.112\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}\,yr^{-1}} = 1120\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$ total, times 4 per cent = **45 g m⁻² yr⁻¹** of carbon — some 400 times the overlying marl's, the monsoon's Nile in numbers, and the reason S1 is visible across the basin on a seismic section: an entire climate regime, recorded as a black ribbon a hand can hold.
:::

::: warning The seafloor records with an eraser, correlation is not dating, preservation filters everything, and "anoxic" does not mean "lifeless"
Five errors. **"Every site has a continuous record"**: the seafloor is as much a place of erosion and non-deposition as accumulation — contourites scour, turbidites cut channels, the CCD deletes carbonate, and a "hiatus" can be millions of years thick in outcrop and invisible in a core; the continuous-record sites are a chosen minority, and their continuity is a finding, not an assumption. **"The δ¹³C excursion at this site dates the event"**: it dates the excursion **at this site**, and excursion onsets are diachronous by centuries to millennia through a basin's circulation and biology — event stratigraphy needs multi-site, multi-proxy ties (tephra, magnetic, biostratigraphic) before "simultaneous" is allowed. **"Preservation differences are noise"**: they are signal-bearers — the CCD's own depth through time is a carbon-cycle record (its glacial shoaling records the ocean's CO₂ drawdown), and reading a dissolution-influenced isotope curve as pure source signal is the field's most common over-interpretation; the preservation index belongs beside every curve precisely because the "noise" is the other half of the paper. **"Cretaceous OAEs prove rapid anoxia from any warming"**: OAEs required anoxic endmember climates, province-scale volcanism and long residence times; they are the envelope of the possible, not a template for the century — the honest use is mechanism (productivity, stratification, nutrient feedbacks), not rate. **And "the subseafloor is rock"**: it is a biosphere with a carbon and energy flux comparable in places to the seafloor's input, its metabolisms moving carbon, sulphur and (critically for the reader) **altering isotopic signatures after deposition** — early diagenetic iron–manganese remobilisation, sulphate-driven processes that reset sulfur isotopes — so the archive's most recent revision is the recognition that part of what we read as ancient ocean is ancient *sediment biology*, best read with the pore-water chemistry in hand.
:::

::: history
The archive predates its science: the **Challenger** expedition (1872–76) named the ooze provinces (Globigerina, Radiolaria, red clay) from thousands of soundings and dredges and founded marine sedimentology without ever seeing a column. The column came with **petroleum coring** in the 1940s–50s and, decisively, with the **Deep Sea Drilling Project** (1968–83, the *Glomar Challenger*), whose first legs tested plate tectonics and whose K/T and South Atlantic recoveries rewrote stratigraphy; the **Ocean Drilling Program** (1985–2003, *JOIDES Resolution*) and **Integrated Ocean Drilling Program** (2003–13, with the riser ship *Chikyu* reaching into the deep biosphere and the deep past) industrialised the grid, and their sedimentological syntheses — the CCD curves, the dust fluxes, the OAE transects — built the Cenozoic as a system. The isotope reading grew from **Cesare Emiliani**'s foraminiferal palaeotemperatures (1950s), **Shackleton and Opdyke**'s 1973 demonstration that the deep-sea δ¹⁸O waves were the ice sheets' global signal tuned to Milankovitch's astronomy (the paper that made the ice-age theory a dated science), **Shackleton, Imbrie and colleagues**' stage nomenclature and the SPECMAP tuning of the 1970s–80s, and **Lisiecki and Raymo**'s 2005 LR04 stack — the community chronology still in daily use. The Mediterranean school — from the **Messinian evaporite** discovery during DSDP Leg 13 (1968, when the drill hit halite beneath the seafloor and the basin's desiccation became fact) through the **sapropel** tradition of Ryan and the Cretaceous OAE syntheses — supplied the discipline's event thinking, and its Black Sea flood hypothesis, revised downward by better cores, remains the best-taught lesson in the field about the seductiveness of catastrophes.
:::

::: summary
Marine sediments are the climate archive with the best time base and the worst memory: four sediment families (carbonate, opal, terrigenous, hydrogenous) laid down at rates spanning six orders of magnitude, filtered by dissolution (the CCD) and smudged by burrowers, recovered since 1968 by drilling programs that turned the seafloor into a dated grid. Foraminifera carry the load in both registers — assemblages for ecology, chemistry (δ¹⁸O, Mg/Ca, δ¹³C, Cd/Ca) for ice volume, temperature and circulation — and the benthic stack is the Quaternary's master clock. The events — Cretaceous OAEs, the PETM's marine chapter, Mediterranean sapropels and the Messinian's salt — are the archive's proof that the system changes regime, while the Black Sea's demoted flood and the subseafloor biosphere's after-thought edits are its proof that the archive must be audited as carefully as it is read.
:::
