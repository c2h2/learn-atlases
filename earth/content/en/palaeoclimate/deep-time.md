The Holocene, with its dependable monsoons and its 280-ppm ceiling, is one mood of the Earth system — and the least typical one on record. Over the 540 million years of animal shells, the planet has spent most of its time as a **greenhouse**: no permanent polar ice, crocodilians breeding above the Arctic Circle, sea level a hundred metres higher than today, atmospheric CO₂ in the thousands. It has shivered as an **icehouse** several times, and at least twice — in the Neoproterozoic, and perhaps before — it appears to have frozen **all the way to the equator**, a white planet with a blue ocean only under the ice, whose volcanoes spent millions of years pumping CO₂ until the greenhouse broke the ice in geologically abrupt fashion. Between those moods the system has thrown faster events: the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum, when 5,000–10,000 gigatonnes of carbon entered the cycle in fewer than twenty millennia, extinctions followed the deep sea, and palm trees colonised the Antarctic coast — the closest geological analogue to the century now running, and the reason deep-time palaeoclimate is no longer antiquarian. This lesson is the catalogue of moods: what the worlds looked like, what forced them, and what the transitions say about the one we are editing.

## The greenhouse worlds

::: definition Mesozoic–Palaeogene hothouses and their controversies {#def-greenhouse-worlds}
The Cretaceous (145–66 Ma) and Palaeogene (66–34 Ma) are the canonical greenhouse states: continental configurations far from today's (the South Atlantic a seaway, India mid-transit, Antarctica–Australia still wedged), no polar ice caps (with the possible exception of brief latest-Cretaceous glaciation, still argued from isotopes), sea level 100–250 m above present with half of the continents shallowly flooded (the Western Interior Seaway, the epicontinental seas of Europe), and CO₂ estimates — from boron, alkenone δ¹³C, stomata, carbonates, and the borosilicate and paleosol systems — clustering at 500–2,000 ppm, with the Cretaceous's high end (4,000 ppm, oceanic plateau volcanism) among the most contested numbers in the field. The controversies are where the physics is: **high-latitude warmth** (annual-mean deep-time temperatures of 10–15 °C at 75–85° palaeolatitude, from fossil alligators, taxodiaceae forests, and clumped isotopes) requires either extreme polar amplification or a different heat-transport regime, and the **tropical sea-surface temperature debate** — clumped and Mg/Ca proxies once read 35–40 °C (near the physiological ceiling), while the boron, TEX86-reassessment and foraminiferal assemblage evidence of the 2020s reads a more modest 29–32 °C — resolved substantially **against** the hothouse tropics, leaving a lower equator-to-pole gradient than any model reproduces: the "problem of Cretaceous–Palaeogene climates" is now honestly a **lapse-rate and cloud problem** (moist, low-cloud-dominated tropics with weak seasonality) rather than a CO₂ problem, and it remains the sharpest test of whether our models can do boundary conditions far from the Holocene. The **early Eocene Climate Optimum** (~52–50 Ma, the PETM's bigger sibling: foraminiferal δ¹⁸O implying deep waters at 10–12 °C, no frost in the Arctic, possibly no permanent Antarctic ice) is the warmest Cenozoic state and the sensitivity end-member; the **Azolla event** (~49 Ma, the massive freshwater-fern bloom that sequestered carbon in the newly isolated Arctic Ocean, proposed as one trigger of the long cooling to come) and the slow build of the Circum-Antarctic Current complete the arc toward the icehouse threshold of 34 Ma — the Eocene–Oligocene transition, when CO₂ fell through the **~750 ppm** door (measured in boron by Anagnostou and colleagues) and Antarctica grew its ice sheet within a few hundred thousand years, permanently.
:::

::: example The lapse-rate lever {#ex-lapse}
A Cretaceous model with doubled CO₂ reproduces the poles only if the equator–pole surface gradient is halved. Take the tropics at 30 °C and the pole at 12 °C (modern: 30 and −18). If tropical warming is +3 °C and polar amplification is 2× global mean, find the gradient before and after, and explain why **moist-adiabatic** heat transport and low clouds, not extra CO₂ alone, are invoked for the Cretaceous gradient.
::: solution
Modern gradient $30-(-18) = 48$ °C; Cretaceous $30-12 = 18$ °C — a third of modern, achieved by a warm pole, not a cool equator. CO₂ doubling warms the polar regions more in **models**, but never by the 30 °C needed; the required state is a troposphere running near the **moist adiabat** (lapse rate ~5 K km⁻¹ rather than the modern high-latitude 8–9), which couples surface warmth aloft and forces weak static stability, plus optically thick low cloud that traps winter heat — mechanisms whose parameterisations are exactly what deep-time model intercomparison tests, and exactly where the proxies (clumped isotope palaeoaltimetry of the Laramide, TEX86 seasonality) now push hardest.
:::
:::

## Icehouse worlds and their triggers

::: definition Four icehouse regimes and the carbon connection {#def-icehouse-worlds}
The Earth has been glaciated at least five times, and the pattern of triggers is instructive. The **Huronian** (2.4–2.1 Ga, possibly multiple glaciations to low latitudes) follows the Great Oxidation Event: methane — the Archean's powerful, short-lived greenhouse — was oxidised away by the new oxygen, and a Sun 20 per cent fainter did the rest; the first snowballs, if snowballs they were, are a warning that **greenhouse budgets can collapse when a trace gas is removed**, the most direct deep-time analogue to deliberate modern emissions in reverse. The **Hirnantian** (end-Ordovician, 445 Ma) glaciation is the shortest and strangest: a full ice age with sea-level fall and mass extinction at the peak of an otherwise greenhouse Phanerozoic, tied by different teams to the explosive arc weathering of the Taconic orogeny, the appearance of deep-rooted and burrowing **land plants** accelerating continental weathering (the 2020s bio-weathering case, with its own dissenting literature), and the palaeogeography of Gondwana over the South Pole. The **Late Paleozoic Ice Age** (360–260 Ma) is the land-plant ice age in its full expression: the coal swamp and root-permeated continents of the Carboniferous drew down carbon (the coal itself is the stored drawdown), CO₂ fell to glacial values, Gondwanan ice grew, and the same swamps' burial of organic carbon left the **oxygen excursion** (~30–35 per cent O₂ — the era of the giant dragonfly *Meganeura* and 70-cm millipedes breathing 35-per-cent air) that the insect record reads directly. The **Cenozoic icehouse** (34 Ma–present) is the one we inhabit: Antarctica first (through the 750-ppm door), the Northern Hemisphere later (the Greenland bridge closing, the Isthmus rising, 2.7 Ma of intensifying 41-kyr then 100-kyr cycles — the orbital-cycles lesson's subject), and each ice age a reminder that the current state is **bimodal**: glacial and interglacial are not anomalies but the two stable modes of the icehouse, with the Holocene the warm mode and the Anthropocene, arguably, a third. The through-line from Huronian to Holocene is the same variable: **CO₂ sets the mood** — 750 ppm for Antarctic glaciation, 180–280 ppm for the glacial–interglacial swing, 425 ppm and rising for the present perturbation — and the deep-time record is the only experiment that has measured the climate's response across the full range.
:::

::: example The faint young Sun and its fix {#ex-fys}
The Archean Sun emitted 70 per cent of today's flux. With today's albedo (0.30) and greenhouse, Earth's equilibrium temperature would be ~−15 °C instead of 15 °C — a 30 °C deficit. Estimate the extra greenhouse forcing needed (use $\lambda = 0.8$ K per W m⁻²... equivalently $F = \Delta T/\lambda$), and express it as a CO₂-equivalent multiplier over the modern 390 W m⁻² of natural greenhouse.
::: solution
$F = 30/0.8 = 37.5$ W m⁻² of extra forcing — roughly **a tenth of the incoming solar beam**, deliverable by a few per cent CO₂ (which saturating bands make cheap at high concentration) plus a methane-hydrocarbon haze whose 2010s photochemistry models find sufficient if organic hazes are right; the paradox is considered resolved not by one gas but by the **carbonate–silicate thermostat's patience** (low solar flux → slow weathering → high CO₂) and methane's contribution — the deep-time proof that the surface energy balance is a solvable accounting problem even for planets without ice cores.
:::
:::

## Snowball Earth and the carbon-cycle brake

::: definition Ice-albedo runaway, volcanic rescue, cap carbonates {#def-snowball}
The **snowball Earth** hypothesis — that the Sturtian (717–660 Ma) and Marinoan (650–635 Ma) glaciations reached the equator, with open water or thin ice at low latitudes — rests on three observations and one mechanism. The observations: glacial deposits (dropstones, tillites) at **palaeo-equatorial** latitudes from palaeomagnetically constrained sections (Namibia, Svalbard, Death Valley, China — the work of **Joseph Kirschvink**, who named the hypothesis in 1992, and **Paul Hoffman, Dan Schrag** and colleagues' 1998–2000 Namibia transects that made it a programme); the **cap carbonates** — metre-scale dolostones with distinctive sedimentary structures and negative δ¹³C that drape the glacial deposits on every continent, interpreted as extreme-greenhouse, alkalinity-flooded post-glacial seas; and the **banded iron formations** of the Sturtian and Marinoan (the Rapitan and equivalents), whose return to the sedimentary record after 1.8 billion years' absence implies anoxic, iron-rich ocean water under the ice. The mechanism is the **ice-albedo feedback as a one-way door**: once ice reaches the equator's sun, planetary albedo (~0.6) exceeds the threshold at which even equatorial summer cannot melt it, and the only escape is geological — volcanism continuing (mid-rift under the ice, and the weathering brake that nearly stops CO₂ drawdown) for **millions of years** until CO₂ reaches the tens of per cent (the classic models need 0.1–0.4 bar) and the greenhouse detonates deglaciation in perhaps a few thousand years, recorded as the cap carbonate's chemical shock. The biological epilogue is the field's most seductive argument: the Ediacaran biota's radiation, and possibly the Neoproterozoic oxygen rise, follow the Marinoan melt — glaciation-as-speciation-filter remains contested, but the **Gaskiers** (580 Ma) and other Snowball-Earth-era "in-between" glaciations keep the experiment's replication statistics interesting. Snowball Earth is, in the end, the discipline's proof that the carbon-cycle thermostat is **bistable** — that the same planet can hold two radically different energy balances — and the reason "tipping point" is a geological term long before it was a policy one.
:::

::: example Breaking the ice with volcanoes {#ex-snowball-co2}
A snowball Earth at 635 Ma receives 6 per cent less sunlight than today, has albedo 0.62 (vs 0.30 today), and must warm ~45 °C to clear the equator. With $\lambda = 0.8$, find the required forcing, and the CO₂ level if it is all CO₂ (use $\Delta F = 5.35\ln(C/C_0)$ with $C_0 = 280$ ppm).
::: solution
The albedo deficit alone is $0.5\times1360\times0.94\times(0.62-0.30) \approx 137$ W m⁻²; the required warming adds $45/0.8 = 56$ W m⁻², so total $\approx 190$ W m⁻², and $C = 280\,e^{190/5.35}$ — astronomically absurd, which is the point: **no logarithmic gas works at these doses**; the real escape used the full radiative-transfer nonlinearity (CO₂ bands, water-vapour feedback at 350+ K, methane) and the classic model values of 0.1–0.4 bar CO₂ — 150–400 times the preindustrial — deliver it. The exercise teaches why snowball papers do full radiative transfer and why the cap carbonates' uranium and strontium isotope anomalies (weathering rates 10–100× modern in the melt) are the geochemical signature of exactly that hothouse.
:::
:::

## The PETM: deep time's fast lane

::: definition The Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum {#def-petm}
The **PETM** (55.9–56 Ma, the type hyperthermal) is the deep-time record's best-resolved rapid carbon-cycle event: a negative carbon-isotope excursion of 3–6 ‰ in both marine and terrestrial sections (global, complete in perhaps 4,000–20,000 years), a 5–8 °C warming (tropical SSTs +4–5 °C, high latitudes more), a **benthic foraminiferal mass extinction** (30–50 per cent of deep-sea species — the only known Phanerozoic extinction of soft-bottom deep-sea life, from warming and carbonate-corrosive deep water), a terrestrial revolution (first appearances of modern mammal and plant orders on two continents, an aridification pulse), and a carbonate-flooding and clay-accumulation spike in the oceans — all followed by isotope-based **recovery over ~100–200 kyr**, the carbon-cycle residence-time clock made visible. The carbon: **5,000–10,000 GtC** released (the CIE's mass balance), at ~0.3–1 GtC yr⁻¹ sustained — a rate bracketing today's 10 GtC yr⁻¹ fossil flux by an order of magnitude in the wrong direction — from a source that was itself ¹³C-light: the debate between **methane-clathrate release** (the classic, now demoted by isotope-mass-balance and modelling: clathrate alone cannot supply the carbon without leaving other signatures) and **volcanism** — the North Atlantic Igneous Province's sill province intruding organic-rich and evaporitic sediments, contact metamorphism releasing thermogenic methane and CO₂ (the **Svensen, Planke** and colleagues' hypothesis, now the consensus's centre) — is the field's longest-running forensic case, and its resolution (volcanic trigger, possibly clathrate amplifier) is a masterclass in multi-isotope mass balance (δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O, Os, Hg anomalies as the volcanic tracer, Δ¹⁴C unavailable, of course, at 56 Ma). The PETM's modern salience is precise, not rhetorical: it is the only geological event with (i) a known carbon flux, (ii) a measured climate and extinction response, and (iii) an **acidification signature** (the deep-sea carbonate dissolution horizon, the benthic extinction's likely co-author), and its lessons — rate matters more than peak for the deep sea, the recovery takes 100,000 years, the biotic damage tracks the pace — are the ones the present is collecting.
:::

::: example The carbon budget of the PETM {#ex-petm-carbon}
The PETM carbon-isotope excursion is −4.5 ‰; the added carbon was about −60 ‰ (methane/thermogenic) against a sedimentary-exchange background of ~0 ‰; the exchangeable surface carbon reservoir (ocean + atmosphere + soft biosphere) was ~40,000 GtC. Estimate the carbon release, and compare its average rate (over 10 kyr) with the modern fossil flux of 10 GtC yr⁻¹.
::: solution
Mass balance: $f = 4.5/60 = 0.075$ of the reservoir, $0.075\times40{,}000 = 3000$ GtC minimum (the classic estimate is 5,000–10,000 once the sedimentary reservoir and compensation-depth feedbacks are included — the exercise's simple box underestimates, as it must). At 10 kyr the mean flux is $3000/10{,}000 = 0.3$ GtC yr⁻¹ — a **thirtieth** of the modern rate; even the full 10,000 GtC over 20 kyr is half the modern flux. The PETM's damage came from sustained geological rates; the present is at 30–100× the PETM's pace, which is precisely why the present's expected deep-sea response is not the PETM's but worse, and why the honest use of the PETM is as a **mechanism and boundary condition**, not a forecast.
:::
:::

::: warning Deep-time analogues are mechanisms, not forecasts; "CO₂ was higher, so no worries" is a category error; proxy extremes are contested; and the biosphere is not a passenger
Five errors. **"The PETM proves 6 °C of warming is survivable"**: it proves 6 °C happened **at 0.3 GtC yr⁻¹ over 100 kyr**, with migration and adaptation possible at that pace, and it also records a deep-sea extinction at that pace — the present, at 30–100× the flux, is a different experiment whose biotic answer is not written but whose physics (rate-of-change damage) is the PETM's clearest lesson. **"Cretaceous CO₂ was 2,000 ppm and life flourished"**: it did — for a biosphere with 70 Myr of adjustment, no ice sheets to lose, and no human agriculture; the relevant deep-time fact is not the level but the **rate**, and the Cretaceous's own hyperthermals (the OAEs, the mid-Cenomanian event) carry their own extinction statistics. **"The Eocene shows poles at 20 °C"**: the high-latitude numbers are real (mean annual 10–15 °C, frost-free) but the **tropical** hothouse (35–40 °C) has been substantially walked back by the 2020s proxy revisions — deep-time climate's most dramatic recent lesson is that **proxy calibration drifts**, and old syntheses must be re-read as often as new data are added. **"Snowball Earth is settled"**: low-latitude glaciation is well-documented, but the state's details — hard snowball versus slushball with equatorial open water, the Sturtian's 57-Myr duration (a number that still embarrasses every mechanism), the "snowball-albedo" versus "low-obliquity" escape routes — remain live, and the hypothesis's sociology (a paradigm that grew from heresy to textbook in a decade) is taught as a case study in how good evidence moves. **And "the climate varied naturally, so attribution is impossible"**: the deep-time record is precisely the evidence that the **forcings** were never natural-plus-nothing (large igneous provinces, continent configurations, oxygen and methane regime changes, orbit) — the Earth system has no baseline of quiet; the attribution question is not whether climate changes but **which forcing is doing it now**, and the isotope fingerprint of fossil carbon answers that in the present as surely as the PETM's δ¹³C answered it for the Eocene.
:::

::: history
Deep-time climate is as old as stratigraphy — **Lyell**'s gradualism, the 19th-century coal-swamp reconstructions, and **Svante Arrhenius**'s 1896 note that Cretaceous CO₂ explained Cretaceous warmth set the questions early. The quantitative field begins with isotope geochemistry: **Harold Urey, Samuel Epstein and Lowenstam**'s oxygen-isotope palaeothermometry of the 1950s gave the first numbers, and the deep-sea drilling programs gave the sections. The snowball chapter is a textbook sociology of science: **Brian Harland**'s documentation of low-latitude tillites (1960s–80s) waited decades for a mechanism; **Joseph Kirschvink** supplied the name and the palaeomagnetic argument (1992); and **Paul Hoffman, Alan Kaufman, Gal Halverson and Dan Schrag**'s Namibian Marinoan and cap-carbonate transects (1998–2004), with **Raymond Pierrehumbert**'s radiative-convective models of the 30-per-cent-CO₂ escape, converted heresy into standard model. The PETM exploded with the Ocean Drilling Program's 1990s legs and the isotope syntheses of **James Zachos, Richard Norris, Andy Knoll, Lee Kump** and **Philip Gingerich**'s mammal-side corollaries (1990s–2000s); the volcanic trigger came from **Sverre Planke** and colleagues' sill-province and contact-metamorphism studies (2004–2018); and the tropical-temperature revision is the work of **Peter Sexton, Dana Bice, Mark Huber**, **James Zachos** and **John Eiler**'s clumped-isotope school in the 2020s — a field rewriting its own textbook in real time, one calibration at a time.
:::

::: summary
The Earth system has multiple stable moods: greenhouse worlds with frost-free poles and contested tropical temperatures (Cretaceous–Eocene, the lapse-rate problem), icehouse regimes triggered by weathering and geography and gated near 750 ppm (Huronian's methane collapse, Hirnantian's, the Carboniferous forests', the Cenozoic's), and — the proof of bistability — snowball states from which only million-year CO₂ accumulation could return the planet, leaving cap carbonates and banded iron as receipts. The PETM is the fast lane: 5,000–10,000 GtC at geological rates, a deep-sea extinction, and 100-kyr recovery — the best mechanism-study the present could ask for and, at 30–100× its pace, a boundary condition rather than a forecast. Deep time's unified field theory is carbon: CO₂ sets the mood, rate sets the damage, and the isotope fingerprints that read ancient carbon are the same ones now reading ours.
:::
