Every palaeoclimate claim hides a date, and every date hides a fight. "The CO₂ rose before the temperature" — by how long, and how do you *know* which came first when both signals carry thousand-year age uncertainties? "The event lasted two centuries" — is that a climate catastrophe or a badly tuned core? Palaeoclimate's second discipline — after proxy physics — is **geochronology**, and its history is a series of inventions for stealing time from matter: radioactive clocks whose ticks are nuclear constants, annual layers that can be counted like pages, chemical fingerprints that tie archives together across continents, and orbital cycles that tune sediment like a radio to a carrier wave. The rules are unforgiving — every clock has a range, a closed-system assumption, and a calibration of its own — and the discipline's honesty is measured by how loudly it prints its error bars, because the questions palaeoclimate asks (lead–lag, causation, rate of change) are all questions about **differences between dates**, where uncertainties add rather than cancel.

## Radiometric clocks

::: definition Decay, radiocarbon, and the other clocks {#def-radiometrics}
Radioactive dating rests on one exponential: $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$, half-life $t_{1/2} = \ln2/\lambda$, and the closed-system requirement that the sample neither gained nor lost parent or daughter since it formed. The clocks that matter to climate Quaternary science are four. **Radiocarbon (¹⁴C)**: cosmic rays make ¹⁴C in the stratosphere, it oxidises to CO₂ and enters the carbon cycle, and every living thing carries the atmospheric ratio until death, when the ¹⁴C begins to decay (half-life 5730 yr; ages are reported "conventionally" against the older 5568-yr Libby value and corrected for fractionation — a convention the reader must always check). The range is ~50,000 years (past five or six half-lives the signal drowns in machine background — though accelerator mass spectrometry counts atoms, not decays, and pushes the boundary), the materials are anything that exchanged carbon (wood, charcoal, shells, peat, coral, foraminifera, ice's occluded CO₂), and the pitfalls are a curriculum: contamination by a percent of modern carbon makes a 40-kyr sample 6 kyr too young; the "old carbon" effect makes hard-water shells and volcanic-region plants appear millennia ancient; the **reservoir age** makes ocean and lake carbon read centuries-to-millennia older than the atmosphere that fed it; and the atmospheric ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio has **never been constant** (production varies with the geomagnetic field and solar wind), which is why radiocarbon years are not calendar years and why the calibration curve exists. **Uranium–thorium**: ²³⁴U decays to ²³⁰Th (half-life 245 kyr) which is insoluble and absent from forming carbonate, so the daughter's ingrowth dates the shell or cave directly — ±decades-to-centuries precision out to ~500 kyr, the chronometer of speleothems, corals, and the U-Th/¹⁴C intercalibration that anchors the last glacial cycle. **Argon–argon**: ⁴⁰K to ⁴⁰Ar in sanidine and biotite, the tephra timer — a single volcanic glass shard, ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar-dated to ±0.1 per cent, can fix a million-year-old ash bed that ties marine cores, lake sediments and ice across a continent. **U–Pb on zircon**, the billion-year backbone that dates the deep-time sections the other lessons read.
:::

::: example Radiocarbon arithmetic {#ex-frc}
Charcoal gives a conventional ¹⁴C age of 11,460 ¹⁴C yr BP. Using the physical half-life 5730 yr, find the fraction of ¹⁴C remaining, and explain why the calibrated calendar age is **younger** than 11,460 calendar years for most of the last glacial period.
::: solution
11,460 yr is two physical half-lives, so $(1/2)^{2} = 0.25$ — a quarter of the original ¹⁴C. The calendar-age correction runs the other way because glacial atmospheres held **more** ¹⁴C (stronger geomagnetic shielding weaker, production higher): a glacial sample started with more clock-fuel, so its true calendar age is younger than its radiocarbon age — the IntCal curve's job is exactly this mapping, and the exercise's real lesson is that ¹⁴C years and calendar years differ by up to ~6,000 years at 30 kyr BP, enough to misdate an entire ice-age event by a phase.
:::
:::

## Calibration and the stratigraphic clocks

::: definition IntCal, layers, fingerprints, reversals, orbits {#def-stratigraphic-clocks}
**Calibration**: radiocarbon is converted to calendar years against **IntCal**, the community curve built from independently dated archives — dendrochronology (counted tree rings to 13.9 kyr in the German pine and bristlecone sequences, extended by floating chronologies), varved lakes and marine sediments, U-Th-dated corals and speleothems, and the ¹⁴C itself measured in each — with the marine curve (Marine20) carrying regionally varying reservoir corrections and the Southern Ocean's millennium-scale offsets being a standing caution to every glacial chronology. The curve's wiggles are useful in their own right: **wiggle-matching** a series of dated ¹⁴C ages to IntCal's ups and downs can date an archaeological site or a tephra to a human generation, and the **bomb pulse** — thermonuclear testing doubled atmospheric ¹⁴C by 1963, and the post-1963 decline is a date-code precise to a year — dates wine, ivory, forensic remains and the growth layers of corals and teeth with an accuracy no natural clock matches. **Layer counting**: varves (annual couplets of summer and winter lake sediment, counted continuously at varve-type lakes to 100+ kyr, and the backbone of the Swedish De Geer and Finnish winter-sediment traditions), annual ice laminae (next section), and the coral's own bands. **Tephrochronology**: volcanic glass is a chemical barcode — major, trace and isotope fingerprints match shards to a named eruption, and famous horizons (the 186 CE **Taupo** ash dated by tree rings and ice sulphate; the Minoan **Aniakchak/Santorini** debates; the Vedde Ash and the 13-kyr Laacher See tephra that ties Eifel volcanism to a Greenland climate event) become isochrones that synchronise archives to within a human lifetime. **Magnetostratigraphy**: sediments lock the field's polarity as they settle; the Matuyama–Brunhes reversal at 0.78 Ma and the shorter excursions (the Laschamp at ~41 ka, dated in ice and lava alike) give coarse but unambiguous polarity zones through millions of years of section. **Orbital tuning (astrochronology)**: precession and obliquity are clockwork — their periods (23.7, 22.1, 19.1, 405 kyr) are known from astronomical solution — so matching a sediment or ice cycle pattern to the orbital template transfers astronomical precision to archives beyond radiometric range, the method behind the LR04 marine stack's 5.3-Myr chronology and the 2013 astronomical recalibration of the Paleocene–Eocene boundary; its circularity (assuming the climate responded to the orbits you tuned to) is the discipline's most disciplined assumption, defended by the 405-kyr eccentricity metronome that resolves the ambiguity.
:::

::: example Reservoir and the oldest carbon {#ex-reservoir}
A marine shell dates at 4,300 ¹⁴C yr BP with a regional reservoir age of 400 yr; a terrestrial charcoal from the same beach ridge dates 3,900 ¹⁴C yr BP. Show the pair is internally consistent, and find the reservoir age implied if the shell read 5,400 and the charcoal 3,900.
::: solution
Reservoir consistency: shell $= 3900 + 400 = 4300$ ✓ — the shell inherited 400 years of "old" carbon from upwelled ocean. The second case implies $5400 - 3900 = 1500$ yr of reservoir age, an upwelling-strength signature in itself (eastern boundary currents run 1,000–2,000 yr), and the reason paired marine–terrestrial dates are the standard way to *measure* past reservoir ages rather than assume them.
:::
:::

## Ice, and the race past fifty thousand years

::: definition Ice-core chronologies and the tuning of deep time {#def-ice-chronology}
Ice cores carry their own layers — couplets of dusty winter and clean summer snow visible to the millimetre in shallow core, resolvable in electrical-conductivity and dust records far deeper — and **layer counting** (the tally of visible and chemical annual cycles) delivers the gold-standard chronology of the last glacial: **GICC05**, the Greenland ice chronology built by counting 60,000 annual layers down through the last glacial, carries stated counting uncertainties of a few centuries at 60 kyr — the reference clock against which every North Atlantic event is timed, and the instrument that dated the abrupt events of the next lesson to human-scale precision. Below the countable (where flow thins annual layers below resolution and ice mixes at the base), chronologies become **flow-model**: ice thickness, accumulation and deformation integrated through depth, pinned by volcanic sulphate spikes matched to dated eruptions, by field reversals recorded in the ice's own dust fabric, and by gas-age–ice-age physics (the air trapped in closing pores is younger than the ice that holds it by the firn-close-off difference, up to 800 years in cold sites — a correction that matters enormously to the CO₂–temperature lead–lag debates). The deep Antarctic cores (EPICA Dome C to 800 kyr, and the recent "Beyond EPICA" extension) are dated by orbital tuning of their isotope and dust cycles plus gas-phase correlation, and the oldest recoverable ice (~1.5–2 Myr, sought in Little Dome C and the "blue-ice" search areas) will be dated almost entirely by astronomy and chemistry. Below ice and beyond 50 kyr ¹⁴C, the deep sea runs on the orbital metronome and the argon of its tephras; the continents run on U–Th, OSL (the luminescence clock of buried sand, ~200-kyr range) and the tephra network; and the great cross-checks — U-Th-dated corals against ¹⁴C, tree rings against varves, ice sulphate against argon-dated tephras — are the interlocking that makes the whole palaeo-timescale one coherent, if perpetually revised, calendar.
:::

::: example Counting Greenland {#ex-gicc05}
GICC05 counts ~60,000 layers to 60 kyr with a stated uncertainty of ~1,200 yr at the base (accumulation-thinning modelled, some sections counted with low confidence). If the Bølling warming onset is at 14,642 ± 4 b2k in the same count, find the maximum possible lag between the two records' event timings implied by chronology alone, and why the stated ±4 is not the real uncertainty for correlation.
::: solution
The onset's *stratigraphic* precision (±4 counted years) is superb, but correlating a *marine* or speleothem record to it imports that record's own age error — often centuries to millennia — so the honest lag uncertainty is the quadrature sum, dominated by the partner archive. The exercise is the whole lead–lag literature's first law: **event correlation across archives is limited by the worse clock and the correlation tie-point, not by the beautiful one** — which is why the field spends its money on shared isochrons (tephra, sulphate) rather than on more counting.
:::
:::

## The statistics of age–depth

::: definition Age–depth models and Bayesian chronologies {#def-agemodel}
Between the dated tie-points (¹⁴C ages, tephra, counted layers, tuned cycles) and the climate curve lies the **age–depth model**, and the discipline has learned the hard way that this interpolation step is where reputations die. The naive linear interpolation between adjacent dates is biased in three known ways: sedimentation rate is autocorrelated (real rates wander, they do not jump), dating errors are often non-Gaussian (old-carbon contamination skews one way), and sampling is sparse exactly where change is fast. The modern answer is **Bayesian**: programs in the OxCal and Bacon lineage place priors on accumulation-rate smoothness and memory, fold in each date's likelihood, and return not an age but a **posterior distribution** at every depth — with the crucial outputs being the 95 per cent credible interval on every event age and the model's own diagnostics (how much the dates fight the smoothness prior, which tie-point the data reject). The consequences for how climate papers may speak are now codified: lead–lag claims must compare **overlapping credible intervals**, not best-fit lines; event durations must exceed the chronology's resolution to be claimed (a "centennial event" in a core with 800-year credible intervals is an artefact waiting for reviewers); and model intercomparison — Bacon versus linear versus orbital tuning on the same core — is the cheap robustness test every serious chronology now reports. The deepest lesson is epistemological: the age model is a **statistical inverse problem**, and like all of them its answer contains its prior — which is why the field prints priors the way high-energy physics prints systematics, and why "the chronology is the paper" is a compliment.
:::

::: example Credible intervals decide the argument {#ex-credint}
A speleothem's δ¹⁸O shift is dated 16.0 ± 0.4 ka (95 per cent) and the marine isotope transition it is claimed to have "triggered" is dated 16.3 ± 0.6 ka. Find the age difference and its uncertainty (quadrature), and state what the two records can and cannot claim.
::: solution
Difference $= 0.3$ kyr with uncertainty $\sqrt{0.4^{2}+0.6^{2}} = 0.72$ kyr — the offset is **less than half its own error bar**, so the records are consistent with simultaneity and cannot support any causal ordering. The exercise is the lead–lag literature's referee rule in one square root: "A before B" requires the difference to exceed roughly twice the quadrature uncertainty, and most published ordering claims that survive do so only because shared isochrons (tephra, correlation tie-points) shrank the errors, not because the central dates were far apart.
:::

::: warning Every clock has a range, a closed system, and a calibration of its own — and dates are not ages
Five errors. **"That layer is 30,000 years old"**: it is a model-dependent estimate with a stated uncertainty, on a stated timescale (¹⁴C years? calendar years? b2k? ka BP with which half-life?), from a sample whose context (reworking? old carbon? bioturbation?) is the real argument — the number without the metadata is noise. **"U-Th and ¹⁴C disagree, so one is broken"**: they date different things (the carbonate's formation versus the carbon fixed from a reservoir), and the disagreement is usually the science — reservoir ages, dead-carbon fractions, open-system uranium migration in speleothems (the "U-Th trouble" that forced honest error inflation in glacial-cycle chronologies). **"Orbital tuning is circular"**: it is, in a sense — you assume climate paced by orbits — but the 405-kyr eccentricity metronome, whose period is stable while the target cycles are not, breaks the circularity the way a long-wavelength interferometer breaks an ambiguity; the discipline's verdict is that tuned timescales are the best available and their residual errors are quantified, not ignored. **"More decimal places = better"**: the worst age-model sin is printing precision the chronology cannot carry — a Bayesian age model's 95 per cent credible interval, not its mean, is the result, and lead–lag claims must use the intervals, not the central lines that every figure plots. **And "dating is settled"**: the field's biggest revisions are dating revisions — the 2012 recalibration of the geomagnetic polarity timescale, the 2013 astronomical recalibration of the Palaeocene–Eocene boundary (moving ages ~800 kyr and forcing a rethink of the PETM–impact coincidence), the Santorini eruption-date war (1600s versus 1500s BCE, still unresolved in the details) — and every "known" age in this atlas should be read as the current best fit of the current network, improved the way all of it has been improved: by better clocks finding each other's errors.
:::

::: history
**Willard Libby** invented radiocarbon (1946, building on **Franz Riehl**'s insight that cosmic rays should make ¹⁴C in the atmosphere), proved it on Egyptian tombs of known age, and took the 1960 Nobel Prize; his calibration-era successors — the bristlecone pine curves of **Hans Suess** (who found the tree-ring offset that bears his name and, decades later, the fossil-fuel dilution that also bears his name), the IntCal collaboration's community curve now in its fifth generation, and the Marine20 ocean curve — turned a radioactive clock into a calendar. **Gerrit De Geer** founded varve counting in the 1910s–1920s (and his family's chronology over-reached spectacularly when Swedish varves appeared to compress the Ice Age into too short a span — a cautionary over-extension the field still cites); tephrochronology grew from 1960s–70s work on Yellowstone and Cascades ashes into the isochrone network of modern correlation; and the **geomagnetic polarity timescale** — built on the Vine–Matthews seafloor-spreading reversal stripes of 1963 and refined through the Kent–Gradstein compilations to the 2012 recalibration — gave the sea floor its barcode. **Dansgaard** counted the first annual ice layers at Camp Century in the 1960s alongside his isotopes; the GRIP and GICC05 counting tradition (**Sune Olander Rasmussen** and colleagues, 2006) carried layer-counting to 60 kyr, and **Claude Raynaud**'s and **Jean-Robert Barnola**'s gas work at Byrd began the ice-age CO₂ story whose dating refinements still drive the lead–lag debates. The through-line: every generation's dates were the next generation's proxies.
:::

::: quiz
The "reservoir age" of a marine radiocarbon date is:
- [ ] the time the shell lived before dying.
- [x] the apparent age gained by carbon that left the atmosphere centuries before the organism fixed it.
- [ ] the decay correction for ocean salinity.
- [ ] the difference between Libby and physical half-lives.
::: solution
Ocean interior carbon has been out of atmospheric contact for centuries to millennia, so surface water (and the shells in it) starts "old"; reservoir ages average ~400 yr, exceed 1,000 in upwelling zones, and must be subtracted — or measured with paired terrestrial dates — before marine ¹⁴C becomes calendar time.
:::

::: summary
Geochronology is palaeoclimate's second physics: exponential clocks (¹⁴C to 50 kyr, U–Th to 500 kyr, Ar–Ar and U–Pb beyond), counted layers (rings, varves, ice couplets — GICC05's 60,000 Greenland years), chemical isochrones (tephra barcodes, sulphate spikes, the bomb pulse), and the astronomical metronome that tunes everything the radiometrics cannot reach. Every clock has a range, a closed-system assumption and a calibration of its own, and the discipline's hard-won etiquette — credible intervals over central values, paired dates over assumed reservoirs, shared isochrons over eyeballed correlations — exists because the questions are about *differences* of dates. The dates of today are the proxies of tomorrow: the field's greatest hits are dating revisions, and its standing invitation is to check the age model before believing the curve.
:::
