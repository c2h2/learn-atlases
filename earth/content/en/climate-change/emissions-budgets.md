On 2 November 2021, with the Glasgow climate pact's "phase-down of unabated coal power" still being negotiated two hundred metres away, the International Energy Agency released something its chief economist announced "will not be found in any of the scenarios in today's report": net-zero emissions by 2050, a road map of 400 milestones that the IEA — the 1974-founded guardian of oil-supply security, publisher of the world's most-watched energy projections — presented as the narrow path consistent with 1.5 °C. The document's headline number was the one that mattered to everyone outside the negotiating rooms: of the 2050 net-zero goal's required technology suite, **two-thirds was already commercially demonstrated** — the barrier was no longer physics or even cost but deployment and politics. That distinction is the subject of this lesson, which is about the *accounting* layer of climate science: the ledgers of what we emit (41.6 GtCO₂-equivalent a year across all gases, the equivalent of pouring 1,300 tonnes of CO₂ into the atmosphere every second), what the atmosphere has kept (about 45 % of everything we have ever emitted — the rest absorbed by land and ocean at a cost of acidification and anoxia), what the historical ledgers say about who emitted what (the cumulative-emissions table that makes climate politics a fight over arithmetic), and what remains (the *remaining carbon budget* — the single most consequential number in climate policy, and the most misunderstood). The reason this accounting layer exists at all is a physical fact discovered in the 2000s and now assessed by AR6 with high confidence: **peak warming is determined by cumulative CO₂ emissions**, not by the date on a calendar — which converts every temperature target into a budget, every budget into a deadline, and every deadline into a question about who pays.

## The ledger: what we emit and what stays

::: definition The greenhouse-gas inventory: gases, metrics, and the numbers {#def-ghg-inventory}
The human climate forcing is a portfolio of gases with radically different natures. **Carbon dioxide** (424 ppm in 2024, +52 % over 280 ppm preindustrial, growing ~2.5–3.0 ppm/yr — ~0.6 %/yr of the stock) is the only one that matters for peak warming *cumulatively*: its lifetime is not a number but a curve (the fast ocean and land uptake removes ~a quarter of a pulse in decades; the remaining ~20–40 % persists for millennia as the carbonate-chemistry limit), so it is the currency of budgets. **Methane** (1,930 ppb, +160 % over 730 ppb, AR6 ERF ~0.54 W/m² plus comparable indirect effects through ozone and the hydroxyl radical) is the fast-melting ice of the portfolio: its atmospheric lifetime is ~11 years against oxidation by OH, so its concentration responds to emission changes within a decade — the reason "methane is the low-hanging fruit" (the 2021 Global Methane Pledge's 30 %-by-2030 target, and the fact that 2020–21 saw the largest two-year growth in the 400,000-year ice-core/surface record, ~15–17 ppb/yr, partly from tropical wetlands feedback and partly from fossil and agricultural emissions, remains the field's most-watched feedback warning). **Nitrous oxide** (346 ppb, +24 %, lifetime 116 years, the strongest *per-molecule* of the long-lived gases and rising at ~1 ppb/yr, mostly from nitrogen fertiliser — the Haber–Bosch process that feeds half the world's population is the second-largest biogeochemical alteration of the planet after fossil carbon), the **fluorinated gases** (HFCs, PFCs, SF₆ — tiny in mass, enormous per-molecule, the Kigali Amendment's HFC phase-down being the single protocol estimated to avoid ~0.4 °C by 2100), and the **short-lived climate forcers** (black carbon, tropospheric ozone from NOₓ and CO, methane's own ozone effect — together roughly cancelling to a net positive forcing of ~0.5–1 W/m², with black carbon's Arctic snow-darkening the sharpest regional term). To add these into one ledger the field uses **global warming potentials** (GWP100: methane 27–30, nitrous oxide 273, AR6 values; GWP20: methane ~80) and their newer cousins (GWP*, the temperature-over-time metric that treats continuous methane emissions as a *temperature plateau* — the metric that finally makes "methane is not CO₂" arithmetic-legible). The 2019 all-gas total was **~59 GtCO₂-e/yr** (AR6 WGIII: 59 ± 6.6), of which CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry is ~35, land-use CO₂ ~4–5, methane ~16 (CO₂-e), nitrous oxide ~4, and the rest F-gases and indirect effects — and the by-sector decomposition (electricity and heat 30 %, industry 24 %, agriculture-forestry-other-land 22 %, transport 15 %, buildings 6 %) is the map every national strategy must eventually colour in.
:::

::: definition The global carbon budget: sources, sinks, and the airborne fraction {#def-carbon-budget}
The atmosphere is a ledger with two entries and one balance. **Sources**: fossil-fuel and cement emissions were **36.8 GtCO₂ in 2023** (the Global Carbon Budget's 2024 edition — the highest year in human history), land-use-change emissions ~4.2 GtCO₂ (deforestation, drainage, peat fires — the "gross" deforestation term ~10–12 Gt offset by regrowth and management), giving total anthropogenic sources ~41 GtCO₂/yr. **Sinks**: the ocean absorbs ~2.7 GtCO₂/yr (the solubility and biological pumps, the former weakened ~1–2 % per decade by stratification — the "sink efficiency" question of the SOCAT/GO-SHIP repeat-hydrography programme), the land ~3.4 GtCO₂/yr (the CO₂ fertilisation effect, nitrogen deposition, and the regrowth of abandoned farmland — offset by the same land-use emissions and increasingly threatened by drought and fire, whose variability the 2015, 2019, 2023 anomalous-growth years expose). **The balance**: the atmosphere gained ~5.2 GtC (~19 GtCO₂) in 2023, an **airborne fraction of ~45–50 %** of total emissions — a ratio that has been *constant for seven decades* (the "Keeling–Bacastow" result: the airborne fraction tracks emissions because the sinks scale with the concentration perturbation, first-order linear carbon-cycle behaviour), with the constancy itself the warning: if the land and ocean sinks were losing efficiency at the observed decadal variability rate, the airborne fraction would have risen measurably, and the 2020s' high-growth years (2015 and 2023's El Niño-driven +3.0 ppm spikes) are the land sink's variability, not its trend, according to the Global Carbon Budget's decomposition. The budget's measurement infrastructure is one of modern science's quiet triumphs: ~700 background CO₂ stations (the GAW/ICOS network), ~80 ocean-surface CO₂ ships and moorings (SOCAT's 14 million measurements), the GOSAT and OCO-2/OCO-3 satellites (the latter's 2014–2025 column maps now resolving city-scale plumes — the "verification and attribution" capability that makes the Paris transparency framework's inventory reports checkable rather than declarable), and the ¹³C/¹⁴C and O₂/N₂ isotope budgets that close the mass balance between fossil, land and ocean terms. The honest uncertainties are the land sink's *trend* (the CO₂-fertilisation response measured by FACE experiments and the free-air enrichment meta-analyses is smaller than many models assumed — the "fertilisation fade" that would raise the airborne fraction), the permafrost term (the ~1,400–1,600 Gt of soil carbon whose 2020s fluxes are measured but whose century-scale trajectory the budgets carry as an explicit "excluded feedback" caveat), and the post-2020 question of whether the ocean's carbon uptake has begun to saturate (the 2023 Nature Synfholt et al. and the Global Carbon Budget's 2024 "ocean sink stalled in the 2010s" finding, still contested by the model community).
:::

::: example The pandemic experiment: what a forced emissions pause taught {#example-pandemic}
In April 2020, global fossil CO₂ emissions fell ~17 % below the 2019 baseline as lockdowns emptied roads and factories — the largest abrupt emissions drop in history, larger than the 2008 financial crisis (−1.4 %) or the 2011 Fukushima shock. The atmospheric response was the experiment the carbon-cycle community had never been able to run: the growth rate in 2020 fell to ~2.4 ppm (against ~2.5–3.0 expected), a reduction of only ~half a ppm — because the *concentration* kept climbing and the sinks, responding to concentration not emissions, barely noticed. Three lessons in one year: (1) **concentration is cumulative, emissions are the tap** — a 17 % one-year cut moved the atmospheric growth rate by ~5 % and the concentration by hundredths of a ppm, the physical basis for the cumulative-emissions framing; (2) **the land sink's variability dominates interannual noise** — 2020's record land carbon uptake (the Amazon's wet year, the Eurasian boreal fire year's offsetting sign) swamped the human signal in the land term, the reason the Global Carbon Budget's annual release is a forensic exercise in separating ENSO, volcanoes and policy; and (3) **the rebound was as fast as the fall** — 2021 emissions recovered to ~99 % of 2019 (the power-sector rebound in China and India, the aviation and shipping collapse persisting), the "build back better" stimulus delivering ~2 % of the recovery spending to clean energy against the IEA's 35 % "Sustainable Recovery" blueprint. The exercise's question: if a 17 % one-year drop is invisible in the concentration record, what emissions trajectory *is* visible, and on what timescale?
::: solution
Only sustained multi-year declines: the airborne fraction's constancy means the atmosphere reports *cumulative* emissions, so a 7 %/yr decline (the 2015–2030 rate the 1.5 °C pathways require) becomes visible as a *falling growth rate* within ~5–10 years (the growth-rate trend is detectable against ENSO noise with ~10 years of data — the "carbon-cycle detectability" studies), and as a concentration plateau only at net zero. This is why the verification science moved to satellite inverse modelling of *emitters* rather than global-mean concentration: the signal policy must produce is at the source, not the stock.
:::
:::

::: example The equity ledger: who used the budget {#example-equity}
Cumulative CO₂ emissions since 1850 (fossil + industry, the Global Carbon Budget/Carbon Brief accounting) total ~1,780 GtCO₂, distributed roughly: United States 499 Gt, EU-27 472 Gt (as a bloc; the UK alone 75), China 284 Gt, Russia 171, India 95, Japan 42, Africa ~50. Per-capita cumulative emissions tell the moral story the totals hide: the average American is responsible for ~1,400 tCO₂ of the historical stock, the average European ~600, the average Chinese citizen ~190, the average Indian ~65 — a 20-fold spread between the top and bottom quartiles of humanity. Now overlay the remaining budget: AR6's 1.5 °C carbon budget from 2020 is **510 GtCO₂ for a 50 % chance** (2°C: 1,310 GtCO₂), and at 2023 emission rates the 1.5 °C budget is spent in ~13 years. If the remaining budget were shared by population (the "contraction and convergence" and per-capita-convergence frameworks the developing world insists on), the United States' share of the remaining 1.5 °C budget would be ~4 % of it for 4 % of humanity — implying domestic net zero well before 2040 — while India's population share would permit emissions to *grow* several-fold before peaking. The 2015 Paris Agreement's solution to this arithmetic was deliberate ambiguity: "common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities" in the light of national circumstances — equity asserted as a principle, its arithmetic deferred to each country's self-judged "nationally determined contribution." The 2013–15 carbon-budget literature (the Allen et al. and Matthews et al. TCRE papers, the Myhre et al. linearity work) is what made the deferral quantifiable: once warming is a budget, every tonne anywhere draws down a global commons account, and the question "who has used how much?" becomes a balance-sheet question with names on it.
::: solution
The arithmetic is the argument: the historical ledger (the US and EU's ~55 % of cumulative emissions with ~12 % of population) and the remaining-budget ledger (the same countries' per-capita drawdown obligations) are the two numbers behind every COP fight over "loss and damage," "finance," and "phasing down" language — and both are measurements, not opinions, which is why the science of this lesson is so political: it does not choose the equity principle, it prices every principle against the same finite budget.
:::

## Budgets, gaps, and the accounting of promise

::: definition The remaining carbon budget and the TCRE: the most consequential number in policy {#def-rcb}
The linear carbon–climate response — that peak warming rises ~proportionally with *cumulative* CO₂ emissions (the transient climate response to cumulative emissions, **TCRE**, AR6: 0.45 [0.27–0.63] °C per 1,000 GtCO₂) — is the discovery that reorganised climate policy, and its mechanism is elegant: as CO₂ accumulates, the ocean's uptake per tonne falls roughly in proportion to the diminishing solubility-buffer (the Revelle factor), so the airborne fraction stays near-constant and the warming per airborne tonne stays near-constant, the two effects cancelling in the right direction. The policy instrument is the **remaining carbon budget** (RCB): the cumulative future CO₂ emissions consistent with limiting peak warming to a chosen level, computed as (target − historical anthropogenic warming − non-CO₂ warming contribution)/TCRE, and reported by AR6 for 1.5 °C as **510 GtCO₂ from 2020 (50 % chance)** or 2,500 GtCO₂ for 83 % confidence-equivalent certainty — the *width* of that range (a factor of five between coin-flip and near-certainty) being the number the policy literature usually drops. The uncertainties are not statistical padding: the TCRE's own range (carbon-cycle and climate-sensitivity uncertainty), the non-CO₂ forcing term (whether methane stays flat, and whether aerosol declines as air quality improves add ±0.1–0.2 °C, i.e. ±200–400 GtCO₂ to the budget), the "permafrost and wetland" feedback terms deliberately excluded from the AR6 budget (their omission is a stated conservatism that cuts the other way), and the definition itself (peak warming vs the 20-year mean, exceedance-and-return vs never-exceed). The 2018 IPCC Special Report on 1.5 °C made the budget a public object — 420 GtCO₂ remaining from 2018 for a 50 % chance of 1.5 °C, i.e. ~10 years of then-current emissions — and the 2020s updates (the Forster et al. 2023 and 2024 carbon-budget updates, the GCB's "budget spent" counters) have kept the arithmetic current: from early 2025, the 1.5 °C/50 % budget stands near **~200–250 GtCO₂**, roughly five years of current emissions, with the honest scientific caveat that a 1.5 °C *peak* followed by slow decline (the Paris goal's actual wording, "holding warming to well below 2 and pursuing 1.5") is a different, larger budget than never exceeding 1.5. The budget's critics in the economics literature (the "overshoot and dependence on negative emissions" pathways — the IPCC AR6 WGIII's finding that nearly all 1.5 °C pathways use some carbon dioxide removal, mostly land-based and forest-based, at scales the land-sustainability literature questions) and its defenders (the "budget-first" school: the budget is the only robust bridge from emissions to temperature, and every alternative framing — temperature targets, concentration targets, rate targets — is less directly controllable by policy) are arguing about the same physics from different risk preferences, which is exactly the line this lesson keeps drawing between science and choice.
:::

::: definition Emissions pathways, NDCs and the gap: the accounting of promise {#def-pathways}
Between the budget and the physics sits the layer of human pledges, and its science is accounting. The **SSP–RCP pathway family** (the CMIP6 scenario design: SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, SSP5-8.5 — the "shared socioeconomic pathways" describing *worlds* — sustainability, middle-of-the-road, fragmented, fossil-fuelled — crossed with the radiative-forcing targets) is the projection literature's input, covered in the next lesson; the **policy-gap literature** (the UNEP Emissions Gap Report, the IEA's World Energy Outlook, the COP's annual Synthesis Report on the NDCs) answers a different question: *what do the pledges actually deliver?* The 2024 accounting: global emissions in 2023 were ~57 GtCO₂-e (all gases), the 1.5 °C/50 % pathway requires ~**42 % below 2019 levels by 2030** and net CO₂ zero around 2050, current policies put 2030 emissions ~25 *above* 2019, and the unconditional NDCs (the pledges on the table) imply **~2.6–3.1 °C** of warming by 2100 — the "emissions gap" of ~15–20 GtCO₂-e/yr in 2030 between pledge and pathway, larger than Russia's or the US footprint. The gap's anatomy is instructive: the pledges' coverage (which sectors and gases are included — aviation and shipping excluded from many frameworks, methane from manure and rice often soft), their conditionality (finance-contingent NDCs from developing-country pledges), their vintage (many NDCs were built on 2010s technology expectations that solar's 90 % cost fall since 2010 has made conservative — the IEA's repeated finding that announced pledges *if implemented* would roughly halve 2030 emissions versus current policy), and the implementation record (the Pindyck–Hepburn–Ritchie 2019–22 meta-analysis finding ~50 % of pledges achieved, and the Climate Action Tracker's long-standing finding that "pledges are not policies"). The frontier is **carbon accounting's fine print**: production-versus-consumption accounting (the ~20–25 % of emissions embodied in traded goods, which moves the responsibility ledger from China's factory to Europe's consumer), the "net" in net zero (the SBTi and ISO net-zero standards' fight over offset quality — the 2023–24 investigations finding most avoided-deforestation and cookstove credits non-additional, the carbon-credit system's integrity crisis that the Guardian/Die Zeit/Der Spiegel investigation of January 2023 detonated), and Article 6 of the Paris Agreement (the markets mechanism whose accounting rules — "corresponding adjustments" preventing double-counting — were operationalised at COP26–28 and are the difference between a trade and a loophole). None of this is soft science: every item is a measurement or a bookkeeping rule whose violation is detectable by the satellite-verification systems above, which is the quiet revolution the transparency framework represents — the first regime where the emissions *can be checked from orbit*.
:::

::: example Methane's two surprises: the 2020–21 growth spike and the satellite reckoning {#example-methane}
Atmospheric methane grew by ~15–17 ppb in each of 2020 and 2021 — the largest two-year increase since measurements began in 1983 and larger than any year in the 800,000-year ice-core record's industrial section. The source attribution (the Global Carbon Project's methane budgets, the GOSAT/TROPOMI satellite observations of super-emitter plumes) found a three-part story: continued growth in tropical wetland emissions (the temperature and precipitation feedback that the models projected, now measured), steady anthropogenic emissions from livestock and rice (~32 % of anthropogenic methane, the enteric-fermentation term that a plant-diet shift addresses but no technology does), and fossil-oil-and-gas emissions (~36 % of anthropogenic, of which the flaring and venting that satellite constellations — MethaneSAT, launched 2023, and the Kayrros/Methane Hydespace detection services — now find at 3–5 % of production, the "1.3 °C by 2100" prize of the 2021 Global Methane Pledge's 30 % cut hiding in plain sight: the IEA and the UN Environment Programme's 2021 assessment found ~75 % of the oil-and-gas methane abatement is available at *negative* net cost — capture-and-use or flaring elimination that pays for itself within the gas value). The second surprise is the accounting one: the satellites' super-emitter detections (the Turkmenistan and Russia pipelines, the Perm Basin plumes, the 2022 Nord Stream releases — the largest single methane event ever measured, ~1–2 MtCH₄ in days) turned methane from an inventory estimate (nations' self-reported "national communications," built on emission factors) into a measured quantity, and the first country-level comparisons (the 2020s US, EU and national studies) found inventories *understating* oil-and-gas methane by 30–70 % — the verification revolution arriving first to the gas where the physics (a strong absorber in the atmospheric window, a short lifetime, a sharp satellite signature) makes it easiest. The exercise: using the Global Methane Pledge's arithmetic — 30 % methane cut by 2030 — and the AR6 methane TCRE-equivalent (~0.5 °C of current methane forcing, with the ~0.2 °C of *aerosol-masking* caveat that makes methane's net warming the largest short-lived term), explain why methane is simultaneously the fastest lever on temperature and no substitute for CO₂ action.
::: solution
Methane's lifetime (~11 yr) means a 30 % cut lowers its concentration within a decade and slows the *rate* of warming immediately (the half-degree-scale benefit by 2050, the model intercomparisons' range), while CO₂'s persistence means its warming is cumulative and only net-zero *stops* it — methane buys time and saves crop yields (its ozone precursor effect) but bends the curve's slope, not its endpoint; the endpoint is CO₂'s ledger, which is why the two gases need different accounting (GWP* for methane's plateau, cumulative CO₂ for the budget) and why "CO₂-equivalent" totals that mix them with GWP100 mislead in both directions.
:::
:::

::: example The cost revolution the models of 2010 did not project {#example-cost}
Between 2010 and 2024 the levelised cost of solar photovoltaics fell ~90 %, of lithium-ion battery packs ~89 %, and onshore wind ~70 % (the BloombergNEF and IRENA series), so that by 2024 new solar was the cheapest electricity in history across most of the world (the IEA's "cheap electricity era" framing) and coal and gas plants were being retired early on economics, not policy. The relevance to this lesson is scientific, not commercial: the integrated assessment models (IAMs) that generated the 2014–18 IPCC scenarios embedded technology-cost assumptions that made solar+viable-storage a 2040s–2050s option, and the resulting pathways "needed" carbon capture and nuclear to compete — the "IAM pessimism" the post-2020 literature documents (the Pindyck critique of IAM damage functions, the Way et al. 2022 *Joule* paper on experience curves showing learning-driven cost decline is the *norm* for module technologies, the "demand-side" school's finding that the AR6 WGIII chapter's own suite under-represents electrification and efficiency potentials). The accounting consequence: the emissions *gap* between pledges and pathways is partly a knowledge gap — the pledges were written against old cost curves, and the Paris ratchet mechanism (NDCs updated every five years, progressively more ambitious — the "ratchet" of decision 1/CP.21) is a mechanism for the pledges to catch up with the physics *and* the economics. The 2023 Global Stocktake's finding — "the world is not on track" but "the foundations are in place" (the solar-installation rate of 2023 alone, ~420 GW, being ~1.5× the rate the 1.5 °C pathways require for the power sector) — is the first time the accounting layer reported both facts in one sentence: the budget says we are late, and the deployment curves say the tools arrived.
::: solution
The lesson for the budget arithmetic: cheaper solar does not enlarge the carbon budget — the physics is indifferent to the cost curve — but it collapses the *cost* of staying inside it, and the gap literature's "least-cost 1.5 °C pathway" of 2024 is a fundamentally different machine (electrified transport, heat pumps, green steel, solar-plus-storage baseload) than the 2015 pathways' CCS-and-nuclear-heavy portfolios. The budget is fixed; the price of the ticket fell 90 %, and the accounting of promise has not yet repriced the ticket.
:::
:::

::: warning The five accounting traps: budgets that aren't budgets, nets that aren't zero, and offsets that aren't reductions
**1. "The budget is for 1.5 °C" — which one?** The 510 GtCO₂ figure is a *coin-flip for a 50 % chance of a 20-year-mean peak*; the never-exceed-1.5 budget is ~half that, and the "temporarily overshoot and return" budget is larger but depends on removals at contested scale. Every budget number must be quoted with its probability and its definition. **2. "Net zero" is not zero.** Net-zero definitions (the SBTi 2021, the ISO 14068) allow residual emissions balanced by removals; the "net" in a national pledge can be satisfied by land-carbon accounting whose permanence is drought- and fire-fragile (the 2018–19 and 2023 Canadian and Siberian forest-fire seasons turned land sinks to sources in weeks — the "buffer-reversal risk" the removals literature prices). **3. Offsets are not reductions.** The 2023 investigation of the voluntary carbon market (the Guardian/Die Zeit/WSJ series on Verra's avoided-deforestation credits, the ~70 % over-estimation findings of the West et al. and Arcusa et al. forest-credit studies) showed the additionality problem at scale: credits paying for forests that were not in fact about to be cut. The carbon-cycle arithmetic is unforgiving — a tonne emitted is a tonne added regardless of the ledger's bookkeeping. **4. Production accounting hides consumption.** A third of China's emissions (roughly) embody European and American final demand; national inventories that stop at the factory gate mis-assign responsibility by ~20–25 % globally, and both framings are legitimate — the choice is normative and must be stated. **5. The airborne fraction is not a policy target.** Its constancy (the good news: sinks keep working) is also its warning (the bad news: they keep working *proportionally*, so the atmosphere keeps its ~45 % no matter how much we emit, and any sink weakening — the 2023 land-sink anomaly, the Amazon's 2010/2015/2015–16 drought-driven dieback studies, the peat and permafrost terms — raises the fraction and shrinks every budget in the lesson at once).
:::

::: history From the Keeling curve to the carbon budget: the accounting revolution {#history-budget}
The measurement era begins with **Charles David Keeling's** 1958 Mauna Loa baseline (315 ppm) and his 1960 discovery of the seasonal cycle — proof of a living, exchanging surface biosphere — and the 1970s realization (the Houghton–Woodwell and Bolin bookkeeping) that the airborne fraction was roughly constant. The **ice-core era** (Friedli et al./Stauffer et al. at Bern, 1980; Barnola's GRIP work; the EPICA Dome C 800,000-year record of 2008) established the 180–300 ppm natural range and the CO₂–temperature coupling that makes the modern 424 ppm a 3-million-year outlier. The **ocean and land sink quantification** came next: the Key West–Tahiti ship transects and the VOCES time series (the 1990s), the Global Ocean Ship-Based Hydrographic Investigations Program's repeat sections, the FLUXNET eddy-flux tower network (1994–), the Free-Air CO₂ Enrichment experiments (FACE, 1996–2005) that measured the fertilisation response directly, and the **Global Carbon Project** (founded 2001, its annual Global Carbon Budget the field's ledger). The **policy-invention era** ran 2008–2014: the Meehl et al. (2009) and Matthews et al. (2009) *Nature* papers showing that stabilising warming requires *cumulative* emission limits (the "carbon-climate response" papers, building on the Allen et al. 2009 cumulative-emissions experiments and the 2008–09 Meinhausen–Allen synthesis), the Myhre et al. (2013) and Macdonald et al. (2009) observational TCRE constraints, the **2013–14 IPCC AR5 WGIII** chapter's budget tables, and the 2014–15 "carbon budget" deployment into the Paris negotiations (the Davos/Durban-era "budget" framing, the 2014 IPCC Synthesis Report's 1,000 GtCO₂-for-2 °C table, the 2015 Paris Agreement's Article 4.1 "in the context of sustainable development" language that quietly adopted the budget as the organizing device). The **verification era** (2009– ): OCO-2 (2014), the GOSAT-3/CO2M lineage, the Copernicus Anthropogenic CO₂ Monitoring system (2023– , the first operational satellite verification of national emissions), the TROPOMI methane images (2018– ) and MethaneSAT (2023), the ICOS and urban-inverse-model networks making city inventories checkable, and the 2021–24 carbon-market integrity crisis that taught policy what the accountants already knew: in a budget world, the bookkeeping *is* the physics.
:::

- [ ] Rising sharply, as the sinks saturate

**Roughly constant at ~45 %** — the Keeling–Bacastow result: the land and ocean sinks absorb roughly 55 % of emissions year after year because their uptake is driven by the *perturbation* concentration the emissions create (first-order linear carbon-cycle behaviour). The constancy is the reason cumulative emissions map so cleanly to cumulative concentration and (via TCRE) to peak warming — the whole carbon-budget policy edifice stands on this measured near-linearity, whose slow drift and interannual variability (the El Niño years' 55–60 % airborne fractions) are the budget literature's stated uncertainties.

The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C (50 %) from 2020 was ~510 GtCO₂ and emissions run ~40 GtCO₂/yr. How many years until exhaustion, and why does the answer not mean "we have until 2033 to act"?
::: solution
510/40 ≈ 13 years — but the budget is a *cumulative* account: every year of inaction draws it down, the peak-warming physics has no grace period, and the 50 % probability means half the pathways exceed 1.5 at this budget already; the date is when the budget is *spent*, not when the limit is *reached* (peak warming follows cumulative emissions with a small lag).

Using TCRE = 0.45 °C per 1,000 GtCO₂, compute the peak-warming increase implied by the 510 GtCO₂ remaining budget above the 2020 level, and add the ~1.1 °C already reached to test the budget's internal consistency.

0.45 × 0.51 = 0.23 °C; 1.1 + 0.23 ≈ 1.33 — the apparent shortfall against 1.5 is the budget's built-in terms: the non-CO₂ warming (~0.1–0.2 °C, mostly methane) and the definition of "above 1850–1900" versus "above 2010–19," which the AR6 budget arithmetic carries explicitly — the exercise's point is that the budget is not simply (1.5 − 1.1)/TCRE.

Atmospheric CO₂ grows ~2.6 ppm/yr on a stock of 424 ppm. Compute the fractional growth rate and the doubling time (rule of 70).
::: solution
2.6/424 = 0.61 %/yr; 70/0.61 ≈ 115 years — the "doubling time" that makes the 2×CO₂ equilibrium-sensitivity literature (the 2.5–4 °C ECS range) a 22nd-century question and the *rate* of change a this-decade question, the distinction the rate-of-warming and tipping-point literatures keep separate.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="40"}
The 1.5 °C pathway requires 2030 emissions ~42 % below 2019 (all gases, ~59 GtCO₂-e in 2019) while current policies run ~25 % above 2019. Compute the 2030 "emissions gap" in GtCO₂-e in this crude all-gas arithmetic.
::: solution
Pathway 2030: 0.58 × 59 = 34; current-policy 2030: 1.25 × 59 = 74; the gap is 74 − 34 = 40 GtCO₂-e — larger than the UNEP Emissions Gap Report's published figures because those use narrower gas coverage and different baselines, which is the lesson's real point: the gap's size depends on the accounting, and the reports' methods sections are the most political pages in climate science.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="2.7"}
Satellite inventories find national oil-and-gas methane reports understate actual emissions by 40 %. If reported fossil methane is 70 MtCH₄/yr, compute the true value, and its GWP100 CO₂-e equivalent (27.9) in GtCO₂-e to one decimal.
::: solution
True = 98 MtCH₄/yr; ×27.9 = 2.7 GtCO₂-e — roughly India's entire fossil CO₂ footprint hiding in a gas whose abatement is 75 % negative-cost, the single largest near-term lever in the inventory and the reason the satellite-methane revolution (TROPOMI, MethaneSAT) is a climate policy as much as a scientific instrument.

::: exercise {level=3 check="2.05"}
If the land and ocean sinks weakened so that the airborne fraction rose from 45 % to 50 %, by how many GtCO₂ would the 2023 atmospheric gain (19 GtCO₂) have increased, and what does that imply for the budget's lifetime at constant emissions?
::: solution
41 × 0.05 = 2.05 GtCO₂/yr of extra airborne gain (about a quarter more annual growth); the budget's lifetime shortens by ~10 % — the "sink saturation" sensitivity that every budget update tests and that the 2023 land-sink anomaly made concrete: a carbon cycle that is 10 % less generous is a decade less of budget, purchased by no one's policy.

::: quiz
The physical basis for a "carbon budget" (cumulative emissions ↔ peak warming) is:
- [ ] The exponential decay of CO₂ in the atmosphere
- [x] The near-cancellation of the declining sink efficiency and the roughly constant warming per airborne tonne (TCRE linearity)
- [ ] The linear forcing of CO₂ (which alone suffices)
- [ ] The 100-year GWP convention
::: solution
**The two linearities cancel**: as CO₂ accumulates, each additional tonne is taken up *less* efficiently by ocean and land (the Revelle-factor effect), but each airborne tonne warms *more* as the baseline grows — and the two effects offset, leaving warming proportional to *cumulative* emissions (the carbon–climate response literature: Matthews 2009, Allen 2009, the AR6 TCRE assessment). Linear forcing alone would not do it; the budget exists because the carbon cycle cooperates with the radiative physics in exactly the wrong way for delay to be safe.
:::

::: summary
- The inventory: ~59 GtCO₂-e/yr across gases (2019), CO₂ at 424 ppm (+52 %), methane 1,930 ppb (+160 %, with the 2020–21 growth spike the field's warning), N₂O 346 ppb from fertiliser, F-gases small but potent — and the metrics (GWP100/20, GWP*, cumulative CO₂) are choices that must be stated, not constants.
- The carbon cycle keeps a near-constant ~45 % airborne fraction because sinks scale with the perturbation — the measured near-linearity (Global Carbon Project, isotope and satellite constraints) that makes cumulative emissions the master variable.
- Cumulative emissions since 1850 (~1,780 GtCO₂ fossil) are distributed 55 % to the US and Europe with ~12 % of humanity — the equity ledger behind every COP fight — while the remaining 1.5 °C budget (AR6: 510 GtCO₂ from 2020 at 50 %) is now ~5 years of current emissions.
- Peak warming ∝ cumulative CO₂ (TCRE ~0.45 °C/1,000 GtCO₂) is the discovery that converted temperature targets into budgets and budgets into deadlines; the budget's probability, definition and excluded feedbacks must travel with the number.
- The policy layer is accounting: NDCs deliver ~2.6–3.1 °C, the 2030 gap is ~20 GtCO₂-e, the "net" in net zero and the offset markets' integrity are bookkeeping problems the physics cannot see — and the new satellite verification (OCO-2, Copernicus, TROPOMI, MethaneSAT) is making the ledgers checkable from orbit, the first climate regime where the numbers can be audited rather than believed.
:::
