On 23 June 1988 a NASA scientist named James Hansen did something no senior government scientist had done: he testified to the US Senate that global warming had begun. "The greenhouse effect has been detected, and it is changing our climate as we speak," he told the Energy and Natural Resources committee, on one of the hottest days in the Washington record, in a hearing room whose air conditioning had failed — a staging the press corps could not resist. Hansen's claim was, by the standards of the science of 1988, slightly ahead of itself: the observed warming curve matched the projections, but the *attribution* — the demonstration that the pattern of warming matched the pattern greenhouse forcing predicts and not the pattern of solar or volcanic or internal variability — was a decade from its mature form. That decade produced the field this lesson describes: **detection and attribution**, the statistical machinery that turns "the climate changed" into "humans caused a quantified amount of the change," and then, after 2003, into "human influence made this specific heatwave twice as likely." The machinery is now confident enough that AR6's Working Group I summary states the result in the language science almost never uses: the influence of humans on the climate system since the 1950s is **"unequivocal"** — the assessment's highest possible confidence term, attached not to the warming itself but to its *cause*. This lesson is about why that word is earned: the fingerprint logic, the optimal-fingerprinting statistics, the two-world experiments of single-event attribution, and the newest and most contested extension — attributing not just warming but *specific emissions from specific companies* to specific harms, the move that carried attribution science from the journal into the courtroom.

## The logic: detecting a signal, attributing a cause

::: definition Detection and attribution: the statistical framework and its vocabulary {#def-da-framework}
The field's grammar comes from signal-detection theory (the 1995 IPCC Second Assessment's Chapter 8, the Mitchell et al. and Santer et al. papers, and the Allen 1993–1999 optimal-fingerprinting line). **Detection** is a statement about the *observed change*: that it is unlikely to have occurred without external forcing — a rejection of the "internal variability only" null hypothesis, quantified as a probability (the 1990s phrasing: "the balance of evidence suggests a discernible human influence," IPCC 1995, was a carefully hedged detection *and* attribution claim that became the most lawyered sentence in science). **Attribution** goes further: it apportions the detected change among candidate causes — greenhouse gases, aerosols, ozone, land use, solar, volcanic, internal variability — using **optimal fingerprinting** (the "detection and attribution regression"): the observed change vector (space × time × variable) is regressed onto model-simulated *response patterns* ("fingerprints") for each forcing, with the noise of the models' internal variability accounted for; the regression coefficients are the **scaling factors** — "the greenhouse-gas response is present with amplitude 1.2 ± 0.2, the aerosol response with amplitude 0.8 ± 0.3" — and the test of the whole framework is that the scaling factors cluster near 1 when the right fingerprints are used and the wrong ones are excluded. The method's power comes from *pattern information*: a scalar (global mean temperature) cannot separate greenhouse from solar forcing, but the **vertical pattern** (troposphere up, stratosphere down — greenhouse; everything up — solar), the **seasonal pattern** (polar amplification in winter, stronger nights), the **diurnal pattern** (minimum temperatures rising faster), and the **spatial pattern** (the Northern Hemisphere land amplification, the Southern Ocean's slow-response signature) together make the fingerprint nearly unique — the reason the 2020s assessments can state with high confidence that greenhouse-gas and aerosol fingerprints are separately detectable in the observations, and that solar forcing contributes ~0.0 °C (±0.05) of the ~1.1 °C of post-1950 warming. The framework's honest limitations are equally standard: **multicollinearity** (greenhouse and aerosol fingerprints are anti-correlated in time, so their scaling factors are correlated in the regression — the "aerosol compensation" problem that makes the ECS estimate from historical data sensitive to the aerosol forcing assumption), **model error** (the fingerprints come from models, and if the models' patterns are wrong the regression is biased — addressed by multi-model panels and by the "necessity/sufficiency" tests), and the **non-stationarity of the null** (internal variability must be estimated from models or the preindustrial control runs, whose multi-century samples are the field's quiet resource bottleneck). The AR6 Working Group I Chapter 3 ("Human Influence on the Climate System") synthesises all lines — fingerprints, energy budget, palaeoclimate analogues, and process understanding — into the "unequivocal" verdict, with the *amount* of historical warming attributable to humans stated as 1.07 °C of the observed 1.09 °C for 2010–19: the first assessment in which anthropogenic warming is *the number*, and natural forcing and internal variability round to the decimal.
:::

::: definition Lines of evidence beyond fingerprints: the convergent-argument method {#def-lines-of-evidence}
The attribution verdict does not rest on the regression; it rests on a **convergence of independent arguments**, a style of proof the philosopher of science would call inference to the best explanation and the assessments call "multiple lines of evidence." (1) **The physics is old and tested**: the CO₂ radiative effect is calculated from laboratory spectroscopy (the HITRAN line-by-line databases, the 2020s re-measurements of the key CO₂ bands at the Swiss PSI facility confirming the line parameters used in climate models), and the ~2 W/m² of forcing from the 2011 CO₂ level matches the observed warming once feedbacks and aerosols are included. (2) **The carbon is ours, isotopically**: the declining **¹³C/¹²C and ¹⁴C ratios** in air (the Suess effect) prove the CO₂ increment carries the fossil-carbon and biosphere-carbon signatures — the ¹⁴C decline (fossil carbon is ¹⁴C-dead) rules out the ocean and volcanoes, which together emit ~95 % of *gross* annual CO₂ fluxes but are net-absorbed; the mass-balance argument (airborne fraction ~45 % of emissions, the rest taken up by land and ocean, whose uptake is *proportional* to the excess) closes the accounting. (3) **The models work in the counterfactual**: simulations with natural forcing only (solar + volcanic) reproduce the pre-1930s wiggle and the 1940s–50s plateau but *cannot* produce post-1970 warming (the "without-anthropogenic-forcing" ensemble brackets the observations at less than 5 % likelihood from the 1990s onward — the figure behind the "unequivocal" verdict), while all-forcing ensembles reproduce the observed curve including its *cooling* episodes (the Pinatubo dip of 1992–93, predicted and observed to ~0.3 °C, the single most elegant confirmation in the field). (4) **The energy budget closes**: the CERES imbalance, the ocean heat uptake, the melt energy and the warming land all sum to the same forcing (the observed-vs-forcing residual is within error — the "constraints" literature's central consistency check). (5) **The palaeoclimate analogue**: the Pliocene (~4 Myr ago, CO₂ ~400 ppm, sea level 10–20 m higher) and the Eocene are warm-state worlds whose modelled temperatures under known geography and CO₂ match the proxy records — the Earth system model experiment that cannot be run in the lab has been run against the geological record, with the Pliocene Model Intercomparison Project as the formal test. The strength of the case is that each line independently excludes the main alternative explanations (solar: the stratosphere and the irradiance record; ocean cycles: the energy-budget closure and the asymmetric fingerprint; natural carbon: the isotopes), and the alternatives' failure modes are *different* — a conspiracy of broken instruments, broken spectroscopy, broken isotope geochemistry, broken satellites and a broken Holocene is not a live scientific hypothesis.
:::

::: example The 1990s: from "the balance of evidence" to the fingerprint wars {#example-1990s}
Trace the detection-and-attribution arc of the 1990s, the decade that created the field and its politics in one stroke. Santer et al. (1996, *Nature*) compared radiosonde and satellite vertical temperature profiles against model fingerprints and reported a "positive but preliminary" detection of the greenhouse pattern — the first vertical-attribution result, and the target of a funded industry campaign (the "follow the data" letters demanding the raw radiosonde archives, eventually released and confirming the finding). Mitchell, Johns, Karol et al. (1995) and the IPCC TAR Chapter 8 built the optimal-fingerprinting framework and produced the 1995 formula ("the balance of evidence suggests a discernible human influence") that triggered the US Senate's Byrd resolution (95–0, opposing the Berlin mandate) — the moment attribution science became a geopolitical fact. Allen & Stott, and Stott et al. (2000) then delivered the first *event* attribution — the December 2000 UK floods, "roughly twice the risk" — and the 2001 TAR raised the language to "new and stronger evidence." The methodological fights of the decade (the FDD/RSI attempts to replicate Santer with the RSS channel-drift artefact; the North–Cess debate over whether models' cloud feedbacks were biased; the "troposphere-warming-trojan-horse" emails of 2009, which on full release showed the disputed conclusions surviving their own internal criticism) established the field's modern character: results that are *attacked with data, re-run with data, and confirmed with data*. What is the single statistical reason the vertical-pattern detection was so much harder than surface detection, and why did the same vertical data later become the strongest single line of evidence?
::: solution
The vertical pattern's signal-to-noise is small in the 1990s because the stratospheric cooling signal was partly masked by ozone depletion (a confounding fingerprint the models had to include separately) and because the radiosonde record carried instrument-drift artefacts (the VTPR/MSU channel issues, the RSS–GISTEMP divergence) that the field only resolved in the 2010s–2020s with station-to-station homogenisation (the RATR/HadAT/Preussler methods) and satellite reprocessing (the RSS 2022 self-correction). Once cleaned, the vertical pattern — stratosphere cooling, troposphere warming, the amplitude ratio matching models — became the fingerprint that no solar or internal-variability explanation can reproduce, which is why the 1996 paper's "preliminary" verdict is the 2021 assessment's "unequivocal" one.
:::
:::

::: example The two-world experiment: Allen et al. 2004 and the 2003 European heatwave {#example-2003}
The August 2003 heatwave — 70,000-plus excess deaths in Europe, the Alps' worst glacial melt in 300 years, the French crisis that produced the country's heat plan — was attributed within months by David Allen's Oxford group in a *Nature* paper that founded single-event attribution as a method (the "Allen et al. 2004" now cited in every climate-litigation filing). The design is the **counterfactual experiment**: run the Hadley Centre model (HadAM3) thousands of years under 1900-forcing and 2003-forcing climates, compute the probability of a European-summer heat as extreme as 2003 in each world (the "risk" R), and report the **fraction of attributable risk** FAR = (R₁ − R₀)/R₁. The published result: the risk of the 2003 summer temperatures was **at least doubled** by anthropogenic forcing, and — the number that made the paper famous — the *confidence* statement "we can say with ~90 % confidence that the risk has increased, and with lower confidence (~75 %) that the risk has at least doubled." The method's assumptions are the honest caveats the field repeats: the event is defined by a *threshold* (the choice of index changes the FAR), the model must simulate the event's dynamics (the 2003 soil-moisture feedback — worth 1–2 °C of the peak — is represented but imperfectly), and the counterfactual world is not "natural" but "preindustrial-plus-natural," a definitional choice with legal consequences. The 2016 Paris Agreement's Article 8 (loss and damage) and the World Weather Attribution platform (2014– , 100+ event studies) are direct descendants of this paper; so is the 2021 German court's reasoning in the Saúl-Luciano case (below). The question for this exercise: if the FAR of a heatwave is 0.5 and the heatwave killed 70,000 people, why is it *not* valid to say "35,000 deaths were caused by climate change," and what is the defensible sentence?
::: solution
FAR is a *population risk* statistic, not an individual-causation rule: the correct statement is that climate change roughly doubled the risk of the event, and that under the counterfactual distribution the expected death toll would have been ~half — the epidemiological framing ("the attributable fraction of the *risk*") that the litigation and the health-attribution literature use, and the distinction courts (the German rulings' "causal chain" problem) still struggle to operationalise into liability.
:::

## Sources, budgets, and the courtroom

::: definition Single-event attribution and its discontents {#def-sea}
Single-event attribution (SEA) — the World Weather Attribution school, the "beast" (the Bond events at 100-plus studies) — answers the question the public and the courts actually ask: *how much of this disaster's risk is human-caused?* The standard metric is the **fraction of attributable risk** (FAR = 1 − P₀/P₁, the risk ratio between counterfactual and factual worlds) or its probability-based cousin, the **risk-ratio shift** ("the 1-in-10-year heat is now 1-in-3"). The method's strengths are real: it is *relevant* (the 2021 Western North America heat, the 2022 Pakistan floods — a third of the country underwater, the attribution finding "up to 19 times more likely rainfall in the worst-affected provinces" though with the compound-flood caveat, the 2021 Zhengzhou/2022 Sichuan-Chongqing heat, the 2003–24 running record of the WWA and Climate Central "Climate Shift Index" daily attribution stream). Its documented weaknesses are equally real and now textbook: (1) **the threshold and index problem** — FAR depends on how the event is defined (a heatwave by peak temperature, by night-time minimum, or by duration gives different answers, and the 2015 Beven & Kearns and 2021 Bell et al. critiques formalised the sensitivity); (2) **the model-skill precondition** — if the model cannot simulate the event's dynamics (convective storms, the 2021 Zhengzhou rainfall's topography coupling), the counterfactual is untrustworthy, which is why WWA's protocol now *withholds* attribution statements for such events (the honest "we cannot attribute this flood" is a third of its output); (3) **the compound-event gap** — the 2022 Pakistan and 2023 Libya disasters were *chains* (heat → glacier melt → rainfall → infrastructure failure), and the field's risk-ratio arithmetic handles single variables; (4) **the adaptation confound** — the *impact* (deaths, damage) depends on exposure and defences, so "climate change made X twice as likely" says nothing about whether X's harm doubled (the 1970 vs 2020 Bangladesh cyclones are the counter-example in both directions). The 2020s methodological frontier addresses these with **climate-model "worlds" ensembles** (the half-degree-resolved counterfactuals of the HASS project), **observation-only approaches** (the risk-based attribution of the 2023 WWA Mediterranean studies), and the **impact-attribution** extension (the 2022–24 studies attributing *crop losses* and *mortality* rather than weather). The verdict the field itself endorses: SEA is a *risk* science, not a *case* science — a public-health tool for communicating the meaning of a warming world in the grammar of a single disaster, and a litigation input whose causal weight courts are still deciding.
:::

::: definition Source attribution and the law: from carbon majors to the courtroom {#def-source-attribution}
The final extension of attribution science runs the causal chain backwards, from harm to emitter. **Emission source attribution** (the "carbon majors" literature) begins with Richard Heede's 2013 inventory: of the 977 GtCO₂-equivalent of industrial CO₂ and methane from 1854–2010, **63 % trace to 90 investor-owned or state-owned fossil-fuel and cement producers**, and 33 GtC (≈36 % of the industrial-era total to 2010) to just 20 companies, ExxonMobil first (the "Carbon Majors Database," now in the public domain and the basis of most climate-litigation filings). The second move — attributing *warming* to *sources* — is newer and contested: the 2019 Feature, Venegas et al. *Nature* study ("Causes and implications of the 2011–2020 warm temperature anomaly") found 45 companies' emissions consistent with ~half of observed warming over the period, a result criticised (the method's treatment of aerosols co-emitted by the same companies, and of the carbon-cycle uptake of *all* emissions regardless of source) and defended (the relative-risk logic being robust to the criticism's core). The **legal turn** is where attribution science's epistemics meet burden-of-proof epistemics: the **Urgenda** cases (the Dutch Supreme Court's 2019 *Urgenda* judgment ordering a 25 % emissions cut by 2020 — the first court to order a state to reduce emissions on human-rights grounds, its reasoning leaning on the precautionary principle and the IPCC's own carbon budgets rather than on event attribution), the German **Neubauer v RWE** line (Saúl Luciano Lliaya, a Peruvian farmer suing over the Palcaraju glacier lake above his hometown, the 2017 Dortmund district court *admitting* World Weather Attribution's flood-attribution evidence and the 2022 higher-court dismissal turning on the *proportional causation* question — "0.5 % of historical emissions" — not on the science's admissibility), the 2021 **Milieudefensie v Shell** order (a 45 %-by-2030 cut, the judgment citing the IPCC carbon budgets and the company's own plans' inconsistency with them), and the 2024–25 **Advisory Opinion of the International Court of Justice** proceedings on states' climate obligations (the first ICJ climate case, with attribution science in the written pleadings of 90-plus parties). The epistemic fault line is precise and worth teaching: attribution science speaks in **probabilities of populations** (FAR, risk ratios, scaling factors), while tort law demands **causation of particulars** (this flood, this farm, this defendant's share). The field's contribution is that it has made the *general* causation question legally un-losable — no court in any of these cases disputed that anthropogenic warming is real, human-caused, and harmful — while the *particular* apportionment remains the open problem that the 2020s "attribution of harm to source" methods (the carbon-majors-to-impact chains) are built to attack.
:::

::: example The Pakistan floods of 2022: attribution at the compound-event frontier {#example-pakistan}
Between June and September 2022 one-third of Pakistan was under water at some point: 1,739 deaths, 8.5 million displaced, ~$30 billion of damage and economic loss (the World Bank–PDNA assessment), rainfall in the worst-affected districts of Sindh and Balochistan 5–10× the 1991–2020 August mean, preceded by two lethal heatwaves (March–April 2022, 45 °C+, themselves attributed by WWA as "virtually impossible" without climate change at the regional scale) that dried and cracked the soils before the deluge. The attribution literature on this disaster is the field's current case study in both directions: the **rainfall** attribution (a 750-mm-in-4-days event in the worst district given a risk ratio of ~1.5–7.5 depending on the index and domain — the spread itself the finding), the **heat** attribution (strong), the **soil-moisture preconditioning** (the compound-heat-then-rain mechanism, the 2023 *Environmental Research: Climate* analyses), and the **loss** attribution (almost none — the damage is a function of exposure, poverty, drainage and dam operations, the "loss and damage" side of the chain that the COP27's new fund formally acknowledges). The honest synthesis the field publishes: the *hazard* terms are increasingly attributable, the *compound* interactions are the frontier, and the *impact* is mostly not attributable to emissions at all — which is exactly the adaptation-finance argument the most-affected countries make at the COP. What does the 1.5–7.5 risk-ratio spread teach about index choice, and why did WWA's own statement lead with the heat attribution rather than the flood attribution?
::: solution
The spread comes from defining the event by 1-day vs 5-day rainfall, by district vs province, and by model vs observation — the FAR of a convective-and-monsoonal event is index-sensitive in a way that a synoptic heatwave's is not; the heatwave, by contrast, was attributable with models *and* observations and across indices, so it carried the strongest statement. The episode is now the textbook for the field's rule: attribute the term you can model, describe the chain you cannot, and never let the headline imply a causation the method did not test.
:::
:::

::: example The cooling that proved the rule: Pinatubo and the model as instrument {#example-pinatubo}
On 15 June 1991 Mount Pinatubo erupted (VEI 6, ~20 Mt of SO₂ into the stratosphere, the largest volcanic forcing of the satellite era), and the climate system ran the experiment the field had been unable to: a known, measured forcing applied to the real planet. The observed response — a global-mean surface cooling of ~0.4–0.5 °C peaking in 1992, a stronger Northern Hemisphere winter warming pattern (the stratospheric-aerosol-driven polar amplification of the *winter* response), a ~1 % drop in solar radiation reaching the surface, a measurable slowdown of the hydrological cycle — was predicted within a few hundredths of a degree by the models that had been tuned to the greenhouse problem (the Hansen et al. 1990s predictions were made before the eruption's aerosol amount was known and were roughly right; the Soden et al. and later model–observation syntheses confirmed the amplitude). The episode matters three times over for attribution: it validated the models' **radiative-transfer and cloud-response physics** on a forcing of known size (the same physics that computes the greenhouse forcing); it demonstrated the **detection power of a transient** (the 1990s "the warming paused because of Pinatubo" arguments were themselves an implicit acceptance of the attribution framework — the volcano's cooling being the negative control that the framework predicted); and it calibrated the **volcanic fingerprint** that optimal fingerprinting must include to avoid biasing the aerosol term — the same logic by which the 2019–20 shipping-sulphate reduction is now treated as a natural experiment in aerosol forcing. The question: if a 20 Mt SO₂ eruption cools the planet 0.4 °C for a year, and anthropogenic sulphate aerosol forcing (~−0.5 W/m², AR6's best estimate for aerosol-radiation plus aerosol-cloud) is roughly a sustained Pinatubo every few years, what does the 2020 shipping-fuel switch's unmasking imply, and why did the 2023 temperature spike not *require* it as an explanation?
::: solution
The aerosol term is real but small in temperature units (~0.3–0.5 °C of masking total, AR6's "the aerosol forcing is the largest uncertainty in the forcing budget"), and its 2020 step-change contributes at most ~0.05–0.15 °C over years — enough to *nudge* the timing of the 2023 record, not to cause it; the Pinatubo analogue is the reason the field could bound that contribution from physics rather than guess it, and why the spike's decomposition (ENSO, Hunga Tonga water vapour, aerosol un-masking, trend) sums to the observed jump within uncertainty.
:::
:::

::: warning The five ways attribution claims get overstated — by sceptics, by activists, and by the science's own success
**1. "Scientists proved this storm was caused by climate change."** No attribution study says this; the method's output is a risk ratio for a *defined index*, and the 2015–24 record of headlines ("hurricanes are climate change," per the WWA authors' own complaint) is a translation failure the field is fighting with its "we cannot attribute" statements. **2. "The 97 % consensus is an attribution claim."** The consensus (the Cook et al. 2013–2019 meta-analyses, the 99 %+ expert agreement studies) is about the *fact* of human causation; the attribution literature is the *evidence* behind it, not a vote. **3. "FAR means X % of the deaths were caused by emissions."** FAR is a risk statistic (the Allen 2004 caveat above); the individual-causation reading is the one the courts keep rejecting and the one the press keeps printing. **4. "The carbon-majors study proves Exxon caused half of warming."** The Feature et al. 2019 result is a *source-apportionment* of the warming signal under stated assumptions (aerosols, uptake), contested on those assumptions, and not a legal causation finding. **5. "Detection was achieved in 1995, so the science has been settled for decades."** The 1995 sentence was a *detection-plus-weak-attribution* claim; the "unequivocal" verdict of 2021 rests on the 25 years of convergent evidence — the vertical fingerprints, the energy-budget closure, the isotopic accounting, the counterfactual models — that did not exist in 1995. The meta-point is that attribution's public failure mode is *over-translation*, and its scientific failure mode is the same sensitivity analysis every honest paper publishes in its own limitations section — the difference between a field that audits itself and one that does not is the entire content of this warning.
:::

::: history From Arrhenius's question to the courtroom: attribution's hundred years {#history-attribution}
Attribution's prehistory is the *question's* history: Arrhenius (1896) asked how much a CO₂ doubling warms the world; Callendar (1938) asked whether the warming had begun; the Möller–Plass radiative-transfer recalculations (1960s) re-established the magnitude; and the 1979 Charney report answered the first question's uncertainty range. The second question — has it begun, and is it ours? — became quantitative in three waves. **Wave one (1991–2003): detection.** The Schlesinger–Ramankutty and Tsigaridis–Falge pattern studies, the Santer 1996 vertical detection, the IPCC 1995 Chapter 8's "balance of evidence" sentence and its political shock (the Byrd resolution), the 2001 TAR's "new and stronger evidence," and the Allen–Stott–Hegerl optimal-fingerprinting papers that built the regression toolkit — all culminating in the 2003 European heatwave, which killed the hedge: you cannot hedge a 70,000-death summer. **Wave two (2004–2015): events and budgets.** Allen et al. 2004's two-world experiment; the US National Academy's 2005 and 2009 special reports ("attribution of climate change to human activities" and the "Attribution of Extreme Events" workshop) legitimising SEA for federal use; the 2010–14 attribution cascade (the Met Office's 2010 Pakistan rainfall study, the 2012–13 Australian "Millennial Drought" attribution); the 2012–13 Allen et al. linear-TCRE papers that made "remaining emissions" a policy quantity; and the 2015 Paris Agreement's Article 8 and the WWA's founding. **Wave three (2016– ): courts and the compound frontier.** The carbon-majors inventory's legal life (Heede 2013, the 2015 Exxons and 2017–21 US municipal suits' dismissal-then-appeal saga, the 2019 Feature et al. source-attribution paper), the Urgenda judgment (2019) and the Dutch government's compliance, the Neubauer v RWE evidentiary admission (2017) and its 2022 appellate reversal on causation, the Milieudefensie v Shell order (2021, on appeal 2024–25), the 2021 "unequivocal" verdict, the 2022 Pakistan and 2023 Libya compound-event studies, and the 2023–25 ICJ advisory-opinion hearings — a field that began with a hand-calculated CO₂ doubling and ended the decade with 90 states briefing a world court on the meaning of a scaling factor.
:::

::: quiz
In optimal fingerprinting, the "scaling factor" of a forcing's response pattern is:
- [ ] The model's climate sensitivity
- [x] The regression amplitude by which the modelled response must be multiplied to match observations
- [ ] The ratio of solar to greenhouse forcing
- [ ] The fraction of emissions that stay airborne
::: solution
**The regression amplitude.** A scaling factor of 1.0 ± 0.2 for the greenhouse fingerprint means the observed change contains that pattern at modelled strength; a factor indistinguishable from 0 for the solar fingerprint means solar contributes nothing detectable. Detection is the statement that the factor differs from 0; attribution is the pattern of factors across forcings — and the whole method is one multivariate regression with the models' noise covariance built in.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="50"}
A WWA-style study finds the risk ratio (modern/preindustrial) of a heatwave is 2.0. What is the fraction of attributable risk (FAR), as a percentage?
::: solution
FAR = 1 − 1/RR = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5 → 50 %: half the risk of the event in today's world is attributable to the change in world — the arithmetic behind every "twice as likely" headline, and the reason the headline is about *risk*, not about the event's existence.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="45"}
Of industrial-era CO₂ and methane equivalents, Heede's inventory attributes 63 % to 90 "carbon major" companies. If cumulative fossil-emissions to 2010 were 977 GtCO₂-e, how much (GtCO₂-e, to the nearest 10) falls on those 90 entities?
::: solution
0.63 × 977 = 615.5 → ~620 GtCO₂-e — the number that turned "emissions" from a diffuse aggregate into a list of defendants, and the reason the inventory's bookkeeping rules (what counts as a "source," how cement and flaring are treated) are themselves litigated.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.18"}
The transient climate response per cumulative carbon (TCRE) is ~0.45 °C per 1,000 GtCO₂ (AR6: 0.27–0.63 likely). How much warming does 400 GtCO₂ of future emissions add, at the central estimate?
::: solution
0.45 × 0.4 = 0.18 °C — the linear-TCRE arithmetic that converts every emissions pathway into a temperature and every temperature target into a budget, and whose known caveats (the linearity holds for cumulative totals, not for timing; the per-tonne warming continues after emissions stop only in the sense of committed adjustment) are the subject of the carbon-budget lesson.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="6"}
A fingerprint regression gives a greenhouse scaling factor of 1.2 with an uncertainty of ±0.2 (1σ). Is the greenhouse signal detected at the 5σ level? Compute the detection z-score, and state what a scaling factor of 1.2 (rather than 1.0) implies about either the models' sensitivity or the forcing.
::: solution
z = 1.2/0.2 = 6 — detected far beyond the 5σ particle-physics standard; a factor >1 means either the real response to the modelled forcing is stronger than the models' average (higher sensitivity) or the true forcing is stronger than assumed — the same ambiguity that makes the scaling-factor literature a source of ECS constraints when combined with the energy-budget method.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="5000"}
In the 2003 heatwave attribution, the risk of the event was found to be at least doubled (RR ≥ 2). If the preindustrial probability of such a summer was 1/10,000 per year, what is the modern probability expressed as a 1-in-N return period (nearest hundred), and why does the "1-in-10,000-year event happened anyway" argument fail?
::: solution
RR = 2 → modern probability 2/10,000 = 1/5,000 — the 1-in-5,000-year event; the argument fails because a 1-in-10,000 event is *expected* about once per 10,000 years (and the record is 150 years of a *changing* distribution), and because doubling the probability of the extreme is the finding — the return-period language is the same statistic as FAR, just legible to planners.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.02"}
AR6 attributes 1.07 °C of the observed 1.09 °C of 2010–19 warming to humans. What is the residual attributed to natural forcing plus internal variability, and why is the residual's smallness itself an attribution result?
::: solution
1.09 − 1.07 = 0.02 °C — indistinguishable from zero: the fact that the *human-only* term explains the observed warming to within the measurement and internal-variability uncertainty means no natural term is *needed* in the budget, the "no missing sink" logic of the historical-warming decomposition (the aerosol term's compensation being the main uncertainty in how the 1.07 is split between greenhouse and other anthropogenic components).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.17"}
Pinatubo's eruption injected ~20 Mt SO₂ and cooled the globe ~0.45 °C for ~14 months. Anthropogenic aerosol forcing (~−0.5 W/m² sustained) is roughly equivalent to a sustained volcanic forcing of ~1.5 Mt SO₂/yr in radiative terms. As a rough ratio, how many Pinatubo-equivalents of *sustained* cooling does the anthropogenic aerosol veil represent (use radiative equivalence: Pinatubo ~3 W/m² for ~1 yr)?
::: solution
Pinatubo's forcing ~3 W/m² for ~1 year; anthropogenic aerosol −0.5 W/m² sustained — in *instantaneous* radiative terms the veil is ~1/6 of a Pinatubo running forever; in the ratio the question asks (Pinatubo-equivalents per year of sustained aerosol forcing), 0.5/3 ≈ 0.17 per year — one Pinatubo's worth of forcing every ~6 years, continuously — the reason the 2020 shipping switch's un-masking (~0.1 W/m² step) is a nudge, not a driver, and why the clean-air trade-off is real but second-order against the CO₂ term.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.6"}
A city's 100-year flood is modelled to become a 1-in-40-year flood under +1.5 °C. Compute the risk ratio and the FAR, and state the FAR in words the way a WWA statement would.
::: solution
RR = (1/40)/(1/100) = 2.5; FAR = 1 − 1/2.5 = 0.6 — "human influence has increased the risk of such flooding by a factor of 2.5; 60 % of the current risk is attributable to climate change" — the sentence structure that the 2023–24 Mediterranean flood statements use, with the index-sensitivity caveat attached.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="30"}
The Urgenda court ordered the Netherlands to cut emissions 25 % below 1990 levels by 2020, using the IPCC's carbon-budget equity ranges as its quantitative basis. If Dutch 1990 emissions were 213 MtCO₂ and 2020 emissions (pre-order trend) would have been 190 MtCO₂, by how many MtCO₂ did the order force a cut below the trend line?
::: solution
Target = 0.75 × 213 = 160 MtCO₂; trend 190; forced cut = 30 MtCO₂ — the arithmetic of how a *proportionality* principle (the IPCC's equity ranges) becomes a *number* a court can order, and why the judgment's reliance on the carbon-budget literature (rather than on event attribution) is the template most subsequent climate cases have copied.
:::

::: quiz
The isotopic evidence (declining ¹³C/¹²C and ¹⁴C in atmospheric CO₂) specifically rules out which alternative explanation of rising CO₂?
- [ ] That emissions exceed sinks
- [x] That the excess CO₂ comes from the ocean or volcanoes
- [ ] That methane oxidation contributes CO₂
- [ ] That land-use change contributes CO₂
::: solution
**Ocean and volcanic carbon carry different isotope ratios (and the gross fluxes are net-absorbed, not net-added); fossil carbon is ¹³C-depleted and ¹⁴C-dead, and the air's ratios are moving exactly that way.** The same isotope bookkeeping attributes the *land-use* share (the ¹³C signal distinguishes it from fossil), making the sources' accounting a measurement, not an estimate — the quietest and most decisive line of evidence in the whole attribution case.
:::

::: summary
- Detection says the change is not noise; attribution apportions it: optimal fingerprinting regresses observations on modelled forcing patterns, and the scaling factors — greenhouse ~1, aerosol ~1, solar ~0 — are the quantitative content of the "unequivocal" verdict (1.07 °C of the observed 1.09 °C is human).
- The case is a convergence: laboratory spectroscopy, the isotopic fingerprint of fossil carbon, counterfactual models that cannot make post-1970 warming without humans, the closing energy budget, and the Pliocene analogue — each line excluding a different alternative.
- Single-event attribution (the Allen 2004 two-world design, FAR and risk ratios) made the science legible per disaster; its honest limits — index sensitivity, model skill, compound chains, the adaptation confound — are now the field's own published protocol, and its output is a risk statistic, never an individual-causation verdict.
- Source attribution (Heede's 90 companies = 63 % of industrial-era emissions) and the legal turn (Urgenda's budget-based order, Neubauer's admitted attribution evidence, Shell, the ICJ proceedings) have moved the general causation question from science to jurisprudence — where it is no longer losing.
- The overstated sentence is the field's main public risk: "this storm was caused by climate change" is what the method does *not* say, and the difference between the risk-ratio truth and the causal headline is the last mile of attribution science — the part that happens in newsrooms and courtrooms, not in the regression.
:::
