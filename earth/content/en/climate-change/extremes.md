## The tail of the distribution: statistics, physics and the heat record

The mean is a headline; the tail is the damage. Human health, power grids, rivers, crops and insurance losses respond to the *edges* of the climate distribution — the hottest afternoons, the wettest days, the longest dry spells — and those edges move differently from the middle. A small shift of a distribution's mean can multiply the probability of its far tail many-fold, because tail probabilities are exponential functions of the distance from the mean: a 1 °C warmer summer is a modest fact about the mean and a revolutionary one about the 1-in-50-year week. This lesson is organised around four confidence tiers: the heat-extreme record is strong and human-attributed with high confidence; the heavy-precipitation record is strong but its attribution thinner; the drought record genuinely mixed; and the tropical-cyclone record the most contested, because its long-term data are the shortest and least homogeneous. Keeping those tiers straight is the difference between understanding extremes and collecting them.

::: definition What counts as an extreme: tails, indices and return periods {#def-tails}
An extreme is the tail of a *specific variable, place and season*, never an absolute: 35 °C is record-shattering in Helsinki and unremarkable in Kuwait, and the statistics are built locally. The operational vocabulary is the ETCCDI index set — 27 indices developed under the World Meteorological Organization's expert panels and computed identically in every country's climate service — which summarises the tails rather than the means: TXx (annual hottest day), TNx (warmest night), TX90p (frequency of hot days, the fraction of days above the 90th percentile of the local baseline), CDD (longest dry run), RX5day (wettest 5-day total), R95p (precipitation fraction from the wettest 5 % of days). The indices solved a problem — every country had defined "hot" differently — and created another: percentile thresholds are computed on a *fixed baseline period*, so under warming TX90p rises even when the tail's *shape* is unchanged — "extreme" indices quietly encode the trend they detect (the relative-versus-absolute-threshold debate, resolved by reporting both). Extreme-value theory is the second framework: annual maxima fit a generalised extreme-value distribution whose parameters set how fast the tail falls. From it comes the **return period**: the event exceeded on average once every $T$ years has annual exceedance probability $1/T$, so a "100-year flood" has a 1 % yearly chance and roughly a 63 % chance of appearing at least once in a century — the misreading that costs the most here. The decisive statistical fact, developed in the climate-statistics chapter ([[climate-system/climate-statistics]]) and used in every attribution study since, is the **shifted-distribution logic**: if the whole summer temperature distribution moves right by $\Delta\mu$ with unchanged spread, the probability of exceeding a fixed far-tail threshold grows by a factor of roughly $\exp(z\Delta\mu/\sigma - \Delta\mu^2/2\sigma^2)$ where $z$ is the threshold's original standard-score — for a 1-in-50 summer week ($z \approx 2.3$) and a spread of 3 °C, a 1.5 °C shift multiplies its probability about four-fold. Whether real distributions also *widen* matters less than the assessments concluded: Hansen and Sato's 2012 analysis of the 2010 Russian and 2011 Texas events, and the AR5's verdict, both found distribution *shifts* dominate tail changes for temperature over land — the reason heat-extreme projections are more confident than drought ones.

:::

::: definition Observed heat extremes: what the surface record shows {#def-heat-record}
The assessed picture (AR5 WGII Chapter 2 and the AR1 Special Report on Extremes; AR6 WGII Chapter 11) in one sentence: hot extremes have increased in most regions since the 1950s (high confidence), cold extremes have decreased (virtually certain), and both changes are human-caused (high confidence for temperature extremes at the global scale). The details carry the lesson. Over land, hot daytime extremes have warmed somewhat *faster* than the mean — the AR6 assessed high confidence that hot extremes over most land areas intensified about 1.2–1.5× the global-mean rate — while the *coldest* nights have warmed fastest of all, the famous day–night asymmetry (minimum temperatures warming faster than maxima, itself a greenhouse fingerprint because clouds and humidity suppress nighttime cooling, covered under the attribution lesson [[climate-change/attribution]]). Heatwaves — consecutive hot days relative to a local baseline — have become more frequent, longer and more intense over most land areas, with the strongest documented increases in Europe, eastern North America, East Asia and Australia; the Fischer and colleagues 2021 "2040 heatwave" study found the 2003-type heat that struck roughly once per half-century pre-industrial becomes about 5 times more frequent at +2 °C and about 14 times at +4 °C. Three caveats discipline the record. Cold extremes have *decreased* everywhere, but the "vanishing polar vortex" controversy was resolved by the assessments: severe cold events still occur, their frequency declines with warming, and their attribution finds natural variability dominant — the US record high-to-low ratio near 2:1 since the 1990s is the statistically literate way to read any "they still have winters" argument. Urbanisation confounds individual stations, but the homogenisation audits — Hansen and Wu's 2012 GHCN audit and the Karl and colleagues' 2010 READERs re-siting experiment — found the urban contamination of the global land trend is negligible (well under 0.01 °C/decade), because the warming signal is broad and the heat island narrow. And the Arctic, warming two to four times faster than the globe, has lost cold extremes fastest while its *hot* extremes remain modest in absolute terms — the 2020 Siberian 38 °C at Verkhoyansk preceded the 2021 heat dome by a year and shared its blocking driver.

:::

::: definition Heatwave physics: blocking, soil moisture and the wet-bulb limit {#def-heat-physics}
Three mechanisms stack to make a heatwave, and their interaction is why extremes outpace means. **Dynamics first**: persistent anticyclonic "blocking" — quasi-stationary high-pressure systems with subsiding, compressing, cloud-free air — sets the stage; Pfahl and Wernli's 2012 finding that European heatwaves were some six times more likely under blocked patterns than composite summers quantifies the trigger, and whether warming makes blocking more frequent remains unresolved (the assessments' verdict: low confidence in any blocking-frequency trend). **Land feedbacks amplify**: once soils dry, the surface energy balance flips — with moisture available, most incoming energy evaporates water (latent cooling); when the soil's top metres empty, the same energy heats air instead, and the heatwave feeds itself. Dugma and colleagues' 2011 analysis showed the 2010 Russian heatwave's soil moisture in April — before the heat — was already at a centuries-scale deficit, and the "soil moisture memory" mechanism — a dry spring pre-loading a hot summer — now underpins operational seasonal heat forecasts; the same mechanism explains why the hottest days sit downwind of dry-anomalous soil (Seneviratne and colleagues' 2010 synthesis). **Advection finishes the job**: anomalous flow from warm source regions (Saharan air into Europe, desert air into the Great Plains) adds several degrees in a day. The **urban heat island** keeps cities warm at night — the lethal detail, since most heat mortality occurs when the body cannot recover overnight: the 2021 Vancouver toll fell overwhelmingly on indoor deaths during a three-day night-time floor above 19 °C in a city with almost no domestic cooling. Finally the biological bound: human thermoregulation works by evaporating sweat, so the relevant extreme is **wet-bulb temperature** — the temperature a wet surface reaches under ventilation — and Sherwood and Huber's 2010 PNAS analysis argued sustained values much beyond 35 °C (at skin temperature and full humidity, evaporation stops) exceed our species' physiological range even for the young at rest in shade. Raymond and colleagues' 2020 survey found wet-bulb values above 31 °C have roughly doubled in frequency since 1979, with brief reported excursions to 35 °C in the Persian Gulf and the Indus valley — though reanalysis artefacts (the 2023 reassessments) mean the survivability threshold has *not* credibly been crossed at duration, only approached. The projection that a large fraction of the tropics' outdoor labour season becomes taxing at +2 °C makes heat the most economically consequential extreme for the global South.

:::

::: example The 2003 European heatwave: the birth of extreme-event attribution {#example-2003}
The 2003 summer broke every continental record then held: France's August maximum exceeded 44 °C, and the excess mortality reached about 70,000 deaths across nine countries (Robine and colleagues' 2007 *Lancet* audit — France alone about 15,000, most of them people over 75 in cities). Two years later Stott and colleagues published the paper that created this lesson's sub-field: using the then-new multi-model ensembles of the Detection and Attribution Model Intercomparison Project, they compared the observed distribution of French summer maximum temperatures against thousands of years of modelled pre-industrial climate and found that the 2003-class temperature extreme was *at least twice as likely* in the world with anthropogenic greenhouse gases as in the world without them — a statement conditional on both the model and the model-years over which the probability is computed, and deliberately conservative because the models' simulated heatwave variability probably exceeded the real atmosphere's. The study's method — compare the observed event's probability under factual and counterfactual climates, report the ratio — became the template for every attribution statement since. Croisière and colleagues' 2019 reinterpretation sharpened the physics: when the heatwave is defined by *temperature alone*, the 2003 risk ratio climbs to about 3.4, but when defined by *soil moisture* (the land-feedback pathway), the anthropogenic signal is weaker and the event's rarity was partly natural spring drying — the same event, two defensible numbers, because "the 2003 heatwave" is not a single physical quantity.
::: solution
The 2003 case is this lesson's Rosetta stone: it established (i) the method (factual-versus-counterfactual probability ratios from model ensembles), (ii) the confidence language (the calibrated "at least doubled," designed so the claim survives the model uncertainty), and (iii) the definitional caution (a heatwave is a chosen index, and different indices — air temperature, soil moisture, apparent temperature — attribute differently because they have different causal pathways). The excess-death accounting it spawned is why heat is assessed as the deadliest weather-related hazard in Europe and North America, and why the 2003 event triggered the continent's heat-action plans — the 2015 and 2019 European heatwaves, of comparable or greater intensity, killed a fraction as many per degree, the clearest evidence in this lesson that vulnerability is a variable, not a constant.
:::
:::

::: example The 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome: the "impossible" number {#example-heat-dome}
In late June 2021 a quasi-stationary anticyclonic "heat dome" parked over the Pacific Northwest and British Columbia, sending Portland to 46.7 °C and the town of Lytton, 250 km up the Fraser canyon, to 49.6 °C — 4.6 °C above the previous Canadian record, a magnitude of record-breaking that regional climate models, run at the resolution used for impact planning, found essentially unattainable in their historical climate. The human toll was unprecedented for a Canadian peacetime disaster: the province's chief coroner recorded 619 heat-related deaths in five days, mostly indoor deaths of older adults, and the wildfire that burned much of Lytton two days after the record ignited during the record's own heat. The scientific response defined the 2020s attribution debate. The World Weather Attribution group's rapid analysis concluded the event had been "virtually impossible without climate change" — a phrase built from the fact that no member of the natural-only model ensembles produced anything comparable — while the peer-reviewed treatment (Philip and colleagues' 2022 *SWEET* study and Eisele and colleagues' tree-ring reconstruction) put the amplitude in context: the heat dome was the most extreme June temperature anomaly in at least 50,000 years of the combined reconstruction, and its probability had increased at least 150-fold with anthropogenic warming. The "virtually impossible" wording drew a methodological critique the field now teaches openly: model ensembles cannot report an event impossible in the *real* world as impossible in the *model* world, and the 2023 reassessments (including the modelling studies of the event's soil-moisture preconditioning) settled on the defensible statement — an event of this magnitude was of vanishing likelihood in the pre-industrial climate and is now roughly 200 times more likely, with the 1-in-500-year phrasing the most-cited single number.
::: solution
The heat dome's lesson is about probability language under model limitation. "Impossible" in an ensemble means "outside the sampled model distribution," which is a statement about the models' support, not the atmosphere's — the same reason no GCM simulates a Category 6 hurricane yet none is impossible. The defensible instruments are (i) the physics: the event's thermodynamic requirements (the static stability, the soil-moisture preconditioning, the concurrent "Blob 2.0" marine heatwave) are all in the direction warming pushes; (ii) the reconstructions: palaeoclimate context (50,000 years of tree rings) bounds natural variability in a way no model run does; and (iii) the scaling: attribution of the *magnitude* (+5–6 °C above the modern norm) rather than the *event* (a June heatwave is not impossible naturally). The 2021 sequence — marine heatwave, heat dome, fire, then the December atmospheric river that flooded the burned watersheds — is the course's cleanest **cascading** event, and the compound-event literature (Zscheischler and colleagues' 2022 review) exists because single-hazard attribution understates such chains.
:::
:::

## Water at the edges: rain, drought and cyclones

If heat extremes follow the shifted-distribution logic, water extremes must pass a second gate: moisture physics — the thermodynamic ceiling on rainfall, the soil-moisture memory behind drought, and the ocean-energy engine of tropical cyclones each add a constraint statistics alone cannot supply, each with its own confidence tier.

::: definition Heavy precipitation: the Clausius–Clapeyron ceiling and the observed record {#def-heavy-rain}
The atmosphere's moisture-holding capacity sets the physical envelope for rain extremes: saturation vapour pressure rises about 6.5–7 % per degree of warming near surface temperatures (the Clausius–Clapeyron relation, derived in the moisture lesson [[meteorology/moisture]]), and radiosonde and GPS-met observations confirm column water vapour and its extremes are rising near that rate. The observed record matches the ceiling where measurable: Westra and colleagues' 2013 *BAMS* synthesis found sub-daily heavy-rain extremes intensifying near 7 %/°C, and Myhre and colleagues' 2021 global update added the structure that matters for floods — intensification per degree is largest for short convective events (near the full CC rate) and smallest for monthly totals (near 3 %/°C), because storm *frequency* need not increase even as storm *intensity* does. The national records are unambiguous where the data are long: the US National Climate Assessment documented a 40 %+ increase in the heaviest 1 % of precipitation events in the Northeast and Midwest since 1958, and the AR6 assessed it *likely* that heavy precipitation increased over much of the land since the 1950s, with attribution finding human influence contributed to several record-rainfall events (the 2021 Ahr-valley flood assessed 3–19 % more intense; the 2022 Pakistan monsoon deluge — one quarter of the country underwater — with the Link and colleagues 2023 analysis attributing up to half the July rainfall intensification to warming). Three cautions. The thermodynamic term is robust; the *dynamic* term (storms' tracks, frequency, organisation) is where models disagree, and the attribution verdict is high confidence for *some* regions, medium at best for others. Convective wildcards (wind shear, cold pools, storm-organised convergence bands) mean the biggest rainfalls — 2011 Thailand, 2021 Zhengzhou, 2022 eastern Australia — are as much synoptic accidents as thermodynamic certainties. And the flood *impact* record is a different story from the rainfall record: global river-flood damages have risen almost entirely because of exposure, while gauged flood *discharges* show no global increasing trend — the "rainier but not floodier" paradox resolved by infiltration losses, reservoirs and hydrological filtering ([[hydrology/floods-droughts]]).
:::

::: definition Drought: four definitions, one contested trend {#def-drought}
Drought is the least tractable of the extremes because it has no single definition: **meteorological** drought (precipitation deficit over a season), **agricultural** drought (soil-moisture deficit stressing crops), **hydrological** drought (streamflow and groundwater shortfall) and **socioeconomic** drought (supply failing demand) can occur without one another, and a wet year can follow a hydrological drought that lasts a decade because aquifers refill slowly ([[hydrology/groundwater-flow]]). The indices multiply accordingly (SPI, SPEI, the US Drought Monitor), and the choice of index determines the answer to "is drought increasing?" — the honest core of the assessment verdict. AR6's position: no globally coherent increase in precipitation-based drought is detected (the global record is dominated by natural variability — ENSO, the Pacific and Atlantic multidecadal modes, covered in [[climate-system/variability]]), *but* evaporation-driven soil-moisture drought has increased in the Mediterranean, western North America, southwestern South America and southern Africa, and human influence has been *detected and attributed* in several specific cases: the 2020 *Nature Climate Change* analysis by Cook and colleagues found the 2000–2021 "megadrought" soil-moisture deficit in the upper Colorado basin was the driest in 1,200 years of reconstruction and roughly half-cause anthropogenic — it was both drought and warming, roughly half each; the 2015–16 California drought's extreme heat was found (Diffenbaugh and colleagues 2015) to have been made many times more likely by warming even as its rainfall deficit lay within natural range; and Cape Town's 2017–18 "Day Zero" was assessed as about three times more likely from warming's temperature term while its rainfall shortfall was ordinary — the "hot drought" pattern, a permanent evaporation tax no precipitation trend has to confirm. The counterweights belong in the record too: the Sahel's 1970s–80s catastrophe (100,000+ famine deaths) has partially *reversed* since 2000; the 2020–23 Horn of Africa failure of five rainy seasons sits at the edge of detectability (the Indian Ocean dipole drives it and models disagree on its future); and Australian attribution found the Millennium Drought's southwest drying robustly human-caused and its southeast counterpart natural. The projection follows the physics: soil-moisture droughts intensify with warming almost everywhere, precipitation droughts increase in the subtropics and Mediterranean ("likely" in AR6) and decrease in the high latitudes — the two-drought-future that is the century's water-geography headline.
:::

::: example Hurricane Harvey: attributing a rainfall record {#example-harvey}
Harvey made landfall on the Texas coast on 25 August 2017 as a Category 4, then did the thing that kills more Americans than wind: it stalled. For four days the rainbands rotated over the same coastal plain while a weak steering flow held the centre within 50 km of where it came ashore, dropping 1,539 mm at a monitored stream gauge near Houston — the greatest rainfall total ever recorded for a single continental US tropical cyclone. The attribution analyses that followed (the 2018 studies by Wang and colleagues and by de Bono and colleagues, using model ensembles with and without anthropogenic forcing) put the storm's rainfall increase at roughly 15–40 %, with the central estimate about a third — the thermodynamic term dominating (storm-adjacent moisture rose near 10 %/°C, slightly super-CC on the Gulf's 0.75 °C warm anomaly), with a smaller contribution from the storm's slower motion. Two complications the field now teaches with the example. The urban signal rivalled the greenhouse signal locally: Gifford and colleagues' 2020 analysis found Houston's own expanded urban surface contributed perhaps half again as much rain *over the urban area* as the anthropogenic climate signal did, a warning that "climate change made Harvey's rain" and "Houston's growth made Harvey's rain" are both partly true. And the return-period arithmetic exposed the label's fragility: the same storm was simultaneously a "1-in-500-year" rainfall event by the coastal NOAA atlas, a "1-in-100-year" event by the updated Atlas-14 point frequencies, and a twice-in-three-years event for the specific stalled-analogue pattern — the lesson that return periods are properties of *datasets and definitions* as much as of the atmosphere (the same non-uniqueness the flood-frequency lesson [[natural-hazards/floods]] dissects for river gauges).
::: solution
Harvey is the reference case for the *conditional* structure of extreme-event attribution: the thermodynamic component (rainfall per unit storm) is robustly increased and high-confidence; the dynamic component (why this storm stalled here) is where attribution weakens, because blocking and steering patterns have no established anthropogenic trend (the same low-confidence verdict as heatwave blocking). The storm also anchors the "attributable *damage*" question: the 2017 Atlantic season's $300 billion-plus total (Harvey, Irma, Maria) is dominated by exposure growth in coastal Texas and Florida, not by any detected change in cyclone climatology — the distinction between "extreme made worse" and "loss made bigger" that the adaptation-finance debate depends on.
:::
:::

::: example The 2010 Russian heatwave: the "rareness" fight {#example-russia-2010}
The summer of 2010 is the textbook case of how *not* to argue about extremes. Moscow's July ran about 7 °C above its daily means, the wheat belt failed its harvest, peat fires blackened the capital, and Russian statistics recorded roughly 56,000 excess deaths across July and August — the deadliest single extreme of the 2010s in Europe. When the event was attributed, the science split in public. Barston and colleagues' 2012 *Nature Climate Change* paper introduced an "unusualness" index combining amplitude and spatial extent and concluded the 2010 anomaly was not more unusual than other natural warm anomalies of similar size elsewhere — a defensible statistical point that the press rendered as "scientists: Russian heatwave was natural," while the soil-moisture analysis (Dugma and colleagues 2011: the April soil-moisture deficit was unprecedented in the millennium-scale reconstructions) and the later attribution studies (finding the *heat* itself strongly human-caused) pointed the other way. The resolution, worked out in the exchange between Räisänen and colleagues and the original authors, is a lesson in metrics: "unusualness" averaged amplitude against areal extent, and the 2010 event was enormous but not amplitude-record-breaking, so the composite called it ordinary while every single-variable statistic called it extreme — and single-variable attribution found the greenhouse contribution to the July anomaly itself to be large. The event's other enduring data point is the *atmospheric* one: the 2010 blocking was concurrent with the strongest La Niña of the decade and a stratospheric state favouring Ural ridging, so the honest statement is multi-cause: natural variability loaded the gun (the blocking, the La Niña), warming pulled the trigger (the amplitude, the soil drying), and vulnerability decided the toll (the post-Soviet collapse of public health capacity, a policy choice made decades earlier).
::: solution
The 2010 fight settled two questions the extremes field now treats as closed. First, "was it natural or anthropogenic?" is the wrong grammar: every event is caused by everything at once, and the only answerable question is the counterfactual one — would an event of this size have been this likely without the anthropogenic perturbation? — which is why the attribution method's output is a probability ratio, never a verdict of sole cause. Second, "unprecedented" claims are metric-dependent and must name their metric: the 2010 event was not the largest areal anomaly in the record, was the largest soil-moisture anomaly in a millennium, and was the deadliest extreme of the decade — three true statements that support three different headlines. The case is also the origin of the "risk of non-stationarity" warning in the climate-statistics chapter [[climate-system/climate-statistics]]: if the distribution is moving, every record-based frequency statement (every "100-year" label) is a description of a climate that no longer exists — the reason engineering codes are moving to conditional-on-future-scenario design values.
:::

::: warning Five ways extreme-event claims get distorted — by sceptics, by headlines, and by the science's own success
1. **The records fallacy.** "More hot records prove warming" and "fewer hot records disprove it" are both wrong: records are the *maxima* of finite samples and their statistics are dominated by coverage and station growth (the US record-station count peaked in the 1930s). The statistically literate signals are the high-to-low record *ratio* (skewed about 2:1 toward highs across the contiguous US since the 1990s, near parity in the early 1900s), the *disappearance* of cold records, and the percentile indices — not the raw count.
2. **The "impossible" overclaim.** Rapid attribution groups' press language ("virtually impossible without climate change") means "no natural-only model member produced it," which is a statement about model support, not the atmosphere. The defensible form is a probability ratio with the model ensemble named — and the 2023 methodological reassessments of the "impossible" genre are the field correcting itself in public, which is the process working, not failing.
3. **The drought conflation.** Heat attribution is high-confidence; drought attribution is not, because "drought" is index-dependent and precipitation variability is huge. Transferring heat-attribution confidence to drought claims (in either direction) misreads the confidence tiers this lesson is organised around.
4. **The event-versus-climate swap.** A single heatwave, flood or cyclone is never "evidence" in the falsificationist sense; the evidence is the distributional shift, the attribution ratios accumulating across events, and the physical consistency. The corollary cuts both ways: one cool summer or landfall-free Atlantic season is no more evidence against warming than one hot summer is for it — basin-scale cyclone activity is internally noisy at decadal scales.
5. **The attribution–vulnerability collapse.** "Climate change caused 70,000 deaths in 2003" and "619 in 2021 British Columbia" are not comparable statements: the 2021 event was physically more extreme per degree, and the toll difference is mostly *vulnerability* — age structure, air conditioning, warning systems, housing stock. Attribution quantifies the hazard shift; the mortality is hazard times exposure times fragility, and conflating them hides the adaptation lesson (the 2003-to-2015 European mortality improvement per degree is the largest single success story in this lesson).
:::

::: history From the Dust Bowl files to the courtroom: extreme-event science's long century {#history-extremes}
The scientific study of extremes long predates the attribution era. The US Weather Bureau's 1890s–1930s station network made the first continental extreme records (and the Dust Bowl the first "was it us?" debate — the "rain follows the plow" optimists versus the conservationists, settled by the 1935 Soil Conservation Act and reopened by the 2010s land-use reanalyses). Extreme-value statistics itself is a century old — Fisher and Tippett's 1928 asymptotes, Gumbel's 1958 *Statistics of Extremes* written for flood engineers, the 1960s–70s stationarity assumptions still embedded in every design storm — and the percentile-index tradition reached the mainstream with Karl and Knight's 1998 finding that US *minimum* temperatures had risen faster than maxima. The modern field begins with three 2003–2005 papers: Stott and colleagues' 2004 *Nature* attribution of the European heatwave (the "at least doubled" paper), the 2004 Meehl and colleagues PCCP "hot spots" framing, and the 2005 Santer and colleagues *Nature* fingerprint that made model-observation pattern comparison routine; the 2011 National Academy of Sciences report and the 2012 *Bulletin* special issue "Explaining Extreme Events in a Warming Climate" (the "E3" annual collections, still running) turned attribution into an industry with rapid-response protocols. The 2010s institutionalised it: the 2014–15 US National Academies consensus report endorsing probabilistic event attribution, the 2015 founding of World Weather Attribution (Friederike Otto's open-science rapid group), the Copernicus Climate Change Service's operational attribution bulletins, and the 2021–23 maturation into the *SWEET* series and the compound-events synthesis (the Zscheischler, Ridder and colleagues' 2021–22 multivariate-event frameworks). The courtroom entered in the same decade: Rigo and colleagues' 2015 attribution of the 2013–14 California drought's heat component, the 2021 Neubauer and colleagues critique of double claiming in emissions-budget law, cited in the debate around the German Constitutional Court's 2021 climate decision, and the 2023 Luciano and colleagues *Nature* attribution of a Scottish fire loss plus the 2023–25 US municipal suits that cite attribution evidence — the first time an extreme-event probability ratio carried legal weight, and the reason the "definitional caution" paragraphs of this lesson are now read by lawyers.
:::

::: quiz
A region's summer temperature distribution shifts +1.5 °C with unchanged spread. The probability of its former 1-in-50 warm week:
- [ ] Increases about 1.5-fold, in proportion to the shift
- [x] Increases several-fold, because tail probabilities respond exponentially to mean shifts
- [ ] Stays the same — only the mean changed
- [ ] Cannot change, because the 1-in-50 threshold is by definition the 50-year value
::: solution
**Several-fold**: tail probabilities are exponential in the threshold distance measured in standard deviations, so a shift small enough to be invisible in the mean multiplies the far tail — for a 1-in-50 threshold and a 3 °C spread, +1.5 °C roughly quadruples the probability. The "by definition" trap confuses the *fixed threshold* (a temperature) with the *fixed return period* (a probability): under warming the old 50-year temperature recurs far more often.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="14"}
By what percentage does the atmosphere's moisture-holding capacity increase for a 2 °C warming, using the Clausius–Clapeyron rate of 7 %/°C?
::: solution
2 × 7 % = 14 % — the thermodynamic floor under heavy-rain intensification: a storm of the same dynamical strength carries about 1 % more water per degree.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="200"}
A heat threshold is exceeded with an annual probability of 1/200. What is its return period, in years?
::: solution
Return period is the reciprocal of annual exceedance probability: 1/(1/200) = 200 years. The companion fact worth memorising: a 200-year event still has roughly a 22 % chance of occurring at least once during a 50-year design life (1 − (199/200)⁵⁰ ≈ 0.22) — the "design life versus return period" confusion that undercharges coastal projects.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="8"}
If the risk of a 2003-class heatwave doubles for every 1 °C of global warming, by what factor is it more likely in a +3 °C world?
::: solution
Doubling per degree compounds: 2³ = 8. The exponential-in-warming structure (not linear) is why the 1.5-versus-2 °C difference is a *frequency* difference of about 2-fold for heatwaves even though the temperature difference is a third of a degree — the arithmetic behind every "fractional degrees matter" assessment graphic.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.9"}
Pre-industrial probability of a heatwave p₀ = 1/1000 per year; current probability p₁ = 1/100 per year. Compute the fraction of attributable risk, FAR = (p₁ − p₀)/p₁.
::: solution
FAR = (0.01 − 0.001)/0.01 = 0.009/0.01 = 0.9 — 90 % of today's risk of this heatwave is attributable to the anthropogenic shift, the statement "this heatwave would be 10 times less likely without climate change" in its precise form (a statement about risks, not about the single event's existence).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="3.3"}
Attribution finds a former 100-year heat now recurs on average every 30 years. By what factor has its frequency increased? Round to one decimal.
::: solution
Frequency ratio = 100/30 = 3.3. The same fact in probability language: annual exceedance rose from 1 % to 3.3 %, a FAR of 0.70 — and note the two framings ("3.3 times more frequent," "70 % of current risk attributable") describe one number, which is why attribution press releases must state which they use.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="132.6"}
A station's R95p (annual total from the wettest 5 % of days) is 120 mm in the baseline period. If it scales with the Clausius–Clapeyron rate of 7 %/°C, what is its value after 1.5 °C of warming, in mm?
::: solution
120 × (1 + 0.07 × 1.5) = 120 × 1.105 = 132.6 mm. The linear-in-CC scaling is the assumption, not the observation — percentile totals mix the intensity effect (near-CC) with possible frequency effects (unknown), which is why observed R95p trends scatter from 0 to 10 %/°C across the world's stations.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.9"}
For the heatwave of Exercise 4 (p₀ = 1/1000, p₁ = 1/100), compute the probability of necessity PN = (p₁ − p₀)/p₁ and interpret it.
::: solution
PN = (0.01 − 0.001)/0.01 = 0.9 — a 90 % probability that an event *of this risk class* occurring now would not have occurred (or would have been less severe) absent the forcing. The subtlety the field insists on: PN is a statement about the *population of events*, not the individual one — "90 % of the risk," never "90 % of this storm."
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.05"}
A compound event: the annual probability of a heatwave season is 0.10, and given a heatwave season the probability of concurrent flash drought is 0.50. What is the joint probability of the compound event in a year?
::: solution
Joint probability = 0.10 × 0.50 = 0.05 (a 1-in-20 compound event). The compound-event literature's warning: the product rule assumes the conditional is stationary, and warming changes *both* terms and their dependence — the copula-correlation problem (the 2021 Ridder and colleagues framework) — so compound-event projections carry wider uncertainty than either hazard alone.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="800"}
A city of 4 million has a heat-mortality rate of 2 deaths per 100,000 population per day during heatwave days. How many excess deaths does a 10-day heatwave produce?
::: solution
4,000,000/100,000 = 40 hundred-thousands; 40 × 2 × 10 = 800 excess deaths. The adaptation arithmetic: the 2003-to-2015 European record is a fall in this rate by roughly a factor of 3–5 per degree through warning systems, cooling centres and grid preparedness — the same multiplication run backwards, and the largest demonstrated return on adaptation spending in this lesson.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which extreme-event trend has the *weakest* observational evidence base?
- [ ] Hot temperature extremes over land
- [ ] Heavy daily precipitation over land
- [x] Long-term trends in tropical cyclone intensity
- [ ] Decline of cold extremes over land
::: solution
**Tropical cyclone intensity**: the satellite era is barely four decades, the pre-satellite "best track" is corrected for aircraft and ship coverage changes, and the 2024 intensity-trend papers (Kossin and colleagues' ~15 %/°C versus the disputed reconstructions) disagree on whether the trend exists at all — the tier-4 confidence this lesson's structure is built to expose, versus tier-1 for heat and cold extremes and tier-2 for heavy rain.
:::

::: summary
- Extremes are tail statistics: a small mean shift multiplies far-tail probabilities exponentially, so "the mean moved 1.2 °C" and "1-in-50 heat became 1-in-10" are the same fact stated twice — and return periods are properties of thresholds, definitions and datasets, never bare natural kinds.
- The confidence tiers are the lesson: heat extremes (strong increase, human-attributed, high confidence), heavy precipitation (strong increase near the 7 %/°C moisture ceiling, attribution growing), drought (index-dependent, no global precipitation trend, but soil-moisture "hot drought" increasingly attributed — the Colorado megadrough half-human-caused), and tropical cyclones (four decades of clean data, contested trends, robust *rainfall* and *sea-level-surge* physics).
- The physics explains the statistics: blocking sets heatwaves' stage and dry soils amplify them; Clausius–Clapeyron caps rain near 7 %/°C; cyclones draw ocean energy with ~10 %/°C more rainfall, and sea level lifts every storm surge.
- Attribution statements are conditional and definitional: probability ratios from model ensembles, sensitive to how the event is indexed (temperature versus soil moisture), to the metric of "unusual," and to the models' own support — the 2003, 2010 and 2021 cases are case studies in getting this right, and the "impossible" genre in getting it wrong.
- Losses are not extremes: flood and cyclone damages track exposure and vulnerability, not climatology, and the mortality record (2003's 70,000 versus later heatwaves' far smaller toll per degree) proves vulnerability is the fastest-moving variable — the bridge to [[natural-hazards/risk-reduction]].
:::
