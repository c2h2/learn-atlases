## What a tipping point is — and what it is not

In the popular telling, a tipping point is any threshold that matters: the moment the ice "starts" melting, the forest "starts" dying, the currents "stop." In dynamical systems language — the language the climate literature actually uses since the 2008 PNAS special issue on tipping elements — the term is far narrower and far more frightening: a tipping point is a critical threshold at which a *small* perturbation pushes a system across a boundary into a qualitatively different regime, and the crossing is **self-perpetuating** (internal feedbacks take over from the external forcing) and, on human timescales, **irreversible** (the original state does not return when the forcing is reduced, because the system sits in a different basin of attraction). The difference between the two usages is the difference between "worrying" and "unbounded worry," and this lesson's first job is keeping them apart. The second job is the honest accounting: which candidate systems have genuine bistability in the evidence (the AMOC, the ice sheets on millennial scales, coral reefs ecologically, the Amazon's eastern fringe partially), which have thresholds that are model artefacts of coarse physics, and what the assessments — AR6's "abrupt regional climate change very unlikely this century but not ruled out beyond" — actually claim. The third job is the 2020s reversal: the field that spent the 2010s dismissing the 2000s tipping-point literature as alarmism has, through better statistics (early-warning indicators), better observations (satellite altimetry over Greenland, the RAPID array, Amazon flux towers) and a startling 2022 synthesis claiming five elements may already be committed to crossing, moved the question from "if" to "how fast and how sure."

::: definition Tipping dynamics: bistability, hysteresis and early-warning signals {#def-tipping-dynamics}
The mathematics is one-dimensional at the pedagogical level and it explains everything. A system with a **double-well potential** (two stable equilibria separated by an unstable threshold) tips when pushed past the threshold, and the potential's asymmetry determines which well survives; a **saddle-node (fold) bifurcation** — the canonical climate tipping — occurs when a slow change in a control parameter (CO₂, freshwater input) makes the *current* stable state disappear entirely, forcing a jump to the remaining one, with no way back at that parameter value: this is **hysteresis**, the loop in which the collapse threshold and the recovery threshold differ, so a tipped system restored to its pre-tipping forcing does not return (the AMOC's freshwater hysteresis in models is tens of Sverdrups; the ice sheets' sea-level hysteresis is metres). Four signatures distinguish a true tipping element from a merely nonlinear one, and the assessments apply them like a checklist: (i) the forcing needed to tip is *small relative to the natural variability* the system has survived; (ii) the change *self-perpetuates* through internal feedbacks after the trigger (surface-albedo retreat, meltwater lubrication, vegetation dieback drying the local climate); (iii) the change is *persistent* — it does not reverse when the trigger is removed on the relevant timescale; and (iv) the system exhibits **bistability** in model or palaeo evidence (two distinct attractors, not a continuum). A fifth mode the 2010s added is **rate-induced tipping**: systems with no bifurcation at all can tip if the forcing is merely *fast* (the "tipping without bifurcation" theses of the Rataiczak and Wunderlich school, 2022–23) — the mechanism behind the "rate of warming matters more than its amount" literature and the reason the palaeocene–eocene and PETM rate comparisons do the work they do. The empirical programme is **early-warning signals**: near a fold bifurcation, recovery from small perturbations slows (**critical slowing down**), which statistically shows up as rising lag-1 autocorrelation and rising variance of the observable — indicators validated in lake eutrophication experiments, epileptic seizures and climate-model AMOC simulations (the Lenton and Ditlevsen schools' methodological core), and the instrument behind the 2020s AMOC alarms. The honest caveat travels with the method: rising autocorrelation also appears when the *noise* changes or when the trend is mis-specified, and no observational record yet contains a *verified* climate tipping prediction — the field's own admission, stated in every methods review.
:::

::: definition The AMOC: the ocean conveyor's stability, observed weakening, and collapse arithmetic {#def-amoc}
The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation — the wind-driven and density-driven loop in which North Atlantic surface waters cool, sink, and return south at depth, carrying ~17–20 Sv of volume and ~1.3 PW of heat northward — is the climate system's best-documented candidate for a fold bifurcation, for one physical reason and one historical one. The physical reason is the salt-advection feedback: the sinking that drives the circulation requires salty water, the circulation itself imports salt, and reducing the import freshens and stabilises the surface — a positive feedback Stommel formalised in his 1961 two-box model, which produces two stable states (on and off) and a hysteresis loop from a single freshwater forcing parameter. The historical reason is the palaeo record: the last deglaciation is punctuated by abrupt North Atlantic cold events (the Heinrich events and the Bølling–Allerød warming / Younger Dryas cold snap, in the Broecker and the Clark syntheses) whose fingerprint — North Atlantic cooling coincident with southern warming (the "bipolar seesaw") — is exactly what an overturning shutdown should produce, and the Early Holocene 8.2 ka event (the Laurentide lake outburst, ~1–1.6 °C Northern Hemisphere cooling in model replays) demonstrates the mechanism operating at the last opportunity. The modern observations split into three claims of decreasing confidence. **First (high confidence): the AMOC weakened over the twentieth century.** The 2023 Thornalley and colleagues fingerprint analysis (sea-surface-temperature pattern method, extending the record to 1915) estimated ~15 % weakening (17 ± 2 Sv) since mid-century, consistent with the "fingerprint" method of the 2014 Cain and colleagues and the Caesar and colleagues 2018 model-observation work; the direct RAPID array at 26.5°N (2004–present) shows large internal variability with *no* unambiguous trend over its two decades — the "the array is too short" reconciliation both groups accept. **Second (medium confidence): the models say the AMOC will continue to weaken this century but that a full collapse before 2100 is "very unlikely"** — AR6's assessed wording, with "abrupt onset very unlikely this century" for the related AMOC-collapse category and low confidence beyond 2100; the CMIP ensemble's hysteresis loops are real but its thresholds sit beyond the 2100 forcing of even SSP5-8.5 in most members. **Third (contested): the statistical early-warning literature.** Ditlevsen and Ditlevsen's 2023 *Nature Communications* analysis of the twentieth-century fingerprint record claimed the critical slowing-down signal implied collapse between ~2025 and ~2095 (central ~2057); the 2024 methodological critiques (sensitivity to record length, to the assumed linear trend, to the noise model — the Woodhouse and the Smith and colleagues commentaries; the 2024 Copenhagen reassessments that moved the window later when extended) left the alarm undated but the *mechanism* — that the observed weakening might be approaching a threshold the models place further out — live. The 2024 Lenton and colleagues global-synthesis and the 2025 Global Tipping Points report kept AMOC on the "already close" list at 1.1–1.8 °C of warming; AR6's "very unlikely this century" remains the assessment position, and the gap between the two is the sharpest open argument in scientific risk communication.
:::

::: example The Ditlevsen alarm: anatomy of a tipping-point prediction {#example-ditlevsen}
In July 2023 Peter and Michael Ditlevsen published what may become the most consequential statistical paper in climate science: modelling the two-century Atlantic "warming hole" fingerprint as a noisy relaxation oscillator, they found its fluctuations had become progressively more persistent since ~1950 — critical slowing down — and projected that the deterministic trend would reach the historical tipping threshold between 2025 and 2095 (95 % interval), with a central estimate around 2057. The headline "AMOC collapse could come as early as 2025" outran the paper's own caution, and the critique that followed is a masterclass in why tipping predictions are hard. The record is short: the fingerprint proxy is only ~160 years of instrumental data, and the early-warning statistic's behaviour on records this length, under a *misspecified* trend (the forcing is not linear, the aerosol dip of 1950–80 is real structure), was shown by the 2024 commentaries to be fragile — extending the record with reconstructions, changing the detrending, or adding the solar term each moved the window by decades. The model evidence cuts the other way: in the CMIP ensembles where AMOC does collapse under extreme forcing, the early-warning signals appear only centuries before collapse, not decades — if that physics transfers, the 2023 signal should not exist, which either means the models' bifurcation structure is wrong or the statistic is reading something else. And the observation programme (the RAPID/SINIC and OSNAP arrays, the 2025–2030 fingerprint network expansion) is precisely designed to settle it: a dense salinity-transport observing system can detect an approaching threshold through the *physics* (volume and freshwater transport) rather than the statistics.
::: solution
The case teaches the epistemology of low-probability, high-impact events. The paper's logic — early-warning signals plus trend extrapolation — is the only method available for an event that models cannot simulate credibly on century timescales and observations have never seen; its fragility — window spanning 70 years, mechanism-dependent assumptions — is equally intrinsic. The rational posture the risk literature (the 2024 Lenton and Rose and colleagues "should we worry" exchange) recommends is the asymmetric-loss one: a prediction that cannot be ruled out and whose outcome is civilisation-scale justifies the monitoring investment (the arrays cost a few tens of millions a year) regardless of the point probability — the same logic as earthquake and pandemic preparedness, and the reason "very unlikely" in an assessment is a statement about expected value, never a licence to stop watching.
:::
:::

::: example Stommel's box model: sixty years of hysteresis from four equations {#example-stommel}
Stommel's 1961 two-box model — the Atlantic and a global ocean box, driven by freshwater and temperature boundary conditions, with the meridional overturning strength feeding back on the salinity difference — remains the most influential object in physical oceanography because four lines of algebra contain the entire controversy. Its steady states solve a cubic-like balance: at intermediate freshwater forcing there are *two* stable circulations (the modern north-sinking "thermal" mode and a reversed or shut-down "haline" mode) separated by an unstable one; increasing the freshwater forcing past a fold collapses the strong state, and reducing it again does not restore it until a second, lower fold — the hysteresis loop that every subsequent model, from the 1990s hosing experiments (the Manabe–Stouffer GFDL CO₂-doubling runs, in which the circulation weakened or died in most of the 1990s-generation models, and the Stocker–Wright and Rahmstorf stability analyses) through the EMICs of the 2010s to the eddy-permitting CMIP6 members, reproduces with wildly different thresholds. The model also encodes the reconciliation of the 2023–24 alarm with AR6: whether the observed 15 % weakening is "approaching the fold" or "a linear-ish response far from it" depends on the distance to the bifurcation, which is exactly the parameter no observation has yet pinned — the "AMOC stability" question reduces to measuring the *curvature* of the system's potential, for which the 2024–25 school (the Karabatsiotis and Lenton freshwater-flux variance method, the 2025 Copenhagen stability estimates) is proposing exactly the array-based protocols.
::: solution
Stommel's lesson is the difference between *structural* certainty and *parametric* ignorance. Nobody disputes that the salt-advection feedback exists — it is as well-established as the greenhouse effect — so bistability somewhere in parameter space is near-certain; what nobody knows is where the fold sits relative to the forcing trajectory, and that number is set by eddy transport, mixing and wind stress that even 2020s models disagree about by factors of several. The history is therefore a warning against both dismissal and panic: the mechanism is real (models and palaeo agree), the thresholds are unknown (models disagree), and the observation programme is the only instrument that can close the gap — which is why the AMOC is simultaneously the best-understood and most-uncertain of the tipping candidates.
:::
:::

## The other candidates: ice, carbon, forest, reef

The 2008 PNAS inventory listed fifteen global "tipping elements"; the 2022 Science special issue cut the list with explicit thresholds and probability judgements, and the 2024–25 Global Tipping Points reports added the possibility of *system-wide* Earth-system tipping — a scenario the assessments do not yet recognise. The candidates below are ordered by the strength of their bistability evidence, and each carries its own honest confidence label.

::: definition The ice sheets: thresholds on millennial clocks {#def-ice-tipping}
Greenland and West Antarctica are the elements with the most certain *consequences* (metres of committed sea level, covered in the sea-level lesson [[climate-change/sea-level-rise]]) and the least usable *timescales*. The Greenland surface-melt threshold — the warming beyond which surface mass balance losses permanently outpace accumulation and the elevation–albedo feedback takes over — is assessed from model ensembles and the palaeo constraint (the last interglacial's 5–6 m and the Pliocene's higher seas) at roughly 0.8–3 °C of global warming with central estimates near 1.7 °C (the Armstrong McKay and colleagues 2022 range; the Smith and colleagues commitment analyses), meaning the element is plausibly already committed to a multi-metre, multi-millennium retreat — a "tipping" whose clock runs over ten thousand years, not this century. West Antarctica is the opposite structure: the marine-instability geometry (the Thwaites and Pine Island retrograde beds) means the *ocean* forcing already delivered commits the Amundsen Sea sector to centuries-scale retreat (the Naughten and colleagues 2022 finding that 1.5 °C-level ocean warming is already locked in for the Amundsen), while the full WAIS collapse (5 m of sea level) has thresholds assessed at 1.5–2 °C with low confidence and timescales of one-to-few millennia. The 2022 Science special issue's most quoted verdict was exactly this two-tier statement: at 1.5 °C, "multiple tipping elements become a real risk," with the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets and tropical coral reefs listed as elements whose thresholds may be exceeded *at* Paris-agreement temperatures. The honest counterweights the assessments insist on: the threshold numbers are ensemble medians with 50 %+ relative uncertainty, "committed" means committed on the element's own clock (centuries to millennia), and the hysteresis that makes these systems irreversible also makes them slow — the two properties are the same physics.
:::

::: definition Permafrost, the Amazon and reefs: carbon, forest and biology at the threshold {#def-bio-tipping}
The permafrost carbon entry is the field's most corrected. The reservoir (~1,400–1,600 Gt of soil carbon, roughly twice the pre-industrial atmosphere) is real and thawing; the 2000s "time bomb" framing — an abrupt, self-perpetuating Holocene-style release — was displaced by the synthesis literature (the Schuur and colleagues 2022 reassessment, the AR6 carbon-cycle chapters): the *global* permafrost flux this century is a gradual 5–15 % pool loss under high emissions (the ~5–55 GtCO₂/yr CO₂+CH₄ range flagged as unquantified risk in the projections lesson [[climate-change/projections]]), not a bifurcation — *but* the abrupt micro-physics is real (thermokarst collapse, lake drainage, the 2024 Nature analyses finding abrupt thaw already affecting a third of the permafrost zone), the timescale asymmetry (the carbon is coming over centuries regardless of what mitigation does now) is itself a commitment problem, and the "permafrost is not a tipping point" verdict is a statement about *this century's global budget*, not about the landscapes involved. The Amazon is the most contested forest candidate. The mechanism — reduced recycling of moisture downwind of deforestation, pushing the eastern-southern basin from rainforest to savanna-like states — has genuine bistability evidence (the satellite greenness and mortality trends of the eastern arc, the Gatti and colleagues 2021 *Nature* finding that the eastern Amazon now *emits* CO₂, the fire-drought feedbacks of the 2005, 2010, 2015–16 and 2023–24 events), and the threshold literature (the Lovejoy–Nobre 2018 "tipping at 20–25 % deforestation" analysis, current deforestation ~17–18 %) places the system uncomfortably close to its proposed boundary; the 2024 Gatti and colleagues modelling scenario — up to 90 % eastern dieback by 2100 under maximum deforestation and warming — was contested as scenario-driven, and the AR6-level verdict remains low confidence for a basin-wide abrupt flip with medium confidence for *regional* savanna transition in the deforested southeast. Tropical coral reefs are the ecological tipping element with the cleanest threshold data: bleaching mortality scales with degree-heating-weeks, the 2023–25 event bleached ~84 % of reef area — the largest recorded (the NOAA and Global Coral Reef Monitoring Network 2024–25 reports) — and the AR6's high-confidence statement that 1.5 °C implies 70–90 % loss and 2 °C >99 % makes reefs the first element whose *ecological* threshold is being crossed in real time at current warming, with the 2024–25 Lenton syntheses formally classifying them as tipped-at-1.2 °C. The remaining 2008-list candidates (the boreal forest, the West African and Indian monsoons, the Barents Sea ice edge, the El Niño–Southern Oscillation itself) have been progressively downgraded by the model-generation evidence — the monsoon "tipping" of the 2008 inventory is now assessed as a gradual intensification, the Barents edge as a seasonal ice-state change without hemispheric bifurcation — the quiet pruning that makes the surviving list credible.
:::

::: example The 2022 special issue: when the community puts thresholds in writing {#example-2022-issue}
In November 2022 *Science* published the most consequential risk statement in the field's history: Armstrong McKay and colleagues, applying a standardised protocol (literature review, expert elicitation, model and palaeo triangulation) to dozens of candidate elements, concluded that five — the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, tropical coral reefs, Arctic permafrost, and the Labrador Sea convection — have thresholds within the warming already experienced (1.1–1.2 °C), that crossing 1.5 °C (likely this decade) puts "multiple" further elements (including the AMOC and the Amazon) at "high risk," and that the probability judgements, however subjective, were now *auditable*: explicit ranges, explicit confidence, explicit elicitation method. The response mapped the fault lines of the field. The sceptical critique (the 2022–23 exchanges in *Science* and the quarterly journals): the thresholds are model medians with wide, often log-normal tails; "threshold exceeded" for a ten-thousand-year ice-sheet commitment is not a this-century event; and elicitation probabilities ("10–35 % for AMOC at 1.5 °C") are expert priors, not frequencies. The defenders' reply (Lenton and colleagues' 2023–25 rejoinders and the 2024–25 Global Tipping Points reports): the old assessments' "unlikely" language was itself elicitation, just unrecorded; the asymmetry of the losses demands that auditable subjectivity beat unauditable optimism; and the 2024 report's genuinely new claim — that *coupled* socio-economic-ecological feedbacks (food-web trade cascades, insurance retreat, fossil-fuel stranding) could push the *whole* Earth system toward a "Hothouse" attractor — was placed, deliberately, outside the assessment system's competence.
::: solution
The special issue's methodological lesson generalises beyond tipping points: when a question is structurally unanswerable by models or observations alone (thresholds beyond the experiment), the choice is not "no answer" but "which answer-producing method has the least bad epistemology" — and explicit elicitation with published ranges beat the implicit optimism it replaced on every audit the 2023–25 commentaries performed. The policy lesson is equally sharp: a threshold list with numbers attached converts the precautionary principle from slogan into expected-value arithmetic, which is why both the 1.5 °C politics and the "tipping points are hype" industry now cite the same eleven pages at each other.
:::
:::

::: example The 2023–25 bleaching: watching an ecological threshold being crossed {#example-reefs}
The fourth global bleaching event began in the Caribbean in January 2023, went global with the 2023–24 marine heatwave (the +0.2 °C El Niño step plus the long-term trend pushed ocean heat content to record anomalies in every basin), and by early 2025 the NOAA Coral Reef Watch record showed ~84 % of the world's reef-building areas had experienced bleaching-level heat stress — the largest extent in the 37-year satellite record, across all three ocean basins simultaneously (the 2024–25 NOAA and Global Coral Reef Monitoring Network reports; the 2025 confirmations of mass mortality from the Florida Reef Tract — where summer 2023 sea temperatures exceeded 38 °C, lethal within weeks — to Western Australia's Ningaloo and the Maldives). The event matters for this lesson's logic in three ways. First, the threshold was not statistical but physiological: the bleaching-to-mortality relationship (degree-heating-weeks, the 4/8 DHW thresholds) is laboratory-measured, so unlike the ice sheets and the AMOC, the reef threshold has no structural uncertainty — only exposure does. Second, the response was *not* uniform: the 2024–25 surveys found refugia (upwelling reefs, deep-water coral assemblages, the Persian Gulf's pre-adapted heat-tolerant symbionts) and the first documented range expansions (the 2024–25 Florida and Japan poleward colonisation records), the "winners and losers" structure that the palaeontology of past hyperthermal reef collapses documents and that distinguishes ecological tipping from ecological annihilation. Third, the feedbacks are real but local: reef loss removes the nursery structure for coastal fisheries (the 2024 synthesis estimates 250 million people's protein and coastal defence exposure), and dead-coral erosion lowers reef crests faster than regrowth can rebuild them — a hysteresis in *structure* even where the *species* survive.
::: solution
The bleaching era is the case study for the lesson's central distinction: reefs are the first element whose threshold crossing is being *observed* rather than projected, because their threshold is measured physiology at current warming — the AR6's 70–90 % loss at 1.5 °C is an exposure calculation, not a bifurcation guess. Whether that constitutes a "tipping point" depends on the definition kept: the ecosystem state (framework-accreted reef) shows genuine hysteresis at local scales (algal-dominated states resist recolonisation for decades), while the *climate* forcing is not self-perpetuating through the reef itself — the feedback is socio-ecological (fisheries, coastal erosion, lost carbon uptake), not Earth-system. The honest verdict: reefs are the first *ecological* tipping in progress, and the cleanest demonstration that at 1.2 °C the question has moved from "will thresholds be crossed" to "which ones, how fast, and where the refugia are."
:::

::: warning Five ways tipping points get distorted — by the panicked and the complacent alike
1. **The two-meanings swap.** The colloquial tipping point (any threshold that matters) and the dynamical one (self-perpetuating, irreversible regime shift) get traded mid-argument: headlines import the fear of the first and the authority of the second. Ask, every time: is *irreversibility on human timescales* claimed, and on what clock?
2. **"Already triggered" read as "collapsing now."** The 2022 five-elements verdict means thresholds may be exceeded — for the ice sheets, committing metres over millennia. It does not mean Greenland is lost this century. The timescale must travel with the claim in both directions: "committed" is not "imminent," and "not imminent" is not "avoidable."
3. **False precision.** Thresholds quoted as points ("the Amazon tips at 20 % deforestation," "the AMOC collapses at 0.45 Sv") are ensemble medians with factor-of-two ranges and low confidence; the honest statement is a probability distribution over thresholds, and any policy built on the point estimate is built on the median of a guess.
4. **Bifurcation-versus-rate blindness.** Systems can tip from *speed* alone without any threshold (rate-induced tipping), and systems with thresholds can survive slow forcing that far exceeds the old point estimate. A single "degrees of warming" dial cannot diagnose either mode — the trajectory's rate and history matter, which is why the overshoot-and-return debate is a real scientific question, not a semantic one.
5. **The "very unlikely" inversion.** AR6's "AMOC collapse very unlikely before 2100" is a ~1-in-10-to-1-in-100 statement about a civilisation-scale outcome — expected-value territory where even 1 % justifies monitoring and contingency, exactly as it does for floods and pandemics. Quoting "very unlikely" as "won't happen," or quoting the 2023 alarm as "predicted," are mirror-image misreadings of the same probability statement.
:::

::: history From Stommel's boxes to the Hothouse question: tipping science's sixty years {#history-tipping}
The intellectual lineage is short and distinguished: Stommel's 1961 box model (the bistable ocean from four equations), Broecker's 1975 "climatic-ice-age theory" (the shutdown-as-ice-age-trigger hypothesis and the first hosing speculation), and the 1980s–90s proof-of-mechanism era — the Manabe–Stouffer CO₂-doubling and hosing experiments of 1990–99, Rahmstorf's 1995 stability syntheses, and Broecker's 1993 *Nature* "great thermohaline catastrophe" note that introduced the abrupt-change vocabulary to the public. The palaeo evidence matured in parallel: the Heinrich-event stratigraphy (the 1990s Bond cycle papers), the bipolar-seesaw ice-core chronologies of the Blunier–Jouzel and Pedro–Brooks schools, and the 8.2-ka event's 2003–12 outburst modelling. The term "tipping point" itself arrived through Gladwell's 2000 popular sociology and was captured for the geosciences by the 2008 PNAS special issue (the Lenton, Schellnhuber and colleagues inventory of fifteen elements with thresholds and timescales — the founding document), followed by the 2009–2012 "climate wars" over its tone (the 2010 Copenhagen diagnosis, the AR5's 2013 "medium confidence, thresholds poorly constrained" verdict). The observational turn came with the instruments: GRACE and ICESat Greenland mass balance from 2002–03, the RAPID array from 2004, the Amazon flux network's 2005-and-2010 drought papers, and the 2015–16 and 2023–25 global bleaching records. The 2019–2025 phase is the one still running: Schellnhuber's 2019 "state of the planet" five-tipping-points paper, Lenton's 2019 *Nature* "tipping the tipping point" essay (the 2019–2022 school of "tipping towards hope," deliberately imported from the sustainability-transition literature), the 2022 *Science* special issue and its elicitation protocol, the 2023 Ditlevsen AMOC alarm and 2024 critiques, the 2024 Gatti Amazon dieback scenario, and the 2024–25 Global Tipping Points reports' Hothouse question — the first time the Earth-system community formally asked whether the whole system, not its parts, has attractors, and the point at which the field's oldest instrument (the box model) meets its newest problem (a planet with a civilisation attached).
:::

::: quiz
A system is "tipped" in the technical sense only if the change is:
- [ ] Large and fast
- [x] Self-perpetuating through internal feedbacks and persistent (irreversible on relevant timescales) after the trigger is removed
- [ ] Driven by a threshold crossing of external forcing alone
- [ ] Predicted by at least one model
::: solution
**Self-perpetuation plus persistence**: size, speed and model support are neither necessary nor sufficient — a slow, small-forcing-driven change that feedbacks take over and that does not reverse when the forcing relaxes is a tip; a large, rapid, externally-driven change that reverses when the forcing does (an ENSO extreme) is not. The hysteresis loop is the signature: the system's history matters, which is exactly what makes tipping points irreversible-in-practice.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="2.6"}
The AMOC transports about 17 Sv of volume; observations and fingerprints suggest a ~15 % weakening since 1950. How many Sverdrups of transport has it lost?
::: solution
0.15 × 17 = 2.6 Sv — roughly the flow of the Amazon multiplied several times over, lost from the overturning in seventy years; the number that makes "the weakening is real" vivid without answering the stability question (internal variability at the 26.5°N array is ±5 Sv on decade scales).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="1.7"}
Permafrost soils hold ~1,500 Gt of carbon; the modern atmosphere holds ~900 Gt. What is the ratio of the permafrost pool to the atmospheric pool?
::: solution
1,500/900 = 1.7 — the permafrost reservoir is more than half again the atmosphere's entire carbon, which is why even the assessed gradual 5–15 % loss this century matters to the carbon budget, and why the "time bomb" framing died hard: the pool's size made it a story before its dynamics were known.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="6"}
Bleaching onset corresponds to about 4 degree-heating-weeks (DHW). If summer sea-surface temperatures run 0.5 °C above the bleaching threshold for 12 weeks, what is the accumulated DHW?
::: solution
0.5 °C × 12 weeks = 6 DHW — past the bleaching threshold (4) and approaching the mortality threshold (8), the arithmetic that makes 1.5 °C a "70–90 % reef loss" world: marine heatwaves of this class, once decadal, now recur several times per decade on most reefs.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1.75"}
In hosing experiments, AMOC collapse in a given model follows a sustained ~5 % reduction in North Atlantic surface salinity. From a baseline of 35.0 PSU, how large is that reduction, in PSU?
::: solution
0.05 × 35.0 = 1.75 PSU — the size of the freshwater anomaly required, against the ~0.5 PSU freshening already observed in subpolar surface waters since 1900 (the "the anomaly is a third of the way there" statistic that both fuels and outruns the alarm, depending on which threshold the model actually used).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.5"}
A committed 5 m of eventual sea-level rise from Greenland equilibrates over ~10,000 years. What is the implied mean rate, in mm/yr?
::: solution
5,000 mm / 10,000 yr = 0.5 mm/yr — modest per year, catastrophic summed: the rate at which the last interglacial's seas rose during deglaciation was several times this, and the point of the commitment arithmetic is the *total*, not the annual increment.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.2"}
In a Stommel-type model, AMOC collapse occurs at freshwater forcing 0.45 (arbitrary units) and recovery requires forcing below 0.25. What is the width of the hysteresis range?
::: solution
0.45 − 0.25 = 0.20 units — the hysteresis band in which both states are stable and history decides; inside it, "is the AMOC safe?" has no answer without knowing where the system started, which is why "it recovered in the model" and "it collapsed in the model" can both be true of the same forcing.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="70"}
The 2023 Ditlevsen AMOC analysis gave a 95 % collapse window of 2025–2095. What is the window's width, in years?
::: solution
2095 − 2025 = 70 years — a range so wide it is compatible with "very unlikely this century" and with mid-century collapse simultaneously, the visualisation of why the paper was simultaneously the most alarming and the least falsifiable statement of the year, and why the critics attacked the interval's construction rather than its central value.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.06"}
A risk framework assesses P(AMOC collapse before 2150 | warming exceeds 2 °C) = 0.1 and P(exceeding 2 °C this century) = 0.6. What is the unconditional probability of collapse by 2150?
::: solution
0.1 × 0.6 = 0.06 — a 6 % chance of a hemispheric-scale regime shift. The expected-value point: with civilisation-scale damages, 6 % justifies large monitoring and precaution spending even though "unlikely" will be the correct ex-post word if it does not happen — the decision-theory the risk triangle lesson [[natural-hazards/risk]] formalises for a new hazard class.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3"}
The Amazon savanna-transition threshold is assessed at 20–25 % basin deforestation; current deforestation is ~17 %. How many percentage points of additional deforestation reach the lower bound of the threshold range?
::: solution
20 − 17 = 3 percentage points — roughly a decade of current clearing rates, the number that makes the Amazon the rare tipping candidate where the *local* driver (deforestation, fire, logging) is as decisive as the global one (warming), and therefore the one where local policy has the most leverage.
:::

The strongest evidence that the AMOC has *bistability* in the real world comes from:
- [ ] The RAPID array's two decades of transport measurements
- [x] The palaeo record of abrupt glacial-interglacial Atlantic changes plus model hysteresis loops
- [ ] The 2023 statistical early-warning signal
- [ ] Satellite salinity maps since 2011
::: solution
**Palaeo plus models**: the array is too short to see a threshold, the statistics are contested, and satellites see only the surface — but the Younger Dryas, Heinrich events and the 8.2-ka outburst demonstrate the switch actually operated under past forcings, and every model generation reproduces a hysteresis loop. Bistability is inferred from history and theory together, never from the modern trend alone — the same evidence structure as the attribution fingerprint.

::: summary
- A tipping point is technical, not rhetorical: self-perpetuating, persistent regime change across a critical threshold, with hysteresis as its signature — and the field's honest catalogue separates systems with real bistability evidence (AMOC, ice sheets, reefs) from those whose thresholds were model artefacts (the monsoons, most of the 2008 list's circulation entries).
- The AMOC is the reference case: the salt-advection feedback and the palaeo record make bistability near-certain, the observed ~15 % weakening is real, the models say collapse this century is very unlikely, the early-warning statistics say the window may be open — and the disagreement is about where the fold sits, which only a growing observing system can settle.
- The 2022 threshold audit changed the field's epistemics: explicit, elicited, auditable ranges replaced implicit optimism, and its verdict — that Greenland, West Antarctica, coral reefs and permafrost may already be past their thresholds at 1.1–1.2 °C — is best read as commitment-on-the-element's-clock, not collapse-on-ours.
- Timescales are the whole story: reefs are tipping now (ecological, years), the Amazon's eastern arc is regionally transitioning (decades, deforestation-driven), the ice sheets are committed for millennia, and the permafrost "bomb" is a slow fuse with abrupt fuses inside — one word, five physics.
- Under asymmetric losses, "very unlikely" is a reason to monitor, not to stop worrying: the monitoring arrays, the threshold audits and the early-warning programmes cost orders of magnitude less than the outcomes they inform — the bridge to the responses lesson [[climate-change/responses]] and the risk logic of [[natural-hazards/risk]].
:::
