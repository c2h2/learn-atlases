In August 2021 the west coast of North America experienced the heat dome whose numbers the models of a decade earlier had said were centuries away at current emissions: Lytton's 49.6 °C, Portland's 46.7 °C, the Pacific Northwest's 200-year-magnitude anomaly arriving in year 30 of the 21st century under a scenario (SSP2-4.5, the middle) whose 2021–40 assessed warming was ~1.5 °C, not the ~2.5 °C the event implied. The event became the projection lesson's perfect case study when the attribution and modelling teams finished their work: the heat was *not* a model failure in the simple sense — the CMIP ensembles' 1.5 °C world, examined at the tail of its distribution and in the right synoptic configuration, contained such events as rare-but-possible, and the "impossible" framing came from reading the *central* projection as a boundary rather than as the middle of a distribution whose tails the models under-sample. This is the first rule of the discipline this lesson describes: **a projection is a probability statement about a world whose emissions are themselves a choice**, and every number in it carries three separate uncertainties — which scenario humanity chooses, how the climate responds (the model spread), and what the internal dice roll (the variability) — with the embarrassing property that the first, largest and most policy-relevant uncertainty is not a scientific uncertainty at all. The projection literature is therefore a three-storey building: the scenarios (the human story, the SSP-RCP design), the models (CMIP, the coupled model intercomparison whose 100,000-plus model-years are the field's laboratory), and the statistics (how to read a range, a likelihood, and a "least unlikely future").

## The scenario architecture and the model laboratory

::: definition Scenarios: from IS92 to the SSP-RCP design {#def-scenarios}
Climate projections are conditional: "if emissions go this way, the climate goes that way," and the "if" is supplied by **scenarios** — internally consistent stories about population, growth, energy, land use and climate policy, built by integrated assessment models (IAMs) and used by the climate models as forcing inputs. The lineage: the IPCC's **IS92** sets (1990, written as single-line emissions paths), the **SRES** families (2000: A1, A2, B1, B2 — the "storyline" design that produced the still-quoted A1B and the A1FI fossil-fuelled world), and since 2010 the **RCP–SSP architecture**: four **representative concentration pathways** (RCP2.6, RCP4.5, RCP6.0, RCP8.5 — named for their 2100 radiative forcing in W/m², computed from emissions by simple climate-carbon-cycle models, with RCP8.5's coal-heavy, no-policy path designed as a *high-end boundary*, not a business-as-usual forecast — the 2016 Pester–Kendall and O'Neill–Kriegler analyses showing the world is tracking between RCP4.5 and RCP6.0, and the 2020s literature's "RCP8.5 is implausible as a central path but remains valid as a boundary and useful as a worst-case for impacts" verdict) crossed with five **shared socioeconomic pathways** (SSP1 sustainability, SSP2 middle-of-the-road, SSP3 regional rivalry, SSP4 inequality, SSP5 fossil-fuelled development — narrative-plus-model descriptions of *worlds*, each with its own population, GDP and land-use trajectories). The CMIP6 protocol ran the standard matrix (the "tier 1" five: SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, SSP5-8.5), and the AR6 headline numbers are its output: relative to 1850–1900, assessed warming for 2021–40 is ~1.5 °C in *all* scenarios (the near-term is locked in — the budgets lesson's cumulative physics), while for 2081–2100 the range runs **1.4–1.8 °C (SSP1-1.9), 1.3–1.7 (SSP1-2.6), 2.1–2.5 (SSP2-4.5), 2.7–3.5 (SSP3-7.0) and 3.3–4.6 (SSP5-8.5, likely ranges)** — a five-fold spread in the century's end that is almost entirely the *scenario*, the single clearest demonstration in science that the dominant uncertainty in this field is a human choice. The scenario layer also carries the **emulator** machinery (the reduced-complexity models — MAGICC, FaIR — that turn any emissions path into a temperature path using the assessed TCRE and climate-feedback parameters, the tools behind the carbon-budget and NDC-triage arithmetic of the previous lesson) and the newer **overshoot pathways** (the >1,000 IAM scenarios behind AR6 WGIII, most of which exceed 1.5 °C and return, their dependence on carbon dioxide removal the subject of the removals-scepticism literature).
:::

::: definition The model laboratory: CMIP, climate sensitivity, and why the range persists {#def-cmip}
The projection engines are **coupled general circulation models** — atmosphere, ocean, sea ice, land surface, and increasingly chemistry, carbon cycle, ice sheets and dynamic vegetation — integrated over centuries under scenario forcing, and coordinated for four decades by the **Coupled Model Intercomparison Project** (CMIP1 1996 through CMIP6 2014– , ~60 modelling centres, the published output exceeding 3 petabytes, the "DECK" protocol of standard 150-year runs making every model's sensitivity, variability and pattern directly comparable). The headline diagnostic is **equilibrium climate sensitivity** (ECS — the warming after CO₂ quadruples, equilibrated): AR6's assessed range **2.5–4.0 °C (best estimate 3.0)**, a range *narrower* than the 1979 Charney report's 1.5–4.5 not because the physics got better but because the low and high tails were excluded by three independent lines (the instrumental record's constraint — the history-climate "emergent constraint" literature; palaeoclimate — the Last Glacial Maximum's temperature–CO₂ relationship; and process understanding of clouds, the CFMIP and DYCOMS-II/EPIC observational campaigns that pinned the marine-low-cloud and entrainment parameters). The **transient** response (the warming during a ramp, what matters this century) is ~1.8 °C best estimate, and the CMIP6 models' median sensitivity *rose* relative to CMIP5 (several new models' cloud-feedback parameterisations pushed ECS to 4–5.4 °C — the Hausfather–Zelinka–Takahashi 2020 reconciliation showing the outliers were a cloud-microphysics artefact and the assessed range unchanged) — a public example of the community auditing its own ensemble. The models' skill record is the projection literature's credibility: they reproduce the observed seasonal cycle, the ENSO spectrum, the Pacific Decadal patterns, the diurnal cycle (after convection-parameterisation tuning), the global warming "fingerprint" of the last 40 years, the Antarctic Oscillation's trend, and — the hardest test — the *vertical* and *regional* structure of the observed trend; their known failures are equally catalogued (the double-ITCZ bias, the Atlantic Niño amplitude, the "too persistent" MJO, the Southern Ocean jet's position, the systematic under-representation of African easterly waves, and the well-known "cold bias" of the tropical Pacific that shapes the hiatus debate below). The frontier is the **high-resolution and "storm-resolving" generation** (the DYAMOND and exascale projects running ~1–7 km global models that resolve mesoscale eddies and tropical cyclones explicitly — the 2020s finding that higher resolution raises climate sensitivity by changing the cloud response, and the 2024–25 first multi-year 1-km simulations whose cyclone-climate signals are now *modelled* rather than inferred statistically).
:::

::: example The "hiatus" that was a forecast: how the models predicted their own pause {#example-hiatus}
Between 1998 and 2013 the surface-warming rate fell to ~0.05–0.12 °C/decade against the models' projected ~0.2 — the "hiatus" or "pause," the most exploited anomaly in the political history of the field. The scientific resolution, published across 2014–2018 and now textbook, has three parts worth teaching as a *projection-methodology* lesson. (1) **The models were never wrong about the heat's destination**: the ocean heat content (Argo) and the energy-budget (CERES) records show no pause in total heat uptake; the surface pause was a *redistribution*, with the Pacific's trade winds (the positive PDO/IPO phase and the strengthened Walker circulation) pumping anomalous heat below the ~700 m layer via the subtropical cells and the Southern Ocean's wind-driven subduction (the Meehl 2011, England 2014, Kosaka–Xie 2013 "what caused the hiatus" attribution to the Pacific, and the 2017–18 studies showing the missing heat resurfaced with the 2015–16 El Niño). (2) **The forced pattern was visible in the right places**: the hiatus-era surface cooling of the eastern tropical Pacific, the deep-ocean warming, and the accelerated sea-level rise over the western/Indian oceans (the "sea-level fingerprint" of the Pacific sequestration, the Church–Godedange and Gregory studies) were all *predicted* features of a paused world, and the 2023–24 surface warming spike is their mirror image. (3) **The prediction-vs-projection distinction**: decadal *predictions* (the initialized hindcast systems — the UK Met Office's GloSea5, the GFDL and ECMWF systems — which start from the observed ocean state and therefore *can* anticipate the Pacific's phase) sketched the 2000s slowdown in advance (the Meehl and Collins school's 2009–2013 papers), while the uninitialized *projections* (CMIP's free-running ensembles, which know nothing about the Pacific's dice-roll) could only bracket it. The hiatus is therefore the field's cleanest demonstration of the three-storey uncertainty structure: the scenario was irrelevant at 15 years, the model spread was small, and the internal variability — the term the public mistakes for the projection — dominated everything, exactly as the uncertainty-decomposition mathematics predicts for horizons under ~20 years.
::: solution
The decomposition arithmetic: for a 20-year mean of global surface temperature, internal-variability variance is comparable to the forced-signal difference between adjacent scenarios, while for a 50-year mean the scenario term dominates (the Hawkins–Sutton 2009 and Dessai–Lempert follow-up "dead zones" plots — the figure showing that for the 2080s, scenario uncertainty dwarfs everything, and for the 2020s, variability and model spread do). The hiatus decade fell in the dead zone where honest forecasting is about *risk*, not trajectory — the same regime the "warming-slowdown this decade?" headlines keep re-entering.
:::
:::

::: example The acceleration question: is the warming rate itself rising? {#example-acceleration}
In 2023–24 the observed warming rate over the most recent 20 years (~0.25–0.3 °C/decade) sat at the top of, or slightly above, the CM6 multi-model range for the same period under historical forcing, prompting the question the field debates openly: is the *rate of warming accelerating beyond the models' scenario* — as the Hansen et al. 2023–24 "warming is accelerating" papers argue (attributing the excess to declining shipping aerosol, lower biomass-burning black carbon, and possible cloud-feedback acceleration) — or is it the expected combination of higher emissions (the 2023 fossil CO₂ path tracking the *upper* part of the historical inventory), the El Niño ramp, and internal variability landing on the trend (the-forcing-response school: Forster et al.'s state-of-the-climate accounting, the 2024 Manomaichai–Forster trend-decomposition finding observed trends within model ensembles once aerosol and ENSO are accounted)? The question is *quantitatively* framed: the equilibrium feedback parameter λ (the models' ~0.8–1.2 W/m²/K, the observed record's ~1.0–1.4) and the emergent-constraint literature's narrowing of the "too-sensitive models" problem (the Sherwood 2020 constraint from the seasonal cycle and the pattern-effect literature) both allow a true ECS nearer 4 than 2.5 without breaking anything, and the 2020s' observed trends are the first to mildly prefer the upper half. The projection consequence is concrete: if the true transient response is 20 % above the ensemble median, the 2081–2100 SSP2-4.5 range's upper half (2.5 °C rather than 2.1) is the planning number, the 1.5 °C arrival dates move up ~5–10 years, and the "locked-in" near-term warming (the ~1.5 °C of 2021–40) becomes ~1.6–1.7. The exercise: state which of the three uncertainty terms (scenario, model, variability) the acceleration question is *actually* about, and why the answer determines whether the debate is settled by observations or by the next decade of them.
::: solution
It is a **model-spread** question (is the ensemble's central response biased low?) contaminated by a **variability** signal (the 2023–24 ENSO-volcano-internal alignment) and a **scenario** fact (emissions beat the historical-assumption inputs) — the three terms move in the same direction in the 2020s, which is why the debate is live; the field's resolution path is the classic one: more years of data (the variability term decays), better aerosol and cloud constraints (the model term narrows), and the emissions ledger (the scenario term is whatever we choose). The honest projection stance: treat the upper half of every assessed range as the planning default, since the cost asymmetry of the error is not symmetric.
:::
:::

::: example The emergent-constraint method: how a model spread becomes a measurement {#example-emergent}
Equilibrium climate sensitivity cannot be measured directly — no experiment doubles CO₂ and waits a millennium — so the 2010s field built proxies for it: **emergent constraints** link an observable *present-climate* statistic to a model's future sensitivity across the ensemble, then use the observed value of that statistic to narrow the projection. The canonical successes: the **Sheerwood et al. 2020** constraint on tropical low-cloud sensitivity from the observed response of stratocumulus coverage to seasonal sea-surface temperature changes (narrowing the likely ECS to 2.6–3.9 °C and *excluding* the 4.5+ °C CMIP6 outliers), the **Wood et al. 2014** Southern-Ocean cloud-albedo/seasonal-cycle constraint, the **Caldwell–Bretherton** upper-tropospheric-water-vapour lineage, and the pattern-effect/"climate sensitivity is not the feedback you think" school's cautionary result (the Boers–Stevens and Forster–Storelvbo analyses showing that the observed *warming pattern* — the Atlantic–Pacific trend contrast — biases history-based sensitivity estimates low if the pattern is treated as forced). The method's failure modes are a graduate seminar in itself: the regression must be *causal*, not just correlated across models (a model's sensitivity and its observable statistic can co-vary because both are driven by a shared parameterisation bug — the "spurious constraint" problem the Atwood–Allen and Sherwood–Huber validity tests police), the ensemble must span the real physical range (a constraint from a 30-member ensemble that excludes the true physics is a precise wrong answer), and the observation must be unbiased (the satellite cloud-record homogeneity wars apply here with full force). The general lesson for reading projections: an emergent constraint is a *hypothesis-tested narrowing*, and the assessments accept them only when the physical mechanism is understood independently of the regression — which is why AR6's ECS range cites the constraints alongside the palaeo and instrumental lines rather than replacing them.
::: solution
The epistemology: emergent constraints convert model spread into an observational inference by betting that a relationship *across models* reflects a real physical mechanism *in the world* — a bet that pays when the mechanism is independently explained (cloud–circulation coupling, entrainment mixing) and fails silently when it is not, which is why the field's constraint-validation papers (the Schneider–O'Gorman and Caldwell–McCoy validity frameworks) now test constraints against held-out models and against the historical record before an assessment cites them.
:::
:::

## Reading the ranges: statistics, downscaling, and honest uncertainty

::: definition Uncertainty decomposition and pattern scaling: how to read a projection {#def-reading}
A projection range is a statement about *three* distributions, and the professional literature keeps them separate. **Scenario uncertainty** (the emissions choice) grows with time and dominates beyond ~30 years; **model uncertainty** (the response — sensitivity, clouds, carbon-cycle feedbacks) dominates at mid-century; **internal variability** dominates at 10–20 years and for regional variables at any horizon (the reason a 20-year regional "projection" of precipitation is closer to a statement of *risk* than of trajectory — the "predictability horizon" for precipitation is effectively zero at decadal scales in the mid-latitudes, a fact the impacts literature states far less often than the public hears). The decomposition is computed from ensemble variances (the Hawkins–Sutton variance-fraction method, the AR6 "uncertainty hierarchy" figures), and its policy translation is the "climate services" school's insistence on **decision-relevant framing**: not "2.1 °C" but "the range of outcomes across which your adaptation choice must be robust" (the Lempert–Dessai robust-decision and "deep uncertainty" methods developed for water utilities and coastal defence, the same logic as the risk-triangle lesson in the hazards course). **Pattern scaling** is the workhorse that turns global numbers into regional ones: each model's *response pattern* (the ratio of regional to global warming — the Arctic at 2–4×, the continents at 1.4–1.6×, the North Atlantic "warming hole" at 0.6×, the tropical oceans at ~1.0×) is multiplied by the global projection, giving the AR6 regional statements ("land warming ~1.6× global; Arctic surface annual mean 2–3.5× global by 2081–2100 with high confidence") and the "cubic scaling" refinements for extremes (the per-degree warming of the warmest days exceeding the mean). **Downscaling** fills the last scale gap: **dynamical** (regional climate models — the CORDEX programme's ~25 km and ~12 km Europe/Africa/Americas ensembles, nested in GCM boundaries, adding mesoscale realism: the Mediterranean's summer drying, the Alpine and Andes orographic signals, the West African monsoon's sensitivity) and **statistical/empirical** (bias corrections and quantile mapping, regression downscaling — cheap, ubiquitous, and prone to the "change of variance" artefacts the bias-correction wars of the 2010s documented), with the honest verdict that downscaling *adds pattern and extremes realism but not independent information* — a regional model run inside a biased parent inherits the bias, which is why the CORDEX ensembles are presented as ranges conditional on their parents. The final interpretive rule — the one every assessment chapter states and every headline ignores — is that **the range is not a fence**: the assessed "likely" interval is a 66–90 % statement about the *central estimate*, the tails are physically populated (the low-probability/high-impact "ice-sheet, permafrost, AMOC" caveats that AR6 lists as its explicit deep-uncertainty set), and the probability statements themselves rest on ensembles whose members are *not* independent samples of the physics (the "model weighting" and "independence" literatures — the Knutti–Nichols and Sandstad effective-sample-size analyses showing a 40-model ensemble carries the information content of ~10–15 independent estimates, which is why the assessments widen rather than narrow the ranges when they add members).
:::

::: definition Projections of the water, ice and carbon cycles: what the models can and cannot say {#def-proj-water}
The projection confidence hierarchy follows the physics: **temperature** (high confidence, tight ranges), **large-scale precipitation patterns** (high confidence in the *sign pattern* — the wet-get-wetter/seasonal-shift/ subtropical-dry structure, the AR6 statement that Mediterranean and southwestern North America summer drying is "likely" and West African monsoon precipitation increases "likely" over West Africa itself), **regional precipitation amounts** (low confidence — the "models disagree on sign" map for the tropics and monsoon interiors), **cryosphere** (sea ice: high confidence in the September decline, the ice-free September mid-century range; Greenland and Antarctica: the *low-confidence tail* problem — the ice-sheet contributions are bounded by process understanding of marine ice-sheet instability and cliff failure, which is why AR6's 2100 sea-level ranges are wider at the top than the models alone produce, covered in the sea-level lesson), and **carbon-cycle feedbacks** (the land sink's weakening is "likely" in direction, unconstrained in magnitude; the permafrost carbon–climate feedback — the AR6 assessed ~5–55 GtCO₂/yr of combined permafrost CO₂+CH₄ under high emissions by 2100 in the deeper literature's range, excluded from the carbon budgets and flagged as the largest *unquantified-risk* term). The **monsoon and circulation** projections carry their own paradoxes the field teaches openly: the South and East Asian monsoons warm and wet *thermodynamically* while the *circulation* component weakens (the "warming but weakening" Indian monsoon literature, the AR6 "likely increase in Indian summer monsoon rainfall" verdict resting on the thermodynamic term), and the Atlantic overturning weakens in 100 % of simulations (the AMOC "likely decline this century" with "collapse very unlikely before 2100" — the language calibrated by the 2023 Ditlevsen–Ditlevsen early-warning statistical alarm and the Copenhagen school's 2025 reassessment, covered in the tipping-points lesson). The **extremes** projections follow from the distributions plus the pattern scalings (the wettest 5-day precipitation intensifying near Clausius–Clapeyron ~7 %/°C over land with high confidence; hot extremes' frequency scaling non-linearly — the "3 °C world's" summer-mean +5 °C over land; compound hot-drought and fire-weather-day increases "likely" in the fire-prone Mediterranean, California, Australia and the Amazon), and the **carbon-budget–climate interaction** closes the loop: every scenario's CO₂ path embeds a carbon-cycle model's sink projection, and the sinks' weakening under warming (the "carbon-cycle climate feedback," the ~+0.3–0.8 °C of extra warming it adds to high-emission pathways in the ESMCM-Fed (ESM Feedback) literature) means the scenarios are *less* self-consistent than the fan diagrams imply — the second-order term the assessments now carry explicitly.
:::

::: example The sea-level low-end fight: how the community re-drew a range {#example-slr-range}
The 2014–15 sequence of sea-level projection papers is the cleanest case study in the field's uncertainty governance. The AR5 (2013) 2100 ranges — 0.28–0.98 m across scenarios — were built from process models and tide-gauge-validated semi-empirical relations; Kopp et al. (2014) and Bamber et al. (2016) then argued from the palaeoclimate constraint (the Pliocene and last-interglacial 6–9 m seas, and the deglaciation melt-pulse record) that the ice-sheet models' structural uncertainty extended far higher — "1–2.5 m plausible, low-probability high-impact" — and the 2019 SROCC and 2021–22 AR6 process: the assessment *widened* the low-likelihood upper bounds (AR6's SSP5-8.5 "low-likelihood" addition of a further ~1 m above the likely range by 2100 and ~7 m by 2300), explicitly because the ice-sheet groups' structural uncertainty (marine ice-sheet instability at Thwaites and the Dronning Maud Land cliffs, the 2023 DeConto–Pollard-de Vries two-studies controversy over whether the Antarctic plateau's cliff-failure term belongs in the models at all) cannot be sampled by running more model members. The lesson generalises to every "the models don't cover it" question — the AMOC tail, the permafrost tail, the Amazon transition: the assessments' probability language ("likely," "very high confidence," "low confidence but plausible") is a *deliberate* instrument for separating "we can compute a distribution" from "we can bound the process," and the 2023–25 "sea level rise in 2100: the tide-gauge ensemble says 0.6 m, the ice-mechanistic studies say 2 m" press war is a fight about which epistemic instrument to trust for an *unprecedented* forcing — a question with no purely scientific answer, which is why the coastal-engineering standard (the Dutch and UK "managed realignment plus scenario-plus-margin" practice, the US ACE's "physics-informed upper bound" guidance) now designs against the *upper plausible* rather than the *likely* range for century-scale assets.
::: solution
The epistemics: model ensembles sample *within-structure* uncertainty; palaeo and mechanistic arguments sample *structural* uncertainty; when the forcing exceeds the record (as now, everywhere), structural uncertainty dominates and the honest range must be widened by judgement — the "expert elicitation" methods (the Sheffield–Lewis sea-level and the STC/UKCIP deep-uncertainty applications) whose 2010s revival is the field's admission that some 21st-century questions outgrow ensembles. The projection lesson for every reader of a range: ask what sampled it — members, models, scenarios, or experts — before deciding what the number means.
:::
:::

::: history From Manabe's columns to exascale oceans: the projection machine's sixty years {#history-projections}
The first quantitative climate projections were **one-dimensional radiative-convective models**: Manabe and Wetherald's 1967 model (fixed relative humidity, convective adjustment) produced the 2.9 °C for a doubling that remains within the modern range, and the 1970s chaos revolution (Lorenz 1963's determinism problem, Leith and Epstein's stochastic-dynamics framing, Thompson and Schneider's 1979 GCM "climate predictability" limits) established *why* projections must be probabilistic. The **coupling era** began with the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory's coupled ocean–atmosphere models (Manabe and Bryan's 1969 stagnant-ocean prototype, the 1990s coupled models of the Hadley Centre, GFDL and the Bjerknes Centre school), the **scenario era** with the IPCC's 1990 IS92 sets and the 1992 UNFCCC's "dangerous anthropogenic interference" mandate, and the **intercomparison era** with the AMIP (1989) and CMIP (1995) protocols — the sociological revolution that made model *comparison* (not model advocacy) the field's evidence standard. The **carbon-cycle era** (the 1990s–2000s: the coupled climate–carbon-cycle models of the Hadley Centre's HadGEM and the CNRM/CSIRO lineage, the AR4's 2007 "the carbon cycle amplifies warming" verdict) turned the models from physical into Earth-system simulators, and the **CMIP5/CMIP6 era** (2010, 2014– ) added the paleoclimate (PMIP), ice-sheet (ISOMIP/MISMIP), emissions-driven (C4MIP), and decadal-prediction (DCPP) sub-protocols — one coordinated experiment structure feeding three assessment traditions (the AR5's 2013 "likely 1.5–4 °C by 2100," the AR6's scenario-fan table, and the 1.5 °C Special Report's 90-plus pathway analysis). The **machine-learning frontier** (2020– ): the emulators that make million-member uncertainty sampling routine (the Gaussian-process and deep-learning surrogates of the simple climate models), the ML parameterisations in pilot runs (the Gentine–Schneider school's neural-network convection, the 2023–25 "climate-model 2.0" debate over whether learned physics preserves the conservation laws the subject is made of), and the artificial-intelligence weather models (the GraphCast, Pangu and NeuralGCM generation — the 2024–25 finding that ML weather models beat numerical ones on forecast skill while their *climate* behaviour is an open question) — the latest chapter in a sixty-year argument about whether the climate problem is best attacked by solving the equations finer, sampling them wider, or learning them cheaper, with the honest answer so far being "all three, and the projections still disagree at the tails."
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="3.7"}
Compute the radiative forcing of a CO₂ doubling using the standard logarithmic expression ΔF = 5.35 × ln(C/C₀) W/m².
::: solution
5.35 × ln 2 = 5.35 × 0.693 = 3.71 W/m² — the number every climate calculation starts from, and the reason "CO₂ forcing is logarithmic, warming is not zero": each doubling adds the same ~3.7 W/m², and with feedback parameter λ ≈ 1 W/m²/K the no-feedback response is ~3.7 °C, the reference against which the ~3 °C ECS (feedback factor ~0.8) is defined.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="3.7"}
With feedback parameter λ = 1.0 W/m²/K, compute ECS for a CO₂ doubling.
::: solution
ΔT = ΔF/λ = 3.7/1.0 = 3.7 °C — the no-feedback value; the assessed ECS of ~3 °C implies λ ≈ 1.2 (or feedback factor f ≈ 0.78), and the AR6 2.5–4.0 °C range is, physically, a range in λ of ~0.9–1.5 — the entire sensitivity debate compressed into one number's uncertainty.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="3.36"}
AR6's pattern statements give land warming at ~1.6× the global mean. For a 2081–2100 global warming of 2.1 °C (SSP2-4.5 central), what is the assessed land-surface warming?
::: solution
1.6 × 2.1 = 3.36 °C over land — the number that makes "1.5/2 °C" feel like "3+ °C" to anyone living inland away from the ocean, and the reason the "well below 2 °C" goal is, over the inhabited mid-latitude continents, a ~3 °C target for summer means and a ~4–5 °C target for the hottest days.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1.7"}
An uncertainty decomposition finds variance fractions for 2080s global warming of 65 % scenario, 25 % model, 10 % internal. If a policy study uses a single scenario and 20 models, by what factor does it understate the total standard deviation relative to the full scenario×model×variability design?
::: solution
Full σ² = 1.0; the study's σ² = 0.35 (model + variability) → σ ratio = √0.35 = 0.59, i.e. it understates σ by a factor 1/0.59 ≈ 1.7 — the standard "single-scenario studies cut the range by a third" result, and the reason the assessment reports run the full scenario×model×member cube and report the fan.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="6.3"}
Pattern scaling: the Arctic warms at 3× the global mean in the models' 2081–2100 SSP2-4.5 central (2.1 °C). Compute the Arctic warming, and state the two physical amplifiers behind the factor.
::: solution
3 × 2.1 = 6.3 °C — driven by (1) the **lapse-rate/planck term**: surface-amplified warming in a region where the surface energy budget is dominated by the seasonal ice–albedo and inversion structure, and (2) **amplification by the ice–albedo and snow-season feedbacks** plus the northward ocean and atmosphere heat transport (the "Arctic amplification" attribution literature's decomposition; the observed ~3–4× since 1979 is at the top of the models' historical range, the "is the Arctic rising faster than projected?" question that the 2020s satellite-era re-analyses keep finding "roughly at the top of, not beyond, the range").
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="7"}
By what percentage does the wettest-5-day-precipitation intensity scale per degree of land warming (the AR6 high-confidence Clausius–Clapeyron scaling)?
::: solution
~7 %/°C — the observed and modelled scaling of extreme daily precipitation over land, giving ~+14 % for the 2 °C world; the caveat that makes regional projections hard is that the *frequency* term (storm tracks, convection organisation, monsoon timing) is dynamical and uncertain, while the *intensity* term is thermodynamic and robust — the cleanest example in the whole projection literature of "what we know" versus "what we don't."
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.26"}
The Knutti–Sandstad effective-sample-size analysis finds a 40-member ensemble carries the information of ~12 independent models. If the ensemble's standard deviation of ECS is 0.9 °C, what is the standard error of the ensemble mean, and why does the naive σ/√40 mislead?
::: solution
σ_eff = 0.9/√12 = 0.26 °C vs the naive 0.9/√40 = 0.14 — the ensemble mean's true uncertainty is ~2× the naive value because members share parameterisations, code and designers (the "model genealogy" problem); the assessments handle this by widening ranges and by weighting schemes (the independent-climate-sensitivity and "equally-weighted-then-screened" debates), and the 2020s' "the ensemble is not a probability distribution" literature is the statistical footnote every projection headline should carry.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="2.7"}
A coastal utility compares the AR6 SSP2-4.5 likely sea-level central estimate (~0.55 m by 2100) with the ice-sheet-informed upper plausible bound (~1.5 m). Compute the ratio of the upper bound to the central estimate, and state which number the robust-decision framework designs against when failure is irreversible.
::: solution
1.5/0.55 = 2.7 — and the robust-decision answer is the upper plausible bound (or better, a staged-adaptation pathway robust across the range), because expected-value analysis requires a reliable probability for the tail, and this tail is *structural* — unbounded by model ensembles (the sea-level-range example above). The "avoid the unbounded, keep the options open" logic (the Lempert–Dessai and Hallegatte options-appraisal methods, the Dutch Delta Committees' practice) is the decision-science answer to deep uncertainty.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.15"}
The AR6 near-term lock-in: 2021–40 projected warming is ~1.5 °C in all scenarios, while the "current committed warming" plateau if emissions stopped today is assessed at ~1.35 °C. Compute the difference, and explain what it represents.
::: solution
1.5 − 1.35 = 0.15 °C — the warming still *in the pipeline from future emissions* under even the moderate scenarios, over and above what is already committed by the current atmosphere (the ocean's slow adjustment and the aerosol clean-up un-masking are baked into the 1.35). The projection lesson in one number: the 1.5 °C level is no longer a future target but a present commitment with a ~0.1–0.2 °C margin, and every tenth of a degree beyond it is a pure emissions question.
:::
:::

::: quiz
The correct interpretation of an AR6 "likely 1.8–2.5 °C for 2081–2100 under SSP2-4.5" range is:
- [ ] The world will warm between 1.8 and 2.5 °C under this scenario
- [x] If SSP2-4.5 emissions occur, the assessed central response has ~66–90 % probability of falling in that interval, with physically populated outcomes outside it
- [ ] 90 % of models predict exactly this
- [ ] The range is a measurement error bar
::: solution
**The probability-of-the-central-estimate reading**: "likely" is the IPCC's calibrated word for 66–90 %, the range covers the model-spread and variability around the *scenario's* central response, and the tails (higher sensitivity, ice-sheet processes, carbon-cycle surprises) are explicitly flagged elsewhere in the same chapter — the reading discipline that the AR6 uncertainty-guidance chapter (the 2022 "how to read the ranges" appendix) was written to enforce.
:::

::: summary
- Projections are conditional: the SSP-RCP scenario architecture separates the human choice (the dominant uncertainty beyond ~30 years — 1.4 to 4.6 °C by the 2080s across the assessed pathways) from the physical response, and the near-term (~1.5 °C in the 2020s–30s) is locked in across all of them.
- The CMIP ensemble is the field's laboratory: ECS assessed at 2.5–4.0 °C by three converging lines, model skill demonstrated on the fingerprint, the hiatus and the seasonal cycle, and the ensemble's own tails audited openly (the CMIP6 sensitivity outlier episode, the effective-sample-size correction).
- Read every range as three distributions — scenario, model, variability — with the variability term dominating at 10–20 years and for regional precipitation at any horizon; pattern scaling and downscaling add resolution, not information.
- The confidence hierarchy is explicit: temperature high, precipitation patterns high and amounts low, ice sheets and carbon-cycle feedbacks bounded by process judgement rather than ensembles — the "low-likelihood, high-impact" language is the honest instrument for unprecedented forcing.
- The frontier (storm-resolving models, emulators, learned physics, decadal prediction) is making the machine finer and cheaper, but the structural-uncertainty tail — ice sheets, AMOC, permafrost, Amazon — remains a judgement problem, which is why the decision sciences (robust strategies, options appraisal, upper-plausible design) are now part of the projection pipeline itself.
:::
