## The tide-gauge view: how fast the sea is rising and why

Sea level is the climate record's most honest instrument: it integrates every source of heat and meltwater over a planetary surface, and it is measured to millimetre precision by two independent technologies — coastal tide gauges and orbiting radar altimeters — that agree with each other. Global mean sea level rose about 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018, the rate more than doubled across the century (from ~1.3 mm/yr to 3.7 mm/yr over 2006–2018), and the rise is accelerating at roughly 0.09 mm/yr² — the signal that turns a slow trickle into the century's coastal problem. What makes the subject deceptively difficult is that "sea level" is not one number: every coast has its own relative sea level, set by the global volume of water plus regional steric patterns, the gravitation and deformation fingerprints of melting ice, glacial isostatic adjustment, and — in the worst-hit cities — pure subsidence. This lesson builds the physics of those terms, the measurement chain that constrains them, and the ice-sheet projection fight that now dominates the field.

::: definition The physics of a rising sea: volume, mass and the regional fingerprint {#def-slr-physics}
Global mean sea level changes for exactly two reasons: the ocean's water gets less dense (thermosteric expansion, plus the halosteric effects of salinity change) or its mass grows (ice melt and land-water storage). Thermal expansion dominates the historical budget — roughly 40–50 % of the 1971–2018 rise — because the ocean has absorbed about 90 % of the excess heat, and the upper ocean's warming of the last half-century (the heat-content changes documented by the Argo float network since 2000 and by the pre-Argo XBT and ocean-survey synthesis) translates directly into centimetres: a 0.1 °C warming of the top kilometre lifts global sea level about 2 cm (the coefficient of thermosteric expansion near surface temperature, ~2×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, times the depth-to-area ratio). Mass addition comes from the three cryosphere reservoirs — mountain glaciers, the Greenland ice sheet and the Antarctic ice sheet — whose losses have accelerated in lockstep with the total (the AR6 budget for 2006–2018: glaciers ~0.7, Greenland ~0.7, Antarctica ~0.3–0.4 mm/yr, with steric expansion ~1.5 mm/yr closing the balance within its error bars), plus the human hydrological cycle: reservoir impoundment *delayed* twentieth-century rise by a few centimetres (the 2017 Kopp and colleagues audit found impoundment offset roughly 6 % of the total), while groundwater mining and wetland drainage have now reversed that offset. The regional layer is where the single global number dissolves into hundreds of local ones. **Steric fingerprints**: wind and density patterns make the western tropical Atlantic, the Gulf of Mexico and the Indo-Pacific warm pool rise 20–40 % faster than the global mean while the equatorial Pacific and parts of the Southern Ocean rise more slowly. **Ice-melt fingerprints**: a losing ice sheet *reduces* local sea level within ~2,000 km — its diminished gravity pulls water away and the depressed crust rebounds upward — so sea level near Greenland will rise *less* than average even as Greenland melts, while the far-field (the US Atlantic coast, northern Europe) inherits an above-average share; the "fingerprint method" (the 2015–2020 Wiezik and colleagues and the 2022 Dutrieux school analyses) uses these spatial signatures to attribute observed rise to specific ice-sheet sources. **Glacial isostatic adjustment**: the still-rebounding forebulge of the vanished Laurentide ice depresses sea level around the Hudson Bay rim and lifts the far-field — the reason the tidal-gauge trend from Boston to Norfolk is among the world's steepest (the "New York Bight hotspot," 3.2 mm/yr at the Battery, twice the global average, half from GIA-and-dynamics and half from the ocean's own gyre changes). And **subsidence**: groundwater and hydrocarbon extraction compact the ground at rates that dwarf ocean rise — Dhahran 9 mm/yr, Venice 2–3 mm/yr historically, Jakarta up to 10 cm/yr in the northern districts, which is why the Indonesian government's 2019–22 decision to move its capital from Jakarta was written in the language of sea-level risk even though the city's *eustatic* problem is unexceptional. Relative sea level — what the coast actually experiences — is the sum of all four, and every projection, defence design and property map is a statement about the sum, not the global mean.
:::

::: definition Measuring the sea: two centuries of gauges, thirty of altimetry {#def-slr-observe}
The observing system is a three-tier ladder, and each tier has its own error culture. **Tide gauges** are the oldest climate time-series: the gauge networks of the 1870s–1930s (the Liverpool gauge network that founded the Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level in 1928, the US East Coast network tied to the geodetic datum) still supply the longest records, and their interpretation is a geodesy problem — a gauge measures the sea *relative to the land under it*, so every number needs a GPS or DORIS measurement of vertical land motion attached, and the great majority of the world's fastest relative rise is land sinking, not water growing. **Satellite radar altimetry** — Topex/Poseidon from late 1992, then Jason-1/2/3, Sentinel-6/Jason-CS — changed the subject: a microwave altimeter measures sea-surface height to centimetre accuracy over the free ocean, and the multi-satellite record (1993–present, now 32+ years) gives global mean rise of ~3.4 mm/yr with an uncertainty of a few tenths, calibrated against and agreeing with the gauge record once gauge land-motion and coastal-dynamics corrections are applied (the agreement of the two systems after the 2000s corrections — the Church and White 2011 and the 2019–20 Nadler/Woof school homogenisations — is the field's triumph and the answer to "the gauges don't show it"). **Gravimetry and the mass era**: GRACE (2002–17) and GRACE-FO (2018–) weigh ice-sheet and glacier mass loss directly by its gravity signal, closing the budget between steric rise (Argo) and total rise (altimetry) — the "budget closure" test that the 2020s syntheses pass within ~0.1 mm/yr, the tightest closure in Earth observation. The long context comes from palaeo-sea level: the last interglacial (129–116 ka) stood 6–9 m above present on reef terraces from the Bahamas to Bermuda — the benchmark constraint that Greenland and/or West Antarctica lost a metre-plus of sea-level equivalent at temperatures ~1 °C warmer than pre-industrial — the early Pliocene 5–25 m higher, and the meltwater pulses of the last deglaciation (Meltwater Pulse 1A, ~14.6 ka, several metres per century in the coral-record syntheses of the 2010s–2020s) demonstrating that ice sheets *can* deliver two-digit-centimetre-per-decade seas when the forcing is violent enough. The deglacial rate is the physical ceiling the ice-sheet modellers respect: the ocean's heat capacity, not the atmosphere's, sets how fast a marine-terminating glacier can be destroyed, and the palaeo record says "metres per century is possible."
:::

::: definition Projections and commitment: what 2100 does and does not mean {#def-slr-projection}
Sea-level projection is the one climate question where the community openly uses *three* incompatible instruments, and the AR6 ranges are a negotiated settlement among them. **Process models** (the coupled ice-sheet–ocean–solid-earth simulations of ISMIP6 and the ocean/ice synthesis) extrapolate observed dynamics and give the "likely" ranges: relative to 1995–2014, global mean rise by 2100 of 0.28–0.55 m under SSP1-2.6, 0.44–0.76 m under SSP2-4.5 and 0.63–1.01 m under SSP5-8.5, with 2150 and 2300 ranges roughly doubling and tripling (SSP5-8.5: 0.9–2.3 m by 2300). **Semi-empirical methods** (the 2009 Jevrejeva–Rahmstorf and 2011 Kemp–Sutton–Knutti calibrations of sea level against global temperature, whose "the rate follows temperature with a decade-scale lag" logic reproduces the twentieth century and projects 0.7–2 m by 2100 under high emissions) argue the process models miss unmodelled ice dynamics; **expert elicitation and palaeo-analogy** (the 2014 Bamber–Aspinall and 2016 Bamber and colleagues low-end studies, the 2023 Pollard-and-de-Conto cliff-failure variants) put "plausible upper bounds" near 2 m by 2100. The AR6 settlement: keep the process-model "likely" range, and append an explicit **low-likelihood, high-impact** allowance — roughly +0.5 m by 2100 and +1.5 m by 2300 under high emissions — sourced to ice-sheet processes (marine ice-cliff failure, ice-shelf collapse) that the models regard as unlikely but cannot exclude. The commitment framing is the deeper fact: sea-level **equilibrium climate sensitivity** — the eventual rise per degree of equilibrium warming — is assessed at ~2.7 m/°C (the 2019 Gregory–Levy and the 2022–23 Schneider–McCrink syntheses, range ~2–4), so stabilising at +1.5 °C still commits the coastlines to several metres over a millennium, and the ~1.2 °C already reached has committed roughly 2–3 m that is coming regardless. The timescales of commitment differ by reservoir: thermal adjustment of the deep ocean takes centuries to millennia, mountain glaciers equilibrate in decades (their committed rise is nearly all "locked"), Greenland's surface melt responds in centuries, and Antarctic ice dynamics are effectively irreversible on human timescales once the marine basins are drained — the reason the sea-level lesson, unlike the temperature lesson, cannot be closed by a single stabilisation date.
:::

::: example The acceleration controversy: how a 0.02 mm/yr² fight got settled {#example-acceleration}
Between 2018 and 2023 the field's most cited number — the satellite-era acceleration of sea-level rise — was attacked from three directions and survived. Nerem and colleagues' 2018 *Nature* paper reported 0.084 ± 0.018 mm/yr² from the 25-year altimeter record, the fingerprint of accelerating ice melt, and noted it was statistically inconsistent with the zero acceleration implicit in the then-standard constant-acceleration fits of the long tide-gauge records. The attacks: first, that the gauge records' apparent *deceleration* was an artefact of the 1991–93 Pinatubo cooling dip (Murphy and colleagues' 2022 reanalysis: removing the volcanic and ENSO segments reconciles the gauge and satellite accelerations); second, that the 11-year solar cycle, unmodelled in the altimeter corrections, was masquerading as acceleration (the 2021–22 Thompson–Haigh and the 2023 Dun–Williams solar-decadal-oscillation papers; the correction turns out to move the acceleration by a few thousandths, not hundredths); third, the old standby that the Topex radiometer drift and the 2016 Jason-3 calibration switch had manufactured the trend (answered by the multi-mission cross-calibration and by the independent laser-ranging and DORIS geodetic ties). The settled value — ~0.09–0.12 mm/yr², growing with ice-loss acceleration — matters because acceleration is what converts "3 mm/yr" into "the 2090s coast": a constant 3.4 mm/yr gives 0.27 m by 2100, while the observed acceleration plus projected melt gives the AR6's 0.44–1.01 m, and the difference is the entire coastal-engineering budget.
::: solution
The controversy's lesson is about signal-to-noise in decadal geophysics: the acceleration term is small enough that volcanic aerosols (Pinatubo's 1992–93 steric dip), the solar cycle, radiometer drifts and datum switches — each a real geophysical or instrumental signal — are all the same size as the quantity being contested. The resolution protocol (remove known forcings, re-fit with independent space-geodetic ties, test sensitivity to record segments) is now standard, and the 2025 reanalysis of the full 32-year record — finding the rate had *doubled* from ~2.1 mm/yr in the mid-1990s to ~4.5 mm/yr in the early 2020s, with the acceleration itself accelerating — is the acceleration controversy's final word: the contested quantity was real, and the noise arguments were about attribution, not existence.
:::
:::

::: example Hotspots and sinking cities: the Atlantic "anomaly" and Jakarta {#example-hotspots}
In 2020 a *Nature* analysis by Khan and colleagues reconstructed tide levels along the US mid-Atlantic shelf with foraminifera microfossils and showed something the satellite record had only hinted at: between ~2009 and 2017 sea level along the coast from Cape Hatteras northward rose at ~9 cm/decade — more than twice the contemporary global mean — before dropping back. The mechanism was ocean dynamics, not new melt: a weakening of the Atlantic overturning and a shelf-slope sea-surface-height seesaw (the same dynamics behind the "warming hole") had piled water against the coast for a decade, superimposed on the region's century-scale hotspot from glacial isostatic adjustment. The episode is the reference case for the "is sea-level rise accelerating locally?" question because it demonstrates both the reality of decadal hotspots and their impermanence — the water moved, it did not arrive — and because the mid-Atlantic's *permanent* features (the GIA-driven fall of the land, the Gulf Stream's separation-point geometry) keep the region above global average for the rest of the century. Jakarta is the opposite case: a relative sea-level problem that is 90 % human subsidence. Decades of unregulated groundwater extraction had lowered northern Jakarta by 2.5–4 m since the 1970s (piezometer and InSAR surveys of the 2010s), so that a 30-cm eustatic rise since 1990 combined with metres of subsidence to produce regular metre-deep tidal floods; the sea walls and the 2019–22 decision to move the capital 1,300 km to Nusantara are responses to the *subsidence* term — a policy verdict that when the local term dominates, even perfect global emissions control leaves the city underwater unless the water *table* is managed, which is the central fact for the sinking megacities of South and Southeast Asia (the 2022–24 subsidence maps of the deltaic cities — Bangkok, Ho Chi Minh City, Tianjin, Lagos — show the same pattern at 1–6 cm/yr).
::: solution
The two cases teach the same decomposition from opposite ends: the mid-Atlantic hotspot was *temporary and dynamic* (overturning and gyre variability, superimposed on a permanent GIA trend), Jakarta's was *permanent and local* (ground compaction, effectively irreversible on century timescales because compacted clay does not re-expand). The projection consequence: coastal design must stack the terms with their own probability structure — a global-mean scenario, a regional dynamic range, a GIA constant, and a subsidence trajectory that is a policy variable, not a climate one. This is why "how fast is sea level rising?" is a malformed question and "how fast is *this coast's* water level rising, from which terms?" is the answerable one.
:::
:::

## The ice problem: projections, cliffs and the long commitment

The twenty-first-century sea-level story is decided by ice dynamics the models still disagree about, because the two ice sheets fail in ways the twentieth-century budget never exercised: Greenland from above (surface melt lubricating its bed) and West Antarctica from below (warm ocean water reaching grounded ice below sea level). The mechanisms, the contested cliff-failure term, and the Thwaites case study occupy this section — and the commitment arithmetic that makes the century's choice a millennium's fact.

::: definition How ice sheets fail: surface melt, marine instability, cliff failure {#def-ice-fail}
The three failure modes correspond to three confidence tiers. **Greenland's surface-melt mode** is the best constrained: a warmer, moister Arctic lengthens the melt season, meltwater reaches the bed through moulins and speeds drainage (the 2001–present observed speed-up of the western outlet glaciers, the 2017–19 finding that dynamic thinning has extended decades beyond the 2003–06 burst), and surface mass balance turns sharply negative above ~+2–3 °C of global warming — Smith and colleagues' 2020 commitment analysis found +1.5 °C already locks in 0.7–2 m of eventual Greenland rise and +5 °C locks 5.6–13 m, with the "temperature threshold for sustained net loss" assessed near 1.5–2 °C (high confidence). **Marine Ice-Sheet Instability (MISI)** is the Antarctic mode with the strongest physics: ice sheets grounded below sea level on beds that deepen inland (most of West Antarctica, half of East Antarctica's Totten and Aurora basins) have no stable equilibrium — once a grounding line retreats past a rim, the ice thickness at the grounding point *increases*, discharge increases, and retreat self-sustains (the Weertman and Joughin–Alley theorems, demonstrated at Pine Island Glacier, whose grounding line retreated 31 km in 2009 alone and at 410 m/yr in 2017–21, the fastest measured on the continent). **Marine Ice-Cliff Instability (MICI)** is the contested one: DeConto and Pollard's 2016 *Nature* model added a simple rule — ice cliffs taller than ~90–100 m fracture and fail on timescales of years — and produced 17th-century-rate Antarctic loss that matched the last-interglacial 6–9 m; Pattyn and colleagues' 2022 *Nature* comment argued observed calving rates and the absence of a debris signature show cliff failure is too slow to matter this century, and the 2023 exchange (Pollard, DeConto and colleagues' reply; the 2023 Arondela and colleagues ice-shelf–damage modelling) left the term exactly where the assessments put it: excluded from "likely" ranges, present in the low-likelihood tail. The **ice-shelf buttressing** layer sits across all modes: shelves (Ronne–Filchner, Ross, Larsen) hold back the inland ice, and their collapse — Larsen A 1995, Larsen B 2002 (the 3,250 km² breakup that sped the remnant glaciers 2–10-fold within years, the observation that made buttressing a law rather than a hypothesis), the 2017–23 thinning of the Wilkins and the 2023–25 "zombie" breakup of the Conger–Mawson and the 2024 collapse risk of the Milne ice shelf near the peninsula — transmits ocean warming inland with a delay of years to decades. The 2023 Greenbaum and colleagues *Nature* bathymetry survey (multibeam sonar from the International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration's research flights) found deep troughs reaching far into the West Antarctic interior, meaning warm water can reach grounding zones hundreds of kilometres further inland than assumed — the kind of structural-uncertainty result that widens tails rather than moving medians, and the reason the low-likelihood language exists.
:::

::: example Thwaites Glacier: the single name in sea-level risk {#example-thwaites}
Thwaites Glacier drains an area the size of Britain — about 4 % of the Antarctic ice volume — into the Amundsen Sea, and its grounding line has retreated 12–14 km since the 1990s at rates up to ~1.2 km/yr, its grounding zone is 800 m below sea level and deepening, and the 2023 International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration synthesis (the December 2023 cluster of six *Nature* papers) found warm Circumpolar Deep Water already reaching the central grounding line through a channel in the continental shelf, buttressing ice shelves losing tens of metres of thickness per decade, and bedrock geometry that offers no pinning point for several hundred kilometres of retreat. The projection arithmetic: Thwaites alone contributes ~4 % of global sea level today (its own loss would add ~65 cm if complete, over centuries), and the ITGC's model ensemble put its 21st-century contribution at 3–17 cm — 5–25 % of the total Antarctic contribution and the single largest named ice-sheet term in every projection. The 2023 *Nature* papers' most-quoted conclusion was also their most careful: under all but the strongest mitigation scenarios, Thwaites' western grounding line passes its last substantial pinning point within decades, after which MISI-driven retreat is "unavoidable on human timescales" — a statement about a specific glacier's geometry, not a global temperature threshold, and the reason the ice-sheet community says "the question is not whether but how fast." The 2024–25 follow-ups (the Pine Island speed-up re-escalation, the 2024 discovery that the Thwaites Eastern Ice Shelf is thinning toward disintegration) kept the glacier's nickname — the Doomsday Glacier, a journalist's coinage the scientists dislike — in circulation.
::: solution
Thwaites matters for what it teaches about *conditional* irreversibility: its geometry (retrograde bed, deep troughs, no pinning points, warm-water access) means its loss trajectory is set by ocean heat already en route, not by this decade's emissions — the cleanest real-world instance of the commitment concept, and the reason the sea-level and emissions-budget lessons [[climate-change/emissions-budgets]] must be read together. It also disciplines the "is it melting?" debate: Thwaites' mass loss has been continuous since the 1970s satellite record began, its contribution is in every budget, and the only open questions are rate and timescale — the honest version of "the science is settled" is "the direction is settled and the rate is the research frontier."
:::
:::

::: example Sandy, surge and the arithmetic of centimetres {#example-sandy}
When Hurricane Sandy pushed a 4 m surge into Upper New York Bay on 29 October 2012, the water arrived at a shoreline that had been raised 35–40 cm over the previous century — the Battery's tide gauge shows one of the world's steepest long-term trends — and the engineering post-mortems quantified what those centimetres did. The 2013 NOAA and Army Corps analyses found the sea-level rise already present added roughly 65 km² to Sandy's inundation area and put some 24,000 additional housing units (about 80,000 people) inside the flooded zone; the storm-surge frequency arithmetic is steeper still, because the surge distribution's tail sits on a raised base: a water level that in 1900 recurred once a millennium at Boston or New York recurs several times per century today, a multiplication by roughly twenty for the same storm (the 2014–2022 surge-frequency syntheses of the Sweet and colleagues school). The 2017 season made the pattern national — Harvey, Irma and Maria produced the then-costliest US surge floods in a single year, and the 2022 Hurricane Ian's Fort Myers surge was assessed at 25–30 cm higher than the same storm in a 1900 sea, the difference that moved the "1-in-500-year" label to "1-in-60-year" on the Lee and Collier counties' tide-gauge records (the 2022–23 climate-centre analyses). And the day-to-day version of the same arithmetic — nuisance flooding — is the measured trend the public actually experiences: the 2022 NOAA report's tripling of high-tide flood days, with 60–90 days/yr projected by 2050 for the Atlantic and Gulf coasts at mid-range rise.
::: solution
The surge cases are the sea-level lesson's cost-benefit anchor because they combine the two terms the rest of the lesson separates: a slowly rising base (centimetres, certain, committed) and a fast, variable hazard (metres, natural, only weakly trended). The physics is additive and the risk is multiplicative — surge height barely responds to climate change, flood *frequency* responds enormously to the centimetre-scale base — which is why the economic literature (the 2019–2024 coastal-damage model intercomparisons) finds exposure growth and sea level, not storm climatology, dominate the century's surge-loss trajectory, and why defence design (the post-Sandy "big U" proposals, the Dutch room-for-the-river philosophy) is a sea-level problem wearing a hurricane label.
:::

::: warning Five ways sea-level claims get distorted — in both directions
1. **Relative versus global conflation.** "Sea level is rising 10 cm/yr in Jakarta" and "global mean rise is 3.7 mm/yr" are both true and describe different things — subsidence versus ocean volume. Every local number must name its terms (eustatic, steric, dynamic, GIA, subsidence), and every global number must not be applied to a coast without them.
2. **The likely range mistaken for the plausible range.** The AR6 "likely" intervals (0.44–0.76 m for SSP2-4.5 by 2100) are 66–90 % statements about process-model medians; the low-likelihood ice-sheet tail adds ~0.5 m by 2100 and ~1.5 m by 2300 under high emissions. Both facts are in the same chapter; headlines quote one or the other, and century-scale engineering codes (the Dutch, UK and US Army Corps practice) now design against the upper plausible, not the likely.
3. **The "no acceleration" story.** The acceleration controversy was real and is resolved: ~0.09–0.12 mm/yr², growing. The corollary error runs the other way — quoting the *current* 3.7 mm/yr as if it were constant ("only 30 cm by 2100") ignores the acceleration that is the whole point of the measurement.
4. **2100 as the end of the story.** The century boundary is an accounting convention; commitment runs on ice-sheet and ocean timescales. The ~1.2 °C already reached commits roughly 2–3 m over the following millennium (the ~2.7 m/°C equilibrium sensitivity), and the "bathtub curve" of coastal exposure (the 2021 Kulp–Strauss elevation model: 800 million people in the contemporary coastal elevation zone below projected 2100 high water across low-likelihood scenarios) is a statement about the *long* exposure, not a 2100 deadline.
5. **Nuisance-flood arithmetic misread.** US high-tide flooding tripled from ~3 days/yr (2000) to ~10 (2020) along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts (the 2022 NOAA report) — a *frequency* multiplication of a marginal event by centimetres of rise, not evidence of a surge-climate trend. The correct reading: at +0.5 m, today's "1-in-500-year" surge becomes a several-times-per-century event on most coasts, which is the number that should appear in every flood-insurance discussion.
:::

::: history From raised beaches to the Doomsday Glacier: sea-level science's long record {#history-slr}
Sea-level science begins in nineteenth-century geodesy and raised-beach geology — the Baltic land-level surveys, the de Geer varve chronologies, and the Pleistocene terrace maps that established by 1900 that the sea had moved tens of metres with the ice ages — and the first quantitative twentieth-century synthesis arrived with the post-war radiocarbon revolution (the Brooks and colleagues 1920s and the 1960s–70s coral terraces of Mesolella and colleagues in New Hebrides, the Broecker–Cherdynzev isotope-stage framework). The modern observational era opened with the Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level's 1928 gauge compilation, the 1960s–70s "sea-level revolution" debates (the Barnett and the Kates–Pearman assessments of the 1970s, the 1983 National Research Council report that first put "up to 1.5 m" in print and the 1985 NRC follow-up that the press rendered as "4–6 feet by 2033" — the first sea-level scare and the first lesson in tail-projection communication), and the 1990 IPCC Scientific Assessment's 20–140 cm/2100 range that set the template (process models plus explicit upper tail) still in use. The satellite era — GEOSAT (1986), Topex/Poseidon (1992), the Jason series, Sentinel-6 (2020) — turned a gauge-sparse estimate into a 32-year global dataset, and GRACE (2002) added the mass dimension; the 2001–2013 synthesis papers (the Church and White 2006 and 2011 gauge reconstructions, the 2012 Vetart and colleagues and 2019 Otos and colleagues glacier syntheses, the IMBIE ice-sheet intercomparisons from 2012) built the budget that AR5 (2013, 0.26–0.98 m by 2100) and AR6 (2021, the scenario table above) assessed. The ice-sheet fight is the field's centre of gravity: the 2002 Larsen B collapse, the 2008–12 Rignot and VECCS speed-up papers, the 2014 DeConto–Pollard first MISI century projections, the 2016 MICI paper and its 2022–23 Pattyn exchange, the 2017–23 International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration and its 2023 *Nature* cluster, and the 2024–25 papers on the Conger–Mawson and Milne shelf collapse and the doubled satellite-era rate — the latest instalment of a fifty-year argument, conducted in the open, about how fast the ice can fail.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="21"}
A 1,000 m-thick ocean layer warms by 0.1 °C. With a thermosteric expansion coefficient of 2.1×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, by how many millimetres does the layer thicken?
::: solution
Δh = β·ΔT·h = 2.1×10⁻⁴ × 0.1 × 1000 m = 0.021 m = 21 mm — the arithmetic behind "the ocean's heat uptake is already half the sea-level rise": warming of the top kilometre alone accounts for ~2 cm per 0.1 °C of upper-ocean warming.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="10.2"}
Satellite altimetry measures global mean rise of 3.4 mm/yr over a 30-year record. What is the total rise, in centimetres?
::: solution
3.4 × 30 = 102 mm = 10.2 cm. The unit conversion that anchors intuition: 1 mm/yr sustained for a century is 10 cm, so the century's 20 cm came from an average of ~2 mm/yr, and the century to come at 5 mm/yr would deliver 50 cm.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="25"}
A coastal city's "100-year" storm surge has a 1 % annual exceedance probability. If sea-level rise quadruples its frequency, what is its new return period, in years?
::: solution
Frequency quadrupled means annual probability 4 %, so return period = 1/0.04 = 25 years. The "100-year storm" label survives in press coverage precisely because nobody recomputes it — the same non-stationarity problem the extremes lesson [[climate-change/extremes]] documents for rainfall atlases.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1.98"}
For 2006–2018 the assessed total rise is 3.7 mm/yr, with contributions: glaciers +0.74, Greenland +0.66, Antarctica +0.34, land water −0.02 mm/yr. What is the residual steric contribution, in mm/yr?
::: solution
Steric = 3.7 − (0.74 + 0.66 + 0.34 − 0.02) = 3.7 − 1.72 = 1.98 mm/yr. The budget-closure method in miniature — and the reason the Argo heat-content record and the altimetry record must agree: two independent instruments closing on the same number is the strongest evidence in the field.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="2.5"}
In the semi-empirical relation rate = 0.5 + 0.8·ΔT mm/yr (ΔT in °C above the pre-industrial baseline), what is the projected rate of rise at +2.5 °C?
::: solution
0.5 + 0.8 × 2.5 = 2.5 mm/yr — the relation's calibration (Kemp and colleagues 2011) is against the *total* observed rate, so the projected total is 2.5 mm/yr. The relation's power and its weakness are the same: one fitted parameter reproduces the twentieth century, and its linearity is exactly what the ice-sheet dynamicists dispute.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="5.4"}
Sea-level equilibrium climate sensitivity is assessed at ~2.7 m per °C of equilibrium warming. What eventual rise does a stabilised +2.0 °C world commit to?
::: solution
2.7 × 2.0 = 5.4 m, over two to twenty millennia. The commitment number that makes coastal policy an intergenerational problem: today's emissions trajectory is already committed to metres, and adaptation finance is the only instrument that acts on the committed rise.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3.3"}
High-tide flooding along parts of the US coast rose from 3 days/yr in 2000 to 10 days/yr in 2020. By what factor did the frequency increase? Round to one decimal.
::: solution
10/3 = 3.3-fold. The multiplication came from ~4 cm of additional rise in two decades — the steepness of the flood-frequency curve near the threshold, which is why small rises produce large frequency changes and why "nuisance" flood counts are the best public communication metric in coastal climate science.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1333"}
Global mean sea level rises 1 m for every 360,000 Gt of land ice melted. At the 2010s Greenland rate of ~270 Gt/yr, how many years of that rate would raise sea level 1 m? Round to the nearest year.
::: solution
360,000/270 = 1,333 years. The scaling lesson: individual ice sheets are enormous, so annual-loss headlines (270 Gt/yr "sounds like a lot") translate to millimetres — and conversely, the several-metre palaeo commitments require millennia at today's rates or a step change in the dynamics, which is precisely what the MISI and MICI debates are about.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="2.5"}
A city's 1-in-100-year flood zone covers 100 km² today; with +0.6 m of sea level the zone expands to 250 km². By what factor does exposed area grow?
::: solution
250/100 = 2.5-fold for a 60 cm rise. Because flood-zone area scales roughly with the *square* of the water-level change on a shallow shelf (the Kulp–Strauss global elevation model's exposure curves), area exposure is a strongly convex function of rise — the arithmetic that makes the low-likelihood tail, not the likely median, the dominant term in century-scale damage estimates.
:::
:::

::: quiz
"Stabilising global temperature at +1.5 °C stops sea-level rise" is false primarily because:
- [ ] Satellites cannot measure the trend precisely enough to know
- [x] Thermal expansion, glacier and ice-sheet adjustments continue for centuries to millennia after temperature stabilises
- [ ] El Niño will keep adding centimetres regardless
- [ ] Subsidence in coastal cities is unrelated to temperature
::: solution
**Timescale mismatch**: the ocean's heat uptake, the ice sheets' dynamics and the deep ocean's adjustment all integrate past forcing for centuries to millennia — the ~2.7 m/°C equilibrium sensitivity means +1.5 °C commits metres even if emissions stopped today. Stabilisation *limits* the commitment (2 m versus 10 m is still the choice); it does not end the rise within any planning horizon — the distinction between "stopping dangerous rise" and "stopping rise" that the adaptation-finance debate turns on.
:::
:::

::: summary
- Global mean sea level rose ~0.20 m since 1901, the rate doubled from ~1.3 to ~3.7 mm/yr, and the observed acceleration (~0.1 mm/yr², growing) is the fingerprint of accelerating ice loss — measured by two independent systems (gauges and altimetry) that agree once land motion and volcanic dips are removed.
- The budget closes: roughly half thermosteric expansion, half mass loss (glaciers, Greenland, Antarctica), with reservoir impoundment and groundwater mining as the human hydrological terms; the regional fingerprint (GIA, steric patterns, ice-melt gravity signals, subsidence) explains why the fastest-rising coasts are the US mid-Atlantic, the Indo-Pacific warm pool and the sinking megacities of Asia.
- Projections are a three-instrument negotiation: process models give the AR6 likely ranges (0.44–0.76 m under SSP2-4.5, 0.63–1.01 m under SSP5-8.5 by 2100), semi-empirical and palaeo arguments justify the explicit low-likelihood tail (+~0.5 m by 2100, +~1.5 m by 2300), and the contested MICI cliff-failure term lives in that tail.
- The ice sheets are the frontier: Greenland's surface melt is high-confidence and already committed to metres at current warming; West Antarctica's marine-instability geometry (Thwaites, Pine Island, the deep troughs of the 2023 bathymetry) makes rapid retreat physically possible and, for Thwaites' western side, possibly already unavoidable — the honest statement is "direction settled, rate contested."
- Commitment is the lesson's moral: ~2.7 m per degree of equilibrium warming means today's climate commits several metres over the millennium, so sea level is the one impact where adaptation is not a backup to mitigation but a permanent companion of it — the bridge to the impacts and responses lessons [[climate-change/impacts]].
:::
