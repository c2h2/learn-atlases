On 13 July 2024 the global mean near-surface temperature — that improbable average of every weather station, ship and buoy on Earth — reached 15.10 °C above the absolute zero of the record-keeping, 2.19 °C above the 1991–2020 July average, and for the first time in the instrumental record (and probably in the entire Holocene) the planet spent a full twelve months more than **1.5 °C above the 1850–1900 preindustrial baseline**. The 12-month figure wobbles back below 1.5 later that year — one does not "breach" the Paris threshold, which is defined over decades — but the year as a whole, 2024, landed at about +1.55 °C, making the first calendar year to do so, and it capped a run in which 2023 and 2024 broke the global heat record by margins (~0.15–0.2 °C) far outside the year-to-year noise the statistics can explain. This lesson is the inventory of what has actually been measured, because the credibility of everything that follows — attribution, budgets, projections, impacts — rests on whether the observation base can be trusted, and that base is now 175 years long, multi-platform, cross-checked by independent teams, and unambiguous in every domain where the physics predicts a signal: the surface, the ocean (which absorbed ~90 % of the excess energy), the cryosphere, and the atmosphere's composition and energy budget. The climate did change; the remarkable thing about the change is that its *pattern* — nights warming faster than days, the Arctic warming four times faster than the globe, the stratosphere cooling while everything below warms, the ocean gaining heat to 2,000 m depth — matches the fingerprint of greenhouse forcing and not of any proposed alternative.

## The temperature record and its audits

::: definition The global temperature records: construction, homogenisation, and what the numbers say {#def-temperature-records}
There is no single thermometer; there are five independent analyses of the same physics — HadCRUT5 (Met Office/UEA), Berkeley Earth, NOAA GlobalTemp, NASA GISTEMP v4, and the ECMWF's ERA5 reanalysis — built by different teams from different data (the land network of ~30,000 stations after homogenisation, ships and buoys for the ocean, and for reanalyses, the whole observing system fed through a weather model), and they agree to within hundredths of a degree on the century-scale trend. The construction is where the scepticism dies: land stations are **homogenised** for station moves and instrument changes using neighbour comparisons (the method that produced the US HCRA dataset and whose "urban bias" was tested and found negligible — urban and rural stations trend alike, the counter-intuitive result being that cities' heat islands are small and mostly nocturnal relative to the regional signal), and the ocean switched from **bucket to engine-intake** sampling in the early 20th century — a warm-side step of ~0.3 °C in raw engine data that the records correct using night-ship-air/sea differences (the Kennedy et al. and Coward–Rayner methods), a correction whose size and sign were independently confirmed by volcanic-cooling fingerprints. The headline numbers (AR6 and the 2024 state of the climate reports): human-induced warming reached **1.07 °C for 2011–2020 above 1850–1900** (observed surface warming 1.09 °C, the difference being the ~0.1–0.2 °C masking by aerosols and internal variability), the rate over the past 50 years is **~0.2 °C per decade** (unprecedented over at least the last 2,000 years for the multi-century comparisons — the recent rate is at least three times larger than any 50-year trend in the Common Era reconstructions), and 2023, 2024 and 2025 have run 1.45–1.55 °C above 1850–1900, with 2023's jump (~0.2 °C above 2022) attributed by attribution studies to the Hunga Tonga eruption's stratospheric water vapour (a small positive forcing), the end of the La Niña, and possibly declining shipping sulphate — with the honest scientific answer being that the exact decomposition is still being worked and the *trend* does not depend on it. The **vertical structure** is the fingerprint: the troposphere warms (radiosondes, satellites since 1979 after the RSS/UAH channel-drift controversies were resolved by the group's own 2022–23 reanalyses, which now show land warming consistent with surface records), the **stratosphere cools** (−0.4 to −0.6 °C per decade at 20 km, and the "stratospheric footprint" is the single observation that rules out solar forcing, which would warm both layers), and the **night-time minimum has warmed faster than the day-time maximum** globally (~0.15 vs ~0.11 °C per decade over land), with cold extremes declining roughly twice as fast as warm extremes rise — the asymmetric signature predicted for greenhouse forcing and the reason "global warming" is a misleading name for what the temperature distribution actually does.
:::

::: definition The ocean: heat, carbon, oxygen and the acidification arithmetic {#def-ocean-change}
The ocean is the climate system's memory and its landfill: it has absorbed **~90 % of the excess energy** and **~25–30 % of the emitted CO₂** since 1850, and every one of those absorptions is measured. **Ocean heat content** (OHC, the sum of temperature×heat capacity over depth, dominated by the upper 700 m but now tracked to 2,000 m by the Argo float network — nearly 4,000 profiling floats since 2000) shows a stably accelerating gain of **~1.5–2 × 10²² J per year** over the Argo era (0–2,000 m), equivalent to a global energy imbalance of ~1.3 W/m² — the number that makes the "hiatus" debate of 2000–2014 a closed case, since surface-warming pauses driven by Pacific wind patterns (the PDO/IPO and the deep-ocean heat sequestration later released in the 2014–16 El Niño) are *invisible* in OHC, which never paused. **Sea surface temperature** rose ~0.6 °C over the industrial era with the marine heatwave frequency now ~20–25 % of the ocean surface per year versus ~15–18 % in the 1980s (the 2023–24 North Atlantic marine heatwave, ~1 °C above the 1991–2020 summer mean for months, bleached Florida's reefs and the Florida Current's gulf-stream front), and the **Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC)** — measured by the RAPID array at 26.5°N since 2004 at ~17–20 Sv — shows no collapse but sits alongside a century of proxy and fingerprint evidence (the North Atlantic "warming hole" that is the AMOC's surface signature) keeping AR6's "likely no abrupt collapse this century" verdict paired with its low-confidence caveat. **Carbon and chemistry**: the surface ocean pH has fallen from ~8.2 to ~8.05 — a **30 % increase in hydrogen-ion concentration** (pH being logarithmic, the honest way to state it), with the aragonite saturation horizon shoaling hundreds of metres and seasonally corrosive water now reaching the shell-bearing pteropods of the California current and the Southern Ocean; **oxygen** has fallen ~2 % in the upper 1,000 m (the "oxygen minimum zones" expanding hundreds of metres poleward and vertically, with the Baltic and Black Sea dead zones the visible end of the physics: warming holds ~4 % less dissolved O₂ per °C and stratifies the column against ventilation). The **salinity pattern** — wet-get-wetter, dry-get-drier, visible as the ESCAPade salinity-contrast index rising ~0.015/decade — is the hydrological cycle intensifying at the global scale, the "global drying trend in soils" being its complicated, regionally-negotiated surface expression.
:::

::: example The 2023–24 temperature spike and how science audits a surprise {#example-2024spike}
Global mean surface temperature in 2023 reached ~1.45 °C above 1850–1900 and 2024 ~1.55 °C — 0.2 °C above 2022, a jump ~4–5× the standard deviation of natural year-to-year variability, which triggered the correct scientific response: a hunt for the causes rather than a victory lap. The candidate explanations, each with evidence: (1) the end of the 2020–23 La Niña (a triple-dip La Niña that had suppressed the record, its El Niño successor adding ~0.1–0.2 °C); (2) the Hunga Tonga eruption of January 2022, which injected ~145 Tg of water — an unusual *water* injection, 4–5 % of the stratospheric burden — with modelled global-mean warming of ~0.03–0.06 °C over 2–5 years (positive because water vapour is a greenhouse gas, unlike sulphate aerosols, and the eruption's own sulphate was too little and too low to cool); (3) the January 2020 shipping-fuel sulphate switch (IMO 2020), which un-masked ~0.05–0.15 °C of aerosol cooling over the shipping lanes with a well-documented microphysical cloud-albedo response; and (4) a possible boost to the baseline trend from reduced Antarctic sea ice and Indian Ocean variability. None of these is a "gotcha": the point of this exercise is that the **trend line** (0.19–0.27 °C/decade since 1980) passes within a whisker of 2023–24 once the ENSO and volcanic terms are removed, and every proposed explanation was itself a prediction of the physics (a warming world's ENSO swings ride a higher baseline; less sulphate means more warming; stratospheric water vapour warms). What does the existence of a fast, partially-explained jump do to the confidence in the 1.5 °C-per-decade framing of the Paris target?
::: solution
Nothing about the *physics* and a great deal about the *politics*: the decadal-scale thresholds of the Paris Agreement are defined over 20–30-year averages precisely to be robust to such jumps, and the 2023–24 spike's real content is that the 2010s-era carbon-budget arithmetic (the 1.5 °C remaining budget of ~500 GtCO₂ from 2020) was, if anything, optimistic — the warming per tonne (~0.45–0.65 °C per 1000 GtCO₂, the TCRE range) has not moved, so a spike that burns through a fraction of the remaining budget early is a budget fact, not a weather story.
:::
:::

::: example The 2,000-year temperature context: the hockey stick debate, settled {#example-hockeystick}
For two decades the "hockey stick" — Mann, Bradley & Hughes 1998, the first millennium-long Northern Hemisphere temperature reconstruction from tree rings, ice cores, corals and sediments — was the most attacked graph in science (the Wegman committee 2000, the "climategate" emails 2009, the BBC dispute 2005), and it is now the most independently re-run result in palaeoclimate: the PAGES2k consortium's 2019 reconstruction (700+ proxy records, 2,000 years, six continents) confirms the shape with modern methods — multi-proxy ensembles, Bayesian hierarchical models, and forward-modelling of the proxies themselves. The current assessed picture (AR6 Chapter 2, and the 2019 PAGES2k special issue): the **Medieval Climate Anomaly** (c. 950–1250) was warm *regionally* (Norway, parts of North America and China) but not globally coherent, and the **Little Ice Age** (c. 1300–1850, with its worst century 1600–1700 and the 1815 Tambora "year without a summer") was the coldest sustained interval of the last two millennia — both explicable by natural forcing (solar minima, the Samalas 1257 and other large eruptions) without any anthropogenic term. The quantitative verdicts: the last decade of the record is warmer than any 50-year period in at least 1,000 (likely) to 2,000 (high confidence for the Northern Hemisphere) years; the *rate* of 20th–21st century warming exceeds any multi-decadal change in the reconstructions by a factor of several; and the reconstructions' **variance** is smaller than instrumental variability at multi-decadal scales — partly real (the preindustrial world was less noisy without fossil-forcing amplification), partly the "low-frequency damping" problem of proxy methods, which the field addresses openly with the spectral-adjustment techniques (EMOS, "vt2015") that *increase* rather than decrease the reconstructed natural range. The lesson generalises to all of observation: the instrumental record is short, the proxy record is noisy, and the two agree exactly where they overlap — which is the definition of a validated measurement system.
::: solution
The reconstruction's modern re-runs (PAGES2k 2019, the Moberg and Esper schools' different methods) bracket the original within their uncertainty bands while disagreeing about the amplitude of the Medieval–Little Ice Age swings — the pattern of a field converging on the robust conclusion (the 20th-century upswing is unprecedented in the window) while still arguing the details (how cold was the LIA, how warm was MCA). The 1.5 °C of 2024 sits above the entire reconstructed range with no overlap in the 2,000-year window.
:::

## Ice, snow, and the water cycle

::: definition The cryosphere: the measurements and the scores {#def-cryosphere-change}
Snow and ice are the climate's most legible instruments because their response is a *level*, not a reading: mass balance integrates weather into geometry. **Arctic sea ice** is the most extreme case: the September minimum extent has fallen **~12–13 % per decade** since 1979 (the satellite record), from ~7 million km² in the late 1970s to a record 4.12 million km² in 2012 and ~4.3–5.3 million km² in the 2020s, while **thickness** (submarine sonar, ICESat, CryoSat) fell from ~3.1 m to ~1.8 m in the deep basins — a volume loss of ~75 % — so that the multi-year ice that survived summers is now a fraction of its 1980s area; the "ice-free September" definition (1 million km²) is projected mid-century on all AR6 pathways, with the first such event possible in the 2030s on high-emission tracks (the 2020 and 2020s June–July Siberian heatwaves and the 2022/2023/2024 minimum sets are the running calibration). **Antarctic sea ice** broke the opposite pattern: after a near-flat to slightly rising total (the "Antarctic paradox", partly the ozone-hole-driven wind intensification isolating the continent), it collapsed to records of 1.5–2.0 million km² below normal in 2023 and 2024 — a two-year regime shift whose mechanism (ocean heat, the Weddell and Ross sectors' polynya activity, possible predictability limits) is the open question of the year, and a reminder that the ice–ocean system's stability can be non-linear on decadal scales. **Greenland** has lost mass every year since 1998 (GRACE and GRACE-FO gravimetry plus altimetry and input–output: **~270 Gt/yr** over 2002–23, the surface melt now contributing ~60 % of the loss and the Jakobshavn/Sermeq-Kujalleq outlet glaciers having sped up and retreated past their grounding-point pinning points), and **Antarctica** ~150 Gt/yr (concentrated in the Amundsen Sea sector — Thwaites and Pine Island — whose warm-circumpolar-deep-water intrusion under the ice shelves is the "marine ice-sheet instability" question of the century, the 2023 DoD-funded "doomsday glacier" survey's unglamorous finding being that the grounding line has retreated ~14 km in Thwaites' eastern tributary since 1992 and that the ice shelf's melt is running at the upper end). **Mountain glaciers** lost ~1.2 m of water-equivalent thickness per year in 2000–19 (the 2019 and 2023 "year in review" numbers), with the European Alps losing ~10 % of remaining volume in the two melt seasons 2022–23 alone (the Swiss GLOSH inventory's 2023 survey finding the 2022–23 melt the worst on record, ~10 % of the remaining Alps ice), and the **Hindu Kush–Himalaya**'s ~240 glaciers feeding ten major rivers losing mass at rates that make the "water tower" the most exposed freshwater reservoir on Earth. **Snow cover**: spring snow-cover extent in the Northern Hemisphere has declined ~1.5–2 % per decade since 1970 (the seasonal snowline rising ~1–2 m of elevation per decade on every continent surveyed), with the Western US snowpack — the storage reservoir of 7 billion people's water in aggregate — down ~15–20 % since 1950 (the April 1 snow-water-equivalent decline at 2,000–3,000 m in the Sierra and Cascades being partly a *phase* effect: precipitation increasingly falls and melts as rain, the "snowline" having risen ~70 m since 1986 in the Sierra).
:::

::: example The Alps' two bad summers {#example-alps}
The Swiss and French Alps lost an estimated ~10 % of their remaining glacier ice in the two melt seasons 2022 and 2023 combined: the 2022 summer (zero snow at 3,000 m in winter at several stations, record melt, and the first rain-on-snow event observed at the Jungfraujoch station at 3,580 m) followed by the 2023 summer (a further ~10 % of the remaining volume in the Swiss Alps per the national glacier monitoring network's preliminary figures, the "Matterhorn glacier paradise" ski area's permafrost so degraded that access routes were moved off the ice). The physics is a three-stage amplifier: (1) the winter snowline rose above the accumulation zone at many elevations, so the bare ice that absorbed ~60 % of summer sunlight instead of reflecting ~80 % of it was exposed earlier; (2) dust and Saharan-blowover darkening lowered albedo further; and (3) rock-slope permafrost — the cement of high-alpine terrain — warmed by ~0.5–1 °C at the Swiss PERMOS boreholes (Murtèl, Scex Rouge) over 2008–23, driving the rockfall and route-closure news that the guide companies now track as a business input. Quantify the albedo term: bare ice absorbs ~350 W/m² more net shortwave than snow at these latitudes in June; over a 100-day melt season that is enough energy to melt roughly how many metres of ice (latent heat 334 kJ/kg, ice density 900 kg/m³)?
::: solution
350 W/m² × 100 d × 86,400 s/d = 3.0×10⁹ J/m²; ÷ (334,000 J/kg × 900 kg/m³) = 10 m of ice — the reason a snowline that rises 300 m in elevation (the observed European shift since the 1970s) translates into metres-per-year of glacier thinning, and why the "elevation-dependent warming" of the high mountains is one of the clearest amplifications of the global signal in the observations.
:::
:::

::: definition The hydrological cycle, clouds, and the energy budget: the measurements nobody talks about {#def-energy-water-change}
The most fundamental observation of this lesson is the one with the shortest record: Earth's **energy imbalance** — the difference between absorbed solar and emitted infrared at the top of atmosphere — measured by the CERES radiometers since 2001 at **~0.8–1.4 W/m²** (the number rising as the aerosol veil thins and the greenhouse blanket thickens), cross-checked against the ocean-heat-content budget (the two agree within their error bars, the strongest consistency test in climate science) and the deep-ocean warming rate. The **water cycle** responds at two speeds: thermodynamically, the Clausius–Clapeyron relation fixes **~7 % more water vapour per °C** of near-surface warming, and the observations confirm it — total-column water vapour has risen ~0.4–0.5 mm/yr (a ~4 % increase since 1970, satellite and GPS-radio-occultation networks), precipitable water and extreme-precipitation intensity rising together; dynamically, the storm tracks have shifted poleward ~0.5–1° latitude per decade (the AR6 "high confidence" verdict on the drying of the subtropics — the Mediterranean, the US Southwest, the Murray–Darling and southern Africa's multi-decadal "run of dry years" 2019–24 that ended in 2024's Indian-Ocean dipole rains). **Clouds** remain the largest uncertainty in the whole subject — and the largest *observed* ambiguity: the ISCCP, CALIPSO-CLOUDSAT (2006–) and MODIS records show regionally coherent changes (a poleward shift of the storm-track clouds, possible tropical-anvil and low-marine-cloud adjustments) but no consensus global cloud-feedback number, which is why the ECS range in AR6 (3.3 °C best estimate, likely 2.5–4) narrowed from AR5 mostly by *excluding* the very low and very high tails with emergent-constraint and process studies rather than by measuring the clouds directly. The **observed precipitation** picture (GPCC, GPCP, ERA5): global land precipitation up slightly (~1–2 %/decade since 1980 in heavy categories), the *intensity* of the heaviest daily events rising at close to the Clausius–Clapeyron rate over land (the US Northeast's extreme-precipitation share up ~30 % since 1958, Europe's 5-day maxima up ~0.5–1.5 mm/day per decade), and the **aridity and soil-moisture** picture regionally mixed — the "drying subtropics" robust, the mid-latitude land drying of the 2010s (the 2018–22 European and 2020–23 Horn of Africa droughts) partly attributed to the combination of the dynamical shift and the evaporative demand that warming adds regardless of rainfall.
:::

::: example Attribution of a single event: the 2021 Lytton heat dome {#example-lytton}
On 29 June 2021 the village of Lytton, British Columbia — a dry, interior, fire-prone town whose 2015 wildfire risk study had already flagged it — recorded 49.6 °C, breaking the Canadian record by 4.6 °C; the next day a fire driven by the same drought came through town and burned 90 % of the structures. World Weather Attribution's rapid analysis, published within weeks, made two quantitative claims now standard in the field: the heatwave was "virtually impossible" without climate change in the *preindustrial* climate (extreme even for the 2021 world, its return period in the modern climate ~1-in-300-to-1-in-1,000 years), and the *warming signal* — the 2–4 °C by which the event exceeded what the same atmospheric dynamics would have produced in 1900 — was attributable to the ~1.2 °C of global warming with the ridge's dynamics being natural. The methodological point that made this the template event: single events are attributed in *pairs of worlds* (the counterfactual climate simulation), the dynamical "cause" (the omega block) stays unattributed, and the *impact* — the fire that destroyed Lytton — is a compound-event question (heat + drought + fuels) that attribution science handles less well, a gap the 2023 Lahaina and 2023 Derna (Libya, Storm Daniel, ~11,300 deaths) analyses exposed. Walk through what was and was not claimed, and why the distinction between "the event was caused by climate change" and "the event was made 2–4 °C more intense" is the difference between a scientific result and a headline.
::: solution
The attribution is of the *intensity*, not the *occurrence*: ridges form naturally; the fuel of the ridge — a 2–5 °C hotter land surface with drier soils (the soil-moisture feedback contributing ~1–2 °C of the Lytton peak in model experiments) — is the attributed term. The compound chain (heat → drought → fire → smoke → further radiative effects) is where single-event attribution stops and risk-based attribution (how the probability of the *whole chain* shifted) begins — the frontier the field reached with the 2022–24 compound-event literature.
:::
:::

::: history The observation programme: from Tyndall's flasks to Argo's floats {#history-obs}
The measurement story has four acts. **Act I — the physics in the laboratory (1856–1950):** Eunice Foote's 1856 bellows-and-sunlight experiment and John Tyndall's 1859 thermopile-and-tube measurements established that water vapour and CO₂ absorb terrestrial radiation (and that N₂ and O₂ do not), Svante Arrhenius's 1896 hand calculation predicted ~5–6 °C for a CO₂ doubling (within a factor of 1.5 of AR6's central 3 °C — a famous coincidence of compensating errors), and Guy Callendar's 1938 paper compiled 147 stations' century of warming ("the Callendar effect") against the then-assumed oceanic absorption of excess CO₂. **Act II — the curve and the network (1958–1990):** Charles David Keeling's Mauna Loa and South Pole records (started for the 1957–58 International Geophysical Year) produced the Keeling curve — 315 ppm in 1958, the sawtooth of the biosphere's breathing, the ¹³C/¹²C decline that fingerprints fossil carbon — while the weather-services networks, the radiosonde archive, and the first satellite radiance measurements (the 1969–70 VTPR experiment) built the multi-platform base; the 1979 Charney report settled the *theory's* uncertainty range (1.5–4.5 °C, unchanged in 1979 and 2024 except for the tails) and the first climate research programmes (NOAA's ERALM, the US CO2 Program) institutionalised observation as a standing commitment. **Act III — the space and float era (1979–2010):** the satellite record (sea ice from 1972/79, GRACE gravimetry 2002, CALIPSO/CloudSat 2006, CERES 2001, ICESat CryoSat altimetry) and the **Argo float array** (2000–07 deployment, 4,000 floats profiling the ocean every 10 days, the single greatest oceanographic instrument since the HMS Challenger) turned the ocean's heat and the ice sheets' mass from extrapolations into measurements; the GRACE-FO continuation (2018) and Deep Argo (2024–) extend both records. **Act IV — the era of the audit (2010– ):** the surface-record homogenisation debates (the US HCRA network's release, the Berkeley Earth self-audit of 2011–12 that confirmed the surface-warming trend with the sceptics' own methods), the satellite-temperature channel-drift reconciliation (the RSS 2022 revision, the UAH group's own corrections), the ocean-heat-content intercomparison (the IPCC-chapterled multi-team reconciliation of XBT-vs-Argo era biases), and the post-2023 spike audits — a field whose most distinctive feature is that its loudest controversies have been *self-inflicted*, each ending with the original number confirmed or slightly revised upward.
:::

::: quiz
The single observational fact that most directly rules out "the Sun caused recent warming" is:
- [ ] CO₂ rising at Mauna Loa
- [x] The stratosphere cooling while the troposphere and surface warm
- [ ] Sea level rising
- [ ] More record-hot days than record-cold days
::: solution
**A warming Sun would brighten the whole column — stratosphere included. Greenhouse trapping warms below and cools above** (less trapped heat escapes from the stratosphere to space, and the CO₂ increase there radiates more efficiently than it absorbs). Radiosonde, satellite and ozone-sonde records all show the stratospheric cooling (~0.5 °C/decade at 20 km) predicted for greenhouse forcing, while total solar irradiance (measured by satellite since 1978) shows no upward trend across the recent warming — the fingerprint argument in its cleanest form.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="90"}
Of the excess energy trapped by the greenhouse increase, roughly what percentage goes into the ocean (the figure AR6 states with high confidence), and why does this make "the atmosphere is where climate happens" a misleading frame?
::: solution
~90 %: the atmosphere's tiny share of the *energy* explains why surface air temperature is a noisy, fast-responding indicator while the ocean's huge share sets the committed warming and the long lag — the 90 % term is where the inertia, the "hiatus" decades, and most of the sea-level rise all live.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="30"}
Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from 280 to 420 ppm. Express the increase as a percentage, and state the pH change of the surface ocean it produced (from 8.2) and the corresponding percentage change in hydrogen-ion concentration.
::: solution
(420−280)/280 = 50 %; pH 8.2 → ~8.05, i.e. a 30 % increase in [H⁺] (10^0.15 ≈ 1.41 — the "30 % more acidic" figure; the pH number alone understates the change, which is why the acidification literature quotes [H⁺]).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="38.6"}
Arctic sea-ice September extent fell from 7.0 million km² (1979–83 mean) to 4.3 million km² (2024). Compute the total percentage decline over the satellite era.
::: solution
(7.0−4.3)/7.0 = 38.6 % — against the textbook's ~12–13 % per decade *linear* trend from the full satellite record; the two numbers describe the same loss under different estimators, which is why AR6 reports the trend, the endpoints and the record minimum (4.12 million km², 2012) together.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="26"}
Earth's energy imbalance is ~1.0 W/m² averaged over the globe. Express the total imbalance power in terawatts and compute how many times civilisation's total primary power use (~20 TW) it equals.
::: solution
Imbalance = 1.0 W/m² × 5.1×10¹⁴ m² = 5.1×10¹⁴ W = 510 TW; ÷ 20 TW = ~26 — the standard "the forcing we cause dwarfs our energy system" comparison: the planet is absorbing twenty-six civilisations' worth of extra heat, which is why mitigation is about the *fuel*, not the wattage.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="7"}
By approximately what percentage does saturation water-vapour capacity increase per 1 °C of warming near the surface (the Clausius–Clapeyron rate quoted throughout the extremes literature)?
::: solution
~7 %/°C: evaluating the Clausius–Clapeyron slope de_s/dT = L/(R_v T²) at 288 K gives 5,400/288² ≈ 6.5–7 %/K — the number that sets the pace of extreme-precipitation intensity, observed atmospheric water-vapour increase, and the "atmospheric river" rainfall rates, and it is why the humidity (not the temperature) is the variable that makes heat lethal.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1.14"}
Greenland loses ~270 Gt/yr and Antarctica ~150 Gt/yr of ice (GRACE era). How many mm/yr of sea-level equivalent do they contribute together (1 mm of global sea-level equivalent = 367 Gt of water)?
::: solution
(270+150)/367 = 1.14 mm/yr — about a quarter of the observed ~4.5 mm/yr total rise, the rest being ocean thermal expansion (~50 %) and mountain glaciers and land water (~0.5–1 mm/yr); the ice sheets are the term with the *option value* (in both senses) in every sea-level projection.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="4"}
The 2023 global temperature jump was ~0.2 °C above 2022, against a natural year-to-year standard deviation of ~0.05 °C. How many sigma is the jump, and if the trend since 1980 is 0.22 °C/decade, how many years of trend does one record-shattering year represent?
::: solution
0.2/0.05 = 4σ (a ~1-in-30,000-year event under stationarity — which is exactly the argument that the distribution itself has shifted); 0.2/0.022 = 9 years of trend in one year — the arithmetic of why single years make headlines but the 20-year mean is the Paris-relevant statistic.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.93"}
Argo measures ocean heat content gain of ~1.5×10²² J/yr (0–2,000 m). Convert this to W/m² over Earth's surface area (5.1×10¹⁴ m²).
::: solution
1.5×10²² J/yr ÷ 3.15×10⁷ s/yr = 4.75×10¹⁴ W; ÷ 5.1×10¹⁴ m² = 0.93 W/m² going into the ocean alone — against a CERES top-of-atmosphere imbalance of ~1.0–1.4 W/m², the ~90 % ocean share closes the planet's energy budget from two independent measurement systems (radiometers in orbit, floats in the sea) within their uncertainties, the strongest single confirmation of the greenhouse diagnosis in this lesson.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="12.5"}
If the remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C from 2020 was ~500 GtCO₂ (50 % chance) and emissions run ~40 GtCO₂/yr, in how many years from 2020 is the budget exhausted, and what does the 2023–24 spike's ~0.1 °C "overshoot" imply for the budget if the spike is partly permanent?
::: solution
500/40 = 12.5 years — the "2032" budget arithmetic of the 2018 IPCC Special Report, now re-dated to the early 2030s with 2024 emissions at ~41.6 GtCO₂ (Global Carbon Budget 2024); if ~0.05–0.1 °C of the spike persists, the budget shrinks by ~0.05/0.45 × 500 ≈ 55–110 GtCO₂ (a year or more of emissions) — the arithmetic that makes every warm year a budget year.
:::

::: quiz
The "hiatus" or "pause" in warming of 1998–2014 was ultimately explained (and the pause retired as an artefact) primarily by:
- [ ] New stations showing the old ones were biased
- [x] Ocean heat content showing no pause, plus corrected SST sampling and the Pacific's deep-heat sequestration later released by the 2015–16 El Niño
- [ ] A recalibration of satellites
- [ ] The pause having been real but harmless
::: solution
**OHC never paused**: the Argo-era ocean kept gaining heat through the surface-slow decade (the Pacific PDO/IPO phase storing heat at depth, much of it in the Southern Ocean, and the 2015–16 El Niño releasing part of it), while the surface records themselves were revised upward by the boat-vs-bucket bias correction (the Coward–Rayner/Kennedy methods) and the HadISST2 sampling fixes. The episode is now the textbook case of why the *multi-domain* observation base — not any single temperature line — is what the assessments assess.
:::
:::

::: summary
- Surface warming is 1.07–1.09 °C above 1850–1900 (AR6), rising ~0.2 °C/decade, with 2023–24 the first years past 1.45–1.55 °C; five independent record teams agree, and the record's own audits (homogenisation, bucket-to-engine corrections, satellite channel drifts) have each confirmed or raised the trend.
- The fingerprint is patterned, not scalar: stratosphere cooling under a warming troposphere, nights faster than days, cold extremes falling faster than warm rising, the Arctic at 3–4× the global rate — greenhouse forcing, not solar, not internal variability alone.
- The ocean holds the story: ~90 % of the excess energy (OHC rising ~1.5–2×10²² J/yr, never "paused"), ~30 % of the carbon (pH 8.2→8.05, +30 % [H⁺]), less oxygen (−2 % upper 1,000 m), and the AMOC's monitored but unresolved stability.
- The cryosphere is the legible ledger: Arctic September ice −12–13 %/decade and −75 % volume, Greenland ~270 Gt/yr, Antarctica ~150 Gt/yr with the Amundsen Sea sector the watch-list, mountain glaciers −1.2 m w.e./yr and the Alps down ~10 % in two summers, Northern Hemisphere spring snow −1.5–2 %/decade.
- The water cycle is intensifying at Clausius–Clapeyron's ~7 %/°C (water vapour and heavy-rain intensity both rising) with the storm tracks and the arid belt moving poleward; the energy imbalance (~1 W/m², CERES vs Argo closing within errors) is the single number that makes the whole diagnosis one consistent measurement.
:::
