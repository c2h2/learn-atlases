## The risk framework — and the biology that is already moving

The impacts chapter of an atlas used to be a catalogue of projections. Since AR6 (2022) it opens differently: *observed impacts have been attributed to human-caused climate change* — high confidence, a whole chapter of them — and the assessment's organising concept is not "warming" but **risk**, the product of hazard, exposure and vulnerability. That reframing is this lesson's spine. A glacier retreats because of the hazard; a city runs out of water because of the hazard *times* its dependence on that glacier *times* its inability to pipe water from elsewhere; and the two countries facing identical temperature changes can face incomparable risks because exposure and vulnerability — population, poverty, governance, the colour of one's passport — are doing most of the arithmetic. The lesson proceeds from the framework to the biology (species, reefs, forests: the systems whose response is already measured) to the human systems (water, food, health, cities) and ends where the assessments end: with the distribution of harm, which is the sharpest inequality number in all of science.

::: definition Climate risk: hazard, exposure, vulnerability — and their adaptation {#def-risk-framework}
AR6's definition is precise: climate risk arises from the *interaction* of climate hazard (the event or trend), exposure (the people, assets and ecosystems in its path) and vulnerability (the propensity to suffer harm — sensitivity plus the capacity to cope), with **adaptation** as the intervening variable that bends all three curves at once. The framework, inherited from the 2012 SREX special report and the hazards-course risk triangle ([[natural-hazards/risk]]), has four consequences the assessments hammer. First, *trends in impacts are not trends in hazards*: flood deaths per person have fallen by two orders of magnitude across the twentieth century in rich countries and by a large fraction globally (the WMO disaster atlas: from ~50,000 flood deaths/yr in the 1970s to a few thousand/yr in the 2010s despite more exposure) because warning, insurance and dams moved the vulnerability term — the single most hopeful fact in the impacts literature and the least reported. Second, *exposure is a choice*: 3.3 billion people live in contexts of high vulnerability (the AR6 headline), and the same report counts several hundred million more living in floodplains and low-lying coasts than a generation ago — decisions about where to build, not the climate, produced most of the flood-damage trend (the losses-normalisation literature of the 2020s). Third, *vulnerability is graded by everything except the climate*: the 2023 WMO analysis found ~90 % of disaster deaths in the 1970–2021 record occurred in low-income countries, and the 2024 *Nature* study by Kotz, Levermann and Wenz found income losses from warming already sustained since 1960 are ~20 times the value put on carbon globally — the *present-tense* distributional number. Fourth, the framework's frontier concepts: **soft limits** (adaptation possible but blocked by finance, knowledge or institutions) versus **hard limits** (no adaptation preserves the system — some reefs, some islands, some glaciers), and **loss and damage** (the residual harm after adaptation limits, given institutional home at the 2022 COP27 fund). The framework is not rhetoric: it determines which harms get counted, who pays for which, and why the same 0.5 °C is a manageable nuisance in Copenhagen and a civilisational event in Dhaka.
:::

::: definition The moving biota: ranges, phenology, mismatch and extinction risk {#def-biota}
The biological response to the ~1.2 °C achieved so far is documented well enough to be quantitative. **Ranges**: the meta-analytic average is a poleward shift of ~17 km per decade and an upward elevational shift of ~11 m per decade (the 2011 Chen and colleagues synthesis, updated with the same sign and marine-terrestrial contrast — ocean species move 3–4× farther per decade than terrestrial ones, tracking the faster-moving isotherms), with the strongest shifts in the tropics per degree (the "tropical squeeze": species living near their thermal maxima have nowhere to go — the 2022 Strugstad and colleagues *Global Change Biology* analysis found tropical terrestrial ectotherms already experiencing the largest fitness costs per degree, the "metabolic habitat loss" that makes 2 °C in the tropics physiologically like 4 °C in the temperate zone). **Phenology**: spring events (leaf-out, insect emergence, bird laying, coral spawning) have advanced by days per decade across the temperate record (the Parmesan and colleagues 2006 and 2018 syntheses), and the danger is not the shift itself but **mismatch** — the interdependent events tracking different cues (temperature versus daylength) decoupling, the canonical case the Dutch oak–winter-oak-moth–tits system (the 2006–2022 Visser lineage: great tits now miss the caterpillar peak in warm springs, with measurable breeding failure) and the 2020s coral-spawning versus temperature-cue studies. **Extinction risk**: the 2022 *Science* assessment (Urban and colleagues) put 4–8 % of ~50,000 evaluated species at high extinction risk from climate change alone at +2 °C, 8–12 % at +3 °C, and 12–16 % (rising to ~half of some poorly-dispersing taxa) at +4.3–5 °C — numbers that *add to*, not replace, the non-climate drivers behind the IPBES 2019 estimate of ~1 million species threatened (habitat loss and land use remain the dominant extinction driver this century; climate is the fastest-growing one). **Ecosystem reorganisations** are the observed-impact headline AR6 could finally assess with confidence: Arctic sea-ice biomes (the polar-bear, seal and Inuit-hunting system), alpine communities climbing to mountaintop "escalator to extinction" endpoints (the 2022 Steinke and colleagues *Nature* synthesis: local plant extinctions from climate already documented on European and Californian sky-islands), kelp forests (the 2011–2016 northeast Pacific warm blob's collapse of 90 %+ of local kelp, replaced by turf — a regime shift with hysteresis), and the tropical reef systems covered in the tipping lesson [[climate-change/tipping-points]]. The honest epistemics: observed-impact attribution is harder than hazard attribution (ecological baselines are noisy, confounded by land use and exploitation), and AR6's "human influence has contributed to observed ecosystem changes" verdict rests on the consistency of direction across thousands of independent local studies — the fingerprint logic applied to a million small experiments instead of one large one.
:::

::: example The Blob and the PNE: marine heatwaves as ecosystem experiments {#example-blob}
The 2013–2016 "warm blob" — a 200,000-km² mass of anomalously warm water that parked off North America, amplified by ~45 % according to the 2016 Jaumdish and colleagues attribution analysis into an event that would have been very rare unaided — and its successor, the 2019–2024 "Blob 2.0" / Pacific Node Event (PNE) that by 2023–24 had raised nearshore temperatures off the US West Coast by up to 25 °C in places, produced the largest unplanned ecosystem experiment in ocean history. The PNE's body count, catalogued in the 2023–25 synthesis papers (the 2023 *Oceanography* special issue, the 2024 *PNAS* and *Science* papers): ~1 billion (estimates range 0.5–2 billion) common murres died in the Gulf of Alaska in 2015–16 alone — 62 % of that population, the largest wildlife mortality event ever attributed to a marine heatwave (the 2020 Piatt and colleagues *PNAS* analysis); 36,000 northern fur-seal pups starved in the Bering Sea in 2023 (the 2024 NOAA report); the 2023–24 PNE collapsed the Gulf of Alaska cod fishery to closure levels, pushed Dungeness crab fisheries to repeated shutdowns, and carried subtropical species (vampire squid, yellowtail) to Alaska. The mechanisms are now textbook: the heatwave killed the small lipid-rich copepods and left only jelly-like low-calorie prey (the 2023 *Science* "energy flux" papers: the food web's energy available to predators fell by ~70 % at peak), the warm water stratified and starved the shelf, and the 2023 addition of an unprecedented freshwater lid (the 2023 atmospheric-river year) extended the anomaly's reach. The 2024–25 verdict: the PNE's marine ecosystem has *not* returned to its pre-2014 state at the northern margin — the regime-shift question the kelp and murre data pose is the marine version of the tipping lesson, with the difference that the ocean's "thresholds" are being crossed at a pace the geological record rarely matches.
::: solution
The marine-heatwave decade supplies the impacts lesson's cleanest causal chain — hazard (attribution-amplified heatwave) to mechanism (size and lipid mismatch in the food web) to impact (documented million-scale mortality, fishery closure) — because the ocean concentrates the signal (heat capacity makes the anomalies enormous) and the fisheries data provide the economic tail. It also demonstrates the **persistence** problem: murres have not recovered a decade on, kelp remains turf-dominated in British Columbia and Tasmania (the 2021–24 "greening" reversals in some Tasmanian reserves showing recovery is possible where urchins are grazed back), and the 2024 finding that marine-heatwave *frequency* has doubled globally since 1982 (the 2023–24 Oliver and colleagues updates) means the recovery interval, not the event, is now the binding constraint on marine ecosystems.
:::
:::

::: example Australia 2016–2020: the compound-event laboratory {#example-australia}
Between the 2016 coral bleaching (two-thirds of the Great Barrier Reef's corals affected in the back-to-back 2016–17 events, the 2018 Hughes and colleagues *Nature* surveys — the first event with formal attribution: the 2016 bleaching made ~1.75× more likely by warming, the King and colleagues 2016 analysis) and the 2019–20 "Black Summer" fire season (18.6 million ha burned and ~3 billion animals killed or displaced — the 2020 Boon and colleagues estimate, widely cited and methodologically debated), Australia ran the full compound sequence on a single continent: marine heatwave and reef bleaching (2016–17), the Millennium Drought's successor "Big Dry" (2017–19, southeast storages to 20 %), the 2019 record heat and fire season (the World Weather Attribution 2020 analysis: the hot, dry, fire-prone weather was ~30 % more likely from climate change), the catastrophic 2019–20 summer, then the 2021–22 La Niña "triple dip" that flooded the same catchments three times (the 2022 Sydney floods — twice-in-a-year 1-in-100-year events, the 2022 BoM and WWA analyses finding the heavy rainfall made ~25–50 % more intense by warming). The ecological ledger is the most complete anywhere: the 2021–23 studies documented the first climate-driven Australian mammal extinctions (the 2016 government delisting of the Bramble Cay melomys — the first mammal formally extinct with a climate cause on the IUCN-informed list — plus the 2022–23 reviews concluding that more endemic species should be listed as climate-threatened), the 2022–23 finding that the fire-bleaching-drought sequence has pushed parts of the Wet Tropics and the reef catchments into novel states, and the recovery science (the 2023–24 GBRMPA surveys: reef coral cover rebounding in some northern sectors while the 2024 sixth bleaching struck).
::: solution
Australia's value to the framework is the *sequence*: the hazards were individually attributable, individually moderate-to-extreme, and their clustering — a phenomenon the compound-event literature (the 2022 Zscheischler synthesis) says warming makes more likely through shared drivers (the Indian and Pacific Ocean states, the persistent anticyclone) — produced impacts no single-hazard analysis would have projected. The policy ledger cuts both ways and both belong in the lesson: Australia's per-capita emissions are among the developed world's highest and its adaptation capacity high, yet the reef and the wet tropics face hard-limit questions — proof that vulnerability and hazard are not the same axis, and that "developed country" protects balance sheets, not ecosystems.
:::
:::

## Water, food, health, cities — and who bears the cost

Human impacts follow the same hazard-exposure-vulnerability algebra with larger numbers and uglier geography. The assessed picture: water security and food systems are already degraded in the drylands and the tropics; health impacts are led by heat and are concentrated among the old, the poor and the outdoor-working; cities are the exposure epicentre; and the burden falls on those who contributed least — the arithmetic that makes climate a justice question as much as an environmental one.

::: definition Water and food: the two billion and the four crops {#def-water-food}
**Water.** Roughly 1–2 billion people depend on mountain snow and glacier storage as dry-season supply (the Hindu Kush Himalaya assessment's "third of a billion by century's end" arithmetic; the AR6 high-confidence statement that glacier retreat has *already* reduced water availability and hydropower potential in multiple regions — the 2021–23 observed impacts: the Andean puna's well failures, the 2022–23 record-low Himalayan snow that presaged the 2024 floods, the 2022 European drought that shut Rhine barging and cooling-water-limited reactors). The drylands are where the hazard and the exposure meet worst: the 2020–23 East African drought (five failed rainy seasons, 36 million food-insecure by 2023 — the 2023 WFP tally, with the attribution literature finding the *heat* strongly human-influenced and the rainfall's Indian-Ocean driver uncertain), the 2019–20 and 2023–24 southern Africa, the 2020–23 megadrought arithmetic of the Colorado (half human-caused, covered in the extremes lesson [[climate-change/extremes]]). **Food.** The yield sensitivities from the 2019 Zhao and colleagues *PNAS* multi-model ensemble — per +1 °C of global mean temperature, global yields decline ~6 % (wheat), ~7.4 % (maize), ~3.2 % (rice), ~3.1 % (soybean), *before* CO₂ fertilisation and adaptation — remain the citation anchor, and the 2024 *Nature* attribution analysis (Pan and colleagues) found the realised effect over 1961–2021: climate change has already slowed the growth of global calorie production by ~1/3 relative to a no-change counterfactual, with the loss concentrated in the tropics and the "green revolution" gains in the temperate zone partly climate-fuelled. The fisheries number: the 2022 *Science* analysis (Free and colleagues) found climate change has already reduced maximum sustainable catch by ~4–5 % globally and ~5–7 % in the tropics since 1930 — a present-tense hit on the protein of 3 billion people. The price-and-conflict tail: the 2010 Russian heatwave cut the wheat harvest ~25–30 % and the export ban that followed contributed to the 2010–11 spike in global wheat prices (the World Bank and Headey–Fan analyses), one link in the chain the 2015 May and colleagues *PNAS* study estimated contributed ~6–10 % of the variance in the timing of the Arab Spring uprisings — a finding the conflict-literature replication debates (the 2024 *Nature* climate-conflict meta-analysis: warming increases organised-violence risk ~11–14 % per °C of *global* warming, with strong regional concentration) have narrowed but not broken.
:::

::: definition Health and cities: the oldest deaths and the newest exposure {#def-health-cities}
**Health.** The Lancet Countdown's 2022–24 tallies anchor the numbers: heat-related deaths among people over 65 averaged ~489,000/yr in 2018–2022, a 37 % increase over the 1990s baseline (the 2023 report's headline; the 2024 update found a further record — 61 % above the 1990s), and the WHO's older 2030s projection (~250,000 additional deaths/yr from heat, floods, diarrhoea, malaria and childhood malnutrition combined, excluding the malnutrition-driven child stunting line) remains the policy anchor. The vector map is moving with the isotherms: the 2023–24 Americas dengue record (>6 million reported cases, PAHO) occurred in a range expanded both poleward and upward (the 2020s *Lancet* and *PNAS* analyses attribute a ~15–20 % expansion of *Aedes aegypti* climatic suitability since 1980 to warming, with urbanisation the co-author), and malaria's highland frontier (the 2022 Ethiopian and East African highland resurgences) tracks the same logic. The labour number is the economic one: the ILO's 2019 assessment projected heat stress to cost ~2.2 % of total hours worked in South Asia and West Africa by 2030 (tens of millions of full-time-equivalent jobs), and the 2021–24 studies found ~2–3 % of annual labour hours already lost to heat in the hottest regions — the mechanism behind the "heat divides nations" growth literature. **Cities.** The 2021 analysis that 96 % of cities have left or are leaving the Holocene-like climate niche in which agriculture and infrastructure were built (the Chen and colleagues *Environmental Research Letters* study: the average city's climate is heading toward an analogue ~3.5 °C warmer, two-thirds of cities toward analogues 23 °C warmer — the "climate analogues" framing) makes the urban chapter self-evident, and the exposure stack compounds: heat island plus heatwave (the 2021 Pacific Northwest and 2022 European mortality), coastal squeeze (the sea-level lesson's nuisance-flood arithmetic), and the informal-settlement trap — the ~1 billion residents of informal settlements concentrated on the floodplains, slopes and shores that formal development avoided, the AR6's single largest concentration of climate vulnerability. The adaptation-dividend counterweight belongs here too: the 1970 Bhola cyclone (300,000–500,000 deaths, pre-warning Bangladesh) versus the 2020s (cyclone deaths in the thousands at comparable intensity, the WMO attribution of the decline to warning and shelters) is the demonstrated proof that the vulnerability term is the most tractable in the risk equation — and the reason AR6's language on "feasible, effective, low-regret adaptation" is more hopeful than its language on hazards.
:::

::: example Pakistan 2022: the distribution of an attributable season {#example-pakistan}
The 2022 Pakistan sequence is the impacts lesson's reference case because every term of the risk equation appears in it with a number attached. The March–April 2022 northwest heatwave (40 °C in the normally temperate foothills, +9 °C above normal) was found — in the 2022 Irfan and colleagues and WWA analyses — to be ~30 times more likely under anthropogenic warming; it ripened the soil and the snowpack before the monsoon arrived. The June–September monsoon then delivered 390 mm in a single Mekran-coast week and a season total ~3× the 30-year mean (the Link and colleagues 2023 analysis attributed up to half of the July rainfall intensification to warming), while the Indus basin simultaneously carried melt from the heatwave-primed glaciers and lake outbursts from the 2021 "third-pole" heat — a compound flood no single-hazard model had projected. The exposure: a third of the country under water at peak, 8 million displaced, 1,700+ deaths, ~$30 billion in damages and reconstruction needs (the 2023 UN assessment) — 9 % of GDP for a country whose lifetime emissions contributed ~0.4 % of the CO₂ in the atmosphere. The vulnerability terms were explicit in the damage map: the Sindh smallholder and the Karachi urban poor (whose drowned slums were built in the 2010s flood encroachments the development authorities had mapped and not enforced against) versus the gated Bahria Town enclaves that the same flood spared. The loss-and-damage politics followed: Pakistan's finance minister chairing the G77's loss-and-damage group, the 2022 COP27 decision to create the fund, and the 2023–25 argument — unresolved at the time of writing — over whether "adaptation" finance that rebuilds the same floodplain counts against it.
::: solution
Pakistan 2022 is the case study because it is *simultaneously* attributable, compound, exposure-driven, vulnerability-graded and justice-framed — the five properties the impacts literature had developed separately. Its methodological lesson: single-event attribution of the *rainfall* (up to half) and of the *heatwave* (30-fold) are robust statements; attributing the *flood* requires the land and river engineering history too (the 2023 World Bank and ADB flood reports found a majority of the damage occurred on the 2010-floodplain encroachments — the exposure term doing what it usually does, which is dominate). Its justice arithmetic — 0.4 % of emissions, 9 % of GDP — is the empirical core of the loss-and-damage movement, and the reason the 2024–25 fund negotiations matter more to the impacts literature than any new projection.
:::
:::

::: example Cape Town's "Day Zero": the near-miss that became a template {#example-day-zero}
Cape Town's 2015–18 drought — three consecutive dry winters, reservoirs falling to 14 % of usable storage by January 2018, the city publicly calculating the date its taps would run dry ("Day Zero," moved from April to May to August 2018 as demand fell) — is the assessed reference case for urban water risk (the AR6 WGII case study; the 2018–19 Weather and Climate Extremities and WWA analyses). The attribution verdict is the lesson's in miniature: the rainfall shortfall over the catchments was within natural variability (the multi-century tree-ring and instrumental context: comparable droughts occurred in 1920s and in the reconstructed 1830s–40s), while the *heat* term — the ~1.5 °C of additional evaporation the warming trend added to the demand side — was found to have roughly tripled the odds of a Day-Zero-class event (the 2018 WWA and the 2019–21 follow-ups; the 2021 *Nature Sustainability* framing "a hot drought, not a dry one" in the region's attribution literature). The response is the world's only completed experiment in demand-side collapse of urban water use: tiered tariffs, curfews, pressure reduction and a public-shame campaign cut the metro's consumption by more than half in eighteen months — the "meanwhile, the dam in your head" campaign's numbers (4.5 to under 2.5 m³/household/day) are now in every utility textbook — and Day Zero never arrived. The equity ledger is equally explicit in the record: the informal settlements (the Cape Flats' water-point households, whose per-capita use was already a fiftieth of the wealthy southern suburbs') bore the strictest rationing, and the 2018–19 analyses counted the taps-per-household distribution as the primary vulnerability variable. And the governance aftermath is the maladaptation catalogue: the emergency desalination and groundwater projects that followed are now the case study in whether drought-driven infrastructure locks in high-carbon, high-cost supply (the 2022–24 water-sector reviews) or buys the resilience the next drought — which the 2022–23 winter recovery of storage to over 60 % showed was not paranoia.
::: solution
Day Zero's lesson for the framework is the independence of the terms: the hazard was natural (rainfall within the record), the amplification was climatic (the evaporation tax, attributable), the exposure was two decades of reservoir-building and in-city migration that out-ran the 1990s cap on water rights, and the vulnerability was the taps-per-household map. That decomposition — not "climate change caused the drought" — is the sentence the 2018 press cycle got wrong in both directions, and the reason the case now anchors both the attribution-confidence hierarchy (extremes lesson [[climate-change/extremes]]) and the urban-water adaptation literature: the city that halved its water use in eighteen months proved the demand side of the water equation is the most elastic term in the whole impacts lesson.
:::

::: warning Five ways impact and harm claims get distorted
1. **The everything-is-climate fallacy.** Flood *damages* rise with coastal construction, crop yields rose for fifty years *despite* warming, and the 2020s famine scares have repeatedly resolved into conflict-and-aid failures (the 2017 South Sudan and 2020s Yemen food crises are political-economy events with a climate subtitle). The attribution literature is deliberately narrow for a reason: "climate contributed" is a measured claim, "climate caused" is usually not.
2. **Hazard attribution swapped for impact attribution.** "The heatwave was made 5× more likely" says nothing automatic about the mortality — which depends on warning systems, housing and age structure (the 2003-versus-2015 European comparison). Both terms are real and they answer different questions; conflating them inflates some claims and hides the adaptation success.
3. **The static-vulnerability frame.** Projected 2100 damages assume the world of 2020 frozen in place; the actual century will include income growth, migration, technology and (sometimes) maladaptation. The honest assessments (the AR6 chapter structure, the 2023–25 synthesis updates) present damages as *pathways* — SSP1 versus SSP5 differ by factors of several at the same temperature — and the "climate alone" damage number is a thought experiment, not a forecast.
4. **The maladaptation blind spot.** Adaptation that shifts harm across space, time or population is counted as success in its own ledger: the 2023 *Nature* review's catalogue (seawalls that starve downdrift beaches, groundwater irrigation that mines the aquifer, air conditioning that loads the grid at peak heat) made "maladaptation" a formal AR6 category — the concept that separates adaptation finance that works from adaptation finance that books a project.
5. **The inequality number misquoted.** "The poorest half contributes 10 % of emissions" and "the top 10 % contribute half" are both true (the 2020–22 Oxfam and Lucas and colleagues distributional accounts) and both get misused: the accounts are of *consumption* emissions by income class, global and cumulative versions differ by factors of two, and the justice argument does not require the precise split — it requires only the sign, which every account agrees on.
:::

::: history From Malthus to the Santiago Network: impacts science's two centuries {#history-impacts}
Impacts science is older than climate science: the late-nineteenth-century "CO₂ makes the deserts bloom" speculations of agricultural chemistry (with Arrhenius's own 1893–96 notes welcoming a warmer Europe and Callendar's 1938 paper forecasting richer harvests), and the mid-century Soviet school (the Budyko programme, whose "the greenhouse warms the northern breadbasket" thesis remains the intellectual ancestor of every benefits-claim) preceded the modern era. The modern field opens with the 1970s Sahel famine (the 1973–75 disaster that made "desertification" a UN category and, through the Charney and the 1979 Charney-report biogeophysics, made human-albedo-and-vegetation feedbacks a research programme), runs through the 1980s ozone-and-acid-rain school that invented modern risk assessment, and the 1990s institutionalisation: the IPCC's 1995 WGII (the first assessment to say "the balance of evidence suggests a detectable human influence" — about impacts, not temperature), the 1990s damages-function debates (the Nordhaus DICE lineage and its critics), and the 2006–07 Stern Review's conversion of impacts into economics (the "5–20 % of GDP versus 1 % of mitigation" framing that dominated a decade of policy). The 2010s were the decade of the framework and the attribution: the 2012 SREX special report (the risk definition above), the AR5 WGII 2014 "risks of severe, pervasive and irreversible impacts" verdict, the World Weather Attribution rapid-attribution network's impact-side applications, and the 2018 SR1.5's 0.5 °C differential (the coral 70–90-versus-99 % number, the Cape Town and 1.5 °C episodes). The 2020s brought the observed-impact turn: the AR6 WGII 2022 chapter "observed and projected surface and freshwater change" and its attribution chapter (the first assessment to state, with high confidence, that human influence has contributed to *observed* ecosystem and food-system impacts), the 3.3-billion-vulnerable headline, the 2022 Pakistan and 2022–23 Horn of Africa loss accounting, the COP27 Santiago loss-and-damage fund, and the 2024 *Nature* distributional-damages papers — the moment the impacts literature's numbers became the reparations literature's numbers.
:::

**Exposure and vulnerability**: the hazard term is the one both countries share; the differences in deaths and losses come from population in harm's way, assets, poverty, warning systems, insurance and governance — which is why adaptation and development are not climate policy's afterthought but its largest available lever, and why the AR6's most hopeful fact (falling disaster mortality per person from 1970–2020) is a vulnerability story.

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="85"}
Meta-analyses find species' ranges shifting poleward at ~17 km per decade on average. How far, in km, is the average shift over 50 years?
::: solution
17 × 50 = 85 km — roughly the distance from London to the Lincolnshire coast, for the average studied species, in half a century; the number that makes "climate change is moving the biota" a measured fact rather than a projection, and the reason protected-area networks designed for stationary species need connectivity corridors.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="12"}
The multi-model ensemble finds global wheat yields decline ~6 % per °C of global mean warming. What is the assessed decline at +2 °C, before CO₂ fertilisation and adaptation?
::: solution
6 × 2 = 12 %. The two caveats are load-bearing: CO₂ fertilisation adds back several percent (and the net is what field experiments measure), and adaptation (earlier sowing, new cultivars) adds more — the 6 %/°C is the *no-adaptation* sensitivity that anchors the risk, not the expected outcome.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="1.47"}
Heat-related deaths among Europeans over 65 averaged ~0.49 million/yr in 2018–2022 (the Lancet Countdown). If the rate triples by 2100, how many millions of deaths per year would that be?
::: solution
0.49 × 3 = 1.47 million/yr. The projection arithmetic the adaptation chapter answers: the same tripling applied to 1990s mortality *rates* (which were themselves higher) is what the 2003-style events demonstrated adaptation prevents — the 2015 and 2022 European heatwaves were hotter per capita-lethally than 2003 at lower mortality, the demonstrated elasticity.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="15"}
Russia's 2010 heatwave cut the wheat harvest by ~25 %. From a 60 Mt harvest, how many megatonnes were lost?
::: solution
0.25 × 60 = 15 Mt — ~1.5 % of global production, withdrawn behind an export ban that helped trigger the 2010–11 wheat-price spike (the breadbasket-cascade mechanism: a single exporter's climate shock propagates through trade policy, not just yield).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="19"}
Coral reefs lose 70–90 % (midpoint 80 %) of their extent at +1.5 °C and more than 99 % at +2 °C. How many additional percentage points of loss does the half-degree from 1.5 to 2 °C add, using the 99 % figure?
::: solution
99 − 80 = 19 percentage points — the "0.5 °C difference" that the 2018 SR1.5 made famous: for reefs (and for ice sheets, megadroughts and poverty), the damage function is convex, so the last half-degree costs more than the first.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="9600"}
The 2021 climate-analogues study finds 96 % of cities are leaving the Holocene-like climate niche. In a world of 10,000 cities above 50,000 population, how many cities leave the niche?
::: solution
0.96 × 10,000 = 9,600. The framing's force is that the Holocene niche is where *infrastructure* — crop calendars, cooling norms, building codes, water engineering — was designed; leaving it is the exposure term's definition, and the 4 % that remain (mostly cool maritime cities) are the world's climate-lucky minority.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.12"}
A West African season has P(heatwave) = 0.30, and given a heatwave, P(drought) = 0.40. What is the probability of the compound hot-drought season?
::: solution
0.30 × 0.40 = 0.12 — a 1-in-8 compound season. The food-system arithmetic follows: with staple yields falling 20 % in compound years, expected annual production loss is 0.12 × 20 % = 2.4 %, which compounds through storage and prices — the reason compound-event *dependence*, not just frequency, drives the adaptation design.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="7.4"}
Distributional accounts find the global top 10 % of income earners cause ~52 % of consumption-based emissions and the bottom 50 % cause ~7 %. What is the ratio of the top decile's share to the bottom half's?
::: solution
52/7 = 7.4-fold — the inequality number's headline form. The caveats that keep it honest: consumption (not territorial) accounting, per-capita within-class averaging, and the fact that the bottom half's share *rises* with any development pathway — which is precisely why the carbon-space debate [[climate-change/emissions-budgets]] is a distributional fight in disguise.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="450"}
A 1-m surge threatens 500,000 people in a coastal city. Without warning the fatality rate is 0.1 %; with early warning, 0.01 %. How many deaths does the warning avert?
::: solution
500,000 × 0.001 = 500 deaths without warning; 500,000 × 0.0001 = 50 with it; 450 averted. The adaptation-dividend calculation in its purest form — the 2022 WMO finding that five-fold increases in early-warning coverage since 2015 have averted tens of thousands of deaths/yr is the same multiplication at global scale, and the cheapest large-number impact reduction in the entire lesson.
:::

**Observed-impact attribution**: the shift from "projected risks" to "detected and attributed impacts" changed the field's evidentiary status — the same transition temperature attribution made in 2013. It rests on consistency across thousands of independent studies (ranges, phenology, yield trends, reef loss), and it is what makes "loss and damage" an empirical claim rather than a forecast.

::: summary
- Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability, with adaptation intervening in all three: the framework that organises AR6 WGII, and the reason identical hazards produce 1970-Bhola and 2020s-Cyclone-Amphan outcomes — vulnerability is the most tractable term in the equation.
- The biological response is measured, not projected: ranges moving ~17 km/decade, phenology mismatching, marine heatwaves killing a billion seabirds in a single event, the first formally climate-extinct mammal — with land use still the larger extinction driver and climate the fastest-growing one.
- The human ledger is already open: water security degraded for the glacier-dependent billion, calorie growth slowed by a third by realised warming, ~half a million heat deaths a year and rising, 96 % of cities outside their design climate — and compound, cascading events (Pakistan 2022, Australia 2016–22) are the pattern single-hazard analysis misses.
- The distribution is the headline: ~90 % of disaster deaths in low-income countries, the top income decile causing ~half of consumption emissions, present-damage inequality already exceeding carbon prices twentyfold — climate impacts are the largest intergenerational and international transfer in history, already in motion.
- The counterweights are real and belong in the summary: disaster mortality per person has fallen for fifty years, adaptation demonstrably works when funded, and the damage functions are pathways, not fates — the bridge to the responses lesson [[climate-change/responses]].
:::
