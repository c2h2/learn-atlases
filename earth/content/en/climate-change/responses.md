## The mitigation ledger: options, costs, and the deployment gap

Mitigation is the one part of the climate problem where the assessments can speak in engineering and prices rather than probabilities, and the 2022–23 AR6 working-group III verdict is consequently the strangest document in the literature: *the science and economics are not the barrier*. The chapter-level facts: most low-emission options cost less than the business-as-usual alternatives they replace (electric end uses, efficiency, solar and wind, demand measures); the costs of the core renewables fell 85 % (solar), 55 % (wind) and 64 % (batteries, cell prices) across 2010–2019; the world's ability to implement has improved in every region; and yet every major assessment since 2001 has documented a widening gap between modelled pathways and implemented policy — the "deployment gap" that makes the emissions curve the most honest graph in the field. This lesson reads the mitigation portfolio the way the assessments do (supply, demand, removal), then the adaptation ledger (options, finance, limits), and closes on the arithmetic of choice: what the budgets, prices and pledges actually commit, and where the language of "net zero" outruns the plans that would deliver it.

::: definition The economics of mitigation: cost curves, learning and the price gap {#def-mitigation-economics}
The marginal abatement cost curve — options ranked by cost per tonne abated, negative-cost (money-saving) options first — is the assessment's organising device, and its AR6 reading is that the curve's left end is now enormous: energy efficiency, methane abatement from oil and gas (the 2021 Global Methane Assessment: ~40 % of fossil-methane emissions abatable at negative cost), and demand reduction all save money over asset lifetimes. The deeper economics is **learning**: manufactured technologies follow Wright's law (costs fall a fixed percentage per doubling of cumulative production — ~20 %/doubling for modules and cells), which converts deployment policy into cost policy with a decade's lag, and is why the 2010s German and Chinese solar programmes are read in the 2020s literature as climate *investment* with global returns (the 2021–23 Mercator and Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air analyses of early-feed-in-tariff learning spillovers). The policy instrument economics: carbon prices internalise the externality, and the 2023–25 audits (the World Bank's carbon-pricing report, the 2023 *Science* assessment) find ~70+ systems covering only ~23 % of global emissions with a **median price under $10/tCO₂** against social-cost estimates of $50–200 (the US interagency's 2024 interim figure ~$200) — the "price gap" that makes carbon pricing the most under-used cost-effective tool in the assessment. The non-price instruments do the heavy lifting regardless: renewable portfolio standards, building codes, vehicle efficiency standards and public R&D account for most realised abatement (the policy-evaluation literature's near-consensus: the 2019–2022 *Nature Energy* and *Research Policy* assessments of what actually decarbonised grids — standards and mandates, not prices, did most of the work in the EU power sector and California). The frontier concept is **cost of inaction versus cost of transition**: the stranded-asset literature (the 2022–24 carbon-budget-consistency analyses: fossil reserves worth ~$1–4 trillion more than any 1.5–2 °C pathway permits extracting — the "unburnable carbon" question) and the macro-financial school (the 2017–19 Carbon Tracker and NGFS stress tests) reframe mitigation spending as insurance against a write-down, not as a cost.
:::

::: definition The energy and demand portfolio: supply options, storage, and the 40–70 % {#def-energy-portfolio}
**Supply.** The AR6 supply-side ranking is unambiguous on cost: solar and wind are now the cheapest new electricity in most markets (the 2023–25 LCOE datasets: utility solar $20–50/MWh, onshore wind $30–60, both below the marginal operating cost of much of the existing coal fleet in the same markets), and the 2023–24 deployment confirms the economics — renewables supplied ~90 % of all new global capacity in 2023–24 (the IRENA and Ember records: ~510 GW added in 2023, ~580 in 2024, solar two-thirds of it), with China installing more solar in 2023 than the entire world did in 2022. The dispatchability problem is real and addressable: the portfolio is storage (lithium-ion pack prices fell ~88 % over 2010–2024, the 2024–25 storage additions tripling annually; the 2025 grid-scale fleet approaching 100 GWh), flexible demand and transmission (the binding constraints in the US, Europe and China are interconnection queues and grid build rates, not generation — the 2023–25 grid studies: connection backlogs of several times installed capacity), hydro/geothermal/bioenergy where siting permits, and **nuclear** — whose assessed facts are: lifecycle emissions ~4–12 gCO₂/kWh (the lowest of any firm source), a 2022–23 restart of interest (the 2023 COP28 "triple nuclear" pledge of 22 countries; the 2021–25 small-modular-reactor enthusiasm versus the 2020s cost evidence — Flamanville and Vogtle at 2–4× budget, the 2024–25 SMR first-of-a-kind delays), and the honest verdict that nuclear's economics are a construction-cost story (the 2016–2022 Sovacool and Lovering analyses) while its system value rises with variable-renewable share. **Demand.** The AR6's most under-reported number: demand-side measures — efficiency, electrification of heat and transport, material efficiency, circularity, and the harder "sufficiency" measures (the 2022–23 *Nature Energy* literature: the 2022 IPCC chapter credits demand measures with 40–70 % of 2050 final-sector mitigation potential) — are cheap, fast and chronically under-funded relative to supply (the 2023 IEA: efficiency investment fell for the first time in 2020–23 even as solar investment tripled). The electrification arithmetic is the century's main act: direct electrification of vehicles and heat (the 2023–25 EV sales: 14 million in 2024, ~40 % share in China, the 2024–25 battery-price collapse below $100/kWh pack) plus clean electricity is the single largest modelled abatement lever, and its feasibility is now an engineering consensus (the 2021–25 100 %-variable-renewables grid studies, the Denmark/Portugal/UK integration demonstrations) whose remaining obstacles are political economy: permitting, grid tariffs, and the incumbency of fossil industrial policy.
:::

::: example The solar learning curve: the cheapest technology transfer in history {#example-solar}
Crystalline-silicon modules sold for ~$106/W in 1976 (the US space-solar and early terrestrial market) and for ~$0.10/W in 2024 — a decline of 99.9 %, the steepest sustained cost fall in the history of energy, following Wright's law with a learning rate near 20–24 % per doubling of cumulative production (the 2010–2023 cost-economics literature: the Nemet, Wustman/Gregory-Hansen and 2023–25 updates). The causal history is the policy lesson: the demand was manufactured twice before it was self-sustaining — the Japanese and German residential programmes of 1974–2011 (the 1974 Japanese Sunshine Project, the 2000–2004 German feed-in tariff that created the first bankable mass market), then the Chinese industrial policy from 2006 (the 2006–09 polysilicon and wafer scale-up, the 2013–18 central subsidies) that drove the 2011–2014 price collapse which killed the Western subsidy-era manufacturers and made grid parity inevitable. By 2023 solar was, in the 2023 IPCC's phrase, "the cheapest electricity in history" in most sunlight-rich markets, and the 2024–25 module oversupply (prices briefly below cash cost, the 2024–25 consolidation of the Chinese tier-one firms) is the pathology of a technology that has outgrown its own economics. The remaining frontiers are the ones learning curves cannot fix alone: manufacturing's own decarbonisation (the polysilicon and quartz-ingot energy, the 2024–25 "green polysilicon" hydro-powered shifts), supply-chain concentration (China's ~80–95 % share across several stages — the 2023–25 energy-security counter-mobilisation), and the integration costs that arrive when solar's share passes ~30 % (the duck-curve, storage pairing, and the 2024–25 "negative-price hours" in California and Australia — the moment cheap energy starts costing money, which is the grid-portfolio argument in one sentence).
::: solution
The solar case is the lesson's answer to "is decarbonisation affordable": for the largest single emissions source (electricity) and the fastest-scaling technology, the answer flipped from no to yes within one decade, through policy-induced learning whose benefits were global while its costs were national — the free-rider structure that explains both the 2010s under-investment in climate R&D (the 2019–24 assessments of clean-energy R&D spillovers in the Goulder–Nordhaus lineage: public clean-energy R&D ~4–10× under-justified by the spillover economics) and the 2022–25 industrial-policy turn (the US IRA and EU Green Deal Industrial Plan as the first attempts to *price* the spillovers into deployment). The corollary the assessments state plainly: solar alone does not decarbonise a grid, and the technologies that must follow it (firm storage, grid-scale long-duration, clean industry heat) need the same learning subsidies now, while their cost curves are where solar's were in 1990.
:::
:::

::: example The Energiewende: thirty years of the largest grid-decarbonisation experiment {#example-energiewende}
Germany's feed-in tariff (2000) and its successor instruments built the world's largest early renewables deployment — the 2000–2014 solar boom that drove global module prices down (the 2021–23 learning-spillover analyses attribute a substantial share of early cumulative demand to German subsidies), the wind fleet that made onshore wind cheap across Europe, and by 2024 a system where renewables supplied ~55–60 % of public net generation (the Fraunhofer and AG Energiebilanzen tallies: 52 % in 2023, ~59 % in 2024, solar and wind past coal for the first time in 2022). It also demonstrated the failure modes the assessments now catalogue: the 2011 nuclear shutdown (the post-Fukushima decision that removed ~15 % low-carbon supply at short notice and extended coal's life — the 2021–23 ex-post analyses (the DIW and *Nature Energy* lineage) finding the phase-out imposed a modest but persistent emissions penalty and billions in opportunity cost, while the 2023 final three-reactor shutdown passed with negligible grid impact thanks to the solar surge), the EEG surcharge's distributional politics (household electricity prices tripled to the EU's highest, the industrial exemptions that made the system regressive and legally contested), the transmission bottleneck (the north-south power corridors 15 years late), and the 2022 gas crisis (which cut gas use fast but re-lignified temporarily and, per the 2023–24 reviews, permanently radicalised the build-out: the 2022–23 "Easter package" and 2023 renewables acceleration law). The 2024 verdict from the evaluation literature (the 2023–25 Agora Energiewende and 2024 *Joule* assessments): the Energiewende's first twenty years were a *deployment* success and a *design* education — costs fell globally, German emissions fell ~40 % from 1990 (two-thirds of that from the 1990 reunification deindustrialisation and gas switching, a fact the emissions-accounting debates keep re-litigating), and the 2020s redesign (carbon pricing via EU ETS pass-through, the 80 %-renewable-2030 target, the permit-acceleration laws) is the second experiment: whether a mature industrial economy can decarbonise its heat, transport and industry on a decade's schedule.
::: solution
The Energiewende's lessons are the framework's: sequencing matters (shutting nuclear before the storage and grid were ready cost more than it saved), design matters more than slogans (the FIT's learning spillovers were global while its surcharge was regressive — co-benefits and distribution are policy choices, not physics), and the *demonstration* effect is itself a mitigation instrument (the "Germany is failing" genre of the 2013–2020 press cycle delayed no pathways, but Germany's success genre of 2023–25 — solar cannibalisation solved by storage auctions, the coal exit legislated for 2038 — is now the exportable product). The 2024–25 open question is the one every late-industrialising economy faces: whether the grid, industrial-heat and transport layers can move at the speed the layer-one (power) experience suggests is possible, or whether the hard sectors are a different physics and a different politics.
:::
:::

## Removal, adaptation, and the arithmetic of choice

::: definition Carbon dioxide removal: the portfolio, the potentials, and the moral hazard {#def-cdr}
The AR6's central 1.5 °C pathways all include carbon dioxide removal — a median of ~0.5 GtCO₂/yr by 2050 across the pathways that hold 1.5 °C with no or limited overshoot, with the overshoot-dependent sets running to several GtCO₂/yr — and the assessed portfolio splits into two families with opposite profiles. **Land-based removals** (reforestation and afforestation, soil carbon, blue carbon, avoided harvest) are cheap ($10–100/tCO₂), deliver co-benefits, and are limited by land competition (the AR6 sustainable-land potential ~2–5 GtCO₂/yr with large uncertainty), by impermanence (the 2020–23 fire and drought reversals: the 2021–23 Canadian and Siberian fire seasons converting managed forest to net source in single years), and by accounting integrity — the 2023–24 audits of avoided-deforestation credits (the West and colleagues 2023 *PNAS* analysis: most baseline-deforestation claims overstated; the May 2023 investigative series on the largest registry's flagship methodology, and the 2024–25 registry reforms and litigation) is now the case study in how not to build a removal market. **Engineered removals** — bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (the AR6 sustainable potential ~0.6–5 GtCO₂/yr, the 2023–25 demonstration fleet near-zero — the largest near-term projects being the Illinois ethanol-and-DAC hub and the 2023 UK government-backed Drax proposal, the latter granted against the Climate Change Committee's advice), direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS at $300–1,000/tCO₂ in the 2023–25 plant economics — the Iceland Orca/Mammoth and the 2024–25 US megasite permitting records; energy and water demands that make siting the constraint), enhanced weathering, biochar, and ocean alkalinity (the 2023–25 field-trial governance fights) — are permanent, scalable in principle, and expensive-to-unproven, with the 2022–24 "removals pledge" literature (the 2023 *Nature* correspondence: the G7 5-Gt-by-2035 removals target exceeding every credible supply estimate by an order of magnitude) documenting the gap between political and physical feasibility. The **moral-hazard** question is assessed, not rhetorical: the 2016–2024 literature (the McLaren school's "sacrifice zone" and delay analyses; the 2023–24 econometric tests) finds large-scale removals pledges already correlate with weaker near-term targets — the arithmetic being that any budget that assumes future removals spends it in advance, and the 2023 synthesis report's finding (at 2022–2025 rates the 50 %-chance 1.5 °C budget is exhausted within about three years) is the accounting correction.
:::

::: definition Adaptation: options, the finance gap, and the limits {#def-adaptation}
The AR6 adaptation chapter's structure is a feasibility catalogue: for every system (coasts, water, agriculture, cities, health, insurance) it lists options with cost, maturity and limits, and its verdict is that **implementation is lagging feasibility everywhere** — the 2023–25 UNEP Adaptation Gap Reports put developing-country needs at ~$200–370 bn/yr by 2030 against ~$50 bn/yr of international flows (a 4–7-fold gap; the 2022–23 double-digit percentage growth in flows after the 2021 "double adaptation finance" pledge still leaves the multiple unmet), and the 2023 *Nature Climate Change* audit (Bhag and colleagues) found adaptation finance from major donors is substantially mislabelled development aid, while the 2019 Young and colleagues portfolio audit found only ~39 % of assessed adaptation projects measurably reduced vulnerability. The option classes: structural (defences, drainage, water infrastructure — the Netherlands' Room for the River, the 1993/95 Rhine-Meuse floods' €2.3 bn programme of floodplain reconnection and the "delta standards" risk design now the textbook of building-with-nature), institutional (early warning — the 2022–23 "Early Warnings for All" initiative after the 2022 WMO finding that half of countries lack multi-hazard systems; insurance and social protection — the 2023 Insuring Our Future reports: insurance covers only a small fraction of disaster losses in developing countries), ecosystem-based (mangrove and reef protection — the 2021–24 co-benefit analyses: mangroves' storm-surge attenuation worth billions, with limits at >2–3 m surge and at the sediment supply that sea level rise requires), and planned relocation (the 2019–25 Pacific and West African relocations — the Fijian village relocations' legal protocols, the 2023–25 loss-and-damage framing of "migration with dignity"). The **limits** taxonomy is the assessment's most consequential conceptual output: *soft* limits (options exist but are not implemented — finance, institutions, information) versus *hard* limits (no adaptation preserves the system — the small-island freshwater lenses, the coral-algal regime flips, the 2022 WGII finding that hard limits are *already being reached* in the most vulnerable systems and will be crossed at 1.5 °C in several more). The adaptation–mitigation relation is convex: the SR1.5 and AR6 cost chapters find adaptation costs and residual losses rise steeply with temperature (the 2018 special report's headline that limiting warming to 1.5 rather than 2 °C *reduces adaptation needs*, quantified sector by sector), which is the precise sense in which mitigation is the deepest adaptation — and the 2022–23 "loss and damage" institutionalisation (the COP27 fund decision, the 2023–25 transitional-committee fights over whether the fund is new-and-additional or re-labelled aid) is the first global instrument for the residual.
:::

::: example Bangladesh and the Netherlands: the adaptation divide in two deltas {#example-deltas}
The 1970 Bhola cyclone killed 300,000–500,000 people in the then-East Pakistan delta; the 2007 Sidr and 2009 Aila storms killed ~3,500 and fewer than 500 in the same country with comparable or stronger storm surges; the 2020s cyclone death tolls are counted in the tens to hundreds at similar intensity (the WMO and Bangladesh Ministry of Disaster Management records). The mechanism is the most-replicated adaptation story in development economics: the 1972–2020 build-out of ~700 multi-purpose cyclone shelters, the community-based early-warning chain (the 1980s–90s Red Crescent and GP20/25 volunteer networks), the embankment programme (5,000+ km, maintained with food-aid labour), and the post-1991 standing cyclone-plan architecture — a system that moves a million people in 48 hours and whose residual failures (the 2007 Sidr deaths concentrated where shelters were unreachable and warnings late) are themselves the subject of the adaptation-effectiveness literature (the 2015–2022 *Nature Sustainability* and ICPSR assessments: mortality down two orders of magnitude, asset losses *up* because exposure grew faster than defences — the textbook hazard-exposure divergence). The Netherlands is the same story at the other end of the income scale and the same lesson about limits: a thousand years of polder engineering (the 1953 Delta Works after the 1,800-death storm surge, then the 1993–95 Rhine-Meuse floods that forced the €2.3 bn Room for the River programme — lowering floodplains rather than raising dikes, whose post-completion evaluations are now the canonical "build with nature" assessment), the 2021–25 Delta Programme's 2050 adaptation-pathways documents (the follow-on room-for-the-river programme and the IJsselmeer freshwater measures), and the 2021 RMMV/Deltares research stating the hard limit: at +2 m of sea level the delta's freshwater balance, not its dikes, becomes the binding constraint — the rich country's version of the island's question.
::: solution
The two deltas teach the adaptation ledger's two halves. Bangladesh is the proof that the vulnerability term is tractable even under poverty and rising exposure — the mortality decline is one of the largest demonstrated returns to any climate policy anywhere, and the reason the adaptation-finance gap (a few tens of billions/yr against hundreds needed) is the most cost-effective injustice in the literature. The Netherlands is the proof that wealth buys time and options, not immunity: the Dutch delta standard (1-in-10,000-yr flood risk design) is the world's most conservative, and its own scientists publish its limits — the freshwater balance under high sea level (the 2021–24 Deltares and national-assessment analyses) is becoming the binding constraint before the dikes are, and the 2021 Ahr valley disaster — a 1-in-500-year flood in a German district whose defences had not been upgraded in decades, next door to the 1-in-10,000 Dutch design philosophy — lands the point that design standards are statements about the *modelled* future. Adaptation, in short, is the term in the risk equation where policy works fastest and where the physics eventually says no.
:::
:::

::: example Net-zero pledges and carbon markets: the integrity decade {#example-net-zero}
By 2023 the net-zero pledges architecture covered ~88 % of global GDP and ~83 % of emissions (the 2023 Net Zero Tracker and UN records) — and the 2023 audits found the architecture hollow: the 2022 *Nature* study (Hale and colleagues) found only a minority of national pledges had policies consistent with their targets and the 2022 Emissions Gap Report's finding that few countries' 2030 NDCs were even fully implemented; the 2023 corporate audit (Bailey and colleagues, *Climate Policy*/Oxford) found 72 % of corporate net-zero targets lacked any near-term plan, and the 2023–24 "carbon neutral" litigation and regulatory wave (the 2023 EU and UK greenwashing rulings, the 2024 FCA and SEC disclosure fights) arrived on top of the 2023 offset-market integrity crisis (the West and colleagues 2023 *PNAS* avoided-deforestation analysis; the May 2023 registry investigation; the 2024–25 ICVCM credibility standards and the 2024 VCMI corporate-claiming reforms). The 2024–25 correction is the live experiment: the 2024 Oxford policy guidance on net-zero claims (the "beyond offsets" hierarchy — reduce first, neutralise residual only, compensate never as a claim), the 2024–25 EU green-claims directive, the 2024 Article 6.2 first-issuance trades (the January 2024 Singapore–Papua New Guinea transfer, the 2024 Ghana–Switzerland agreement, whose environmental integrity the 2024–25 *Nature* and Carbon Brief audits dispute), and the 2024–25 domestic-emissions reality check (the 2023–24 Global Carbon Budget: fossil CO₂ at a ~37 GtCO₂ plateau — neither the rising curve of the 2010s nor the falling curve the pledges imply; the 2024 Ember finding that global power-sector emissions fell for the first sustained year in 2023 as solar and EVs outran demand).
::: solution
The integrity decade's lesson is the distinction the assessments police: a *target* is a modelled future, a *pathway* is a policy portfolio, and a *pledge* without the second is a statement of preference. The 2023–25 reforms (standards, disclosure, litigation) are the first attempt to make the three commensurable, and the economic logic is the same as the learning-curve logic in reverse: capital allocation, insurance pricing and public policy all now depend on claims being auditable, which is why the accounting fights (what counts as removal, what baseline, what leakage) are not bureaucratic — they are where the 1.5 °C budget either exists on paper or does not. The honest summary of the 2025 state: pledges cover the emissions, policies cover a third of them, and the gap is the subject of the next decade's policy science.
:::

::: warning Five ways response claims get distorted — by optimists and obstructionists alike
1. **Potential swapped for deployment.** "The technical potential is 3× current demand" and "the technology is ready" are both true and both irrelevant without the deployment question — grid queues, permitting, finance and incumbents. The assessments' "feasible and cheap" language is about the option; the emissions curve is about the implementation, and the gap between them is the actual subject of policy science.
2. **The moral-hazard inversion.** Removals and offsets are simultaneously necessary (every 1.5 °C pathway uses them) and dangerous (pledges of them delay cuts). The resolution is arithmetic, not rhetoric: removals must be *additional, permanent and counted against the budget they claim to offset* — the 2023–25 integrity reforms are the first attempt to enforce the sentence.
3. **"Cheapest power source" ≠ "cheapest system."** Solar's LCOE excludes integration (storage, backup, grid reinforcement, the negative-price-hours cannibalisation), and the honest system-cost literature (the 2020–2024 *Joule* and *One Earth* integration-cost studies) finds the premium real but shrinking — while the mirror error ("intermittent, therefore expensive") ignores that the comparison class (coal plus its own transmission, fuel-price risk and health costs) lost the argument around 2020.
4. **Pledges read as plans.** Net-zero-2050 without a 2030 policy stack is a destination without a vehicle — the 2023 audits' finding is not that pledges are worthless (they anchor finance and regulation) but that *only the near-term policy stack is evidence*, and the 2024–25 disclosure regimes exist precisely to make the difference legible to capital.
5. **The adaptation-finance shell game.** "Tripling adaptation finance" from a base of a few billion, counting re-labelled development as adaptation (the 2022–23 Bhag and colleagues *Science* and *Nature Climate Change* findings), and counting disaster *response* as *adaptation* are the three moves that let the 4–7-fold gap persist in headlines that claim it is closing. The 2023 Global Goal on Adaptation indicators and the 2024–25 needs-based assessments are the audit instruments; the gap is the number.
:::

::: history From Toronto to the first global stocktake: response policy's forty years {#history-responses}
The institutional history begins with the 1985 Villach and 1988 Toronto conferences (the Toronto "Changing Atmosphere" conference's 20 % CO₂ stabilisation pledge — the first, and the template for every unmet pledge since), the 1990 IPCC First Assessment and the 1992 Rio UNFCCC (the "dangerous anthropogenic interference" mandate and the common-but-differentiated-responsibilities bargain that still structures every finance fight). The 1997 Kyoto Protocol invented the instrument set — binding targets for Annex I, emissions trading, the Clean Development Mechanism — and lost the United States to the 95–0 Byrd Resolution, the 2005 EU ETS's first phase (the 2006–07 windfall-profits and price-crash lesson that taught the allocation-design generation), and the 2009 Copenhagen collapse (the 2 °C target captured, the $100 bn/yr finance pledge made and missed, the accord's 111-country near-consensus that became the 2015 Paris architecture). The 2010–2015 decade was the quiet one and the decisive one: the Kyoto second period (Doha, 2012, toothless), the EU's 20-20-20 package, the US shale-and-efficiency emissions decline that no treaty caused, and the solar learning curve doing what learning curves do. The 2015 Paris Agreement's inversion — bottom-up nationally determined contributions, a ratchet, a transparency framework, the 1.5/2 °C temperature goal whose scientific operationalisation came from the 2018 SR1.5 — converted the problem from treaty-compliance to pathway-governance, and the 2018–2023 wave (the 2019 European Green Deal, the 2021 US re-entry and Infrastructure Law, the 2022 IRA's $369 bn of climate industrial policy, the 2022–25 China clean-tech export dominance) is the policy-science literature's current subject (the 2023–25 *Science* and *Nature* assessments of carbon pricing versus industrial policy as the 2020s' decisive instrument choice). The 2021–23 Glasgow–Sharm el-Sheikh–Dubai sequence wrote the endgame language — "phase-down of unabated coal" (2021, the first treaty text naming fossil fuels), the loss-and-damage fund (2022), the first global stocktake's "transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems" (2023, with the renewables-tripling and efficiency-doubling pledges) — and the 2024–25 implementation cycle (the new NDCs due 2025, the 2025 stocktake of the stocktake) is the live experiment in whether the ratchet ratchets: the 2024–25 Global Carbon Budget's plateau and the 2024 power-sector emissions decline are the first data suggesting the policy stack has bent the global curve, while the 2025 pledges' aggregate shortfall says the bend is not yet the slope the budgets require.
:::

::: quiz
"Net zero CO₂" is reached when:
- [ ] CO₂ emissions fall 45 percent from 1990 levels
- [x] Anthropogenic CO₂ emissions balance anthropogenic removals, so cumulative emissions stop growing
- [ ] All energy is renewable
- [ ] Non-CO₂ gases are eliminated
::: solution
**Emissions = removals**: net-zero CO₂ halts the growth of cumulative CO₂ and (with the other forcings held) roughly halts warming — the near-linear temperature-budget relation that makes it the physically meaningful target. The caveats the assessments attach: residual non-CO₂ gases (methane, nitrous oxide, aviation) must *also* fall for the temperature to hold, overshoot leaves committed rise even after net zero, and "net" claims that hide gross reductions behind unverified offsets invert the physics — the integrity-decade lesson.

## Exercises

::: exercise {level=1 check="1000"}
Solar module prices fell from ~$106/W in 1976 to ~$0.10/W in 2024. By approximately what factor did they fall?
::: solution
106/0.10 ≈ 1,000-fold — a 99.9 % decline, the steepest cost curve in energy history, and the number behind every "solar is the cheapest electricity in history" statement in the 2023 assessment.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.512"}
A technology follows Wright's law with a 20 % learning rate (cost falls 20 % per doubling of cumulative production). If cumulative production increases eightfold (three doublings), by what factor does unit cost fall?
::: solution
0.8³ = 0.512 — cost halves for an eightfold production increase. The arithmetic that makes early deployment policy (subsidies, mandates) a down-payment on the technology's future price, and why the 2000s German solar programme is read as global climate investment rather than German energy policy.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="600"}
The 2023 assessment reports solar PV module costs fell 85 % over 2010–2019. From a 2010 cost of $4,000/kW, what is the 2019 cost in $/kW?
::: solution
4,000 × 0.15 = $600/kW — the level at which solar undercuts coal operation in most markets, the economic fact that the 2023–24 deployment wave (90 % of new capacity renewable) merely reported.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="165"}
The AR6 credits demand-side measures with 40–70 % of 2050 final-sector mitigation potential. Using the midpoint, how many GtCO₂ of a 300 GtCO₂ final-sector baseline could demand measures abate?
::: solution
55 % × 300 = 165 GtCO₂ — larger than any single supply-side option, and the number that makes the 2023 finding (efficiency investment *falling* while solar investment tripled) the most surprising misallocation in the assessment.

::: exercise {level=2 check="4"}
Developing-country adaptation needs are estimated at ~$200 bn/yr by 2030 while international adaptation finance flows are ~$50 bn/yr. What is the shortfall multiple?
::: solution
200/50 = 4-fold (the UNEP Gap Report's range is 4–7×). The number that makes the adaptation-finance debate arithmetic rather than rhetoric — and the 2021 "double adaptation finance" pledge's arithmetic: doubling $20 bn to $40 bn still leaves the multiple unmet.

::: exercise {level=2 check="40"}
The US interagency's 2024 interim social cost of carbon is ~$200/tCO₂; the median existing carbon price is ~$5/tCO₂. What is the ratio?
::: solution
200/5 = 40-fold. The price-gap magnitude behind the assessment verdict that carbon pricing is the most under-used cost-effective instrument — and the political-economy magnitude that makes "pricing is dead, long live industrial policy" (the 2023–25 *Science* debate) a live question rather than a settled one.

::: exercise {level=3 check="12.5"}
The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C (50 % chance) from early 2020 was ~500 GtCO₂; global CO₂ emissions run ~40 GtCO₂/yr. How many years of current emissions does the budget last?
::: solution
500/40 = 12.5 years — exhaustion around the early 2030s, the 2023 synthesis report's headline arithmetic. The caveats that keep it honest: the budget is for *all* CO₂ (the 37 Gt fossil share leaves slightly longer), non-CO₂ forcing shortens it, and every pathway that "holds" 1.5 °C beyond the 2030s is spending removals or overshoot it has not yet bought.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="2.5"}
The 1.5 °C pathways use a median ~5 GtCO₂/yr of CO₂ removals by 2050; current anthropogenic removals are ~2 GtCO₂/yr (almost all forestry). What scale-up multiple is required?
::: solution
5/2 = 2.5-fold — modest until one notes the *quality* shift: the 2 Gt is land-based and impermanent, while the pathways' second half leans on engineered removals whose 2025 fleet is ~0.01 MtCO₂/yr — a five-order-of-magnitude scale-up in the permanent fraction, the number the G7 5-Gt-by-2035 removals pledge (2022) never reconciled with supply forecasts.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="132"}
Lithium-ion pack prices fell ~88 % over 2010–2024. From a 2010 pack price of $1,100/kWh, what is the 2024 price in $/kWh?
::: solution
1,100 × 0.12 = $132/kWh — below the ~$150/kWh threshold long cited as parity-with-ICE territory for vehicles, and the reason the 2024–25 EV and grid-storage demand curves, not the policy targets, are the emissions story of the late 2020s.

::: quiz
The AR6 verdict on adaptation is best summarised as:
- [ ] Feasible options are exhausted; loss and damage is the only remaining category
- [x] Many feasible, effective options exist but implementation lags everywhere, finance is 4–7× short, and hard limits are already being reached in the most vulnerable systems
- [ ] Adaptation works equally well everywhere given enough funding
- [ ] Adaptation costs fall with warming because technologies learn
::: solution
**Feasible but lagging, with real limits**: the assessment's three-part verdict — option inventories full, implementation gaps wide, and hard limits already reached in some islands, reefs and rainfed systems — is why adaptation cannot substitute for mitigation: the convexity of residual loss with temperature is the one direction every scenario agrees on.

::: summary
- The mitigation economics flipped within a decade: most core options (solar, wind, batteries, efficiency, electrification, methane abatement) are now cost-competitive or money-saving, the 2023–24 deployment records (90 % of new power capacity renewable, EVs at 14 M/yr) are the learning curve's payout, and the binding constraints have moved to grids, permitting, finance and political economy.
- The gap is implementation, not feasibility: pledges cover ~83 % of emissions, policies cover a fraction, carbon prices sit ~40× below social costs, and the 2023–25 integrity decade (offset audits, net-zero verification, Article 6 fights) is the first attempt to make claims auditable — because a budget spent on paper is still spent.
- Removals are necessary and dangerous in the same sentence: every 1.5 °C pathway uses them, current supply is four orders of magnitude short of the permanent-fraction need, and the moral-hazard correlation between removals pledges and weaker near-term targets is an assessed finding, not a slogan.
- Adaptation is where policy works fastest (Bangladesh's two-order-of-magnitude mortality decline) and where physics eventually says no (the Dutch freshwater limit, the reef flips, the already-reached hard limits) — with a 4–7-fold finance gap that is the cheapest large-number justice failure in the literature.
- The responses ledger closes the course's argument: the budgets are finite and nearly spent, the tools are ready and under-used, the distribution of both cause and harm is the central political fact, and the 2020s are the decade in which the difference between pledges and pathways became the whole subject — the reason this course ends not with a forecast but with an accounting.
:::
