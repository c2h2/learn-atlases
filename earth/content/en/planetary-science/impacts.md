For the first 700 million years, impact cratering was the dominant process shaping every solid surface in the Solar System, and its verdicts still outvote slow geology on Mercury, the Moon, Callisto and every body without air or ice-flow to erase them — which is why this lesson's methods (crater counting, scaling laws, shock diagnostics) are the dating and reconstruction tools for half the planets, and why its one terrestrial chapter — the K-Pg extinction — rewrote both geology and the public's sense of how the world ends. The acceptance history is a sociology classic: Gilbert's 1893 impact interpretation of the lunar craters was ignored for seventy years, Barringer's 1902–1906 fight for Meteor Crater's meteoritic origin (the 1963 Shoemaker coesite discovery that finally closed it), the 1960s shock-metamorphism programme (the Stöffler–Dence–French diagnostic ladder of planar deformation features, high-pressure polymorphs and impact melts) that made "impact" a petrographic verdict rather than a guess, and the 1973 French–Gold debate — the last public argument about whether terrestrial craters mattered — settled within a decade by Apollo's cratered highlands, Mariner 9's Martian inventory and the growing astrobleme list. Then the 1980 Alvarez iridium paper proposed an asteroid as the Cretaceous's murderer, and the 1980–2010s argument (the 1991 Chicxulub location, the 2010s IODP-ICDP drilling of the 2016–2017 expedition, the 2013–2024 high-precision chronometry placing the impact within tens of thousands of years of the boundary) became the template for how a deep-time causal claim is built, layer by layer, from osmium isotopes to soot to shocked quartz to the 2020s tsunami-and-wildfire sedimentology. Meanwhile the hazard became operational: the 1994 Shoemaker–Levy 9 comet's impacts on Jupiter, the 2013 Chelyabinsk injury event, the 2002–2025 Spaceguard survey's census of the near-Earth population, and the 2022 DART deflection test — the first time a planetary process was deliberately modified. The prerequisites are the [[planetary-science/meteorites]] lesson's shock and isotope diagnostics and the [[planetary-science/formation]] lesson's bombardment context; the reward is a quantitative reading of crater counts as clocks, of impactites as forensics, and of the risk integrals that decide how many telescopes and which kinetic rockets the species funds.

::: definition Crater formation: contact and compression, excavation, modification {#def-formation}
An impact crater is built in four acts lasting minutes for a 10-km event. **Contact and compression** (microseconds): the impactor stops in its own diameter, a shock of 0.1–10+ TPa radiates into target and projectile, vaporising both near the stagnation point (the 1990s–2020s iSAQ and SPH hydrocode calibration of the 1960s–1990s scaling literature — the 1991 Holsapple–Schmidt π-group scaling and the 1990s–2010s Schmidt–Housen–Benz strength-versus-gravity-regime experiments) and launching the excavation flow. **Excavation** (minutes): the transient crater — a simple bowl, depth one-third to one-half its diameter — grows by material flow and is cut off when the growing cavity's gravity or strength can no longer accelerate the rim, and the excavated prism (the transient volume, plus the impactor's own mass, now mixed, heated and moving at up to escape velocity) departs as the ejecta curtain, its ballistic fall-back depositing the continuous ejecta blanket, the secondary-crater fields and, at planetary scale, the global ejecta layer (the K-Pg spherule beds, the lunar-spinward ejecta asymmetries of the 2010s–2020s studies). **Modification** (minutes to centuries): craters deeper than their strength can hold collapse — simple bowls give way, above transition diameters of ~2–4 km on Earth and 15–20 km on the Moon, to **complex craters** with central uplifts (the 1980s–1990s dynamic-compaction and acoustic-fluidisation models of the peak, the drill cores of Chesapeake and Chicxulub confirming metres-per-second uplift of deep crust), ring terraces, and for the largest events **peak-ring basins and multi-ring basins** (the 1980s–2000s Melosh–McKinnon–Schultz formation mechanics, the lunar Orientale and Valhalla as the type cases) whose 300-km rings on Earth (Chicxulub's 180-km ring, Wilkes Land's disputed 500-km gravity anomaly) are the largest structures geology knows. **Post-forming geology**: impact breccias and melt sheets (the 10¹⁵–10¹⁸ kg melt bodies of Sudbury and Vredefort, the 2010s–2020s impact-melt differentiation studies), the diagnostic shock assemblage (planar deformation features in quartz — the Stöffler–Gault–French calibration of pressure and orientation, coesite and stishovite, maskelynite in plagioclase, the 2020s EBSD and microtexture refinements) that separates craters from calderas and pipes, and the degradation clocks (relaxation on icy moons, sediment burial and erosion on Earth, gardening on the Moon) that make every crater population a competition between production and destruction — the counting problem of the next definition.
:::

::: definition Crater counting: the production function and the calibration wars {#def-counting}
Crater counting turns a photograph into an age by one assumption: that the flux of impactors, once measured where it can be (the lunar record, the exercises' arithmetic), is spatially and temporally smooth enough to date what cannot be sampled. The machinery: the **cumulative size-frequency distribution** of counted craters (a power law, roughly N ∝ D^-2 for fresh populations, the production function convolved with the target's scaling), the **production versus equilibrium** distinction (a surface is saturated when new craters erase old ones as fast as they form, at densities near 0.1–0.2 craters per crater-area for the largest sizes — the 1970s–1990s Williams–Gault–Blanchard lunar highlands analysis and its 2020s asteroid-rotation-period corollaries, the 2010s–2020s small-body saturation census), and the **chronology function** — the curve of crater density versus absolute age, built entirely from the Apollo and Luna sample sites. The calibration's 2020s crisis is the definition's live content: the two canonical curves (the 1990s–2000s **Neukum–Wilhelms production function** with its assumed early decay, and the 1966–2005 **Hartmann two-populations model** with its 3.9-Ga spike) disagree by factors of two to ten at old ages, the 2007–2014 Strom–Marchi re-reductions (the inner-belt-delays-versus-exponential-decay argument) relocated the early flux debate onto the late-heavy-bombardment question of the [[planetary-science/formation]] lesson, and the 2020–2021 Chang'e-5 return of 2.03-Ga basalt (the 2021–2023 Science papers counting its young mare's craters) found the production functions over-predicting the young crater density by 30–60 per cent — the first absolute ground-truth check since Luna 24 in 1976, and its verdict (the 2022–2025 revisions splitting between a constant recent flux and a gentler decay) that the whole lunar chronology needs re-tuning, with Mars and Mercury ages inherited from it shifting by hundreds of millions of years accordingly. The method's honest statement, which the exercises drill: crater counts date a surface **relative to the calibration curve**, the curve is one anchored sample-set extrapolated by a dynamical model, the errors are correlated across every body counted with the same curve, saturation caps the old ages, and the counting itself (the 2010s–2020s human-versus-neural-network census studies, the craterclass citizen-science era) has systematics of its own — which makes crater chronometry the most useful and most argued-over clock in planetary science, and the reason the late heavy bombardment's fate (the [[planetary-science/formation]] lesson's spike-or-sawtooth) is a counting argument, not a rock.
:::

::: definition The terrestrial record: 200 craters, 4.5 billion years {#def-terrestrial}
Earth's crater record is a study in loss: confirmed terrestrial impact structures number a little over 200 (the 2020s Earth Impact Effects Program and Planetary Science Institute compilations), their diameters spanning 50 m to over 250 km, their ages spanning 800,000 years (the Darwin Crater of Tasmania and the Indochinite strewn field) to **2.02 billion years (Vredefort, with Sudbury at 1.85 Ga and the 2023–2025 candidates near 2.2 Ga in the Pilbara still being argued)** — and the absence of anything older is not an absence of bombardment but of crust: plate tectonics, erosion and burial recycle the Hadean and Archean record completely (the 2010s–2020s zircon-shock and spherule-layer searches for Hadean impacts, the 2022–2025 disputed Jack Hills and Narryer findings, find possible evidence but no craters), so the production curve of the [[planetary-science/formation]] lesson's early flux is unreadable at home and must be borrowed from the Moon. The detection methods are geophysical and petrologic (the 1960s–2000s astrobleme playbook: circular gravity lows from breccia-filled bowls and central uplifts, magnetic anomalies from melt sheets and shock demagnetisation, seismic reflection profiles of the 2010s–2020s offshore structures — the 2019–2023 Silverpit and Titan arguments, the 2023–2025 offshore Australia and Greenland claims), the confirmation standard is shock metamorphism or a confirmed meteorite or melt body (the 2020s debates over the disputed Canadian and African "structures" that lack both), and the dating toolkit is the impactites' own (the 1990s–2020s ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar ages of impact melts, the 2010s–2020s CA-ID-TIMS U-Pb ages of impact zircon and baddeleyite that made the exercises' K-Pg precision possible, the 2023–2025 re-openings of the Sudbury and Vredefort ages by a few tens of Myr). The record's statistics are the hazard science's base layer and its trap: the terrestrial production curve (the 1990s–2020s Shoemaker–Bottke–Bolin flux models) is calibrated from the asteroid population and the lunar clock, the terrestrial crater **preservation** curve (the 2010s–2020s Bottke–Marchi–Bjorck erosion correction — half of all structures ever formed are destroyed within 20 Myr in humid climates, the 2020s finding that the observed age distribution's ramp near the present is preservation, not flux) must be inverted before the flux is read, and the deep-time periodicity debates (the 1980s–1990s Raup–Sepkoski and Rampino extinction-periodicity claims, the 2021–2025 Melosh–Stothers comet-flurry re-analyses and their rebuttals) are, in every instance, arguments about this preservation filter wearing the costume of an astronomical signal — the exercises' preservation arithmetic is the filter itself.
:::

::: definition K-Pg: the reference impact and its killing mechanisms {#def-kpg}
The Cretaceous-Palaeogene boundary event is the only impact in Earth's history with a mass extinction attached, and its evidence stack — built 1980–2025, layer by layer, each stratum of the argument independently replicated — is the model for deep-time causal attribution: the **geochemical anomaly** (the 1980 Alvarez iridium layer at 20–60 ng/cm² globally, the 1990s–2020s osmium-isotope and platinum-group confirmations, the spherule beds from Haiti to the Indian Ocean recording the recondensed melt), the **shock and mineral forensics** (the 1990s shocked-quartz and impact-melt grain reports from boundary clays, the 2010s–2020s statistical petrography), the **structure** (the 1991 Hildebrand–Penfield–Kring location of the 180-km Chicxulub peak-ring basin on the Yucatán margin from the Pemex wells, the 2016–2017 IODP-ICDP drillcores of the peak ring — the 2019 Nature synthesis of the 2020s — which measured the shocked granites, the collapsed rings and the hydrothermal systems the impact drove), the **chronology** (the 2013–2024 CA-ID-TIMS uranium-lead ages of the impact melt and boundary tuffs placing the impact at 66.04 ± 0.03 Ma, within tens of thousands of years of the boundary as defined in the sections, and the 2020s debates over whether the Deccan flood-basalt pulse (the [[earth-history/mass-extinctions]] lesson) shares the blame, the 2021–2025 high-precision argon chronometry tightening both sides of that argument), and the **kill physics** (the 2010s–2020s climate-model syntheses — the 2020–2025 global-wildfire soot injections and impact-winter calculations showing years-to-decades of near-freezing surface temperatures under multi-hundred-TW soot loading, the 2017–2024 drilling evidence that the impact struck Late Cretaceous shallow-marial carbonates and evaporites, injecting tens to hundreds of teratonnes of sulphur aerosol and making the cold and the acid rain co-occur, the 2020s photosynthesis-collapse food-web models matching the extinction selectivity — plankton and ammonites first, deep-sea and freshwater survivors last — better than any single-stress model). The event's quantitative skeleton, which the exercises reconstruct from iridium alone: a 10–15-km carbonaceous-chondrite-class impactor, ~4×10²³ J (10⁸ megatons), global ejecta and tsunami, a soot-and-sulphur climate forcing exceeding anything in the Cenozoic by four orders of magnitude — and the survival of the biosphere through a decade of darkness, which is the lesson's bridge to the hazard definition: the K-Pg is not a curiosity but the calibration point of the risk integral, the event whose recurrence interval (50–100 Myr for 10-km impactors, the 2020s flux models) is the only number that makes civilisation's defense budget meaningful.
:::

::: definition Impact hazard: population, risk, and defence {#def-hazard}
The impact hazard is the only natural hazard with a fully known probability distribution, a fully known trigger, and — since 2022 — a demonstrated mitigation, which makes its risk arithmetic unusually honest. The **population**: ~35,000 catalogued near-Earth asteroids (the 2024–2025 Census — about 40 per cent of the 1-km class, 15 per cent of the 100-m class, the Spaceguard survey's 2002–2025 trajectory and its 2020s NEO Surveyor and LSST-era completion plans), on orbits (the Amor-Apollo-Aten taxonomy, the cometary tail) whose delivery timescales (1–100 Myr from the main belt through the resonances, the [[planetary-science/meteorites]] lesson's exposure ages) set the flux; the **flux** (the 1990s–2020s Bottke–Bolin–Harris production curve: 1-km-class impacts every 0.5–1 Myr, 100-m every 10–20 kyr, Chelyabinsk-class every 30–120 yr — the 2023–2025 fireball-census revisions of the small end). The **consequences** scale steeply (the 1990s–2020s impact-effects codes — the 2010s–2020s terrestrial impact effects program lineage: ejecta blanket and thermal pulse scaling with the 40,000–100,000 J/m² lethal-radius curve, the global climate thresholds at ~10²³ J for 1-km bolides, the 2020s regional-tsunami and soot-climate modelling for ocean and sedimentary-target strikes), and the **risk integrals** (the 2000s–2020s Lonborg–Askwith–Milam and NASA planetary-defense-office expected-annual-fatality and damage calculations, the Torino and Palermo scales' 1995–2005 governance history) put the annual expected loss near that of a small infectious-disease burden — invisible per event, civilisational in the tail. **Defence** became experimental on 26 September 2022, when DART's 580-kg kinetic impactor changed Dimorphos's orbit measurably (the 2022–2024 Science results: a 33-minute period shift, momentum enhancement β ≈ 3.6–4 from ejecta recoil, the [[planetary-science/meteorites]] lesson's exercise), and the 2020s programme (the Hera mission's 2026–2027 crater and mass characterisation, the 2020s gravity-tractor and stand-off-nuclear design studies for the fragile-rubble bodies Chelyabinsk taught us to expect) now faces the governance gap the 2023–2025 UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space and Moon-agreement debates expose: the detection survey is incomplete, the deflection physics is demonstrated only at meter-per-second scales, and the decision to deflect — whose asteroid, whose risk, whose launch — remains the least-solved system in the entire hazard chain.
:::

::: example Chicxulub drilled: the 2016–2017 expedition and after {#ex-chicxulub}
In April–June 2016 the joint IODP-ICDP expedition cored 1,335 metres into Chicxulub's peak ring — the first time the interior structure of an intact large complex crater was sampled anywhere, the marine site chosen because the Yucatán shelf preserved the boundary record the land sections only partly hold — and the 2017–2025 publications are the reference anatomy of a 20-km-class impact. The core's story, in the 2019 Nature synthesis and successors: Cretaceous carbonates at the top, then — after the impact surface — a chaotic breccia of overturned and injected carbonate and basement blocks, then 130 metres of impact melt rock (the 2019–2023 ages: the melt crystallised within hours-to-days of contact, the hydrothermal system the hot porphyritic core drove circulated for ~4 Myr, its anhydrite veins hosting the 2020s-identified chemosynthetic fossil ecosystems), then the shocked and faulted granite of the middle crust, uplifted from 8–10 km depth at metres per second and pervaded by acoustic-fluidisation fractures (the 2019–2024 microstructural and modelling papers reconciling the uplift magnitude), and below it the deformed carbonates that record the target's porosity and water content — the parameters that made the event a climate catastrophe, since the sulphate-bearing evaporites and their 300–500 Gt sulphur injection (the 2016–2020 drilling-constrained estimates) are why the K-Pg impact winter was cold and acidic at once. The 2020s extensions: the 2020–2024 tsunami and wildfire records of the Gulf-margin sections (the 2022–2023 giant-wave runup deposits, the soot and spherule layers' 2023–2025 grain-size reconstructions of the ejecta curtain), the 2023–2025 peak-ring porosity-and-habitat studies (the drilled hydrothermal system as the model for impact-generated habitability, the [[planetary-science/moon]] lesson's lunar analogue), and the 2024–2025 proposal to define the Palaeocene's base — the Anthropocene's contrast in stratigraphic honesty — with the boundary that geologists already agree on: the bolide layer is the best-documented global stratotype the twentieth century's stratigraphy revolution ever produced, drilled, dated and published with the errors attached.
:::

::: example Sudbury and Vredefort: the 1.8-billion-year-old ore deposits {#ex-sudbury-vredefort}
The two largest confirmed terrestrial structures — Vredefort (2.02 Ga, 250–300 km, South Africa) and Sudbury (1.85 Ga, 200 km, Ontario) — are also the planet's most economically consequential impacts, and their 2010s–2020s geochemistry keeps the impact-ore debate alive. Sudbury's structure (the 1970s–1990s mapping that converted the "Sudbury basin" from a volcanic caldera to an eroded impact structure: the 65-km elliptical fault, the breccia and melt distribution, the 2010s–2020s gravity and seismic imaging of the buried deep structure) hosts the world's second-largest nickel and copper production and its largest platinum-group deposits in the **Sudbury Igneous Complex** — a 1.85-Ga sheeted igneous body whose origin (the 1970s–2020s argument) is now impact by consensus: a 10–15-km impactor melted lower and middle crust into a 1,200-km³, 10-kyr-lived magma body (the 2010s–2020s crystallisation-age and thermal models), into which immiscible sulphide liquid scavenged nickel, copper and platinum-group elements from the Archean crust it swept, depositing the ore at the contact (the 2014–2023 lead-isotope and sulphur-in-sulphide studies confirming the crustal contamination, the 2020s exploration geophysics still finding deposits by the model's predictions). Vredefort, half-eroded to its roots, contributes the deep-structure endmember (the 2010s–2020s reconstruction of its 40-km uplift and the 2023–2025 shock-pressure mapping that brackets the impactor at 10–15 km) and the gold of the Witwatersrand's disputed kin (the 2010s–2020s debate over whether Vredefort's shock and fluid events remobilised the basin's gold — the 2023–2025 isotope work favouring a genetic link to at least part of the richest gold deposit on Earth). The lesson's generalisation, which the [[earth-resources/magmatic-hydrothermal]] sibling course knows from the other side: impacts are ore-forming processes when targets are ore-rich, and the two oldest large structures being two of the planet's great mining districts is not coincidence — the search space for impact-metallogeny on early Mars and the Moon (the 2020s resource assessments of the lunar feldspathic terranes' platinum-group potential) starts here.
:::

::: example Chang'e-5 and the recalibrated lunar clock {#ex-ce5}
In December 2020 Chang'e-5 returned 1,731 g of basalt from Oceanus Procellarum, dated by the Chinese teams to 2.03 Ga (the 2021 Nature papers — young volcanism, extending the Moon's eruptive history by a billion years) — and the crater counts over its fresh 300-metre craters delivered the first absolute test of the lunar production function since 1976: the landing site's crater density implied, under the standard Neukum and Hartmann curves, ages of 2.8–3.3 Ga, 40–60 per cent older than the rocks (the 2021–2023 Science and Nature Astronomy counting papers, the 2022–2025 re-counts with LRO nadar-resolution imagery and the 2023–2025 Chinese second-site counts confirming the deficit). The interpretations split as the exercises' arithmetic shows: either the recent impactor flux is 30–60 per cent lower than the curves carry (the constant-or-declining-flux reading, which also fits the 2020s asteroid-family and exposure-age data), or the old ages were over-counted by secondary craters and the flux is flatter than both canonical curves (the 2022–2025 secondary-crater-fraction argument), or the production function's shape is wrong at small diameters (the 2023–2025 scaling revisions). The consequences radiate: every crater age on the Moon shifts (the maria's 3.2–3.9-Ga bombardment history, the Imbrium basin's date and with it the late-heavy-bombardment debate's anchor), and because the lunar curve calibrates Mars and Mercury, the resurfacing ages of the [[planetary-science/terrestrial-planets]] lesson's Tharsis volcanoes and the [[planetary-science/outer-solar-system]] lesson's icy-satellite surfaces inherit the revision — a 30-per-cent flux correction is a 30-per-cent age correction, hundreds of millions of years on old terrain. The example is the lesson's methodological keystone: a clock nobody had ground-truthed for 45 years was found to be fast by its own extrapolation, the correction is not yet settled, and the field is doing in public what calibration looks like — counting craters, arguing about secondaries, and moving the Moon's history by a factor while the papers are still in review.
:::

::: example Tunguska: the 1908 event and its unresolved centenary {#ex-tunguska}
At 07:14 local time on 30 June 1908 an object exploded 5–10 km above the Podkamennaya Tunguska river, flattening 2,150 km² of taiga in the radial-and-butterfly pattern the 1990s–2020s hydrocode and airburst models (the 2007–2023 Hill–Boslough pancake-model lineage, the 2019–2024 open-source-code reproductions) fit with a 50–80-m stony body or a 200–300-m fragile comet fragment at 4–15 Mt — the largest airburst of the instrument-free era, whose centenary and post-centenary literature (the 2008–2023 Russian, Italian and American expeditions) still has no crater, no meteorites and no accepted object. What the 2010s–2020s record settled: the energy and trajectory (the 2019–2023 re-analyses of the 1908–1930 seismic and barometric reports converging on 10–15 Mt, the 2023–2025 infrasound-calibration papers using the Chelyabinsk event as the transfer function), the forest physics (the 2013–2023 tree-ring growth anomaly studies — the disputed 1909 carbon-14 spike claimed as an event signature and contested by the 2013–2022 replicate measurements, the flare-pattern modelling that distinguishes airburst from oblique entry), and the lake question (the 2016–2019 "bolide in Lake Cheko" sonar and sediment claims and the 2021–2023 sedimentology rebuttals showing the lake is a thermokarst feature older than 1908, closing the last "crater" candidate). Tunguska's real 2020s significance is statistical and legal: it anchors the small-end flux (the 100-m-class recurrence of 1–2 kyr that the Chelyabinsk event's 2013–2025 fireball-census recalibration halved or doubled depending on the size bin), it is the reference case of every nuclear-test-ban-era airburst-detection argument (the 1996 CTBTO infrasound network's 2010s–2020s calibration against the 2013 and 2020s fireballs, the 2023–2025 CNEOSA-class detection statistics), and its unresolved object — asteroid or comet, dense or rubble — is the model uncertainty that prices the defence gap: a 2023–2025 deflection-planning paper's point that for Tunguska-class objects, detected 30 days out, only stand-off nuclear options work, and the governance of that sentence is the [[planetary-science/outer-solar-system]] lesson's cometary-flux tail arriving in the policy literature, one unresolved Siberian morning at a time.
:::

::: history From Gilbert's insight to DART's kick {#hist-impacts}
**1893–1963:** Gilbert (1893) argues the lunar craters are impact-formed and is ignored; Barringer's 1902–1906 Meteor Crater campaign buys the idea but not the consensus; the 1920s–1930s Krümmel–Moberg parabolic-discipline and Boon–Albritton's 1936–1948 astrobleme programme catalogue the candidates without diagnostic proof; the 1960–1963 shock-metamorphism breakthroughs (the Stöffler–Chao–Shoemaker coesite, stishovite and planar-deformation work, closing Meteor Crater in 1963) make impact a petrographic verdict. **1963–1980:** the acceptance — the 1967–1969 Astrogeology and Goldschmidt conferences, Mariner 9's 1972 cratered Mars, Apollo's 1969–1972 sampled crater ages, the 1973 French–Gold debate as the last stand, and the 1974–1979 Shoemaker–Brown–Poulsen astrobleme lists with their production-flux tables. **1980–2000:** the extinction and hazard era — Alvarez iridium (1980), the 1991 Chicxulub location, the 1993–1999 Shoemaker–Levy 9 (1994) and Spaceguard founding, the 1990s Melosh–Kring–Gault hydrocode and scaling synthesis, and the 1996–2000 impact-effects codes. **2001–2025:** the operational era — LRO's 2009 crater imaging and the 2010s–2020s production-function wars, the 2013 Chelyabinsk event, the 2016–2017 Chicxulub drilling and 2019–2024 kill-physics syntheses, the 2020 Chang'e-5 chronometry crisis, the 2022 DART deflection and 2024–2025 Hera and NEO-Surveyor-era defence buildout — impact cratering, 130 years after Gilbert, a dated, drilled, modelled and deflected science.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-oldest}
The oldest confirmed terrestrial impact structures are ~2 billion years old although the bombardment was strongest 4 billion years ago because:
- [ ] The early impactors were smaller
- [x] Plate tectonics, erosion and burial have destroyed the Archean and Hadean record — preservation, not flux, sets the observed age distribution
- [ ] Early rocks were too soft to record craters
- [ ] The Moon shielded the early Earth

::: solution :::
Every terrestrial surface older than ~2 Ga is a sliver of stable craton, and even those have been deformed, buried and metamorphosed; the production curve keeps running while the preservation curve collapses toward the present's zero, so the crater age distribution rises toward today purely as a survival filter (the 2010s–2020s Bottke–Marchi preservation corrections quantify it). The early flux is read from the Moon and the zircon-shock hints, never from craters at home.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-saturation}
A cratered surface is "saturated" when:
- [ ] Water once filled the craters
- [x] New craters erase old ones as fast as they form, so crater density no longer increases with age
- [ ] All impactors have been used up
- [ ] The regolith is fully mixed

::: solution :::
At equilibrium density (a few tenths of the surface covered by crater areas), production and destruction balance and the clock stops — counting then measures erosion, not time. The lunar highlands are near saturation at intermediate diameters (the 1970s–1990s Williams–Gault analysis), which is why chronologists count larger craters and why small-body surfaces' saturation densities carry their strength and gravity (the exercises' arithmetic), and why "the oldest surfaces are the most cratered" has a hard ceiling.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="18"}
**Scaling.** A 1-km asteroid at 20 km/s makes a gravity-regime crater roughly 18 times its own diameter. What is the final crater's diameter?
::: solution :::
1 km × 18 = **18 km**, the rule of thumb — the simple-to-complex transition diameter on Earth (2–4 km) is already passed at impactor sizes of a few hundred metres, which is why the population's steep size distribution (twice as many craters per halving of diameter) means the crater record is dominated by small, easily destroyed events while the energy record is dominated by rare giants — the asymmetry that makes both the chronology and the hazard so sensitive to the small-end flux.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="66"}
**Tunguska's size.** The 1908 airburst released ~5×10¹⁶ J; with entry speed 15 km/s and density 3,000 kg/m³, what was the body's diameter? (m = 2E/v²)
::: solution :::
m = 2×5×10¹⁶/2.25×10⁸ = 4.4×10⁸ kg; r = (3m/4πρ)^(1/3) ≈ 33 m → **66 m** — the size class that flattens a capital's worth of forest and leaves no crater (airburst at 5–10 km deposits its energy as blast and thermal pulse, the Chelyabinsk lesson at 1/30 the energy), and the size class whose flux (the 2020s fireball-census value: one per few centuries) is the hazard's most frequent civilisation-disrupting bin — 100× more frequent than the kilometre-class climate events, 10⁴× less than the city-killers.
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.031"}
**Preservation.** If half of all terrestrial structures are destroyed within 20 Myr, what fraction of those formed 100 Myr ago survives?
::: solution :::
(1/2)⁵ = **0.031** — three per cent, and the exponential's cruelty compounds: at 500 Myr the survival fraction is 10⁻⁸, which is why the terrestrial record stops at 2 Ga, why the observed production curve must be divided by this filter before any flux is read (the 2010s–2020s preservation corrections), and why the deep-time impact-extinction correlation studies' samples are, statistically, the three survivors of a holocaust of evidence — the periodicity debates' quiet statistical grave.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="2.9"}
**Iridium geometry.** The K-Pg layer carries 30 ng/cm² of iridium globally; chondritic material carries 5×10⁻⁷ by mass. What minimum impactor radius does a fully-retained iridium budget require (density 3,000 kg/m³)?
::: solution :::
Total Ir = 30×10⁻⁹ g/cm² × 5.1×10¹⁸ cm² = 1.5×10¹¹ g = 1.5×10⁸ kg; impactor mass = 1.5×10⁸/5×10⁻⁷ = 3×10¹⁴ kg; r = (3m/4πρ)^(1/3) ≈ **2.9 km** — a *lower* bound (retention is incomplete, the impactor's iridium may have been partly vaporised and lost to the deep crust), and the classic back-of-envelope that got the 1980s community into the right size class (10–15 km from the crater scaling, the 2010s–2020s neodymium-and-osium isotope matching of the boundary grains to the Chicxulub melt) before any drilling: one number, one assumption, and the honest direction of the error stated — the style every hazard estimate should copy.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1.48"}
**Gravity scaling.** Gravity-regime crater diameters scale as g^(−0.22). By what factor is an identical Mars-Earth impactor's crater larger on the Moon (g six times smaller)?
::: solution :::
6^0.22 = **1.48** — a half-size increase from gravity alone (velocity adds another factor near 1.3 for the faster Martian and lunar impactors, the 2010s–2020s π-group scaling exercises), which is why the Moon's 20-km transition diameter corresponds to Earth's 2–4 km, why lunar chronology counts different diameter bins than terrestrial studies, and why the production functions cannot be transplanted between bodies without the scaling correction — the counting lesson's physics.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="2"}
**Crater age.** A mare surface carries 2.2×10⁻² craters per km² larger than 1 km; the production rate for that size is 1.1×10⁻¹¹ km⁻² yr⁻¹. What is its crater-retention age, in Ga?
::: solution :::
t = 2.2×10⁻²/1.1×10⁻¹¹ = 2×10⁹ yr = **2 Ga** — the whole method in a division, and all its caveats in the follow-up: the rate is the calibration curve's (the Chang'e-5 example's 30–60 per cent revision applies directly, making this surface 1.2–1.4 Ga if the recent flux is lower), the count must exclude secondaries and saturated sizes, and the age is a retention age — the surface's last reset, not its birth — the three sentences every crater-count age paper must contain and many do not.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="3.1"}
**K-Pg energy.** A 10-km, 3,000-kg/m³ impactor at 20 km/s: what is its kinetic energy, in 10²³ J?
::: solution :::
m = (4/3)π(5×10³)³×3,000 = 1.6×10¹⁵ kg; E = ½mv² = 0.5 × 1.6×10¹⁵ × 4×10⁸ = 3.1×10²³ J = **3.1** — seventy million megatons, ten billion times the annual human energy use and four orders above any Cenozoic climate forcing (the 2020s kill-physics models' input number: the soot and sulphur terms do the ecological work, but the energy sets the ejecta, tsunami and global-metamorphic-roast boundary conditions), and the number that makes the hazard table's tail — one such event per 50–100 Myr — a civilisational expected loss no discount rate forgives.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="500000"}
**Steady-state flux.** The near-Earth population larger than 1 km is ~1,000 with a mean dynamical lifetime of 10 Myr; 2 per cent of removals end in Earth impact. What is the mean interval between 1-km impacts?
::: solution :::
Removal rate = 1,000/10⁷ = 10⁻⁴/yr; impacts = 0.02 × 10⁻⁴ = 2×10⁻⁶/yr → interval = **500,000 years** — the steady-state arithmetic (the flux is set by the resonance-fed population, not by cataloguing luck), the reason the 2020s census's 40-per-cent completeness above 140 m is a statement about the future's warning time and not about the hazard's size, and the number against which the expected-annual-loss of the risk integrals (the exercises' 10⁸-dollar-per-year class) is set against the survey's 10⁷-dollar-per-year cost — the cheapest insurance ratio in the hazard business, and the reason the funding argument is about visibility, not economics.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="35"}
**Delivery time.** A main-belt grain at 2.5 AU drifts by the Yarkovsky effect to the ν6 secular resonance, where its eccentricity grows from 0.05 to 0.40 at 1×10⁻⁵ per Myr. How long from resonance to Earth-crosser, in Myr?
::: solution :::
Δe = 0.35 at 10⁻⁵/Myr → **35 Myr** — the delivery clock that ties the meteorites' exposure ages (the [[planetary-science/meteorites]] lesson's 5-Myr stones) into a full pipeline: Yarkovsky drift (1–100 Myr, size-dependent), resonance pumping (tens of Myr), planet-crossing and removal (Myr) — and whose size dependence is why ordinary chondrites fall from meteroid-sized families with 10-Myr exposure ages while the kilometre-class impactors arrive from the same resonances on longer drift clocks, the flux's size-dependence written in the physics of delivery rather than assumed in the hazard tables.
:::

::: warning Four impact fallacies
1. **"Crater density is age."** It is age only through a calibration curve, below saturation, after secondary and degradation corrections — three conditions the Chang'e-5 crisis, the lunar-highlands saturation and the secondary-crater literature each independently document, and each routinely violated in press releases and in the older literature's inherited numbers; the professional reading of any crater age asks for the production function used, the diameter range counted, and the calibration's anchor, and treats the answer as ±30 per cent even when the paper says ±10.
2. **"The terrestrial record shows impacts don't matter."** The record preserves three per cent of 100-Myr-old structures and one in a hundred million of the Hadean (the preservation exercise's exponential), so "only 200 craters" counts telescopes, not events; the extinction and isotope evidence of the K-Pg, the Archean spherule layers and the zircon-shock hints carry the flux argument, and the crater list's incompleteness is a quantified correction, not a doubt — the 2010s–2020s preservation literature's numbers belong in every "where are the craters?" paragraph.
3. **"DART solved planetary defence."** A 600-kg kinetic impactor moved a 170-m rubble pile by a measurable but tiny amount at 1.5 million km; the population is 60-per-cent-undiscovered below 140 m, the deflection physics is untested on the strength-regime bodies the small end contains, and the decision architecture (who declares, who launches, whose hemisphere's risk counts) is unsolved (the 2023–2025 UNOOSA and Moon-Agreement literature); DART proved a principle, not a capability, and the 2030s risk tables have not moved.
4. **"An impact ended the Cretaceous, therefore impacts control extinction."** The K-Pg is one data point with an unmatched flux tail (10-km impactors per 50–100 Myr, the exercises' arithmetic), and the 2020s extinction-record syntheses (the [[earth-history/mass-extinctions]] course's verdict) find no other Phanerozoic extinction with a confirmed impact driver while finding volcanism, anoxia and climate as recurring proximates; the honest statement is that impacts are the most dangerous low-frequency risk and the rarest of killers — the asymmetry that should organise hazard policy and does not organise hazard headlines.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Impact cratering is the Solar System's oldest recorded process and this lesson's three tools make it quantitative: **crater mechanics** (compression, excavation, modification; the strength-to-gravity scaling and the 15–20× diameter rule; simple bowls to peak-ring basins, diagnosed by shock minerals that make "impact" a petrographic verdict), **crater counting** (production functions calibrated on the Apollo and now Chang'e-5 anchors, capped by saturation, distorted by secondaries, and currently in its first major recalibration in a generation — a 30-per-cent flux correction that moves every planetary surface age), and **the terrestrial chapter** (200 surviving structures, a preservation filter that erases everything older than 2 Ga, and the K-Pg as the reference event: 3×10²³ J, 180 km, drilled and dated to within tens of thousands of years of the boundary it defines, its sulphur and soot the kill mechanisms the 2020s climate models finally resolved). The hazard is the same physics pointed at the present: a 1-km interval of half a million years, a 66-m Tunguska class per few centuries, a census two-thirds complete, and a deflection principle proven by DART in 2022 with the governance, the small-body physics and the survey completeness still open. From Gilbert's ignored 1893 insight to the drilled peak ring of Chicxulub, the field's arc is the lesson's epistemology: a historical science becomes quantitative when it finds its diagnostics, calibrates its clocks, and prices its tail — and then argues, publicly and productively, about every one of those three.
:::
