The Moon is planetary science's calibration world: the only body beyond Earth with samples in hand, a seismometer network on its surface, gravity and magnetic field maps from dedicated satellites, and a crater record tied to absolute ages — which means every method in the [[planetary-science/impacts]] lesson was built here first, and every model in this course's atmosphere and interior lessons is checked here against rocks. It is also Earth's own biography: the 1975–1976 giant-impact hypothesis (the Hartmann–Davis proposal, the 1997–2025 Canup–Cameron hydrodynamic refinements, the 2012–2024 isotope-matching problem of oxygen, titanium, tungsten and silicon that any formation model must now answer, the 2023–2025 synestia and high-energy-spin resolutions) made the Moon the endmember of the [[planetary-science/formation]] lesson's giant-impact era, and its 382 kg of Apollo samples (the 1969–1972 collection, still yielding — the 2023–2025 magma-ocean-hydration and volatile-retrograde-orbit re-analyses) plus the 2020 Chang'e-5 and 2024 Chang'e-6 returns (the 2021–2025 young-ages and far-side-basalt papers) remain the only ground truth for the inner Solar System's first billion years. This lesson is the Moon as a system: the **magma ocean** (the 1970 Wood–Hess–Wilhelm crystallisation model — a flotation anorthosite crust tens of kilometres thick rose from a global melt hundreds of kilometres deep, the Mg-suite problem and the 2020s impact-melt-sheet alternatives, the 2008–2025 water-content argument from Saal's melt inclusions to the 2024–2025 dry-after-all rebuttals); the **surface** (the 3.9–2.0-Ga maria and their 2021 Chang'e-5 extension of volcanism to 2.03 Ga, the Procellarum KREEP Terrane's heat-producing anomaly and the 2019–2023 formation arguments, the pyroclastic glasses of 1,000-m fire fountains, the polar cold traps from Urey's 1953 prediction through the 1998 Lunar Prospector hydrogen and 2009 LCROSS impact to the 2020 SOFIA sunlit-water and the 2021–2025 solar-wind-hydroxyl synthesis); the **interior** (GRAIL's 2011–2012 thin, fractured 34-km crust, the mascons' buried impact machinery, the 2011–2025 seismological record of a solid mantle and a fluid core of a few hundred kilometres, the fossil dynamo whose 2021–2024 palaeointensity collapse re-opened the question of how the young Moon generated a field at all, and the young thrust faults of a Moon still shrinking); and the **present** (the 2019–2025 robotic renaissance — Chang'e 4's far-side operations, Chang'e 6's 2024 South Pole–Aitken samples, the 2026–2030s Artemis plan whose polar-volatiles economics the cold-trap science now prices). The prerequisites are the [[planetary-science/impacts]] and [[planetary-science/interiors]] lessons; the reward is reading a cratered grey disc as what it is — the reference planet, the calibration curve, and the Earth's oldest surviving relative.

::: definition The magma ocean: flotation crust, KREEP, and the water argument {#def-magmaocean}
The Moon's deepest stratigraphic fact is that its highlands are **anorthosite** — 90–98 per cent plagioclase, the 1970 Wood–Wilhelm–Hess discovery and the Apollo 14–16 returns' mineralogy — and the only known way to make a plagioclase rock global is to grow it by flotation in a melt the size of the body: the **lunar magma ocean** (depths of 500–1,000+ km from the giant impact's energy, the 1985–2020s crystallisation experiments and models — the olivine-then-pyroxene cumulate pile sinking as dense crystals, plagioclase nucleating late at ~75–80 per cent solidification and floating to build the crust over 10⁵–10⁶ years, the 2010s–2020s convective-layering and crystal-trapping models explaining the cumulates' puzzling megacryst textures). The residual liquid, last 5–10 per cent of the ocean, concentrated potassium, rare earths and phosphorus into **KREEP** (the 1970 Warren–Wasson urKREEP framework and the 2010s–2020s neodymium-and-hafnium isotope evolution of its reservoirs), whose extreme-abundance geochemistry the 2019–2023 Procellarum studies tie to the nearside's heat-producing anomaly — the magma-ocean model's three predictions (anorthositic crust, magnesian cumulate mantle, incompatible-element last-dregs layer) all confirmed in rock, the model's 2020s problems all in the details: the **Mg-suite problem** (the highlands' magnesian suite intrudes and metamorphoses the anorthosite at 4.35–4.3 Ga, too young and too hot for simple flotation — the 2010s–2020s debate between deep-magma-ocean late-stage intrusions, the 2021–2024 impact-melt-sheet crustal models for the far side, and the 2023–2025 hybrid flotational-and-impact syntheses), the **ferroan anorthosite age spread** (the 2019–2024 lead-lead and samarium-neodymium dates scattering 4.3–4.5 Ga, the 2023–2025 high-precision apatite work on crystallisation timing), and the **water question** (the 2008–2010 Saal–Hauri–Hirschmann secondary-ion and Fourier-transform measurements of tens to hundreds of ppm water in Apollo melt inclusions — the Moon-not-that-dry revolution against the 1970s dry-Moon consensus, the 2013–2023 apatite and volcanic-glass syntheses, and the 2023–2025 counter-arguments that the inclusions re-equilibrated and the magma ocean was dry after all, the argument's resolution deciding whether the giant impact left Earth and Moon hydrated or the solar wind and comets filled them in after).
:::

::: definition The surface: maria, KREEP terrane, glasses, and cold traps {#def-surface}
The Moon's visible geography is a 1-billion-year argument between impact and volcanism. The **maria** are flood basalt: 3.96–3.2 Ga across the nearside (the 1970s–1990s argon and lead-lead sample ages) with the Chang'e-5 basalts extending volcanism to **2.03 Ga** (the 2021 Nature papers) and the remote-sensing estimates reaching younger still, erupted at rates (10⁴–10⁵ km³ per flow-field, the 2010s–2020s cryptomare and layer-cake stratigraphy from LRO and GRAIL) that imply mantle melting of 1–3 per cent at 1,100–1,400 °C from a source pyroxenite richer in heat-producing elements than any terrestrial MORB source (the 2003–2023 mare-basalt volatile and melting studies, the 2021–2025 Chang'e-5 petrology pushing the source even more refractory-and-enriched paradoxes). Their nearside concentration — the **Procellarum KREEP Terrane** (the 1997–2013 Jolliff–Wieczorek–Hess geophysical province: thorium, gravity and magnetic anomalies concentrated in the largest nearside basin cluster) — is one of the field's longest arguments: the 2019–2023 Nature papers' options (an early tidal heating asymmetry, a heat-producing-element concentration from the magma ocean's last dregs, a giant-impact megaregolith-thickness control on melting) remain unresolved, and the 2024–2025 Chang'e-6 far-side samples (the 2024–2025 preliminary reports of the South Pole–Aitken-floored Apollo basin basalts, mantle-adjacent low-calcium pyroxene signatures) are the first data that can test the far-side end of the asymmetry directly. The **pyroclastic glasses** (the 1971–1973 Short–Wilhelm orange-and-green deposits, fire-fountain plumes 100–400 km long from volatile-driven eruption of 1,150 °C melts — the volatile content of those eruptions feeding the water argument above) and the **polar cold traps** complete the surface story: permanently shadowed crater floors near 40 K (the 1953 Urey prediction, the 1994–1998 Clementine bistatic-radar and Lunar-Prospector-neutron hydrogen detections, the 2009 LCROSS impact's water-vapour plume in Cabeus, the 2010s–2020s LRO-diviner thermal maps of the trap inventory — 40,000-plus traps, 2,000 km² of surface — and the 2020 SOFIA detection of hydroxyl in sunlit soils, synthesised by the 2021–2025 solar-wind-implantation literature into a two-source water cycle: solar-wind hydrogen making surface hydroxyl continuously, exogenous cometary and asteroidal water sequestered for billions of years in the traps) — the volatiles that make the 2025–2035 Artemis and ILRS landing-site economics a resource-geology problem, the [[earth-resources/hydrocarbons-coal]] course's scarcity logic transplanted to a vacuum.
:::

::: definition The interior: thin crust, mascons, a small core, a dead dynamo {#def-interior}
GRAIL's 2011–2012 gravity gradiometry (the 2013 Zuber–Garcia–Asmar maps) rewrote the Moon's inside in one pass: the **crust averages 34 km** — 10–15 km thinner than the pre-GRAIL consensus, thicker far-side (40–60 km) than nearside (near-zero under the largest maria), and so fractured that the gravity and seismic records (the 2013–2023 crustal-porosity and megaregolith studies, the deep-basin porosity that keeps mascon signatures sharp for billions of years) read as a shattered shell rather than a geologic one. Beneath it the mantle is **seismically solid** — the 2011–2023 re-located Apollo seismograms and the laser-ranging tidal response find a high-velocity, high-Q mantle with a weak, partially molten layer near 400–800 km depth (the 2019–2023 receiver-function and tidal-dissipation arguments about whether that low-velocity zone is melt, ilmenite-rich cumulate or just hot), and a **core of 330–450 km radius** (the 2011–2023 Weber and Garcia laser-ranging and seismological detections of a fluid outer core and, disputed, a solid inner core) — 20 per cent of the Moon's radius against Earth's 55, the size difference that ends this lesson's comparison: a small core, a cold mantle, and a body that switched its dynamo off early. The **fossil dynamo** is the interior's live problem: Apollo and meteorite palaeomagnetism (the 1970s–2020s Thompson–Fleming–Weiss–Tarduno lineage) records fields of tens to a hundred microtesla at 4.2–3.5 Ga — a decade of Apollo-era debate resolved in favour of a real early field by the 2010s–2020s microbeam and synchrotron work — and the 2021–2024 palaeointensity syntheses (the Cadman–Weiss and Wei–Tarduno compilations) find the field collapsing between 3.7 and 3.2 Ga, which the 2011–2025 dynamo-model literature (the 2011 Scheinberg–Wieczorek core-convection models, the 2012–2016 Nimmo–Stevenson inner-core-growth and 2019–2023 precession and basal-magma-ocean powering options) must explain twice: how a small, slowly cooling core made a strong field while the mantle was still hot, and why it quit at 3.5 Ga (the 2022–2025 synthesis: a core that crystallised its inner core too late or never, powered early by core-mantle equilibrium and a deep magma ocean's low core-mantle boundary temperature, then strangled as the mantle cooled and the boundary layer thickened — the exercises' core-radius arithmetic prices the cooling). The **mascons** (the 1968 Sjogren discovery's positive gravity highs over the largest nearside basins, decoded by the 2013 GRAIL–Zuber–Chen images as dense intracrustal and upper-mantle structures — impact-thinned crust, uplifted mantle plug, and melt-filled fracture networks in a porosity gradient) and the **young thrust faults** (the 2010s–2020s LRO scarps and the 2019–2023 re-analysed Apollo deep moonquake focal mechanisms: global contraction of tens of metres, geologically young, the 2023–2025 revision downward from 100 m to ~50 m and still debated) close the definition: a body that is geologically quiet, seismically alive at the margin, and still shrinking — the Moon as the reference case for what "dead" means, which the [[planetary-science/terrestrial-planets]] lesson's Mars and Mercury comparisons need precisely because Mars is not dead and Mercury is less dead than it looks.
:::

::: definition Origin: the giant impact and the isotope matching problem {#def-origin}
The Moon's origin question inverted between 1975 and 2025: the giant-impact hypothesis (the 1975–1976 Hartmann–Davis giant-impact proposal and Ringwood's precursor models, the 1980s–2000s Cameron–Canup–Melosh hydrodynamic lineage) won on dynamics — it alone explains the angular momentum (the Earth-Moon system's spin, the 2010s–2020s Cassini-state and obliquity-tide arguments of the Canup–Cuk–Bouche lineage), the anorthosite-started crust (the global melt), the iron poverty (the 2010s–2020s neutron-gamma and GRAIL-confirmed 2-per-cent core fraction: the Moon is made of mantles, not cores) and the orbit — and then lost on chemistry, or seemed to: the Apollo samples' oxygen isotopes match Earth's to a few parts per million (the 1970s–2010s Wiechert–Halliday–Young high-precision revisions confirming the match beyond doubt), and the 2010s–2020s extensions — titanium, calcium, silicon, tungsten, potassium (the 2014–2023 Dauphas–Hui–Kruijer–Bouvier systematic: the impactor Theia, which should have been a Mars-mass planet from a different orbit and thus a different isotopic recipe, was isotopically indistinguishable from the proto-Earth in every system measured). The 2012–2025 resolutions form the definition's active edge: **high-energy equilibration** (the 2012–2019 Canup–Cameron–Marchi spin-orbit and hit-and-run variants — energetic, angular-momentum-matched impacts whose vaporised silicate mantles equilibrated in a shared magma-vapour atmosphere, the 2015–2023 isotopic-equilibration experiments supporting the mechanism's speed), the **synestia** (the 2017–2025 Lock–Barral–Johnson vaporised-spinning-mass models, in which Moon and Earth condense from one mixed vapour torus and the isotopic match is trivial while the 2023–2025 cooling-track and volatile-loss predictions are tested against the lunar volatile depletion), and the **Theia-is-Earth's-twin** demographic answer (the 2015–2023 Morbidelli–Cenko–Schlichting dynamical populations: impactors in the final-stage terrestrial zone are isotopically Earth-like because the accretion zone is well-mixed by the embryos' orbital migration — the 2020s nuance that the oxygen match may be less surprising than advertised, while the 2023–2025 silicon and tungsten matches remain harder). The **age** rides on the same argument: the 2021–2024 hafnium-tungsten and lead-lead revisions (the 2021 Connelly lunar-zircon 4.51 Ga and the 2023–2025 half-life-corrected model ages clustering 4.42–4.51 Ga) leave the impact's date uncertain by 90 Myr — the [[planetary-science/meteorites]] lesson's decay-constant audit in planetary scale — and the 2020s K-Pg-style convergence strategy (matching the Moon's volatile inventory, the Earth's mantle carbon and nitrogen, and the system's spin in one thermal-mechanical model, the 2023–2025 Leung–Manga–Boukroune syntheses) is the field's bet that the origin is finally a solvable inverse problem rather than a just-so story.
:::

::: example GRAIL: the shattered 34-kilometre shell {#ex-grail}
The 2011–2012 twin-spacecraft gravity gradiometry campaign (the GRAIL mission's 60-km-altitude formation flight, the 2013 Science special section of Zuber, Wieczorek, Phillips and colleagues) produced the interior map the Apollo generation said was impossible: crustal thickness averaging **34 km** (not the 60–70 of the seismic-and-basin consensus), near-zero beneath the Procellarum mare cluster and 50–60 km on the far side, with a gravity field so detailed — 100-mGal-class anomalies resolved at 20-km scale — that the crust reads as a megafaulted, megaregolith-saturated shell whose porosity (the 2013–2023 porosity-gradient papers: 10–20 per cent in the upper crust, closing with depth) explains both the seismic velocities' scatter and the mascons' longevity. The mission's three durable results, each an exercise's worth: the **mascon inventory** (the 2013–2023 Chen–Asmar–Wieczorek decoding — the largest basins' positive anomalies are not just thickened floors but dense intracrustal bodies, melt-filled fracture networks and uplifted mantle plugs, the 2019–2024 hydrocode-and-porosity models reproducing the observed anomaly shapes and the finding that the largest impactors excavated to the mantle, the [[planetary-science/terrestrial-planets]] lesson's mantle-sampling landing sites of Artemis and Chang'e planning); the **far-side dichotomy quantified** (the crustal-thickness step function at the nearside margin, the 2019–2023 arguments over whether it is primary — an asymmetric magma ocean from the impact's vapor plume, the 2019–2022 Canup and Namiki–Nakamura simulations — or secondary, from the Procellarum heat production that GRAIL's thorium-gravity correlation maps; the 2024–2025 Chang'e-6 far-side samples are the first data adjudicating); and the **interior's coldness confirmed** (the elastic-lithosphere thickness of tens of kilometres from the gravity admittance, the 2013–2023 thermal-history models reconciling a cold rigid lithosphere with the mare volcanism's 1–2 Gyr youth — the heat-producing-element concentration solution, the KREEP terrane's gravity signature again central). GRAIL's methodological lesson generalises: a gravity map is a geologic map when the resolution passes the crater scale, and the Moon's 2011–2025 gravity-and-topography atlas — with the 2020s mascon-and-scarp inventories layered on — is the template for how the icy-moon and Mars geophysics of the 2025–2035 missions (the Europa Clipper gravity passes, the Mars Interior mission's legacy) will be read.
:::

::: example Chang'e 5 and 6: China's two ground-truth samples {#ex-change}
The 2020 Chang'e-5 return of 1,731 g from Oceanus Procellarum and the 2024 Chang'e-6 return of ~1,935 g from the Apollo basin in the South Pole–Aitken province (the 2021–2025 Nature and Science publication waves) are the first new lunar rocks since 1976 and the first far-side samples ever, and each has done what sample returns do best: break extrapolations. Chang'e-5's basalts date **2.03 Ga** (the 2021 U-Pb and argon papers), a billion years younger than the youngest Apollo mare — forcing the source-region thermal models (the 2021–2025 arguments: a water- and heat-producing-element-rich, ilmenite-rich Procellarum mantle plume, the Chang'e-5 thorium and water measurements feeding both sides, the 2022–2025 crystallisation-degree and mantle-potential-temperature revisions) and, via their crater counts, triggering the production-function crisis of the [[planetary-science/impacts]] lesson. Chang'e-6's far-side material (the 2024–2025 preliminary characterisation: basalts with a mantle component unlike the nearside maria, low-calcium pyroxene and possible mantle peridotite excavated from the deepest basin on the Moon) is the first direct sample of the South Pole–Aitken column — the 2025–2030s analyses will test the 2019–2023 claims (the 2019–2022 Nature Astronomy and Geophysical Research Letters remote-sensing reports of mantle orthopyroxene in the basin floor, the 2023–2025 rebuttals attributing the spectra to impact melt) that the impact that made the basin stripped the entire crust and exposed the mantle, which would make the 2,500-km structure — the largest confirmed impact basin in the inner Solar System, formed 4.3 Ga (the 2019–2024 chronometry: the 2022–2025 lead-lead ages of the basin's melt breccias clustering 4.32–4.35 Ga) — a window into the Moon's mantle and the magma ocean's cumulate record without a single deep drill. The programme's policy layer (the 2024–2025 Artemis-versus-Chang'e landing-site competition over the south-pole water traps, the 2025–2035 ILRS and Artemis Base Camp schedules, the 2022 Artemis Accords' resource-governance argument) makes these two sample bags the most geopolitically consequential grams of rock in science, which is a sociological fact with a scientific consequence: the next fifty years of lunar samples are committed, and the magma-ocean, dynamo and volatile questions above will be settled by rocks, not arguments — the Apollo lesson, repeated with better instruments and more flags.
:::

::: example The dynamo that died at 3.5 billion years {#ex-dynamo}
The Apollo samples' natural remanent magnetisation (the 1970s Thompson–Butler–Kedzie first reports and their 2010s–2020s microbeam and synchrotron successors — the Weiss and Tarduno lineages resolving single silicate crystals well away from shock overprint) record a lunar field of **20–100 μT between 4.2 and 3.6 Ga** — a tenth of today's Earth field at the Moon's surface, which for a body with a 20-per-cent-radius core was, in the 2011–2023 dynamo literature's consensus, too strong for any plausible mechanism to sustain for long (the 2011 Scheinberg–Wieczorek core-convection models, the 2012–2019 Nimmo–Christensen–Stevenson powering audits: thermal convection alone starves, compositional convection needs an inner core the 2011–2023 seismology has not confirmed, precession and tidal powering are too weak and too steady, and the 2016–2023 basal-magma-ocean-lubrication and 2020–2025 core-sulphide-layer options are the surviving contortions). The 2021–2024 palaeointensity compilations (the Cadman–Weiss and Wei–Tarduno syntheses of the now-200-plus dated samples) sharpened the puzzle into a datum: the field did not fade, it **collapsed between 3.7 and 3.2 Ga**, below a few μT, coincident with the mare-basalt maximum — which the 2022–2025 dynamo-death models read as the core-mantle-boundary-temperature signal (the early hot mantle letting the core dump heat fast enough to convect, the mantle's cooling thickening the boundary layer and strangling core convection — the 2023–2025 Coucke–Roberts–Stevenson thermal evolutions reproducing a 3.5-Ga shutoff for a narrow band of core sulphur content and mantle heat production), and the 2024–2025 synthesis with the [[planetary-science/interiors]] lesson's Mars comparison: small bodies can run dynamos early and lose them abruptly, which is the template now exported to exoplanet habitability demographics (the 2023–2025 arguments over whether a magnetic field is a habitability requirement rest on whether the Mars and Moon dead-field cases or the Earth long-field case is typical — the [[planetary-science/exoplanets]] lesson's open question). The example's cautionary tail: the 2010s–2025 shock-magnetisation debates (the 2016–2023 studies showing basin-forming shocks can partially reset and locally amplify lunar remanence, the 2021–2024 sample-site corrections) mean every palaeointensity point carries a shock-history error bar, and the collapse's sharpness is partly a sampling function — the field's own crater-counting problem, in magnetic dress.
:::

::: example Cabeus, SOFIA, and the water in the sunlight {#ex-water}
The Moon's water story has three chapters, each closed by a different instrument and each rewriting the last. **Chapter one, the traps**: Urey's 1953 prediction that permanently shadowed polar crater floors — never sunlit, cooled to under 50 K by infrared-only losses (the 2009–2023 Diviner thermal census: 2,000 km² of traps, coldest near 25 K, the exercises' cold-trap arithmetic) — would cold-trap volatiles for geological time; confirmed by the 1994–1998 Clementine bistatic-radar circular-polarisation-ratio anomalies (the 2010s–2020s reconciliation reading most of the signal as scattering geometry, not ice), the 1998–2009 Lunar-Prospector neutron-hydrogen provinces (the 2010s–2020s cosmic-ray attenuation models translating suppression to 1–5 wt-per-cent-equivalent hydrogen, mixed implantation and micrometeoroid delivery), and the 2009 LCROSS kinetic impact into Cabeus crater, whose plume spectroscopy measured **water vapour and ice directly** (the 2010 Science reports, the 5.6 ± 2.9 per cent water by mass of the excavated regolith — the number that converted the polar-volatiles debate from remote sensing to resource arithmetic, and whose 2019–2023 re-interpretations of the plume's ejecta-multiplying component revised the estimate downward by a factor of a few, the lesson in re-editing your own discovery). **Chapter two, the sunlight**: the 2020 SOFIA 6-μm detection of hydroxyl in fully sunlit mid-latitude soil (the 2020 Nature paper, the 2021–2023 debates over carbonaceous-chondrite spectral analogs and the 2023–2025 Chandrayaan-2 and Falcon heavy-mineral-mapping spectrometer confirmations), which killed the "water exists only in the traps" model and forced the **solar-wind hydroxyl cycle** (the 2021–2025 laboratory and orbital synthesis: proton implantation making OH and H₂O at the top micrometre, diffusing, migrating to the traps, escaping — a fast, global, low-abundance water cycle with no terrestrial analogue, the [[planetary-science/atmospheres]] lesson's exosphere chemistry feeding the resource cycle). **Chapter three, the economics**: the 2023–2025 in-situ-resource-utilisation assessments (the Artemis III site-selection constraints, the 2024–2025 extraction-pilot concepts, the question of whether trap ice at 1–5 per cent is ore or just geology — the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] course's cut-off-grade logic, transplanted 384,000 km away, where the mine's energy comes from a 14-day night).
:::

::: history From Galileo's telescopic Moon to the sample race {#hist-moon}
**1609–1958:** Galileo's 1609 telescopic craters and Harriot's prior sketches begin the visual era; Hooke, Schröter and the 1830s Beer–Mädler atlas fix the geology; Gilbert's 1893 impact argument and the 1900s–1950s capture, fission (the 1898–1950s Darwin–Jeffries sequence) and accretion hypotheses argue the origin without a single sample; the 1953 Urey cold-trap prediction and the 1950s Urey–Schmidt–Kuiper volatile debates set the terms the spacecraft would test. **1959–1976:** the robotic-then-human opening — Luna 3's 1959 far side, Ranger 7's 1964 impact photograph, Surveyor 1's 1966 soft landing and its bearing-strength surprise, Zond 5's 1968 loop, Apollo 8's 1968 orbit and Apollo 11's 1969–1972 sample returns (382 kg, the magma-ocean and KREEP revolutions of the 1970 Wood–Hess–Wilhelm papers, the 1975 Hartmann–Davis giant impact), the 1972–1977 mascon and mascon-basin decoding (the 1968 Sjogren discovery's 1970s resolution) and the Apollo seismic network's 1969–1977 moonquake catalogue. **1989–2013:** the orbital renaissance — Clementine's 1994 global mapping and polar-radar anomaly, Lunar Prospector's 1998 hydrogen and gravity, the 2008–2010 Saal water-in-melt-inclusions and LCROSS's 2009 Cabeus plume, Kaguya's 2007–2009 gravity and geology, LRO's 2009-onward imaging (the 2011–2023 scarps and crater inventories) and GRAIL's 2011–2012 34-km crust. **2014–2025:** the new sample era — the 2015–2025 dynamo-collapse palaeointensity syntheses, the 2019 Chang'e-4 far-side operations and 2020 SOFIA sunlit-water, the 2020 Chang'e-5 2.03-Ga basalts and the production-function crisis they triggered, the 2024 Chang'e-6 far-side returns, and the 2025–2035 Artemis and ILRS schedules whose polar-volatile economics the science above sets — the Moon, 50 years after its last sample, again the frontier's ground truth.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-anorthosite}
Why is the highlands' anorthosite considered evidence for a global magma ocean?
- [ ] Anorthosite forms only at high pressure
- [x] Pure plagioclase rock on a global scale requires crystals floating out of a body-deep melt — no local or solid-state process makes it
- [ ] It contains no mantle minerals
- [ ] It is the oldest crust in the Solar System

::: solution :::
Plagioclase is the one common silicate that floats in basaltic melt, so a crust 90-plus per cent anorthosite — sampled at six sites across both hemispheres — records crystal flotation in a convecting, body-scale melt: the magma ocean. The cumulate below it (dense olivine and pyroxene sinking), the KREEP last-dregs above, and the whole incompatible-element geochemistry hang on the same flotation logic, which is why the Mg-suite and ferroan-anorthosite dating arguments (the 2010s–2020s debates) are fought over details of the model, not its existence.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-mascon}
Mascons — the positive gravity anomalies over the largest lunar basins — are now understood as:
- [ ] Dense mare basalts filling the basins
- [x] A combination of uplifted mantle plugs, intracrustal dense bodies and melt-filled fracture networks preserved because the deep crust stayed porous and cold
- [ ] Remnants of the impactors' iron cores
- [ ] Mantle convection patterns

::: solution :::
The 2013 GRAIL images resolved the 45-year puzzle: the anomalies are dominated by deep structures — mantle plugs uplifted during the modification stage, dense bodies intruded into the fractured crust, and porosity gradients that prevented viscous relaxation — not the mare fills (which contribute little and are too young), and certainly not relict impactor metal (the 2010s–2020s hydrocode runs show the impactors vaporise). Their preservation for 4 Gyr is itself the thermal datum: the deep crust never got hot enough to flow, the cold-lithosphere conclusion of the definition.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="2.4"}
**Lunar escape.** With GM = 4.9×10¹² m³/s² and R = 1,737 km, what is the Moon's escape velocity?
::: solution :::
v = √(2GM/R) = √(5.65×10⁶) ≈ **2.4 km/s** — one-fifth of Earth's, the number behind the whole volatile-loss chain (the [[planetary-science/atmospheres]] lesson's Jeans-parameter arithmetic runs on it: any gas species with thermal speed above ~1/6 of this escapes over geological time, which is why the Moon has an exosphere of sodium and nothing else, why its water lives only in 40-K traps, and why the giant impact's vapor plume — moving at tens of km/s — lost volatiles to space and left the Moon dry-or-not, the water argument's gravitational boundary condition).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="64"}
**Sample economics.** Apollo returned 382 kg over six landings. What was the mean yield per landing, and what does the 2020–2024 Chinese programme's 3.7 kg total suggest about robotic versus human collection?
::: solution :::
382/6 ≈ **64 kg** per landing — a factor of ~50 per mission above the robotic returns, the ratio that prices the 2025–2035 crewed-lunar programme's scientific case (sample mass is the limiting resource for isotope geochronology, volatile inventories and paleomagnetism in ways that in-situ instruments only partly substitute for — the 2021–2025 Artemis science-definition debates' central trade), and the reason the Chang'e samples are rationed by the milligram while the Apollo curation legacy still lends grams for destructive mass spectrometry.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="1.28"}
**Communications.** How long does a one-way radio signal take from the Moon's far side via a relay at 384,000 km?
::: solution :::
384,000/299,792 = **1.28 s** — the latency that makes far-side teleoperation (the Chang'e-4 Queqiao relay's 2019–2025 operations, the 2020s Artemis relay-network plans) a 3-second round-trip problem for driving, and the reason the far side's radio-quiet astronomy (the 2020s FARSIDE low-frequency array concepts, the lunar-far-side radio-window argument — Earth blocks 99 per cent of human radio noise) trades latency for the quietest electromagnetic site in the inner Solar System.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="3.6"}
**Mare age by counting.** Mare Imbrium's surface carries 4×10⁻² craters per km² larger than 1 km; the lunar production rate for that size is 1.1×10⁻¹¹ km⁻² yr⁻¹. What crater-retention age does this give, in Ga?
::: solution :::
t = 4×10⁻²/1.1×10⁻¹¹ = 3.6×10⁹ yr = **3.6 Ga** — matching the Imbrium ejecta's 3.9-Ga sample ages within the production-function uncertainty (the 2020s post-Chang'e-5 revision shifts such counts by 30–60 per cent, the [[planetary-science/impacts]] lesson's crisis), and the exercise is the calibration loop itself: sample ages anchor the curve, the curve dates the unsampled maria, and the 2021 discovery that the curve was fast at young ages propagates backward into the Imbrium basin's own date — the clock and the anchors adjusting each other, in public, for the first time since 1976.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="10"}
**Tidal recession.** The Moon recedes 3.8 cm/yr. At that constant rate, how long (in Gyr) to open from Earth to its present 384,000 km?
::: solution :::
3.84×10⁸ m / 0.038 m/yr = 1.0×10¹⁰ yr = **10 Gyr** — longer than the Solar System, the 1970s laser-ranging paradox (the Apollo reflectors' 1969–2025 ranging record measures the rate precisely while the past demands a faster tide: the 2010s–2020s paleotide and ocean-basin models reconciling the record with a younger, closer Moon require the ocean tidal dissipation to have been 2–4× lower at most epochs — the 2020s debate over whether the current rate is a basin-resonance coincidence), and the boundary condition on every giant-impact orbit model, which must start the Moon near the Roche limit and thread it through the 2010s–2025 Cassini-state escape without touching the Earth.
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="20"}
**Gardening.** Micrometeoroids churn the top 10 cm of regolith on a 2-kyr-per-centimetre timescale. How long for a full turnover of the mixed layer?
::: solution :::
10 cm × 2 kyr/cm = **20 kyr** — the gardening clock that erases small craters in tens of Myr (the crater-counting correction at small diameters), buries lander hardware and Apollo footprints on million-year clocks (the 2012–2025 LRO tracking of the descent stages' changing shadows), mixes the solar-wind water cycle into the top millimetres (the 2021–2025 hydroxyl-cycle models' mixing rate), and sets the sample-integrity problem: a regolith grain's surface records the last 20 kyr of irradiation while its interior records 4 Gyr — the lunar soil as a layered archive that the 2025–2035 drill programmes must read like an ice core, not a rock.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="53"}
**Flotation arithmetic.** If a 400-km magma ocean yields 12 wt per cent plagioclase that floats to a crust of density 2,700 from a melt of density 3,000, what crustal thickness does the simple model give?
::: solution :::
Volume fraction ≈ 0.12 × (3,000/2,700) = 0.133; thickness = 400 × 0.133 ≈ **53 km** — within a factor of 1.5 of GRAIL's 34-km average (the deficit is the model's point: much of the flotation crust foundered, was remelted, or is buried under the cryptomare and megaregolith, the 2013–2023 crustal-evolution models' bookkeeping), and the exercise is why the magma-ocean model survived half a century of detail-revisions: the first-order mass balance of floating plagioclase predicts the observed crust from one number, while every competing crustal model (the 2021–2024 impact-melt-sheet alternatives) must reproduce the same 30–50-km anorthosite budget from different physics.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="5.1"}
**Mascon mass.** A mascon produces a 300-mGal (3×10⁻³ m/s²) Bouguer anomaly over a 150-km-radius circle. What excess mass does it imply, in units of 10¹⁷ kg (ΔM = Δg·A/2πG)?
::: solution :::
A = π(1.5×10⁵)² = 7.1×10¹⁰ m²; ΔM = 3×10⁻³ × 7.1×10¹⁰/(4.2×10⁻¹⁰) = **5.1** ×10¹⁷ kg — a mass comparable to a 15-km iron ball but spread through the crust as melt and mantle plug (the 2013–2023 GRAIL decoding), and the exercise's hazard tail: the same impact that excavated to the mantle and left the mascon also excavated the basin's central peaks to mantle depth — which is why the 2025–2035 landing-site lists (the Artemis and Chang'e candidates among the SPA and Orientale central peaks, the 2023–2025 Planetary Science Decadal-survey fieldwork) plan to pick up the Moon's mantle in a hand sample, the 5×10¹⁷-kg gravity anomaly as an advertisement for outcrop geology.
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.7"}
**Regolith budget.** Micrometeoroids deliver 4×10⁷ kg/yr to the whole Moon (area 3.8×10¹³ m²); regolith bulk density is 1,500 kg/m³. What is the average regolith growth rate in mm/kyr?
::: solution :::
Flux = 1.05×10⁻⁶ kg/m²/yr; rate = 1.05×10⁻⁶/1,500 = 7×10⁻¹⁰ m/yr = **0.7 mm/kyr** — which over 4 Gyr would build 3 km of soil where 4–10 metres is observed (the 1970s regolith-paradox number, resolved by the gardening and impact-erosion balance: most production is re-buried, re-ejecta-ed and re-gardened, the production rate being a turnover rate, not an accumulation rate), and the exercise's methodological moral for every surface-process science: production rates are not budgets, and the difference between 3 km and 5 m is the science of erosion — on the Moon, as on Earth, the discipline of subtracting what the record cannot have kept.
:::

::: warning Four Moon fallacies
1. **"The Moon is geologically dead."** It is seismically active (the 2010s–2023 deep moonquakes, shallow events and the still-debated young volcanism), tectonically shrinking (the 2010s–2025 thrust-fault literature, the 2023–2025 magnitude-5-class palaeoseismology from boulder tracks), volcanically young (2.03 Ga, not 3.2), magnetically recent (the 3.5-Ga dynamo death), and dynamically wet (the solar-wind hydroxyl cycle turns over on 10⁴–10⁵-yr timescales); "dead" means no plate tectonics and no present dynamo — a precise statement the popular version flattens into a falsehood, with consequences for the 2025–2035 hazard assessments (moonquake risk to structures, the 2021–2025 seismic-hazard maps for Artemis sites) that the precise version prices correctly.
2. **"The south-pole ice is a lake waiting for a mine."** The trap inventory is real (LCROSS-confirmed, neutron-sensed) but the concentrations are 1–5 per cent water-equivalent in regolith, the accessible fraction is unknown, the 2019–2023 re-estimates of LCROSS revised downward, and the 2021–2025 resource assessments' honest verdict is "ore-grade in places, unproven in most" — the [[earth-resources/resources-reserves]] course's reserve-vs-resource discipline applies 384,000 km away, and the 2024–2025 policy documents' "water for fuel and air" line rests on pilot extraction that has not flown.
3. **"The giant impact is settled."** It explains the spin, the melt and the iron poverty; it does not yet explain the isotopes (the 2012–2025 matching problem above), the age (90 Myr of uncertainty, the 2021–2024 hafnium-tungsten revisions), or the orbit's Cassini-state exit in one self-consistent model — the 2023–2025 synestia-versus-high-energy-spin contest is live, and the field's consensus is the hypothesis's core (mantle-derived, impact-generated) plus a list of open problems that would fill a review; the popular "we know how the Moon formed" sentence is a 20-per-cent-confidence statement reported at 100.
4. **"Apollo dust proves we did not go."** The 1969–1972 landings' physical evidence is now multi-national and multi-generational: the 2009–2025 LRO imaging of the descent stages, rover tracks and retroreflector arrays (the 2012–2025 Kaguya, LRO and Chandrayaan-2 orbital photographs), the 1969–2025 laser-ranging to the Apollo reflectors at centimetre precision (the exercise's tidal-recession record comes from them), the samples' isotopic match to the 2020–2024 Chang'e returns where they overlap (the 2021–2025 oxygen and titanium comparisons), and the third-party tracking of the trans-lunar injections — the conspiracy literature's survival being a communication-studies problem, not a scientific one, and the reflectators' 55-year working life the longest continuous experiment in planetary science.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
The Moon is the calibration world: its anorthosite crust (GRAIL's 34-km shattered shell, 50–60 km far-side to near-zero nearside) records a global magma ocean whose flotation mass balance, KREEP last-dregs and cumulate mantle are confirmed in rock at seven sites; its 3.9–2.0-Ga maria and Procellarum heat anomaly carry the nearside-farside dichotomy that Chang'e-6's far-side samples are now testing; its mascons, cold-lithosphere gravity signal, 330–450-km fluid core and 3.5-Ga dynamo collapse make it the reference case for how small bodies cool and switch off; and its polar traps plus solar-wind hydroxyl cycle make it the only body besides Earth with a quantified, if modest, water budget — the 1–5-per-cent ice that prices the 2025–2035 landing-site economics. Its origin — a Mars-mass impact whose isotopic matching problem (oxygen through tungsten identical to Earth) drove the 2012–2025 synestia and equilibration models, whose date is uncertain by 90 Myr, and whose 3.8-cm-per-year recession the Apollo reflectors have timed for 55 years — remains the field's flagship unsolved problem, solved enough to anchor the giant-impact era of planet formation, unsolved enough to need the next sample return. Every method in this course was built on the Moon first: the crater clock, the magma-ocean paradigm, the fossil-dynamo logic, the sample-curated isotope chronology. That is what a calibration world is for — and why the Moon, 50 years after Apollo's last landing and 5 years after Chang'e-6's first far-side grams, is still where planetary science measures its instruments, its arguments and its ambitions.
:::
