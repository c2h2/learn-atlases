Darwin knew the objection better than anyone. If evolution works by the slow accumulation of small variations, why does the fossil record show the major animal groups appearing together, fully formed, in the earliest Cambrian rocks, with no ancestors below? In the last chapter of the *Origin* he conceded that the case "has not been entirely solved", and for a century the answer was that the ancestors must have existed but were too small and soft to be preserved — a convenient hypothesis, since it could not be tested.

They have now been found, and they are not the ancestors. The Ediacaran biota, discovered in 1946 at Ediacara in South Australia and dated from the 1980s onward to between $575$ and $539\ \mathrm{Ma}$, are large, complex, and almost entirely unlike anything that came after: quilted, fractal, modular organisms with no mouth, no gut, no anus and no skeleton, most of which left no descendants at all. Between them and the Cambrian fauna lie the small shelly fossils, the first burrows and the first reefs. What happened in the twenty-five million years after $539\ \mathrm{Ma}$ is that animals invented skeletons, eyes, jaws, burrows, shells, swimming, crawling, predation and filter feeding — and did it in a geological instant, against a background of a billion years of eukaryotic stasis. This chapter is about that event, about the four hundred million years of the Palaeozoic that followed, and about the two times in that interval when life on land and in the sea changed the planet's chemistry as decisively as oxygen had.

## The explosion, and what it was

::: definition The Cambrian radiation {#def-cambrian-radiation}
The **Cambrian explosion** or **Cambrian radiation** is the appearance, between $539$ and $514\ \mathrm{Ma}$, of essentially all living animal phyla and of several that are not, in the marine fossil record. Its evidence is threefold: the **small shelly fossils** ($525$–$515\ \mathrm{Ma}$), which are mineralised plates, spines and tubes of uncertain affinity; the **trace fossil revolution**, when burrowing reaches deep into the sediment for the first time and destroys the microbial matgrounds; and the **Lagerstätten** — Chengjiang and Maotianshan in China at $518\ \mathrm{Ma}$, the Burgess Shale at $508\ \mathrm{Ma}$, Sirius Passet in Greenland at $518\ \mathrm{Ma}$ — which preserve the soft-bodied fauna in detail. The **Cambrian substrate revolution** is the accompanying transformation of the sea floor from a firm, sticky, laminated matground to a mixed, oxygenated, burrowed sediment, and it is arguably the more important event, because it changed the environment of every animal that came after.
:::

::: example How fast was it, really? {#ex-explosion-rate}
Thirty animal phyla first appear in the fossil record between $539$ and $514\ \mathrm{Ma}$. Eukaryotes had existed for about $1800\ \mathrm{Myr}$ before that, and animals for at least $85\ \mathrm{Myr}$ (the oldest sponge biomarkers and the Doushantuo and Ediacaran candidates). Find the rate of first appearances in the Cambrian in phyla per million years, and compare it with the rate over the whole Precambrian eukaryote record.
::: solution
$$
\frac{30\ \text{phyla}}{25\ \mathrm{Myr}} = 1.2\ \text{phyla per Myr}, \qquad \frac{30\ \text{phyla}}{1800\ \mathrm{Myr}} = 0.017\ \text{phyla per Myr}.
$$
The Cambrian rate is seventy times the Precambrian average. Two cautions keep the number honest. First, "first appearance" is not "origin": every Cambrian first appearance is a minimum age, and molecular clocks put many phyla in the Ediacaran or Tonian, which would lower the Cambrian rate by a factor of two or three and raise the Precambrian one. Second, phyla are a human classification, and the number of body plans is not a clock; a fairer measure is the disparity of form, which does rise steeply in the early Cambrian and then, remarkably, does not rise much again.

The real puzzle is not that the rate is high but that the **pattern** is wrong for gradualism: the groups appear in a sequence that is roughly ecological — burrowers, then grazers, then predators with eyes, then the first reefs — as if the ecosystem were being assembled in an order, with each innovation opening a niche for the next.
:::
:::

## Why then? Four candidate causes

**Oxygen.** Collagen synthesis, and therefore a mesoglea, a cuticle and a coelom, requires oxygen-dependent hydroxylation, and an animal of centimetre size that is not flat needs either a circulatory system or a lot of oxygen. The Neoproterozoic rise of oxygen to a few per cent of present levels, dated from molybdenum and uranium isotopes between $0.8$ and $0.55\ \mathrm{Ga}$, precedes the explosion by a few tens of millions of years, and the second, smaller rise at $0.55$–$0.52\ \mathrm{Ga}$ precedes the Cambrian fauna. The correlation is good and the mechanism plausible, but the relationship is not tight: sponges tolerate a few per cent of present oxygen, and the timing gap between the oxygen rise and the explosion is of the same order as the dating uncertainty.

**Sea chemistry.** The Neoproterozoic and Cambrian oceans were rich in calcium and magnesium relative to carbonate saturation, and the first skeletons are of high-magnesian calcite and aragonite, minerals that precipitate more easily from such water. The argument has been weakened by the recognition that the "aragonite sea" and "calcite sea" oscillations are not as clean as claimed, but the point survives in a weaker form: the Cambrian oceans were closer to saturation with respect to calcium carbonate than the modern ocean, so building a shell was cheaper.

**Predation.** The first shells are, in large part, armour. The evidence is direct: small shelly fossils repaired after attack, borings in shells, the first drilling predators, and the appearance of eyes at about $515\ \mathrm{Ma}$ in trilobites and anomalocaridids, which turns predation from chemical and random into visual and targeted. Once an animal can see and hunt, an arms race follows, and arms races are fast.

**Genetics.** The developmental toolkit — the Hox cluster and the rest — is present in the simplest animals and is deeply homologous across the whole animal kingdom, so the genetic capacity for a body plan existed long before the body plans. What the toolkit needs is ecological selection, and this is the modern synthesis of the argument: the genes made the explosion possible, the ecology made it happen, and the timing is set by the oxygen and the chemistry that made large, active, mineralised animals worth building.

## The Palaeozoic, in order

::: definition Sepkoski's three evolutionary faunas {#def-faunas}
Jack Sepkoski's 1981 analysis of 10,500 marine genera at family level divided the Phanerozoic fauna into three assemblages with different diversification histories. The **Cambrian evolutionary fauna** (trilobites, nautiloids, inarticulate brachiopods, archaeocyathids) rises in the Early Cambrian, peaks near the end of it and survives at low diversity. The **Palaeozoic evolutionary fauna** (articulate brachiopods, bryozoans, crinoids, corals, graptolites, cephalopods) rises through the Ordovician, dominates for two hundred and fifty million years, and is reduced by the end-Permian to a third of its peak. The **modern evolutionary fauna** (bivalves, gastropods, decapod and neognath crustaceans, teleost-grade fishes, echinoids) is negligible before the Permian, doubles its share of diversity in the Triassic and reaches parity with the Palaeozoic fauna by the Jurassic. The three faunas differ ecologically: the modern fauna is characterised by infaunal habit, higher mobility, greater predatory and grazing intensity and, above all, by the escalation that the Mesozoic marine revolution documents.
:::

::: definition The Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event {#def-gobe}
The Cambrian explosion produced the body plans; the **GOBE**, between about $485$ and $450\ \mathrm{Ma}$, produced the diversity within them. Marine family diversity roughly triples, from about $100$ to about $300$ families of genera, and the change is ecological: the Cambrian fauna was dominated by mobile epifaunal grazers and predators on a soft substrate, and the Ordovician fauna by sessile filter feeders — brachiopods, bryozoans, crinoids, corals, graptolites, and the first complex reefs — living on a hard, burrowed, cemented substrate in a three-dimensional, tiered seafloor. The driver is usually taken to be the combination of the substrate revolution having run its course, the spread of shallow epicontinental seas as Gondwana and Laurentia converged, and the cooling climate that oxygenated the oceans.
:::

The **Ordovician** ends with the first of the Big Five: the Hirnantian extinction at $445\ \mathrm{Ma}$, associated with a brief, severe Gondwanan glaciation and a fall in sea level that removed the tropical shelves, and with isotopic evidence for a drop in $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ and in nickel, which has been read as large igneous province volcanism. It took about $25\%$ of marine genera.

The **Silurian** and **Devonian** are the centuries of the colonisation of the land. Cryptospores — the walls of the first land plants — appear at about $470\ \mathrm{Ma}$; *Cooksonia*, a centimetre-high axis with a spore capsule and no leaves, at $425\ \mathrm{Ma}$; the Rhynie chert fauna and flora at $410\ \mathrm{Ma}$, with mites, springtails and the first known parasitic fungi; the first forests (*Wattieza*, then the progymnosperm *Archaeopteris*) at about $385\ \mathrm{Ma}$; and the first deep roots and true soils at about $370\ \mathrm{Ma}$. The consequences were global. Roots and mycorrhizas broke up rock and drew down $\mathrm{CO_2}$ by enhanced weathering; the nutrient load delivered to the sea eutrophied it and, on the "nutrient collapse" hypothesis of Algeo and Scheckler, caused the anoxia and extinction of the Late Devonian; and the first soils changed the colour, the drainage and the chemistry of the continents.

The **Devonian** is also the age of fishes: placoderms such as *Dunkleosteus*, the first sharks, and the lobe-finned sarcopterygians whose descendants walked out. *Tiktaalik* at $375\ \mathrm{Ma}$ has a neck, a wrist and a ribcage; *Acanthostega* at $365\ \mathrm{Ma}$ has eight digits and limbs still built for swimming. The transition was not a bid for the land but a slow adaptation to shallow, vegetated, oxygen-poor water.

::: example The Carboniferous oxygen maximum {#ex-carboniferous-o2}
The burning of fossil carbon is the source of the atmospheric oxygen we breathe, because the carbon buried as coal, oil and methane was not respired back. During the Carboniferous and Permian, the fraction of primary production buried rose from its modern $0.1\%$ to perhaps $1\%$, and atmospheric oxygen reached $30$–$35\%$. Take the modern rate of organic carbon burial as $1\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ C\ yr^{-1}}$, the Carboniferous rate as ten times that, and the oxygen reservoir as $3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$. If the excess burial persisted for $20\ \mathrm{Myr}$, by how much would the oxygen reservoir rise, in present atmospheric levels, and why is the real answer much smaller?
::: solution
The excess flux is $9\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ yr^{-1}}$, so over $2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{yr}$ the addition is
$$
9\times10^{13}\times2\times10^{7} = 1.8\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{mol},
$$
which is $49$ times the present oxygen reservoir. The real answer is that oxygen rose by only about $15$ percentage points, because the oxygen produced is consumed by the oxidation of the same reduced carbon when it is later exposed, by the oxidation of the methane and carbon monoxide released from the swamps, and above all by **fire**: a high-oxygen atmosphere burns more completely, and the charcoal record of the Late Carboniferous shows a step in fire frequency exactly where oxygen is highest. The carbon–oxygen cycle is self-limiting through fire, which is the argument of David Bellocq and colleagues and of Timothy Scott and colleagues, and it is why the Carboniferous coal measures are also the first extensive charcoal in the rock record.

The biological consequences are real even so. Insects breathe by diffusion through tracheae, and the diffusive capacity of a trachea limits body size in direct proportion to the oxygen partial pressure; *Meganeuroid* dragonflies with $70\ \mathrm{cm}$ wingspans, centipede-like *Arthropleura* at $2.5\ \mathrm{m}$, and the first giant dragonfly nymphs are consistent with $30$–$35\%$ oxygen and inconsistent with $21\%$. The relationship is measurable in living insects, which grow larger in hyperoxic chambers and smaller in hypoxic ones, and it is the clearest case in the fossil record of atmospheric chemistry setting the ceiling on animal design.
:::
:::

The **Carboniferous** and **Permian** are also the time of Pangaea's assembly: the collision of Gondwana with Laurentia and Baltica builds the Variscan and Appalachian mountains, closes the ocean that had been the Iapetus, and creates a supercontinent with an arid interior, monsoonal margins, and the largest desert on Earth. The coal swamps of the equatorial belt — the cyclothems of repeated marine flooding and peat accumulation, each cycle a few metres thick and representing tens of thousands of years — are the product of that configuration: a low-lying, subsiding, equatorial belt at sea level, with no land plants yet able to build deep peat on dry ground. When the climate dried and the ice retreated, the swamps went, and with them the largest perturbation of the carbon cycle between the end-Permian and the present.

::: example How much shelf does a glaciation remove? {#ex-shelf}
The modern continental shelf from the coast to $200\ \mathrm{m}$ depth has an area of $1.9\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{km^2}$, of which about $1.0\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{km^2}$ lies shallower than $100\ \mathrm{m}$. The Hirnantian glaciation lowered sea level by an estimated $80$–$100\ \mathrm{m}$. Find the fraction of shelf area lost, and explain why the extinction was selective for endemic epicontinental taxa rather than for the deep-water fauna.
::: solution
A $100\ \mathrm{m}$ fall removes $1.0\times10^{7}/1.9\times10^{7} = 53\%$ of the shelf. Because the shelves of an icehouse world are narrower than today's in the tropics, the loss of *tropical* shelf — the habitat of the great Ordovician brachiopod, bryozoan and trilobite faunas — was larger still, and the surviving shelf was cooler and less extensive.

The selectivity follows from species–area: endemic epicontinental taxa with small ranges and low dispersal have their area cut in half and go extinct, while eurytopic, deeper-water and brooding taxa with larger ranges survive on the slope and in refugia. The prediction is testable and has been tested: the Hirnantian survivor fauna is dominated by the deeper-water and more eurytopic genera, and the extinction is concentrated in the tropical palaeolatitudes of Gondwana's neighbouring shelves. It is the simplest of the extinction mechanisms and, for once, the arithmetic of habitat loss is enough to account for the pattern.
:::
:::

::: example The carbon in one coal seam {#ex-coal-seam}
A Pennsylvanian coal seam is $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ thick, covers $10{,}000\ \mathrm{km^2}$, has a bulk density of $1300\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and is $60\%$ carbon. It formed from peat that compacted by a factor of ten. Find the mass of carbon buried, the oxygen it represents, and the fraction of the present atmospheric oxygen reservoir ($3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$) that it added.
::: solution
$$
M_C = 1.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^2}\times1300\times0.60 = 1.2\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{kg} = 9.8\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{mol\ C},
$$
so the seam represents $9.8\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{mol}$ of oxygen that was produced and not consumed — $2.6\%$ of the present atmospheric reservoir, from one seam. The Euramerican coal belt has hundreds of seams over $10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^2}$, so the budget closes: repeated burial at this level for $50\ \mathrm{Myr}$ is exactly the perturbation needed to push oxygen from $21\%$ to $32\%$.

The peat thickness is the other useful number: $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ of coal is $15\ \mathrm{m}$ of peat, and peat accumulates at $0.1$–$1\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, so the seam represents $15{,}000$–$150{,}000$ years of accumulation — the same order as a glacial–interglacial cycle, which is why the cyclothems of the Pennsylvanian are read as glacio-eustatic: each cycle is a period of peat growth during a highstand or a lowstand, drowned by the next marine flooding.
:::

::: warning "Complexity increases through the Palaeozoic"
Three things are wrong with the phrase. There is no trend in the maximum or the mean complexity of organisms, only in the range of them, and the range grows because the empty corners of the possible are filled. There is no direction: the most abundant and diverse animals of every Palaeozoic period are, by any reasonable measure, the simplest — the brachiopods, the crinoids, the foraminifera, the cyanobacteria. And the pattern is not smooth: the Palaeozoic ends with $96\%$ of marine species gone, and the fauna that replaces the Cambrian and Palaeozoic faunas — the "modern fauna" of burrowing bivalves, gastropods, decapod crustaceans and teleost-grade fishes — is different in its ecology rather than more advanced. The Palaeozoic is a history of environments being invented, not of progress.
:::

It is the burrowing event: animals that dig destroy the laminated microbial mat that had carpeted the Precambrian sea floor, and in doing so oxygenate and rework the sediment, change its chemistry, and make infaunal life possible. It is dated by trace fossils rather than by bodies, and it is one of the clearest cases in the record of behaviour transforming an environment.

::: history
The Cambrian fauna was first described from Bohemia by Joachim Barrande in the 1840s, whose eleven volumes of "Système silurien inférieur" established that the "Primordial" fauna was complex and older than the Silurian, and helped create the argument with Roderick Murchison that ended in the creation of the Cambrian and Ordovician systems. Charles Doolittle Walcott's monographs on the Burgess Shale (1911–1931) are the foundation of the subject and, in their interpretations, its most durable embarrassment. The Ediacaran biota were found by Mary Shein in 1946 in the Flinders Ranges and described by Martin Glaessner and James Playford in the 1960s, and their age — Precambrian — was settled only in the 1980s by the rubidium–strontium dating of the overlying volcanic beds, which made them the oldest complex life and, when the Cambrian was redated in 2011 to $538.8\ \mathrm{Ma}$, put them within a few million years of the explosion. Harry Whittington and his students Harry Toogood and Derek Briggs re-worked the Burgess fauna between 1971 and 1985 and founded the discipline of evolutionary developmental biology's fossil wing; the discovery of the Hox cluster and its deep homology, announced in 1983 by Walter Gehring's group, made the genetic explanation possible. James Valentine's and Douglas Erwin's account of the ecological assembly of the Cambrian fauna, and Graham Budd's and Sören Jensen's arguments about what is and is not an arthropod, are the framework of the current debate.
:::

::: summary Key takeaways
- The Cambrian radiation is real, fast (about 25 Myr for the phyla) and not an ancestor problem: the Ediacaran biota are a separate, mostly extinct experiment.
- The Cambrian substrate revolution — burrowing replacing the matground — is as important as the animals themselves, and is dated by trace fossils.
- The causes are four and not exclusive: oxygen, sea chemistry, predation and the pre-existing genetic toolkit, with the ecology setting the timing.
- The GOBE tripled family diversity in the Ordovician by filling the sessile, filter-feeding, tiered ecospace that the Cambrian had left empty.
- The colonisation of the land between 470 and 370 Ma changed the planet: soils, roots, weathering, carbon dioxide drawdown, nutrient loading of the shelves.
- Carboniferous oxygen of 30–35% set the size of the first giant insects and was checked by fire.
- The assembly of Pangaea created the arid supercontinent, the monsoons, the coal swamps and, at the end of the Permian, the conditions for the largest extinction of all.
:::

::: exercise The GOBE {level=1 check="5.7"}
Marine family diversity rises from about $100$ to about $300$ families between $485$ and $450\ \mathrm{Ma}$. Find the mean net rate of family gain (families per Myr), and explain why the gross origination rate must be higher.
::: solution
$(300-100)/35 = 5.7$ families per Myr. Because families were also going extinct throughout, the gross origination rate is the net gain plus the extinction rate; with a background family extinction rate of the same order, the true origination rate is roughly double the net figure.

::: exercise Oxygen and insects {level=1 check="2.2"}
Tracheal diffusive capacity scales with the cross-sectional area of the tracheae and with the oxygen partial pressure. If a dragonfly's maximum mass scales as the $3/2$ power of the oxygen partial pressure (a simple allometric model of the diffusion limit), find the ratio of the maximum mass at $35\%$ oxygen to that at $21\%$.
::: solution
$(35/21)^{1.5} = 1.667^{1.5} = 2.15$, about $2.2$. A doubling of the largest possible body mass for a $67\%$ increase in oxygen is roughly what the Carboniferous giantism requires, and it is why the model is usually written with an exponent near $1.5$ — but note that the argument applies to insects that breathe by tracheal diffusion as larvae and adults, and not to those whose larvae are aquatic with gills.
:::

::: exercise Coal and oxygen {level=2 check="2.2"}
The Carboniferous coal measures of Euramerica are estimated to contain $10^{13}\ \mathrm{t}$ of carbon. If all of it had been respired rather than buried, how much oxygen would that have removed from the atmosphere, in moles, and how does it compare with the present reservoir of $3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$?
::: solution
$10^{13}\ \mathrm{t} = 10^{16}\ \mathrm{kg} = 8.3\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{mol\ C}$, and one mole of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per mole of carbon gives $8.3\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{mol}$, which is $2.2\%$ of the present oxygen reservoir. A single basin's coal is therefore a small part of the budget; it takes the global, repeated, $60\ \mathrm{Myr}$-long burial of the Carboniferous to move the oxygen level by tens of per cent, which is the scale of the problem.
:::
:::

::: exercise Weathering and cooling {level=2 check="10"}
Root-enhanced silicate weathering increases the weathering flux by a factor of $10$ on vegetated rock. If the weathering flux is the sink that balances a $\mathrm{CO_2}$ source of $10^{12}\ \mathrm{mol\ C\ yr^{-1}}$, and the weathering rate depends on $\mathrm{CO_2}$ as $W \propto P_{\mathrm{CO_2}}^{0.3}$, find the fractional fall in $P_{\mathrm{CO_2}}$ needed to restore the balance after the flux is doubled by the plants.
::: solution
Doubling the weathering coefficient at fixed climate doubles the sink, so to restore balance $P_{\mathrm{CO_2}}$ must fall by the factor $2^{1/0.3} = 10$. That is a fall from, say, $30\times$ present to $3\times$ present, and it is the standard form of the argument that the greening of the Devonian caused the Late Palaeozoic ice age. The uncertainty is in the exponent $0.3$ and in how much of the rock was newly exposed; a factor of ten in either is the whole argument.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reading a Lagerstätte {level=2 check="30"}
A Burgess-Shale-type fauna yields $12{,}000$ specimens assigned to $120$ species, of which $30$ are known from a single specimen and $15$ from exactly two. Estimate the number of species that the sample has failed to record, using the Chao1 estimator.
::: solution
With $f_1 = 30$ and $f_2 = 15$, the estimate is $S = 120 + 30^2/(2\times15) = 150$, so the number missing is $30$. A quarter of the fauna is unrecorded even in the most famous conservative Lagerstätte in the world, which is worth remembering when a Cambrian diversity curve is compared with a modern one.
:::

::: exercise Deriving the oxygen ceiling {level=3}
Oxygen flux through a trachea of length $L$ and cross-section $A$ is $J = D A \Delta C/L$, where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient and $\Delta C$ the concentration difference. If an insect's oxygen demand scales as $M^{0.75}$, its tracheal area as $M^{0.5}$ and its tracheal length as $M^{0.33}$, derive the scaling of maximum mass with the ambient oxygen concentration $C_a$, and evaluate the exponent.
::: solution
Supply scales as $J \propto A/L \propto M^{0.5}/M^{0.33} = M^{0.17}$, and demand as $M^{0.75}$. Setting supply equal to demand at the maximum size, and writing the supply with the ambient concentration explicitly, $J \propto C_a M^{0.17}$, so
$$
C_a M^{0.17} \propto M^{0.75} \Longrightarrow M \propto C_a^{1/(0.75-0.17)} = C_a^{1.72}.
$$
The maximum mass scales as the $1.7$ power of the oxygen concentration, which is the exponent used in the previous exercise. The empirical literature on living insects gives exponents between $1$ and $2.5$ depending on the group and the life stage, and the model's value is not its precision but the fact that it makes a prediction that can be tested in a chamber.
:::

::: exercise The end-Ordovician {level=3}
The Hirnantian extinction at $445\ \mathrm{Ma}$ removed about $85\%$ of marine species, most of them brachiopods, bryozoans and trilobids of the tropical shallow shelves. Two candidate causes are a $50$–$100\ \mathrm{m}$ eustatic fall with the loss of shelf area, and anoxia on the surviving shelves during the subsequent transgression. Set out the evidence each hypothesis needs, the isotopic or sedimentological test, and why both may be true.
::: solution
**Sea-level fall** predicts that extinction severity should scale with the area of shelf that was drained, and that the survivors should be deep-water and slope faunas. Test: palaeobathymetric reconstruction of the surviving and extinct faunas, and the correlation of extinction with the palaeogeographic shelf area of each continent.
**Anoxia during transgression** predicts that the extinction should be concentrated in the *transgressive* systems tract rather than in the lowstand, and should be recorded by black shales, by positive $\delta^{238}\mathrm{U}$ and $\delta^{98}\mathrm{Mo}$ and by elevated Mo, U and Re concentrations at the level of maximum extinction. Test: high-resolution chemostratigraphy across the Hirnantian beds.

Both are supported, and the mechanism that reconciles them is the ocean's response to glaciation: cooling oxygenates the deep water, which then sinks and spreads, and the transgression after the glaciation floods the shelves with water that has become anoxic and sulphidic while it sat in the deep basin. The first kill is habitat loss, the second is poisoning, and the order is recorded in the stratigraphy — which is why the two-kills model of the end-Ordovician, like that of the end-Permian, is a stratigraphic argument before it is anything else.
:::

::: exercise A supercontinent's climate {level=3}
Pangaea at $300\ \mathrm{Ma}$ had $60\%$ of its land area more than $500\ \mathrm{km}$ from the sea, against $20\%$ today. Explain the expected effects on (a) the amplitude of the continental seasonal temperature cycle, (b) the distribution of reefs, (c) the global weathering rate and $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and (d) the preservation of the fossil record, and say which of the four is easiest to test.
::: solution
(a) **Continentality** increases: a larger seasonal cycle, with periglacial and arid facades in the interior, and monsoonal margins. Test: the sedimentary record of evaporites, aeolian sand and loess, and the palaeobotanical leaf physiognomy.
(b) **Reefs** are confined to the narrow cool temperate-to-tropical margins where the shelf is flooded, and the supercontinent's total reef area is small — which is one reason the Late Palaeozoic has few great reef complexes despite warm equatorial climates.
(c) **Weathering** is reduced in the arid interior but concentrated on the mountain belts of the collision zones; the net effect of an orogeny at $30^\circ$ latitude with a monsoon is a large weathering flux and a $\mathrm{CO_2}$ drawdown, and this is the standard explanation of the ice age.
(d) **Preservation** is biased: a supercontinent has fewer epicontinental seas and more erosion surface, so the rock record of the interior is thin and the diversity curve is pulled down.

The easiest to test is (d), because it is a counting exercise on the rock record itself, and it is the one that has to be settled before any of the others can be argued from the diversity curve.
:::
