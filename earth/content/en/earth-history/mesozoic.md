The Mesozoic is the Earth of the imagination and the Earth we are heading towards: a greenhouse world with no ice at either pole, carbon dioxide at four to ten times the pre-industrial level, crocodilians and palm trees above the Arctic Circle, sea level $100$–$170\ \mathrm{m}$ higher than today, and a tropical ocean that some proxies put at $35$–$40\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — hotter than any sea on the modern planet. It lasted $186$ million years, from the end-Triassic extinction to the asteroid, and it is the last time the Earth was in this state. Everything we say about the climate of the coming centuries is anchored, one way or another, to how well we understand this one.

It is also the age in which the world we live in was assembled. Pangaea broke apart, and the positions of the continents, the widths of the oceans, the mountain belts, the oil, the salt and the monsoons are all Mesozoic inheritances. The flowering plants, the bees, the mammals, the modern faunas of the sea and the coral reefs are Mesozoic inventions. This chapter is about the break-up, the greenhouse, and the two great biological innovations — the angiosperms and the escalation of predation — that made the living world.

## Pangaea comes apart

::: example The salt of the South Atlantic {#ex-salt}
When South America and Africa separated, the young South Atlantic was a narrow, restricted basin into which the Atlantic could not fully circulate, and between about $129$ and $121\ \mathrm{Ma}$ it deposited a layer of halite up to $2\ \mathrm{km}$ thick over an area of $1.3\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^2}$. Take the mean salt thickness as $800\ \mathrm{m}$, the halite density as $2160\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and the salt content of seawater as $3.5\%$. Find the volume of seawater that must have evaporated, and the mean inflow through the gateway needed to supply it over $8\ \mathrm{Myr}$.
::: solution
The salt volume is $1.3\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^2}\times800\ \mathrm{m} = 1.04\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, with a mass of
$$
1.04\times10^{15}\times2160 = 2.2\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg}.
$$
At $3.5\%$ salt this required $6.4\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of seawater, a volume of $6.2\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, which is $0.005\%$ of the world ocean. The mean inflow needed over $8\ \mathrm{Myr}$ is
$$
\frac{6.2\times10^{16}}{2.5\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s}} = 250\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}},
$$
which is less than the flow of the Amazon and about $10^{-5}$ of the present Atlantic's overturning transport.

The exercise makes the real point: the salt is not limited by the supply of seawater, which is effectively unlimited, but by the **restriction** — the basin has to be cut off enough that evaporation exceeds inflow and that the salt is not washed away. The Aptian salt therefore dates the moment when the Walvis Ridge and the Romanche fracture zone still stood above the circulation, and its top dates the moment when the seaway deepened enough for the Atlantic to flood the basin. It also buries the hydrocarbon system beneath it: the pre-salt oil of Brazil lies under this same salt, in lacustrine source rock laid down in the freshwater lake that preceded the marine incursion.
:::

The break-up is dated by the magnetic anomalies on the ocean floor, which give the age of the crust and, once the poles and the plate motions are known, the geometry at any time. The sequence runs: the Central Atlantic at $200$–$170\ \mathrm{Ma}$, with the Central Atlantic magmatic province lavas at $201.6\ \mathrm{Ma}$; the separation of Africa from South America beginning at about $133\ \mathrm{Ma}$ in the south and completing at $100\ \mathrm{Ma}$; the opening of the equatorial Atlantic and the separation of Africa from Antarctica at $160$–$130\ \mathrm{Ma}$; India's flight north at $120\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ from $120\ \mathrm{Ma}$, the fastest large plate ever measured, ending with the Deccan at $66\ \mathrm{Ma}$; Australia's separation from Antarctica at $95$–$45\ \mathrm{Ma}$; and the opening of Drake Passage, whose timing is one of the most argued numbers in palaeoceanography.

::: example Four times the carbon dioxide {#ex-cretaceous-co2}
Cretaceous atmospheric $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is estimated from boron isotopes, alkenones, soil carbonates and stomatal indices at $1000$–$2000\ \mathrm{ppm}$, against a pre-industrial $280\ \mathrm{ppm}$. Find the radiative forcing and the equilibrium warming with a modern feedback parameter, and compare it with the Cretaceous deep-time temperature reconstructions, which put the global mean $8$–$12\ \mathrm{K}$ above pre-industrial and the tropical sea surface $5$–$10\ \mathrm{K}$ above modern.
::: solution
$$
\Delta F = 5.35\ln\frac{1400}{280} = 5.35\times1.609 = 8.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}},
$$
and with a no-feedback-plus-modern-feedback parameter of $0.8\ \mathrm{K\,(W\,m^{-2})^{-1}}$,
$$
\Delta T = 6.9\ \mathrm{K}.
$$
That accounts for the global mean, but not for the pattern. The Mesozoic and Cretaceous poles were $15$–$25\ \mathrm{K}$ warmer than today, with frost-free forests to $80^\circ$ latitude and no evidence of ice sheets, while the tropics were only $5$–$10\ \mathrm{K}$ warmer. The ratio of polar to tropical warming — the **equability** — is far larger than any modern climate model produces, which is the "mesozoic equable climate problem".

The solutions proposed are not exotic: polar amplification is stronger when there is no sea ice and when the atmosphere is moist, because water vapour is a greenhouse gas that condenses out in the cold; high clouds at the poles warm; the absence of the ice-sheet albedo is worth several degrees at high latitude; and the ocean heat transport into the polar basins, which were differently shaped and differently connected, was larger. When the boundary conditions are corrected and the cloud feedback is allowed to be strong, models with $1120\ \mathrm{ppm}$ reproduce much of the record. The residual — the claim of $40\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ tropical sea surfaces from the TEX86 palaeothermometer — is more likely a calibration problem than a climate problem, and the argument over it is a case study in how a proxy's biology can become a geological paradox.
:::
:::

## The anoxic oceans

::: definition Oceanic anoxic events {#def-oae}
**Oceanic anoxic events (OAEs)** are intervals of the mid-Cretaceous, of which OAE 1a ($120\ \mathrm{Ma}$), OAE 1b, OAE 2 ($94\ \mathrm{Ma}$) and OAE 3 are the best known, in which the deep and middle waters of the world ocean became oxygen-depleted and black, organic-rich mud accumulated on every margin and in every epicontinental sea. Each is marked by a positive carbon isotope excursion of $1$–$2.5\%$, by the enrichment of molybdenum, uranium and rhenium, by the biomarkers of green sulphur bacteria in the photic zone (though the last claim has been re-examined and partly withdrawn), and by a rise in the temperature. They are the source rocks of a large fraction of the world's oil.
:::

::: proposition Anoxic events need a warm ocean, a nutrient supply and a trap {#prop-oae}
The supply of oxygen to the deep sea is by ventilation from the surface, and the solubility of oxygen falls with temperature: a Cretaceous ocean is born with less oxygen and holds less of it. The demand is the rain of organic matter, which is set by the nutrient supply, and the mid-Cretaceous had a great deal of it — the weathering flux from the large igneous provinces, from the young mountain belts, and from the phosphorus released from an anoxic seafloor. The third requirement is a trap: a basin whose water column is stably stratified, so that the oxygen minimum zone can thicken without being ventilated. Remove any of the three and the event does not happen, which is why the mid-Cretaceous has several and the Palaeogene has none.
:::

::: example The size of the Cretaceous carbon perturbation {#ex-oae2-carbon}
OAE 2 is marked by a positive $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ excursion of $1.5\%$ in bulk marine carbonate, lasting about $800\ \mathrm{kyr}$. A positive excursion means that more organic carbon than usual was buried, leaving the ocean's dissolved inorganic carbon isotopically heavy. Take the ocean's inorganic carbon as $3.8\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{mol}$ and the fractionation as $25\%$, and find the extra organic carbon buried.
::: solution
From the mass balance of [[earth-history/oxygen-proterozoic]], an excursion of $\Delta\delta$ corresponds to a change $\Delta f = \Delta\delta/\Delta$ in the fraction of carbon buried as organic matter. Writing the extra burial as a fraction of the total burial flux acting for a time $\tau$, and taking the modern burial flux as $1\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ C\ yr^{-1}}$:
$$
\Delta f = \frac{1.5}{25} = 0.06,
$$
so the organic burial rate rose by $6$ percentage points of the total carbon flux. Over $8\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{yr}$ at a total burial flux of $5\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ yr^{-1}}$ the extra carbon is
$$
0.06\times5\times10^{13}\times8\times10^{5} = 2.4\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{mol} = 29{,}000\ \mathrm{Gt\ C}.
$$
That is a tenth of the ocean's dissolved inorganic carbon, buried in less than a million years, and it is the reason the Cretaceous is both the warmest and the most prolific hydrocarbon-generating interval of the Phanerozoic: the same event that drew down the carbon dioxide left the oil.
:::

## The living Mesozoic

::: definition The Mesozoic Marine Revolution {#def-mmr}
Vermeij's **Mesozoic Marine Revolution** is the escalation of predation and defence that begins in the Jurassic and Cretaceous: drilling and crushing predators (nudibranchs, crabs, teleost fishes, mosasaurs) select for thicker shells, tighter apertures, spines, burrowing and the loss of the flat, epifaunal, low-mobility mollusc guilds that dominated the Palaeozoic. The evidence is in the shells themselves — the increase in the thickness-to-height ratio, the appearance of drill holes in a rising fraction of mollusc shells, the replacement of the strophomenid and productid brachiopods by infaunal bivalves — and it is the clearest case in the fossil record of an arms race shaping whole faunas.
:::

The **angiosperms** are the other great Mesozoic innovation, and Darwin called their "intense and abrupt appearance" an "abominable mystery" for the same reason the Cambrian was one: they appear in the Early Cretaceous (about $130\ \mathrm{Ma}$ in the pollen and leaf record, with the oldest crown-group fossils a little earlier) and are ecologically dominant within $40\ \mathrm{Myr}$. The explanation is not that they evolved fast but that they were built for a disturbed, high-carbon-dioxide, fire-prone landscape: they are, relative to conifers, fast-growing, cheap-tissued, efficient-water-users with a high leaf vein density, and they co-evolved with their pollinators and dispersers — the bees appear with the Cretaceous mid-point, the beetles before them, the birds and the marsupials after. By the Maastrichtian the forests of the high latitudes were deciduous angiosperms, and the first grasses were present but unimportant; the grasslands and the grazing mammals are a Cenozoic invention.

::: example The skeleton of a very large animal {#ex-dinosaur-bone}
The strength of a weight-bearing bone scales with its cross-sectional area, so static similarity requires the diameter $d \propto M^{1/2}$. Empirical data on mammals and birds give $d \propto M^{0.38}$, because large animals stand more upright and shorten their moment arms. A $5\ \mathrm{t}$ theropod has a femur mid-shaft diameter of $10\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find the diameter required for a $30\ \mathrm{t}$ animal under each scaling, and say what the empirical exponent implies about posture.
::: solution
Static similarity: $d = 10\times(30/5)^{0.5} = 24.5\ \mathrm{cm}$. Empirical: $d = 10\times6^{0.38} = 19.9\ \mathrm{cm}$.

The $20\ \mathrm{cm}$ animal is $20\%$ narrower than the geometric one, and the difference is paid for by posture: a large animal moves its limbs towards the vertical, which reduces the bending moment on the bone for the same mass, and it also broadens the feet and reduces the relative speed. The largest theropods and the largest sauropods are at the limit that this mechanics allows — a $30\ \mathrm{t}$ theropod is close to the maximum at which the femur can be built within the space available, and the $70\ \mathrm{t}$-class sauropods reach their size only by being quadrupedal, by air-sacred vertebrae that cut the mass of the skeleton, and by a columnar limb that is closer to a pillar than to a lever. The square-cube law does not prevent giantism, but it writes the design brief.
:::

::: warning "The Mesozoic was a greenhouse, so it is the analogue for the future"
It is an analogue for some things and a bad one for others. The Mesozoic had no ice sheets, a different coastline, a different ocean circulation, no Antarctic Circumpolar Current for most of it, different vegetation, and carbon dioxide levels four to ten times the pre-industrial, so its *equilibrium* climate is not a forecast for a world at $550\ \mathrm{ppm}$. What transfers is the sensitivity: the Mesozoic says that at four times carbon dioxide the world is $8$–$12\ \mathrm{K}$ warmer, that the poles warm two to three times as much as the tropics, that sea level is tens of metres higher, and that the anoxic events of the mid-Cretaceous show what happens when a warm ocean is also loaded with nutrients. What does not transfer is the timescale: the Cretaceous changes took hundreds of thousands of years, and the present change is taking decades.
:::

::: quiz
The mid-Cretaceous "equable climate problem" is that
- [ ] the tropics were too cold for the models
- [x] climate models cannot reproduce the very small temperature difference between the equator and the poles implied by the proxies
- [ ] the proxies disagree about the carbon dioxide
- [ ] the ocean was anoxic, which confounds the temperature proxies
::: solution
The record shows frost-free forests at $80^\circ$ latitude and no ice, implying polar warming of $15$–$25\ \mathrm{K}$ against tropical warming of $5$–$10\ \mathrm{K}$ — a much flatter meridional gradient than any model gives for a plausible carbon dioxide level. The solutions are stronger polar amplification (more water vapour and high cloud at the poles, no sea ice, greater ocean heat transport into the polar basins), and the residual disagreement is one of the reasons the mesothermal proxies, especially TEX86, are being re-examined.
:::

::: history
Alfred Wegener's Pangaea of 1912 was a hypothesis about the fit of the continents and was rejected for fifty years; the seafloor spreading maps of the 1960s turned it into a chronology, and the plate reconstructions of the last thirty years, driven by satellite altimetry for the fracture zones and by ocean-drilling ages, have made the break-up a dated, testable history. Charles Darwin wrote to Joseph Dalton Hooker in 1879 about the "abominable mystery" of the angiosperms' sudden appearance; the mystery was resolved in the 1980s and 1990s by the recognition that the Cretaceous fossil record of pollen and leaves was richer than anyone had thought and that the radiation, while fast, was not instantaneous. Georg Wagner's and Peter Franks' stomatal proxies of the 1990s, and the boron isotope work of Andy Ridgwell, Ellen Schrag and others, turned the plants and the foraminifera into carbon-dioxide meters. The mid-Cretaceous black shales were described from the Atlantic drilling in the 1970s and synthesised as "oceanic anoxic events" by Kenneth Hsu and then by Dean, Arthur and others in the 1980s; the biomarker claim for green sulphur bacteria in the photic zone, made in 1992, was largely withdrawn in 2004 when the same molecules were shown to be made by cyanobacteria. Gregory Vermeij's Mesozoic Marine Revolution of 1977 is the classic statement of escalation from the shells themselves, and the feathered dinosaurs of Liaoning, beginning with *Sinosauropteryx* in 1996, turned the origin of birds from a comparative-anatomy argument into a direct record.
:::

## Where this leads

The break-up and its magmatism are in [[physical-geology/plate-tectonics]] and [[physical-geology/igneous-rocks]]; the carbon cycle of the events in [[biogeochemistry/carbon-deep-time]]; the ocean's chemistry and stratification in [[oceanography/ocean-chemistry]] and [[oceanography/overturning]]; the Cretaceous climate modelling in [[palaeoclimate/deep-time]]; the end of it all in [[earth-history/mass-extinctions]]; and the aftermath in [[earth-history/cenozoic]].

::: summary Key takeaways
- Pangaea broke apart in a dated sequence from 200 Ma, and the ocean gateways that opened and closed during it control the climate, the circulation and the biogeography of the rest of the story.
- The Aptian salt of the South Atlantic dates the restriction of the young ocean and buries the pre-salt petroleum system.
- Cretaceous carbon dioxide of 1000–2000 ppm gives 7–9 K of forcing, which explains the global mean but not the warmth of the poles: the equable-climate problem is about polar amplification.
- The mid-Cretaceous oceanic anoxic events are the product of a warm, nutrient-loaded, stratified ocean, and they are the source rocks of much of the world's oil.
- The Mesozoic Marine Revolution and the angiosperm radiation are the two biological events that made the modern fauna and the modern flora, both driven by escalation and by disturbance rather than by progress.
- Giantism is a mechanics problem: bone diameter scales as roughly M^0.4, and the largest dinosaurs reached the limit that posture and pneumaticity could work around.
- The Mesozoic is an analogue for equilibrium climate sensitivity and for polar amplification, and a poor one for the rate of change.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Spreading rates {level=1 check="23"}
The equatorial South Atlantic is $3000\ \mathrm{km}$ wide and started opening $130\ \mathrm{Ma}$ ago. Find the mean full spreading rate in $\mathrm{km\,Myr^{-1}}$, and explain why a rate of $140\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ cannot be right.
::: solution
The mean full rate needed to open $3000\ \mathrm{km}$ in $130\ \mathrm{Myr}$ is
$$
\frac{3000}{130} = 23\ \mathrm{km\,Myr^{-1}} = 23\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
The $140\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ of the question would give $18{,}200\ \mathrm{km}$, which is six times the observed width, and it is the rate of the fast-spreading East Pacific Rise rather than of a slow, magma-poor margin. The exercise is a warning about the difference between a half-rate and a full rate and between slow and fast spreading, which together are the commonest arithmetic errors in the subject.
:::
:::

::: exercise Forcing {level=1 check="7.4"}
Find the radiative forcing of $1120\ \mathrm{ppm}$ of carbon dioxide relative to $280\ \mathrm{ppm}$.
::: solution
$\Delta F = 5.35\ln(4) = 7.4\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. With a sensitivity of $3\ \mathrm{K}$ per doubling this is $6\ \mathrm{K}$ of warming, and with the Cretaceous feedbacks of $6\ \mathrm{K}$ per doubling, $12\ \mathrm{K}$.
:::
:::

::: exercise The anoxic event {level=1 check="0.06"}
An OAE carbon isotope excursion is $1.5\%$. What change in the fraction of carbon buried as organic matter does it imply, with a fractionation of $25\%$?
::: solution
$\Delta f = 1.5/25 = 0.06$, i.e. six percentage points of the carbon flux. Because the excursion is positive, the change is an increase in burial; a negative excursion of the same size is a decrease, or an input of light carbon.
:::
:::

::: exercise Salt {level=2 check="9.4"}
Using the method of [[#ex-salt]], find the volume of seawater (in $10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^3}$) needed to produce a salt layer $1200\ \mathrm{m}$ thick over $1.3\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^2}$.
::: solution
Salt volume $1.56\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, mass $3.4\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg}$, seawater $9.6\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{kg} = 9.4\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^3}$. The scaling is linear in the thickness, so the answer for $1200\ \mathrm{m}$ is $1.5$ times the $800\ \mathrm{m}$ case.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bone scaling {level=2 check="2.83"}
Two sauropods differ in mass by a factor of $8$. By what factor does the required femur diameter differ under static similarity ($d \propto M^{0.5}$), and by what factor under the empirical exponent $0.38$? (Enter the static-similarity factor.)
::: solution
Static: $8^{0.5} = 2.83$. Empirical: $8^{0.38} = 2.21$. The heavier animal must be nearly three times as thick in the femur for eight times the mass under pure geometric loading, and the $20\%$ saving from the more upright posture is a real, measurable difference in the bones of large mammals and dinosaurs.
:::
:::

::: exercise Angiosperm diversification {level=2 check="0.09"}
Angiosperm families rise from about $10$ at $130\ \mathrm{Ma}$ to about $400$ at $90\ \mathrm{Ma}$. Find the mean net diversification rate per Myr, and compare it with a typical background family origination rate.
::: solution
$r = \ln(400/10)/40 = \ln 40/40 = 3.69/40 = 0.092\ \mathrm{Myr^{-1}}$, about $9\%$ per million years net of extinction. For families this is high but not extraordinary — the GOBE and the post-extinction radiations reach similar values — which is the honest answer to the "abominable mystery": the angiosperm radiation is fast for a plant group and unremarkable beside an adaptive radiation after an extinction.
:::

::: exercise Deriving the equability {level=3}
The meridional temperature gradient in an aqua-planet model scales as the ratio of the equator–pole energy transport to the radiative damping. If the equator–pole insolation difference is unchanged from today but the poles are $20\ \mathrm{K}$ warmer and the tropics $7\ \mathrm{K}$ warmer, by what factor has the gradient changed, and what does that require of the ocean and atmosphere heat transport?
::: solution
Today's surface gradient is about $30\ \mathrm{K}$ from the equator to the pole; the Cretaceous gradient was $30 - 20 + 7 = 17\ \mathrm{K}$, a factor of $0.57$. The diffusive energy budget gives the transport as proportional to the gradient divided by the damping, so either the transport rose by a factor of about $1.8$ at the same damping, or the damping fell (a moister, more optically thick polar atmosphere damps less), or both.

The required transport is the interesting part: the modern ocean carries about $1.3\ \mathrm{PW}$ across $30^\circ\mathrm{N}$ and the atmosphere about $3\ \mathrm{PW}$. A Cretaceous ocean with an open North Atlantic, a Tethys seaway across the tropics and no Antarctic barrier can plausibly carry more poleward heat, and high-latitude deep water formation, which is today confined to the North Atlantic, may have occurred in the North Pacific as well. The models that reproduce the gradient do so by increasing the moisture and the cloud feedback at the poles rather than by inventing a new ocean, which is the current consensus.
:::

::: exercise Reading the pre-salt {level=3}
A seismic section shows, from the base: continental rift volcanics and lacustrine shales; a thick evaporite sequence with halite and potash; a shallow-marine carbonate platform; and open-marine pelagic chalk. Interpret the history, date each stage by the available methods, and explain what controls the quality of the reservoir.
::: solution
The sequence is the classic passive-margin break-up: **rifting** with continental extension, volcanism and closed freshwater lakes (the lacustrine shales are the source rock, and their oil is waxy and low-sulphur); **marine restriction** with evaporation exceeding inflow, precipitating halite and then potash, which requires an arid climate at low latitude and a shallow, leaky gateway; **flooding** as the gateway deepens, giving shallow-marine carbonates (the microbial and coated-grain carbonates that form the reservoir); and **subsidence** to open-marine chalk.

Dating: the rift volcanics by argon–argon or uranium–lead; the base of the salt by the first marine microfossils or by rhenium–osmium in the shales; the top of the salt by the first occurrence of marine planktonic fossils and by the magnetic anomalies once the crust is oceanic; the chalk by the nannofossil and foraminiferal zonation. The reservoir quality is controlled by the original carbonate facies (grain size and mineralogy), by the meteoric and burial diagenesis that created the vugs and the molds, and by the seal — the salt itself, which is the best cap rock known and the reason the pre-salt plays are so large and so hard to explore.
:::
:::

::: exercise The Mesozoic as an analogue {level=3}
A policy brief states: "Cretaceous carbon dioxide was $1400\ \mathrm{ppm}$ and sea level was $100\ \mathrm{m}$ higher, so at $550\ \mathrm{ppm}$ we should expect tens of metres of sea-level rise." Assess the argument, using the concepts of equilibrium sensitivity, of the timescale of ice-sheet adjustment, and of the difference between an ice-free and an iced world.
::: solution
The argument conflates three things. The Cretaceous sea level of $100$–$170\ \mathrm{m}$ above present is mostly **thermal expansion and ocean-basin geometry** in a world with no ice sheets at all; the part attributable to ice is zero, because there was none to melt. Today's $550\ \mathrm{ppm}$ commits the world to melting the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets over many millennia, which is $15$–$20\ \mathrm{m}$, and to a smaller contribution from East Antarctica over a longer time.

The equilibrium sensitivity transfers: the Cretaceous and Eocene both indicate that a doubling of carbon dioxide warms the planet by $3$–$6\ \mathrm{K}$, and that is the number the policy brief should use. The **rate** does not: the Cretaceous changes were forced by volcanism over hundreds of thousands of years, and the biogeochemical and ice-sheet systems had time to equilibrate. The correct statement is that at $550\ \mathrm{ppm}$ the equilibrium warming is $4$–$5\ \mathrm{K}$, the committed sea-level rise is metres over millennia, and the near-century rise is set by the ice dynamics and by the thermal inertia, not by the Cretaceous.
:::
