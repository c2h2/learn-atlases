Fifty-six million years ago, in the space of about twenty thousand years, the deep ocean warmed by $4$–$5\ \mathrm{K}$, the global mean temperature rose by $5$–$8\ \mathrm{K}$, the ocean became more acidic, a third of the deep-water foraminifera went extinct, and the mammals of the northern continents grew smaller, migrated north, and replaced one another across whole continents in a single generation of the fossil record. The carbon isotope composition of marine carbonate fell by $3$ to $6$ per mil. This is the Palaeocene–Eocene thermal maximum, the PETM, and it is the best natural experiment the Cenozoic offers on what happens when a large quantity of carbon is put into the atmosphere-ocean system faster than the carbonate machine can absorb it.

The rest of the Cenozoic is the story of the planet cooling out of that experiment and out of the greenhouse that followed it: the freezing of Antarctica, the raising of Tibet and the drying of Central Asia, the arrival of ice in the Northern Hemisphere, and the million years of ice-age cycles whose rhythm is set by the geometry of the Earth's orbit and whose amplitude is set by carbon dioxide. It is also the interval in which the modern flora and fauna — the grasslands, the grazing mammals, the whales, the modern plankton and, at the end, us — took their present form.

## The early Cenozoic greenhouse

::: example The PETM carbon release {#ex-petm}
Across the PETM the $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ of marine carbonate fell by $3\%$ to $6\%$ over less than $50\ \mathrm{kyr}$. Take the ocean's dissolved inorganic carbon as $3.8\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{mol}$ at $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C} = 0\%$, and find the mass of carbon that must have been added if the source had $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ of (a) $-6\%$, thermogenic carbon from the intrusion of the North Atlantic igneous province into organic-rich sedimentary rocks; (b) $-20\%$, a mixed organic source; (c) $-60\%$, methane hydrate.
::: solution
The mixing relation gives $C_a = C_o\,\Delta\delta/(\delta_o - \delta_a)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta_a = -6\% &: \quad C_a = 1.9\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{mol} = 22{,}800\ \mathrm{Gt\ C},\\
\delta_a = -20\% &: \quad 5.7\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{mol} = 6{,}800\ \mathrm{Gt\ C},\\
\delta_a = -60\% &: \quad 1.9\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{mol} = 2{,}300\ \mathrm{Gt\ C}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The same isotope excursion requires a factor of ten in the mass of carbon depending only on the isotopic composition of the source, which is why the debate about the PETM's cause has run for thirty years and is not settled. A hydrate source needs a quarter of the modern fossil-fuel reserve; a thermogenic source needs nine times it.

What is certain is the **rate**. Even the smallest estimate, $2{,}300\ \mathrm{Gt\ C}$ over $20\ \mathrm{kyr}$, is $0.1\ \mathrm{Gt\ C\ yr^{-1}}$, an order of magnitude slower than the present $10\ \mathrm{Gt\ C\ yr^{-1}}$ — and the excursion's shape, a fast onset and a slow, $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$-long recovery by silicate weathering, is exactly what a fast pulse followed by carbonate dissolution and weathering predicts. The PETM is not a prediction of the present, but it is the closest the record comes, and the recovery time it measures — $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$ or more — is the time the Earth takes to clean up a carbon perturbation.
:::
:::

The early Eocene was warmer still. Between $53$ and $49\ \mathrm{Ma}$ the **early Eocene climatic optimum** put palm trees and crocodilians in Germany, sea-surface temperatures of $30\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at the equator and $20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at the Antarctic coast, and carbon dioxide at perhaps $1000$–$2000\ \mathrm{ppm}$. The **Azolla event** at about $49\ \mathrm{Ma}$ — the massive bloom of the freshwater fern *Azolla* in the newly isolated, fresh-surfaced Arctic Ocean, which buried enough organic nitrogen to have drawn down a large quantity of carbon dioxide — is a charming hypothesis with a contested budget, and is a good example of how a single beautiful observation can outrun its isotope constraints.

The cooling that followed has three candidate causes, and all three probably contributed. The **opening of the Southern Ocean gateways** — the Tasman Gateway between Australia and Antarctica from about $45\ \mathrm{Ma}$ and the Drake Passage between South America and Antarctica, whose timing is still argued between $41$ and $30\ \mathrm{Ma}$ — allowed the Antarctic Circumpolar Current to form, thermally isolating the continent and allowing the sea ice to persist. The **India–Asia collision** and the growth of Tibet and the Himalaya raised albedo, intensified the monsoon and, on the Raymo–Ruddiman hypothesis of 1992, accelerated silicate weathering, which draws carbon dioxide out of the air. And the **burial of organic carbon and of pyrite** continued to lower the greenhouse gas level. The weathering hypothesis has been badly dented by the osmium isotope record, which shows no global increase in weathering flux at $40$–$30\ \mathrm{Ma}$, and by the lithium isotope record; the cooling of the late Eocene and Oligocene is now attributed more to the gateways, to the Antarctic ice sheet itself, and to a continuing drawdown.

::: example The gain from the ice-age cycle {#ex-ice-core-gain}
Over the last $800{,}000$ years of the Antarctic ice-core record, the global mean temperature varied by about $9\ \mathrm{K}$ between glacial and interglacial states while carbon dioxide varied from $180$ to $290\ \mathrm{ppm}$. Find the radiative forcing from the carbon dioxide change alone, the warming it would produce with a no-feedback sensitivity of $0.8\ \mathrm{K\,(W\,m^{-2})^{-1}}$, and the amplification factor that the remaining warming requires.
::: solution
$$
\Delta F = 5.35\ln\frac{290}{180} = 2.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}, \qquad \Delta T_\text{CO}_2 = 0.8\times2.6 = 2.1\ \mathrm{K}.
$$
The observed swing is $9\ \mathrm{K}$, so the amplification factor is $9/2.1 = 4.3$.

That number is the whole subject of Quaternary climate dynamics in one ratio. Carbon dioxide is not the thermostat of the ice ages — the orbital forcing starts the cycle, and the carbon dioxide responds to it, lagging the Antarctic temperature by several hundred years — but it **amplifies** the orbital forcing by a factor of two to three, and the rest of the amplification is the ice-sheet and snow albedo, the vegetation, the dust and the clouds. The causal chain runs both ways in the same record, which is why the slogan "carbon dioxide follows temperature" is true of the lag and useless as an argument: the same feedback that makes carbon dioxide a follower in a slow orbital cycle makes it a driver when its concentration is changed directly, as it is now.
:::
:::

## The orbital theory, and the 100,000-year problem

::: definition The three orbital parameters {#def-orbital}
**Eccentricity** $e$ describes the shape of the Earth's orbit and varies between about $0.005$ and $0.058$ with dominant periods of $100$ and $405\ \mathrm{kyr}$; it changes the **annual mean** insolation almost not at all, but it scales the amplitude of the seasonal contrast. **Obliquity** $\varepsilon$, the tilt of the spin axis, varies between $22.1^\circ$ and $24.5^\circ$ with a period of $41\ \mathrm{kyr}$; a larger tilt gives more summer and less winter insolation at high latitude, and it is the parameter that matters for the ice sheets. **Precession** is the rotation of the axis and of the orbit relative to one another, with dominant periods of $19$ and $23\ \mathrm{kyr}$; it decides which hemisphere leans sunward at perihelion, and therefore which hemisphere has strong seasons. The forcing used by Milankovitch, and by most of the subject since, is the **summer insolation at $65^\circ\mathrm{N}$**, chosen because that is the latitude where the great ice sheets grew and where summer melting is decisive.
:::

::: example The size of the orbital forcing {#ex-insolation}
The daily mean insolation at the summer solstice at latitude $\phi$ is
$$
Q = \frac{S_0}{\pi}\left(\frac{d_\text{m}}{d}\right)^{2}\left[h_0\sin\phi\sin\delta + \cos\phi\cos\delta\sin h_0\right], \qquad \cos h_0 = -\tan\phi\tan\delta,
$$
with $S_0 = 1361\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Compute $Q$ at $65^\circ\mathrm{N}$ for an obliquity of $22.5^\circ$ and of $24.5^\circ$, and then, for an obliquity of $24^\circ$, for an eccentricity of $0.055$ with the perihelion at the June solstice and with the aphelion there.
::: solution
With $\delta = 22.5^\circ$, $h_0 = \arccos(-\tan 65^\circ\tan 22.5^\circ) = \arccos(-0.577) = 2.19\ \mathrm{rad}$, and
$$
Q = 478\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} \quad\text{against}\quad Q = 512\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} \text{ at } \delta = 24.5^\circ,
$$
a change of $7\%$ between the obliquity extremes. For the precession at $e = 0.055$ the distance factor $(d_\text{m}/d)^2$ at the June solstice ranges from $0.90$ (aphelion in June) to $1.12$ (perihelion in June), so
$$
Q = 451 \rightarrow 562\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}},
$$
a range of $25\%$. At the low-eccentricity end, $e = 0.005$, the same precessional cycle gives only $498$–$508\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, a $2\%$ range.

This is the key to the **100,000-year problem**. The strongest insolation signal at high latitude is the precessional one, but its amplitude is switched on and off by the $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$ eccentricity cycle, and the climate of the last $800{,}000$ years is dominated by $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$ cycles that no insolation curve contains. The resolution is not a new forcing but a nonlinear system: an ice sheet grows slowly until it is thick enough to be unstable, and then collapses rapidly at any provocation, so the periodicity is set by the ice sheet's own time constant and the eccentricity merely times the opportunities. The mid-Pleistocene transition, when the dominant period changed from $41$ to $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$ between $1.2$ and $0.7\ \mathrm{Ma}$, coincides with the fall of carbon dioxide below a threshold and with the growth of the Laurentian and Eurasian ice sheets past the size at which the $41\ \mathrm{kyr}$ cycle could no longer remove them.
:::

## The ice-age world

::: definition Glacials, interglacials and their numbering {#def-glacial}
A **glacial** (a glacial stage, a cold stage, an even-numbered marine isotope stage) is an interval of large ice sheets, low sea level and low carbon dioxide; an **interglacial** (odd-numbered) is the warm interval between them, with the ice sheets close to their modern size. The **marine isotope stages** are the couplets of the benthic oxygen isotope record, numbered from the present downwards: MIS 1 is the Holocene, MIS 5e the Last Interglacial at $129$–$116\ \mathrm{ka}$, MIS 2 the Last Glacial Maximum at $26\ \mathrm{ka}$, and MIS 11 the long interglacial of $424$–$374\ \mathrm{ka}$. An **interstadial** is a warm excursion *within* a glacial (the Bølling–Allerød, the Dansgaard–Oeschger events) and a **stadial** a cold one (the Younger Dryas, the Heinrich stages). The distinction matters because the abrupt events of the last glacial are interstadials and stadials rather than part of the main glacial–interglacial cycle, and confusing them is the commonest error in reading a Pleistocene stratigraphy.
:::

::: proposition Ice ages need ice-sheet instability and a carbon dioxide floor {#prop-iceage}
A planet with no ice can be made to grow ice by a small forcing only if the ice, once grown, changes the climate enough to keep itself: the albedo feedback, the elevation–climate feedback (a thick ice sheet lowers its surface into colder air, which is a positive feedback while it grows and a negative one once it is gone), and the isostatic and dynamic limits of the bed. The threshold behaviour of the ice sheets — stable small, unstable medium, unstable large — is what turns a sinusoidal forcing into a sawtooth response, and the fall of glacial carbon dioxide below about $200\ \mathrm{ppm}$ is what allowed the large, unstable, $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$ ice sheets of the late Pleistocene to exist at all.
:::

The evidence for the Quaternary ice ages is read from a dozen archives that agree. **Marine isotope stratigraphy** numbers the glacial–interglacial cycles (the marine isotope stages) and matches them to the orbital tuning; **coral terraces** and the fossil reef sequences give the sea level directly, and the last interglacial at $129$–$116\ \mathrm{ka}$ stands $6$–$9\ \mathrm{m}$ above present, with the implication that the Greenland and West Antarctic sheets were largely gone. **Loess–palaeosol sequences** on the Chinese plateau and the deep-sea dust records give the aridity and the wind strength. **Pollen** and plant macrofossils give the vegetation zones, shifted hundreds of kilometres and compressed into no-analogue assemblages. **Ice cores** give the atmosphere itself: the EPICA Dome C and Dome F records reach $800{,}000$ years and Beyond EPICA has pushed the record to $1.2$ million, with carbon dioxide between $180$ and $300\ \mathrm{ppm}$, methane between $350$ and $700\ \mathrm{ppb}$, and dust concentrations ten times the modern.

Within the glacial periods the Northern Hemisphere record is wilder than the Southern: the **Dansgaard–Oeschger events** are abrupt warmings of $10$–$15\ \mathrm{K}$ over Greenland in decades, followed by slow cooling over centuries to millennia, and the **Heinrich events** are the discharges of armadas of icebergs that shut down the Atlantic overturning circulation. The **Younger Dryas**, a return to cold conditions between $12.9$ and $11.7\ \mathrm{ka}$ in the middle of the last deglaciation, is the youngest and best-dated of these abrupt changes and the reason the possibility of one in the present warming is a live question.

::: example The sea-level budget of a warm interglacial {#ex-sea-level}
The Last Interglacial, $129$–$116\ \mathrm{ka}$, had sea level $6$–$9\ \mathrm{m}$ above present with carbon dioxide near $280\ \mathrm{ppm}$ and high-latitude summer insolation a few per cent above today's. The Greenland ice sheet's contribution is estimated at $2$–$3\ \mathrm{m}$, the West Antarctic at $2$–$4\ \mathrm{m}$, and the East Antarctic at $0$–$3\ \mathrm{m}$. Find the residual, and explain why the Last Interglacial is the most important number in sea-level science.
::: solution
The central estimates sum to $4$–$10\ \mathrm{m}$, which brackets the observed $6$–$9\ \mathrm{m}$: the budget closes without invoking any mechanism we do not already know. The significance is not the arithmetic but the **state**. The Last Interglacial was only a few tenths of a degree warmer than the present, and it had $6$–$9\ \mathrm{m}$ of sea-level rise, most of it from ice sheets that are still largely intact and that are, at present, losing mass. It is therefore an empirical demonstration that a few tenths of a degree of additional warming, sustained for thousands of years, commits the planet to metres of sea level — and that the marine-based sectors of West Antarctica and the marine margins of Greenland are the ones that fail.

The counter-argument, that the Last Interglacial was forced by orbital geometry that no longer applies, is true and irrelevant: the orbital forcing was a few per cent of the insolation, whereas the carbon dioxide forcing of the present is several $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and the equilibrium sensitivity converts either into a similar temperature. The correct use of the Last Interglacial is as a constraint on the **equilibrium** sea-level response to a warm world, which is exactly the quantity that ice-sheet models disagree about.
:::
:::

## Life in the cooling world

The mammal radiation after the end-Cretaceous is a story of empty ecospace: the body-mass distribution of mammals jumped from a ceiling of about $10\ \mathrm{kg}$ in the Maastrichtian to $50\ \mathrm{t}$ within $15\ \mathrm{Myr}$, and the modern orders appear in the earliest Eocene of North America and Asia in what looks like a single pulse — the "first appearances" that Cuvier and later Simpson took for a creation. The Eocene was the age of the archaic orders and of the nummulites, the giant foraminifera whose shells built the limestone of the Pyramids' cores; the Oligocene and Miocene saw the rise of the modern ungulate orders, driven in part by the spread of **C4 grasslands**, whose carbon isotope signature ($\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ near $-13\%$ against $-25\%$ for C3 plants) appears in the soils and the tooth enamel of horses and bovids first in Africa at $8$–$6\ \mathrm{Ma}$ and later in Asia and South America. The C4 pathway is an adaptation to low carbon dioxide, high light and seasonality, so its expansion is both a consequence and a measure of the late Cenozoic fall in carbon dioxide.

On land the cooling and drying shrank the rainforests to their modern refugia, created the savannas that made bipedalism an option, and produced the **Great American Biotic Interchange** when the Isthmus of Panama closed at about $3\ \mathrm{Ma}$, letting North American carnivores and ungulates into South America and the marsupials and xenarthrans the other way — with the asymmetry, the northward migrants outnumbering the southward ones several to one, still argued about. In the sea the diatoms and the modern plankton took over the silica and calcium cycles, the whales radiated with the cooling and the productivity, and the coral reefs retreated from their Eocene heights.

The last of these changes is the one with our names on it. The late Quaternary megafaunal extinctions removed most genera of large mammals from Australia at about $46\ \mathrm{ka}$, from the Americas at $14$–$12\ \mathrm{ka}$, and from the islands of the Mediterranean and the Pacific in the last five thousand years; in every case the arrival of humans precedes the loss, and in every case the climate was also changing, which is why the argument has run for forty years. The current consensus, from the Bayesian overlap of human and extinction dates and from the fact that the loss is far worse on islands where climate change was small, is that hunting was the dominant driver on most landmasses, with climate as a modifier, and that the extinction of the grazers and browsers fed back into the fire regime and the vegetation.

::: warning "The ice ages prove that carbon dioxide does not drive climate"
The argument is made from two true facts — that the orbital forcing starts the cycle and that carbon dioxide lags the Antarctic temperature by several hundred years — and reaches a false conclusion. The lag is a lag in the *feedback*, not in the *causation*: the Southern Ocean warms, its carbon dioxide outgasses, and the gas spreads the warming to the rest of the planet, which is why the Antarctic record shows the lag and the tropical records show carbon dioxide leading. And the amplification factor of [[#ex-ice-core-gain]] shows that without the carbon dioxide change the ice-age cycle would be a third of its size. What the ice ages actually prove is that the carbon dioxide feedback is powerful, that the system has thresholds, and that the amplitude of the response to a small forcing can be enormous — which is the opposite of the intended conclusion.
:::

::: history
Milutin Milankovitch, a Serbian engineer working in Budapest during the First World War, computed the insolation of the past few million years from the orbital solutions of his time and published the theory in 1941, in a treatise written to make his name in a scientific world that had no use for it; the theory was dismissed by most geologists until the deep-sea isotope record of Cesare Emiliani, and then of Nicholas Shackleton and Robert Opdyke in 1973, showed that the climate had the periods of the orbit. The Imbries' SPECMAP project of the 1980s tuned the isotope curves to the orbit and made the chronology of the last half-million years, and the argument about whether that was circular — tuning the data to the theory that the data were to test — was answered by the independent dating of corals and by the $405\ \mathrm{kyr}$ eccentricity cycle, which is too long to be anything but orbital. The Vostok ice core, reported by Barnola and colleagues in 1987 and by Raynaud and colleagues in the same year, gave the first continuous record of the glacial atmosphere, and the European Project for Ice Coring in Antarctica, reported in 2005, extended it to $800{,}000$ years and showed that carbon dioxide had not exceeded $300\ \mathrm{ppm}$ in all of it. The PETM was recognised in the early 1990s from isotope excursions in the Deep Sea Drilling Project cores, and has been worked on ever since as the Cenozoic's experiment in carbon release. Ma Raymo and Bill Ruddiman's 1992 paper on Tibet and the cooling launched a decade of argument that the osmium and lithium isotopes have largely settled against, and is a model of how a bold hypothesis advances the field even when it is wrong.

## Where this leads

The proxies are described in [[palaeoclimate/proxies]] and the orbital theory worked out in [[palaeoclimate/orbital-cycles]]; the ice cores in [[palaeoclimate/ice-cores]] and the marine records in [[palaeoclimate/ocean-sediments]]; the abrupt changes in [[palaeoclimate/abrupt-change]] and the last two thousand years in [[palaeoclimate/holocene]] and [[palaeoclimate/last-two-millennia]]; the ice-sheet mechanics in [[glaciology/ice-sheets]] and the sea-level record in [[oceanography/sea-level]]; and the uplift of Tibet in [[physical-geology/plate-tectonics]] and [[structural-geology/orogens]].

::: summary Key takeaways
- The PETM, at 56 Ma, is the Cenozoic's carbon-release experiment: 5–8 K of warming, a 3–6 per mil carbon isotope excursion, a deep-sea extinction, and a recovery that took 100 kyr.
- The early Eocene was the warmest interval since the end-Cretaceous, with 1000–2000 ppm of carbon dioxide and no polar ice.
- The cooling to the modern icehouse was driven by the opening of the Southern Ocean gateways, the growth of the Antarctic ice sheet and a long drawdown of carbon dioxide; the weathering-and-Tibet hypothesis is not supported by the osmium record.
- Antarctica froze at 34 Ma, the Northern Hemisphere at 2.7 Ma, and the ice-age cycles changed from 41 to 100 kyr between 1.2 and 0.7 Ma.
- The orbital forcing is dominated at high latitude by precession, whose amplitude is switched on and off by the 100 kyr eccentricity; the ice sheets answer the envelope, which is the 100,000-year problem.
- Carbon dioxide lags Antarctic temperature in the ice cores and amplifies the orbital forcing by a factor of two to three; the lag is a feedback, not a refutation.
- The Last Interglacial, only slightly warmer than today, had 6–9 m of sea level: an empirical constraint on the equilibrium response of the great ice sheets.
- The Cenozoic biota — grasslands, C4 grazers, modern plankton, whales, and the human-driven megafaunal extinctions — is the product of cooling and drying, not of the K–Pg emptying alone.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Insolation {level=1 check="9"}
Using the method of [[#ex-insolation]], find the percentage change in the June insolation at $65^\circ\mathrm{N}$ between an obliquity of $22.1^\circ$ and $24.5^\circ$.
::: solution
$Q$ rises from about $470$ to $512\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, a change of $9\%$. The obliquity range is smaller than the precessional range of $25\%$ at high eccentricity, which is why the precession matters more where the eccentricity is high.
:::
:::

::: exercise Forcing {level=1 check="2.6"}
Find the radiative forcing of the glacial–interglacial carbon dioxide change from $180$ to $290\ \mathrm{ppm}$.
::: solution
$\Delta F = 5.35\ln(290/180) = 2.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Small enough to look unimpressive, and it accounts for about a fifth of the $9\ \mathrm{K}$ swing before the feedbacks are counted.
:::
:::

::: exercise The ice-core gain {level=1 check="4.3"}
If the observed glacial–interglacial temperature swing is $9\ \mathrm{K}$ and the carbon dioxide alone gives $2.1\ \mathrm{K}$, what is the amplification factor?
::: solution
$9/2.1 = 4.3$. The rest is the ice and snow albedo, the vegetation, the dust, the clouds and the water vapour, with the ice albedo the largest single term in the Northern Hemisphere.
:::
:::

::: exercise The PETM source {level=2 check="4600"}
Using the method of [[#ex-petm]], find the mass of carbon required for a $6\%$ excursion with a source at $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C} = -60\%$.
::: solution
$C_a = 3.8\times10^{18}\times6/60 = 3.8\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{mol} = 4{,}600\ \mathrm{Gt\ C}$. Doubling the isotope excursion doubles the mass, and halving the source's depletion doubles it again — which is why the excursion's amplitude alone cannot distinguish a hydrate from a thermogenic source.
:::
:::

::: exercise The eccentricity switch {level=2 check="22"}
At $e = 0.055$ the June insolation at $65^\circ\mathrm{N}$ ranges from $451$ to $562\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and at $e = 0.005$ from $498$ to $508$. Find the ratio of the two ranges, in percent of the mean, for the high-eccentricity case.
::: solution
The high-eccentricity range is $111\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ on a mean of $507$, i.e. $22\%$; the low-eccentricity range is $10$ on $503$, i.e. $2\%$. The eccentricity therefore acts as an amplitude switch, and the $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$ period of the ice ages is the period of the switch.
:::
:::

::: exercise Sea level {level=2 check="5"}
The Last Interglacial sea level was $6$–$9\ \mathrm{m}$ above present. If the Greenland contribution is $2$–$3\ \mathrm{m}$ and the West Antarctic $2$–$4\ \mathrm{m}$, what range is left for the East Antarctic and other sources?
::: solution
$6 - (3+4) = -1$ at the low end and $9 - (2+2) = 5\ \mathrm{m}$ at the high end, so $0$–$5\ \mathrm{m}$, consistent with the modelled $0$–$3\ \mathrm{m}$. The important point is that no residual requires an unknown mechanism, and that even the low end of the total implies that both the Greenland and the West Antarctic sheets lost mass.
:::
:::

::: exercise Deriving the insolation {level=3}
Derive the daily mean insolation formula of [[#ex-insolation]] from the instantaneous insolation $S_0 (d_\text{m}/d)^2(\sin\phi\sin\delta + \cos\phi\cos\delta\cos h)$, and show that at the solstice the annual mean insolation at the top of the atmosphere is independent of obliquity. Why, then, does obliquity matter to the ice sheets?
::: solution
Integrate the instantaneous insolation over the daylight hours and divide by $24$: the daylight half-angle $h_0$ satisfies $\cos h_0 = -\tan\phi\tan\delta$, and the daily mean is
$$
Q = \frac{S_0}{\pi}\left(\frac{d_\text{m}}{d}\right)^2\left[h_0\sin\phi\sin\delta + \cos\phi\cos\delta\sin h_0\right],
$$
with $h_0$ in radians. Averaging over the year, the $\cos h$ term integrates to zero and the $\sin\phi\sin\delta$ term integrates to zero because $\delta$ is symmetric about the equinoxes, so the annual mean depends only on the latitude and the distance — not on the tilt.

Obliquity matters because the ice sheets are not driven by the annual mean but by whether the summer is warm enough to melt the winter's snow. A larger tilt gives a warmer summer and a colder winter at high latitude; the summer melting, which sets the mass balance, responds to the summer, and the winter accumulation responds to the moisture supply rather than to the local temperature. This is the physical content of Milankovitch's choice of $65^\circ\mathrm{N}$ in July, and it is why a parameter that changes no annual energy at all can pace an ice age.
:::
:::

::: exercise The 41,000-year problem {level=3}
For the last $3$ million years the obliquity forcing at $65^\circ\mathrm{N}$ has an amplitude of about $25\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and the precessional forcing an amplitude of about $50\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, yet the ice-volume record between $3$ and $1.2\ \mathrm{Ma}$ is dominated by $41\ \mathrm{kyr}$. Give two explanations that have been offered, and the observation that would distinguish them.
::: solution
**(i) A linear response with a high-latitude geometry.** The obliquity forcing is global and always in phase between the hemispheres, whereas precession is out of phase between them; with ice in both hemispheres the precessional signals cancel, and the obliquity survives. Test: compare the Northern and Southern Hemisphere records for the $3$–$1.2\ \mathrm{Ma}$ interval — if the hemispheres are anti-phased at $23\ \mathrm{kyr}$, the cancellation is real.
**(ii) A nonlinear ice-sheet response.** Ice sheets of the early-Pliocene size had a $41\ \mathrm{kyr}$ stability cycle, and the $100\ \mathrm{kyr}$ behaviour requires the larger, thicker sheets of the mid-Pleistocene. Test: the numerical experiments in which the same forcing is applied to ice sheets of different initial thickness, and the sedimentary records of the ice-rafted debris that show when the Laurentian and Siberian sheets became big.

Both are partly supported, and the second is currently favoured because the transition is also a change in the *amplitude* and the *shape* of the cycles, not only in the period — a signature of a threshold rather than of a cancellation.
:::
:::

::: exercise The Anthropocene in Cenozoic context {level=3}
The Cenozoic carbon cycle has several hyperthermal events (the PETM, the ETM2, the PETM's cousins) with carbon release rates of $0.1$–$1\ \mathrm{Gt\ C\ yr^{-1}}$ and durations of $10$–$100\ \mathrm{kyr}$. Present emissions are $10\ \mathrm{Gt\ C\ yr^{-1}}$, of which half is retained in the atmosphere. Compare the two, and state what the comparison does and does not establish.
::: solution
The present *rate* of carbon addition to the atmosphere, about $5\ \mathrm{Gt\ C\ yr^{-1}}$, is five to fifty times the fastest Cenozoic hyperthermal, and the total so far, about $1000\ \mathrm{Gt\ C}$, is between a fifth and a half of the smallest of them. The comparison establishes that the **rate** is unprecedented in the last $56\ \mathrm{Myr}$, which is a statement about the capacity of the sinks — the ocean's mixing time of a thousand years, the weathering time of a hundred thousand — to keep up.

It does not establish the equilibrium outcome, which depends on the total rather than the rate: at $1000\ \mathrm{Gt\ C}$ the eventual warming is a few degrees, against the $5$–$8\ \mathrm{K}$ of the PETM, and the PETM's worst effects — the deep-sea extinction, the anoxia, the methane feedback — are threshold effects that may or may not be crossed. And it does not transfer the PETM's biotic response, because the Cenozoic fauna and flora had never seen a carbon pulse and had no refugia from a warm world, whereas the present biosphere's problem is that the change is faster than the migration. The honest summary is that the rate is the unprecedented thing, and that the record constrains the sensitivity and the timescale of recovery rather than predicting the outcome.
:::
