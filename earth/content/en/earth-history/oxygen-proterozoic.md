For two billion years the ocean was green-brown and the sky a dull pink-orange, and nothing that breathed oxygen existed. Then, in a geological instant at $2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$, the sulfur isotope record switched, the first red beds appeared, the detrital grains of uranium and pyrite stopped surviving transport, and the atmosphere became oxidising. The organisms responsible had been at work for at least a billion years, and probably two: cyanobacteria, whose oxygenic photosynthesis had been pumping out a waste gas that the planet's chemistry absorbed as fast as it was made, until the sinks were full and the gas began to accumulate.

The event is called the Great Oxidation, and it is the most important chemical change in the Earth's surface environment since the oceans condensed. It caused the first mass extinction, of the anaerobic life that the oxygen poisoned; it caused the first snowball Earth, by destroying the methane that had warmed a fainter Sun; it made possible the eukaryote, the multicellular organism and the animal, all of which are, in the plain physical sense, machines for exploiting oxygen's high redox potential; and it left a mineral deposit — the banded iron formation — that supplies most of the world's steel. This chapter is about why it took so long, how we know the timing to within a few tens of millions of years, and what the two billion years of the Proterozoic did with the new atmosphere.

## Why oxygen did not accumulate for two billion years

::: definition Sources and sinks {#def-o2-budget}
Atmospheric oxygen is a **disequilibrium** maintained by a net flux. The sources are the burial of reduced carbon (photosynthesis that is not respired), the burial of pyrite (which buries reduced sulfur instead of oxidising it), and the escape of hydrogen to space. The sinks are the oxidation of rocks at the surface — of iron, of sulfur, of the carbon in them — of volcanic and hydrothermal gases ($\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{H_2S}$, $\mathrm{SO_2}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{CO}$), and of the reduced species dissolved in the ocean. In the modern ocean the net burial flux is about $1\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ O_2\ yr^{-1}}$ and the volcanic and crustal sink is about $8\times10^{12}$, so the margin that maintains $21\%$ oxygen is a factor of only about two.
:::

::: proposition Oxygen accumulates only when the sinks are saturated or shrunk, not when the source rises {#prop-why-lag}
The Archaean ocean and crust were enormous reductant reservoirs, and the volcanic flux of reduced gases was several times the modern value because the mantle was hotter and the crust more mafic. A source that doubles while the sinks also double changes nothing. The geological evidence is that the Archaean produced oxygen — the isotopic and biomarker evidence for cyanobacteria and for organic carbon burial is older than $2.7\ \mathrm{Ga}$ — and that the oxygen simply did not accumulate, because the sinks were larger than the source by a factor of a few. What changed at $2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$ was the balance: the crust had matured to a granitic, less reducible composition; the continental area and the burial of organic carbon in basins between the cratons had grown; and the ocean's inventory of dissolved ferrous iron was being drawn down.
:::

::: example How big is the oxygen reservoir, and how fast does it turn over? {#ex-o2-residence}
The atmosphere has a mass of $5.15\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and a mean molar mass of $0.029\ \mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$, and is $21\%$ oxygen by volume. The net global burial of organic carbon is $1\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ C\ yr^{-1}}$, and photosynthesis produces roughly one mole of $\mathrm{O_2}$ per mole of carbon buried. Find the size of the atmospheric oxygen reservoir and its residence time.
::: solution
$$
n_\text{air} = \frac{5.15\times10^{18}}{0.029} = 1.78\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{mol}, \qquad n_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 0.21\times1.78\times10^{20} = 3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}.
$$
$$
\tau = \frac{3.7\times10^{19}}{1\times10^{13}} = 3.7\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{yr}.
$$
The whole oxygen atmosphere would be consumed in a few million years if burial stopped. That is the quantitative form of the point that oxygen is not a stable feature of a planet with life but a continuous, expensive product of it — and it is why the Proterozoic oxygen level could swing by orders of magnitude in a few tens of millions of years, and why the Cretaceous oceans could anoxify within a few hundred thousand years.

It also bounds the Archaean problem. If the sinks were $3\times$ the modern value, the source would have had to be $4\times$ modern before any net accumulation, and the source is limited by the area of the continental shelves available for organic carbon burial.
:::
:::

::: example Oxidising the ocean's iron {#ex-fe}
Suppose the Archaean deep ocean held $15\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of dissolved ferrous iron, and take the ocean volume as $1.3\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{m^3}$. How much oxygen is needed to oxidise it, given $4\mathrm{Fe}^{2+} + \mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{Fe}^{3+} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}$, and how does that compare with the present atmospheric reservoir of $3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$?
::: solution
$$
n_\mathrm{Fe} = 1.3\times10^{18}\times10^{3}\times15\times10^{-6} = 2.0\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{mol},
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{O_2}} = \frac{n_\mathrm{Fe}}{4} = 4.9\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{mol},
$$
which is $0.013\%$ of one present atmospheric level. Even at $100\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ the ocean's iron is only $0.09\%$ of the reservoir.

So the ocean's iron was never the thing that held oxygen down, and neither were the BIFs: precipitating the entire iron content of a ferruginous ocean would have used a thousandth of the oxygen that the atmosphere now holds. The sinks that mattered were **fluxes**, not reservoirs — the continuous supply of $\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{H_2S}$ and reduced iron from the mantle and from the weathering of a mafic crust, which Holland calculated at several times the modern value. This is the single most misunderstood point in the whole subject, and it is why the rise of oxygen is a story about the Earth's interior and the growth of the continents, not about the invention of photosynthesis.
:::
:::

## Dating the rise: five proxies that agree

The Great Oxidation is not dated from one measurement but from five independent ones, all of which change within the interval $2.50$–$2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$:

1. **Mass-independent sulfur isotopes** ($\Delta^{33}\mathrm{S} \ne 0$) disappear at $2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$, which requires an ozone layer and therefore oxygen above about $10^{-5}$ of present.
2. **Detrital uraninite and pyrite** grains, which do not survive transport in oxidising water, vanish from the palaeo-sandstones at about $2.4\ \mathrm{Ga}$.
3. **Red beds** — sandstones cemented with hematite rather than with green chlorite and pyrite — appear for the first time at about $2.4\ \mathrm{Ga}$, though they remain rare for another billion years.
4. **Manganese deposits** of the kind that requires oxidising Mn(II) appear at $2.3\ \mathrm{Ga}$, and large ones at $2.2\ \mathrm{Ga}$ (the Kalahari manganese field).
5. **Chromium isotopes** ($\delta^{53}\mathrm{Cr}$) become positive, which requires Cr(VI), which requires an oxidising surface weathering environment; the excursion begins at about $2.8\ \mathrm{Ga}$, peaks at $1.9\ \mathrm{Ga}$, and is the one proxy that hints at oxygen before the sulfur signal.

::: theorem The carbon isotope mass balance measures organic carbon burial {#thm-d13c}
Marine carbonate precipitates with the carbon isotope composition of the dissolved inorganic carbon, $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}_\text{DIC}$, while photosynthetic organic matter is depleted by about $25\%$ because Rubisco discriminates against $^{13}\mathrm{C}$. If a fraction $f$ of the carbon entering the ocean–sediment system is buried as organic matter, then
$$
\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}_\text{carb} = \delta^{13}\mathrm{C}_\text{DIC} + f\times\Delta,
$$
where $\Delta \approx 25\%$ is the fractionation, so a positive excursion in the $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ of carbonate is a direct measure of an increase in the burial of organic carbon — and therefore of a net source of oxygen.
:::

::: proof
The isotope mass balance for the sedimentary sink is
$$
\delta_\text{DIC} = f\,\delta_\text{org} + (1-f)\,\delta_\text{carb},
$$
since the carbon leaving the ocean is partitioned between the two sinks. Writing $\delta_\text{org} = \delta_\text{carb} - \Delta$ and rearranging,
$$
\delta_\text{DIC} = \delta_\text{carb} - f\Delta \Longrightarrow \delta_\text{carb} = \delta_\text{DIC} + f\Delta .
$$
With $\Delta = 25\%$ and $f$ in the range $0.1$–$0.3$, a doubling of $f$ from $0.2$ to $0.4$ raises $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}_\text{carb}$ by $5\%$, which is exactly the size of the largest Proterozoic excursions. The Lomagundo-Jatuli carbonate of Zambia, at $2.22$–$2.10\ \mathrm{Ga}$, records a rise from $+1\%$ to $+9\%$ sustained for $100\ \mathrm{Myr}$, the longest and one of the largest carbon isotope anomalies in Earth history, and the interpretation — a great pulse of organic carbon burial, and therefore of oxygen production — is confirmed by the molybdenum and uranium isotope records of the black shales deposited at the same time. ∎
:::

## Eukaryotes, and the energy argument

::: example The energy cost of being big {#ex-eukaryote-energy}
An aerobic bacterium respires at a rate limited by its surface-to-volume ratio; a eukaryotic cell of the same volume contains mitochondria, each of which is an internalised bacterium with its own membrane surface. Take a bacterium of radius $1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ respiring $10^{-17}\ \mathrm{W}$, and a eukaryote of radius $10\ \mu\mathrm{m}$. If the eukaryote were a single membrane-bound cell with respiration limited by its own surface area, by what factor would its respiration exceed the bacterium's, and by what factor does its volume — and hence its requirement — exceed it?
::: solution
Surface scales as the square of the radius, so a $10\times$ larger cell has $100\times$ the membrane and, if respiration is membrane-limited, $100\times$ the respiration. Volume scales as the cube, so it needs $1000\times$ the energy. A single-celled organism of that size is therefore energy-starved by a factor of ten.

The solution that life found is to internalise the respiratory membrane: a eukaryote with $10^3$ mitochondria has $10^3$ times the bacterial membrane inside the same cell, and can afford to be $1000\times$ bigger. This is the quantitative content of the argument, due to Nick Lane and Martin, that the origin of the eukaryote was an **energy** event rather than a genetic one, and that the endosymbiosis of the mitochondrion — which supplied internal membranes with their own genomes and their own control — was the single bottleneck that kept the Earth microbial for two billion years. On this view the delay is not an accident of chemistry but a combinatorial improbability: the event requires an archaeon to engulf a bacterium and both to survive it, and it has happened exactly once.
:::
:::

The fossil evidence for eukaryotes runs from the disputed $2.1\ \mathrm{Ga}$ acritarch-like bodies of the Huronian, through *Grypania spiralis* at $1.9\ \mathrm{Ga}$ (a coiled ribbon of uncertain biology), the Francevill biota of Gabon at $2.1\ \mathrm{Ga}$ (possibly the oldest multicellular organism, though possibly a mineral pattern), to the red alga *Bangiomorpha pubescens* at $1.05\ \mathrm{Ga}$, which is unambiguous — it has differentiated cells and what appear to be tetrasporangia — and to the Ediacaran megafossils at $575\ \mathrm{Ma}$. Molecular clocks put the last common ancestor of living eukaryotes at $1.8$–$1.0\ \mathrm{Ga}$ and the origin of the group earlier, and the sterane biomarkers put eukaryotic membranes at $1.65\ \mathrm{Ga}$ at the latest and, on the most contested measurement, $2.7\ \mathrm{Ga}$.

## Snowballs, and the boring billion

::: example A methane greenhouse collapses {#ex-methane-collapse}
Before the Great Oxidation the atmosphere may have held $10^{-3}$ of methane, giving $15$–$25\ \mathrm{K}$ of greenhouse warming. Oxygen converts methane to carbon dioxide and water by reaction with the hydroxyl radical, with an atmospheric lifetime that falls from about $10$ years at $10^{-6}$ oxygen to months at higher levels. If the methane mixing ratio falls from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$, estimate the change in radiative forcing using $\Delta F = 5.35\ln(C/C_0) + 0.036(\sqrt{C}-\sqrt{C_0})$ for methane, and the resulting temperature change at $\lambda = 3.2\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$$
\Delta F = 5.35\ln(10^{-6}/10^{-3}) + 0.036(\sqrt{1} - \sqrt{1000}) = 5.35\times(-6.91) + 0.036(1-31.6)
$$
$$
= -37.0 - 1.1 = -38\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}, \qquad \Delta T = -38/3.2 = -12\ \mathrm{K}.
$$
A twelve-degree global cooling, on top of the $18\ \mathrm{K}$ deficit from the faint young Sun, is enough to put the planet into a runaway ice-albedo feedback, and the Huronian glacial deposits at $2.45$–$2.22\ \mathrm{Ga}$ — the Makganyene, the Gowganda, the Lakhaj — are found at palaeolatitudes that have been argued to be near the equator, with dropstones and with cap carbonates. Whether the Huronian was a full snowball or a "snowball-ish" series of glaciations is still argued, but the causal chain — oxygen rises, methane falls, the climate collapses, the glaciation buries the organic carbon that would otherwise have been respired, oxygen rises further — is the first clear case of biology driving the climate, and it is the template for every argument since about the coupling of the biosphere and the atmosphere.
:::
:::

Between $1.8$ and $0.8\ \mathrm{Ga}$ the record is monotonous in a way that has become famous. No banded iron formations, no true red beds, no large carbon isotope excursions, no animal-grade fossils; stromatolites reach a diversity peak and then decline; the ocean appears to have been largely anoxic and, on the sulfur and molybdenum isotope evidence, ferruginous with a sulphidic deep reservoir — the "Canfield ocean". Eukaryotes were present and diversifying in the microfossil record, and the first fossils that can be assigned to living groups (the red alga at $1.05\ \mathrm{Ga}$, possible green algae and the first fungi at about $1.0\ \mathrm{Ga}$) appear late in it. Whether the boredom is a cause or a consequence of low oxygen is the question, and the current answer is that they are coupled: oxygen below a few per cent of present levels caps the size, the motility and the metabolic rate of any organism that relies on diffusion, and the ecosystems of the Proterozoic were, in a literal sense, starved.

The second step in the oxygen rise comes in the Tonian and Cryogenian, between about $0.8$ and $0.55\ \mathrm{Ga}$, from the molybdenum and uranium isotope records, from the cerium anomaly of chemical sediments, and from the appearance of the first animals — sponges, whose requirement for oxygen has been measured and which cannot survive below about $0.08$ of present atmospheric levels. The Ediacaran biota appear at $575\ \mathrm{Ma}$, and the Cambrian fauna at $539\ \mathrm{Ma}$.

::: warning "The rise of oxygen was a good thing"
It was the largest mass extinction in Earth history, though we cannot count the victims because almost none of them fossilised. Oxygen and its derivative ozone are toxic to anaerobes, which by then were most of the planet's biomass; the methane that had warmed the planet was destroyed and the Huronian glaciation followed; and the new oxidising conditions removed the reduced chemicals — the nickel, the molybdenum in solution, the dissolved iron — on which much of the existing biosphere depended. The event is also a warning about proxies: for twenty years the $2.7\ \mathrm{Ga}$ age of cyanobacterial biomarkers rested on 2-methylhopanoids in shales of that age, and in 2023 Aviva Brocks and colleagues showed that those molecules in the older rocks were contaminant hydrocarbons introduced in the laboratory, and that the genuine cyanobacterial signal is no older than $1.65\ \mathrm{Ga}$. The rise of oxygen did not move, but the timing of its inventor did, by a billion years.
:::

::: quiz
Banded iron formations end at about $1.8\ \mathrm{Ga}$. The best explanation is
- [ ] the hydrothermal sources of iron were shut off by a change in ocean chemistry
- [x] the deep ocean's inventory of dissolved ferrous iron was finally oxidised and the seafloor no longer supplied iron to a water column that could not carry it
- [ ] the silica that bands with the iron was removed by the first silica-secreting organisms
- [ ] the BIFs of younger age have been subducted
::: solution
BIFs require a water column that carries much dissolved iron and a mechanism to oxidise it at a distance from the source. Once the deep ocean became permanently oxidised or sulphidic, dissolved iron was precipitated at the vent as pyrite and never reached the basin, and the deposit type disappeared. The return of BIFs in the Sturtian and Marinoan is consistent with the same mechanism in reverse: ice cover cut off the oxygen, and hydrothermal iron accumulated again. The silica-secreting-organism option is a real hypothesis for the change in chert abundance, but it is not the reason the iron formations stopped.
:::
:::

::: history
The idea that the Precambrian atmosphere lacked oxygen was argued from geology long before it could be measured: Preston Cloud's 1968 paper "Inadequacy of atmospheric oxygen during the late Precambrian" assembled the absence of red beds, the detrital uranium and pyrite, and the iron formations into a single case. Heinz Lowenstam and, later, Geert Garrels and Robert Friend laid the chemical sedimentation groundwork. Erik and Marguerite Holland's *Oxygenation of the Atmosphere and Ocean* (1984, second edition 2006) is the book that made the subject quantitative, with its budget of sources and sinks and its insistence that the fluxes rather than the reservoirs control the oxygen level. James Farquhar, Huiming Bao and Mark Thiemens discovered mass-independent sulfur fractionation in Archaean sulphides in 2000, and with it a clock for the rise of ozone; Reinhard, plan and colleagues' chromium isotope work of 2009 added a second clock. Joseph Kirschvink proposed Snowball Earth in 1992, Paul Hoffman and Andy Knoll tested it in the Neoproterozoic carbonates of Namibia from 1998, and Gideon Rothman's 2003 model of the oscillating Proterozoic sulfur cycle gave the boring billion its mechanism. The 2023 redating of the cyanobacterial biomarkers, by Brocks and colleagues, is a reminder that in this subject the evidence is often younger than the rocks.
:::

## Where this leads

The isotope systems used as redox proxies are developed in [[geochemistry/stable-isotopes]] and [[geochemistry/radiogenic-tracers]]; the sulfur and carbon cycles themselves in [[biogeochemistry/phosphorus-sulfur]] and [[biogeochemistry/carbon-deep-time]]; the ocean's chemistry and its controls in [[oceanography/ocean-chemistry]]; the snowball mechanism and the climate of a frozen Earth in [[palaeoclimate/deep-time]] and [[climate-system/feedbacks-sensitivity]]; and the Cambrian outcome in [[earth-history/palaeozoic]].

::: summary Key takeaways
- Oxygen is a maintained disequilibrium: the atmospheric reservoir turns over in a few million years, so its accumulation requires a persistent surplus of burial over weathering.
- The two-billion-year lag is explained by sinks, not by a late invention of photosynthesis: the Archaean volcanic and crustal reductant fluxes were several times the modern value.
- Oxidising the ocean's dissolved iron was worth only about one ten-thousandth of the present oxygen reservoir; fluxes, not reservoirs, control the atmosphere.
- Five independent proxies — mass-independent sulfur, detrital uranium and pyrite, red beds, manganese deposits, chromium isotopes — all change between 2.50 and 2.45 Ga.
- Positive carbon isotope excursions measure organic carbon burial, and therefore oxygen production, by a simple mass balance.
- The Great Oxidation destroyed the methane greenhouse and probably caused the Huronian glaciation: the first case of biology driving climate.
- The eukaryote's origin was an energy event; mitochondria supply the internal membrane area that a big cell needs, and the event may have happened only once.
- The Mesoproterozoic "boring billion" was low-oxygen, ferruginous and possibly sulphidic, and the second rise of oxygen in the Neoproterozoic precedes and probably permits the animals.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Reservoirs and fluxes {level=1 check="7.4"}
If the net oxygen source were halved from $1\times10^{13}$ to $5\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{mol\ yr^{-1}}$, what would the new residence time of atmospheric oxygen be, in Myr?
::: solution
$\tau = 3.7\times10^{19}/5\times10^{12} = 7.4\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{yr}$, i.e. $7.4\ \mathrm{Myr}$. The reservoir is small enough that the oxygen level tracks the burial flux on geological timescales, which is why the Cretaceous and Palaeogene oxygen level could rise and fall with the area of anoxic seas.
:::
:::

::: exercise Proxies {level=1 check="2.45"}
Which proxy for the rise of oxygen depends on ultraviolet photochemistry, and what is the minimum oxygen level it implies?
::: solution
Mass-independent sulfur isotope fractionation ($\Delta^{33}\mathrm{S}$), which requires ultraviolet light below $220\ \mathrm{nm}$ to reach $\mathrm{SO_2}$. Its disappearance implies an ozone layer and therefore oxygen above about $10^{-5}$ of the present level. The signal switches off at $2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Banded iron {level=1}
Why are banded iron formations regarded as evidence for an anoxic ocean rather than for an oxygenated one?
::: solution
Because they require dissolved ferrous iron to be transported through the water column and precipitated only where it met an oxidant. In an oxygenated ocean the iron would be oxidised at the vent and precipitated locally, as it is today, and no basin-scale chemical sediment would form. The bands are the product of a stratified ocean with a deep anoxic, iron-rich reservoir and a shallow oxidising zone.
:::
:::

::: exercise Carbon isotope mass balance {level=2 check="5"}
Using the relation of [[#thm-d13c]], find the change in $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}_\text{carb}$ if the fraction of carbon buried as organic matter rises from $0.20$ to $0.40$, with a fractionation of $25\%$.
::: solution
$\Delta\delta = \Delta\times\Delta f = 25\times0.20 = 5\%$. This is the size of the largest Neoproterozoic and Palaeoproterozoic excursions, and it is why a carbon isotope curve is read as a record of the carbon cycle's partitioning rather than of the carbon's abundance.
:::

::: exercise Methane and climate {level=2 check="8"}
Using the methane forcing formula of [[#ex-methane-collapse]], find the forcing and the temperature change for a fall in methane from $5\times10^{-4}$ to $5\times10^{-6}$.
::: solution
$\Delta F = 5.35\ln(0.01) + 0.036(\sqrt{5}-\sqrt{500}) = -24.6 + 0.036(2.24-22.36) = -24.6 - 0.72 = -25.3\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, giving $\Delta T = -7.9\ \mathrm{K}$, about $8\ \mathrm{K}$. Note that the logarithmic term dominates and the square-root term contributes only $3\%$; for large changes in methane the simple logarithmic form is adequate.
:::

::: exercise The lag {level=2 check="2.4"}
If the Archaean reductant sink was $3\times$ the modern value and the modern sink is $8\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{mol\ O_2\ yr^{-1}}$, by what factor would the net burial flux have had to rise above its modern value of $1\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ yr^{-1}}$ before oxygen could accumulate?
::: solution
The sink was $2.4\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{mol\ yr^{-1}}$, so burial had to exceed that: a factor of $2.4$ above the modern value. Had the sink been $4\times$ modern, the factor would be $3.2$. The point is that the required change is a factor of a few, which is entirely achievable by an increase in the area of shallow epicontinental seas — and that no "invention" of new biology is needed.
:::

::: exercise Deriving the carbon isotope relation {level=3}
Derive $\delta_\text{carb} = \delta_\text{DIC} + f\Delta$ from the two-reservoir mass balance, and then apply it: a Palaeoproterozoic carbonate records $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C} = +9\%$ while the coeval marine carbonate baseline is $+1\%$. Estimate $f$, the fraction of carbon buried as organic matter, taking $\Delta = 25\%$, and compare with the modern value of about $0.2$.
::: solution
From $\delta_\text{DIC} = f\delta_\text{org} + (1-f)\delta_\text{carb}$ and $\delta_\text{org} = \delta_\text{carb} - \Delta$:
$$
\delta_\text{DIC} = \delta_\text{carb} - f\Delta \Longrightarrow f = \frac{\delta_\text{carb} - \delta_\text{DIC}}{\Delta}.
$$
Taking the baseline carbonates as recording $\delta_\text{DIC} = +1\%$, the excursion gives
$$
f = \frac{9-1}{25} = 0.32 ,
$$
against a modern value of about $0.2$. More than three-tenths of the carbon entering the ocean was being buried as organic matter instead of as carbonate — a half again as much as today, sustained for $100\ \mathrm{Myr}$. That is why the Lomagundo anomaly is read as a genuine perturbation of the whole carbon cycle, sustained for $100\ \mathrm{Myr}$, and as the most likely source of the oxygen that produced the $2.22\ \mathrm{Ga}$ glaciation and the first manganese deposits.
:::

::: exercise A snowball's deglaciation {level=3}
A snowball Earth has zero weathering (the rock is under ice) and zero biological oxygen production, but continues to receive volcanic $\mathrm{CO_2}$ at $0.9\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{mol\ C\ yr^{-1}}$. Estimate the time to build enough carbon dioxide to deglaciate, taking the required additional forcing as $30\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and using $\Delta F = 5.35\ln(C/C_0)$. State the assumptions.
::: solution
The required concentration ratio is
$$
\ln(C/C_0) = 30/5.35 = 5.6 \Longrightarrow C/C_0 = 272,
$$
i.e. $272\times4\times10^{-4} = 0.11\ \mathrm{bar}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$. The atmosphere holds $1.78\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{mol}$ of air, so the required carbon is $0.11\times1.78\times10^{20} = 2.0\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$, and at $0.9\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{mol\ yr^{-1}}$ the time is
$$
t = \frac{2.0\times10^{19}}{9\times10^{11}} = 2.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{yr},
$$
about $20$ million years. That is the classical "the snowball must have lasted tens of millions of years" argument, and it is why the Sturtian, which lasted $55\ \mathrm{Myr}$, is a problem: at the required deglaciation carbon dioxide level the model should have melted it sooner.

The assumptions that fail are the interesting ones. Weathering does not fall to zero under thin ice or in ice-free equatorial regions ("snowball with a refugium" or "slushball"). Volcanic outgassing may be higher, and the sea floor weathering sink (which takes up $\mathrm{CO_2}$ without needing continents) continues. And the required forcing of $30\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ depends on the ice albedo and on the cloud cover of a planet with no liquid ocean, which is one of the hardest problems in the subject.
:::

::: exercise The energy argument {level=3}
Test the argument of [[#ex-eukaryote-energy]] quantitatively: a eukaryote of radius $10\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ contains $1000$ mitochondria of radius $0.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$. Find the ratio of total respiratory membrane area to cell volume for (a) the bacterium, (b) the eukaryote's own plasma membrane, and (c) the eukaryote including mitochondria, and comment on the conclusion.
::: solution
For a sphere the area-to-volume ratio is $3/r$.
(a) Bacterium: $3/1 = 3\ \mu\mathrm{m^{-1}}$.
(b) Eukaryote plasma membrane: $3/10 = 0.3\ \mu\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, ten times worse.
(c) Mitochondria: each has $3/0.3 = 10\ \mu\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ of its own surface, and $1000$ of them have a total area of $1000\times4\pi(0.3)^2 = 1130\ \mu\mathrm{m^2}$ against a cell volume of $4189\ \mu\mathrm{m^3}$, giving $0.27\ \mu\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ from the mitochondria plus $0.3$ from the plasma membrane.

The mitochondrial contribution is therefore comparable to the whole plasma membrane of the cell, and the total is $0.57$ against $0.3$ — about twice the value a single-membrane cell of that size could achieve, and $19\%$ of the bacterium's. The argument is thus not that the eukaryote has as much membrane per unit volume as a bacterium (it does not) but that it has far more than it could have without endosymbiosis, and that the mitochondrion's internal cristae multiply the area further — the inner membrane of a mitochondrion is several times its outer surface, which is the detail that makes the difference. The conclusion survives: the eukaryote's size and metabolic rate are paid for by internalised respiration, and the payment mechanism requires an endosymbiosis.
