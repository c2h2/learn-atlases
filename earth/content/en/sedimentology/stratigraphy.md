Stratigraphy is the arithmetic of deep time. Its task is to turn a pile of rock into a history — to decide which beds are older, how much time each one represents, how much time is missing, and which bed in one valley is the same bed in another. The principles are three centuries old and can be stated on a postcard; the practice is a continuous argument about time, because a bed is a **body of rock** and time is not a body of rock, and every method of getting from one to the other makes assumptions that fail in different places.

## The principles

::: definition The laws of stratigraphy {#def-laws}
**Superposition**: in an undeformed succession, each bed is younger than the one beneath it. **Original horizontality**: beds were deposited approximately horizontally. **Original lateral continuity**: a bed continues laterally until it thins to a pinch-out or is cut off against an unconformity. **Cross-cutting relations**: a feature that cuts a rock is younger than the rock it cuts. **Inclusions**: a clast enclosed in a rock is older than that rock. **Uniformitarianism**: the processes now operating were operating in the past in a similar way. **Faunal succession**: fossil assemblages succeed one another in a determinable order, and an assemblage identifies a time. The first six are Steno's (Steno) and Hutton's and Lyell's; the last is Smith's (Smith) and Cuvier's, and it is the only one that gives **time** rather than **order**.
:::

::: definition Unconformities and hiatus {#def-unconformity}
An **unconformity** is a surface between rocks that represents a **hiatus** — a gap in the record during which either sediment was not deposited or, more usually, sediment was deposited and then removed. An **angular unconformity** has tilted or folded beds below and younger, less deformed beds above, and it records deformation, erosion and renewed subsidence. A **disconformity** is a parallel-surfaced hiatus, commonly marked by a palaeosol, a hardground, a channel or a lag. A **nonconformity** is sedimentary rock on igneous or metamorphic basement. A **paraconformity** is a hiatus with no physical expression at all, detectable only from the fossils or from an isotope curve, and it is the commonest and most consequential unconformity, because it is invisible and it can be tens of millions of years long.
:::

::: example How much time is missing? {#ex-hiatus}
A 500-m formation spans 12 Myr from its base to its top. Detailed work identifies four surfaces, each of which is a paraconformity with a hiatus of 1.5 Myr, and the beds between them are conformable. Find the average sedimentation rate, the rate during actual deposition, and the fraction of the formation's time span that is actually recorded.
::: solution
The four hiatuses total $4\times1.5 = 6\ \mathrm{Myr}$, so the time of actual deposition is $12 - 6 = 6\ \mathrm{Myr}$. The **average** rate over the whole span is
$$
\bar{r} = \frac{500}{12} = 42\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}},
$$
and the rate **during deposition** is
$$
r = \frac{500}{6} = 83\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}},
$$
a factor of two higher. Half the time span is missing, so half the events that happened in this basin left no record, and any rate, any evolutionary rate, any extinction rate or any accumulation rate computed from the formation's thickness and its total time span is **too low by a factor of two**.

This is the central quantitative fact of stratigraphy and it is almost always ignored. A typical Palaeozoic carbonate or shelf succession records, on the estimates of Sadler and of Paul, **one per cent or less** of the time available: the average rate over a kilometre of Palaeozoic section is tens of metres per million years, but the rate during the episodes of actual deposition was hundreds, and the gaps are not evenly distributed but are concentrated in the surfaces. The consequence for every rate calculation in the rock record is that the number is a **lower bound on the event rate and an upper bound on the duration**, and that the correct procedure is to compute the rate twice — once with the total span and once with the sum of the preserved durations — and to report both.
:::
:::

::: history
The four laws of stratigraphy were stated by Steno (Steno) in 1669 in a fifty-page dissertation on a solid body within a solid, and were extended by Hutton (Hutton) and Playfair, whose theory of the Earth of 1788 and whose "circular" view of the rock cycle introduced deep time. Charles Lyell's (Lyell) Principles of Geology of 1830–33 made uniformitarianism the discipline's philosophy. William Smith (Smith) established faunal succession empirically between 1793 and 1815 while digging the Somerset Coal Canal, and published his map of the strata of England and Wales in 1815; Cuvier and Brongniart established the same principle in the Paris basin, and d'Orbigny's (d'Orbigny) stage concept of 1842–51 is the ancestor of the chronostratigraphic series. The lithostratigraphic and biostratigraphic codes were settled by the international congresses of the 1960s and 1970s and by the International Stratigraphic Guide of Hedberg and of Salvator; the Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point procedure, which requires a boundary to be defined at a single point in a single section, was adopted in 1977 and is the most rigorous convention in the geosciences. The measurement of hiatuses and of the resolution of the record is the work of Sadler (Sadler) in 1981 and of Miller, Peters and Gaines, whose 2007 and 2012 papers on the scaling of sedimentary accumulation revived Raup's (Raup) and Phillips's (Phillips) warning that the diversity curve is partly a sampling curve. Cyclostratigraphy is the achievement of Alfred Wegener's (Wegener) brother Kurt, of Van Houten's (Van Houten) work on the Permian of the Bighorn Basin in 1986, and above all of Alfred Fischer (Fischer) and of the Berger and de Vlugt school, and it is now the highest-resolution geochronology available for the Mesozoic and Cenozoic.
:::

## Time and its units

::: definition The three stratigraphies {#def-three}
**Lithostratigraphy** divides rock into bodies by their lithology: formation, member, tongue, group. Its units are **bodies of rock**, its boundaries are diachronous, and its virtue is that it can be mapped without fossils. **Biostratigraphy** divides rock by its fossils: the **taxon range zone**, the **interval zone**, the **assemblage zone**, the **concurrent range zone** and the **lineage zone**. Its units are **time-equivalent** in principle and diachronous in practice, because a taxon's first appearance is controlled by the environment as well as by evolution. **Chronostratigraphy** divides rock by **time**: eonothem, erathem, system, series, stage. Its units are bounded by isochrones, its boundary stratotypes are the **Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Points** (GSSPs, "golden spikes") of the International Commission on Stratigraphy, and its virtue is that it is the only one of the three that can be used between basins. A **chronostratigraphic** unit is a body of rock, and its boundary is a **surface**, not an instant; the corresponding **geochronologic** unit is the interval of time.
:::

::: definition Correlation methods {#def-correlation}
Correlation is the establishment of equivalence between two sections, and its methods rank by their independence from lithology. **Biostratigraphic** correlation by a zonal fossil is the workhorse and is limited by the diachroneity of first appearances. **Magnetostratigraphic** correlation matches a section's polarity sequence to the geomagnetic polarity timescale, and it is the only method with a global, absolute-time reference for the last 330 Myr, limited to sections that are continuously deposited and suitably sampled. **Chemostratigraphic** correlation matches an isotope or elemental curve — $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$, $\delta^{18}\mathrm{O}$, Sr, Os — and it can be extremely precise where an excursion is sharp and global, as at the Paleocene–Eocene thermal maximum, and worthless where the curve is flat or the signal is diagenetic. **Cyclostratigraphic** correlation matches the orbital cycles in the bedding and can date a section to a few per cent of its own age. **Event** correlation matches a single instantaneous deposit — a **bentonite**, an impact spherule layer, a tephra — which is the strongest correlation available, because an ash bed is isochronous to within a year and can be dated radiometrically.
:::

::: example A bentonite does the work {#ex-bentonite}
Two marine sections 800 km apart both contain a 30-cm bentonite. In section A the bentonite lies 120 m above a limestone with the ammonite *Sonneratia* and 40 m below a sandstone with *Schloenbachia*; in section B it lies 200 m above the same *Sonneratia* horizon and 15 m below a sandstone with *Schloenbachia*. A U–Pb zircon date on the bentonite gives $95.4 \pm 0.2\ \mathrm{Ma}$. Correlate the sections and find the sedimentation rate in each.
::: solution
The bentonite is an **isochronous marker**: it fell over both sections within days, so it is the same horizon in both, and the correlation is exact and independent of the fossils. The two sections are therefore tied at a point dated to $95.4 \pm 0.2\ \mathrm{Ma}$.

The biostratigraphy then gives the ages of the two bracketing horizons. *Sonneratia* and *Schloenbachia* are upper Cenomanian index ammonites, and their first appearances are dated elsewhere at about 96.5 and 95.0 Ma. In section A the bentonite lies 120 m above the *Sonneratia* first appearance and 40 m below the *Schloenbachia* one, so the section between the two fossil horizons is $120 + 40 = 160\ \mathrm{m}$ thick and spans $96.5 - 95.0 = 1.5\ \mathrm{Myr}$, giving
$$
r_A = \frac{160}{1.5} = 107\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}} .
$$
In section B the same fossil interval is $200 + 15 = 215\ \mathrm{m}$ thick, giving
$$
r_B = \frac{215}{1.5} = 143\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}} .
$$
Section B accumulated sediment 34 per cent faster, which is a statement about its position relative to the sediment source, and it is a conclusion that could not have been reached without the ash bed, because the two sections' lithologies are entirely different and their fossil content is too coarse to distinguish 1.5 Myr.

The caution is the diachroneity of the **first appearance**. A ammonite's first appearance in a section is the first time it reached **that** basin, which may be a quarter of a million years after its first appearance anywhere, and the rate calculated above carries that error. The rigorous method is to use the ash bed as the tie and the **last** appearances, which are less facies-controlled than the first, and to state the uncertainty as the resolution of the biozone rather than of the radiometric date.
:::

::: example Building an age model {#ex-agemodel}
A 40-cm deep-sea core contains the Olduvai subchron, which occupies the interval from 32 cm to 14 cm below the top of the core. The base of the core, at 40 cm, is dated by its foraminiferal assemblage at 1.95 Ma. Assuming a constant sedimentation rate, find the rate and the age of the top of the core, and state what would make the answer wrong.
::: solution
The Olduvai subchron spans $1.95 - 1.78 = 0.17\ \mathrm{Myr}$ and occupies $32 - 14 = 18\ \mathrm{cm}$, so
$$
r = \frac{18\ \mathrm{cm}}{0.17\ \mathrm{Myr}} = 106\ \mathrm{cm\,Myr^{-1}} = 1.06\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}} .
$$
The base of the core at 40 cm is 1.95 Ma, so the top at 0 cm is
$$
1.95 - \frac{40}{106} = 1.95 - 0.38 = 1.57\ \mathrm{Ma}.
$$
The mean age of the core is therefore 1.76 Ma and it spans 0.38 Myr, of which the Olduvai is 45 per cent — a suspiciously large fraction, and the first sign that the assumption of a constant rate is doing too much work.

Four things would make the answer wrong. **The rate is not constant**: pelagic sedimentation varies with productivity and with carbonate dissolution, and a rate derived from one 18-cm subchron is applied over 40 cm. **The core has gaps**: a single small burrow or a current-earned hardground can remove 10 cm of a 40-cm core, and coring itself loses the top few centimetres to recovery failure. **The assemblage date at the base is a biozone**, not a point, and its uncertainty is a quarter of a million years — two-thirds of the core's whole span. **And the Olduvai's boundaries are themselves dated to a few tens of thousands of years**, which is small but not negligible. The honest age model quotes an uncertainty of $\pm0.1\ \mathrm{Myr}$ at the top, and the way to improve it is not to argue about the rate but to **add a second tie** — another polarity reversal, an ash bed, or a biostratigraphic datum — and to test whether the rate between the two ties is the same as the rate below them.
:::

## Time-average and resolution

::: example How old is a shell bed? {#ex-timeaverage}
A coquinite — a bed of whole, articulated, well-preserved shells — is 25 cm thick. The background sedimentation rate is $60\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}}$, the shells are 2 cm long, and the bed shows no evidence of winnowing or reworking from below. Estimate the time over which the shell bed accumulated, and say what it means for a species' range.
::: solution
If the bed accumulated at the background rate, its 25 cm represents
$$
\frac{0.25}{60}\times10^{6} = 4200\ \mathrm{yr}.
$$
The bed is therefore a **time-average of four millennia**: it is a mixture of shells that lived over four thousand years, and it cannot resolve any event shorter than that. If the bed is a **concentrated lag** winnowed from a thicker interval — which the absence of winnowing structures argues against but does not exclude — the time-average could be a hundred times longer, because a lag concentrates the shells of a whole sequence into one surface.

The consequence for the fossil record is the one that Sadler (Sadler) and Miller made quantitative. A species' **range** in a section is bounded by its first and last appearance, and both are biased: the first appearance is **too young** and the last is **too old**, because the true boundary lies in a hiatus or below the section, and because the species was rare at the edge of its range and is unlikely to be collected there. The measured duration of a species is therefore an **overestimate** of its true duration, and the measured gap between two successive species' ranges is an **underestimate** of the true gap. In a section with a one-per-cent record, a species that lived for a million years may be recorded in three discontinuous outcrops, and its apparent extinction and reappearance is a sampling artefact. The correction is statistical, it requires abundance data, and it is now standard practice in the studies of origination and extinction rates that the raw record would otherwise make meaningless.
:::
:::

::: warning The rock record is a filter, and its resolution gets worse with age
Sedimentation rates are not constant in time. On the estimates of Sadler and of Kump and of Peters and Gaines, the **average** preservation rate of continental sediment falls steeply with the age of the rock, from hundreds of metres per million years in the Quaternary to a few metres in the Palaeozoic, and the fall is not a decline in sediment production but the **destruction of the record by burial, erosion and non-deposition**. Three consequences follow. **The older the rock, the coarser the resolution**: a one-metre bed of Quaternary mud may be a decade, and a one-metre bed of Cambrian shale may be a million years, and the same field method — measuring a section and counting beds — gives answers whose precision differs by five orders of magnitude. **The record is biased toward the times and places that were being deposited on**: a eustatic highstand preserves a shelf, a lowstand removes it, and the surviving Palaeozoic record is dominated by the intervals and the cratons that happened to be subsiding. And **the apparent diversity curve is partly a sampling curve**: the increase in marine diversity through the Palaeozoic that Jack Phillips saw in 1860 was argued to be an artefact of the greater area and better preservation of younger rocks for a century before Raup's (Raup) work on sampling standardisation, and the argument is still not fully closed for the Cambrian and the Ordovician. The discipline is to state, for every rate and every duration taken from the rock, what the resolution of the method is and how the filter would have to have behaved to produce the observed pattern from a different one.
:::

::: quiz
A formation 300 m thick spans 6 Myr and contains no visible unconformity. What is the best inference?
- [ ] Sedimentation was continuous and uniform.
- [x] The section may contain many paraconformities, and the average rate of 50 m/Myr is a lower bound on the true deposition rate.
- [ ] The section is incomplete but the missing time is small.
- [ ] The sedimentation rate cannot be estimated at all.
::: solution
An average rate over a span that includes hiatuses is always a **lower bound** on the rate during deposition, and in a shallow-marine or carbonate setting the missing fraction is commonly 50–99 per cent even where no surface is visible. The absence of a visible unconformity means only that the hiatuses are **paraconformities** — surfaces with no physical expression — and these are detected by the fossils, by an isotope curve or by cyclostratigraphy, not by the eye.
:::

## Where this leads

The relative-dating principles are applied in [[earth-history/relative-dating]] and the timescale itself is in [[earth-history/geological-time]], the absolute dates come from [[geochemistry/geochronology]], the isotope curves used for correlation are in [[geochemistry/stable-isotopes]], the orbital cycles are in [[palaeoclimate/orbital-cycles]] and [[palaeoclimate/dating]], and the sequence-stratigraphic surfaces are in [[sedimentology/basins-sequences]].

::: summary
- Superposition gives order; only faunal succession gives time, and every method of getting time from rock rests on an assumption.
- Lithostratigraphy maps bodies of rock, biostratigraphy maps fossil ranges, and chronostratigraphy maps time; only the last is usable between basins.
- A formation's average sedimentation rate under-estimates the rate during deposition by the fraction of its time that is missing, and the fraction is usually large.
- An isochronous marker bed — a bentonite, an impact layer — is the strongest correlation available, because it can be dated and it is the same horizon everywhere.
- A fossil assemblage is time-averaged over the thickness of its bed divided by the sedimentation rate, commonly thousands of years.
- The resolution of the record degrades with age, and the diversity curve is partly a sampling curve.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Superposition and cross-cutting {level=1}
A sandstone contains granite clasts and is intruded by a basalt dyke that is itself cut by a fault. Give the order of the four events.
::: solution
Oldest to youngest: **granite** (the source of the clasts, by the law of inclusions), **deposition of the sandstone** (superposition and inclusions), **the basalt dyke** (cross-cutting the sandstone), **the fault** (it cuts the dyke, so it is younger than the dyke). This is the entire method of relative dating, and it works without a single fossil.
:::
:::

::: exercise Sedimentation rate {level=1 check="80"}
A 400-m formation spans 5 Myr. Find the average sedimentation rate in m/Myr.
::: solution
$r = 400/5 = 80\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}}$, or $0.08\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$. This is a high but entirely normal rate for a deltaic or shelf succession, and it is an **average** that hides the hiatuses.
:::
:::

::: exercise Unconformity type {level=1}
Cretaceous flat-lying sandstone rests on Carboniferous strata that are vertical and cleaved. Name the surface and reconstruct the history.
::: solution
An **angular unconformity**. The history is: deposition of the Carboniferous sequence; **deformation** — folding and cleavage — during the Variscan orogeny; **uplift and erosion** of the folded pile down to a near-planar surface; a **hiatus** of some 150 Myr during which the folded rocks were weathered and their sediment carried away; then **subsidence** and the deposition of the Cretaceous sandstone flat on the eroded surface. The surface is the most useful structure in the field, because it separates two deformation events and two subsidence events, and because the sandstone at its base contains pebbles of the underlying cleaved shale and therefore dates the erosion to before the sandstone.
:::
:::

::: exercise Biozones {level=2}
Taxon A ranges from 101 to 98 Ma and taxon B from 99 to 96 Ma in a given region. What biozone is defined by their overlap, what is its span, and what is the weakness of the zone?
::: solution
Their **concurrent range zone** — the interval in which both taxa co-occurred — spans $99$ to $98\ \mathrm{Ma}$, a duration of $1\ \mathrm{Myr}$. It is the most precise of the biozones because it is bounded by two independent first and last appearances.

Its weakness is that both boundaries are **local first and last appearances**, not global ones. If taxon A arrived in this region 0.4 Myr after its global first appearance, the base of the concurrent range zone is 0.4 Myr too young, and a section correlated on the base of the zone is correlated to the wrong time. The remedy is to define the zone on taxa with **rapid global evolution and no facies control** — conodonts, graptolites, planktonic foraminifera, ammonites — and to test the correlation with an independent method.
:::
:::

::: exercise Magnetostratigraphy {level=2}
A 240-m continuously exposed lacustrine section yields a polarity sequence of normal, reversed, normal, reversed, normal, reversed over its length, and the section's base is dated by tephra at 2.60 Ma. Which polarity chrons are present, and what is the mean sedimentation rate?
::: solution
The base at 2.60 Ma lies just below the **Gauss–Matuyama reversal** at 2.58 Ma, so the six polarity units read, upward: the top of the **Gauss** normal chron, reversed **Matuyama**, the **Reunion** normal subchron (2.14–2.12 Ma), reversed Matuyama again, the **Olduvai** normal subchron (1.95–1.78 Ma), and reversed Matuyama above the Olduvai. The pattern matches the geomagnetic polarity timescale exactly, and the correlation is unique, because two short normal subchrons inside a long reversed chron are a fingerprint that no other interval carries.

The section spans from about 2.60 Ma at its base to about 1.7 Ma at its top, a duration of $0.9\ \mathrm{Myr}$, so
$$
r = \frac{240}{0.9} = 267\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}} .
$$
This is a high rate, typical of a subsiding lacustrine rift basin, and it is the kind of number that only the magnetic pattern can give, because a lacustrine section without fossils has no biostratigraphy at all.
:::
:::

::: exercise Cyclostratigraphy {level=2 check="41"}
A 13.2-m marl–limestone rhythmite succession contains 32 couplets, and radiometric dates at its base and top show that it spans 1.31 Myr. Find the period of the cycle in kyr, identify the orbital forcing, and find the sedimentation rate.
::: solution
The period is $1.31\times10^{6}/32 = 40\,900\ \mathrm{yr}$, or **41 kyr**, which is the **obliquity** period. The sedimentation rate is $13.2/1.31 = 10.1\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}}$, so one cycle occupies $13.2/32 = 0.41\ \mathrm{m}$ of section — a couplet of marl and limestone 41 cm thick. The identification is secure only if the same 41-kyr period is recovered from a second proxy in the same section, because a 32-cycle succession could equally be recording 32 cycles of something else.
:::
:::

::: exercise Time-averaging {level=2 check="4300"}
A shell bed 15 cm thick accumulated at a background rate of $35\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}}$. Find its time-average in years.
::: solution
$t = 0.15/35\times10^{6} = 4300\ \mathrm{yr}$. The bed is a mixture of four millennia of shells, and any event shorter than that — a storm, a drought, a volcanic winter, the lifetime of a species' local population — is invisible in it.
:::
:::

::: exercise A correlation problem {level=3}
Three sections across a basin contain the same 15-cm K-bentonite. Section A: 60 m below the ash, a conodont *Amorphognathus* assemblage; 25 m above, a graptolite *Dictyonema* assemblage. Section B: 140 m below the ash, the same conodonts; 8 m above, the graptolites. Section C: the ash is at 200 m in the section, the conodonts are absent, and the graptolites are 90 m above. Correlate, find the three sedimentation rates, and identify which section is most complete.
::: solution
The ash is the isochronous tie, and the two fossil horizons are the second and third ties. Between the conodont and graptolite horizons the thicknesses are $60 + 25 = 85\ \mathrm{m}$ in A, $140 + 8 = 148\ \mathrm{m}$ in B, and, in C, the conodont horizon is missing so the interval cannot be measured directly; if the correlation is extended by the ash-to-graptolite distance, C gives $90\ \mathrm{m}$ against A's 25 m and B's 8 m.

Taking the biostratigraphic interval as 1.2 Myr from independent graptolite zonation, the rates are
$$
r_A = \frac{85}{1.2} = 71, \qquad r_B = \frac{148}{1.2} = 123\ \mathrm{m\,Myr^{-1}} .
$$
Section C's ash-to-graptolite distance of 90 m against A's 25 m means that C accumulated **3.6 times** as much sediment in the same time as A above the ash, so C was the most subsiding and most proximal site.

The completeness test is not the rate but the **absence of the conodonts in C**. Two readings are possible: the conodonts were never present because the facies was too deep or too dilute for them, or they were present and the horizon has been removed by an unconformity that is not visible because the lithology is uniform. The first is the more likely if the facies at C is a deep-water mudstone and the conodonts are a shallow-water fauna; the second must be tested by looking for a condensed surface, a phosphatised hardground, or a break in the conodont evolution. The honest conclusion is that **B is the most complete** of the three, because it has the thickest section between two secure ties and no missing horizon between them, and that C cannot be assessed until the conodont question is settled.
:::
:::

::: exercise A rate that is wrong {level=3}
A 900-m-thick carbonate spans the interval 466 to 462 Ma (4 Myr) and contains 22 sequence-boundary exposure surfaces. A brachiopod lineage that evolves at $2\times10^{-3}$ morphological units per kyr is measured across the formation and appears to evolve at $1.5\times10^{-4}$ units per kyr. Reconcile the two numbers.
::: solution
The apparent rate from the whole formation is
$$
\frac{900\ \mathrm{m}}{4\times10^{6\ \mathrm{yr}}} = 0.225\ \mathrm{m\,kyr^{-1}},
$$
and the observed evolutionary rate of $1.5\times10^{-4}$ against the expected $2\times10^{-3}$ is a factor of
$$
\frac{1.5\times10^{-4}}{2\times10^{-3}} = 0.075
$$
too low — the lineage appears to have evolved **thirteen times more slowly** than it should.

The reconciliation is the hiatus. If the 22 exposure surfaces each represent a hiatus, and if the total missing time is $f$ of the 4 Myr, then the **recorded** time is $(1-f)\times4\ \mathrm{Myr}$ and the evolutionary change measured across the formation happened in that shorter interval. Setting the observed rate equal to the expected rate times the recorded fraction,
$$
0.075 = 1 - f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f = 0.925,
$$
so **92.5 per cent of the interval is missing** and only 0.3 Myr of the 4 Myr is actually recorded. Each of the 22 surfaces would then represent on average $3.7/22 = 0.17\ \mathrm{Myr}$, which is entirely reasonable for a sequence boundary.

This is the Sadler result in miniature and it is the reason that rates of evolution, of extinction, of carbon-cycle perturbation and of speciation computed from the stratigraphic ranges in a shallow-marine carbonate are systematically wrong. The lineage did not evolve slowly; the rock recorded only a thirtieth of the time, and the morphological change that happened in the missing 92 per cent is simply not there. The correction is to date the section by an independent method — conodont or graptolite biozones, an isotope curve, an ash bed — and to compute the rate against the **independent** time rather than against the thickness.
:::
