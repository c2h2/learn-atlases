Nineteenths of the ocean is below the depth at which carbonate dissolves, so the carbonate factory — a set of shallow, clear, warm, oligotrophic shelves that cover four per cent of the seafloor — produces something like a quarter of all marine sediment. Carbonate rocks are also the largest single store of hydrocarbon reservoirs, the largest store of groundwater, and the only place where most of the Phanerozoic's reef fauna is preserved. They are difficult because they are made by organisms whose productivity depends on the water's chemistry, because their primary porosity is destroyed or created after deposition by groundwaters that no longer exist, and because their classification is a fight between two systems that disagree about what a rock is.

## The factory and its products

::: definition Carbonate grains and the Dunham classification {#def-dunham}
A carbonate rock is a framework of **allochems** — grains — in a matrix of **micrite** (carbonate mud finer than 4 µm) and a cement of **sparite** (coarser crystalline calcite). The allochems are **skeletal** (bioclasts: foraminifera, algae, corals, brachiopods, echinoderms, bryozoans), **non-skeletal** (ooids, intraclasts, peloids and pellets), and **terrigenous** (quartz and mica, whose presence measures the distance from land). The **Dunham classification** sorts the rock by the texture that controls its permeability: **mudstone** (less than 1 per cent grains, mud-supported), **wackestone** (grains floating in mud), **packstone** (grains in contact, mud-filled), **grainstone** (grain-supported, mud-free), **boundstone** (grains bound during growth), and **floatstone** (grains supported by framework-building organisms in a mud-poor matrix). The **Folk classification** instead names the grains and the spar or micrite, and the two are complementary: Dunham tells the reservoir engineer where the pores are and Folk tells the palaeontologist what the rock is made of.
:::

::: definition Carbonate factory types {#def-factory}
A **tropical (carbonate-sand) factory** builds in warm, shallow, clear, nutrient-poor water and is dominated by **aragonite** and **high-magnesium calcite**, the two metastable forms of calcium carbonate that dissolve faster than the low-magnesium calcite and calcite that dominate the older rocks. Its three elements are the **platform interior** (peritidal flats and lagoon), the **margin** (a reef or a sand shoal), and the **slope and basin** (periplatform muds and talus). A **cool-water factory** lives at high latitude or on a wind-driven upwelling shelf where the water is cold and nutrient-rich, is dominated by **bryozoans, bivalves, barnacles and non-reefal red algae**, produces **bryomol** and **rhodolith** gravels rather than ooids and reefs, and builds **heterozoan** or **carbonate-sand** ramps with no rim. A **mound factory** builds **microbial** and **sponge** mounds on a slope below wave base, in water too deep or too turbid for a photic reef, and it dominates several intervals of the Palaeozoic. The three are not a climatic sequence: cool-water carbonates are common in the geological record and are not, as was once thought, a sign that the Earth was colder than it is now.
:::

::: example A rock in two languages {#ex-dunham}
A thin section of 300 points gives 118 ooids, 46 skeletal fragments of red algae and foraminifera, 21 intraclasts of peloidal mudstone, 95 micrite and 20 sparry calcite cement. Classify it in both systems and interpret the environment.
::: solution
The grains total $118 + 46 + 21 = 185$ of 300, or **62 per cent**, and the micrite is 32 per cent. Because the grains are **not** in contact with one another — 62 per cent grain volume is below the about 64 per cent random-close-packing threshold, and the thin section shows micrite between most grains — the rock is **mud-supported**, and with more than 1 per cent grains it is a **wackestone** in the Dunham scheme. Had the point count given 70 per cent grains in contact, it would be a **packstone**, and with no micrite a **grainstone**.

In the Folk scheme it is a **peloid–ooid–bioclast packstone to wackestone with micrite**, and the grain names carry the environment: **ooids** require a shallow, agitated, carbonate-saturated, mud-poor water, **red-algal fragments** require light and a hard or firm substrate, **foraminifera** require normal marine salinity, and **intraclasts of peloidal mudstone** require that a mud flat existed nearby and was torn up. The micrite, at 32 per cent, means the water was **not** efficiently winnowed, so this is not a shoal.

The environment is a **shallow subtidal lagoon or a restricted platform interior**, in which ooids were supplied from a nearby shoal, algae and foraminifera lived in place, and the mud was produced in situ by microbial and algal activity and by the flushing of a peritidal flat. The **intraclasts** are the evidence that the flat was periodically emergent and its mud was periodically torn up. The rock is a **shallowing-upward** unit's base, and the ooid content should rise if the section is followed upward into the shoal.
:::
:::

## Platforms, ramps and mounds

::: definition Platform architecture {#def-platform}
A **rimmed (bounded) platform** has a margin that stands above the adjacent basin floor and separates platform facies from basin facies sharply; the Bahamas and the Adriatic are the modern examples, and most great buildups of the record are rimmed. Its zones, from the land outward, are the **peritidal** (supratidal algal laminites and dolomicrite, intertidal peloidal packstone, subtidal stromatolites), the **shallow subtidal lagoon** (peloidal packstone and wackestone with pellets), the **shoal** (ooid and oncoid grainstone), the **margin** (boundstone, or a grainstone rim if there is no reef), the **slope** (rudstone, floatstone and talus), and the **basin** (condensed micrite and periplatform wedge). A **homoclinal ramp** has no rim and no break in slope: it deepens gently and continuously into the basin, its facies zones are arranged parallel to the depth contours, and it is the architecture of cool-water and heterozoan shelves. An **isolated platform** grows on a structural high in deep water, and its vertical succession records the race between carbonate production and subsidence.
:::

::: example Keep-up, catch-up, back-step, drown {#ex-keepup}
A Middle Jurassic isolated platform is 940 m thick and its fossils indicate that its top was at sea level throughout. It was built between 168 and 160 Ma. Find the mean subsidence rate and the mean production rate, and compare them with the maximum vertical growth rate of a modern reef. Would the platform have drowned if the subsidence had been ten times faster?
::: solution
The mean subsidence, and therefore the mean rate at which the platform grew because its top stayed at sea level, is
$$
\frac{940\ \mathrm{m}}{8\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{yr}} = 1.2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} = 0.12\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
Modern reef and platform-margin production ranges from about $0.1\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ on a low-productivity platform interior to $3$–$10\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ on a high-energy framework margin, with a commonly quoted maximum near $10\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ for a healthy Holocene reef. The Jurassic platform was therefore growing at about **one hundredth of the biological maximum**, and it was **subsidence-limited**, not production-limited: the rock is a record of how fast the basin floor sank, not of how fast the organisms could build.

If the subsidence had been ten times faster, at $1.2\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, the platform would still have kept up, because that is well within the capacity of a framework margin. It would have **drowned** at a subsidence of order $10\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ sustained for longer than the millennia needed to build up to light, which is the rate found above subduction-erected terraces and above the edges of salt-withdrawal basins. The diagnostic field signature of drowning is a **sharp, condensed, often ferruginous or siliceous** surface at the top of the carbonate, overlain by **starved basinal mud or black shale**, with no transition through deeper-water facies — the platform sank faster than it could fill, and the fauna was buried in anoxia.

The four terms of the title are the vocabulary of this race. A platform **keeps up** if it produces as fast as the basin subsides; **catches up** if it was drowned, starved or back-stepped and later recovered; **back-steps** when its margin retreats landward as production fails at the deepening margin; and **drowns** when subsidence outruns production and the carbonate factory is shut down. All four are read from the vertical succession of facies and from the conodont and foraminiferal depth indices, and all four are statements about the tectonics of a basin floor that is no longer there.
:::

::: history
The classification of limestones is the celebrated quarrel of the 1950s and 1960s. William (Weller) and the American school described carbonates by their grains, and Robert Folk (Folk) codified that approach in 1959; Robert Dunham (Dunham) of the Survey of Canada proposed the textural classification of 1962 that a generation of reservoir geologists has used ever since because it sorts rocks by permeability; and the argument was never settled, because the two answer different questions. The depositional models come from the study of the modern Bahamas, beginning with the work of Shinn, Shinn and Mullins, and from the great outcrop studies of the Devonian of the Canning Basin, the Carboniferous of the Alpine Trias and the Jurassic of the Great Basin, to which Fischer (Fischer), Montaccini and Longacre (Longacre) gave the sequence-stratigraphic reading. The **Schmoker and Halley (Halley)** porosity-depth curves of 1982 were the first quantitative reservoir prediction for carbonates and are still the first thing a carbonate petrophysicist plots. The **meteoric diagenesis** of platforms was discovered in the Pleistocene reefs of Barbados by Land (Land) and by Schneider and in the Florida Keys by Ham and by Schroeder, and the **mixing-zone** model of Land and of Longacre and the **seepage-reflux** and **burial** dolomite models of Sibley and Hancock, of Choquette and Steinen, and of Machel and Mountjoy (Mountjoy) made dolomite a diagenetic problem rather than a facies. The **carbonate factory** concept, and the recognition that cool-water and heterozoan carbonates are a global and permanent feature rather than a cold-climate curiosity, is the work of James (James) and of Lees and of Mutti and Simo in the 1990s, and it overturned a hundred years of uniformitarianism-by-analogy with the Bahamas.
:::

::: example How wide is a tidal flat? {#ex-tidalwidth}
A rimmed platform's interior has a subtidal profile slope of 1 : 2000 (0.5 m of depth per kilometre). Find the width of its intertidal zone for tidal ranges of 1.5 m, 3 m and 10 m, and say what the facies are in each case.
::: solution
The intertidal width is the tidal range divided by the slope,
$$
w = \frac{\Delta h}{s}, \qquad s = 5\times10^{-4},
$$
so
$$
\Delta h = 1.5\ \mathrm{m}:\ w = 3\ \mathrm{km}; \qquad
\Delta h = 3\ \mathrm{m}:\ w = 6\ \mathrm{km}; \qquad
\Delta h = 10\ \mathrm{m}:\ w = 20\ \mathrm{km}.
$$
At a microtidal range of 1.5 m the intertidal zone is a 3-km band, the platform interior is dominated by **subtidal peloidal packstone and wackestone**, and the peritidal facies — algal laminites, desiccation-cracked dolomicrite, bird's-eye and fenestral fabric — are thin and restricted to the landward margin. At a macrotidal range of 10 m the intertidal zone is 20 km wide, and the platform interior is **dominated** by peritidal and intertidal facies: extensive algal bindstones, tidal-flat dolomicrites, and a mosaic of sand-flat, mud-flat and channelling that produces the **heterolithic, flaser-bedded, bioturbated** peritidal facies of the macrotidal platform.

The consequence for interpretation is that the **relative thickness of the peritidal facies in a platform interior is a measurement of the palaeotidal range**, provided the profile slope can be constrained from the coeval margin. This is the basis of the tidal-range estimates made for ancient platforms, and it is one of the few ways to measure a Palaeozoic tide. The caution is that the profile slope is not constant: a platform with an abundant mud supply has a flatter profile and a wider intertidal zone at the same tidal range, and a platform swept by waves has a steeper profile and a narrower one. The estimate is therefore good to a factor of two, which is enough to distinguish a microtidal from a macrotidal coast — the distinction that controls the whole architecture of the platform interior.
:::
:::

## What happens after deposition

::: definition Diagenetic settings and porosity {#def-diagenesis}
A carbonate's post-depositional history is written in its cements and is controlled by the **fluid** that made them. **Marine** cementation occurs on the seafloor and in the shallow marine burial environment, produces **fibrous and pendulous** aragonite and high-magnesium calcite cements that line pores without filling them, and forms a hardground. **Meteoric** diagenesis occurs when a platform is exposed, and it is the most important: fresh, CO$_2$-rich groundwaters dissolve aragonite and high-magnesium calcite and precipitate **low-magnesium calcite**, producing **pendant and meniscus** cements, **calcrete** palaeosols, **karst** and **vuggy and moldic porosity**. **Mixing-zone** diagenesis at the fresh–salt interface precipitates **equant spar** that fills rather than lines pores, and it is the classic cause of porosity destruction in a rimmed platform's flank. **Burial** diagenesis produces **equant, non-ferroan and then ferroan, dolomitic** calcite and **dolomite**, and it is the setting of the deep, hot, dolomitising and hydrothermal fluids that create the best carbonate reservoirs and the worst drilling kicks.
:::

::: example Reading a cement stratigraphy {#ex-cements}
A grainstone shows, in order: (1) fibrous aragonite cement lining pores; (2) blocky low-magnesium calcite cement filling pores, with **pendant** cements hanging from grains; (3) equant ferroan dolomite rhombs and saddle dolomite in residual pores; and (4) stylolites and a dissolution seam. Reconstruct the history and say where the porosity went.
::: solution
**(1)** The fibrous aragonite is a **marine** cement, precipitated on the seafloor or in the shallow marine burial environment. It **lined** the pores without filling them, so it left the porosity intact and reduced the permeability by coating the grains.
**(2)** The blocky low-magnesium calcite with **pendant** cements is the diagnostic **meteoric** cement, and the pendant form proves that the cement grew downward from a **freshwater meniscus** in the vadose zone. The platform was therefore **exposed**, and the same episode dissolved the aragonite shells and the high-magnesium cements, creating **moldic and vuggy porosity** while simultaneously filling other pores with spar. The net effect on porosity is unpredictable from the cement alone and must be measured.
**(3)** The **ferroan** dolomite and **saddle** dolomite are **burial and hydrothermal** cements. Iron in the cement means a reducing, organic-rich, warm fluid; the saddle habit means a crystallisation temperature above about 60 $^\circ$C. This is the stage at which the last primary porosity was filled, and it is also the stage at which **dolomitisation** may have created secondary porosity by the volume reduction that accompanies the replacement of calcite by dolomite.
**(4)** The **stylolites** record **chemical compaction** at effective stresses of tens of megapascals, at burial depths of hundreds of metres to a kilometre, with the dissolved calcium carbonate precipitated as the calcite seam and the insoluble clay concentrated along it.

The porosity went in three places. It was **reduced** by the marine lining cement, by the meteoric blocky spar, by the burial and hydrothermal spar, and by the pressure solution at the stylolites; it was **created** by the meteoric dissolution of aragonite and high-magnesium calcite and, possibly, by dolomitisation; and it was **preserved** wherever the cements failed to connect. The net is what a core or a log measures, and the cement paragenesis is the only way to explain whether a given sample's porosity is primary, secondary or a residue — which is the difference between a reservoir that can be predicted from its facies and one that cannot.
:::
:::

::: warning Carbonate rocks record their own destruction, and the record is not the deposition
Two-thirds of the primary porosity of a carbonate and all of its most important secondary porosity were created **after** deposition, by fluids whose distribution was controlled by a palaeogeography that no longer exists. Three consequences follow. **The reservoir is not the facies**: the best porosity in a rimmed platform is commonly in the **meteoric dissolution zone** beneath a palaeo-highstand surface, not in the shoal that supplied the grains, and the flow that made it followed a water table that existed for a few tens of thousands of years at a sea level that has since moved. **The dolomite is not a facies**: dolomite is a diagenetic product, and a dolostone's texture may preserve the original facies faithfully (a **dolomudstone** that was a mud flat) or may destroy it completely (a **saddle-dolomite** replacement that has no relationship to anything that was deposited). **And the classification is a compromise**: a Dunham **grainstone** and a Folk **ooid grainstone with spar** are the same rock described for two purposes, and a rock that is a packstone in one part of a bed and a grainstone in another is one facies with a textural gradient, not two facies. The discipline is to describe the rock twice — once for the depositional texture and once for the diagenetic overprint — and to state which of the two is being used when an environment is inferred.
:::

::: quiz
A limestone shows pendant calcite cements, moldic porosity from dissolved shells, and a calcrete palaeosol at its top. What is the environment of the diagenesis?
- [ ] Marine burial at 2 km depth.
- [x] A meteoric, vadose and phreatic environment beneath an exposure surface.
- [ ] A hydrothermal, deeply buried, reducing fluid.
- [ ] Early marine cementation on the seafloor.
::: solution
Pendant cements grow downward from a freshwater meniscus and form only in the **vadose zone** of a subaerially exposed carbonate; moldic porosity from the dissolution of aragonite and high-magnesium calcite requires the CO$_2$-rich acidic water of a **freshwater** lens; and a calcrete is a soil. A hydrothermal cement would be **saddle dolomite** or **equant ferroan calcite**, and an early marine cement would be **fibrous** and would **line** rather than fill the pores.
:::
:::

## Where this leads

The classification and the depositional textures are applied to the environments in [[sedimentology/marine-environments]] and [[sedimentology/facies]], the cements' chemistry is in [[geochemistry/stable-isotopes]] and [[geochemistry/trace-elements]], the seawater chemistry that controls the mineralogy is in [[oceanography/seawater]] and [[geochemistry/aqueous-geochemistry]], the subsidence that the platform races against is in [[geophysics/flexure]] and [[sedimentology/basins-sequences]], and the dolomitising fluids are in [[hydrology/groundwater-flow]] and [[geochemistry/aqueous-geochemistry]].

::: summary
- The carbonate factory is small, shallow and productive, and it is dominated by metastable aragonite and high-magnesium calcite in the tropics and by bryozoans and bivalves in cool water.
- The Dunham classification sorts a carbonate by its depositional texture and therefore its permeability; the Folk classification names its grains.
- A rimmed platform has a margin, a zoned interior and a slope; a ramp has neither margin nor break in slope; a mound grows below wave base.
- A platform's thickness records the race between production, which can reach 10 mm/yr, and subsidence, which is usually far slower.
- Most carbonate porosity is diagenetic, and the meteoric dissolution zone beneath a palaeo-exposure surface is commonly the best reservoir.
- Dolomite is a diagenetic product, not a facies, and a dolostone's texture may or may not record the original sediment.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Dunham classification {level=1}
A limestone contains 15 per cent skeletal grains scattered in 85 per cent micrite. Name it.
::: solution
A **wackestone**: more than 1 per cent grains, mud-supported, with the grains not in contact. With less than 1 per cent grains it would be a **mudstone**, and with the grains in contact and mud filling the pores it would be a **packstone**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Grain type {level=1}
A limestone is composed almost entirely of concentric coated grains with a 20-µm nucleus. Name the grain and the environment.
::: solution
**Ooids** (oöids), and the environment is a **shallow, agitated, carbonate-saturated, mud-poor shelf** — the modern examples being the ooid shoals of the Bahamas, Shark Bay and the Persian Bank. Ooids require wave or current agitation, supersaturation with respect to calcium carbonate, and a scarcity of mud, so an ooid grainstone is a **high-energy, shallow, clear-water** facies and a sequence-stratigraphic marker of a shallowening-upward trend.
:::
:::

::: exercise Porosity and depth {level=1 check="0.15"}
Using $\phi = 0.46\,e^{-z/445}$ for a rimmed platform, find the interparticle porosity at 500 m.
::: solution
$\phi = 0.46\,e^{-500/445} = 0.46\times0.326 = 0.15$, or 15 per cent. This is the depth at which a rimmed-platform carbonate stops being a good interparticle-porosity reservoir unless the porosity is secondary.
:::
:::

::: exercise Porosity and depth, ramp {level=2 check="0.24"}
Using $\phi = 0.61\,e^{-z/1049}$ for a non-rimmed platform, find the porosity at 1000 m and compare it with the rimmed-platform value at the same depth.
::: solution
$\phi = 0.61\,e^{-1000/1049} = 0.61\times0.386 = 0.24$, or 24 per cent, against 4.9 per cent for the rimmed platform. The ramp carbonate retains **five times** the porosity at the same depth, which is why many of the world's great carbonate reservoirs — the Carboniferous of the North Sea, the Jurassic of the Middle East's ramps, the Cretaceous of West Africa — are mud-rich, cool-water or heterozoan carbonates rather than clean platform-margin grainstones.
:::
:::

::: exercise Factory type {level=2}
A Cretaceous shelf carbonate contains bryozoan and bivalve hash, rhodoliths, no ooids, no corals, and 20 per cent glauconite, and its bioclasts are low-magnesium calcite. Which factory, and what does it say about the water?
::: solution
A **cool-water (heterozoan) factory**. The evidence is the dominance of bryozoans and bivalves over corals and algae, the presence of **rhodoliths** (non-spherical nodular coralline algae, which are common in cool, nutrient-rich, well-illuminated water), the **absence of ooids and corals**, the **glauconite** (which forms slowly on a low-sedimentation-rate shelf, commonly under an oxygen-minimum or upwelling regime), and the **low-magnesium calcite** mineralogy of the bioclasts, which is characteristic of cool-water biota.

The water was **cool, nutrient-rich, well-mixed, and probably subject to wind-driven upwelling**, and the shelf was **not** a rimmed tropical platform. The exploration consequence is that the reservoir is a **bryomol or rhodolith gravel** with interparticle and intraparticle moldic porosity, arranged as **shore-parallel belts** on a ramp rather than as a rimmed-platform margin, and that the seal is the upshelf mud rather than a platform-interior dolomicrite.
:::
:::

::: exercise Drowning {level=2}
A platform carbonate is overlain sharply by 20 cm of phosphatic hardground with reworked ooids and then by 40 m of black laminated shale. Interpret, and say what the alternative would look like.
::: solution
This is a **drowning** or **starvation** surface. The hardground with **reworked ooids** is a transgressive or condensation surface cut on the platform top, the phosphorite marks a period of sediment **starvation**, and the black laminated shale records the arrival of **anoxic bottom water** at a depth the carbonate factory could not keep up with. The platform sank, or the sea rose, faster than the factory could fill, the carbonate stopped, and the basin became anoxic.

The alternative — a **gradual deepening** — would show a transition through deeper-water carbonate facies: the peritidal and shoal facies passing up into open-shelf packstone and wackestone with a progressively deeper-water fauna, then into marl and finally into shale, over tens of metres and with no hardground. The absence of that transition, and the presence of a condensed phosphatic surface, is what makes the drowning interpretation, and it is the field signature of a **rapid** change — an earthquake-triggered subsidence event, the onset of an oceanic anoxic event, or the flooding of a rifted margin.
:::
:::

::: exercise A platform synthesis {level=3}
A 1200-m-thick Devonian platform shows, from the base: 200 m of basinal cherty mudstone with conodonts; 60 m of algal bindstone with stromatolites; 700 m of peritidal dolomicrite and peloidal packstone with 40 exposure surfaces bearing palaeokarst; 200 m of ooid grainstone with 15-m-deep tidal channels; and 40 m of basinal mudstone with a phosphatised hardground. Interpret the whole and estimate the subsidence rate if the platform existed for 12 Myr.
::: solution
The **base** is the starved, condensed, cherty **basin** on which the platform nucleated — the chert and the conodonts say a silica-rich, sediment-starved, pelagic setting. The **algal bindstone with stromatolites** is the **nucleation and establishment** stage: a microbial flat that trapped and bound sediment on a shallow bank and, because microbial binding is the only way to build in a high-energy setting without a skeletal framework, it is the classic Early Devonian factory — there were no reef-building corals yet. The **700 m of peritidal dolomicrite and packstone with 40 exposure surfaces** is a **mature rimmed platform interior** that was repeatedly emergent, and the 40 palaeokarst surfaces are 40 falls of relative sea level, each one a **sequence boundary** and each one a potential zone of meteoric dissolution porosity. The **200 m of ooid grainstone with 15-m tidal channels** is the **shoal and its tidal inlets**, and its arrival marks a change to a higher-energy, more open, more swept platform. The **top** is the drowning surface and the return of the basin.

The mean subsidence is $1200/12\times10^{6} = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} = 0.10\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, comfortably below the Devonian platform's production capacity, so the platform was **subsidence-limited** throughout and its 1200 m is a record of the basin floor's cooling and loading. The 40 exposure surfaces give a mean spacing of $12/40 = 0.3\ \mathrm{Myr}$ per cycle, which is a **fourth-order or eccentricity-driven** cyclicity, and the fact that the cycles are peritidal rather than deepening-upward means the platform stood at sea level and the cycles are **shallowing-upward** — the signature of a sediment-supplied, low-subsidence, low-relief platform.

The weakest link is the interpretation of the **dolomicrite**. A dolomicrite may be a primary dolomite precipitated in a hypersaline lagoon, in which case the platform interior was **restricted and evaporative**, or it may be a **secondary** replacement of an original lime mudstone by a later burial or seepage-reflux dolomite, in which case the original water may have been normal marine and the restriction is an artefact. The two are distinguished by the **relict texture**, by the ordering of the dolomite and its replacement relationships to the early cements, and by the isotopes and the trace elements of the dolomite itself — and the answer changes the interpretation of the whole 700 m.
:::
:::

::: exercise A reservoir prediction {level=3}
Two carbonate reservoirs at the same depth of 2500 m have the same grainstone facies and the same ooid content. Reservoir X has 4 per cent porosity and is homogeneous; reservoir Y has 14 per cent porosity, is vuggy, and contains moldic grains and 2-m calcite-filled caves. Explain the difference and say how you would map reservoir Y.
::: solution
Reservoir X has followed the **compaction and burial cementation** path: its primary interparticle porosity was progressively filled by marine, burial and pressure-solution cements, and 4 per cent is what the exponential porosity-depth curve predicts at 2500 m for a shallow-marine carbonate. Reservoir Y has an additional **secondary porosity** of ten porosity units, and its three features identify the process: **moldic grains** are dissolved aragonite or high-magnesium calcite, **vugs** are the dissolution cavities of a meteoric or burial fluid, and **calcite-filled caves** are the conduits of a **karst** system.

Reservoir Y was therefore **exposed** and flushed by meteoric water, or was dissolved by a **burial or hydrothermal** fluid, and its porosity is not a function of its facies but of its position relative to a **palaeo-water table or a fault**. The mapping consequence is that Y cannot be predicted from the grainstone facies map. It must be mapped from the surfaces and the conduits that controlled the dissolution: the **palaeo-exposure surface** (a sequence boundary, mapped seismically as an erosional truncation and a channelised top), the **karst** (mapped as collapse features, as a velocity drop, and as a seismic "pipe" or "sinkhole" fabric), the **faults and fractures** that channelled the fluid, and the **caves** themselves, which are the extreme case and the drilling hazard.

The prediction test is the **moldic content against depth below the exposure surface**: meteoric dissolution is strongest in the **freshwater phreatic and vadose zones**, tens to a few hundred metres below the palaeo-surface, and it fades with depth below the surface as the fluid becomes saturated and saline. A plot of porosity against depth below the sequence boundary, made in every well, is the map of reservoir Y, and if the trend is instead aligned with a fault, the dissolution was structural and the reservoir is a **fracture-cave** system whose sweet spots are on the fault damage zones.
:::
:::
