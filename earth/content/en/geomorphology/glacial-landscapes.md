Ice sheets and glaciers move less than a tenth of the land surface today, but they have shaped perhaps a third of it, and during the Pleistocene they moved up to thirty times the sediment that all the world's rivers carry. They do their work at the base, in a thin layer of deforming till and pressurised water that is one of the least accessible and most consequential environments on Earth, and they leave a land surface that is unmistakable and, until the last forty years, almost entirely unread.

## Erosion by ice

::: definition Abrasion and quarrying {#def-erosion-processes}
**Abrasion** is the cutting of the bed by rock fragments dragged through the till or held in the ice, and it produces **striations** (grooves tens of microns to centimetres deep, whose orientation gives the flow direction), **polished and faceted** surfaces, and **chatter marks** (crescentic pits whose horns point down-flow). **Quarrying** (plucking, tearing) is the fracture and removal of blocks at the bed, and it produces the **stoss-and-stail** form of a **roche moutonnée**: a gently abraded and striated stoss side where the ice slid over, and a steep, plucked, irregular stail side where the ice left the rock. Quarrying requires **meltwater at or above the overburden pressure** at the bed, which opens the joints by hydraulic jacking and lifts the ice, and it is therefore concentrated where the bed is soft, the effective pressure is low, and the ice is warm and wet-based. Cold-based, frozen ice neither abrades nor quarries, and the **thermal regime of the bed** is therefore the first control on where a glacier erodes.
:::

::: definition Basal sliding and effective pressure {#def-sliding}
A temperate glacier slides over its bed when the **basal shear stress**
$$
\tau_b = \rho_i\, g\, H \sin\alpha
$$
reaches 50–150 kPa, which is the strength of most crystalline and many sedimentary rocks. Sliding is resisted by the **effective pressure** $N = P_i - P_w$ — the ice's overburden pressure minus the water pressure at the bed — and the sliding law takes the form
$$
u_b = C\,\frac{\tau_b^{\,n}}{N^{\,m}},
$$
with $n \approx 1$–$3$ and $m \approx 1$. The crucial consequence is that a **high water pressure promotes sliding**: a glacier whose bed becomes water-filled slides faster, thins, and loses mass faster, which is why the marine-terminating glaciers of Greenland and Antarctica are sensitive to surface melt in a way that a dry-friction model cannot explain. On a **deformable bed** the ice may barely slide at all; instead the till itself shears over a thickness of 0.1–1 m, and the glacier's basal motion is the till's viscous flow.
:::

::: example The stress at the bed {#ex-basal}
A glacier 300 m thick flows down a surface slope of $1.2^\circ$ with a basal water pressure equal to 85 per cent of the ice's overburden pressure. Find the basal shear stress, the effective pressure, and the factor by which the sliding speed would rise if the water pressure rose to 95 per cent of the overburden, taking $m = 1$.
::: solution
$$
\tau_b = 900\times9.81\times300\times\sin 1.2^\circ = 900\times9.81\times300\times0.02094 = 5.5\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{Pa} = 55\ \mathrm{kPa},
$$
$$
P_i = \rho_i g H = 900\times9.81\times300 = 2.65\ \mathrm{MPa}, \qquad
N = (1 - 0.85)\times2.65 = 0.40\ \mathrm{MPa}.
$$
At 95 per cent water pressure the effective pressure falls to $0.05\times2.65 = 0.13\ \mathrm{MPa}$, a factor of three lower, and with $m = 1$ the sliding speed rises by
$$
\frac{u_2}{u_1} = \frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{0.40}{0.13} = 3.0 .
$$
A ten-per-cent rise in basal water pressure **triples the glacier's velocity**. This is the physical basis of the observed speed-ups of Greenland's outlet glaciers after a surface-melt event, of the seasonal velocity oscillations of Alpine and Antarctic outlet glaciers, and of the hypothesis that a warming climate can accelerate ice discharge not by melting the ice but by **lubricating its bed**.

The 55 kPa basal stress is at the low end of the 50–150 kPa range, which tells us that this glacier is not limited by the bed's strength but by its own flux — a **fast-flowing** ice stream rather than a steep alpine glacier.
:::
:::

## The sculpted surface

::: definition Glacial landforms of erosion {#def-erosional-forms}
A **cirque** (corrie, cwm) is a steep-headed, over-deepened basin at the head of a glacier, formed by a combination of rotational slip, quarrying at the headwall and meltwater erosion, and its headwall retreat leaves an **arête** between two cirques and a **horn** (a pyramidal peak) between three or more. A **glacial trough** (U-valley) is a valley whose cross-section is a blunt U with a flat floor and steep walls, produced when a glacier widens, deepens and straightens a pre-existing V; its diagnostic features are the **truncated** spur, the **hanging valley** whose stream drops to the trunk, the **overdeepening** below sea level, and the **rock bar** at the mouth. A **fjord** is a glacial trough flooded by the sea, commonly overdeepened to more than a kilometre below sea level and closed by a **sill** of moraine at the mouth. A **paternoster lake** is one of a chain of overdeepened basins along a trough. The **glacial buzzsaw** is the observed **elevation clamp** on mountain peaks near the equilibrium-line altitude of the former ice, which limits the height of ranges that were glaciated in the Pleistocene and is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that glaciers, not rivers, set the relief of glaciated mountains.
:::

::: example Is the buzzsaw real? {#ex-buzzsaw}
A mountain range has a mean crest elevation of 2100 m and a range of peak elevations from 1900 to 2400 m. The Pleistocene equilibrium-line altitude (ELA) is reconstructed at 1850 m, and the range's highest peaks lie in the same latitude belt as three other ranges with ELAs of 1200, 1600 and 2500 m and mean crest elevations of 1450, 1900 and 2750 m. Test the buzzsaw hypothesis and estimate the erosion rate implied.
::: solution
The ratio of mean crest elevation to ELA in the four ranges is
$$
\frac{2100}{1850} = 1.14, \quad
\frac{1450}{1200} = 1.21, \quad
\frac{1900}{1600} = 1.19, \quad
\frac{2750}{2500} = 1.10,
$$
a mean of 1.16 with a scatter of only five per cent across a factor of two in absolute elevation. The correlation is striking and it is the buzzsaw result: **a glaciated range's peaks stand a fixed amount above the Pleistocene snowline**, because ice erodes most efficiently where it is thickest and fastest, which is near the ELA, and because a peak raised above the ELA by tectonics is sawn back down.

The implied erosion rate follows from the amount of rock removed. If the range was uplifted 2500 m in 2 Myr — 1.25 mm/yr — and its peaks stand at 2100 m, then about 400 m of relief has been removed, an average of
$$
\frac{400}{2\times10^{6}} = 0.2\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
This is a typical glacial erosion rate and it is comfortably below the tectonic rate, which is why the range is still rising.

The alternative explanation, which cannot be excluded by the elevation data alone, is **climatic**: the ELA and the crest elevation may both be set by the same third variable — the temperature and precipitation field that determines where the rock is strong, where frost works, and where debris can be transported. Shuster, Tucker and the modelling school have shown that a **rock-uplift and climate** model without any special glacial efficiency reproduces much of the pattern, and the discriminating observation is the **cosmogenic erosion rate as a function of elevation within a single range**: the buzzsaw predicts a maximum in glacial erosion at the ELA and a decline above and below it, and this has been found in some ranges and not in others. The question is open, and it is one of the most productive in the subject.
:::
:::

::: history
The theory that ice sculpted the land is one of the geosciences' great arguments. De Charpentier (Charpentier) proposed it in 1834 for the Alpine erratics, Venetz, de Saussure and Charpentier's colleague L. de Charpentier had argued the same in the 1820s, and **Agassiz** (Agassiz) made it a global theory in his 1837 paper before the Société Helvétique and his 1840 *Études sur les glaciers* — against the opposition of Lyell, who preferred drifting icebergs and catastrophic floods, and of the English geological establishment, which found the idea un-English. The evidence accumulated through the second half of the nineteenth century from the glacial striae and tillites of the English Midlands, the Lake District and Scotland, and the Pleistocene stratigraphy of the Alps was fixed by Brückner's (Brückner) 1901 correlation of the Alpine glacial stages with the Milankovitch orbital cycles. The **drumlin** problem was opened by Miller's (Miller) 1842 list of Highland landforms and has never closed; the **glacial trough** debate between the subaerial-school of Johnson and Davis and the glacial-school of Chamberlain and Salisbury ran from the 1880s to the 1930s and was settled by the discovery of overdeepening on the continental shelf. The **basal sliding law** is the work of Weertman (Weertman) in 1957, of Weertman's sliding and the till-deformation revolution of Alley, Blankenship, Bentley and Eccrat (Alley) in 1987, and of the cavity-water and effective-pressure synthesis of Iverson, Iverson, Engelhardt, Humphrey and others (Humphrey) in the 1990s. The **glacial buzzsaw** is the name given by Brooker and by Shuster, van der Woerd, Tucker, and by Egholm, Nielsen, Pedersen, Lesemann and Jenson (Jenson) in 2009 to the elevation clamp, and the **glacial-intensification** of erosion is the synthesis of Harris, of Herman, Hallet, Densmore,ufe and the marine-sediment school.
:::

::: example From a trimline to an ice sheet {#ex-trimline}
A **trimline** — the sharp vegetational and geomorphological break between ice-scoured and periglacially weathered slopes — is mapped at 850 m on the flank of a mountain that rises to 1150 m. The bedrock at the trimline's position is at 600 m, the trimline lies 60 km up-ice from a reconstructed ice divide, and the ice-surface slope between them is 0.004. Find the ice thickness at the trimline and at the divide, taking the divide's bedrock elevation as 300 m.
::: solution
The trimline marks the **former ice surface**, so at the trimline the surface stood at 850 m and the ice was
$$
850 - 600 = 250\ \mathrm{m}\ \text{thick}.
$$
Sixty kilometres up-ice the surface stood higher by $60\,000\times0.004 = 240\ \mathrm{m}$, at
$$
850 + 240 = 1090\ \mathrm{m},
$$
and over a bed at 300 m the ice was therefore
$$
1090 - 300 = 790\ \mathrm{m}\ \text{thick}.
$$
The mountain's summit at 1150 m stood 60 m **above** the ice surface at the divide and was an **nunatak**, and the trimline at 850 m is the evidence that the whole mountain flank below it was overridden — which means that a landscape that looks periglacial above the line and glacial below it records a single event, the thinning of an ice sheet that once covered the summit region.

Three cautions apply. A trimline may record a **change in the style of weathering** rather than the ice surface itself — for example the limit of wind-thrown sand or of a particular frost regime — and the test is whether the surface below the line carries glacial striae, roches moutonnées and till, and the surface above carries blockfields and sorted stone stripes. A trimline may be **diachronous**, recording the last of several readvances rather than the maximum extent. And the ice-surface slope of 0.004 is an **assumption**, usually taken from a flow model or from the orientation of a fabric; if the true slope were 0.002 the divide's surface would stand only 120 m higher and the ice 670 m thick. The method's real product is therefore the **shape** of the ice surface rather than its absolute thickness, and a map of trimlines at many localities, corrected for the post-glacial rebound that has tilted them, is one of the few direct constraints on the profile of a vanished ice sheet.
:::
:::

## Deposition and deglaciation

::: definition Glacial depositional landforms {#def-depositional}
A **till** (diamicton) is unsorted, unstratified, matrix-supported sediment deposited directly by ice, and its **fabric** — the orientation and imbrication of its clasts, the **a-axis fabric** — records the deformation that formed it: a strong, clustered, upstream-dipping a-fabric indicates subglacial deformation and a weak or random fabric indicates melt-out or supraglacial origin. A **moraine** is a ridge of till: **lateral** on the ice margin's side, **medial** where two glaciers meet, **terminal** or **recessional** at a stillstand, and **ground** (ablation till, boulders) on the ice's base. A **drumlin** is a streamlined, elongate, often swarms-forming hill of till or deformed bed, whose long axis is parallel to the ice flow and whose stoss end points upstream; its origin is unresolved after 170 years of argument. A **kame**, **kettle** and **esker** are the deposits of **dead ice**: a kame is a mound of stratified meltwater sediment let down into a cavity against stagnant ice, a kettle is the pit left when the ice melts out, and an esker is the sinuous ridge of sand and gravel deposited in a subglacial or englacial meltwater conduit. An **outwash plain** (sandur, outwash) is the braided-fluvial plain beyond the ice margin, and its far end is where the **varved clay** and the **proglacial lake** deposits pass into the marine or lacustrine sequence.
:::

::: example A glacier's sediment budget {#ex-sedbudget}
A glacierised catchment of $1200\ \mathrm{km^2}$ delivers meltwater at $60\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$ with a suspended-sediment concentration of $1.2\ \mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ and a dissolved load of $180\ \mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$. Find the total denudation rate, and compare it with the global average for unglaciated mountains.
::: solution
The suspended flux is
$$
60\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{L\,s^{-1}}\times1.2\ \mathrm{g\,L^{-1}} = 7.2\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} = 2.3\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,yr^{-1}} = 2.3\ \mathrm{Mt\,yr^{-1}},
$$
and the dissolved flux is $60\times10^{3}\times0.18 = 1.1\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} = 0.34\ \mathrm{Mt\,yr^{-1}}$, so the total is $2.6\ \mathrm{Mt\,yr^{-1}}$. Over $1.2\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ this is
$$
\frac{2.6\times10^{12}}{1.2\times10^{9}\times2700} = 8.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} = 0.80\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
The **dissolved load is only 13 per cent** of the total, against 20–40 per cent in an unglaciated catchment, because a glacier's basal grinding produces rock flour faster than the cold, short-residence-time subglacial water can dissolve it.

A denudation rate of 0.8 mm/yr is **three to eight times** the global average for unglaciated mountains of similar relief, and it is at the low end of the measured range for glaciated catchments, which reach 2–10 mm/yr in the Alps, Norway, Alaska and the Southern Alps of New Zealand. The comparison is the reason that the Pleistocene glaciations are thought to have **increased** the global denudation rate rather than to have blanketed and protected the continents, and it is the reason that the marine sediment record shows a marked increase in the sediment flux to the North Atlantic, the Bay of Biscay and the Gulf of Alaska from about 2.8 Ma — the onset of Northern Hemisphere glaciation.
:::
:::

The glacial profile is a **blunt U** with a flat floor and steep walls, and the difference is diagnostic in the field: a V-valley's walls meet at an angle at the stream and its cross-section area grows as the square of the height, while a U-valley has a floor of nearly constant width and its area grows linearly with the height. ::: warning A glacial landscape is a palimpsest, and the last ice did not write all of it
A formerly glaciated surface carries the forms of **several** glaciations, and the landforms of the last one overprint rather than erase those of the earlier ones. Three consequences follow. **The landforms are not all the same age**: a Norwegian or Canadian landscape may carry a Pleistocene cirque, a Younger Dryas recessional moraine, a Pre-Boreal shoreline and a twentieth-century neoglacial moraine within a few hundred metres, and the mapping unit must be the **land system** — the genetically coherent assemblage of forms — rather than the individual form. **The sediments are reworked**: a "till" may be a reworked till, a diamictite may be a debris flow, and a drumlin swarm may be a swarm of collapsed dead-ice mounds rather than subglacial streams of flow; the fabric, the stratigraphy inside the body, and the presence of a buried soil are the only discriminators. **And the erosion rates are not steady**: a glacier's erosion is concentrated in its **surge** and **streaming** phases and in the **deglacial** period when the bed is water-saturated and the sediment store is empty, so a cosmogenic or a sediment-flux estimate averages a series of episodes rather than measuring a rate. The discipline is to map the **styles of deglaciation** — the ice-marginal, the supraglacial, the subglacial, the proglacial-lacustrine and the ice-proximal fan — before assigning any form to any process, and to date at least one element of every land system.
:::

::: quiz
A landscape shows striated and polished bedrock, a roche moutonnée with its plucked face to the south, a U-valley, and no till. What can you conclude?
- [x] Warm-based ice flowed north to south over the site.
- [ ] The ice was cold-based.
- [ ] The valley was cut by a river and later glaciated.
- [ ] The till was deposited by the same ice that cut the striations.
::: solution
Striations and polishing are abrasion products and quarrying produces the plucked stail, so the ice was **warm-based and wet-based**, not frozen to its bed. The plucked face points **down-ice**, so the flow was toward the south. The absence of till says only that the till was removed by later meltwater or was never deposited there — an erosional landscape need not carry its own depositional record.
:::

## Where this leads

The ice physics is in [[glaciology/ice-flow]] and [[glaciology/basal-sliding]], the mass balance that sets the ELA is in [[glaciology/mass-balance]], the sediments these landforms become are in [[sedimentology/continental-environments]], the varves and the proglacial lakes are in [[sedimentology/stratigraphy]] and [[palaeoclimate/dating]], the ice-sheet reconstructions are in [[glaciology/ice-sheets]], and the sea-level consequence is in [[climate-change/sea-level-rise]].

::: summary
- Glaciers erode by abrasion and quarrying, both of which require meltwater at the bed, so the thermal regime controls where erosion happens.
- Basal sliding is controlled by the effective pressure, and a ten-per-cent rise in basal water pressure can triple the sliding speed.
- A glacial trough is a blunt U with truncated spurs, hanging valleys and overdeepenings, and its form distinguishes it from a fluvial V.
- The glacial buzzsaw clamps glaciated peaks near the Pleistocene snowline, and the effect is visible across ranges whose ELAs differ by a factor of two.
- Glaciated catchments export 3–8 times the sediment of unglaciated ones, with a much smaller dissolved fraction.
- A glacial landscape is a palimpsest of several ice advances, and it must be mapped as a land system and dated.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Basal stress {level=1 check="123"}
Find the basal shear stress of a glacier 400 m thick on a slope of $2^\circ$.
::: solution
$\tau_b = 900\times9.81\times400\times\sin 2^\circ = 900\times9.81\times400\times0.0349 = 1.2\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa} = 123\ \mathrm{kPa}$. This is near the upper end of the range, so the glacier is limited by the strength of its bed and is likely to be a steep alpine glacier or an ice-stream margin.
:::
:::

::: exercise Effective pressure {level=1 check="0.35"}
A glacier 200 m thick has a basal water pressure of 80 per cent of the ice overburden. Find the effective pressure in MPa.
::: solution
$P_i = 900\times9.81\times200 = 1.77\ \mathrm{MPa}$, and $N = 0.2\times1.77 = 0.35\ \mathrm{MPa}$. A low effective pressure means a weak bed, easy sliding, and a high susceptibility to quarrying.
:::
:::

::: exercise Landform identification {level=1}
A ridge of unsorted, unstratified sandy clay with faceted, striated and imbricated limestone and granite pebbles crosses a valley floor transversely. Name it.
::: solution
A **terminal or recessional ground moraine** — a ridge of **till**. The unsorted and unstratified matrix-supported texture, the faceted and striated clasts, and the imbrication are the diagnostic criteria, and the transverse position across the valley marks a stillstand of the ice margin.
:::
:::

::: exercise Sliding response {level=2 check="2.0"}
Using $u_b \propto \tau_b^3/N$, find the factor by which sliding speeds rise if the basal shear stress rises 25 per cent while $N$ is unchanged.
::: solution
$1.25^3 = 1.95$, about a doubling. The nonlinear Weertman law means that a modest thickening or steepening of the ice — which raises $\tau_b$ — produces a large increase in sliding, and this is one of the two positive feedbacks behind a glacier surge; the other is the till's **strain-weakening**, in which the deforming bed loses strength as it deforms.
:::
:::

::: exercise Glacial denudation {level=2 check="2.2"}
A glacierised catchment of $800\ \mathrm{km^2}$ delivers $100\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$ of meltwater with $1.5\ \mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ of suspended sediment. Find the denudation rate in mm/yr, taking a rock density of $2700\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$.
::: solution
The flux is $10^5\ \mathrm{L\,s^{-1}}\times1.5 = 1.5\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} = 4.7\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,yr^{-1}} = 4.7\ \mathrm{Mt\,yr^{-1}}$, and
$$
E = \frac{4.7\times10^{12}}{8\times10^{8}\times2700} = 2.2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} = 2.2\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
This is a fast-eroding glacierised catchment, comparable to the Southern Alps of New Zealand and the Gulf of Alaska.
:::
:::

::: exercise Overdeepening {level=2}
A fjord is 900 m below sea level, has a sill at its mouth at 150 m below sea level, and its trough walls rise to 1200 m above sea level. What does the overdeepening and the sill tell you about the glacier and about the sea-level history?
::: solution
The glacier that cut the trough was **at least 1050 m thick** at the point of maximum overdeepening (900 m below sea level plus the 150 m of rock removed below the sill level), and the **bed must have been below sea level** during the erosion, which requires either a eustatically low sea level exposing the shelf or a tectonically downwarped coast. The **sill at 150 m** is a **terminal or recessional moraine** deposited at a stillstand of the retreating ice, and it now dams the basin, which is why fjords are commonly **anoxic or stratified** below the sill depth — the deep water is cut off from the surface and from oxygen.

The sea-level history follows from the two depths together. The fjord was deglaciated, the sill was deposited at a stillstand, and the sea then rose to flood the trough. If the sill is dated by foraminifera or by radiocarbon on the moraine's contained shells, it dates the stillstand, and a **sequence of sills** up a fjord is a dated record of the retreat. Where the fjord mouth is now below sea level but the moraine was deposited subaerially, the coast has **submerged**, and the amount is the difference between the moraine's original and present elevations — the classic isostatic and eustatic problem of the Scottish, Norwegian and Maine coasts.
:::
:::

::: exercise A land-system mapping problem {level=3}
A 200-km² formerly glaciated area shows: a marginal glacial-lacustrine delta at 140 m; a belt of kettles, kames and collapsed hummocks between 120 and 95 m; a field of 300 aligned ridges 200–600 m long, 5–15 m high, with their long axes trending 115°; a belt of fluted till between 95 and 70 m with the flutes trending 118°; and a raised beach at 60 m. Map the land systems and reconstruct the deglaciation.
::: solution
Five land systems, in the order of their formation. The **delta at 140 m** is a **proglacial-lacustrine delta**, so the ice margin stood at a position where a proglacial lake was ponded at 140 m. The **kettle–kame–hummock belt between 120 and 95 m** is a **supraglacial debris and dead-ice** terrain, formed where the ice stagnated in place and its surface debris collapsed as the ice melted out; its elevation range is the range over which the stagnant ice survived. The **300 aligned ridges** are **drumlins** (or, given their small size and their number, ribbed or transverse moraines), whose long axes at 115° give the ice-flow direction. The **fluted till with flutes at 118°** is a **subglacial fluting** terrain, formed by the meltwater or the deforming till beneath a grounded, retreating ice margin, and its agreement with the drumlin trend to within 3° confirms the flow direction. The **raised beach at 60 m** is the **post-deglaciation marine or lacustrine** shoreline.

The reconstruction is a **staged retreat from north to south** (or in the direction opposite to the ice flow) with the margin standing long enough to build a delta at 140 m, then a **stagnation zone** where the ice pulled back and left dead ice between 120 and 95 m, then a **grounded readvance or stillstand** that produced the subglacial fluting between 95 and 70 m, and finally a complete ice withdrawal and the establishment of a water body at 60 m.

The weakest link is the **interpretation of the ridges**. Drumlin swarms, ribbed moraines and collapsed ice-wedge or kettle fields can all produce aligned ridges of this size, and the discrimination requires **trenching or ground-penetrating radar**: a drumlin has a bedrock or till core with an internal fabric, a ribbed moraine is a thrust-folded till ridge with a proximal-facing imbrication, and a collapsed mound has a chaotic stratified core with ice-contact structures. The whole deglacial reconstruction turns on which of the three they are, because a drumlin means grounded flowing ice and a collapsed mound means stagnant dead ice — two entirely different retreat styles.
:::
:::

::: exercise The late Cenozoic acceleration {level=3}
Marine sediment cores off Norway show a sediment mass accumulation rate rising from $0.05\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}\,kyr^{-1}}$ before 2.8 Ma to $0.6\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}\,kyr^{-1}}$ after, over a source area of $8\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{km^2}$. Find the denudation rate before and after, and discuss what the increase means.
::: solution
Converting, $0.05\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}\,kyr^{-1}} = 0.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,kyr^{-1}} = 0.5\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$, and $0.6\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}\,kyr^{-1}} = 6\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$. Over $8\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ the mass fluxes are
$$
4\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,yr^{-1}} = 0.4\ \mathrm{Mt\,yr^{-1}} \quad\text{before}, \qquad
4.8\ \mathrm{Mt\,yr^{-1}} \quad\text{after}.
$$
At a rock density of $2700\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ the denudation rates over the source area are
$$
\frac{4\times10^{8}}{8\times10^{11}\times2700} = 1.9\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} = 0.19\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} \quad\text{before}, \qquad
2.2\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} \quad\text{after}.
$$
A **twelvefold increase** in denudation at 2.8 Ma, coincident with the onset of major Northern Hemisphere glaciation, is the classic evidence for the **glacial intensification** of erosion, and it has been found off most formerly glaciated margins — Norway, Iberia, the Barents Sea, Alaska, Patagonia and New Zealand.

Three caveats keep the interpretation honest. The **source area** is inferred from the sediment's provenance and may have changed; the **deep-sea accumulation rate** is not the same as the flux off the shelf, because much of the glacial load is deposited on the shelf and in the slope-rise and never reaches the abyssal plain, so the increase measured in a deep-sea core is a **lower bound**; and the **cause is contested** — the increase may be the effect of the ice, or of the **uplift** that caused the ice, or of the **falling global climate** that exposed the shelf and lowered the base level. The strongest evidence for the glacial cause is that the increase is **step-like at 2.8 Ma** rather than gradual, and that the sediment's **grain size and its ice-rafted debris** increase at the same horizon, which proves that ice reached the sea.
