A soil is not dirt. It is a reactor a metre thick in which the lithosphere, the atmosphere, the hydrosphere and the biosphere meet, and in which the Earth's surface chemistry is decided. It holds a third of the terrestrial carbon, filters the fresh water, feeds half the world's food, and is being destroyed ten to forty times faster than it is formed. It is also the most under-theorised object in the Earth sciences: it has no equations of state, its classification is a compromise between chemistry and utility, and its formation rates are measured in millimetres per thousand years while its losses are measured in decades.

## The profile and its factors

::: definition Soil horizons {#def-horizons}
A **soil profile** is the vertical succession of **horizons** produced by the processes that build it. The **O** horizon is organic litter and humus; the **A** horizon is the mineral topsoil, mixed with organic matter and darkened by it; the **E** horizon is a zone of **eluviation**, bleached by the removal of clay, iron and organic matter; the **B** horizon is the zone of **illuviation**, into which the eluviated material is deposited, and it carries the clay skins, the iron pan and the humus coatings that record the translocations; the **C** horizon is the unaltered or weakly altered parent material; and **R** is bedrock. A **solum** is the A plus B, and it is where the soil's properties are made. A soil with no A or B is an **entisol** and it is young, or it is being eroded as fast as it forms, or it is being deposited on.
:::

::: definition Jenny's equation {#def-jenny}
The state of a soil at time $t$ is a function of five factors,
$$
S = f\bigl(\text{climate},\ \text{organisms},\ \text{relief},\ \text{parent material},\ \text{time}\bigr),
$$
due to Dokuchaev (Dokuchaev) and formalised by Jenny (Jenny). The factors are not independent and the function is not known, so the equation is a statement of what a soil depends on rather than a prediction of what it is. Its value is diagnostic: a soil that differs from its neighbour across a boundary in the parent material is **lithogenic**, one that differs across a slope break is **toposequential**, one that differs with depth at a constant climate is a **chronosequence**, and one that differs along a climate transect at constant parent material is a **climosequence**. The whole of field soil science is the isolation of one factor by holding the others constant, and the classic examples are the Holocene chronosequences of the Oregon dunes and the Lake Michigan shore, the climosequences of the Amazon–Sahara transect, and the lithosequences of the western United States.
:::

## The processes

::: definition Soil-forming processes {#def-processes}
**Lessivage** is the downward translocation of clay from the A into the B, and it builds the argillic horizon of an **alfisol** or **ultisol**. **Podzolisation** is the acid-driven mobilisation of iron, aluminium and organic matter by organic acids from a coniferous or heath litter layer, and it builds the bleached **albic** E horizon and the spodic B of a **spodosol**; it requires a sandy, free-draining parent material and a litter that is acid and slow to decompose. **Calcification** is the precipitation of calcium carbonate in or below the B horizon under a semi-arid climate with a summer-dry regime, and it builds the **calcic** horizon and the calcrete of an **aridisol**. **Gleisation** is the reduction of iron under waterlogged conditions, which turns the soil grey-blue with **redoximorphic** features and, in waterlogged organic conditions, builds a **histosol**. **Lateritisation** (ferrallitisation) is the complete leaching of silica and bases under a hot, wet, well-drained, old landscape, leaving a soil of kaolinite, hematite and gibbsite with a CEC of 5 cmol/kg and a fertility that resides entirely in the biomass above it. **Salinisation** and **sodisation** are the accumulation of soluble salts and of exchangeable sodium, and they are the most widespread of the human-made soil processes.
:::

::: definition Soil chemistry {#def-cec}
The **cation exchange capacity** (CEC) is the charge per unit mass on the edges and interlayers of the clay minerals and on the organic matter, measured in $\mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$, and it is the soil's capacity to hold nutrient cations against leaching. Typical values are **kaolinite 3–15**, **illite 20–50**, **montmorillonite 80–150**, and **organic matter 100–300** $\mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$, so a soil's CEC is a direct statement about what its clay is and how much humus it carries. The **base saturation** is the fraction of the exchange complex held by the basic cations,
$$
\mathrm{BS} = \frac{\mathrm{Ca^{2+}} + \mathrm{Mg^{2+}} + \mathrm{K^+} + \mathrm{Na^+}}{\mathrm{CEC}}\times 100\ \%,
$$
and it is the single best chemical index of a soil's weathering intensity and its fertility. A base saturation above 50 per cent is a base-rich, little-leached soil; below 20 per cent the exchange complex is dominated by aluminium and hydrogen, the soil is acid, and the plants growing on it are adapted to aluminium toxicity.
:::

::: example Reading a soil's chemistry {#ex-cec}
Two soils have the following exchangeable cations in $\mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$. Soil 1: Ca 9.2, Mg 3.1, K 0.7, Na 0.4, Al 0.2, H 0.6, CEC 14.2. Soil 2: Ca 1.1, Mg 0.3, K 0.2, Na 0.1, Al 6.4, H 3.9, CEC 12.0. Find the base saturation of each, and interpret.
::: solution
$$
\text{Soil 1:}\quad \mathrm{BS} = \frac{9.2 + 3.1 + 0.7 + 0.4}{14.2}\times100 = \frac{13.4}{14.2}\times100 = 94\ \%;
$$
$$
\text{Soil 2:}\quad \mathrm{BS} = \frac{1.1 + 0.3 + 0.2 + 0.1}{12.0}\times100 = \frac{1.7}{12.0}\times100 = 14\ \% .
$$
Soil 1 is a **base-saturated, little-weathered** soil — a mollisol, an alfisol or an aridisol — with a CEC of 14 that indicates an illitic or mixed-clay mineralogy with some organic matter. Its fertility is inherent in the mineral soil. Soil 2 is an **acid, highly leached** soil with the exchange complex dominated by aluminium and hydrogen — an **ultisol** or **oxisol** of a hot, wet, old landscape. Its CEC of 12 is low for its clay content, which indicates a **kaolinitic or oxidic** clay with a low permanent charge and a pH-dependent charge that is largely neutralised.

The practical difference is the reason that temperate agriculture is productive and tropical agriculture on the same soils is not. In soil 1 the nutrient pool is held on the clay and is released slowly; in soil 2 the nutrient pool is held in the **biomass**, and when the forest is cleared and burned the nutrients go with the ash, the aluminium becomes toxic, and the soil supports two or three crops before it is exhausted. This is the mechanism behind the "slash-and-burn" cycle and behind the failure of the Cerrado and the Amazon before lime and fertiliser were applied at industrial rates.
:::
:::

::: history
Soil science begins with Dokuchaev (Dokuchaev), the St Petersburg geologist who in 1870 argued that a soil is a **natural body** with its own genesis, that it is a function of climate and biota rather than of the rock beneath it, and that the world's soils can be mapped as zones. His students Sibirzev, Glinka and Ramann carried the idea into Europe, and Hilgard (Hilgard) carried it into the United States, where the Pedology school of the 1920s and 1930s — Jenny, Marbut, Baldwin, Kellogg — made it quantitative. Hans Jenny's (Jenny) 1941 monograph **Factors of Soil Formation** set down the five-factor equation and is the founding text of process soil science. The soil taxonomy of the United States Department of Agriculture, first published in 1960 and revised in 1975, 1990, 1992, 1998 and 2015, and the World Reference Base of the FAO are the two rival classifications, the first keyed to measurable properties and the second to a diagnostic-horizon language. The **critical zone** and **soil production function** revolution came from the geomorphologists: the exponential production function is the work of Heimsath, Dietrich, Nishiizumi and Finkel (Finkel) in 1997 and of Moore, Kirchner, Dietrich and Finkel in 2007; the cosmogenic measurement of soil production and of catchment denudation is the work of Bierman, Finkel, Caffee and Small in the 1990s; and the global soil-erosion budgets are the assessments of the World Resources Institute, of Montanarella, and of the 2015 Intergovernmental Technical Panel report on soils, which put the loss-to-formation ratio at ten to forty. The recognition that soil organic carbon is twice the atmospheric pool is the synthesis of Batjes, of Scharlemann and of the Global Carbon Project.
:::

::: example How long does a soil take to acidify? {#ex-acidify}
A soil 1 m deep with a bulk density of $1.3\ \mathrm{Mg\,m^{-3}}$ has a CEC of $20\ \mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$ and a base saturation of 100 per cent. Drainage carries $20\ \mu\mathrm{mol_c\,L^{-1}}$ of base cations at a rate of $0.5\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ of drainage water, and weathering supplies bases at $3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol_c\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$. How long until the base saturation falls to 20 per cent?
::: solution
The exchange complex holds
$$
20\ \mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}\times1300\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}} = 2.6\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cmol_c\,m^{-2}} = 260\ \mathrm{mol_c\,m^{-2}}
$$
of bases. The leaching loss is
$$
0.5\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}\times1000\ \mathrm{L\,m^{-3}}\times20\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{mol_c\,L^{-1}} = 0.010\ \mathrm{mol_c\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}},
$$
and the weathering supply is $0.003\ \mathrm{mol_c\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$, so the net mining of the exchange complex is
$$
0.010 - 0.003 = 0.007\ \mathrm{mol_c\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
Falling from 100 to 20 per cent base saturation means removing $0.8\times260 = 208\ \mathrm{mol_c\,m^{-2}}$, which takes
$$
\frac{208}{0.007} = 3.0\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{yr}.
$$
**Thirty thousand years** — long enough that acidification is a geological process in an undisturbed soil, and short enough that the acid deposition of the twentieth century, which added $0.02$–$0.05\ \mathrm{mol_c\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$ of hydrogen and aluminium to European and North American forests, could exhaust a century's worth of the buffer in a few decades of added leaching. The same arithmetic explains why the recovery of acidified soils after the clean-air legislation of the 1990s is measured in decades rather than years: the exchange complex must be reloaded from a weathering supply that has not changed.
:::
:::

::: example A reservoir sediment budget {#ex-sedbudget}
A reservoir with a trapping efficiency of 75 per cent has accumulated $1.2\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ of sediment over 40 years. Its catchment is $400\ \mathrm{km^2}$ of cultivated land. Find the specific sediment yield in $\mathrm{t\,km^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$ and the mean denudation rate, and compare them with the soil production rate of $2000\ \mathrm{t\,km^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$.
::: solution
The gross sediment yield is $1.2\times10^{6}/0.75 = 1.6\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ over 40 years, or $4\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^3\,yr^{-1}}$. At a bulk density of $1.3\ \mathrm{Mg\,m^{-3}}$ this is
$$
4\times10^{4}\times1300 = 5.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{kg\,yr^{-1}} = 52\,000\ \mathrm{t\,yr^{-1}},
$$
and over $400\ \mathrm{km^2}$,
$$
Y_s = \frac{52\,000}{400} = 130\ \mathrm{t\,km^{-2}\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
The mean denudation rate is
$$
\frac{4\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^3\,yr^{-1}}}{4\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m^2}} = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} = 100\ \mu\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
The yield is only $130/2000 = 6.5$ per cent of the soil production rate, and this is the central fact about a cultivated catchment's sediment: **the hillslopes are not all contributing**. If the whole catchment were eroding at the rate the gullies and the field drains deliver, the yield would be thirty times larger and the reservoir would have silted in four years. The sediment comes from a small fraction of the area — the gully heads, the channel banks, the convergent hollows, the fields at the end of the furrows — and the rest is either in equilibrium or receiving sediment.

The practical consequence is that a sediment-yield number cannot be compared with a soil-erosion number measured on plots. Plot erosion measures the **gross** detachment on the field; the reservoir measures the **delivery** at the outlet, and the ratio between them — the sediment delivery ratio, commonly 0.05 to 0.3 — is the fraction that leaves. A catchment-management programme that reduces the plot erosion by half may reduce the reservoir's siltation by a tenth, because the sediment was never coming from the fields that were treated.
:::
:::

## Soil as a landscape

::: proposition Steady-state soil thickness {#prop-soil-thickness}
Where the soil production rate decays exponentially with thickness, $\omega = \omega_0 e^{-ch}$, and the hillslope removes soil at a rate $E$, the steady-state thickness is
$$
h^* = \frac{1}{c}\ln\frac{\omega_0}{E}.
$$
The thickness therefore responds **logarithmically** to the erosion rate: a tenfold increase in erosion thins the soil by only $2.3/c$, and a soil can persist on a fast-eroding slope only if $\omega_0$ is large or $c$ is small. The corollary is that a soil's thickness is a poor indicator of its age and a good indicator of the balance between the climate that makes it and the slopes that remove it.
:::

::: example How thick can a soil be? {#ex-thickness}
With $\omega_0 = 120\ \mu\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ and $c = 0.08\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, find the steady-state soil thickness on a slope eroding at 20, 100 and $500\ \mu\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$, and find the erosion rate above which no soil can survive.
::: solution
$$
E = 20:\quad h^* = \frac{1}{0.08}\ln\frac{120}{20} = 12.5\times1.79 = 22\ \mathrm{m};
$$
$$
E = 100:\quad h^* = 12.5\times\ln 1.20 = 12.5\times0.18 = 2.3\ \mathrm{m};
$$
$$
E = 500:\quad h^* = 12.5\times\ln 0.24 = -18\ \mathrm{m}.
$$
The negative value is the answer to the last question: no steady state exists once $E$ exceeds $\omega_0 = 120\ \mu\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$, because the slope removes soil faster than the fresh rock can produce it at any thickness. A slope eroding at 500 µm/yr is **stripped**, and the observation that such slopes carry colluvium rather than soil, and that their bedrock is exposed, is the field signature of the condition.

The logarithmic response is the reason that the world's deep soils — the 30-m laterites of Australia and Africa, the 20-m loess-derived profiles of China — occur on **ancient, stable, slowly eroding** surfaces, and that the fertile, deep, base-rich soils of the world's breadbaskets occur on **young, slowly eroding, flat or gently rolling** ground. A soil is a balance sheet, and the erosion rate is the debit that decides whether it can accumulate.
:::
:::

The curve is a descending logarithm that crosses zero at $E = \omega_0$ and diverges as the erosion rate falls, which is why the model must be capped: at $E$ of 1 µm/yr it predicts a 60-m soil, and real profiles are limited by the depth at which the weathering front reaches groundwater, by the transport of the soil downslope, and by the fact that the exponential production function does not hold to zero. The practical reading is that a soil thicker than a few metres requires an erosion rate below about 10 µm/yr sustained for hundreds of thousands of years, and that the deep soils of the stable cratons are therefore among the oldest land surfaces on Earth.

::: warning A soil is a non-renewable resource on any human timescale, and the numbers are not close
The global average soil production rate on cropland is of order $50$–$100\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$, or a few tens of micrometres per year, and the global average tillage and water erosion of cropland is of order $50$–$100\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$ on average but several hundred on the steepest and most cultivated land. The mean ratio of loss to production is commonly quoted as 10 to 40, and on the world's most erodible cultivated slopes it exceeds 100. A soil 30 cm deep, which took 3000 years to form at 100 µm/yr, is removed in a century of cultivation at 30 mm/yr. Three consequences follow. **The loss is not uniform**: a few per cent of the world's fields supply most of its sediment, and the erosion is concentrated on the steep, the overgrazed and the newly cleared. **The loss is not only thickness**: tillage erosion, compaction, the loss of organic carbon, salinisation and acidification degrade the soil's function long before its thickness is gone, and these are the processes that dominate the developed world's soil accounts. **And the carbon is real**: the soil holds about twice as much carbon as the atmosphere, and its loss to erosion and oxidation is a source of a quarter to a half of the carbon released by land-use change. The honest statement is that soil is being treated as a renewable resource when its production function makes it a finite one, and that the only remedy with a geological timescale behind it is to keep the ground covered and the slopes unploughed.
:::

::: quiz
A soil has a CEC of 8 $\mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$, a base saturation of 8 per cent, a clay content of 40 per cent and a depth of 2 m. What is it?
- [x] A highly leached, kaolinitic or oxidic oxisol or ultisol of an old, stable, hot and wet landscape.
- [ ] A young, base-rich mollisol.
- [ ] A waterlogged gleyed histosol.
- [ ] A calcareous aridisol.
::: solution
A CEC of 8 at 40 per cent clay means about 20 $\mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$ of clay, which is kaolinite or an iron and aluminium oxide, not a 2:1 mineral. A base saturation of 8 per cent means the exchange complex is dominated by aluminium and hydrogen. A 2-m depth means the profile has been forming for hundreds of thousands of years on a stable surface. Together these are the classic oxisol or ultisol of the Brazilian Cerrado, the Congo basin or the Australian plateau — deep, ancient, acid, and fertile only in its biomass.
:::

## Where this leads

::: summary
- A soil profile is a succession of horizons — O, A, E, B, C, R — each produced by a process, and the B horizon is where the translocations are recorded.
- A soil's state depends on climate, organisms, relief, parent material and time, and the isolation of one factor is the whole of field soil science.
- The cation exchange capacity says what the clay is, and the base saturation says how far the leaching has gone.
- Steady-state soil thickness responds logarithmically to erosion rate, and no soil survives where the erosion exceeds the maximum production rate.
- Deep soils are old and stable, and fertile soils are young and slowly eroding.
- Soil is being lost ten to forty times faster than it is formed, and on a human timescale it is not renewable.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Horizons {level=1}
A soil has a bleached grey E horizon over a red B horizon thick with clay skins. Name the process and the soil order.
::: solution
**Lessivage** (clay illuviation) with iron coatings, and the soil is an **alfisol** or, if it is old, acid and highly leached, an **ultisol**. The bleached E is the eluvial zone from which the clay and iron have been washed and the argillic B is where they were deposited; the red colour is hematite and goethite coating the grains.
:::
:::

::: exercise Base saturation {level=1 check="75"}
A soil has exchangeable Ca 6.0, Mg 2.0, K 0.6, Na 0.4 and CEC 12.0 $\mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$. Find the base saturation.
::: solution
$\mathrm{BS} = (6.0+2.0+0.6+0.4)/12.0\times100 = 9.0/12.0\times100 = 75\ \%$. A base-rich, moderately leached soil — a fertile one.
:::
:::

::: exercise Clay CEC {level=1}
Which holds more potassium, a montmorillonitic or a kaolinitic clay, and why?
::: solution
The **montmorillonitic** clay, by an order of magnitude: a CEC of 80–150 against 3–15 $\mathrm{cmol_c\,kg^{-1}}$. The charge comes from isomorphous substitution in the **interlayer** of the 2:1 lattice, which is also where the potassium is held, in the hexagonal cavities of the silicate sheet and, once fixed, between the layers where plant roots cannot reach it. A kaolinite is a 1:1 mineral with no interlayer and only edge charge, so it holds almost nothing.
:::
:::

::: exercise Soil thickness {level=2 check="14"}
With $\omega_0 = 100\ \mu\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ and $c = 0.10\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, find the steady-state soil thickness at an erosion rate of $25\ \mu\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$h^* = (1/0.10)\ln(100/25) = 10\times1.386 = 13.9\ \mathrm{m}$. A deep soil, and one that requires the erosion rate to have stayed near 25 µm/yr for the hundred thousand years or more needed to build 14 m.
:::

::: exercise Jenny's factors {level=2}
Along a single hillslope you measure, from the crest to the valley: a thin soil on shallow bedrock at the crest, a deep soil on the convex mid-slope, a deep and dark soil on the flat shoulder, and a grey, mottled, waterlogged soil in the concave hollow. Which factor is doing the work, and what process makes each?
::: solution
**Relief** (topography) is the factor, and the sequence is a classic **catenary** or toposequence, with climate, organisms, parent material and time held constant. The crest is **transport-limited by its own steepness**: soil is removed as fast as it forms and the profile is thin and immature. The convex mid-slope is a zone of **divergent subsurface flow** and of net soil production, and it carries a deep, well-drained, moderately leached profile. The flat shoulder has the **slowest erosion of all** and therefore the deepest and, because it also has the longest water residence time, the most strongly leached and darkest profile. The concave hollow is a **convergent** zone of water and colluvium, and its grey mottling is **gleisation** — iron reduction under a perched water table.

The exercise's point is that a soil map drawn on a landscape of any relief is a map of **water and of the convergence and divergence of the sediment flux**, not of the parent material, and that a soil-sampling design that ignores the topography will confound the two.
:::
:::

::: exercise Soil carbon {level=2 check="312"}
A grassland soil 1 m deep has an organic carbon content of 2.4 per cent and a bulk density of $1.3\ \mathrm{Mg\,m^{-3}}$. Find the carbon stock in $\mathrm{Mg\,ha^{-1}}$ (equivalently t/ha).
::: solution
Mass of soil per hectare to 1 m is $10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^2}\times1\ \mathrm{m}\times1.3\ \mathrm{Mg\,m^{-3}} = 1.3\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{Mg\,ha^{-1}}$, and the carbon is $0.024\times1.3\times10^{4} = 312\ \mathrm{Mg\,ha^{-1}}$. A ploughed soil of the same texture commonly holds 100–150, so the conversion of grassland to arable has released half the stock — the classic result of the North American and Ukrainian prairie conversion.
:::

::: exercise A soil chronosequence {level=3}
A sequence of soils on sand dunes of known age gives the data below. Interpret the trend, fit the thickness, and say what limits it.

| Age (kyr) | 2 | 8 | 30 | 100 | 300 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A-horizon thickness (cm) | 1 | 3 | 7 | 14 | 22 |
| B-horizon thickness (cm) | 0 | 1 | 5 | 14 | 28 |
| Base saturation (%) | 95 | 80 | 45 | 20 | 8 |
::: solution
The A horizon grows roughly as the **square root of time** — 1, 3, 7, 14, 22 against ages 2, 8, 30, 100, 300, whose square roots are 1.4, 2.8, 5.5, 10, 17.3, giving ratios of 0.7, 1.1, 1.3, 1.4, 1.3 — so the A thickens diffusively, as a mixing and bioturbation front should. The B horizon grows roughly **linearly** with time, at about 1 cm per 10 kyr, which is a constant rate of clay illuviation and therefore of lessivage. The base saturation falls roughly as the **logarithm of time**, from 95 to 8 per cent, which is the expected form for a leaching system in which the base-rich exchange sites are progressively replaced by hydrogen and aluminium as the supply of weatherable minerals runs out.

The interpretation is a **weathering and soil-formation chronosequence** of the classic Oregon-dune type, in which the A is built by biology at a diffusion-limited rate, the B by clay translocation at a supply-limited rate, and the chemistry by the exhaustion of the primary minerals. The limit on the thickness is not time but **water and material**: the B cannot thicken once the easily weathered minerals (biotite, feldspar) are gone, and the A cannot thicken once the biomass production reaches the carrying capacity of the dune. Extrapolating the B's linear trend to 3000 kyr would predict a 3-m B, which is not observed — the trend breaks at about 1 m when the supply of translocatable clay is exhausted.

The weakest link is the assumption that the dunes' ages are the soils' ages. A dune may be reworked, may be buried by later sand, and may carry inherited organic matter, and the correct procedure is to date the **soil itself** — by radiocarbon on the humic fraction, by the carbonate or the silica coatings, or by cosmogenic nuclides in the sand grains at the base of the profile.
:::

::: exercise A global soil budget {level=3}
The world's cropland covers $1.6\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{ha}$. Tillage and water erosion remove on average $35\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$ and soil production averages $20\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$. Find the global annual soil deficit, the area that would be lost if the deficit were concentrated at a rate of $5\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, and the time in which the world's 30-cm topsoil would be exhausted at this rate.
::: solution
The deficit is $(35 - 20)\times1.6\times10^{9} = 2.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{t\,yr^{-1}}$. At a bulk density of $1.3\ \mathrm{Mg\,m^{-3}}$ this is
$$
\frac{2.4\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{kg}}{1300\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}} = 1.8\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^3\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
Spread over the whole cropland this is $1.8\times10^{10}/1.6\times10^{14} = 1.1\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}} = 0.11\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, which sounds trivial. Concentrated on the 10 per cent of the area where the erosion is worst it is $1.1\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, and at $5\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ the whole deficit could be carried by
$$
\frac{1.8\times10^{10}}{5\times10^{-3}} = 3.6\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^2} = 3.6\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{ha},
$$
which is **22 per cent of the world's cropland**. In other words the arithmetic permits the loss to be concentrated on a fifth to a quarter of the cultivated area at a rate of 5 mm per year while the remainder is in balance.

The exhaustion time for a 30-cm topsoil at the **mean** deficit rate of 0.11 mm/yr is $300/0.11 = 2700$ years, and at the **hotspot** rate of 5 mm/yr it is 60 years. Both numbers are correct and both are misleading: the first hides the fact that the loss is concentrated, and the second assumes the concentration is permanent and the production zero. The honest statement is that a few hundred million hectares are being mined of their topsoil at a rate that will exhaust it within a few human generations, that the rest is essentially stable, and that the global average — the number that gets quoted — is the least useful of the three.
:::
