Global mean sea level has risen twenty centimetres since 1900 and is now rising at $3.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, two-and-a-half times the early-twentieth-century rate, with an acceleration of $0.09\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-2}}$. Not one term of the budget is a mystery and not one is settled: we can weigh the melting of Greenland from orbit to five per cent, measure the warming of the whole upper ocean with $4000$ drifting profiling floats, and still disagree by a factor of two about how much sea level there will be in 2100. The uncertainty is not in the physics. It is in the Antarctic.

## The budget

::: definition What sea level is, and what changes it {#def-budget}
Sea level is not a surface, it is a reference. The geoid is the equipotential surface of the Earth's gravity field; the mean sea surface departs from it by $\pm 80\ \mathrm{m}$ permanently (the static topography, due to density differences in the deep ocean) plus $\pm 1\ \mathrm{m}$ of dynamic variability. Sea-level rise means a change in the height of the sea surface relative to the geoid, measured about the Earth's centre of mass — the "geocentric" or "absolute" sea level, which is what matters for coasts and what satellites measure.

The budget has four independent terms:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{GMSL}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\rho_0 A}\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{ocean}}}{\mathrm{d}t}}_{\text{barystatic: mass}} + \underbrace{\int \alpha\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\,\mathrm{d}z}_{\text{steric: volume}} - \underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{\mathrm{basin}}}{\mathrm{d}t}}_{\text{basin capacity: GIA, sediment}} + \underbrace{\text{fingerprint terms}}_{\text{gravity, rotation, deformation}}
$$

Barystatic sea-level rise is water added to the ocean: glaciers and ice caps ($0.74\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, 2006–2018), Greenland ($0.69$), Antarctica ($0.38$), land water storage ($0.39$, mostly groundwater depletion and reservoir impoundment). Steric rise is water expanding: thermosteric ($0.84$) from warming, halinosteric ($0.10$) from freshening. The sum of the AR6 observed components for 2006–2018 is $3.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, matching the altimeter to within $0.2$, which is the closure that makes the whole edifice credible.

Conversion factors worth memorising: $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ of global mean sea level is $361\ \mathrm{Gt}$ of water; the Greenland Ice Sheet holds $7.4\ \mathrm{m}$, the Antarctic Ice Sheet $58\ \mathrm{m}$, the West Antarctic alone $3.2\ \mathrm{m}$, all glaciers $0.4\ \mathrm{m}$; warming the whole ocean by $1\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ would give $\sim 60\ \mathrm{cm}$.

The regional pattern is not the global mean, and the difference is not noise. Melting an ice sheet changes three things locally: its own gravity pull on the surrounding ocean falls, so sea level falls near the melting ice and rises far away; the solid Earth rebounds and tilts; and the meltwater's freshwater and the changed rotation redistribute the ocean. The result is a sea-level "fingerprint": Greenland melt raises the US east coast by three to four times the global mean and lowers it in the Southern Ocean, and Antarctic melt does the reverse. This is not a correction; it is the dominant signal at regional scale, and it is why "sea-level rise" is a poor name for what is happening.
:::

::: definition Measuring sea level, and what the record says {#def-measure}
Three instruments, three timescales, three systematics.

Tide gauges measure relative sea level against a benchmark on land. The longest records — Brest, Amsterdam, Calais, New York, Lundakvarv — run $150$ years, and their value is unmatched for extremes, for the twentieth century, and for the coasts satellites cannot see. Their problems are that the land moves: glacial isostatic adjustment unloads Hudson Bay and Scandinavia at $5$–$12\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, sediment compaction and groundwater pumping subside deltaic coasts at $5$–$30\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, and the gauge cannot tell the two apart. Global mean sea level from tide gauges requires a spatial reconstruction with a $\pm 0.3\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ uncertainty and a GIA correction of $+0.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$.

Satellite radar altimetry from TOPEX/Poseidon (1992–2002), Jason-1, OSTM/Jason-2, Jason-3 and Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich (2020–) measures the range to the sea surface to a few centimetres on a $10$-day repeat orbit, and after correcting for the ionosphere, the wet troposphere, the sea state, the tides and the pole motion, gives a global mean to $\pm 0.4\ \mathrm{mm}$ per year. The 1993–2023 trend is $3.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ with an acceleration of $0.09\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-2}}$; the biggest single risk is not the noise but the inter-calibration between missions, and a $0.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ bias between two satellites is the same size as the acceleration being measured.

Argo, $\sim 4000$ profiling floats since 2000 sampling to $2000\ \mathrm{m}$, measures the steric height directly and has resolved the old "closure problem": the upper-ocean steric rise is $1.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, the deep ocean below $2000\ \mathrm{m}$ adds $\sim 0.3$, and the barystatic terms measured by GRACE and GRACE-FO gravity satellites and by differential GPS of the ice sheets make the balance close. The remaining open term is deep ocean warming below Argo depth, where repeat CTD sections show a warming that adds $\sim 0.5$–$1.0\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ to the Earth's energy imbalance estimate.

The palaeo and geological record brackets the future. During the last deglaciation, sea level rose $120\ \mathrm{m}$ over $18{,}000$ years, punctuated by meltwater pulses of $14$–$20\ \mathrm{m}$ per few centuries — the only direct evidence that multi-metre-per-century rates are physically possible, and the reason the upper end of the 2100 range is not zero. The last interglacial, $125{,}000$ years ago, at global mean temperature $1$–$2\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ above preindustrial, stood $6$–$9\ \mathrm{m}$ higher; the mid-Pliocene, $3$ million years ago at $2$–$3\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, stood $3$–$24\ \mathrm{m}$ higher. Both are above any twenty-first-century projection, and both were reached in millennia, not decades.
:::

::: definition Projections, ice-sheet instability, and the coast {#def-future}
Projections come in two families, and the difference between them is the whole policy question. The process-based family sums observed components, each extrapolated from its own physics: IPCC AR6 gives $0.38$–$0.67\ \mathrm{m}$ for SSP1-2.6 and $0.63$–$1.01\ \mathrm{m}$ for SSP5-8.5 by 2100, with low confidence in the upper tail because marine ice-sheet instability is "not well captured". The expert-elicitation and ice-sheet-model family adds a structural term for Antarctic marine ice-cliff instability and gives up to $2\ \mathrm{m}$ for high-emission, high-uncertainty scenarios. Both are defensible; the disagreement is about whether a cliff taller than $\sim 250\ \mathrm{m}$ can fail on a century timescale, which is a fracture-mechanics question that the observational record has not yet answered.

Two mechanisms make the Antarctic different from Greenland. Marine ice-sheet instability (MISI) applies where the bed slopes downward inland, so that retreat places the grounding line in deeper water, which thickens the ice there, which increases the discharge, which retreats the line further — an unconditional positive feedback with no equilibrium. The West Antarctic Ice Sheet, holding $3.2\ \mathrm{m}$, is entirely of this type, and the Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers alone hold $0.65\ \mathrm{m}$ and are thinning at $1$–$2\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$. Marine ice-cliff instability (MICI) is more speculative: if a retreating glacier loses its ice shelf, the exposed cliff may fail under its own weight once it exceeds $\sim 250$–$300\ \mathrm{m}$, and the modelled Antarctic contribution jumps by $\sim 1\ \mathrm{m}$ by 2100. There is no modern observational example of MICI; there is geomorphological evidence for it in the Pliocene and in the last interglacial.

On the coast, the hazard is not the mean level but the tail. Extreme water levels exceed their long-term mean with a frequency that grows approximately exponentially with mean sea level,
$$
\frac{N(h_0 + \Delta h)}{N(h_0)} \approx \exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta h}{x_0}\right), \qquad x_0 \approx 0.1\text{–}0.2\ \mathrm{m},
$$
so $0.5\ \mathrm{m}$ of rise makes a present hundred-year flood an annual one on most low-lying coasts, and "nuisance" or "tidal" flooding on the US Atlantic and Gulf coasts has tripled since 1960. Add the other consequences — permanent inundation of $0.2$–$0.4$ of many deltaic and atoll coasts, salt-water intrusion into coastal aquifers and rice land, the drowning of salt marsh and mangrove where sediment supply cannot keep pace, and the failure of wastewater and storm drainage that cannot gravity-drain above the new high tide — and the cost of a given rise is set by elevation, subsidence and income, not by the rise itself. The population living below the projected 2100 high-tide line is $0.6$–$1.2$ billion, of whom the great majority are in the Ganges-Brahmaputra, the Mekong, the Nile, the Yangtze, the Pearl and the Chao Phraya deltas and Jakarta — the same places where groundwater extraction is sinking the land three times faster than the ocean is rising.
:::

::: example Steric rise from ocean warming {#ex-steric}
If the upper **$700\ \mathrm{m}$** of the ocean warms by **$0.5\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$** with a thermal expansion coefficient of $2.1\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$, find the global mean steric rise. What warming rate does the observed $1.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ of upper-ocean steric rise imply?
::: solution
$$
\Delta h = \alpha\,\Delta T\,H = 2.1\times10^{-4}\times0.5\times700 = 0.074\ \mathrm{m} = 74\ \mathrm{mm}.
$$
Inverting, $1.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ over $700\ \mathrm{m}$ corresponds to
$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{1.4\times10^{-3}}{2.1\times10^{-4}\times700} = 0.010\ \mathrm{^\circ C\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
One hundredth of a degree a year, averaged over seven hundred metres of the world ocean, is one third of all sea-level rise.

The number to hold onto is the ratio: the ocean has absorbed about ninety per cent of the excess heat of global warming, and the same heat that makes it the planet's buffer makes it the largest single contributor to sea-level rise. The rate of $0.01\ \mathrm{^\circ C\,yr^{-1}}$ is imperceptible to any instrument on a boat and irreversible on a millennium: even if emissions stopped today, the thermosteric term alone would continue for centuries as the heat mixes downward.

The halinosteric term partly cancels it. The ocean is freshening at the surface in the high rainfall and melting regions, and fresh water is less dense, so freshening raises sea level too — the two are not opposite. What partly offsets thermosteric rise is the salt added by evaporation in the subtropics, and the net halinosteric contribution is only $0.1\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, small but not zero, and regional enough that the salinity field must be measured and not inferred.
:::
:::

::: example From ice mass to sea level {#ex-barystatic}
GRACE measures the Greenland Ice Sheet losing $270\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$. Find the global mean sea-level contribution, and the fraction of its total sea-level equivalent that would be released in a century at this rate.
::: solution
One millimetre of global mean sea level is
$$
3.61\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m}\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} = 3.61\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{kg} = 361\ \mathrm{Gt},
$$
so the contribution is $270/361 = 0.75\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$. Over a century at this rate, $75\ \mathrm{mm}$ of the $7400\ \mathrm{mm}$ equivalent, or one per cent.

The two numbers together are the whole policy problem. Greenland is losing an amount of ice that would be unimaginable in any human frame — two hundred and seventy thousand million tonnes a year, ten times the volume of all the world's rivers discharging into the sea — and it is raising sea level by less than a millimetre. At that rate the entire ice sheet would take ten thousand years to melt. The rate is not the worst case; the rate is the baseline, and the question is the derivative.

The same arithmetic applied to Antarctica is more consequential: $150\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$ is only $0.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, but the West Antarctic holds $3200\ \mathrm{mm}$, so a ten-fold increase in the Antarctic rate is $4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ from one continent — more than the entire present global rise. This is why every serious sea-level projection is in the end a projection of one number: the grounding-line behaviour of a handful of Antarctic outlet glaciers.
:::
:::

::: example Correcting a tide gauge {#ex-tidegauge}
A tide gauge on a delta records a relative sea-level rise of $4.5\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$. Continuous GPS on the gauge benchmark shows the land sinking at $2.5\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, and glacial isostatic adjustment contributes a further $0.3\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ of subsidence. Find the eustatic rise, and the rate a resident experiences.
::: solution
The eustatic (geocentric, climate-driven) component is
$$
4.5 - 2.5 - 0.3 = 1.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} .
$$
The resident experiences the full $4.5\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ — two-and-a-half times the global mean.

This is the central injustice of sea-level rise. The subsidence is not geological bad luck but groundwater and hydrocarbon extraction and the sediment a dam traps upstream: Jakarta, Bangkok, Ho Chi Minh City, New Orleans, Venice, Tianjin and Shanghai are all sinking faster than the ocean is rising, and all are on the coasts with the most people per metre of elevation. Jakarta has subsidised the relocation of its capital for this reason.

The methodological point is equally important. A tide gauge alone cannot distinguish a rising sea from a sinking shore, which is why the global tide-gauge network now co-locates continuous GPS, why the satellite altimeter is immune to land motion but blind to the coast, and why the two must be combined with a glacial isostatic adjustment model whose Earth viscosity parameters are themselves uncertain at the $\pm 0.3\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ level in formerly glaciated regions.
:::
:::

::: example How a hundred-year flood becomes an annual one {#ex-flood}
On a coast the exceedance frequency of water levels follows $N(h) \propto \exp(h/x_0)$ with $x_0 = 0.15\ \mathrm{m}$. By what factor does the frequency of the present hundred-year extreme increase with $0.5\ \mathrm{m}$ of sea-level rise, and what is its new return period?
::: solution
$$
\frac{N(h_0 + 0.5)}{N(h_0)} = \exp\!\left(\frac{0.5}{0.15}\right) = e^{3.33} = 28 .
$$
A hundred-year event becomes a three-and-a-half-year event. At $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ the factor is $e^{6.67} = 790$, and the hundred-year flood arrives ten times a decade.

The result looks too neat to be true, and the caveats matter. The exponential form holds only where the still-water level distribution is exponential, which it is on most tide-dominated coasts above the spring-tide range and is not where waves dominate. The parameter $x_0$ is measured, not assumed, and ranges from $0.05\ \mathrm{m}$ in the microtidal Mediterranean to $0.35\ \mathrm{m}$ in the macrotidal Bristol Channel. And the storm climate may shift with the same forcing, which can increase or decrease the frequency independent of the mean level.

Nevertheless the observation is already in the record: nuisance flooding on the US Atlantic and Gulf coasts has tripled since 1960 and doubled since 2010, with the timing and spatial pattern matching the mean-level explanation rather than any change in storm frequency. This is the cleanest demonstration in climate science that a small change in a mean produces a large change in a tail — and the reason sea-level policy is about the few centimetres, not the metres.
:::
:::

::: history
Johann Benedict Listing (Listing) coined the word "geoid" in 1872, giving the subject its first essential distinction: sea level is a gravity surface, not a shape. The Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level in Liverpool has collected tide-gauge monthly means since 1933, and and the network's oldest continuous records, Brest from 1873 and Amsterdam from 1878, are the foundation of every twentieth-century sea-level reconstruction. Richard Fairbanks (Fairbanks) read the last deglaciation out of coral $\delta^{18}\mathrm{O}$ and thorium ages in 1989 and revealed the meltwater pulses — steps of four to twenty metres in a few centuries — which Peter Clark and colleagues later dated and attributed to Antarctic and Laurentide collapse. Jerry Mitrovica (Mitrovica) developed the gravitationally consistent theory of sea-level fingerprinting from the 1990s, showing that melting an ice sheet lowers sea level beside it and raises it on the far side of the planet. Satellite radar altimetry began with GEOSAT and SEASAT, became a climate record with TOPEX/Poseidon in 1992, and was turned into a usable global mean by Anny Cazenave (Cazenave), Steve Nerem (Nerem), Joshua Willis (Willis) and the CNES/CLS, NOAA and NASA teams who have maintained the record across six successive satellites. John Church and Neil White (Church) reconstructed the twentieth-century global mean from residual-tide-gauge data in 2011, closing the budget for the century before satellites, and Willis, Dean Roemmeling and colleagues closed the satellite-era budget with Argo and GRACE in 2008. Stefan Rahmstorf (Rahmstorf) built the first semi-empirical projection in 2007, Robert Kopp, Jonathan Bamber and Michael Oppenheimer (Kopp) introduced probabilistic and expert-elicitation projections in 2008–2013, James Hansen (Hansen) argued from the Pliocene and the last interglacial for multi-metre commitment from 2008, and Benjamin Strauss (Strauss) and the Climate Central team mapped the population exposed to the high-tide line, which revised the US exposure upward by a factor of three. The Ice Sheet Mass Balance Inter-comparison Exercise, convened by Andrew Shepherd (Shepherd) and Isabella Velicogna (Velicogna) in 2012 and repeated four times since, forced the glaciological and satellite communities to one number for each ice sheet, and is the reason the modern sea-level budget closes at all.
:::

::: warning The mean is well measured, the map is not, the future is not, and the local number is the only one that matters
Seven cautions. Global mean sea level is the best-determined quantity in climate science and the least useful for any particular coast. The global mean is known to $\pm 0.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$; the trend at a given tide gauge is known to $\pm 1\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ or worse after GIA, and the projection for that gauge in 2100 spans $0.5\ \mathrm{m}$. Satellite records are not one record. Six missions with different orbits, different delay calibrations and different retrievals must be cross-calibrated at sea cycles, and a $0.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ bias between two of them is the same size as the acceleration the record is used to detect; the published accelerations differ by a factor of two depending on how the Jason-1 to OSTM transition is handled. Argo does not see the deep ocean, the boundary currents or the poles. The floats profile to $2000\ \mathrm{m}$, are absent north of $70\ \mathrm{^\circ N}$, under sea ice and on shelves, and the unmeasured deep and polar steric terms are the largest residual in the budget. GRACE cannot separate ice mass from water storage from GIA at small scales. The mascon solutions leak signal between land and ocean, and the Antarctic trend carries a GIA uncertainty comparable to the trend itself. The tide-gauge record is spatially aliased. Two-thirds of the coastline has no record longer than twenty years, the long records cluster in northwest Europe and North America, and the global mean from gauges depends on the spatial interpolation used to fill the oceans nobody measured. Projections are not predictions of the ice sheets we have. Every model is evaluated against a twentieth-century Antarctica that was not losing marine-based ice at the rate observed since 2002, and no model has reproduced a meltwater pulse. And the number that decides a coastline is not the global mean. It is the local high tide, the local subsidence, the local storm climate, the local sediment supply and the local ability to build something — and of those five, only the first is in any model that carries the name projection.
:::

::: summary
- Global mean sea level has risen about 20 cm since 1900 and is rising at 3.7 mm yr⁻¹ with an acceleration of about 0.01 mm yr⁻².
- The budget closes: steric 1.5, glaciers 0.74, Greenland 0.69, Antarctica 0.38, land water 0.39 mm yr⁻¹, matching the altimeter.
- One millimetre of sea level is 361 Gt of water; Greenland holds 7.4 m and the Antarctic Ice Sheet 58 m of equivalent.
- Sea-level rise is not uniform: gravitational, rotational and deformational fingerprints make it two to four times the global mean on coasts far from the melting ice.
- Tide gauges measure relative change and must be corrected for isostatic adjustment and for human subsidence, which on deltaic coasts exceeds the ocean rise severalfold.
- Extreme flooding frequency grows roughly exponentially with mean sea level, so the first decimetres cost more than the metres that follow.
- The unresolved term is marine ice-sheet instability; whether it operates this century is a fracture-mechanics question the observations have not yet answered.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Glaciers to millimetres {level=1 check="0.15"}
Mountain glaciers lose $55\ \mathrm{Gt\,yr^{-1}}$. Find their sea-level contribution.
::: solution
$55/361 = 0.15\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$. Fifty-five gigatonnes — twenty Great Lakes worth of ice every twenty years — is a sixth of a millimetre, and the world's glaciers are still the second-largest single contributor to sea-level rise after the ocean's own expansion.
:::

::: exercise Steric rise {level=1 check="63"}
Find the steric rise if the top **1000 m** of the ocean warms by $0.3\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ ($\alpha = 2.1\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$).
::: solution
$\Delta h = 2.1\times10^{-4}\times0.3\times1000 = 0.063\ \mathrm{m} = 63\ \mathrm{mm}$. Two-thirds of a decimetre from a third of a degree spread over the whole upper ocean — which is why the ocean heat content, not the surface temperature, is the honest measure of how much warming has been committed.
:::
:::

::: exercise The implied warming {level=2 check="0.01"}
The observed upper-ocean steric rise is $1.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ over $700\ \mathrm{m}$. Find the implied warming rate.
::: solution
$\partial T/\partial t = 1.4\times10^{-3}/(2.1\times10^{-4}\times700) = 0.0095 \approx 0.01\ \mathrm{^\circ C\,yr^{-1}}$. One hundredth of a degree a year, over seven hundred metres, over three quarters of the planet — and that imperceptible number is one third of all sea-level rise and the reason the rise cannot be stopped on any human timescale.
:::
:::

::: exercise Correcting a gauge {level=2 check="2.3"}
A deltaic tide gauge records $6.2\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$; GPS shows $3.5\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ of anthropogenic subsidence and models give $0.4\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ of glacial isostatic adjustment. Find the eustatic rise.
::: solution
$6.2 - 3.5 - 0.4 = 2.3\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$. The climate signal is a thirty-seven-per-cent fraction of the measured signal, and the residents experience the whole $6.2$. On this coast stopping groundwater extraction buys more time than any emission scenario will.
:::

::: exercise Flood frequency {level=2 check="20"}
With $x_0 = 0.20\ \mathrm{m}$, by what factor does the frequency of the present hundred-year flood increase with $0.6\ \mathrm{m}$ of sea-level rise?
::: solution
$\exp(0.6/0.20) = e^{3} = 20$ — a hundred-year event every five years. This is the arithmetic behind every coastal building code currently being rewritten, and the reason the insurance market left Florida, Louisiana and north Queensland before the engineers did.
:::

::: exercise Greenland's mass {level=2 check="2.7"}
The Greenland Ice Sheet holds $7.4\ \mathrm{m}$ of sea-level equivalent. Find its mass in $10^{21}\ \mathrm{kg}$.
::: solution
$7400\ \mathrm{mm}\times361\ \mathrm{Gt\,mm^{-1}} = 2.67\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{Gt} = 2.7\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{kg}$. The actual estimate is $2.7\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{kg}$ — the conversion is exact by construction, which is the point: sea-level equivalent is not a model output, it is a unit, and any ice volume can be converted without a single assumption about the future.
:::

::: exercise Closing the budget {level=3 check="0.39"}
The observed rise is $3.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$; the steric, glacier, Greenland and Antarctic terms are $1.5$, $0.74$, $0.69$ and $0.38$. Find the required land-water term.
::: solution
$3.7 - 3.31 = 0.39\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$. The residual is positive, meaning water is moving from land to ocean — groundwater pumping dominates, partly offset by water stored behind reservoirs. That $0.39$ is one of the most remarkable numbers in environmental science: human water extraction is now a tenth of all sea-level rise, comparable to the Antarctic Ice Sheet, and it is almost entirely irreversible because pumped groundwater is not returned.
:::

::: exercise A century of rise {level=3 check="1.7"}
Global mean sea level rose $200\ \mathrm{mm}$ between 1900 and 2020. Find the mean rate, and the ratio to the 2006–2018 rate of $3.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$200/120 = 1.7\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, and $3.7/1.7 = 2.2$. The rate has more than doubled in a century. The ratio is the whole argument for non-linearity, and the whole argument against extrapolating the 1900–2020 mean to 2100: a linear projection from the twentieth century gives $17\ \mathrm{cm}$ by 2100, less than half what has already occurred in the last fifteen years alone, and wrong by a factor of four against every process-based projection.
:::
