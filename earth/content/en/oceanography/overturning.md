The deep ocean is not a cellar but a conveyor: water that sank in the Labrador, Greenland and Weddell seas two thousand years ago is now rising, warm and oxygen-poor, into the North Pacific, and the twenty sverdrups that leave the Atlantic as North Atlantic Deep Water are the reason the North Atlantic is warm, the Southern Ocean is fertile, and the atmosphere's carbon dioxide is twenty per cent lower than it would otherwise be. This chapter is about how the deep layers are made, named, dated and driven.

## Water masses

::: definition Water masses and the property triangle {#def-watermasses}
A water mass is a body of water with a coherent history, recognised by a narrow range of potential temperature and salinity and bounded by sharp gradients. Because both temperature and salinity change when water is moved adiabatically, the correct density variable is the potential density $\sigma_\theta$ (referenced to the surface) or, better, a neutral density $\gamma^n$ whose surface is everywhere perpendicular to the mean buoyancy flux, so that observed mixing follows it. The canonical water masses of the global ocean, in order of formation, are:

$$
\begin{array}{llccl}
\text{water mass} & \text{source} & \sigma_\theta & \theta\ (^\circ\mathrm{C}) & S \\ \hline
\text{Surface / central} & \text{subtropical subduction} & 20\text{–}26 & 15\text{–}25 & 33\text{–}37 \\
\textbf{AAIW} & \text{subantarctic convergence} & 26.1\text{–}26.9 & 5\text{–}15 & 33.4\text{–}34.3 \\
\text{North Pacific Intermediate} & \text{Sea of Okhotsk} & 26.5\text{–}26.9 & 2\text{–}4 & 33.5\text{–}34.1 \\
\text{Mediterranean / Red Sea} & \text{evaporative basins} & 28.9\text{–}29.4 & 13\text{–}21 & 36\text{–}38.5 \\
\textbf{NADW} & \text{Nordic Seas + DSOW} & 27.65\text{–}28.10 & 2\text{–}4 & 34.7\text{–}35.0 \\
\text{Antarctic Intermediate (deep)} & \text{circumpolar} & 27.0\text{–}27.4 & 0.5\text{–}2 & 33.8\text{–}34.1 \\
\textbf{AABW} & \text{A shelf water, polynyas} & 28.1\text{–}28.35 & -1.9\text{–}1.5 & 34.6\text{–}34.72 \\
\end{array}
$$

The diagnostic tool is the $\theta$–$S$ diagram: because adiabatic motion follows an isopycnal, and because $\sigma_\theta$ varies monotonically along most isopycnals, a $\theta$–$S$ curve is a fingerprint of a water mass, and a straight line between two end-members on it means mixing between them. Wüst's "water mass analysis" of the 1930s identified every layer of the ocean this way from bottles, and the method still underlies every CLIVAR, GEOTRACES and GO-SHIP section.

Two facts organise everything. **The North Atlantic is salty and the North Pacific is fresh** — by **1 PSU** at the surface and by **0.5 PSU** at **2000 m** — because the **Atlantic** loses more water than it gains at **all** latitudes north of **40 °S** while the **Pacific** gains. **And the Atlantic's deep water is warm and oxygen-rich while the Pacific's is cold, oxygen-poor and carbon-rich** — because the Pacific's deep water is the **same water**, **two thousand years older**, having **respired** organic matter across the **Indo-Pacific** without ever touching the air.
:::

::: definition The overturning circulation and its transports {#def-moc}
The meridional overturning circulation is the streamfunction
$$
\Psi(y, z) = \int_{-H}^{z}\int_{x_w}^{x_e} v\, \mathrm{d}x\, \mathrm{d}z ,
$$
whose maxima identify the cells. In the Atlantic there are two: an upper cell of $17$–$19\ \mathrm{Sv}$ at $26.5\ \mathrm{^\circ N}$ carrying NADW south at 1000–4000 m, and a lower cell of $5$–$10\ \mathrm{Sv}$ carrying AABW north along the bottom. The direct measurement by the RAPID/MOCHA array, running continuously since 2004, gives $17 \pm 2\ \mathrm{Sv}$ with no significant trend and a winter-to-summer range of $\pm 5\ \mathrm{Sv}$; the 2009–2010 minimum of $5\ \mathrm{Sv}$ was six months long and was wind, not convection.

The global deep circulation is a single pipe. About $15$–$20\ \mathrm{Sv}$ of NADW forms in the North Atlantic, $25$–$30\ \mathrm{Sv}$ of AABW around Antarctica, and the two fill the Pacific and Indian, which have no deep sources. The deep ocean's radiocarbon age of $1000$–$2500$ years and its CFC age of $50$–$200$ years both imply a global deep-water residence time of about a thousand years, and the same thousand years is the timescale on which the deep ocean buffers atmospheric CO₂ and heat.

The energy is what makes it matter. The Atlantic overturning carries $1.3$–$1.8\ \mathrm{PW}$ of heat northward across $26.5\ \mathrm{^\circ N}$ — a quarter of the total northward heat transport of the hemisphere, and twice what the atmosphere carries at that latitude in the same direction. It also carries salt out of the Atlantic at $1.5\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$, which is why the Atlantic is freshening as the overturning slows.
:::

::: example Identifying a water mass {#ex-watermass}
A profile at 30 °N, 50 °W shows a salinity maximum of 36.4 at $\theta = 20\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ and 800 m, a potential vorticity minimum at $\theta = 26.5$, a temperature minimum of 4 °C with $S = 35.0$ at 2500 m, and an oxygen maximum of 6.5 mL L⁻¹ at 3000 m. Identify the layers and explain the oxygen maximum.
::: solution
The salinity maximum at 800 m is the Mediterranean Underlay ($\sigma_\theta \approx 29.1$, spreading west and then down from the Gibraltar sill). The potential-vorticity minimum at $\theta = 26.5$ is the mode water of the subtropical gyre — the winter mixed-layer outcrop where the central water was last at the surface. The cold, fresh temperature minimum at 2500 m is not NADW but the Deep Western Boundary Current of Labrador Sea Overflow Water, which arrives along the boundary colder and fresher than the interior. The oxygen maximum at 3000 m is the core of the NADW itself.

The oxygen maximum is a clock. NADW leaves the Labrador and Greenland seas near saturation because winter convection ventilates it to the atmosphere; from the moment it sinks its oxygen only decreases, consumed by the respiration of the organic matter it carries. So the oxygen minimum of the North Atlantic off West Africa and the oxygen minimum of the North Pacific are maps of age, and the oxygen maximum at 3000 m at 20 °N marks the spine of the youngest, most recently ventilated water in the Atlantic interior.

The same logic gives the reverse result in the Pacific: its deep water is 4 °C, oxygen-poor ($1.5\ \mathrm{mL\,L^{-1}}$), phosphate-rich and radiocarbon-dead because it is the end of the conveyor — the same water that left the Labrador Sea two millennia ago, having respired every gram of labile organic matter between here and there. The oxygen minimum zones of the tropical Pacific and Atlantic, and the anoxic mid-water of the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal, are therefore not local pollution signals but circulation features — and their observed de-oxygenation over 1960–2020 is a combination of warming-driven solubility loss and stratification-driven reduced ventilation, in roughly equal parts.
:::
:::

::: example Radiocarbon ventilation age {#ex-radiocarbon}
Deep Pacific water at 4000 m has a radiocarbon fraction of 0.74 of the contemporary surface value. Estimate its ventilation age, and explain why the true age is larger.
::: solution
With a radiocarbon mean life of $8033$ years,
$$
\tau = -8033\,\ln(0.74) = 2400\ \mathrm{yr}.
$$
The true ventilation age is larger for two reasons. Marine reservoir: the surface water that sank already carried "old" carbon — the global surface ocean has an apparent $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ age of $\sim 400$ years because it has been isolated from the atmosphere while respiring old organic carbon — so the water was already 400 years "old" at the moment of subduction. Benthic mixing: diffusive exchange with older water above and below dilutes the signal and biases the apparent age young in some places and old in others.

The real value of radiocarbon is spatial, not absolute. The difference between the Atlantic and Pacific deep-water $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ ages — about 800 years in the modern ocean — is a direct measure of how much of the conveyor's ageing happens in which basin, and benthic foraminiferal $\Delta^{14}\mathrm{C}$ shows that during Heinrich event 1, 20,000–14,000 years ago, that contrast grew to $\sim 2000$ years: the Atlantic deep water stopped being ventilated, the Pacific deep water aged further, and the carbon that the Atlantic stopped burying appeared in the atmosphere as the 120-ppm deglacial rise recorded in the EPICA and TUNU ice cores.
:::
:::

::: definition What drives the conveyor: buoyancy loss, mixing and the Southern Ocean {#def-engine}
Three mechanisms supply the energy, and their **relative** importance is the central open question of the subject.

Buoyancy loss at the surface makes dense water. The North Atlantic surrenders $60$–$120\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ to the atmosphere in autumn and winter, most of it by evaporation driven by cold, dry polar-air outbreaks — the process described in [[oceanography/air-sea-fluxes]]. Convection in the Labrador, Irminger, Greenland and Norwegian Seas reaches 1000–2000 m and sets the potential density of the upper NADW. Around Antarctica the mechanism is different: sea-ice formation rejects salt into the under-ice water in coastal polynyas and leads, producing High Salinity Shelf Water that is dense because it is salty rather than because it is cold, and which then cascades down the shelf break to become AABW. Sea ice makes the densest water in the world ocean without cooling it.

Diffusion in the interior lifts the dense water back. Munk's 1966 argument is still the starting point: if abyssal water is denser than thermocline water, then a downward diffusive buoyancy flux $\kappa_z \partial\rho/\partial z$ must be balanced by upwelling $w$ across the thermocline, giving
$$
w \sim \frac{\kappa_z}{h}, \qquad \kappa_z \sim 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-1}}, \quad h \sim 1000\ \mathrm{m} \;\Longrightarrow\; w \sim 6\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 2\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}},
$$
and over the global ocean area of $3.6\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$ an upwelling of $22\ \mathrm{Sv}$ — the right number, obtained from a microstructure measurement of mixing made in a bottle. The complication, discovered over thirty years of tracer releases and microstructure casts, is that $\kappa_z$ is not constant: it is $10^{-5}$ in stratified pelagic clay regions and $10^{-2}$ over rough topography, because internal tides and deep flows break on seamounts, fracture zones and sills. The global mixing is therefore topographically controlled, and the conveyor's strength is in part a property of the seafloor.

The Southern Ocean is where the deep water comes back. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current exposes $30$–$40\ \mathrm{Sv}$ of circumpolar deep and bottom water to the atmosphere between $40\ \mathrm{^\circ S}$ and $65\ \mathrm{^\circ S}$, and the wind drives an Ekman divergence that pulls it to the surface north of the ACC, where it subducts again as mode and intermediate water. Crucially, eddies carry much of the transport: the eddy-induced (Bolus) velocity cancels most of the wind-driven isopycnal displacement, so that increasing the wind saturates the upwelling — a phenomenon called eddy saturation and the reason coupled models disagree about how the conveyor will respond to a strengthening Southern Hemisphere westerly.

The stability problem was posed by Stommel in 1961 and has never been closed. A two-box model with a transport proportional to the density difference and a freshwater forcing $F$ at high latitude has steady states
$$
F = m(1 - m), \qquad m = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4F}}{2},
$$
where $m$ is the dimensionless haline contrast. For $F < 1/4$ there are two equilibria — a strongly overturning "thermal" state and a weakly overturning "haline" state — and the salt-advection feedback (a stronger flow carries more salt north, which increases the density contrast, which strengthens the flow) makes the good state self-reinforcing and the bad state self-trapping. The model is crude, but it captures the only mechanism the palaeoclimate record actually uses: the conveyor can be switched off, and it takes a large, sustained freshwater anomaly to switch it back on.
:::

::: example The heat transported by the overturning {#ex-heat}
The Atlantic overturning at $26.5\ \mathrm{^\circ N}$ carries $1.5\ \mathrm{PW}$ northward with a total transport of $18\ \mathrm{Sv}$. Find the effective temperature difference between the upper and lower limbs, and compare it with the observed values.
::: solution
With $\rho c_p = 4.08\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$,
$$
\Delta T = \frac{H}{\rho c_p \mathcal{M}} = \frac{1.5\times10^{15}}{4.08\times10^{6}\times1.8\times10^{7}} = 20\ \mathrm{K}.
$$
The observed upper limb at $26.5\ \mathrm{^\circ N}$ is $21\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ and the lower limb $4\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, a difference of $17\ \mathrm{K}$. The remaining $3\ \mathrm{K}$ is made up by air–sea heat loss from the upper limb as it flows north and by the north Atlantic Current extension — that is, the overturning does not merely carry heat, it carries heat that it is losing on the way, which is exactly why the heat is released over the Nordic Seas and not at $26.5\ \mathrm{^\circ N}$.

The number to remember is that $1.5\ \mathrm{PW}$ is about one per cent of the total northward energy the Earth moves across the equator, and that the atmosphere carries a comparable amount in the opposite direction at the same latitude. The two are coupled: if the overturning weakens by $5\ \mathrm{Sv}$, the atmosphere takes up most of the difference, and the surface warming of the subpolar North Atlantic is much smaller than the ocean-only argument suggests. This is the single most important reason the "AMOC collapse freezes Europe" scenario is not what models produce — the real signal is a regional cooling of $1$–$3\ \mathrm{K}$ over the subpolar Atlantic, a southward shift of the tropical rain belts, and an amplified sea-level rise along the North American east coast from the gravitational and dynamic response of the sea surface.
:::
:::

::: example The Stommel two-box model {#ex-stommel}
In the **two-box** model the steady states satisfy $F = m(1-m)$, where $m$ is the dimensionless **haline** contrast and $F$ the dimensionless high-latitude **freshwater** forcing. Find the equilibria for $F = 0.16$, and the value of $F$ at which the overturning state disappears.
::: solution
$$
m^{2} - m + 0.16 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; m = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 0.64}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm 0.60}{2} = 0.20 \;\text{or}\; 0.80 .
$$
The **low**-$m$ branch ($m = 0.20$) has a **transport** $1 - m = 0.80$ and is the **overturning** state; the **high**-$m$ branch ($m = 0.80$) has a transport of **$0.20$** and is the **stalled**, **salt-stratified** state. The **discriminant** vanishes at $4F = 1$, i.e. $F_c = 0.25$: **above a freshwater forcing of one quarter of the thermal forcing, no overturning state exists at all.**

The hysteresis is the point. Starting from the overturning state, increasing $F$ past $0.25$ forces a jump to $m = 0.80$ and a collapse of the transport from $0.80$ to $0.20$; but reducing $F$ back to zero does not restore the original state — the system stays trapped on the haline branch until $F$ falls far below the value at which it left. The transition is irreversible over a wide range of forcing, and this is the mathematical content of every claim that the AMOC has a "tipping point".

The honest assessment is that no model of intermediate or full complexity produces a clean fold in the modern climate, and that the IPCC AR6 assessment of "likely" continued weakening through the twenty-first century with "very unlikely" abrupt collapse rests on hundreds of simulations in which the overturning slows by $10$–$40$ per cent and never crosses a threshold. The counter-argument — that all such models are tuned to be stable and that the palaeoclimate record contains at least twenty-five abrupt D/O-scale transitions — has not been refuted, and remains the sharpest open question in climate physics.
:::

::: warning Water masses are conveniences, the conveyor is a metaphor, and both are being pushed past their limits
Six cautions. Water masses are not conserved. Every $\theta$–$S curve is a snapshot of a balance between formation, mixing and advection; the Mediterranean Underlay loses half its salinity anomaly within 2000 km of Gibraltar, the NADW core splits into DSOW, LSOW and ISOW that mix with each other within a basin, and AABW is unrecognisable in the Canada Basin. The property triangle is degenerate. Two different histories can give the same $\theta$ and $S$ — the Nordic Seas and the Weddell Sea both produce cold, fresh deep water — so water-mass analysis must be backed by oxygen, silicate, CFCs or isotopes, and Wüst's method alone cannot close a budget. The conveyor is not a belt. Water does not flow around a loop; it sinks in two places, spreads three-dimensionally, upwells mostly in the Southern Ocean and through boundary-mixing, and returns by routes that take anywhere from a decade to three thousand years. The metaphor is useful for carbon and misleading for heat, ecology and timing. The measured transport is not the climate signal. The RAPID record's $\pm 5\ \mathrm{Sv}$ seasonal swing and its 2009–2010 six-month minimum are wind-driven Ekman and boundary effects, not convection; the climate signal is a decadal mean, and thirty years of record is at the edge of detecting one. The models are not the ocean. Every CMIP model produces an AMOC, but their control climates sit off the observed $\theta$–$S$  curve by enough to change the sensitivity, and the observed "cold blob" of 1998–2015 is reproduced by some models as a conveyor slowdown and by others as a random ocean fluctuation. And the freshwater forcing is now real. The subpolar Atlantic has freshened by $\sim 0.15\ \mathrm{PSU}$ since 1900, the Greenland Ice Sheet delivers $\sim 0.04\ \mathrm{Sv}$ of meltwater and the Arctic river discharge has risen by $\sim 10$ per cent — small against the $\sim 0.5\ \mathrm{Sv}$ of total freshwater input, large against the $\sim 0.1\ \mathrm{Sv}$ threshold that box models say matters, and the reason the question Stommel asked in 1961 is still open.
:::
::: history
Knut Wüst (Wüst) published in 1936 the first water-mass analysis of the Atlantic, tracing every layer from the Arctic to the Antarctic bottom from bottle data alone and describing the deep ocean as a single ventilated system — the conveyor idea, forty-five years before the word. Henry Stommel (Stommel) wrote *"Thermohaline convection with two stable regimes of flow"* in 1961, a four-page paper with two boxes and no space dependence that showed the overturning can have two equilibria; Kirk Bryan (Bryan) extended it to basin models in 1969, and Henning Rooth (Rooth) gave the freshwater-perturbation version in 1982 that every AMOC-collapse paper still cites. Wallace Broecker (Broecker) named the "great conveyor belt" in 1991 after a decade of carbon-cycle argument, and with Tai-Lai Peng (Peng) wrote *Tracers in the Sea* in 1982, the book that made phosphate, alkalinity, radiocarbon and tritium into circulation instruments. Michael Swallow's parachute floats of 1955 and the deep western boundary current arrays that followed established that the return flow is boundary-hugging and deep. Harry Bryden (Bryden) and John Marotzke (Marotzke) measured the overturning at 24.5 °N geostrophically through the 1990s, and Stuart Cunningham, Tor Kanzow, Johannes Karsten, Susan Wijffels, Molly Baringer and Marotzke deployed the RAPID/MOCHA array at 26.5 °N in 2004, producing the first continuous record and the 17 ± 2 Sv value with its 2009–2010 minimum. Lutz Speer (Speer) and Eli Tziperman (Tziperman) mapped the rates of water-mass formation in 1992, and the CLIVAR and GO-SHIP sections mapped its chemical properties. James Renwick, Jean Jouzel and Edward Brook and the EPICA team matched those sections to the ice-core carbon dioxide record. Jerry Toggweiler (Toggweiler), Jeffrey Russell (Russell) and Broecker argued in 2006 that the Southern Ocean, not the North Atlantic, controls the conveyor's strength and the glacial carbon-dioxide drawdown — a claim that reorganised the field and that the OSNAP, SOCCOM and SIMAX arrays of the 2010s were built to test. Lloyd Keigwin (Keigwin) and Michael Sarnthein (Sarnthein) documented the abrupt Atlantic reorganisations of the last deglaciation from benthic $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ and protactinium, and Jerry Adkins (Adkins) used radiocarbon on corals and foraminifera to show that carbon accumulated in the glacial Pacific while the Atlantic stalled.
:::

## Where this leads

The **buoyancy** that makes deep water is removed in [[oceanography/air-sea-fluxes]], the **density** surfaces it follows are defined in [[oceanography/seawater]], the **wind** that fills the thermocline is [[oceanography/wind-driven]], the **mixing** that lifts it back is driven by the **internal waves** of [[oceanography/waves-tides]], the **carbon** it sequesters is [[oceanography/ocean-chemistry]] and [[oceanography/productivity]], the **heat** it carries is [[climate-system/heat-transport]] and [[climate-system/energy-budget]], its **collapse** is the canonical **abrupt** change of [[palaeoclimate/abrupt-change]] and [[palaeoclimate/ocean-sediments]], its **future** is [[climate-change/projections]] and [[climate-change/tipping-points]], its **sea-surface** fingerprint is [[oceanography/sea-level]] and [[climate-change/sea-level-rise]], its **freshwater** source is [[glaciology/ice-sheets]] and [[hydrology/water-balance]], and its **opposite** state is [[palaeoclimate/deep-time]].

::: quiz
Why is deep Pacific water older, colder, more oxygen-poor and more carbon-rich than deep Atlantic water?
- [ ] Because the Pacific is deeper and more enclosed.
- [x] Because it is the same water, thousands of years further along the conveyor, having respired organic matter without re-ventilating.
- [ ] Because the Pacific receives more river carbon.
- [ ] Because the Pacific has no wind mixing.
::: solution
The Pacific has no deep source. Its deep water is NADW and AABW, two thousand years older, having respired organic matter continuously and never re-contacted the atmosphere. Oxygen and radiocarbon both only decrease after ventilation, so they are clocks, and the Pacific is simply older.
:::

::: summary
- Water masses are defined by potential temperature and salinity, identified on θ–S diagrams, and named for where they sank; there are only about eight of global importance.
- The North Atlantic Deep Water cell carries 17 ± 2 Sv and the Antarctic Bottom Water cell 5–10 Sv; together they ventilate the entire world ocean on a thousand-year timescale.
- Deep water is made by buoyancy loss — winter cooling and evaporation in the North Atlantic, and salt rejection by sea-ice formation around Antarctica.
- The deep water returns to the surface by mixing at about 2 m yr⁻¹, set by the diffusivity over the thermocline depth, and mostly in the Southern Ocean.
- The overturning carries 1.3–1.8 PW of heat northward in the Atlantic, an amount the atmosphere largely offsets, so its weakening cools the subpolar Atlantic only slightly.
- Stommel's two-box model has two equilibria below a critical freshwater forcing and an irreversible collapse above it; whether the real AMOC has such a threshold is unresolved.
- Oxygen and radiocarbon are clocks: the deep Pacific's 2400-year radiocarbon age is the age of the conveyor itself.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise The overturning temperature difference {level=1 check="16"}
The Atlantic overturning carries **1.3 PW** with a transport of **20 Sv**. Find the effective temperature difference between the limbs ($\rho c_p = 4.08\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$).
::: solution
$\Delta T = 1.3\times10^{15}/(4.08\times10^{6}\times2\times10^{7}) = 16\ \mathrm{K}$. This is **precisely** the observed contrast between the **$21\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$** upper limb and the **$4\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$** deep limb — one of the few places in climate science where a **transport**, a **flux** and a **temperature** close an **arithmetic** identity without a free parameter.
:::
:::

::: exercise Munk upwelling {level=1 check="3.15"}
Find the diffusive upwelling velocity in $\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$ for $\kappa_z = 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-1}}$ and a thermocline depth of **1000 m**.
::: solution
$w = \kappa_z/h = 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 3.15\ \mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$. Three metres a year of **uniform** upwelling over the whole planet — the **Munk** estimate of **1966**, obtained from a **single** number that was not even **measured** until **1990s** microstructure casts confirmed it to within a **factor of two**.
:::
:::

::: exercise The global upwelling {level=2 check="36"}
Take $w = 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ over the global ocean area of $3.6\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$. Find the upwelling in sverdrups.
::: solution
$3.6\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,s^{-1}} = 36\ \mathrm{Sv}$. This is twice the observed overturning, and the discrepancy is the whole content of Southern Ocean dynamics: eddy-induced bolus transport cancels much of the wind-driven residual, so the net upwelling is less than the local Ekman value and the mixing estimate is an upper bound.
:::
:::

::: exercise A radiocarbon age {level=2 check="1790"}
A deep-water sample has a radiocarbon fraction of **0.80** of the surface value. Find its ventilation age (mean life 8033 yr).
::: solution
$\tau = -8033\ln(0.80) = 1790\ \mathrm{yr}$. Add $\sim 400$ years for the marine reservoir age of the surface water that sank, and correct for bombs and fossil-fuel dilution in the modern ocean, and you have the age that Argo-era geochemistry reports for the intermediate waters of the North Pacific — and roughly double it for the abyss.
:::
:::

::: exercise The Stommel threshold {level=2 check="0.3"}
In the two-box model with $F = 0.21$, find the salinity contrast $m$ of the **strongly** overturning branch.
::: solution
$m = \left[1 - \sqrt{1 - 4(0.21)}\right]/2 = (1 - 0.40)/2 = 0.3$, with a transport of $1 - m = 0.7$. The other root, $m = 0.7$ with a transport of 0.3, is the stalled state. At $F = 0.25$ the two merge and the overturning branch ceases to exist — the mathematical skeleton of every "climate tipping point" argument in the literature.
:::
:::

::: exercise The deep-ocean residence time {level=2 check="2000"}
The ocean below **1000 m** holds $1.25\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$. Find the residence time in years for a total deep-water removal of **20 Sv**.
::: solution
$1.25\times10^{18}/(2\times10^{7}) = 6.3\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{s} = 2000\ \mathrm{yr}$. This is the same as the radiocarbon age of the deep Pacific to within the reservoir correction — one of the most satisfying consistencies in earth science: a flow rate measured by current meters and an age measured by nuclear physics agree, which is why the conveyor metaphor survived thirty years of attacks on it.
:::

::: exercise Polynya dense-water formation {level=3 check="12"}
Antarctic coastal polynyas covering $4\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$ produce **4 m yr⁻¹** of new ice. If freezing water of salinity **34.5** rejects all its salt into water made **1.5 PSU** saltier, find the dense-water formation rate in sverdrups.
::: solution
Salt balance gives $V = V_{\mathrm{ice}}\,S/\Delta S$:
$$
V = 4\times10^{12}\times4\times\frac{34.5}{1.5} = 3.7\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,yr^{-1}} = 1.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,s^{-1}} = 12\ \mathrm{Sv}.
$$
The observed AABW formation rate is $10$–$30\ \mathrm{Sv}$. Note what this means: the densest water in the world ocean is made by freezing seawater, not by cooling it — the water under Antarctic sea ice is dense because ice is fresh, and if sea ice were salt the conveyor's lower limb would not exist.

::: exercise The freshening threshold {level=3 check="0.25"}
A box model has a present dimensionless freshwater forcing of $F = 0.18$. By what factor must $F$ increase for the overturning state to disappear?
::: solution
The critical value is $F_c = 0.25$, so the factor is $0.25/0.18 = 1.4$ — a forty-per-cent increase in high-latitude freshwater input. The subpolar Atlantic freshening of 1900–2020 is equivalent to a five-to-ten-per-cent change in $F$, and the projected Greenland plus Arctic river increase by 2100 is another ten-to-twenty per cent. On this model the twenty-first century is not enough to cross the threshold — which is exactly the kind of reassuring arithmetic that Stommel's hysteresis makes untrustworthy, because the distance to a tipping point measured in a stable model is not a measure of stability.
