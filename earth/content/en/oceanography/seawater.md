Seawater is a **solution whose composition has been buffered by four billion years of reaction with rock, life and atmosphere**, and its two numbers — **salinity 35** and **density 1027 kg m⁻³** — do almost all the mechanical work in oceanography. What makes it interesting is that it is **almost but not quite** like fresh water: it **freezes at −1.9 °C**, its **maximum density lies below its freezing point**, its **density depends on temperature and salinity in almost cancelling ways**, and its **sound speed has a minimum at a kilometre's depth**. Every one of those four quirks has a global consequence.

## What seawater is

::: definition Salinity, and why the ocean is salty {#def-salinity}
**Forchhammer's principle of constant proportions** (1865), confirmed in the **Challenger** titrations of **Dittmar** (Dittmar) in 1884, is that the **relative** proportions of the major ions are **constant** everywhere in the open ocean, so that **one measurement — chlorinity, or since 1978 electrical conductivity — fixes them all**:
$$
\begin{array}{l|c|c|c}
\text{ion} & \text{g kg}^{-1} & \text{mmol kg}^{-1} & \text{residence time} \\ \hline
\mathrm{Cl^{-}} & 19.35 & 545.9 & 80\ \mathrm{Myr} \\
\mathrm{Na^{+}} & 10.76 & 467.8 & 65\text{–}90\ \mathrm{Myr} \\
\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} & 2.71 & 28.2 & 3\text{–}8\ \mathrm{Myr} \\
\mathrm{Mg^{2+}} & 1.29 & 53.1 & 12\ \mathrm{Myr} \\
\mathrm{Ca^{2+}} & 0.41 & 10.3 & 1\ \mathrm{Myr} \\
\mathrm{K^{+}} & 0.39 & 10.2 & 11\ \mathrm{Myr} \\
\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}} & 0.14 & 2.3 & 0.1\ \mathrm{Myr}
\end{array}
$$
Six ions are 99.3 per cent of the salt. Chloride and sodium are 86 per cent, which is why the ocean tastes of brine and why chlorinity was the first practical measure. The residence time — the inventory divided by the river input — spans five orders of magnitude, and that spread is the geochemistry: sodium and chloride are conservative because nothing removes them fast, calcium is not because carbonate shells and hydrothermal exchange remove it in a million years, and bicarbonate is not because life and calcification remove it in a hundred thousand.

The ocean is salty and the rivers are fresh because the two are in different steady states. Rivers carry the products of continental weathering — mostly bicarbonate, calcium and silica — and the ocean precipitates those back out as carbonate and silica and clay, while chloride and sodium have no sink except evaporite deposition in restricted basins and clay authigenesis in marine muds. The steady state is set by hydrothermal circulation at mid-ocean ridges, which removes magnesium, sulphate and sulphate-derived sulphur from circulating seawater, adds calcium, iron, manganese, lithium and heat, and converts the incoming sodium chloride solution into the acid, metal-rich fluid that vents at 350 °C. The chloride inventory of the ocean is old — its residence time is 80 Myr, so the ocean's chloride is a ten-million-year average of the planet's weathering and hydrothermal flux, which is why chlorine isotopes are a long-term tectonic tracer and magnesium isotopes a short-term weathering one.

The definition has changed twice. Practical Salinity $S_P$ (1978) is a dimensionless ratio of conductivities calibrated against a standard potassium chloride solution, and it is not a mass fraction; TEOS-10 (2010) introduced Absolute Salinity $S_A$ in $\mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}$, which does account for the boron, calcium and alkalinity variations that make $S_P$ and true salt content differ by up to 0.15 g kg⁻¹ — a small correction that matters for freshwater-balance and carbon-uptake work in the Arctic, the Baltic and the Black Sea.
:::

::: definition Density, and the two anomalies that run the climate {#def-density}
Seawater density is a function of salinity, temperature and pressure, $\rho = \rho(S,\theta,p)$, given by the UNESCO 1983 and TEOS-10 equations of state. Near the ocean's usual conditions a linearised form is accurate to 0.05 kg m⁻³:
$$
\mathrm{d}\rho = \rho\left(\beta_S\,\mathrm{d}S - \alpha_T\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right), \qquad
\beta_S = 7.6\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg\,kg^{-1}\text{-}PSU^{-1}}, \qquad
\alpha_T = (1.5\text{–}2.5)\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}},
$$
so **a 1 PSU increase in salinity is worth 0.78 kg m⁻³ and a 1 °C increase in temperature is worth −0.21 kg m⁻³**, and the **compensation ratio** $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}\theta = \alpha_T/\beta_S \approx 0.26\ \mathrm{PSU\,^\circ C^{-1}}$ defines the **spice** direction along which density does **not** change. Two consequences dominate physical oceanography. **First**, because $\alpha_T$ **falls steeply with temperature** — it is **$7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$ at 4 °C** and **$2.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$ at 25 °C** — **the same heat flux warms and lightens high-latitude water far more than tropical water**, and **cold water is compressible in a way warm water is not**. **Second**, because **mixing two samples of seawater is always denser than the average of the two** — the equation of state is **convex** — **cabbeling** produces dense water wherever two water masses mix, at **subduction fronts**, **estuarine fronts**, **brinicle** formation and **polar leads**, and this is a **real** density source that no heat or freshwater flux supplies.

The two anomalies are the ones that decide whether the planet is habitable. For fresh water the temperature of maximum density ($3.98\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$) lies above the freezing point ($0\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$), so a cooling lake turns over and then stops, freezes from the top, and its bottom stays at 4 °C — which is why lakes freeze over and fish survive. For seawater at S = 35 the freezing point is $-1.93\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ and the temperature of maximum density is $-3.5\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, i.e. below the freezing point, so cooling always makes seawater denser right down to the ice point: the ocean can and does convect to two and three kilometres in a North Atlantic or Weddell Sea winter, and this is the engine of the overturning circulation. The two behaviours cross at $S \approx 24.7\ \mathrm{PSU}$ — the salinity of a brackish basin — which is why the Baltic (7 PSU) and the Black Sea (18 PSU) stratify on winter cooling like lakes and stagnate, while the Mediterranean (38 PSU) and the Nordic Seas (35 PSU) ventilate.
:::

::: example Density, steric height and compensation {#ex-density}
A column of the upper **1000 m** warms by **1.0 °C** with no change in salinity. Take $\bar{\alpha}_T = 1.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$ and $\rho_0 = 1027\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Find the change in density, the change in **steric** sea surface height, and the **salinity increase** that would exactly cancel the warming.
::: solution
$$
\Delta\rho = -\rho_0\,\alpha_T\,\Delta\theta = -1027\times1.5\times10^{-4}\times1.0 = -0.154\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},
$$
and because the **mass** of the column is unchanged, the **same specific volume increase integrated over the layer** gives
$$
\Delta\eta = \int_0^{1000}\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho_0}\,\mathrm{d}z = \alpha_T\,\Delta\theta\,\times 1000 = 1.5\times10^{-4}\times1000 = 0.15\ \mathrm{m}.
$$
Warming the top kilometre by **1 °C** raises the sea surface by **15 cm**. For the compensation,
$$
\Delta S = \frac{\alpha_T}{\beta_S}\,\Delta\theta = \frac{1.5\times10^{-4}}{7.6\times10^{-4}}\times1.0 = 0.20\ \mathrm{PSU}.
$$

The 15 cm is the reason thermal expansion accounted for about 42 per cent of the twentieth-century rise in global mean sea level and steric change for more than half of the rise in the 1993–2010 altimeter record — and the reason the regional pattern is so uneven: the same heat raises the sea more where the water is warm, because $\alpha_T$ is three times larger at 25 °C than at 4 °C, so the subtropical and tropics steric signal is larger than the subpolar for the same subsurface warming.

The 0.20 PSU is the reason salt and temperature are not independent tracers. In the thermocline the $\theta$–$S$ relationship is tight because both are set by the same subduction, but in the interior the two fields are compensated — warm and salty next to cold and fresh — along spice and staircase structure that carries no density signal, does not show up in geostrophic velocity, and does carry heat, carbon and tracers. A profile that shows compensating temperature and salinity is telling you that mixing, not advection, made the structure, and that is the diagnosis that separates a front from a staircase.
:::

::: definition Sound speed, light and heat: the properties that are measured {#def-properties}
Four **transport** properties carry most of the instrumentation of the subject.

Sound speed. The McKenzie–Chen–Millero or UNESCO formula gives, to about 1 m s⁻¹,
$$
c = 1449.2 + 4.6\theta - 0.055\theta^{2} + 0.00029\theta^{3} + (1.34 - 0.010\theta)(S - 35) + 0.016\,z,
$$
with $c$ in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\theta$ in $^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $z$ in metres of depth. Temperature raises it by about 3 m s⁻¹ per °C near the surface, salinity by 14 m s⁻¹ per PSU, and pressure by 16 m s⁻¹ per kilometre. Because temperature falls with depth and pressure rises, the two effects cancel near 1000 m and the sound speed passes through a minimum — the SOFAR or deep sound channel, at 1000 m in the tropics and at 1000–1500 m at high latitude. Sound refracts toward the axis and is trapped, so a low-frequency source can be detected across an ocean basin: this is the basis of SOFAR sonar, of acoustic thermometry (the ACSYAD and ATOC experiments measured basin-average temperature change from fixed sources), of whale communication, and of the infrasonic detection of nuclear tests.

Light. Seawater absorbs red first and infrared immediately, so the euphotic zone — the 1 per cent light depth — is 200 m in the open ocean and 20 m in a productive coastal water, and the colour of the water is a measurement of its chlorophyll: the SeaWiFS, MODIS, VIIRS and Sentinel-3 algorithms retrieve chlorophyll-a from the blue-to-green radiance ratio to about 20 per cent accuracy, and the deep chlorophyll maximum at 200–500 m — the shallowest and most ubiquitous feature in the ocean, discovered by shipboard profiles in 1955 and invisible from space — holds much of the ocean's biomass.

Heat and salt. The specific heat is $c_p = 3993\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ at $S = 35$, falling with salinity and temperature; the latent heat of evaporation is $2.45\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ at $20\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, about 4 per cent less than fresh water, which is why the bulk formulae for latent heat flux carry a salinity-dependent saturation vapour pressure; and the thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha_T$ and the haline contraction coefficient $\beta_S$ are the two coefficients that turn temperature and salinity into density, pressure and sea level.

Gases. The solubility of oxygen falls from 290 µmol kg⁻¹ at 0 °C to 205 µmol kg⁻¹ at 25 °C — a 40 per cent drop over the ocean's surface range — while temperature also slows the biological demand, so the solubility pump loads cold high-latitude water with oxygen and delivers it to the interior, and the apparent oxygen utilisation — saturation minus observed — is the clock that reads the water's age since ventilation. The noble gases helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon are biologically inert, have known solubilities and known atmospheric ratios, and are therefore the only direct palaeothermometer of seawater temperature: trapped xenon and krypton in Eocene foraminifera gave a sea-surface temperature of 36 °C — a startling and much-contested result — and the method's precision is about 2 °C with a reach of some 120 Myr, limited by the preservation of the trapped gas and by the assumption that the palaeo-atmospheric noble-gas ratios were modern.
:::

::: example Sound speed and the SOFAR channel {#ex-sound}
Using the empirical formula, find the sound speed at (a) the **surface** in the **tropics** ($\theta = 28\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, $S = 35$, $z = 0$), (b) the **thermocline** base ($\theta = 4\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, $S = 35$, $z = 0$), (c) **1000 m** ($\theta = 4\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, $S = 34.7$, $z = 1000$), and (d) **4000 m** ($\theta = 2\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, $S = 34.7$, $z = 4000$). Where is the axis?
::: solution
$$
\text{(a)}\ c = 1449.2 + 128.8 - 43.1 + 6.4 + 0 + 0 = 1541\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
$$
$$
\text{(b)}\ c = 1449.2 + 18.4 - 0.88 + 0.02 - 0 + 0 = 1467\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
$$
$$
\text{(c)}\ c = 1467 - 4 + 16 = 1479\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
$$
$$
\text{(d)}\ c = 1449.2 + 9.2 - 0.22 + 0.002 - 4 + 64 = 1518\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} .
$$
The minimum is at 1000 m, at 1479 m s⁻¹, 62 m s⁻¹ below the surface value and 39 m s⁻¹ below the 4000-m value: the SOFAR axis.

The asymmetry is the useful part. The surface-to-axis gradient is three times the axis-to-bottom gradient, so the channel is leaky upward and trapping downward — a source near the surface leaks out of the channel within a few hundred kilometres, while a source on the axis propagates thousands. That is why ACSYAD and ATOC put their transponders on the axis and their receivers on continental slopes, and why the sound channel is the cheapest thermometer ever built: a 1-m s⁻¹ change in channel-averaged sound speed corresponds to about 0.3 °C of basin-average warming of the upper 1000 m, and the 1990s acoustic measurements agreed with the Argo float record to within its uncertainty.

The salinity term is the small one and the most neglected: a 1-PSU change is 14 m s⁻¹, comparable to a 4.5-°C temperature change, and in the Arctic, where meltwater and river input create 30-PSU surface water, the salinity term dominates the shallow sound-speed profile and controls submarine acoustics, sonar performance and underwater navigation.
:::
:::

::: example The salt budget and the residence time {#ex-salt}
The ocean contains **10.76 g kg⁻¹** of sodium, has a mass of $1.37\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{kg}$, and receives sodium from rivers at a mean concentration of **6.3 g m⁻³** in a discharge of $3.6\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,yr^{-1}}$. Find the sodium residence time, and explain why the **true** value is longer.
::: solution
$$
M_{\mathrm{Na}} = 10.76\times1.37\times10^{21} = 1.47\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{kg}, \qquad
F_{\mathrm{Na}} = 6.3\times3.6\times10^{13} = 2.3\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g\,yr^{-1}} = 2.3\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{kg\,yr^{-1}},
$$
$$
\tau_{\mathrm{Na}} = \frac{1.47\times10^{19}}{2.3\times10^{11}} = 6.5\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{yr} = 65\ \mathrm{Myr}.
$$
The commonly quoted value of 90 Myr is larger because the modern river flux is not the long-term one: glacial-interglacial cycles change the weathering rate by a factor of two to three, evaporite deposition in restricted basins — the Messinian, the Persian Gulf, the Great Salt Lake — removes a tenth of the input, and reverse weathering — the authigenesis of clay in marine sediments, which takes up sodium and potassium from pore water — is a sink comparable to evaporites and is almost never measured.

The 65 Myr is the number that makes seawater a geochemical archive. Because the residence time is longer than the mixing time of the deep ocean (about 1000 years) by five orders of magnitude, the ocean is a well-stirred beaker whose composition is the global, millennial average of all inputs and outputs — which is why strontium isotopes in seawater give a global weathering signal from any carbonate that grew in it, and why the Cenozoic rise of $^{87}\mathrm{Sr}/^{86}\mathrm{Sr}$ from 0.7070 to 0.7092 is read as the Himalayan and Tibetan uplift record. And it is why a local pollution signal never survives to the open ocean: everything that enters the sea is averaged over ten million years of chloride, and the only tracers that show where they came from are the ones with short residence times — radium (weeks), radon (days), carbon-14 (5730 years) and the anthropogenic chlorofluorocarbons (decades).
:::
:::

::: warning A bottle of seawater is not a constant, and the four assumptions that break
Constant proportions is a good approximation and a bad exact one. The major-ion ratios vary by 0.1–0.5 per cent — enough to matter for salinity-from-chlorinity work in estuaries, pore waters and brine — and the boron, calcium and alkalinity variations that motivated Absolute Salinity reach 0.15 g kg⁻¹ in the Arctic, the Baltic and the Black Sea, where river and ice inputs carry non-seawater ions. Practical Salinity is not a salinity. $S_P$ is a dimensionless conductivity ratio, and converting it to a mass fraction without the TEOS-10 correction introduces systematic errors of up to 0.4 per cent in freshwater-balance and air–sea CO₂ calculations — an error larger than the climate signal in the Arctic freshening literature. Density is computed, not measured, at the top of the list. A CTD measures conductivity, temperature and pressure; density comes from an equation of state, and an uncalibrated or drifting conductivity cell gives spurious density structure that looks exactly like fine structure and staircases — the classic artefact is a density-compensated spice signal that is really a cell thermal-inertia problem. Potential temperature is not conservative. $\theta$ is defined by adiabatic displacement to a reference pressure, and the adiabatic lapse rate itself varies with temperature, salinity and pressure, so $\theta$ is not exactly conserved on cross-sections that span a large range of salinity — the neutral density $\gamma^n$ and the steric anomaly $\xi_0$ of  Jackett and McDougall are the TEOS-10 fix, and the 2010 switch changed water-mass definitions in the Southern Ocean measurably. And the ocean is not at steady state. The salt budget, the oxygen inventory, the carbon inventory and the strontium isotope composition are all changing on decadal to millennial timescales, and every residence time, every saturation value and every end-member in this chapter is a present-day number attached to a system that is being perturbed faster than its own mixing time.
:::

::: example Freezing, sinking and the two regimes of the ocean {#ex-freeze}
Take the freezing point of seawater as $T_f = -0.055\,S\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$. Find $T_f$ at $S = 35$, $S = 20$ and $S = 7$. Given that the difference between the **temperature of maximum density** and the **freezing point** is $3.98\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ at zero salinity and **falls** by $0.162\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ per PSU, find the salinity at which the two coincide, and interpret the contrast between the **Baltic**, the **Black Sea** and the **Nordic Seas**.
::: solution
$$
T_f(35) = -1.93\ \mathrm{^\circ C}, \qquad T_f(20) = -1.10\ \mathrm{^\circ C}, \qquad T_f(7) = -0.39\ \mathrm{^\circ C},
$$
and the two temperatures coincide where $3.98 - 0.162\,S = 0$, that is at
$$
S^{*} = \frac{3.98}{0.162} = 24.6\ \mathrm{PSU}.
$$
Below 24.6 PSU seawater behaves like fresh water: cooling makes it denser until it reaches its maximum density, below which further cooling makes it lighter, so a cooling basin stops convecting before it freezes and stratifies from above. Above 24.6 PSU it behaves like the open ocean: cooling always makes it denser right down to the ice point, so it convects until it runs out of heat or space.

The consequences are written on the map. The Baltic at 7 PSU is a lake-like basin: winter cooling stratifies it, its deep water is stagnant, anoxic or near-anoxic for thousands of years at a time, and its salinity is set by the balance of river input and intermittent North Sea inflows — the major Baltic inflows of 2014 and 2020 were the first renewals of the deep water in a decade. The Black Sea at 18 PSU is the same mechanism in the extreme: a fresh river-fed surface over a salty Mediterranean intrusion, permanently stratified, anoxic below 150 m, and the world's largest hydrogen-sulphide reservoir. The Nordic Seas, the Labrador Sea and the Weddell-Ross coasts at 34–35 PSU are the opposite regime: autumn and winter cooling drive convection to 2000–3000 m, forming the North Atlantic Deep Water and Antarctic Bottom Water that ventilate the world's interior — and freshening them by melting ice and increased precipitation moves them toward the 24.6-PSU boundary, which is the mechanism behind every overturning-collapse scenario.

The 24.6 PSU is therefore not a curiosity but a tipping threshold: a basin above it can ventilate, and a basin below it cannot, and the crossing — whether by glacial freshening, by ice-sheet melt or by human diversion — switches the basin from oxygenated to stagnant on a timescale of centuries.
:::

::: history
Aristotle (Aristotle) reasoned that the sea is salty because the sun draws the fresh water out of it, and Sir Humphry Davy (Davy) showed in 1815 that the salt is supplied by rock weathering in the rivers rather than being primordial. J. H. G. von Forchhammer (Forchhammer) enunciated the constant-proportions principle in 1865 and William Dittmar (Dittmar) proved it in 1884 by analysing the 77 Challenger samples, establishing that one measurement could fix all the major ions. Martin Knudsen (Knudsen) published the hydrographical tables and the chlorinity-to-salinity relation in 1901, and the Copenhagen school of Knudsen, Helland-Hansen, V. W. Ekman and Fridtjof Nansen (Nansen) made salinity and current into numbers; Nansen's insulated sampling bottle and current meter, built for the *Fram* voyages of 1893–1896, were the first instruments that could measure the interior. W. V. Bibby (Bibby) invented the electrical conductivity cell in 1930; R. C. Foster and J. H. C. Smith (Foster) built the first inductive salinometer in 1962, which removed the electrode fouling that had limited the method; and A. G. Worthington (Worthington) published his 1972 atlas of the 7000 Nansen bottles of the WWSS and IDOE era, the dataset on which every water-mass map since has rested. H. U. Sverdrup, M. W. Johnson and R. H. Fleming (Sverdrup) wrote *The Oceans* in 1942, still the clearest book in the subject. The 1978 Practical Salinity Scale and the UNESCO 1983 equation of state of N. P. Fofonoff and R. C. Millard (Fofonoff) standardised the field, and the TEOS-10 framework of Trevor McDougall, David Jackett, Frank Feistel, Olivier Koblinsky, Stephan Seitz and Paul Barker (McDougall) in 2010 replaced both with Absolute Salinity, entropy and neutral density. The sound-speed equation is the work of Victor Del Grosso (Del Grosso) in 1974 and of Chen and Millero (Chen) in 1982; the deep sound channel was identified by Maurice Ewing and Joseph Worzel (Ewing) in 1948, and Walter Munk and Carl Wunsch (Munk) proposed acoustic climate thermometry as ACSYAD in 1979, tested by Peter Worcester, Walter Munk and the ATOC and AUSTAC consortia (Worcester) between 1989 and 2003. The noble-gas palaeothermometer is the work of Stuart Jenkins, David Hall and the Leeds school (Jenkins) in 1996–2000, and the seawater strontium isotope curve was established by Jan Veizer (Veizer) in 1989 and David Hodell, J. Curtis and G. Jones (Hodell) in 1990, giving the Cenozoic rise of $^{87}\\mathrm{Sr}/^{86}\\mathrm{Sr}$ from 0.7070 to 0.7092 as a weathering and uplift record.
:::

::: quiz
Two samples of seawater, one at 4 °C and one at 30 °C, both at salinity 35, are mixed in equal parts. The mixture is compared with the average density of the two samples. What is true?
- [ ] The mixture is exactly the average, because density is linear in temperature.
- [x] The mixture is denser than the average, because the equation of state is convex.
- [ ] The mixture is lighter than the average.
- [ ] It depends on the pressure.
::: solution
This is **cabbeling**. Because the thermal expansion coefficient is larger at high temperature, the warm sample **contracts more on cooling** than the cold sample **expands on warming**, so the mixture is **denser** than the mean of the two — by about **0.14 kg m⁻³** for these values. Mixing is a genuine **density source**, independent of any heat or freshwater flux.
:::

::: summary
- Seawater is 99.3 per cent six ions; constant proportions means one conductivity measurement fixes them all, and residence times spanning 10⁵ to 10⁸ years are what make the ocean a geochemical archive.
- Density responds at 0.78 kg m⁻³ per PSU and −0.21 kg m⁻³ per °C, so a 0.26-PSU change exactly compensates a 1-°C change along the spice direction.
- The equation of state is convex, so mixing is always denser than the average — cabbeling is a real dense-water source at fronts, leads and subduction zones.
- Below 24.6 PSU seawater behaves like a lake and stratifies on winter cooling; above it the ocean convects to kilometres, which is the difference between the Baltic and the Labrador Sea.
- Sound speed has a minimum near 1000 m because the temperature and pressure gradients cancel, creating the SOFAR channel and the cheapest basin-scale thermometer ever built.
- Oxygen solubility falls 40 per cent between 0 and 25 °C, which is the solubility pump; apparent oxygen utilisation then reads the water's age since ventilation.
- Practical Salinity is a conductivity ratio, not a mass fraction, and the TEOS-10 switch to Absolute Salinity and neutral density changed Southern Ocean water-mass definitions measurably.
:::

::: exercise Haline density {level=1 check="0.39"}
Find the change in density produced by a **0.5 PSU** increase in salinity at constant temperature, with $\rho_0 = 1027\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\beta_S = 7.6\times10^{-4}$.
::: solution
$\Delta\rho = 1027\times7.6\times10^{-4}\times0.5 = 0.39\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. For comparison, a 1-°C warming gives −0.21 kg m⁻³, so half a PSU of evaporation outweighs a two-degree warming — which is why salinity dominates the Mediterranean and Red Sea density budget and temperature dominates the subpolar one.
:::

::: exercise The compensation ratio {level=1 check="0.39"}
A water mass warms by **2.0 °C** with no density change. Find the required salinity change, taking $\alpha_T = 1.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$ and $\beta_S = 7.6\times10^{-4}$.
::: solution
$\Delta S = (\alpha_T/\beta_S)\,\Delta\theta = 0.197\times2.0 = 0.39\ \mathrm{PSU}$. A compensated warm-and-salty / cold-and-fresh pair of this amplitude carries heat and salt but no density anomaly, is invisible to geostrophy and to dynamic topography, and is exactly what mixing and salt-finger staircase structure produce — which is why spice is a mixing diagnostic.

::: exercise Steric rise {level=2 check="60"}
The upper **700 m** of the ocean warms by **0.5 °C**. With $\bar{\alpha}_T = 1.7\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}$, find the steric rise of the sea surface in **millimetres**.
::: solution
$\Delta\eta = \alpha_T\,\Delta\theta\,H = 1.7\times10^{-4}\times0.5\times700 = 0.060\ \mathrm{m} = 60\ \mathrm{mm}$. Observed upper-ocean warming of 0.5 °C over 700 m across the 1993–2020 altimeter era contributes about 1.5 mm yr⁻¹ of the 3.4 mm yr⁻¹ total rise — and the remaining 1.9 mm yr⁻¹ is mass added by ice sheets and glaciers, a division that is the single most important attribution result in sea-level science.

::: exercise Sound speed {level=2 check="1507"}
Find the sound speed at $\theta = 15\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$, $S = 35$, $z = 0$.
::: solution
$c = 1449.2 + 69.0 - 12.4 + 0.98 + 0 + 0 = 1507\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The 15-m s⁻¹ difference from the 4-°C value is one per cent, and a one-per-cent sound-speed error is a one-per-cent error in every acoustic range — which is why XCT and CTD profiles are still taken before underwater operations, and why ocean acoustic tomography can invert for temperature at all.
:::

::: exercise The pressure term {level=2 check="48"}
At constant temperature and salinity, by how much does the sound speed differ between the surface and **3000 m**?
::: solution
$\Delta c = 0.016\times3000 = 48\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The pressure term alone is larger than the whole surface-to-1000-m temperature effect in the subpolar ocean, which is why the deep sound channel exists at all — and why a sound ray that dives below the axis is bent back up no matter how much temperature structure it meets.
:::

::: exercise A shorter residence time {level=2 check="92"}
If the riverine sodium flux were $1.6\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{kg\,yr^{-1}}$ instead of $2.3\times10^{11}$, what would the sodium residence time be?
::: solution
$\tau = 1.47\times10^{19}/1.6\times10^{11} = 9.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{yr} = 92\ \mathrm{Myr}$. A 30 per cent change in the input moves the residence time by 30 per cent, and the glacial-interglacial change in weathering flux is a factor of two to three — which is why every published seawater residence time carries an order-of-magnitude uncertainty and why isotope budgets, not concentration budgets, are the defensible form of the argument.
:::

::: exercise A brackish sea {level=3 check="-1.1"}
Find the freezing point of the **northern Baltic** at $S = 20$.
::: solution
$T_f = -0.055\times20 = -1.1\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$. At 20 PSU the basin is below the 24.6-PSU threshold, so it stratifies on winter cooling and never convects to its bottom — which is why the Baltic deep water is stagnant, anoxic and centuries old, and why its renewal depends entirely on rare dense-water inflows from the North Sea rather than on local winter cooling.
:::

::: exercise Cabbeling {level=3 check="0.14"}
Mix equal volumes of seawater at $S = 35$, $\theta = 10\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ ($\sigma_t = 26.82$) and at $S = 35$, $\theta = 30\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ ($\sigma_t = 23.40$). Using $\sigma_t(35,20) = 25.25$, find how much denser the mixture is than the arithmetic mean of the two densities.
::: solution
The mean of the two densities is $(26.82 + 23.40)/2 = 25.11$, and the mixture, whose **temperature** is very nearly the mean, has $\sigma_t = 25.25$ — denser by
$$
\Delta\sigma = 25.25 - 25.11 = 0.14\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}.
$$
That 0.14 kg m⁻³ is free dense water: it is produced by mixing alone, with no heat loss, no evaporation and no ice formation. It drives shelf convection at river plumes, brine-basin and ice-shelf overflow formation, subduction-front mode water formation, and the Antarctic shelf water mass transformation — and it is systematically omitted from most surface-flux budgets of dense-water formation, which is one reason those budgets have never closed.
:::
