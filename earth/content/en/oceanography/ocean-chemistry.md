Seawater holds $38{,}000$ gigatonnes of dissolved inorganic carbon — sixty times the preindustrial atmosphere's $590$ GtC — and the entire fossil-fuel emission of the last two centuries. It holds it almost entirely as bicarbonate, a form that cannot outgas without first being re-protonated, and that single chemical fact is why the Earth has been habitable for half a billion years, why atmospheric carbon dioxide is forty per cent lower than a physically unbuffered ocean would give, and why the pH of the surface sea is falling faster than at any time in the twenty-six million years the sediment record can see.

## The carbonate system

::: definition The four-carbon system and its two controls {#def-carb}
Dissolved inorganic carbon exists in three inorganic species in acid–base equilibrium, plus dissolved organic carbon:
$$
\mathrm{CO_2^{*} \rightleftharpoons HCO_3^{-} \rightleftharpoons CO_3^{2-}}, \qquad K_1^{*} = \frac{[\mathrm{H^{+}}][\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2^{*}}]}, \qquad K_2 = \frac{[\mathrm{H^{+}}][\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]}{[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}]}
$$
with $K_1^{*} \approx 1.5\times10^{-6}$ and $K_2 \approx 4.7\times10^{-10}$ at 25 °C and $S = 35$, so at the natural seawater pH of 8.1 the split is roughly $1\% : 88\% : 11\%$. Because $K_2/K_1^{*} \approx 3\times10^{-4}$, the ocean sits on the second dissociation — the bicarbonate/carbonate pair — which is exactly the pH at which a solution resists acid most strongly. That is not a coincidence; it is the carbonate compensation depth writing itself into surface chemistry over half a million years.

Two conservative measurements fix the whole system, and any two of {DIC, TA, pH, pCO₂, [CO₃²⁻]} give the other three:

- Dissolved inorganic carbon $\mathrm{DIC} = [\mathrm{CO_2^{*}}] + [\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}] + [\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]$, typically $1950$–$2250\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$, rising with depth as organic matter respires.
- Total alkalinity $\mathrm{TA} = [\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}] + 2[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] + [\mathrm{B(OH)_4^{-}}] + [\mathrm{OH^{-}}] - [\mathrm{H^{+}}] - [\mathrm{HSO_4^{-}}] - \cdots$, typically $2300$–$2400\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ — the excess of proton acceptors over donors, set by river input of bicarbonate and removed by calcium carbonate burial.

The single most important derived number is the Revelle factor,
$$
R = \frac{\Delta\mathrm{pCO_2}/\mathrm{pCO_2}}{\Delta\mathrm{DIC}/\mathrm{DIC}} \approx 10\text{–}17\ \text{(surface)}, \quad 8\text{–}10\ \text{(deep)},
$$
the degree to which the buffer amplifies a carbon addition into a partial pressure change. $R = 15$ means that adding one per cent of the DIC raises pCO₂ by fifteen per cent. The buffer is what lets the ocean hold fifty times the atmosphere's carbon at a pCO₂ near equilibrium — and the same buffer is why adding carbon to it costs nine tenths of the added carbon back out again at the surface. $R$ falls with temperature, rising alkalinity and falling temperature, and rises as the ocean takes up more carbon — which is a positive feedback on every emission scenario.
:::

::: definition Saturation, the compensation depth and the pumps {#def-saturation}
Calcium carbonate saturation is
$$
\Omega = \frac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}][\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]}{K_{\mathrm{sp}}^{\prime}} \;=\; \frac{[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]_{\mathrm{measured}}}{[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]_{\mathrm{saturation}}},
$$
with $\Omega > 1$ supersaturated, $\Omega < 1$ corrosive. $K_{\mathrm{sp}}^{\prime}$ increases with pressure and decreases with temperature, so $\Omega$ falls downward through the water column for two independent reasons: respiration adds CO₂ (raising DIC, lowering $[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]$) and cold, high pressure dissolves calcite more readily. The surface ocean has $\Omega_{\mathrm{calcite}} \approx 4$–$5$ and $\Omega_{\mathrm{aragonite}} \approx 3$–$4$; aragonite ($\Omega = 1$) is reached at $500$–$1000\ \mathrm{m}$ in the Pacific and $1500$–$2000\ \mathrm{m}$ in the Atlantic; calcite saturation at $1500$–$2500\ \mathrm{m}$ in the Pacific and $2800$–$3800\ \mathrm{m}$ in the Atlantic; and the calcite compensation depth (CCD), below which no calcite survives burial, at $4500\ \mathrm{m}$ in the Atlantic and $5000$–$5300\ \mathrm{m}$ in the Pacific.

The CCD is the ocean's own thermostat, and it operates on $10^{5}$–$10^{6}$-year timescales. If atmospheric CO₂ rises, more carbonate dissolves in deep sea water, the CCD shoals, less carbonate is buried, alkalinity builds up, and the surface ocean draws down the excess CO₂. If CO₂ falls, the CCD deepens, burial exceeds input, alkalinity falls, and CO₂ recovers. The same feedback, operating through silicate and carbonate weathering rates, is what has kept the Mesozoic and Cenozoic climate inside habitable bounds for $500$ million years, and it is the only known mechanism that can remove anthropogenic CO₂ completely — on a timescale of $200{,}000$ years.

Three pumps move carbon between the surface and the interior:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\textbf{Solubility pump} & \text{cold high-latitude subduction} & \Delta\mathrm{DIC} \sim 160\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}} & \text{physical; raises deep }\mathrm{pCO_2}\text{ by }\sim\!1200\ \mu\mathrm{atm} \\
\textbf{Soft-tissue pump} & \text{photosynthesis, respiration at depth} & \text{Redfield } -106:-16:-1 & \text{biological; the } \mathrm{O_2}, \mathrm{NO_3^{-}}, \mathrm{PO_4^{3-}} \text{ cycles} \\
\textbf{Carbonate pump} & \text{calcifiers sink, dissolve at depth} & \text{alkalinity } +2 \text{ per } \mathrm{CO_3} & \text{opposite sign: }\textbf{releases}\ \mathrm{CO_2}\text{ where it operates}
\end{array}
$$

The soft-tissue pump is the one that matters for atmospheric CO₂: Redfield stoichiometry,
$$
106\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 16\,\mathrm{NO_3^{-}} + \mathrm{H_3PO_4} + 122\,\mathrm{H_2O} \;\rightleftharpoons\; (\mathrm{CH_2O})_{106}(\mathrm{NH_3})_{16}(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}) + 138\,\mathrm{O_2},
$$
means that every mole of organic carbon respired at depth removes one mole from the surface and adds $1.3$ moles of oxygen demand. Model experiments that turn ocean biology off raise preindustrial atmospheric pCO₂ by several tens of µatm — the published range is $20$ to $50\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$ — with the solubility pump accounting for most of the remainder.
:::

::: definition Ocean acidification, the anthropogenic sink, and the tracers that prove it {#def-acid}
The ocean has absorbed $\sim 175\ \mathrm{PgC}$ of the $\sim 250\ \mathrm{PgC}$ emitted since 1850 — about twenty-five to thirty per cent — at a present rate of $2.6\ \mathrm{PgC\,yr^{-1}}$, equivalent to a global mean air–sea flux of $0.6\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$. The same chemistry that makes the ocean a sink makes it an acid:
$$
\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O + CO_3^{2-} \rightarrow 2\,HCO_3^{-}},
$$
so every mole of absorbed CO₂ consumes about one mole of carbonate. Surface pH has fallen from $8.21$ (preindustrial, reconstructed from coral $\delta^{11}\mathrm{B}$ and foraminiferal B/Ca) to $8.10$ — a fall of $0.11$ units, which is a twenty-nine-per-cent increase in [H⁺] and a sixteen-per-cent decrease in [$\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$]. On business-as-usual emissions, 2100 pH is $7.75$ — a larger change in one century than any in the past $26$ million years of the sediment record.

The biological consequences follow $\Omega$, not pH. Pteropods, coccolithophores, coralline algae and the larval stages of most shellfish build aragonite or high-magnesium calcite, whose saturation horizon is shoaling by $\sim 25\ \mathrm{m}$ per decade and is already crossed seasonally on the US west coast shelf, where oyster hatcheries have failed twice. Calcareous nannoplankton calcification falls by $\sim 3$ per cent per $0.1$ pH unit in meta-analysis; siliceous and non-calcifying phytoplankton are unaffected or favoured; and the whole marine food web above the CCD is being re-ordered by a change in one decimal place.

Four tracers prove the carbon is fossil and the sink is real:

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
^{14}\mathrm{C}\ \text{in ocean DIC} & \text{fossil carbon is }^{14}\mathrm{C}\text{-dead} & \text{surface DIC is measurably diluted} \\
\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}\ \text{in DIC and rings} & \text{C}_3\text{ plants are } \delta^{13}\mathrm{C}\approx-25\%\text{, sea water } 0\% & \text{the Suess decline of }-0.25\%\ \text{since }1850 \\
\text{CFC / SF}_6\ \text{ages} & \text{anthropogenic transit-time distribution} & \text{ventilation age of each water mass} \\
\text{Anthropogenic DIC from TA, O}_2, \mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{app}} & \text{Grachten and back-calculation} & 175\ \mathrm{PgC}\ \text{inventory}
\end{array}
$$

The $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ test is the cleanest: fossil carbon has no radiocarbon at all, so the $^{14}\mathrm{C}/^{12}\mathrm{C}$ ratio of ocean DIC has fallen in exact proportion to the fossil fraction of the added carbon — an unambiguous isotopic fingerprint that rules out any natural outgassing explanation of the atmospheric rise.
:::

::: example The Revelle factor {#ex-revelle}
Surface water has $\mathrm{DIC} = 2100\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{pCO_2} = 280\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$. Take $R = 15$. Find the new $\mathrm{pCO_2}$ after adding $100\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ of anthropogenic DIC, and the fraction of the added carbon that the ocean could have kept if $\mathrm{pCO_2}$ had stayed at $280$.
::: solution
$$
\frac{\Delta\mathrm{pCO_2}}{\mathrm{pCO_2}} = R\,\frac{\Delta\mathrm{DIC}}{\mathrm{DIC}} = 15\times\frac{100}{2100} = 0.714
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{pCO_2} = 280\times1.714 = 480\ \mu\mathrm{atm}.
$$
A 4.8-per-cent addition of carbon produces a seventy-one-per-cent increase in partial pressure. Since the air–sea flux is proportional to $\Delta\mathrm{pCO_2}$, the ocean would have had to hold all $100$ µmol kg⁻¹ at a pCO₂ of $480$ — nearly double the atmosphere — which is impossible. Most of the added carbon is therefore left in the atmosphere. The inventory ratio follows: the same $100$ µmol kg⁻¹ would raise atmospheric CO₂ by only about $20$ µatm if the ocean were unbuffered, and by $100$ µatm or more once the Revelle buffering is imposed — which is why the airborne fraction of fossil emissions is forty-five per cent and not ten.

The Revelle factor is not constant. It is $8$–$10$ in cold, alkaline, nutrient-rich subpolar water and $15$–$18$ in warm, oligotrophic subtropical water, and it rises as the ocean ages chemically. The global mean has risen from $\sim 10$ preindustrial to $\sim 12$ today, and models put it at $14$–$16$ by 2100 — meaning the fraction of emissions the ocean absorbs declines with every tonne emitted. This is the carbon-cycle feedback, and it is already detectable as a falling fractional uptake in the surface ocean $\mathrm{pCO_2}$ time series at Bermuda, Hawaii, ALOHA and the SOCAT transects.
:::
:::

::: example Carbonate speciation from alkalinity and DIC {#ex-speciation}
A surface sample has $\mathrm{DIC} = 2020\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{TA} = 2440\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$, $[\mathrm{B(OH)_4^{-}}] = 200\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ and $[\mathrm{CO_2^{*}}] = 10\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. Find $[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]$ and $[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}]$, and the saturation state if the saturation $[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]$ is $55\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$.
::: solution
Dropping borate, sulphate and hydroxide from the alkalinity definition leaves two equations,
$$
[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}] + [\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] = 2020 - 10 = 2010
$$
$$
[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}] + 2[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] = 2440 - 200 = 2240
$$
whose **difference** gives $[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] = 230$ and $[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}] = 1780\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. Then
$$
\Omega = \frac{230}{55} = 4.2 .
$$
The arithmetic is trivial; the point is what the numbers mean. Ninety per cent of the ocean's carbon is bicarbonate, which cannot outgas without first becoming carbonate, and carbonate is only eleven per cent of the total. Acidifying the ocean converts carbonate into bicarbonate, and that is the whole of ocean acidification — not the addition of acid, but the consumption of the base that made the ocean a buffer.

The $\Omega = 4.2$ is a modern preindustrial-like surface value. At $\mathrm{pCO_2} = 560\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$ the same alkalinity gives $[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] \approx 190$ and $\Omega \approx 3.4$; at $\mathrm{pCO_2} = 950$ it gives $\sim 150$ and $\Omega \approx 2.7$. Every calcifying organism in the surface ocean must pump against a thermodynamic gradient that weakens by twenty per cent per doubling of CO₂, and the cost of calcification rises accordingly.
:::
:::

::: example Regenerated carbon from oxygen {#ex-redfield}
A deep water mass has an apparent oxygen utilisation of $90\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ and a preformed phosphate of $0.40\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. Using Redfield stoichiometry, find the regenerated DIC and the total phosphate.
::: solution
Redfield **respiration** consumes $138\ \mathrm{O_2}$ per $106\ \mathrm{C}$, so
$$
\Delta\mathrm{DIC}_{\mathrm{reg}} = 90\times\frac{106}{138} = 69\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}, \qquad
\Delta\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}} = \frac{69}{106} = 0.65\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}},
$$
and the total phosphate is $0.40 + 0.65 = 1.05\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$.

The method is the workhorse of transit-time-distribution geochemistry. Subtracting the regenerated component recovered from oxygen leaves the preformed DIC, which is the value the water had when it last saw the atmosphere — and comparing preformed DIC between old and young water masses separates the anthropogenic uptake from the natural redistribution, which is the hardest number in the whole carbon budget.

The caveat is that Redfield stoichiometry is not constant. Organic matter reaching depth is preferentially depleted in phosphorus and nitrogen relative to carbon, so the true C:P rises from $106:1$ near the surface to $130$–$160:1$ in deep water, and regional denitrification decouples N from C entirely. Errors of twenty per cent in regenerated DIC are routine, and they are comparable to the anthropogenic signal itself — which is why the ocean carbon sink inventory carries $\pm 20\ \mathrm{PgC}$ uncertainty after thirty years of repeat sections.
:::
:::

::: example What a pH unit means {#ex-ph}
Surface pH has fallen from $8.21$ to $8.10$. Find the percentage increase in $[\mathrm{H^{+}}]$, and the factor by which $[\mathrm{H^{+}}]$ would rise if pH reached $7.75$.
::: solution
$$
\frac{[\mathrm{H^{+}}]_{\mathrm{now}}}{[\mathrm{H^{+}}]_{\mathrm{preind}}} = 10^{0.11} = 1.29 \quad (+29\%), \qquad
\frac{[\mathrm{H^{+}}]_{7.75}}{[\mathrm{H^{+}}]_{8.21}} = 10^{0.46} = 2.9 .
$$
A zero-point-one-one fall in pH is a twenty-nine-per-cent increase in acidity, and the end-of-century projection is nearly a three-fold increase. Because pH is a logarithm, the last decimal place is the whole story, and this is the single most misreported number in climate science.

The corresponding carbonate loss is steeper than the hydrogen gain, because added CO₂ consumes carbonate directly:
$$
\mathrm{CO_2 + CO_3^{2-} + H_2O \rightarrow 2\,HCO_3^{-}} .
$$
A twenty-nine-per-cent rise in [H⁺] is accompanied by a sixteen-per-cent fall in [$\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$] and a fall in $\Omega$ of the same fraction — and $\Omega$, not pH, sets whether a pteropod shell dissolves. No organism on Earth today lives in water at pH $7.75$, and the last time atmospheric CO₂ crossed $1000\ \mathrm{ppm}$, during the PETM 56 million years ago, deep-sea carbonate completely dissolved over $\sim 10^{4}$ years and the CCD shoalled by $\sim 2\ \mathrm{km}$.
:::

::: history
William Dittmar (Dittmar) established the constancy of seawater composition from the Challenger samples in 1884, and H. W. Harvey (Harvey) published *The Chemistry and Fertility of Sea Waters* in 1930, the book that made nutrient, oxygen and carbon distributions one continuous argument. Alfred Redfield (Redfield) noticed in 1934 that the ocean's nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio of $16:1$ matched the ratio in living plankton, and in 1958 he generalised it into "the biological control of chemical factors in the environment", the single paper that founded biogeochemistry. Hans Oeschger, Ursula Siegenthaler, Bernhard Stauffer and Thomas Stocker recovered glacial carbon dioxide from Antarctic ice through the 1980s, Charles Keeling (Keeling) measured the atmospheric rise from 1958 and the carbon-isotope Suess effect from tree rings, and Pieter Tans (Tans) used radiocarbon to prove that the missing carbon was fossil and that the land and ocean sinks were real. Wallace Broecker and Tai-Lai Peng (Peng) wrote *Tracers in the Sea* in 1982, the reference manual of transit-time carbon geochemistry, and Jorge Sarmiento (Sarmiento) and Nicolas Gruber (Gruber) turned it into global anthropogenic-CO₂ inventories in the 1990s and 2000s, now maintained as GLODAP by Christopher Sabine, Toste Tanhua, Rik Wanninkhof and colleagues. William Berger (Berger) established the carbonate compensation depth as a sedimentological process in the 1970s, and David Archer (Archer), Andy Ridgwell (Ridgwell) and Richard Zeebe (Zeebe) worked out the $10^{4}$–$10^{5}$-year alkalinity neutralisation that sets the ultimate fate of fossil CO₂. Ken Caldeira and Michael Wickett (Caldeira) published "Anthropogenic carbon and ocean pH" in 2003, Scott Doney, Victoria Fabry, Chris Langdon and Ulf Riebesell (Riebesell) the first synthesis of the biological consequences in 2008, and Nina Bednaršek (Bednaršek) and Chris Langdon documented pteropod dissolution and oyster-hatchery failure in California Current water that upwelling had already corroded. The measurement infrastructure — Andrew Dickson's thermodynamic constants, the SOCAT and Surface Ocean $f\mathrm{CO_2}$ syntheses, the SOCEP and OceanSODA climatologies, and twenty-five years of repeat GEOTRACES, CLIVAR and GO-SHIP sections — has reduced the global air–sea carbon flux to $2.6 \pm 0.3\ \mathrm{PgC\,yr^{-1}}$, one of the best-determined numbers in earth system science.
:::

::: warning The chemistry is exact, the biology is not, and the timescales are beyond any experiment
Seven cautions. The carbonate system is solved, not measured. Any two of DIC, TA, pH, pCO₂ give the rest only through apparent equilibrium constants whose temperature, salinity and pressure dependencies were fitted in the 1990s and revised twice; inconsistent pairs of measurements still disagree by $5$–$10\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$ in $f\mathrm{CO_2}$, which is the size of the tropical source and half the temperate seasonal cycle. Redfield stoichiometry is a mean, not a law: C:N:P varies from $106:16:1$ in surface production to $130$–$160:1$ in deep respiration, diazotrophs decouple N from P, cyanobacteria and diatoms decouple Si from C, and regional denitrification makes the nitrogen budget non-Redfield by twenty per cent. The biological pump is not the carbon export flux. Most exported carbon respires within $100\ \mathrm{m}$ of the surface; the fraction reaching $1000\ \mathrm{m}$ is $1$–$30$ per cent and varies by an order of magnitude between the subtropical gyre and the Southern Ocean, so a model that gets the export right can get the sequestration wrong. The carbonate counter-pump works against the soft-tissue pump: calcification releases CO₂ ($2\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}} \rightarrow \mathrm{CaCO_3} + \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}$), so a bloom of coccolithophores can make a region a source, and the net sign of the biological pump depends on the calcifying fraction no remote sensor measures. Ocean acidification experiments do not reach the projection. Nearly all mesocosm and shipboard work runs days to weeks on single species at constant CO₂, whereas the real selection operates over generations, on multispecies communities, in water that also warms, stratifies, de-oxygenates and acidifies on seasonal cycles larger than the century trend. The sediment record cannot resolve the present. Borate and boron-isotope pH proxies carry $\pm 0.05$–$0.1$ pH uncertainty and decadal to millennial averaging, so the claim that the present rate is unprecedented rests on the rate, not the level, and on the PETM as the only close analogue. And the remediation timescale is geological. Carbonate compensation restores about half the perturbation in $10^{4}$–$10^{5}$ years and silicate weathering the rest in $10^{5}$; the fossil carbon we have released will leave the ocean more acid and the CCD shallower than at any time in the Neogene, for longer than human civilisation has existed.
:::

::: summary
- Seawater carbon is 88 per cent bicarbonate, 11 per cent carbonate and 1 per cent CO₂*, and the system is fully specified by any two of DIC, TA, pH, pCO₂ and carbonate.
- The Revelle factor of 10–17 is the reason the ocean holds sixty times the atmosphere's carbon and still leaves forty-five per cent of our emissions in the air.
- Saturation state falls with depth for two reasons — respiration and pressure — giving aragonite and calcite horizons and a compensation depth near 4500–5000 m.
- The solubility, soft-tissue and carbonate pumps move carbon into the interior; only the first two lower atmospheric CO₂, and the third partly undoes them.
- The ocean has taken up about 175 PgC, a quarter of emissions, at 2.6 PgC yr⁻¹, and its fractional uptake falls as the Revelle factor rises.
- Surface pH has fallen 0.11 units, a twenty-nine-per-cent increase in hydrogen ion and a sixteen-per-cent fall in carbonate; the saturation horizon is shoalling by tens of metres a decade.
- Carbonate compensation will neutralise the perturbation, in part within 10,000 years and completely within 100,000.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise The Revelle response {level=1 check="540"}
Water with $\mathrm{DIC} = 2050\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{pCO_2} = 400\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$ takes up $60\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ of anthropogenic carbon. With $R = 12$, find the new $\mathrm{pCO_2}$.
::: solution
$\Delta\mathrm{pCO_2}/\mathrm{pCO_2} = 12\times60/2050 = 0.35$, so $\mathrm{pCO_2} = 540\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$. The water is now above the atmosphere and outgasses — which is the mechanism of the falling uptake efficiency: as the ocean ages chemically, its surface $\mathrm{pCO_2}$ rises faster than the atmosphere's, and the fractional sink declines.
:::

::: exercise Acidity is a logarithm {level=1 check="1.6"}
Find the factor by which $[\mathrm{H^{+}}]$ rises when pH falls from **8.10** to **7.90**.
::: solution
$10^{0.20} = 1.6$ — a sixty-per-cent increase from a zero-point-two-unit fall. Every additional $0.1$ unit adds another twenty-six per cent, which is why the last decimal of the projection matters more than the first.
:::

::: exercise Regenerated carbon {level=1 check="46"}
Find the regenerated DIC corresponding to an apparent oxygen utilisation of $60\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$\Delta\mathrm{DIC} = 60\times106/138 = 46\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. The ratio $106/138 = 0.77$ is the single conversion factor between the oxygen and carbon cycles, and because oxygen is measured a thousand times more often than DIC, it is the most used number in the subject.
:::

::: exercise The Pacific nutrient signal {level=2 check="297"}
Deep North Pacific water has $2.8\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ more phosphate than its source. Find the regenerated DIC.
::: solution
$\Delta\mathrm{DIC} = 106\times2.8 = 297\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. The observed excess is $150$–$200\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$, and the shortfall is the carbonate counter-pump and the circulation: some of the regenerated carbon has already dissolved calcite on the way, which raises alkalinity and re-absorbs CO₂. This is the quantitative reason the Pacific is the world's largest carbon reservoir and the Atlantic's overturning is the reason it is not the Pacific's turn.
:::
:::

::: exercise Saturation state {level=2 check="3.5"}
Find $\Omega_{\mathrm{calcite}}$ where $[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] = 190\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ and the saturation value is $55\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$\Omega = 190/55 = 3.5$. This is a plausible end-of-century surface value, still comfortably supersaturated — which is the point often missed: open-ocean surface water will not become corrosive this century. The damage is done to the deep, the upwelling, the polar and the coastal, where $\Omega$ is already near one and a twenty-per-cent reduction crosses the threshold.
:::

::: exercise The global flux {level=2 check="0.65"}
The ocean absorbs $2.8\ \mathrm{PgC\,yr^{-1}}$. Find the global mean air–sea flux in $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$.
::: solution
$2.8\times10^{15}/12 = 2.33\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{mol\,yr^{-1}}$, over $3.61\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$, giving $0.65\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$. Against a global mean gas exchange piston velocity of $5$–$6\ \mathrm{m\,d^{-1}}$, this requires a global mean $\Delta\mathrm{CO_2}$ of only $\sim 10\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$ — a few per cent of the natural $\mathrm{pCO_2}$ range, and smaller than the regional spread between the North Atlantic sink and the equatorial and Eastern Pacific sources.

::: exercise A consistency check {level=3 check="400"}
Preindustrial surface water had $\mathrm{DIC} = 1900$ and $\mathrm{pCO_2} = 280$. Surface DIC has risen by $68\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. With $R = 12$, find the modern $\mathrm{pCO_2}$ and compare with the observed $415\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$.
::: solution
$\mathrm{pCO_2} = 280\times(1 + 12\times68/1900) = 400\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$. The calculation reproduces the observed value to four per cent — one of the strongest validations of the whole carbon-cycle framework, since it links a shipboard DIC titration, a flask $\mathrm{CO_2}$ measurement and a nineteenth-century equilibrium constant. The residual $15\ \mu\mathrm{atm}$ is warming ($4\%$ of $\mathrm{pCO_2}$ per °C), reduced solubility, and the regional biology and circulation that make the global mean a useful fiction.
:::
:::

::: exercise How long to neutralise {level=3 check="5000"}
The ocean's anthropogenic carbon inventory is **175 PgC** and riverine alkalinity input is $3\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{mol\,yr^{-1}}$. Find the time in years for river alkalinity to balance a carbon perturbation of that size, and say why the literature quotes $10^{4}$–$10^{5}$ years.
::: solution
$175\times10^{15}/12 = 1.5\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{mol}$, and
$$
t = \frac{1.5\times10^{16}}{3\times10^{12}} = 5000\ \mathrm{yr}.
$$
The literature value is twenty to forty times larger for three reasons. Only a third of riverine alkalinity is net — the rest is already balanced by calcite burial in a steady-state ocean. The alkalinity must reach the deep ocean, which mixes on $10^{3}$ years, and the atmosphere must then re-equilibrate through a surface ocean whose Revelle factor rises as it absorbs. And the feedback is self-limiting: as alkalinity builds, the CCD deepens, calcite burial increases, and the net alkalinity gain falls toward zero before the perturbation is gone. The honest summary is that half the excess is neutralised in $10^{4}$–$10^{5}$ years and essentially all of it in $10^{5}$ — a timescale longer than the Quaternary, and the reason the Pert is still visible in the sediment record fifty-six million years later.
:::
