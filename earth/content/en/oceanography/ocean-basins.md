The ocean is not a bathtub with a floor: it is a **deep, narrow, young and cold** layer of water in basins whose shape was made by **plate tectonics** and is now the single most important **topographic** control on the planet's climate. Its statistics are the first thing to get straight, because almost every argument in oceanography turns on them. It covers **3.61×10⁸ km²** — **70.8 per cent** of the surface — holds **1.335×10⁹ km³** of water at a **mean depth of 3688 m**, and its floor is **bimodal**: a **rim** of shallow shelves at about **130 m** and a **peak** of abyssal plain at about **4400 m**, with very little in between. Half the planet's surface is **flat, dark, near-freezing sediment** at four kilometres' depth, and it is the largest single habitat on Earth.

## The container

::: definition The shape of the basins {#def-bathymetry}
The seafloor divides into **six provinces** whose areas, depths and roles could hardly be more different:
$$
\begin{array}{l|c|c|l}
\text{province} & \text{area} & \text{mean depth} & \text{what it does} \\ \hline
\textbf{continental shelf} & 8.9\% & 130\ \mathrm{m} & 40\%\ \text{of production, all fisheries, most sediment} \\
\textbf{continental slope} & 5.4\% & 1500\ \mathrm{m} & canyons, turbidity currents, slumps \\
\textbf{continental rise} & \text{with slope} & 2500\ \mathrm{m} & the sediment wedge that feeds the abyssal plains \\
\textbf{abyssal plain} & \sim 50\% & 4200\ \mathrm{m} & the largest landform on Earth \\
\textbf{mid-ocean ridge} & 19.5\% & 2500\ \mathrm{m} & the volcano-tectonic conveyor, $65{,}000\ \mathrm{km}$ long \\
\textbf{trench} & 0.25\% & 7300\ \mathrm{m} & subduction; the deepest point is $10{,}935\ \mathrm{m}$
\end{array}
$$
The ridge system is the longest mountain range on the planet — $65{,}000\ \mathrm{km}$ girdling the globe at a crest depth of 2500 m, with a 2–3 km axial rift valley in fast-spreading segments and a 10–15 km deep detachment-fault valley in slow-spreading ones, cut by transform faults whose fracture zones preserve the offset of the spreading centre as linear scars extending thousands of kilometres beyond the ridge axis. The abyssal plains are the flattest surfaces on Earth, with gradients of 10 cm per kilometre, formed by turbidite blankets shed from the margins through 15,000-odd submarine canyons. The seamounts — at least 30,000 of them rising more than 1 km above the abyssal plain, and probably several times that — are hotspot and ridge-flank volcanoes, and the flat-topped ones, the guyots, are drowned atolls that record subsidence of hundreds of metres per ten million years.

The **age** of the floor is the fact that makes the ocean different from the continents: **no ocean crust is older than about 180 Myr** except the **340-Myr** sliver beneath the **Eastern Mediterranean**, **half** the sea floor is younger than **60 Myr**, and the **sediment** that drapes it is **200–10,000 m** thick, averaging **500 m**, its thickness being very nearly **age × sedimentation rate** — which is why the sediment is **thickest in the western Pacific**, where the crust is oldest and the **productivity** highest, and **thinnest at the ridge**.
:::

::: definition Margins, and why the shape matters {#def-margins}
The two margin types are **opposites in every way that matters to geology, biology and hazard**:
$$
\begin{array}{l|l|l}
 & \textbf{Atlantic type (passive)} & \textbf{Pacific type (active)} \\ \hline
\text{origin} & \text{rifting and thermal subsidence} & \text{subduction and accretion} \\
\text{shelf} & 100\text{--}400\ \mathrm{km}\ \text{wide} & 10\text{--}30\ \mathrm{km}\ \text{or absent} \\
\text{sediment} & 5\text{--}15\ \mathrm{km}\ \text{in the rift basin} & \text{accretionary prism and trench wedge} \\
\text{volcanism} & \text{none after breakup} & \text{continuous arc} \\
\text{earthquakes} & \text{rare, intraplate} & \text{megathrust, } M_w > 9 \\
\text{examples} & \text{Nameib, Brazil, Norway, India} & \text{Peru-Chile, Cascadia, Sumatra, Japan}
\end{array}
$$
Three consequences follow. First, the passive margin is where the world's sedimentary basins, petroleum and groundwater are, and where 90 per cent of the terrestrial sediment is trapped before it can reach the deep sea — the shelf is the planet's sediment filter, and the canyons and turbidite systems are the punctures in the filter. Second, the active margin is where the great earthquakes, tsunamis and slope failures are, and where the trench is the only place on the planet where the surface reaches 10 km depth. Third, the shelf's area — 8.9 per cent of the ocean, but 18 per cent of the emerged land — sets the nutrient, light and residence-time conditions that make it produce roughly as much carbon as the rest of the ocean combined, and the same geometry makes it the sink for the planet's river-borne sediment, plastic and mercury.
:::

::: example The ocean's inventory {#ex-inventory}
Given an ocean volume of $1.335\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$, an area of $3.61\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$, a mean salinity of $35\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}$, a mean density of $1025\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and a continental area of $1.49\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$: find the mean depth, the depth of a global water film on a smooth Earth, the mass of dissolved salt, and the thickness of a salt layer spread over the continents at the density of halite ($2160\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$).
::: solution
$$
\bar{d} = \frac{1.335\times10^{9}}{3.61\times10^{8}} = 3698\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad
h_{\mathrm{film}} = \frac{1.335\times10^{9}}{5.10\times10^{8}} = 2618\ \mathrm{m},
$$
$$
M_{\mathrm{salt}} = 0.035\times\left(1.335\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}\times1025\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\right) = 4.8\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{kg},
$$
$$
\text{thickness} = \frac{4.8\times10^{19}}{1.49\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}\times2160\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}} = 149\ \mathrm{m}.
$$
A **2.6-km global ocean** on a smooth Earth, and **150 m of salt** blanketing every continent — the two numbers that make the **salt cycle** intelligible and that explain the scale of the **Messinian salinity crisis**, which deposited **two kilometres** of evaporite in a basin that today holds **four** of water.

The mean depth is the number that governs the ocean's capacity for everything: heat, carbon, oxygen, nutrients and space. Because the mean depth is 3.7 km and the mixed layer is only 50–150 m thick, less than 3 per cent of the ocean's volume is in direct contact with the atmosphere on a seasonal timescale — which is why the ocean buffers climate so well and recovers from a perturbation so slowly. And because the volume is fixed while the area of the shelves is not, sea-level change is a reshuffling between a deep reservoir and a shallow one, which is why a metre of eustatic rise floods tens of thousands of square kilometres of coast and why the shelf is the most climate-sensitive part of the ocean.
:::
:::

::: example Pressure and density in the trench {#ex-trench}
Find the pressure at the bottom of the **Challenger Deep** ($10{,}935\ \mathrm{m}$, $\rho = 1050\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) in **MPa** and **atmospheres**, and the fractional compression of the water there if the bulk modulus is $2.34\ \mathrm{GPa}$. What does the compression do to the **sound speed** and to **deep convection**?
::: solution
$$
P = \rho g h = 1050\times9.81\times10{,}935 = 1.13\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{Pa} = 113\ \mathrm{MPa} = 1112\ \mathrm{atm},
$$
and
$$
\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} = \frac{P}{K} = \frac{1.13\times10^{8}}{2.34\times10^{9}} = 4.8\ \text{per cent}.
$$
Nearly five per cent of the water column's density is generated by pressure alone over the deepest eleven kilometres.

The consequences are three. Sound speed rises with pressure by about 3 m s⁻¹ per 100 bar, which is why the deep sound channel exists at about 1000 m, where the temperature decrease and the pressure increase cancel, and why acoustic thermometry can measure basin-scale temperature change from a fixed sound source. Adiabatic compression warms a descending parcel by about $2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ per metre, so a potential temperature must be used before water masses can be compared across a 4-km depth range — and the correction, 0.4 °C, is larger than the differences between most water masses. And deep convection is limited by it: a North Atlantic winter convection event reaches 2000–3000 m because the adiabatic warming stabilises the column long before the surface density anomaly can carry it to the bottom.
:::
:::

::: definition Why the ocean, not the atmosphere, runs the climate {#def-capacity}
The ocean's **heat capacity per unit area** is
$$
\rho_w c_w h = 1025\times4000\times h = 4.1\times10^{6}\,h\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}},
$$
against the atmosphere's $\;p_s g^{-1} c_a = 1.04\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$. The two are **equal** at $h = 2.5\ \mathrm{m}$: **the top two and a half metres of the ocean hold as much heat as the entire atmosphere above it.** The **seasonally mixed layer** of **50–150 m** therefore has **20–60 times** the atmosphere's heat capacity, and the **whole** ocean about **1000 times** — which is the whole of the reason the **Southern Hemisphere** is less **seasonal** than the Northern, the **coastal** climate is **milder** than the **continental**, and the **effective thermal inertia** of the climate system is set by the **top hundred metres** of a **four-kilometre-deep** ocean.

The same geometry sets the carbon and oxygen budgets. The ocean holds 38,000 Pg C against the atmosphere's 850 Pg C and the land biosphere's 2000 Pg C, and 93 per cent of the carbon in fast exchange with the atmosphere; it has taken up about a quarter of our emissions, at a present rate of 2.8 Pg C yr⁻¹ — an average of $0.65\ \mathrm{mol\,C\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$ spread over the whole surface. On oxygen the intuition must be reversed: the atmosphere holds far more oxygen than the ocean does, and the ocean's oxygen minimum zones are not a supply problem but a demand problem — respiration exceeds supply where the water is old and the productivity above it is high, which is exactly the eastern boundary current and North Pacific middle-depth regime.
:::

::: example The ocean's thermal inertia {#ex-inertia}
Compute the heat capacity per unit area of (a) the whole atmosphere, (b) a **100-m** mixed layer, and (c) the **whole** ocean at its mean depth. Then compute the temperature rise of a **100-m** mixed layer exposed to the current global **radiative imbalance** of $1.0\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ for one year.
::: solution
$$
\text{(a)}\ \frac{p_s}{g}c_a = \frac{1.013\times10^{5}}{9.81}\times1004 = 1.04\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}},
$$
$$
\text{(b)}\ \rho_w c_w h = 1025\times4000\times100 = 4.1\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}} \quad (39\times\ \text{the atmosphere}),
$$
$$
\text{(c)}\ 1025\times4000\times3688 = 1.51\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}} \quad (1450\times\ \text{the atmosphere}).
$$
For the mixed layer,
$$
\Delta T = \frac{1.0\times3.16\times10^{7}}{4.1\times10^{8}} = 0.077\ \mathrm{^\circ C\ yr^{-1}},
$$
and the observed upper-ocean warming is about $0.015\ \mathrm{^\circ C\ yr^{-1}}$ — a factor of five less, because the mixed layer entrains, subducts and exports its heat into the thermocline and the intermediate and deep ocean.

That factor of five is one of the most important numbers in climate science, and it is the reason global warming is slow. Of the excess energy the Earth has accumulated since 1971, 91 per cent has gone into the ocean, 5 per cent into the land, 3 per cent into ice and 1 per cent into the atmosphere; and 60 per cent of the ocean's uptake is in the upper 700 m, with the rest below. A $1\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ imbalance sustained for a century would warm the whole ocean by only $0.24\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ and the atmosphere by $24\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ if the ocean were not there — which is the sense in which the ocean is the planet's thermostat and, once its surface warms, its accelerator.
:::
:::

::: example The ridge volume and Cretaceous sea level {#ex-ridge}
The mid-ocean ridge system occupies **19.5 per cent** of the ocean floor at a mean depth of **2500 m**. If a **50 per cent** increase in the full-spreading rate shallows the whole ridge system by **200 m**, find the first-order **eustatic** sea-level change, and explain why the Cretaceous rise was far larger.
::: solution
A shallower ridge occupies more of the basin and displaces more water, so
$$
\Delta h = \frac{0.195\times200\times A_{\mathrm{ocean}}}{A_{\mathrm{ocean}}} = 39\ \mathrm{m}.
$$
The Cretaceous sea level was 100–250 m higher than today, so the ridge-volume mechanism supplies perhaps a quarter to a third of the required rise. The rest comes from the feedbacks: a higher sea transgresses onto the cratons, the water load flexurally downdrops the margin and deepens the basin, sediment and compaction change, the glacio-eustatic contribution is zero in an icehouse-free world, and the large igneous provinces of the same age loaded and subsided the crust directly.

The mechanism matters because it is the only way to change global sea level on million-year timescales without changing the mass of water: the alternatives are ridge volume, sediment volume, seafloor weathering, forebulge collapse and true polar wander, and all of them are geometry, not water. And the modern analogue is exact: today's sea-level rise is mass added to a basin of fixed geometry, whereas the Cretaceous rise was geometry changed at fixed mass — and the two produce completely different coastal geographies, because a geometry change floods the cratons and a mass change floods the shelf.
:::
:::

::: warning The seafloor is the least-observed large surface on Earth, and every map of it is a model
Six errors are endemic. The depth is measured by satellite altimetry, not by sound: over 90 per cent of the seafloor the bathymetry comes from gravity-derived models whose root-mean-square error is 200–400 m and which cannot resolve anything narrower than about 20 km — so seamounts, canyons, fault scarps and submarine landslides are invisible, and the goal of a complete high-resolution map by 2030 is less than a quarter done. The shelf is mapped and the slope is not: the slope and rise, where the canyons, turbidite channels, gas hydrates and cable-breaking landslides are, are the least-surveyed large surfaces on the planet, and submarine cable-damage statistics are the best evidence we have of how little is known. The bimodality is assumed to be the whole story: oceanic plateaus, large igneous provinces, megaturbidite systems, hydrothermal fields and cold seeps are not in the textbook histogram and are not in the general circulation models that use it. The age of the seafloor is confused with the age of the water: the crust beneath a point in the western Pacific may be 180 Myr old while the water above it was last at the surface 600 years ago, and the two ages control completely different processes. The sediment budget is assumed closed: 90 per cent of the terrestrial sediment is trapped on the shelf, and the 10 per cent that escapes does so through a few hundred canyons during a few extreme events — so the deep-sea record is wildly non-continuous and biased toward earthquake, cyclone and flood events. And the trench is assumed to be the bottom: the Challenger Deep has been visited by fewer people than have walked on the Moon, and the hadal zone — below 6000 m, in some 45 separate trench systems — is the deepest of Earth's environments, the least studied, and the place where microplastic fibres and organophosphate flame retardants were first detected in hadal amphipods.
:::

::: history
The first deep soundings were made by William Scoresby (Scoresby) in the Greenland Sea in 1817 and by Captain John Ross (Ross) in Baffin Bay in 1818, who reported that the bottom could not be reached and was therefore ridiculed for half a century. Matthew Fontaine Maury (Maury) published the first wind and current chart and the first physically organised *Geography of the Seas* in 1855, and organised the international synoptic wind and current sheets that made the trade-wind routes efficient. The Atlantic cable surveys of 1857–1858, and Darwin's (Darwin) three Challenger-period papers on coral atolls and subsidence, established the depth of the ocean and the subsidence of its floor. Charles Wyville Thomson (Thomson) and John Murray (Murray) led HMS *Challenger* of 1872–1876, whose 362 deep soundings, thousands of new species and 50-volume report founded oceanography as a discipline; John Murray (Murray) published the ocean's depth-to-area curve in 1885, the first statement of the bimodality that this chapter opens with, and coined the phrase "the oceanic deep-sea floor". Harry Hess (Hess) proposed seafloor spreading in 1962 and Robert Dietz (Dietz) named it in 1961; Maurice Ewing, Bruce Heezen and Marie Tharp (Tharp) discovered the global mid-ocean ridge system, its rift valley and its fracture zones between 1953 and 1959, and Tharp's and Heezen's 1957 and 1977 physiographic maps gave the first pictures of the whole floor. Abyssal plains were identified by Heezen, Ewing and Tharp (Heezen) in 1955, and guyots were named by Harry Hess (Hess) in 1946 after the Princeton geologist Arnold Guyot (Guyot). The Challenger Deep was sounded by HMS *Challenger* in 1875 and reached by the bathyscaphe *Trieste* of Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh (Piccard) in 1960; the Kaikō (1998), James Cameron (Cameron) in the *Deepsea Challenger* (2012) and the Limiting Factor of 2019 followed. Francis Shepard (Shepard) wrote *Submarine Geology* and founded coastal and shelf sedimentology, and the modern global canyon census of 15,044 canyons is the work of Harris and the GEBCO Submarine Canyons Working Group (Harris) in 2014. The sediment thickness of the ocean floor was compiled by Divins and Ludwig (Divins) in 1976 and, on a global gravity-controlled basis, by Chen and the GlobSed compilation (Chen) in 2015. Hadal biology was opened by the Soviet *Vityaz* soundings of 1952–1961, which named the Vityaz Deep, and by Anton Bruun (Bruun) and the Galathea deep-sea expeditions of 1950–1952, which recovered hadal amphipods and defined the hadal fauna; the Hadal Bioblitz of 2021 surveyed 45 trench systems in a single field season.
:::

Why is the seafloor's depth-to-area distribution bimodal?

The two peaks — shelf at about 130 m and abyssal plain at about 4400 m — are the direct expression of the **crustal dichotomy**: continental crust is granitic, thick and buoyant and stands high; oceanic crust is basaltic, thin and dense and stands low. ::: summary
- The ocean covers 70.8 per cent of the planet, holds 1.335 × 10⁹ km³ at a mean depth of 3688 m, and its floor is bimodal: an 8.9 per cent shelf at 130 m and a 50 per cent abyssal plain at 4400 m.
- The mid-ocean ridge is the longest mountain range on Earth, the trenches are 0.25 per cent of the area and the only place the surface reaches 11 km, and no ocean crust is older than about 180 Myr.
- Passive margins carry the shelves, the basins, the petroleum and the sediment trap; active margins carry the megathrusts, the arcs and the tsunamis.
- The top 2.5 m of the ocean holds as much heat as the whole atmosphere; the mixed layer holds 20–60 times as much and the whole ocean 1000 times as much.
- Pressure at the bottom of the Challenger Deep is 113 MPa, 1112 atmospheres, and compresses the water there by nearly 5 per cent — which is why potential temperature is required.
- Ninety per cent of the terrestrial sediment is trapped on the shelf, so the deep-sea record is sparse, event-driven and biased toward catastrophe.
- Most of the seafloor is mapped from satellite gravity at 200–400 m accuracy; the slope, the canyon and the trench remain effectively unknown.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise The global water film {level=1 check="2618"}
If the Earth's surface were perfectly smooth, how deep would the ocean be? Use $1.335\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{km^{3}}$ and $5.10\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$.
::: solution
$h = 1.335\times10^{9}/5.10\times10^{8} = 2618\ \mathrm{m}$. A **2.6-km** global ocean would drown every mountain on Earth except the highest **Himalayan** peaks — a useful way to remember how **small** the planet's **topographic** range is compared with its **water** inventory, and why the **African** peneplain and the **Australian** shield are so **close** to being submerged.
:::

::: exercise The shelf's share {level=1 check="32"}
Find the area of the continental shelf in million $\mathrm{km^{2}}$, given an ocean area of $3.61\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ and a shelf fraction of 8.9 per cent.
::: solution
$0.089\times361 = 32\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$ — an area larger than Africa. It receives 90 per cent of the river-borne sediment, produces 40 per cent of the ocean's carbon fixation and supports essentially all the world's capture fisheries, on less than a tenth of the ocean's area.
:::

::: exercise Pressure on the slope {level=1 check="351"}
Find the pressure, in **atmospheres**, at the base of a **3500-m** continental slope ($\rho = 1035\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$).
::: solution
$P = 1035\times9.81\times3500 = 3.56\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, and $3.56\times10^{7}/1.013\times10^{5} = 351\ \mathrm{atm}$. At 350 atmospheres a cylinder of water weighs 35 kg per square centimetre, which is why an uncompensated instrument housing implodes and why the slope — the most geomorphically active surface on the planet — is studied by cameras on wires and autonomous vehicles rather than by people.
:::
:::

::: exercise The heat capacity ratio {level=2 check="39"}
Find the ratio of the heat capacity per unit area of a 100-m mixed layer to that of the whole atmosphere.
::: solution
$\rho_w c_w h /(p_s c_a/g) = 4.1\times10^{8}/1.04\times10^{7} = 39$. A 39-fold thermal inertia is why the sea-surface temperature varies by a few degrees over a year while the continental temperature varies by tens, why El Niño can move the global mean by 0.2 °C, and why the diurnal cycle of the sea surface is barely a tenth of a degree.
:::

::: exercise The sediment thickness {level=2 check="600"}
A patch of Pacific crust **120 Myr** old receives pelagic sediment at **5 m per million years**. Find the sediment thickness, and explain why the observed thickness of the same crust is often **twice** that.
::: solution
$h = 120\times5 = 600\ \mathrm{m}$. The observed thickness is greater because turbidites — the event deposits shed from the continents — are not in the pelagic rate and can double or triple the section near a margin, and because hydrothermal and volcanic material, cosmic dust and redeposited carbonate add further. The pelagic rate is a background; the real section is event-dominated, which is exactly why the deep-sea record is biased toward catastrophe.
:::
:::

::: exercise The carbon flux {level=2 check="0.65"}
The ocean takes up $2.8\ \mathrm{Pg\ C\ yr^{-1}}$. Find the mean flux in $\mathrm{mol\ C\ m^{-2}\ yr^{-1}}$ over the whole ocean surface.
::: solution
$2.8\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g\ yr^{-1}}/12 = 2.33\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{mol\ yr^{-1}}$, and $/3.61\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{2}} = 0.65\ \mathrm{mol\ C\ m^{-2}\ yr^{-1}}$. Averaged over the globe this looks tiny; the real uptake is concentrated in the Southern Ocean, the subpolar North Atlantic and the subduction regions, where the local flux is ten times the mean — and the pattern, not the mean, determines where the carbon goes and where the acidification bites first.
:::
:::

::: exercise The ridge and sea level {level=3 check="39"}
The mid-ocean ridge system occupies 19.5 per cent of the ocean floor. If a **50 per cent** increase in the full-spreading rate shallows the ridge system by **200 m**, find the resulting first-order eustatic sea-level change.
::: solution
The ridge gains volume and displaces water, so $\Delta h = 0.195\times200 = 39\ \mathrm{m}$. The real Cretaceous rise was 100–250 m, because the first-order number is amplified by the marine transgression onto the continents — which loads the margin and downdrops it — by sediment compaction, by forebulge collapse, and by the crustal loading of the large igneous provinces of the same age. Ridge volume is a real and large mechanism, but it is not the whole of the Cretaceous, and the argument over its share has run for fifty years.
:::

::: exercise The ocean's oxygen {level=3 check="31"}
The ocean's dissolved oxygen inventory is $1.2\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{mol}$ and the atmosphere's is $3.7\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{mol}$. Find the ratio of atmospheric to oceanic oxygen, and explain why the ocean's oxygen nevertheless controls the **deep biosphere** and the **palaeoredox** record.
::: solution
$3.7\times10^{19}/1.2\times10^{18} = 31$. The atmosphere holds thirty-one times more oxygen than the ocean, and yet the ocean's inventory is the one that matters, for three reasons. Supply is bottlenecked at the surface: oxygen enters only by gas exchange across the mixed layer and is carried into the interior only by ventilation — the overturning circulation — so a stratified or stagnant ocean loses its deep oxygen within a few thousand years of being cut off. Demand is concentrated: respiration consumes oxygen at the middle depths where the sinking organic matter is remineralised, which is why the oxygen minimum zones sit at 200–1000 m and not at the surface. And the reservoir is small where it matters: the deep Pacific holds water last ventilated a thousand years ago and close to anoxic at its middle depths, so the Pacific is the ocean that records the carbon cycle and hosts the denitrifying and methanogenic ecosystems, and the mid-Palaeozoic and Cretaceous anoxic events are ocean events, not atmosphere events.

The ratio is therefore the wrong number to care about, and getting it right is the point: the atmosphere is the reservoir and the ocean is the pipe, and it is the conductance of the pipe, not the size of the reservoir, that decides whether the deep sea is aerobic — which is why oxygen is a circulation proxy and not a productivity proxy, and why the marine anoxic sedimentary deposits of the Cretaceous are read as evidence of a sluggish, stratified ocean rather than of a low-oxygen atmosphere.
:::
:::
