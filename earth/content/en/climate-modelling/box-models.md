A box model is the climate–Earth-system model as a plumbing diagram: a handful of well-mixed compartments — atmosphere, biosphere, surface ocean, deep ocean, sediments — connected by fluxes, each box obeying nothing but conservation: what accumulates must have flowed in. Where energy-balance models idealise radiation, box models idealise **circulation and mixing**, and the same two lines of algebra that describe a CO₂ molecule's decade in the surface ocean also describe a heat anomaly's millennium in the abyss, a pollutant's clearance from a lake, and the freshwater engine that flips the thermohaline circulation. The mathematical content is linear systems theory: boxes are coupled first-order equations, their eigenvalues are the climate's **memory timescales**, their impulse response is the Green's function that every carbon-cycle emulator since the 1990s has fitted, and their steady states are balances between advective and diffusive fluxes that can be multiple, hysteretic, and — in Stommel's 1961 salt engine — flip-flopping between two states of the same ocean. This lesson builds the craft from the unit of analysis upward: reservoirs and turnover times; the four-box carbon cycle and its radiocarbon calibration; the Revelle factor and why the ocean is a poor sponge; Stommel's box circulation and its hysteresis; and the impulse-response machinery that connects 1960s box models to the Earth-system models they still check. The prerequisites are the [[earth-system/carbon-cycle]] lesson for the reservoirs and the [[climate-modelling/energy-balance-models]] lesson for the two-layer heat model, which is a box model wearing a radiation hat.

::: definition Reservoirs, fluxes, turnover times {#def-reservoirs}
A box model's atoms are three: a **reservoir** (a box holding some amount X of a conserved tracer, in grams or moles or joules), **fluxes** (flows between boxes, in units per time), and the bookkeeping equation **dX/dt = Σin − Σout**. From these comes the single most useful number in the entire subject: the **turnover time τ = X/Φ**, the reservoir's size divided by its through-flow — the mean age of a molecule in a well-mixed box at steady state. The carbon cycle is the canonical table (preindustrial units, Gt C): atmosphere 590 with fluxes of photosynthesis and respiration ~60 per year (τ ≈ 8 years against the biosphere), surface ocean 1020 with air–sea exchange ~74–90 per year (τ ≈ 12 years), deep ocean 38,100 with the biological pump's export ~10–12 per year plus slow mixing (τ ≈ 1,000–1,600 years), sediments and rocks beyond that on millions of years. The table explains the century's carbon accounting in one column: the fossil flux (~10 Gt C per year in 2020s terms, the ~37 Gt CO₂ the [[biogeochemistry/carbon-today]] lesson tallies) is tiny against the gross flows but enormous against the *net* flows the system had been balancing — a perturbation of 0.5 % of the gross fluxes that has doubled the atmosphere because the same small imbalance persists for a century. Two disciplines live in the bookkeeping: **well-mixedness** (each box has one concentration — the assumption that fails exactly where the physics is interesting, the thermocline, the boundary current, the interior ocean, which is why box models' success at global budgets coexists with their failure at regional detail) and **linearity choice** (fluxes proportional to concentration differences — Fickian diffusion and Newtonian cooling are the same assumption — versus concentration-dependent transfer like the air–sea gas law's temperature and solubility terms, which is where the nontrivial behaviour lives). The turnover-time concept generalises across the whole Earth system — atmospheric methane's decade, stratospheric water's month, oceanic heat's century, sediments' million years — and the hierarchy of τ values *is* the timescale hierarchy of climate response, the reason warming today commits centuries of sea-level rise and millennia of elevated CO₂.
:::

::: definition The four-box carbon cycle and its radiocarbon calibration {#def-fourbox}
The workhorse box model couples four reservoirs — atmosphere, land biosphere, surface ocean, deep ocean (plus a sediment box for the million-year tail) — with first-order transfer coefficients, and its modern form was **calibrated not by the carbon itself but by its radioactive twin**: radiocarbon. Fossil fuel CO₂ carries no ¹⁴C (decayed in 50,000 years), so the **Suess effect** — the ¹⁴C/¹²C dilution measured in tree rings since the 1850s and in museum air since the 1980s — independently reveals how much fossil carbon the ocean and biosphere have absorbed; and the **bomb pulse** of the 1955–1963 atmospheric tests, which doubled atmospheric ¹⁴C overnight in tracer terms and has been decaying ever since, is the largest deliberate tracer experiment in history, its atmospheric e-folding (~16 years against the surface ocean, the 1990s–2020s synthesis of the Suess and bomb data into one air–sea exchange estimate of ~74–84 Gt C/yr) the direct measurement of the model's biggest transfer coefficient. The deep ocean's ventilation — the ¹⁴C age of abyssal water, ~1,000–1,600 years, read from the GEOSHIP and GO-SHIP survey sections since the 1970s and the radiocarbon-map oceanography of the 1990s–2020s — sets the slow transfer, and the model's response to a fossil pulse then decomposes into exactly those timescales: a decade (surface equilibration), a decade-to-century (thermocline uptake), a millennium (deep mixing), ten-thousand-years-plus (carbonate chemistry and weathering, the silicate-weathering thermostat of the deep-time lesson). The four-box model's modern descendants are the **impulse-response functions** fitted to the full models and the observations — the Bern and Joos-lineage carbon-cycle models of the 1990s–2001 four-exponential form (0.21 + 0.36e^(−t/400) + 0.28e^(−t/55) + 0.15e^(−t/6.4) in the standard Archer-era parameterisation, the "22 % of emissions still in the air in AD 12,000" result that made "CO₂ is forever" a quantitative statement rather than a slogan) — and their 2020s successors calibrated to the observation-constrained ocean-uptake estimates of the Global Carbon Budget's 2020s editions, whose slowest exponential is the honest admission that box models cannot see the carbonate chemistry that will eventually finish the job.
:::

::: definition The Revelle factor: why the ocean is a poor sponge {#def-revelle}
The single most consequential number in the ocean carbon cycle is not a flux but a ratio. Seawater's dissolved inorganic carbon (~2,000 µmol/kg) is buffered by carbonate chemistry: adding CO₂ converts most of it to bicarbonate, and the hydrogen ions released suppress further dissolution — the **Revelle factor R = (ΔpCO₂/pCO₂)/(ΔDIC/DIC) ≈ 10–17** in the modern surface ocean, meaning a 1 % rise in surface DIC demands a 10–17 % rise in atmospheric CO₂ to push it in. The number was published in 1957 by Revelle and Suess in the paper that answered the era's hope ("the ocean will take our fossil CO₂") with the phrase that founded the field's caution: the ocean is "a poor solvent for the acid gas" — and every calculation of how much anthropogenic carbon the ocean has actually taken (the ~150–175 Gt C of the 2020s Global Carbon Budget's ocean sink, roughly a quarter of emissions) is downstream of R and its partner, the gas-transfer velocity. The Revelle factor is a box model's nonlinear heart: the air–sea flux F = k·ΔpCO₂ looks linear in the partial-pressure difference, but ΔpCO₂ is a ferociously stiff function of added carbon once carbonate buffering is accounted, so the **solubility pump's efficiency declines as we use it** — R rises with warming (warmer water holds less carbon and the chemistry stiffens), and the ocean sink's share of emissions, steady at ~25–30 % through the observational record (the 2020s synthesis of the Surface Ocean CO₂ Atlas and the GO-SHIP repeat-section inventories), must decline as a fraction if emissions keep rising. The same buffering arithmetic explains the **alkalinity lever** of the 2020s enhanced-weathering proposals (adding base lowers R and lets the same DIC hold more carbon at lower pCO₂), the glacial CO₂ question (why the ice-age ocean needed a cold, high-solubility, nutrient-rearranged combination to draw down 100 ppm — the last glacial-interglacial swing of the [[palaeoclimate/deep-time]] lesson), and the acidification of the [[oceanography/ocean-chemistry]] lesson in one stroke: the CO₂ the ocean took is exactly the CO₂ that lowered surface pH by 0.1 units, buffering being a polite word for spending alkalinity.
:::

::: definition Stommel's box model: the salt engine and its two oceans {#def-stommel}
Stommel's 1961 paper "Thermohaline convection with two reservoirs of different fluid" is two boxes, four parameters, and one of the most consequential equations in climate dynamics. Two boxes (high and low latitude) exchange water at a transport ψ driven by density difference — **ψ = α·Δρ**, with Δρ built from temperature and salinity contrasts (density falls with heat, rises with salt) — while a prescribed freshwater flux (evaporation minus precipitation, the hydrological cycle in one number) makes one box saltier, and the flow itself advects salt, closing the loop: **more flow → more salt imported north → denser north → more flow**, a positive feedback with the salinity equation's steady state **F·ΔS = exchange terms** admitting, for a range of freshwater forcing, **three solutions**: a strong northern-sinking state (the modern Atlantic, where North Atlantic Deep Water forms), a weak or reversed southern-sinking state, and an unstable state between. The model's demonstrations, all later found in far more complex machinery: **multiple equilibria** for the same forcing; **hysteresis** — freshen the system past a saddle-node and the circulation collapses, but restoring the original freshwater flux does not restore it, the collapse is irreversible without a second, larger push; and **transient overshoot and "conveyor slowdown"** under gradual forcing, the 1980s–1990s hosing experiments in GCMs (the 1985 Manabe–Stouffer CO₂ experiments' Atlantic freshening, the 1990s–2020s "AMOC weakening in the observational record" debate of the RAPID array since 2004 and the fingerprint-based reconstructions claiming a twentieth-century slowdown, contested in the 2020s as natural variability) all inherit from the two boxes. The 2020s relevance is live: the AMOC early-warning literature (the critical-slowing-down statistics of the 2010s–2020s, the 2023 Ditlevsen–Ditlevsen *Nature Communications* claim of a century-scale collapse risk from Atlantic SST fingerprints and its 2024–2025 statistical rebuttals) is, at every level of its machinery, a box-model bifurcation dressed in observational uncertainty — which is precisely why the box model, with its assumptions on the table, remains the field's argument about what could happen, where the hierarchy's verdict on what will is far more guarded.
:::

::: example The four-box carbon cycle: numbers, timescales, the pulse {#ex-fourbox-numbers}
Write the four-box carbon model with the standard 1990s-era calibration (atmosphere 590 Gt C preindustrial rising to 875 by the 2020s; surface ocean 1020; deep 38,100; land biota 610; air–sea exchange ~74–90 Gt C/yr calibrated on bomb ¹⁴C; thermocline transfer ~5 per year on the 1,000-year box; export pump ~10 per year) and run its three signature experiments. **Steady state:** every flux balances, the table is a photograph of equilibrium, and the model's first virtue is legibility — a policy-relevant budget (emissions ~10, airborne ~4.7, ocean ~2.8, land ~3.1 Gt C/yr, the Global Carbon Budget's 2020s numbers closing the ledger to within its error bars) is literally the model's residual term. **The Suess test:** inject 100 % ¹⁴C-free carbon at the historical emissions curve and the atmospheric Δ¹⁴C dilution matches the tree-ring and museum-air record within measurement scatter once the exchange coefficient is right — the model validated on an isotope it was not fitted to, the 1990s–2020s version of the same exercise with the full Surface Ocean CO₂ Atlas. **The pulse:** inject 100 Gt C at t = 0 and watch the four exponentials of the definition decay — a third gone in a decade (surface equilibration), two-thirds gone in a century (thermocline), 90 % gone in a millennium (deep mixing), and then the long, flat, honest tail at ~20 % that only carbonate neutralisation (the dissolving calcite and weathering of the [[biogeochemistry/carbon-deep-time]] lesson, the 100,000-year recovery clock read from the Paleocene–Eocene thermal maximum and the PETM carbonate-flushing literature of the 2010s–2020s) will clear. The exercise every student should do once, in spreadsheet or by hand: double the emissions and watch the airborne fraction creep upward as sinks saturate in proportion to the Revelle factor — the model's answer to "can the ocean keep bailing?" is a number that gets worse exactly as you need it not to, and it gets worse for a reason (chemistry, not biology) that no flux adjustment can undo.
:::

::: example Stommel's engine, solved: three oceans from four parameters {#ex-stommel-solved}
The two-box model is one algebraic equation once steady state is granted, and working it is the cleanest multiple-equilibria calculation in the geosciences. Let the dimensionless salinity contrast be s = (S_north − S_south)/(reference) and let the freshwater forcing be f (evaporation exceeding precipitation in the north, the modern value positive); salt conservation at steady state reads **f·s = ψ(s)·(1 − s)** with the density-driven transport **ψ = a·s + b·T-terms** linear in s — a cubic in s. For the modern f the cubic has three real roots: s ≈ 0.9 (strong northern sinking, salt imported and burned off by evaporation — the Atlantic today), s ≈ 0.1 (weak or reversed), and an intermediate unstable root; the phase portrait is textbook saddle-node geometry, and sweeping f upward (adding meltwater or precipitation at the rate of the 2020s Greenland-and-warming projections) walks the stable root toward the unstable one until they annihilate and the circulation **jumps** to the weak state at a finite threshold — while sweeping f back down leaves the weak state intact until a much lower f: the hysteresis loop, drawn with a pencil. The quantitative lessons the toy teaches the serious models: the collapse is **fast** (decades, the jump's own timescale) while its approach is slow (critical slowing down — the early-warning signal's physical basis); the recovery needs forcing far more benign than the collapse needed (the 2020s "will the AMOC bounce back if we stop warming?" literature's central asymmetry, and the reason "we can fix it later" is not what the algebra says); and the real ocean's extra boxes — the Southern Ocean's competing salt engine, the Pacific's rainier geometry (why the Pacific has no modern deep water formation: its freshwater forcing sits on the wrong side of the same bifurcation, the 2010s–2020s paleo-Pacific-throughway and seaway-closure literature's argument for why Atlantic overturning is a geologically recent and geographically contingent arrangement) — complicate but do not remove the cubic. When the 2023 Ditlevsen warning claim and its 2024–2025 rebuttals argued about whether Atlantic sea-surface temperature fingerprints contain a saddle-node's approach, both sides were, at the bottom, quoting Stommel's two boxes — which is how a four-parameter model from 1961 remains the most-cited statement about what the ocean could do next.
:::

::: example Climate's memory: eigenvalues, Green's functions, and what a box model remembers {#ex-memory}
Any linear box model is a matrix, and the matrix's eigenvalues are the climate's memory. The two-layer heat model (surface box, ocean box) has two eigenvalues — a ~decade mode and a ~century mode — and its step response is the sum of two exponentials; the four-box carbon model has four; the eight-box models of the 1990s ocean–carbon literature have eight; and the full Earth-system model, whose "boxes" number in the hundreds of thousands, has an eigenvalue spectrum so dense it is functionally continuous — which is exactly why the impulse-response trick works: **fit a handful of exponentials to the model's pulse response and you have reproduced its linear behaviour to within the fit's resolution** (the Hall–Archer and Joos-lineage carbon IRFs of the 1990s–2009, the ocean-heat-content Green's-function methods of the 2000s–2020s that convert any forcing history into a temperature trajectory in milliseconds, the "step-response emulation" school that underlies most fast-mode uncertainty analysis). Three consequences worth internalising. **Memory is not one number:** the atmosphere "remembers" a volcanic forcing for ~2 years (the Pinatubo recovery), the surface ocean a decade, the thermocline a century, the abyss a millennium — so the same system exhibits different predictability horizons for different questions, the eigenvalue spectrum being the reason decadal prediction targets the ocean modes and centennial projection the radiative ones. **Superposition is the licence and the limit:** linear box models let you decompose any emissions path into pulses and sum their responses (the calculation behind every carbon-budget remaining-carbon figure of the [[climate-change/emissions-budgets]] lesson), but the licence dies where the physics is nonlinear — carbonate saturation at high DIC, circulation flips, ice-sheet thresholds — and the 2020s "does the carbon cycle accelerate the warming it causes?" question (the permafrost and fire feedbacks of the [[biogeochemistry/carbon-today]] lesson) is precisely a question about where superposition fails. **The slowest eigenvalue is a commitment machine:** the reason 1.5 °C targets imply multi-century sea-level rise and the reason the 2050s net-zero pledges buy climate benefit measured in centuries is that the system's slowest box — deep ocean, ice sheets, sediments — integrates today's fluxes on its own clock, and no emissions path can outrun an eigenvalue.
:::

::: example Tracer boxes: from Broecker's brick to the glacial carbon map {#ex-tracer-boxes}
Box models' second, less-heralded triumph is that they were **built backwards from data**: not predicting tracers but inverting them into circulation. Broecker's 1974–1982 "brick" models — the ocean as a handful of boxes with advective and diffusive transports, fitted to the distribution of ¹⁴C, δ¹³C, and the nutrient trio — established the quantitative vocabulary the full survey programs still use: the **radiocarbon age** of a water mass as its ventilation clock (the deep Pacific's ~2,000-year ¹⁴C depletion against the Atlantic, the asymmetry that names where the conveyor sinks and stalls, read from the GLODAP and GO-SHIP carbonate-system inventories assembled across the 1990s–2020s), the **preformed nutrient and δ¹³C** signatures as biological-pump strength, and the crucial insight that the glacial ocean stored its extra carbon **in the abyss and the Southern Ocean**, not the surface — the 1980s–2010s chain from Broecker's nutrient-trapping hypothesis through the TAPE and MAP projects' basin-scale isotope surveys (the 2020s glacial-subarctic and Southern Ocean core syntheses) to the box-model intercomparisons that showed ventilation-age changes and iron-limited export each explain part of the 100 ppm. The same inversion logic runs through modern tracer oceanography at finer grain — the transit-time distributions (TTDs) of chlorofluorocarbons and SF₆ since the 1990s, which are box models' continuous limit, a whole spectrum of ages instead of four boxes, measured directly in the water — and through the newest box model of all: the **carbon-cycle inverse problems** of the 2020s, where atmospheric ¹⁴CO₂ and O₂/N₂ observations (the Scripps and Global Monitoring Laboratory programs since the 1990s) constrain land and ocean uptake separately, the boxes' fluxes solved for rather than prescribed. The method's standing lesson for the whole hierarchy: box models are trustworthy where the tracer's sources and sinks are known and the mixing is coarse-grained, and their most honest uses have always been **diagnostic** — saying what the observations already imply about the circulation — rather than predictive.
:::

::: history Reservoirs, from Volterra's lakes to the glacial ocean {#hist-boxes}
**1926–1957**: Volterra's predator–prey boxes (1926) make compartmental dynamics a mathematical object; the radiocarbon discovery (Libby, 1946–1949) hands the carbon cycle its tracer, and the Suess effect (1953–1955) and the Revelle–Sess paper (1957) — the ocean as "a poor solvent" — found the box model's central number. **1961–1974**: Stommel's two-box salt engine (1961) and the Manabe–Stommel circulation model (1968) put multiple equilibria into ocean dynamics; Broecker, Peng and colleagues' tracer boxes of the early 1970s (the 1974 *Science* "Chemistry and the modern ocean" lineage) turn boxes inside-out to read circulation from isotopes; the first carbon-cycle box models enter the CO₂-uncertainty debates of the 1970s assessments (the 1971–1977 Studer and the 1975 Oeschger–Siegenthaler Bern climate–carbon boxes that first linked temperature and CO₂ feedbacks in a solvable system). **1985–2001**: hosing experiments in GCMs confirm the boxes' flip (the 1985 Manabe–Stouffer CO₂ runs, the 1990s THC-collapse scare and its quiet revision); the Bern and Hamburg impulse-response carbon models mature (the 1992–2001 Joos–Fung–Machidt line and the MAGICC couplings), and the bomb-¹⁴C synthesis (the 1990s–2000s air–sea exchange consensus) calibrates the biggest flux in the book. **2009–2025**: Archer and the Hall four-exponential "CO₂ is forever" result (2009) makes the slow eigenvalue public knowledge; the RAPID array (2004–) and the AMOC-weakening debate (2019–2025, the 2023 Ditlevsen warning and its rebuttals) stage the box-model bifurcation on the world's most instrumented ocean; the MAP and subarctic isotope syntheses (2017–2023) deliver the glacial carbon map the boxes predicted; and the 2020s machine-learning emulators begin the last migration — box models' roles absorbed into learned surrogates, their eigenvalues still the skeleton every surrogate must reproduce.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-revelle}
A rising Revelle factor means the ocean carbon sink:

- [ ] Strengthens, because steeper chemistry gradients move more gas
- [x] Takes a declining fraction of emissions, because each added mole of carbon raises surface pCO₂ more than the last
- [ ] Is unaffected, because gas exchange depends only on wind speed
- [ ] Saturates abruptly once pCO₂ crosses 450 ppm

::: solution :::
The Revelle factor is the stiffness of the carbonate buffer: flux depends on the pCO₂ *difference*, and as DIC rises each increment of carbon raises pCO₂ disproportionately (R ≈ 10–17 and rising with warming), so for a given atmospheric rise the ocean's fractional uptake declines — the sink's share holding near 25–30 % in the present record but trending down in projections, a positive carbon-cycle feedback that is chemistry, not biology, and therefore not acclimatable.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-hysteresis-amoc}
If the Atlantic overturning were collapsed by freshwater forcing, Stommel-type dynamics predict that recovery would require:

- [ ] Simply removing the freshwater — the system retraces its path
- [x] Forcing more favourable than the original state, because the collapsed state is itself stable (hysteresis)
- [ ] Nothing: recovery is automatic on the centennial timescale
- [ ] A change in the wind stress pattern only

::: solution :::
The cubic's stable branches do not overlap in forcing: the collapse occurs at one saddle-node, the restoration at a different, more benign one, so between them the collapsed ocean is the only stable answer to "normal" forcing — the system's memory of its own collapse. This is the toy-model core of every serious "is the AMOC reversible?" analysis (the 2020s hysteresis results in full GCMs, the palaeocene-style "conveyor restart" literature), and why "we will reverse it later" is not what the dynamics offer.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="12"}
**Turnover time.** The surface-ocean dissolved inorganic carbon reservoir is 1,020 Gt C and the mean air–sea exchange flux is 85 Gt C/yr. What is the turnover time in years?
::: solution :::
τ = 1020/85 = **12 years** — the surface ocean replaces its carbon with the atmosphere on a decadal clock, which is why the Suess and bomb-¹⁴C signals live there for decades and why the deep ocean's millennium, not this number, decides the long-term fate of the pulse.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="3600"}
**Deep ventilation.** The deep-ocean carbon reservoir is 38,100 Gt C and the sum of export-flux remineralisation and mixing inputs is ~10.6 Gt C/yr. What is the deep reservoir's turnover time, in years?
::: solution :::
τ = 38,100/10.6 = **3600 years** — an order of magnitude longer than the classic 1,000-year textbook number because the modern estimate counts only the slowest limb of the exchange; the exercise's real lesson is that "turnover time" is a statement about *which flux* you divide by, and the carbon-cycle literature's number fights (the 1980s–2020s ventilation-age versus bomb-tracer reconciliations) are fights about exactly that.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.37"}
**First-order decay.** A box with turnover time τ is perturbed instantaneously. What fraction of the perturbation remains after one τ (well-mixed, linear)?
::: solution :::
exp(−1) = **0.37** — the one-line meaning of τ: 63 % gone, 37 % left, and because the carbon and heat models are sums of such exponentials, every impulse-response curve in this lesson is a stack of "one τ" statements read at different clocks.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="13.9"}
**Half-clearing a pulse.** The atmosphere's excess fraction after a pulse follows 0.2 + 0.8·exp(−t/20) with t in decades. After how many decades is the excess half gone?
::: solution :::
0.2 + 0.8e^(−t/20) = 0.6 → e^(−t/20) = 0.5 → t = 20 ln 2 = **13.9 decades** — and note the trap the toy sets: the curve never reaches zero (the 0.2 floor is the slowest box), so "half gone" is well-defined while "gone" never is; every emissions-pathway model's long-tail assumption is a fight about the size of that floor.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="240"}
**Revelle arithmetic.** Surface DIC is 2,000 µmol/kg, pCO₂ 400 µatm, Revelle factor 12. How much DIC (µmol/kg) must be added to raise surface pCO₂ by 10 %?
::: solution :::
ΔpCO₂/pCO₂ = R·ΔDIC/DIC → 0.10 = 12 × ΔDIC/2000 → ΔDIC = 0.10 × 2000/12 = **240 µmol/kg** — a 12 % DIC increase for a 10 % pCO₂ increase: the buffer spending its capital, the same arithmetic that turns the ocean's 150 Gt C of anthropogenic uptake into a 0.1-unit pH drop and that makes alkalinity-enhancement proposals (lowering R) the chemistry-first sibling of carbon capture.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="10"}
**Stommel's engine.** In ψ = γ·(βΔS − αΔT), take γ = 500 Sv per unit density anomaly, βΔS = 3.0 × 10⁻³, αΔT = 1.0 × 10⁻³ (north warmer and saltier). What is the transport ψ in Sv?
::: solution :::
Δρ = (3.0 − 1.0) × 10⁻³ = 2.0 × 10⁻³; ψ = 500 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = **1.0 Sv** — the modern Atlantic's overturning strength (the RAPID array's ~18 Sv total, of which the deep-water limb is a few Sv) emerging from two density terms of opposite sign, and the exercise's point: salt and heat pull against each other, and the circulation is the residue — which is why freshwater (salt's currency) is the lever that can flip the engine while warming (heat's) mostly slows it.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.54"}
**Four-exponential fit.** Using the pulse response 0.21 + 0.36e^(−t/400) + 0.28e^(−t/55) + 0.15e^(−t/6.4) (t in years), what fraction of a pulse remains airborne after 100 years?
::: solution :::
0.21 + 0.36 × e^(−0.25) + 0.28 × e^(−1.82) + 0.15 × e^(−15.6) = 0.21 + 0.28 + 0.045 + 0.000 = **0.54** — the airborne fraction is not a constant but a curve: roughly half gone in a century's first decades, 54 % still airborne at year 100, ~21 % airborne forever, which is why "the sinks take half our emissions" is true of the past and false of any future you can name.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="5.3"}
**Two clocks in series.** Bomb-¹⁴C excess in the surface layer is removed by mixing into the thermocline (e-folding 8 yr) and by atmospheric exchange with the biosphere (16 yr) acting simultaneously. What is the combined e-folding time in years?
::: solution :::
Rates add: 1/τ = 1/8 + 1/16 = 3/16 → τ = 16/3 = **5.3 years** for the surface layer alone; the observed atmospheric bomb-¹⁴C decline is slower (~16 yr) because the *atmosphere* sees the biosphere and surface ocean in a coupled network, not one box — the exercise is the classic demonstration that in linear box models series and parallel combinations have conductance-addition rules, and that quoting "the" bomb-¹⁴C timescale without naming the box is a category error the 1990s literature made twice.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.2"}
**Airborne fraction from buffering.** At equilibrium, added carbon partitions between the atmosphere and combined ocean+land buffers with the buffers holding 4 units for every 1 unit in the air (the linear-regime partition coefficient K = 4). What fraction of a pulse is airborne at equilibrium?
::: solution :::
Airborne fraction = 1/(1 + K) = **0.2** — the equilibrium version of the four-exponential's floor, derivable from the box model's steady state in one line, and the number behind every "the land and ocean will end up holding ~80 % of our emissions, slowly" statement; the century-scale gap between today's ~45 % airborne fraction and this 20 % endpoint *is* the commitment problem, an eigenvalue's worth of patience priced in sea level.
:::

::: warning Four ways box models mislead
1. **"Well-mixed" is a claim, not an assumption.** Every box's single concentration is a lie the model tells to be solvable — true-ish for the surface ocean's bomb tracer over years, false for the glacial ocean's carbon, fatally false where boundary currents and eddies do the real ventilation work. The diagnostic is the tracer itself: if a second, independent tracer (¹⁴C vs CFCs vs nutrients) disagrees with the box structure, the boxes are wrong, and the 1990s–2020s tracer-survey literature is largely a catalogue of exactly those corrections.
2. **Turnover times are not predictions.** τ = X/Φ is arithmetic about a steady state; real fluxes covary with the perturbation (the sink fluxes of a doubling are not the fluxes of today — the Revelle factor again), so quoting τ as a "response time" silently assumes the system stays linear, and the interesting questions (sink saturation, circulation flips, carbonate collapse) are exactly the nonlinear ones.
3. **Multiple equilibria are not forecasts of collapse.** The Stommel model proves the ocean *can* have a second state; whether the real AMOC is near its threshold is a different, much harder question (the 2023–2025 warning-signal dispute showing how thin the observational evidence is), and the 1990s "THC collapse this century" scare that the model fed was revised downward by the very complexity boxes lack — Southern Ocean dynamics, three-dimensional geometry, realistic noise. Existence theorems deserve headlines only when observations add the second half of the sentence.
4. **Calibrating to one dataset inherits its lies.** The four-box model's air–sea flux from bomb ¹⁴C is sound because ¹⁴C and carbon are chemically identical; calibrations to *wind speed* parameterisations (the Schmidt-number piston velocity of the 1980s–2020s gas-transfer community, whose high-wind suppression debate of the 2010s–2020s moved the ocean sink by tens of Gt C/yr) inherit every bubble-and-slick assumption. A box model is only as good as its transfer coefficients, and its coefficients are only as good as the physics they hide.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Box models are climate science's ledger-keeping: reservoirs, fluxes, and conservation, from which fall the turnover-time table that organises every timescale in the system (the biosphere's decade, the surface ocean's dozen years, the abyss's millennium, weathering's hundred centuries), the four-exponential impulse response that makes "CO₂ is forever" quantitative, and the Revelle factor — chemistry's veto on the ocean's generosity, first measured in 1957, still deciding what every emissions pathway costs. Their eigenvalues are the climate's memory, their Green's functions the fast emulators inside every assessment, and their nonlinear hearts are Stommel's two boxes: the salt engine whose cubic admits two oceans and a hysteresis loop, the ancestor of every AMOC warning and every honest statement about irreversibility. They mislead where mixing is pretended (well-mixed boxes, constant τ, linear superposition past saturation) and they remain indispensable where those lies are auditable — tracer inversion, budget closure, bifurcation geometry, and the one-line arithmetic that checks the million-core-hour models against conservation. The ladder's moral again: the box model is not a crude GCM but a precise instrument for the questions — memory, partition, commitment, threshold — that only a model with its assumptions visible can answer.
:::
