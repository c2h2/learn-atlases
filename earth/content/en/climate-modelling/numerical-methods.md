Between the equations of motion — the beautiful, continuous, infinitely-differentiable primitive equations of the [[atmospheric-dynamics/equations-of-motion]] lesson — and a climate projection sits a machine that can only add, and can only add numbers at points. **Numerical methods** are the art of replacing derivatives with differences, integrals with sums, and smooth fields with finite lists of values, in a way that keeps the answers honest: consistent (the discrete equations approach the continuous ones as the grid refines), conservative (mass, energy, tracers are neither created nor destroyed by the arithmetic), stable (errors do not grow without bound), and — the demand that makes the field an engineering discipline rather than an applied-mathematics seminar — fast enough on real hardware to run a century in weeks. This lesson is the working mechanics of the hierarchy's top rungs: how a grid is chosen and what its poles cost; why the **Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition** chains the time step to the grid spacing and thereby sets the sixteenfold cost law of the previous lesson; why the simple advection equation, discretised naively, manufactures fake diffusion and fake oscillations that pollute twenty-year integrations (and how upwinding, spectral methods and the semi-Lagrangian trick each answer the poison); how implicit solvers buy time steps at the price of elliptic solves; and how the **dynamical core** — the named, benchmarked, contested numerical engine of every centre's model, from the spectral-transform tradition to the finite-volume and mimetic grids of the 2010s — is tested against standard cases before a single cloud is parameterised. No prior numerical-analysis course is assumed; the exercises ask you to compute the numbers that decide real model configurations, because in this subject the numerics are never innocent: every truncation error is a parameterisation in disguise, and the 2010s–2020s "grey zone" debates about convection-permitting models are the proof that the field still argues about where arithmetic ends and physics begins.

::: definition Discretisation, truncation error, order of accuracy {#def-discretisation}
Replace ∂f/∂x by a difference quotient and you have made a **discretisation**: the central difference [f(x+Δx) − f(x−Δx)]/2Δx equals the true derivative plus a **truncation error** — Taylor's theorem gives −(Δx²/6)f‴ for the central scheme, −(Δx/2)f″ for the one-sided — and the scheme is called **second order** or **first order** according to how fast that error vanishes as the grid refines. The consequences are the working arithmetic of convergence studies: halving Δx cuts a second-order error by four, so the **Richardson extrapolation** (combine the coarse and fine solutions as (4·fine − coarse)/3 to cancel the leading error) turns two runs into a better one and, run across three resolutions, into an **order-of-accuracy check** — the method of manufactured solutions and the standard verification battery that a dynamical core must pass before any physics is trusted (the Jacot–Whitaker and Williamson test-suite tradition of the 1990s–2020s). Two deeper cautions live in the definition. **Consistency is not convergence:** a consistent scheme can be unstable (the next definition's CFL), and the Lax equivalence theorem — for well-posed linear problems, consistency plus stability implies convergence, with convergence at the scheme's order — is the field's founding promise and its perpetual audit: nonlinear, rotating, stratified, discretised-twice climate dynamics sits outside the theorem's hypotheses, which is why every new core is *tested*, not proved. And **what is conserved:** a scheme can be second-order accurate on a passive tracer and still destroy total mass or energy at the level of round-off-plus-truncation over a century integration — the reason modern cores (the finite-volume families of the 2000s–2020s, the mimetic and structure-preserving schemes of the same decades) are designed to make discrete analogues of the conservation theorems exact by construction, letting accuracy vary and conservation be a guarantee. The EBM and box models of the earlier lessons hid all this; in a GCM it is the difference between a simulation and an artefact.
:::

::: definition Stability: the CFL condition and the tyranny of the time step {#def-cfl}
Discretise the advection equation ∂q/∂t + u·∇q = 0 with the forward-in-time, central-in-space scheme and von Neumann's trick — insert a wave e^(i(kx−ωt)), watch its discrete amplitude — yields the verdict that launched a thousand supercomputers: the scheme is unstable for *any* nonzero time step, because the discrete phase speed is wrong in a way that feeds the wave's own error back upward. The fix and the law come together: explicit schemes are stable only while information stays inside the stencil, **CFL: u·Δt/Δx ≤ C_max** (the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy number, 1 for many schemes, lower for multi-stage Runge–Kutta), and for the compressible atmosphere the binding wave is not the wind but the fastest signal — gravity and acoustic waves at tens to hundreds of m/s — which is why the time step is chained to the grid at ~Δx and why the previous lesson's sixteenfold cost law holds. The definition's three professional corollaries. **Filtering is a licence:** semi-implicit and soundproof (anelastic) formulations remove the acoustic waves from the system — the 1950s quasi-geostrophic filtering that made Richardson's dream affordable, extended by the horizontal-implicit Helmholtz solves of modern semi-implicit cores (the ECMWF IFS tradition since the 1990s–2020s) buy Δt beyond the explicit CFL limit at the price of an elliptic solve per step, trading the easy parallelism of local stencils for global linear algebra — a decision as much about supercomputer architecture (the 2010s–2020s exascale critique of global solves) as about mathematics. **Diffusion has its own CFL:** explicit parabolic terms demand Δt < Δx²/2κ, the *quadratic* law that makes turbulence closures (the Smagorinsky and turbulent-kinetic-energy schemes of the boundary layer) the hidden time-step dictators at fine resolution, and implicit diffusion its own elliptic tax. **The time step is a tuning parameter in practice:** every centre's Δt is a negotiated compromise among CFL limits, physics-coupling frequency (the split-step operator splitting of the physics — radiation hourly, convection every step, the Strang-splitting error analysis of the 2010s showing climate-relevant sensitivities to coupling order), and round-off accumulation at exascale concurrency — and the honest model intercomparisons (the dynamical-core comparison projects of the 2000s–2020s, the aqua-planet CMIPs) exist partly to separate what the physics said from what the numerics allowed.
:::

::: definition Transport schemes: numerical diffusion, dispersion, and the semi-Lagrangian trick {#def-transport}
Advecting a front is where discretisation errors become visible weather: the exact solution translates the profile unchanged, but every scheme alters it, and *how* it lies is written in its modified equation. **First-order upwind** is stable and monotone — and diffusive, adding an effective diffusivity u·Δx/2 that smears a front over tens of grid lengths in a day's integration, which in a 20-km model is a fake mixed layer thicker than the real one. **Second-order central and Lax–Wendroff** schemes kill the smearing — and replace it with **dispersion**: oscillations that ring around the front, unphysical in compressible tracers and unstable when they go negative (the positivity problem for water vapour and cloud species). The field's answers, all live in production: **limited high-order schemes** (the flux-corrected transport of the 1970s–2000s, the monotone finite-volume families of the 2000s–2020s — PPM, MPDATA's iterative anti-diffusion, the positive-definite FV dynamical cores of GFDL's 2004–2020s lineage — which blend high-order accuracy with limiter-enforced monotonicity, accepting local order reduction at extrema to banish ringing); **spectral and semi-Lagrangian global methods** (the spectral-transform tradition of the 1970s–2020s ECMWF and climate models, exact for the global wave field up to truncation, paired since Robert's 1966 insight and the operational adoptions of the 1990s with trajectory-following advection that steps along the wind and interpolates, remaining stable at wind speeds far past the explicit CFL limit — the technique that made ~10-km global models with hour-length time steps routine); and **spectral-element and discontinuous-Galerkin cores** of the 2010s–2020s (the NCAR–DUKE–ETSM high-order hierarchy and the ICON and MPAS efforts) that push accuracy up by raising polynomial order within elements rather than refining grids. The climate-relevant punchline, learned the hard way across the 2000s–2020s: transport errors are not noise — they are **climate biases**. The numerical diffusivity of the transport scheme sets how sharp ocean fronts and cloud bands stay; the dispersion errors rectify (bias) tracer fields near gradients; and the 2010s–2020s "eddy saturation and eddy killing" literature showed that coarse-model numerical dissipation damps the very ocean eddies whose absence then changes heat transport — numerics and physics so entangled that the question "is this bias physical?" has no answer except "run it finer and see."
:::

::: definition Grids, poles, and the dynamical core {#def-grids}
Where to put the points is a geometry problem with a political history. **Latitude–longitude grids** are the natural mapping of the sphere's coordinates and the home of the spectral-transform method (which sidesteps the longitude points by working in wavenumber space, the T42-to-T1275 ladder of ECMWF's operational history from the 1970s to the 2020s) — but they oversample the poles by 1/cosφ, forcing tiny grid cells (and tiny CFL time steps) at the top of the world, tamed by polar filters, reduced grids, and clustering tricks whose artefacts (the polar noise of the 1990s–2020s comparisons) never fully die. **Quasi-uniform alternatives** spread the points evenly: the **icosahedral hexagonal–pentagonal mesh** (the 1990s Ringler–Randall–Purser lineage, grown into the HOMME core of NCAR's Community Earth System Model and Japan's NICAM legacy), the **cubed sphere** (Rančić and Sadourny's 1992 lineage, grown into FV3, the finite-volume core that became the 2016–2020s community and operational standard at NOAA's modelling centres), the **icosahedral–geodesic mesh** of Germany's ICON (the Max Planck Institute for Meteorology's 2000s–2020s nonhydrostatic icon-atmosphere), and the **hexagonal-prism mesh** of MPAS (the National Center for Atmospheric Research community's 2010s–2020s MPAS (Model for Prediction Across Scales) atmosphere-and-ocean family). The proliferation is the point: by the 2020s every major centre has a **dynamical core** — the named, versioned, benchmarked numerical engine below the physics — and the **dynamical-core renaissance** (the 2000s–2020s replacement of the 1980s spectral-transform and latitude–longitude finite-difference cores) was driven by one engineering fact: nonhydrostatic, km-scale, cloud-resolving dynamics on a sphere need a mesh without poles, a scheme without fake diffusion at the front, and a solver whose parallel decomposition scales to the hundred-thousand cores of the exascale era (the 2020s–2020s scaling studies of FV3, ICON and MPAS on Frontier-, LUMI- and MareNostrum-class machines, where the elliptic solves of semi-implicit cores and the load-balance of adaptive meshes became first-order design questions). The standard tests that arbitrate the contest — the Jacot–Whitaker and Williamson baroclinic instability case (the 1992 test suite whose reference solutions every new core reproduces to machine ambition), the Rossby–Haurwitz and mountain tests, the aqua-planet climate drift of the CMIP-style intercomparisons — are the field's peer review of arithmetic, and their existence is the difference between the GCM era and Richardson's: nobody has to take a dynamical core's word for anything any more.
:::

::: example Choosing a time step, in public, at three centres {#ex-timestep}
The CFL number is not abstract: it is the first configuration decision of every model run, and its arithmetic is worth doing in the open. Take a global model at Δx ≈ 50 km (~1°) with peak explicit signals: gravity–buoyancy waves with phase speeds up to u + c ≈ 100 m/s in the upper atmosphere where the wind jets run. Explicit compressible CFL with C_max ≈ 0.5 gives Δt ≈ 0.5 × 50,000/100 = 250 s — and indeed the latitude–longitude finite-difference models of the 1990s–2000s ran 15–30 minute steps at this resolution, the lower values because vertical staggering and physics coupling shave the safe number. Now three variations the real world forces. **Semi-implicit filtering** removes the acoustic and (horizontally) gravity-wave constraints: the same model solves a Helmholtz equation per step and runs Δt = 20–30 minutes at 25 km, where the explicit model would need ~5 — the exact trade of elliptic solves for step size, and the reason ECMWF's spectral-transform line stayed competitive at ever-finer grids through the 2000s–2020s. **Nonhydrostatic km-scale:** at Δx = 2.5 km with updrafts and acoustic modes unfiltered, Δt falls to seconds (the 2010s–2020s ICON and NICAM configurations run 10–20 s steps at 2.5–5 km), and a week of simulation is ~50 million steps — the reason global cloud-resolving weeks (the DYAMOND projects of 2019–2024) were exascale-adjacent events and remain the most expensive integrations in the field. **The ocean's own clocks:** the ocean model's CFL binds on baroclinic currents (~1 m/s) and, worse, its explicit viscosity drags in the Δx²/2κ law — which is why ocean models historically ran at 1° with days-long steps while the atmosphere ran at 100 km with minutes, and why the 2010s–2020s "eddy-permitting to eddy-resolving" transition (the 1/4° and 1/12° configurations of the CMIP6 HighResMIP and the 2020s digital-twin prototypes) multiplied coupled-model cost faster than Moore's law politely kept pace. The exercise's general form — write down the fastest signal, divide, apply the scheme's C_max, subtract for physics — is the calculation every modelling email thread argues about without writing down.
:::

::: example A front in a box: measuring the fake diffusion {#ex-front}
Run the classic laboratory experiment — advect a smooth-step tracer once around a nondivergent circulation and compare to where it started — and the schemes confess. With first-order upwind at u = 10 m/s and Δx = 100 km, the modified equation carries an effective diffusivity u·Δx/2 = 5 × 10⁵ m²/s, fifty times the ocean's observed background mixing and comparable to the most aggressive boundary-layer schemes: after one day the front's gradient is halved by arithmetic alone, and after a month the front is gone, replaced by a smooth ramp that conserves the tracer perfectly while lying about its structure. This is not a pedagogical curiosity: coarse ocean models of the 1990s–2000s carried numerical diffusivities of exactly this order in their western boundary currents and mixed layers, and the 2000s–2020s literature on "eddy killing" — coarse grids and their transport schemes damping mesoscale eddies, the eddy kinetic energy falling by factors of several between 1/10° and 1° configurations (documented across the CMIP6 HighResMIP ocean analyses and the 2010s–2020s global ocean model intercomparisons) — is the same experiment at planetary scale, with heat-transport and carbon-uptake consequences attached. The quantitative repairs are the definition's schemes: monotone high-order finite volume (PPM's piecewise-parabolic reconstruction, MPDATA's iterative anti-diffusion that computes and subtracts its own leading error — the Smolarkiewicz 1980s lineage still running in cloud and atmospheric chemistry models of the 2020s), shape-preserving limiters that sacrifice order at fronts to keep positivity, and — for the ocean's interior — the Gent–McWilliams and displacement-based formulations that deliberately mix along isopycnals instead of across them, because that is where the physics mixes. The lesson generalises to every conserved scalar in the course: before asking "what does the model's mixing parameterisation do?", ask "what does its transport scheme do by default?", because in coarse models the answer has historically been "more, and it was never in the documentation."
:::

::: example The baroclinic wave that audits the world's cores {#ex-baroclinic}
In 1992 Williamson and colleagues published an initial condition so simple it became the field's lie detector: a frictionless, land-free, physics-free aqua-planet with an analytically specified jet and a small sinusoidal height perturbation — a baroclinic wave that grows, breaks, and radiates, with everything predictable from the equations alone. Every dynamical core must now reproduce the reference case (the test suite's vorticity and height fields at day 6 and day 15, compared on the same interpolation) before its developer's climate claims are read seriously, and the results are a public census of numerical philosophy: spectral-transform cores (the T42–T340 ladder) show their global accuracy and their truncation noise; latitude–longitude finite-difference cores show their polar filtering artefacts; icosahedral and cubed-sphere cores show their mesh-direction ripples; the semi-Lagrangian transporters show their interpolation smoothing at jet speeds. The test's real value is that it separates the two ways a core can be wrong: **linear errors** (phase speed, amplitude damping — visible in the day-6 wave and computable from the modified equation) and **nonlinear errors** (how the breaking wave's potential-vorticity filaments smear or ring, which no analysis closes and only the intercomparison sees — the potential-vorticity gradient eroding differently in every core, the 2000s–2020s "conservative versus advective form" literature's live controversy about whether discrete PV conservation should be built into the stencil). The same battery, extended — the Rossby–Haurwitz wave, the mountain test with its spurious gravity-wave noise, the aqua-planet climate-drift protocol of the CMIP dynamical-core comparisons of the 2010s–2020s — now arbitrates the questions the previous lesson's cost law makes expensive: whether the semi-implicit spectral tradition's global solves survive the exascale critique (the 2020s move of several centres to local-stencil, explicit, mesh-uniform cores is partly an arithmetic-performance argument, floating-point operations per byte before it is a physics argument), and whether "the model's jet bias is dynamical-core noise" can be distinguished from "the model's jet bias is the physics" — the honest answer of the intercomparisons being that at current resolutions the two are entangled at the level of a metre per second of jet, which is the same fraction of a signal that matters for European and monsoon climate. The baroclinic test is where the hierarchy's top rung does its homework; a centre that cannot show the plot has not done the arithmetic.
:::

::: example The grey zone: when the numerics become the convection scheme {#ex-greyzone}
Between the parameterised world (clouds smaller than a grid cell, the [[climate-modelling/parameterisation]] lesson's domain) and the resolved world (deep convective clouds as explicit updrafts) lies the **grey zone** — grid spacings of roughly 1–4 km where the transport scheme, the diffusion, and the subgrid mixing are simultaneously too coarse to represent convection honestly and too crude to ignore it — and the 2010s–2020s cloud-permitting literature has had to confront the fact that model outcomes there depend on the numerics as much as the physics. The documented cases are a curriculum: the stratocumulus-capping-inversion depth and the transition to cumulus fields shift by hundreds of metres with the advection scheme's diffusivity across the 250-m-to-4-km ladder of the 2010s large-eddy-to-lesser-eddy studies; the diurnal precipitation cycle over land — fixed by convection-permitting resolution in the UK's 1.5-km and the GermanICON-D2 studies of the 2010s–2020s — is partly won because the numerics stop smearing the moist static energy that drives the afternoon storm; and the 2024–2025 stratocumulus-breakup scare (cloud-resolving simulations at ~25 m finding a dramatically lower CO₂ threshold for low-cloud dissipation than the parameterised models carried, the CMIP-generation response pending) is the grey-zone problem in its most consequential form: the answer moved when the numerics got finer, and nobody can yet say whether the new numerics or the new physics moved it. The methodological response is the field's best practice and this lesson's thesis: **numerical convergence is a scientific result**, not a detail — run the ladder, document what changes (front sharpness, EKE, cloud skewness, precipitation phase spectra), and treat any grey-zone claim without a convergence study as provisional. The same discipline applies to the physics-coupling split: the 2010s–2020s operator-splitting experiments showed climate-relevant differences (land-surface and radiation coupling order shifting daily-mean fluxes by several W/m²) from nothing but the sequence in which the boxes are stepped — Strang splitting versus Lie splitting, hourly versus per-step radiation — arithmetic choices that no equation in the documentation's dynamical-core section admits making.
:::

::: history From one hand-worked day to exascale weeks {#hist-numerics}
**1910–1950**: Richardson hand-integrates a six-hour forecast (1910, published 1922) and fails for want of filtering and smoothing; the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy theorem (1928) and von Neumann's stability analysis of difference schemes (1947, with Richtmyer and the Los Alamos shock work) found numerical analysis; and Charney, Fjørtoft and von Neumann's 1950 one-day baroclinic integration on the ENIAC — Richardson's dream realised by filtering out the sound waves — proves the whole programme in twenty-four hours of machine time for six weeks of computation. **1956–1979**: Phillips's 1956 GCM and the first generation of spectral methods (the 1970s Orszag–Bourke–Hoskins spectral-transform line, ECMWF's operational spectral ladder from 1975) make the sphere computable; the semi-Lagrangian advection insight (Robert 1966) waits a decade for computational maturity; the flux-corrected-transport and positivity schemes of the 1970s (Zalesak, Smolarkiewicz's MPDATA lineage from 1983) answer the advection poisons. **1980–2000**: superparameterisation and the first global cloud models presage the grey zone; the semi-Lagrangian revolution reaches operations (the 1991–1993 ECMWF and Canadian adoptions) and buys the resolution decade; the Williamson baroclinic test suite (1992) and the dynamical-core intercomparisons establish arithmetic peer review; the icosahedral and cubed-sphere cores mature (Ringler–Randall–Purser's 1990s–2000s work, Rančić–Sadourny's legacy into FV3). **2000–2025**: the nonhydrostatic, mesh-uniform renaissance — NICAM's global cloud-resolving runs (2006–), ICON (2010s–), MPAS (2010s–), FV3's operational adoption (2016–) — with semi-implicit spectral cores defending their position into the 2020s; the DYAMOND and TWINS week-to-season kilometre-scale experiments (2019–2024) demonstrate the exascale regime; and the 2020s arithmetic-performance and ML-surrogate literatures reopen the field's oldest question — the price of a time step — with GPUs, mixed precision and learned physics now in the ledger.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-cfl}
Why does refining a model's horizontal grid by a factor of two force the time step to shrink, for explicit schemes?

- [ ] Because finer grids need more vertical levels
- [x] Because the CFL condition requires information to stay within the stencil: the fastest waves cross a smaller cell in less time
- [ ] Because the physics parameterisations run more often
- [ ] Because round-off error grows with grid size

::: solution :::
Stability demands u·Δt/Δx below the scheme's limit, so Δt scales with Δx: halving the cell halves the step, and combined with eight times the points (three dimensions) and twice the steps, cost rises sixteenfold per simulated year — the cost law of the model hierarchy, derived here from a single dimensionless number. Semi-implicit and semi-Lagrangian schemes relax the bound for selected wave types, which is precisely why they are worth their elliptic-solve and interpolation taxes.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-greyzone}
The "grey zone" of convection refers to grid spacings where:

- [ ] Clouds are too small to matter and are ignored
- [x] Convection is partially resolved by the dynamics while the parameterisation is still active, so numerics and parameterisation compete
- [ ] Only the ocean's mixed layer is unresolved
- [ ] Radar observations are too coarse to validate models

::: solution :::
At ~1–4 km, updrafts occupy a few cells: the transport scheme's diffusion smears what the convection scheme simultaneously tries to represent, and double-counting or cancellation depends on both. This is why cloud-permitting results require convergence studies across the spacing ladder (the 2010s–2020s large-eddy-to-grey-zone literature) and why the 2024–2025 stratocumulus-breakup result is treated as provisional until the numerics-physical attribution is settled: at the resolution frontier, changing the arithmetic changes the climate.
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="200"}
**CFL step.** An explicit scheme with C_max = 1 advects at u_max = 50 m/s on a Δx = 10 km grid. What is the maximum stable time step, in seconds?
::: solution :::
Δt = C_max·Δx/u = 10,000/50 = **200 s** — the acoustic/gravity-wave ceiling at 10 km; double the resolution, halve the step, and note that the real number is lower still once C_max < 1 for multi-stage time stepping and physics coupling — every centre's advertised step is this division with a safety margin written into the fine print.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="4"}
**Order of accuracy.** A second-order dynamical core is run at Δx and at Δx/2. By what factor should the truncation-error norm shrink?
::: solution :::
Second order means error ∝ Δx², so halving the grid shrinks the error by 2² = **4** — the ratio a convergence study must find (log-log slope 2) before the core is trusted; finding 1 means your limiter is eating the order at every front, the standard first failure of production transport schemes.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="50000"}
**Diffusive CFL.** Explicit diffusion with κ = 10⁵ m²/s (an ocean mesoscale eddy viscosity) on a Δx = 100 km grid requires Δt < Δx²/(2κ). What is the limit, in seconds?
::: solution :::
Δt = (10⁵)²/(2 × 10⁵) = 10¹⁰/2 × 10⁵ = **50000 s** (~14 h) — the quadratic law in action: this parabolic limit, not the currents, set the time step of a whole generation of coarse ocean models, and implicit diffusion (another elliptic solve) is the standard escape — with the same implicit-versus-parallel trade as the acoustic filtering.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="5"}
**Fake mixing ratio.** First-order upwind advection at u = 10 m/s on a 100 km grid has effective diffusivity u·Δx/2. How many times the ocean's canonical background diffusivity of 10⁵ m²/s is that?
::: solution :::
u·Δx/2 = 10 × 10⁵/2 = 5 × 10⁵ m²/s; ratio = **5** — the coarse model mixes five times the "tuned" background before any parameterisation is switched on, which is why the 1990s–2000s practice of tuning eddy diffusivities in coarse models produced values that were partly numerics wearing physics's clothes, and why the 2010s–2020s eddy-permitting generation re-tuned everything.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="8192"}
**Spectral grid counting.** A T42 spectral model's linear grid has 128 longitudes × 64 latitudes. How many points per level?
::: solution :::
128 × 64 = **8192** — the entire atmosphere of the CMIP1-era models per vertical level (with ~10 levels, ~80,000 prognostic numbers for the globe), against the 2020s production models' 10⁷–10⁸ per level: the resolution history of climate modelling is the history of that factor of a thousand, and the cost law says each factor of 2 in Δx cost 16× per year simulated, which is the whole budget story in two sentences.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="36"}
**Semi-Lagrangian departure point.** A semi-Lagrangian step of 30 minutes at u = 20 m/s traces the wind backwards. How far (km) does the trajectory go?
::: solution :::
20 m/s × 1,800 s = 36,000 m = **36 km** — past the neighbouring cell, which is exactly why the scheme is stable beyond the explicit CFL limit: it doesn't propagate information cell-to-cell, it fetches the value from the departure point by interpolation — paying for the licence in interpolation smoothing — the scheme's own modified-equation diffusion that the monotone semi-Lagrangian literature of the 2010s (the FV3 and ICON transport papers) keeps trying to un-spend.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.47"}
**Richardson extrapolation.** A second-order model gives a regional precipitation bias of 2.0 K-equivalent at coarse resolution and 1.6 at half the grid spacing. What is the extrapolated "exact" value, (4·fine − coarse)/3?
::: solution :::
(4 × 1.6 − 2.0)/3 = (6.4 − 2.0)/3 = **1.47** — the two-run estimate of the continuum limit, the statistic every resolution-forcing study should report (and the 2010s–2020s "resolution is a tuning parameter" critique's demand that it does), valid only if the two runs differ by truncation error and not by which parameterisations woke up at the finer grid — the grey-zone caveat again.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="175"}
**Effective resolution.** A modelling rule of thumb: a grid resolves wavelengths down to ~7 grid intervals. What is the shortest wavelength usefully represented at Δx = 25 km?
::: solution :::
7 × 25 = **175 km** — the number that ends the "our model is 25 km so it sees mesoscale eddies" claim (the first baroclinic deformation radius, 10–50 km, is invisible: the model is eddy-permitting at best in the richest regions and eddy-parameterised everywhere else), and the reason "grid spacing" and "resolution" are different words that model developers never confuse and users constantly do.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.01"}
**Round-off accumulation.** A climate integration takes 10¹⁰ floating-point updates per variable with single-precision (ε ≈ 10⁻⁷) round-off, accumulating as a random walk (√N·ε relative). What is the accumulated relative error?
::: solution :::
√(10¹⁰) × 10⁻⁷ = 10⁵ × 10⁻⁷ = **0.01** — one per cent by arithmetic alone, which is why climate models historically ran double precision (the 2020s mixed-precision and GPU-era reassessments, including the machine-learning cores' tolerance of low precision, are revisiting the choice with explicit error-budget studies), why "bit-for-bit reproducible" is a configuration flag people actually fight over, and why a 0.5 % energy drift in a millennium run is a numerical result, not a physical one.
:::

::: warning Four ways numerics masquerade as physics
1. **"Finer is more accurate."** Finer is more accurate *of the discretised system you solved*; if the scheme's fake diffusion was holding a spurious balance (coarse-model jets kept in place by numerical damping, western-boundary-current leakage set by grid-and-a-half rather than topography), refining changes the climate — and the 2010s–2020s resolution-forcing literature's surprises (sensitivity, ENSO skewness and precipitation phase all moving with Δx at fixed physics) are the polite form of "the coarse model was wrong in an unmodelled way." Resolution studies are physics experiments; treat them as such.
2. **"The scheme is conservative, so the tracer is safe."** Global conservation and local positivity are different theorems: a skew-symmetric central scheme conserves variance exactly and rings negative moisture into the stratosphere at every jet; a monotone limiter keeps moisture positive and diffuses the front. Which failure your science can survive is a question about your tracer (salt tolerates ringing better than cloud water tolerates negativity), and the 2000s–2020s positive-definite finite-volume generation exists because someone finally asked.
3. **"The parameterisation is the uncertainty."** Coupling order, time step, physics-coupling frequency and transport-scheme diffusivity all move climate-relevant fluxes by W/m²-scale amounts (the operator-splitting and grey-zone results above) — errors of the same size as the forcing of the decade you are simulating. Model-uncertainty attribution studies that budget physics and ignore numerics are reporting half the ledger, and the dynamical-core intercomparisons exist because the other half signs its name on the same output files.
4. **"It validated against the test suite, so it's right."** The baroclinic and manufactured-solution tests bound the *arithmetic*; they say nothing about whether the discretised equations are the right equations, and every core passes some tests while carrying scheme-specific biases (polar noise, mesh ripples, interpolation smoothing) that no test's reference solution contains because the reference is another model. Verification is necessary, validation is separate, and the field's honest phrase for the gap is "we do not yet know how much of this bias is the core."
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Numerical methods are where the climate equations pay their toll to finite machines: derivatives become differences with truncation errors whose order sets the convergence rate; stability becomes the CFL number, chaining the time step to the grid and generating the sixteenfold cost law that organises the entire model hierarchy; advection becomes a choice between fake diffusion and fake oscillation, answered by limited high-order schemes, spectral transforms, and the semi-Lagrangian licence to step past the wind; and the sphere itself becomes a contested mesh — latitude–longitude with its polar tax, icosahedra, cubed spheres, hexagonal prisms — arbitrated by the Williamson baroclinic test and the aqua-planet drift before any physics is believed. The deep lesson of the resolution era is that numerics are not scaffolding around the physics but a participant in it: coarse-model climates contain scheme-made jets, numerically-damped eddies and split-step fluxes, the grey zone blurs arithmetic and convection into one another, and the 2020s frontier — exascale kilometre-scale weeks, mixed-precision and learned cores, the stratocumulus-breakup scare that moved with the numerics — makes convergence studies a scientific result rather than a footnote. The hierarchy's moral, one last time in engineering dress: know what your discretisation believes, because it will vote on every question you ask it.
:::
