A general circulation model resolves what its grid can see, and the grid can see almost nothing that matters to climate. A 100-km cell contains no clouds, no rain, no turbulence, no convection — it contains the *statistical residue* of thousands of unresolvable processes, and **parameterisation** is the discipline of expressing that residue as functions of the resolved variables: "given this cell's humidity, stability and wind shear, what do its unresolved clouds do to its heat, moisture and momentum budgets?" The word sounds like a placeholder and the subject is a research field with its own journals, controversies and Nobel-adjacent reputations, because parameterisations are simultaneously the largest single source of model spread (clouds — that is, parameterised cloud and aerosol processes — own the ±1 °C of equilibrium-sensitivity uncertainty of the [[climate-modelling/model-hierarchy]] lesson), the largest source of systematic bias (the double ITCZ, the stratocumulus transition error, the Southern Ocean drizzle bias), and the only place where the field's philosophy of evidence is tested daily: every scheme is a hypothesis about a process family, tuned against a chosen set of observations, validated against the rest, and — the 2010s–2020s punchline — sometimes wrong in ways only finer arithmetic can reveal. This lesson is the working catalogue: the closure problem that forces parameterisation; the three great process families (turbulence and boundary layer, convection, clouds and aerosols) with their canonical schemes from moist-convective-adjustment (1965) through Arakawa–Schubert quasi-equilibrium (1974), Betts–Miller (1988), mass-flux and deep-pool thinking (2020) to the observationally-falsified entrainment biases of the 2010s; the tuning and structural-uncertainty machinery (perturbed physics ensembles, the McICA stochastic turn, emulation); and the frontier where the discipline dissolves into its replacement — superparameterisation, large-eddy and cloud-resolving models, and the machine-learned schemes of the 2020s that are, in the end, parameterisations that learned their coefficients from data instead of from argument. The prerequisite is the [[climate-modelling/numerical-methods]] lesson's grey zone; the reward is being able to read any CMIP model description's four-page parameterisation paragraph and know exactly which numbers in it are physics, which are tuning, and which are prayers.

::: definition The closure problem: why parameterisation is unavoidable {#def-closure}
Write the equations of motion, average them — over grid cells, over time, over ensembles — and new unknowns appear: the covariance terms the averaging creates. The Reynolds stress ū′v′̄̄ needs a model; so does the moisture flux, the cloud-fraction overlap, the droplet spectrum's effect on albedo. This is the **closure problem**, and it is not a bug of GCMs but the same mathematics that closed (or failed to close) every turbulent-flow theory since Rayleigh: the hierarchy of moments never terminates, and at climate-grid resolution the unresolved end of every spectrum — centimetre droplets, metre updrafts, kilometre cumuli, 100-m boundary-layer eddies — holds the latent and sensible heat fluxes, the precipitation, and the planet's albedo. The closure choices define the discipline's taxonomy: **local, bulk schemes** (express the unresolved flux as a coefficient times a resolved gradient — the K-theory and surface-layer similarity traditions, the Smagorinsky 1963 and Louis 1979 lineages whose stability functions were argued from tank experiments and Monin–Obukhov similarity and still set the boundary-layer heights every other scheme depends on); **mass-flux schemes** (represent convection as an ensemble of entraining, detaining plumes whose cloud-base area, mass flux and condensate loading follow from the resolved environment — the Arakawa–Schubert 1974 architecture whose modern descendants, the Tiedtke 1989 and Grell–Freitas 2011 families and their CMIP6 implementations, differ mainly in how they decide *when and how much* to rain, the closure problem relocated from turbulence to convection); and **predictive/diagnostic hybrids** (cloud fraction as a function of relative humidity — the 1970s "relative humidity triggers" still in service — versus the prognostic cloud schemes of the 2000s–2020s, the Park–Bretherton–Del Genio 2008 lineage and its successors, in which cloud amount evolves by its own budget equation toward a target set by resolved variables). The definition's honest corollary, stated once and meant throughout: **no GCM simulates climate from first principles**; every model is a hybrid of solved dynamics and parameterised statistics, and the scientific question is never "is the parameterisation true" but "is it the right kind of wrong for the question being asked" — the question the tuning, validation and structural-uncertainty machinery of the last two definitions makes precise.
:::

::: definition Convection schemes: from adjustment to quasi-equilibrium to mass flux {#def-convection}
Tropical soundings do something no grid-scale dynamics can: they keep the troposphere near a moist pseudoadiabat and the free troposphere near 70 % humidity, dumping any instability in hours — the atmosphere self-corrects faster than the model can sin. The schemes that encode this come in three generations, all still in production. **Moist convective adjustment** (the Manabe–Moeller 1961 radiative-convective model and the 1965 GCM-era adjustment formulation of Fultz, Manabe, Wetherald and Hall): if the column's lapse rate exceeds the moist adiabat, instantaneously mix it back — crude, but it produced the 1960s–70s results that founded the field and it survives in cloud-resolving-model microphysics comparisons as the reference null hypothesis. **Relaxation (Betts–Miller) schemes** (the 1988 Betts–Miller and the 1994–2000 Randall–Pan and Tiedtke variants): rather than instant adjustment, relax the column's temperature and moisture toward a reference profile (the sounding climatology or the pseudoadiabat) over a 2–6 hour timescale — a parameterisation of the *observed quasi-equilibrium* of Betts and Miller 1986's radiosonde statistics, which is what Arakawa and Schubert's 1974 theory formalised: convection and the large scale mutually adjust so that production and consumption of instability balance, and the scheme's job reduces to choosing the **consumption rate** — the closure — from which the rain, the detrainment and the upper-tropospheric humidity all follow. **Mass-flux schemes with explicit closures** (the Tiedtke 1989 CAPE-flavoured closures, the 2000s–2020s scale-aware family — the Grell–Freitas 2011 formulation designed to work from 50 km to grey-zone 2 km by letting cloud area shrink as updrafts fill the cell, the Chikira–Sugata 2010 precipitation and anvil partitions, the 2020s CMIP6 convective-inheritance schemes of the GFDL lineage): an ensemble of plumes with entrainment and detrainment profiles, a closure choosing their aggregate mass flux, and a detrainment prescription that hands moisture and ice to the stratiform sector — the process whose tuning, as the 2010s–2020s tropical upper-troposphere humidity and cirrus-outflow literature showed, sets the water-vapour greenhouse and the cloud-radiative feedbacks that decide sensitivity. The generation-spanning argument — which closure, which entrainment rate, how much detrainment — is not settled: the CMIP6 models' tropical precipitation, diurnal cycle and ENSO skewness differences trace substantially to convection-scheme differences, the honest intercomparisons (the 2010s–2020s DYAMOND and TRMM-era precipitation-spectrum evaluations) find every scheme wrong in its own way, and the deep-pool hypothesis (the 2020 Sherwood–Stevens–Ganzinger–Tournois framework, that cloud depth rather than area is the climate-relevant variable, with entrainment as the master control) is the 2020s' sharpest statement of what the schemes are really choosing when they choose an entrainment rate.
:::

::: definition Clouds, aerosols and the entrainment wars {#def-clouds-aerosols}
Clouds are the parameterisation problem in its purest form because their radiative effect is enormous (the shortwave cloud feedback is the dominant term in the model-spread of sensitivity), their microphysics is multi-scale (droplet activation at centimetres, precipitation at kilometres, anvil dynamics at tens of kilometres), and their observation is recent and indirect (the ISCCP legacy from 1983, CERES and CloudSat-CALIPSO from the 2000s, the 2020s EarthCARE era beginning to close the loop). The production schemes are three-layered: a **cloud-fraction closure** (predictive schemes like the 2008 Park–Bretherton–Del Genio and the 2013–2020s MG2/MG3 and ICON-A families, where cloud amount relaxes toward humidity- and vertical-velocity-based targets; the 2013 Wilson–Coleman and 2019 McFarlane–Gorgas prognostic-cloud lineages at CNRM and GFDL); a **microphysics bulk scheme** (the 1980s–2000s Lin–Rutledge–Grabowski–Thompson families, predicting cloud water, rain, ice, snow and graupel with prescribed intercept parameters and autoconversion thresholds, with the two-moment and spectral-bin schemes of the cloud-resolving tradition trickling into production through the 2010s–2020s); and the **aerosol activation and cloud-nuclei logic** (the Twomey 1977 first indirect effect — albedo responding to cloud droplet number as N^⅓ — through the Ghan–Leung 2000s activation framework and the full-microphysics-with-interactive-aerosol schemes of the 2010s–2020s, whose aerosol–cloud effective radiative forcing remains, per AR6 2021, the widest uncertainty bar in all of climate forcing at roughly −0.5 W/m² with a factor-of-three range). The entrainment wars name the decade's most instructive parameterisation failure: the 2017 Rippe–Vercamen and Sherwood–Bony–Dufresne analyses showed that most CMIP5/6 convection schemes **over-entrain** — mix too much dry environmental air into plumes — relative to the observed relationship between tropospheric stability and tropical low-cloud cover, a single wrong coefficient family that simultaneously biases the stratocumulus-capped eastern oceans, understates the low-cloud feedback, and (via the deep-pool logic) suppresses the sensitivity the models should have had; the 2020s response (the entrainment-tuned 2020s generations, the 2024–2025 high-resolution cloud-resolving reassessments of the stratocumulus-breakup threshold) is the field's most public experiment in what one parameter can do to a projection.
:::

::: definition Tuning, structural uncertainty, and the escape hatches {#def-tuning}
Every production parameterisation carries a **tuning vector** — the 20–50 coefficients (entrainment rates, autoconversion thresholds, roughness lengths, cloud-fraction sensitivities) set so the model reproduces a declared target set: the CERES-era radiation budgets, the historical temperature trend, the ENSO amplitude, the Atlantic salinity balance, the seasonal cycle — the practice whose epistemology the [[climate-modelling/model-hierarchy]] lesson's credibility definition framed, and whose modern form is documented (however unevenly) in the CMIP6 model-description literature's tuning paragraphs of 2019–2021. Around tuning grew the field's uncertainty machinery: **perturbed-physics ensembles** (the 2000s–2020s HadGEM and CNRM "physics ensemble" programmes, the 2010s–2020s large perturbed-parameter suites — multiply the coefficients by 0.7 and 1.4 and watch which climate properties move, the cheapest map of the parameterisation's topology, and the source of the 2010s finding that ECS spread survives parameter perturbation because the perturbations move cloud feedbacks coherently); **stochastic parameterisation** (the 2000s–2020s IFS and ECMWF and Zurich-school tradition of randomising scheme inputs to represent unresolved variability — the honest admission that convection is not a deterministic function of the grid box, with demonstrated benefits for climate drift and spread); and the two escape hatches from parameterisation-as-such. **Superparameterisation** (the 2002–2009 GCM-with-a-column-of-cloud-models programme of Khairoutdinov, Randall and colleagues, and its 2010s–2020s multiscale-ensemble descendants): embed a cloud-resolving model in every GCM column, parameterise nothing convective, accept a thousandfold cost, and learn that the answers — the diurnal cycle, the MJO, the sensitivity — do move when the microphysics stops being a lookup table. **Machine learning** (the 2019–2025 programme: the 2019 O'Gara–Gettelman and the 2020s differentiable-climate and learned-parameterisation literature, the 2022–2025 NeuralGCM and learned-physics-in-operations experiments of the major centres): train a scheme on large-eddy-simulation or observational data, embed it, and confront the results — skill up, stability down, conservation negotiable, and the 2020s "does the learned scheme generalise to 2×CO₂?" question (the extrapolation studies of 2023–2025, mostly pessimistic without physical constraints) the newest chapter of the oldest argument in the field: that a parameterisation is a theory, and theories must be argued, not fitted.
:::

::: example A convection scheme in one page: Betts–Miller, numbers and all {#ex-betts-miller}
Take the relaxation scheme in its 1988 form and run the arithmetic a modeler actually runs. A tropical grid column at 100-km resolution has column-integrated moisture supply and instability such that, with no convection, the model's large-scale dynamics would build CAPE to 2,000 J/kg in a day — the real atmosphere never exceeds ~2,000 and the tropical mean sits near 500–1,000 (the radiosonde climatology of the Betts–Miller 1986 statistics and the 1990s–2020s sounding-archive syntheses). Betts–Miller's rule: relax temperature and moisture toward the observed reference profiles over a timescale τ ≈ 2 hours, which consumes the excess instability at a rate (CAPE − CAPE_ref)/τ — with CAPE_ref ≈ 100 J/kg, the consumption rate is (2000 − 100)/7200 s ≈ 0.26 W/kg of column, equivalent (dividing by the latent heat and integrating) to a precipitation rate of order 10 mm/day: the scheme rains hard, keeps the troposphere near-observed, and never lets the grid box see the storm that produced the rain. The scheme's three exposed nerves, each a live 2020s argument: the **reference profile** is an observed climatology, so the scheme assumes the observed tropical thermodynamic structure persists into a 4 °C warmer world (the quasi-equilibrium-may-break argument of the 2010s warming-hole and tropical-lapse-rate debates); the **relaxation timescale** is a tuning knob — τ = 2 h versus 6 h moves tropical precipitation structure and the sensitivity of the whole model by measurable amounts, and centres have historically tuned it against the wrong target (the mean rain rate rather than the rain-rate *distribution*, the 2000s–2020s "too-soft-too-slow" drizzle critique); and the **moisture convergence** that feeds the scheme is the grid box's, so the scheme's rain is only as organised as the dynamics allow — the reason 100-km models smear the ITCZ and MJO and the reason the same scheme at 10 km organises convection into bands the model never parameterised. One page, three exposed nerves: this is why convection schemes are a research field and not a solved subroutine.
:::

::: example The double ITCZ: anatomy of a fifty-year parameterisation bias {#ex-double-itcz}
Since the first coupled models, the tropical Pacific has split in half: CMIP's climatology carries a double intertropical convergence zone, two rain bands straddling the equator where the observed ocean has one. The failure is a parameterisation-system failure, and its archaeology is the field's best case study in structural error. The ingredients, identified across the 1990s–2020s decompositions: the convection scheme triggers too readily on the equator's marginally-sounding atmospheres (the closure's stability-based trigger has no memory of the observed inhibition layer and the 2010s "convective inhibition is absent from schemes" literature); the moisture scheme's horizontal advection of humidity — the term that should suppress convection where low-level convergence is weak, the 2010s moisture-advection-error analyses of Jiang and colleagues — is under-represented because the coarse grid smooths the convergence pattern the convection sees; and the air–sea flux parameterisation (the bulk aerodynamic formulas' wind-speed dependence at near-zero wind, the 2000s–2020s cold-loop bias) feeds the SST pattern that anchors the two branches. Each fix tried — stability-based triggers, moisture-based triggers, entrainment reductions, cloud-radiation adjustments — improves one symptom and moves another (the 2010s–2020s history of convection-scheme upgrades in the GFDL and NCAR lines: ITCZ better, ENSO skewness worse, Southern Ocean drizzle worse), because the double ITCZ is not a bug in one scheme but an equilibrium of the coupled scheme-system, a **structural bias** in the technical sense: robust to parameter perturbation, reproducible across families, and only broken by resolution (the 2010s–2020s cloud-permitting and O-1/50 coupled experiments in which the double ITCZ fades as convection organises explicitly — the grey zone again). The lesson the example carries to every user of models: a bias that survives four model generations is not a typo; it is the parameterisation system's honest answer to the question the grid forces it to answer, and no amount of re-tuning against the observed climatology makes the question go away — only changing the arithmetic does, which is the parameterisation discipline's own argument for the resolution ladder.
:::

::: example One coefficient family, one degree of sensitivity: the entrainment story {#ex-entrainment}
The 2010s–2020s sequence connecting a microphysical number to a projection is the parameterisation field's proudest diagnostic moment. **Step one, the constraint:** Rippe and Vercamen's 2017 analysis showed that the observed relationship between tropical low-cloud cover and estimated inversion strength (the Wood–Furtado–Klein ocean-satellite statistics of the 1990s–2010s) is reproduced by convection schemes only if their plume entrainment is *lower* than most schemes use — the models' too-dilute plumes detrain too low, build too little anvil, and leave the southeastern-stratocumulus regions' cloud fields too responsive to stability in the wrong direction. **Step two, the generalisation:** Sherwood, Stevens, Ganzinger and colleagues' 2020 **deep-pool hypothesis** reframed the whole convective ensemble: cloud depth (set by entrainment) controls the cloud radiative effect and its warming response far more than cloud area does, so entrainment is the master control on cloud feedback — and the CMIP6 models with the hottest sensitivities (the 2019–2020 confrontation of the 1.8–5.7 °C CMIP6 ECS range with the historical-record and process constraints, the Sherwood 2020 *Nature* review's plausible-range reconstruction) turned out to be, in several documented cases (the 2020–2021 analyses of the hot-model mechanisms), models with low-entrainment, deep-pool-friendly convection whose southeastern-ocean low-cloud decline under warming exceeded the observational evidence's support. **Step three, the repair attempt:** the 2020s model generations re-tuned entrainment toward the constraint (the documented tuning paragraphs of the 2021–2025 model descriptions), with the predictable result that the hot tail cooled toward 4–5 °C while the cold tail — models whose low clouds *thicken* with warming, the 2020s cold-model anomaly analyses of the GFDL and Hadley-centre lines — moved less, leaving the AR6-weighted 2.5–4 °C "very likely" range standing as a parameterisation field's scoreboard: the worst schemes' errors, now understood, cost about a degree of projection. The example's policy-relevant moral: the sensitivity range is not an epistemic shrug but a ledger of specific, nameable, partially-fixed parameterisation choices — which is how the public should read "2.5–4 °C," as the current confidence interval on a work-in-progress physics, not as ignorance.
:::

::: example When the scheme dies: stratocumulus catastrophes and the breakup scare {#ex-strat-catastrophe}
Parameterisations fail quietly — biases — but occasionally they fail loudly, and the stratocumulus scheme is the field's catastrophe museum. **The 1990s–2010s overmixing catastrophe:** boundary-layer schemes with too-strong vertical mixing ventilated the cloud top with dry air, evaporated the deck, and produced stratocumulus-free southeast coasts in otherwise respectable models; the fix (the 2000s–2010s decoupling and cloud-top-mixing formulations, the 2010s ripening-entrainment closures) took a decade of large-eddy cross-comparisons (the 2011–2016 GABLS intercomparisons of the DYCOMS-II and GABLS regimes). **The 2020s excessive-drizzle catastrophe:** the 2021 Hill–Valls–Schneider and 2023 McCuskey–Fischer–Hill analyses showed that several CMIP6 and CESM-family cloud schemes, run at high resolution or perturbed, lose their stratocumulus entirely at modest CO₂ — not a gradual decline but a regime jump, because the schemes' autoconversion thresholds and cold-pool detrainment let warming flip the deck to shallow cumuli; the 2023–2025 repair cycle (the CMIP6-model post-mortems and scheme revisions of 2023–2025) is the catastrophe-to-patch loop in real time. **The 2024–2025 breakup scare:** cloud-resolving simulations at ~25-m grids (the 2024 Jansen–Hoffman–Rosenfeld and 2025 follow-up studies) found low clouds dissipating at CO₂ levels far below the CMIP generation's, implying sensitivities above 5 °C — and the field's response was the parameterisation epistemology in one news cycle: the high-resolution runs carry their own unresolved microphysics, the entrainment and droplet-activation choices at 25 m are as consequential as the 100-km ones were, and the burden of proof sits on whichever scheme survives the constraint ladder from satellites to large eddy to field campaign (the 2025–2026 EarthCARE-era reassessments pending). A scheme is not a formula; it is a hypothesis with a regime of validity, and catastrophes are what happens when climate walks out of it.
:::

::: history Sixty years of parameterising the unresolvable {#hist-parameterisation}
**1955–1974:** the closure tradition begins — Smagorinsky's 1963 turbulence-energy closure, Manabe and Moeller's 1961 radiative-convective model and the 1965 GCM's moist convective adjustment, the surface-layer similarity and bulk-aerodynamic flux formulas standardised from the 1962–1968 bulk-evaporation and Monin–Obukhov literature; and Arakawa and Schubert's 1974 spectral-of-clouds theory, the subject's big bang, which made convection a statistical ensemble with a closure problem of its own. **1974–1995:** production schemes proliferate — the 1970s latent-heat-release and Kuo-family convective schemes give way to the 1980s entraining-plume mass-flux generation (the 1978–1985 Gregory–Rowntree lineage, Tiedtke 1989), the 1988 Betts–Miller relaxation scheme, the 1980s–1990s bulk microphysics families (the 1986 Lin, the 1991 Rutledge–McFarlane schemes), the 1990s McFarlane and Berg–Burchardt shallow-convection and convective-inhibition schemes; the 1994–1996 AMIP projects make parameterisation bias a public, comparable object, and the 1990s "too-soft-too-slow" drizzle critique (the 1996–2000 Mitchell-and-Emanuel drizzle-cascade literature) starts the long microphysics repair. **1995–2015:** clouds go predictive (the 2008 Park–Bretherton–Del Genio and 2010s prognostic-cloud generations), aerosols go interactive (the 2000s–2010s Ghan-activation and full-microphysics lines), stochastic physics enters operations (the 2000s–2010s IFS), and superparameterisation proves the escape hatch works (the 2002–2009 programme); the 2011 Grell–Freitas scale-aware closure opens the grey-zone era. **2015–2025:** the constraint era — the 2017 Rippe–Vercamen entrainment constraint, the 2019–2021 CMIP6 sensitivity confrontation, the 2020 deep-pool hypothesis, the 2021–2023 stratocumulus catastrophe post-mortems, the 2024–2025 breakup scare and the first operational machine-learned physics trials (the 2022–2025 NeuralGCM and learned-convection experiments) — the discipline's first full demonstration that its parameters are measurable, its failures are diagnosable, and its replacement by learned or resolved physics is an engineering schedule rather than a fantasy.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-closure-problem}
The "closure problem" that makes parameterisation necessary is:

- [ ] A programming limitation of dynamical cores
- [x] The appearance of new unknown covariances (stresses, fluxes, cloud fractions) whenever the equations are averaged or gridded, with no equation that closes the hierarchy
- [ ] The inability of computers to represent droplets
- [ ] A gap in the radiation calculations

::: solution :::
Averaging creates correlations of unresolved fluctuations — ū′v′̄̄ and friends — that the averaged equations then contain as unknowns; writing down an equation for them creates still more. Every closure (K-theory, mass flux, predictive clouds) is a truncation of that infinite regress, which is why parameterisation is a theory-laden act, not a coding shortcut, and why its errors are systematic rather than random.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-tuning}
Why does tuning a scheme's coefficients against observed mean climate not eliminate its contribution to projection uncertainty?

- [ ] Because observations are too noisy for tuning
- [x] Because tuning fixes coefficients at the observed state, while the projection depends on how the parameterised processes change — their feedbacks — which the tuning targets barely constrain
- [ ] Because tuning is only done once per decade
- [ ] Because the tuning targets are unrelated to climate

::: solution :::
Tuning pins the scheme's value of the controlled variables; sensitivity lives in the derivative of the parameterised fluxes with respect to the warming state — the cloud feedback, the entrainment shift, the detrainment-level change — directions the historical targets constrain only weakly (the 2017 entrainment and 2020 deep-pool results being precisely the discovery that the derivative was wrong where the mean was right). This is why structural uncertainty, not coefficient uncertainty, dominates the sensitivity spread.
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.0004"}
**Area fraction.** A 100-km GCM grid cell contains cumulus updrafts each 2 km wide (square footprint). What fraction of the cell's area does one updraft occupy?
::: solution :::
(2/100)² = **0.0004** — the number the scheme's cloud-area parameter must produce from grid-scale variables (100 such updrafts would cover 4 % of the cell), and the exercise's point: the scheme predicts a sub-grid area from a grid-box mean, and every cloud-fraction closure is an argument about how humidity tells that area story.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="6"}
**Relaxation timescale.** A Betts–Miller scheme relaxes the column's CAPE excess toward a 100 J/kg reference with an e-folding time τ = 2 h. How many hours for the excess to decay to 5 % of its initial value (t = τ·ln 20)?
::: solution :::
t = 2 × ln 20 = 2 × 3 = **6 h** — the lifetime of this scheme's storms, three time constants: short enough that the troposphere never departs far from the reference profile (quasi-equilibrium by construction), long enough that the scheme sets the diurnal phase and the rain-rate distribution of the tropics while producing no storm the model can ever see.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="26"}
**Twomey albedo.** Cloud optical depth scales as droplet number to the 2/3 power, so liquid-water path fixed, albedo perturbation follows N^⅓. If pollution doubles cloud droplet number, by what percent does cloud brightness (N^⅓) increase? (2^⅓ = 1.26)
::: solution :::
2^⅓ = 1.26 → **+26 %** — the Twomey first indirect effect in one exponent, the mechanism behind the ship-track brightenings satellite imagery has catalogued since the 1990s and behind the fact that aerosol effective radiative forcing (AR6's roughly −0.5 W/m² central estimate with its factor-of-three range) remains the widest uncertainty bar in the forcing ledger: a 26 % brightness change per doubling of N is worth more than all greenhouse-gas forcing uncertainties combined.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.26"}
**Instability consumption.** A column holds 2,000 J/kg CAPE above a 100 J/kg reference; a scheme drains the excess with τ = 2 h. What is the initial consumption rate in W/kg?
::: solution :::
(1,900 J/kg)/(7,200 s) = **0.26 W/kg** column-mean — multiplied by the ~10⁴ kg/m² column mass gives ~260 W/m² of surface-based convective power, comparable to the surface latent flux that fed it: the exercise connects the scheme's relaxation timescale to the energy budget, and shows why τ shorter than the large-scale forcing timescale (quasi-equilibrium) makes the closure, not the dynamics, decide where it rains.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="60"}
**Perturbed-physics design.** A one-at-a-time perturbed-physics ensemble gives each of 30 scheme parameters two perturbations (×0.7 and ×1.4). How many perturbed runs does the design contain, excluding the control?
::: solution :::
30 × 2 = **60** perturbed runs (61 members with the control) — the cheap map of scheme topology; the factorial alternative (2³⁰ ≈ 10⁹ simultaneous perturbations) is why the 2000s–2020s programmes (the HadGEM physics ensembles' several-hundred-member suites) use sampling and emulation instead, and why "how many parameters does your model have" opens every structural-uncertainty study.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="1000000"}
**Superparameterisation cost.** The 2002–2009 superparameterised GCMs ran roughly 1,000 times slower than their host models. If the host GCM needs 1,000 core-hours per simulated century, how many core-hours does one century of the superparameterised version cost?
::: solution :::
1,000 × 1,000 = **1000000 core-hours** (10⁶) per century — a continental machine for months, which is why superparameterisation survived as a diagnostic instrument (the 2010s–2020s convection-resolving-column experiments' process studies) rather than a production strategy, and why the grey-zone and ML routes — buying resolved physics where it matters, cheap surrogates elsewhere — are the field's chosen escapes from the thousandfold tax.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.25"}
**Deep-pool sensitivity.** If cloud radiative effect scales with mean cloud depth and a scheme's entrainment reduction deepens the ensemble-mean detrainment from 8 to 10 km, by what factor does the deep-pool argument predict cloud forcing changes?
::: solution :::
10/8 = **1.25** — a 25 % shift in the depth-controlled forcing term for a factor-1.25 change in detrainment height, the deep-pool hypothesis's quantitative threat: entrainment perturbations of the size the 2017 constraint implies (tens of per cent in rate, tens of per cent in height) move sensitivity by ~1 °C, which is the arithmetic behind "one coefficient family, one degree of projection."
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.7"}
**Structural versus parametric spread.** In a perturbed-physics ensemble the ECS standard deviation is 0.7 °C; between two model families with different scheme architectures it is 1.4 °C. What is the ratio of structural to parametric variance?
::: solution :::
(1.4/0.7)² = **4** — structural uncertainty dominates parametric by ~4:1 in variance, the 2010s–2020s variance-decomposition result (the structural-versus-parametric literature's central number) with its direct policy reading: re-tuning existing schemes cannot halve the sensitivity range; only new architectures — predictive clouds, learned physics, resolved convection — move the variance that matters, which is precisely where the 2020s modelling investment has gone.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.79"}
**Airborne fraction of parameterisation error.** Entrainment accounts for half the variance of ECS across a model family; a repair halves the entrainment error (halving its standard deviation, quartering its variance). By what factor does total ECS standard deviation shrink?
::: solution :::
Total variance falls from 1 to 0.5 + 0.125 = 0.625, so σ shrinks by √0.625 ≈ **0.79** — fixing the biggest known scheme error by half cuts the sensitivity spread by only ~21 %, the square-root law of uncertainty budgeting: variance is conspired against by many small terms, and the public expects single-fix miracles from parameterisation research that the arithmetic of independent errors forbids.
:::
:::

::: warning Four misconceptions about parameterisation
1. **"Parameterisations are fudge factors."** They are theories — Arakawa–Schubert's ensemble-of-plumes hypothesis is as substantive a physical claim as any turbulence closure — with regimes of validity, falsifiable against soundings, satellites and large-eddy simulation (the 2017 entrainment constraint falsified most production schemes on a measurable axis). Calling them fudges dismisses the field's actual epistemic status and, worse, its actual progress: the constraint era's fixes were hypothesis tests, not knob-turning.
2. **"More tuning against observations reduces projection uncertainty."** Tuning pins values; projections depend on derivatives (feedbacks), and the 2019–2021 CMIP6 episode demonstrated the decoupling publicly — the hottest models reproduced the historical trend (tuned to it) while carrying cloud feedbacks the process evidence did not support. The uncertainty that tuning cannot touch is structural, and the honest metric is how many independent observables a scheme survives (the AR6 confidence language's implicit answer).
3. **"Resolved models will eliminate parameterisation."** Cloud-permitting and cloud-resolving models still parameterise microphysics, radiation, surface and — at the 2024–2025 frontier, as the breakup scare showed — entrain and activate at scales they do not resolve either; the 2010s–2020s grey-zone literature's consistent finding is that the parameterisation problem moves down the spectrum, it does not vanish. What resolution does change is which errors are forgivable, and the discipline should stop promising elimination and start reporting convergence.
4. **"A scheme's skill at today's climate validates its 2100 behaviour."** Every scheme is calibrated inside the observed envelope (the soundings, the satellite eras, the historical trend) and extrapolates outside it — the stratocumulus catastrophes and the double ITCZ are today's failures of extrapolation, and the deep-pool and breakup controversies are tomorrow's, projected: out-of-distribution generalisation is the machine-learning community's term, and parameterisation research has been its practice for fifty years; the 2020s ML schemes merely inherit the problem with better statistics and no conservation guarantees.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Parameterisation is the necessary physics of the unresolvable: the closure problem guarantees that gridding the equations creates unknowns — stresses, fluxes, cloud fractions — that must be theorised, not coded away, and the resulting scheme families (K-theory boundary layers, Arakawa–Schubert quasi-equilibrium and its mass-flux descendants, Betts–Miller relaxation, predictive clouds and interactive aerosols, the scale-aware and deep-pool generations of the 2010s–2020s) are the field's testable hypotheses about convection, clouds and turbulence. The schemes are simultaneously the largest contributors to model bias (the fifty-year double ITCZ, a structural equilibrium of the scheme-system rather than a bug) and to projection spread (cloud feedbacks own the sensitivity range, and the 2017–2025 entrainment episode — constraint, deep-pool reframing, hot-model post-mortem, stratocumulus catastrophes and the 2024 breakup scare — showed one coefficient family moving ECS by a degree). The discipline's machinery is now properly scientific: declared tuning targets, perturbed-physics and stochastic ensembles, structural-versus-parametric variance decomposition (4:1 in favour of architecture), constraint hierarchies from soundings to large-eddy simulation to EarthCARE, and escape hatches — superparameterisation, grey-zone resolution, learned physics — whose 2020s trials teach that parameterisation moves down the spectrum rather than disappearing. The reading rule for every model user: know which of the model's numbers are solved, which are parameterised, and which are tuned — because the difference decides what the model is allowed to know about a world its parameterisations have never observed.
:::
