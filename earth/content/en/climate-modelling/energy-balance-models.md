Energy-balance models are the climate model reduced to its irreducible argument: the planet's temperature is set by the balance between the sunlight it absorbs and the infrared it emits, and everything else — winds, storms, clouds, forests — is plumbing in service of that balance or its latitudinal redistribution. Written as one ordinary differential equation, the model can be calibrated in an afternoon, solved in a minute, and interrogated for the deepest structural facts in climate science: the amplification factor of feedbacks, the existence of **multiple equilibria** created by the ice–albedo feedback, the **hysteresis** that makes an ice-free and a snowball Earth both stable under identical sun, and the timescale of transient warming set by the ocean's reluctance. This lesson builds the family in full: the zero-dimensional "blackbody with a thermostat" model and its two-parameter linearisation; the one-dimensional diffusive Budyko–Sellers model and its bifurcation diagram; the time-dependent versions — mixed-layer and two-layer — that connect to the observed transient warming and the two-layer emulators used to estimate climate sensitivity from the historical record; and the honest catalogue of what EBMs cannot do (weather, dynamics, clouds as anything but a parameter). The prerequisite is the [[climate-system/energy-budget]] lesson; the exercises ask you to reproduce, by hand, results that once filled journals — because an EBM you have bifurcated yourself is the best defence against misreading the expensive models downstream.

## The Zero-Dimensional Model

::: definition The zero-dimensional energy-balance model {#def-zerod}
Strip the Earth to a sphere-averaged thermometer: one temperature T, one energy budget. The model reads **C dT/dt = (1 − α)S/4 − I(T)**, where S/4 is the solar constant diluted over the sphere (1361/4 ≈ 340 W/m²), α the planetary albedo (~0.30), and I(T) the outgoing longwave radiation. Three closures for I give the model's three personalities. The **blackbody** closure I = σT⁴ yields the effective radiating temperature T = (S(1−α)/4σ)^¼ = 255 K — the temperature from space, 33 K colder than the surface, the whole greenhouse effect as a single unmodelled gap (the exercise that founded the subject, from Fourier's 1824 "pure air" argument onward). The **linearised** closure I = A + BT — the two-parameter fit to the actual radiation codes that is the workhorse of the entire field (A ≈ 210 W/m², B ≈ 2 W m⁻² K⁻¹ for the present climate; equivalently the net-exit form I = F₀ + λT with the Planck feedback λ ≈ 3.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹ when the surface, not the top of the atmosphere, is the variable) — turns equilibrium into arithmetic: T_eq = ((1−α)S/4 − A)/B, and perturbations into the amplification law ΔT = ΔF/(B − dASR/dT), where any feedback that makes absorbed sunlight grow with temperature (ice–albedo, water vapour) eats away the denominator and multiplies the response. The **feedback-form** closure I = F₀ + λ₀T − ΣλᵢT writes the same equation as ΔT = ΔF/(λ₀ − Σλᵢ) = f·ΔT₀, the "no-feedback response times amplification factor" of the [[climate-system/feedbacks-sensitivity]] lesson in its purest readable form, with the amplification f = 1/(1 − Rλ₀) where R is the summed feedback strength; in words: the sensitivity of the system is the Planck response divided by how much feedbacks have eaten of it, and **runaway** begins when they eat it all (λ_net → 0). The zero-dimensional model's virtues are total transparency (every bifurcation is a sketch on one axis) and total geography-free-ness: it cannot see a hemisphere, a season, or a cloud, and it is the interpretive backbone for everything that can.
:::

::: definition The ice–albedo feedback and the one-dimensional diffusive model {#def-icealbedo}
The zero-D model's one great addition is latitude, and the one great nonlinearity is ice. **Ice–albedo feedback**: snow and ice raise α from ~0.15 (ocean) to ~0.6–0.8, so a colder planet is a more reflective planet, which is a colder planet — a positive feedback with a threshold geometry, because the switch operates violently only in the temperature band where ice margins sit (parameterisations like α(T) = α₀ + (α₁ − α₀)·(T_m − T)/ΔT ramping over a few degrees around a melting temperature T_m). **Diffusive heat transport**: the atmosphere and ocean move heat down the temperature gradient, and the simplest closure that conserves energy makes the meridional heat flux proportional to the curvature of temperature, F_y = −C·∂T/∂y with the divergence becoming a diffusion term C·∂²T/∂y² (the North 1979 taxonomy's "large-scale diffusion" class, with the single diffusivity D tuned so the model's equator–pole contrast matches the observed ~60 K). The **Budyko–Sellers model** (Budyko 1969, Sellers 1973) combines them: **C(1−α(T))S(μ)/4 − (A + BT) + D·(d²T/dμ²) = 0** with μ = sin(latitude), solved as a boundary-value problem with zero flux at the poles. Its discoveries are the reason the model is taught fifty-five years on: for the modern sun the warm solution is unique and stable; as solar input falls (or ice-albedo coupling strengthens), the warm state and a **cold, ice-covered state coexist** over a range of parameters, separated by an unstable branch — and the transition is **hysteretic**, because melting ice and retreating albedo reinforce in one direction and the reverse only begins at a much lower ice-albedo forcing. The snowball-Earth bifurcation of the model (collapse to global ice at ~6 % reduced sun or equivalent, recovery requiring hundreds of times present CO₂ in the model, matching the ~10⁵ ppm cap of the Neoproterozoic deglacial carbonate-cap record, the 1992 Kirschvink–1998 Hoffman–2017 stratigraphic saga) is the simplest demonstration in climate science that **the system's response to slow forcing can be discontinuous** — the template for every tipping-point argument in the [[climate-change/tipping-points]] lesson.
:::

::: definition Time-dependent EBMs: mixed layers, two layers, and transient response {#def-transient}
Give the zero-D model a heat capacity and a forcing history and it becomes the interpreter of the observed record. The **single-layer (slab) model** C dT/dt = F(t) − λT has the exponential solution T(t) = (F/λ)(1 − e^(−t/τ)) with e-folding time τ = C/λ: with the mixed layer's C ≈ 10⁸ J m⁻² K⁻¹ (25–50 m of ocean) and λ ≈ 3.3, τ ≈ 1 year — the pace at which the surface ocean accepts a forcing step. The **two-layer energy-balance model** (a surface layer C₁ over a deep ocean C₂ exchanging heat at efficiency κ — a class used since the 1980s and formalised for observational estimation in the 2000s) adds the one process that makes transient and equilibrium climate differ: **ocean heat uptake**, N = κ(T₁ − T₂), which soaks up a third of today's forcing and keeps observed warming far below its equilibrium value; its solution has two timescales — the decade-scale "penetration" mode and the centennial "adjustment" mode — and its fitted parameters (λ and κ) are the standard EBM route from the historical temperature and heat-content records to ECS and TCR (the Lewis-and-Curry 2014 two-layer estimate of ECS ≈ 1.6 °C and its subsequent critique, the 2020s "ECS from the historical record" literature that still divides the field, are the case study in what an EBM can and cannot claim). The **globally-averaged but energy-resolved** extensions — separate budgets for surface and atmosphere, longwave and shortwave channels, land and ocean — keep the ODE character while fixing specific zero-D pathologies (the "surface-based vs top-of-atmosphere forcing" confusion dissolves in the two-layer bookkeeping). The honest summary of the family's modern role: EBMs are no longer forecasts, they are **transfer functions** — the cheap, differentiable, interpretable surrogates that turn a forcing scenario into a temperature trajectory inside integrated-assessment models (the MAGICC line, from the 1990s Wigley and Raper versions to the 2011 Meinshausen MAGICC6 calibrated to the full CMIP ensemble) and inside uncertainty studies that need ten thousand futures rather than one.
:::

::: example The full zero-dimensional model, worked in one page {#ex-zerod-worked}
Calibrate, perturb, break — the whole zero-D model in the four numbers a student should own. **Calibrate:** with S = 1361 W/m², α = 0.30, the absorbed mean is 238 W/m²; set I = A + BT with A = 210 and B = 2.0 and the equilibrium is T = (238 − 210)/2 = 14 °C — within a degree of the actual surface mean, with two parameters and no greenhouse physics named (the linearisation is fitted *through* the present, which is the model's entire trick and its entire honesty). **Perturb by CO₂:** doubling gives ΔF = 5.35 ln 2 ≈ 3.7 W/m²; with only the Planck-like B = 2 the warming is 3.7/2 ≈ 1.9 °C — but the surface-based Planck parameter is 3.3, giving the no-feedback 1.2 °C of the exercises; the difference (B fitted at top-of-atmosphere versus λ at the surface) is the first lesson in reading EBM parameters carelessly. **Add feedbacks:** water vapour (≈ +1.8), surface albedo (≈ +0.8), clouds (≈ +0.6 net in most models) against lapse rate (−1.1) sum to a net feedback that eats ~2.1 of the 3.3, leaving λ_net ≈ 1.2 and ΔT_eq = 3.7/1.2 ≈ 3 °C — the Charney number, reproduced by a one-line equation whose parameters were measured in GCMs and satellites. **Break it:** raise the ice–albedo term to 3 W m⁻² K⁻¹ in the cold state and λ_net turns small, the amplification factor exceeds 10, and the warm equilibrium vanishes below a threshold sun — the snowball of the next example, arriving as arithmetic. The four steps are the model's whole professional life: it is calibrated through the present, trusted for the sign and rough size of the response, distrusted for the magnitude whenever a feedback is state-dependent, and *informative precisely when it breaks*, because a bifurcation in a one-line model is a theorem about what the real system could do.
:::

::: example Budyko's diagram: snowball Earth from a chalkboard {#ex-budyko}
Budyko's 1969 calculation fits on a blackboard and changed what climate theory could contain. Plot, against global-mean temperature, the absorbed solar (high in the warm ice-free state, low where ice makes the planet a mirror) and the outgoing longwave (rising steeply with T, nearly linear). Where the curves cross, the planet balances; where absorbed exceeds emitted, it warms; the reverse, it cools. For today's sun there is one crossing — the warm state — and it is stable. Reduce the sun by a few per cent (or strengthen the albedo coupling) and the ice-albedo kink in the absorbed-solar curve produces **three crossings**: a warm stable state, a cold stable state, and an unstable state between them. The system's history then depends on its past: cooled gently enough, the warm state slides toward the ice margin until no crossing remains and the planet **falls** to the cold branch — a discontinuous transition from a smooth forcing change; warmed from the cold branch, it stays frozen until CO₂ (in the model, several hundred times preindustrial; in the Neoproterozoic record, the ~10⁵ ppm read from the isotopic and carbonate-cap geology of the Sturtian and Marinoan glacial deposits, the 2010s–2020s U–Pb-dated stratigraphy) erases the warm branch's competitor and the planet jumps back. The hysteresis loop this draws is the field's founding example of three ideas it still argues about: **tipping** (the fold bifurcation where equilibria disappear), **hysteresis** (the path-dependence that makes deglacial CO₂ need far exceed the eventual stabilising value), and **early warning** (critical slowing down near the fold — the 2009–2020s statistical-detection programme whose applicability to the real, noise-driven, partially-observed climate remains contested, as the 2023–2025 AMOC warning-signal dispute re-showed). The model's quantitative snowball thresholds (6 % solar reduction) were later revised by better albedo and CO₂ weathering treatments (the "hard snowball vs slush" debate of the 2000s–2010s, resolved partly by the equatorial-open-water refugia implied by fossil evidence), but the qualitative structure — the S-shaped equilibrium curve — survives in every textbook and every tipping-point briefing a decade of assessments later.
:::

::: example The two-layer EBM and the fight over the historical record {#ex-twolayer}
The two-layer energy-balance model — surface layer, ocean layer, forcing in, radiation and heat uptake out — is four numbers and two differential equations, and in 2014 it produced the most contested climate-sensitivity estimate of the decade. The setup: C₁ dT₁/dt = F(t) − λT₁ − κ(T₁ − T₂); C₂ dT₂/dt = κ(T₁ − T₂) − (deep-ocean leakage), driven by the best-estimate forcing history (greenhouse gases, aerosols with their stubborn negative sign, the 2013–2021 revision of aerosol forcing upward in magnitude that reshaped this literature twice) and fitted to the 1850–2013 temperature record and, increasingly, to the ocean-heat-content record (the AR6-era realisation that heat content, not surface temperature, is the better-constrained integral of the energy budget). Lewis and Curry's 2014 two-layer fit returned an ECS of ~1.5–1.7 °C — below the CMIP5 range — and the argument that followed is a masterclass in EBM epistemology. The **critiques**: the estimate leans on the mid-century aerosol cooling and its uncertain forcing (the 2010s–2020s clean-air and ship-track literature), on the assumed pattern of internal variability (the Pacific warming pattern of the 2000s "hiatus" that made the early-2010s record look low-sensitivity until the pattern's natural mode was accounted for, the 2015–2019 literature on pattern effects and "response surface" methods), and on the linearity of λ across climates — the last testable only in the GCMs the EBM was built to bypass. The **defences**: the method's assumptions are explicit and auditable, unlike a million-core-hour model's, and the heat-content-constrained versions (the 2020s fits using the Argo-era ocean warming) pull ECS back toward 2.5–3.5 °C. The 2020s synthesis (the National Academies 2019 report and AR6's 2021 triangulation) treats EBM estimates as one line of evidence — credible for the physics they contain, blind to the feedback structure they cannot represent — which is exactly the hierarchy's verdict: the two-layer model is the right instrument for asking "what does the historical record alone imply, given these four assumptions," and the wrong instrument for asking "is that all there is to it."
:::

::: example EBMs off Earth: the habitable zone as a bifurcation diagram {#ex-exoplanet}
Energy-balance models' most unexpected second career is exoplanet climatology, where they are not toy models but the state of the art — there is exactly one planet with a weather station. The **habitable zone** (the range of orbital distances where surface liquid water is stable) is calculated as precisely the Budyko geometry: absorbed stellar flux against outgoing radiation, with the inner edge set by the **moist greenhouse/runaway** limit (water vapour reaching the stratosphere and photodissociating to escape — the 1993 Kasting-era 1-D radiative–convective calculations, the 2010s 3-D GCM re-evaluations that moved the inner edge inward and found asynchronous-rotation "eyeball" planets between tidally-locked states) and the outer edge by the **CO₂-condensation and maximum-greenhouse** limits (how much CO₂ can thicken before it too condenses or becomes radiatively saturated — the 2013–2020 maximum-CO₂ studies of the outer edge). The zero-D model's textbook result — T ∝ S^¼, so a planet at Venus's orbit (1.9× Earth's flux) is only ~45 K hotter by blackbody logic, which is why Venus's 460 K difference is a greenhouse-story not a distance-story — frames every "is Venus a runaway greenhouse?" discussion, and the ice–albedo bifurcation frames the "frozen Earth-sized planet" question: for M-dwarf stars, the 2010s–2020s tidal-locking and rotation-state literature (the 2013–2016 Yang and colleagues circulation-resonance results, the 2020s TRAPPIST-1 system papers with JWST's 2023–2025 observations beginning to test the predictions) turns the EBM's three equilibria into a census problem — which of the observed planets sit on which branch. The cautionary half of the story is equally instructive: EBMs with fixed clouds and prescribed albedos got the **inner edge of the habitable zone wrong by a factor of two** across the 1990s–2010s because cloud feedbacks in the moist greenhouse are the answer, and the 2019–2024 3-D GCM and lab-astrobiology reassessments (the runaway limits of the 2020s, the "vega-like planet" false-positive literature) are the demonstration that the zero-D model's honesty — its assumptions written on one line — is also its ceiling: when the missing physics *is* the feedback, the bifurcation diagram is a hypothesis about a planet, not a statement about one.
:::

::: history Two centuries of balancing the planet's books {#hist-ebm}
**1824–1896**: Fourier notes that Earth's surface is far warmer than its radiating temperature implies and proposes the atmosphere as an insulator ("like a box with glass covers," 1824); Tyndall's 1859–1861 experiments identify water vapour and CO₂ as the absorbing gases; Arrhenius, guided by his student Ekholm's CO₂ estimates and using Langley's solar-spectrum measurements, computes in 1896 that doubling CO₂ warms a layer model by several degrees — the energy-balance argument and the greenhouse calculation are born in the same decade. **1950–1969**: the modern era opens — Möller's 1963 and Kuhn's 1961 radiative-convective feedback computations; Budyko's 1962 *Heat Balance of the Earth's Surface* establishes the planet-as-ledger method; the 1965 Manabe–Wetherald CO₂ model and the 1967 water-vapour-feedback version follow; and Budyko's 1969 ice–albedo model publishes the first climate bifurcation, immediately contested by the meteorological establishment (the 1970s "new ice age" scare it fed was defused by aerosols and ocean physics, not by the model being wrong about the geometry). **1973–1979**: Sellers's 1973 radiatively-active energy-balance model generalises the albedo scheme; North's 1979 classification of energy-balance models (by transport law, radiation law, albedo law) fixes the taxonomy still taught; the first snowball hints enter the geological literature. **1992–2003**: Kirschvink names Snowball Earth (1992), Hoffman and colleagues publish the cap-carbonate case (1998, the 2017 Neoproterozoic dating programme completing the stratigraphy), and the EBMs find their second life as integrated-assessment engines (the MAGICC lineage, the 1990s–2011 Wigley–Raper–Meinshausen calibrations to GCMs). **2014–2025**: Lewis and Curry's historical-record ECS estimate opens the two-layer wars; AR6 (2021) makes ocean heat content the arbiter; the JWST era turns EBM bifurcation diagrams into observable-exoplanet predictions, and the 2023–2025 "runaway limits" reassessments complete the circle from Arrhenius's layer model to planets around other stars — the one-line model outliving every model built to replace it.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-snowball-co2}
Why does escaping a global icehouse in an EBM require far more CO₂ than the amount that eventually stabilises the thawed climate?

- [ ] Because silicate weathering only begins once the ice is gone
- [x] Because the ice–albedo feedback is hysteretic: the frozen state's high albedo must be overcome before the feedback reverses, so the deglacial threshold exceeds the post-thaw stabilising level
- [ ] Because the ocean's heat capacity delays warming permanently
- [ ] Because CO₂ is absorbed by sea ice

::: solution :::
On the S-shaped equilibrium curve the system sits on the cold branch until the warm branch reappears — which requires forcing large enough to eliminate the cold equilibrium, not merely to cross the unstable threshold. The geological resolution of the mismatch (the model's several-hundred-times-CO₂ deglacial requirement versus the cap-carbonate isotope record's estimates) involves the slow drawdown by weathering after thaw (the "weathering thermostat" of the 2000s–2010s that makes high CO₂ self-limiting on million-year timescales) — the EBM's hysteresis was right, and the geology supplied the missing slow term.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-diffusive}
The diffusive EBM's equator–pole temperature contrast is:

- [ ] Too large compared with observations, because eddies are inefficient in the model
- [x] Too small for a given diffusivity once the ice–albedo feedback is included, and the required diffusivity is not a constant but a tuned number — a sign the real transport is nonlinear and eddy-limited
- [ ] Exactly right, which is why diffusion is the standard closure
- [ ] Independent of the diffusivity chosen

::: solution :::
With a constant diffusivity the model's cold poles strengthen the ice-albedo feedback and over-freeze (the "too cold poles" pathology), while the observed atmosphere's eddy transport saturates when baroclinic instability runs out of available energy — the diffusivity is really a state-dependent, limited quantity (the 1990s–2020s "eddy saturation" and moisture-dependent transport literature, and the moisture/energy-based transport models of the 2010s as alternatives). The lesson generalises: EBMs fail precisely where their transport closure pretends geography away, and their repair history maps the real climate's nonlinearity.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="255"}
**Radiating temperature.** With S = 1361 W/m², α = 0.30 and σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴, compute Earth's effective radiating temperature T = [S(1−α)/4σ]^¼, in kelvin (nearest K).
::: solution :::
Absorbed = 1361 × 0.70/4 = 238.2 W/m²; T⁴ = 238.2/5.67 × 10⁻⁸ = 4.20 × 10⁹ K⁴; T = **255 K** — the temperature satellites feel, 33 K below the surface, the entire greenhouse effect as one unmodelled number and the starting point of every radiation model in the course.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="15"}
**Linearised balance.** In the model I = A + BT with A = 210 W/m² and B = 2.0 W m⁻² K⁻¹, what equilibrium temperature (°C) balances an absorbed solar flux of 240 W/m²?
::: solution :::
T = (240 − 210)/2.0 = **15 °C** — the calibration trick: two parameters fitted through the present reproduce the mean state, which is why EBMs are trusted for *changes* and not for absolutes, and why the parameters' provenance (fitted where?) must always be stated.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="1.5"}
**Mixed-layer timescale.** With mixed-layer heat capacity C = 1.5 × 10⁸ J m⁻² K⁻¹ (about 35 m of ocean) and λ = 3.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹, what is the e-folding time τ = C/λ in years (3.15 × 10⁷ s/yr)?
::: solution :::
τ = 1.5 × 10⁸/3.3 = 4.55 × 10⁷ s = **1.5 years** — the speed limit on surface-climate response to any forcing pulse (volcanic eruptions recover on this clock, the 1991 Pinatubo dip and 1993–1995 rebound are the natural experiment), and the reason the *observed* warming lag is much longer: the pulse diffuses into the deep ocean, which is the two-layer model's whole subject.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="11"}
**Amplification near runaway.** If the ice–albedo feedback strength in the cold state is 3.0 W m⁻² K⁻¹ against the Planck response λ₀ = 3.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹, by what factor is warming amplified (f = λ₀/(λ₀ − Σλ))?
::: solution :::
f = 3.3/(3.3 − 3.0) = **11** — a 1.2 °C no-feedback response becomes 13 °C, and pushing the feedback to 3.3 makes the answer infinite: the amplification formula is the runaway greenhouse, the snowball fall and the "cloud feedback eats the Planck response" ECS debate written as one fraction.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.9"}
**Ocean heat uptake.** Today's net forcing is F = 3.7 W/m² (a doubling's worth, for argument) and Earth's energy imbalance (heat uptake) is N = 0.8 W/m². With λ = 3.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹, what warming is *expressed* at the surface, T = (F − N)/λ?
::: solution :::
T = (3.7 − 0.8)/3.3 = 0.88 ≈ **0.9 °C** — the two-layer model's central accounting: two-fifths of the forcing is hidden in the ocean, which is why observed warming understates equilibrium warming, why "committed warming" exists even at zero emissions, and why the ocean-heat-content record (the AR6-era arbiter) is the honest measure of how much is stored, not lost.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="24"}
**Approach to equilibrium.** A two-layer EBM's long (adjustment) timescale is τ₂ = 8 years for a sustained forcing. How long until the surface reaches 95 % of its equilibrium warming (t ≈ 3τ)?
::: solution :::
1 − e^(−3) = 0.95, so t = 3 × 8 = **24 years** — the reason "equilibrium" sensitivity is invisible in the first decades of any scenario, why TCR (the transient, roughly half of ECS) is the quantity the 2020s actually experiences, and why stopping emissions stops *forcing* quickly but leaves the surface adjusting for decades.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="7.5"}
**Deglacial arithmetic.** A snowball planet needs CO₂ at 100× preindustrial to escape. Using F = 5.35 ln(C/C₀) and λ₀ = 3.3 (no ice-albedo help yet), what is the no-feedback warming from the CO₂ alone, in K?
::: solution :::
F = 5.35 × ln 100 = 24.6 W/m²; ΔT = 24.6/3.3 = **7.5 K** — enough, with the collapsing ice-albedo feedback riding on top, to cross back to the warm branch: the exercise reproduces why the Neoproterozoic escape needed *geologically extreme* CO₂ (the ~10⁵ ppm cap-carbonate estimates) and why the same 100× today, without ice sheets to lose, would be a ~7–8 K no-feedback catastrophe — the EBM's two answers to the same number, one reassuring and one not.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="2.1"}
**ECS from the record.** The historical record gives: 2020 forcing F = 2.7 W/m², imbalance N = 0.7 W/m², observed warming ΔT = 1.1 K. Fit λ = (F − N)/ΔT and compute ECS = F₂×/λ with F₂× = 3.8 W/m².
::: solution :::
λ = (2.7 − 0.7)/1.1 = 1.82 W m⁻² K⁻¹; ECS = 3.8/1.82 = **2.1 °C** — the Lewis-and-Curry family of low-sensitivity estimates in one division, and the critique in the next breath: the answer hangs on N (the Argo-era heat content, revised upward by 2020s measurements, which raises λ and pulls ECS up) and on assuming λ has been constant while cloud regimes reorganised — the EBM gives the cleanest possible statement of what the record implies *and* of everything it must assume to say so.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="22.4"}
**Stellar flux scaling.** A planet receives 1.4× Earth's absorbed flux with the same albedo and emission law. By how many K does its blackbody temperature exceed Earth's 255 K (T ∝ S^¼; 1.4^¼ = 1.088)?
::: solution :::
T = 255 × 1.088 = 277.4 K, ΔT = **22.4 K** — the quarter-power law that makes distance a weak control on temperature (Venus receives 1.9× and is 460 K hotter: the difference is atmosphere, not orbit), that sets the habitable zone's width to a few tens of per cent in flux, and that is why the runaway and condensation *thresholds* — nonlinear, cloud- and chemistry-limited — rather than the ¼-power itself, decide where the edges of the zone actually lie.
:::
:::

::: warning Four ways EBMs mislead
1. **"The model says 15 °C, so models nail the temperature."** The zero-D model's mean state is *fitted* through the present — A and B are chosen so the answer is today. The model has no content about the absolute temperature; its content is entirely in the *differential* (how the balance shifts when something changes), and every claim should be read with that asterisk. The 33 K greenhouse gap is the loud version of the same warning: the blackbody temperature is a statement about emission to space, never about the surface where we live.
2. **"λ = 3.3, so sensitivity is 1.2 °C and the debate is over."** The Planck response is one term in λ_net; the feedbacks that fill the remaining ~2 W m⁻² K⁻¹ are state-dependent, cloud-dominated and pattern-dependent — and EBM fits that treat λ as a constant across the historical record (the 2014–2020 ECS-from-the-record literature's central assumption, and central criticism) are measuring an *effective* λ averaged over a particular forcing pattern and climate state, not a property of the atmosphere.
3. **"A bifurcation in a model is a forecast."** The snowball solution proves the real system *can* have a cold alternative state; it does not say Earth is near the fold, that the anthropogenic perturbation points toward it (it points away — warming), or that the model's 6 % solar threshold is the real threshold (weathering feedbacks, cloud, palaeogeography move it by factors). Bifurcations in toy models are existence theorems and risk framings — the tipping-point communication failures of the 2010s–2020s (the "AMOC collapse this century" headlines against the AR6 low-likelihood assessment) are what happens when the theorem is read as a prediction.
4. **"EBMs are for teaching; real work uses GCMs."** The 2020s reality: EBMs and their extensions run inside every integrated-assessment model that prices carbon (the MAGICC-DICE chain), every large-ensemble emulation study, every fast scenario screen a policy modeler touches, and every exoplanet habitability paper. The right frame is the hierarchy: the EBM is not a bad GCM, it is a *different instrument* — the one whose assumptions are auditable, whose bifurcations are theorems, and whose disagreement with the complex models is the most informative number in the room.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Energy-balance models reduce climate to one ledger — absorbed sunlight against emitted infrared — and the reduction is productive: the zero-D model's amplification law ΔT = ΔF/(λ₀ − Σλ) contains the entire feedback formalism and shows runaway as the denominator's vanishing; the Budyko–Sellers ice–albedo model adds latitude and one nonlinearity and thereby proves the field's deepest structural facts — multiple equilibria, fold bifurcations, hysteresis — with two curves on a blackboard, the template for snowball Earth, its ~10⁵ ppm deglacial CO₂, and every tipping-point argument since; the time-dependent two-layer model adds ocean heat uptake and becomes the honest interpreter of the historical record, its four parameters (λ, κ, C₁, C₂) the most debated numbers in sensitivity science (the Lewis-and-Curry episode, the heat-content era's revisions). EBMs fail instructively — tuned means, constant λ, geography-free transport, clouds as parameters — and each failure names a process the ladder above must carry. Their modern role is not prediction but *transfer*: forcing-to-temperature functions inside assessment and integrated-assessment machinery, hypothesis generators for exoplanet climatology, and the course's own calibration instrument — because every student who has found the snowball by hand knows exactly which assumption did it, which is the literacy the million-core-hour models cannot supply about themselves.
:::
