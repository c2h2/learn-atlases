A climate model is a hypothesis written in a language that cannot be ambiguous: given these equations, these parameters, this grid and these boundary conditions, the climate does *this*. But there is no single "climate model" — there is a **hierarchy**, from a one-line energy balance you can solve on paper to Earth system models that run millions of processor-hours per simulated century, and the central skill of this course is knowing which rung answers which question. The hierarchy exists because of a cruel scaling law: the computer cost of resolving the atmosphere and ocean grows roughly with the sixteenth power of each resolution doubling, so the comprehensive model is always too expensive for all but a handful of experiments, and the cheap models are not training wheels but the interpretive instruments of the expensive ones. This lesson sets the vocabulary for the whole course: what a model is and can be (none can be verified, all can be falsified, most are *tuned*), the resolution–cost–complexity trade-off that organises the field, the distinction between initial-value prediction and boundary-condition projection that decides what a "forecast to 2100" can mean, and the epistemic division of labour — explanation, attribution, projection, exploration — that different rungs of the hierarchy serve. The prerequisite is the [[climate-system/feedbacks-sensitivity]] lesson; the payoff is stated in the course tagline: a model you have written and broken once teaches more about uncertainty than a hundred pages of description.

## What a Climate Model Is

## The Ladder of Idealisation

::: definition The model hierarchy: from one line to a planet {#def-hierarchy}
Climate models are organised as a ladder of idealisation, each rung keeping the physics it needs and discarding the rest. At the bottom sit **energy-balance models** — global mean temperature as one number, or a one-dimensional latitude column with diffusion of heat and an ice-albedo feedback (the Budyko–Sellers class of the 1960s–70s, solvable in seconds, still the standard tool for bifurcation analysis and snowball-Earth questions). Above them, **box models**: a few well-mixed volumes exchanging heat, carbon and salt (the Stommel 1961 two-box thermohaline model with its multiple equilibria, the four-box carbon models behind every "airborne fraction" discussion). Then **radiative–convective columns** (Manabe and Wetherald's 1967 model, one vertical dimension, the first to show water-vapour and lapse-rate feedbacks in action and the workhorse of radiative-forcing calculations to this day), **general circulation models (GCMs)** — atmosphere-only, ocean-only, then coupled — which solve the rotating, stratified fluid equations on a three-dimensional grid with the sub-grid physics parameterised, and at the top **Earth system models (ESMs)**, which add interactive carbon cycles, chemistry, aerosols, dynamic vegetation, ice sheets and biogeochemistry (the standard CMIP6 configuration of the 2020s runs 2,000–5,000 model years of coupled integration per participating modelling group per exercise). Between and beside these rungs live the intermediate complex models — regional climate models, earth-system models of intermediate complexity (EMICs) like the CLIMBER and MIT models that run the glacial cycles, convection-permitting models that drop the convective parameterisation altogether. The ladder is not a ladder of *quality* but of **affordability and interpretability**: a 10-km coupled week costs what a 100-km century costs, and the field's method is to bounce between rungs — discover in the simple model what matters, confirm it in the complex one, and use the complex one's output only where the simple one can explain why it came out that way.
:::

::: definition Resolution, cost, and why the comprehensive model is always too expensive {#def-resolution}
The hierarchy is enforced by turbulence. The atmosphere and ocean cascade energy from the scale of the forcing down to the dissipation scale across four to six orders of magnitude, and a grid must resolve the energetic eddies whose scale separation matters: roughly 50–100 km for the free atmosphere (where the large-scale dynamics are quasi-geostrophic and the dangerous scales are planetary), but ~10–20 km for the ocean, where the first baroclinic Rossby radius — the size at which rotation controls eddies — shrinks to 10–20 km in the mid-latitudes and under 5 km in the tropics, so that eddy-permitting (1/4°) ocean models still get western-boundary heat transport wrong and only the eddy-resolving ("km-scale") generation of the late 2010s–2020s (the DYCOMS and HiResASM/ DYAMOND intercomparisons, the 2020s DYAMOND and TWINS simulations of a globally turbulent ocean) capture observed eddy kinetic energy and deep-water formation realistically. The cost of resolution compounds: halving the grid spacing multiplies grid points by 8 (two horizontal, one vertical), and the stable time step halves with it (the CFL condition of the next lesson), giving a **sixteenfold** compute increase per doubling — and the fully-implicit "all-scale" dream that would break the time-step lock is fought over at every model centre. The consequence is the field's economics: a 100-km coupled model century costs ~10⁵ core-hours, a 10-km one ~10⁹, so the comprehensive model can afford a handful of runs while the questions (uncertainty, ensembles, carbon-cycle exploration) want thousands — the gap that emulators and machine-learning surrogates of the 2020s attack, and that Levins's 1966 trade-off triangle (a model can maximise realism, generality or precision, not all at once) long ago declared unavoidable. The honest summary is Stevens and Schär and the convection-permitting community's 2019 formulation: the gigametre-scale world is now computable for weeks, not centuries — the hierarchy will be reorganised by Moore's-law successors and exascale machines, but not yet.
:::

::: definition Prediction versus projection: initial values and boundary conditions {#def-projection}
Weather forecasting and climate projection are different mathematical problems wearing the same equations. A **prediction** is an initial-value problem: the atmosphere's state is observed (imperfectly), integrated forward, and its useful life ends when the smallest errors have grown to the scale of the flow — 1–2 weeks in midlatitudes (the Lorenz 1963 discovery of finite predictability, the 2002–2020s documentation that skill has extended ~1 day per decade, and the 2023–2025 arrival of machine-learning forecasters like the ECMWF's GraphCast and Huawei's Pangu-Weather at the skill of the best physics-based systems). A **projection** is a boundary-condition problem: run the model to its statistical equilibrium, or run ensembles whose individual members are meaningless weather but whose means and variances are the climate, and ask how that distribution shifts when CO₂ doubles; the initial state is forgotten in a few years and the scenario takes over. The 2007 Brankovic-and-Row and Hawkins-and-Sutton clarifications distinguished "prediction of climate" (decadal, initialised, partly predictable because the ocean's memory is slow) from "projection of climate change" (scenario-driven): they answer different questions with different error budgets. The practical distinctions the whole course turns on: a projection is **not a forecast** (no one predicts the emissions pathway — scenarios are conditional, "if RCP8.5 then X"); decadal prediction can legitimately be skilful for the North Atlantic overturning and the global-mean temperature trend of the 2010s–2020s (the decadal-prediction experiments of CMIP5 and CMIP6, and the 2010s renaissance of Mohn's 1890s prediction of European warming) while being hopeless for a specific European winter; and the variance of a projection decomposes — cleanly under additivity — into internal variability (reducible by ensembles), model uncertainty (reducible by better models, slowly) and scenario uncertainty (irreducible, a property of the future), whose relative shares shift with time and variable (the 2009 Hawkins-and-Sutton frames: for 2100 temperature, scenarios dominate; for 2030 precipitation, models and noise do).
:::

::: definition What models can and cannot be: tuning, falsification, credibility {#def-epistemology}
No climate model can be **verified** — the experiment it predicts (the 2080s under SSP5-8.7) will not be run — so the field's epistemic standards are weaker and more careful than "the model was right". The working philosophy is a three-part discipline. **Tuning**: free parameters that cannot be derived from first principles (cloud fractions, entrainment rates, drag coefficients) are set so that the model reproduces a chosen set of observables — the radiation budgets of the CERES era (2000 onward), the seasonal cycle, the historical warming trend — and every tuning target is a promise about what the model will get right *by construction*; the evaluation chapter of this course is largely the art of testing models on what they were *not* tuned to. **Falsification without rejection**: when a model fails an observation (the CMIP3-era underestimate of Arctic sea-ice decline that made 2007's record a genuine surprise, the cold-bias double-ITCZ problem that has survived four decades of model development, the CMIP6 2019 finding that several models' equilibrium sensitivities sat above the historical-record constraint), the response is not to discard the model but to locate the failure in a process, a parameterisation, a resolution — the hierarchy's diagnostic function. **Credibility by triangulation**: Oreskes and colleagues' 1994–2001 argument that models earn trust the way detectives do — not from any single test but from the consistency of many predictions against many observations, the survival of failed attempts to refute them, and the robustness of results across model families (the same physics in different codes, the same feedbacks in different hierarchies). This is why the assessment process (the IPCC's confidence language of AR6, 2021) grades statements by *lines of evidence* — palaeoclimate, historical record, theory, emergent constraints, multi-model agreement — rather than by model authority, and why the course's recurring motto is C. C. Moore's: models are not evidence, models are interpreters of evidence. The failure modes of the discipline are equally instructive — the overconfident 1970s "compute a new ice age" proposals falsified by aerosol and ocean-physics reality, the 1990s model spread that shrank by tuning rather than understanding (the 2020s "structural uncertainty" literature's central caution) — and the discipline's one genuine triumph, the 1979 Charney report's 3 ± 1.5 °C surviving four decades of model generations essentially untouched, is the case study that the next three definitions unpack.
:::

::: example One number, five rungs: where "3 °C per doubling" comes from {#ex-ecs-rungs}
The equilibrium climate sensitivity — the global-mean warming after CO₂ doubles and everything slow has adjusted — is the field's flagship number and the cleanest demonstration of the hierarchy working as a system. **Rung 1, paper:** the no-feedback Planck response (σT⁴ differentiated) gives 288 K/4 ≈ 30 K per e-folding, or ~1.2 °C per doubling once the 3.7 W/m² forcing is divided by the ~3.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹ Planck parameter — the exercise every student does, and the reason "3 °C" means feedbacks tripled the bare answer. **Rung 2, radiative–convective columns:** Manabe and Wetherald's 1967 model (fixed relative humidity, prescribed 50 % cloudiness) computed 2.9 °C with water-vapour and lapse-rate feedbacks live — within a whisker of the 2021 AR6 best estimate of 3 °C, achieved fifty-four years earlier with a single vertical dimension. **Rung 3, process synthesis:** the feedback arithmetic of the [[climate-system/feedbacks-sensitivity]] lesson — water vapour, lapse rate, surface albedo, and the cloud term that still swamps the sum — assigns the ±0.5 °C expert ranges of the 2012 and 2020 assessments (the IPCC AR6 2–5 °C "very likely" and the 2.5–4 °C of the National Academies' 2019 report). **Rung 4, GCMs and ESMs:** the CMIP ensembles' equilibrium sensitivities (the CMIP5 range 2.1–4.7 °C, the CMIP6 1.8–5.7 °C whose upper tail triggered the 2019–2020 confrontation with the historical record) supply the distribution's shape and the failure modes — the low-cloud feedbacks of the southeastern-stratocumulus models that run hot. **Rung 5, observations and palaeoclimate:** the emergent-constraint programme (the 2012 Brient-and-colleagues tropical-anvil constraint, the 2020 Sherwood and colleagues *Nature* review that folded process knowledge, historical data and the Last Glacial Maximum into a 2.3–4.5 °C plausible range) and the paleo-based CO₂-equivalence arguments that exclude both tails. No single model produced the number; the number is what the hierarchy's agreement *means*, and the disagreement about clouds is exactly where the same hierarchy concentrates its remaining work.
:::

::: example The cheap models that were right first: Budyko, Stommel, Manabe–Wetherald {#ex-cheap-models}
Three two-page papers of the 1960s–70s still teach the hierarchy's value better than any supercomputer run. **Budyko's 1969 ice–albedo model** (independently Sellers's 1973 version) put global temperature in one equation — absorbed sunlight with a state-dependent albedo against linearised outgoing radiation, diffused across latitude — and produced the first quantitative **multiple equilibria**: for CO₂ halving or modest insolation change, a fully ice-covered Earth becomes the only stable state, and the transition between ice-free and snowball is hysteretic. The prediction outlived its author's skepticism (the snowball-Earth geological evidence of the 1990s–2000s, the palaeoclimate modelling of the 2010s that had to invoke lower CO₂ thresholds than the simple model implied), and the same bifurcation geometry now frames every tipping-point discussion from the Amazon to the AMOC. **Stommel's 1961 two-box salt engine** — two boxes, a prescribed freshwater flux, density-driven exchange — showed that the thermohaline circulation can have **two stable states** (northern sinking, southern sinking) and can be flipped by a freshwater pulse past a threshold, the mechanism behind every "freshwater hosing" hysteresis experiment since and the reason the AMOC's 2020s warning-signal literature (the critical-slowing-down statistics of van Westen and the Ditlevsen sisters' 2023 *Nature Communications* early-warning claim, contested in the 2024–2025 statistical-methods debate) is framed as box-model geometry at all. **Manabe and Wetherald's 1967 column** — one vertical dimension, convective adjustment, fixed relative humidity — found 2.9 °C per CO₂ doubling, identified water vapour as the amplifying feedback and the lapse rate as the brake, and has been re-run, upgraded and cited for fifty-five years without its central numbers moving much. The lesson the three share: the cheap models are not approximations of the expensive ones, they are **theorems with parameters** — proofs that a behaviour is possible from stated mechanisms alone — which is precisely what the expensive models then test for reality.
:::

::: example Right climate, wrong weather: what a GCM's daily output is and is not {#ex-weather-climate}
In 2018 the weather-forecast community's mockery of climate models ("they can't even get tomorrow right") met its clean answer, and it is worth being able to give in one paragraph. A general circulation model run freely drifts into its own weather: after two weeks its atmospheric state bears no more correlation with reality than a shuffled deck, and no honest modeler claims skill for any individual simulated storm, monsoon onset or European summer. What the model claims — and what is tested — is the **statistics**: the distribution of daily temperatures, the frequency of heat extremes, the position and intensity of the storm tracks, the seasonal cycle of the ITCZ, the variance of ENSO, whose reproduction across thousands of independent model-days is a formidable, falsifiable statement about the climate system's attractor, not its trajectory. The 2010s–2020s quantified the split: model "weather" errors (the daily-variance biases, the MJO representation, the double-ITCZ geometry) are exactly the fields where models disagree most, while the forced climate signals — the annual-mean warming pattern of the historical ensemble, the tropical amplification, the Arctic amplification of sea-ice loss — are robust across the ensemble even when individual members' weather is wrong (the "signal-to-noise" framing of the [[climate-change/projections]] lesson). The instructive failure is the success story: CMIP3 models systematically **underestimated** observed Arctic sea-ice decline through the 2000s — too much ice, too stable a summer — and the 2007 minimum arrived as a genuine surprise; the post-mortem (the 2018 Stroeve-and-Notz projection-of-record analysis showing the ensemble mean's bias and the observed trajectory tracking the ensemble's steeper members) both embarrassed the hierarchy and demonstrated its honesty: the models were wrong in a locatable way — thinner-ice dynamics, melt-pond parameterisations, ocean heat uptake — and the corrected generation of CMIP6 now brackets the observations. Weather wrong, climate right, and when climate is wrong, wrong for reasons: that is the contract.
:::

::: example The 2020s rearrange the ladder: km-scale models and machine-learning surrogates {#ex-ai-km}
Two technologies of the 2020s look like they abolish the hierarchy and, on inspection, redistribute it. **Convection-permitting and km-scale modelling**: the UK's 1.5-km UKCP18 summer-convection runs (2015–2019), the CMIP6 HighResMIP decade (2017–2025), and the DYAMOND and TWINS projects' global ~10-km ocean and ~1–3-km atmospheric week-to-season simulations (2019–2024) showed what the ladder's top rung buys: diurnal-cycle precipitation phasing fixed, the Madden–Julian oscillation's organisation sharpened, ocean heat-transport fronts and deep-water properties much closer to the World Ocean Atlas — and, crucially, that the *climate sensitivities* of the km-scale runs are not dramatically different from their 100-km ancestors, because the global energy balance is not where the missing physics lives (the 2020s stratocumulus-b breakup of the 2024–2025 high-resolution cloud-resolving studies, which did find large cloud-feedback changes, is the caution against over-generalising that conclusion). **Machine learning**: GraphCast (DeepMind, 2023), Pangu-Weather and their successors (2023–2025) trained on reanalyses now match or beat physics-based numerical weather prediction at lead times under ~7 days at a fraction of the compute, and by 2024–2025 the first ML climate emulators — neural parameterisations of convection and radiation, learned surrogates of full model hierarchies — were in testing inside GCMs. The hierarchy absorbs both as it absorbed everything else: ML forecasters are initial-value machines — trained on the observed atmosphere, untrustworthy off-distribution, with no guarantee of conserving energy or water — so they accelerate ensembles and exploration but do not, by themselves, license a 2100 projection; and km-scale physics still needs the cheap models to say *why* the answer changed. The ladder's next configuration, visible in the 2025 horizon: ML surrogates doing the thousand-member sampling, physics-based km models doing the process truth, and the paper-and-pencil rungs doing what they have always done — deciding which of the other two are allowed to believe.
:::

::: history A century of computing the climate {#hist-hierarchy}
**1904–1922**: Vilhelm Bjerknes proposes that weather is an initial-value problem solvable by integration, and Lewis Fry Richardson's *Weather Prediction by Numerical Process* (1922) hand-integrates a six-hour step — wrong by the equivalent of a large storm, but right in kind — and imagines the 64,000-human "forecast factory" that electronic computers will replace in one afternoon. **1943–1956**: the race to make Richardson right — Rossby's Bergen and Chicago schools, Charney's 1947 quasi-geostrophic filtering of the sound waves that made the time step survivable, Fjørtoft's cascade analysis of what resolution means, and Phillips's 1956 general circulation integration at Princeton producing jet streams from equations. **1961–1975**: the hierarchy forms — Manabe and Wetherald's 1967 radiative–convective model, Smagorinsky's 1963 turbulence closure and 1966–1969 GCM experiments, Manabe and Bryan's 1969 coupled ocean–atmosphere model, Budyko's and Sellers's energy-balance bifurcations, Stommel's box models — and in 1975 the first US National Academy assessment (the Charney report follows in 1979) fixes 3 ± 1.5 °C. **1975–1995**: coupling and validation — the sea-ice and land-surface schemes of the 1980s, the atmospheric-model intercomparison projects (the AMIP protocols of 1989) invent model *evaluation* as a method, the 1990s coupled models reproduce (and misplace) the ITCZ, and the IPCC assessments become the institutional consumer of the hierarchy. **1995–2014**: the ensemble era — the HadCM3 and ECHAM multi-century runs, the 2001–2013 CMIP3 and CMIP5 architectures, the 2009–2014 variance-decomposition literature (the Hawkins-and-Sutton frames), the 2013 Palaeoclimate Model Intercomparison Project's Last-Glacial-Maximum test of the models' biggest extrapolation, and the 2014 CMIP5 atlas that made model-spread maps as standard as the maps themselves. **2014–2025**: the Earth-system and exascale era — CMIP6's DECK protocols and 1.8–5.7 °C sensitivity surprise (2017–2019), the AR6 evidence-hierarchy assessment (2021), the 2021 COP26 "1.5 °C with limited overshoot" special-report modelling chains, the km-scale DYAMOND/TWINS simulations (2019–2024), and the machine-learning forecasters (2023) whose arrival reopens Richardson's original question — what, exactly, is computation for? — at the scale of the planet.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-ivbvp}
Why can a decadal prediction system be skilful for the global-mean temperature of the 2020s while being unable to predict next winter?

- [ ] Because the ocean model is more accurate than the atmosphere model
- [x] Because slow ocean and ice modes evolve on decadal scales and their initial states are observed, while atmospheric weather loses memory in weeks
- [ ] Because decadal runs use smaller time steps
- [ ] Because greenhouse-gas concentrations damp atmospheric chaos

::: solution :::
The initial-value problem has a predictability horizon set by the fastest error growth — atmospheric baroclinic chaos, ~2 weeks. But the climate system also contains slow, observed modes (thermocline heat content, North Atlantic salinity, sea-ice state) whose evolution over 5–10 years is partly determined by their initial condition; initialising them buys skill for global-mean temperature trends and the Atlantic sector specifically, while individual winters remain in the noise. Boundary-condition projections use the same models with the opposite strategy: forget the initial state, average over members, interrogate the forced response.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-levins}
Levins's triangle (realism, generality, precision) explains why:

- [ ] All climate models must eventually converge to one comprehensive model
- [x] A model optimised for one purpose (e.g., bifurcation geometry) is deliberately not optimised for another (e.g., regional precipitation realism)
- [ ] Simple models are always preferable to complex ones
- [ ] Model complexity should track computer availability

::: solution :::
The trade-off says the three desiderata compete, so the rational strategy is a division of labour across a pluralistic ladder — each rung maximising one vertex (box models: generality and interpretability; GCMs: realism; ensembles: precision) rather than one model maximising all. "More resolution" is not "better" but "different vertex," which is why the hierarchy survives every generation of supercomputers; exascale machines buy the top rung, not the ladder's abolition.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="1.2"}
**Planck sensitivity.** The radiative forcing of doubled CO₂ is 3.7 W/m² and the Planck feedback parameter is 3.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹. What is the no-feedback equilibrium warming?
::: solution :::
ΔT = F/λ = 3.7/3.3 = **1.2 °C** — the one-line answer every hierarchy rung starts from; the famous 3 °C is this number multiplied by the net feedback factor f = 1/(1 − λ_feedback·...) ≈ 2.5, and the entire field of feedback analysis is about why f is not 1.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="10368"}
**Grid counting.** How many grid cells does a latitude–longitude atmospheric grid with 2.5° × 2.5° cells have?
::: solution :::
(360/2.5) × (180/2.5) = 144 × 72 = **10,368** columns per level — and the honest footnote is that latitude–longitude grids oversample the poles (the cosine factor), which is why dynamical cores have moved to icosahedral and cubed-sphere meshes whose cell count is uniform; the exercise's real lesson is that "resolution" is a statement about geometry before it is one about physics.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="200"}
**Time step.** A model's stable time step obeys the CFL condition Δt < Δx/u_max with u_max = 50 m/s (gravity waves) on a 10-km grid. What is the maximum time step?
::: solution :::
Δt = 10,000/50 = **200 s** — versus ~1,200 s on the same physics at 100 km: halving Δx halves Δt, one of the two reasons (with the 8× point count) resolution costs 16×, and the reason "horizontally implicit" dynamical cores that step past the acoustic limit are a serious attempt to break the lock.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="4096"}
**The sixteenfold rule.** A centre moves its coupled model from 100-km to 12.5-km horizontal grid, doubles vertical levels, and re-tunes the time step by CFL. By what factor does cost per simulated year grow?
::: solution :::
Three resolution doublings: points ×8³ = 512, time step ÷8, so cost ×512 × 8 = **4,096** — a CMIP6 centennial ensemble (10⁵ core-hours) becomes a 4 × 10⁸-core-hour single run: four hundred million core-hours, a continental supercomputer for months per century, which is the whole reason the 2020s km-scale projects simulate weeks and the ladder survives.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="36"}
**Ensemble sizing.** Internal variability gives a 0.3 °C spread of individual 20-year means around the forced response. How many ensemble members are needed so that the ensemble-mean noise is 0.05 °C?
::: solution :::
σ/√N = 0.05 → N = (0.3/0.05)² = **36 members** — the arithmetic behind every "large ensemble" of the 2010s–2020s (the CESM and GFDL hundred-member projects, the UK's hundred-member HadGEM3 decadal suites): signal detection in a noisy climate is a sampling problem, and sampling is cheap compared to resolution — which is exactly the trade-off the hierarchy exists to exploit.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="5000"}
**Spin-up.** Deep-ocean adjustment is diffusive: t ≈ z²/κ with κ ≈ 10⁻⁴ m²/s. How long does a 4,000-m water column take to equilibrate (in years, 3.15 × 10⁷ s/yr)?
::: solution :::
t = (4×10³)²/10⁻⁴ = 1.6 × 10¹¹ s ÷ 3.15 × 10⁷ = **~5,000 years** — the reason coupled models are spun up for millennia (the CMIP DECK protocol's 500-year preindustrial control runs are a compromise, not an equilibrium), why the ocean's heat-uptake timescale makes transient and equilibrium sensitivity differ by ~40 %, and why "the model equilibrated" is always a claim about the fast components, never the abyss.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="76"}
**Variance budget.** For 2080–2099 mean temperature in a Hawkins-and-Sutton-style decomposition, the standard deviations are: internal 0.2 °C, model 0.4 °C, scenario 0.8 °C. What percentage of the total *variance* comes from scenario uncertainty?
::: solution :::
Variances: 0.04 + 0.16 + 0.64 = 0.84. Scenario share = 0.64/0.84 = **76 %** — the number that ends the "models disagree, so projections are useless" argument for late-century temperature (the scenario dominates) and starts the honest argument for 2030s regional precipitation (where models and internal noise dominate and scenarios barely register): the variance decomposition is question-, variable- and horizon-specific, and reading it backwards is the most common projection-literacy failure in policy documents.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="100"}
**Emulator economics.** A GCM member costs 10,000 core-hours; a trained linear emulator costs 1 core-hour per member. A study needs either 100 GCM members or 10,000 emulator members for the same parameter exploration. What is the ratio of total GCM cost to total emulator cost?
::: solution :::
GCM: 100 × 10,000 = 10⁶ core-hours; emulator: 10,000 × 1 = 10⁴; ratio = **100** — the emulator argument in one division, with its two asterisks: the emulator is only valid inside the training envelope of the expensive runs that made it (the 2020s "out-of-distribution" literature's warning to ML climate surrogates), and 10,000 samples of a 20-parameter space is where the curse of dimensionality starts biting — the reason Gaussian-process emulators (the history-matching school of the 2006–2020s UK climate-risk programme, the HadCRME and DICE line) dominate over brute force.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="5.5"}
**Time of emergence.** A forced warming signal grows as ΔT(t) = 0.02·t °C (t in years) and annual-mean local noise is σ = 0.3 °C. Using the "2σ/√N" criterion with N = 30 ensemble members, when does the signal emerge?
::: solution :::
Threshold = 2 × 0.3/√30 = 0.11 °C; t = 0.11/0.02 = **5.5 years** — a toy version of the Hawkins-and-Sutton "time of emergence" maps that structure public communication (the tropics emerge in the 2020s, high-latitude winter precipitation mid-century, regional precipitation perhaps never within the scenario spread): emergence is a property of signal, noise, *and* how many members you bring, which is why "when will we see it" is partly a question about how hard you look.
:::
:::

::: warning Four misconceptions about climate models
1. **"Higher resolution is always better."** Resolution buys realism at one vertex of Levins's triangle and costs sixteenfold at every other; the 2020s km-scale experiments improved precipitation phasing and ocean fronts while leaving global sensitivity largely where it was, and the stratocumulus-breakup scare of the high-resolution cloud studies shows resolution can even move the answer in the wrong direction if the cloud physics is wrong at the new scale. The right question is never "finer?" but "finer for which process, at which cost, tested against which observable?"
2. **"Tuning makes models circular."** Tuning to today's mean state is no more circular than calibrating a scale: the model is set to reproduce a chosen observable set, and its *credibility* comes from what it then does with data it never saw — the seasonal cycle it was not tuned to, the palaeoclimate states, the volcanic responses, the independent satellite eras. The circularity critique only bites when the tuning targets and the evaluation targets are the same (the 2020s "tuning-for-sensitivity" literature's genuine concern about historical-temperature-constrained ECS), which is exactly why the evaluation chapter of this course insists on held-out testing.
3. **"A model that failed once is useless."** Every model fails — the double ITCZ has survived 40 years, CMIP3's Arctic ice was too thick, CMIP6's sensitivities ran hot — and the discipline's actual method is *failure localisation*: a wrong ice-albedo parameterisation is a known, bounded error, not a refutation of fluid dynamics. The useless claim is the one that survives by being unfalsifiable; the useful model is the one whose failures are locatable, because those become the next rung's research agenda.
4. **"Machine learning will replace physics models."** ML forecasters of 2023–2025 are initial-value machines trained inside the observed distribution: they inherit the reanalyses' biases, have no conservation guarantees, and have never extrapolated a climate state in their training lives. What ML genuinely takes over is the ladder's *middle* — emulators, parameterisation surrogates, ensemble sampling, downscaling — and what it cannot replace is the hierarchy's function: a model whose failures are physical statements about the world, which is the only kind of model that can be *wrong about 2080* in an informative way.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Climate modelling is a division of labour across a ladder of idealisation — energy-balance models, box models, radiative–convective columns, GCMs, Earth system models, and the regional, intermediate-complexity and convection-permitting variants between them — organised by a single economic fact: each resolution doubling costs sixteenfold, so the comprehensive model is permanently too expensive for the questions that need sampling, and the cheap models are permanently necessary to interpret the expensive ones. The ladder answers different questions: initial-value prediction (two-week atmosphere, decade ocean), boundary-condition projection (scenario-driven ensembles whose members are meaningless weather and whose statistics are the object), and process-theorem demonstration (Budyko's two equilibria, Stommel's two states, Manabe–Wetherald's 2.9 °C — cheap models as existence proofs). Knowledge in this system is never verification but calibrated credibility: tuning to a declared observable set, falsification by held-out testing and palaeoclimate, triangulation across independent model families and hierarchies, and the assessment practice (AR6's evidence hierarchies) that grades claims by lines of evidence rather than model authority. The 2020s — km-scale weeks, ML forecasters, sensitivity controversies resolved by observational constraints rather than computer speed — have changed the ladder's economics, not its logic: the model you choose is the model whose failures you can interpret for your question. The next rung in this course is the simplest rung, worked out in full: the energy-balance models where bifurcations, hysteresis and the snowball Earth first become arithmetic.
:::
