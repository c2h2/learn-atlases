One model, one future, one number: the format is indefensible and, until the 1990s, it was all the field had. The ensemble revolution — driven by Lorenz's 1965 discovery that the atmosphere's initial-value problem has a two-week horizon, by the weather-prediction practice of running perturbed initial conditions operationally from the early 1990s, and by the recognition that for climate the interesting uncertainties are not just where we start but what model we believe and what the century does — replaced the single projection with a **distribution over futures**, and the scenario revolution (SRES 2000, the RCPs 2005–2010, the SSPs 2014–2016, the ScenarioMIP design and the 2023–2025 arguments about scenario realism) replaced the single storyline with a **designed space of pathways**. Together they define how projections are actually made, communicated and used: the variance decomposition that says which uncertainty matters for which question at which date (the 2009 Hawkins–Sutton result that scenario uncertainty dominates late-century temperature and is nearly irrelevant for mid-century precipitation), the large initial-condition ensembles that turn "when is the first ice-free Arctic September?" from a projection into a date distribution (the 2020s hundred-member generation), the multi-model ensembles whose spread is simultaneously the field's honesty and its accounting problem, and the shared socioeconomic pathways whose narratives — sustainability, middling roads, fossil-fuelled development — carry more policy freight than any other object in this course, including the 2023 dispute over whether the high-forcing lane (SSP5-8.5) should still be driven at all. This lesson is the statistics and the politics of the ensemble era: the taxonomy of uncertainty and its decompositions; the zoo of ensemble types and what each is for; the scenario architecture from RCPs to SSPs with its integrated-assessment machinery and its 2020s critiques; and the interpretation rules — when an ensemble mean is a signal, when it is an artefact, when its spread is a probability, and when it is a governance choice dressed as a percentile. The prerequisites are the [[climate-modelling/model-hierarchy]] and [[climate-modelling/evaluation]] lessons; the reward is reading any IPCC figure's fan charts, ribbons and whiskers with full literacy — which is to say, knowing exactly what each line does and does not license you to say.

::: definition The uncertainty taxonomy: initial conditions, models, scenarios {#def-taxonomy}
Every projection's error budget has three accounts, named by the question they answer. **Internal variability** (the "initial-condition" account): the climate's own chaos — the same model, same forcing, different weather start, giving different 2040s and, less, different 2090s; reducible by averaging over ensemble members at the rate σ/√N, irreducible in principle, and dominant for the variables with loud dynamics (precipitation, storm tracks, monsoon onset) at horizons under a few decades. **Model uncertainty** (the "what we believe" account): the spread across structures and parameters — itself decomposable, per the 2000s–2020s variance-partition literature, into parameter perturbation (cheap, broad) and structural difference (expensive, deep, and usually the larger term, the 4:1 structural-to-parametric ratio of the [[climate-modelling/parameterisation]] lesson); reducible only by better models, sampled by multi-model ensembles whose members are, the genealogy literature warns, far from independent. **Scenario uncertainty** (the "what humanity does" account): the choice of emissions, land use and aerosol pathway — irreducible by any scientific effort, growing with the horizon, and the account that late-century temperature projections are mostly spending. The organising result is the 2009 Hawkins–Sutton variance decomposition (and its 2010s–2020s descendants and corrections): for **global-mean temperature**, internal variability dominates the first decade or two, model spread dominates mid-century, and scenario spread dominates after mid-century — the figure that ends the "models disagree, so projections are useless" argument for late-century warming (the disagreement is dwarfed by what we choose) and, equally, ends the claim that scenarios matter for 2040s regional precipitation (where they barely register and model spread and noise own the budget). The definition's discipline, stated once: **the decomposition is per variable, per region, per horizon, per statistic** — the 2010s corrections' emphasis — and quoting a share without those four labels is the ensemble era's most common statistical malpractice, the one to check first in every assessment slide and op-ed fan chart.
:::

::: definition The ensemble zoo: what each kind is for {#def-zoo}
**Perturbed-initial-condition (weather) ensembles** — the ECMWF and NCEP operational systems running since 1992–1994, 10–50 members, bred vectors and singular vectors — answer "what are the odds of the storm," and their probabilistic calibration (the reliability diagrams of verification science) is the standard every later ensemble secretly meets. **Perturbed-physics ensembles** (the 2000s–2020s HadGEM and CNRM physics suites, the 2010s–2020s large perturbed-parameter experiments): same code, jittered coefficients; they map scheme topology and, per the [[climate-modelling/parameterisation]] lesson, reveal that structural choice dominates parameter choice. **Large initial-condition ensembles** — the 2012–2020s generation (the CESM Community Large Ensembles' 34 members in 2012 and hundred in 2020, the GFDL and Hadley-centre tens-to-sixties suites, the UK's hundred-member decadal programmes) — exist to separate forced signal from noise: time of emergence, detectability, and the **timing distributions** that single-member projections cannot produce (the 2020s ice-free-Arctic-September date distributions of the hundred-member analyses, mid-2030s medians with decade-wide five-to-ninety-five ranges under mid-tier scenarios), and — the 2010s–2020s use that changed extremes science — the statistics of **rare events in a changeable world**, the "was this heatwave made 30× more likely" arithmetic of the attribution fast-response papers of 2015–2025 that need ten thousand summers to say anything about a one-in-fifty-year event. **Multi-model ensembles** (CMIP's 30–50 model families, the CMIP6 archive's 2019–2024 census): the field's parliament, sampled spread and shared biases at once, their mean a signal only where members agree for independent reasons (the 2010s–2020s independence-audit literature), their spread an uncertainty measure only after genealogy correction. **Constrained and weighted ensembles** (the 2010s–2020s programmes: observation-weighted means, emergent-constraint narrowing, the 2016–2023 debates over whether weighting helps or double-counts): the attempt to make the parliament vote with evidence-weighted voices, whose 2020s verdict is modest — weighting narrows ranges and changes central estimates little for temperature, more for regional circulation. **Decadal prediction systems** (the NMME's 2011–2019 multi-model initialised runs and their successors, the 2020s CMIP6-initialized-plus-perturbed-physics designs): the hybrid that bets real skill on the ocean's slow modes, and whose 2020s results (skill for global-mean temperature and North Atlantic sea-surface temperature at 5 years, near-zero skill for most regional precipitation) are the honest boundary of "prediction" in the prediction-versus-projection distinction of the [[climate-modelling/model-hierarchy]] lesson. The zoo's rule: **choose the ensemble by the question's noise structure** — timing questions need initial-condition sampling, feedback questions need structural sampling, late-century questions need scenario sampling — and the 2020s best practice (the AR6 chapter designs, the hundred-member-plus-multi-model-plus-SSP-matrix experiments) is the full matrix, run as a designed experiment rather than an archive accident.
:::

::: definition Scenario architecture: SRES, RCPs, SSPs {#def-scenarios}
Scenarios are the climate enterprise's encounter with the future as a design problem, and the architecture has been rebuilt twice. **SRES (2000)** — the Nakicenovic–Swart special report's A1/A2/B1/B2 families — were demographic-economic storylines run through emission models, with no climate policy by construction (the 1990s' decision to exclude "stabilisation" scenarios, the post-AR4 regret its most consequential policy failure) and no probability interpretation, only narratives; their A1B middle lane nonetheless became the de-facto baseline of the 2000s literature, an accident of availability. **The RCPs (2003–2010)** inverted the design: four pathways (2.6, 4.5, 6.0, 8.5 W/m² radiative forcing by 2100) produced by reverse-engineering emissions from integrated-assessment models (the IMAGE, MESSAGE, MiniCAM and AIM families) to hit forcing targets, with the 2.6 lane's invention (the 2006–2010 debate over adding an aggressive-mitigation RCP, resolved in favour) making the 2 °C target computable and — via the 2009–2011 US and UK committee processes and the 2010 Moss and colleagues and 2011 van Vuuren and colleagues papers — giving CMIP5 its four-lane experimental design; the RCPs' genius was making policy targets the independent variable, their flaw that the "pathways" were concentration trajectories detached from any account of whether the world could plausibly contain them. **The SSPs (2014–2016)** completed the separation: five **shared socioeconomic pathways** (the 2014 van Vuuren–Edmonds framework paper and the 2016 O'Neill and colleagues ScenarioMIP design) describing alternative societal roads — sustainability (SSP1), middle roads (SSP2), regional rivalries (SSP3), inequality (SSP4), fossil-fuelled development (SSP5) — crossed with the RCP-style forcing levels (the ScenarioMIP's nine designed experiments, SSP1-1.9 and SSP5-8.5 at the corners), so that narrative and forcing are explicit, combinable design choices; the AR6 2021 projections and every national assessment since run the matrix, and the 2020s policy-tracking literature (the UNEP Emissions Gap and NDC-synthesis reports) reads current policies against the SSP-RCP grid, finding the world near SSP2-4.5's lane with the tail risk of SSP3-7.0's. The architecture's 2020s arguments fill a sub-literature: the **scenario realism dispute** (the 2023 Hope and colleagues PNAS analysis arguing SSP5-8.5's fossil-expansion assumptions are economically dead on arrival, and the 2023–2025 responses from scenario scientists and social scientists defending high-emission lanes as the only honest way to bound the risk of the policies we did not choose — the "should we stop driving the lane we fear" question, which the field answered by keeping the lane and labelling its plausibility); the **overshoot design problem** (the SR1.5 2018 special report's 1.5 °C-limited-overshoot pathways and their dependence on late-century negative emissions, the 2020s critiques of the IAM machinery's cost-of-removal assumptions); and the **low-end question** (whether SSP1-1.9's net-zero-2050s engineering is representable in the models at all, the 2020s ScenarioMIP-generation debate that makes the projections' left edge as model-dependent as its right).
:::

::: definition Reading the fan chart: what ensemble statistics license {#def-fancharts}
The ribbon on the figure is an argument, and the field is still learning its grammar. **The ensemble mean** is a legitimate estimator of the forced signal only when members sample noise symmetrically and share no common bias — for global-mean temperature across CMIP, approximately true and usefully so; for regional precipitation, monsoon timing or extremes, often not, the mean of wrong-phased members producing a climatology no member has (the 2000s–2020s "ensemble mean as artefact" literature on diurnal cycles and ITCZ position). **The spread** is a probability only under calibration: weather ensembles earn theirs with reliability diagrams and per-decade inflation corrections; climate ensembles mostly do not — the 2010s–2020s spread-versus-truth studies find multi-model ranges that cover historical warming comfortably but underestimate the total error by factors of one to two for many variables (missing structural families, shared tunings, the unknown-unknowns of the [[climate-modelling/evaluation]] lesson's overconfidence finding), which is why AR6's ranges are hand-widened by evidence-line triangulation rather than read off the model percentiles, and why the assessment language "very likely 2.5–4 °C" is a judgement about physics, not a 90 % interval of a sample. **Fan charts and ribbon plots** (the Hadley-centre 2011–2020s decadal fan charts, the Global Carbon Budget's emission-path ribbons, the 2023–2025 "is warming accelerating" ribbons that accompanied the 2023 record debate) layer forced-response uncertainty over unforced variability, and their honest reading is the 2023 lesson: a point leaving the ribbon falsifies the ribbon's calibration, not the trend. **Percentiles of the ensemble are not risk percentiles** — the distinction the insurance and central-bank climate-stress-test literatures of the 2020s (the NGFS scenarios' 2020–2025 iterations) discovered painfully: model ensembles sample what modelers can build, not what the world can do (the ice-sheet, permafrost and AMOC tails live outside most distributions), so risk-oriented use requires **storyline supplementation** — the low-likelihood-high-impact branch method of the [[climate-change/tipping-points]] lesson — not percentile extrapolation. And **the scenario axis is a policy variable, not a random variable**: the spread across SSPs is not uncertainty to be averaged over (the 2020s critique of "expected warming" framings — averaging over scenarios is averaging over policy choices) but the projection's most controllable dimension, the one term in the variance budget that a negotiated agreement can shrink, which is the single sentence that connects this lesson's statistics to the [[climate-change/emissions-budgets]] lesson's arithmetic: the fan chart is widest on the right because we are free, and the width there is not ignorance but possibility.
:::

::: example The Hawkins–Sutton figure: uncertainty through the century {#ex-hawkins-sutton}
The 2009 figure — variance shares stacked by decade for global-mean temperature and for regional precipitation — is the most-reproduced image in projection science and deserves the close reading the exercises make you do. For **global temperature**: internal variability owns the 2010s (the noise of a decade against a 0.2 °C/decade trend), model spread owns the 2030s–2050s (the feedback-fight era), and scenario spread owns the 2070s onward (the lanes' divergence, ~4 °C between SSP1-1.9 and SSP5-8.5 by 2100, swamps the ~1 °C model spread). For **Sahel and monsoon precipitation**: model spread dominates nearly every decade and scenario share stays small to mid-century — the figure's silent policy implication, that the 2040s water question cannot be answered by choosing a lane, only by better models, and that adaptation finance aimed at mid-century precipitation is financing a model-uncertainty problem with a scenario-shaped budget. The 2010s–2020s corrections matter as much as the original: the decomposition depends on how model spread is estimated (the single-model-versus-multi-model debate of the 2010s — single-model perturbed-physics suites understate structural spread), on the statistic (variance shares differ for means versus extremes), on the region (the scenario share for Arctic sea ice is enormous by 2050, for Southern Ocean temperature nearly nil because the lanes have not arrived there yet), and on the metric's nonlinearity (shares in variance space versus degrees space tell different stories, the 2020s communication-error literature's quiet correction). The figure's one durable message, re-earned by every generation since: **uncertainty is not one number, and "how uncertain?" is answerable only after "for what, where, when, and measured how?"** — the four-question reflex the rest of this lesson drills.
:::

::: example When is the first ice-free September? The hundred-member answer {#ex-icefree-date}
The question "when does the Arctic lose its September sea ice?" is unanswerable from one simulation — the year depends on which El Niño lands when — and the hundred-member generation (the CESM2-LENS2's 100 members run under matched SSP scenarios, the 2020 release, and the UK's hundred-member decadal suites) turned it into the ensemble era's showcase measurement. The 2020s analyses (the LENS2-based timing syntheses of 2020–2024 and the ice-free-Arctic definition debates — the 2023–2025 community papers on what "ice-free" means, 1 million km² versus 40 % cover, because the date moves by years with the definition) deliver: under SSP2-4.5-like forcing, first-ice-free Septembers with **medians in the late 2030s and five-to-ninety-five ranges spanning the 2030s to mid-2050s**; under SSP5-8.5, a decade earlier; under SSP1-2.6, avoidance this century in most members but not all — the tail that single-member narratives omit. The result's structure is the lesson in miniature: the **timing distribution** (not the date) is the scientific object; the scenario axis shifts the distribution's location by a decade (policy moves percentiles, a sentence no single-run projection could support); the internal-variability width (a full decade at fixed scenario) is irreducible and was the 2010s single-model projections' hidden error; and the definition-dependence (1 vs 40 % ice cover) is the honest public-health analogue of "what counts as an extreme" — the indicator must be specified before the probability means anything. The downstream uses — the shipping, policy and "Arctic summer without ice" communication literatures of the 2020s, the insurance-style "first ice-free by 2040" risk quotes — are what large ensembles are for: converting a frightening anecdote into a dated, bounded, scenario-conditional distribution whose every percentile can be audited back to a member.
:::

::: example The 2023 SSP5-8.5 realism fight: should we drive the lane we fear? {#ex-ssp585}
In March 2023 a group including GCI's Hope and colleagues published a PNAS analysis arguing that SSP5-8.5 — the CMIP design's high-forcing lane, 8.5 W/m² by 2100, built on a fossil-fuelled-development world with coal persisting and gas exploding — was economically implausible even before climate policy, given solar and storage cost curves and the health-cost arithmetic of burning coal; the paper's press coverage ("scientists: worst-case scenario is unrealistic") and the community's response (the scenario scientists' 2023–2025 rejoinders, the CMIP Steering-Committee-adjacent statements, the social-ecological-literature interventions on why "unrealistic" is the wrong operator for a risk boundary) is the scenario era's purest argument about what scenarios are **for**. The technical layers, in order: SSP5's own construction (the 2016 Fujisaki and colleagues and 2017 O'Neill-generation SSP5 papers' explicit design brief — a high-fossil counterpart lane requested precisely because RCP8.5's use as a baseline had drawn the 2013–2016 criticism that it was becoming a self-fulfilling "business as usual," the design intent being risk-bounding, not forecasting); the economics (the 2023 paper's cost-optimisation runs versus the counter-reading that unpriced health damages, energy-service inequality and geopolitical coal-revival paths — the 2020s post-Ukraine gas and coal price shocks as the decade's irony — keep high-emission worlds reachable); and the statistics (a scenario's probability is undefined in a one-shot future, so "realistic" can only mean "consistent with some credible model of human behaviour," and SSP5-8.5 clears that bar via several credible ones — stalled climate policy, resource-rent states, security-of-supply politics). The resolution the field actually reached is procedural: keep the lane for bounding, stop using it as a baseline (the 2020s CMIP and national-assessment practice of labelling SSP5-8.5 "used for risk management only"), and publish the plausibility argument alongside the projection — the scenario axis treated, at last, as the designed policy variable the definition demands, with the 2023 fight teaching a generation of users that the right end of the fan chart is a choice about fear, not a forecast of it.
:::

::: example SR1.5's pathways: the ensemble that negotiated a number {#ex-sr15}
The 2018 special report's 1.5 °C-limited-overshoot pathways — the ~420 Gt CO₂ remaining-carbon-budget figure (67 % confidence variant) that became the movement's slogan and the 2020s net-zero laws' arithmetic — are the ensemble method pushed to its political limit, and their construction is the lesson's synthesis case. The machinery: the IPCC's scenario database (thousands of IAM pathways from the MESSAGE, IMAGE, REMIND, WITCH and GCAM families, run against reduced-complexity climate models — the MAGICC-line emulators of the [[climate-modelling/box-models]] lesson — calibrated to the CMIP5 generation's ECS and heat-uptake distributions), filtered by the report's definitions (overshoot means exceeding 1.5 °C then returning by 2100, a definition whose permissiveness — the 2019–2025 critiques noting that "1.5 °C-limited-overshoot" admits multi-decade, multi-impact exceedance — lives in the pathway selection, not the physics); the uncertainty stack (the budget's width from ECS spread, the ocean-heat-uptake and non-CO₂-forcing terms, and the negative-emissions dependence — the 2019–2025 literature's finding that most pathways need carbon-dioxide removal at scales without precedent, the AR6 WGIII 2022 chapter's sobering feasibility cross-walks); and the interpretive fault lines (the 2019–2024 arguments that the 67 % budget is a coin flip dressed as a target, that "limited overshoot" normalises breach, and that the budget's arithmetic silently assumes the carbon-cycle feedbacks of the [[climate-modelling/earth-system-models]] lesson stay linear). The ensemble-reading lesson generalises to every headline budget and ribbon since: the number is the **conjunction** of a climate definition, an emulator calibration, an IAM cost structure and a risk tolerance, each auditable, none of them physics alone — and the honest use is to name the conjunction, which the assessments' Summary for Policymakers, in their compression, structurally cannot do.
:::

::: history From Lorenz's butterfly to the SSP matrix {#hist-ensembles}
**1963–1994:** Lorenz's 1963 discovery of sensitive dependence and his 1969 "predictability" argument found the initial-value ceiling; the 1974 Joint Organizing Committee proposals and the 1980s CRU and GISS multi-model comparisons invent model-spread sampling; and the operational ensemble-prediction systems (Ensemble Prediction Systems) of ECMWF 1992 and NCEP 1994 make perturbed-initial-condition probability an everyday operational product. **1995–2010:** the scenario era begins — the 1992–1997 IPCC scenario process and the 2000 SRES publication (the policy-free storyline design and its stabilisation-exclusion decision), the 2001–2005 invention of the RCPs (the 2003 O'Neill–Lacis reverse-engineering concept, the 2006–2010 multi-model design committees, the 2010 Moss and colleagues RCP paper), the 2009 Hawkins–Sutton decomposition, and the 2009–2011 carbon-cycle-constraint and detection-attribution expansions that make ensembles the standard currency of both. **2011–2019:** the big-ensemble era — the 2011–2012 Community Large Ensembles' 34 members and the 2011–2019 NMME decadal systems, the 2014–2016 SSP framework papers (the 2014 van Vuuren–Edmonds narratives, the 2016 O'Neill ScenarioMIP design and the SSP5 companion papers), the 2015–2019 attribution fast-response era built on hundred-member counterfactuals, and the 2018 SR1.5's scenario-database-emulator machinery negotiating the most-quoted number in the field's history. **2020–2025:** the hundred-member and SSP-matrix generation (the 2020 Community Large Ensembles version 2 release, the AR6 2021 full-matrix projections, the 2022–2025 national and central-bank stress-test scenario programmes, the 2023 SSP5-8.5 realism fight and the 2023–2025 record-year ribbon debates) — the ensemble era complete, its statistics load-bearing in policy, and its oldest question — what should the spread mean? — more consequential than ever.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-ensemble-mean}
The multi-model ensemble mean is a trustworthy estimate of the forced response:
- [ ] Always, because averaging removes all error
- [x] When members' errors are independent and unbiased for the variable in question — often true for global temperature, often false for regional precipitation patterns
- [ ] Never, because models are not independent
- [ ] Only when weighted by skill

::: solution :::
Averaging removes noise, not bias, and removes noise only if it is independent across members — for global-mean temperature across CMIP families the approximation works (the mean tracks the observed trend within a few hundredths of a degree), for monsoon phase, diurnal cycles and double-ITCZ-prone precipitation the members' errors are correlated and phase-scrambled means produce climatology no member has. The trust test is the error structure, not the sample size — the ensemble-mean-as-artefact literature's standing warning.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-rcp-names}
The RCP names (2.6, 4.5, 6.0, 8.5) refer to:
- [ ] Years since industrialisation
- [x] Radiative forcing targets in W/m² by 2100, reverse-engineered by integrated assessment models into emission pathways
- [ ] Degrees of warming
- [ ] Gt CO₂ annual emissions

::: solution :::
The RCPs were designed forcing-first: integrated assessment models were run backwards to hit 2.6–8.5 W/m² in 2100, and the emissions, land use and aerosol pathways fell out — the inversion that made policy targets the independent variable of the CMIP5 design and that the SSPs kept while adding explicit societal narratives, since a concentration trajectory alone says nothing about the world that produced it, which is exactly what the SSP-RCP grid (SSP2-4.5, SSP5-8.5) now encodes: a story and a target, jointly designed.
:::
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="9"}
**Noise reduction.** A regional summer temperature has 0.9 °C of internal-variability spread per member. How many initial-condition members are needed for the ensemble-mean noise to reach 0.3 °C?
::: solution :::
N = (0.9/0.3)² = **9** — the square-root law that prices every large-ensemble project (the 2010s–2020s hundred-member generation buys noise down to a tenth of a degree, at which point model and scenario terms dominate and more members buy nothing); the exercise's professional habit: before asking "what does the ensemble say?", ask "how many effective members does it have?" — the answer often being far fewer than the plot's dots.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.5"}
**Variance shares.** For a 2050s precipitation projection the variance components are: internal 0.09, model 0.16, scenario 0.25 (mm²/day²). What is the scenario share?
::: solution :::
0.25/0.50 = **0.5** — half the variance from the emissions lane, and the four-label discipline (variable, region, horizon, statistic) satisfied by the question's phrasing; note that for the same region's 2030s projection the scenario term would be nearly nil, the whole point of the Hawkins–Sutton time axis: uncertainty has a calendar.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="4"}
**Attribution odds.** A heatwave has a 1-in-50 chance per year in the preindustrial control ensemble. In the present-day ensemble its rate is 1-in-12.5. What is the fraction-of-attributable-risk multiplier (present/preindustrial probability ratio)?
::: solution :::
(1/12.5)/(1/50) = **4** — the "quadrupled the risk" statement of the attribution fast-response letters of the 2015–2020s, computed as a ratio of probabilities from two ensembles of the same model (the counterfactual design's strength: model error largely cancels in the ratio, unlike absolute projections), and the one ensemble statistic that has entered everyday language — check whether the public version mentions that it is a risk multiplier, not a claim the event "would not have happened," which ensembles cannot say.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="16"}
**Decadal prediction skill.** If initialised decadal forecasts explain 36 % of the observed variance of North Atlantic sea-surface temperature at year 5, what is the anomaly correlation?
::: solution :::
r = √0.36 = **0.6** — the correlation-from-variance arithmetic behind the NMME-generation skill tables (and their honest reading: r = 0.6 at year 5 is real skill for the ocean's slow modes and near-worthless for regional precipitation, where the same systems score near zero — the 2020s decadal-prediction literature's careful, variable-by-variable verdict on what initialising the ocean buys).
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.2"}
**Effective members.** A 25-member multi-model ensemble has pairwise intermodel correlation of errors ρ = 0.8. What is its effective sample size, N_eff = N/(1 + (N−1)ρ)?
::: solution :::
N_eff = 25/(1 + 24 × 0.8) = 25/20.2 ≈ **1.2** — the genealogy correction in one formula: with 80 % shared error, 25 models carry barely more information than one, the 2010s–2020s independence-audit literature's demonstration that the CMIP parliament's voting power is a fraction of its headcount, and why ensemble-weighting and constraint programmes exist.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="10"}
**Timing distributions.** The first-ice-free-September distribution across a 100-member ensemble under a mid-tier scenario is centred on 2038 with a 5–95 % range of 2031–2051. If a policy document quotes "ice-free Arctic by 2035," which percentile is it implicitly claiming?
::: solution :::
2035 sits near the 15–20th percentile of that distribution — the exercise's literacy point: single-date claims from timing distributions are percentile choices masquerading as forecasts, and the professional question of every risk quote ("by when with what confidence?") recovers the hidden percentile; the 2020s ice-free-Arctic communication literature's style guide, in one number.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.31"}
**Budget conjunction.** A 1.5 °C carbon budget's uncertainty is dominated by terms of roughly equal weight (sensitivity, heat uptake, non-CO₂ forcing, carbon-cycle feedbacks). Treating the total error as normal, what is the probability that the true budget lies more than half a standard deviation below the quoted central value?
::: solution :::
P(Z > 0.5) = **0.31** — a one-in-three chance the honest budget is meaningfully smaller than the headline, from the convolution alone before any structural surprise; the exercise's content is that the quoted budget's confidence statement is a sum of physical and structural uncertainties, and any single term's error (a lower ECS, a stronger carbon feedback of the [[climate-modelling/earth-system-models]] lesson) moves it by a fifth — the SR1.5-critique arithmetic showing "the budget is not a constant of nature" is a calculation, not a slogan.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.5"}
**Scenario versus model spread at 2100.** Under SSP1-1.9 and SSP5-8.5, 2100 warming is 1.4 and 4.4 °C; model spread within each scenario is ±1.0 °C (1σ). What is the ratio of the scenario half-distance to the model spread?
::: solution :::
Half-distance (4.4 − 1.4)/2 = 1.5; ratio = 1.5/1.0 = **1.5** — at 2100 for global temperature the lane choice outweighs the model choice by a factor of 1.5 in this configuration (and by far more in variance terms, (1.5/1.0)² = 2.25), the Hawkins–Sutton late-century regime stated as a ratio — and the exercise's audit habit: recompute such ratios from the figure's own numbers, because the published fan charts' widths are hand-widened by evidence-line judgement (the AR6 practice) and the raw model spread is narrower than the honest range.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="2.25"}
**Variance ratio.** For the configuration of the previous problem, what is the ratio of scenario variance to model variance at 2100?
::: solution :::
(1.5/1.0)² = **2.25** — scenario variance more than twice model variance at the century's end for global temperature, the number that ends two arguments at once (the "models disagree so projections are useless" argument, and the "scenarios don't matter because models are uncertain" argument), each of which is wrong in the wrong decade and the wrong variable — the four-label decomposition, done.
:::
:::

::: warning Four ensemble fallacies
1. **"The ensemble spread is the uncertainty."** It is the uncertainty the ensemble can see: shared tunings, missing structural families, absent processes (ice-sheet cliffs, abrupt permafrost, AMOC tails) and the unknown-unknowns all live outside the ribbon, and the 2010s–2020s calibration studies' verdict — coverage of the historical record is fine, total error is underestimated by factors of one to two for many variables — means ensemble percentiles are the floor of honest ranges, not the ranges; the assessments' hand-widening is a feature, not a fudge.
2. **"More members reduce uncertainty."** Members reduce the noise term at 1/√N and nothing else; the 2020s hundred-member era's own papers state the crossover — beyond a few dozen members, initial-condition sampling buys timing precision, not projection narrowing, and the compute is better spent on structural diversity, resolution or scenario coverage; the fallacy is budget-shaped as much as statistical (the funding proposals that promise "1,000 members" as if uncertainty were a sampling problem).
3. **"The scenario is a prediction."** SSP5-8.5 is not more likely because it is on the figure, and SSP1-1.9 is not a fantasy because it is at the edge; the lanes are designed policy variables with no probability attached (the 2023 realism fight's durable lesson), and the two symmetric errors — using the high lane as a baseline or deleting it as unrealistic — both launder a normative choice through a statistic, which is the one thing the scenario architecture was rebuilt twice to prevent.
4. **"The ensemble mean is the best estimate."** For global temperature across independent-ish families, serviceable; for anything with phase, pattern or thresholds — monsoons, diurnal cycles, ice-free dates, tipping elements — the mean of wrong-phased members is a climatology no member produces and a date no system implies, and the threshold-relevant object is the distribution's shape (bimodality, tails, first-passage times), which averaging destroys; the fan chart's centre line is the most-read and least-informative stroke on the figure.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
The ensemble era replaced the single projection with a designed sampling of the three accounts of uncertainty — internal variability (σ/√N, the timing distributions of the hundred-member generation, the risk multipliers of attribution), model uncertainty (parametric and, four-to-one in variance, structural; sampled by parliaments of models whose effective size the genealogy corrections cut to a handful), and scenario choice (the SRES storylines' policy-free design, the RCPs' forcing-target inversion that made 2 °C computable, the SSPs' narrative-cross-forcing matrix, and the 2023 SSP5-8.5 realism fight's settlement: keep the lane for bounding, label it risk-management, never baseline). The Hawkins–Sutton decomposition organises the whole: uncertainty has a calendar and a geography — noise owns the 2010s, models the mid-century, and human freedom the late century — so the ensemble's statistics must be read per variable, region, horizon and statistic, with the mean trusted only where errors are independent, the spread widened by evidence lines rather than read as probability, and the percentiles audited back to members and their definitions (the ice-free-Arctic date moving by years with "ice-free"). The fan chart is an argument about physics, computation and human choice in one image, and the literacy this lesson installs — the four-label decomposition, the effective-member count, the hidden percentile, the scenario-as-policy-variable — is the difference between using projections and being used by them.
:::
