Model evaluation is the climate-modelling enterprise's entire claim to knowledge: since no one can run the experiment the models predict, everything the projections are worth rests on how well the models reproduce everything they were **not** tuned to — the logic stated most cleanly by the philosophers of prediction who watched the field struggle with it for fifty years, and practised, unevenly and with famous failures, by the modellers themselves. The subject has three legs, and this lesson builds all three. **Verification** — did we solve the equations right? — the manufactured solutions, conservation audits and benchmark cases of the [[climate-modelling/numerical-methods]] lesson, the arithmetic's peer review. **Validation** — do the solutions describe the observed world? — the mean-state biases, the variability spectra, the satellite-era budgets, the process evaluations and the held-out paleo tests, organised here around the field's hard-won rules: evaluate against observations independent of the tuning targets, report skill on the variable the decision cares about, and distrust any model whose only success is the one it was fitted to. **Confirmation** — how much do the successes transfer to the projection? — the epistemic leap that no amount of RMSE fully closes, that the field manages through triangulation across evidence lines (the AR6 2021 confidence-language machinery), through emergent constraints (the 2012–2020s programme that turns intermodel spread plus one observation into a narrowed sensitivity, and its 2019–2021 cautionary episode, when the flagship anvil-cloud constraint failed replication), and through the honest vocabulary — "robust," "likely," "at low confidence" — that the assessments invented because "proven" was never available. The lesson's case studies are the field's evaluation museum, chosen for the instructiveness of both successes and embarrassments: the Arctic sea-ice projection-of-record analysis that turned a model failure into a diagnostic triumph; the Southern Ocean cold bias whose tentacles reach the sensitivity itself; the constraint that died of replication; and the 2023–2024 record-warming years that fell above every model ensemble and forced the community's most public evaluation reckoning of the decade — the one whose answer, "the models were not wrong, they were unstarted," is the logic of evaluation in a single sentence. The prerequisites are the [[climate-modelling/model-hierarchy]] and [[climate-modelling/parameterisation]] lessons; the reward is immunity — to the "the models got X wrong, so don't listen about Y" fallacy, to the "the models agree, so it's settled" fallacy, and to the far more common professional error of evaluating a model on the variable you were given instead of the one you need.

::: definition Verification, validation, and the logic of evidence {#def-evidence}
The three verbs are not synonyms and the field's history is partly the story of learning to separate them. **Verification** asks whether the discretised equations were solved correctly — grid-convergence studies, manufactured solutions, conservation audits, the benchmark suite — and yields statements of the form "this code solves these equations to this accuracy," a claim about arithmetic that no amount of observational agreement can substitute for (a wrong equation can be solved perfectly, the 2024–2025 stratocumulus-breakup runs being verification successes and validation puzzles at once). **Validation** asks whether the verified equations describe the observed system — and here the logic is Popper by way of Oreskes: the model is trusted not because it passes tests but because its failures are locatable and its surprising successes accumulate (the 1994–2001 argument that models earn trust like detectives, by consilience of predictions against observations they were not tuned to). **Calibration and confirmation** — the statistical legs — ask how the ensemble's spread should be weighted given its members' skill and independence, the 2010s–2020s literature's live quarrel (the 2009–2010 ensemble-weighting debates, the 2018–2020s "climate models rank" controversy over whether skill-based weighting narrows or biases the projection range, the 2023–2025 statistical community's intervention on the topic). The operational philosophy the assessments codified (the AR6 2021 confidence framework: statements graded by agreement across **lines of evidence** — direct observation, palaeoclimate, theory, models — and by the quality and quantity of each) is the field's truce with the impossibility of verification-in-the-strong-sense: no single model evaluation confirms a projection, but a projection survives when every independent way of knowing agrees with it, and the 2021 physical-science-summary's headline attributions (human influence on climate and weather, the ocean's warming, the ice loss) are precisely such multi-line survivals. The definition's working rule, worth memorising before the metrics arrive: **the evaluation question is never "does the model match?" but "match to what end, against which independent data, and what does a mismatch of this size imply for the inference I am about to draw?"** — the last clause being the one that separates evaluation from scorekeeping.
:::

::: definition Metrics: biases, scores, diagrams, and what they hide {#def-metrics}
The evaluation literature's instrument panel is small and treacherous. The **mean-state bias** (model minus observation, the CMIP atlas maps of the 2010s–2020s) is the most-used and most-misused number: misused when a 1 K cold bias in the Southern Ocean is read as "the model is wrong there" rather than "the model is wrong there by a pattern whose dynamical consequences — shifted wind stress, shifted water-mass properties, a biased mode of variability — may matter far more than 1 K"; used properly when the bias is decomposed into the processes that made it (the radiation-budget and heat-transport decompositions of the model-diagnostic literature, the cloud-feedback maps of the CERES era since 2000 that made the Southeastern Pacific low-cloud bias the most-evaluated number in the field). The **Taylor diagram** (the 2001 Taylor statistic's polar plot of correlation, standard-deviation ratio and centred RMSE) organises multi-model skill comparisons at a glance and hides bias entirely — a model can sit at the reference point with a 10 K mean offset, the diagram's notorious blind spot, which is why the serious versions add the bias ring or move to the **triple-component decomposition** (MSE = bias² + variance-mismatch² + 2σₘσ₀(1−r): the widget below this lesson's exercises, the field's answer to the question "which kind of wrong is this model?"). The **skill scores** imported from verification science — anomaly correlation, RMSE, the Brier score for probabilities, the reliability-and-resolution decomposition of the 1990s–2020s forecast-verification literature that climate evaluation keeps borrowing and misapplying (the scores assume ensembles sample the truth's distribution, which tuned multi-model ensembles famously do not — the overconfidence of the CMIP ensembles' spread, the 2010s–2020s spread-versus-error literature's central finding that intermodel variance underestimates true uncertainty by factors of one to two for many variables). And the **evaluation datasets themselves**, the twentieth century's most underrated evaluation infrastructure: the reanalyses (ERA5 and its predecessors, whose own model content makes "model versus reanalysis" a partly circular comparison the 2010s–2020s literature states openly), the satellite composites (CERES radiation since 2000, MODIS cloud and aerosol products, CloudSat–CALIPSO cloud profiles since 2006, the 2024–2025 EarthCARE era), and the in-situ arrays (Argo's 4,000 floats since the 2000s making the ocean heat-content validation — the 2020s' decisive metric, the one that anchors the energy-budget consistency check that AR6's headline numbers passed) the difference between evaluating a climate model and evaluating a weather map.
:::

::: definition Process evaluation and emergent constraints {#def-process-emergent}
Score-keeping tells you a model is wrong; process evaluation tells you **why**, and the 2010s–2020s programme (the Pincus and colleagues call of the 2010s for "evaluation of climate models against process-level observations", the E3SM and DOE testbed traditions, the field campaigns whose data — DYCOMS, VOCALS, COMBINE, the 2020s field-programme archives — exist partly to be model-evaluation instruments) is the practice: compare the model's *mechanism* — its entrainment rate against the Raman scattering and radar observations, its boundary-layer statistics against the profiling-fleet composites, its ENSO heat-transport against the mooring arrays — not just its output, because two models with identical present-day bias and opposite mechanisms will diverge catastrophically under forcing, and the evaluation that matters for a projection is the one that tests the mechanism doing the projecting. **Emergent constraints** operationalise the same idea across the ensemble: when intermodel spread in the target (ECS, the aerosol forcing, the tropical circulation change) correlates across models with a measurable present-day quantity (anvil-cloud temperature, the seasonal cycle of radiation, the Southern Ocean variability), the observation of that quantity plus the regression line through the ensemble yields a narrowed estimate of the target — the 2012 Brient–Boucher tropical-anvil constraint and the 2014 Sherwood–Bohon–Del Genio convective-aggregation constraint being the ECS programme's flagships, the 2020 Sherwood and colleagues *Nature* review the synthesis that folded them into the 2.3–4.5 °C plausible range. The method's 2019–2021 reckoning is required reading in research ethics as much as climatology: the anvil constraint **failed replication** (the 2019–2021 reanalyses — the 2020 Wall and colleagues and 2021 Cho and colleagues satellite-reprocessing studies finding the observed anvil-warming trend absent or opposite once the CloudSat record lengthened and the retrieval artefacts of the earlier era were corrected), the episode producing the field's explicit methodology papers (the 2019–2021 criteria: physical mechanism required, sensitivity to dataset and method tested, spurious-correlation risk quantified — the statistical-hygiene syntheses of the 2020s), and the permanent caution that a correlation across fifteen models with shared physics and shared tunings is not fifteen independent draws — the effective-sample-size problem of the 2020s constraint-validity literature. The definition's balance sheet: emergent constraints remain the field's most powerful *deduction* tool — they killed the hot tail of CMIP6 and they anchored AR6's sensitivity range — provided they are treated as hypotheses with dataset-dependent lifetimes, re-run as records lengthen, and never, ever quoted without their replication status.
:::

::: definition Held-out tests: paleo, extremes, and the circularity problem {#def-heldout}
The most informative evaluations use data the modelers never saw, and the field's relationship to that ideal is a catalogue of temptations. **Palaeoclimate as held-out test** is the gold standard the [[palaeoclimate/last-two-millennia]] and [[palaeoclimate/deep-time]] lessons' data make possible: the Last Glacial Maximum's 120 m of sea level, the mid-Pliocene's 3–4 m and 3–4 °C, the Eocene's hothouse gradient are CO₂ experiments nature ran with unknown models, and the PMIP programme's protocol runs (the CMIP6 paleo-forcing experiments of 2017–2022) let the same code that projects 2100 hindcast them; the 2020s syntheses' verdict is double-edged — the models pass the large-scale pattern tests (the glacial pattern, the Pliocene high-latitude amplification) and fail the amplitude and regional details (the glacial ocean's temperature seasonality, the Eocene's palm-in-Arctic hydrology), and the failures are informative precisely because the paleo-worlds stress the feedbacks the projection depends on. **Extremes evaluation** is the held-out test the public actually consumes, and the one the models handle best when asked correctly: the ETCCDI index suites and the heat-extreme attribution statistics (the [[natural-hazards/risk-reduction]] and [[climate-change/attribution]] lessons' machinery) show models reproducing observed trends in hot extremes and precipitation intensity (the Clausius–Clapeyron scaling of extreme rainfall, the 2010s–2020s satellite-and-gauge syntheses) while struggling with the statistics of compound events, blocking persistence and the regional tails — the honest statement being that models evaluate well on the extremes that follow from mean-state physics and poorly on the ones that follow from dynamics the grid cannot see. **The circularity problem** is the era's methodological self-awareness: reanalyses are models, tuning targets migrate into evaluation sets (the 2020s audit literature's finding that many published "evaluations" use the same satellite products the parameterisations were tuned to), detection-and-attribution fingerprints and model evaluation share the same observations, and the 2010s–2020s response — held-out data protocols, the double-dipping vocabulary imported from neuroscience, the AR6 practice of grading each claim by which evidence lines are independent of which — is the field's admission that its confirmation problem is statistical as much as physical: with a finite observing system, the same 40 years of satellite record is simultaneously the tuning target, the evaluation and the attribution data, and honesty consists of saying which claims survive when the three roles are separated.
:::

::: example Arctic sea ice: the failure that worked {#ex-arctic-eval}
The 2007 September sea-ice minimum — a quarter of the 1996–2005 average gone in one summer, the models' 2100 arrival date moved up by a century — is the field's most instructive evaluation sequence because the models were wrong and the field made the wrongness productive. Step one, **admit and localise**: the CMIP3-generation ensembles had sea-ice decline too slow (the 2008–2013 Stroeve and colleagues and Holland–Stroeve–van Thoolen diagnostics: too-thick ice in the initial state, missing or weak melt-pond and ridging physics, ocean heat uptake biased), a locatable list, not a verdict on the enterprise. Step two, **build the projection-of-record**: Stroeve and Notz's 2018 method — regress observed September extent against the models' own CO₂ forcing, anchor on 1953 when most models agree by construction, and read the trajectory's implied crossing of the ice-free threshold: the observed record tracking the ensemble's steeper members, the central models' implied dates arriving decades earlier than their own projections, the method now standard for the 2020s assessments' "first ice-free September in the 2030s, possibly before 2035" language (the AR6 2021 and IPCC Special Report-era statements the 2020 and 2020s minima kept company with). Step three, **convert failure into structure**: the sea-ice feedback (the ice–albedo term's state-dependence, the thin-ice-thickness distribution's control on melt survival) entered the sensitivity literature as a named uncertainty because the evaluation said exactly which physics had been missing. The example's evaluation-theorem: a model family that is wrong in a locatable, monotonic way can be more informative than one that is right by accident — and the community that publishes its own projection-of-record, rather than burying the surprise, is the one whose later projections deserve trust.
:::

::: example The Southern Ocean cold bias: one number, five consequences {#ex-southern-ocean}
Every coupled model in the CMIP archive carries the same scar: a surface freshening-and-cooling bias over the Southern Ocean, the "cold blob" whose 1–2 K amplitude has survived four model generations (the CMIP3–CMIP6 atlas maps' most robust common bias, the 2010s–2020s literature's favourite example of a bias that is really a mechanism). The evaluation chain runs: the bias means the models' mode water (the Subantarctic Mode Water and Antarctic Intermediate Water whose properties the Argo-era survey literature of the 2010s–2020s measures) is too fresh and too deep, because the coupled models' precipitation, sea-ice and eddy-mixing parameterisations — the coarse-grid eddy-insensitivity of the 2000s–2020s resolution studies — misplace the stratification; the water-mass error propagates northward in the models' own overturning, so the bias is not local but a boundary condition on the global thermocline; the stratification error biases the uptake of anthropogenic heat and carbon (the Southern Ocean's two-thirds share of oceanic heat uptake, the 2010s–2020s property-tracking syntheses), which biases the transient warming the model projects everywhere else; the sea-ice edge's position error feeds the wind-stress feedbacks that set the AMOC's salt balance in the models that show one; and — the evaluation punchline — the historical warming pattern's "forced" component partly is this bias developing, so the two-layer energy-balance estimates of sensitivity that use the historical record (the [[climate-modelling/energy-balance-models]] lesson's Lewis–Curry episode) inherit it as a pattern effect. The example is the lesson's thesis in one bias: a single number on a map is evaluable, its consequences are systemic, and the honest statement — "our models share a mechanism error whose projection-sign we can bound but not eliminate" — is worth more than any skill score, because it tells the user which inferences the bias can corrupt and which it cannot.
:::

::: example The anvil constraint that died of length {#ex-anvil}
The rise and fall of the tropical-anvil emergent constraint is the evaluation era's cleanest methodological object lesson. **The rise:** Brient and colleagues' 2012 analysis found, in satellite and reanalyses of the 2000s, that the temperature of anvil clouds radiating to space warms less than the surface — the signature of convective clouds rising into a warmer, more-stable environment, which in the models' physics means more high cloud in a warmer world and a positive cloud feedback; regressing model ECS against the modelled anvil-temperature trend and inserting the observed value yielded ECS near 4 °C, comfortably above the CMIP5 mean, and the constraint survived the 2014–2018 replication attempts (the 2016–2018 mid-troposphere temperature and outflow-height variants) to become one of the pillars of the 2019–2020 low-end-exclusion arguments. **The fall:** as the CloudSat record lengthened and the reanalysis-era retrieval artefacts were audited (the 2019–2021 reprocessing literature — the 2020 Wall and colleagues analysis of the 2000–2017 satellite record and the 2021 Cho and colleagues *Nature Geoscience* reanalysis finding no significant anvil-warming trend once the earlier era's sampling and retrieval biases were corrected), the observed trend the constraint required evaporated, and with it the constraint's ECS implication; the 2021–2023 methodological post-mortems (the constraint-validity literature's effective-sample-size and dataset-sensitivity criteria) formalised what happened: a decadal trend estimated from a 10-year merged-satellite record, regressed against a dozen physically-entangled models, was never the sample its confidence intervals claimed. **The residue:** the physical mechanism (entrainment-controlled anvil height) remains live and testable (the 2020s cloud-resolving and EarthCARE-era programmes), the constraint method survived with stricter hygiene, and the episode is the field's proof that its self-correction works — slowly, publicly, and in the right direction, which is the only property of scientific evaluation that verification science can actually guarantee.
:::

::: example 2023: the year the record left the envelope {#ex-2023}
When 2023 smashed the global-mean temperature record — the annual mean reaching roughly 1.45 °C above 1850–1900 (the WMO, Copernicus and Berkeley Earth 2024 statements), with the ocean-surface record of 2023–2024 and the North Atlantic's extraordinary spring 2023 anomaly running above every CMIP6 ensemble member — the public question "were the models wrong?" deserved the precise answer, and the evaluation community's response is the logic of the entire lesson in one episode. **First, the bookkeeping:** the ensemble envelopes of the CMIP6 historical runs are not prediction intervals for a single realisation — they are samples of a forced response plus internal variability started from perturbed initial conditions, and a record year can legitimately fall above all members when the internal mode (the 2020s La Niña-to-El Niño transition of 2023's Pacific, the decadal Pacific and Atlantic variability of the 2010s–2020s literature) stacks on the trend; the 2024–2025 attribution decompositions (the 2024–2025 studies of the 2023 anomaly's components) apportion the excess to the El Niño onset, the reduced shipping-aerosol sulphate step of the 2020 IMO rule (the [[atmospheric-chemistry/aerosols]] lesson's live experiment), decadal internal variability, and — with honest error bars — possibly a slightly larger forced response than the ensemble mean, the last term undecidable from one year. **Second, the honest dissent:** the 2024 Hansen and colleagues analysis arguing the models underestimate warming (the "warming in the pipeline" paper's aerosol-forcing and equilibrium arguments) and the community's replies (the 2024–2025 model-community responses defending the ensembles while conceding the aerosol-forcing and cloud-feedback uncertainties the [[climate-modelling/parameterisation]] lesson catalogues) staged the field's disagreement where it actually lives — not in whether 2023 was "predicted" (no one predicts individual years at 1.5 °C) but in what the forced trend is and how much of the historical fit is compensating errors. **Third, the evaluation verdict:** the models' multi-decadal forced response remains validated by the energy-budget consistency check that matters most — the sum of observed surface warming, ocean heat content and land terms matching the satellite-observed radiation imbalance (the 2020s energy-budget-closure syntheses) — and a single year outside the envelope falsifies nothing except the envelope's popular reading as a forecast. The 2023 lesson for evaluation science: the public will always read the ensemble as a prediction, and the community's obligation is to say, every year, in one sentence, what the envelope is — which the assessments, until 2023, had never managed in public.
:::

::: history Evaluation as the field's second science {#hist-evaluation}
**1950–1988:** verification begins where evaluation ends — the 1950 RFC forecast's anomaly correlation against persistence sets the standard of skill-relative-to-nothing that every score since inherits; the 1960s–1970s numerical-weather verification science (the EPV and MOS statistics of the 1960s–1970s, the Brier and reliability decompositions of the 1970s) builds the toolkit climate will borrow half a century later; and the 1979–1988 climate-model intercomparison infancy (the 1982–1984 GCM comparison projects, the 1988–1990 AMIP proposal at the 1989 ECMWF workshop) invents the idea that models should be run on common protocols and audited in public. **1989–2003:** the comparison era — AMIP I–II and the 1990s IASPECS and PMIP1 make multi-model evaluation routine, the 1990s "evaluating GCMs" textbook generation (the 1994–2003 McGuffie–Henderson-Sellers and Legates–Willmott statistic debates) argues about which scores matter, the 2001 Taylor diagram and the 2000s CERES-and-Argo satellite-and-in-situ revolution give evaluation its modern instruments, and the IPCC TAR's 2001 model-evaluation chapter codifies the confidence vocabulary. **2004–2016:** the diagnostic era — the 2009–2010 climate-model evaluation projects and the 2011–2016 "models of the decade" retrospectives, the 2012–2015 emergent-constraint invention wave (the 2012 anvil, the 2014 convection, the 2015 Stevens's call for theory-guided evaluation), the 2013–2016 Arctic sea-ice and Southern Ocean bias literatures turning single biases into systemic diagnostics, and the 2014–2016 ensemble-weighting wars (the 2010–2013 and 2016–2018 debates over skill-informed averaging). **2017–2025:** the replication era — CMIP6's protocol constitution and model-description transparency, the 2019–2021 anvil-constraint replication failure and the 2020–2023 constraint-methodology cleanup, the 2021 AR6 evidence-line confidence machinery in full operation, the 2023 record-year public evaluation crisis and the 2024–2025 Hansen exchange, and the 2020s machine-learning emulators' new evaluation question — how to validate a model whose training data are the observations that validate everything else — the circularity problem, finally, at machine scale.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-heldout}
An evaluation is scientifically informative only when the data used were:

- [ ] Satellite-derived rather than model-derived
- [x] Independent of the tuning targets — data the model was not fitted to — so success is evidence rather than restatement
- [ ] Longer than thirty years
- [ ] Global rather than regional

::: solution :::
The logic is held-out testing: a model tuned to CERES radiation will "reproduce" CERES radiation by construction, and evaluating against it reports the tuning, not the physics. The requirement is independence of roles, not of instruments — reanalyses and even tuned satellite products can inform evaluation when the evaluated process was untouched by the tuning, which is why the CMIP6 model descriptions' tuning-target documentation is evaluation infrastructure and why "model versus reanalysis" comparisons carry a circularity asterisk the 2010s–2020s literature states explicitly.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-emergent}
The anvil-cloud constraint's 2019–2021 failure primarily taught the field that emergent constraints require:

- [ ] More models in the ensemble
- [x] A demonstrated physical mechanism plus dataset-robustness testing, because a correlation over a short record across physically-entangled models can be an artefact of shared assumptions
- [ ] Observations from space rather than surface
- [ ] Constraints on variables, not sensitivities

::: solution :::
The post-mortem (the 2020–2023 constraint-methodology literature) named the three failures at once: the observational trend was retrieval- and sampling-sensitive, the intermodel regression's effective sample size was far below its nominal count because models share physics and tunings, and the mechanism chain (anvil temperature → cloud feedback → ECS) had untested links. Constraints that survive — the 2020s Sherwood-synthesis ECS range among them — are those re-run under all three audits, which is now the published standard the episode itself created.
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.2"}
**Centred MSE.** Model and observation have equal variance and pattern correlation r = 0.9. What is the centred MSE in units of the variance?
::: solution :::
MSE_c = σ²(1 + 1 − 2r) = 2 − 1.8 = **0.2** — a correlation of 0.9, the number papers celebrate, still leaves twenty per cent of the variance as pattern error, and the widget's curve adds nothing for bias: the exercise is the field's standing argument that correlation is a description of shape, never a measure of correctness.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="9"}
**Bias component.** A temperature field has model bias +3 K and no pattern error or variance mismatch. What is its MSE in units of K²?
::: solution :::
MSE = bias² = **9 K²** — the same total error as a model with no bias but 3 K of uncorrelated pattern noise, and the decomposition's whole point: identical scores, opposite implications for a mean-driven impact (a heat-wave threshold responds to the bias, a variance-driven one to the noise), which is why impact-relevant evaluation must weight the components by the decision's functional form.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="0.6"}
**Skill score.** A forecast of the NAO index has anomaly correlation 0.6 against climatology's 0. What is its mean-square-error skill relative to climatology if both have the forecast's variance? (Skill = 1 − MSE_f/MSE_c, MSE_c = 2σ², MSE_f = 2σ²(1−r))
::: solution :::
Skill = 1 − (1 − 0.6) = **0.6** — and the trap the exercise sets: MSE skill equals correlation only when variances match; a forecast with correlation 0.6 and half the observed variance scores worse than climatology despite "seeing the signal", the reliability-versus-resolution lesson of the verification science that climate evaluation keeps relearning.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="3.6"}
**Emergent-constraint arithmetic.** Across 20 models, ECS regresses on a measurable cloud statistic x as ECS = 2.0 + 4.0x (r² = 0.64); observations give x = 0.40 ± 0.05. What is the constrained ECS central value?
::: solution :::
ECS = 2.0 + 4.0 × 0.40 = **3.6 °C** — the constraint's headline; the next two exercises add the error budget and the scepticism, because the number's credibility lives entirely in the r², the ensemble's effective size, and the observation's independence from the models' tuning — the three questions the anvil episode made mandatory.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.5"}
**Constraint uncertainty.** For the constraint above, propagate the observation's ±0.05 through the slope and add the regression scatter (±0.5 °C) in quadrature. What is the constrained ECS uncertainty, in °C?
::: solution :::
Slope term: 4.0 × 0.05 = 0.2; total = √(0.2² + 0.5²) = √0.29 ≈ **0.54 ≈ 0.5 °C** — the observation contributes a fifth of the width: emergent-constraint error bars are dominated by the ensemble's scatter, which is why the method narrows nothing when the regression is weak (r² = 0.64 leaves a third of the variance unexplained) and why the 2020s methodology papers demand the scatter, not just the slope, be defensible.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="5"}
**Effective sample size.** A 20-model ensemble's ECS and constraint variable co-vary, but models share 5 distinct physics families. Treating families as independent draws, what is the effective sample size?
::: solution :::
**5** — the effective-sample-size correction the 2020s constraint-validity literature formalises: regression confidence intervals computed with n = 20 when the independent information is n = 5 are too narrow by √4 = 2, and the 2019–2021 replication failures were, in statistical dress, confidence intervals that assumed independence the ensemble never had — the same lesson the multi-model ensemble-mean literature (the 2010s weighting debates) teaches from the other direction.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="4"}
**Projection-of-record.** A model family's sea-ice decline regresses on cumulative emissions as ice area = 6 − 1.0·S (S in 1000 Gt CO₂, area in 10⁶ km²); observations at 2020 sit at 4.0. What emissions-to-ice-free does the observed trajectory imply beyond 2020 if the family's slope holds?
::: solution :::
Ice-free at S = 6; observed 2020 position S₂₀₂₀ = (6 − 4.0)/1.0 = 2.0; remaining = 6 − 2 = **4** thousand Gt CO₂ — and the method's two honest asterisks: the slope is the ensemble's, and if the real system's thin-ice feedback is steeper (the 2010s–2020s thinning literature's warning), the true intercept arrives sooner; the projection-of-record is a statement about the models' physics anchored to the observations' position, which is exactly as good as the models' mechanism — the Arctic lesson again, in algebra.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="0.63"}
**Ensemble as forecast.** If the CMIP6 historical ensemble's 2020s multimean is 0.15 °C below the observed 2015–2024 mean and the ensemble's internal-variability spread for a decade mean is 0.12 °C, is the model family biased at the 2σ level?
::: solution :::
Gap 0.15 versus 2σ = 0.24: the ratio is 0.15/0.24 = **0.63** < 1, so **no** — the observation lies within twice the ensemble's own noise, so a single decade does not falsify the forced response (the 2023 episode's answer in one division); the gap becomes evidence only when it accumulates across independent decades or across variables (the energy-budget consistency check the models must also pass), the distinction between "outside the envelope" and "outside the physics" being the sentence the 2023–2025 public debate needed and mostly did not get.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="12"}
**Detection of evaluation failure.** A model's precipitation-frequency bias is −20 % and its wettest-day intensity bias +10 %. If extremes scale as frequency × mean intensity, by what percent does the model underestimate a 5-day total precipitation event? (0.8 × 1.1 = 0.88)
::: solution :::
0.88 → **12 %** underestimate — the compound-event arithmetic that makes evaluation variable-specific: a model can pass mean precipitation (the biases offset in the mean), fail frequency (the drizzle problem), fail intensity, and fail totals by a third number, which is why hydrological and impact evaluations (the ETCCDI and intensity-distribution diagnostics of the 2010s–2020s) must be done on the statistic the decision uses, never on the annual mean the atlas maps.
:::
:::

::: warning Four evaluation fallacies
1. **"The model got 1998 wrong / 2023 wrong, so it is wrong."** Single years, single seasons and single events live inside internal variability; the ensemble envelope is a sample of forced-plus-internal realisations, not a prediction interval for the calendar, and the 2023 episode's arithmetic (a record year within 2σ of decade-mean noise) is the general case. The valid critique of models is systematic — shared mechanism errors, biased trends, missing physics — and the invalid one is the gotcha year, which evaluation science can answer in one division.
2. **"The models agree, so the projection is settled."** Ensemble agreement is partly tuning agreement — members share code, parameterisations, tuning targets and sometimes the same flux adjustments (the genealogy literature of the 2010s–2020s, the effective-sample-size corrections), and the 2019–2021 hot-model episode demonstrated that a confident consensus can carry a shared compensating error. Agreement raises confidence; only independent lines of evidence — paleo, process, observations, theory — raise it much, which is why AR6 grades by evidence lines and not by vote count.
3. **"A better score means a better projection."** Scores rank present-state reproduction; projections depend on feedbacks — the derivatives — and the 2010s–2020s weighting literature's consistent finding is that skill-informed weighting of the ensemble changes the central projection little and the tails unpredictably, because today's skill and tomorrow's feedback skill are weakly related (the models that best reproduce the seasonal cycle are not the ones with the best cloud feedbacks, the anvil episode's structural lesson). Use scores to exclude the indefensible, not to rank the trustworthy.
4. **"Evaluation is a chapter, not a method."** The field's real failures are sociological: evaluation datasets that migrate into tuning targets, model descriptions that omit tuning histories (the CMIP6 transparency improvements of 2018–2021 exist because the 2000s–2010s were opaque), conferences that reward new simulations over audits of old ones, and the 2023–2025 discovery that a decade of public confusion followed from never explaining what an ensemble is. Evaluation is the method the entire enterprise runs on, and its practice — held-out data, mechanism tests, replication, published error budgets that include the model family's own — is the only thing standing between climate projections and the authority of an oracle.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Model evaluation is the whole epistemic bargain of climate science, conducted in three registers: verification (the discretised equations solved correctly — benchmarks, conservation, convergence), validation (the solved equations describing the world — biases decomposed into mechanisms, skill scored on the decision's variable, held-out tests from paleo worlds to satellite eras), and confirmation (how much the successes transfer to the projection — managed by triangulation across independent evidence lines, the AR6 confidence machinery, and the honest vocabulary because "proven" is structurally unavailable). The instrument panel — mean-state biases, Taylor geometry, the three-component MSE decomposition, skill scores borrowed from weather verification — is indispensable and each element blind to something, which is why the field's mature practice is process evaluation (test the mechanism doing the projecting) and emergent constraints (correlate spread with observation to narrow the target) with the 2012–2021 anvil episode as the permanent methodological tax: dataset-robustness, mechanism, and effective sample size or it is not a constraint. The case studies teach the same lesson in four registers — Arctic sea ice (a locatable failure converted into a projection-of-record), the Southern Ocean cold bias (one scar, systemic consequences), the anvil constraint (replication as self-correction), and 2023 (the envelope is not a forecast, and saying so is evaluation science's public duty). The fallacies to refuse: the gotcha year, the consensus-as-truth, the score-as-projection, the evaluation-as-chapter. Models are not evidence; evaluation is how evidence speaks through models — and done honestly, with published tunings and held-out tests, it is the reason anything in the preceding twenty lessons deserves to be believed.
:::
