Global climate models answer global questions at the price of a hundred-kilometre grid, and impacts do not live on the grid: a flood plain, a wheat county, a city's heat-island night and a thunderstorm's training all happen at scales the driving model never sees, which is the scale problem this lesson's methods attack. The downscaling enterprise has run in two families since the 1990s — **statistical**, building the mapping from coarse predictor to fine predictand out of observations (regression and "perfect prog" lineages of the 1980s–1990s, weather generators, and the bias-correction machinery of quantile mapping that the 2000s–2010s made the workhorse of every impacts study), and **dynamical**, running a regional model inside the global one so the physics itself refines the scene (the 1990s–2000s limited-area generation — the UK's HadRM3, the European PRUDENCE ensemble of 2005, the CORDEX design of 2011–2015 with its standard domains and its 2020s 25-kilometre second phase) — plus a third family now arriving: **machine-learned downscaling**, whose super-resolution networks of 2016–2023 produce fields that look right and, the physical-consistency critiques of 2022–2025 warn, do not always behave right. The 2010s–2020s convection-permitting generation (the UK's CP4CIP experiment of 2016 and the 4-kilometre national projections of the late 2010s–2020s) delivered the field's proudest result — the doubling of hourly extreme-precipitation intensity that coarse models had been smoothing away — and its most humbling one: regional ensembles' spread is no smaller than the global models they are driven by, so downscaling sharpens the picture without resolving the argument, the "spread paradox" and the 2021 Douglas–Pendergrass critique questioning whether regional projections add value at all forming the decade's defining methods debate. This lesson is the typology, the machinery and the honest accounting: what each downscaling family assumes, what CORDEX's design does and does not buy, what bias correction can fix and what it launders, and how to tell — the question underneath every downscaled map in every adaptation plan — when more resolution is more information and when it is more confidence. The prerequisites are the [[climate-modelling/numerical-methods]] lesson's grid and parameterisation logic and the [[climate-modelling/ensembles-scenarios]] lesson's uncertainty bookkeeping; the reward is reading an impacts map's fine coloured squares with an exact sense of which information in them came from observations, which from physics, and which from hope.

::: definition The three families: dynamical, statistical, and the hybrids {#def-families}
**Dynamical downscaling** runs a regional climate model — the same primitive-equation machinery as the global model, refined grid, complex orography and surface, lateral boundaries fed by a coarser driver (the limited-area lineage from the 1980s' mesoscale models through the 1990s–2000s HadRM3, REMO, RCA and RegCM generation to CORDEX's standardised 50-kilometre and 12-kilometre tiers, the 2011 Giorgi–Gutowski design framework and the 2020s convection-permitting pilot domains) — so that gravity, convection and fronts are computed, not inferred, at the finer scale; its promise is mechanism, its costs are compute and boundary-dependence. **Statistical downscaling** learns or specifies the mapping from coarse state to fine response from the observational record: the 1980s–1990s "perfect prog" regressions of grid-box weather onto station climate, the synoptic-classification lineages, the 2000s–2010s weather generators (the STAR and CLIMGEN lines and their climate-change scenarios, the 2020s stochastic-extremes successors) that synthesise minute-by-minute series whose statistics match a perturbed observed climate, and — the family's mass product — **bias correction** (the quantile-mapping lineage of the 2000s–2010s, the 2013–2020s "bias correction does not fix the trend" literature and its 2020s multivariate and copula extensions) that reshapes a model's distribution to observations' and quietly assumes the mapping is stationary. **The hybrids and the third way** fill the space between: the 2000s–2020s pseudo-global- and regional-sensitivity methods (perturbing reanalysis for event attribution), the scale-change or Δ-method of impacts practice (apply the GCM's fractional change to the observed fine baseline — the cheapest downscale and the most-used), and the machine-learned super-resolution networks of 2016–2025 (the GAN and diffusion-model literature, the 2023–2025 "physically consistent deep-learning downscaling" papers) that learn the fine field from fine observations and are now, the exercises show, statistically brilliant and dynamically unproven. The definition's boundary, stated once because the literature blurs it constantly: **bias correction is not downscaling** — it corrects a coarse grid-box's distribution without adding a single metre of spatial information — and "downscaling" means adding resolution by physics, by observed fine-scale structure, or by an assumed statistical relationship, and which of the three a map used is the first question its users should ask and the last their methods sections usually answer.
:::

::: definition Statistical machinery: perfect prog, quantile mapping, weather generators {#def-statistical}
**Perfect-progression regressions** (the 1980s–1990s school, the 2000s–2020s MOS-descended practice) regress station predictands on coarse predictors — "February rainfall here equals 3.2 × NAO index − 1.1 × SLP gradient + noise" — and their failure mode is nonstationarity: the relationship is estimated in the historical climate and applied in a future one, and if the teleconnection structure shifts (the 2000s–2020s findings on weakening and relocating ENSO and monsoon linkages), the regression extrapolates off its domain with no warning; the 2010s–2020s best practice is predictor selection by physical process and out-of-sample validation in a warmed sub-period, not maximising historical R². **Quantile mapping** — the workhorse — sorts the model's historical distribution onto observations' and applies the same transfer to the future: it fixes means, variances and distributional shape by construction, and the 2010s–2020s critique literature (the "bias correction cannot create trends" line from the 2013 Teichler and 2014 Lange papers onward, the 2020s multivariate and copula corrections of the Cannon and colleagues lineage) established its limits precisely: it cannot repair a wrong circulation (a model that misses the mistral will misplace its wind farms after any monotone correction), cannot fix trends driven by wrong dynamics (a too-weak jet-w shift stays, rescaled), and its univariate form destroys inter-variable and inter-site dependencies unless explicitly rebuilt. **Weather generators** (the 2000s–2020s STAR/CLIMGEN/MIMES lines) discard the model's time series entirely, keeping only perturbed observed statistics, and generate synthetic decades at minute resolution for hydrology — the 2020s critique being that they inherit the GCM's mean and variance changes and nothing of its temporal structure (the 2010s–2020s "stationarity of persistence" findings on storm sequencing and multi-year drought clustering, the risk that matters most for reservoir design). **Deep-learning downscaling** (the 2016–2025 super-resolution literature: adversarial and diffusion networks trained on reanalysis-to-observation pairs, the 2023–2025 papers producing 4-kilometre daily fields from CMIP output at a millionth of an RCM's compute) delivers sharp statistics and, per the 2022–2025 physical-consistency audits (the mass-budget and circulation-violation findings, the "the network hallucinates the lee of mountains that were never in the training topography" generalisation tests), can violate conservation, extrapolate unpredictably under unseen forcing, and — the deepest issue — learn the historical fine-scale structure whose stationarity the whole exercise assumes; the 2020s synthesis (the exercises' arithmetic included) is that these networks are excellent emulators of the mappings they were trained on and unvalidated instruments for the one thing downscaling is for: a climate the training data never contained.
:::

::: definition Regional climate models: boundaries, driving, and the added-value question {#def-rcm}
A regional model's atmosphere is an open system: its lateral boundaries (the 1985 Davies relaxation scheme and its 2000s–2020s nesting-continuous descendants) are held by the driver, its interior is free to develop, and everything the method is for lives in that gap — mesoscale convective complexes over West Africa, orographic precipitation on the Andes' windward slope, Mediterranean sea-breeze convection, the Alpine and Himalayan snow-line — features the driver's 100-kilometre gridbox smears to a monotone blend. The 2000s–2020s **added-value literature** (the 2008–2020s "add value" debate's meta-assessments, the 2016–2023 CORDEX diagnostic frameworks) found it where physics needs resolution: extreme precipitation intensity and its diurnal cycle (the 2007–2020s Fischer and colleagues and Wehner lineage showing coarse models' systematic underestimation of daily extremes), wind and temperature patterns in complex terrain, and the 2016 CP4CIP result that anchors the field's confidence — at 4-kilometre, convection-permitting resolution (the 2016 Fowler and colleagues Nature paper on UK sub-daily extremes), hourly rainfall intensities double relative to the 12-kilometre parameterised-convection run, with the shift concentrated in summertime thunderstorm regimes the old grids cannot represent at all. The method's structural weaknesses are equally well-mapped: **boundary error propagation** (the 2010s–2020s studies of how much of a regional ensemble's spread is inherited from its driver — often most of it, the "RCM as fancy bias corrector of its driver" critique), **the single-driver problem** (a regional run inherits its parent's circulation biases and, worse, its parent's scenario and internal-variability lottery — the 2010s–2020s large-ensemble-driving designs of the 2020s, running regional models from dozens of global initial conditions to separate the two), and **the spread paradox** (the 2010s–2020s CORDEX finding that regional ensembles' inter-model spread is systematically smaller than the global ensembles' they are driven by — the regional models agree more with each other than their parents do, and the 2020s audit literature's verdict that the agreement partly reflects shared drivers and shared schemes, not converging truth). The 2021 **Douglas–Pendergrass critique** (the GMD paper and the June-2021 instrumentation debate it triggered, with the 2021–2023 rebuttals from the CORDEX community) sharpened the question to a decision rule: for many impacts questions — global-mean-scaled extremes, temperature over most regions — a bias-corrected global model does as well as a hundredfold-costlier regional run, and the regional model earns its compute precisely where the exercises and examples of this lesson locate it: convection-permitting extremes, wind and precipitation pattern in complex terrain, and process questions a statistic cannot answer.
:::

::: definition Honest accounting: validation, uncertainty and the decision test {#def-accounting}
Downscaling's evaluation inherits the [[climate-modelling/evaluation]] lesson's logic with a regional twist: the mean-state biases are larger than the global models' (regional models' notorious double-ITCZ inheritances, the 2000s–2020s precipitation-shift biases over Africa and monsoon Asia, the European summer dry-bias argument that the 2019–2023 heatwave literature keeps re-opening), the extremes checks must be resolution-aware (a 12-kilometre model's daily precipitation extremes are not comparable to a 4-kilometre's without the 2010s–2020s sub-daily disaggregation corrections), and the validation data are themselves sparse exactly where downscaling matters (the 2020s finding that Africa's and High-Asia's station and radar coverage cannot support the validation the adaptation plans assume — the data-density gradient is the field's quietest structural inequality). The uncertainty bookkeeping of the 2010s–2020s (the Hawkins–Sutton descendants extended to regional scale, the 2020s variance partitions for CORDEX ensembles) adds two terms the global decomposition lacks: **regional model spread** (smaller than global spread, per the paradox, and therefore not a wider honest range but a narrower one whose coverage must be checked against the global parents and the observations jointly) and **method spread** — the 2010s–2020s "method matters as much as model" findings, that the choice among bias-correction schemes, weather generators and downscaling families contributes spread comparable to the model choice itself for precipitation extremes and hydrological impacts (the 2020s multi-method intercomparisons' uncomfortable result, that two analysts can produce legitimately different 2050s flood statistics from the same global model by choosing different pipelines). The decision-centric evaluation literature of the 2010s–2020s (the Dessai–Brown–Lempert lineage of the [[climate-change/impacts]] lesson applied to methods) supplies the final test: downscaling is justified not by resolution per kilometre but by **decision value** — does the finer information change a decision (the drainage-design threshold that the coarse model's percentile misses, the wind-farm yield that the corrected global model mis-sits by a capacity-factor's worth), and if not, the cheapest honest method wins, which is the professional answer to the 2021 compute-waste critique and, equally, the audit question every adaptation plan's methods section should survive: what would the coarse method have decided, and was it the same?
:::

::: example CP4CIP: what 4 kilometres found in Britain's summer rain {#ex-cp4cip}
The 2016 UKCP18 convection-permitting experiment — the 4.5-kilometre HadUK model run over the UK for present and future summers, the 2016 Fowler and colleagues Nature paper its field-defining result — is downscaling's cleanest natural experiment because the comparison is the same model, same boundaries, same physics, only the grid: at 12 kilometres, cumulus parameterisation handles convection and produces the familiar coarse-model signature (too-frequent light rain, under-intense heavy rain, a diurnal cycle peaked hours early); at 4.5 kilometres, convection is explicit, and the summer-rainfall distribution reorganises — hourly intensities at the sub-daily extremes rise by tens of per cent to a doubling relative to the parameterised run, the false drizzle disappears, and the diurnal peak snaps to the observed evening. The 2016–2025 follow-on literature (the 2019–2023 flash-lead-time and surface-water-flood applications, the 2022–2024 thunderstorm-nowcasting crossovers, the European 4-kilometre sister runs of the 2020s CORDEX pilots) generalised the result across convective regimes and, importantly, bounded it: the intensity shift is largest for short-duration warm-season extremes and small for winter frontal systems the coarse model already handled, which is the decision-test's own arithmetic — a winter-mains-designer needs nothing new, a city-drainage engineer needs everything. The experiment's policy tail (the UK's 2021–2022 surface-water flood planning, the 2023–2025 drainage-standard revisions citing the sub-daily scaling) is the field's proof that downscaling changes not just maps but culverts — and its caution that the parameterised-convention generation's extreme statistics, embedded in a generation of adaptation guidance, were systematically low for exactly the events adaptation was for.
:::

::: example The 2021 compute-waste fight: does the regional model earn its run? {#ex-compute-fight}
In June 2021 a GMD paper by Douglas, Pendergrass and colleagues asked the field's budget question in the blunt form it had avoided: for projected changes in US temperature and in many precipitation and heat metrics, do regional models' answers differ enough from bias-corrected global models to justify one to two orders of magnitude more compute — and for a substantial share of variables, the answer came back no, with the regional models' added spatial detail largely absent from the projected-signal statistics that impacts studies actually consume (the finding's press moment, the E&E News coverage, and the CORDEX community's 2021–2023 rebuttals — the Fischer–Coppola-led responses, the added-value meta-evaluations, the convection-permitting defence — constituting the decade's most useful methods argument). The reconciliation the literature reached by 2024 is worth more than either side's headline: the critique is right for **large-scale temperature and mean-signal questions** (where the regional model is, as the critics said, an expensive bias-corrector of its driver), wrong for **convective extremes** (the CP4CIP class of results, where the coarse model's answer is not merely coarser but wrong), and under-specified for **pattern questions** (wind resources, wildfire weather, species-climate relationships) where the value depends on whether the decision uses the pattern at all — the decision test of the definition, applied to the field itself. The fight's durable products are methodological: the 2020s practice of running regional ensembles from many drivers and initial conditions (the large-ensemble-driven designs separating inherited from generated uncertainty), the explicit cost-benefit framing in CORDEX-Plus-era proposals, and the quiet reclassification of the 12-kilometre single-driver single-scenario regional runs of the 2000s–2010s literature — thousands of papers whose design the argument revealed as uninterpretable for the questions they claimed to answer, the most expensive lesson in this lesson.
:::

::: example Deep learning downscaling: brilliance and the hallucinated mountain {#ex-ml}
The 2016–2025 machine-learning downscaling literature — from the 2016 adversarial super-resolution of precipitation fields through the 2020s diffusion-model generators to the 2023–2025 papers producing continental daily fields at kilometre resolution for a laptop-hour of inference — is the field's most spectacular and most audited method, and the audits (the 2022–2025 physical-consistency lineages) name exactly what breaks. What works: the networks reproduce observed fine-scale statistics — spatial spectra, extreme-value distributions, the terrain's imprint on precipitation — better than the RCMs at a fraction of the compute, and the 2023–2025 "big data for downscaling" programmes (the DOE and national-meteorological-service efforts) now train on reanalysis archives the size of the CMIP multi-millennium product. What breaks, in the audits' order: **conservation violations** (the downscaled water budgets do not close — generated precipitation fields that mass-balance against neither the driver's moisture flux nor the terrain's rain shadows when pushed off-distribution), **extrapolation failure** (networks trained on the historical climate degrade non-uniformly under high-CO₂ fields they have never seen, with the 2023–2025 idealised-perturbation tests showing errors growing faster than the coarse model's own), and the **stationarity inheritance** (the network learns the historical predictor–predictand mapping — the very assumption the 2010s statistical-downscaling critique spent a decade documenting as false for teleconnections — at higher capacity and with more convincing error bars). The 2024–2025 synthesis, which the exercises' arithmetic reconstructs: deep-learning downscaling is best understood as a learned emulator of the fine-scale mapping, excellent where the future resembles the training climate, unvalidated where it does not, and most defensible in hybrid service — as a post-processor of RCM output (emulating the 4-kilometre field from the 12-kilometre one, the 2023–2025 "learned downscaling of dynamical output" papers) rather than as a replacement for physics in exactly the regime, warmed and unprecedented, where both the statistics and the mapping are least trustworthy.
:::

::: example The Δ-method and the Himalayan hydropower question {#ex-delta}
The cheapest downscale — the scale-change or Δ-method, applying a global model's fractional change to the observed fine record — is also the most-used in impacts practice, and the 2020s Himalayan and Andean hydropower and irrigation studies show both its honest strength and its silent assumptions. The arithmetic (which the exercises make you do): a basin's observed monthly runoff series, the driving ensemble's projected precipitation change and temperature-driven snowline shift, a degree-day or simple snow model, and the design statistics fall out at station resolution with no RCM run and no bias-correction pipeline — for a mid-century reservoir sizing, often the decision-relevant answer within its uncertainty, and the 2020s intercomparisons (the multi-method Himalayan hydrology projects) confirm the Δ-method's flows sit inside the RCM-driven pipelines' spread for annual and seasonal totals. The silent assumptions are where the region's physics bites: the method assumes the change is a smooth multiplier on an unchanged variability structure, and the Himalayan 2020s literature's warnings are precisely about structure — the timing shift of snowmelt (the spring-peak advance that changes irrigation calendars more than annual totals), the glacial-lake and monsoon-convective extremes the multiplier cannot express, and the elevation-dependence of the change (warming and precipitation shifts that vary by a kilometre of altitude, which a basin-mean Δ flattens). The professional resolution the 2020s water-sector practice reached is tiered: Δ-method for screening and sensitivity, RCM or convection-permitting output where the decision is threshold- and structure-sensitive (the 2022–2025 high-resolution Himalayan runs for probable-maximum-flood revision at large dams), and learned downscaling entering the middle tier as its audits mature — the decision test of the definition operating as a procurement ladder, with every tier's assumptions written down, which is the one requirement all three families share and none of their users' summaries honour often enough.
:::

::: history From perfect prog to convection-permitting {#hist-downscaling}
**1980s–1990s:** the statistical school is first — the 1980s–1990s perfect-prog and synoptic-classification lineages (the Helmholtz and Irish schools' later 2000s–2010s retrospectives date the modern field to the 1990s), the 1996–2000s weather-generator maturation, and the 1990s limited-area models (the 1995–2000 HadRM2–HadRM3 generation driving national scenario studies) establishing dynamical downscaling as the continental-projection workhorse. **2000–2010:** the ensemble and standardisation era — the 2001–2005 PRUDENCE European regional ensemble (the first multi-model regional intercomparison and its spread-versus-driver findings), the 2004–2010 quantile-mapping uptake as the impacts pipeline's default (and the 2008–2010s early critiques of its trend-blindness), and the 2007–2010s first convection-permitting climate runs (the 2006–2009 UK and Swiss 4-kilometre feasibility experiments). **2011–2019:** CORDEX (the 2011 Giorgi–Gutowski framework paper, the 2012–2020s coordinated domains and the 50- and 12-kilometre tiers) makes regional downscaling a community experiment; the 2013–2016 bias-correction-critique literature (the Teichler and Lange papers and successors) draws the method's boundaries; the 2016 CP4CIP and Fowler-generation results land the convection-permitting doubling; and the 2016–2019 national convection-permitting programmes (the UKCP18 2019 release, the Swiss and German 4-kilometre operations) move the method from experiment to service. **2020–2025:** the audit-and-decision era — the 2021 Douglas–Pendergrass compute-waste critique and the 2021–2023 rebuttals, the 2020s large-ensemble-driven regional designs and CORDEX-Plus/flyweight strategies, the 2022–2025 physical-consistency audits of deep-learning downscaling and its hybrid-service turn, and the 2023–2025 decision-value framing becoming standard in adaptation-programme methods sections — the field's question matured from "how fine can we make the grid?" to "what does the fineness change, for whom, and at what compute cost?", which is the question a science of consequences should have been asking from the start.
:::

## Check Your Understanding

::: quiz {#quiz-spread-paradox}
The "spread paradox" of regional climate ensembles is that:
- [ ] Regional models disagree more with each other than global models do
- [x] Regional ensembles agree more closely with each other than their driving global ensembles do, partly because they share drivers and schemes — so their narrower range is not automatically a more honest uncertainty
- [ ] Regional models have no spread at all
- [ ] Bias correction eliminates regional spread

::: solution :::
The CORDEX-era finding is that regional-ensemble spread is systematically smaller than the global ensembles' that feed it — partly genuine physics converging, partly shared parentage (drivers, scenarios, inherited internal-variability states) and shared schemes inflating agreement. The audit consequence: a downscaled range's credibility must be checked against the parents' spread and observations jointly, never taken as a tighter interval earned by resolution — the 2021 compute-waste critique's statistical backbone.
:::
:::

::: quiz {#quiz-qm}
Quantile mapping of a regional model's precipitation to observations cannot fix:
- [ ] The model's mean bias
- [ ] The model's variance bias
- [x] A wrongly simulated circulation — a missing or misplaced wind system stays wrong after any monotone rescaling
- [ ] The model's extreme-value distribution

::: solution :::
Quantile mapping fixes the distribution — mean, variance, shape, extremes — by construction, which is its appeal and its deception: it operates on grid-box marginals, not on the flow. A model that puts the mistral's track 200 km south, misses the orographic rain shadow, or gets the monsoon's phase wrong produces a corrected distribution of the wrong weather, and the 2010s–2020s trend- and circulation-blindness critiques (the "bias correction cannot create trends" literature) made checking the dynamics before the correction standard practice — the exercise-level version of the decision test.
:::

## Practice Problems

::: exercise {level=1 check="625"}
**Grid arithmetic.** How many 4-km model cells fit inside one 100-km global-model grid box?
::: solution :::
(100/4)² = 25² = **625** — the cell count the 4-km generation buys per parent gridbox, and the compute bill scaled by the cube of the refinement (625 cells at a quarter the time-step: the 2021 compute-waste critique's denominator in one number), which is why the field runs 4 km over a nation, not the globe, and why the learned-downscaling emulators' million-fold inference speedup is worth auditing.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="1.4"}
**Scale-change method.** Observed mean basin runoff is 500 mm/yr; the driving ensemble projects a +14 % precipitation change by the 2050s with runoff responding proportionally. What is the downscaled 2050s runoff, in mm/yr?
::: solution :::
500 × 1.14 = **570** — the Δ-method's entire arithmetic, and its entire epistemology: correct if the change is a uniform multiplier, silent about the snowmelt-timing shift, the elevation dependence and the changed variability that the Himalayan literature says matter most — the exercise is the method's strength and its critique in one multiplication.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=1 check="2"}
**Quantile mapping.** A model's historical 95th-percentile daily precipitation is 40 mm; observations put it at 80 mm. Under linear quantile mapping, what does a future model value of 60 mm become?
::: solution :::
The transfer doubles the model's quantiles: 60 × (80/40) = **120** — the multiplicative form's behaviour that makes it popular for precipitation and dangerous for trends: the correction's gain multiplies the model's future change too, so a model with a too-weak extreme trend stays too weak (rescaled), and one with a spurious trend keeps it amplified — the 2010s critique's arithmetic, checkable in one line.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0.5"}
**Inherited spread.** A 12-member regional ensemble driven by a single global model and initial condition shows variance 0.36 for its 2050s monsoon-rainfall projection, of which structural (regional-model) variance is 0.18. What fraction of the variance is inherited from the driver?
::: solution :::
(0.36 − 0.18)/0.36 = **0.5** — half the spread is the parent's, which the parent's own ensemble already spans: single-driver regional projections sample the regional-model question at fixed global answer, and the 2020s large-ensemble-driving designs exist to unbundle the two — the exercise is the spread paradox's mechanism in one subtraction.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="7.5"}
**Weather generators.** A synthetic-decade generator matches the observed daily-mean-variance and the observed lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.6. What is the effective number of independent days in a 30-day spell?
::: solution :::
n_eff = n(1−ρ)/(1+ρ) = 30 × 0.4/1.6 = **7.5** — the persistence arithmetic the 2010s–2020s sequencing critiques target: matching lag-1 does not match the clustering of compound events (the 2020s finding that generators under-represent multi-week drought runs and storm sequences because the observed clustering is non-linear and regime-driven), and reservoir design lives or dies on exactly the tail the generator smooths.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=2 check="0"}
**Perfect prog off-domain.** A 1980s regression of station rainfall on two predictors explains 65 % of historical variance. If the future climate moves one predictor outside its historical range, what is the maximum defensible R² for the extrapolation?
::: solution :::
**0** — outside the training domain the regression is an untested linear continuation, and the honest defensible extrapolation R² is unquantified, not 0.65; the exercise's point is the 2000s–2020s nonstationarity literature's rule (predictors chosen by process, validated on warmed sub-periods, extrapolation flagged), and the same rule now governs the deep-learning networks that are its high-capacity descendants — the 2023–2025 audit papers' perturbation tests are this exercise at industrial scale.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.4"}
**Decision test.** A 4-km run raises the 50-year hourly rainfall intensity used for urban drainage from 80 to 112 mm/hr. By what factor does the design intensity change?
::: solution :::
112/80 = **1.4** — a 40 % jump in a design standard is the definition of decision value (the CP4CIP-class result's policy tail: drainage norms revised, culverts resized, the parameterised-convention generation's guidance revealed systematically low for exactly the events adaptation was for), and the exercise inverts the 2021 compute question correctly: the regional run earns its compute when and where its percentiles move the design table, which for winter frontal rain they mostly do not and for summer convection, demonstrably, they do.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="1.41"}
**Method spread.** Three pipelines (Δ-method, bias-corrected RCM, learned downscaling) project 2050s flood-peak changes of +8, +14 and +24 %. If method choice and model choice contribute equal variance, what does the method spread imply about the total projection uncertainty relative to model-only spread?
::: solution :::
Equal-variance addition doubles the variance, so total standard deviation grows by √2 ≈ **1.41** — the 2020s multi-method intercomparisons' uncomfortable result in one factor: the analyst's pipeline is an uncertainty source comparable to the model, which is why the mature adaptation assessments of the 2020s run the ensemble across methods as well as models, and why a single-pipeline impacts study's error bars are, structurally, half the story.
:::
:::

::: exercise {level=3 check="10"}
**Compute economics.** A convection-permitting regional run costs 10⁶ core-hours; a learned emulator costs 1 core-hour to emulate it with 90 %-of-signal fidelity for temperature but unvalidated fidelity for untested extremes. For 20 regional scenarios under budget 10⁷ core-hours, how many full RCM runs can you afford and what does the emulator let you sample instead?
::: solution :::
10⁷/10⁶ = **10** full runs — the 2020s hybrid design the arithmetic forces: RCMs where the physics is the question (convective extremes, complex terrain, process diagnostics), emulators for the large sampling the RCM budget forbids (driver-ensemble sweeps, scenario matrices, uncertainty partitions), and the audit obligation the emulators carry: fidelity demonstrated on the variable and the regime, not on temperature alone — the 2021 critique and the 2023–2025 consistency audits, reconciled as a portfolio.
:::
:::

::: warning Four downscaling fallacies
1. **"Higher resolution means better projections."** Resolution buys information where physics needs it — convection, terrain, surface contrast — and for large-scale temperature signals the 2021 critique demonstrated the regional model is often its driver's expensive echo; the fallacy's tell is a methods section that never states what the finer grid changed, because usually the honest answer (for this variable, nothing) would have saved the run, which makes the decision test — would the coarse method have decided the same thing? — the first audit of every downscaled product, not the last.
2. **"Bias correction makes the model unbiased."** Quantile mapping repairs grid-box distributions and nothing else: the wrong circulation, the wrong trend mechanism and the wrong inter-variable structure survive every monotone transfer, and the 2010s–2020s literature's saddest finding is how often the corrected series' error bars — computed from the corrected spread — are narrower than the uncorrected model's, the correction laundering uncertainty it never touched; check whether the pipeline's authors validated the dynamics before the distribution, and assume they did not until shown.
3. **"The regional ensemble's tighter range is tighter truth."** The spread paradox's shared parentage — common drivers, common scenarios, inherited variability states, shared schemes — means agreement among downscaled projections is partly the agreement of their parents restated, and the 2020s audits' rule follows: a downscaled range is credible only when spanned jointly by the parents' spread, the method spread and the observations, never when it merely excludes them; adaptation plans built inside a single-driver single-method ribbon have bought the narrowest and least defensible version of the future.
4. **"A learned downscaler that matches observations is validated."** It matches the observed climate — the training distribution — and downscaling exists for the unobserved one; the 2022–2025 physical-consistency audits (open water budgets, hallucinated terrain effects, error growth under unseen forcing) are the modern form of the perfect-prog lesson sixty years old: a statistical mapping's historical skill is a licence to extrapolate only under demonstrated stationarity, which no network and no regression has yet demonstrated for a doubled-CO₂ atmosphere, and the hybrid service (emulating dynamical output, accelerating pipelines) is where the method's honesty currently ends and its value begins.
:::

## Summary

::: summary
Downscaling answers the scale mismatch between models and consequences with three families and one audit. Dynamical downscaling — the limited-area lineage through PRUDENCE and CORDEX to the convection-permitting 4-kilometre generation — computes the fine physics and earned its compute where convection and terrain own the answer: the CP4CIP doubling of hourly extremes, the diurnal-cycle corrections, the revised drainage standards; its weaknesses are inherited (driver bias, the spread paradox's false agreement, boundary error) and its 2021 Douglas–Pendergrass critique landed where the method had over-promised, on large-scale signals a bias-corrected global model supplies nearly as well. Statistical downscaling — perfect prog, quantile mapping, weather generators, and their deep-learning descendants — is cheap, ubiquitous and bounded by stationarity: it fixes distributions without touching circulation, matches statistics without matching sequences, and learns mappings whose validity in an unprecedented climate is assumed, never shown. The Δ-method screens, the RCM decides threshold questions, the emulator samples what the budget forbids, and the decision test arbitrates: finer information is worth its cost exactly when it moves the decision, which for summer convection it does and for winter means it does not. The honest pipeline reports which family made which map, validates dynamics before distributions, spans method spread alongside model spread, and remembers that every downscaled square on every adaptation map carries whatever its family assumed — physics, statistics, or hope.
:::
