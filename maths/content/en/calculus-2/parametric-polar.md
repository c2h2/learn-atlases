Paint a dot on the rim of a bicycle wheel and ride in a straight line. The dot traces a curve of arches touching the road, the **cycloid**. It is not the graph of a function $y = f(x)$ in any convenient sense, and its Cartesian equation is a mess. But it is easy to describe *in time*: after the wheel of radius $1$ has turned through an angle $t$, its centre is at $(t, 1)$ and the dot is at

$$
x = t - \sin t, \qquad y = 1 - \cos t .
$$

Describing a curve by giving both coordinates as functions of a third variable is the **parametric** point of view. It is the natural language of motion — planets, projectiles, robot arms, the pen of a plotter — and it handles curves that loop, cross themselves or turn back, which graphs of functions cannot do.

A second alternative to $y = f(x)$ is to describe a point by its *distance* $r$ from the origin and its *direction* $\theta$. These **polar coordinates** turn circles centred at the origin into $r = \text{const}$, spirals into $r = \theta$, and the orbit of a planet into $r = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}$. In this chapter we develop calculus for both descriptions: tangent lines, arc length and area.

## Parametric curves

::: definition Parametric curve {#def-parametric}
A **parametric curve** in the plane is a pair of continuous functions $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$ defined on an interval $I$. The variable $t$ is the **parameter**, the equations $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$ are a **parametrisation**, and the set of points $\set{(f(t), g(t)) : t \in I}$ is the **trace** of the curve. As $t$ increases the point moves along the trace in a definite direction, the **orientation**.
:::

The same set of points can be traced in many ways. The unit circle is traced anticlockwise once by $(\cos t, \sin t)$ for $0 \le t \le 2\pi$, clockwise by $(\cos t, -\sin t)$, and twice by $(\cos 2t, \sin 2t)$ for $0 \le t \le 2\pi$. Some standard parametrisations:

- **Line** through the point $(x_0, y_0)$ with direction $(a, b)$: $x = x_0 + at$, $y = y_0 + bt$, $t \in \R$.
- **Circle** of radius $r$ centred at $(h, k)$: $x = h + r\cos t$, $y = k + r\sin t$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$.
- **Ellipse** $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$: $x = a\cos t$, $y = b\sin t$.
- **Graph** of $y = F(x)$: $x = t$, $y = F(t)$. Every graph is a parametric curve, but not conversely.
- **Cycloid** generated by a wheel of radius $r$: $x = r(t - \sin t)$, $y = r(1 - \cos t)$.

To see what curve a parametrisation describes, we can try to **eliminate the parameter** and find an equation relating $x$ and $y$ directly.

::: example Eliminating the parameter {#ex-eliminate}
Describe the curves (a) $x = 2\cos t$, $y = 3\sin t$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$, and (b) $x = t^2$, $y = t^3$, $t \in \R$.
::: solution
(a) Since $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$, we have $\left(\frac x2\right)^2 + \left(\frac y3\right)^2 = 1$: an ellipse with semi-axes $2$ (horizontal) and $3$ (vertical). At $t = 0$ the point is $(2, 0)$ and at $t = \frac\pi2$ it is $(0, 3)$, so the ellipse is traced once, anticlockwise.

(b) From $x = t^2$ we get $x \ge 0$, and $y^2 = t^6 = x^3$. So the trace lies on the curve $y^2 = x^3$, and conversely every point of that curve with $x \ge 0$ is reached (take $t = y^{1/3}$). This **semicubical parabola** has a sharp point, a **cusp**, at the origin, although $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are polynomials: at $t = 0$ the velocity $(2t, 3t^2)$ is zero, so the moving point stops and turns around.
:::
:::

::: warning Eliminating the parameter can add points
The equation obtained by eliminating $t$ may describe more than the trace. The curve $x = \sin^2 t$, $y = \sin^2 t$ satisfies $y = x$, but its trace is only the segment from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$, traversed back and forth, because $0 \le \sin^2 t \le 1$. Always record the range of $x$ and $y$ (and the direction of motion), not just the equation.
:::

## Tangent lines

If a parametric curve passes through a point with non-zero horizontal velocity, then near that point it is the graph of a function, and its slope is the ratio of the two velocities.

::: theorem Slope of a parametric curve {#thm-param-slope}
Let $f$ and $g$ be continuously differentiable near $t_0$, with $f'(t_0) \ne 0$. Then near $t_0$ the curve $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$ is the graph of a differentiable function $y = Y(x)$, and at $x_0 = f(t_0)$

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} = \frac{g'(t_0)}{f'(t_0)} .
$$ {#eq-param-slope}
:::

::: proof
Since $f'$ is continuous and $f'(t_0) \ne 0$, $f'$ has constant sign on some interval $J$ around $t_0$, so $f$ is strictly monotone on $J$ and has an inverse function $f^{-1}$ on the interval $f(J)$, differentiable with $(f^{-1})'(x) = 1/f'(f^{-1}(x))$ (the inverse function rule from [[calculus-1/chain-rule]]). On $J$ the curve is the graph of $Y = g\circ f^{-1}$, and by the chain rule $Y'(x_0) = g'(t_0)\cdot\frac{1}{f'(t_0)}$.
:::

Formula [[#eq-param-slope]] is easy to remember in Leibniz notation, where the $dt$'s "cancel". It gives:

- **horizontal tangents** where $\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$ and $\frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0$;
- **vertical tangents** where $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} \ne 0$ (the roles of $x$ and $y$ swap, and $x$ is locally a function of $y$ with $\frac{dx}{dy} = 0$);
- possible **singular points** (cusps, corners, stops) where both derivatives vanish; these need individual study.

For the second derivative, apply the same rule to the function $\frac{dy}{dx}$, regarded as a function of $t$:

$$
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt} .
$$ {#eq-param-second}

::: warning The second derivative is not a ratio of second derivatives
It is tempting to write $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}$. This is wrong: for the line $x = t^2$, $y = t^2$ ($t > 0$) it would give $\frac{2}{2} = 1$, while the true second derivative of $y = x$ is $0$. Use [[#eq-param-second]]: here $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2t}{2t} = 1$, whose $t$-derivative is $0$.
:::

::: example The tangent to a cycloid {#ex-cycloid-tangent}
For the cycloid $x = t - \sin t$, $y = 1 - \cos t$, find the tangent line at $t = \frac\pi2$, show that every arch is concave down, and find the slope near the cusps.
::: solution
We have $\frac{dx}{dt} = 1 - \cos t$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} = \sin t$, so for $t \ne 2k\pi$

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\sin t}{1 - \cos t}.
$$

At $t = \frac\pi2$ the point is $\left(\frac\pi2 - 1, 1\right)$ and the slope is $\frac11 = 1$, so the tangent line is $y - 1 = x - \frac\pi2 + 1$, i.e. $y = x + 2 - \frac\pi2$.

For concavity, by [[#eq-param-second]],

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\sin t}{1 - \cos t}\right) = \frac{\cos t(1 - \cos t) - \sin^2t}{(1-\cos t)^2} = \frac{\cos t - 1}{(1-\cos t)^2} = -\frac{1}{1 - \cos t},
\qquad
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -\frac{1}{(1 - \cos t)^2} < 0 .
$$

So each arch is concave down. Near the cusp at $t = 0$, use $1 - \cos t \approx \frac{t^2}{2}$ and $\sin t \approx t$: the slope is approximately $\frac{t}{t^2/2} = \frac2t$, which tends to $+\infty$ as $t \to 0^+$ and to $-\infty$ as $t \to 0^-$. The arches meet the road vertically, forming cusps.
:::
:::

The vector $\bigl(f'(t), g'(t)\bigr)$ is the **velocity** of the moving point; it is tangent to the curve whenever it is non-zero, and its length $\sqrt{f'(t)^2 + g'(t)^2}$ is the **speed**. This vector point of view is developed in [[multivariable/vector-functions]].

::: quiz
For $x = t^2$, $y = t^3 - 3t$, at which parameter values is the tangent line horizontal?
- [ ] $t = 0$
- [x] $t = \pm 1$
- [ ] $t = \pm\sqrt3$
- [ ] There are none.
::: solution
$\frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 - 3$ vanishes at $t = \pm1$, where $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t = \pm2 \ne 0$: horizontal tangents at $(1, \mp2)$. At $t = 0$ we have $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} = -3 \ne 0$, which gives a *vertical* tangent at the origin. At $t = \pm\sqrt3$ the curve passes through $(3, 0)$ twice: it crosses itself there, with slopes $\frac{6}{\pm2\sqrt3} = \pm\sqrt3$.
:::
:::

## Arc length

In [[calculus-1/integral-applications]] the length of the graph of $y = F(x)$ for $a \le x \le b$ was found to be $\int_a^b\sqrt{1 + F'(x)^2}\,dx$. For a parametric curve, approximate the curve by a polygon and let the mesh shrink.

::: theorem Arc length of a parametric curve {#thm-arc-length}
Let $f$ and $g$ have continuous derivatives on $[a, b]$, and suppose the curve $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$ is traversed exactly once as $t$ increases from $a$ to $b$. Its length is

$$
L = \int_a^b\sqrt{f'(t)^2 + g'(t)^2}\,dt = \int_a^b\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt .
$$ {#eq-arc-length}
:::

::: proof {collapsed}
*Proof sketch.* The length of a curve is defined as the limit of the lengths of inscribed polygons as the partition is refined. Take a partition $a = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = b$ with $\Delta t_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$. The polygon through the points $P_i = (f(t_i), g(t_i))$ has length

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n\abs{P_iP_{i-1}} = \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{\bigl(f(t_i) - f(t_{i-1})\bigr)^2 + \bigl(g(t_i) - g(t_{i-1})\bigr)^2} = \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{f'(t_i^*)^2 + g'(t_i^{**})^2}\;\Delta t_i ,
$$

where $t_i^*$ and $t_i^{**}$ lie in $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$ by the mean value theorem applied to $f$ and to $g$ separately. If $t_i^*$ and $t_i^{**}$ were equal, this would be a Riemann sum for [[#eq-arc-length]]. They need not be, but because $g'$ is uniformly continuous on $[a, b]$, replacing $g'(t_i^{**})$ by $g'(t_i^*)$ changes the sum by an amount that tends to $0$ as the mesh tends to $0$ (using $\abs{\sqrt{u^2 + v^2} - \sqrt{u^2 + w^2}} \le \abs{v - w}$). So the polygon lengths converge to the integral. The details are in [[multivariable/vector-functions]].
:::

For a graph $x = t$, $y = F(t)$ the formula reduces to the one from Calculus I. The integrand is the speed, so [[#eq-arc-length]] says that **distance travelled is the integral of speed**.

::: example The length of one arch of a cycloid {#ex-cycloid-length}
Find the length of one arch of the cycloid $x = r(t - \sin t)$, $y = r(1 - \cos t)$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$.
::: solution
We have $x' = r(1 - \cos t)$ and $y' = r\sin t$, so

$$
x'^2 + y'^2 = r^2\left(1 - 2\cos t + \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t\right) = 2r^2(1 - \cos t) = 4r^2\sin^2\frac t2 ,
$$

using $1 - \cos t = 2\sin^2\frac t2$. For $0 \le t \le 2\pi$, $\sin\frac t2 \ge 0$, so the speed is $2r\sin\frac t2$ and

$$
L = \int_0^{2\pi}2r\sin\frac t2\,dt = \Bigl[-4r\cos\frac t2\Bigr]_0^{2\pi} = 4r - (-4r) = 8r .
$$

One arch is exactly four times the diameter of the wheel — a striking result, since the length of the circle itself involves $\pi$.
:::
:::

::: warning Traversing a curve twice doubles the integral
Formula [[#eq-arc-length]] measures the distance *travelled*, which equals the length of the trace only if no part is traversed more than once. For $x = \cos 2t$, $y = \sin 2t$ with $0 \le t \le 2\pi$ the integral is $\int_0^{2\pi}2\,dt = 4\pi$, twice the circumference of the unit circle, because the circle is traced twice. Check the range of the parameter before integrating.
:::

### Area under a parametric curve

Areas bounded by parametric curves follow from the substitution rule.

::: proposition Area under a parametric curve {#prop-param-area}
Let $f$ be continuously differentiable and strictly increasing on $[a, b]$, and let $g$ be continuous and non-negative there. The area of the region under the curve $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$, above the $x$-axis and between $x = f(a)$ and $x = f(b)$, is

$$
A = \int_a^b g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt .
$$
:::

::: proof
Because $f$ is strictly increasing, the curve is the graph of $Y = g\circ f^{-1}$ over $[f(a), f(b)]$, and the area is $\int_{f(a)}^{f(b)}Y(x)\,dx$. Substituting $x = f(t)$, $dx = f'(t)\,dt$ ([[calculus-1/integration-techniques]]) gives $\int_a^b g\bigl(f^{-1}(f(t))\bigr)f'(t)\,dt = \int_a^b g(t)f'(t)\,dt$.
:::

In Leibniz notation this is simply $A = \int y\,dx$ with $dx = \frac{dx}{dt}\,dt$. For the ellipse $x = a\cos t$, $y = b\sin t$, the upper half is traced from right to left as $t$ goes from $0$ to $\pi$; reversing the direction (so that $x$ increases), the area of the upper half is $\int_\pi^0 b\sin t\,(-a\sin t)\,dt = ab\int_0^\pi\sin^2t\,dt = \frac{\pi ab}{2}$, and the whole ellipse has area $\pi ab$. Two of the exercises use the proposition for the cycloid and the astroid.

## Polar coordinates

Fix a point $O$, the **pole** (the origin), and a ray from it, the **polar axis** (the positive $x$-axis). A point $P$ is described by its distance $r$ from $O$ and the angle $\theta$ from the polar axis to the ray $OP$, measured anticlockwise.

::: definition Polar coordinates {#def-polar}
The point with **polar coordinates** $(r, \theta)$ is the point with Cartesian coordinates

$$
x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta .
$$ {#eq-polar}

Conversely, $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, and for $(x, y) \ne (0,0)$, $\tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}$ (when $x \ne 0$), with $\theta$ chosen in the quadrant of $(x, y)$. A negative $r$ is allowed: $(r, \theta)$ with $r < 0$ is the point at distance $\abs r$ in the direction $\theta + \pi$.
:::

Polar coordinates are not unique: $(r, \theta)$, $(r, \theta + 2\pi k)$ and $(-r, \theta + \pi)$ all describe the same point, and the origin is $(0, \theta)$ for every $\theta$. This flexibility is harmless but must be remembered when finding intersections of curves.

::: example Converting between coordinates {#ex-convert}
(a) Find polar coordinates of the point $(-1, 1)$. (b) Write $r = 2\cos\theta$ in Cartesian form. (c) Write the line $x = 3$ in polar form.
::: solution
(a) $r = \sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt2$. Then $\tan\theta = -1$, but $\arctan(-1) = -\frac\pi4$ points into the fourth quadrant, while $(-1, 1)$ is in the second. So $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{4}$: the point is $\left(\sqrt2, \frac{3\pi}{4}\right)$ (or $\left(-\sqrt2, -\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$, among others).

(b) Multiply by $r$ to create recognisable terms: $r^2 = 2r\cos\theta$, that is $x^2 + y^2 = 2x$, or $(x-1)^2 + y^2 = 1$. This is the circle of radius $1$ centred at $(1, 0)$, passing through the origin. It is traced once as $\theta$ runs over $[0, \pi)$.

(c) $r\cos\theta = 3$, so $r = 3\sec\theta$ for $-\frac\pi2 < \theta < \frac\pi2$.
:::
:::

::: warning The angle is not simply arctan(y/x)
The formula $\theta = \arctan\frac yx$ only gives angles in $\left(-\frac\pi2, \frac\pi2\right)$, the right half-plane. For points with $x < 0$ add $\pi$, and treat $x = 0$ separately. Programming languages provide the two-argument function `atan2(y, x)`, which returns the correct angle in $(-\pi, \pi]$.
:::

### Polar curves

A **polar curve** $r = f(\theta)$ is the parametric curve $x = f(\theta)\cos\theta$, $y = f(\theta)\sin\theta$ with parameter $\theta$. Many beautiful curves have simple polar equations:

- $r = a$: circle of radius $\abs a$ about the origin; $\theta = \alpha$: line through the origin.
- $r = 2a\cos\theta$, $r = 2a\sin\theta$: circles through the origin.
- $r = a(1 + \cos\theta)$: **cardioid** (heart-shaped).
- $r = \cos(k\theta)$: **rose** with $k$ petals if $k$ is odd and $2k$ petals if $k$ is even.
- $r = a\theta$: **Archimedean spiral**; $r = e^{c\theta}$: **logarithmic spiral**.
- $r^2 = \cos 2\theta$: **lemniscate** of Bernoulli (a figure eight).

To sketch $r = f(\theta)$, tabulate $r$ for $\theta$ at multiples of $\frac\pi6$ or $\frac\pi4$, note where $r = 0$ (the curve passes through the origin) and where $\abs r$ is largest, and use symmetry: if $f(-\theta) = f(\theta)$ the curve is symmetric about the $x$-axis; if $f(\pi - \theta) = f(\theta)$ it is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

**Tangents to polar curves.** Since $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ with $r = f(\theta)$, the product rule and [[#eq-param-slope]] give

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta} .
$$ {#eq-polar-slope}

If the curve passes through the origin at $\theta = \theta_0$, with $r = 0$ and $\frac{dr}{d\theta} \ne 0$ there, this reduces to $\tan\theta_0$: the tangent at the origin is the line $\theta = \theta_0$. For example, the rose $r = \cos 2\theta$ passes through the origin at $\theta = \frac\pi4$, tangent to the line $y = x$.

:::

## Areas in polar coordinates

In Cartesian coordinates the basic area element is a thin rectangle; in polar coordinates it is a thin circular **sector**. A sector of radius $r$ and angle $\Delta\theta$ is the fraction $\frac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi}$ of a disc, so its area is $\frac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi}\cdot\pi r^2 = \frac12r^2\,\Delta\theta$.

::: theorem Area in polar coordinates {#thm-polar-area}
Let $f$ be continuous and non-negative on $[\alpha, \beta]$, where $0 < \beta - \alpha \le 2\pi$. The region bounded by the polar curve $r = f(\theta)$ and the rays $\theta = \alpha$ and $\theta = \beta$ has area

$$
A = \frac12\int_\alpha^\beta f(\theta)^2\,d\theta = \frac12\int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta .
$$ {#eq-polar-area}
:::

::: proof
Partition $[\alpha, \beta]$ into subintervals $[\theta_{i-1}, \theta_i]$ of width $\Delta\theta_i$, and let $m_i$ and $M_i$ be the minimum and maximum of $f$ on the $i$th subinterval. The part of the region between the rays $\theta_{i-1}$ and $\theta_i$ contains the sector of radius $m_i$ and is contained in the sector of radius $M_i$ (with the same angle), so its area lies between $\frac12m_i^2\Delta\theta_i$ and $\frac12M_i^2\Delta\theta_i$. Adding,

$$
\sum_i\tfrac12m_i^2\,\Delta\theta_i \;\le\; A \;\le\; \sum_i\tfrac12M_i^2\,\Delta\theta_i .
$$

Since $f \ge 0$, $m_i^2$ and $M_i^2$ are the minimum and maximum of $f^2$ on the subinterval, so these are lower and upper Riemann sums of $\frac12f^2$. As $f^2$ is continuous, both converge to $\frac12\int_\alpha^\beta f^2\,d\theta$ when the mesh tends to $0$ ([[calculus-1/integrals]]), and $A$ is squeezed between them.
:::

::: example The area of a cardioid {#ex-cardioid-area}
Find the area enclosed by the cardioid $r = 1 + \cos\theta$.
::: solution
The cardioid is traced once for $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$, with $r \ge 0$. By [[#eq-polar-area]],

$$
A = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi}(1 + \cos\theta)^2\,d\theta = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\left(1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta\right)d\theta .
$$

Over a full period $\int\cos\theta\,d\theta = 0$ and $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \int\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}\,d\theta = \pi$, so $A = \frac12(2\pi + 0 + \pi) = \frac{3\pi}{2}$. That is $1.5$ times the area of the unit disc.
:::
:::

::: example The area between two polar curves {#ex-between}
Find the area of the region inside the circle $r = 3\cos\theta$ and outside the cardioid $r = 1 + \cos\theta$.
::: solution
*Intersections.* $3\cos\theta = 1 + \cos\theta$ gives $\cos\theta = \frac12$, so $\theta = \pm\frac\pi3$ (at $r = \frac32$). Between these angles the circle is outside the cardioid, since $3\cos\theta > 1 + \cos\theta$ exactly when $\cos\theta > \frac12$.

*Area.* The region is swept by rays from $\theta = -\frac\pi3$ to $\frac\pi3$, each running from the cardioid out to the circle. Subtracting the two sector areas,

$$
A = \frac12\int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\left[(3\cos\theta)^2 - (1 + \cos\theta)^2\right]d\theta = \frac12\int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\left(8\cos^2\theta - 2\cos\theta - 1\right)d\theta .
$$

Using $8\cos^2\theta = 4 + 4\cos 2\theta$, the integrand is $3 + 4\cos 2\theta - 2\cos\theta$, with antiderivative $3\theta + 2\sin2\theta - 2\sin\theta$. Evaluating between $\pm\frac\pi3$ gives $2\left(\pi + \sqrt3 - \sqrt3\right) = 2\pi$, so $A = \pi$.
:::
:::

::: warning Trace each region exactly once
Formula [[#eq-polar-area]] adds up sector areas as $\theta$ runs from $\alpha$ to $\beta$; if part of the region is swept twice, it is counted twice. The three-petalled rose $r = \cos 3\theta$ is traced twice as $\theta$ runs over $[0, 2\pi]$, so $\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 3\theta\,d\theta = \frac\pi2$ is *twice* its area $\frac\pi4$. Integrate over one petal ($-\frac\pi6 \le \theta \le \frac\pi6$) and multiply by the number of petals. Similarly, when finding intersection points, solving $f(\theta) = g(\theta)$ can miss intersections where the curves pass through the same point at different angles — in particular the origin. Always draw a sketch.
:::

::: solution
The circle $r = 2\sin\theta$ (radius $1$, centre $(0, 1)$) is traced once as $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$; for $\pi < \theta < 2\pi$, $r < 0$ and the same circle is traced again. So the area is $\frac12\int_0^\pi r^2\,d\theta = 2\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi$, as it should be for a circle of radius $1$. The first integral counts the disc twice, giving $2\pi$; the other two forget to square $r$.
:::
:::

**Arc length in polar coordinates.** With $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$,

$$
\left(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\right)^2 = \left(r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta\right)^2 + \left(r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta\right)^2 = r^2 + r'^2,
$$

so the length of $r = f(\theta)$, $\alpha \le \theta \le \beta$, traced once, is $\displaystyle L = \int_\alpha^\beta\sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta$.

::: example The length of the cardioid {#ex-cardioid-length}
Find the length of the cardioid $r = 1 + \cos\theta$.
::: solution
Here $r^2 + r'^2 = (1 + \cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta = 2 + 2\cos\theta = 4\cos^2\frac\theta2$. By symmetry about the $x$-axis we can double the length of the upper half, $0 \le \theta \le \pi$, where $\cos\frac\theta2 \ge 0$:

$$
L = 2\int_0^\pi 2\cos\frac\theta2\,d\theta = 2\Bigl[4\sin\frac\theta2\Bigr]_0^\pi = 8 .
$$

(Over the whole interval $[0, 2\pi]$ one must write $\sqrt{4\cos^2(\theta/2)} = 2\abs{\cos(\theta/2)}$; forgetting the absolute value gives the wrong answer $0$.)
:::
:::

::: application Kepler's laws in polar form
Kepler published in 1609, in *Astronomia nova*, his discoveries that a planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at a focus, and that the line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. In polar coordinates centred at the Sun, the orbit is $r = \dfrac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}$ with eccentricity $0 \le e < 1$ ([[#exr-conic]]). If the angle is a function $\theta(t)$ of time, the area swept from time $t_0$ to $t$ is $A(t) = \frac12\int_{\theta(t_0)}^{\theta(t)}r^2\,d\theta$, so by the chain rule $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac12r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt}$. The second law says this **areal velocity** is constant: the planet moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun. Newton showed that this is equivalent to the force being directed towards the Sun (conservation of angular momentum).
:::

::: history
Galileo Galilei gave the cycloid its name and studied it for decades. Gilles Personne de Roberval showed in the 1630s that the area under one arch is three times the area of the rolling circle, and in 1658 Christopher Wren proved that its length is eight times the radius ([[#ex-cycloid-length]]). The cycloid then turned out to solve two famous problems: Christiaan Huygens showed in *Horologium oscillatorium* (1673) that a pendulum bob sliding on an inverted cycloid has a period independent of its amplitude (the tautochrone), and Johann Bernoulli's 1696 challenge to find the curve of fastest descent (the brachistochrone) was answered, again by the cycloid, by Johann and Jacob Bernoulli, Leibniz, L'Hôpital and Newton. Polar coordinates appear in Newton's *Method of Fluxions* (written around 1671, published 1736) and in work of Jacob Bernoulli published in 1691.
:::

## Where this leads

Parametric curves become vector-valued functions $\mathbf r(t)$ in [[multivariable/vector-functions]], where velocity, acceleration and curvature are studied in space, and line integrals along such curves are the subject of [[multivariable/line-integrals]]. Polar coordinates generalise to cylindrical and spherical coordinates, and the area element $\frac12r^2\,d\theta$ becomes $r\,dr\,d\theta$ in double integrals ([[multivariable/change-of-variables]]). The geometry of curves — curvature, torsion, the Frenet frame — is the starting point of [[differential-geometry/curves]], and in complex analysis polar coordinates appear as the modulus and argument of a complex number ([[complex-analysis/complex-numbers]]).

::: summary
- A parametric curve $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$ describes a moving point; eliminating $t$ gives an equation for the trace, but record the actual range and orientation.
- Slope: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$ where $\frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0$; second derivative $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)\big/\frac{dx}{dt}$; points where both derivatives vanish need individual study (cusps).
- Arc length is the integral of speed: $L = \int_a^b\sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2}\,dt$ for a curve traversed once. One arch of a cycloid has length $8r$.
- Polar coordinates: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$; choose $\theta$ in the correct quadrant, and remember that $(r, \theta)$ and $(-r, \theta + \pi)$ are the same point.
- Polar curves $r = f(\theta)$ include circles, cardioids, roses, spirals and conics; slopes come from the parametric formula with $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$.
- Polar area: $A = \frac12\int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta$, built from sectors; polar arc length: $\int\sqrt{r^2 + r'^2}\,d\theta$. Make sure every region is traced exactly once.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise A line in disguise {level=1}
Eliminate the parameter from $x = 1 + 2t$, $y = 3 - t$ and describe the curve.
::: solution
From the second equation $t = 3 - y$; substituting, $x = 1 + 2(3 - y) = 7 - 2y$, i.e. $x + 2y = 7$. It is the whole line through $(1, 3)$ with direction $(2, -1)$, traversed from upper left to lower right as $t$ increases.
:::
:::

::: exercise A slope {level=1 check="9/4"}
Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for the curve $x = t^2$, $y = t^3 - 3t$ at $t = 2$.
::: solution
$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t}$, which at $t = 2$ is $\frac{12 - 3}{4} = \frac94$.
:::
:::

::: exercise From Cartesian to polar {level=1}
Write the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4y$ in polar form, and the point $(1, -\sqrt3)$ in polar coordinates with $r > 0$ and $0 \le \theta < 2\pi$.
::: solution
Substituting $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and $y = r\sin\theta$: $r^2 = 4r\sin\theta$, so $r = 4\sin\theta$ (the origin is included at $\theta = 0$). For the point: $r = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$, and the point lies in the fourth quadrant with $\tan\theta = -\sqrt3$, so $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{3}$.
:::
:::

::: exercise An arc length {level=2 check="7/3"}
Find the length of the curve $x = \frac{t^2}{2}$, $y = \frac{t^3}{3}$ for $0 \le t \le \sqrt3$.
::: solution
$x' = t$ and $y' = t^2$, so the speed is $\sqrt{t^2 + t^4} = t\sqrt{1 + t^2}$ (for $t \ge 0$). With $u = 1 + t^2$,

$$
L = \int_0^{\sqrt3}t\sqrt{1 + t^2}\,dt = \Bigl[\tfrac13(1 + t^2)^{3/2}\Bigr]_0^{\sqrt3} = \tfrac13\left(8 - 1\right) = \tfrac73 .
$$
:::
:::

::: exercise One petal {level=2 check="pi/12"}
Find the area of one petal of the rose $r = \sin 3\theta$.
::: solution
One petal is traced for $0 \le \theta \le \frac\pi3$, where $\sin3\theta \ge 0$. Its area is

$$
\frac12\int_0^{\pi/3}\sin^23\theta\,d\theta = \frac12\int_0^{\pi/3}\frac{1 - \cos6\theta}{2}\,d\theta = \frac14\cdot\frac\pi3 = \frac{\pi}{12}.
$$
:::
:::

::: exercise Inside the cardioid, outside the circle {level=2 check="2 + pi/4"}
Find the area of the region inside $r = 1 + \cos\theta$ and outside $r = 1$.
::: solution
The curves meet where $\cos\theta = 0$, i.e. $\theta = \pm\frac\pi2$, and the cardioid is outside the circle for $\abs\theta < \frac\pi2$. So

$$
A = \frac12\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\left[(1 + \cos\theta)^2 - 1\right]d\theta = \frac12\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\left(2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta\right)d\theta = \frac12\left(4 + \frac\pi2\right) = 2 + \frac\pi4 .
$$
:::
:::

::: exercise Tangents to the cardioid {level=2}
Find the points of the cardioid $r = 1 + \cos\theta$ where the tangent is horizontal.
::: solution
With $x = (1 + \cos\theta)\cos\theta$ and $y = (1 + \cos\theta)\sin\theta$, we have $\frac{dy}{d\theta} = -\sin^2\theta + (1 + \cos\theta)\cos\theta = 2\cos^2\theta + \cos\theta - 1 = (2\cos\theta - 1)(\cos\theta + 1)$ and $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = -\sin\theta(1 + 2\cos\theta)$. So $\frac{dy}{d\theta} = 0$ when $\cos\theta = \frac12$ or $\cos\theta = -1$. At $\theta = \pm\frac\pi3$, $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = \mp\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot 2 \ne 0$, giving horizontal tangents at $r = \frac32$, i.e. the points $\left(\frac34, \pm\frac{3\sqrt3}{4}\right)$. At $\theta = \pi$ both derivatives vanish: this is the cusp at the origin. Near it the curve approaches the origin from both sides along directions $\theta \to \pi$, that is, tangent to the $x$-axis, but it turns back sharply instead of passing through smoothly, so we do not count it as a horizontal tangent.
:::
:::

::: exercise The area under a cycloid {level=2 check="3*pi"}
Using $A = \int y\,dx = \int_0^{2\pi}y(t)\,x'(t)\,dt$, show that the area under one arch of the cycloid $x = t - \sin t$, $y = 1 - \cos t$ is $3\pi$, three times the area of the rolling circle.
::: solution
Since $x(t)$ increases from $0$ to $2\pi$ as $t$ does, substitution in $\int_0^{2\pi}y\,dx$ gives

$$
A = \int_0^{2\pi}(1 - \cos t)(1 - \cos t)\,dt = \int_0^{2\pi}\left(1 - 2\cos t + \cos^2t\right)dt = 2\pi - 0 + \pi = 3\pi .
$$
:::
:::

::: exercise Conics in polar form {level=3 #exr-conic}
Let $0 < e < 1$ and $p > 0$. Show that the polar curve $r = \dfrac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}$ is an ellipse with one focus at the origin, and find the lengths of its semi-axes.
::: hint
Write $r = p - e\,r\cos\theta = p - ex$, square, and complete the square.
:::
::: solution
Since $1 + e\cos\theta > 0$, $r > 0$ for all $\theta$, and $r + er\cos\theta = p$ gives $r = p - ex$. Squaring, $x^2 + y^2 = p^2 - 2pex + e^2x^2$, i.e.

$$
(1 - e^2)x^2 + 2pex + y^2 = p^2 .
$$

Completing the square with $c = \frac{pe}{1-e^2}$: $(1 - e^2)(x + c)^2 + y^2 = p^2 + (1 - e^2)c^2 = \frac{p^2}{1 - e^2}$. Dividing,

$$
\frac{(x + c)^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, \qquad a = \frac{p}{1 - e^2}, \quad b = \frac{p}{\sqrt{1 - e^2}} .
$$

This is an ellipse centred at $(-c, 0)$ with semi-axes $a \ge b$. Its foci are at distance $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = \frac{p}{1 - e^2}\sqrt{1 - (1 - e^2)} = \frac{pe}{1-e^2} = c$ from the centre, so one focus is at $(-c + c, 0) = (0,0)$, the origin. Conversely, every point of this ellipse lies on the polar curve: on the ellipse $x \le a - c = \frac{p(1 - e)}{1 - e^2} = \frac{p}{1+e} < \frac pe$, so $p - ex > 0$, and the squared equation $r^2 = (p - ex)^2$ therefore gives $r = p - ex$, which is $r(1 + e\cos\theta) = p$. The relation $r = p - ex$ says that the distance to the focus is $e$ times the distance to the line $x = p/e$ (the *directrix*). For $e = 1$ the same computation gives a parabola, and for $e > 1$ a branch of a hyperbola.
:::
:::

::: exercise The area of an astroid {level=3 check="3*pi/8"}
The astroid is the curve $x = \cos^3t$, $y = \sin^3t$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$. Show that it has length $6$ and encloses area $\frac{3\pi}{8}$.
::: hint
Work in the first quadrant ($0 \le t \le \frac\pi2$) and multiply by $4$. For the area, $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4t\cos^2t\,dt = \frac{\pi}{32}$.
:::
::: solution
*Length.* $x' = -3\cos^2t\sin t$ and $y' = 3\sin^2t\cos t$, so $x'^2 + y'^2 = 9\sin^2t\cos^2t(\cos^2t + \sin^2t) = 9\sin^2t\cos^2t$. In the first quadrant the speed is $3\sin t\cos t$, and the quarter length is $\int_0^{\pi/2}3\sin t\cos t\,dt = \frac32$. The total length is $4\cdot\frac32 = 6$.

*Area.* In the first quadrant, as $t$ decreases from $\frac\pi2$ to $0$, $x$ increases from $0$ to $1$. The area under that arc is

$$
\int_0^1y\,dx = \int_{\pi/2}^{0}\sin^3t\,\left(-3\cos^2t\sin t\right)dt = 3\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4t\cos^2t\,dt = \frac{3\pi}{32}.
$$

By symmetry the total area is $4\cdot\frac{3\pi}{32} = \frac{3\pi}{8}$.
:::
