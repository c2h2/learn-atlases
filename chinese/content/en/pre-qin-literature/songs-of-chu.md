---
title: "The *Songs of Chu*"
---

Qu Yuan 屈原 is the summit of the *Songs of Chu* (*Chuci*); this lesson comes down the mountain to look at the whole ecosystem: the temple of eleven sacrificial songs, the form of the *Heavenly Questions* (*Tianwen* 天问) ripped open by one hundred and seventy questions, the breathing of a syntax built on the character 兮 (*xī*), and, after Song Yu 宋玉 takes the baton, how southern literature flows into the great canal of *fēngsāo* 风骚 ('the Airs and the Sao').

## The *Nine Songs*: temple and field of love {#sec-jiuge}

::: definition File on the *Nine Songs* {#def-jiuge}
The *Nine Songs* (*Jiuge* 九歌) are eleven pieces (the title says "nine", yet in fact there are eleven — the old account takes 'Rites for the Souls' (*Lihun* 礼魂) to be the *luàn* 乱, the coda-song that sends the gods off — and teaching accepts this: "nine" is **the name of a song suite, not a number in fact**): 'The Eastern Sovereign, Taiyi' (东皇太一, the supreme god of heaven), 'The Lord amid the Clouds' (云中君), 'The Lord of the Xiang' (湘君), 'The Lady of the Xiang' (湘夫人), 'The Greater Master of Fate' (大司命), 'The Lesser Master of Fate' (少司命), 'The Lord of the East' (东君, the sun), 'The River Earl' (河伯), 'The Mountain Spirit' (山鬼), 'Those Who Died for the State' (国殇, soldiers killed in battle), 'Rites for the Souls' (礼魂, sending the gods off). The ground colour is **shamanic sacrifice in the land of Chu**: the shamans together bring the gods down, and **write the welcoming and the sending-off of the gods as a love between human and god** — “浴兰汤兮沐芳，华采衣兮若英” ("I bathe in a hot bath of orchids, ah, and wash in fragrance; robes of splendid colour, ah, like blossoms." (Our translation.)) (the shaman dressed in the god's costume); “沅有芷兮澧有兰，思公子兮未敢言” ("The Yuan 沅 has angelica, ah, the Li 澧 has orchids; I think of the young lord, ah, and have not dared to speak." (Our translation.)) ('The Lady of the Xiang' — the hesitation of a one-sided love, a southern enhanced edition of the *Book of Songs* escorting someone across the Qi). 'The Mountain Spirit': “若有人兮山之阿，被薜荔兮带女罗……风飒飒兮木萧萧，思公子兮徒离忧” ("There seems to be someone, ah, in a fold of the mountain, clothed in creeping fig, ah, and belted with dodder… The wind soughs, ah, the trees sigh; I think of the young lord, ah, and bear my grief in vain." (Our translation.)) — **the first complete image of a female ghost in Chinese literature**: at once wild and gorgeous, waiting until death. 'Those Who Died for the State' changes to a metallic sound: “操吴戈兮被犀甲，车错毂兮短兵接……身死兮心不惩” ("I grasp a spear of Wu, ah, and wear rhinoceros armour; chariot-hubs lock, ah, and short weapons meet… The body dies, ah, but the heart is not chastened." (Our translation.)) — martial music for the sacrifice to heroic spirits, and, apart from the 'Wu yi' 无衣 ('No Robe') of later times, **another Chinese tune of honour in death**. The two accounts are filed together: Wang Yi 王逸 holds that Qu Yuan **saw the popular rites as rough and clumsy, and rewrote them** (lodging in them the sense of “发愤”, 'venting pent-up indignation'), and Zhu Xi's 朱熹 *Collected Annotations* (*Jizhu* 集注) follows him; people today lay more weight on their value for **an anthropology of shamanic culture** (the several layers of bringing the gods down, love between human and god, and death and disorder) — whoever the authorship is assigned to, the "Nine Songs" are **one of the secret places of production of the Chinese lyric tradition**: fragrant herbs, waiting, arriving late, a missed appointment — the four great devices of feeling in the *Songs of Chu* leave the factory here fully fitted, for the first time.
:::

## The *Heavenly Questions* and the lingering echo {#sec-tianwen}

::: definition The *Heavenly Questions* form and the dialogue form {#def-tianwen}
The ***Heavenly Questions***: more than one hundred and seventy questions in one breath (“遂古之初，谁传道之？上下未形，何由考之？” ("Of the beginning of remotest antiquity, who passed the account down? Above and below had not yet taken form; by what could one examine it?" (Our translation.)) — asking about the origin of the cosmos; “鸱龟曳衔，鲧何听焉？” ("The owl and the turtle dragged and held it in their mouths; what did Gun 鲧 heed?" (Our translation.)) asking about events in mythical history; asking about the rise and fall of the Three Dynasties — the last section falls back into anecdotes of Wu and Chu), **four-character lines strung together, questioning straight through to the end, the whole pre-Qin mass of prodigies, feats of strength, disorder and spirits swept up in one net** — the judgement, put in the vernacular: **the only poem in Chinese built out of question marks**, asking and not answering — and, beyond the “赋比兴” of the *Book of Songs* — *fu*, *bi* and *xing* (exposition, comparison, evocation) —, it makes a fourth use: ***wènxìng*** 问兴 ('evocation by asking'). Its meaning as a genre: **a general audit of knowledge** — cosmology, mythical history and the legends of the Chu ancestors are submitted together to questioning; the most extreme aesthetic specimen of the pre-Qin "spirit of doubt" (set with the *Analects*' “不语怪力乱神” ("He did not speak of prodigies, feats of strength, disorder or spirits." (Our translation.)), it makes exactly a north–south pair: one avoids, the other asks everything). **'Divining Where to Dwell'** (*Buju* 卜居) and **'The Fisherman'** (*Yufu* 渔父): short pieces in question-and-answer form (“吾宁悃悃款款朴以忠乎？将送往劳来斯无穷乎？” ("Shall I rather be earnest and attentive, plain and loyal? Or shall I see off those who leave and toil to receive those who come, and so without end?" (Our translation.)) — **antithetical alternative questions**, the first note of the "seven" form of the Han rhapsody (the eight sections of question and answer in *Seven Stimuli* (*Qifa* 七发) are their descendant); in 'The Fisherman', “沧浪之水清兮，可以濯吾缨” ("When the water of the Canglang 沧浪 is clear, ah, it will do to wash my cap-strings." (Our translation.)) — **a choice of what person to be, written as an allegorical dialogue**. The fisherman's “与世推移” ("moving as the world moves") against the Sanlü 三闾's “怀瑾握瑜” ("jade at the breast and jade in the hand") is the first **paired scene in Chinese literature of leaving the world and entering it**; afterwards the script — Dongfang Shuo 东方朔, 'Answering a Guest's Objections' (*Dakenan* 答客难); Han Yu 韩愈, 'An Explication of Progress in Learning' (*Jinxuejie* 进学解); Qu Yuan against the fisherman — has been played for two thousand years.
:::

::: example The Mountain Spirit: a performance with no audience {#ex-shangui}
“若有人兮山之阿，被薜荔兮带女罗。既含睇兮又宜笑，子慕予兮善窈窕。乘赤豹兮从文狸，辛夷车兮结桂旗。表独立兮山之上，云容容兮而在下……风飒飒兮木萧萧，思公子兮徒离忧。” ("There seems to be someone, ah, in a fold of the mountain, clothed in creeping fig, ah, and belted with dodder. She casts a sidelong glance, ah, and is fit for smiling; you adore me, ah, so fine is my grace. She rides a red leopard, ah, a striped wildcat in her train, in a carriage of magnolia, ah, with flags of cassia knotted on. She stands out alone, ah, upon the mountain; the clouds roll thick, ah, and lie below… The wind soughs, ah, the trees sigh; she thinks of the young lord, ah, and bears her grief in vain." (Our translation.)) — Please analyse: ① the camera blocking of the entrance; ② the structure of "waiting"; ③ what to keep and what to drop among the four accounts of who she is (god / shaman / human being / Qu Yuan figuring himself).
::: solution
① Shots: **the scene first, the person after** (a fold of the mountain — a secluded fixing of place) → **costume** (薜荔 as clothing, 女罗 as a belt — finery made of wild plants: she is the beauty of the mountain forest itself) → **a close-up of expression** (含睇宜笑, a sidelong glance and a smile that suits her — a woman's confidence, rare in Chinese literature, under a direct frontal gaze) → **the retinue** (a carriage team of red leopard and striped wildcat — fierce beasts kept as pets, beauty's quota of violence) → **an extreme long shot** (“表独立兮山之上，云容容兮而在下” ("She stands out alone, ah, upon the mountain; the clouds roll thick, ah, and lie below." (Our translation.)) — solitude starts the camera, and the tragic shot-scale is in place); ② the structure of waiting: **going to the tryst — waiting, and the other does not come — self-doubt — resentment without anger** (“留灵修兮憺忘归，岁既晏兮孰华予” ("I linger for the Fair One, ah, and in my ease forget to return; the year is already late, ah, who will bring me to flower?" (Our translation.)) — the fair one's late season is for the first time coupled to **the tyranny of time**; the final section closes the show in wind and rain, and the appointment never comes — **the "missed appointment" motif of the *Songs of Chu*** is fixed in its form here, purer than the abandoned wife in 'The Commoner' (*Meng* 氓): even the faithless one never appears; there is only absence itself); ③ the four accounts: the Mountain Spirit (a god of the unseen dark), a shaman dressed as a goddess (role-play in bringing the god down), a love poem (between human and god, or a folk love song), Qu Yuan lodging his own case in it (“思公子” = thinking of the ruler) — this lesson chooses to **file them together**: at the level of the text it is a love poem of shamanic sacrifice; at the level of editing, Qu Yuan rewrote it as an allegory of character on the pattern “等待—失约” ("waiting — a missed appointment"); at the level of reception it becomes the fixed shot of “香草美人” ("fragrant herbs and the fair one") (Li He 李贺, 'Song of the Spirit-Strings' (*Shenshan qu* 神弦曲), and the “山鬼图” ("pictures of the Mountain Spirit") in painting, all the way to Fu Baoshi 傅抱石 — **she is one of the female leads most often painted in Chinese art**).
:::

::: history From "writing the language of Chu" to "the collected southern volume of Chinese literature" {#sec-nanfang}
**Liu Xiang 刘向 compiles the *Songs of Chu*** (Western Han: Qu Yuan, Song Yu and imitations by Han writers are gathered into a collection — "Songs of Chu" begins here as **a book title**; Wang Yi's *Chapter and Verse* (*Zhangju* 章句) is the earliest complete commentary that now survives); the ***Records of the Grand Historian*** — “屈原放逐，乃赋《离骚》” ("Qu Yuan was banished, and thereupon composed *Encountering Sorrow* (*Li Sao*)." (Our translation.)) — enters the narrative of general history; **Zhaoming of the Liang, the *Selections of Refined Literature*** (*Wenxuan* 文选), opens a separate class, the "Sao" — the classification of genres acknowledges it as a house of its own; **Liu Xie 刘勰, 'Discriminating the Sao' in *The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons*** (*Wenxin diaolong* · *Bian sao* 辨骚): “其衣被词人，非一代也” ("It has clothed men of letters; it is not of one age only." (Our translation.)) — **the theoretical fixing of the joint name "the Airs and the Sao"** — the gist of the verdict: 奇文郁起，衣被词人非一代 ("Marvellous writing rose in a lush growth; it has clothed men of letters for more than one age." (Our translation.)); **Hong Xingzu 洪兴祖 of the Song, with his supplementary commentary, and Zhu Xi's collected commentary** (scholars of the Learning of the Way, *dàoxué* 道学, enter it on the register again); **Wang Yuan 汪瑗 of the Ming and Jiang Ji 蒋骥 of the Qing** understood *Chu* by way of geography (a study of walking the roads); **from the modern period on**: documents unearthed in the land of Chu and the transmitted *Songs of Chu* corroborate one another and form one line of a new learning (reading materials from underground against words on paper); **today**: the *Nine Songs* enter dance-drama, and 'The Mountain Spirit' enters game skins — **the southern imagination keeps being hired back**.
:::

::: intuition Syntax is geography too: the breathing of the character 兮 {#int-xi}
The difference between the *Songs of Chu* and the *Book of Songs* that the naked eye can see lies in the **sentence**: the four-character lines of the *Songs* are as compact as the loess tableland, while the *Songs of Chu* use the character “**兮**” to draw the breath out long (“帝子降兮北渚，目眇眇兮愁予” ("The Sovereign's child descends, ah, on the northern islet; eyes gaze far, ah, and it grieves me." (Our translation.)) — 兮 stands in the middle of the line, and is **the pause-point of the shaman's dance**; in *Encountering Sorrow*, “名余曰正则兮，字余曰灵均” ("My name was given as Zhengze 正则, ah; my courtesy name was given as Lingjun 灵均." (Our translation.)) — 兮 stands at the waist of the line, and the part before it and the part after it each make one of a pair), together with modal particles such as “只” and “些” (in *Summons of the Soul* (*Zhaohun* 招魂), a swarm of spells on the character “些”: “些” at the end of every line — **the fossilised sound of the shaman's spell**). A longer sentence form = a greater capacity for lyric feeling: four-character lines suit calling the roll and labour; long lines suit lingering and weeping aloud. **The south has much water, many mountains and many shamans, and so the Chinese language learned to breathe deep** — from the length of the *Songs of Chu* to the length of the Han rhapsody, and on to the long-and-short lines of the Tang and Song, this route on which "the breath grows longer" starts at that character “兮”.
:::

::: warning Four pits in reading the *Songs of Chu*
One: **reading the south in the orthodox pronunciation of the Central Plains**. “书楚语，作楚声，纪楚地，名楚物” ("It writes the language of Chu, makes the sounds of Chu, records the places of Chu, and names the things of Chu." (Our translation.)) (the sense of what Huang Bosi 黄伯思 of the Song said) — 兰芷荃蕙 are not necessarily *bi* and *xing*; they may also be **a flora**; besides a symbolic reading, keep the literal sense. Two: **taking the number "nine" as real** (the riddle of the eleven pieces has already shown it: the name of a suite). Three: **treating Song Yu, in every respect, as a supporting role**. In *Nine Arguments* (*Jiubian* 九辩), “悲哉，秋之为气也” ("How sad! Autumn, as the *qi* it is." (Our translation.)) — **the person who registered the Chinese motif of "grieving over autumn" is Song Yu** (“登山临水兮送将归” ("I climb the mountain and look on the water, ah, and see off the one who is going." (Our translation.))); in the *Rhapsody on the Wind* (*Fengfu* 风赋), “风起于青苹之末” ("The wind rises at the tips of the green duckweed." (Our translation.)), and the morning cloud and evening rain of *Gaotang* (高唐) and *The Goddess* (*Shennü* 神女) — **the erotic imagination of the scholar and the beauty, together with love between human and god, has its title registered in Song Yu's name** (the source of the allusion “下东墙”, 'down the eastern wall'). Four: **treating the *Heavenly Questions* as the index to an encyclopaedia**. It is **a poem**; its value for the study of questions lies in "the posture of asking", not in a list of answers — do not read it as an FAQ.
:::

::: exercise A script for bringing the god down {level=1}
Choose 'The Lord of the Xiang' or 'The Lady of the Xiang' and, on the pattern “迎神—候神—遇（幻遇）—送神” ("welcoming the god — waiting for the god — meeting (an illusory meeting) — sending the god off"), write a prompt book (two columns: set, and feeling), and mark in which line the "missed appointment" makes its turn.
::: solution
A sample, 'The Lady of the Xiang': welcoming (“帝子降兮北渚” ("The Sovereign's child descends, ah, on the northern islet." (Our translation.)) — the fantasy that she descends) → waiting (“袅袅兮秋风，洞庭波兮木叶下” ("Trailing soft, ah, the autumn wind; on Dongting 洞庭 the waves rise, ah, and the leaves fall from the trees." (Our translation.)) — **filming with an autumn scene**: the most famous set-line in Chinese literature) → an illusory meeting (“鸟何萃兮蘋中？罾何为兮木上？” ("Why do the birds gather, ah, in the duckweed? What is the fishing net doing, ah, up in the tree?" (Our translation.)) — **nature out of its order = a foreboding that the meeting has failed**: birds flock in the water-plants, a fishing net hangs on the tree-tips, and the order of the world has slipped out of place) → sending off (“时不可兮骤得，聊逍遥兮容与” ("The time cannot, ah, be had at a stroke; for the while I wander free, ah, and linger at ease." (Our translation.)) — it closes by consoling itself). Judging the turn: from the **fantasy of life after the wedding** in “筑室水中，葺之兮荷盖” ("I build a house in the water, and thatch it, ah, with lotus for a roof." (Our translation.)) on, imagination has already crossed the line, and reality has already left the theatre. Set, feeling and the turn — three pairs — are enough.
:::

::: exercise The person who sets the *Heavenly Questions* {level=2}
Imitate the form of the *Heavenly Questions* and, on one piece of present-day common knowledge, write twelve questions (cosmos, myth, history, science and technology — choose any of them; mainly four-character lines; ask only, and do not answer), and attach a paragraph of explanation: which of your questions is the most "dangerous".
::: solution
Marking: ① the form fits (four-character lines strung together, questions and no assertions; a failure to rhyme is allowed); ② they form a sequence (asking from the large down to the small, or from the ancient to the present, with one consistent **logic of audit**); ③ a self-assessment of the "one dangerous question": point out which question in fact **pulls out the ground from under the questioner** (for example, “成功学之所谓功，谁道之？” ("Of what success studies call success, who passed the account down?" (Our translation.))). The highest grade: let the absence of an answer become the answer itself (the true transmission of the *Heavenly Questions*).

::: exercise Short essay: why the Airs and the Sao are joint ancestors {level=3}
Write eight hundred to one thousand two hundred characters. The *Book of Songs* and the *Songs of Chu* are named together as "the Airs and the Sao"; how has this joining of the case worked, from Liu Xie's 'Discriminating the Sao' to modern literary history? Requirements: ① give one comparison in each of three sets — genre, subject matter, and device of feeling (four-character line / the sao form; marketplace / temple; resentful complaint / venting pent-up indignation); ② point out what "joint ancestry" conceals (**the shamans and ghosts of the *Songs of Chu* were for a long time whitewashed by a reading that turned them into classical scholarship**); ③ discuss the gains and the risks of the "north–south binary" template (you may cite “书楚语” ('writing the language of Chu') and “思无邪” ('no depraved thoughts') as a banner for each side).
::: solution
Points for the mark: the three sets of comparison are accurate; the claim of "concealment" has a target (the turning of the songs into steadfast loyalty in Wang Yi's *Chapter and Verse* is the specimen — every love poem, to the last one, is entered in the household register of loyalty to the ruler); the conclusion should be able to remind the reader that **the north–south binary is a tool of description, not a component of blood** (Qu Yuan himself is a mixed-blood project of “用南方之声写中原之志” ("writing the intent of the Central Plains in the voice of the south") — many of the allusions in *Encountering Sorrow* are based on the *Songs* and the *Documents* (*Shu* 书)).

::: summary
Four public cases in the ecosystem of the *Songs of Chu*: the temple and the field of love in the eleven pieces of the *Nine Songs* (the missed-appointment motif; the complete first performance of the Mountain Spirit); the architecture of the question mark in the *Heavenly Questions* (the only poem in Chinese built out of questions); the dialogue form of 'Divining Where to Dwell' and 'The Fisherman' (alternative questions that open the "seven" form of the Han rhapsody; the first paired scene of leaving the world and entering it); Song Yu taking over (the register of grieving over autumn, and of morning cloud and evening rain). Add the deep breath of the character “兮” — the southern half is filled in. The next chapter counts the master ledger: the inventory of the legacy that pre-Qin literature left to the two Han dynasties, and the final hearing of the joint case of "the Airs and the Sao".
:::
