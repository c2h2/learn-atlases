The previous chapter collected the file; this chapter enters the workshop: why the drumbeat of the four-character line has, across three thousand years, come back on stage only in times of archaism; how repeated stanzas with variation use the refrain to bring a change of feeling out; **_fu_, _bi_ and _xing_** (exposition, comparison, evocation; for *xing* also 'stimulus' and 'affective image') — the three oldest characters in Chinese literary theory, and the three most given to quarrel.

## The four-character line: the shortest line-length {#sec-siyan}

::: definition The golden age of the four-character form, and its retirement {#def-siyan}
The *Book of Songs* is **mainly four-character** (a two-and-two beat: "关关/雎鸠——参差/荇菜", '*guan guan* / the ospreys — uneven / the water-mallow' (Our translation.)), several lines to a stanza, the singing layered as it runs on down. The four-character line was made to order for Old Chinese: a language of mainly monosyllabic words + music and dance accompanied by a drumbeat = one word to a beat, two and two making the line, and the beat is the semantic unit ("桃之/夭夭", 'the peach / so tender' (Our translation.); the character 之 is a padding sound and holds no meaning). Its golden age is the whole of it: the *Songs of Chu* broke into lines of mixed length; the Han *raoge* 饶歌 copied its outward look; **after the *Book of Songs* the four-character line lived only twice** — Cao Cao 曹操, "东临碣石，以观沧海" ('Eastwards I come to Jieshi, to gaze on the blue-green sea.' (Our translation.)), and four-character brushwork of the Mao Zedong 毛泽东 kind, on the pattern of "江山如此多娇" ('The rivers and mountains are so full of grace.' (Our translation.)) (one powerful and deep, one political rhetoric) — and, in addition, the inscriptions, eulogies and temple-and-court hymns of every later age (whose full-time post is solemnity). Why the retirement? The disyllabification of Old Chinese ([[modern-vocabulary/morphemes-words]]) gradually lamed the single-beat word, and the four-character line could neither swallow nor utter compound concepts — **the verse form changed generation; the revolution in word-formation came first, and taste afterwards**. A mnemonic for reading the four-character line: read aloud by the beat, read the function words light, and let the drumbeat divide the lines for you.
:::

## Repeated stanzas with variation: the engineering of the refrain {#def-chongzhang}

::: definition Three ledgers of incremental repetition {#def-futa}
Repeated stanzas with variation = the frame recurs from stanza to stanza, and particular characters are changed ("采采芣苢：薄言采之→有之→掇之→捋之→袺之→襭之" — 'Gathering the plantain: now I gather it → have it → pick it up → pull it off → tuck it in the skirt → tuck it at the sash' — six verbs, one montage of the actions of labour. (Our translation.)). Three ledgers: ① **the music ledger**: the stanzaic structure of oral song; to change a character is to make a variation; the cost of memory is halved, and the life of the song in transmission is doubled (the memory-card format of oral culture, echoing the intuition lesson [[classical-chinese-1/punctuating]]); ② **the feeling ledger**: one sings and three sigh in answer; incremental repetition (*futa* 复沓) is **compound interest on feeling** — "知否知否" ('Do you know, do you know?' (Our translation.)) is still a Song writer's continuation on this same frame; ③ **the meaning ledger**: the characters that change are the shots of the montage — in 'Plantain' (*Fouyi* 芣苢), from 采 ('gather') to 有 ('have') to 掇 ('pick up') to 捋 ('pull off') to 袺 ('tuck in the skirt') to 襭 ('tuck at the sash'), the action grows greedier and the harvest fuller, and **the whole poem has not one line that states a feeling; the progress bar is the feeling**. The stacked lines of later tune titles (*cipai*), the four-season tunes of folk song, and the refrains of popular song are all branch shops of this engineering.
:::

::: example Dissection on the shop floor: 'No Clothes' {#ex-wuyi}
"岂曰无衣？与子同袍。王于兴师，修我戈矛，与子同仇。" ('Who says there are no clothes? With you I share my robe. The king is raising the army; we ready our dagger-axes and spears; with you I share a foe.' (Our translation.)) ('Airs of Qin', *Qin feng* 秦风, 'No Clothes', *Wuyi* 无衣; three stanzas; 袍/泽/裳, 矛/戟/堂) — to show how a change of characters makes a progression inside the incremental repetition.
::: solution
The skeleton of the three stanzas does not change (同袍→同泽→同裳: from underclothes to the battle-skirt; the degree of sharing increases = the private self is ceded, grade by grade, to the bond of those who share a robe); the words for arms change (戈矛→戈戟→作堂: the forming-up moves from weapons to the array, and the war machine heats up); every stanza closes on "与子同仇/偕作/偕行" ('with you I share a foe / with you I rise to act / with you I march' (Our translation.)) — **同仇 (a consensus of feeling) → 偕作 (getting into action) → 偕行 (setting off for the front)**. Inside the shell of the incremental repetition there runs a straight line of the progress of mobilisation. The hardest-edged poem in the *Book of Songs*, and yet the technique is the most "simple": the program is written into the refrain. **Repeated stanzas are not repetition: the frame is conserved, the shot is displaced.**
:::
:::

## *Fu*, *bi* and *xing*: a thousand-year court for three characters {#sec-fubixing}

::: definition The judgement on the three meanings {#def-sanyi}
Their designation comes from the "six poems" in the Offices of Spring in the *Rites of Zhou* (*Zhouli* 周礼, *Chun guan* 春官) — 风赋比兴雅颂, Airs, exposition, comparison, evocation, Odes, Hymns — and the Mao Preface (*Mao shi xu* 毛诗序) renamed them the "six principles". **They were originally six courses taught by the music officers, and a thousand years of literary theory read them as six techniques.** The verdict was settled on three sentences of Zhu Xi 朱熹 (cross-checked, back and forth, between the *Collected Commentaries on the Songs*, *Shi jizhuan* 《诗集传》, and the *Collected Commentaries on the Analects*, *Lunyu jizhu* 《论语集注》): **for *fu*, 赋者，敷陈其事而直言之** ('As for exposition, it sets the matter out and states it directly.' (Our translation.)) ("五月斯螽动股，六月莎鸡振羽" — 'In the fifth month the grasshopper works its legs; in the sixth month the katydid shakes its wings.' (Our translation.) — the narrative is laid out and steps straight onto the stage); **for *bi*, 比者，以彼物比此物** ('As for comparison, it uses that thing to compare with this thing.' (Our translation.)) ("手如柔荑，肤如凝脂" — 'Hands like the soft shoots of the rush; skin like set fat.' (Our translation.) — the figures of simile and metaphor); **for *xing*, 兴者，先言他物以引起所咏之词** ('As for evocation, it first speaks of some other thing, so as to lead in the words that are sung.' (Our translation.)) ("关关雎鸠，君子好逑" — '*Guan guan* cry the ospreys; a good match for the gentleman.' (Our translation.) — the birds sing off to one side, and the poem opens over here). The trouble is with "*xing*": the ospreys and love **do not join up as a matter of reason, but lock together as a matter of sound** — for two thousand years, interpreters of the *Songs* have either forced a link of meaning (Zheng Xuan 郑玄: *xing* is the moralised version of *bi*, "以善物喻善事", 'using a good thing to figure a good matter' (Our translation.)), or else simply recognised only the opening impetus of sound and feeling (*xing* is close to an **instrument of atmosphere**: it gives the opening note, settles the scene, and opens the door for feeling). Two modern camps: the **opening theory** (*xing* = the opening routine of musical song; the study of formulae, and oral-formulaic theory — "关关雎鸠" and "桃之夭夭", '*Guan guan* cry the ospreys', 'The peach, so tender' (Our translation.), are a batch of **ready-made modules for the opening *xing***) versus the **association theory** (thing and feeling are in fact subtly connected; the ethical legend that the ospreys are "挚而有别", 'devoted, and yet they keep a distinction', is this very line of thought). This lesson files the two together and teaches them as one: ***xing* is a hybrid rice of musical system and poetic meaning — when you reap the rice of the musical rules, do not throw away the barnyard grass of poetic meaning.**
:::

::: definition Comparing with virtue: the largest dividend for later ages {#def-bide}
*Bi* and *xing* are assembled into one symbolic machine: the ***bide* tradition** (*bǐdé* 比德, 'granting natural things a moral standing') — natural things acquire a moral rank: 黍稷 ('millet') = the ancestral state ('Airs of the Royal Domain', *Wang feng* 王风, 'Drooping Millet', *Shuli* 黍离: "行迈靡靡，中心摇摇" — 'On I walk, slowly, slowly; the heart within me shakes.' (Our translation.) — "黍离之悲", 'the sorrow of the drooping millet', has had its template ever since); 桃李 ('peach and plum') = the household gate, 松柏 ('pine and cypress') = integrity, 兰蕙 ('orchid and *hui*') = the gentleman, 鸱鸮 ('the owl') = the one who steals power ('Airs of Bin', *Bin feng* 豳风, 'The Owl', *Chixiao* 鸱鸮: the mother bird weeps out her plaint — the first fable in a bird's words, and the first to break that ground). Once *fu*, *bi* and *xing* had been given definite form by the *Songs*, they became **the operating system of Chinese poetic method**: the elaborate laying-out of the Han rhapsody (the sense of *fu* expanded into a genre); the fragrant herbs of the *Songs of Chu* (*bide* scaled up into a system); the evocative image in Tang poetry (*xìngxiàng* 兴象; *xing* raised in rank to *yìjìng* 意境, 'the realm of meaning a poem creates'); the veiled purport of the song lyric (*jìtuō* 寄托, in the *ci*; *bi* and *xing* joined into one dim and boundless sorrow — Zhang Huiyan 张惠言, in his *Ci xuan* 《词选》 (*Selection of Song Lyrics*), reads every line of Wen and Wei 温韦 as "《骚》自命", 'professing the *Sao*', that is *Encountering Sorrow*, the *Li Sao*) — one workshop shipped the goods, and for three thousand years the supply has never broken off.
:::

::: history The battle for the right to define "*xing*" {#sec-dingyiquan}
**Han**: Zheng Zhong 郑众, in a commentary on the *Rites of Zhou*, writes "兴者，托事于物" ('*Xing* is the lodging of a matter in a thing.' (Our translation.)) — the first shot of moralising; Zheng Xuan's note on the "six poems" classes *xing* with "美见" ('the seeing of what is excellent'); **Song**: Zhu Xi's three definitional sentences gave the final word ("先言他物", 'first speaking of another thing'), and at the same time he admitted with his own mouth that *xing* is the hardest to explain — "那兴字最难看" ('That character *xing* is the hardest to see clearly.' (Our translation.)) (the candid remark in the *Classified Conversations of Master Zhu*, *Zhuzi yulei* 《朱子语类》); **Qing**: Yao Jiheng 姚际恒 and Fang Yurun 方玉润 set poetics against Han Learning; from the middle of the last century on, linguistic anthropology and oral-formulaic theory entered China, and "formulae and the opening *xing*" were restored to their place in the industry of musical song ("葛藟", 'creeping vines', and "日月", 'sun and moon', are each a high-frequency module); **the present**: the "conceptual metaphor" of cognitive linguistics has again opened talks to renew the contract for *bide* ("坚贞像松柏" — 'To be steadfast is like the pine and the cypress.' (Our translation.) — is a conceptual mapping, not a decoration of rhetoric). The history of defining the single character "*xing*" is a route-map of how Chinese literary theory set out from the study of the classics, passed through aesthetics, stopped at cognitive science, and set out again.
:::

::: warning Three precautions in the analysis of technique
First, **the compulsion to paste on a category**: every poem must be marked "*fu* / *bi* / *xing*" — the three are ideal types, not a cabinet of three drawers; the mixed blood of "比而赋、兴而比" ('comparison and yet exposition; evocation and yet comparison') is what is normal on the shop floor (Zhu Xi himself, in a great many notes, writes "比也", 'it is comparison', "兴也", 'it is evocation', and also "赋而比也", 'it is exposition and yet comparison'); second, **taking the poem apart into spare parts**: once the technique has been explained, one forgets that 'No Clothes' makes a person want to go on campaign, and 'Plantain' makes a person want to join in, hands on — **technique is a subordinate clause serving why it is good**; third, **mechanical application across ages**: taking the Song writers' "theory of veiled purport" and reading everything by it (reading "关关雎鸠" until an interlocking chain of subtle words and great meanings comes out) — when you read the *Songs*, ask first about the music of Zhou, then about the Han classicists, and only at the end is it the turn of the Song classicists' magnifying glass.
:::

::: exercise An experiment with the beat {level=1}
Rewrite one stanza of 'Reeds' (*Jianjia* 蒹葭) once as five-character lines and once as seven-character lines, read them aloud and compare, and explain where the four-character line cannot be replaced.
::: solution
A sample: the seven-character "芦苇苍苍秋露白，所谓伊人水一方" ('Reeds grey-green, the autumn dew white; the one spoken of is on one reach of the water.' (Our translation.)) — add words, and at once it becomes a commentary on the picture; the **scenes set side by side, a juxtaposition with no verb** in the original "蒹葭苍苍，白露为霜" ('The reeds are grey-green; the white dew becomes frost.' (Our translation.)) (two measures, four images: 蒹葭–苍苍–白露–为霜, reeds – grey-green – white dew – becomes frost) is diluted by grammatical glue; the five-character line has the same fault. Where the conclusion should go: the bleakness of the four-character line = juxtaposition and space left blank; simple word-formation is precisely a weapon of atmosphere; the rewriting experiment is on-the-spot proof of "the same meaning, not interchangeable" (echoing [[rhetoric/choosing-sentences]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise A search for *xing* modules {level=2}
In the *Book of Songs*, look up two examples each of the three modules for an opening *xing* or for a thing compared — "南山" ('the southern hill'), "河水" ('river water') and "桃李" ('peach and plum') — judge whether the use in each poem is the same module (opening, versus a stirring of feeling, versus comparing with virtue), and set out your classification.
::: solution
For reference: for instance "陟彼南山" ('I climb that southern hill.' (Our translation.)) ('Cocklebur', *Juan'er* 卷耳 / 'The Grass-insect', *Caochong* 草虫 — climbing high as an opening *xing* + anxious thought settling the scene); "河水清且涟猗" ('The river water is clear, and it ripples.' (Our translation.)) ('Felling the Sandalwood', *Fa tan* 伐檀 — a real riverscape turning to argument, an opening *xing*); "桃之夭夭" ('The peach, so tender.' (Our translation.)) ('The Tender Peach', *Taoyao* 桃夭 / 'How Rich the Bloom', *He bi nong yi* 何彼秾矣 — a module of wedding festivity). Three grades for the classification: a pure opening (sound), a stirring of feeling (atmosphere), *bide* (meaning). One poem may occupy two grades, if you say so. Marking points: the lines cited are accurate + the classification has a standard; do not strain to set up as the one who understands.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: three thousand years of one technique {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters: choose one of "*xing*", "*bide*" and repeated stanzas, and write a biography of its parts: ① the scene in the *Book of Songs*; ② one transformation in the *Songs of Chu* or the Han rhapsody; ③ a further transformation in Tang poetry and the Song song lyric; ④ one use of your own (a composition, song lyrics, or a barrage comment will all do) — the conclusion answers: is the classical technique still alive in the modern age, or are only the terms left?
::: solution
Marking points: no hard flaw at the four stations (if, for example, you carry "*xing*" all the way to the source, in landscape poetry, of "感物动情", 'feeling stirred by the things one meets', that counts as having made the link); terms correspond accurately to the works; the station of your own use is honest and concrete (a barrage comment, "蒹葭+1", 'Reeds +1', also counts as a specimen); the conclusion refuses both extremes (neither a "living fossil" nor "already dead"), and comes down on **a change of scene**: from musical song, to text, to the interface.
:::

::: summary
The workshop delivery note: the four-character line = the retired champion, made to order as the drumbeat of the age of monosyllabic words (a return to the stage depends on solemnity and archaism); repeated stanzas = the refrain engineering of a conserved frame and a displaced shot (the progress bar of 'Plantain', the mobilisation order of 'No Clothes'); *fu*, *bi* and *xing* drifted from the music officers' timetable of courses into the operating system of poetic method; Master Zhu's three sentences are the precedent, and the right to define *xing* is still before the court; *bide* wrote natural things into the dictionary of morals, and has supplied the oil for three thousand years. Technique is a subordinate clause in the service of "why it is good" — the next chapter changes exhibition halls: historical prose, from the hard speech of the proclamations of high antiquity, to the veiled censure of the Spring and Autumn brush, and on to war broadcast live.
:::
