title: "Sentence patterns and types"
---

The same pile of words, with the "mood packaging" changed, is another sentence; the same skeleton, lacking a subject and short of a predicate, communicates all the same. This lesson has two nets of classification: **sentence patterns divided by structure** (subject–predicate sentences and non-subject–predicate sentences), and **sentence types divided by mood** (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory). The two nets cross, and every sentence has its coordinates.

## Sentence patterns: marshalled by structure {#sec-juxing}

::: definition Subject–predicate sentences and non-subject–predicate sentences {#def-feizhuer}
**Subject–predicate sentence**: subject and predicate both complete (她笑了／今天冷得很). By the core of the predicate they divide again into four squads: **verbal-predicate sentences** (他看书), **adjectival-predicate sentences** (花很美｜他大我三岁), **nominal-predicate sentences** (今天星期三；你好热情！ — even with the copula 是 absent, the nominal can still predicate, relying on context and mood), **S–P predicate sentences** (他|身体很好｜这事|我心里有数 — a major subject plus a minor subject–predicate serving as the predicate; Chinese's signature sentence pattern, the purest specimen of topic structure). **Non-subject–predicate sentence**: by nature it has no need of a complete subject and predicate (下雨了！／禁止吸烟／哎呀！／火车！). Mark the difference from an **elliptical sentence**: an omission can be restored from the context (“你去吗？”“去。”), whereas a non-subject–predicate sentence **has no need of restoration at all** (the 天 of * 天下雨了 cannot be supplied). Year after year the two are muddled together on examination papers, and year after year the field quarrels over them; the teaching line draws the boundary by "whether it can be restored with certainty".
:::

::: example Telling an S–P predicate sentence {#ex-zhuwei}
Distinguish them: ①他身体很好。②他的身体很好。③身体很好。 — Which is an S–P predicate sentence? Which cut belongs to which?
::: solution
① An S–P predicate sentence: the major subject 他 plus the predicate 身体很好 (a minor subject–predicate serving as the predicate); ② an ordinary adjectival-predicate sentence (the subject = the modifier–head phrase 他的身体); ③ a non-subject–predicate sentence (adjectival non-subject–predicate). The test: **whether 是 or a topic pause can be inserted** (他呀，身体很好✓ — the major subject is a topic; it can be brought forward and it can be paused. This is the watershed between an S–P predicate sentence and a sentence whose subject is a modifier–head phrase). Then test by restoring possession: 他的身体很好 ≡ ① is an equivalence only in most situations; in a contrastive context the 他 of ① is **the entity concerned, not the possessor** (他书读得多 ≠ he possesses all the books).
:::
:::

## Sentence types: packaged by mood {#sec-julei}

::: definition The four sentence types and the system of questions {#def-si}
**Declarative sentences** (the default packaging), **interrogative sentences**, **imperative sentences** (禁止吸烟／你走吧／千万别说), **exclamatory sentences** (多么美啊／好大的雾). Each of the four forms of question has equipment of its own: the **yes–no question** (+ 吗 / intonation ↑: 你去吗？), the **specific question** (the interrogative pronoun stays in its original position: 谁去？你买了什么？ — Chinese does not move the question word, and this is where it parts company with English wh-fronting), the **A-not-A question** (去不去／好不好／是不是 — a Chinese speciality, the copying technique of V-not-V), the **alternative question** (还是你走，还是我走？). Mood is **a further layer of obligatory packaging, beyond the tense of the sentence**: the same proposition, 你走, is legal under all four packagings (你走了。你走吗？你走！你走啊！？ — the four sentences have entirely different emotive tasks). Semantics speaks of the proposition; grammar speaks of the line of packaging.
:::

::: history Two stumbling-blocks in Chinese grammatical scholarship {#sec-hulu}
Once Ding Shengshu 丁声树 and others had argued the case systematically in *Xiandai hanyu yufa jianghua* 现代汉语语法讲话 ('Talks on Modern Chinese Grammar', 1961), the "S–P predicate sentence" became a standard component of teaching: it brought back to life countless sentences of the 他身体很好 kind, and it also declared the doctrine "subject = agent" bankrupt. The name "non-subject–predicate sentence" took its settled form through the great debates of the 1930s and the 1950s (over who would admit that a single word on the pattern of 静夜 constitutes a sentence); behind it lies the victory of "a sentence = a unit of communication" over "a sentence = subject–predicate logic". Li Jinxi 黎锦熙's early scheme of forced splitting, "every sentence has a subject and a predicate" (if a subject cannot be found, call it "ellipsis"), has been swept into the museum of grammar — **admitting that Chinese can speak without a subject is the moment when the study of Chinese grammar was weaned and came of age**.
:::

::: warning Four mistakes about patterns and types
The first mistake is **"a nominal-predicate sentence = 是 omitted"**: 今天星期三 is a legal sentence pattern, not a fragment — the decision looks to the linguistic intuition of the speech community (“今天*是星期三” is, in most situations, stiff instead). The second mistake is **taking a rhetorical question for a question**: “谁知道啊！” (= nobody knows) carries interrogation in its form and states an attitude in its function — sentence type is judged by **illocutionary function**, not by the question mark. The third mistake is **a one-word sentence = a sentence-word**: “火灾！” is a non-subject–predicate sentence in exclamatory packaging, same in form as the word 火灾 and different in duty. The fourth mistake is **taking "subject and predicate both complete" for the normal case in Chinese**: in dialogue, titles, slogans and colloquial speech, non-subject–predicate sentences hold half the field — "subject and predicate complete" is an illusion of a Europeanised written voice (it joins the chapter on sentence style in [[rhetoric]]).
:::

::: quiz
“他一个人走了。”“一个人走了。” The latter （　）.
- [ ] An elliptical sentence (the subject omitted as carried over from what precedes)
- [x] Non-subject–predicate or elliptical must be told by the context: what can be restored with certainty is an elliptical sentence
- [ ] An S–P predicate sentence
- [ ] A faulty sentence
::: solution
The teaching line: **whether it can be restored with certainty**. A monologue that offers new information = a non-subject–predicate sentence; dialogue that continues the sentence before = an elliptical sentence — the same surface, two household registers.
:::
:::

::: quiz
“是不是他来？” is （　）.
- [x] An A-not-A question (“是不是” as a whole serves as the predicate frame)
- [ ] A yes–no question
- [ ] An alternative question
- [ ] A specific question
::: solution
An A-not-A question of the V-not-V copying kind; only “是…还是…” is an alternative question — the equipment differs; do not mix them.
:::
:::

::: quiz
“王冕死了父亲” is judged in pedagogical grammar to be （　）.
- [ ] A faulty sentence (Wang Mian did not die)
- [x] A special subject–predicate sentence, legal though agent and possessor are misaligned on the subject
- [ ] A non-subject–predicate sentence
- [ ] Existential sentences do not exist
::: solution
An event of losing what is possessed (verbs of the 死 class allow the possessor to serve as the topic subject) — the pattern is legal and the semantics are special, and this is precisely the display window of the subject–predicate sentence's "topic-prominent" house style.
:::
:::

::: exercise Coordinates on the two nets {level=1}
Give each of the following sentences a double coordinate, sentence pattern plus sentence type: ①明天刮台风。②严禁烟火！③你怎么才来呀？④好香的咖啡！
::: solution
① A subject–predicate sentence of the verbal-predicate kind (明天, a noun of time, as subject, plus 刮台风) plus a declarative sentence (it may also be analysed as a subjectless sentence of the existential type; note both accounts and the mark is given); ② a verbal non-subject–predicate sentence plus an imperative sentence; ③ a subject–predicate sentence of the verbal-predicate kind (怎么 as the adverbial) plus a specific question; ④ a nominal non-subject–predicate sentence plus an exclamatory sentence. All eight labels correct is full marks.
:::
:::

::: exercise A rhetorical-question translation machine {level=2}
Turn three rhetorical questions into statement meanings and trace the equipment back: “谁稀罕？”“这我可不答应吧？”“他不就去了一次吗？”
::: solution
= “没人稀罕” (the interrogative pronoun 谁 shifted to arbitrary reference, plus falling-intonation exclamatory packaging); = “我不答应” (吧 weakened, plus a rhetorical retort emphasised by 可, and the proposition reversed); = “他就去过一次” (“不是…吗” comes to the same meaning either way: the law of polarity reversal in the rhetorical question). Tracing the equipment back: a pronoun of arbitrary reference, a modal particle, and an A-not-A frame can all carry a rhetorical retort — **interrogative form ≠ interrogative function**, the patch from the study of function on the judgement of sentence type.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short essay: is Chinese a topic-type language? {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. With this lesson's three pieces of evidence (the S–P predicate sentence, the patient-topic sentence “这事我早忘了”, and the embryo of the flowing sentence (*liushuiju*)), assess the label "Chinese = a topic-prominent language" (the typology of Li & Thompson 1976): what use has it for second-language teaching, and where is it open to a charge of overgeneralisation in syntactic research? The essay must include one sample topic sentence from your own dialect or colloquial speech, and must close in the manner of "the label is useful, but a scale has to be added".
::: solution
Marking points: one sentence of analysis for each of the three pieces of evidence; the double edge of the label (in teaching, it explains why Chinese can say ‘水果我爱吃’; in research, it conceals the difference of level between topics in the main clause and in the subordinate clause, and the difference between a stressed and a light reading); the sample is genuine; the close gives the due measure of "topic prominence is a parameter on a continuum; Chinese stands at the high end, but is not the only such language".
:::
:::

::: summary
Sentence patterns are marshalled by structure (four squads of subject–predicate sentences, plus four types of non-subject–predicate sentence), and sentence types are packaged by mood (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory; each of the four forms of question has its own equipment). The process by which the two great "stumbling-blocks", the S–P predicate sentence and the non-subject–predicate sentence, were taken onto the books is the process by which Chinese grammar threw off the doctrine "a subject and a predicate in every sentence" and admitted **a topic-prominent house style**. The double coordinates locate any sentence: one dimension of structure, one dimension of mood, plus one dose of a functional patch (a rhetorical question does not question). The next lesson enters the museum of special sentence patterns: the *ba* construction (disposal construction), the *bei* passive, the existential sentence, and the serial-verb and pivotal constructions — the four great constables of Chinese grammar.
:::
