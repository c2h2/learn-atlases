title: "Sentence constituents"
---

A phrase, once it dons intonation, becomes a sentence, and on the skeleton are hung seven job titles: subject, predicate and object; attributive, adverbial and complement; and, besides, independent elements and prompting constituents. This lesson issues two sets of tools — **symbol extraction** (the line-marking system of the Chinese class) and **semantic orientation analysis** (the other half of an eye, beyond distribution).

## The general table of constituents and the symbol method {#sec-chengfen}

::: definition The seven constituents and a one-sentence general principle {#def-qi}
A sentence = a communicative unit with pauses and intonation before and after it, able to be used on its own. The system of constituents: three pieces of skeleton — **subject** (what is stated about) — **predicate** (the core of the statement) — **object** (what the action involves); three sets of attachments — **attributive** (a modifier of a noun, with 的), **adverbial** (a modifier of a verb, with 地), **complement** (a supplement with 得, or of result, duration or frequency — not an English complement, as in *She is a teacher*); plus **independent elements** (constituents outside the sentence, of the “看来、据说、哎呀、北京—上海” kind: vocatives, insertions, prompts). One sentence of general principle: **nouns, verbs and adjectives each keep to their place — nominals serve as subject and object, verbs and adjectives as predicate, adjectives and nouns as attributive, adverbial and complement**. The line-marking symbol system (the school-Chinese version): a double horizontal line for the subject, a vertical line for the predicate, a wavy line for the object, （ ） for the attributive, ［ ］ for the adverbial, 〈 〉 for the complement — six sets of marks, one sentence.
:::

::: example Line-marking in practice {#ex-jiaxian}
Analyse with the line-marking symbols: “他十分仔细地观察着那里的地形。” ('He is very carefully observing the terrain there.') (Our translation.) And answer: does the whole sentence have a complement? What constituent is “那里的”?
::: solution
Subject “他”; predicate head “观察” plus the aspect particle “着” (the particle is a hanger-on, and is not analysed on its own); object head “地形”; attributive “（那里的）”; adverbial “［十分仔细地］” (the adverbial–head phrase as a whole serves as the adverbial; “十分” modifies “仔细”). Complement: none — the trap in this question lies exactly here. Two easy mistakes: “那里的” is an attributive, not an adverbial (one pass through “的/地” separates them at once); “着” is not set up as a constituent of its own.
:::
:::

::: theorem Three tests for constituents {#rule-sanshi}
① **The questioning method**: (who) + the predicate? → subject; the predicate + (whom / what)? → object; the predicate + (how / where / for how long)? → adverbial or complement; (what kind) + 的 → attributive. ② **The “的地得” sieve**: 的 of attributive–head, 地 of adverbial–head, 得 of predicate–complement — three signpost words, each in charge of one class of attachment (a zero-form attributive, as in “木头桌” ('wood table'), and an unmarked adverbial, the “快” ('fast') of “快跑” ('run fast'), are settled by a further judgement from position and word class). ③ **Extraction and comparison**: “昨天在礼堂我们开了会” ('Yesterday, in the assembly hall, we held a meeting.') (Our translation.) set against any reordering — **the law of multi-layer adverbial order**: time ＞ place ＞ scope and manner ＞ the party introduced by a relational preposition ＞ the verb (“昨天下午在礼堂跟代表们热烈地讨论了问题” ('Yesterday afternoon, in the assembly hall, [we] enthusiastically discussed the matter with the delegates.') (Our translation.) — not one layer out of place).
:::

::: example Semantic orientation: one bowl of soup, two kinds of wok aroma {#ex-zhi}
“我们跟他谈了很久。” ('We talked with him for a long time.') (Our translation.) — does “很久” point to the action or to the person? “他老婆病了半个月了” ('His wife has been ill for half a month.') (Our translation.) can be said; “*他老婆病了半个月” ('*His wife was ill for half a month.') (Our translation.) cannot — why? “只有你知道” ('Only you know.') (Our translation.) and “你知道的只有我” ('The only one you know is me.') (Our translation.) — where does the orientation of “只有” differ?
::: solution
“很久”, as a duration complement, points to **the length of the action** (one may ask “谈了多久” ('talked for how long?')), and does not point to “汤/他”; if one switches to “汤热了很久” ('The soup has been hot for a long time.') (Our translation.), “很久” points to **the continuation of a state** — the semantic orientation of the complement differs, while the syntactic position is the same. A duration complement requires a predicate that **can continue** (“病” can continue, and so can take “半个月”; “死” cannot, so “死了三天” refers to the state after death, not to the continuation of the act of dying — semantic orientation analysis mops up after the tense–aspect system). The adverb “只有” in the first sentence points to **the set of objects of knowledge** (restricting “who”), and in the second sentence points to **quantity** (restricting the range of “我知道的”) — one adverbial: whoever the semantic lens is aimed at, the meaning follows; constituent analysis gives the position, orientation analysis gives the sights.
:::
:::

::: history Three descents of constituent analysis {#sec-xiachen}
The syntax of pedagogical grammar has three generations under one roof. The first generation is “中心词分析” ('head-word analysis') (constituents marked with lines; it took settled form and entered secondary-school textbooks in the 1950s, and to this day is the grammatical mother tongue of the whole population). The second generation is structuralist immediate-constituent analysis (the binary trees of the previous lesson, which have burrowed onto the university lectern). The third generation is generative grammar (the constituent-command relation c-command, empty pronouns, movement — from the 1980s it took over professional research). The three generations each do their own work: the first for correcting compositions, the second for analysing ambiguity, the third for doing theory. The history of “内卷” ('involution') in the study of grammar proves: **there is no better analysis, only an analysis better suited to the occasion** (this course’s position: instrumental pragmatism).
:::

::: warning Four pits
Pit one, **subject = agent**: in “台上坐着主席团” ('On the platform sits the presidium.') (Our translation.) the subject is a place, not an agent; “他给了我一本书” ('He gave me a book.') (Our translation.) and “这本书我看过” ('This book, I have read.') (Our translation.) (a patient-topic sentence) — **the Chinese subject is a topic position; it does not take charge of semantic roles** (topic-prominence is Chinese’s typological identity card). Pit two, **independent elements slip through the net**: constituents outside the sentence such as “据说、看来、哎呀、北京—上海” do not serve as subject, predicate or object, and still make a sentence; in analysis list them apart as “independent elements”; force them into the skeleton and the analysis warps. Pit three, **the theory that 是 does every job**: “是” may be a judgement (他是我哥 'He is my elder brother.'), existence (门口是一棵树 'At the doorway is a tree.'), or focus (我是知道 'I do know.') — three “是”, three syntaxes; do not sign one contract for all three. Pit four, **forgetting that a sentence ≠ a phrase**: only a phrase plus intonation makes a sentence, so “很好” ('very good') is a phrase and “很好。” ('Very good.') (Our translation.) is a sentence — whether subject and predicate are both in place is the sentence’s business; “下雨了” ('It has started to rain.') (Our translation.) has no subject and is still a sentence (non-subject–predicate sentences are taken up again in a chapter of their own).
:::

::: quiz
In “昨天下午在礼堂我们开了会”, “昨天下午在礼堂” functions as （　）.
- [ ] subject
- [x] adverbial (two layers, time and place)
- [ ] independent element
- [ ] complement
::: solution
Noun phrases of time and of place serve directly as adverbials (a privilege of time-words, [[modern-grammar/parts-of-speech]]); a sentence-initial adverbial does not stay in the subject position.
:::

::: quiz
The semantic orientations of “很久” in “汤热了很久” and in “谈了很久” are, respectively, （　）.
- [ ] both point to the action
- [x] continuation of a state / length of an action
- [ ] noun / verb
- [ ] both point to a noun
::: solution
The value of orientation analysis lies exactly here: the same syntactic position, two semantic arrows.

::: quiz
The subject of “门口站着一个哨兵” ('At the doorway stands a sentry.') (Our translation.) is （　）.
- [ ] 一个哨兵
- [x] 门口 (locative subject; an existential sentence)
- [ ] no subject
- [ ] 站着
::: solution
The existential sentence “处所+动+存现物” ('place + verb + the thing that exists or appears'): pedagogical grammar judges the place to be the subject (yet another embodiment of the topic position); generative grammar has another analysis — noting the disagreement is what earns a high mark.
:::

::: exercise Line-marking in full {level=1}
Give “代表们热烈地讨论并通过了大会的报告。” ('The delegates enthusiastically discussed and passed the report of the general meeting.') (Our translation.) the full set of line marks; explain the method of checking collocation when a coordinate predicate shares an object; and make up yourself a faulty sentence in which the collocation fails.
::: solution
‖代表们‖‖［热烈地］讨论并通过‖<了>（大会的）报告‖: subject “代表们”; coordinate predicate “讨论并通过” (“了” attaches after the verb); object “报告” plus attributive “（大会的）”; adverbial “［热烈地］”. **Collocation check**: a shared object must collocate with each of the two verbs separately — “讨论报告” ✓ “通过报告” ✓, so the coordination holds. A counter-example of one’s own, such as “看见和听到了一阵歌声” ('saw and heard a burst of singing') (Our translation.) （*看见歌声 'saw singing'）, is the family of faulty sentences “动宾搭配不当” ('verb–object collocation mismatch'); for the chapter given over to them, see [[modern-grammar/faulty-sentences]]. Line marks all correct, plus the checking method clearly stated, plus a counter-example that holds, and that is full marks.
:::
:::

::: exercise Cracking the case by orientation {level=2}
Is “他爸爸批评得很厉害。” ('His father criticised [someone] very severely.' / 'His father was criticised very severely.') (Our translation.) ambiguous? Explain by semantic orientation. Then make up a further sentence containing “差点没”, and explain the polarity-reversal mechanism of its orientation (optional).
::: solution
Yes. The complement “很厉害” may point to **the force of the criticism** (批评得厉害 'criticised severely') or to **the wretched state of the father after he was criticised** (fortunately the *ba* construction (disposal construction) can pull them apart: “把他爸爸批评得很厉害” ('criticised his father very severely') (Our translation.) points to the father) — when unmarked, the default is to point to the degree of the verb, and context can flip it. “差点没摔倒” = 没摔倒 ('did not fall') (a wish) / “差点没考上” = 考上了 ('did pass') (redundant negation of a negative wish) — polarity is decided by **the direction of the wish**; orientation analysis conspires with pragmatics. Full marks when the analysis is on the mark.
:::

::: exercise Short essay: what is a subject? {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. The structuralist subject = the nominal constituent that the predicate states something of; the logical subject = the agent; the pedagogical subject = “that lump under the double horizontal line”. What verdict does each of the three give on “台上坐着主席团/连他都不认识你/这件事我早忘了”? You are to explain to the head teacher why “汉语主语是动作发出者” ('The Chinese subject is the one who produces the action.') (Our translation.) was marked with a cross on the child’s paper — write a letter of three hundred characters, then develop the argument in linguistic terms.
::: solution
Marking points: the three definitions each take a turn in the match and own up to their cost (the structural definition lets subject–predicate predicate sentences through; the logical definition dies on existential sentences and patient-subject sentences; the pedagogical definition = a convention, but a stable one); the letter fits the register (no showing off of academic jargon); the core argument lands on the typological fact that “the Chinese subject = a topic position”, and leads on to the course’s next stop: the chapter on sentence types (the place where subject–predicate predicate sentences and existential sentences have their names set right).
:::

::: summary
The seven constituents in one sentence of general principle: subject, predicate and object as the skeleton; attributive, adverbial and complement as the attachments; independent elements outside the sentence. The line-marking symbols are the grammatical mother tongue of the whole population, but only the three tests (questioning, 的地得, extraction and ordering) give them teeth; semantic orientation supplies the half of an eye that the study of position lacks — whoever an adverbial or a complement is aimed at, the meaning follows. The three generations of analysis each keep their own post: correcting compositions, analysing ambiguity, doing theory. The greatest lesson: **the Chinese subject is a seat for the topic, not a post for the agent** — for the household-registration problem of the two sentence types, the existential sentence and the patient-subject sentence, please step across to the next lesson, the record of sentence types.
:::
