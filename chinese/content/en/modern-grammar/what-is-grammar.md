title: "What grammar is"
---

'On what basis can what a person says be understood?' Besides a vocabulary list, there must also be a set of assembly rules — and that set of rules is grammar. This lesson first 'fixes the framework' for the study of grammar: units, relations, and why Chinese grammar looks nothing like that of the Indo-European languages.

## The parts of grammar and their assembly {#sec-danwei}

::: definition Four levels of grammatical unit {#def-siji}
A morpheme (the smallest pairing of sound and meaning; see [[modern-vocabulary/morphemes-words]]) → word → phrase → sentence. The study of grammar deals with two things: **units** (which level is which, and where the boundaries lie) and **relations** (the rules by which units combine). Relations fall into two kinds: **syntagmatic relations** (the horizontal axis: whether subject + predicate, and verb + object, can be paired) and **paradigmatic relations** (the vertical axis: which members may be substituted in the same slot — '吃/做/炒+饭' ('eat / make / stir-fry + rice') is one paradigm). Being able to form sentences = command of syntagmatic relations; being able to choose words = command of paradigmatic relations. These two axes of Saussure are the coordinate system of every grammatical description.
:::

::: definition The three characters of Chinese grammar {#def-xingge}
① **Lack of morphology**: nouns have no case and no number, and verbs have no conjugation; '我打他/他打我' ('I hit him / he hits me') depends entirely on position and function words — **word order therefore becomes the primary grammatical device**. ② **Words have no fixed form, and one item fills many roles**: '研究' is a noun in '进行研究' ('carry out research') and a verb in '研究问题' ('look into a problem'); Chinese word classes are **distributional classes**, not morphological classes. ③ **Phrases, compounds and sentences share one and the same set of structures**: '下雨' can be a word, a phrase, or a sentence ('下雨了。', 'It's raining.') — **three-level isomorphism** is a characteristic of Chinese, and it is also the root of the trouble in every dispute over 'word or phrase?' and 'what is the subject?' (the echo, at the level of grammar, of the characteristics of Chinese in [[modern-vocabulary/morphemes-words]]).
:::

::: example Working the case by the distributional method: what kind of word is '研究'? {#ex-yanjiu}
A: 进行研究. B: 研究问题. C: 很研究. D: 研究的. — The grammatical reactions in the four environments differ; on that basis one assigns '研究' its identity.
::: solution
A: governed by '进行' = a gerund-like use (in Chinese, that is, a nominalised use of a verb; the mainstream is not to treat it as 'dual class membership'). B: taking an object = the typical distribution of a verb ✓. C: '很 + adjective' is the typical frame for an adjective, and '很研究' has low acceptability — which shows that '研究' is not behaving according to an adjective's distribution. D: the '的' = a nominalising construction ('研究的' = the [thing/person] of 研究). Conclusion: **Chinese word classes are 'multifunctional'** — a verb, in its own class, can already serve as subject or object without changing its word-form ('调查正在进行', 'the investigation is under way'), and this is precisely where Chinese parts company with the Indo-European equation 'nominalisation = a change of morphological form'.
:::
:::

::: theorem The three-level account of grammatical units (the Huang–Liao framework) {#rule-sanji}
Teaching grammar adopts three levels: word — phrase — sentence (morphemes are assigned to lexicology, and sentence groups to the study of text). Word class is fixed by distributional criteria: **the frames in which it can appear** ('很×', '×不×', '×着/了/过', '数量+×' ('numeral–classifier + ×'), and so on), with meaning taken as a reference. The transitional zones between the three levels are precisely the deep water of Chinese: a subject–predicate phrase may serve as subject ('你不去也好', 'it is just as well if you do not go'), and a single word may make a sentence ('禁止吸烟。', 'No smoking.' / '喂！', 'Hey!') — **level is judged by function, not by length**.
:::

::: intuition Grammar is a 'social convention', not a 'law of logic'
'一个人一个人地排队' ('queue one-person by one-person') is grammatical; '一个一个人地排队' ('queue one, one-person') is awkward — there is no logical explanation, only a distributional fact. A grammarian is first of all like a linguist, not like a member of the legislature: record the patterns the community recognises (description), and only then, with caution, offer proposals (the norm). The two faces must be kept apart (isomorphic with the two faces of the dictionary in [[modern-vocabulary/dictionaries]]): judging '别' to be an adverb rather than a conjunction is a scholarly dispute; judging '凯旋归来' to be a crime rather than a faulty sentence is purism about register — **what a grammar lesson trains is the ear that tells these two things apart**.
:::

::: history From *judou* to the study of grammar: two changes of blood in the Chinese orthodox tradition {#sec-fatong}
Traditional China had a highly developed study of prose composition and a theory of *judou* (traditional pause marks), but no study of grammar with 'word classes and constituents' as its backbone. The first change of blood: in 1898 *Mashi Wentong* 《马氏文通》 borrowed a Latin framework to legislate for Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) — a conscious dislocation, “因西文四读之线画中国字句” ('drawing Chinese words and sentences along the lines of the four readings of Western writing') (Our translation.). The second change of blood: from the 1920s the object was **living spoken language** and the criterion was **distribution** (“凡词、依句辨品、离句无品”, in Li Jinxi 黎锦熙, *Xinzhu guoyu wenfa* 《新著国语文法》 (*A New Grammar of the National Language*), 'for any word, the class is distinguished according to the sentence; apart from the sentence there is no class' (Our translation.), is the radical version; see [[modern-vocabulary/morphemes-words]]), and the normalisation movement of the 1950s in turn fixed this description as a nationwide 'sense of right and wrong' (*Yufa xiuci jianghua* 《语法修辞讲话》 (*Talks on Grammar and Rhetoric*) — Chinese-language textbooks — papers for the higher-education entrance examination, strung on a single line). The 'subject, predicate, object, attributive, adverbial, complement' of today's classroom — complement (补语) is not the complement of an English verb phrase — is the sedimentary rock of three changes of blood over these three hundred years — **each layer is a reasonable age's unreasonable compromise**.
:::

::: warning Three things to guard against on entering grammar
First, guard against **testing Chinese by English grammar**: 'Does Chinese have passive sentences / progressive aspect / conversion of word class?' is a question for the study of grammar (one may ask for the answer); 'Chinese ought to have changes of tense before it can count as complete' is a joke of the study of grammar — morphological devices, and the devices of word order and function words, are merely different routes, with no higher and no lower. Second, guard against **making constituent analysis all-powerful**: constituent analysis (head — adjunct) is good at the syntactic shell and poor at meaning ('鸡不吃了' has two readings and the same tree); once constituent analysis has been learned, one must go on to learn transformations and semantic orientation. Third, guard against **taking the norm for grammar**: exercises on faulty sentences are the minimum standard for public writing, not the highest standard for natural language — what is 'non-standard' in a literary work is often the most economical expression (for rhetoric, see the next course).
:::

::: exercise A distributional test {level=1}
Design three distributional frames for '代表', and by them determine respectively its uses as a noun and as a verb; then give one pair of words, one for which '很×' can be said and one for which it cannot, and explain their classes.
::: solution
'三位代表/代表团' = a noun frame (数量+×, 'numeral–classifier + ×'); '代表大家发言' = a verb frame (+ agent subject + clause); the '×们' test (代表们 ✓, and dual class membership is confirmed). '很高/很聪明' ✓ (adjectives); '很桌子/很跑步' ✗ (nouns and verbs do not enter the '很' frame alike) — hard cases such as '区别词/状态词' ('distinguishing words / state words') emerge precisely from the differences between frames (developed in the next chapter).
:::

::: exercise The two faces {level=2}
For two pieces of online material under '无语不成句，无句不成篇' ('without an expression there is no sentence; without a sentence there is no text') — '绝绝子' and 'Given 名字' — write one verdict along each of the two lines, description and norm, and state the basis of each.
::: solution
The descriptive line: record the constructions ('绝绝子' = an AABB-type online intensifying construction; 'Given+X' = a pragmatic marker of bilingual code-mixing) together with the communities that use them, and the registers. The normative line: judge how suitable they are for the public domain (not appropriate in formal documents; legitimate inside a subcultural circle). Two verdicts, and one basis for each, meet the standard; stirring the two lines into one pot ('错！' / '都对！', 'Wrong!' / 'Both right!') scores nothing either way — the first training aim of this lesson is precisely the ability to keep separate accounts.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short essay: the grammatical instinct {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. A grandmother who has never had a grammar lesson can tell by 'feel' that '饭吃了被我' does not hold together, yet she cannot name subject, predicate and object — what kind of knowledge is hers? Is this course's relation to her 'conferring rules', 'setting intuition in order', or 'rewriting the feel for the language'? You must argue from three real example sentences taken from your own community (one that is grammatical but that textbooks do not teach, one that does not match the textbook but that everyone says, and one that no one says but that is theoretically grammatical), and respond to one view that 'grammar is useless'.
::: solution
Points for marking: the three example sentences are real and the analysis hits the mark (distribution, frequency and register, one of them discussed for each); the landing point for 'the nature of the knowledge' is there (procedural knowledge versus declarative knowledge; teaching grammar is a second-order setting-in-order of it); the reply to the 'useless' view relies not on sentiment but on examples (revision in writing, second-language teaching, and language technology are all places where first-order intuition fails).
:::

::: summary
Grammar = assembly rules: four levels of unit (morpheme — word — phrase — sentence) multiplied by the two axes of syntagmatic and paradigmatic relations. The three characters of Chinese — no morphology, multifunctional words, three-level isomorphism — determine that the study of its grammar must work its cases by the distributional method rather than the morphological method, and also determine the distinctive recipe of every dispute it has (the boundary of the word, word class, the subject). The 'subject, predicate, object, attributive, adverbial, complement' of teaching grammar is the sedimentary rock of three changes of blood since *Mashi Wentong*; when you use it, remember the age of the strata, and remember still more: you have long possessed a whole body of grammatical knowledge, and this course only invites it in from intuition onto the ledger.
:::
:::
