---
title: Writing and language
---

You may be able to write five thousand Chinese characters (sinograms), and still never have seen what they look like when they are at work. This chapter does not discuss the history of any one character. It discusses the device that is the 'character' itself: what writing is, which layer of language it records, why Chinese characters alone have never come apart through three thousand years of violent sound change, and which word is wrong in the sentence everyone can say, 'Chinese characters record meaning, the Latin alphabet records sound.' A theory lesson? No — this is the very foundation you will be standing on later, when you learn the six categories (*liushu*), tell traditional characters from simplified ones, and wrestle with input methods.

## What writing is

::: definition Writing {#def-wenzi}
Writing is **a visible symbol system that records language**, made up of three things: a set of named units (characters or letters), a principle of formation for 'how speech is written down' (a system of writing), and norms of graphic form that the community enforces. Writing is not a transparent coat pulled over language. It has **its own substance** (shape), its own units (characters), and its own impetus to change — and all of these can be examined apart from 'the sound of speech', just as one can examine a set of chess pieces without first replaying a game.
:::

::: definition Morphosyllabic writing {#def-yisuxiyin}
Chinese characters are **morphosyllabic writing** (morphosyllabic): the basic function of one character is to record one syllable–morpheme unit of Chinese. “山” [ʂan⁵⁵] is one syllable, and usually one morpheme as well; only the two characters “葡萄” together are one morpheme; and “们” is one morpheme but has no status as an independent syllable. This placing — 'cut by the morpheme, written by the syllable' — determines every feature of the character inventory: the number is huge (each one has to be memorised on its own), the script is stable across dialects (the shape is not tied fast to a sound value), and it also determines why the popular imagination of 'one character, one meaning' is always half true and half false.
:::

::: theorem Three places where writing and language fall out of step {#rule-butongbu}
(1) **The substance is different.** Language is sound (and time); writing is shape (and space). 'Reading' decodes shape back into language, 'writing' encodes, and neither step is translation. (2) **The speed of change is different.** Graphic form is conservative by nature: a copy has to look like the original, so the graph lags behind, while the sounds of speech wear away day and night. The 'original character' and the 'original meaning' therefore cannot keep up with the 'popular character' and 'popular writing', and the landscape of graphic forms holds fossils from every age. (3) **The systems differ in how tightly they are bound.** A phonetic script follows the sounds: three hundred years of sound change, and the original text has become an unreadable book (people in Britain today cannot read Chaucer). Chinese characters weld meaning onto shape. The Yue (Cantonese), Southern Min and Japanese Kan-on readings of the character “见” all differ, yet on the page they share one and the same *Analects* — **a sound-recording system drifts with pronunciation; a shape-recording system is anchored by the character dictionaries**.
:::

::: proof Why Chinese characters did not become a phonetic script {#proof-hanzi-pinyin}
It is not that Chinese people 'valued the Way and slighted implements', nor that Chinese characters are 'advanced' or 'backward'. It is a combination of three conditions. Morphemes in Chinese naturally come in blocks (one character is basically equal to one morpheme, and the cuts are clean). The gulf in pronunciation among the dialects means that 'spelling by sound' must be one spelling in each place, and can never be written as one piece. And the character dictionaries, together with the civil-service examinations, supplied the strongest **graphic anchor** in the world: for one thousand three hundred years the standard answers all lay in 'how to write'. Two of the three conditions have already changed today (the common language has spread, and the phonological anchor can be replaced by Pinyin), and so 'Will Chinese characters be Latinised?' became a question that the twentieth century tried in earnest, and in earnest stopped ([[chinese-characters/simplification]] gives the detailed account).
:::

## The error in 'ideographic writing'

::: definition The spectrum of recording meaning and recording sound {#def-jiyi-jiyin}
The typology of writing divides its spectrum by **what the basic unit records**. What records morphemes is called morphemic writing (Chinese characters are the representative). What records phonemes is called phonemic writing (Latin, Cyrillic). What records syllables is called syllabic writing (kana is the representative; Greek, too, came this way in its history). Note that between the three spectra there are **tendencies**, not pure breeds. A meaning-recording factor lives inside English numerals (“4” is read four/quatros/四 in all three languages), and a sound-recording factor lives inside Chinese phono-semantic compounds (semantic-phonetic compounds): the “胡” of “湖” supplies the sound and nothing else. Therefore **the sentence 'Chinese characters = ideographic, letters = phonetic' is wrong in its equals sign**. Every mature script is a mixture of recording meaning and recording sound. What is special about Chinese characters is the recipe of the mixture: from 75 to 90 per cent of characters carry a phonetic component (phonophore) of their own, and yet the semantic component (signific) fixes the domain.
:::

::: example Seeing through the popular account that 'the six categories = methods of creating characters' {#ex-liushu-method}
Textbooks often say that the six categories are 'six methods of creating characters', so that indicatives and pictographs come first, phono-semantic compounds afterwards, and Chinese characters 'evolve' to become ever cleverer. Where has this account worked loose?
::: solution
The six categories are first of all an **analytical framework** (terms people of the Qin and the Han took up when they pulled characters apart and classified them), not a checklist of origins. Pictographs and indicative characters are a tiny share of the writing system (under 2 per cent in a modern list of characters in general use), and phono-semantic compounds already present in the oracle-bone inscriptions show that the evolutionary story 'compound ideographs (associative compounds) first, phono-semantic compounds last' does not hold. Early Chinese characters were already advancing on both roads at once (recording meaning, and recording sound). More troublesome are the two categories *zhuanzhu* (mutually glossing characters; disputed) and phonetic loan (rebus). Explanations have always diverged so far that the theory of 'four forms and two uses' simply expels them from the methods of creating characters (see [[chinese-characters/six-categories]]). The conclusion: the six categories are good to use, but they are 'spectacles', not a 'family tree'.
:::
:::

::: example The ledger of the character “打” {#ex-da}
“打水的打、打伞的打、打电话的打、‘一打铅笔’的打” ('the 打 of drawing water, the 打 of holding an umbrella, the 打 of making a telephone call, the 打 of "a dozen pencils"'). Look the character up in *The Contemporary Chinese Dictionary*, and the senses of “打” run to a dozen and more. Does one character equal one word?
::: solution
It does not. What “打” records is **several morphemes**, alike in sound and alike in shape. The verbal series “打击” ('to strike') is the ledger of the word's own extended meanings. The classifier (measure word) “一打” (a phonetic transcription of *dozen*) is purely a character borrowed for its sound, and has no kinship whatever with the hand radical. The dictionary lists the senses separately according to how the meanings are related, and what cannot be split apart counts as two homographic morphemes. The ideal of morphosyllabic writing, 'one character, one sound, one meaning', is broken here at “打” by homophonic borrowing and by differentiation through extended meaning, both at once. This is the common failing of every mature script: the symbols are finite, language is infinite, and once things are squeezed together there are always identical shapes.
:::

::: intuition What you read is not the character, but the word: an experiment in passing {#intuition-ci}
“研表究明，汉字的序顺并不定一能影阅响读。” At a first reading one notices nothing odd; only on a second look does one find that every word has been pulled apart and scrambled. This is not a joke. It is evidence. A practised reader takes the **word** as the unit of recognition: so long as the outline of the word-shape is there, the order inside it can bluff its way through. The Chinese writing system gives this way of reading a double reason: one character to a morpheme, and words mostly of two syllables, so that visual word-shapes are highly regular. The other way about, the experiment also explains why wrong characters of the “每下八年” sort (“每” is in fact “每”, and “八” is in fact “久” — you most likely failed to notice again) can still survive after three rounds of proofreading. You are not reading characters. You are reading what you expect.
:::

::: remark Writing can also 'cover' language
A literate person, reading their own language, is always looking through the spectacles of graphic form. We feel that “句子” ('sentence') is the natural name of the unit (语＋字?); when we say “他这个人不行” ('he is no good') (Our translation.), what comes up in the mind is a shape, not a voiceprint; even the ledger of phonemes gets rewritten by the written shape — learners from dialect areas often think the final of “对” has 'one u too many', because Pinyin writes duì. A linguist has to wear two pairs of spectacles: language first, writing afterwards; and once writing is on the job, it turns about and assigns how language is perceived. Every point in this chapter condenses into one sentence: **writing is the servant of language, and also the editor of language.**
:::

## A character has three household registers: form, sound and meaning

::: definition The three layers of a character {#def-xingyinyi}
Every Chinese character in circulation carries a file in three layers: the **form layer** (strokes, components, order of writing, and the printed shape), the **sound layer** (the syllable and tone it corresponds to in the language, drifting with the history of pronunciation), and the **meaning layer** (the morpheme meaning and the word meaning it bears, drifting with the history of the vocabulary). Looking a character up, scrutinising its form and correcting its pronunciation are all a matter of consulting the file layer by layer. Stir the three layers together, and that is where common faults of usage are produced. 'The character “果” has not got that dot' (writing regular script out as Song type is a matter of the print layer). '“空” is archaic and elegant only when read in the fourth (falling) tone' (a matter of the sound layer; there is a separate ledger of pronunciation review, see [[sounds-of-mandarin/putonghua-test]]). 'The original meaning of “笨” is the white membrane of bamboo, and therefore…' (philology at the meaning layer, which need not govern today's usage).
:::

::: example Asking layer by layer {#ex-sanceng}
Judge, for each of the following claims, which layer it goes wrong in, or where it holds. (1) The character “日” is round, and the dot in the middle is the sun. (2) “奇” in “奇数” ('odd number') is read jī, not qí. (3) “冏” is a pictograph; its original meaning is “光明” ('brightness'); therefore it 'had this meaning all along'.
::: solution
(1) Half right and half wrong at the form layer. The oracle-bone “日” is indeed a circle with a dot added (a pictograph); the modern form is what the clerical change wore flat. To equate the regular script's 'box with a dot' directly with the 'picture' of three thousand years ago is to step across the layer of script forms. (2) Correct at the sound layer. A character with more than one reading is pronounced according to its meaning; the reading of “奇数” as jī is on the dictionary's books (the pronunciation-review table also enters the reading jī). (3) Correct at the meaning layer, wrong at the pragmatic layer. The original meaning of “冏” does have a source (the *Shuowen jiezi* places it in the “光” class), but its use, twenty years on, as the character “窘” is purely a new life of **homophonic borrowing**. An original meaning cannot govern a borrowed meaning, just as “楷” (楷树, the *kai* tree) cannot govern the everyday ledger of “楷模” ('model') (the question of the phonetic component in [[chinese-characters/phonetic-compounds]] is the same in principle). Checking each layer against its own ledger is the basic skill this course drills again and again.
:::
:::

::: theorem Four rulers for the norms of writing {#rule-sizibiao}
Whether a graphic form can count as 'correct' is measured, in order, by four rulers. **The rationale of the system** (whether the structure accords with the precedents of characters: the upper part of “冒”, for example, ought not to be “曰”). **The tradition of the character dictionaries** (the line *Shuowen jiezi* — *Kangxi Dictionary* — *Xianhan*, that is, *The Contemporary Chinese Dictionary*). **The state's character lists** (the *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters*, with print and handwriting set up as separate standards, [[chinese-characters/standard-characters]]). **The habit of the community** (what has been fixed by convention holds the final veto — the old case of changing the reading of “呆板” to dāibǎn?, and the retirement of the qiǎo reading of “确”, are both long negotiations between habit and the norm). Anyone studying this course must train the awareness that 'all four rulers are present': neither smash what is current with a character dictionary, nor use what is current to cry off the character dictionary.
:::

::: quiz
“汉字是表意文字，所以不反映读音。” ('Chinese characters are ideographic writing, so they do not reflect pronunciation.') (Our translation.) The hole in this sentence lies mainly in ( ).
- [ ] Chinese characters are in fact spelled entirely by sound
- [x] The great majority of Chinese characters contain a phonetic component, and ideographic versus phonetic is a tendency, not a pure breed
- [ ] Only simplified characters are ideographic
- [ ] The hole lies in the word 'reflect'
::: solution
Phono-semantic compounds are the absolute majority, and the phonetic component is precisely the element that records sound ([[#def-jiyi-jiyin]]). Every mature script is a mixed recipe, and the equals sign is where the mistake lies.
:::
:::

::: quiz
“他看不见。” ('He cannot see.') (Our translation.) Rewrite “见” as the homophone “建”, and the sentence will not read. This shows that ( ).
- [ ] Writing Chinese characters is prone to error
- [x] Chinese characters give homophonous morphemes different forms according to meaning, and telling them apart in writing is one of the script's core functions
- [ ] The tones of Putonghua (standard Mandarin) do no distinctive work
- [ ] A wrong character is also a legitimate variant form
::: solution
One sound with many slots, and separate characters keeping separate books, is precisely the house skill of morphosyllabic writing ([[#def-yisuxiyin]]). 'Homophone substitution' was restricted with caution in the simplified-character project (mergers of the “后/後” type are special cases, and they are disputed). In everyday writing it deserves no name but wrong character.
:::
:::

::: history
For the ancients, 'what is writing?' was a question for myth. The story that Cangjie created the characters was already current by the Warring States at the latest (the *Xunzi* and the *Huainanzi* each have a line on it), and the Han further supplied the observational scenario of “见鸟兽蹄迒之迹” ('seeing the tracks of birds and beasts'). The myth itself is very knowing: it hands 'the origin' to one historical person, which shows that the ancients knew writing was **made**. The modern scholar's answer is colder. The incised signs of Jiahu and the pottery signs of Liangzhu are scattered, and do not form a system. Writing is a **collective project**, brought forth over a long time by administration, sacrifice and the art of memory. The oracle-bone inscriptions are already a mature system, and before them there should still be a 'childhood' on the scale of a thousand years ([[chinese-characters/origins]] looks closely at this disputed case). In the twentieth century the question was raised into typology: Andersson (安特生), Ge Laonai (葛劳耐), Tang Lan (唐兰), Qiu Xigui (裘锡圭)… From 'picture–writing' and 'the debate on sound and meaning' to 'a new interpretation of the six categories', each generation answered anew what a Chinese character actually is. What this chapter gives is the consensus in its contemporary textbook version, and the consensus itself is still on duty.
:::

Everyday practice: when you read the newspaper this week, notice three scenes in which 'graphic form covers speech sound' (seeing, for instance, the dispute over “账/帐”, or hearing a misreading of the character “裸”), and bring them to the next chapter.

::: application The table of why wrong characters arise is the negative list of the principles of writing
All the mix-ups of “在/再”, “须/需” and “的/地/得” happen at the interface of **homophonous morphemes**. Because Chinese characters match form to sound, the step of choosing a character is retrieving the form by the sound, and retrieving the wrong one is the error. (This is why a wrong character of this kind is a “别字” and not a “错字”: the characters are all correct; they have been paired wrongly.) Errors such as adding one dot too many to “步”, or writing “肺” with the component of “氾”, happen when components give way at the **form layer**. How the two kinds of error are shared out correlates strongly with age and with the input method. Once a Pinyin input method has automated 'retrieving the form by the sound', wrong characters that look alike grow fewer, and homophonous wrong characters rise. Reports back from the classrooms of teachers of Chinese have long confirmed it: the tool does not change the writing; it only changes how the errors are distributed. Understand this layer, and putting the errors right has a prescription. Homophone errors must **go back to the meaning** (distinguish them by the words they form). Errors of similar form must **go back to the form** (trace the components to their source, the training of [[chinese-characters/strokes-radicals]]).
:::

::: summary
- Writing is a visible symbol system that records language, with its own substance, its own units and its own impetus to change. It is the servant of language, and also the editor of language.
- Chinese characters are classed as morphosyllabic writing: cut by the morpheme, written by the syllable. That is what explains their stability across dialects, and the illusion of 'one character, one meaning'.
- Writing and language fall out of step in three places (substance / speed / tightness). That Chinese characters were not Latinised is the combined answer given by morphemes coming in blocks, the gulf among the dialects, and the strength of the character dictionaries.
- 'Ideographic / phonetic' is a spectrum of tendencies, not a classification into pure breeds. Chinese characters have a mixed recipe (phono-semantic compounds as the main stock, the semantic component fixing the domain).
- A character has three household registers, of form, sound and meaning. The norm is measured, in order, by four rulers: rationale, character dictionaries, character lists and habit.
:::

## Exercises

::: quiz
The most reasonable explanation of 'wrong characters are still there after three rounds of proofreading' is ( ).
- [ ] The proofreader is not good enough
- [x] A practised reader reads by the word and by expectation, and the details of graphic form give way to the word-shape as a whole
- [ ] The forms of Chinese characters are not stable in the first place
- [ ] Printed type and handwriting are different
::: solution
The word-superiority effect, and filling in by expectation ([[#intuition-ci]]): what is read is 'the word-shape as a whole, plus a prediction from the context', and a local reversal does not set off the alarm. The remedy is the opposite of what intuition suggests: read backwards, read character by character, read in a different typeface. Break the path of expectation, and the details become visible again.
:::

::: exercise Judging what is recorded {level=1}
Classify the following units of writing as 'recording a morpheme / recording a syllable / recording a phoneme', and give one example of each: the Chinese character “河”, the Japanese kana ka, the English affix -ing, the Arabic letter ب. Which common kind of unit does this fourfold division (with the third type, the recording of syllables, added) lack?
::: solution
“河” records a morpheme (at the scale of the syllable); ka records a syllable; -ing and ب record phonemes (the former is an affix and also marks a grammatical meaning, and sits at the edge of 'quasi meaning-recording'). The missing kind is the **meaning-recording symbol** (numerals, &, @, gender symbols and other cross-linguistic pieces that record meaning). The main text has already seen it come back to life in “4” ([[#def-jiyi-jiyin]]). The mark depends on whether you can give “-ing” a reason for going either way.
:::
:::

::: exercise Three household registers {level=1}
For each of “特”, “注” and “您”, build a three-layer file (form layer: structure, plus the essentials of stroke order; sound layer: syllable and tone, plus a literary and colloquial reading or an alternative reading; meaning layer: the ledger from the original meaning to the meaning in common use), and for each mark the one place where an error is most likely.
::: solution
A sample, “您”. Form layer: “你＋心”, a top-and-bottom structure, nine strokes. Sound layer: nín, second (rising) tone, no alternative reading. Meaning layer: formed from “你” with a respectful meaning added (“心” marks respect; a compound ideograph); in speech a respectful form of address, and for the plural “您几位”. Easy to get wrong: using “您” as “你们” (the dispute over whether “您们” is legitimate is precisely the semantic ledger of 'a respectful address has no plural'). Set one point firmly on each of the three layers. The mark is given for all three being there.
:::

::: exercise Taking apart the 'theory of evolution' {level=2}
“象形→指事→会意→形声” ('pictograph → indicative → compound ideograph → phono-semantic compound') is often told as an evolutionary ladder of Chinese characters, from lower to higher. Using the view of this chapter, list three holes in that narrative, and in each blank put one concrete character or fact.
::: solution
(1) Phono-semantic compounds already exist in large numbers in the oracle bones. Water-names such as “河” and “洹” take their sound from 水, and people of the Shang already knew how to assemble 'semantic component + phonetic component', so the ladder's rung, 'purely pictographic at the early stage', does not hold. (2) Meaning-recording characters are few and hard to add to, and the sound-recording road is the main force of expansion. 'Evolution' is a drift in the proportions, not an upgrade of ability. (3) 'Lower / higher' implies a teleology with 'phonetic spelling as the end point'. That Chinese characters did not take the phonetic-spelling road has structural reasons ([[#rule-butongbu]] and [[#proof-hanzi-pinyin]]), and it is nothing to do with intelligence. Citing a character is enough to score. There is no need to take a side, either 'Chinese characters are superior' or 'Chinese characters are backward'.
:::

::: exercise Reading the statistics off the graph {level=2}
On this chapter's 'graph of the climbing share of phono-semantic compounds', write a graph-reading report of two hundred characters. Make clear what kind of source the data have (the counts of the various scholars), why the oracle-bone proportion is the least reliable, and how 'disyllabification' pushed phono-semantic compounds to accelerate.
::: solution
The main points. (1) Scholars differ in how many deciphered characters they count, and in the standard by which they classify, so that the same batch of material can be made to yield a gap of more than ten percentage points. A citation must note the version. (2) The proportion of oracle-bone graphs that have been identified is low, and they are mostly proper nouns (personal names and clan names), so the sample is skewed. (3) After the vocabulary became disyllabic, new words formed their characters by 'assembling morphemes', and the phono-semantic template (choose a semantic component, and choose a phonetic component) is the only production line. Full marks go to any conclusion written as 'about' or 'the usual account', with the source left in a footnote. That is itself the norm of reading a graph which this chapter teaches.
:::
:::

::: exercise Judging the case with the four rulers {level=3}
After the pronunciation review of 1985, “骑” has the unified reading qí, and the old reading jì was abolished; in recent years, in the recitation of poetry, “一骑（jì）红尘” ('one rider, the red dust') (Our translation.) has become a trending search again. “帧” has the unified reading zhēn, but the film and television trade still commonly reads zhèng, as in “一帧”. For each of these two cases, write an adjudication of one hundred and fifty characters, using the four rulers — rationale, character dictionaries, character lists and habit — and say how you would rule if you were the one on duty.
::: solution
A line one might follow. “骑”: at the rationale layer, the old reading jì has a source in the rhyme dictionaries (the sense “乘人”, 'a mounted person', has its own *fanqie* spelling); at the character-list layer, the unified reading qí in the *Pronunciation Review Table* is already the final judgement; at the habit layer, the public pronunciation was qí all along. The revived jì is an 'old-pronunciation meme', not a living usage. Examinations and broadcasting follow qí. In the setting of classical chanting it may be kept as a curiosity and left unjudged, and it must not be called 'the only correct reading'. “帧”: at the rationale layer, zhēn has a basis; at the character-list layer, the unified reading is zhēn; at the habit layer, zhèng in professional circles has already taken shape. A technical term may run on two tracks (the trade pronunciation and the standard pronunciation each govern one stretch), just as the literary and the colloquial readings of “血” are tacitly allowed. The mark looks at whether the four rulers are each brought into court, and whether the conclusion can be carried out, not at taste.
:::
