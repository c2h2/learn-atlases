---
title: "Traditional and simplified characters"
---

‘Simple’ and ‘complex’ are not an opposition that began only today: across three thousand years the Chinese character has **tended overall towards simplicity**, and the simplified characters are only the newest wave on that long current, and the most institutionalised. This chapter answers four questions: what a simplified character is (it has existed since antiquity); what the schemes of the 1950s ‘simplified’, and how they simplified it; how the **loss of information** caused by merger is made good; and the right way to approach the relation between simplified and traditional.

## Definition: simplified, traditional and variant are three concepts {#sec-dingyi}

A **simplified character** is a way of writing whose strokes are pared down from the standard form previously in use — 国 as against 國, 从 as against 從. Their figures were already there in the Song and Yuan *pínghuà* 平话 ('vernacular tales'), in non-standard characters cut on steles, and in the ledgers of trading houses (the older learning called them 俗字, 'popular forms'). A ‘traditional character’, for its part, means the way of writing which corresponds to a simplified form of the standard now in force, and which for a long stretch of history served as the standard form. The two are **two regulated forms of one and the same grapheme**: to say ‘traditional’ is not to say ‘orthodox’, and to say ‘simplified’ is not to say ‘newly made’.

::: definition Sorting variant characters is another track {#def-yiti}
A **variant character** is a different writing of the same character, the forms existing side by side at one time (“峰峯、畧略、詠咏” are all such). The principle of sorting is ‘keep one, and weed out the rest’. The *First List for Sorting Variant Characters* (《第一批异体字整理表》) of 1955 eliminated more than a thousand variant forms (as in “峯”歸“峰”, “畧”歸“略”, “麄”歸“粗”). What it governs is **competition among the writings of one character**; what the *Scheme for Simplifying Chinese Characters* (《汉字简化方案》) governs is **the merger of traditional and simplified graphemes**. The two matters are often talked of as one. A fair part of the simplified characters (such as “灶、尘、体”) is at the same time a result of the sorting of variants — they were already on the list of candidates.
:::

::: theorem Simplification is not an invention of 1956, but a seventy-year relay {#rule-jiejli}
In 1922 Lu Feikui published ‘Opinions on Simplifying Chinese Characters’ (《汉字简化的意见》), and opened formal discussion in intellectual circles; in 1931 compilations such as Xu Zemin’s *Draft of Commonly Used Simplified Characters* (《常用简体字稿》) appeared; in August 1935 the Ministry of Education of the National Government promulgated the *First List of Simplified Characters* (《第一批简体字表》, 324 characters), and **it was soon withdrawn the following year because of controversy**; in 1936 the Preparatory Committee for the Unification of the National Language drew up the *Register of Simplified Characters* (《简体字谱》), containing more than a thousand characters, filed it, and did not put it into force. In the 1950s the Committee for Reforming the Chinese Written Language took the work over and made a project of it: in January 1956 the State Council promulgated the *Scheme for Simplifying Chinese Characters* (《汉字简化方案》; 227 simplified characters and 14 simplified components, brought in by stages, the introduction completed in 1958); from 1959 to 1964 supplements and corrections followed in turn; in May 1964 the *General List of Simplified Characters* (《简化字总表》) was promulgated, containing **2236 characters** in all. In 1977 the *Second Scheme for Simplifying Chinese Characters (Draft)* (《第二次汉字简化方案（草案）》) was tried out, and **in June 1986 the State Council abolished it by explicit order**; in the same year the *General List of Simplified Characters*, sorted afresh, was reissued as the basis now in force; in 2013 the *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters* (《通用规范汉字表》, 8105 characters) gathered the whole together. In short: the natural history of simplified characters is nearly a thousand years, their statutory history nearly a hundred, and along the way there were also two sudden stops, a withdrawal and an abolition.
:::

## The seven methods of simplification {#sec-shoufa}

::: definition A catalogue of methods of formation {#def-shoufa}
① **Reusing ancient characters**: an early original character, or a form in the ancient script, is brought back into use — “云（雲）、网（網）、从（從）、礼（禮，《说文》以“礼”为古文）” (the *Shuowen jiezi*, *Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*, takes “礼” as the ancient-script form); ② **Adopting popular forms**: the folk saving of strokes since the Song and Yuan — “体、坛、惊、灶、护、处、电、开、丧”; ③ **Regularising cursive into regular script**: the outline of running and cursive is turned into regular-script strokes — “书、长、东、乐、与、龟、鸟、门”; ④ **Homophone substitution (phonetic loan)**: an existing simplified character is taken to record a traditional character as well — “谷（穀）、余（餘）、面（麪）、只（隻）”; ⑤ **Replacing the phonetic component**: the old phonetic is exchanged for one with fewer strokes — “护（護→户声）、认（認→人声）、惊（驚→京声）、灯（燈→丁声）、邻（鄰→令声）、远（遠→元声）” (户, 人, 京, 丁, 令 and 元 as the new phonetics); ⑥ **Omitting components**: “习（習）、声（聲）、术（朮）、岛（島）、们（們）”; ⑦ **Symbol substitution (conversion into marks)**: simple marks that indicate neither sound nor meaning replace components in batches — the “又” family (对、戏、欢、仅、劝) and the “乂” family (赵、凤、这、还).
:::

::: example An appraisal, character by character {#ex-jianding}
Decide which method each of “国、习、护、灯” uses, and explain why one character can be entered under two or three headings at once.
::: solution
“国”: a popular form (already in the Tang and the Five Dynasties one meets “国” written as the reduced form with “玉” inside 囗, and it also carries a tinge of a new reading as a compound ideograph, an associative compound) — chiefly ②; “习”: the standard form in the *Shuowen jiezi* is “習，数飞也” ('習 is to fly repeatedly.' (Our translation.)), and “习” is a popular form with strokes omitted — ②＋⑥; “护”: a new phono-semantic compound (semantic-phonetic compound), “護” changing the phonetic 蒦 to the phonetic 户 — chiefly ⑤, and with a source in a popular form as well; “灯”: “燈” changes the phonetic 登 to the phonetic 丁 — ⑤. That one character should involve several methods is nothing to wonder at: **the layer of origin (where, in history, it came from) and the layer of operation (how the scheme treated it) are two systems of coordinates**. The great majority of the entries in the simplification scheme stand at the meeting-point of ‘a popular writing long in circulation’ and ‘one statutory recognition by those who did the sorting’.
:::

## Merger cases and the trap of ‘returning to traditional’ {#sec-hebing}

::: example Converting nine high-frequency merger pairs to traditional {#ex-jiudui}
Convert the following words to traditional characters: 头发、发展、干净、干戈、乾隆、皇后、谷子、余数、叶子.
::: solution
頭髮／發展／乾淨／干戈／乾隆／皇后／穀子／餘數／葉子. Four points. **Fix the character by the sense** — “乾” read in the gān meaning (乾燥、乾淨) is simplified as “干”, while what is read qián (乾隆) is retained; the “幹” of 幹才 and of 枝幹 is simplified as “干”, and it and “乾” each go their own road; “后” is the original character (皇后、后土) and was borrowed to record the “後” of “前後”, so that in converting to traditional one restores it only at “前後、後來”; the “穀” of “穀物” goes to “穀”, and the “谷” of “山谷” remains what it is. For the nine pairs there is no other method. What is memorised is **the boundary of the merger**, not the whole table.
:::
:::

::: theorem What the mergers lost: a problem in arithmetic {#rule-suan}
The source characters taken over by ten high-frequency merging simplified forms: “发” takes over 髮 and 發; “干” takes over (the original character 干, and) 乾 and 幹; “后” takes over 後; “谷” takes over 穀; “面” takes over 麪; “台” takes over 臺、檯 and 颱; “叶” takes over 葉; “余” takes over 餘; “郁” takes over 鬱; “涂” takes over 塗 — **fourteen characters, more or less**, in all. What is lost is entirely **distinctions among homophones at the level of writing**: the pronunciation has not changed, the meanings of the words have not changed, and a disyllabic context can restore nearly all of them (“頭髮” does not meet “發展”, and “山谷” does not meet “五穀”). Only two domains still really need a compensation of their own: the inscriptions and colophons of calligraphy, and the conversion of old books and of personal and place names — and, besides, one statistical fact: in everyday reading the actual rate of ambiguity of merged characters is **far below** what public opinion imagines.
:::

::: warning Three confusions under the slogan ‘restore traditional characters’
(1) **Taking variant forms for simplified and traditional**: “峰／峯” is a competition between variants, and simplified and traditional have nothing to do with it; (2) **Taking a phonetic loan character (*tongjia*) for a conflation**: the merger of “云／雲” only restores a phonetic-loan relation (假借) already present in the classics. It is not ‘the original meaning of “云气” has been destroyed’ — “云” in the sense of “雲”, a wrong character for a thousand years, became the normal state of the two streams joining; (3) **Taking surnames for errors**: the old surname “范” and the surname “範” were originally two families; after simplification they have the same form, and a search of genealogies and of biographies on stone must go back to the original character. A misconception in the reverse direction is also to be guarded against: the supposition that simplified characters are all ‘new creations out of a rupture’ — “礼、从、网、处” all come from the ancient-script forms of the *Shuowen jiezi* and from a thousand years of popular writing. Between simplified characters and the ancient stock of characters the relation is one of **inheritance**, not of a break. Before the policy is evaluated, these three accounts are to be kept apart.
:::

::: intuition The historical place of the view that Chinese characters are backward
In the 1920s–1940s, Qian Xuantong, Zhao Yuanren (Yuen Ren Chao), Li Jinxi and others advocated cutting the complex down to the simple, and even Latinisation. The background was an anxiety about the efficiency of learning to read, under a crisis of the country’s ruin — the illiteracy rate, on the adult measure, was then reckoned at eight tenths, and the radical arguments had their historical justification (for the line from *qièyīn* characters, 切音字, ‘characters that spell by cutting the sound’, to the ‘revolution in Chinese characters’, see [[sounds-of-mandarin/syllables-pinyin]]). The simplification of the 1950s was this line made institutional. The reckoning today: simplification did lower the threshold for the beginner (the fall in the average number of strokes of common characters, and the shortening of the time taken to learn to read, are measurable facts), while the cost was the distinctions, at the level of writing, among meanings of the merger type, and a ‘sense of a fault line’ in historical texts. To call it ‘the destruction of tradition’, or ‘a perfect piece of engineering’, is inaccurate either way — **a standardisation with gains and with losses, open to repair, and not in need of being pulled down**.
:::

::: example “游／遊” and “余／餘”: the fates of two kinds of merger {#ex-liangzhong}
Is the standard writing of “游览” “游览” or “遊览”? What relation holds between the “余” of “余暇” and the first-person “余”?
::: solution
In the sorting of variants in 1955, “遊” was eliminated as a variant of “游”. Flowing water, swimming, travel and sightseeing are all written “游” — this is a **merger of variant forms on one file**, not a merger of simplified and traditional. The second question is a trap: the first-person “余” is the original character and remains so, while “餘” drops the “人” component and is merged into “余” — and thus “余” holds two posts, the original character of the personal pronoun plus the simplified form of “餘”. When one converts to traditional, the personal “余” is still written “余”, and “其餘、年餘、業餘” are in every case restored as “餘”. **The short cut, in a merger case, to the ‘boundary of returning to traditional’ is to check ‘what existed of old’**: a merger in which an ancient character holds an extra post (后、余、云) always raises a question of the original character, and the rules for returning to traditional must be memorised character by character.
:::
:::

## A world in which simplified and traditional coexist {#sec-bingcun}

::: history The rise and fall of the second-scheme characters: anatomy of a radical experiment {#sec-erjian}
The *Second Scheme for Simplifying Chinese Characters (Draft)* (《第二次汉字简化方案（草案）》), promulgated in December 1977, pushed the logic of the first scheme to its limit: go on saving strokes, go on converting forms into marks, go on annexing homophones. Some textbooks, reference works and the typesetting of the press tried it for a short period. The problems showed at once: the system of character-forms had not settled — mastheads, schoolbooks and the moulds for lead type were each altered in their own way, and the copybooks of a generation of pupils and the lead type of the publishing world did not recognise one another. At the same time it touched the old lesion of the merger-type simplified characters: **merge once more, and the chance of setting the meaning back in place is gone too**. In June 1986 the State Council approved and forwarded the national commission’s ‘Request for Instructions on Republishing the *General List of Simplified Characters*’ (国家委, 《关于重新发表〈简化字总表〉的请示》), expressly ordered the second scheme abolished, and reissued the general list, sorted afresh, as the sole basis. Of the legacy of the ‘second simplification’, mainly two things remain today: in popular handwriting, an inertia of saving strokes that will not go away (as when “副” is reduced to a temporary popular form of that sort — it is not within the norm), and a lesson in the history of policy: **the cost of changing a standard of the script is counted in decades, and any impulse to ‘simplify once more’ must first answer the account of the system’s stability**.
:::

- [ ] 324 characters

::: solution
The general list of 1964 contains 2236 simplified characters (including the series of characters inferred from the simplified components), and the reissue of 1986 carried that figure on. 324 is the first list of the Republican period, of 1935; 227 is the strict count in the scheme of 1956; 8105 is the total number of characters in the *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters* of 2013.

::: quiz
Which of the following belongs to the method of ‘replacing the phonetic component’? ( )
- [ ] 书（書）
- [x] 灯（燈）
- [ ] 谷（穀）
- [ ] 习（習）
::: solution
“灯” replaces the phonetic “登” with “丁”, a typical change of phonetic component. “书” is cursive regularised into regular script, “谷” is homophone substitution, and “习” is a popular form with strokes omitted.

Of the traditional writings corresponding to the three words “乾净、乾隆、枝干”, the correct one is ( ).
- [ ] 乾净、乾隆、枝幹
- [x] 乾淨、乾隆、枝幹
- [ ] 幹净、乾陆、枝干
- [ ] 乾净、乾陆、枝干

::: exercise Classifying by method {level=1}
File the following simplified characters under the seven kinds of method: “网、乐、认、欢、术、处、远”.
::: solution
网 — reusing ancient characters; 乐 — regularising cursive into regular script; 认 — replacing the phonetic component; 欢 — symbol substitution (the “又” family); 术 — omitting components (“朮” is borrowed for “術”, and strokes are then omitted); 处 — adopting a popular form (“處” in the popular form “处”); 远 — replacing the phonetic component (袁 → 元). Marking allows two accounts to stand together for a particular character, provided the answer can justify itself.
:::

::: exercise Finding the errors {level=1}
A simplified–traditional conversion exercise contains three wrong examples: ① “頭髮” written as “头發”; ② “乾隆” written as “干隆”; ③ the word meaning ‘what is left over’ written as “餘下”. Point out where each goes wrong, and which type of error it belongs to.
::: solution
① The merger was not divided by household — “髮” should be simplified as “发”; ② after 干／乾／幹 had been joined three into one, a wrongful merging — the reign title “乾隆” cannot be written “干”; ③ an error in the reverse direction: “餘下”, simplified, should be “余下”; here what ought to be traditional has been simplified. Names of the error-types: for a merged character, **not separated where it should have been separated**, and **joined where it should not have been joined**.
:::

::: exercise Converting to traditional in practice {level=2}
Convert the following short sentence, in the manner of an inscription on stone, into traditional characters (decide for yourself what to adopt and what to drop, and explain): “敕建后土庙，乾隆九年重修，谷旦敬书。” 'The Houtu Temple, built by imperial order, was rebuilt in the ninth year of Qianlong; respectfully written at 谷旦.' (Our translation.)
::: solution
“敕建后土庙，乾隆九年重修，穀旦敬书（書）”. 'The Houtu Temple, built by imperial order, was rebuilt in the ninth year of Qianlong; respectfully written (書) on a good dawn.' (Our translation.) The difficult points: ① “后土” is the original-character name of a god, and **is not written “後”** — the classic touchstone of a merger case; ② the phrase “谷旦” comes from the allusion to “穀旦” in the ‘Airs of Chen’ of the *Book of Songs* (善旦, a good dawn), and when an inscription follows the old forms it should be restored as “穀旦”; ③ if the whole sentence follows the old forms, “书” is written “書”, while “敕、修、敬” are all original characters and are unchanged. What the marking of a conversion to traditional looks at is **awareness of the boundaries**: an original character is not altered without cause, and a merged character is checked word by word.
:::

::: exercise A survey of the data {level=2}
In the texts you have written or typed over the past week or so, count how often the merged characters appear (发、干、后、谷、面、台、叶、余、郁、涂), and spot-check five instances: if they were converted to traditional, would there be a risk of ambiguity? Write a summary of two hundred characters.
::: solution
A passing answer must give a real count (even if the sample is only a hundred or so characters), a judgement, instance by instance, of ‘restorable / at risk’ for the five examples (for instance the “干” of “晾干” is restored as “乾”, and that of “干才、躯干” is restored as “幹” — in everyday collocations a person resolves this automatically, but a machine needs a word-list), and a conclusion: the ambiguity of merged characters hides mainly in **low-frequency disyllabic frames** (is the “干” of “干系” the original character? The simplification of “關係” as “关系” is another case of variants merged on one file).

::: exercise A short essay: can the dispute over simplified and traditional be had both ways? {level=3}
Compromise proposals such as ‘recognise the traditional and write the simplified’ and ‘write the traditional and hear the simplified’ have circulated for several decades. Write a short essay of from eight hundred to twelve hundred characters: where is the workable boundary of a dual track, simplified and traditional? You must use each of this chapter’s three concepts, ‘method / merger / variant’, once, and answer one opposing view.
::: solution
Points for marking: the three concepts are used accurately (‘recognise the traditional and write the simplified’ involves the distinction between **grapheme and regulated form**; those who argue for ‘having it both ways’ mostly ground their case in the reading of old books and in calligraphy, and one must check whether the ‘sense of a fault line’ is really caused by simplification — texts in traditional characters are hard for people today to read as well, and the statistical fact in this chapter may be used to press the question); the essay can distinguish a problem of class hours (the ability to read traditional characters) from a problem of institutional uniformity (which set of characters one writes); the reply to the other side is in earnest (no straw man); there is no hard error of fact (a mistake in a year, in the name of a table, or in a number of characters loses marks at each such place).
:::

::: summary
Simplified forms are not new creations: a long current of popular forms across nearly a thousand years, plus a seventy-year relay from 1922 to 1956; the *General List of Simplified Characters* of 1964, with 2236 characters, reissued in 1986 and kept in use, and in 2013 taken into the *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters* of 8105 characters; the ‘second simplification’ tried in 1977 and abolished in 1986. Seven methods: reusing the ancient, adopting the popular, regularising cursive, borrowing by sound, replacing the phonetic, omitting, and converting into marks. Merger-type simplified characters (发干后谷面台叶余郁涂, taking over fourteen source characters, more or less) lose only distinctions among homophones at the level of writing; a disyllabic context basically restores them, and the heavy damage is in inscriptions and in conversion. The sorting of variants, the merger of simplified and traditional, and phonetic loan ancient and modern are three accounts to be reckoned apart; in the reality of simplified and traditional existing together, ‘fix the character by the sense, and check the boundary of the merger’ is the whole of the technique.
:::
