'Reading one half' (读半边) is something teachers of Chinese have scolded for decades, yet linguists want to put in a fair word for it: **reading one half was, in the age of character-creation, basically accurate**; what is not accurate is three thousand years of sound change calling the account in. The phonetic component (phonophore) is a set of phonological archives buried in the system of Chinese characters — a person who can read it learns characters twice as fast, guesses an old pronunciation and is sure of it nine times out of ten, and even has an extra ear when memorising English onomatopoeia. This chapter does three things: it 'establishes title' for the phonetic component (on what ground it recorded this sound at the time), it 'opens a case' on what is 'not accurate' (a table of correspondences under the sound laws), and it draws the line for use (when one may read one half, and when reading one half is simply a wrong character).

## Establishing title for the phonetic component: what a phonetic series is

::: definition Phonetic series {#def-xiesheng}
A set of phono-semantic compounds (semantic-phonetic compounds) derived from the same phonetic element (a sound-symbol) constitutes a **phonetic series** (*xiéshēng* 谐声): for example the 青 family (清, 请, 静, 精, 睛) and the 也 family (地, 池, 驰, 施, 蛇). Within a series, in the **age of character-creation**, initial and rhyme were the same or extremely close, and the rhyme groups, for their part, are almost always the same group — this is the grand conclusion of the statistics on more than two thousand series since Karlgren (高本汉). The phonetic series is a core source of data for the history of Chinese: the reconstruction of the Old Chinese initial system (the dispute over consonant clusters rests on it entirely), the corroborating evidence of rhyming in the *Book of Songs*, and the points at which dialects and outward transcriptions link up, all lie on this one table ([[phonology/old-chinese]]).
:::

::: theorem Three ways a phonetic component is born {#rule-chusheng}
Characters taken to serve as phonetic elements are born in three ways. **Sound-borrowing characters** (originally there was no character for the word, so a homophonous character was written: 也, 它). **Differentiation within a family** (the older and the newer word come from one source, and the making of characters carries the line on: 干 is the original character for the meaning 'bank', and 旱 is the differentiated character for 'dry', with the sound continued as the same; the original meaning is borrowed away, and the original character is left behind to record the sound). **Pure recording of sound** (when a phono-semantic compound is created, a character of similar sound is chosen directly and hung up as the signboard: 青, 工). The three births explain two temperaments of the phonetic component. One is that **a near sound is enough** (they need not be identical; dialect, and the pronunciation of the time, allow a margin). The other is that **meaning often has a ghost in it** (a phonetic component that comes from differentiation within a family conveniently carries a little meaning along with it: the reasonable kernel of what is called 'there is meaning in the sound', and the way in to etymology; but if one pushes it on to the 'right-hand element' (*yòuwén* 右文) of every character, one slides into looking at a picture and telling a story).
:::

::: example One family, four initials {#ex-gui}
圭 is read *guī*, yet its kinsmen have scattered four ways: 珪 *guī*, 闺 *guī*, 桂 *guì*, 眭 *suī*, 畦 *qí*, 娃 *wá*, 鞋 *xié*, 街 *jiē*. Explain this scattering by the phonetic-series law and the correspondence law.
::: solution
First establish the title. In the *xiesheng* age (Old Chinese) the initials of this family all dwelt together in the *jian* series (the *k* class) — the zero initial, *x* and *j* in the modern readings of 畦, 娃, 鞋 and 街 are the forks of historical sound change under each final's own environment. Fine finals of the *jian* initial palatalise out onto the *j* and *x* roads (the changes of 街, 鞋 and 眭 each have their own scholarly lineage, and reconstructions in the field do not agree), while before closed broad finals the *g*/*k* road is kept (珪, 闺, 桂). 圭 read *guī* is, on the contrary, the conservative party within the family. The conclusion: **same family ≠ same modern pronunciation; same family ＝ one stretch of historical recording that has been sliced.** The phonetic component does not recognise a Pinyin scheme; it recognises the syllable of three thousand years ago.
:::
:::

## The regularities of what is not accurate: sound change keeps the law

::: theorem A law of correspondence, not a law of single cases {#rule-duiying}
The bulk of the deviation between the modern pronunciation and the phonetic element follows **regular correspondence**. (1) **Palatalisation** — *g*, *k* and *h*, before fine finals (*i*, *ü*, and finals beginning with *i* or *ü*), become *j*, *q* and *x*. The classic example is **the pair '工 *gōng* — 江 *jiāng*'**: both are the *jian* initial; before a broad final it stays at its post, and before a fine final it changes its surname. '家 (the *fanqie* 古牙切) *jiā* — 古 *gǔ*' proves the point once more. One and the same ancestor divides the household according to the environment of the final, and the phonetic component registers the descendants of both forks on the family genealogy. (2) **Devoicing of voiced initials**: the fully voiced initials of Middle Chinese were devoiced by the rule 'level tone aspirates, oblique tones do not', and within one family the split by level and oblique tones produces a difference of aspiration (皮, a level tone, aspirated *p-*; in its family, 被 and 坡, once set on the road of devoicing, each went its own way). (3) **Merger and split of rhyme groups**: successive mergers of main vowels and codas leave the finals of one family, today, with only a 'relation of kinship' and not a 'relation of the same rhyme'. Once the three laws have been gone through, the small remainder of 'reading it inaccurately' is the true exceptions (popular readings, gloss readings (*xùndú* 训读), and characters rewritten to match a dialect pronunciation).
:::

::: proof Why sound change can 'keep to the rules' like this
Because sound change acts on **a class of sounds**, not on a single character. When northern speech decided that '*g*, *k* and *h*, on meeting *i*, must palatalise', it did not consult a dictionary, and it did not separate familiar characters from unfamiliar ones; it carried the change out across the board — unless blocked by conditions such as a separating sound, the neutral tone, or a borrowed character (what the theory of lexical diffusion discusses is precisely 'how neatly the change is carried out'). This is the load-bearing wall of historical linguistics: **sound laws have no exceptions** (the slogan of the Neogrammarians). An exception either reveals a deeper condition (the phonemic environment, analogy) or is not a sound change at all (a gloss reading, a borrowing). The phonetic series is the finest autopsy report on this wall: the 'disorder' in two thousand groups of series can almost all be lined up into a few dozen correspondences, and the few that remain have become celebrated cases in the field (for example certain disputes over mutual *xiesheng* contact between the *xiao* initial and the *ming* initial).
:::

::: example When one may read one half {#ex-bianbian}
Grade the following cases of 'reading one half' by the modern standard pronunciation. ① 诣 read as 旨. ② 栈 read as 戋. ③ 酗 read as 凶. ④ 眈 read as 尤 — 眈 takes its sound from 冘; the modern pronunciation of 冘 is *yín*, while the standard reading of 眈 is *dān*.
::: solution
① Wrong. The standard pronunciation of 诣 is *yì*, and initial and rhyme are both far from 旨 *zhǐ*; it is a hard exception outside the three laws (look up 五礼切 in the *Guangyun* 《广韵》, and the account can be traced). ② Wrong, but close to reasonable. 栈 *zhàn* and 戋 *jiān* are close in final and different in initial (in Old Chinese they met as a *duan*–*yuan* type; in Middle Chinese each went off down its own road), and analogy can reach only as far as 'a near sound'. ③ A typical popular reading. The standard pronunciation of 酗 is *xù*; reading it *xiōng* is a high-frequency accident in the media, pulled off course by the shape of 凶. ④ 眈 *dān* takes its sound from 冘, yet both initial and final have moved, and reading one half is necessarily wrong. Three pumps at which the rate of support falls away: **rare characters, a break in the record, popular readings**. The mnemonic in one sentence: **recognise a character as the whole character, and guess a sound from the whole family; a single character is not to be trusted, but a series may be cited in evidence.**
:::
:::

What the student is to carry away is **'a divergent reading can be looked up, and is not a thing to be startled at'** — a widening of the phonetic distance within a family is the normal state; whether, once it has widened, there is a track to follow is the point that matters.

::: history
The study of the phonetic component is almost the same age as the *Shuowen jiezi* (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*): Xu Shen's 许慎 terminology '从某、某声' ('from such-and-such, with such-and-such as the sound') is the first general registration of the study of *xiesheng*. In the Qing, Duan Yucai's 段玉裁 single sharp shout, '同谐声者必同部' ('Those that share a phonetic must be in the same rhyme group.') (Our translation.), in the *Liushu yinyun biao* 《六书音均表》, pushed the division of the ancient rhymes from ten groups to seventeen, and the phonetic series formally became legitimate evidence for reconstructing old pronunciations. From 1923 Karlgren tested the Old Chinese initials against tables of more than two thousand series; Zeng Yunqian 曾运乾, Lu Zhiwei 陆志韦, Dong Tonghe 董同龢 (Tung T'ung-ho) and Li Fanggui 李方桂 (Li Fang-kuei) added to the work in turn, and the consonant-cluster hypothesis (types such as *kl- and *ml-) went from a clap of thunder to ordinary talk. In the contemporary system of Zhengzhang Shangfang 郑张尚芳, Baxter (白一平) and Sagart (沙加尔), phonetic series are examined at a joint hearing of three parties — comparison with Tibeto-Burman, archaic layers preserved in the dialects, and the pronunciations outside China in the Chinese-character cultural sphere — and from the single character 各 one can write half a scholarly history of Old Chinese. In one sentence: **the phonetic component is the key that Chinese characters left for linguistics.**
:::

::: definition The phonetic-series law {#def-xielü}
Two statistical facts. **(1)** In the *xiesheng* age, characters with the same phonetic element had the same initial, or an extremely close one (the same place of articulation and the same manner is the usual case; accommodation between neighbouring initials is the exception). **(2)** Their rhyme groups are almost necessarily the same (or stand in a strict *duìzhuǎn* 对转). 'Almost necessarily' is a quantified conclusion: those who have tested the more than nine thousand characters of the *Shuowen* systematically find that the proportion whose rhyme group falls outside the set is short of a few per cent, and that these are concentrated in characters of late origin and in characters corrupted in transmission. These two facts are the licence on which 'linkage by phonetic series' may be used as evidence for old pronunciations, and they are also the mathematical foundation of 'reading one half' being, on some occasions, 'accurate for no reason one can name'.
:::

::: intuition The phonetic component accelerates character-learning for children and for foreigners, and it is also their trap
When a seven-year-old learns 请, 清, 晴 and 睛, the child spontaneously concludes that 'the right-hand side is read *qing*', and efficiency shoots up — this is the natural acquisition of a folk phonetic-series law. Afterwards the child produces errors of the type 'the 青 of 青色 is read *jīn*', and is then corrected. Native speakers' trust in the phonetic component peaks in the third and fourth years of primary school, is knocked down in the fifth year by 'marks deducted for a misreading', and for the rest of their lives they half believe and half doubt. The aim of this course is to rebuild **an adult's trust**: to know the phonetic component's period of validity (Old Chinese to Middle Chinese), to know the exchange-rate table of the three laws, and to know the way in for checking. The consensus of research on the teaching of character recognition is broadly this: learners with a strong awareness of phonetic components, and correctly guided, are clearly ahead both in the hit rate for guessing the reading of a rare character and in memory for word families — the tool is not guilty; what misleads is 'teaching only what is accurate, and not teaching what is not'.
:::

::: example Take the measure of your intuition {#ex-直觉}
Without looking in a dictionary, and relying on the phonetic component, fix the pronunciation of the following characters (the tone counts as part of the sound): 兑, 炬, 殉, 诣, 粒, 帖. Then look them up in the *Xianhan* 《现汉》 and check your answers.
::: solution
Common answers from inside one's head: 兑 *duì* (right), 炬 *jù* (right), 殉 *xùn* (right; the phonetic element 旬 *xún* differs only in tone), 诣 *yì* (most people misread it *zhǐ*), 粒 *lì* (right; 立 is the same sound), 帖 *tiē*/*tiě*/*tiè* (three readings; the phonetic element 占 gives only an approximation). Five to one: this is precisely the true water-line of an adult's trust in the phonetic component. Keep this question as a 'medical check'. From now on, with every unfamiliar character, guess first and look it up afterwards; in a month you will recalibrate what you expect of this employee of three thousand years' standing.
:::
:::

## Application: the statistics of recognising characters, and a curiosity about etymology

::: application Four pronunciations from a difference of one character: a lesson on phonetic components in pronunciation order
祗 *zhī* (respect), 祇 *qí* (an earth spirit; also read *zhǐ*), 柢 *dǐ* (root, the fundamental), 低 *dī*, 纸 *zhǐ* — the right-hand half has been tangled for a thousand years between the two dots of 氏 and 氐 (the 'distinction of 氐 and 氏'), and a difference of one character draws out four initials. It is exactly a holographic sample of the zone where linkage by the phonetic component fails. When one looks a character up by pronunciation order in a dictionary, recognising the component correctly ＝ choosing the reading correctly ＝ landing on the right page; writing the component wrongly ＝ the character is not there to be found. In the same way, mistaking 部署 for 布署, and 脉络 for 脉洛, is half of it 'reading one half' running out of control when one writes from memory. **Upgrading the phonetic component from a crutch for recognising characters into a tool for linking them** (memorise one family, bring a string of them along, and when one is wrong check three) is the bill that this course makes up on behalf of the Chinese class.
:::

A suggestion for practice: for each of the five families 也, 工, 青, 各 and 圭, list twenty characters, mark the modern initials, count the proportion 'accurate / inaccurate', and try to sort them under the correspondence law. When you have finished this lesson, your attitude to 'reading one half' will have become your attitude to an old person: respect, but do not believe everything.

::: summary
- A phonetic series ＝ the family of characters derived from the same phonetic element; in the age of character-creation, initial and rhyme were the same or extremely close, and the rhyme groups are almost constantly the same.
- The three births of the phonetic component (sound-borrowing, differentiation within a family, pure recording of sound) explain its two temperaments, 'a near sound is enough' and 'meaning often has a ghost in it'.
- The bulk of 'reading inaccurately' in the modern pronunciation follows regular correspondence: palatalisation, devoicing of voiced initials, and merger and split of rhyme groups; only the exceptions are the way in to real learning.
- Sound laws have no exceptions, plus lexical diffusion: the phonetic component is the autopsy report on how law-abiding sound change is.
- The practical stance: recognise a character as the whole character, and guess a sound from the whole family; reading one half is a statistical tool, not a privilege.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Taking stock of a series {level=1}
Write six characters each for the phonetic series with the sound 工 and the phonetic series with the sound 主, mark the modern pronunciations, and calculate the proportion that agree completely with the modern pronunciation of the phonetic element.
::: solution
The 工 family: 功 *gōng*, 攻 *gōng*, 贡 *gòng*, 恐 *kǒng* (the lower part is from 心), 项? not of the family — replace it with 紅 *hóng*, 空 *kōng*, 控 *kòng*. The 主 family: 注 *zhù*, 住 *zhù*, 柱 *zhù*, 蛀 *zhù*, 炷 *zhù*, 砫? replace it with 徏? replace it with 珠 *zhū* and 蛛 *zhū*. The proportion in complete agreement (wholly the same as *gōng*/*zhǔ*): for the 工 family, 2/8≈25%; for the 主 family, depending on the difference of tone between 主 *zhǔ* and 注 *zhù*, a range of 0–30%. The real aim of this question: **measure once, with your own hands, the proportion of 'accurate' and of 'not accurate'**; that holds more firmly than any preaching.
:::

::: exercise Pairing the palatalisation {level=1 check="3"}
Of the three initials *j*, *q* and *x* in Modern Chinese, which three initials are the old three households among their historical sources (writing letters for the initials, or the names under the heading 牙音, is also acceptable)? On that basis, judge how many of the four characters 家, 去, 虚 and 告 have already 'changed face' as between the modern reading and the old initial (do not count double readings).
::: solution
The old three households: **the *jian*, *xi* and *qun* initials (*g*, *k*, [*g*]) palatalised to *j*, *q* and *x*** (fine finals of the *ying*–*xiao* group are another line). The characters that changed face: 家 (the *jian* initial → *j*), 去 (the *xi* initial → *q*), 虚 (the *xiao* initial → *x*, on the *ying*–*xiao* line) — 告 has not changed (*gào*, before a broad final it remains as it was). If, leniently, one counts only the *jian*–*xi*–*qun* line: the two characters 家 and 去; noting that 虚 is palatalisation of the *xiao* initial may also be given the mark.
:::

::: exercise A detective of tone categories {level=2}
共, 供 and 恭 are of one origin and follow one from another (the *jian* initial, the *dong* rhyme, and the various departing and level readings); 被 and 皮 are the same family at different grades. Using 'level tone aspirates, oblique tones do not' and the table of the devoicing of voiced initials, explain one legitimate source of the fact that 'characters of the *qun* initial are read today both with unaspirated *k* and with aspirated *kʰ*', and give one character for each.
::: solution
Two main channels. ① A fully voiced level tone becomes aspirated — 奇 (the *qun* initial, level tone → *qí*). ② Fully voiced rising and departing tones (the oblique tones) are devoiced to unaspirated — 巨 (the *qun* initial, the 语 rhyme, rising tone → *jù*), 共 (the *qun* initial, the 用 rhyme, departing tone → *gòng*). Thus one and the same old initial splitting today into the two roads *k* and *q* is a division of labour under the conditions of devoicing, and does not count as an exception. Marking looks at whether one can state the 'condition' as an environment (tone category / division) rather than recite an example.
:::
:::

::: exercise Catching the exceptions {level=2}
皆 *jiē* takes its sound from 比 *bǐ*. Check it against the three laws: is this a problem on the road of palatalisation, of devoicing, or of rhyme groups? Can it be lined up in a correspondence? When it cannot, what is your fallback explanation?
::: solution
In Old Chinese 皆 had the *jian* initial and a rhyme of the 质/脂 class, and 比 had the *bang* initial and the 脂 rhyme group — the discrepancy of the initials, *k* against *p*, is accommodated under the hypothesis of **consonant clusters or neighbouring initials** (比 has the further readings 频眉切 and 房脂切, among others, which can serve as a bridge), while the rhymes are a *pángzhuǎn* 旁转 of 脂 and 皆. Three stances are allowed in a student's answer: leave it in doubt, cite the consonant-cluster account, or cite the account from variant readings — but one must write explicitly that 'this is a hard case which the three laws cannot govern', and one may not force it.
:::

::: exercise A short essay {level=3}
The '*youwen* theory' (advocated by Wang Shengmei 王圣美 of the Song: the phonetic element also governs the meaning; for example 戋 has the meaning 'small' — the characters 浅, 钱, 残, 盏 and others all contain the sense of 'small' or 'few'): is it the forerunner of etymology, or a wrong turning into a game played with characters? Write four hundred characters. Give one example in which it gets things right and one in which it makes a mess of them, and give an **operational standard for testing** 'there is meaning in the sound' (hint: sound and meaning in step, plus evidence cited from texts, plus not an isolated proof).
::: hint
Materials: Shen Jianshi 沈兼士, *Youwen shuo zai xungu xue shang zhi yange ji qi tuichan* 《右文说在训诂学上之沿革及其推阐》 (*The development of the youwen theory in the study of exegesis, and its further elaboration*); in any dictionary of the 《源字》 type, look up the guide to the book under 'seeking the meaning from the sound' (因声求义).
:::
::: solution
An example that gets it right: the 仑 family — 伦, 理, 论 and 抡 all contain the semantic feature 'order, sequence'; the majority within the family share the meaning, and there is evidence in the books, and etymology claims them to this day (old notes of the type '伦者，理也' — '伦 is 理.' (Our translation.) — are its forerunner). An example that makes a mess of it: whoever forces the claim that 'every phonetic must contain a meaning' collapses as soon as they collide with a loan character inside the same family. An operational standard: ① a **majority** of the members of that phonetic element's family share some semantic feature; ② ancient books have examples of the use; ③ it is not an isolated proof (there is no parallel counter-example outside the family). Marking looks at whether one can distinguish a 'statistical tendency' from a 'verdict passed character by character'.
:::
