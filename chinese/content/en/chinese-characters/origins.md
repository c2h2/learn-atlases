---
title: "The origins of Chinese writing"
---

“仓颉四目，见鸟兽之迹而造字” — 'Cang Jie had four eyes; seeing the tracks of birds and beasts, he made writing.' (Our translation.) The story is a moving one, but the answer under the archaeologist's spade is older, and more piecemeal: the characters were not made by one person; they were slowly ground out by generations of people who kept accounts, who divined, and who cast tripods. This chapter settles three questions: on what grounds one says 'this is writing' and 'that is only a sign'; whether the Jiahu incised marks, the Liangzhu incised marks and the Chengziya pottery graphs each come up to the mark; and why textbooks throughout China agree that **a mature system of Chinese characters can, as things stand, be traced only as far as the oracle bones of three thousand years ago** — and, for the 'childhood' of about a thousand years before that, where the evidence is, and where it is missing.

## Criteria of judgement: when a 'picture' becomes writing

::: definition The emergence of writing, and proto-writing {#def-qianwenzi}
**Proto-writing** (前文字; also called primitive record-keeping) means devices of signs that help memory along, but are not yet fixed to the units of sound and meaning in a language: knotted cords, notched tallies, counter-like tokens for reckoning, clan emblems, pictorial narrative. They 'can convey a meaning', and yet they **do not correspond to the linear sequence of language** — a reader can only guess at the drift, and cannot read off syllables or grammar. **Writing** must satisfy a three-way tie to language: each fixed form corresponds to a **reusable unit** in the language (a word or a morpheme), the order of the forms corresponds to the order of speech, and it can **form sentences** and generate new records without limit.
:::

::: theorem The three-part test {#rule-santaojian}
For an archaeological sign to be promoted to 'writing', it must pass three gates at once. (1) **Unit status** — the same form recurs, and a sound value corresponding to a morpheme can be analysed out of it (it can be cross-checked against later writing, or against linguistic material of the same period). (2) **Sequentiality** — the forms are arranged in strings, and the order carries grammar, or at least a fixed reading. (3) **Productivity** — the system can record sentences never seen before (the criterion: components are reused among the graphs, and there are rules of combination, rather than each mark being a token on its own). Miss any one of the three, and they can be called only 'incised marks', or a 'sequence of graphs': fit to be written into the first chapter of a study of the Chinese script, and not into a corpus of Old Chinese.
:::

::: proof Why the threshold has to be set so high
Because 'looking like a character' is the cheapest kind of resemblance: a potter's mark, a clan emblem, a shaman's sign, each of them with strokes that look thoroughly real. Relax the standard, and the Jiahu incised marks can drag the history of Chinese characters back 8,600 years, and Egypt and Mesopotamia are 'earlier' as well — the history of the field shows it again and again. Whenever a Neolithic site yields an object that bears signs, the media at once announce that 'the origin of writing has been rewritten'; what the discipline wants is **material from which speech can be read out**. A high threshold is not conservatism. It is method. Each piece of the three-part test has a procedure of cross-check that can actually be carried out (unit status by statistics of recurrence, sequentiality by the rules of arrangement, productivity by the analysis of components). Produce no cross-check, and the whole of it is classed as 'unresolved'. This is the real kit this chapter puts in the reader's pack: **know how to doubt, and know still better how to verify.**
:::

::: example Measuring Jiahu with the three-part test {#ex-jiahu}
On the tortoise shells and the bone objects from the Jiahu site at Wuyang, in Henan, a batch of incised signs has been found (several dozen examples, of several kinds; the numbers differ slightly from one scholar's count to another's). Radiocarbon dates them to about 6600–6200 BC, and a few resemble the later characters 目 and 日. Please take them through the three-part test, gate by gate.
::: solution
Unit status: the signs for the most part **occur only once**, and the rate at which they repeat from one object to another is low, so it is hard to prove that they correspond to fixed morphemes. A few of the graphs look uncannily like the oracle-bone 目 and 日 of three thousand years later, but a likeness of shape is not the same thing as a common origin — where the lines are simple, the rate at which they converge is extremely high (everywhere on earth, a drawing of an 'eye' is a circle plus a pupil). Sequentiality: not a single example forms a string, so there is no order of reading to speak of. Productivity: no system of reused components can be established. None of the three gates is passed. The conclusion: Neolithic **incised marks**, which **bear on** the origin of Chinese characters (the tradition of notching and incising is one source of the characters; the next section), but not writing. The judgement does not rest on 'I believe' or 'I do not believe'. It rests only on the checkable procedure of the three-part test.
:::
:::

## The chain of candidates: incised marks, graphs, and that thousand-year missing link

::: theorem The sources of the characters: two river-courses {#def-lianghetiao}
On the received view in the field, Chinese characters were formed by the joining of two river-courses, **recording events in pictures** and **keeping counts by notches**. Pictures supply the rationale of character-making that 'links graphs by their form' (graphs of the kind 字, 日, 月, 象 and 斗); notching supplies abstract indicatives and numeral signs (the ancient forms of 一二三 and of 上下, and the original meaning of 契 itself). The two courses meet in the **merging of graphs with incised marks** at the Longshan–Erlitou stage (a clan emblem with a character added, a count with a form added), and in the hands of the Shang diviners they crystallise into a system that can be deciphered. The strength of this account is that it explains why, among the oracle-bone inscriptions, pictographs are few while phono-semantic compounds are already many (the climb of the phono-semantic compound, [[chinese-characters/writing-and-language]]). The merging is not evolution along a single line. It is two techniques set on the same track, and speeded up.
:::

::: remark Some hard pieces on the chain of evidence
**Liangzhu culture** (about 3300–2300 BC): incised marks are abundant on the jades and the pottery of the Jiangsu and Zhejiang region. What has drawn most attention are the designs of a god and a beast-mask laid out as whole scenes, and the occasional cases of **several marks incised in a row**. The **five marks incised in a row** on a sherd of Longshan pottery from **Chengziya, at Zhangqiu in Shandong**, are the strongest single case, on the eastern coast, of 'graphs strung together' — the decipherments offered by various scholars are many (the reading 旦于出日, 'at dawn the sun comes forth' (Our translation.), has had the wider influence), and none has won general acceptance. **Erlitou** (from about 1750 BC on): more than twenty kinds of marks incised on pottery, and cases, too, of **signs combined**. The date and the place fall squarely on the gap. The three sites each tell their own story, and there is no passage of decipherment from one to another — this is both hard evidence for the 'missing-link thesis' and the reason why 'several accounts stand side by side'.
:::

::: intuition The 'starting point' is a lie told by the material
Note a point of method that is easy to overlook. Almost everything we can now see as 'earliest' is what is **best able to survive being buried** — tortoise shell and animal bone, bronze and stone. The more ordinary everyday writing of the Shang and the Zhou was written on bamboo slips, wooden tablets and silk, or even straight onto the ground and onto wall plaster, and it rotted away long ago (slips and tablets that are unearthed are a piece of luck: the conditions of burial happened to be right). So 'Chinese characters suddenly appeared three thousand years ago' is bound to be an illusion: **it is not that the characters were not there; it is that the carriers will not show them to us**. Two things follow. First: any claim that 'such-and-such incised marks equal the earliest writing' has first to ask whether the ordinary carriers, later than those marks and earlier than the oracle bones, are there at all. Second: the thousand years missing from the childhood of the characters are for the most part not a blank, but **rot**. Engrave the bias of materials on the mind, and half the headlines that 'rewrite history' can be kept off.
:::

::: example How to read the five linked marks from Chengziya {#ex-chengziya}
The five marks incised in a row on the Longshan pottery sherd from Chengziya have, ever since they were unearthed, drawn one decipherment after another (the reading 旦于出日 has had the wider influence), and not one of them has won general acceptance. Please weigh this: even if some scholar's decipherment were correct, could it rewrite the 'starting point of writing'?
::: solution
No, and the reason is worth learning by heart. To rewrite history one needs **a systematic body of contemporary material that can be deciphered**. A single isolated case can raise a question; it cannot settle one. Suppose the five linked marks really do read 旦于出日 (one sentence!): there is still no evidence in between them and the oracle-bone inscriptions as to whether the line of the graphs descends from one to the other (where are the characters of the same time, of the same place, and in batches?). The moment they did come in batches, they would satisfy the three-part test of themselves, and the 'suppose' would no longer be needed — that is the fate of an isolated case: **either it is the missing link (waiting on the next spadeful of earth), or it is only a coincidence**. That the history of the field cites Chengziya with restraint ('a clue worth noticing') is not timidity. It is honesty about the grade of the evidence.
:::
:::

::: theorem How legend sits at the same table as archaeology {#rule-chuanshuo}
Three pieces of evidence for the story that Cang Jie created writing. The Warring States *Shiben* 世本 ('Genealogical records') has “仓颉作书”, 'Cang Jie made writing.' (Our translation.) The 'Jiebi' chapter of the *Xunzi* 荀子·解蔽 ('Dispelling Blindness') has “**好书写也者众矣，而仓氏独传，以一者也**”, '**Those who were fond of writing were many indeed, yet the house of Cang alone was handed down, and that by single-mindedness.**' (Our translation.) The 'Benjing' chapter of the *Huainanzi* 淮南子·本经 ('The fundamental canon') has “昔者仓颉作书而天雨粟，鬼夜哭”, 'In ancient times, when Cang Jie made writing, heaven rained millet and ghosts cried in the night.' (Our translation.) The sentence in the *Xunzi* is the one most worth having: people who wrote were very many, and the reason the house of Cang left a name is “**专一**”, '**single-mindedness**' — a sociological explanation that rewrites 'the credit for inventing it' as 'specialisation', and that sits entirely at the same table as the archaeology. Writing began among the many craftsmen, was brought to completion among the shaman-scribes, and the name was left to an agent of the collective. The Han fitted Cang Jie out with 'four eyes', a 'dragon countenance', and a plot in which he observes the tracks of birds. That is the standard equipment a myth has bolted on (each item magnifies the invention story of 'observing things and taking images from them', in the service of that age's need to hold 'writing sacred').
:::

::: application Take the three-part test along when you read the news
Search for any report of the form 'site so-and-so rewrites the history of writing', and check it in three steps. First, do the signs **recur, and in a number worth counting** (unit status)? Second, do they **form strings** (sequentiality)? Third, can the researchers produce an analysis in which **components are reused** (productivity)? If all three are 'no', and the headline says 'the earliest writing', the ordinary way to enter it is 'incised marks discovered'. The greatest return on the training in this chapter is not remembering how many years ago Jiahu was. It is acquiring a band of immunity: the next time a viral piece claims that 'such-and-such is earlier than Mesopotamia', you can ask the right question in three seconds.
:::

::: quiz
The hardest single piece of evidence for judging that 'some system of signs is already writing' is ( ).

- [x] The forms can be tied, in a way open to checking, to the units of sound and meaning in a language, and can form strings that express sentences
- [ ] A high frequency of occurrence
- [ ] A resemblance to the graphs of later ages
::: solution
The three-part test as a conjunction ([[#rule-santaojian]]). Date, frequency and likeness of form are all evidence from the side; 'speech can be read out of it' is the evidence that tells directly.
:::

::: quiz
Radiocarbon dating, stratigraphic typology, and internal evidence from the divination records (royal titles, and script styles: the two classes) — for 'fixing the date of the oracle-bone inscriptions', these three methods of dating ( ).
- [ ] The three have nothing to do with one another; any one of them will do
- [x] Internal evidence is the most direct; dating fixes the span; stratigraphy fixes the order — and joining the three lines is in fact the most secure
- [ ] Only radiocarbon dating is a scientific method
- [ ] The script itself cannot be dated; one can rely only on texts transmitted to the present
::: solution
The normal state of dating is **corroboration along several lines at once** (Dong Zuobin 董作宾's oracle-bone chronology, which divides by period and by class, began precisely from internal evidence, and cross-checks it against stratigraphy). Each single-line method has its own systematic error: dating gives a span, and samples may be contaminated; stratigraphy gives only earlier and later, not an absolute year; internal evidence depends on the chain of samples being complete — only where the lines cross can one speak of 'reliable history'.
:::
:::

::: quiz
The view supported by the *Xunzi*'s “好书写也者众矣，而仓氏独传，以一者也” is ( ).
- [ ] Cang Jie really existed, and invented writing on his own
- [x] Writing was made by many people; Cang Jie may have been the one who set it in order, or the representative in name of some school
- [ ] Cang Jie, observing the tracks of birds and beasts, was the first to make writing and tallies
- [ ] People of the Warring States generally held that the origin of writing cannot be got to the bottom of
::: solution
“众造而一人传”, 'made by the many, and transmitted through one person', is precisely the Warring States way of putting 'writing originates in a collective, and becomes known through an agent' ([[#rule-chuanshuo]]); “观迹造字”, 'making writing by observing tracks', is a mythic script bolted on in the Han.
:::

::: history
The rediscovery of the oracle-bone inscriptions is a rich seam in the history of the discipline. In 1899 Wang Yirong 王懿荣, a scholar of bronzes and stones, recognised incised writing on 'dragon bones'; in 1903 Liu E 刘鹗's *Tieyun canggui* 铁云藏龟 ('Tieyun's collection of tortoise shells') was reproduced in facsimile and so passed down; Sun Yirang 孙诒让's *Qiwen juli* 契文举例 ('Examples of the incised graphs') was a first draft, and not yet exact; Luo Zhenyu 罗振玉 established that the place where the oracle bones were unearthed was Yinxu, and deciphered the names of the Shang kings in their generations; Wang Guowei 王国维 then corroborated, from the divination records, the genealogy of the Shang kings recorded in the 'Basic Annals of Yin' of the *Records of the Grand Historian* — within twenty years, a single medicinal ingredient had become a discipline, 'oracle-bone studies', which pushed China's reliable history up to the Shang, and rewrote the discussion of 'the origin of writing' from a myth into an archaeological question. From 1928 the Academia Sinica excavated Yinxu on scientific lines; in 1936 the one pit YH127 produced more than 17,000 pieces of divination records — the humility of this discipline, and its eloquence, both lie buried in pits of that kind.
:::

::: example The dossier of one oracle bone {#ex-keci}
Read the frame of a typical divination record (rewritten after the usual practice of transcriptions): “癸巳卜，殻贞：旬亡祸？王占曰：来庚? 简化为——王占曰：有祟。五日戊戌，雷雨有……” — 'Divined on the day *guisi*, 殻 inquired: no disaster in the ten-day week? The king prognosticated and said: the coming *geng*? Simplified to — the king prognosticated and said: there is a curse. On the fifth day, *wuxu*, there was thunder and rain……' (Our translation.) Name each component, and explain why the record satisfies the 'three-part test' on all counts at once.
::: solution
The common form of a divination record has five parts: the **preface** (the date in the *gānzhī* 干支, the cycle of stems and branches); the **charge** (what the diviner asks: “旬亡祸” — 'no disaster over the ten days to come?'); the **prognostication** (the judgement the king gives on reading the cracks); the **verification** (a note entered afterwards to confirm what happened, often on the pattern “七日己亥允……” — 'on the seventh day, *jihai*, it indeed……'); and additions such as the signature and the engraver. Against the three-part test: the charge and the prognostication are **Chinese that forms sentences** (the subject is habitually left out, and an interrogative pattern such as “旬亡祸” is fully in place); the same diviner's name (殻) recurs from pit to pit (unit status, and the manner of an individual hand); upwards of a thousand divination records use one and the same set of components to generate endlessly new sentences (productivity) — which is why 'speech can be read out of it' is the one piece of evidence, in the judgement of what counts as writing, that does not lie. Note in passing that the divination records are already a highly formulaic **mature genre**. The formulas are precisely the age spots of 'this writing has already been in use a long time'.
:::

## What comes next

What the oracle-bone inscriptions look like, and how they are read, is set out in full in a lesson of its own: [[chinese-characters/oracle-bronze]]. What the *Shuowen jiezi* weighs in the 'step-graph of the character dictionaries' is taken up directly in [[chinese-characters/six-categories]]. The latest excavations at Erlitou, and of still earlier signs, are renewed year by year; for the reading, it is enough to test oneself with the three-part test of this chapter. For the real skill: find a compendium of oracle-bone graphs, copy ten characters with the component 水 and ten with the component 木, and count the evidence that components are reused — a first-hand meeting with the three-part test is more use than a hundred pieces of popular science.

::: summary
- The line between writing and proto-writing is the three-part test: unit status (a fixed form corresponds to a morpheme that can be used again), sequentiality (strings that can be read aloud), productivity (components reused into a system).
- The Jiahu incised marks, the Liangzhu incised marks and the five linked marks from Chengziya are each an important clue; none of them may be entered on the roll of 'writing', because the three-part test is not met; an isolated case does not change history.
- The two source-courses of the characters (recording in pictures, plus counting by notches) join at Longshan–Erlitou, and crystallise in the oracle bones.
- The legend of Cang Jie is not the same thing as reliable history, but the sentence in the *Xunzi*, “众造独传”, 'made by the many and transmitted alone' (Our translation.), is an insight by which the ancients made room for a collective credit for the invention.
- As the dictionaries accumulate, the number of known characters rises to fifty or sixty thousand, while the three thousand five hundred in common use keep the same old faces — 'the sea of characters rises; the floor of the characters does not' (Our translation.).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise A quick judgement by the three-part test {level=1}
Judge whether each of the following already constitutes writing, and in one sentence say which piece is missing. (1) Knotted cords, “大事大结，小事小结”, 'for a great matter a large knot, for a small matter a small knot' (Our translation.). (2) At one and the same site, 37 incised marks of the same form are unearthed, each occurring on its own, with no string. (3) On a roll of silk, whole sentences of writing that can be read against an ancient book transmitted down to the present.
::: solution
(1) Not writing: unit status cannot be verified (a knot has no sound value, and a cord has no reading); it belongs to the record-keeping of proto-writing. (2) It reaches unit status, and it lacks sequence and productivity — it is still incised marks. (3) All three are met (it can be cross-checked against the language, it forms sentences, it is productive): it is writing, and it forms a system. The marking turns on whether the **criteria are cited item by item**. Answering only 'yes' or 'no' is not allowed.
:::
:::

::: exercise Casting the character accounts {level=1 check="4500"}
About how many distinct oracle-bone characters, in total, are known at present? Roughly what proportion of them has been deciphered? What do these figures mean for the claim that 'the childhood of the characters still has its missing link'?
::: solution
About four thousand five hundred (the graph compendia of the various scholars give 4,500 to 4,600); fewer than seven tenths have been deciphered (the rate is high for characters in common use, and low for proper names and for clan names). The implications are two. The so-called 'mature system' still has more than a thousand characters lying in the dark. For a mature script used over several centuries, a few thousand characters is on the small side to begin with, which suggests that the oracle-bone inscriptions are **the dedicated vocabulary of the diviners' group**, and not the whole of the writing of the time — the missing link may be lying precisely outside the oracle bones (one further notch on the method of [[#def-qianwenzi]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Reviewing a viral piece {level=2}
Find a report whose title contains 'China discovers the earliest writing' (better if it falls within the last five years). Using the three-step check in the application, write an appraisal of two hundred characters, and draft for the report a new title that is 'academically correct'.
::: solution
The shape of a piece of work that passes: (1) extract the report's sentences of evidence (how many signs, the date, whether they form a string, and who deciphered them); (2) tick or cross each item of the three-part test; (3) a sample new title — 'Site ×× has yielded a set of **Neolithic incised signs**, and offers a new clue for research into the origin of Chinese characters.' Anyone whose check does not cite the original definition of the three-part test ([[#rule-santaojian]]) loses half the marks; anyone who dares to write 'the earliest writing' again in the title proposed is given none — what this question trains is precisely that professional alertness.
:::

::: exercise A checklist of bias by material {level=2}
For the four carriers — oracle-bone inscriptions, bronze inscriptions, slips and tablets, and silk manuscripts — list the 'conditions of survival' of each and the 'bias that goes with it' (what each over-represents, and what each wipes out), and explain why 'the everyday characters of the Shang must have been more vernacular than the oracle bones' can be inferred from the list.
::: solution
Conditions of survival, and the biases. Oracle bones — reserved for divination by the royal house; they over-represent the religious register and the royal register, and they wipe out the secular. Bronze inscriptions — aristocrats having a record engraved; they over-represent the set phrases of rite and of feats of arms. Slips and tablets — they need a special burial (dry, sealed, or waterlogged); the regions where they turn up lean towards the frontier garrisons of the north-west and the Chu region in the south. Silk manuscripts — the same as slips and tablets, but rarer; texts of a pictorial kind are, by luck, singularly numerous. The list yields 'everyday characters were more vernacular'. All four are the writing of **solemn occasions**; the more everyday and the more urgent something was, the more it was written on a thing that does not survive. The diviners and the casters, what is more, were charged from the start with the office of the 'standard form'. Set it against 'what of writing survives' today — your WeChat messages, and a contract on paper: which is the more likely to be dug up in three thousand years? That is why the everyday of the people of Shang goes silent in our eyes.
:::
:::

::: exercise How the river-course account is proved {level=2}
'Two river-courses, pictures plus notches' is a hypothesis about how the script arose. For each side, list two kinds of evidence **that could be dug up in the field** (what sort of find would strengthen it? what would weaken it?), and point out the weakest link in the hypothesis at present.
::: solution
A case that would strengthen it: **combined incised marks found in batches**, at a place and in a span of time that fall where the two courses meet (western Henan and southern Shanxi, Longshan–Erlitou), and with components that show both a source in pictographs and a source in indicatives. A case that would weaken it: if it were established that, before the mature oracle-bone script, the Central Plains had no tradition of notching and incising, or that its 'pictorial' source was wholly from outside. The weakest link: the material for the **critical period** from 'signs' to 'a text that can be read' (2000–1300 BC) is still thin, and no one has yet got hold of that key pottery sherd which would be 'in a string, and readable against other text' — so behind the two words 'received view' there stands, in fact, an account that has not been closed. This question does not ask you to take a side. It asks only that you can point out where the account is still open.
:::

::: exercise A short essay {level=3}
Under the title 'The value of legend', write four hundred characters. The story of Cang Jie is known full well not to be history: why, even so, does a history of the study of the Chinese script still tell it? Prompts: social memory; the sacralisation of a profession; the rational layer in the *Xunzi*; and myth as 'a filler for gaps in the evidence', both the push it has given the history of research and the way it has got in the way.
::: hint
Materials you may use: the original texts of 'Jiebi' in the *Xunzi*, of 'Benjing' in the *Huainanzi*, and of the postface to the *Shuowen jiezi* are all short; then consult the way the introduction of any textbook of the study of the Chinese script treats 'legends about the origin of writing'.
:::
::: solution
Points you may take as a guide. Legend is a fossil of the ancients' **sense of a problem** — behind 'who made the characters' lies 'on what grounds writing is to be trusted' (both divination and the orders of government depended on it). A professional group, the shaman-scribes, used myth to confer sacredness on itself. And the *Xunzi*'s “众造独传” proves that the rationality of the Warring States was well able to yield its place to myth. Seen from the history of research: myth supplied the first hypothesis (observing things and taking images from them → the pictograph comes first), and was afterwards bitten in return (the blithe assumption that 'Cang Jie created the writing all of a piece' stood in the way of a gradualist account). It is a specimen of just this: questions drive a discipline forward, and preconceived views drag it back. The marking looks at the layers of the argument, not at whether the postface to the *Shuowen jiezi* has been recited from memory.
:::
