title: "Standard characters and common errors"
---

‘字无二法’ (‘there is no second way to write a character’) is the new rule of propriety for the citizen who writes in modern times. This chapter does two things: first it sets up a **coordinate system of the standard** — what is meant by ‘standard characters’, of which layers of documents they are composed, and which occasions allow an exception; then it opens an **error clinic** — the six major classes of character ailment most frequent among native speakers, giving, class by class, the case, the pathology and the prescription.

## The coordinate system of the standard {#sec-guifan}

::: definition Standard characters {#def-gf}
**Standard characters** are the Chinese characters in general use that have been systematically arranged and issued by the state in a standard form. In substance they include: the simplified characters of the *General Table of Simplified Characters* (reissued in 1986), and the traditional forms that correspond to them; the standard forms retained in the *First List for the Arrangement of Variant Forms* (1955); the glyph forms and stroke orders prescribed by the *Table of Glyph Forms of Chinese Characters in Common Use for Printing* (1965) and the *Standard for the Stroke Order of Characters in Common Use in Modern Chinese* (1997); and, the latest overall compilation, the ***Table of General Standard Chinese Characters* (2013, 8,105 characters)** — which divides characters ‘in general use’ into three levels: the Level 1 list, 3,500 characters (the foundation of education); Level 2, 3,000 characters (6,500 cumulatively, the characters in general use for publishing); Level 3, 1,456 characters (characters for specialised domains such as personal names and place names). For a character inside the table, ‘one character, one correct form’. A character outside the table does not, in principle, enter a standard text addressed to the public.
:::

::: theorem The skeleton of the legal system {#rule-fazhi}
The *Law on the National Common Language and Script* (passed on 31 October 2000, and in force from 1 January 2001) establishes Putonghua and standard characters as the **national common language and script**. Article 17 at the same time sets out a list of exceptions — cultural relics and historic sites, inscriptions on works of calligraphy and seal carving, handwritten dedications, old glyph forms on shop signs that have already been approved, and occasions such as the research and publication of Classical Chinese (the texts) — on which **traditional characters and variant forms may be retained or restored to use**. The true spirit of the standard is not ‘traditional = wrong’, but **governance by domain**: teaching, government affairs, publishing and on-screen character banks obey the standard tables; inscribed boards, stone inscriptions, facsimile reprints of old books, and the making of calligraphy each have room of their own. The standard for characters in place names has a history of its own. The 1955 *Directive on Changing Obscure Characters in Place Names* changed 1,056 characters at a single stroke (a renaming that exchanged old characters hard to recognise for characters in current use; 雩都 → 于都 is a continuation of this wave), and the Level 3 characters of the 2013 table in turn gave specialised characters for place names and personal names, of the kind of 垚 and 䴗, their household registration back. The practical interface for characters in personal names is the Ministry of Public Security's ‘character bank for names’ (based on the GB 13000.1 character set, of 20,902 characters). It decides which character you can register at a police station — 䙻, 頔 and 镡 each have a small history of a case being filed and the judgement then reversed.
:::

::: warning A wrongly written character, a misused character and a non-standard character are three ailments
A **wrongly written character** (错字) is the writing of a character that does not exist (adding one dot too many to 步, so that it becomes another ‘character’). A **misused character** (别字) is the writing of another ready-made legitimate character (writing 甘拜下风 as 甘败下风). A **non-standard character** (不规范字) is the writing of a corresponding form that is legitimate but is not within the standard for this domain (學 appearing in the body of a textbook, or a shop sign using an abolished glyph form from the Second Scheme, 二简). The prescriptions for the three are entirely different: a wrongly written character must be **corrected**, a misused character must be **distinguished**, and a non-standard character must be **judged by domain** — 學 in a calligraphy classroom is not an ailment; only 學 in a composition book is. Systems of assessment, the marking of examinations, and the proofreading of official documents likewise count the faults separately along these three tracks.
:::

## The error clinic: six major classes {#sec-pianwu}

::: definition The clinic's master table {#def-zhenbiao}
① **Details of strokes**: writing one dot or one horizontal stroke too many or too few (the dot family; the three horizontal strokes inside 真; the top of 冒 is not 日). ② **Confusion of similar forms**: 戍戌戊, 己已巳, 未末, 拆折, 梁粱. ③ **Homophonous misused characters**: 部署／布署, 重叠／重迭, 账簿／帐簿, 安装／按装. ④ **Misplaced components**: the components are all correct, but the position is wrong (left and right exchanged, top and bottom inverted, the frame of an enclosure lost). ⑤ **Non-standard glyph forms**: a return of the stroke-saving of the Second Scheme, private cutting and trimming, and the reuse of variant forms already abolished. ⑥ **A wrong sound migrating into the form**: a misreading that carries a miswriting with it (青睐 written in error as 亲睐; 针砭 written in error as 针贬).
:::

::: example Clinic ①: what a single dot is worth {#ex-yidian}
Judge right and wrong, and state where the dot belongs: 兔, 龟, 象, 免, 皮, 琴, 盗, 步, 武, 真, 具.
::: solution
兔 has a dot (like the dot of a rabbit's tail); simplified 龟 has no dot; 象 has no dot; 免 is the character 免 itself and has no dot (免 and 兔 differ by exactly that one dot; in the 免 family, 挽, 晚 and 勉 all follow the dotless 免); do not add a dot beside 皮; the bottom of 琴 follows 今 and has no dot (by contrast, 含, 岭 and 念 have 丶); the 次 on top of 盗 has its dots; the lower right of 步 is 少 transformed by the omission of its dot, and that dot **is not there** to be supplied again (the popular writing that fills it out as 少 is precisely a stroke too many); there is **no dot** on the slanting hook of 武 (the old account that 武 is 止 and 戈 must be kept apart from the popular writing that adds a dot to 弋); and the middle of 真, of 具 and of 俱 is in each case **three horizontal strokes**. Conclusion: the dot is the **detail component** of the highest frequency among Chinese characters. Within the standard, every rule is particular to its character, and there is no unified derivation — one can only look them up, and one can only memorise them.
:::
:::

::: example Clinic ②: a genealogy of similar forms {#ex-xingjin}
Match each of 戍, 戌, 戊 and 戎 with a word or a four-character idiom, and, by the meaning of the character, remember how the strokes differ.
::: solution
戍 shù (戍边, 卫戍 — one dot, like a person holding a dagger-axe, 戈, and guarding the frontier); 戌 xū (戌时, 戊戌变法 — the middle is made with one horizontal stroke, that is, the mnemonic 横戌, ‘horizontal 戌’); 戊 wù (戊寅, 戊己 — empty, with not one stroke inside 戊); 戎 róng (投笔从戎, 戎马 — a cross, and a general name for weapons). The mnemonic ‘点戍、横戌、戊中空、十字交叉念作戎’ (‘A dot for 戍, a horizontal stroke for 戌, 戊 empty in the middle, and a cross is read 戎.’) (Our translation.) has long been handed down in teaching. The frequency with which 戊戌变法 is read or written as 戊戍 or 戌戌 almost ranks alongside the swapping of 梁 and 粱, and it is a standing test point in secondary-school Chinese.
:::
:::

::: example Clinic ③: five items on distinguishing misused characters {#ex-biezi}
Point out the errors and correct them: ① 迫不急待; ② 再接再励; ③ 甘败下风; ④ 怨天尤人 (this item is correct as it stands; ask why it is so often ‘corrected’ into an error).
::: solution
① It should be 迫不及待 — the 及 is the 及 of 来得及 (‘to be in time’), not of 着急 (‘to be anxious’). ② It should be 再接再厉 — 厉 is a whetstone (厉 is the original character of 砺), the action of a fighting cock grinding the shell of its beak, not 鼓励 (‘to encourage’). ③ It should be 甘拜下风 — 拜 is to cup the hands and bow the head, and has nothing to do with 失败 (‘defeat’); the side component is exchanged because the meaning of the word has been misunderstood, and this belongs to the highest grade of **semantic misused character**. ④ 怨天尤人 is 尤 to begin with (in the sense of laying blame on, and of reproaching), yet ‘well-meaning’ marking often changes it to 忧. Diagnosis: seven tenths of misused characters are **near-synonyms exceeding their authority**. Merely memorising the shape of the character is useless; the meaning of the word must be nailed fast.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why errors of the 青睐／亲睐 kind are common even among adults {#int-bing}
Because the retrieval that goes with writing takes a **phonetic shortcut**: to write qīng lài, one first takes the high-frequency morphemes 亲/青, and the candidates in the character bank put 亲 out first — unless the meaning of the morpheme is activated (青 means the black of the eyeball; if one is unfamiliar with the allusion to Ruan Ji 阮籍 and his 青白眼, there is no immunity), no correction takes place. A misused character is in essence a **tug of war between the meaning of the morpheme and the association rate of the input method**. This is also why ‘internet phonetic loans’ (网络通假字; 酱紫, 童鞋) come partly from deliberately playing a gag, and are partly the same mechanism of retrieval wearing a joke as an outer garment. The prescription for correcting a misused character has never been to copy it out ten times more, but **the allusion filled back in + an analysis of the morpheme**: 青 (the colour of the eyeball) and 睐 (to look); 针砭 (a stone needle); 部署 (the posting of personnel, not 布); 重叠 (叠 is piling in layers; after 1955, 迭 no longer takes on that extra duty).
:::

::: history From 'the study of correct characters' to the age of character tables {#sec-zhengzi}
China has an extremely long tradition of fixing the correct character. *Hanjian* 汗简 (‘Sweat Slips’), *Wujing wenzi* 五经文字 (‘Characters of the Five Classics’) and *Jiujing ziyang* 九经字样 (‘Character Models for the Nine Classics’) collate the characters of the Stone Classics. Yan Yuansun 颜元孙's *Ganlu zishu* 干禄字书 (‘The Character Book for Seeking Emolument’) divides forms into the three grades ‘vulgar, current and correct’. The character books of the Ming and the Qing (*Zihui* 字汇, ‘A Compendium of Characters’; *Zhengzi tong* 正字通, ‘A Comprehensive Account of Correct Characters’) decide, character by character, which form is correct and which is vulgar. All of this rested on the authority of scholars and on the habits of the block-carving shops, and there was no concept of a ‘national standard’. The turn came after printing and the spread of education: **the 1955 table of variant forms, the 1965 table of glyph forms, the 1988 table of characters in common use, the 1997 standard of stroke order, the 2000 language law, and the 2013 Table of General Standard Chinese Characters** — a set of six, making up the ‘code’ of the Chinese character in our time. The question of the correct character turned from ‘a judgement of scholarly principle’ into ‘the application of law and of standards’. It is also a rare undertaking in the history of the world's writing: one regime, for all the writing of five hundred million writers, prescribing the order and the shape of every single stroke. Behind every clinic slip in this chapter, some article of this code is at work.
:::

::: quiz
The standard that is the overall compilation of the number of characters now in use is ( ).
- [ ] the *General Table of Simplified Characters*, 2,236 characters
- [x] the *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters*, 8,105 characters
- [ ] the number of characters included in the *Xinhua Dictionary*
- [ ] the 6,763 characters of GB 2312
::: solution
The *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters* of 2013, with 8,105 characters (three levels: 3,500 / 3,000 / 1,456), is the overall compilation now in force. 2,236 is the number of simplified characters. GB 2312 is the character-set standard of 1980 (the old national standard for information processing). The number of characters a dictionary includes is still less a standard.

- [ ] 琴 (the lower half written as 金)

The lower right of 步 has no dot; there is no dot on the hook of 武; 琴 follows 今; and the inside of 真 is three horizontal strokes — of the four, only ‘武 with no dot’ is the writing of the standard now in force.

::: exercise Marking {level=1}
Each of the following expressions is right or wrong. Correct it, and note the type: ① 布署; ② 按装; ③ 重迭; ④ 慷慨解囊; ⑤ 慨当以慷.
::: solution
① 部署 (a misused character: 布 → 部, a near-synonym exceeding its authority); ② 安装 (按 → 安); ③ 重叠 (迭 → 叠; after 1955, 迭 no longer concurrently holds the office of 叠); ④ and ⑤ are not wrong — both characters of 慷慨 are the correct characters. This question sets a trap with 慨／概; it is 气概 and 大概 that take 概.
:::

::: exercise A drill on the dot family {level=1}
State whether each of the following contains a dot: 兔, 龟, 象, 免, the right-hand side of 逸, the upper part of 盗, the lower right of 步, and above the 戈 of 武.
::: solution
兔 has a dot; 龟 (the standard simplified form) has no dot; 象 has no dot; 免 has no dot (it differs from 兔 by exactly one dot; as you write, ask yourself ‘does it need a tail?’); 逸 follows 免 and therefore has no dot; the left of the 次 on top of 盗 is written as two dots; the lower right of 步 has no dot; the slanting hook of 武 has no dot. When you mark homework of the same kind, building a ‘dot file’ for these eight characters is the cheapest teaching resource.
:::

::: exercise Collecting errors {level=2}
On a campus or in a community, photograph or collect ten real uses of characters (shop signs, posts from a public account, and notices will all do). Classify them on the three tracks ‘wrongly written character / misused character / non-standard character’, and write one sentence giving the reason for each judgement. Include at least one example that is ‘correct by domain’.
::: solution
What counts as a pass: the classification agrees with the diagram in section 9; the reason for the judgement comes down to the three questions ‘does the character exist / is it some other character / what are the domain-rules of the occasion’; for what is ‘correct by domain’, if, for example, the calligraphic 發財 on the sign of a long-established shop (legitimate in the domain of inscriptions) has been misjudged as wrong, one must explain that **if it is moved into the body of a textbook it at once becomes a non-standard character** — the ailment is in the occasion, not in the character.
:::
:::

::: exercise Judgement by domain {level=2}
Judge, one by one and in the spirit of Article 17 of the Language Law, whether the use of a traditional character or a variant form is compliant on each of the following occasions: ① the body of a secondary-school history textbook quotes the place name 開封 from an old map; ② the signboard of a housing development reads 龍府 (someone has been invited to inscribe it); ③ a student's composition is in traditional characters throughout; ④ in the editing and typesetting of old books, the body of the *Shisanjing zhushu* 十三经注疏 (‘Commentaries and Subcommentaries on the Thirteen Classics’) is set with 學.
::: solution
① It tends towards compliance (the domain of quotation from old books; old characters in proper names may be retained, and it is enough for the textbook to state its conventions). ② Compliant (old characters in the calligraphy of the domain of inscriptions on shop signs; the provisions state this direction plainly). ③ Not compliant (the domain of texts for teaching should use standard characters; judge it a ‘non-standard character’ rather than a wrongly written character, and in commenting one should first distinguish the domain and only then correct the character). ④ It depends on the conventions of the edition: a photographic reprint of old books, and vertical setting that follows the old forms, are compliant; the body of a modern commentary or a modern translation uses the standard character 学. The weight of the marking falls on the procedural awareness of ‘settle the domain first, and only then assign the offence’.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: for whom is the input method writing? {level=3}
Taking this chapter's ‘tug of war between morpheme meaning and association rate’ as the starting point, write a short essay of 800 to 1,200 characters. How are Pinyin input, speech input and AI continuation rewriting the distribution of competence in Chinese characters? Should one be anxious about ‘taking up the pen and forgetting the character’ (提笔忘字)? You must cite at least three concepts from this chapter (governance by domain, three-track fault-counting, and the retrieval shortcut), and give two practicable proposals that you support (at the level of teaching, of a product, or of a standard).
::: solution
Points on which the marks turn: the writer can point out that the withering of ‘ability to write characters by hand = the channel that produces glyph forms’ is a measurable fact (as with the items set in the test of applied proficiency in Chinese characters, and in the various examinations of writing), and does not exaggerate into a ‘theory that Chinese characters will die out’; the writer distinguishes the different speeds at which the two channels, **recognition / production**, decline; proposals that come down to something concrete gain marks (for example: Chinese classes keeping timed writing from memory, and the distinguishing of similar forms; products for characters and words offering feedback that ‘attributes the cause of a wrongly written character’; standards for character banks making no judgement of legitimacy about ‘handwritten variants’). Whoever confuses ‘AI writing on one's behalf’ with ‘wrongly written and misused characters’, or advocates that legislation prohibit traditional characters, is mixing concepts, and marks are deducted.
:::

::: summary
Standard characters = a code in six pieces (the 1955 table of variant forms, the 1965 table of glyph forms, the general table reissued in 1986, the 1997 standard of stroke order, the 2000 language law, and the 2013 *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters*, 8,105 characters, on a three-level system). Governance by domain is the root: inscriptions, stone carvings and old books each have their exceptions, while teaching, publishing and display on screen must follow current usage and follow the tables. Three tracks of character ailment: the wrongly written character (it does not exist — correct it), the misused character (it is another character — distinguish the meaning of the morpheme), the non-standard character (it has left its domain — judge the occasion). Six clinics: details of the dot family, a genealogy of similar forms, homophonous misused characters, misplaced components, private cutting and trimming, and a wrong sound migrating into the form. The mechanism of the misused character is the victory of the phonetic retrieval shortcut over the meaning of the morpheme, so the prescription is the allusion and the meaning of the word, not copying set as a punishment. In the age of standards, the ‘right’ in writing a character no longer depends on complaisance or on the feel of the hand, but on a table that you can look up.
:::
