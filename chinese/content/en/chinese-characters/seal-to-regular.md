---
title: "From seal script to regular script"
---

Set an oracle-bone 為 beside the regular-script 为: above, the hand that leads an elephant; below, only four strokes left, a horizontal-turning hook and dots. What happened across the three thousand years in between has a technical name, **the clerical change** — a "pulling the house down and rebuilding it" in the age of the ancient script, and the stretch of engineering history this lesson most needs to fix in the mind. This chapter makes clear what each of the four scripts in common use is (seal, clerical, cursive and regular), which came before which, why they changed, and, after the change, the fatal question: **why the places where today's characters "cannot be given a coherent rationale" lie, for the most part, on the knife-cut of the clerical change.**

## Script names: know the tiles before you play

::: definition Large seal script, small seal script, and "seal" {#def-zhuan}
**Large seal script** is the later conventional cover-name for the scripts of the Western Zhou, the Spring and Autumn period and the Warring States, before the Qin unification (bronze inscriptions, the Stone Drum inscriptions and the ancient scripts of the Six States all sit in this large basket, and the basket itself is imprecise). **Small seal script** means specifically the official standard form after the Qin had regularised the script. The 9,353 characters recorded in the *Shuowen jiezi* (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*) take it as their system of head characters. Tradition has it that Li Si 李斯 composed the *Cangjie pian* 仓颉篇, Zhao Gao 赵高 the *Yuanli pian* 爰历篇, and Humu Jing 胡毋敬 the *Boxue pian* 博学篇, as models for the characters (all three texts are lost; the titles survive in catalogues). The temperament of small seal script: lines even and rounded, structure tall and fixed, one character with one form — **the first nationwide standardisation of character forms in the history of Chinese characters**, though the standard was carried through by documentary administration and by the transmission of scribal learning.
:::

::: definition Clerical script: the name states the origins {#def-lishu}
The original meaning of the character 隶 extends to "subordinate, conscripted servant". Clerical script takes its name from "the business of assisting the clerks" (佐隶之事). Everyday documents sought speed, and **broke up** the sinuous long lines of seal script into level, straight, short strokes: rounded turns became square bends, long lines were split into horizontals and right-falling strokes, and components were conflated, omitted and merged. As the archaeology shows, **the popular hands of the Qin lineage in the Warring States had already opened the way** (the Qin slips from Shuihudi and the wooden tablets from Qingchuan are "ancient clerical script"). Current in the Western Han, it became "Han clerical script" (the "silkworm head and wild-goose tail, one wave in three turns" of Eastern Han stele carving is its artistic peak, not its everyday norm). The clerical change completed the watershed between ancient and modern script. From then on **character form no longer depended on a compositional rationale**. Chinese characters passed from a system of "distinguishing the form by the meaning" into a system "mainly of signs" (from this point the "four rulers of rationale" in [[chinese-characters/writing-and-language]] are short one hard bar).
:::

::: definition Cursive, running and regular {#def-cao-xing-kai}
**Cursive script** arose from the linked, hastily written strokes of clerical script (one artistic line: *zhāngcǎo* 章草 — *jīncǎo* 今草, "modern cursive" — *kuángcǎo* 狂草, "wild cursive"). **Regular script** (true script, *zhēnshū* 真书; standard script, *zhèngshū* 正书) came out of the abbreviated transformation of the new clerical script at the end of the Han. Zhong You 钟繇 is put forward as the early master. By the Wei and the Jin it, together with running script (semi-cursive), which lies between regular and cursive, with "true" and "running" mixed, was given a settled form by the brush of the Two Wangs, father and son. In the Sui and the Tang it became the officially fixed standard form, and it has been in use ever since. Watch the trap in the order: **cursive script is not "after" regular script**. Cursive, regular and running long ran in parallel and took from one another. The genealogy of scripts is a tree of forks and crossings, not a column in single file (see the figure below).
:::

## The clerical change: a "reconstruction" of Chinese characters

::: theorem The four scalpels of the clerical change {#rule-libian}
(1) **Conversion into strokes.** Lines are changed into strokes. One stroke that "hangs" down in seal script is split into horizontal, vertical, left-falling and right-falling strokes, and the character form changes from a "picture" into a "string of strokes". (2) **Conflation.** Components that were originally different are merged into one and the same shape (the left half of 服, the tail of 燕 and the tail of 鱼 — several sources, one form; the rationale is buried from that point). (3) **Omission and merger.** Repeated components are written as one, and difficult components are cut down (灋 is abbreviated to 法; of the three components 廌, 去 and 水, two remain; cases of this kind take the tables of omitted and merged characters as their standard). (4) **Phonetisation and reanalysis.** A sign that is hard to explain is given a new explanation as a compound ideograph (associative compound). 射 originally depicted an arrow set on a bow; after it was fixed in clerical script it became 身＋寸, and people of the Han then produced forced readings of the "寸身之射" sort ("shooting by a cun of the body") — a popular rationale replaced the ancient rationale.
:::

::: proof Why it had to change
The driving force has never been more than one word: **speed**. Diplomatic documents of the Warring States, the Qin empire's daily quota of "weighing the documents by steelyard and stone-weight" (以衡石量书), the registers of criminal cases — the clerks of knife and brush wrote at speed with the brush on bamboo and wooden slips. The rounded turns of seal script "could not be written fast", and so came abbreviation, linking, squaring and straightening. The court's standard forms (stele carvings, tiger tallies, edict plates) and the everyday hands of the junior clerks were like the surface of a river and its bed: the surface is calm, while an undercurrent along the bed has already cut a new channel. Only once clerical script had already been written into a fixed form did the court's stele carvings (the Xiping Stone Classics use clerical script) recognise it after the fact. **The history of scripts is a cycle of "the popular form rebels, and the refined form offers it amnesty".** Regular script taking over clerical script's shift, and the Song-style character skeleton taking over regular script's shift, are no exceptions. ([[chinese-characters/strokes-radicals]] keeps a separate account of printed character forms.)
:::

::: example A three-thousand-year file on the character 為 {#ex-wei}
Describe, layer by layer, the history of the form of 為, and judge whether the rationale survives or is lost: on the oracle bones, a hand leading an elephant; the seal form, an abbreviated transformation; clerical and regular script write 爲; the simplified character is 为.
::: solution
On the oracle bones a "hand" leads an "elephant", and this speaks the meaning "to do, to labour" (the explanation that the elephant helped with ploughing and labour service is in common use in the field, though the etymology is still under discussion). In the Warring States the Chu lineage and the Qin lineage each abbreviated and transformed it. The seal form inherits the Qin lineage, and the shape of the hand is already corrupted. The clerical change pressed the pictographic part into a string of hooked-stroke signs that cannot be read — **the ancient rationale is entirely broken off**. Modern explanations that split 爲 on the pattern "爪＋母" are all reinterpretations. The regular-script 爲 was popularly written by reducing it in cursive, and in the end the simplified character took the cursive form and fixed it as 为 — **there is simply no new rationale; it depends on recognition and on learning by heart**. One character walks the whole course "picture — composed meaning — sign — fixed from the cursive", a specimen of textbook grade.
:::
:::

::: example The trap of 曰 and 日 {#ex-yue-ri}
In clerical and regular script the three forms 日, 曰 and 目 differ by only a dot or a horizontal. And from seal script upwards? Why is a primary-school pupil's difficulty with "characters to be distinguished" precisely a souvenir of the clerical change?
::: solution
From seal script upwards the boundaries of the three forms are clear (one solid stroke in the middle of 日; 曰, the shape of an open mouth breathing out, or, as some say, one stroke above 口; and 目, the two eye-sockets opened vertically — each unrelated to the others). The clerical change pressed the elliptical frames uniformly into square frames, and the distinction was left only in **the shape of the one stroke inside the frame** — the after-effect of the operation of conflation. This explains two things. First, in the recutting of old books, 曰 and 日 being mistaken for each other is a regular visitor in the study of collation. Second, in the teaching of character recognition, the difficulty of "graphically similar characters" comes almost entirely from the conversion into signs after the clerical change in antiquity. It is not that the child is careless. It is that the cut of three thousand years ago was made too clean.
:::
:::

::: theorem The state tradition of standardisation {#rule-biaozhun}
The other half of the history of scripts is **the politics of orthography**. The Qin "unification of the script" took small seal script as the model for the empire, and used clerical script in official business (the standard and the popular on two tracks at once). For the Xiping Stone Classics of the Eastern Han, Cai Yong 蔡邕 wrote the text in vermilion for carving in stone, and fixed the characters of the Five Classics outside the gate of the Imperial Academy (at the time, "乘车看视者日数千百辆" — "those who came by carriage to look numbered several thousand, or a hundred, carts a day" (Our translation.)). In the **third year of Zhengshi** of the Wei (242) the **Three-Script Stone Classics** were set up, carved side by side in the three scripts of ancient script, seal and clerical — one and the same classic, making a file of the character forms of three ages. In the Tang, Yan Yuansun's 颜元孙 *Ganlu zishu* 干禄字书 ("Character Book for Seeking Emolument") divided three forms, "popular, common and standard" (俗, 通, 正), to govern writing in the examinations, and stood at the head of later character books of popular and standard forms. The regularity in one sentence: **every time scripts change generation, there is bound to be a wave of state projects to fix the standard characters** — because "what counts as written correctly" directly decides how far examinations, statutes and the classics can be trusted.
:::

::: remark The account on the calligraphy side
The evolution of scripts and the art of calligraphy are cause and effect of each other, but they are different accounts. The "jade chopsticks" of seal script, the "silkworm head and wild-goose tail" of clerical script, and the "eight methods of the character 永" of regular script are all **the stylisation of features of a script**. Conversely, the brush traces of famous masters (Zhong You, the Two Wangs, Ou, Chu, Yan and Liu) were recut by later people into **new standards of character form**. The skeleton of today's Song type is Tang regular script filtered twice, through block carving and through the carving of written models. So, in looking at one stele or model-book, one must read on three levels: what script it is (history), what calligraphic manner it is (art), and who carved it into what appearance (the layer of the carving). This chapter need only remember one fact: **when we write "regular script", we are in fact enshrining, as orthodox regular script, the most popular "cursive" (the new form) of sixteen hundred years ago**. Zhong You's "statute script" (章程书) was very fast in its own time, too.
:::

::: intuition Strokes are the fossils of stroke order
The shapes of today's strokes are in fact **the remains of movements of the brush**. A left-falling stroke is the finish of a quick delivery towards the lower left, the brush gradually lifting; a right-falling stroke is one opening sweep after a press and a pause towards the lower right; a dot is the shortest single touch of the brush tip on the paper; a horizontal is the straightening of the right hand's natural arc towards the left. The habit of "reversed strokes" in children and beginners (writing a left-falling stroke as a vertical, a dot as a right-falling stroke) is, across the generations, built the same way as the confusion at the time of the clerical change, when people at large "wrote lines as strokes". What a clerk did on bamboo slips two thousand years ago, and what you do in the *tian*-character grid (田字格), is the same conversion into strokes. Once this is understood, the rules of stroke order are no longer commandments. They let the brush tip reproduce the history of the character form along the path of least effort.
:::

::: application Given an old rubbing, first give it a three-step check-up
First, look at the **stroke fingerprint**. Silkworm head and wild-goose tail? Even jade-chopstick lines? Lifting, pressing, pausing and turning all present? — a rough judgement of the script and its date. Second, look at **structure and posture**. Elongated forms that take a vertical impetus are mostly close to seal and clerical; forms that are square, level and straight are close to regular script; forms that are slanted and continuously linked belong to running and cursive. Third, look at **the support and the craft**. On a stele carving, look at the intent of the knife (a squared stroke is not necessarily the original look of the writing; a carver can produce a "broken-knife head", 折刀头). On a model-book, look at the generations of cut marks and ink traces (Song rubbings and Ming rubbings differ in whether the edges of the characters are fat or thin). On slips, wooden tablets and silk manuscripts, look at stroke order in vermilion and in ink. The last step is to check: the catalogue record, the place where it is kept, the maker of the rubbing, and the pedigree of recuttings. Once this set of moves has been gone through, you have passed from "looking at characters" into "reading the materials". That is precisely the foundation of all coursework in the history of calligraphy and the history of scripts (the introductory method of [[calligraphy]] is this).
:::

::: quiz
"The 'silkworm head and wild-goose tail' of clerical script was the original aim of the clerical change." This statement (　).
- [ ] Right — without the wave and flare (波磔) it does not count as clerical
- [x] Wrong — the wave and flare is a product of fast writing and of the writing materials, and it is the artistic peak, not the motive
- [ ] Wrong — the silkworm head and wild-goose tail is purely a fake made by Song-dynasty copying and carving
- [ ] Right — the stele carvings of Cai Yong are all like this
::: solution
The motive of the clerical change was "to write fast, and to write economically" ([[#rule-libian]]). The wave and flare is plainly a manner of **artistic elaboration** in the stele carving of the Eastern Han. Everyday clerical script (slips and tablets, documents) is far from having starts and finishes so exaggerated. Taking a style for a cause is the commonest playing of the film backwards in the history of scripts.
:::

- [ ] the transition from oracle-bone inscriptions to bronze inscriptions
- [x] the clerical change — the transformation from ancient script to the sign system of clerical and regular script
- [ ] the First Emperor of Qin's unification of the script

What the unification of the script unified was the **script** (small seal script as the model for the Six States); it did not cross the line between ancient and modern. The clerical change alone is the pulling-down and rebuilding of the house.

::: history
The standard pieces of these fifteen hundred years are a few batches of stone and of bamboo and wood. The batches of Qin slips from Shuihudi in Yunmeng and from Fangmatan in Tianshui (several thousand pieces in all) turned "clerical script arose among the Qin clerks" from a legend (the story of Cheng Cun 程邨 and clerical script) into physical objects. The Han slips from Yinqueshan in Linyi, from Mawangdui and from Dingzhou spread out every growth ring of the clerical change. The surviving stones of the Zhengshi Three-Script Stone Classics of Cao Wei at Luoyang, and the surviving characters of the Xiping Stone Classics in Shandong (together with later recuttings), set up the coordinates of an "official character table". By the Kaicheng Stone Classics of the Tang, the Twelve Classics were carved again in regular script as fixed from clerical forms. Every dynasty felt that it had a duty to carve "the proper characters" for all under heaven to see. On the calligraphers' side, Zhong You's *Xuanshi biao* 宣示表 ("Memorial on the Proclamation") keeps its form through traced copies; the model-books of the Two Wangs take bodily form as carved model-books; and the steles of Ou, Chu, Yan and Liu became the ancestral models of the printed character skeleton. The history of scripts is never in a museum. It is on duty in the character skeleton of this set of "Source Han Sans / Song" (思源黑体/宋体) on your screen.
:::

## What comes next

After the clerical change, "how a character is taken apart" changed its system of rules. The modern account of radicals and strokes is in [[chinese-characters/strokes-radicals]]. Why the six categories (*liushu*) are a good hand at analysing ancient scripts, and often miss when they deal with clerical and regular script (the conflation and reanalysis of [[#rule-libian]] are one such reason), is in [[chinese-characters/six-categories]]. The three branches of calligraphy — brush method, structure, and steles and model-books — belong to [[calligraphy]]. Assignment: pick the five components in the most common use (氵, 阝 on the left, 阝 on the right, 月 (flesh), 艹), write two examples of each of characters for which "the ancient rationale is already broken, but a popular etymology can be told", and bring them to the next chapter to balance the books.

::: summary
- Get the four great names clear: seal (large seal script as a loose cover-name / small seal script as the Qin standard form); clerical (popular fast writing raised in status, the watershed between ancient and modern); cursive (comes first, and forms an artistic line of its own); regular (an abbreviated transformation of the new clerical script, settled in the Wei and the Jin, officially fixed in the Sui and the Tang).
- The four knives of the clerical change: conversion into strokes, conflation, omission and merger, reanalysis — the rationale taken off the shelves over a wide area, and Chinese characters entering an age mainly of signs.
- The driving force of the evolution is the speed of writing (the popular form rebels); the authorities offer amnesty through projects to fix the standard characters (stone classics, character books) — a period when scripts change generation is bound to bring a movement to standardise characters.
- Calligraphy and script form, two ledgers: today's regular script and the printed character skeleton are both the combined force of the popular fast writing of the time and of recutting through the ages.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise The Three-Script Stone Classics {level=1 check="242"}
In which year AD were the Three-Script Stone Classics of Cao Wei set up? Which three scripts do the "three scripts" refer to? Why is it a "coordinate instrument" in the history of scripts?
::: solution
The year 242 (the third year of Zhengshi of the Wei); ancient script, seal and clerical carved side by side. Three character forms of different ages, from one and the same classic, set out in parallel, amount to the authorities publishing a **comparison table of character forms across the ages** — dating, collation and the study of the history of characters each take what they need ([[#rule-biaozhun]]).
:::

::: exercise Scripts, sit in a row {level=1}
Classify the following materials by script and approximate date, and state the grounds: ① a whole stele with silkworm head and wild-goose tail; ② an edict plate, even, round and tall-rectangular; ③ early cursive, linked strokes dashed off, each character kept distinct; ④ the head characters of the *Shuowen jiezi*.
::: solution
① Han clerical script (the stage at which Eastern Han stele carving was made artistic); ② small seal script (Qin edict plates, the standard form on an official object); ③ *zhangcao* (the sense of clerical script retained, characters not linked to one another); ④ the small-seal system (ancient script and *zhou* script, 籀文, also preserved). One ground each: stroke shape (the wave and flare), the line (jade chopsticks), the space between characters (independence), and the format (a character book). Getting them all right is not hard. What is hard is **being able to state each ground** — to recognise a script is to recognise the "stroke fingerprint".
:::

::: exercise Line them up and date them {level=1}
Sort the following "script materials" by order of appearance: ① a whole stele in the manner of the Cao Quan stele (曹全碑); ② the Qin slips from Shuihudi; ③ edict plates in small seal script; ④ the Three-Script Stone Classics; ⑤ copies of the model-books of the Two Wangs. Then match each with one ground for the dating.
::: solution
Order: ③ Qin (after 221 BC) → ② the end of the Warring States to the Qin (ancient clerical) → ① Eastern Han steles (artistic clerical) → ④ the year 242 (a stone with ancient, modern, seal and clerical set side by side) → ⑤ the Eastern Jin (modern cursive, and running and regular, fully mature). One ground each: the standard form on the edict plates / ancient clerical on the Qin slips, the wave and flare not yet fully present / silkworm head and wild-goose tail on the Han steles / the three-script format of the Stone Classics / regular and running mature in the model-books. What this question trains is a "coordinate system of scripts" — first have an axis of dates, then talk about the causes of the evolution.
:::

::: exercise Tracking down conflation {level=2}
The clerical change mixed components of different origins into the same shape. Give at least three groups of examples that are "the same shape today, of different sources in antiquity" (for instance the component 月 and the component 肉; the left component of 服, the lower part of 有? — take reliable examples of characters as the standard), and explain the pit that this kind of conflation sets for "explaining the meaning from the form".
::: solution
Safe examples: ① the 月 component on the left is in fact mostly 肉 (腿, 肌, 背); ② the seal form of 春 is composed of 艸 and of 日 to indicate the sense, with 屯 as the phonetic; after it was fixed in clerical script the phonetic lost its form, and it shares a head with 秦 and 泰, of the same origin and a different substance; ③ the lower part of 有 comes from 肉 (the form of a hand holding flesh) and is today also written 月. For the mechanism of the pit, see the case of 射 (the fourth item of [[#rule-libian]]): after the conflation, taking a character apart and talking of a "rationale" is mostly a storey built on afterwards. Of every claim that "it is composed of such and of such, and therefore has such a meaning", first ask **did it look like this before the clerical change?** — that one question can head off nine-tenths of popular etymology of characters.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write an edict of amnesty for a revolt {level=2}
In the voice of a court official of some reign of the Eastern Han, write a memorial of two hundred characters, arguing for "carrying documents in clerical script, and preserving the classics in seal script" (hint: the advantage of the two tracks, standard and popular; the sacredness of the script of the classics; how pressing efficiency is in official papers).
::: solution
The main points: ① Acknowledge the reality — the registers of criminal cases cannot be managed without clerical script; forcing the people to write seal script is notching the boat to seek the sword. ② Hold the symbol — inscriptions for the suburban sacrifices, and the writing-down of the classics, should keep seal and clerical, so as to show that "the tradition of the script is inherited in succession" (字统相承). ③ Give the supporting measures — establish the study of characters, train boy clerks, collate the characters by the stone classics, and bring both tracks into official learning. Marking looks at how balanced the position is (clinging only to antiquity, or chasing only what is easy, both lose marks). Real discussion of policy has always taken place between the two tracks. The three stone classics of Xiping, Zhengshi and Kaicheng are all the detailed rules for carrying this memorial into effect.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short essay {level=3}
"The driving force of the evolution of scripts lies in efficiency; the stability of a script depends on institutions." Drawing on the material of this chapter (Qin clerical script on the parallel track, the stone classics three times, the printed character skeleton), write a short commentary of four hundred characters, and answer an objection in the form of a counterexample: "If efficiency comes first, why did Chinese characters never in the end take the path of phoneticisation?" (You may answer by citing the three conditions in [[chinese-characters/writing-and-language]].)
::: hint
Materials that may be used: the main text and the figure of this chapter; the number of surviving physical edict plates of the "unification of the script"; for how fragments of the Three-Script Stone Classics survive, one may consult the Language and Writing volume of the *Encyclopaedia of China* (*Zhongguo da baike quanshu* 中国大百科全书, the *Yuyan wenzi* 语言文字 volume).
:::
::: solution
A skeleton for reference. Point one: empirical evidence for the push of popular forms (clerical, cursive and running were all born of fast writing). Point two: empirical evidence for the pull of institutions (the project to fix the standard characters at every change of generation, and the baton of the examinations). Point three: the three conditions under which phoneticisation did not come about (morphemes forming blocks, the gulf between dialects, the anchor of the character books) add their weight precisely on the side of "stability" — efficiency has always succeeded in changing **character form**; when efficiency wants to **change the system**, it runs into the social structure as a whole. Where the conclusion lands: the history of Chinese characters = a tug of war between speed and institutions; the red cloth strip in the middle of the rope is called "the list of characters in general use". Marking looks at whether the counterexample is met head-on rather than dodged.
:::
