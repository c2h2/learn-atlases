Is 武, "from 止 and from 戈", a case of "止戈" ('halting the dagger-axe')? Is 忠 "中心" ('a heart in the centre')? — This is a way of splitting characters that anyone can manage, and it is also the afterglow, among ordinary people, of the analytical framework that Xu Shen 许慎 of the Eastern Han spent a lifetime putting in order. The six categories (*liushu*) — indicatives, pictographs, phono-semantic compounds (semantic-phonetic compounds), compound ideographs (associative compounds), *zhuanzhu* (mutually glossing characters; disputed) and phonetic loans (rebus) — are a "grammar" that Chinese linguists devised for the Chinese character. They have been in use for nearly two thousand years, and they are still, to this day, the common language of the compiling of dictionaries, the teaching of character recognition, and the explication of ancient scripts. What this chapter asks is not that the six definitions be recited, but that one learn **to use the framework and to know its boundaries**: which characters the six categories settle in a single move, which characters two thousand years of argument have not brought to a conclusion, and why the sentence "the six categories are methods of creating characters" is both right and wrong.

## The ledger of the six categories, and its origins

::: definition The name "six categories" {#def-liushu-ming}
The name "six categories" is seen in the "Earth Offices" (地官) of the *Rites of Zhou* (*Zhouli*): "保氏教国子，先以六书" ('The Protector teaches the sons of the state, and begins with the six categories.') (Our translation.) (The original text does not list the items.) Writers of the Han give three lists, and the lists do not agree with one another. The Postface to Xu Shen's *Shuowen jiezi* (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*) — indicatives, pictographs, phono-semantic compounds, compound ideographs, *zhuanzhu*, phonetic loans (with definitions and example characters). Ban Gu 班固, in the "Treatise on Literature" (艺文志) of the *Book of Han* — pictographs, imaging of affairs (象事), imaging of meaning (象意), imaging of sound (象声), *zhuanzhu*, phonetic loans. Zheng Zhong's 郑众 commentary on the *Rites of Zhou* — pictographs, compound ideographs, *zhuanzhu*, disposing of affairs (处事), phonetic loans, harmonised sound (谐声). The names differ a little; what they point to is roughly the same. Later ages follow Xu Shen's names. **The six categories are, first of all, an "analytical classification"**: they answer "into which classes can characters already in existence be placed?", and they do not strictly answer "in what order were the characters made?".
:::

::: theorem Xu Shen's four operational definitions {#rule-shuwu}
The definitions and the example characters of the *Shuowen* Postface are copied out as they stand. Take in, word by word, what they mean as operations:
**Indicatives** — "视而可识，察而见意，上下是也" ('Looked at, they can be recognised; inspected, the meaning comes into view. 上 and 下 are the examples.') (Our translation.): the symbol cannot stand on its own as a picture; one must "inspect" its position and its abstract relations in order to grasp the sense.
**Pictographs** — "画成其物，随体诘诎，日月是也" ('Draw the thing to completion, bending and twisting to follow its body. 日 and 月 are the examples.') (Our translation.): the outer form is depicted, "bending along with the object" without a stroke out of place.
**Phono-semantic compounds** — "以事为名，取譬相成，江河是也" ('Take the matter as the name, and take an analogy so that the two complete each other. 江 and 河 are the examples.') (Our translation.): the semantic component (the signific) fixes the class (water); the phonetic component (the phonophore) matches the sound (可, 工).
**Compound ideographs** — "比类合谊，以见指撝，武信是也" ('Set the classes side by side and join their meanings, so that what is indicated may be seen. 武 and 信 are the examples.') (Our translation.): two or more semantic components are put together to yield a **new meaning**, not a simple sum.
***Zhuanzhu*** — "建类一首，同意相受，考老是也" ('Establish a class under one head; where the meanings agree, each receives the other. 考 and 老 are the examples.') (Our translation.): the definition is a riddle of the ages (the next section).
**Phonetic loan** — "本无其字，依声托事，令长是也" ('At root there was no character for it; rely on the sound and lodge the matter there. 令 and 长 are the examples.') (Our translation.): no new graph is made; a character already to hand is borrowed to write the word.
:::

::: remark Why the four cards are played first
The six items are not six doors standing side by side. The real main force for "making new characters" is the **phono-semantic compound** (nine-tenths of Chinese characters go through this door); next come compound ideographs; pictographs and indicatives are foundation bricks, and there are few of them; *zhuanzhu* and phonetic loan are more like **methods of using characters**. Hence the line of Dai Zhen 戴震, in the Qing, has the theory of "four structural types and two uses" (四体二用: pictographs, indicatives, compound ideographs and phono-semantic compounds are the structural types; *zhuanzhu* and phonetic loan are the uses). Its influence has been far-reaching, but it is not a settled conclusion. The order of study is arranged on the same plan: first recognise the foundation (pictographs and indicatives), then look at the assembly (compound ideographs and phono-semantic compounds), and at last come up against the unsettled cases (*zhuanzhu* and phonetic loan).
:::

## Pictographs and indicatives: foundation and ceiling

::: example How much a pictograph's "likeness" is like {#ex-xiangxing}
日 is a circle with a solid stroke inside, 月 a crescent like a bowstring with a shadow in it, 鱼 has head, tail, fins and scales, 网 is drawn as a square mesh — these are the explanations of the age of the *Shuowen*. Please test them against the graphs of oracle-bone and bronze inscriptions: **is a pictograph a "sketch from life", or "an outline with distinguishing marks added"?**
::: solution
It is the latter. In oracle-bone script, 日 is a circle with a dot in it (or indeed an empty circle); it does not draw the sun giving off light. 月 is a half-ring plus one stroke, only so as to be told apart from 日 and 夕. 鱼 is in fact simplified until only three sets of distinguishing features remain: the head, the spines of the body, and the tail. The conclusion: **a pictograph = a line drawing of the minimal distinguishing features**, on the same principle as a cartoon — it does not pursue likeness; it pursues "being the only one of its kind inside this system". This solves two great puzzles about pictographs. Why are the strokes cut down until they can hardly be cut down further (one stops as soon as the features are enough)? And why do "different characters for the same thing, and the same character for different things" occur from time to time (the system needs distinctions; it does not need to be taxonomically correct)?
:::
:::

::: theorem Indicatives: the narrow gate for abstract meaning {#rule-zhishi}
Indicatives divide into two paths. **Pure symbols**: 一, 二 and 三 (strokes piled up), and 上 and 下 (originally written "丄" and "丅", the position of a short horizontal relative to a long horizontal expressing "above" and "below") — the most abstract. **Pictographs with a mark added** (also called "attached indicatives", 附指事): 刃 (a dot added on the edge of the knife), 本 and 末 (a horizontal stroke added below or above 木 to mark root and tip), 甘 (the one stroke held inside 口). Note: this path **cannot produce new characters in any quantity** — the room for symbols is limited; with 一, 二 and 三 one has already come to "three" and must still produce a 四, and an abstract concept cannot be drawn in outline. Indicatives and pictographs therefore make up a tiny share of the character system (together one or two per cent within tables of characters in general use), and yet they contribute high-frequency characters of the kind 本, 末, 刃 and 甘, and **the whole terminology of analysis**.
:::

::: quiz
The character 亦 (originally the human form 大 with two dots added beneath the armpits) belongs to (　　).
- [ ] pictograph
- [x] indicative (a mark attached to a pictograph)
- [ ] compound ideograph
- [ ] phono-semantic compound
::: solution
A human form plus two dots marking the place of the "armpit": the main body is a picture, and the pointing is done by a symbol. That is precisely an "attached indicative" ([[#rule-zhishi]]). 亦 was later borrowed as an adverb, and the character 腋 was then made — a specimen of one character carrying several cases (see [[#def-jiajie]]).
:::
:::

## Compound ideographs and phono-semantic compounds: assembly and mass production

::: definition Compound ideographs {#def-huiyi}
Two or more **semantic components** are set side by side and combine into a new meaning. Classic structures include 休 (a person leaning on a tree), 明 (sun and moon brought together, or from 囧 and from 月), 涉 (two 止 passing through water) and 采 (a hand upon a tree). The boundary between the compound ideograph and the pictograph is whether the components are themselves still each a character in their own right (果 depicts fruit hanging on a tree, the form of a single thing → a pictograph; 休 is a relation between two events → a compound ideograph). The difficulty in making characters by compound ideograph lies in **dependence on a pictured scene**: the meaning put together has to be concrete enough to be grasped, and so high-frequency abstract words mostly go by the phono-semantic route.
:::

::: definition Phono-semantic compounds {#def-xingsheng}
The semantic component (the signific, 形旁) suggests the class of meaning; the phonetic component suggests the pronunciation. 河 is "from 水, with 可 as the phonetic"; 湖 is "from 水, with 胡 as the phonetic". The industrial sense of "以事为名，取譬相成" ('Take the matter as the name, and take an analogy so that the two complete each other.') (Our translation.) is a **production line of unlimited capacity** — a new word appears, one chooses a semantic component and chooses a character of roughly the right sound to serve as the phonetic component, and the new character can hang out its sign (炉, 颅 and 栌, each following its own class). From the Shang dynasty on, somewhere around nine-tenths of Chinese characters have been made on this pattern. It is the root reason the character system has not died (the graph of the climb, [[chinese-characters/writing-and-language]]).
:::

::: theorem Three steps in judging a phono-semantic compound, and two great traps {#rule-xingsheng-san}
Three steps. First, split (find which half is a candidate for the semantic class, and which half can stand as a character on its own). Second, test (whether the modern sound of the phonetic component is the same as the whole character, or close to it — if not, look up its ancient pronunciation and the history of the sounds). Third, check (take a dictionary, or a book on the origins of characters, and compare the *Shuowen* explanation; watch for "false components" after the form has changed by corruption; for the trap of the 月 (肉) component, see [[chinese-characters/seal-to-regular]]).
Trap one: **position is not grammar** — the semantic component may stand above (空), below (盆), outside (圃), inside (病), on the left (湖) or on the right (歃). Habit favours the left and the bottom; the rule is not absolute.
Trap two: **phonetic components have a date** — 江 and 河 show, in Old Chinese, 鱼 and 谟 cross-rhyming, and there is the controversy over whether *xiesheng* series of the 省 and 睎 type contain traces of consonant clusters. The "inaccuracy" of a phonetic component is often a growth ring in the history of pronunciation (see in detail [[chinese-characters/phonetic-compounds]] and [[phonology/to-mandarin]]).
:::

::: example A classification that fails {#ex-weapon}
武: the *Zuo Tradition* (*Zuozhuan*) has "止戈为武" ('Halting the dagger-axe constitutes 武.') (Our translation.), and King Zhuang of Chu 楚庄王 was already using this account. By the *Shuowen* it is entered under compound ideographs; scholars of the ancient script today, however, say that 止 depicts a foot, and that the original meaning is "to go holding a dagger-axe (to go on campaign)". Please analyse: can this character still be sounded for an answer that lies outside the six categories?
::: solution
It can, and this is precisely the normal condition of the six categories, not the exception. The ancient graph is from 戈 and from 止, and 止, as the form of a foot, indicates moving forward — so "compound ideograph" still holds; only **the meaning that has been assembled has changed** (going on campaign displays 武, rather than the stopping of arms). The "止戈" account is not a conclusion of the study of the script. It is a **political interpretation** by people of the Spring and Autumn period (using the form of the graph to expound an idea — what is called a "nickname from outside the origin of the character"). From this comes the most important piece of method in the chapter: **what an analysis under the six categories takes as its object is the structure of the graph, and an explanation of that structure depends on the age in which the graph stands** — if you explain the rationale of a character from the clerical or the regular form, first ask whether the clerical change ([[chinese-characters/seal-to-regular]]) has replaced the parts.
:::
:::

::: definition The "class" of the semantic component and the "family" of the phonetic component {#def-yifu-shengfu}
What a semantic component indicates is a **semantic class**, not a definition. Characters with the "water" component need not all "have water", but they sit in the semantic field of "liquids, and involvement with water" (江, 洗, 沼 and 漏 each take one corner of it). The 540 radicals of the *Shuowen* are the general ledger of 540 semantic classes; the radicals of character dictionaries are its descendants ([[chinese-characters/strokes-radicals]]). A phonetic component, for its part, draws out an ***xiesheng* family**: a string of phono-semantic compounds that share one phonetic component (the 也 family: 地, 弛, 池, 施, 蛇) was, in the age when the characters were made, identical in sound with the whole character, or close to it. Corroboration inside the family yields the rough outline of the Old Chinese sounds (set out directly in [[chinese-characters/phonetic-compounds]]). An analysis under the six categories is therefore never finished when the splitting is finished: **split the semantic component and you join on to the semantics of the dictionary; split the phonetic component and you join on to historical phonology** — a framework from two thousand years ago, with one end tied to two courses of today.
:::

::: example A small survey of the 也 family {#ex-ye-family}
Taking 也 as the phonetic component, think of ten characters, mark each character's semantic component and semantic class, and then judge how the modern pronunciation stands to 也.
::: solution
地 (the earth component; now read dì), 池 (the water component; chí), 驰 (the horse component; chí), 施 (the 㫃 component; shī), 蛇 (the insect component; shé), 他 (the person component; tā), and then, split off further, 她 and 它. The semantic components inside the family each mark a semantic class (soil, a pool of water, a horse running, insects and the like, personal reference); the phonetic component is 也 in every case. Set against the modern pronunciation: dì keeps an old reading next to the linguals and dentals; chí is an aspirated affricate; shī has been fricativised; tā is a stop that has been devoiced — four or five kinds of initial in a single family. This is precisely living evidence that "what a phonetic component records is a closeness of sound in **Old Chinese**". If on the strength of this one scolds the phonetic component as "unreliable", it is like blaming the map of the old family house, on a genealogy, for not marking the new roads.
:::
:::

## Unsettled cases and borrowed characters: *zhuanzhu* and phonetic loan

::: definition Phonetic loan {#def-jiajie}
"本无其字，依声托事" ('At root there was no character for it; rely on the sound and lodge the matter there.') (Our translation.): the word has a sound and a meaning but no character, so one takes a ready-made character of the same sound and records the word with it. 令 and 长 are Xu Shen's example characters (the 令 and the 长 of a county magistrate and of a senior officer are in both cases a borrowing of the characters for "giving orders" / "long-lasting"). Still older examples are the first person 我 (originally the name of a weapon), 汝 (originally the name of a river) and 而 (originally the hair of the cheeks). **Phonetic loan makes no new graph, and so it is called a "method of using characters"**. It fits the Chinese character with a valve for "recording a word by its sound" — inside a system of characters that indicate meaning, great numbers of abstract words and of function words depend on it entirely. (其 originally depicted the form of a winnowing basket; after it had been borrowed as a particle and as a pronoun, the original meaning was given, separately, to a later phono-semantic compound, 箕, to carry — "the borrowed character seizes the host's place, and the original meaning moves house" is the common second half of a phonetic loan.)
:::

::: theorem *Zhuanzhu* in thirteen schools {#rule-zhuanzhu}
"建类一首，同意相受，考老是也。" ('Establish a class under one head; where the meanings agree, each receives the other. 考 and 老 are the examples.') (Our translation.) The two example characters, 考 and 老, share a radical and a meaning, and their sounds are close as well (the view that the 幽 and 宵 groups interchange between adjacent groups). The definition therefore has two principal explanations. The **theory of graphic transfer**: one establishes a single head-class (a radical), and identical meanings are imparted each to the other (mutual glossing, plus proliferation by adding a phonetic component, as when 老 gives rise to 考). The **theory of sound transfer / the theory of meaning transfer**: the sound turns in the dialects, or near meanings gloss one another — *zhuanzhu* as a making of characters. The schools are still unsettled. Textbooks mostly set out three theories and leave the doubt standing. A practical stance for the beginner: treat *zhuanzhu* as **a record of how families of characters proliferate** (mutual glossing + the same radical + closeness of sound), and in that way handle dictionary entries of the form "such-and-such agrees in meaning with such-and-such"; leave the rest to the specialist history.
:::

::: remark Why "the six categories are methods of creating characters" is both right and wrong
Right: pictographs, indicatives, compound ideographs and phono-semantic compounds really are four **means of building a graph**. Wrong: by the time the six categories had become an established doctrine, people of the Qin and the Han were no longer making characters; the scheme is a **classification after the fact**, drawn up for a stock of writing already in being. And the boundaries of the classification leak. Characters of the 为 and 射 type, conflated and reanalysed (products of the clerical change); the variant patterns of abbreviated semantic and abbreviated phonetic (省形 and 省声: a constituent is partly replaced, and the explanation has to be taken from a character book); and phonetic-loan characters (a character used, not a character made) — all of these prove the point. **The framework was born to explain the mainstream, and the corners dance forever outside the frame.** This is precisely the fate of every "typology". The good of it is that it can be taught, looked up and discussed; the upper bound of the bad of it is that it forces you to cram in what will not go in.
:::

::: history
Half the theoretical history of the six categories is the study of the Classics. The "six categories" in the *Rites of Zhou* are the name of a subject in the elementary curriculum of the aristocracy. Classicists of the Han fitted that name with a list of items (three schools: the "Treatise on Literature", which is based on Liu Xin 刘歆's *Qi lüe* 七略, *Seven Summaries*; Zheng Zhong; and Xu Shen). From the Jin and the Tang on, the bounds of the meanings were drawn gradually tighter. Dai Zhen's "four structural types and two uses" appeared, and Duan Yucai's 段玉裁 commentary on the *Shuowen* helped the waves along (in the notes to the Postface he supplements the account item by item). Then, in modern times, came Tang Lan's 唐兰 three-category theory (三书说: pictographs, imaging of meaning, imaging of sound) and Chen Mengjia's 陈梦家 regrouping, of the same origin but under different headings; and in the present age Qiu Xigui's 裘锡圭 *Wenzixue gaiyao* 文字学概要 (*An Outline of the Study of the Chinese Script*) once more cuts away the overgrowth and keeps to the simple (redrawing the line between "abstract characters" and "combined characters", and taking the position of renaming the phono-semantic compound a "meaning-sound character", 意音字). A "taxonomy" has been worked over for two thousand years. Every generation finds the generation before it not "scientific" enough, and every generation goes on writing its papers with Xu Shen's nouns — **a framework lives long because it is handy to use, not because it is unassailable**.
:::

How phonetic components are used in the study of Old Chinese is treated in [[chinese-characters/phonetic-compounds]]. Drill after the lesson: turn up any twenty characters in a dictionary, classify them one by one (pictograph / indicative / compound ideograph / phono-semantic compound / not among the six categories / left in doubt), and then check the answers against the *Shuowen* explanations and one compendium of ancient-script forms. The distribution of the ones you get wrong is a medical report on your "feel for characters".

::: summary
- The six categories are a classificatory framework that people of the Han made for the stock of characters: the four structural types (pictographs, indicatives, compound ideographs, phono-semantic compounds) give the graph its form, while *zhuanzhu* and phonetic loan have all along been set outside "the making of characters" (four structural types and two uses).
- A pictograph = a line drawing of the minimal distinguishing features; indicatives depend on position and on marks, and the way in is the narrowest.
- Compound ideographs assemble the meaning of a scene, and phono-semantic compounds run on a production line — phono-semantic compounds account for nine-tenths, and are the only engine of the expansion of the Chinese character.
- Judging a phono-semantic compound: three steps (split, test, check) and two pits (position is not absolute; phonetic components have a date). Phonetic loan and *tongjia* are one name with two substances.
- *Zhuanzhu* is a case left hanging for a thousand years. The draughty places along the boundary of the six categories (reanalysed characters, abbreviated variant patterns) are precisely the seam between the history of the graph (the clerical change) and the method of using characters (borrowed characters).
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Quick matching under the six categories {level=1}
Assign each of the following characters a class under the six categories (take the standpoint of the *Shuowen* as the answer, and add a note where the clerical or regular form has diverged): 上, 刃, 齿, 徒, 颈, 鼻.
::: solution
上 = indicative (丄). 刃 = indicative (a mark added on the knife). 齿 = pictograph (the row of teeth and the mouth; after seal script adds 止 as a phonetic, it stands close to a phono-semantic compound — to note this is to gain extra marks). 徒 = phono-semantic compound (从辵、土声: from 辵, with 土 as the phonetic). 颈 = phono-semantic compound (从页、巠声: from 页, with 巠 as the phonetic). 鼻 = phono-semantic compound (从自、畀声: from 自, with 畀 as the phonetic; 自 originally depicted the form of a nose). The crux of this question is that **an answer must be able to produce the "从某某声" formula of a phono-semantic compound** ('from such-and-such, with such-and-such as the phonetic'). If it cannot be produced, the answer is judged not yet mastered.
:::

::: exercise True components and false {level=1}
Of the 月 component, or other apparent semantic components, in the regular-script forms below, which are true semantic components (the semantic class is still on the job), and which are false components from a change of form in the clerical change? Judge, and explain: 肝, 胡, 有, 修, 盆, 京.
::: solution
肝 (月 = 肉, indicating a part of the body: a true semantic component). 胡 (from 肉, with 古 as the phonetic; the 月 component keeps a "flesh" sense of the "ox's throat" kind, which belongs to the original meaning: true, but a character book is needed). 有 (the oracle-bone form is from 又 and from 肉; a hand holding meat is "to have"; the 月 in the body of the regular script is precisely 肉 — true). 修 (from 彡, with 攸 as the phonetic; 彡 indicates patterned ornament: true). 盆 (皿: true). 京 (a pictograph; the small 口 below is a trace of the form, not a component, and there is no call to speak of true or false). The point of the exercise: **first ask what this position is in seal script**, and only then speak of true and false.
:::

::: exercise *Tongjia*, or phonetic loan? {level=2}
Judge, and give the reasons. (1) In old books 蚤 is borrowed for 早. (2) 乌 (originally the name of a bird) is used as an interrogative pronoun and as a negative modal particle. (3) 而, originally the hair of the cheeks, is borrowed as a conjunction. (4) A certain student writes 已经 as 以经.
::: solution
(1) 早 originally had a character of its own, and 蚤 is borrowed for it — ***tongjia*** (the level of writing), not the phonetic loan of the six categories (only a borrowing at the level of character-making, where "本无其字", 'at root there was no character for it', holds, counts as that). (2) An interrogative, and a negative modal particle, have no form that can be depicted, and there was no character ready to hand either, so the bird-name 乌 was borrowed to write them — phonetic loan. (3) 而 is the same case. (4) A student's wrong character: it is neither "本无其字" nor backed by any standard; it is simply a mistake. The difference between (1) and (4) is precisely the examination point of the "threshold" in [[#def-jiajie]].
:::

::: exercise Write a dossier for 亦 {level=2}
Taking 亦 as the subject, write a character dossier of two hundred characters: an analysis of the graph, the original meaning, where the phonetic loan went, the later character, and any dispute over the classification under the six categories (if there is one). For materials one may consult the *Shuowen*, any compendium of ancient-script forms, and *Ciyuan* 辞源.
::: solution
Points the dossier must hit: the form 大 with two dots added, pointing to the two armpits → an indicative (a mark attached); the original meaning "armpit"; borrowed as the adverb 亦 (modal, or continuative); for the original meaning, the later phono-semantic compound 腋; the point in dispute — the borrowed use is current while the original meaning grows obscure, which is the common ecology of "a phonetic loan displacing the original character". The marking looks for all four items to be present, plus a note that 亦 and 腋 are ancient and modern forms of one word. Citing a book down to the edition and the page is better.
:::

::: exercise A short essay {level=3}
"把转注改成两个同义互训字的关系，这个定义就清晰可用了。" ('Recast *zhuanzhu* as a relation between two characters that share a meaning and gloss each other, and this definition at once becomes clear and usable.') (Our translation.) Please write four hundred characters. First, according to whichever line you incline to (graphic transfer / sound transfer / meaning transfer), give criteria that can actually be applied; then say which materials that scheme would injure by mistake; and finally judge whether "leaving the doubt standing" is the more honest way for a textbook to handle the matter.
::: hint
Materials one may consult: Duan Yucai's commentary on the Postface to the *Shuowen*; the section on *zhuanzhu* in Qiu Xigui's *Wenzixue gaiyao*; any journal article that surveys the "history of theories of *zhuanzhu*" (search first on the keywords "转注 综观").
:::
::: solution
A line to follow for reference (the graphic-transfer version). The criteria = the same radical + meanings that connect + sounds that are close + an order in which they appear (examples of the sort often cited are 老 → 考 and 颠 → 顶; before citing them, first check the Old Chinese classes of initials). What gets injured by mistake: **pure differentiated characters** that share a radical and a sound (for instance 益/溢 were originally ancient and modern forms of one word, and yet they are not in the same radical), and characters that receive one another without a shared radical head (variant forms with the graph altered). The concluding paragraph should grant this: what makes the unsettled case "unsettled" is that the definition is only one sentence of twelve characters, and the example words are only a single pair. A textbook's "three theories set side by side + an operational stance" is precisely scholarly honesty translated into a way of teaching — this is not a blurring of the difference; it is marking the **grade of the evidence** for the judgement on the diagram.
:::
