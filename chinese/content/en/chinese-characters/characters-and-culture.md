## Four layers of characters and culture {#sec-ssisi}

**Record layer**: Chinese characters (sinograms) are the storage for a thousand years of canonical writings in East Asia — the four divisions, Classics, Histories, Masters and Collections, together with statutes and ordinances, medical case records, contracts and clan genealogies, handed on from generation to generation along this chain of characters (the methods of [[classical-chinese-1]] begin from this layer). The cultural significance of this layer is the firmest of the four. Without Chinese characters, the rate at which pre-Qin texts survived would have been a catastrophe; and the early writing of Japan, Korea and Vietnam likewise took Chinese characters as its boat.

**Symbolic layer**: The characters themselves are treated as things that have spirit, that have beauty, and that have authority. 书同文 ('unification of the script') is a political myth (Chapters 1 and 2); the Stone Classics are the standard likeness of the classics (Chapter 3); the imperial brush, inscribed boards, Spring Festival couplets, seal impressions and talismans for warding off evil are everyday life turned into characters. The old custom 敬惜字纸 ('revering and sparing paper that bears writing') — paper that has been written on must not be profaned, and must be gathered and burnt — is the most moving piece of folk evidence for the symbolic layer: for more than a thousand years, writing was venerated as a quasi-sacred object.

**Game layer**: Character-splitting, character riddles, palindromes, the pointed wit of name taboo, the art of names, two-part allegorical sayings (*xiehouyu*), drinking games. The game layer is the liveliest, and it is also the easiest to mistake for 'the nature of the Chinese character' — the fourth section of this chapter is given over to settling what the game layer is.

**Conceptual layer**: People's imaginings about Chinese characters shape culture in return. There is the faith in the historian's brush, that Confucius, in making the *Spring and Autumn Annals*, worked by 笔削褒贬 ('the brush records and excises, and thereby praises and blames'); there is Xu Shen's politics of names and stations (名分), in the words 文字者王政之先务 ('Writing is the first business of kingly government.') (Our translation.); there is the late-Qing battle of opinion between the 'theory that Chinese characters are backward' and 'saving the nation by Latinisation'; and there is today's digital anxiety of 'taking up the brush and forgetting the character'.

::: theorem Two cultural dividends, of the same sound and of graphic form
Chinese has few syllables and many homophonous characters, and so the pun on sound becomes a rhetorical tax paid everywhere: 鱼 for 余 ('fish' for 'surplus'), 鹿 for 禄 ('deer' for 'emolument'), 蝠 for 福 ('bat' for 'good fortune'), 瓶 for 平 ('vase' for 'peace'), 笙 for 升 ('sheng pipes' for 'to rise'), 莲 for 连 ('lotus' for 'in succession'), 猴 for 侯 ('monkey' for 'marquis') — patterns, New Year pictures and auspicious objects are put to work for a set of 'pictorial puns'. This is the dividend of **sound**. Chinese characters can be split, can be joined, and can have a sense read straight off the graph (望文生义), and so character-splitting, character riddles, character-divination and veiled language open a separate round. This is the dividend of **graphic form**. Each of the two dividends has its causes in biology (the economy of the channel) and in cultural history (the interaction of literati and commoners). To call them, all together, 'the essence of the culture of Chinese characters' is to take the roots of the tree for the whole forest.
:::

## Social history in the radicals — and its limits {#sec-sss}

The radical (section header) is a semantic ledger of the characters. Open the account book, and a little ancient history can indeed be read out of it:

- **The jade radical (the side-component in the shape of 王)**: 琳, 琅, 琼, 琰, 璋, 璞 — each pendant jade has its own name, and the refinement of a society ordered by ritual can be glimpsed from this. The original meaning of 理 is the working of jade, and the extended meaning is principle — one production line of meaning, 纹理—条理—天理 ('grain of the material — ordered arrangement — the principle of Heaven').
- **The 示 radical**: 祀, 祖, 神, 祈祷, 祸, 福, 祝, 咒 — every character connected with mysterious affairs stands around this altar ('示' depicts the stand for the spirit tablet).
- **The 貝 radical**: 財, 貨, 買, 賣, 賞, 罰, 賠, 賺 — the age in which cowries (sea shells) served as money is sealed inside the graphic form. 罰 is from 刀 and from 貝, so that even 'punishment' carries the tangle of money and the knife.
- **The woman radical**: 姓, 姜, 姬, 嬴, 妘 — the great surnames of high antiquity mostly contain 女, and are widely cited as a surviving shadow of matrilineal clans. But the same radical has also piled up a batch of pejorative characters, '奸, 嫉, 妒, 媚, 妄'. Xu Shen recorded his characters in the Eastern Han, while graphic forms took shape across the ages, so the 'history of gender' in the woman radical is in fact **a history of the ideas of those who made the characters and of those who copied them**, not a true archive of women.

::: warning Characters are not fossils: three disciplines for reading 'the history in Chinese characters'
(1) **The ages do not match.** The characters under a single radical run up and down across several thousand years, and they cannot be pieced into a social snapshot of one and the same moment. (2) **The authors are suspect.** What the graphic form preserves is the worldview of the several layers of people who made characters, who arranged them and who copied them (for the most part the literati stratum), and it carries a standpoint. (3) **Interpretation is reversible.** Moral character-splitting of the 止戈为武 type ('to stop the dagger-axe is to be martial') can be played on any character one likes (split 貨 into 人 and 貝, and one can say 人为财死, 'People die for the sake of wealth.' (Our translation.); split 尖 into 小 and 大, and one can speak of 小大之辨, 'the distinction between the small and the great'). Any interpretation that does not rule out an arbitrary way of splitting has a value of zero as historical evidence. The disciplines are not a ban on cultural reading. They mark each layer of interpretation with a grade of evidence: for the class of institutions and named objects (jade, cowries, 示) the evidence is relatively strong; for the class of evaluative ideas (the pejorative characters under the woman radical) the evidence is weakest, and it needs corroboration from transmitted texts.
:::

## Names and name taboo: a politics of personal names that ran for two thousand years {#sec-ssbhw}

**The pairing of given name and courtesy name.** In earlier times the given name (名) and the courtesy name (字) ran side by side, and the courtesy name is a gloss on the given name. Qu Yuan's given name was Ping and his courtesy name was Yuan (平 and 原 are synonymous: a level plain, 平原). Zhou Yu's given name was Yu and his courtesy name was Gongjin (瑜 and 瑾 are both fine jade). Han Yu's given name was Yu and his courtesy name was Tuizhi (愈 belongs with 'advance', 进, and Tuizhi tempers it by the opposite sense). A pair of names hides a short lesson in semantics (the word-formation in [[modern-vocabulary]], 'synonyms completing one another, antonyms supplementing one another', is the line descended from it).

**Name taboo.** The given name is tabooed for a person one honours: a stroke is omitted, the character is exchanged for another, or the place is left blank and nothing is written.

- **Changing the character**: Liu Bang's taboo was 邦, and people of the Han changed 邦 to 国. Li Shimin's taboo was 世, and the shortened form 观音 for 观世音 came into general use (the birth of another name in common use was, as it happens, brought about by name taboo).
- **Changing the character, the second case**: Liu Zhuang (Emperor Ming of the Han) tabooed 庄, and in the *Records of the Grand Historian* and the *Book of Han* '庄助' was rewritten as '严助' — even a surname could be changed.
- **Omitting a stroke**: Confucius's given name was 丘, and the Yongzheng emperor ordered that 丘 be written with the final stroke left out. Descendants of Confucius's line, and those surnamed 丘, added 阝 to make 邱. This is a proliferation of surnames manufactured by name taboo.
- **Changing the character, the third case**: Kangxi's name was 玄烨, and 玄武门 was changed to 神武门, which hangs there to this day — the city of Beijing itself has on display a physical textbook of the study of name taboo. When 胤禛 came to the throne, the character 胤 in the names of the brothers was in every case changed to 允. Princes having their names changed is the shortest shot in the politics of name taboo.

The study of name taboo thus becomes a hard skill of classical reading. In one and the same book, a Tang edition writes 民, a Song edition writes 人, and a Qing edition omits the final stroke, so that the dating of a work and the identification of editions gain a further clock, a clock of characters ('dating by the taboo character' is one of the basic skills in the study of editions and catalogues; the detail is in [[classical-chinese-1]]).

## The game layer: splitting characters, character riddles, and pasting 福 upside down {#sec-ssyw}

The character riddle (the lantern riddle, the lantern tiger) is an intellectual game peculiar to Chinese characters, and the mechanism comes down to no more than three keys. **Addition and deletion**: 一口咬掉牛尾巴 ('A mouth bites the tail off the ox.') (Our translation.) — take the tail off 牛, add 口, and it makes 告. **Assembly as a compound ideograph** (associative compound): three people make 众, sun and moon make 明, and the small and the great set far apart make 尖. **The homophonic pun**: the face of the riddle says 莲 and the answer locks onto 连; it says 盐 and locks onto 严 — a bridge built of sound, the same mechanism as the auspicious picture 连年有余（鱼） ('abundance year after year', by way of 'fish'). Character-divination (the art of splitting characters) is the same mechanism turned into fortune-telling. One takes a character, splits it, adds to it, shifts it and reads it as an image, and composes a narrative of good and ill fortune. Its technical ceiling is precisely that 'interpretation is reversible' — of one character, ten people give ten readings — and so it is literature, not the study of documents.

Pasting the character 福 upside down, and answering, when asked, 福到（倒）了 ('Good fortune has arrived' — 到, 'to arrive', punning on 倒, 'upside down'.) (Our translation.): this is a late form of the **homophone game**, wearing folk custom as its coat, and the rudiment is already to be seen in the notebook literature (*biji*) of the Qing. It adds a layer of modern comic material to the ancient character 福. In the bronze inscriptions of Shang and Zhou, 福 is the 示 component paired with 畐 (the shape of a wine jar): wine is poured on the ground and sacrifice is offered in order to seek blessing — a compound ideograph of sacrifice, entirely straightforward and proper. Pasting it upside down is only a joke that homophonous characters have opened. The game is not at fault. **Taking the joke for the origin of the character** is the disease.

::: example Grading a 'cultural reading' {#ex-dj}
Rank the following claims by grade of evidence (strong — medium — weak — none). (1) 'Characters under the 貝 radical reflect that cowrie currency (贝币) was once used as money.' (2) 'The original meaning of the character 武 is to bring weapons to a stop.' (3) 'Pasting the character 福 upside down expresses the worship of good fortune.' (4) 'That 祖, 祀 and 祈祷 share a radical reflects the place of sacrifice in the world of ideas.'
::: solution
(1) Strong (archaeology and documents both attest its background in wealth and the use of wealth). (4) Medium (the grouping under a radical supplies evidence of a tendency, which needs corroboration from documents). (2) None (in the oracle-bone inscriptions, 武 is from 戈 and from 行/止, and the original meaning is to advance holding a dagger-axe; the moralised splitting of the character belongs to the Han account of 止戈, 'stopping the dagger-axe'; see [[chinese-characters/six-categories]]). (3) None (pasting it upside down is a late homophonic folk custom, and has nothing to do with the origin of the character — it proves only how people today play with characters). This set of grades is the methodological mark sheet of the present chapter.
:::
:::

::: intuition Chinese characters are the container of culture, not a specimen of culture
The container account: Chinese characters carry the Chinese language, and the Chinese language deposits culture. Read the *Book of Songs* and one reads grasses, trees, insects and fish; read the *Zuo Tradition* and one reads ritual institutions and military plans; what one reads is **culture inside the language**, and the characters are the conduit. The specimen account: reading the graphic form straight as a slice of social history, asserting 'the status of women' the moment one sees 女, celebrating 'the virtue of jade' the moment one sees 玉, and taking the conduit for a cultural relic. The container account welcomes every humanistic curiosity; the specimen account needs the three disciplines of this chapter to bring the fever down. A person of good cultural literacy who writes characters ought to be **a practised containerist + a clear-headed anti-specimenist**.
:::

::: example The two identity cards of Guanshiyin {#ex-gyg}
The shortened form 观音 is a product of Li Shimin's taboo, in the Tang, on the character 世. Explain why 观世音 does not disappear completely from the documents, while the two designations run side by side for a long time.
::: solution
The constraint of name taboo has a boundary. Formal documents, examination papers, and the temple taboo in block-printed books are strictly a matter of omission and substitution; but a name spoken aloud, a scripture copied among the people, and an old title from a former age are hard to wash entirely clean. Hence a double track, of 'the name that complies on paper' and 'the name current in speech'. 观音 spread on account of the taboo (speech was already saving a syllable); 观世音, resting on the original canon, was not discarded (the sacredness of the old titles in the Buddhist scriptures). The two tracks endorsed each other, and a thousand years later they stand in parallel as two 'legitimate' names. Phenomena of the same kind are many. The point is this: **a political change of character can change names and stations, but it cannot change the tongue.** The tug of war between name and actuality is a stock drama in the political history of the culture of Chinese characters.
:::

## The identity of Chinese characters abroad: one cultural sphere sharing one set of characters {#sec-ssyk}

Chinese characters were once the common writing system of East Asia. Japan borrowed Chinese characters to make kana (假名); Vietnam borrowed Chinese characters to devise chữ Nôm (字喃); the Korean peninsula took Literary Chinese (汉文) as its official written language. What the 'Chinese-character cultural sphere' shared was not the characters alone, but also the classics, the statutes and ordinances, the etiquette of letters, and an ideal of civil government in the manner of 'unification of the script'. The concrete reality of this layer is still with us today. Steles of place names in Japan and Korea, phrases that pass current in Buddhism, and even the words of 'Japanese-made Chinese' (和制汉语) that have flowed back into modern Chinese (the travels of disyllabic new words of the sort of 经济, 社会 and 电话; see [[modern-vocabulary]]) are all living debts of the culture of this sphere.

::: history Towers for written paper, and literary peaks: a folk archive of the movement to revere writing
敬惜字纸 ('revering and sparing paper that bears writing') was made an institution in the Song and the Yuan, and flourished in the Ming and the Qing. Pagodas for sparing characters (character storehouses) stood all through town and country; paper bearing writing had to be burnt inside the pagoda, and the ash cast into water. In years when the examinations were held, scholars formed 'societies for sparing characters', and picking up writing and burning it counted as a deed of merit. Works of fiction such as *Stories to Awaken the World* used 褻字纸遭报 ('profaning paper that bears writing, and meeting with retribution') to exhort readers to the good. The deep motive of this movement was a belief of the symbolic layer — characters communicate with the gods and spirits, and characters bear upon emolument and fate — and it was also an identity project **by which the literati stratum, in the age of the explosion of print, maintained its own cultural capital**. The various *Rules for Sparing Characters* published in the Qing have penalties specified down to the fineness of 以字纸包物罪几何 ('Of what degree is the offence of wrapping things in paper that bears writing?') (Our translation.). They are excellent first-hand material for the study of 'the sacredness of writing'. After the Republic had begun, the societies for sparing characters gradually dispersed; but in Taiwan and Malaysia the institution still survives, and it may be visited as a custom remaining in living form.
:::

## Coordinates of this chapter {#sec-bzsz}

::: warning Three pitfalls
(1) **The theory that the graphic form can do anything**: meet a character and it must be split, and a split must contain a principle — nine tenths of the 'principle' split out is a projection of the splitter's own ideas in the present. (2) **Letting custom stand in for origin**: taking folk legends (the upside-down 福, character-divination, the personalities of the zodiac animals) for the origin of a character, or for a definition of the culture. (3) **Causation in one direction only**: 'Chinese characters shaped the way Chinese people think' — a more responsible formulation is 'Chinese characters, the Chinese language, the institutions of the literati, and the economy of writing shaped one another' (the cultural version of the proposition of Chapter 1).
:::

::: summary
Characters and culture divide into four layers. The record layer is the storage of the texts of East Asia. The symbolic layer is the myth of the unification of the script, the Stone Classics, inscribed boards and couplets, and the revering and sparing of paper that bears writing — the making sacred of writing. The game layer is character-splitting, character riddles, character-divination and auspicious homophones — the two dividends, of graphic form and of the same sound. The conceptual layer is the imagining about characters which in turn drives policy and identity. Radicals hold a social history, but the evidence is graded: strong for institutions and named objects, weak for evaluative ideas, and zero for moral character-splitting. Names and name taboo are a politics of personal names that lived for two thousand years — changing the character, omitting a stroke and changing the pronunciation each produced its own results, and even 观音 and the surname 邱 are its products. Chinese characters are the container of culture, not its specimen. East Asia shared one script, and the heritage of that sphere still travels today in words and in objects.
:::

::: quiz
The linguistic facts produced by name taboo do not include （　）.
- [ ] an abbreviated form of a word becoming current (as with 观音)
- [ ] a proliferation of surnames (as with 邱)
- [ ] a changed character in a place name surviving to this day (as with 神武门)
- [x] newly created characters entering everyday use in large numbers

The ordinary means of name taboo are changing the character, omitting a stroke, leaving a blank where the character should be, and changing the pronunciation. None of them **increases the stock of characters in common use** (omitting a stroke produces only a temporary variant form, which does not enter the system of standard characters). The other three cases are all attested in this chapter.

::: exercise The taboo detective {level=1}
In a certain old manuscript copy, the final stroke is missing from every 玄, 弘 does not lack a stroke, and 寧 is not altered. Infer the interval bounded by the upper and lower limits of the date of copying, and state the grounds of the inference and its uncertainties.
::: solution
A missing final stroke on 玄 avoids the taboo on Kangxi's name, 玄烨, so the copying is not earlier than the Kangxi reign (upper limit 1662). 弘 does not omit a stroke, so the taboo on Qianlong's name, 弘曆, has not been avoided, and the copying is not later than Qianlong's accession to the throne (1735). The interval is the two reigns of Kangxi and Yongzheng. The uncertainty must be stated on two points. Lax observance of taboo by copyists among the people is the normal case (the lower limit can be relaxed further); and later hands, copying from a model, will also imitate a missing stroke in order to produce a forgery (the interval is only a first judgement, and needs corroboration from the paper and the ink, and from the style of the hand).
:::

::: exercise A post-mortem on the legends {level=2}
A folk story: when Cangjie created characters, 天雨粟，鬼夜哭 ('Heaven rained millet, and ghosts cried in the night.') (Our translation.). There is also a saying that 文王拘而演周易 ('King Wen, imprisoned, set forth the *Zhou Changes*.') (Our translation.) is the origin of the writing of the hexagrams of the *Changes*. Sort the two pieces of material by 'as historical evidence of what grade they may be used': first grade (reliable history), second grade (material for the history of thought), third grade (interesting and harmless), fourth grade (in need of debunking).
::: solution
The rain of millet and the crying of ghosts: second grade. It is not reliable history of the creation of characters, yet it is first-rate material, for the history of thought, on the origins in high antiquity of **the view that writing is sacred** (the symbolic layer): it attests 'how the ancients understood the power of characters'. The theory that the origin lies in setting forth the hexagrams: second grade, by the same reasoning (a sacred narrative in the learning of the *Changes*); but if a textbook takes it for 'the settled conclusion on the origin of Chinese characters' and teaches it to students, then on that occasion it is raised to the fourth grade, and must be corrected. The key to the grading: the material itself has no grade; **the way it is used fixes the grade**.
:::

::: exercise Make a character riddle {level=3}
By each of the three methods — addition and deletion, assembly as a compound ideograph, and homophony — make one character riddle (the answer must be a character in common use, and the method used must be stated), and attach one sentence of 'the riddle's reasoning'.
::: solution
Reference examples (it is enough for the learner to invent their own; the marks turn on the riddle's reasoning, not on hitting an answer). (1) Addition and deletion — 一口咬掉牛尾巴 ('A mouth bites the tail off the ox.') (Our translation.), and the answer is 告 (the reasoning: drop the tail stroke of 牛 and take in 口; the instruction can be carried out). (2) Assembly as a compound ideograph — 日月并明 ('sun and moon, bright together'), and the answer is 明, or 大小相悬 ('the great and the small far apart'), and the answer is 尖 (both the components and the sense are secured). (3) Homophony — the face uses 莲 and the answer locks onto 连, or uses 瓶 and locks onto 平 (the link of sound must be a strict homophone). Wherever an instruction of addition or deletion cannot delete the component that was fixed, wherever the components assembled do not make a character, or wherever the homophone leaves the rhyme or the tone, judge the riddle faulty.
:::
