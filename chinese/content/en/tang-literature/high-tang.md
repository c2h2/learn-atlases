---
title: "The High Tang"
---

Everyone knows the summit exists. **This chapter accepts the greatness and questions the geography: "High Tang" is a judgment made by people who were not there, ratified over five hundred years, and it organises the poetry in a way that flatters some poets, hides others, and teaches you to look for a peak in every literature you meet afterwards.**

## Two roads {#def-lianglu}

::: definition Wang and Meng: the official and the man out of office {#def-wangmeng}
**The standard account**: two poets of the first half of the eighth century are paired as writers of mountain and water, and the pairing was fixed by later criticism more than by their own projects. **Three distinctions the pairing hides. ① Their situations differ completely** — one was a successful official with estates and a music appointment, twice under a rebel court and twice forgiven; the other never passed into office and died poor. **The landscape they share is therefore two different uses of the same scenery: for the first, quiet as a reward earned inside a career; for the second, quiet as the condition of being able to write at all.** ② **The painter's reputation came later**: it is a Song critic's formula that makes the poet and the painter one man, and the formula did real work — it taught eight centuries of readers to look for the picture and to call the rest of the poem incidental (**the anthology-and-reputation mechanism from the criticism course, in a single famous case**). ③ **Their "school" has no manifesto**; the label was applied afterwards, which is why its membership list shifts with every historian. **Rule of the chapter: pairings are critical inventions, and you should ask what a pairing was for before you use it as evidence of a movement.**
:::

::: definition Gao, Cen and Wang Changling: two lenses on the frontier {#def-biansai}
**The standard account**: several poets of the mid-century wrote from or about the western garrisons, and their work is grouped as frontier poetry, much of it in the older lyric metres. **What the group contains is two incompatible lenses. ① The documentary**: march, weather, dispatches, the geography of a campaign, written by men who had served on staff — **the poem as a report from an institution, and a flattering one.** ② **The lyrics of the rear**: the garrison song, the wife at home, the drink before riding out — **the poem as a conventional voice, often deliberately unsigned, often older than the campaign it seems to describe.** **The chapter's claim: the second lens is the reason the first is bearable to read.** Frontier poetry is not war poetry; **it is a literary fashion that used the frontier as its scenery, at the exact moment when the frontier was the most discussable subject in the empire** (the military governorships, the command appointments, the campaigns everyone had a relative in). **The test of this account: the same poets write the garrison lyric and the pleasant garden piece with the same ease, and the frontier is a register, not an experience — which is not a criticism, since register is what a lyric tradition is made of.**
:::

::: definition One solitary poem and one blank page {#def-gupian}
**Two celebrated events, and neither happened as told. ① The solitary poem**: a single lyric by a nearly unknown author is later praised as a masterpiece standing alone across the ages, and in the twentieth century it is read again as the summit of the whole tradition — **a poem that circulated barely at all for a thousand years** (**standard account**: the fame belongs to the anthologists and to a famous lecture series of the 1940s). **② The blank page**: the story that a great poet, arriving at a tower and finding a poem already written on the wall, put down his brush — **a critical episode in the form of an anecdote: it is about influence, and it is told by critics of the following centuries** (**standard account**: the story is a topos of the poetry-talk tradition, and the poetry-talks' own rule is that the occasion cannot be trusted). **Why both belong in a chapter about a summit: the first shows that canonicity is conferred, sometimes a millennium late, by people with an argument; the second shows that the tradition's own way of talking about influence is a moral story — deference, rivalry, the unrepeatable moment — rather than a report.** **If you take one methodological sentence from this section: whenever a poem's greatness is told as a story about what happened, write down who first told the story and what they needed it for.**
:::

::: example Conceptual archaeology: whose invention is "High Tang"? {#ex-shengtang}
**The task**: date the term and the ranking. **Trace it: a Song treatise that makes the poetry of one period the object of a religious metaphor and tells the learner to "take the High Tang as model"; a Ming grading manual that sorts Tang poets into nine ranks with the eighth-century masters at the summit; a Qing anthology that installs the arrangement in the schoolroom; a twentieth-century chapter on "the golden age".** **Then answer: what does the ranking do to a poet of 760 who does not fit, and to a poet of 830 who does?**

::: solution
**The short genealogy is enough to make the point**: the period name exists before the summit does, and the summit is a teaching curriculum. **Consequences. ① A poet of 760 who writes brilliantly in the "decadent" manner becomes an anomaly needing explanation, and the explanations available (late bloom, decline of the age, personal peculiarity) are all conservative.** ② **A poet of 830 who answers the High Tang deliberately becomes an epigone**, which is the fate this course calls "a standard fixed in a past period" (the criticism course's Yan Yu chapter), and the mechanism is the same one in a literary-historical dress. **③ The ranking was a curriculum, and curricula are not descriptions: the nine-rank scheme was designed to tell a student whom to imitate.** **Marking: whoever notices that the concept of a "peak" is what makes late-Tang poetry a problem, and that without the peak there is simply other poetry, takes an A** — **that is the chapter's whole argument, and it is why the next chapter after this one begins with an argument about whether a certain poet's regulated verse was the natural end of the form or a betrayal of it.**
:::
:::

::: intuition The economics of a peak {#int-gaofeng}
**Why does every tradition need a summit? Four services, none of them aesthetic. ① Ranking cost**: a canon with a summit lets a teacher grade without justifying, which is why examination systems love periods. **② Attention budget**: an anthology can print forty poems if everyone agrees which forty. **③ Recruitment**: a peak is a promise to new writers that the tradition has a standard they might reach, which is the same function the criticism course assigned to the unfinished list, reversed — **a completed list is a lure**. ④ **Defence**: when the canon is attacked, a summit is what you point at. **And the costs are real: the peak model requires decline, so a tradition with a summit must write the next two centuries as loss** (which is precisely what the "Late Tang" chapter exists to undo), **and it under-values experiment, because experiment looks like deviation at the moment it is made.** **The rule for your own reading: when you find yourself defending a peak, ask what work the peak is doing for you — teaching, hiring, justifying a syllabus — and whether the poetry needs you to be an economist at all.**
:::

::: history A curve ratified afterwards {#sec-zhuiren}
**Eighth century**: an enormous surviving body of verse, most of it occasional, much of it written by officials, and nobody using the word "High" about their own time (**standard account**). **Ninth to tenth centuries**: critics sort, grade and anthologise; a few names are already treated as classics, and the argument is about who belongs with them (**standard account**). **Song**: the model is set — a period becomes the object to be imitated, and Du Fu is canonised with an intensity that has no parallel for a poet only a century and a half dead (**standard account**). **Yuan to Ming**: the grade-tables divide the Tang into four periods, a scheme that survives into every modern textbook (**standard account**), **and it is in this period that the summit takes the shape we still use.** **Qing**: imperial anthologies and competing schools argue about the road up the mountain (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: periodisation is imported from European literary history, and "golden age" supplies a new metaphor for an old ranking (**standard account**). **So the curve is real in this sense only: the volume and quality of surviving eighth-century occasional verse is genuinely extraordinary. Everything else — the shape, the summit, the decline after it — is five centuries of ratification, and this chapter has been about the ratifiers.** **Next chapter: what one man did with the finished form, and what it cost him in a political settlement he misread.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Using a pairing as evidence of a school** (**pairings are inventions**); **reading frontier poetry as war testimony** (**a register, not an experience**); **accepting a fame story without dating it** (**who first told it, and why**); **treating decline as a fact after a peak** (**the peak requires it**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: peaks and their accountants {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a literary history organised around a golden age is an accounting device: it converts an uneven distribution of survivors into a curve, and the curve then generates two permanent research programmes — explaining the ascent and apologising for the descent. Neither programme is false; both are artifacts of the decision to draw a peak, and the decision is never argued in the book where the peak appears, because by then it is the table of contents.** Deploy at least six of: **the two roads**, **the two lenses**, **the solitary poem**, **the blank page**, **the conceptual archaeology**, **the four services of a peak**, **the ratification sequence**. Then adjudicate: **can a department teach a literature without a summit — and what would it teach instead?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**an alternative syllabus, not a complaint**). **Passing shapes: teach by problems** (the regulated form and its difficulties; the commission and its genres; the archive's accidents); **teach by venue** (court, itinerary, temple, frontier, printing house), which keeps the institutions visible and the ranking local; **teach by reception**, using the ratification sequence itself as the course, which has the merit of teaching the discipline as part of the object. **Highest marks for the cost stated honestly: without a summit the course loses its assessment instrument** — **grades must then rest on documented judgement, which is slower and more arguable, and departments buy speed** — and **for the closing observation that the chapter on "the High Tang" is the one place where the syllabus reveals what it is for.**
:::
