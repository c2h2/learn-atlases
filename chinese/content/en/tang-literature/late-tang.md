---
title: "Late Tang poetry"
---

This period is taught as a decline after the summit. **The chapter's counter-argument: its two greatest poets wrote in full knowledge that the tradition had produced something unrepeatable, and that condition — writing after — is the ordinary condition of every later literature, which makes these writers our contemporaries and the High Tang's foreigners.**

## The lesser Li and Du {#def-xiaolidu}

::: definition Du Mu: history written as revisable {#def-dumu}
**The standard account**: a poet of the early ninth century, a grandson of a prime minister, served in several posts and wrote the celebrated quatrain about a ruined palace, the one about the red sands, and the ones where a historical scene is reopened. **The device to study is the counterfactual. ① A famous outcome is restated with one changed variable** — had the river not been defended, had a general lived, had a tower not burned — **so the poem's subject is not the event but its contingency.** ② **The tone is a conversational irony addressed to a reader who knows the history**: the poem requires the canonical account in order to undo it, which is why the form that suits it is the quatrain (a complete argument in twenty-eight characters, with the room to presuppose everything). **③ The moral is withheld in favour of the image** — the poem does not argue that fortunes are fragile; it arranges one scene so that the reader says it. **This lesson's claim: this is not decadence but a new literary function — poetry as a commentary on the tradition's own narratives — and it is the function that later occasional and inscription verse keeps. The High Tang did not need it, because its poets could still write a scene without presupposing an essay about it.**
:::

::: definition Li Shangyin: the maze with several doors {#def-lshy}
**The standard account**: a poet of the ninth century, dependent on patronage and caught in the network conflicts of his age, wrote dense allusive lyrics, elegies, and a set of enigmatic pieces whose titles ("The Sealed Letters", "Untitled") invite the very biographical reading they resist. **Three properties and the arguments about them. ① The allusion is deployed for effect rather than for argument** — a myth or a historical fragment is placed for its colour, and the sentence remains grammatically available to a reader who does not catch it, **so the poem works with the key and better with two** (**standard account**: readers have complained about this since the first commentaries). **② Multiple entrances**: several poems can be read as love, as petition for office, as lament for a dead patron, or as an argument about poetry, and **the readings are not alternatives but competing hypotheses about the same words, which is why the tradition's commentators split into camps and why the poems look modern to us.** ③ **The diction is compressed to a degree that makes the syntax uncertain**, and the uncertainty is productive: a nominal sequence can be a description or an invocation. **The methodological point, and it is the chapter's main one: the biographical decoding method is not wrong but unprovable in the ordinary case, and the poems are engineered so that decoding produces conviction without evidence.**
:::

## Three new resources {#def-sanziyuan}

::: definition History, privacy, end-times {#def-late3}
**What the period adds to the repertoire, in three items. ① History as material rather than as lesson** — the past becomes a stock of scenes whose outcome everyone knows, usable for irony (the previous section) — **and the practice is encouraged by the age's own sense of living after** (**standard account**: the great codifications and compendia of the earlier ninth century also make the past unusually available). **② The private**: a poetry of the interior — the bedroom, the illness, the dream of a dead wife, the object on the table — which the occasional and public genres had allowed only as convention; **its growth is measurable in the titles, and it feeds on the same manuscript culture that keeps a poem from becoming a publication** (the criticism course's rule: a text circulated privately says what a text posted publicly cannot). ③ **The end-time**: the empire's ninth century is full of collapses that everyone expects and nobody can prevent, and the poetry registers a distinctive tone — **not despair but competence in a system that is failing, and the pleasure of a beautiful object in a room whose owner is being evicted.** **This lesson refuses two readings: it is not "decline", and it is not "decadence", because both words describe the failure of an expectation the poets did not share. The honest description is a literature adapting to a reduced set of opportunities with an enlarged set of materials.**
:::

::: example Li Shangyin and the background-guessing method {#ex-beijing}
A poem addresses a person who is absent, uses one myth about separation, names an object (a candle, a screen, a letter), and closes on a scene of inability to sleep. **Apply the standard biographical reading, then break it: show that the same evidence supports at least three readings and write what would decide between them.**

::: solution
**The biographical reading**: the absent person is a patron, a lover or a dead wife, and the myth and the object are evidence of that relationship; **commentators have supplied each of these with equal confidence and with the same lines.** **The break: the absent addressee is a convention of the genre; the myth is chosen for its colour, not as testimony; the object belongs to the standard furniture of the nocturnal topos; and the insomnia is the genre's ending.** **So the same evidence supports (a) a personal address, (b) a petition for employment, (c) an exercise in a form.** **What would decide: a contemporary document tying the poem to an event or person — a colophon, a letter, a reply poem, an anthology note. Absent one, the honest report is a set of hypotheses with weights: I would put most on (c) and (b) jointly, and note that (a) is what the tradition preferred because a poet with a love story is more discussable than a man writing well.** **Marking: whoever writes the last sentence rather than choosing a favourite takes an A**, and the general rule is the course's oldest: **an interpretation that cannot be disconfirmed by any further reading of the same text is a preference, and it should be reported as one** (the criticism course's "graded evidence", the academic-writing course's falsifiable claim, arriving in a ninth-century poem).
:::
:::

::: intuition Late clear and evening dusk: two weathers, two politics {#int-tianqi}
**Two phrases from the period's most quoted couplet — the late clear weather and the evening dusk — have become labels for two styles, and the labels carry a politics worth hearing. "Late clear" names a world still ordered, in which light returns and a garden is tended: it is available to writers who still expect the system to work. "Evening dusk" names an aesthetic of the last hour, in which the beauty and the expiry are the same fact** (**standard account**: the couplet is read this way by nearly every commentator, and that reading is itself a late-Tang invention about the Tang). **Three observations. ① The two weathers are not two qualities of writing; they are two positions of the reader: one expects a future, the other does not.** ② **The second is more quotable, so it comes to stand for the age** — and any period whose best-known lines are its most melancholy ones is being summarised by its most defeated writers. ③ **Both are resources for later dynasties**: Song writers use "evening dusk" as a tone they can put on and take off, which is how you can tell that a manner has become a vocabulary (**the idiom-freezing mechanism of the vocabulary course, in literary history**). **Rule: when a period is known for one weather, ask who chose the weather and whether they needed the period to be sad.**
:::

::: history The size of the hat called "Late Tang" {#sec-maozi}
**Early ninth century**: the summit is already an inheritance; the great codifications and the argumentative prose schools make the past unusually available and unusually comparable (**standard account**). **Mid-ninth century**: the two poets of this chapter write, and contemporaries praise them for craft and allusiveness, not for epigonism (**standard account**). **Tenth century**: an anthology of "late Tang" verse is compiled, and the label begins its career as a grade (**standard account**). **Song to Ming**: the four-period scheme fixes a decline curve, and the period is read through it (**standard account**, previous chapter). **Twentieth century**: historians begin to object that the scheme is generic rather than political, and that the ninth century's politics were not decline but a change in how power was distributed — provincial commands, eunuch influence in appointment, and a court whose patronage still worked but no longer set the terms (**standard account**: these are contested questions with strong monographs on both sides, and the chapter's honest verdict is that the poetry does not settle them). **So the hat is sized to a critical argument, and the argument was settled before the poetry was read.** **The chapter ends with the reusable result: three of the four period labels in this course's literary history — early, high, late — were assigned by people who wanted a shape, and the shape is what you should cite when you use the words, not the centuries.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Using "decline" as an analysis** (**it is an expectation the poets did not share**); **confident biographical decoding** (**what would decide it?**); **treating the most melancholy lines as the age** (**survivorship of the quotable**); **forgetting that the period labels are critics' tools** (**cite the argument, not the century**).
:::

::: exercise Reopen a scene {level=1}
Take one historical episode everyone knows and write a twenty-eight-character argument that changes one variable; then state what your poem claims.

::: solution
Marking: **the claim must be about contingency, not about the episode's morality** (Du Mu's device).
:::

::: exercise Weight the hypotheses {level=2}
Take one ambiguous poem or text you know and write the three available readings with the evidence each uses; assign weights and name the missing document.

::: solution
Marking: **weights without evidence are forbidden**; the answer must say which document would change them (the worked example).
:::

::: exercise Essay: writing after {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a literature that knows it has a golden age is doing something a young literature cannot: it uses its own past as material, and its most characteristic effects — irony, allusion, the quotation worn thin, the beauty of a room being emptied — are all second-order. This is not decline; it is a change of raw material, and the critics who call it decline are measuring against a first-order ideal that the tradition itself invented afterwards.** Deploy at least six of: **the counterfactual device**, **multiple entrances**, **allusion as colour**, **the three new resources**, **the two weathers**, **the sized hat**, **the manuscript-culture point**, **the four-period scheme**. Then adjudicate: **is our own literature in a "late" phase by this analysis, and what would be its characteristic second-order effect?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**name the effect, not the mood**). **Strong answers pick one technique that presupposes a prior text rather than a prior world** — for instance: **the recap or "based on" adaptation as a primary genre; the review as the commonest form of narrative prose; the sample or the cover version, where the pleasure lies in recognising the source and hearing it altered; or the inside joke whose entire audience is people who have already seen the thing.** **Highest marks for the two-part conclusion: the analogy predicts that our age will be judged by its most melancholy quotable products, and that this will be as misleading as it was for the ninth century**; **and that the second-order literature is not parasitic but archival — it is how a tradition keeps its own memory usable**, which is exactly what the chapter said about Du Mu's reopened histories, one thousand and one hundred years before the same claim was made about ours.
:::
:::

::: summary
The chapter delivers: history (**contingency as a poetic subject**), the maze (**readings as weighted hypotheses, and the undecodable preference**), resources (**history, privacy, end-time**), weather (**two positions of the reader, one chosen by later anthologists**), period (**a hat sized to an argument**), and one verdict: **writing after is a change of material, not a loss of nerve.** Next course: the Song, where the examination state, the printed book and the commercial city each hand literature a new patron.
:::
