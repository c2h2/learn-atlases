title: "Early Tang poetry"
---

Textbooks begin the Tang with a sunrise. **This chapter begins it with an acceptance inspection: in the first century of the dynasty the most important poetic event was not a great poem but the completion of a machine — the eight-line regulated form — and the chapter's argument is that the machine made the genius possible, not the other way round.**

## Acceptance inspection {#def-gongcheng}

::: definition The completion report of regulated verse {#def-lvshi}
**The standard account**: tones had been made a conscious rule in the late fifth century; by the turn of the seventh and eighth centuries two poets are conventionally credited with settling the couplet-and-linking scheme, and the eight-line form with its fixed tonal pattern, its two obligatory parallel couplets and its restrictions on duplicated characters becomes the poem a man writes when he writes a poem. **Read this as a completion report. ① The specification was social**: an examination, an audience of peers who could hear a fault, and a large volume of occasional verse all required a form that a competent person could produce to standard. **② What the specification buys is comparability**: once every poem has the same load-bearing structure, poems can be ranked line by line, which is what criticism had wanted since the sixth century and could not do while forms were loose. **③ Its cost is that the interesting question moves**: with the surface fixed, all the difference is made inside — in the turn at the neck, in the choice of what to place in parallel, in how much of the emotion you are willing to commit to a noun and an adjective facing each other. **The lesson's claim, and it is the chapter's thesis: a closed form is not a constraint on talent but a distribution device — it makes small differences visible, and a literature in which small differences are visible is one in which many people can be good.**
:::

::: definition The Four Paragons: making a scene at court {#def-sijie}
**The standard account**: four writers of the seventh century, famous as children and unlucky as officials, are grouped under a label that their own age used, and they wrote both the court piece and the personal effusion, often in the same volume. **What they actually did, in three moves. ① They took the court form and put an ungenteel subject in it**: a farewell in exile, a complaint about posting, the indignity of a secretariat desk — **the machinery of the occasional poem, aimed at the writer's own career.** ② **They raised the rank of a genre**: the lyric-fu and the seven-character continuous piece gain dignity in their hands, and their famous line about a willing heart not changing the road's difficulty is exactly the sort of sentence that the court ode had no place for. ③ **They performed the role of genius-in-disfavour inside a court literature** — a pose available to them precisely because they were inside the system as children. **The structural point: they did not reject the court form; they changed what it was for.** That is the mechanism this course calls the repurposing of a sanctioned form (compare the fiction critics of the criticism course, and note that the technique always consists in keeping the apparatus and swapping the interest).
:::

::: definition Chen Zi'ang and the "wind and bone" coup {#def-chenziang}
**The standard account**: a poet of the later seventh century, in a preface to a small series of poems, declares the poetry just before his own time to have lost the "wind and bone" of the ages before the Tang, and writes poems in an archaising mode (his "登幽州台" with no one in front and none behind). **This is not a description and everyone knows it; it is a seizure, and its mechanism deserves the space. ① It invents a golden age and then a loss** — a move available to any literature with a strong textual tradition, and the same move as the criticism course's "earliest is a title awarded by a later discipline". **② It delegitimises living rivals by dating them** — you cannot answer an accusation of lateness. ③ **It offers an imitation programme** — "wind and bone" names nothing checkable, which is exactly why it can be a school's whole platform (the direction-word argument from the criticism course: unverifiable praise is an identity badge). **And yet the poetry is good, which is the chapter's real difficulty: a critical manifesto can be intellectually dishonest and observationally right**, because Chen was describing what the court ode actually felt like to read at the third hundred example. **Rule for the exam: separate the manifesto's argument, the manifesto's function, and the poems' properties, and never let a textbook fuse the three into a story about the dynasty getting younger.**
:::

::: example Review one examination poem {#ex-shiti}
Take a palace-style or examination poem of the period in any anthology. **Reconstruct the commission: the occasion, the patron or examiner, the constraint imposed, the audience expected — and then locate the one place where the poet had a choice that could have gone the other way.**

::: solution
**A workable shape**: an occasion verse in company, rhyming on a shared rhyme word, which commits the writer to a fixed set of rhyme syllables chosen by somebody else; **the expected audience is the room, and the reward is being copied into the collection.** **The site of freedom is almost always the neck couplet's turn** — the place where the described scene must pivot toward the Compliment or the resolution — **where a poet can make the pivot emotional instead of decorative. That one choice is where all early-Tang poems that survive differ from the ones that do not, and it is the reason the form is a distribution device rather than a cage.** **What the reconstruction prevents: reading the poem as self-expression, which misdescribes the transaction, and reading it as formula, which misdescribes the skill.** **Marking: whoever identifies a constraint that is not technical — a shared rhyme, a required mood, a patron's known taste — as the one that shaped the poem most takes an A**; **the chapter's general claim in miniature: in occasional literature, the commission is the author's second self, and the interesting poems are those that let the two disagree in one couplet.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Form comes before genius {#int-xingshi}
**A thought experiment: could Du Fu have written his late regulated verse at twenty? No — the form had to be finished first.** Three arguments. **① The closed form supplies the problems**, and a literature advances by its problems: the tonal rules of the 690s created difficulties that did not exist in 640, and only then could someone be brilliant at them. ② **A canon needs comparators**: to rate a poet you need many poems of the same shape, and that requires the shape to be settled; **praise is cheap when only one person is attempting the thing.** ③ **The reputation lag**: the great names of the eighth century are largely rated by critics of the ninth and after, which is a topic the whole course keeps meeting, and it is why "High Tang" is a later invention (the next chapter's first section). **The uncomfortable generalisation, which the applied-writing course has already made about prose: the availability of a form sets what can be attempted, and genius is largely the ability to exploit a form after it has stopped being interesting to the people who built it.** This is also the answer to anyone who asks why the great age came when it did: **the machine was finished, the examination made everyone a poet, and then the printing trade let the results circulate.**
:::

::: history From the inner court to the realm {#sec-neiting}
**Late sixth to early seventh centuries**: the southern court tradition, with its tonal research and its palace style, is carried north as the prestige literature of a unified empire — **a conquered south exporting its manners to its conquerors** (**standard account**). **Seventh century**: the examination's poetry requirement and the princes' and emperors' circles make composition a career skill; the Four Paragons and their successors are its first professional class; **the regulated form is completed** (**standard account**). **Early eighth century**: court gatherings with group composition, and the anthology habit; **the form becomes the default for serious occasional verse** (**standard account**). **Mid-eighth to ninth centuries**: the examination poet becomes a national figure, the circle moves from the palace to the provincial administration and the travel itinerary, and **poetry acquires a public that no patron controls** — the argument of this chapter's last section, and the precondition for everything after. **Tenth century**: an imperial anthology and, later, the great printed collections fix the canon the textbooks still use (**standard account**). **The reading: the venue moves outward in four steps — inner court, capital circle, provincial itinerary, printed page — and each move changes what a poem is for.** If you remember one thing from this chapter, remember the moves and not the dates: **the same form, four audiences, four literatures.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Reading an occasional poem as confession** (**reconstruct the commission**); **accepting "wind and bone" as a description** (**it is a platform**); **dating greatness to talent** (**the form had to be finished first**); **treating "Early Tang" as a period rather than a phase of a form** (**the boundaries are generic, not dynastic**).
:::

::: exercise Find the pivot {level=1}
Take one regulated poem and identify the couplet where the poet had a choice; describe the alternative he refused.

::: solution
Marking: **the alternative must be describable in the poem's own terms** ("he could have stayed descriptive"), not in generalities (the completion report).
:::
:::

::: exercise Write to a constraint {level=2}
Write eight lines to a fixed constraint (a set rhyme, a mandated mood, a required parallel pair), then write ten words on what the constraint made you say.

::: solution
Marking: **the reflection must name a specific line the constraint produced**; "it was hard" scores nothing (the review example).
:::
:::

::: exercise Essay: forms and their uses {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a literature's great ages are usually preceded by unglamorous decades of standardisation, and the standardisers are read last because their work is the floor the others stand on. This is not ingratitude but visibility: a completed form stops being noticed, and a writer who exploits it well is praised for qualities the form supplied.** Deploy at least six of: **the completion report and its three points**, **the repurposing move of the Four Paragons**, **the three-part anatomy of Chen's coup**, **the commission reconstruction**, **the form-before-genius argument**, **the four moves of the venue**. Then adjudicate: **is any of our own verse forms — the sonnet-length post, the three-minute song, the fifteen-line thread — in its Early Tang phase, and what would its High Tang look like?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**name a form and its phase, with a reason**). **Strong answers pick one platform's form, argue that its rules are still being settled (which constraints are enforced, by whom, at what cost), and describe what its High Tang requires — typically: a critical vocabulary that can rank small differences, and a canon fixed by something other than the platform's own ranking algorithm.** **Highest marks for the sober corollary: a form that is finished and free to copy produces the four-moves effect of this chapter — venue, not talent, decides what a poem is for — and a literature whose distribution device is proprietary has no Early Tang at all, since nobody outside the vendor can standardise anything.**
:::
