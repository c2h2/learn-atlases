title: "Applications of historical phonology"
---

The examination does not test the *Qieyun*, but those who have memorised the *Qieyun* score at every turn. This lesson draws five prescriptions out of the instruments of the previous eight chapters: reading poetry, collating texts, explaining words, correcting pronunciation, and teaching Chinese — the floor of historical phonology's "applied finance".

## Prescription 1: Reading poetry and metre {#sec-shici}

::: example A house-call slip for diagnosing lost rhyme {#ex-shiyun}
Diagnose, and give the line to take in explaining. ① Du Mu, "远上寒山石径斜，白云深处有人家". 'A stone path slants far up the cold mountain; deep in the white clouds there are people's homes.' (Our translation.) ② Liu Zongyuan, "千山鸟飞绝，万径人踪灭". 'On a thousand mountains the birds' flight has ceased; on ten thousand paths the traces of people are gone.' (Our translation.) ③ 'Plantain' (芣苢) in the *Book of Songs*, "采采芣苢，薄言采之。采采芣苢，薄言有之". 'Pick, pick the plantain; now we pick it. Pick, pick the plantain; now we have it.' (Our translation.)
::: solution
① The second division of the Ma rhyme (斜、家、花) — they rhyme in the Tang text, while the modern readings have parted into -ie and -ia. For the explanation, use the line "sound change is without blemish" ([[phonology/why-phonology]]). ② 绝、灭、雪 are of the entering tone (checked tone), the Xue and Yue class (薛、月). Putonghua (standard Mandarin) has lost the stop coda but keeps -üe as the shared trace; in Yue (Cantonese for the speech of Guangzhou and Hong Kong), zit/mit/syut make the whole line ring — playing one Yue reading in class is the recommendation, and it beats a thousand words. ③ 之 and 有之: the Zhi group (之部) and the You group (幽部) **cross-rhyme** (the rhyme changes every other line). A regular pattern of the *Book of Songs*, not a case of leaving the rhyme. The three slips each open one medicine: sound change, regional pronunciation, and verse form.
:::
:::

::: definition A converter between level and oblique tones and the modern pronunciation
Be able to use the three steps for level and oblique tones (also 'level and deflected'). ① **Level tone = yin-level + yang-level** (in Putonghua, of those read in the first or second tone, the greater part may stand as candidates for "level"). ② **Rising, departing and entering are all oblique** — but hidden in the departing tone are oblique characters from **the shift of voiced rising tones to the departing tone** ("市、近、慢" were oblique to begin with), and hidden in the yang-level are oblique characters from **entering tones distributed into the yang-level** ("竹、白、学" were oblique to begin with). ③ Wherever one meets "一三五不论" ('the first, third and fifth need not be insisted on'), one must check the **list of entering-tone characters** (the disaster zone where voiceless entering tones were scattered in their distribution: 笔、竹、屋、北、一、十、出……). Conclusion: **anyone who reads poetry or fills in song lyrics (*ci*) in Putonghua needs a quick-reference table of about three hundred entering-tone characters** — this is precisely the minimum usable deliverable that historical phonology gives to writing.
:::

## Prescriptions 2 and 3: Collating texts and explaining words {#sec-jiaoshi}

::: example A case record of seeking meaning by way of the sound {#ex-qiumi}
The commentaries of the ancients contain many phonetic loans: "信" is to be read as "伸" (the 'Appended Statements' (系辞) of the *Book of Changes*, "屈信相感". 'Bending and stretching answer each other.' (Our translation.)), and "早" is often written as "蚤". The old sound-glosses (*shengxun*) further include "德者，得也" ('Virtue is obtaining.' (Our translation.)) and "羊，祥也" ('The sheep is auspiciousness.' (Our translation.)). Please use historical phonology to grade the sound-glosses, and to tell the credible from the doubtful.
::: solution
The standard for grading: within three grades — a shared initial (*shuangsheng*; the initial the same or close), a shared rhyme (*dieyun*; the rhyme the same or close), and *duizhuan* and *pangzhuan* (the yin, yang and entering rhyme-types turning into one another) — it is credible; step outside the circle and it is spurious. **信=伸**: both are the *shu* initial (书母) and the Zhen group (真部); initial and rhyme are entirely the same, and the phonetic loan character (*tongjia*) is a valid cheque. **蚤=早**: both are the *jing* initial (精母) and the You group (幽部); a same-sound phonetic loan. A warning from the counter-examples: folk auspicious sayings of the homophone type "酒=久、鹿=禄、蝠=福" (wine for 'long-lasting', deer for 'salary', bat for 'good fortune') take only a nearness along one axis of the modern pronunciation, and let the meaning go first. The golden rule of the sound-gloss: **pass the sound first, and only then talk of the sense; if the sound is not enough, call it a "wishful association"** ([[modern-vocabulary/lexicon-layers]], to be taken together with the entry on folk etymology).
:::
:::

::: intuition The dialect is a free laboratory for historical phonology
Make a comparison table of your own dialect (*fangyan*) and Putonghua (start with fifty characters: level-tone characters with old fully voiced initials, plus entering-tone characters, plus fine-sound characters of the *jian* series (见系细音)). You will find that the fully voiced level tone, in Wu, keeps its voicing or a tone split by aspiration; that Min reads "猪、鱼" as the broad sounds of the ancient stage with no labiodentals; and that your dialect reads "街、解" in the k- series — **every strand of personal linguistic intuition can find its account-heading in the ledger of Middle Chinese**. This is why classroom exercises always begin from "read your mother tongue" ([[phonology/why-phonology]], the formal version of the dialect-detective questions): the inductive method of historical phonology digs its evidence out of oneself.
:::

::: history From collation notes to the history of Chinese: the academic employment of historical phonology {#sec-jiuye}
Qing scholars worked on the Classics by historical phonology (Qian Daxin's "in antiquity there were no light-labial initials" came out of notes made while reading the histories, not phonology for phonology's sake); Zhang Taiyan's *Wenshi* 文始 ('The Origin of Words') links word-families by shifts of sound (it opened the mountain of Chinese etymology, and it also opened the reproach of "forced interpretation"); after Bernhard Karlgren, historical phonology joined streams with archaeological material (examining a character in the oracle-bone inscriptions or the bronze inscriptions, one must often ask about the sound: a given character takes a given phonetic, and should be read as a given word); of the newly unearthed Masters texts of the twentieth century (Mawangdui, Guodian, the Shanghai Museum, the Tsinghua slips), almost every batch has had a famous scene of "reading the slips by phonological *tongjia*"; today it is employed in pronunciation adjudication for dictionaries (every choice to keep or drop an "alternative reading" is a paper in historical linguistics), in software for the metre of old poems and song lyrics (behind the checker lies a database of Middle Chinese), in the protection of dialects and in language policy (recording pronunciations from the libretti of intangible cultural heritage, and discussion of revisions to the Pinyin scheme), and in the international teaching of Chinese (error analysis: an authoritative bank of answers to questions of the "why" kind).
:::

::: warning Three kinds of overreach in application
① **Taking *tongjia* for a licence to write the wrong character at will**: *tongjia* is constrained by its period and by the tradition of copying; when literati after the Han write "蚤" as "早", that is imitation of the antique, not *tongjia*. ② **Taking a reconstruction for a licence to correct Putonghua**: correcting another person's pronunciation in the name of "preserving the old" (insisting, for instance, that "斜" must be read xiá), with no basis in the norm, is social suicide. The pronunciation table and the textbooks alone are the law in force ([[modern-vocabulary/dictionaries]], the two ledgers). ③ **Taking metre software for an authority on poetics**: whether the software follows the Pingshui rhymes or the "new rhymes" must be declared first; one who uses an engine of the modern pronunciation to judge Du Fu's poems as "leaving the rhyme" is a cautionary example of the tool.
:::

::: exercise A check-up on level and oblique tones {level=1}
Use the three-step conversion to check the level and oblique tones of Wang Zhihuan's "白日依山尽，黄河入海流" ('The white sun sets by the mountains; the Yellow River flows into the sea.' (Our translation.)) (mark the ancient and modern tone category of every character), and point out where it is level now and was oblique of old.
::: solution
白 (the *bing* initial (並母), entering tone → yang-level; oblique of old), 日 (rì, entering tone, oblique), 依 (level), 山 (level), 尽 (originally rising tone; the voiced rising has shifted to the departing, and it is now jìn; oblique of old either way), 黄 (level), 河 (level), 入 (rù, entering tone, oblique), 海 (rising tone, oblique), 流 (level). The only character level now but oblique of old is **白** — in the five-character line-pattern "仄仄平平仄" ('oblique, oblique, level, level, oblique') it occupies an oblique position, and to judge it level by the modern reading is a mistake. To pick out the identity of the three characters "白、入、尽" is to meet the mark.
:::

::: exercise A small clinic for bamboo slips and silk {level=2}
The relation of the slip text to the received text may be a *tongjia* loan of the same period, or it may be a systematic divergence in the transmission of the text. Open question: please judge what evidence the "*tongjia* theory" and the "divergent-stream theory" each require.
::: solution
The *tongjia* theory needs: examples, of the same period and the same region, of "辩/便" used one for the other, plus conditions of phonological principle (the two characters share an origin in initial and rhyme, and so have the standing to enter). The divergent-stream theory needs: a stemma of the manuscript tradition (which transmitted text the received text belongs to), plus a motive in intellectual history (characters altered under the attacks and defences of Huang-Lao and the Confucians). Marking looks at an awareness of how evidence is graded (closeness of sound is only an entrance ticket; the manuscripts and the textual examples are the main evidence). A settled conclusion is not required.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short essay: the social division of labour in historical phonology {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters: choose two of the five prescriptions in this lesson, and analyse the tension in them between "the professional threshold and public participation" — give one example each of the dispute, in poetry and song-lyric circles, between the new rhymes and the old (*Zhonghua xinyun* 中华新韵 ('The Chinese New Rhymes') vs the Pingshui rhymes) and of the battlefield of public opinion over the pronunciation table. Requirement: give your proposal (for example, a dual-track system, or labelling by grades), and close with the framework of "norm and description" ([[modern-vocabulary/dictionaries]]).
::: solution
Points for the mark: the conflict is described accurately (the tug of war between promoting the *Zhonghua xinyun* and poetry circles, and the storm over the 2016 draft for pronunciation adjudication — it is enough to write one case of each); the proposal shows a sense of boundaries (who follows which set, on what occasion — dividing the governance by register is a common solution); the close lands on the three ledgers, "dictionary / norm / scholarship", without crossed vouchers. Those who blur the difference ("either will do") get no marks.
:::

::: summary
The five prescriptions of historical phonology: reading poetry (diagnosing lost rhyme, plus a quick-reference table of the entering tone), filling in song lyrics (the three-step conversion of level and oblique tones from the modern pronunciation), collating texts (the period licence for *tongjia*), explaining words (a sound-gloss must first pass the axis of sound), correcting pronunciation and teaching (an authoritative bank of answers to errors, plus proof drawn from one's own dialect). The history of its academic employment proves that it has never left the floor: the reading notes of Qing scholars, the interpretation of slips and silk, pronunciation adjudication for dictionaries, engines of metre. The first virtue in application is **keeping separate accounts**: the old sound and the modern reading, norm and description, scholarship and the aesthetic — each back to its own house, each to find its own mother. The moment the vouchers are crossed is the moment historical phonology becomes, once more, a "cut-off learning". The course is completed here. May your linguistic intuition have an archive from now on.
:::
:::
