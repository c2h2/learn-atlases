---
title: "Why historical phonology?"
---

On the course list of a Chinese department, Chinese historical phonology goes by the nickname *juéxué* 绝学 ('a lost art'): the threshold is high, the legends are many, and it drives people off quickly. The first task of this course is to bring it down from its altar — it is not magic; it is **a jigsaw puzzle of three kinds of evidence**. The second task is to prove that it is **useful**: from the mouth of a child reading Tang poems aloud, to the decipherment of excavated bamboo slips and silk manuscripts, its turn comes round to take the stage.

## Begin with a Tang poem that does not rhyme {#sec-buya}

::: example The wrongful case of “斜” {#ex-xie}
Du Mu 杜牧, 'Walking in the Mountains' (《山行》): “远上寒山石径斜，白云深处有人家。” 'Far up the cold mountain a stone path slants; deep in the white clouds there are people's homes.' (Our translation.) In the modern pronunciation, xié set against jiā does not rhyme; teachers of the old school teach “斜读 xiá，古音” ('read 斜 as xiá — the old pronunciation'). Of the two accounts — “叶音改读” ('changing the reading by *xiéyīn* 叶音, an altered reading forced for the rhyme') and “古音本然” ('the old pronunciation was so all along') — which is right?
::: solution
The answer that meets the two halfway, and that is the correct one: **in the Tang, “斜” shared a rhyme with “家” and “花” (open, that is unrounded, Division II (grade) of the *ma* rhyme 麻韵), with a value close to [ɦia]; later change in the final carried it off to xié**. So “not rhyming” is the fruit of sound change, not a blemish in the old poem. As for whether, in the classroom today, one ought to change the reading to xiá — **when reading aloud, follow the modern pronunciation; when explaining, explain the old pronunciation** is now the mainstream way of putting it. To open a *xieyin* back door for the occasional character (the style of Zhu Xi 朱熹 (Chu Hsi), *xieyin* that changes readings at will) would wreck the whole history of the sounds; whereas to explain a lost rhyme by “古人本不如此读” ('the ancients did not originally read it so') (Our translation.) keeps the poem and keeps the linguistics. What this case shows: **whenever a rhyme is lost, ask first about sound change, then about the text, and only at the last consider changing the reading**.
:::
:::

::: definition Three kinds of evidence {#def-sanmen}
① **Internal evidence from verse**: the rhymes in the *Book of Songs* and in the poems and rhapsodies of later ages, reduplicated words (重言), sound glosses (声训), and phonetic loans (rebus; 假借) — rhyme is a table of same-rhyme relations which the ancients registered with their own hands; ② **Rhyme dictionaries and *dengyun***: *fanqie* spellings (反切), the rhyme dictionaries of the *Qieyun* 《切韵》 line, and the rhyme tables of the Song — the **structural records** which earlier scholars left when they tied characters together by rhyme and fixed the sounds by charts (*dengyun* 等韵 is rhyme-table phonology; a rhyme table is in essence a phonemic matrix made by the Song); ③ **Living evidence**: the modern dialects (Yue preserves the codas of the entering tone (checked tone), -p/-t/-k, and the -m coda; the literary and colloquial readings of Southern Min lie layer upon layer, like a section through the strata), the pronunciations of Chinese characters outside China (Japanese Go-on and Kan-on, 吴音/汉音; Korean readings, 朝鲜音; Sino-Vietnamese, 汉越音 — each one a time capsule of some dynasty and some place), and transcriptional materials (Sanskrit–Chinese transcriptions; the *Menggu ziyun* 《蒙古字韵》, 'Rhymes in the Mongol Script', which spells Chinese in the 'Phags-pa script, 八思巴字). Where the three kinds of evidence cross, there is an old pronunciation one can trust.
:::

::: theorem The regularity of sound change is the whole foundation {#rule-guilv}
Chinese historical phonology dares to mark old poems with pronunciations, and dares to divide the *Book of Songs* into rhyme groups (韵部), because it rests on the working hypothesis that **sound laws have no exceptions** (the slogan of the Neogrammarians, and it applies to Chinese): characters of the same kind, under the same conditions, should change in the same way. If “东” has changed and “同” has not, there must be a condition one can point to (the initial, the division, contact between dialects, analogy). Precisely because of this regularity, the three tone-forms “东董送”, of one common origin, and the *xiesheng* family “各洛路” (*xiéshēng* 谐声, a series of characters sharing a phonetic), can serve as premises of inference; conversely, **every “exception” is a condition not yet accounted for** — the catalogue of exceptions is the frontier map of the discipline.
:::

::: example The three kinds of evidence hear one case together: the entering tone {#ex-rusheng}
“千山鸟飞绝，万径人踪灭。……独钓寒江雪。” 'From a thousand mountains the flight of birds has ceased; on ten thousand paths the traces of people are gone. … Fishing alone in the snow on the cold river.' (Our translation.) 绝, 灭 and 雪, in the modern pronunciation jué/miě/xuě, rhyme only with an effort; Yue zit6/mit6/syut3 ends in -t, clean and hard. Reconstruct this case with the three kinds of evidence.
::: solution
Internal evidence from verse: Tang poems take 绝, 灭 and 雪 as one rhyme (the entering-tone class 薛/月), and in the Song scholars' *Qieyun* all nine classes of the entering tone are present; the record in the rhyme dictionaries: the *Guangyun* 《广韵》 pairs 25 entering-tone rhymes with 25 nasal-final rhymes (阳声) — **entering tone = a stop coda -p/-t/-k**, forming a set with the nasal-final rhymes in -m/-n/-ŋ, and this is iron evidence of a typological kind, given jointly by the rhyme dictionaries and the dialects; living evidence: Yue, Hakka and Min keep stop codas (Yue has the complete -p/-t/-k; Southern Min largely has -p/-t/-k/-ʔ), the readings of Chinese characters in Korean keep a final -k (國 guk), and the latent vowel in the Japanese Kan-on of “雪”, setsu, is a fossil projection of the old stop coda; whereas in northern Mandarin (*guanhua* 官话) the entering tone disappeared at the turn of the Yuan and the Ming, and the original entering-tone characters were scattered into the *yin* level, the *yang* level, the rising tone and the departing tone (the “distribution of the entering tone among the other tones”, 入派三声, of the *Zhongyuan yinyun* 《中原音韵》, 'Rhymes of the Central Plain') — the clipped feel of 'River Snow' (《江雪》) is precisely one layer of rhetoric which readers of Putonghua (standard Mandarin) have already missed.
:::

## The lost art is not lost: five uses of historical phonology {#sec-wuyong}

::: definition A list of uses {#def-yongchang}
① **Reading poetry**: understanding where level and oblique tones, adhesion and opposition, and rhyme come from (the whole course [[poetry-metrics]] stands on historical phonology), so that appreciation has one more ear; ② **Reading old books**: phonetic loan characters (*tongjia* 通假; 蚤 = 早, 信 = 伸) are records that the old pronunciations were the same or close; the explanation of alliterative and rhyming binomes (双声叠韵; 参差, 踌蹰) and of *lianmian* words (连绵词) cannot do without initials and rhymes, and the school of exegesis (训诂) which “seeks the meaning by the sound” (因声求义) relies on it alone; ③ **Compiling dictionaries**: normative adjudication of pronunciations (审音), the pronunciations marked in dictionaries, and every choice in language textbooks between an “old reading” and an “alternative reading” (旧读/又音); ④ **Overseeing dialects and standard pronunciation**: differences of north and south, literary and colloquial readings, and the pronunciation standard of overseas Chinese (*huayu* 华语) all require historical phonology to supply a “table of regular correspondences among the dialects”; ⑤ **Linking the linguistics of past and present with Sino-Tibetan comparison**: the sounds of Old Chinese are the pivot of research on the Sino-Tibetan family, and the *xiesheng* system, together with the problem of consonant clusters, remains to this day a rich mine for general linguistics. Besides this, it is also an **excellent course in the training of inference** — three kinds of evidence, the hypothesis of regularity, and the clearing of exceptions: from start to finish, the structure is that of a detective novel.
:::

::: intuition Two roots of the fear of historical phonology, and the antidote
Root one: **traditional characters and rare characters drive people away** — a rhyme dictionary in the original is a page full of “溪群见影、开口三等”, but those are readings off a set of coordinates, not incantations; root two: **the systems of symbols are many, and they do not connect with one another** — phonetic notations in the manner of Karlgren 高本汉, of Wang Li 王力, and of Pan Wuyun 潘悟云 each go their own way. The antidote: **keep a firm hold, the whole way through, on one basic checklist from modern phonology** (place and manner of articulation for consonants + a chart of tongue position for the vowels + the five-level system of tones, all of it already learnt in [[sounds-of-mandarin/speech-sounds]]); every time you meet a term of the old scholars, first turn it into a modern description of the pronunciation, and only then enter it in the table. Terms are the legend of an old map, not a new maze.
:::

::: history The disciplinary turn of “historical phonology” {#sec-zhuanshen}
Traditional historical phonology was an appendage of the study of the Classics (for reading the Classics, for composing poetry, and for collating books); at the end of the Qing it was still handed down by the three house methods “古音学—今音学—等韵” ('the study of old sounds — the study of the “modern” sounds — *dengyun*'). The turn came in the first thirty years of the twentieth century: Karlgren brought the comparative method and the dialect materials onto the same track; Zhao Yuanren 赵元任 (Yuen Ren Chao), working by structuralist methods, wrote *Studies in the Modern Wu Dialects* (《现代吴语的研究》, 1928), and fitted the “living dialects” with instruments; Wang Li's *First Steps in Historical Phonology* (《音韵学初步》) and *A Draft History of the Chinese Language* (《汉语史稿》) recast the three lost arts as one continuous history, a “history of Chinese sounds”; the *xiesheng* and Old Chinese system of Li Fang-Kuei 李方桂 followed. After 1949 it entered the course lists of university Chinese departments; in the new century, computational and typological methods have been laid on top as well (the Baxter–Sagart system, and automatic series-linkage, 自动系联). The “lost” in the lost art was, in origin, only the illusion that the periods were cut off from one another.
:::

::: warning Three common false ways in
(1) **“古诗必须读古音才有味道”** ('An old poem has any flavour only if it is read in the old pronunciation.') (Our translation.) — wrong; the art lies in the structure (level and oblique tones, parallelism, image (*yixiang* 意象)), and the history of the sounds is responsible only for explanation, not for laying down the law of taste (and besides, no one can truly produce the original pronunciation of the Tang); (2) **“姓氏、地名读音必须存古”** ('The pronunciations of surnames and of place names must preserve the old sound.') (Our translation.) — most “broken readings” (读破) are in fact popular readings that arose later, or misreadings that have set hard (“叶 shè”, as a surname, has already returned to xié with the 2016 draft adjudication of pronunciations; although the official version has not been promulgated, the leaning of the field is already clear); (3) **single-character rumours of the form “哪个字古音如何如何”** ('the old sound of such-and-such a character was such-and-such') — historical phonology gives regularities only for **classes** (characters of the *bang* initial, 帮母; characters of the *ma* rhyme), and it cannot give a recording of one character that crosses time and space; whoever hawks “the original pronunciation of Confucius” (孔子原音) character by character is a fraud.
:::

::: quiz
The hardest evidence for judging that “two given characters were homophonous in Old Chinese” is usually ( ).
- [ ] The two characters are similar in shape
- [x] The two characters are phonetic loans of each other, and *xiesheng* or an alternative reading is attested on both sides
- [ ] The two characters are homophonous today in some dialect
- [ ] The two characters are the same in Wang Li's reconstruction
::: solution
A phonetic loan is a vote, at the level of how the ancients used the characters, that the sounds were the same; *xiesheng* and alternative readings are a registration at the level of how characters were made and of how they were transmitted. A dialect's pronunciation today is an item of reference; a reconstruction is the result of inference — the grades of evidence stand clearly apart.
:::
:::

::: quiz
The fundamental problem with the “*xieyin* theory” (the Zhu Xi style of changing the reading of “家” to “音” so as to secure a rhyme) is ( ).
- [ ] The altered readings are too hard to memorise
- [x] It treats a synchronic phonetic loan as a diachronic sound change, and destroys the provability of the sound system
- [ ] It does not match the pronunciation of Southern Min
- [ ] It conflicts with the poetic rhymes of the civil-service examinations
::: solution
To change a reading at will is to declare that the ancients, in writing a poem, invented a pronunciation for the occasion, and every registration of a shared rhyme is void. The historical view of Chen Di 陈第, “音有转移” ('sounds do shift'), is precisely the remedy that meets the case.
:::
:::

::: quiz
The most accurate way to place a “rhyme table” is ( ).
- [ ] A score for singing
- [x] A phonemic matrix (a table of syllables)
- [ ] An anthology of rhymed texts
- [ ] A map of the dialects
::: solution
A rhyme table = a matrix of syllables which takes initial × division × rhyme table as its coordinates; it is a “spelling scheme + a distribution table” made by the Song, with its origins in Indian Siddham studies (悉昙学), and yet it formed a pattern of its own.

::: exercise Diagnosing a lost rhyme {level=1}
Diagnose the cause, in sound change, of the failure of the following lines to rhyme when read today (it is enough to indicate where the rhyme groups went): ① “少小离家老大回，乡音无改鬓毛衰” 'Young, I left home; old, I return. The accent of home is unchanged; the hair at my temples has grown thin.' (Our translation.) (回/衰); ② “远上寒山石径斜” 'Far up the cold mountain a stone path slants.' (Our translation.) (斜/家); ③ “青山横北郭，白水绕东城……萧萧班马鸣” 'Green hills lie across the northern outer wall; white water winds the eastern city. … *Xiao-xiao*, the parting horse neighs.' (Our translation.) (城/鸣).
::: solution
① 回 hui and 衰 shuāi/cuī rhyme across the *wei* rhyme (微) and the *hui* rhyme (灰) (in the Pingshui rhymes, “灰” and “微” are allowed as neighbours, or else this is ancient-style verse with its rhymes opened across groups) — in the reading of today, a difference of nucleus, ui/uai; ② Division II of the *ma* rhyme (家 and 花 in the same rhyme); later “斜” passed over into another stream of the *ma* rhyme; ③ the *geng* and *qing* rhymes (庚 and 青) are rhymed together (城-鸣); today the vowels have grown apart, eng/ing. Conclusion: all three cases are splits and mergers of finals, and not one of them is “the ancients read it wrong”.
:::

::: exercise Dialect detective {level=2}
Ask an elder from your own dialect area to read “国、黑、白、六”, and note the codas. With the three kinds of evidence, set out the sound-class of these characters in the *Guangyun* and their status as entering tone, and explain the rule of “scattering” (散派) behind their modern Putonghua readings (the *yang* level, or second tone / the *yin* level, or first tone / the departing tone, or fourth tone).
::: solution
Yue gwok/hak/baak/luwk, with a -k coda, = entering-tone syllables of the Middle Chinese type -ek/-æk; in the *Guangyun*, the entering-tone rhymes 德/陌/铎/屋 and others. After the distribution of the entering tone among the other tones in Putonghua, entering-tone syllables with fully voiced initials (全浊; 白 and 六 → the *yang* level, and the departing tone) go mostly to the *yang* level, and entering-tone syllables with sonorant initials (次浊; 六 and 绿) go to the departing tone, and so on. The rules are set out more fully in the chapters on sounds that come before [[modern-grammar]] — what this exercise weights is that the record and the classification agree, and a true record of the phonetic values is a pass.
:::

::: exercise Short essay: write a recruitment advertisement for historical phonology {level=3}
For new students in a department of Chinese, write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters: one personal experience of being stuck on a problem of “lost rhyme / phonetic loan / adjudication of a reading” (失韵/通假/审音); how historical phonology solves it; and, along the way, a reply to two common arguments for giving the subject up (“学了没用” 'no use once you have learnt it'; “太符号化” 'too full of symbols'). At least one correct use of a *fanqie* spelling, or of a term from historical phonology, is required. Pseudo-knowledge of the “孔子原音” kind ('the original pronunciation of Confucius') must not appear.
::: solution
Points for marking: a real sense of a problem (a rhyme in the lyrics of a song, onomatopoeia in a foreign language, or a surname misread — any of these will do); the solution must land on one of the three kinds of evidence, and not on loose talk of how “interesting” it is; zero errors in the use of terms; in rebutting the arguments for giving up, use this lesson's figure of the “map / legend”, or an equivalent of your own making; no serious blunders (the phrase “old pronunciation”, 古音, must first be limited to a period).
:::

::: summary
A lost rhyme is not a stain on an old poem; it is the receipt returned by two thousand years of sound change. The foundation of historical phonology is the three kinds of evidence — internal evidence from verse, rhyme dictionaries and *dengyun*, dialects and transcriptional matches from outside China — plus the working hypothesis that “sound laws have no exceptions”. Its uses cover reading poetry, reading old books, compiling dictionaries, overseeing dialects, and the link with Sino-Tibetan comparison. Terms are a legend, not a maze; changing a reading will not save a poem; an “old pronunciation” for a single character is a swindle. The real craft is to find a regularity for a class of characters, and to find a condition for an exception. The lost art is not lost; it was only waiting for a course list — and you are using it now.
:::
