---
title: "The *Qieyun* and the *Guangyun*"
---

If *fanqie* spelling is the brick, the rhyme dictionary is the hall that has been venerated for two thousand years. The blueprint fixed by this "salon for judging sounds" in the Kaihuang years of the Sui was extended, under the two dynasties of the Tang and the Song, into the *Guangyun* 广韵 ('Expanded Rhymes'), and for the thirteen hundred years after that no one has been able to get round it — this lesson takes you into the hall: to see its frame clearly, and also to see clearly the dispute over its character.

## A rhyme dictionary that a "meeting" brought into being {#sec-kaihui}

::: history The birth and transmission of the *Qieyun* {#sec-qieyun}
In the Kaihuang years of the Sui (about the 580s), noted scholars of the generation of Yan Zhitui 颜之推, Xiao Gai 萧该, Lu Sidao 卢思道 and Xue Daoheng 薛道衡 discussed rhymes by night at the home of Lu Fayan 陆法言, “论及南北声读，考校韵文同异，斟酌其折中” ('touching on the pronunciations of north and south, comparing where rhymed writings agreed and differed, and weighing a middle course') (Our translation.) — Lu Fayan recorded their discussion and, in the first year of Renshou (601), wrote out the finished *Qieyun* 切韵 ('Rhymes by *fanqie*') in ten volumes, containing more than twelve thousand characters and divided into one hundred and ninety-three rhymes (or, as some accounts give it, one hundred and ninety-four). Each character has its pronunciation noted by *fanqie*, and the glosses of meaning are kept brief. In the Tang, poetry and rhapsody in the civil examinations grew weightier by the day. Sun Mian 孙愐, with the *Tangyun* 唐韵 ('Rhymes of the Tang'), added characters for it and enriched the notes, and the book was revised again and again. By the first year of Dazhong Xiangfu in the Song (1008), Chen Pengnian 陈彭年, Qiu Yong 丘雍 and others, on an imperial order, recompiled it as the *Dasong chongxiu Guangyun* 大宋重修广韵 ('The *Guangyun*, Recompiled under the Great Song') in five volumes, containing more than twenty-six thousand characters, still divided into two hundred and six rhymes, with notes of *tóngyòng* 同用 ('used together') and *dúyòng* 独用 ('used alone') appended inside the volumes for the examinations. By modern times the original of Lu's book had almost entirely ceased to survive, while fragments of Tang manuscripts unearthed at Dunhuang and Turpan, together with old manuscript copies preserved in Japan, have one after another come to light again (the twentieth century saw various compiled printings, among them the *Tang xieben Qieyun canjuan* 唐写本切韵残卷, 'Fragmentary Tang Manuscripts of the *Qieyun*'). Its skeleton lives on, just as it was, inside the *Guangyun* — **a lost book living by means of a revised edition: this is precisely the normal way of working in the history of rhyme dictionaries**.
:::

::: definition The frame of the *Guangyun* {#def-jianjia}
The five volumes of the *Guangyun* are divided by tone: **the level tone is split into an upper and a lower volume (the characters are many); the rising, the departing and the entering tone (checked tone) have one volume each**. Within a volume the text is divided into rhymes: the level tone, from 东, 冬, 钟 and 江 downwards, fifty-seven rhymes; the rising tone, fifty-five rhymes; the departing tone, sixty rhymes; the entering tone, thirty-four rhymes; two hundred and six in all. Within each rhyme there is a further division into **minor rhymes** (*xiǎoyùn* 小韵: groups of homophonous characters, the first character given a *fanqie*, and beneath it the homophones listed straight down), three thousand eight hundred and ninety of them — **a minor rhyme is the "household register" of one syllable**, and the information of the *fanqie*, the initial category and the division (grade) is all nailed to the head of the minor rhyme. The order of the rhyme headings (东冬钟江支脂之微…) is not an index for looking characters up. It is a classification by the feel of the sound: neighbouring rhyme headings are close in sound, and the splits and mergers between them are nothing but the lawsuits of those who have judged sounds, age after age. First memorise one "set of the four tones": 东—董—送—屋. Level, rising, departing and entering run through as one series, and the entering tone, by its -k coda, stands as a class of its own — **the four tones are not merely pitches; they are four shapes of one and the same rhyme base** (the level tone takes two volumes to itself only because the characters are many; the characters of the 东 rhyme are all in the upper level, and there is no "half-rhyme" that crosses volumes).
:::

::: example One set of the four tones: 东董送屋 {#ex-sisheng}
In the *Guangyun*, the 东 rhyme has “东，德红切”, the 董 rhyme “董，多动切”, the 送 rhyme “送，苏控切”, and the 屋 rhyme “屋，乌谷切”. Set the initial, the final and the coda of the four characters side by side, and explain the logic by which the *Guangyun* arranges them, “以类相从” ('grouping like with like').
::: solution
东德红切 təŋ, 董多动切 təŋX, 送苏控切 sowŋH, 屋乌谷切 ʔuk (the reconstructions are taken from a framework now in general use, and only to illustrate): the three characters 东, 董 and 送 have the same rhyme base, and differ only in tone; the character 屋 carries a -k stop coda, and stands in a set-correspondence with the -ŋ of the *yang* category (nasal-coda finals; the pairing of *yang* and entering). The cataloguing of the *Guangyun* is the cataloguing of a phonology: characters arranged by rhyme, rows divided by tone, the entering tone made a volume on its own — a database with no index, the key a syllable, the value a *fanqie* and a homophone group. Go one step further. Once “送，苏控切” has been found, one can ask on from there, and it is clear at a glance whether character-heads exist, in the level tone and the rising tone, for the same rhyme and the same initial — the vacant places in the minor rhymes where there is “有音无字” ('a sound but no character'; the empty slots) were later filled in, one by one, with circles, by the *Yunjing* 韵镜 ('Mirror of Rhymes').
:::

## The dispute over its nature: which mouth the *Qieyun* records {#doc-xingzhi}

::: theorem The dispute over fixing the rhymes: the sound of one time, or a composite system {#rule-dingyun}
What is the sound system of the *Qieyun*? Three schools. The **single-system school** holds that it records the actual spoken language, or the reading pronunciation, of some one place (Chang'an, Luoyang and Yexia 邺下 each have their advocates). The **composite-system school** (Wang Li 王力 holds this view in 'On the Nature of the *Qieyun*', 《论〈切韵〉的性质》) holds that it “折衷南北、兼存古音” ('strikes a balance between north and south, and keeps ancient pronunciations as well'), and that it is the sound-judging committee's **highest-common-factor system**, not the speech of one time and one place. A further school regards it as a "normative frame of reference" serving the civil examinations and communication across dialects — man-made, and practical. The classroom position: **use the *Qieyun* as "a master list of the correct pronunciations of Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese), with phonetic symbols attached"** — precise enough that one can reason out the structure of initials and finals, and composite enough that one need not answer “老奶奶这样说话吗” ('Did Grandma speak like this?') (Our translation.). Note two facts that support the composite view. First, the division into rhymes is finer than that of any known dialect (the three rhymes 之, 脂 and 支 stand side by side, and the *yang* category and the entering tone are formed into sets so neat as to be, typologically, over-regular). Second, Yan Zhitui, in 'Yinci' 音辞 ('Sounds and Words') of the *Family Instructions* 《家训》, already denounces in the bitterest terms “南染吴越、北杂夷虏” ('The south takes its dye from Wu and Yue; the north is mixed with barbarians.') (Our translation.). Their ambition was precisely **to go beyond the pronunciation of any one place**.
:::

::: example The Pingshui rhymes: the "examination simplification" of the 206 rhymes {#ex-pingshui}
Is the rhyming of Tang poetry looser or stricter than the *Qieyun*? A line of *ping* editions of the *Xinkan yunlüe* 新刊韵略 ('Newly Printed Outline of Rhymes'), from the bookshops of the Jin (commonly called the "Pingshui rhymes"), merges the 206 rhymes into 106: a number of neighbouring rhymes marked "used together" are merged outright. Taking “东 and 冬” and “江 and 阳” as the examples, explain the grounds of the Pingshui mergers, and what follows from them.
::: solution
“东” is reconstructed as -uwng and “冬” as -uwng, and the main vowels differ (the distinction between *-uŋ and *-oŋ; it is enough to take the sense “音近可通”, 'close in sound, and so they may interchange'); “钟” is -jwoŋ and “江” is -æwŋ. In Tang verse, “东” and “冬” were already often not kept apart. The official notes of "used together" went with that trend and merged them in (such things as 东 and 冬 used together, and 江 in *tōngzhuǎn* 通转, 'interchange', with 阳 and 唐). The Pingshui rhymes go further, and raise "used together" and "used alone" to the rank of rhyme groups — with the result that the fixed scheme of poetic rhyme lags the pronunciation of the *Qieyun*'s own time by three or four hundred years, and was still written on poets' lips in 1917. The conclusion: the 206 rhymes are the skeleton of linguistics, and the 106 rhymes are what the study of composition has in force. Both sets of coordinates have to be loaded into the mind at once ([[poetry-metrics]] will use the latter throughout).
:::

::: warning Four guards when reading a rhyme dictionary
(1) **Guard against reading a rhyme heading as a word**: the head of a minor rhyme is a character representing a sound, not a unit of lexical meaning. (2) **Guard against ignoring an alternative reading**: one character with two readings often divides into two meanings (“王”, in the level tone, is a noun, and in the departing tone is the verb “王天下”, 'to rule as king over the world'); if one looks up only the first pronunciation, one will misread both the word class and the meaning (“王”, in the level tone, is a noun, and in the departing tone is the verb “王天下”, 'to rule as king over the world'; “数” and “雨”, on the same principle, distinguish meaning by the rising and the departing tone). (3) **Guard against taking a gloss in the *Guangyun* as the original meaning**: it is a rhyme dictionary, not the *Shuowen jiezi* 说文 (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*); the explanations follow common usage and are kept brief. (4) **Guard against using the sounds of the *Guangyun* to piece together an "ancient pronunciation" for a modern surname or place name**: sound class ≠ sound value, and a genealogy's tale of “读若×” ('read as ×') will not stand up to being checked against the *fanqie*.
:::

::: exercise Restating the structure {level=1}
Without looking at this lesson, draw a diagram of the structure "volume → rhyme → minor rhyme → *fanqie* / homophone group", and answer: why does the level tone occupy two volumes, while the rising, the departing and the entering tone have one volume each?
::: solution
A complete diagram counts as correct. The answer for the two volumes of the level tone: characters in the level tone (in poetry, and among common words) are the greatest in number, and in the compiling the upper and lower volumes were divided according to how many there were — the allocation of volumes is a compromise with the number of characters, not a split in the classes of sound (the characters of the 东 rhyme are all in the upper level, and no "half-rhyme" crosses volumes).
:::

::: exercise An essay taking a stand on its nature {level=2}
Choose one of the three and take that side (a single system / a composite system / a normative reference for the examinations). Write a defence of four hundred characters: cite at least two pieces of evidence from the lesson (the fineness of the rhyme divisions, the attitude of the *Yanshi jiaxun* 《颜氏家训》 (*Family Instructions for the Yan Clan*), the neatness of the *yang*–entering pairing, the notes of "used together" in the Tang manuscripts), and answer the strongest single objection from the other side.
::: solution
Points for marking: the evidence and the position must hang together (one who chooses "composite" may cite the three-way division of 之, 脂 and 支 as having no support in any dialect; one who chooses "single" must explain the neat pairing of *yang* and entering — typologically, it is not impossible). The objection put in the other side's mouth must not be a straw man. A conclusion of "undecidable" is allowed, and the mark follows the quality of the argument.
:::

::: exercise Short essay: from the *Qieyun* to *Xianhan*: two kinds of state that judge sounds {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. The Kaihuang meeting for judging sounds, and the Putonghua (standard Mandarin) committee for judging sounds after 1956, stand one thousand three hundred and fifty years apart, and yet the task is alike — to fix a "standard pronunciation" for a community above the dialects. Compare the two groups in their materials (rhyme dictionaries set against language data), their methods (comparing where things agree and differ, set against counts of word frequency), their predicaments (a compromise between north and south, set against following common usage and keeping to simplicity) and their products (the 193 rhymes, set against the dispute over the draft of the sixth edition of the table for judging sounds). And answer: why were both abused by people of their own time for “改音乱俗” ('changing the pronunciations and throwing custom into disorder')? You must cite one example from this lesson of the *Qieyun* dividing its rhymes, and one example of a controversy over the table for judging sounds.
::: solution
Points for marking: the analogy should land on the structural tension of "standardisation vs description" (an echo of the two faces of [[modern-vocabulary/dictionaries]]). Along the three axes of materials, method and predicament, at least one point on each should be developed. The citations should contain no serious fault (the Sui “南北声读”, 'pronunciations of north and south'; the trending search, in the draft of 2016, over “说” and “骑”). The conclusion may be left open, but it must give a political-linguistic view of "how the costs and the benefits are shared out" (who is required to change the way they speak).
:::

::: summary
The *Qieyun* was completed in 601, out of one night's discussion of sounds among scholars: 193 rhymes, with *fanqie* for blood and the rhyme headings for bone. The Tang *Tangyun* enlarged it, and the Song *Guangyun* extended the building into a five-volume database of 206 rhymes, 26000 characters and 3890 minor rhymes, *yang* and entering paired in sets, the level tone divided across volumes. Its trilogy of "lost — parasitic — unearthed", and the dispute over its nature, "single or composite", remind us that a rhyme dictionary is neither a recording nor a muddled set of accounts, but **the greatest explicit record of the relations among classes of sound**. The Pingshui 106 are its separate volume for the study of poetry; the rhyme tables are its reincarnation as a matrix — in the next chapter we go and read the matrix that the people of the Song drew.
:::
