---
title: "Old Chinese"
---

Push the coordinates of the *Qieyun* 切韵 back another thousand years, and it is the age of the *Book of Songs*: the rhyme groups will not line up, characters that share a phonetic 'confess' against one another, and tones have not yet been born. Old Chinese is the Everest of Chinese historical phonology — the hardest evidence, the most controversy, and the grandest view.

## Three still older bodies of evidence {#sec-zhengju}

::: definition The layers of material for Old Chinese {#def-cailiao}
① **Rhymes of the *Book of Songs***: the rhyming of the *Book of Songs* and of pre-Qin rhymed texts (*Songs of Chu*; the *tuan* and *yao* statements of the *Zhouyi* 周易, the *Book of Changes*) — a rhyme is a table of shared rhymes which the ancients entered in their own hand. ② **The phonetic-series system**: the network of phonetic components in the phono-semantic compounds (semantic-phonetic compounds) of the *Shuowen* 说文 (*Shuowen jiezi*, *Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*) — the 工 family: 空, 江, 红, 巧, 考; the 各 family: 路, 格, 客, 洛, 搁 — in the age when the characters were created, those that shared a phonetic component had the same initial and final, or ones extremely close. This is a **rhyme dictionary buried in oracle-bone script, in characters a phone cannot even type**. ③ **Phonetic loans (*tongjia*) and sound glosses**: phonetic loans (*jiajie*) in transmitted texts (蚤 = 早, 信 = 伸), together with excavated bamboo and silk manuscripts (at Guodian, and in the Shanghai Museum manuscripts, cases of the sort that write 各 for 洛), are a certification of identical sound on which the ancients cast a vote; supplemented by *duruo* 读若 ('read as') notes, sound glosses (*shengxun*), early transcriptions (Sanskrit Buddhist terms, and Western Regions words of the 葡萄 type) and Sino-Tibetan comparison. Phonetic series look at the **layer of character-creation** (Shang and Zhou); the *Book of Songs* looks at the **layer of recitation** (Western Zhou and the Spring and Autumn period); phonetic loans look at the **layer of writing** (at any time) — only when the three layers corroborate one another can one speak of 'Old' as a single age.
:::

::: history The Qing scholars: three hundred years, and the first division into groups {#sec-qingru}
In the Ming, Chen Di 陈第, in his *Maoshi guyin kao* 毛诗古音考 (*An Examination of the Ancient Pronunciations in the Mao Songs*, 1606), laid down 时有古今，地有南北，字有更革，音有转移 — 'Time has its past and its present, place its south and its north, the character its reforms, and sound its shifts.' (Our translation.) He did away with the abuse of *xieyin* 叶音 ('twisting a sound to force a rhyme') and opened the door to the study of ancient pronunciation. Gu Yanwu's 顾炎武 *Yinxue wushu* 音学五书 (*Five Books on Phonology*) gathered the rhyme characters of the *Book of Songs* into **ten groups** of ancient rhymes, the first time every rhyme word was put in order with a statistical eye. Duan Yucai's 段玉裁 *Liushu yinyun biao* 六书音均表 (*Phonological Tables for the Six Categories*) divided them into **seventeen groups**, and pointed out that “**同谐声者必同部**” — '**characters that share a phonetic must belong to the same group**' (Our translation.) — bringing the phonetic series in as a second chain of evidence for the division into groups. The study of ancient rhymes was thereby 'largely complete' (the words of Wang Li 王力). Jiang Yougao 江有诰 and Wang Niansun 王念孙 each added something further (the separation of 脂 and 祭, and the setting in order of the entering-tone system); Kong Guangsen 孔广森 advocated the theory of **no departing tone in antiquity**; and the scholarship of Dai Zhen 戴震 divided and combined the entering tone in fine detail. The Qing scholars had in their hands no phonetic symbols and no excavated bamboo and silk manuscripts. They worked entirely by setting side by side the three hundred poems of the *Book of Songs* and the fifteen thousand characters of the *Shuowen* — **the first large-scale success of distributional analysis in China, three hundred years ahead of Western structuralism**. What is used today is Wang Li's system of **eleven groups** (the various divisions and mergers among 之, 幽, 宵, 侯, 鱼, 支, 脂微, 职德, 药铎, 屋 and 东), and textbooks for the most part follow it.
:::

::: example The confession of the phonetic series: the families {#ex-ge}
各 is the phonetic component: 格, 阁 and 搁 are read g-, 路, 露 and 洛 are read l-, and 客 and 喀 are read k- (kʰ). If the Old Chinese initial were the same as today's, why does a single 各 (the *jian* initial 见母, k-) call in relatives of the **l-** family?
::: solution
No single k- can teach a pupil who comes out as l-. The only economical explanation is that the Old Chinese pronunciation of 各 carried a **consonant cluster** — the Li Fanggui 李方桂 type, *kl- (a complex initial), and the Baxter–Sagart type, *k.r- (with a syllabic prefix) — and that the later branches diverged: those that dropped the liquid element became 格 and 阁 (k-), those that dropped the stop element became 路 and 洛 (*k.rak > lak), and variant readings and alternative pronunciations still keep traces of both sets. **A phonetic series is a dormitory for consonant clusters**: that one phonetic component should teach several families of disciples with 'different initials' is its written confession that, in its own day, it had more than one initial. Qian Daxin's 钱大昕 'no light-labial initials in antiquity' (cases such as 匍匐 sometimes written 蒲服, and characters of the initials 非敷奉微 sharing phonetic series and loans with characters of the 帮 group) and 'no supradental initials in antiquity' (characters of the initials 知彻澄 sharing phonetic series and readings with the initials 端透定) use precisely the same key: **a difference in the modern sound + agreement in the phonetic series = a merger in Old Chinese**.
:::
:::

::: theorem Before tones: the kingdom of -ʔ, -s and the entering tone {#rule-shengdiao}
The problem of tone in Old Chinese is the most alluring unsolved case in the subject. The chain of evidence: ① the pattern of cross-rhyming by tone category in the *Book of Songs* does not line up with the four tones of Middle Chinese (the rising and departing tones are mixed together, while the entering tone stands austerely alone); ② in the phonetic-series system, **departing-tone characters are often of the same family as entering-tone characters** (the 至 family, the 对 family and the like), and **rising-tone characters often carry kinship and diminutive words such as 止, 母 and 女**; ③ Sino-Tibetan comparison, and what remains of the morphology. On this basis the line of Hirayama Hisao 平山久雄, Zhou Fagao 周法高 and Mei Zulin 梅祖麟 proposed: **the departing tone comes from an *-s suffix** (causative, and nominalisation: 王 wàng, 治 zhì and 食 sì are precisely the fossils; see [[phonology/mc-finals-tones]]), and **the rising tone comes from an *-ʔ coda** (a glottal stop, mutually corroborated by colloquial readings of the 飞 and 歌 type in Min and by the corresponding words in Written Tibetan); the entering tone originally bore a stop coda, and is the most conservative. The 'tone' of Old Chinese was thus not yet a contour of pitch, but **the afterglow of a coda** — the four tones are what stops and fricatives left behind as they decayed. This is the most beautiful chapter in the history of Chinese.
:::

::: intuition Confucius and your language barrier
A famous inference of the school of Li Fanggui: travel back to the Spring and Autumn period, and a conversation with Confucius is basically impossible — not because the vocabulary is hard, but because **the whole syllable structure is different**. In his mouth was a system of consonant clusters and of long vowels in open syllables, of the type *k.rak (各) and *maːʔ (马, illustrating the *-ʔ source of the rising tone). Only after the 'avalanche of simplification' of the pre-Qin period and the two Han dynasties (prefixes dropping away, complex initials reduced to single consonants, codas merging) did this settle into the forty-one categories of the *Qieyun* and the twenty-one initials of the *Xianhan* 现汉 (*Contemporary Chinese Dictionary*). **A reconstruction is not a recording of the ancients; it is a model of the regularities.** Each set of reconstructed forms (Karlgren 高本汉, Li Fanggui, Baxter–Sagart and Zhengzhang Shangfang 郑张尚芳, each school of them) is an explanatory machine: the examination paper is the same, and the answers differ. To take one school's reconstruction as 'the standard answer, to be recited' is the first disease in the study of Old Chinese.
:::

::: history A hundred-year racetrack in the age of reconstruction {#sec-gouji}
Karlgren (the 1910s–1920s), projecting upwards from the framework of the *Qieyun*, was the first to draw a complete map of Old Chinese (complex initials that were typologically bizarre came in for criticism). Wang Li's *Shijing yundu* 诗经韵读 and *Hanyu shi gao* 汉语史稿 (*A Draft History of Chinese*) established eleven groups and a cautious system, 'not certifying oddities of one's own'. In 1971–73 Li Fanggui's *Shuowen jiezi duruo kao* 说文解字读若考 and *Shangguyin yanjiu* 上古音研究 (*Studies in Old Chinese Phonology*) brought the phonetic series and the *duruo* glosses entirely within range, and complex initials of the *kl- type became the international rule. From the 1980s on, excavated bamboo and silk manuscripts (Guodian, the Shanghai Museum, Tsinghua) have supplied in a steady stream the phonetic loans of Warring States copies, and have become new evidence for the 'layer of writing'. Zhengzhang Shangfang, and Bai Yiping 白一平 and Sagart 沙加尔 (Baxter–Sagart, 2014), introduced the model of **prefixes and quasi-disyllables**, and Old Chinese drew closer to the morphological picture of Tibeto-Burman. STEDT, and databases of reconstructions that can be searched, have brought the statistics of rhyme groups and of phonetic series into an age in which the sums can be run again. **A history of Old Chinese phonology = a court in which the evidence is continually upgraded and the models are continually overturned.**
:::

::: warning Four prohibitions in the study of Old Chinese
Do not **memorise a reconstruction as holy writ** (the systems of the several schools are incommensurable; a citation should name its system). Do not **take a likeness of sound for an etymology** (a sound gloss of the 天 = 颠 type is a morphological association, not a law of sound change; abuse it and you are at the gate of amateur science; see [[modern-vocabulary/lexicon-layers]], the entry 'folk etymology'). Do not **borrow evidence across layers** (using the materials of a Middle Chinese rhyme dictionary to prove Old Chinese, or the reverse: each chain of evidence has jurisdiction over its own age). Do not **fall into mysticism** (the various claims that 'Old Chinese = English / Tibetan / the language of Nurhaci': whatever has neither regular correspondences nor falsifiable predictions is out — a Sino-Tibetan common origin is a serious subject, not free love).
:::

::: exercise Practice in assigning the groups {level=1}
In 'Guanju' 关雎, in the Zhounan 周南 section of the *Book of Songs*, the two sets of rhyme words 流 and 求, and 芼 and 思: using the eleven-group system, judge which group each belongs to, and explain why the modern pronunciations have lost the connection.
::: solution
流 and 求 are both in the 幽 group (rhymes of the *-u type; the modern pronunciations liú/qiú still keep a trace of the sameness). 芼 belongs to the 宵 group and 思 to the 之 group — they were not the same group to begin with, and this stanza is a case of crossed rhyme (two rhymes crossing). The lost connection between the modern pronunciations máo/sī is the result of the vowels of the 宵 and 之 groups each evolving on their own. Credit is given if the four items are assigned to the right groups; as to the standard of rhyme groups, one may note the system relied on (Wang Li's eleven groups, or another).
:::

::: exercise The case of the complex initial {level=2}
蒙 takes its sound from 冡, and the 冡 family includes 梦 (m-), 龐 (p-) and 龍 (l-). Propose one consonant-cluster solution that explains these three families, and state a testable prediction of your solution (at least one).
::: solution
An open question. An illustrative line of approach: suppose that 冡 carried a stop crowned by a nasal, or a complex initial of pre-initial plus liquid (of the *m.q- type); its descendants then split — keeping the nasal gives 梦 m-, keeping the liquid gives 龍 l- (*r->l-), and losing the pre-initial and then voicing gives 龐 p-. What the marking requires: give any solution of the *C1C2- type, plus at least one testable prediction (for example: alternative readings or phonetic loans in this family should show an m- ↔ l- alternation; the initials of characters in the same family, such as 朦 and 籠, should each split off in a way that fits the rules). A solution that is consistent in itself, with a prediction that can be falsified, receives full marks; one who reports only a reconstructed form and gives no test receives half marks.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: a time-traveller's hearing {level=3}
Suppose you really did take a mobile phone and travel back to the country around Luoyang in 500 BC, and that you wrote, in eight hundred to twelve hundred characters, your 'hearing report': which sounds you cannot tell apart at all (mergers); which sounds strike you as superfluous (oppositions that have now disappeared: consonant clusters, -m/-p codas, -ʔ/-s codas); and how long it would be before the locals could understand you (an imagining of the rate at which syllable structure simplified). Every claim must be anchored to one of the three layers of evidence in this lesson. Lamenting, in the manner of 'it is like a foreign language', where there is no regular correspondence, is forbidden.
::: solution
Marking points: 'cannot tell them apart' must land on a real merger (for example, hearing a consonant cluster as a single consonant, or having no way to distinguish the fine differences among 之, 脂 and 支); the 'sense of the superfluous' must be anchored to one example each of -p/-t/-k, of -m/-n/-ŋ, of -ʔ/-s, and of phonetic series and phonetic loans; the argument about the rate must cite an example of compression along the path from phonetic series to the *Qieyun* to Putonghua (standard Mandarin) — the 各 family is a ready-made case. Label every assertion '据' or '测' ('on evidence' or 'conjectured'). Laments of the 'like a foreign language' kind, with no regular correspondence, lose marks as appropriate.
:::

::: summary
Old Chinese rests on three bodies of evidence: the rhymes of the *Book of Songs* (the layer of recitation), phonetic series (the layer of character-creation), and phonetic loans and sound glosses (the layer of writing); excavated bamboo and silk manuscripts are still adding to the weight of it. Three hundred years of division into groups by the Qing scholars (Chen Di's breaking of *xieyin* — Gu Yanwu's ten groups — Duan Yucai's seventeen groups, and 'a shared phonetic must mean a shared group') was the Chinese premiere of distributional analysis. Modern reconstruction pushes initials towards consonant clusters (*kl-/*k.r-) and pushes tones towards codas (*-s for the departing tone, *-ʔ for the rising tone, and a stop coda for the entering tone), and Confucius can no longer speak the language of today. A reconstructed pronunciation is a model, not a recording. Borrowing evidence across layers, and mysticism, are two great minefields — read any one school with the demand that it be falsifiable, and you have found your way to the gate of this Everest of a subject.
:::
