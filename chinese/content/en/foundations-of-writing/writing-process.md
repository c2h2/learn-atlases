title: "The writing process"
---

The first tool of a writing course is not fine phrases, but the **operations**: the stretch of road from wanting to write to handing the piece in, which most people skip or walk at random. This course runs for nine weeks, and it first puts the production line up — every chapter after this is one station on that line.

## The five stations of the process {#sec-liucheng}

::: definition A model of the writing process {#def-gongwei}
**Accumulate — conceive — draft — revise — proofread** (the station chart of process writing; the names used in teaching differ slightly, the operations are the same): ① **Accumulating** (the materials store: observation notes, extracts, oral accounts — **if you cannot write, it is first because there is no stock**; see [[foundations-of-writing/observation-material]]); ② **Conceiving** (settle the subject, the reader and the use — the "three-question sheet" — then make an outline. Ye Shengtao 叶圣陶's analogy: the source of writing is life; "**这源头要很盈很清，流出来的水才得着不竭与清**" ("This source must be very full and very clear; only then will the water that flows out be unfailing and clear." (Our translation.)) (the gist of *Quan yu xi* 《泉与溪》 ('The Spring and the Stream'), 1934)); ③ **Drafting** (a rotten draft is allowed: **"the right to write a bad first draft"** — the core contract of Peter Elbow's *Writing without Teachers* (1973): **the first draft's task is to exist, not to be excellent**); ④ **Revising** (what you change is not miswritten characters, but **order, fullness and brevity, and stance** — see [[foundations-of-writing/revision]]); ⑤ **Proofreading** (wrong characters, missing characters, punctuation — in the order of operations it must come **last**; to proofread before revising is to mop the floor while you wait for the renovation). The five stations are **a learning scaffold, not a railway track**: a mature writer will skip a station and run back — **but the station "never write a draft, and hand straight in a heaven-sent stroke" is one nobody can jump**.
:::

::: definition A linear chart, a cyclic factory {#def-xunhuan}
What the classroom teaches, "scrutinise the topic → settle the idea → compose the text → revise", is a straight line (because the examination hall delivers in a straight line); the psychological model (Flower and Hayes, 1981) says that real writing is **cyclic**: the three operations of planning, translating and reviewing **nest and go back and forth** — writing as far as the third paragraph, finding that the idea you settled is wrong, and going back to think again, is **normal working time**, not failure. What arises in the crack between the two is **"a misalignment of teaching"**: students treat drafting as the manufacture of a finished piece (every sentence written as the final draft), so they grind at one sentence for ten minutes, have no time afterwards, and revising is entirely absent — **taking proofreading for revising, revising for rewriting, and rewriting for knocking it all down and starting again**, and the whole line jams. The remedy: **timed stages** (the 45-minute examination version: 5 for scrutinising + 8 for the outline + 27 for writing + 5 for reading), and **no eraser during drafting** (Elbow: while you are drafting, ask the doubting mind to leave the room — **the internal editor is an unlawful intruder during the drafting stage**).
:::

::: example A 45-minute race to the limit {#ex-45}
The version compressed for the examination hall: the topic is random (for example 《排队》 ('Queuing')), and 700 characters are to be finished in 45 minutes. Please provide an **operations timing card**, and state the fallback for each station when the work "cannot be finished".
::: solution
Timing card: **scrutinising the topic, 5′** (circle the subject-words and the restricting words, and on scrap paper write the one sentence "Whom am I going to tell, and what am I going to tell them?") → **conceiving, 8′** (1 central sentence + 3 pieces of material + 2 schemes of order; choose 1 and cross out 1; **list a 3-paragraph outline**: opening / middle / close, one line each) → **drafting, 27′** (lay the piece out along the outline; if a piece of material jams, skip the paragraph — **skipping a paragraph is not a breach**; with 3′ left, write the ending, and write an "action", not a "resolution") → **reading and revising, 5′** (read the last paragraph and the first under your breath; repair faulty sentences and put in the punctuation; **only now are you allowed to act as proofreader**). Fallbacks: if scrutinising the topic overruns, force an opening by "making a sentence out of the subject-word"; if conceiving overruns, cut down to "one event, one feeling"; if the draft cannot be finished, **save the ending** (in a lost endgame, one complete close is worth 5 marks — the markers look at the opening and the close); the plan for a total collapse: in the last 8′, expand the central sentence into a short piece in three parts — **a rotten structure is better than a rotten blank**. The true face of this exercise: take "writing ability" apart into **time management + operational discipline**.
:::
:::

::: intuition Revision is not a patch; it is a second incarnation {#int-xiugai}
Beginners think revising = correcting miswritten characters; old hands know revising = **thinking it through again**. Give the classroom a safe version of the golden sentence: "**The point of a first draft is only to let you know what you yourself are thinking**" — writing is the developing fluid of thought. This does not contradict the 意在笔先 ('the intent is there before the brush') of [[foundations-of-writing/theme-structure]]: before the pen moves there is one whole block; in the drafting it will break apart and be put together again — only after it has been developed do you see clearly what that block actually is. Here the Chinese and the foreign meet: Lu Yanrang 卢延让's "吟安一个字，捻断数茎须" ("I chant one character into place, and twist off several stalks of my beard." (Our translation.)) is the romanticisation of revision; Cao Xueqin 曹雪芹's "披阅十载，增删五次" ("Read through for ten years; added and cut five times." (Our translation.)) is the turning of revision into capital — **revision is an operation a fine work must pass through: of those whose process of revision has come down to us, every one became a fine work**.
:::

::: history Two hundred years of one composition lesson {#sec-kecheng}
**1904**, the Renyin–Guimao school system (壬寅—癸卯学制): classroom writing comes into being as arranged coursework (monthly exercises, seasonal examinations); **1920s**: the vernacular (*baihua*) enters the classroom, and "我手写我口" ("My hand writes my mouth." (Our translation.)) becomes a legitimate option; **1924–1930s**: the line of Xia Mianzun 夏丏尊 and Ye Shengtao compiles textbooks of Chinese (*guowen*) and *Wenxin* 《文心》 ('The Heart of Writing', 1934), and **the teaching method of "reading and writing combined" takes shape** — intensive reading is the store of models for writing; **1950–1960s**: teaching is brought under the syllabus, and the three-part system of "narrative composition / expository composition / argumentative composition" is established (**the three-way division of forms of writing is an invention of pedagogy, not a classification of literature** — this lesson stresses the point repeatedly); **1963**: the first national syllabus establishes the "two basics" (*shuangji* 双基, basic knowledge and basic skills); **1978**: the college entrance examination (*gaokao*) is restored, and composition is once again scored; **1980s–1990s**: the teaching experiment "bold compositions — compositions that come to hand" (*fangdan wen* 放胆文 — *deshou wen* 得手文) goes forward together with the training line "observation — thinking — expression"; **1999–2001**: the new curriculum standards write "express with originality" and "reduce the restrictions on the topic" into their articles; **from 2016**: the state-compiled textbooks place writing in the sequence "writing from life"; **outside the examination hall**: process writing enters the literature of research on teaching by way of translation, and **the writing workshop is revived in non-governmental writing education and in university writing centres** — the two tracks, the classroom and the workshop, are still being joined.
:::

::: warning Four breaches of the operations
First, **revising while you write** (keeping an editor during the drafting stage = a slow suicide at 200 characters an hour — the stations of drafting and reviewing must be **kept to physically separate times**); second, **changing only words and sentences, and not the structure** (the difference between the final draft and the first draft lies not in the adjectives but in the **skeleton** — to change the punctuation and not the order is a renovation that has not changed the floor plan); third, **handing the revising over to someone else** ("Teacher, have a look and tell me where it is wrong" — the owner of the revising is the author; the teacher's red pen is a medical report, not a meal eaten in your place — **put the knife to your own draft yourself, first**); fourth, **a superstitious refusal to start without inspiration** ("wait until I feel something, then write" = handing the permit to start work over to the weather — the professional writer's countermeasure is **operations in place of mood**: sit down, set a time, set the pen moving; feeling is a by-product of the process, not the ticket of admission).
:::

::: exercise The three-question sheet {level=1}
Before writing a short piece of 500 characters (you choose the title), first hand in a "three-question sheet": one sentence each for the subject, the reader and the use, plus one central sentence, plus a 3-paragraph outline. Then build to the sheet, and attach at the end an "off-course report" of 50 characters.
::: solution
Marking: the three-question sheet is complete, and the parts **constrain one another** (the use can test whether the central sentence is idling); the off-course report honestly records "which paragraph was written off the topic, and why it ran off" — **the material at the point of running off is often the true interest**, and whoever makes use of the deviation gets an A. Towards a model: subject "the morning market", reader "a secondary-school student who has never wandered a morning market", use "make them want to go once tomorrow" — the central sentence must therefore contain **one action that can be reproduced**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Taking the timing apart {level=2}
Write down the true time-account of the last time you wrote 800 characters (estimate to the nearest 5 minutes): how many minutes did scrutinising the topic, conceiving, drafting and revising each take? Set it against the 45′ proportions, and issue a **personal breach-ticket** (the one most serious error of operation + an action to put it right).
::: solution
A typical diagnosis: conceiving ≈ 0′ (a direct breach, "building with no drawing"); revising ≈ 0′ ("hand it in the moment it is written"); drafting takes eight tenths of the time, and overruns (revising while you write). The action to put it right must be **one that can be carried out** ("Next time, write the central sentence on scrap paper before you set pen to paper" can be carried out a hundred times more readily than "I am going to take it seriously"). Marking points: there is an account, there is a ticket, there is an action; no confessional literature is required.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short paper: process and inspiration {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters: for the "inspiration theory", write an **autopsy report** (how it explains genius, how it frightens beginners off, how it testifies for procrastination), and then make out a prescription from the "operations theory" (including one counter-example of your own — an occasion when "inspiration suddenly came", and after which operation it in fact came).
::: solution
Marking points: the autopsy's three cuts land (the mystification in the narrative of genius; the threshold effect by which beginners are frightened off; "waiting for inspiration" = not entering); the prescription acknowledges inspiration's standing in the study of operations (**Archimedes' bathtub comes after the bath** — a sudden insight is the reward after long accumulation plus concentration; the "sudden insight on the carriage", which the mathematician Poincaré recounted of himself, was preceded by years of hard thinking); the counter-example is real and specific.
:::

::: summary
Goods from this chapter: the **five stations** (accumulate — conceive — draft — revise — proofread) and two licences — the **rotten-draft licence** (during drafting the editor stays away) and the **licence to run back** (the cycle is the normal state); one iron rule: **proofreading must come last**; one truth: once writing is taken apart into operations, "I can't write" shrinks to "I was absent from one station" — **if it can be diagnosed, it can be trained**. The next chapter enters the first station: observation and accumulation — strike the three characters "没得写" ('nothing to write') from the dictionary.
:::
