---
title: "Reading whole texts"
---

The tools of all nine lessons are on the table. This lesson is the full rehearsal: five passages of canonical text, taken once through the six procedures **correct pronunciation (正音) — phonetic loan (*tongjia* 通假) — *judou* 句读 (marking the pauses) — syntax — modern Chinese translation — appreciation**. The end of reading Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) is not "having understood one word"; it is "a piece is alive".

## Case 1: A battlefield sketch from the *Zuo Tradition* (*Zuozhuan*) {#sec-zuozhuan}

::: example Cao Gui discusses the battle (excerpt) {#ex-caogui}
“十年春，齐师伐我。公将战。曹刿请见。其乡人曰：‘肉食者谋之，又何间焉？’刿曰：‘肉食者鄙，未能远谋。’……公与之乘。战于长勺。公将鼓之。刿曰：‘未可。’齐人三鼓。刿曰：‘可矣。’齐师败绩。”——The bare text can be used for a self-test; every tool is brought on.

In the spring of the tenth year, the army of Qi attacked us. The duke was about to fight. Cao Gui 曹刿 asked to be received. A man of his village said: "The meat-eaters are planning it; what need is there for you to join in?" Gui said: "The meat-eaters are crude; they cannot plan far." … The duke had him ride with him. They fought at Changshao. The duke was about to drum the advance. Gui said: "Not yet." The men of Qi drummed three times. Gui said: "It may be done now." The army of Qi was routed. (Our translation.)
::: solution
A checklist of the procedures. **Correct pronunciation:** 间 *jiàn* (to take part), 鄙 *bǐ* (crude and narrow). **Phonetic loan:** no firm case (鄙 is an extended sense of the character itself). ***Judou*:** “肉食者谋之，又何间焉？” is a rhetorical-question frame (焉 = sentence-final mood + question; the modern rendering “又何必参与呢”, "and what need is there to take part?"). **Sentence pattern:** in “战于长勺” the preposition and its object sit at the back (reorder → “在长勺作战”). **Culture:** the 我 of “齐师伐我” = Lu's name for itself — the manner of the *Spring and Autumn Annals* (*Chunqiu*), one's own historical material in one's own voice (ritual institutions in the *Zuo Tradition*: “钟鼓鸣之曰伐，潜师曰侵” — "When bells and drums are sounded it is called *fa*; a concealed army is called *qin*." (Our translation.) — *fa* carries of itself the display of a declaration of war). **The knife-edge of appreciation:** “公将鼓之/未可/齐人三鼓/可矣” — a battlefield telegram cut down to the limit of omission (“鼓之” = beating the drum to advance; 之 fills out the syllable); one “未可” and one “可矣”, six characters, and the battle of Changshao is struck to its end — **the narrative economy of the *Zuo Tradition* = syntactic omission + the blank left by war**.
:::

## Case 2: Fifty characters from the *Analects* {#sec-lunyu}

::: example Three chapters of the "Xue'er" book read in a row {#ex-xueer}
“子曰：‘学而时习之，不亦说乎？有朋自远方来，不亦乐乎？人不知而不愠，不亦君子乎？’”——A little over thirty characters, and from them one reads out the whole stock of sentence patterns in the *Analects*.

The Master said: "To learn and to practise it at the due times — is that not indeed a pleasure? To have friends come from far away — is that not indeed a joy? That others do not recognise one, and yet one is not resentful — is that not indeed to be a gentleman?" (Our translation.)
::: solution
**说 = 悦** (phonetic loan + a changed reading, *pòdú* 破读, *yuè*); **时** = at the due times (one view says "often" — the influence of Zhu Xi's 朱熹 commentary; an honest presentation in which the two views stand side by side is precisely the practical work of the lesson on the layer of commentaries and subcommentaries); **不亦…乎** = a rhetorical-question frame (rendered “不也…吗”, "is it not also …?"; the ledger of tone: rhetorical question = affirmation firm enough to need no dispute); **知** = to be understood (**unmarked passive** — “人不知” = others do not understand me; the plain-clothes passive appearing in court for the Nth time); **愠** *yùn* (anger kept hidden within); three chapters and three parallelisms, each chapter closing on “不亦…乎” — **the musicality of the aphoristic form**: the frame recurs + a step onward (one's own joy → the joy of friends → the joy of character; the figure of climax); **the layer of the theme**: the three sentences, from learning to association to cultivation, are the syllabus of "the study of learning" that opens the *Analects* — commentators call it "the first meaning at the gate of entering upon learning". One chapter drills five lessons; that is the reason for choosing it to stand at the opening.
:::
:::

## Case 3: The boxing art of argument in *Mencius* {#sec-mengzi}

::: example Fish is what I desire (excerpt) {#ex-yuwo}
“鱼，我所欲也；熊掌，亦我所欲也。二者不可得兼，舍鱼而取熊掌者也。生，亦我所欲也；义，亦我所欲也。二者不可得兼，舍生而取义者也。”

Fish is what I desire; bear's paw is also what I desire. The two cannot be had at once; this is to give up the fish and take the bear's paw. Life is also what I desire; rightness is also what I desire. The two cannot be had at once; this is to give up life and take rightness. (Our translation.)
::: solution
**Syntax:** two sets of nominal sentences in “……者也” close the passages, and the matched wording is completely symmetrical — **first set up the grammatical template, then pour the thought into it** (once the template is stood up, the second set automatically completes a psychological coercion of the argument: if you will not accept “舍生取义”, "giving up life and taking rightness", you must first dismantle the “舍鱼取熊掌”, "giving up the fish and taking the bear's paw", which you yourself nodded through); **rhetoric gathered back in:** argument by analogy + climax (desire → having both → taking) + antithesis; the three stock axes of an argumentative essay in Classical Chinese were already a set 2,300 years ago; **traps in a hard translation:** “兼” = to possess at the same time, not “兼职” ("to hold a second post"); do not dramatise “取义” into “争取正义” ("to strive to win justice") (取 = to choose and fix upon; a cold word is not allowed hot blood); **one critical question:** analogy is rhetoric, not logic — the figure of the bear's paw smuggles in the **order of value** "rightness stands above life as bear's paw stands above fish", and this order is precisely what the argument has to prove (a suspicion of circularity — Mencius does not answer, and the lesson does not conceal it) — the advanced move of close reading: to see the tools and the holes together.
:::

## Case 4: The abyss of the *Zhuangzi* {#sec-zhuangzi}

::: example Autumn floods (excerpt) {#ex-qiushui}
“井蛙不可以（之）语于海者，拘于虚也；夏虫不可以（之）语于冰者，笃于时也；曲士不可以（之）语于道者，束于教也。”（The wording of the received text differs slightly; the excerpt takes “井蛙…拘于虚也” as its backbone.）

That one cannot talk with a frog in a well about the sea is because it is confined by its place; that one cannot talk with a summer insect about ice is because it is fixed to its season; that one cannot talk with a rustic scholar about the Way is because he is bound by his teaching. (Our translation.)
::: solution
***Judou*:** three “者” raise a pause and three “也” close it = a template of parallel sentences; **syntax:** a double passive in joint flight — “拘于虚、笃于时、束于教”, three passives in 于 (于 introduces the agent = bound by space, by a limit of time, by teaching — the passive office of 于 held for three terms running); the demonstrative object of “语之” is omitted (supplied: 之 = the well-frog and its kind); **word meaning:** “虚” is a phonetic loan for “墟” (the place where one dwells — another station for a borrowed character); “曲士” = a scholar of the country bends (a scholar of a remote region — to explain it as “一曲之见”, "a view from a single bend", is people of today pushing the boat with the current; the commentators did not necessarily mean that; it may be noted, it may not be settled); **the cost list of a modern Chinese translation:** the geographical depth of the triple run (sea / ice / the Way), put into the vernacular (*baihua*), goes flat — keep the sense and drop the cadence, carried out for the Nth time in this lesson; **a philosophical reading:** the three sections are the threefold cage of knowing (space, time, transformative teaching); Zhuangzi uses the neatness of the sentence pattern to perform "the neatness of limitation" — a Classical Chinese scene of **form as the claim** (corroborating, each by the other, the rhetoric lesson's “整句的秩序预付”, "the order of the parallel sentence is paid in advance").
:::

## Case 5: The eye of Song prose {#sec-songwen}

::: example On loving the lotus {#ex-ailian}
“水陆草木之花，可爱者甚蕃。晋陶渊明独爱菊；自李唐来，世人甚爱牡丹；予独爱莲之出淤泥而不染，濯清涟而不妖，中通外直，不蔓不枝，香远益清，亭亭净植，可远观而不可亵玩焉。”

Of the flowers of plants and trees on water and on land, those that can be loved are very many. Tao Yuanming 陶渊明 of the Jin alone loved the chrysanthemum. From the Tang of the house of Li on, the world has greatly loved the peony. I alone love the lotus's emerging from the silt and not being stained, being washed in clear ripples and not being seductive, open in the centre and straight on the outside, neither putting out creepers nor putting out branches, fragrant and far, clear and clearer still, standing clean and upright, to be viewed from afar but not to be handled with disrespect. (Our translation.)
::: solution
**Genre:** the *shuo* 说 = a short argumentative rhapsody (the *shuo* on loving the lotus; title and cut are one — "On Horses" 马说 and "On Teachers" 师说 are of the same family); **syntax:** “予独爱莲之出淤泥而不染” — **the character 之 between subject and predicate cancels independence** (“莲出淤泥而不染” is lowered in rank to become the object of “爱”; the chief examination point of lesson 4 takes up its post in a famous piece); “可远观而不可亵玩焉” = an adversative turn + sentence-final 焉; **three word-class shifts in a row:** “不蔓不枝” — 蔓 and 枝, nouns used as verbs (to put out creepers, to grow branches; posts on the roster of lesson 5); “香远益清” — fragrant and far, clear and increasingly clear; qualities used as narration, a poeticised syntax, not to be forced apart; **the full ledger of rhetoric:** chrysanthemum = reclusion (the Tao Qian 陶潜 card), peony = wealth and rank (the card of the people of the world), lotus = the gentleman (the self-portrait card) — **contrast + setting-off + symbol**, three seats, and one piece of 119 characters carries a whole spectrum of character; **the characters used:** “蕃” = many (*fán*), “亵” *xiè* (to treat lightly), “植” = to stand (净植 = standing there clean; 植 in its own character, no need to force a phonetic loan). The finale of close reading: from the telegram of the *Zuo Tradition* to the perfume of "On Loving the Lotus" — the tools have not changed; the forms have. **Classical Chinese is not one form; it is a federation of a thousand genres.**
:::

::: intuition The four breaths of close reading {#int-huxi}
The same stretch of writing can be switched among four ways of reading. **Microscope reading** (evidential scholarship character by character; one piece takes a week — the daily lesson of Qing scholars). **Logical reading** (seize only the skeleton of the argument, and draw a diagram of it — the query about circularity in the *Mencius* piece is a product of this reading). **Aesthetic reading** (“以语言得作者的神气” — "by way of the language, catch the author's spirit and bearing" (Our translation.) — Ye Shengtao 叶圣陶 advocated the theory of *měidú* 美读, "aesthetic reading", and one reads out Cao Gui's calm and Zhuangzi's vast sigh). **Intertextual reading** (place "On Loving the Lotus" in the lineage of *tuōwù yánzhì* 托物言志, "expressing intent by way of a thing", reaching up to the discourse on the virtue of jade in the "Faxing" 法行 chapter of the *Xunzi*, and opening downward onto Zhou Dunyi's 周敦颐 character in the Learning of Principle (*lǐxué* 理学) — a single piece read into a group portrait). Secondary-school teachers often complain that “文言教成解剖课” — "Classical Chinese is taught until it becomes a dissection class" (Our translation.) — the root of the illness is that of the four breaths only one is taken. The five cases of this lesson deliberately each lead with one: case 1 the grammar of narrative, case 2 the stock of sentence patterns, case 3 the critique of argument, case 4 form as the claim, case 5 the lineage of genres. **Close reading is not reading fine; it is being fine by choice.**
:::

::: history A thousand years of change in the texts for close reading {#sec-keben}
"Which Classical Chinese one reads" is itself a history of education. The Han prized the classics (an Erudite was established for the *Songs*; the proverb of Zou and Lu, “遗子黄金满籯，不如一经” — "To leave one's son a basket full of yellow gold is not as good as a single classic." (Our translation.) — the account is kept in the "Biography of Wei Xian" in the *Book of Han*); the Tang and Song recruited men by poetry, rhapsody and ancient-style prose (*guwen*), and people of the Song had a saying, “《文选》烂，秀才半” — "When the *Wenxuan* 文选 ("Selections of Refined Literature") is worn to tatters, one is half a *xiucai* 秀才." (Our translation.) — a comprehensive anthology was the textbook; the Yuan and Ming fell back to the chapter-and-sentence study of the Four Books, and elementary schooling read the *Qianjia shi* 千家诗 ("Poems of a Thousand Masters"); **the *Zouding xuetang zhangcheng* 奏定学堂章程 of 1904** set the subject "reading the classics and expounding the classics" side by side with the subject "Chinese writing", and the classics gave up their place to school subjects; **after 1920** lesson texts were put into the vernacular, and close reading of Classical Chinese was cut down into the "Classical Chinese" unit; **in the 1980s the People's Education Press** had Classical Chinese lesson texts at about four tenths, in a tug of war with the radical proposal to "cast off Classical Chinese completely"; **the unified textbooks of 2017** contain over a hundred pieces of ancient poetry and prose, nearly half of all the lesson texts, together with the unified policy of "read much and recite much; do not seek an exhaustive understanding" — **from "a single classic" to "one hundred and twenty pieces", the change of generations in the set texts for close reading is the change of generations in the answer to "what kind of person to form".**
:::

::: warning Four stops in close reading
First, **stopping at the translation**: a full parallel translation is announced as having learned the piece — the begging of the question in "Fish is what I desire", and the art of the blank in Cao Gui, all lie outside the translation. Second, **dying of fragments**: one character lectured for forty minutes, one piece taking three class hours — fragmentation moves no one, and it does not floor anyone in an examination either (the sense of breath in a whole piece has to be read as a whole piece; ten minutes of reading the whole at morning reading > forty minutes of inventorying the parts at evening study). Third, **blind faith in one house of commentary**: only when Zhu Xi's "时 = often" is set out together with the Han commentary's "at the due times" does one see the ecology of commentaries and subcommentaries (the common fault of buying only one reference book). Fourth, **judging the past by the present**: using the values of people today to decide the ancients outright ("Cao Gui's fellow villagers were truly cold"; "Mencius was forcing his argument") — historical context is the air of close reading; leave it and one suffocates.
:::

::: solution
Marking points: the six columns all present, and carried out in order; correct pronunciation and phonetic loans, each at no fewer than two places and with evidence (an annotated edition, or a dictionary page); the syntactic diagnosis names the type of sentence pattern; the scoring points of the modern Chinese translation are labelled by oneself; the appreciation does not retell the translation, and must contain one observation that "form serves the claim" — when all six columns are full, the operation that completes the course is a pass.

::: exercise Comparative reading on the same theme {level=2}
A comparative reading on the theme “勇”: "Cao Gui Discusses the Battle" (“夫战，勇气也” — "As for battle, it is a matter of courage." (Our translation.)), the *Analects* (“勇者不惧” — "The courageous do not fear." (Our translation.)), (the chapter of *Mencius*, “自反而缩，虽千万人吾往矣” — "If on examining myself I find that I am straight, then though they be a thousand or ten thousand men, I will go." (Our translation.)) — draw up a table comparing the three schools' definitions of “勇” and their strategies of argument.
::: solution
Reference table: Cao Gui = courage lies in morale (a variable of the physical situation and of timing; the narrative gives the example); the *Analects* = courage lies in character (a moral constant; defined by a maxim); *Mencius* = courage lies in examining oneself (“自反而缩，虽千万人吾往矣” — rightness sought within; inference + quotation) — the three definitions differ, and the ways of proving differ (example / definition / introspective inference). Marking: definitions accurate + each strategy of argument named + one sentence of conclusion outside the table (“勇” is a difference of co-ordinates, not a dispute of right and wrong).
:::

::: solution
Marking points: the paths of extension must really be walkable (case 3 → the whole of "Gaozi, Upper" 告子上 → a Chinese–Western paired argument on giving up life and taking rightness; case 5 → the lineage of *tuowu yanzhi* + the background in the Learning of Principle); the mechanism of “举一反三” must be stated clearly (to return the three, one must first recognise the type-features of the "one" — that is, the sentence patterns and the genre templates from inside the course); the course list contains the pieces, the order and a way of checking — anyone who talks emptily of "interest" and gives no structure is sent back.

::: summary
Five cases, five breaths: Cao Gui's telegram (omission is the rhythm), the template of the *Analects* (recurrence of the frame is the music), Mencius's coercion (symmetry is the argument), Zhuangzi's cages (neatness is the claim), the spectrum of character in "On Loving the Lotus" (the symbol is the person). The six procedures on the assembly bench — sound, character, reading, method, translation, appreciation — are the whole apparatus of this course: the craft one can carry away, and the texts one cannot carry away, have all passed through the fire on this bench. The ten lessons of *Classical Chinese I* close here: you already have a complete decoding system and five surgical specimens. The next stop is *Classical Chinese II* — the deep mining country of historical biography, the Masters, and Tang–Song prose, meeting the literary scene of the course on pre-Qin literature.
