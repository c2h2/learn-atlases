title: "Punctuating classical texts"

Old books were originally neither divided into paragraphs nor punctuated: a child first reads “大学之道在明明德”, and only then does the teacher allow him to read “大学之道，在明明德” — 'The Way of great learning lies in making bright virtue shine.' (Our translation.) **Where the sentence is divided is where understanding has arrived.** *Jùdòu* 句读 (the second character read dòu; traditional pause marks) is the practical question on the final paper of a Classical Chinese (*wenyan*) course, and the first craft of traditional scholarship.

## The ledger and the history of *judou* {#sec-zhang}

::: definition What *judou* is, and why it is valuable {#def-judou}
**句** = where the sense is complete (a circle / a full stop); **读** = where the sense has not yet arrived and the breath must not be broken (a dot / a pause mark) — “彼童子之师，授之书而习其句读者，非吾所谓传其道解其惑者也” — 'Those teachers of children, who give them books and drill them in their pauses and stops, are not what I call men who transmit the Way and resolve perplexities.' (Our translation.) Han Yu 韩愈 classed 'teaching sentence division' as the lesser learning (小学), and took what he himself would do to be the greater learning (大学). This **system of marks is the reader's homework**: the plain text of a block-printed edition, side-dots on a manuscript copy; to mark a book is to solve the problem (in the old school halls, 'marking the book' meant that the teacher used circles and dots to record a beginner's progress — the circles and dots are themselves the traces of marking). Why *judou* is valuable: **commentarial editions of antiquity very rarely preserve a divergent reading; divide wrongly, and the whole sentence is overturned** — the classic case of a divergent reading is the *Analects* line “民可使由之不可使知之” — 'The people may be made to follow it; they may not be made to know it.' (Our translation.) The difference of a single comma, and Confucius changes his trade. Lü Shuxiang 吕叔湘, *Gushu judou shili* 古书句读释例 ('Illustrative cases of sentence division in old books', 1934), gathers a volume full of two thousand years of sentence-division crashes; it is this profession's accident-investigation report.
:::

::: history From 'separating the classics and discerning aims' to circle-and-dot editions {#sec-lijing}
*Judou* was a regular course with standing in the civil examinations: the *Record of Rites* (*Liji*), in the 'Xueji', has “**一年，视离经辨志**” — '**In the first year, examine the separating of the classics and the discerning of aims.**' (Our translation.) In the first year after admission one was examined on 'analysing the classics and dividing sentences, and discerning aims and intentions'; it was the *judou* class on the official curriculum. In the Han, copies for lecturing a text through (都讲本) already had a learning of 'chapter and sentence' (*zhāngjù* 章句) — the 'Treatise on Literature' (*Yiwenzhi*) in the *Book of Han* had already warned against the bloated fashion of *zhangju*: “说五字之文，至于二三万言”, 'To explain a text of five characters, one runs to twenty or thirty thousand words.' (Our translation.); dividing until the text falls to pieces is also a disease. In the Song, printing houses gradually took up **circles and dots**; the three systems of chapter-and-sentence, critical marking (*pingdian* 评点) and *judou* ran side by side down to the end of the Qing ('On Teachers' (师说), “句读之不知，惑之不解”, 'Not knowing the pauses, and not resolving the perplexities' (Our translation.), is mid-Tang testimony to what was then the ordinary state). **In 1920** the Ministry of Education of the Republic issued a general order that general education use new-style punctuation (in 1920 the Ministry of Education of the Beiyang government ordered the promulgation of the 'Proposal to Adopt New-Style Punctuation Marks', 《采用新式标点符号案》); circles and dots stepped down, and the comma entered the palace — ***judou* turned from 'a skill the reader proves for himself' into 'the author parking the car for the customer', and the responsibility for sentence division was from then on handed to the typesetter**. The craft of this lesson faces both ways: one must be able to divide a plain text, and able to doubt the punctuation.
:::

## Six knives for dividing sentences {#sec-liudao}

::: definition A method for *judou* {#def-liu}
① **Function-word signposts**: sentence-final words (也, 矣, 耳, 焉, 乎, 哉, 欤, 与) = traffic lights; sentence-initial words (夫, 盖, 惟, 然, 是故, 于是) = starting blocks — stop at the final character, set off at the word that leads; the knife with the highest hit rate. ② **Structural matching phrases** (*duìwén* 对文): the templates of parallel pairing, parallelism and repetition draw the lines themselves (a parallel couplet of the type “忠而被谤，故放逐之；信而见疑，故怨生焉”, 'Loyal, and yet slandered, therefore he was banished; trustworthy, and yet doubted, therefore resentment arose.' (Our translation.): divide one place and you know both). ③ **Chain-repetition joins** (*dǐngzhēn* 顶针): the tail of the upper sentence is the head of the lower (“军书十二卷，卷卷有爷名”, 'The army book runs to twelve scrolls; scroll after scroll has Father's name.' (Our translation.)); where the words repeat, there must be a seam. ④ **Heading formulae and forms of address**: in “学而时习之” ('to learn and in due time to practise it'), “子曰”, “诗云” and “曾子曰” = boards that title a section; after every 曰/云/言 a quotation must follow, and the start and end of the quotation are fixed by pairing with 曰. ⑤ **Syntax returned to its place**: a three-corner check of subject (who), predicate (the action) and object (what) — where two verbs compete for one subject, or where the pronoun “之” has nothing to refer to, that is where one should divide. ⑥ **Outside help from editions and from commentaries and subcommentaries**: three commentarial editions read the same sentence differently; there is side evidence quoted in manuscript copies or in encyclopaedias (*leishu* 类书) (a new reading of “民可使由之” may be clever, but the stock of pre-Qin sentence patterns does not support the pause format of “可，使由之” — **sentence division must pass the gate of the grammar of the same period**; it is not that one may alter the classic as soon as it 'reads smoothly').
:::

::: example A practical test on plain text {#ex-baiwen}
Divide the plain text into sentences and state the knife technique: “夫学须静也才须学也非学无以广才非志无以成学” (Zhuge Liang 诸葛亮, 'Letter Admonishing My Son', 诫子书).
::: solution
Divided: “夫学须静也，才须学也。非学无以广才，非志无以成学。” Inventory of the knives: ① “夫” leads off the start, and the two characters “也” are the stops (signposts); ② “非学无以…非志无以…” a matching pair (structure); ③ “学须静/才须学”, a chain-repetition style of matched joint; fourth, “才/学”, each seen twice, form a ring (an A→B→A chain). Check against a translation: 学习须静，才靠学得；不学不能扩才，无志不能成学 — 'Study requires quiet; talent depends on learning. Without study one cannot broaden talent; without resolve one cannot complete study.' (Our translation.) A closed loop of meaning is a closed loop of sentence division. Naming a full three kinds of knife technique counts as a pass.
:::
:::

::: intuition *Judou* is a stance {#int-lichang}
Sentence division = interpretation, and the case files are the proof: besides the thousand-year case of a revised verdict on “民可使由之”, in 1973 the Mawangdui 马王堆 silk manuscripts were unearthed, and the opening of the *Laozi* is written “道可道也，非恒道也” — 'The Way that can be spoken of is not the constant Way.' (Our translation.) One character “也”, and one character “恒” (altered to “常” to avoid the taboo on the name of Emperor Wen, Liu Heng 刘恒), let both the way of dividing and the literal wording of the received text show traces of alteration: **the 'old books' we mark are often versions that people of the Han marked for people of the Han to hear**. *Judou* therefore has two faces: for the reader, it is a thermometer of understanding; for the scholar, it is an X-ray of the edition. Whenever one meets 'it has always been divided this way', ask one question more: who was the first to divide it so, and what copy had he seen? — that one question takes one from student through the door of the research institute.
:::

::: warning Four common errors in sentence division
One, **wrongly dividing a quotation**: the start and end of a quotation must be paired by 曰/云/言, and the persons and the logic of the events must hold together — how far the text runs after “某某子曰” ('Master So-and-so said') is a cause that commentators have been hearing in court year after year. Two, **misidentifying function words**: a sentence-final “也” marks a judgement, but “也” may also be used inside a sentence to mark a cadence (“其闻道也亦先乎吾” — 'His hearing the Way was also earlier than mine.' (Our translation.) “也” is one pause inside the sentence, and only “亦先乎吾” closes it) — **也/焉/乎 all hold a part-time post as 'one pause inside the sentence'**; do not draw a circle the moment you see “也”. Three, **judging the ancient by the modern**: “吾谁欺？欺天乎” — 'Whom do I deceive? Do I deceive Heaven?' (Our translation.) forced into a continuous reading on modern word order (“吾谁” not broken apart). What the joke books of Qing writers collect is precisely this pedant's way of dividing — the seam of a fronted pronominal object must be drawn according to Old Chinese grammar. Four, **lightly altering an old reading**: however fine a new reading, it must first pass the double gate of same-period grammar and of editions — 'freedom of sentence division' is not 'arbitrariness of sentence division'.
:::

::: exercise Five surgical cases {level=1}
Divide the sentences and mark the knife technique: ① “学而时习之不亦说乎有朋自远方来不亦乐乎” ② “臣诚知不如徐公美臣之妻私臣臣之妾畏臣臣之客欲有求于臣皆以美于徐公” ③ “积土成山风雨兴焉积水成渊蛟龙生焉” ④ “宋人有耕者田中有株兔走触株折颈而死” ⑤ “予独爱莲之出淤泥而不染濯清涟而不妖”.
::: solution
① The signposts “子曰/乎乎” close it twice (“说” is a phonetic loan (*tongjia*) for “悦”; where one divides is where the loan is — *judou* shakes hands with the previous lesson); ② “臣之妻/妾/客”, three ranks of matching phrases, gathered at the end by “皆以美于徐公” (the chain-repetition seam “臣臣” plus matching phrases); ③ “焉焉”, two full stops, plus strict matching phrases (a parallel-sentence template); ④ “者” raises a pause and opens the narrative; “株株” is chain repetition; four short clauses run on in a flow (the signposts 者 and 也, plus chain repetition); ⑤ the construction with “之” is a long reading and is not broken; “染/妖” closes it by matching phrases. Naming the knife technique of each of the five cases is full marks.
:::

::: exercise Reversing a wrong verdict {level=2}
Compare two ways of dividing: a certain textbook marks “一鼓，作气；再而衰；三而竭”, which differs from the received text “一鼓作气，再而衰，三而竭” — judge how they agree and differ, and which is better or worse, and give a translation.
::: solution
What the two forms divide is the same (what the position of the comma changes is **the rhythm of the pause, not the syntactic relation**): the received form reads “一鼓作气” whole, as an idiom (*chengyu*); the form “一鼓，作气” emphasises two steps, condition and result (the army of Qi attacked Changshao; Duke Zhuang of Lu was about to beat the drum; Cao Gui 曹刿, “未可” ('Not yet'); after “可矣” ('It will do') comes “一鼓作气”) — better and worse: for reading aloud in class, use the received form (the momentum runs smoothly); for grammatical analysis, the segmented form may be used (subject and predicate are clear); translation: “第一次击鼓，士气振作” — 'At the first beating of the drum, morale is raised.' (Our translation.) Marking points: state clearly 'same syntax, different rhythm', and do not let a shifted comma frighten you into interpreting the sentence afresh — **in a dispute over *judou*, first separate 'a dispute about interpretation' from 'a dispute about the pause'**; this case belongs to the latter.
:::

::: exercise An examination on plain text {level=2}
An unpunctuated practical test (choose for yourself 60–80 characters of material from the course, or, from the *Analects*, *Mencius*, the *Zhuangzi* or the *Zuo Tradition*, take a photocopy of a passage of plain text that has never been punctuated). After dividing the sentences, **first** check your answers against three commentarial editions, count your accuracy, record one place where the commentarial editions do not agree, and write a judgement of 100 characters.
::: solution
Marking points: the photocopy of the plain text is kept on file; the accuracy is calculated by oneself, honestly; where commentarial editions diverge, the page number is recorded; the judgement paragraph must cite at least one kind of ground among function words, matching phrases and editions — a verdict of 'doubt retained, awaiting collation' (存疑待校) is allowed; forcing a solution is forbidden. The aim of this exercise is not to get everything right. It is to make you **see a commentarial edition as you would see the witness box**.
:::

::: exercise Short essay: whom did punctuation turn into an author? {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. Start from the 1920 general order on punctuation and go on to 'the edited editions of old books are the books we really read' — the punctuator stays hidden and yet takes part in the writing (examples from the course: the case of divergent reading in the *Analects*, the case of the character “也” in the silk manuscript, and one further true record of a place you yourself divided wrongly and were then corrected). Argue: is new-style punctuation a 'translation' of *judou*, or a 'tampering'? And propose: when old books are edited, should appending at the end of the book a 'record of divergent sentence divisions' (断句异读记) become a rule of the trade?
::: solution
Marking points: the three cases are cited with no hard errors (the date of the silk manuscript, 1973, is correct); the distinction between 'translation' and 'tampering' is brought down onto the mechanism (punctuation = a choice that reduces the text to a single value, while the original keeps several values at once; every mark is one micro-commentary); the proposal for a rule of the trade is concrete (a sketch of the format for an appended record of variant readings); humour is allowed at the end: the greatest virtue of a punctuator is to let people not see him, and the next is to let people look him up.
:::

::: summary
Three sentences on completing the *judou* course: **where the sentence is divided = where understanding has arrived** (the six knives used together: function-word signposts, matching phrases and chain repetition, quotations fixed by 曰 and 云, syntax returned to its place, outside help from editions); ***judou* = interpretation** (one comma in “民可使由之” revises the verdict on Confucius; one character “也” in the silk manuscript shows up the Han birthmark on the current text); ***judou* = a history of institutions** (from the “离经” class in the 'Xueji' to punctuation entering the palace in 1920, the responsibility for sentence division passed from the reader to the author, and our freedom lessens as the commas grow more numerous). The next lesson is also the last proper procedure of this course: the eight techniques of modern Chinese translation — retain, replace, supply, delete, reorder, match, split and join — fully unpacking a compressed archive of two thousand years.
:::
