---
title: "What classical Chinese is"
---

A Korean, a Japanese and a person of the Tang sit before the same desk, read the same volume of the *Analects*, and read it out as three sounds — and can still, after all, more or less understand. That is Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*): **a written language welded together by Chinese characters, and preserved without relying on the voices of the living**. This lesson first gets a clear look at its three identity cards.

## Identity card one: it is a language, not a pile of famous sayings {#sec-yuyan}

::: definition The definition of Classical Chinese, and three features {#def-wenyan}
**Classical Chinese**: a written system of Chinese that takes the written language of the pre-Qin and the two Han dynasties as its model, and that was stabilised through two thousand years of imitation after the Han (the name itself is old as well: the original sense of “文言” in the *Book of Changes* (*Zhouyi*), under Qian, in the Wenyan, is “文饰之言” ('ornamented words'); taking it as the general name of a language arose only as late as the schools of the late Qing, set up in contrast with “东文” ('Eastern writing') and “西洋文” ('Western writing') — name and substance alike are worth an identity card). Three features of the system: ① **a monosyllable makes a word** — in “国破山河在” ('The state is ruined; the mountains and rivers remain.' (Our translation.)) five characters are five words, while the same sentence in modern Chinese has to grow disyllabic tails, “国家、破碎、河流” ('state', 'broken to pieces', 'rivers') (Classical Chinese = the **morphology** of earlier Chinese, not the 'tone' of earlier Chinese); ② **parataxis as the organising principle** — connectives are few, and the subject can be omitted; the conditional relation in “入则无法家拂士，出则无敌国外患” ('If, within, there are no families who keep the laws and no officers who remonstrate; if, without, there are no enemy states and no troubles from outside.' (Our translation.)) stands by itself entirely on the positions of the parallelism ([[modern-grammar/complex-sentences]] parataxis in its extreme form); ③ **prize brevity and shun repetition** — “一鼓作气，再而衰，三而竭” ('At the first drum, morale is raised; at the second, it falls away; at the third, it is exhausted.' (Our translation.)), and even the character “鼓” is omitted twice. The rate of compression is both the dignity of Classical Chinese and its hidden reef (miss it in translation, and you miss an examination point).
:::

## Identity card two: the vernacular's full brother, not a photograph of it in old age {#sec-baihua}

::: definition The relation of Classical Chinese and the vernacular: two thousand years of a diglossic division of labour {#def-shuangyan}
The greatest misunderstanding must be shot first: “Classical Chinese = what the ancients said.” The spoken language of Confucius's lectures was ten times more long-winded than the *Analects*; people of the Han could already not understand the *Book of Documents* — **Classical Chinese was a written language from the moment it was born**, while the spoken language went on growing into the vernacular (*baihua*) of age after age (Chinese translations of the Buddhist scriptures, Tang transformation texts (*bianwen*), Song recorded sayings, Yuan variety plays (*zaju*), and the fiction of the Ming and the Qing, a voice kept on record the whole way). There thus formed a **diglossic arrangement (diglossia)** of specimen grade in the linguistics of humankind: Classical Chinese took charge of the civil-service examinations, official documents, histories and letters (solemn occasions); the vernacular took charge of household affairs, markets, storytelling and love letters (everyday occasions) — a person who could read was bilingual by nature, and switched as naturally as one switches today between WeChat and email. The Chinese character takes the credit inside this arrangement: it does not chase pronunciation — write “风雨” ('wind and rain') the same way, and the sounds of the Tang, Yue (Cantonese) and Mandarin (*guanhua*) each come readily to the mouth, while the meaning does not move house ([[chinese-characters/writing-and-language]] supra-dialectal character; for the ledger of pronunciation see [[phonology/why-phonology]]).
:::

::: example The compression rate, on the spot {#ex-yashuo}
In 《岳阳楼记》 ('Record of Yueyang Tower'), the eight characters “政通人和，百废具兴” ('Government is in good order and the people are in harmony; every neglected affair rises.' (Our translation.)) — please work out the compression ratio, and identify what the modern Chinese translation has in extra.
::: solution
The compression ratio is about 1:3. Three layers are extra: ① **grammatical glue** (the particles “了” and “的”, coordinating conjunctions, and the supplying of subjects — the frame that parataxis in Classical Chinese leaves out must be made explicit in a modern Chinese translation); ② **disyllabic filling** (“政→政事” ('government → the affairs of government'), “废→荒废的事业” ('abandoned → undertakings left abandoned') — the fee for a change of generation in morphology between ancient and modern, [[modern-vocabulary/morphemes-words]] the ledger of the shrinking of monosyllabic words); ③ **redundancy insurance** (a transcription into speech must leave the hearer a margin for error). Conclusion: **Classical Chinese is not “saying little”; by default, the information all sits in shared knowledge** — a modern Chinese translation = buying back for it the public memory it had left out.
:::
:::

::: definition Identity card three: it has strata, and it can be dated {#def-diceng}
Two thousand years of Classical Chinese are not one single plank: ① **the pre-Qin native stratum** — a true recording of the spoken language, with many exceptions and a wild look (that the *Book of Documents* is tortuous and hard to pronounce shows precisely that it was hard at the time, too); ② **the Han-to-Tang stratum of imitating antiquity** — Sima Qian led the way; the ancient-style prose (*guwen*) movement of Han and Liu settled the form; “文言” became a craft of writing to the models; ③ **the Song–Yuan stratum of vernacular infiltration** — recorded sayings and plain tales (*pinghua*) open a window: in the storyteller's scripts (*huaben*), “恁地” and “次第” (处处, 'everywhere') are already living vernacular words boring into the paper surface of Classical Chinese; ④ **the Ming–Qing stratum of imitating antiquity and of blending** — the 'speaking for the ancients' style of the eight-legged essay (*baguwen*) is the purest, while at the same time *Water Margin* and *Dream of the Red Chamber* let the vernacular pour back the other way; the Tongcheng school's talk of “雅洁” ('refined and pure') is precisely a guarding against the fishy smell of speech. The **dating test-paper** is very simple: the pre-Qin grammar of “再而衰”, the post-Tang name of a thing “契丹” ('Khitan'), the stock phrase of memorials “臣窃以为” ('your servant ventures to consider'), and the density of “的” and “了” — one text, three test-papers. In reading Classical Chinese, ask first: **A person of which age is this, imitating which age?**
:::

::: intuition The right way to open Classical Chinese: the eyes read, the ears help {#int-dufa}
Learning a modern foreign language relies on 'listening and speaking first'; Classical Chinese comes at it precisely the other way: it has no native speakers, and **the ear is borrowed**. The best path is “**vision first + help from the sound in the ear**”: recognise the characters (the proper character and the phonetic loan (*tongjia*) each go back to their own place) → take the sense (the history of word-meaning falls into place) → divide the sentences (*judou*, the traditional pause-marks, just is grammatical analysis, [[classical-chinese-1/punctuating]]) → read aloud (the rhythm of voice and breath helps you lock the grammatical positions of “之/乎/者也” — function words are punctuation for the ear). Memorising here is not shameful, and it is efficient: memorise twenty model pieces, and the grammatical fingerprints are entered in store of themselves — **a feel for the language is a statistic of the sample** (modern research on second-language acquisition has half rehabilitated the reciting of texts in the private school; the condition is that, while reciting, one must grasp the general sense).
:::

::: history The name “文言” {#sec-ziming}
A stretch of the history of the name, asked and answered of itself: in the **pre-Qin** there was no name “文言” (it was all living writing, and there was no need to call it out by name); in the mouths of **Han** masters of the Classics, “文” and “古字” had already begun to draw a line between the ages; when people of the **Tang** discussed literature, “文言” used together grew gradually commoner, and meant literary diction; **Song** Confucians often set “文言” over against “道” ('the Way'), and from this the keyword of literary criticism was fixed in place; in the **late Qing**, the regulations of the schools set language and writing on separate paths, and **around 1904 “文言” at last referred to a kind of language**, seated facing “白话”, and from then on became a term of the discipline; **after the 1920s** it retired once more, into a heritage subject. The history of one word's lifespan is the history of this language's rise and decline.
:::

::: warning Three wrong postures in learning Classical Chinese
One, **memorising the grammar and not the texts**: memorising the nineteen uses of “之” until they make a classic, and still, on meeting a living sentence, being unable to divide it — a rule is a specimen; only a sample is a living body (every grammatical point in this course grows on a selected passage; memorising off the body is not allowed); two, **forcing the vernacular on as a hard fit**: it is not by translating “吾谁欺” ('Whom do I deceive?' (Our translation.)) into the word order of “我骗谁” ('I deceive whom?') that one counts as having understood — **a dislocation of words between ancient and modern is a shallow pit; a dislocation of word order between ancient and modern is a deep pit** ([[classical-chinese-1/special-patterns]]); three, **only translating, and not reading**: Classical Chinese is a system in which sound is written; a hundred silent translations are no match for ten recitations from memory — the breathing of the *judou*, and the weight and lightness of the function words, all show what is real in the mouth.
:::

::: exercise Dating test-paper {level=1}
For each of the four sentences, judge one most likely stratum of its age (pre-Qin / Han–Tang imitation of antiquity / Song–Yuan vernacular infiltration / Ming–Qing), and give one piece of test-paper evidence: ① “恁地不解，却来问人。” ('Not understanding it so, you yet come and ask someone.' (Our translation.)) ② “一鼓作气，再而衰。” ('At the first drum, morale is raised; at the second, it falls away.' (Our translation.)) ③ “伏惟陛下圣德广被，臣无任犬马屏营之至。” ('Bowing, I consider that Your Majesty's sage virtue reaches far; your servant is filled, beyond all bearing, with the anxious dread of a dog and a horse.' (Our translation.)) ④ In the *Book of Songs*, “窈窕淑女” ('gentle and graceful, the fair lady' (Our translation.)).
::: solution
① the Song–Yuan vernacular stratum (“恁地”, a living vernacular word); ② pre-Qin native (“再” used as 'a second time', and the verb omitted as carried forward from before); ③ Ming–Qing memorials imitating ancient prose (the density of stock phrases + the “伏惟” frame — the official-document tone of the late stratum of imitating antiquity); ④ pre-Qin native (alliterative and rhyming binomes, and no function words of later ages). Full marks as soon as each piece of test-paper evidence is correctly paired.
:::

::: exercise A laboratory of the compression rate {level=2}
Choose a passage of not more than 30 characters from 《岳阳楼记》 ('Record of Yueyang Tower') or 《爱莲说》 ('On the Love of the Lotus'); list it character by character (character / sense / the corresponding modern word); count the characters of the modern Chinese translation; and point out how many characters each of the three items, 'grammatical glue, disyllabification, redundancy', eats up.
::: solution
Marking points: no wrong senses in the character list; the three accounts, kept separate, can be checked (glue ≈ the number of function words added in the modern Chinese translation; disyllables ≈ the number of expansions from one syllable to two; redundancy ≈ the beats added in making it colloquial); one question of reflection at the end: which one character is hardest to move house — the hardest characters are the cultural entries (such as “谪” and “岫”), and making a card for the entry is the homework of this lesson.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: is Classical Chinese dead, or has it retired? {level=3}
Write from eight hundred to twelve hundred characters: in 1920 “文言” withdrew from practical use, and yet it returns for an encore every day in four-character idioms (*chengyu*), in four-character words of official documents, in titles and in the naming of brands — please count the Classical Chinese elements in the headlines of one day's newspaper (four-character frames, constructions with 之, monosyllabic verbs), and argue for your verdict: death / retirement / a change of form? You must cite the two concepts of this lesson, 'diglossic division of labour' and 'strata'; the data must be reproducible.
::: solution
Marking points: a sample of headlines of at least 20 items, and a classified count of Classical Chinese elements (formulaic words “诸如/旨在” ('such as' / 'with the aim of'), four-character frames, attributives with 之); the argument uses the frame of 'diglossia' to explain the occasions of the return (the solemn domain = the ground of Classical Chinese has not been lost); a verdict of the kind 'surviving by a change of form' is allowed, but an **inventory of occasions** must be given as evidence of the territory.
:::

::: summary
Three identity cards of Classical Chinese: a **language** with a morphology and a grammar (a monosyllable makes a word; parataxis is the organising principle; brevity is prized and repetition shunned); the vernacular's **full brother**, and not a photograph of it in old age (two thousand years of diglossia; Chinese characters, standing above the dialects, keep it alive); a **specimen that can be dated**, with strata (four strata: native — imitating antiquity — infiltration — blending; in reading Classical Chinese, ask first 'who is imitating whom'). The name itself is also a history of rise and decline: the sense 'ornamented diction' began in the Classics; the name of a language was fixed in the late Qing; it retired in the Republic; it returns for an encore in every solemn title today. The next lesson goes to the shelf of tools: from the *Shuowen jiezi* (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*) and the *Kangxi Dictionary* to the *Ciyuan* and the *Hanyu da zidian* (*Great Dictionary of Chinese Characters*) — the prior skill of making 'looking a book up' into 'reading a book'.
:::
