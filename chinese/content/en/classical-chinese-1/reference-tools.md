---
title: "Dictionaries and reference tools"
---

Half the confidence one brings to reading Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) comes from 'knowing how to look things up' (会查). This lesson teaches four layers of tools: **character dictionaries** (asking about a character's origins), **rhyme dictionaries** (asking about a character's old reading), **commentaries** (asking how the ancients understood it), and **classified encyclopaedias (*leishu* 类书) and databases** (asking for the complete case file on a word). Look a book up, and look it up correctly, and that is to have two thousand years of scholars travelling with you.

## The character-dictionary layer: from the *Shuowen* to the *Hanyu da zidian* {#sec-zishu}

::: definition The *Shuowen*: a court of graphic form, born for reading the Classics {#def-shuowen}
Xu Shen's 许慎 *Shuowen jiezi* (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*) was completed in the years 100–121. It takes in 9,353 characters and sets up **540 radicals** (section headers). It tries its cases by “**先解形、因形说义、因义说音**” ('First explain the form; from the form explain the meaning; from the meaning explain the sound.') (Our translation.): under the character 小 it says “小也，象物微之形” ('It is small; it depicts the form of a minute thing.') (Our translation.). To examine a character, one must first recognise the graph. **Three ledgers for reading it**: ① Xu Shen had not seen oracle-bone inscriptions or bronze inscriptions, and in graphic structure there are misjudgements in cases out of ten — the type is the character “為”, explained as “母猴也” ('It is a female monkey.') (Our translation.), whereas in the bronze script it is plainly the form of a hand leading an elephant to do work; ② what it records is a **system of ancient meanings**, the first consultation when one reads pre-Qin books. Duan Yucai's 段玉裁 *Shuowen jiezi zhu* (*Commentary on the Shuowen jiezi*, 1815), ranked with Gui Fu 桂馥, Zhu Junsheng 朱骏声 and Wang Yun 王筠 as the 'four houses of the *Shuowen*' (说文四家), is the court of second instance for this case; ③ its radicals are a **catalogue by semantic class**, not a method for looking characters up (sections are assigned by the logic of how characters are formed: the 木 section takes in trees, the 艸 section takes in names of grasses). To look a character up, one must first understand the thinking behind the making of characters. A mnemonic for use: **求本义问说文，求用义问后世书** ('For the original meaning, ask the *Shuowen*; for the meaning in use, ask later books.') (Our translation.).
:::

::: definition The *Kangxi Dictionary* and the great character dictionaries of today {#def-kangxi}
The *Kangxi Dictionary* (1716, Zhang Yushu 张玉书, Chen Tingjing 陈廷敬 and others) contains forty-seven thousand characters. Its format, in one sentence: **for each character it first lists the pronunciation glosses (copied straight from such books as the *Tangyun* 唐韵, 'Rhymes of the Tang', the *Guangyun* 广韵, 'Broad Rhymes', the *Jiyun* 集韵, 'Collected Rhymes', and the *Shiji yinyi* 史记音义, 'Sounds and meanings for the *Records of the Grand Historian*'), then lists the senses, and for each sense one earliest citation** — it does not adjudicate, it only files. The chronology of the senses lies hidden in the order of the citations; knowing how to read the citations is the same as knowing how to look up the dating of a word's meaning. Its pitfalls are famous too: mistaken citations and fabricated entries occur now and then (Wang Yinzhi's 王引之 *Zidian kaozheng*, *An Evidential Study of the Dictionary*, corrects more than three hundred), **Qing scholars quoted books from memory, before the age of the keyboard**. The modern standard kit: the ***Hanyu da zidian*** (*Great Dictionary of Chinese Characters*; sixty thousand characters, a full record of the history of form, sound and meaning, the scholarly case file) and the ***Hanyu da cidian*** (*Great Chinese Dictionary*; a network of compounds and citations, the general ledger for looking up 'allusions' (典故) and disyllabic words). Quick reference at the desk is given to the *Ciyuan* (the revised edition, in charge specifically of old books, old readings and old matters) and to Wang Li's 王力 *Dictionary of Common Characters in Classical Chinese* (common characters and common senses set out by frequency, with a small encyclopaedia attached, “古汉语通论”, 'A general account of Classical Chinese').
:::

::: example A consultation on one character: “再” {#ex-zai}
“一鼓作气，再而衰，三而竭” ('At the first drumming their spirit is raised; the second time it declines; the third time it is exhausted.') (Our translation.) — following the route of the *Kangxi Dictionary*, hold a consultation on the character “再”, and show how a reference book puts an end to the mistranslation “再次” ('again').
::: solution
① Locate the headword “再” (the 冂 radical, six strokes); ② check the pronunciation glosses: the various *fanqie* spellings in the *Tangyun*, the *Guangyun* and the rest come under “在代切”, Division I, with no split into a changed reading (*podu* 破读) — the sound does not change; ③ read the senses and the citations: the first sense is “一举而二度也” ('One action, and it amounts to two times.') (Our translation.). The citations go back as early as the *Changes* (《易》) and the *Rites* (《礼》) (“再拜”, 'bow twice', and “再三”, 'two times and three', both belong to the sense-group 'twice'); the adverbial sense 'repetition, again' has citations that plainly appear late — **the order of citations just is the chronological table of word meaning**; ④ verdict: in pre-Qin texts “再” = the second time / two times, so “再而衰” is 'the second time (the drum is beaten), morale declines'. To translate it as “再次” ('again') is to back-fill a pre-Qin text with a word-meaning from after the Jin and the Tang. The four steps done, the mistranslation is out.
:::
:::



::: definition The commentary layer: how the ancients understood, and how we understand again {#def-zhushi}
A dictionary explains characters; a commentary explains sentences. **The formats of commentary have steps**: the “传” of the *Mao Shi zhuan* (*Mao Commentary on the Songs*) is an explanation that presses close to the original sense; **Zheng Xuan** 郑玄 commented throughout the Classics, and “注” became the name of the genre (“注者，著也” — 'To comment is to fix in place.' (Our translation.) — nailing the meaning onto the sentence); Tang scholars wrote a further explanation of the Han commentaries and called it **“疏”** (*shu*, a subcommentary; 正义, 'correct meaning'), and so the standard image of the page is **the three layers, Classic–commentary–subcommentary, on the same page** (*Shisanjing zhushu*, *The Thirteen Classics with Commentaries and Subcommentaries*); a collected commentary (*jijie*) gathers the several explanations (He Yan's 何晏 *Lunyu jijie*, *Collected Commentaries on the Analects*, assembles the various houses; Zhu Xi's 朱熹 *Sishu zhangju jizhu*, *Collected Commentaries on the Chapters and Sentences of the Four Books*, is Song learning making a fresh start of its own). Three postures for reading a commentary: ① **first read the original plain, and only then look up at the commentary** — a commentary is a key, not a crutch; ② recognise the commentary's terms: “曰为” and “谓之” give a definition, “读曰” and “读为” mark a phonetic loan (*tongjia*), “某当为某” is a collation that emends a character, and “之属” and “貌” describe shape and manner; ③ commentators can be wrong too, and **pages are full of lawsuits in which Tang subcommentaries refute Han commentaries and Qing scholars refute Tang subcommentaries** — before you submit to the last opinion, you are in the room.
:::

::: history From rhyme dictionaries to the mouse: how reference books change generation {#sec-huandai}
The ledger of pronunciation glosses is kept in the **rhyme dictionaries**: the *Qieyun* 切韵 ('Cut Rhymes') opened the file in 601, and the Song *Guangyun*, with 206 rhymes, is the general court for the pronunciation of Classical Chinese (variant readings by a changed pronunciation, “读破”, and the search for a sound in a phonetic loan, are both checked against it, [[phonology/rhyme-books]]); when *fanqie* is not enough, there are still the old habits of **direct homophone glosses and forced rhyme-readings** (*zhiyin* 直音 and *xieyin* 叶音) — in 1606 Chen Di 陈第 of the Ming, in a single utterance, “时有古今，地有南北，字有更革，音有转移” ('In time there is ancient and modern; in place there is south and north; characters are remade; sounds shift.') (Our translation.) (*Maoshi guyin kao*, *An Investigation of the Ancient Sounds in the Mao Songs*), overturned the case of *xieyin*, and the study of ancient sounds was thereby opened as a case ([[phonology/fanqie]] continues the ledger). Classified encyclopaedias are the search engines of the age of print: the *Yiwen leiju* (*A Collection of Literature Arranged by Categories*) and the *Taiping yulan* (*Imperial Reader of the Taiping Era*) stockpile citations by category, and for a thousand years the compilers of dictionaries have drawn their material from them. **Digitisation completes the last change of generation**: full-text repositories of Chinese books (Wikisource, and Academia Sinica's 'Full-text Database of Chinese Books', 汉籍全文资料库) upgrade 'looking up character by character' (逐字查) to 'searching the whole web, word by word' (逐词全网查) — **every citation of the character “再” can be listed on one screen, and the dating of word meaning turns from a scholar's virtuoso skill into a secondary-school pupil's homework**. But retrieval cannot settle the verdict: the database gives you a hundred examples, and which sense fits this sentence is still for a person to decide — the tools change generation, the judgement does not.
:::

::: intuition Reference books are a court, not an imperial edict {#int-ting}
Think of looking a book up as opening a hearing: the original text is the party, the dictionary is the household-registration office, the rhyme dictionary is the voiceprint file, the commentaries and subcommentaries are the witness stand, the classified encyclopaedia is the surveillance recording, and you are the judge. The common form of a misreading is **to let the household-registration office serve as the judge**: the dictionary sets two senses of the character “再” plainly side by side (twice / repetition), and which to choose is for the period of the sentence alone to decide. The definition of 'knowing how to look things up' (会查) then comes into view: **knowing which layer of tool answers which layer of question, and, when the evidence conflicts, carrying out the duty to adjudicate.** Looking up three reference books and drawing up one written judgement is better than copying ten pages of a reference translation — and this is the master theme of all the training in this lesson for which 'a citation is evidence' (引用即证据).
:::

::: warning Three diagnoses of dependence on tools
First, **the dictionary-only doctrine**: take a sense-number from the *Ciyuan* and fill the blank with it directly, with no regard to syntactic position or to period — senses are a menu, and to order one must look to the stomach (write the 'repetition' sense of “再” into a pre-Qin translation, and the dictionary is not guilty; you are). Second, **the commentary-and-subcommentary-only doctrine**: Zhu Xi says “格者，至也” ('*Ge* means to arrive.') (Our translation.), and one takes it for a fact of the nature of things — a commentary is a Song scholar's answer to the assignment, not the Classic itself. Third, **the database-only doctrine**: grep your way to twenty citations and take them for the conclusion, without looking at the edition, without minding corrupt characters, and without distinguishing genre — **a search yields frequency; a search does not yield aptness**. The three diagnoses share one prescription: the tools are a chain of evidence, and the page of the verdict is always signed: you.
:::

::: exercise Looking books up, in practice {level=1}
Using any two tools (on paper, or in a database of Chinese books), look up the character “去”: set out its original meaning, two commonly used old meanings and one modern meaning, each with one citation, and fix the period of the “去” in “去皮” ('remove the skin').
::: solution
Points: the original meaning is 'a person leaves' (of the class of “君子于是去之” in *Mencius*, 'The gentleman thereupon left him.' (Our translation.), the sense-group 'to leave'); the commonly used old meanings are 'to remove / to discard' (the stock example of the course, “巫妪行去”, 'the old shamaness was then cast away' (Our translation.) — the removal sense, of 'throwing into the river') and 'distance' (“相去四十里” in 'The Banquet at Hongmen', 'They were forty *li* apart.' (Our translation.) — **the distance sense is easily missed in the judgement, under the meaning current today**); the modern meaning 'to go to' arose later (common in the Ming and Qing vernacular, *baihua*). “去皮” = the sense 'to remove', already current in Middle Chinese; judge it usable for the Tang and the Song. Two tools, plus four meanings with citations, is full marks.
:::
:::

::: exercise Decoding the terms {level=2}
Define the terms of commentary and subcommentary, and give one example of each: 曰/谓之, 读曰/读为, 当为, 貌.
::: solution
“曰” and “谓之” = giving a definition (the format of the same line as the *Erya*'s “母为妣”, 'A mother is a *bi*.' (Our translation.), and “父曰考”, 'A father is called a *kao*.' (Our translation.)); “读曰 / 读为” = resolving a phonetic loan, replacing the loan character with the proper character (in commentaries on the Classics, “某读曰某”, 'X is read as Y', are all of this kind — in the marking, the weight falls on explaining the mechanism); “当为” = a collation that emends a corrupt character (“甲当为乙”, where the graphic form has caused the error: 'A should be B'); “貌” = depicting quality and manner (a descriptive note of the type “莞尔，微笑貌”, '*Guan'er*: the appearance of a slight smile.' (Our translation.)). If the mechanism of the four terms is correct, that is full marks; reasonable variants are allowed in the examples.
:::
:::

::: exercise Practice in written judgements {level=2}
For the “被” of “将军身被坚执锐” ('The general himself put on stout armour and grasped sharp weapons.') (Our translation.): using the four layers of tools (the *Shuowen* / a rhyme dictionary or pronunciation glosses / a commentary or a grammar book / a database), produce one complete written judgement — sound, form, meaning, and a judgement on phonetic loan.
::: solution
Points of the judgement: the *Shuowen* has “被，寝衣也” ('*Bei*: a garment for sleeping.') (Our translation.); the original meaning = a garment for sleeping. The sense here is the extension 'to cover → to wear draped on the body' (the pattern “被+衣/甲”, crossing levels). Sound and sense match: this sense should be read with the sound of “披” (the sound follows the sense; rhyme dictionaries and modern commentaries give the pronunciation *pī*). “披” is a character differentiated later; “被 / 披” stand in the relation of **ancient and modern forms of a word**, not of an occasional borrowing of a sound. The database: citations of “被坚执锐” in the *Records of the Grand Historian* and the *Book of Han* come in a string, all in the sense 'to wear draped'. Verdict: “被” here is the use of “披” as **ancient and modern forms of a word** — not an occasional borrowing of a sound, but the division of labour from before the sense 'to cover / to wear draped' split off. Full marks if each of the four layers has a record and the relation is judged correctly.
:::

::: summary
Four layers of tools, four kinds of question: character dictionaries ask about origins (the *Shuowen* explains meaning by tying it to the form; the *Kangxi* files the citations — **the order of citations is the chronological table of word meaning**), rhyme dictionaries ask about the old reading (the *Qieyun* opens the file; Chen Di breaks *xieyin*), commentaries ask what the ancients understood (the three layers of Classic, commentary and subcommentary; the terms are a programming language), and classified encyclopaedias and databases ask about use throughout the world (retrieval changes generation; judgement does not change). The right way to imagine looking a book up is the opening of a hearing: the tools appear in court to testify, and you are responsible for the verdict — the right to sign cannot be outsourced. The next lesson enters the workshop of content words: the expansion, narrowing and transfer of meaning between ancient and modern, and taboo and pejorative decline — the old foundations of “走”, “去” and “汤”, all caught in one cast of the net.
:::
