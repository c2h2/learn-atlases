---
title: "Word order and special sentence patterns"
---

Modern Chinese word order is passengers queuing to enter the station; Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) word order is a court assembly with its ceremonial escort: **a nominal sentence does not use 是, the passive has four sets of uniform, the object will jump the queue to before the verb, and an attributive can stand behind the head** — and the backbone of whole sentences, sentence after sentence, plays at going missing (omission). This lesson is the imaging department of syntax: four X-ray plates, and one comparison table.

## Plate one: the nominal sentence — 是 is not present {#sec-pan1}

::: definition Frontal and lateral views of the nominal sentence {#def-panduan}
The nominal sentence of Classical Chinese **relies on sentence mood and on a noun predicating directly, not on a verb of judgement**. The regular pattern is "**……者，……也**" (陈胜者，阳城人也, 'Chen Sheng was a man of Yangcheng.' (Our translation.)); 者 may be omitted (和氏璧，天下所共传宝也, 'The He family's jade is a treasure jointly handed down by all the world.' (Our translation.)); 也 may be omitted (陈胜者，阳城人, 'Chen Sheng was a man of Yangcheng.' (Our translation.)); both may be omitted (荀卿，赵人, 'Xun Qing was a man of Zhao.' (Our translation.) — in the *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*) the two-character household register is the most economical). To strengthen the judgement, adverbs are used: **乃, 为, 即 and 则** (此乃臣效命之秋也, 'This is the season for your servant to give his life.' (Our translation.); 人为刀俎，我为鱼肉, 'Others are the knife and the chopping block; I am the fish and the meat.' (Our translation.); 此则岳阳楼之大观也, 'This, then, is the grand prospect of Yueyang Tower.' (Our translation.)). Negative judgement is reserved to "**非**": 人非生而知之者, 'A person is not one who knows it from birth.' (Our translation.); 子非鱼，安知鱼之乐, 'You are not a fish; how do you know the joy of fish?' (Our translation.); 是非君子之言也, 'This is not the speech of a gentleman.' (Our translation.) (the *Book of Rites*, *Liji* 《礼记》) — what 非 negates is the whole predicate, not an ellipsis of 不是. **The greatest trap**: in Classical Chinese 是 is mostly **the demonstrative pronoun "this"** — 是吾忧也 = 这是我的忧虑 ('This is my worry.' (Our translation.)); 是社稷之臣也，何以伐为, 'These are ministers of the altars of soil and grain; why attack them?' (Our translation.) — the judgement-word 是 began to sprout at the end of the Warring States and stood firm after the Han (in 'The Peach Blossom Spring' (《桃花源记》), 问今是何世, 'He asked what age the present was.' (Our translation.), is already the modern pattern). It is grammaticalised precisely out of "this" ([[modern-vocabulary/lexicon-layers]] is one stop on the chain of grammaticalisation).
:::

## Plate two: the passive — four sets of uniform plus one set of plain clothes {#sec-pan2}

::: definition The full spectrum of passive markers {#def-beidong}
① "**于/乎**" introduces the agent: 治于人 ('governed by others') (劳心者治人，劳力者治于人, 'Those who labour with the mind govern others; those who labour with strength are governed by others.' (Our translation.)); 内惑于郑袖，外欺于张仪, 'Within, he was deluded by Zheng Xiu; without, he was deceived by Zhang Yi.' (Our translation.). ② The "**为**" sentence: 身为宋国笑, 'He himself was laughed at by the state of Song.' (Our translation.) (the *Han Feizi* 《韩非子》: the closing statement in the case of the farmer who kept to the stump — the state of Song's number-one meme-pack); 为天下笑者，何也, 'One who is laughed at by all under heaven — why?' (Our translation.) ('On the Faults of Qin', 《过秦论》). ③ "**为……所……**": 为巡船所物色, 'sought out by the patrol boats' (Our translation.); 先即制人，后则为人所制, 'He who is first controls others; he who is behind is controlled by others.' (Our translation.) (the *Records of the Grand Historian*). ④ The character "**见**" stands sentry before the verb: 信而见疑，忠而被谤, 'Faithful, and yet suspected; loyal, and yet slandered.' (Our translation.) ('The Biography of Qu Yuan', 《屈原列传》). Note the second job of 见 — **a referential adverb "towards me"**: 慈父见背 ('a kind father left me behind') (父亲离我而去, 'Father departed and left me.' (Our translation.)); 君既若见录, 'Since you have thus kept me in mind.' (Our translation.); 府吏见督责, 'The clerk of the office presses and reproves me.' (Our translation.) — one and the same 见, one marking the passive and one marking direction; examination papers most love to set the two duelling in the same frame. ⑤ **The plain-clothes passive (unmarked)**: 屈原放逐，《离骚》赋作, 'Qu Yuan was banished, and the rhapsody *Encountering Sorrow* (*Li Sao*) was composed.' (Our translation.); 兵败地削, 'The army was defeated and the territory pared away.' (Our translation.) — the subject is the patient; one relies entirely on the sense of the matter and on parallel phrasing (兵败 and 地削 are set against each other and confirm each other). The history of the passive, in one sentence: **见, 于 and 为 were in service in the pre-Qin period; 为…所… became the established rule in the two Han dynasties; the *bei* passive (被 + verb) gradually arose after the Han** — the original meaning of 被 is "to suffer, to receive" (亮身被七十五创, 'Liang's own body received seventy-five wounds.' (Our translation.), is still a full lexical verb), and the grammaticalisation was completed in Middle Chinese ([[modern-grammar/special-constructions]]: the prequel of the *bei* passive is here).
:::

## Plate three: three queue-jumping channels for the object {#sec-pan3}

::: definition Three types of fronted object {#def-qianzhi}
**A · fronting of an interrogative-pronoun object**: 大王来何操？, 'Great King, what have you brought in coming?' (Our translation.); 沛公安在？, 'Where is the Duke of Pei?' (Our translation.); 臣实不才，又谁敢怨？, 'I am truly without ability; and whom else would I dare resent?' (Our translation.) — when an interrogative pronoun (谁, 何, 安, 孰, 恶) is the object, it stands before the verb: an iron rule of Old Chinese. **B · fronting of a pronoun object in a negative sentence**: 时人莫之许也, 'No one of that time granted it.' (Our translation.); 古之人不余欺也, 'The men of old do not deceive me.' (Our translation.); 不患人之不己知, 'Do not worry that others do not know you.' (Our translation.) — a negative (不, 莫, 未, 毋) plus a pronoun object, and the object moves to before the verb (不余欺 = 不欺余, 'do not deceive me'). **C · 是 and 之 are the standard-bearers that lift the object**: 唯命是从 and 唯利是图, the group of four-character idioms (*chengyu*): 唯利是图 ('pursue only profit'), 唯命是从 ('obey only commands'), 何陋之有, 'What crudeness is there?' (Our translation.), 句读之不知，惑之不解，或师焉，或不焉, 'The pauses (*judou*) not known, the perplexity not resolved: for the one they go to a teacher, for the other they do not.' (Our translation.) — 是 and 之 are dedicated marks for lifting the object, and **in idioms the corpse is still intact** (the modern frame 唯…是… is this grammatical fossil; 唯利是视, 'look only to profit', comes originally from the *Zuo Tradition* (*Zuozhuan*) — the register of ritual is still in use today). The common spirit of the three types: **fronting of the focus** — the interrogative focus, the item set in contrast under negation, and the exclusive character 唯 all receive the courtesy of "look at me first" (the business that information structure and word order have together; the follow-spot at the end of the sentence in [[modern-grammar/sentence-constituents]] is here shone the other way).
:::

## Plate four: postposed attributives and omission {#sec-pan4}

::: definition The back seat of the attributive, and constituents gone missing {#def-houzhi}
**Postposed attributives**, in two patterns. ① Head + **之** + attributive (+ **者**): 马之千里者，一食或尽粟一石, 'A horse that can do a thousand *li* may at one feeding finish a whole *dan* of grain.' (Our translation.); 蚓无爪牙之利、筋骨之强, 'The earthworm has neither sharpness of claw and tooth, nor strength of sinew and bone.' (Our translation.) ('Encouraging Learning', 《劝学》: 锋利的爪牙、强健的筋骨, 'sharp claws and teeth, strong sinews and bones' — postposing rhymes and gives prominence at once). ② Head + attributive (a quantity phrase most often takes the back seat): 铸以为金人十二, 'They were cast so as to make twelve bronze figures.' (Our translation.) ('On the Faults of Qin'); 盖简桃核修狭者为之, 'For he chose a peach stone that was long and narrow, and made it of that.' (Our translation.) ('The Peach-Stone Boat', 《核舟记》 — a long and narrow peach stone; an adjectival phrase too is willing to sit in the back); 带长铗之陆离兮，冠切云之崔嵬, 'I wear a long sword, trailing, ah; I wear the Cut-the-Clouds cap, towering.' (Our translation.) ('Crossing the River', 《涉江》) combines pattern ① with the florid exaggeration of the Chu voice. **Omission** is the everyday power-saving of Classical Chinese: the subject omitted (度我至军中，公乃入, 'Reckoning that I have reached the army, you, sir, may then enter.' (Our translation.) — the subject of the earlier clause, 将军, 'the general', is omitted); the predicate omitted (一鼓作气，再〔鼓〕而衰，三〔鼓〕而竭, 'At the first drum, courage is raised; at the second [drum] it flags; at the third [drum] it is exhausted.' (Our translation.)); the object omitted (竖子不足与〔之〕谋, 'The whelp is not worth plotting [it] with.' (Our translation.); 敢以〔之〕荐, 'I make bold to recommend [him] by [this].' (Our translation.)); the preposition omitted (将军战河北，臣战河南, 'The general fought Hebei; I fought Henan.' (Our translation.), omitting 于; above 加刃, 以 is omitted). **To translate is to fill in: every bracket supplied in a modern Chinese translation is public memory that Classical Chinese has saved** ([[classical-chinese-1/what-is-wenyan]]: the debt of the compression rate is settled here).
:::

::: example A combined reading of the plates {#ex-duopian}
若属皆且为所虏！, 'The lot of you are all about to be taken captive!' (Our translation.) ('The Banquet at Hongmen', 《鸿门宴》) — one sentence, three points on the paper; read the plate and restore it.
::: solution
① The passive 为所 is written in one run: **为〔之〕所虏 omits the agent object — early 为…所… allows what follows 为 to idle empty** (若属皆且为所虏 = 你们都将被他俘虏, 'You will all be captured by him.' (Our translation.)); ② the adverb 且, 'about to', sits in the adverbial position; ③ 属 = 'sort, class' (若属 = 你们这班人, 'you people of this lot' — for the scale of honour and disparagement, see register). Restored to modern word order: 你们都将被（沛公）俘虏, 'You will all be captured (by the Duke of Pei).' (Our translation.) Draw the further cases from this one: 为人所笑, 'laughed at by others', with the agent present, is the regular pattern; bare 为所, running naked, is the early form omitted to the extreme — **omission and the passive shake hands here, across the two lessons**. Mark all three and you are signed out of the department.
:::

::: history The discovery of "inversion", and a dispute of a thousand years {#sec-daozhuang}
People before the Qin did not feel their own word order to be strange — it was their mother tongue. The first to write the "strangeness" into the ledger was ***Mashi wentong*** (《马氏文通》, 'Ma's Grammar') (1898): Ma Jianzhong 马建忠, taking Western grammar as his mirror, set up the heading "**inversion**" and reckoned through the several inversions of "interrogative pronouns, negative pronouns, and 起词 (lifting the object)" — the syntax of Classical Chinese was for the first time X-rayed from the inside ([[modern-grammar/what-is-grammar]] accords it its due place). Thereafter a thousand years of battles with the brush turned on the direction of the "inversion": did the ancients **really speak this way** (the theory that the texts record speech as spoken), or is it a written **variant** (the theory of parallelism and rhyme)? Grammarians of the twentieth century (the line of Yang Bojun 杨伯峻, *Wenyan wenfa* 《文言文法》) gave a cool verdict: **fronting of the object when it is an interrogative or negative pronoun was a living grammatical compulsion of Old Chinese** (the *Analects* and *Mencius* are so regular that there is no room left for an exception); only when later ages imitated the ancients did it sink into rhetoric — in one sentence: the pre-Qin period is discipline, and after the Han it is an archaising tone of voice. **Half of the period-dating eye for reading Classical Chinese grows on these very fingerprints of word order** ([[classical-chinese-1/what-is-wenyan]]: the strata theory is called in).
:::

::: warning Four mistakes in reading the plates
First, **seeing 是 and at once calling it a judgement**: rendering 是吾忧也 as 是我的忧虑 ('is my worry') happens to come through, and only rendering it as 这是 ('this is') fits the ancient language — before you judge 是 to be a verb of judgement, first ask the period (before the Han it is mostly "this"); second, **restoring in disorder**: casually restoring 不己知 to 不知己 does not finish the job — **you must know why it was fronted** (the compulsion of negative + pronoun), or else legitimate patterns which were **not fronted**, such as 知我者 and 不知命也, will instead be altered by you into errors (fronting in a negative sentence has a condition on the pronoun; fronting of the personal pronouns 我 and 尔 is loose: 不我屑 occurs, and 不知我 also occurs — fail to check the condition and you wound the innocent); third, **taking an unmarked passive for an active**: for 兵败地削, rendering it as 军队打败了仗 ('the army lost the fight') will still do, but rendering 地削 as 土地削减 ('the land was reduced') loses the being-pared-away — judge the patient by the sense of the matter, and supply 被 before the verb; fourth, **missing 之 of a postposed attributive**: reading 马之千里者 as 马的千里者 ('the thousand-*li* one of the horse') in one undigested lump — 之 is the **road sign** of postposing, and only when the sign is recognised can the phrase be returned to its place (千里之马, 'a horse of a thousand *li*').
:::

::: quiz
The syntax of 沛公安在？ is (　).
- [ ] inversion of subject and predicate
- [x] fronting of an interrogative-pronoun object (在安=在哪里, 'is where')
- [ ] a nominal sentence
- [ ] postposing of a prepositional object
::: solution
The interrogative pronoun 安, as the object of 在, is fronted — an iron rule of Old Chinese.

::: exercise The operation of restoring to place {level=1}
Restore the word order and label the type: ① 何陋之有 ② 莫之能御也 ③ 客有吹洞箫者 ④ 舜发于畎亩之中 ⑤ 客何为者 ('The Banquet at Hongmen').
::: solution
① the standard-bearer that lifts the object (有何陋, 'what crudeness is there' — 之 is the road sign); ② a pronoun object in a negative sentence (莫之能御=莫能御之, 'none can withstand it'; 之 is fronted, and a modal verb is inserted in the middle — the two conditions in one frame); ③ a postposed attributive of the pattern without 之 (有吹洞箫之客, 'a guest who played the *dongxiao*' — head + a phrase in 者); ④ 于 introduces a place, and this is not a passive (舜从田间被起用, 'Shun was raised to office from the midst of the fields' — here **semantically passive, and syntactically a preposition–object complement**; in judging the 起于/发于 type, the sense of the matter and the syntax must be kept in separate accounts); ⑤ an interrogative-pronoun object (客为何=客人干什么, 'what the guest is doing'). All five cuts landing true is full marks.
:::

::: exercise Contrastive imaging {level=2}
The *Analects*: 不患人之不己知，患不知人也, 'Do not worry that others do not know you; worry that you do not know others.' (Our translation.) — in the same sentence 不己知 is fronted and 不知人 is not. Why?
::: solution
Points for the verdict: the **necessary and sufficient condition of fronting is a pronoun object** — 己 is a reflexive pronoun (a pronoun of Old Chinese, fronted along with the negative pattern); 人 is an ordinary noun (as the object of 知 it is not allowed to jump the queue). The contrast inside one sentence proves just that "fronting has conditions", and not that the ancients were being random — **the sense of rule in a language hides in a difference of this kind, inside one frame**. Full marks for pointing out the opposition of pronoun vs noun.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reading the plates of a whole passage {level=2}
Take a passage of sixty characters from 'The Banquet at Hongmen' (choose for yourself a passage containing 大王来何操 and 若属皆且为所虏) and make an inventory of sentence patterns: mark each sentence normal / nominal / passive / fronted / omitted, single out one sentence as "the most complex", and give a combined verdict.
::: solution
A sample inventory (taking as the standard the passage 大王来何操？曰：不敢复言，私见张良，具告以事…, 'What have you brought, Great King? He said: I dare not speak of it again; I saw Zhang Liang in private and told him the whole matter…' (Our translation.), with the pauses (*judou*) following the textbook): 大王来何操 (an interrogative pronoun fronted); 具告以事 (the preposition and its object postposed [the adverbial takes the back seat] + 之 omitted); 毋内诸侯 (the subject omitted + a negative of command); 若属皆且为所虏 (为所 running naked); 吾属今为之虏矣 (the version of "为 + 之 + 所 / marking the passive" with the agent filled in — with the previous sentence it makes precisely **a contrastive experiment inside the same passage**). It is reasonable to give the verdict "most complex" to 具告以事 (two disorders standing side by side). An inventory entirely correct, plus the contrast seen through, is full marks.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: the hierarchy of word order {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. Classical Chinese has "whoever matters jumps the queue" (fronting of the focus), while modern Chinese has "whoever matters stands at the end of the sentence" (end-weight and the follow-spot) — taking the sentence patterns of this lesson as your evidence, discuss whether the metaphorical relation between word order and power or ritual holds (when the idiom 唯命是从 is used in a modern official document, is that a return to antiquity, or an overstepping of station? and what posture is the passive of 被就业?). You are required to call on the general principle of this lesson and on "lens and stance" in [[modern-grammar/special-constructions]].
::: solution
Marking points: the two principles of word order are stated accurately (jumping the queue = the courtesy shown to focus in the pre-Qin period; end-weight = the modern follow-spot of information); the analysis of 唯命是从 distinguishes **continuation of a format from a revival of the grammar** (in an official document it is a fossil idiom, not a rule in operation); the conclusion is not mystical — a metaphor drawn from word order is rhetoric used in explaining, and syntactic evidence is the discipline of the thing itself (extra marks for one who dares to dismantle his own platform).

::: summary
Five plates: the nominal sentence (the mood of 者…也, plus 乃, 为 and 非; be alert to the proper duty of 是 as a pronoun); the passive sentence (five sets of uniform: 于 / 为 / 为…所… / 见 / plain clothes; the character 被 arrives late); fronted objects (interrogative pronouns, negative pronouns, 唯…是… — three channels by which focus jumps the queue; check the conditions clear before you restore); postposed attributives (the road sign 之…者, plus quantity in the back seat); omission (subject, predicate, object and preposition may all go missing; the modern Chinese translation fills in the account). The general principle, in one sentence: **Classical Chinese lines the queue up as a court assembly; modern Chinese lines the queue up as a metro** — the pre-Qin period is discipline, and after the Han it is an archaising tone of voice. The next lesson learns to recognise loan characters: the phonetic loan character (*tongjia*), ancient and modern forms of a word, and variant forms — the household-registration office for the "wrong characters" of Classical Chinese.
:::
