---
title: "Word-class shifts"
---

The lexicon of Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) is short of hands, and one word often holds a second post: today a noun stands guard as a verb, and tomorrow an adjective takes a part-time job as “使之” ("make it so"). **A word-class shift is not rhetoric; it is grammar** — recognising it, and translating it, is the watershed of reading Classical Chinese.

## Nouns crossing the boundary {#sec-mingci}

::: definition Nouns used as verbs {#def-mingdong}
Once a noun sits in the predicate position and is followed by an object, or by 之, it does the work of a verb: **衣冠** (“皆衣冠” — to put on clothes and a cap; the two nouns both take up the post. "They all put on clothes and caps." (Our translation.)), **军** (“晋军函陵” — to station an army. "Jin stations its army at Hanling." (Our translation.)), **王** (“沛公欲王关中” — to reign as king. "The Lord of Pei wishes to reign as king in Guanzhong." (Our translation.)), **目** (“范增数目项王” — to signal with the eyes. "Fan Zeng repeatedly signals King Xiang with his eyes." (Our translation.)), **坑** (“项王军新安坑之” — to bury alive. "King Xiang's army buries them alive at Xin'an." (Our translation.)). **Three checks on the criterion**: ① position (a noun after the adverbs 皆/数/欲, or after a modal verb, is almost certainly a verb); ② collocation (a noun plus an object or a complement — 补语, the element that completes the verb — as in 衣冠 and 军函陵); ③ parallel wording (衣冠 set over against 履, and 耳 set over against 目, the pairs illuminating each other). **How to translate**: supply a verb (称王, "to reign as king"; 驻军, "to station an army") or use the sense of the action directly (目 → to signal with a look of the eyes).
:::

::: definition Nouns used as adverbials {#def-zhuangyu}
A noun sits directly before the verb, not taking the main job, but only drawing a comparison or stating a place: **箕畚运于渤海之尾** (by means of 箕畚. "They transport it in baskets to the edge of the Bohai." (Our translation.)), **蚕食诸侯** (as a silkworm eats leaves. "They devour the feudal lords as a silkworm eats leaves." (Our translation.)), **云集响应** (gathering as clouds gather, answering as an echo answers — 云/响 are figurative adverbials. "They gather like clouds and answer like an echo." (Our translation.)), **夜缒而出** (in the night. "At night they let themselves down by a rope and go out." (Our translation.)), **豕人立而啼** (standing as a person stands. "The pig stands like a person and cries." (Our translation.)), **日**削**月**割 (day by day, month by month — time nouns marking gradual change. "Pared away by the day and cut away by the month." (Our translation.)). How to judge: a true verb is already in place, and a noun that then squeezes in before it is an adverbial. **Translation template: 用/像/在 + noun** ("using / like / at" + a noun). In modern Chinese only scattered fossils remain, such as 电话联系 ("to get in touch by telephone"); in Classical Chinese they are running through every street.
:::

## Verbs, adjectives and the "art of making" {#sec-shdong}

::: definition Causative, putative and the "for" use {#def-shiyi}
**Causative** (使动): the subject "makes" the object do what the predicate says, or enter that state — “生死而肉骨” (make the dead live again, and make white bones grow flesh; the *Zuo Tradition*. "Bring the dead back to life and put flesh on white bones." (Our translation.)), “项伯杀人，臣活之” (make him live. "Xiang Bo has killed a man; I make him live." (Our translation.)), “先破秦入咸阳者王之” (make him a king. "Whoever first destroys Qin and enters Xianyang, make him king." (Our translation.)), “苦其心志，劳其筋骨” (make his mind and will suffer. "Make his mind and will suffer, and make his sinews and bones toil." (Our translation.)). **Putative** (意动): the subject "considers" the object to be some way, or "takes it as" — “孔子登东山而小鲁” (considers the state of Lu small. "Confucius climbs the Eastern Mountain and considers Lu small." (Our translation.)), “吾从而师之” (takes him as a teacher. "I follow him and take him as my teacher." (Our translation.)), “侣鱼虾而友麋鹿” (takes fish and shrimp as companions, and deer as friends. "He takes fish and shrimp as companions and deer as friends." (Our translation.)), “邑人奇之” (considers him extraordinary. "The townspeople consider him extraordinary." (Our translation.)). **The "for" use** (为动): the verb is performed for the object — “死国可乎” (to die for the affairs of the state. "May one die for the state?" (Our translation.)), “等死，死国可乎” in "The Hereditary House of Chen She" ("Death is the same either way; may we die for the state?" (Our translation.)), “夫人将启之” (to open the gate for her — a passage of the *Zuo Tradition*, of the "for" kind. "The lady is about to open the gate for her." (Our translation.)). **One set of criteria tests the two verb uses**: whether one can insert 使 in the middle (使… = causative) or 认为/以…为 ("consider / take … as"; putative); those of the 死/战/急 kind, issued for the sake of the object, are the "for" use. **The causative changes the world, the putative changes how one sees, and the "for" use changes the stance.**
:::

::: example A five-in-a-row test of shifts {#ex-shan}
Judge the type of shift in each sentence, and give a way to translate it: ① “生死而肉骨” ② “渔人甚异之” ③ “常以身翼蔽沛公” ④ “假舟楫者，非能水也” ⑤ “今亡亦死，举大计亦死，等死，死国可乎”.
::: solution
① Causative (make the dead live, and make white bones flesh) — the test of supplying the character 使 checks out ✓; ② putative (considers it strange — 异 is the showpiece of an adjective in putative use); ③ a noun as adverbial (shielding as a bird's wing does — the template "像 + noun"); ④ a noun used as a verb (水 = to swim; after the modal verb 能 there must be a verb); ⑤ the "for" use (to die for the affairs of the state — 死 is a verb issued for the object). One sentence of criterion on each point is full marks: 使, 认为, 像, 能, 为 — the five test words are five keys.
:::
:::


::: definition A word-class shift is not free love {#def-bianjie}
A shift has **a roster and rules of post**: the nouns that regularly cross the boundary to serve as verbs are names of office, implements, parts of the body and directions (王, 军, 衣, 目, 面, 东); the mountain-and-water class almost never stirs from its place (“山” has no legitimate example meaning "to block the way as a mountain would"; the language of poets is a separate matter); the causative and the putative of adjectives each have a batch of high-frequency households on the register (苦, 劳, 丰, 富／奇, 异, 小, 大, 耻, 师); **"verbs" themselves are seldom shifted again** (the causative is carried by intransitive verbs and by adjectives). The final judgement on "whether it is a shift" lies in **syntactic position**: the same 衣, in “解衣衣我”, is a noun in its proper trade the first time and a shifted verb the second — one character, two posts, and position divides the spoils. Note also the traditional reading in which **the sound turns with the sense**: 王 *wáng* → *wàng* (to reign as king), 雨 *yǔ* → *yù* (down, 降 "to fall"), 衣 *yī* → *yì* (to wear). The old broken reading (*dúpò* 读破) is the aural tag of a shift. One need not force it on modern pronunciation, but a method of reading the texts must know it.
:::

::: history A third ledger, beyond "empty and full" {#sec-zhang}
Ancient philology did not set up the name "word class", yet it has working notes on the shift: when Han scholars annotated the classics, on meeting 王 they read it in the departing tone, and on meeting 衣 they read it in the departing tone, marking the second job by **pronunciation** (the tradition of 读破, the rhyme-dictionary layer of [[classical-chinese-1/reference-tools]]); the *Mashi wentong* (《马氏文通》, "Ma's Grammar", 1898) took Chinese shifts in under the Latin "transumptio" (转品), and opened a line of concept-matching (*géyì* 格义) with “名字用如动字” ("a noun used as a verb") and “形容字用如动字” ("an adjective used as a verb"); twentieth-century grammar debated the **stock** of shifts: the line of Wang Li held that a "word-class shift" is a temporary second job, while Lü Shuxiang pointed out that many "shifts" are in fact **polysemy** (the sense "to signal with the eyes" of 目 had long since entered the dictionary), and "word-class shift" against "multiple class membership" (兼类) is to this day a tug of war over the boundary in textbooks (the dispute over 拿手 in the modern Chinese grammar class is the aftershock of that quake at the surface, [[modern-grammar/parts-of-speech]]). The teaching line of this lesson is fixed thus: **wherever the "proper trade" can be found still alive at high frequency in the corpus of the same period, judge a shift; where the proper trade is dead and the second job has been made permanent, judge a sense** — the criterion is evidence of frequency, not a feeling.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of the shift
One: **see a noun and call it a verb.** “君君，臣臣，父父，子子” is the golden example of noun and verb both at their posts ("Let the ruler act as a ruler, the minister as a minister, the father as a father and the son as a son." (Our translation.)); “父不父，子不子” works in the same way ("If the father does not act as a father, the son does not act as a son." (Our translation.)) — but classes such as “山” and “江” are then forcibly assigned a job. Two: **causative and putative not kept apart.** “苦其心志” (make suffer) and “耻学于师” (take … as a shame) differ by one 使/认为 — there is no slacking on the insertion test. Three: **taking multiple class membership for a shift.** The sense "to wear" (穿) of 衣 is of high frequency in old books; "to wear" is already a sense, not a second job — the criterion looks to frequency (the final judgement in the section above). Four: **a modern Chinese translation that locks the original shut.** Rendering “侣鱼虾而友麋鹿” as “与鱼虾作伴” ("keeping company with the fish and shrimp") drops the game of viewpoint in 以…为 — **in translating the causative and the putative, keep the frame "take as" (当作).**
:::

::: exercise The insertion test {level=1}
Use 使/认为/为着/像·用 to establish the true identity of five sentences: ① 劳其筋骨 ② 邑人奇之 ③ 嫂蛇行 ④ 既泣之三日，乃誓疗之 (the *Bingmei guan ji*, 《病梅馆记》, "Record of the Sick-Plum Lodge") ⑤ 死事，义也.
::: solution
① Causative (make the sinews and bones weary); ② putative (considers him extraordinary); ③ a noun as adverbial (crawls as a snake does); ④ the "for" use (weeps for the plum blossoms — “泣之” = weeps for them; to judge it an ordinary verb taking an object is also acceptable, and setting out the two readings earns extra marks); ⑤ the "for" use (dies for the affair; translate it as 殉, "to die for"). Five judgements, each naming the word inserted, earn full marks.
:::
:::

::: exercise One word, two posts {level=2}
For each sentence below, circle the two posts, "proper trade / second job", and gloss each: ① 解衣衣我 ② 左右欲刃相如 ③ 风乎舞雩 ④ 三顾臣于草庐.
::: solution
① The first 衣 is a noun (an outer garment), and the second 衣 is a verb (to put … on someone; it also carries a tinge of the "for" use and of the causative, and translating it as "let … wear" is acceptable); ② 刃, noun → verb (to kill); ③ 风, noun → verb (to take the cool of a breeze — “风乎舞雩” = to take the breeze at the 舞雩); ④ 顾 is a verb in its proper trade (to pay a visit) — **④ is a distractor: there is no shift**, and judging it the "proper trade" is correct. Circling the posts and glossing each, both in place, is full marks.
:::
:::

::: exercise What is a shift and what is not {level=2}
Judge, and explain why it will not do: a student, writing a composition, drafts a sentence in the ancient manner, “狮吼其林，山立其前” — is “山立” acceptable? Can the 其 of “吼其林” stand in for “于林”?
::: solution
“山立” = to stand like a mountain — a noun used as an adverbial is a legitimate Classical Chinese template (“云集响应” is the same pattern), so yes; “狮吼其林” = to roar in its forest, and taking 其 as "于 + 其" is not legitimate (其 is a genitive, or a modal, and has no sense of "at"), so it should be changed to “吼于林” — **inside the list of shifts, crossing over is allowed; outside the list, a modern person's joke is what is allowed.** Giving grounds for each of "grammatical" and "ungrammatical" is full marks, and adding that "Classical Chinese also has a concession clause for the language of poets" is welcome.
:::
:::

::: exercise Short essay: is a word-class shift grammar or rhetoric? {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. “山立其前” is legitimate, while “山其” does not work; 水 may be used in “能水”, while 川 is rare — using this lesson's theory of "the list and frequency", argue your position: does a word-class shift belong to **a temporary licence of grammar**, or to **an individual's invention in rhetoric**? Requirements: invent two acceptable example sentences and two unacceptable ones of your own (with the criteria attached); cite the historical facts, given in the lesson, of the tug of war between "multiple class membership and the shift"; respond to the view that "the language of poets may be allowed more latitude".
::: solution
Marking points: the invented examples are grammatically coherent (they fit templates such as "像 + noun / 使 + adjective") and one "unacceptable" example is genuinely unacceptable (for instance, a transitive verb forced into the causative); the position cites the frequency criterion rather than a pure feel for the language; the "concession clause for the language of poets" has a boundary (when to be lenient: items set in parallel in verse; when it is not allowed: the syntax of prose). A one-sided view is docked marks for structure.
:::

::: summary
Five contracts of the shift: noun → verb (the position criterion: after a modal, after an adverb, with an object following), noun → adverbial (the templates 像/用/在), causative (changes the world: 使…), putative (changes how one sees: 认为…), the "for" use (changes the stance: 为着…); but a shift is not free love — there is a list, there is frequency, and there is a tag in the pronunciation. The tug of war between Wang Li and Lü Shuxiang over multiple class membership is a reminder: **in judging a "second job" against a "change of trade", the evidence lies in the attendance sheet of the corpus of the same period.** Next lesson, the imaging department of syntax: nominal sentences, passive sentences, fronting and postposing — four X-ray plates of word order in Classical Chinese.
:::
