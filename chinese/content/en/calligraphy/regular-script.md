---
title: "Regular script"
---

Regular script (*kaishu* 楷书; also "standard script") is the **legal tender** among the five scripts: recognising characters depends on it, copybooks write it first, examinations and door plaques supply it — and it is also the script most easily written into **tracing a printed form**. This chapter puts "true script" back into the shape it originally had: **a system of movements with a mechanics of force**, the teaching aid still the single character 永.

## The identity card of regular script {#sec-kai}

::: definition True script, orthodox script and regular script {#def-zheng}
Three names, one history: **true script / orthodox script** (*zhenshu* 真书 / *zhengshu* 正书) (at the end of the Han, as clerical script was breaking up and *zhangcao* (draft cursive) was being used for ease and speed, a new script that **dropped the swallow-tail, reduced the wave and the pressing-stroke, and set up regularity**, and that came into its own in the Wei and the Jin — Zhong You 钟繇 and the Two Wangs are named together as figures of founding rank); the name **regular script** comes from 可为楷法 ('fit to serve as a model'). **楷者，法也、式也。** *Kai* is a rule, and it is a pattern. (Our translation.) From the day it was born it has been another name for the "standard character". The crucial recognition: **regular script is not "writing carefully"; it is a script with a character-method of its own** — how the components are written, the habits of stroke order, and the square or round form of hooks and flicks all have a score (in the Tang, Yan Yuansun's 颜元孙 *Ganlu zishu* 《干禄字书》 (*Character Book for Seeking Emolument*) stratified the forms of characters in three columns, **俗, 通 and 正** ('vulgar', 'current' and 'orthodox') — **which character counted as "correct" has, in history, a ledger**). After the Tang, every dispute over how to write took the character-method of the Tang steles as its court — and within the Tang steles, the three houses of Ou, Yan and Liu are exactly three constitutions.
:::

::: definition The eight principles of 永: eight movements {#def-bafa}
**侧** (*cè*, the dot): not a press downward, but a **throw across** — the oblique is taken while the brush is still in the air, and the moment it enters the paper one presses and at once gathers it in ("**点如坠石**", 'a dot is like a falling stone': before the stone lands it has already taken on weight in mid-air); **勒** (*lè*, the horizontal): enter in reverse and go out level — the tip first pushes slightly to the left (a reverse impetus), and only then travels right; the "level" in "horizontals level, verticals straight" is "**held out level**", not "horizontal" — on the paper take an upward slope of three to five degrees, like a bit in the mouth **drawn taut**; slack and drooping = the character collapses; **努** (*nǔ*, the vertical): straight, but not stiff as a board — though the bow is drawn it still carries spring; too straight, and it goes rigid; there is a slight rise and fall of thick and thin; **趯** (*tì*, the hook): crouch the tip and store the force, then kick out until the point shows — nine-tenths of what is wrong with a hook lies in "kicking before the travelling brush has arrived"; **策** (*cè*, the rising stroke): like raising a whip; **a thick root and a sharp tip depend on acceleration**; **掠** (*lüè*, the long left-falling stroke): let the belly of the brush run long, and send the tip all the way out — do not fling a hollow tip (a *lüe* is like combing the hair; drag it bald and it goes dry); **啄** (*zhuó*, the short left-falling stroke): the beak comes down out of the air, quick and keen; **磔** (*zhé*, the right-falling stroke): press harder as you go, and come out in three beats — "*zhe*" was originally the knife that cuts the sacrificial animal; spread the hairs and travel slowly, and where the foot comes out **pause, then drive forward**. 每作一波，常三过折笔。 Whenever one makes a wave, one usually turns the brush three times. (Our translation.) This is an old formula speaking of the impetus of clerical script (the 《题卫夫人〈笔阵图〉后》, 'Postscript to Lady Wei's *Bizhen tu*' (*Diagram of the Battle Array of the Brush*), is traditionally attributed to Wang Xizhi 王羲之 — this text is itself a specimen of "legendary studies of calligraphy"; see the next chapter), borrowed to describe the three beats of the foot of the right-falling stroke: the start, the travel and the pause.
:::

::: definition Three constitutions of the Tang steles: Ou's peril, Yan's sinew, Liu's bone {#def-sanjia}
**Ouyang Xun 欧阳询, the *Jiucheng gong* 《九成宫》** (*Palace of Nine Perfections*): the rules are stern, and yet **stability is sought within the peril** — the upward tilt of the horizontals is marked, the verticals bow slightly, the central palace (*zhonggong* 中宫) is lifted, and the centre of gravity is often a thread off, and still does not fall (commonly called the **armour script** of "ranks drawn up and guards standing to order": every stroke is as if the bow had been strung); **Yan Zhenqing 颜真卿, the *Duobao ta* 《多宝塔》 and the *Magu xiantan ji* 《麻姑仙坛记》** (*Pagoda of Many Treasures*; *Record of the Immortal Altar of Magu*): **horizontals thin and verticals thick, both shoulders level and full, the central palace broad and open** — the brush-intent of seal script and *zhou* script enters regular script ("Yan's sinew": sinew is spring, not fat; **rounded vigour** is the key phrase); **Liu Gongquan 柳公权, the *Xuanmi ta bei* 《玄秘塔碑》** (*Stele of the Xuanmi Pagoda*): **lean and hard, upright and strong; every stroke breaks off, and every stroke's intent joins on** — the one sentence in which Liu Gongquan remonstrated with Emperor Xianzong of the Tang. 心正则笔正。 If the heart is upright, the brush is upright. (Our translation.) (His biography in the *Old Book of Tang*, 《旧唐书》.) That sentence welded the quality of the writing and the quality of the person fast for a thousand years; the world names the two together "Yan's sinew and Liu's bone" — **sinew is the force that is held in, bone is the force that shows itself**. In choosing a model to study: if you study Ou, guard against slickness; if you study Yan, guard against the ink pig (墨猪); if you study Liu, guard against firewood (柴) — **the illnesses of the three houses are exactly one another's antidote**.
:::

::: example The 永 clinic: the eight principles on one screen {#ex-yong}
A student writes the character 永: the dot is like a grain of rice (no falling impetus), the horizontal like a tabletop (purely level), the vertical like an electric wire (rigid and straight), the hook like a rat's tail (kicked too soon), the end of the long left-falling stroke goes white (a hollow tip), the right-falling stroke like a slide (one sweep, and it is all smooth). Give a prescription of movement stroke by stroke. The two characters 多练 ('practise more') are not to be used.
::: solution
**The dot**: the illness is **starting the stroke from the surface of the paper** — prescription: with the brush one *cun* off the paper (a Chinese inch; its length varied by period), "throw" it down, and let the belly of the brush itself smash out the triangle of the oblique tip ("falling stone" is free fall, not a pressure pump); **the horizontal**: the illness is being level from the first move of the hand — prescription: **first push half a *fen* to the left, then travel right** (a *fen* is a tenth of a *cun*), and take the whole horizontal at an upward tilt of five degrees (the test: turn the paper five degrees, and this horizontal should become "truly level"); **the vertical**: the illness is a straight drag at constant speed — prescription: enter the paper lightly, press while travelling through the middle, and **pause** as it is about to end (the "*nu*" of the bow and crossbow = drawn taut, not a railway track); **the hook**: the illness is the intent leaking away before the travel has arrived — prescription: where it comes to ground, **crouch** (the tip cocks up, then springs into a kick; the formula "**作蹲必三**", 'a crouch must be three': first crouch, second turn, third kick); **the long left-falling stroke**: the illness is the speed falling off — prescription: **send it all the way** (when the tip leaves the paper, three-tenths of the force is still there; combing the hair, the ends are not broken off); **the right-falling stroke**: the illness is a glide at constant speed — prescription: **three beats** (start — press gradually — pause — drive out; count "one, two, three" in the mouth; the *zhe* knife must hesitate a moment before it cuts through). The overall rhythm when the eight strokes are written in one run: **slow (take the reverse impetus) — fast (travel the middle) — pause (gather the close)**. The whole character 永 is one **cycle of breathing**, not eight loose ends of thread.
:::
:::

::: intuition "Level horizontals and straight verticals" are scaffolding, not the building {#int-hengping}
The beginner's formula "horizontals level, verticals straight" has harmed how many people: **it is a stabiliser for children, not the destination for a calligrapher**. Look at a Tang stele and you find: **horizontals are not level (the upward tilt is how the impetus is taken), verticals are not straight (the bow stores force), dots are not round (they land on their side)** — what is called level and straight is **the level and straight of the eye** (a balance of forces), not **the level and straight of geometry** (the level and straight of a ruler). This distinction holds for the whole layer of basic training: **every "norm" is a visual norm** — the "evenness" of the framework (*jianjia* 间架) is an evenness of weight, and the "correctness" of stroke order is a correctness of fluency. The person who takes the scaffolding for the building writes characters that are **neat yet stiff, upright yet lying flat**, which is exactly the root of the illness old teachers call the "accent of art lettering" (*meishu zi* 美术字). The right answer, in teaching: **first give the ruler (the norm of tracing), then hide the ruler (read the peril of the Tang steles), and at last throw the ruler away (the writer's balance calibrates itself)** — **three steps, and that is the distance from writing characters to calligraphy**.
:::

::: history A thousand years of regular method: from the new script to *taige* {#sec-qiannian}
**End of the Han**: the name *kai* was just sprouting; **Zhong You** (151–230) took the running endorsement-hand (*xingya* 行押) into regular script, and later ages retrospectively honoured him as "the ancestor of regular script"; **Wei and Jin**: the modern script of the Two Wangs — the small-regular (*xiaokai*) models, the *Huangting jing* 《黄庭经》 (*Classic of the Yellow Court*) and the *Yue Yi lun* 《乐毅论》 (*Essay on Yue Yi*), fixed the aesthetic standard of "orthodox script"; **Northern Dynasties**: the hands in the stele-carving workshops wrote regular script so that it came out tasting of the knife — Wei steles such as the *Zhang Menglong bei* 《张猛龙碑》 (*Stele of Zhang Menglong*), with their "斜画紧结" ('slanted strokes, tightly bound'), left an arsenal for the stele school a thousand years later; **Sui**: north and south of mixed blood; legend has it that Zhiyong 智永 (a seventh-generation descendant of Wang Xizhi) **wrote eight hundred copies of the *Zhen cao Qianzi wen* 《真草千字文》 (*Thousand-Character Essay* in true and cursive script) and distributed them among the temples** ("the tomb of worn-out brushes", 退笔冢, and "the iron threshold", 铁门限, come out of this same body of legend — the *huaben* layer, the storyteller's tales, of calligraphy history after the Tang); **Early Tang**: Ou and Chu stood side by side, and **the study of character models** (*ziyangxue* 字样学) was made an institution (the *Ganlu zishu*); **middle and late Tang**: Yan and Liu's esteem for method (*shangfa* 尚法) capped it (752, the *Duobao ta* → 841, the *Xuanmi ta* — **a statutory dose of one century**); **Song**: esteem for intent (*shangyi* 尚意) arose, and **regular script lost the first place** (Su Shi 苏轼: “我书意造本无法”. 我书意造本无法。 My writing is made from intent, and at root it has no method. (Our translation.) This is a statement about running and cursive — the method of regular script passed over into **the type of block-printed books**: the remote ancestor of Song type, *songti* 宋体, went on laying down the law in the carvers' hands); **Yuan**: Zhao Mengfu 赵孟頫 returned to the antique, back to Zhong and Wang, and **small regular script revived**; **Ming**: the small regular script of **Wen Zhengming** 文征明, and the industry of carving model-books; **Qing**: the *guange* style (馆阁体) pushed the method of regular script into an instrument of punishment for the examinations (the three-character formula "**乌、方、光**", 'black, square, glossy' — the standard, for those seeking office, of 黑大圆光, 'black, large, round and bright'; **Fu Shan 傅山's “宁拙毋巧” is a manifesto against the *guange* style**. 宁拙毋巧。 Rather clumsy than clever. (Our translation.)); **1905**: the examinations were abolished, and the *guange* style came apart; **the 1980s**: the **hard-pen craze** and fountain-pen copybooks took over the writing by which one learns characters; brush regular script retreated into the exhibition hall and the hobby class — **the status of regular script as "legal tender" was re-anchored as an "aesthetic identity card"**.
:::

::: warning Four pits in the study of regular script
**"Tracing, with tenure for life"** (trace for a year with the model underneath, and the moment you leave the model you are paralysed — **copying with the model in view, and copying from memory, are how the goods come in; tracing only checks the banknote**); **"the cult of printed type"** (taking a regular-script font as the highest standard — **a font is a cadaver specimen of the character**: the neatness comes from a machine for spacing, and the strokes have no coming and no going; to study regular script, study steles and model-books; **a font is only for looking up the character-method**); **"one house decides your whole life"** (three months in, you settle on Ou and do not change for ten years — **a beginner may keep to one house; to go further one must change garrison**: been with Ou so long that it has gone oily? Change to Yan; has Yan fattened into a pig? Change to Liu — the illnesses of the three houses are one another's antidote); **"regular script = slow script"** (to dawdle the whole way is called **tracing**, not regular script — **regular script has the speed that belongs to regular script**: send every stroke through, complete the movement, and it may be fast; the old saying “真书如站立”, 'true script is like standing' — to stand is to store the impetus of walking).
:::

::: exercise An eight-principle check-up form {level=1}
Write the four characters 永, 之, 以 and 九 (they contain the principal members of 侧, 勒, 努, 趯, 策, 掠, 啄 and 磔). Mark yourself stroke by stroke by the eight principles (for each stroke, the name of the movement, plus a degree of completion: ○ △ ×), and attach a before-and-after comparison of the "worst stroke".
::: solution
Marking: the names of the movements **must all be terms of the eight principles** (write “那一捺” and there is no mark; write “磔的三拍” and there is a mark); the self-assessment must dare to give a × (a sheet that is ○ from beginning to end is sent back to be rewritten — **a self-assessment that is too loose has the same source as dependence on tracing**); the before-and-after comparison must show a **difference of movement**, not "using more force" (using force is not the study of movement; brute force does not enter the prescription).
:::
:::

::: exercise A side-by-side review of three models {level=2}
Collect three examples each of the same character from Ou's *Jiucheng gong* 《九成宫》, Yan's *Duobao ta* 《多宝塔》 and Liu's *Xuanmi ta* 《玄秘塔》 (for instance 之, 不 and 大 — all three models have characters that occur often). Set them out in a table and compare: the upward angle of the horizontal, the degree to which the vertical bows, how tight or loose the central palace is, and the crouching impetus of the hook. Write 150 characters of a "brief comparison of the three constitutions".
::: solution
Marking: the table is complete in all four items and uses **the language of measurement** (“Ou's horizontal is about five degrees; Yan's is about level and full” — **an estimate by eye still counts as measurement, so long as it carries a unit**); the summary can hand down a one-sentence verdict, such as "Ou is like guards standing to order, Yan like the hall of state, Liu like a remonstrating official"; extra mark: discovering that the style drifts from page to page within the same stele (**the fingerprints of the carver and of the recut** — reading the model through to the layer of the artefact).
:::
:::

::: exercise A short essay: law and life {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters: "every standard will turn into a *guange* style" — taking the regular-script method of the Tang, the *guange* style of the Qing, and today's formulae for the school composition or the typefaces in a font as your examples, argue the rhythm of **legislation — ossification — reaction — legislation again**; and answer this: a person who is still learning the rules — how does one draw in advance the vaccine of "breaking the rules", and not fall into "no rules"?
::: solution
Points for marking: the argument for the rhythm has **concrete named pieces** (the *guange* formula 乌方光, 'black, square, glossy'; Fu Shan's 宁拙毋巧, 'rather clumsy than clever' — a manifesto of reaction must come with its text attached); a scheme for drawing the vaccine in advance must be one that can be carried out, for example **copying two or more houses so that each corrects the other's bias, comparing the original stele with the printed copy, and using “背临” ('copying from memory') to test whether the rules have grown on one's own body**; the conclusion does not sit on the fence (a conservative conclusion is allowed, but it must be paid for with argument — **write out the price paid for “不似之似”**, 'a likeness in what is not alike'); an empty shout of "innovation" scores zero.
:::
:::

::: summary
The court of regular script closes: the name and its station (the ledger of vulgar, current and orthodox); movement (the eight principles = eight breaths; level and straight belong to the eye, not to the ruler); precedents (Ou, Yan and Liu rescue one another; the illness of each of the three houses is the other's medicine); the sentence (every standard will turn into a *guange* style — **to oppose the *guange* style is not to refuse to make law; it is to let the law keep the warmth of a body**). Once you can breathe while standing, the next chapter learns to walk: running script (semi-cursive) and cursive script (also "grass script") — “楷如立、行如走、草如奔”, 'regular is like standing, running like walking, cursive like running', and the most deeply misunderstood “龙飞凤舞”, 'dragons flying and phoenixes dancing'.
:::
