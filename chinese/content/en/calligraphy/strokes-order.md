---
title: "Strokes and stroke order"
---

A character is building blocks, a stroke is a part, and stroke order is the assembly manual — it sounds like primary school, yet adults crash all the same: **Can you write the character 必? What is the second stroke of 为?** This chapter issues that 'manual' into your hands again, and along the way makes clear on what grounds it deserves to be followed.

## Eight kinds of part, and their martial-art names {#sec-bihua}

::: definition Basic strokes and the Eight Principles of 永 {#def-bafa}
Modern standards group strokes chiefly as five classes, **horizontal, vertical, left-falling, right-falling and dot**, plus derivatives such as the turn, the hook and the rise (when a dictionary counts, **each turning stroke counts as one stroke** — for characters such as 鼎, 凹 and 凸, which stroke the count has reached is settled by the standard table; this lesson only lays down the rule, and does not have you memorise a table of stroke counts). The **Eight Principles of 永** are a thousand-year-old teaching aid for the parts: **侧** (*cè*; the dot — like a stone falling at a slant), **勒** (*lè*; the horizontal — as if reining in a startled horse, with strength in the opening and the close), **努** (*nǔ*; the vertical — a bow, though straight, still carries spring), **趯** (*tì*; the hook — kick! the deeper you squat, the higher you jump), **策** (*cè*; the rise — like a horsewhip sweeping at a slant), **掠** (*lǜè*; the long left-falling — combing long hair; it must not be yanked bald), **啄** (*zhuó*; the short left-falling — a bird pecking at food, quick and sharp), **磔** (*zhé*; the right-falling — spread the hairs and travel gradually; let go, and then hold back) — eight verbs that write the **way of delivering force** of eight kinds of stroke as a martial formula: **a stroke is not a shape; it is a stretch of movement pressed into the paper**.
:::

::: definition General rules of stroke order, and the exceptions {#def-zongze}
In 2020 the Ministry of Education issued 《通用规范汉字笔顺规范》 ('Stroke-Order Standard for Commonly Used Standard Chinese Characters'); the rules in general use are gathered as the **general rules**: (1) horizontal before vertical (十); (2) left-falling before right-falling (人); (3) from top to bottom (三); (4) from left to right (明); (5) outside first, then inside, then close the frame (日 and 田 — **go in first, then shut the door**); (6) the middle first, then the two sides (小 and 水); (7) the households that moved over to inside-before-outside (辶 comes last among its three strokes — for 进, write 井 first, then drag the level right-falling stroke; for 建, 聿 first, then the base of 建). The exceptions all hide in high-frequency characters (see the example) — **a standard is not an unalterable truth, but one act of unified negotiation**: variant orders of the same character have existed since ancient times (the old textbook order and the current edition of 方 and of 火 have both come to blows), and what the teaching standard adopts is the three-advantages principle of '**writing fluently, recognising clearly, and being able to find it in a dictionary**'.
:::

::: example The crash site of common characters {#ex-fanche}
Write the stroke order from memory and check yourself against the standard: 方, 为, 长, 火, 心, 出, 九, 瓦. Point out the three on which you are most likely to crash, and state the type of cause.
::: solution
The standard order: **方** (dot — horizontal — horizontal-turning hook — left-falling; cause of the error: horizontal before the dot, treating 方 as 于 plus 丿); **为** (dot — left-falling — horizontal-turning hook — dot; cause of the error: splitting the horizontal-turning hook into two strokes, or making the last dot too early); **长** (left-falling — horizontal — vertical-rising — right-falling; cause of the error: horizontal before the left-falling); **火** (dot — left-falling — left-falling — right-falling; cause of the error: writing the 人 in the middle first — **a leftover of the old taught order**); **心** (dot — lying hook — dot — dot; cause of the error: the lying hook first, treating the dots as patches); **出** (vertical-turning — vertical — vertical — vertical-turning — vertical; cause of the error: writing it as two 山, upper and lower — **treating stroke order as a jigsaw**); **九** (two strokes: left-falling — horizontal-turning curved hook; cause of the error: writing the turning curved hook first and then supplying the left-falling — taken hostage by 'horizontal before vertical'); **瓦** (four strokes: horizontal — vertical-rising — horizontal-turning curved hook — dot; cause of the error: the inner dot brought forward). **A typology of the causes of error**: (1) being misled by lookalike components (piecing the character together as building blocks); (2) a leftover of the old order (the textbook changes its edition, the teacher changes with the generation); (3) misuse of the general rules (forcing 'horizontal before vertical' onto an exception). How to check yourself: **work backwards from the stroke counts in a dictionary's radical index** + check the table in 《笔顺规范》 — **characters such as 必, 凹 and 凸, on which even teachers crash: do not argue, check the table**.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why stroke order is worth following {#int-zhide}
Three practical reasons and one aesthetic reason: **fluency** — stroke order is a convenient route designed for '**continuity of brush momentum**' (*bǐshì* 笔势); a disordered order = walking with the arm and the leg on the same side, and a fountain pen writing fast is the first to sound the alarm (the linking threads of running script (semi-cursive), *qiānsī* 牵丝, are generated almost entirely along the standard order; see [[calligraphy/running-cursive]]); **clarity** — the correct stroke order lets the shape of the character stand in the right frame (write 小, whose middle comes first, from a side first, and the centre of gravity leans); **instrumentality** — looking characters up by strokes, stroke-based input, and the setting of stroke-order questions (the banks of real questions in Chinese examinations) all eat from this bowl; **aesthetics** — stroke order determines **the order in which the opening and the closing of a stroke make their entrance**, and thereby determines the linking threads, the echoing between strokes (*yìngdài* 映带) and the pulse of *qi* (*qìmài* 气脉) — **one and the same character 川: a different order, a different final path through the air, and the character's complexion is different**. In one sentence: **stroke order is the temporal dimension of a character; calligraphy is only the art of leaving a trace of that stretch of time**.
:::

::: history Stroke order: from elementary learning to the national standard {#sec-guobiao}
**Zhou and Qin**: the literacy primers *Shizhou pian* 《史籀篇》 ('Chapters of Shi Zhou') and *Cangjie pian* 《仓颉篇》 ('Chapters of Cangjie') — **an awareness of stroke order sprouts in 'teaching how to write'**; **Han and Wei**: the stone classics of Cai Yong 蔡邕, character books coming out one after another, and oral instruction, master to pupil, in 'how to set the brush down'; **Tang and Song**: character books for elementary learning and treatises on calligraphy flourish side by side, and **the 'Eight Principles of 永', circulated under the name of Zhiyong 智永**, are handed on and practised (Zhang Huaiguan 张怀瓘 of the Tang gives a systematic record in *Yutang jinjing* 《玉堂禁经》 ('The Prohibited Classic of the Jade Hall') — the eight methods are upgraded from a formula to a curriculum); **Yuan and Ming**: Mei Yingzuo 梅膺祚 of the Ming, in *Zihui* 《字汇》 ('Compendium of Characters'), gathers Chinese characters under two hundred and fourteen radicals, and looking them up accordingly becomes a matter of strokes; **Qing**: *Zhengzitong* 《正字通》 ('A Comprehensive Account of Correct Characters') and the demand for small regular script in the civil-service examinations bind 'order and correctness' to official honours; **the Republic**: the discussion of a 'standard character form' within the movement to unify *guóyǔ* 国语 (the national language); **from 1956**: New China's continuous sorting-out of character forms and stroke order (《汉字简化方案》 ('Scheme for Simplifying Chinese Characters') and 《印刷通用汉字字形表》 ('Table of Forms of Commonly Used Chinese Characters for Printing') — **stroke order becomes a project of national education for the first time**); **1997** publication of 《现代汉语通用字笔顺规范》 ('Stroke-Order Standard for Commonly Used Characters in Modern Chinese') (to go with the need for ordering by strokes in the Unicode internal-code character set — **digitisation forces standardisation by reverse pressure**); **2013** 《通用规范汉字表》 ('Table of Commonly Used Standard Chinese Characters'); **2020** issue of 《通用规范汉字笔顺规范》 ('Stroke-Order Standard for Commonly Used Standard Chinese Characters') — **a two-thousand-year deliberation, from the private accomplishment of elementary learning to a national standard**.
:::

::: warning Three disputes over stroke order: do not dispute them
One, **arguing with the ancients**: the stroke order of celebrated traces on steles and in model-books often does not agree with today's standard (a master's movement of the brush has its own line of transmission, and its own logic of writing fast) — **the standard governs teaching and publishing; it does not cross-examine the ancients**; two, **arguing with the teacher**: textbook editions differ across the generations (what is said of the second stroke of 上, and of the upper part of 脊, has in each case been changed) — **check the table now in force, not your memory**; three, **arguing with the input method**: stroke-input methods tolerate errors according to the old code tables (the two strokes of 儿, the opening stroke of 乃) — the tool is lenient, and the examination is not — **in the end 《笔顺规范》 is the sole referee**.
:::

::: exercise Taking characters apart by stroke order {level=1}
For 好, 明, 问, 雨 and 九, write the standard stroke order character by character and count the strokes; then check one another in pairs, and record the two places that most surprised the other person.
::: solution
For reference: 好 (6 strokes: left-falling dot — left-falling — rise — horizontal left-falling — curved hook — dot; the '撇点' of the 女 side-component is one stroke); 明 (8 strokes: 日 first, then 月); 问 (6 strokes: 门 first, then 口 — a half-enclosure that is not closed); 雨 (8 strokes: horizontal — vertical — horizontal-turning hook — vertical — four dots; **the four dots are not dotted at random**: the two on the left from the inside outwards, and the two on the right in the same way); 九 (2 strokes). Wherever this conflicts with your own memory, mark 'confirm against the table'. The record of the mutual check is written in two columns, 'what I thought — what the standard says'; the more surprising the error, the more firmly it is remembered.
:::
:::

::: exercise Little teacher {level=2}
Teach a child in the lower years (or a colleague) to write the three characters 必, 出 and 片: first confirm them yourself against the table, then record a spoken lesson of 60 seconds. Self-assessment: did you turn the 'why' into 'that is just how the teacher taught it'?
::: solution
Marking: the stroke order of the three characters agrees completely with the table now in force (必 and 片 are precisely the kings of the crash; the rate of checking the table is 100%); the spoken lesson contains **at least one reason** (for example why 出 cannot be treated as two mountains — a vertical-turning stroke written continuously is one stroke; **a stroke is a continuous movement, not an inventory of shapes**); the self-assessment is honest: whenever 'that is just how the teacher taught it' appears, record one case of the **contagion of authority** — the meta-aim of this lesson is to make you **keep a sense of stroke order about whatever slips out too smoothly**.
:::
:::

::: exercise A short essay: where standards come from {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters: taking the dispute over versions of the stroke order of 火 / 方 / 为 as your example, discuss the nature of a 'standard of language and script' — why it cannot be settled once and for all by logic or by tradition, and can be maintained only by **negotiation + a cycle of revision**. Draw an analogy with one modern standard you know well (word frequency in a Pinyin input method, simplified characters, date formats, the error-tolerance rate of a QR code).
::: solution
Points for marking: the historical facts about the versions of the stroke order are accurate (old and new textbooks do indeed differ; it is enough to make the direction clear, and the year is not strictly required); the three properties of a standard are brought out: **purposiveness** (the three-advantages principle), **time-boundedness** (version management, and the reverse pressure from Unicode and from lookup), **limited authority** (it governs publishing and teaching; it cross-examines neither the ancients nor calligraphers); the analogy lines up well (for example, word frequency in an input method = a living stroke-order table, revised every day as use casts its votes); if the conclusion is '**the life of a standard is revision, not eternity**', that is an A.
:::
:::

::: summary
The parts workshop settles its accounts: five main classes of stroke and the derivatives (a turn is one stroke), the Eight Principles of 永 (eight verbs = eight ways of delivering force), seven general rules + the list of exceptions (crashes run high at 方, 为, 长, 火, 心, 出, 九 and 瓦), four reasons for following the order (fluency, clarity, the tool, complexion). **Disputes stop at checking the table; authority stops at negotiation** — this manual is not sacred, but it is the one copy shared by 1.2 billion people. The next chapter takes up structure: the framework (*jiànjià* 间架) — the mechanics of assembling the parts into a house.
:::
