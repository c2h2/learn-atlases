title: "Writing with a pen"
---

After the brush withdrew into the exhibition hall, **the fountain pen took over the everyday persona of the Chinese character** — the signature on a contract, the notes an interviewer glances over, the message on a parent's return slip. This chapter teaches the hard pen as a craft in its own right: it does not ask you to become a calligrapher. It asks only that you be **fast, clear, and good-looking as well**.

## The engineering of hand and pen {#sec-gongcheng}

::: definition Holding the pen, and posture {#def-zhibi}
Three sentences for holding a hard pen: **pinch low** (about one *cùn* from the nib — the nearer the fulcrum, the finer the control), **rest it steady** (the barrel leans against the tiger's mouth, *hǔkǒu* 虎口, the web of the thumb, and makes about 50–55° with the paper — the nib 'bites' the paper and does not skid), **hollow palm, firm fingers** (the palm can hold an egg; the fingers take the strain, and the wrist is not locked). Unlike the brush, with its **shaft vertical and its movement chiefly from the wrist**, the fountain pen's system for small characters is a **mixed transmission of fingers and wrist**: under one *cun*, for a small character, the fingers move much and the wrist little; above one and a half *cun*, the fingers do not move and the wrist does — **the size of the character decides the gear the engine is in**. The posture set of three: body upright, paper aslant (turning the sheet 15–30° into place is cheaper than twisting the wrist), shoulders loose — **the bill for a trembling hand is often addressed to the shoulder**.
:::

::: definition The writing system: from nib to paper {#def-xitong}
Three physical elements of writing with a fountain pen: the **nib** (0.5–0.7 mm is the most honest size for practice — too fine, and it scrapes the paper and exposes everything; too thick, and it blurs the tip of the stroke), **damping** (a fountain pen does not, as a brush does, give the elastic feedback of 'press and lift to adjust the tip'; **a hard pen's thick and thin are simulated entirely by the speed of the stroke and by pause and stress** — travel fast and the line is thin, pause and the line is thick; the 'pause-stroke' is the hard pen's only pedal for a change of tone), **paper** (ink that bleeds ruins the keen edge; slick paper skids and ruins control. Why are examination answer sheets hard to write on? **The damping of coated paper changes all at once, and the whole muscular account of ordinary practice is written off** — before any important piece of writing, try two lines first on the same batch of paper). The 'start — travel — finish' of a hard-pen stroke is **drawn with a curve of speed**, not drawn with the hairs of a brush — and that is the **exchange-rate problem** when the hard pen borrows method from the brush.
:::

::: definition Speeding up in running-regular script: three ways of linking {#def-sanfa}
The adult's battlefield is **running-regular script** (*xíngkǎi* 行楷: the character-forms of regular script, also called standard script, plus the rhythm of running script, or semi-cursive) — three legitimate keys to going faster without losing the shape of the character. **① The linking thread** (*qiānsī* 牵丝: the fine thread drawn out naturally by standard stroke order — **stroke order is the roadbed of the linking thread**; the debt of the previous lesson is collected on this stroke. Iron rule: the thread must be fine and short, and within one horizontal and one vertical **at most one**; a page of cobwebs = the gluttony of linked strokes). **② Borrowing a stroke** (two strokes share one stretch of path: the horizontal-turn and the final horizontal of 口 merge so that the character is two strokes; the left-falling and right-falling strokes of 未 link in one sweep, borrowing the momentum — **what borrowing a stroke saves is time, not the number of strokes**: someone else, following the standard order, can still recognise it). **③ Omitting a stroke** (running script omits and merges, as when the four dots under 点 become one horizontal — **you must look up the character-forms of running script before they go on duty**; a privately invented simplified character = inventing a cipher for the whole workplace). The common red line of the three: **a single character may be cursive (grass script); the piece as a whole must be readable** — what the hard pen is assessed on is the passing on of information, not artistic wilfulness.
:::

::: example Emergency clinic for meeting minutes {#ex-huiyi}
Chief complaint: a certain clerk's characters — 'every one of them is recognisable; strung together, they are not'. His own account: 'the moment I go fast, it falls apart; the moment I go slow, it is a mess (mess = I still cannot go fast)'. Give a four-week prescription, plus the criteria for acceptance.
::: solution
Diagnosis: it is not that the hand cannot get up to speed. It is that **there is no 'system for writing fast'** — slow writing relies on strokes, fast writing on the inertia of components; every time, he is improvising on the spot. Prescription: **Week 1**, practise only the habitual running-regular forms of thirty high-frequency components (the fixed linked forms of '氵讠纟衤木口土日' — **fast = familiar blocks, not a flying hand**); **Week 2**, a permit before a linking thread is admitted (at most one thread to a character; practise 'three and one': three characters written linked may have a linking thread in only one place); **Week 3**, timed gears — copy the same passage once at each of the three gears, 60/80/100 characters a minute, **assessing readability yourself and having someone else assess the feel of the writing** (only when both clear the line do you move up a gear); **Week 4**, a real setting, live (use that day's meeting minutes on the spot; circle every character that 'even you yourself do not recognise', and the next day it goes into the extra-coaching notebook). Acceptance at four weeks: **80 characters a minute, and a third person reads your notes aloud with not a single stumble**. The line to remember: **the secret of 'fast, but not a mess' is not on the speed key; it is in the block memory of characters** — a pianist plays fast by the feel of a chord in the hand; you, by components.
:::
:::

::: intuition The dignity of the hard pen: seeing the writing is seeing the face {#int-zunyan}
Why, in the age of the keyboard, still practise writing? An answer in three layers. The **utilitarian layer** (signatures, annotations, handwritten return slips — **handwriting = proof kept in a personal form**; your characters are the self-introduction that strangers read most often in a year). The **cognitive layer** (speed of writing ≈ the breathing rate of thought — **handwriting that is fast and readable is an external plug-in for thinking**: research on notes shows that handwriting keywords promotes rehearsal more than typing does; ugly characters do not lose marks, **muddled sentence division does**). The **aesthetic layer** (the hard pen is small, but one may still speak of structure, rhythm and the arrangement of white space — one sheet of good-looking hard-pen writing is **a history of calligraphy you carry on you**). Two red lines in the ethics of practice: **designing a signature is not the same as defeating recognition** (forgery is prevented by a **combination of features**, not by mess; you may ask someone to keep a specimen on file); **prettiness cannot stand in for laziness** (if the characters on a handwritten interview form are smoke and cloud, empty content is still out — **the character is the messenger, not the letter**).
:::

::: history Two hundred years of the hard pen in China {#sec-ruhua}
**Late Ming and early Qing**: the fountain pen gradually came in, as tribute and as goods ('自来墨水笔', 'the pen that brings its own ink', is found in the **archives of the Kangxi reign**); **the Tongzhi and Guangxu years**: the counting-houses of foreign firms in the concessions trained the first cohort of hard-pen users; **the 1920s–30s**: a domestic fountain-pen industry got under way (Minsheng and other brands) in parallel with **education to spread the fountain pen**, and the copybook market switched over from the brush; **1935**: the Ministry of Education of the Nationalist Government promoted **standard running script** (the first time a norm for hard-pen characters was finalised by the state — '破体', 'broken forms', entered official schooling); **the 1950s–60s**: the Shanghai fountain-pen trade was merged into **Xingfu** and other factories, and '英雄' ('Hero') and '钢笔' ('fountain pen') entered the vocabulary of the whole people; **the 1980s**: **hard-pen calligraphy** broke out as a movement — pen gatherings in the newspapers, great national exhibitions, the age of the three giants of the fountain-pen copybook — and the China Calligraphers Association also set up a **Hard-pen Calligraphy Committee** (the mid-1980s) — the first time the hard pen was registered as **a category of art**; **from the 1990s**: the number of people who write by hand shrank sharply under the keyboard, and **the demand for practice turned from the job to self-cultivation**; **2013**: the *Guidelines for Calligraphy Education in Primary and Secondary Schools* put calligraphy classes back on the timetable; Chinese classes in Years 3 to 6 are given a calligraphy period each week, with **hard pen and brush side by side** (the detail of the periods is as the original text of the Guidelines has it) — **the state's last finger is still pressing on the barrel**.
:::

::: warning Four ailments of the adult hard pen
**Addiction to tracing** (trace a thousand times with the copybook underneath, and the moment you leave the copybook you are knocked back to your original form — **red tracing is a teaching aid for small children; copying a model has to be 'copying with the model in view + copying from memory'** (*duìlín* 对临 and *bèilín* 背临): look, then close the book and write from memory, and only that is the stock coming in); **horizontals sloping at fifteen degrees** (a whole line of characters topples to the right as one body — the root of the illness is **the paper not turning while the wrist does**, together with vertical strokes that have no vertical to refer to: lay squared paper underneath and set up ten 'telegraph poles'); **a stroke with no pause or stress** (the same thickness all the way, like iron wire — a hard pen may do without training the start and the finish, **but the pause is the hard pen's breathing**: at the opening of the main stroke, pause half a step, and the spirit of the whole character doubles); **privately invented simplification** (廊 written as a frame with three left-falling strokes inside it, 道 reduced until only a shape is left — **the omissions and mergers of running script have their dictionaries** (a tool of the *Dictionary of Running Script* kind, and one lookup tells you); invent your own, and only your mum recognises you — signatures excepted, and her recognition has no legal effect).
:::

::: exercise Special training on thirty components {level=1}
Choose for yourself thirty high-frequency side components and components (including 氵, 讠, 纟, 衤, 木, 口, 土, 日 and others), and for each of them design **one habitual running-regular form** (by the double standard 'others can make it out + you yourself can go fast'), and make them into a card you carry. A week later, use them in earnest in five groups of common characters (for example, write three lines each of 江, 记, 绿, 被, 林, and so on).
::: solution
Marking: every form has a source (**look it up in a dictionary of running script, or in a copybook, and there is a prototype** — a private invention is a one-vote veto); the self-assessment on the double standard comes with a **blind test** (ask two people to read aloud, against the clock, a letter of yours in twenty characters; one point for each place they recognise wrongly; under three points is a pass); the front of the card is the form, the back is the prototype and its source — only speed whose origin can be looked up is a legal way to go faster.
:::
:::

::: exercise A one-minute interview {level=2}
Classmates test one another: copy the same passage under a limit of one minute (eighty to one hundred characters), then swap and mark. ① Characters per minute. ② Circle and note defects of readability. ③ Guess the three strokes the other person has worked hardest on, and the three loosest. Two rounds, taking turns, and record the curve of progress.
::: solution
Marking: the record of the data is complete (speed, number of defects, the guesses); relative to the first round, the second **does not fall in readability while the speed rises**, and that is a pass (only having got a good deal faster, while not a few people can read it, = a half pass, and back into the furnace for the Week 2 prescription); whoever gets every one of 'guess the three loosest strokes' right is awarded the title **Hard-pen Ophthalmology** — the standard at the end of this lesson: **looking at other people's characters is like looking in a mirror; looking at your own is like a post-mortem**.
:::
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the last hundred years of handwriting {level=3}
Write six hundred to one thousand characters. From '见字如面' ('seeing the characters is like seeing the face') to handwriting '退居礼仪性场景' ('withdrawing into settings of ceremony') — taking any one of the family letter, the signature, the annotation and the note as your example, argue 'which functions of handwriting the replacement of writing technologies eliminates, and which it keeps', and answer: **is the part a keyboard cannot replace worth ten minutes of practice a day?**
::: solution
Points for marking: the list of functions is concrete (proof / ceremony / a plug-in for memory / density of feeling — as in 'the weight of a letter in one's own hand comes from its **hesitation and pen-pressure, which cannot be copied**'); the argument about technologies replacing one another has evidence (the telegram eliminated the layer of information, email eliminated the layer of ceremony, the handwritten signature survives by regulation — **each function has a history of life and death of its own**); the conclusion may go in any direction, but it must **pay the cost of argument** — anyone who writes 'practising handwriting is simply cultivating the mind' and describes no mechanism (attention? feedback? an aesthetic of the muscles?) loses marks for arguing empty-handed.
:::
:::

::: summary
Closing the books of the hard-pen workshop: transmission (fingers and wrist changing gear); the medium (speed standing in for the brush hairs, and the economics of the paper's damping); the three ways of speeding up (the roadbed of the linking thread is stroke order; borrowing a stroke does not save strokes; omitting a stroke means checking a dictionary first); the four-week prescription (fast = the block memory of components); two ethical lines (readability first; a signature is not a basis for a crime). The fountain pen is the brush's **commoner cousin** — the next chapter pays a formal call at the brush's door: first learn to recognise all four treasures, then wash off the four characters '不敢下笔' ('not daring to put brush to paper').
:::
