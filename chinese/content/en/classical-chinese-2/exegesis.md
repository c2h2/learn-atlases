---
title: "Traditional exegesis"
---

A modern reader of old books **reads them bare**. An ancient reader **read them with a whole building attached**: under the text of the classic there is a *zhuan*, under the *zhuan* a *zhu*, under the *zhu* a *shu*, and beyond the *shu* there are also the *jijie*, the *zhengyi*, the *zhangju*, the *yinyi* and the *shuzheng* — an **apartment of interpretation** that has been lived in for two thousand years. This chapter teaches you to read the meters: what trade each kind of annotation works at, by what rules it does the work, and how they fight one another.

## A catalogue of layouts {#def-huxing}

::: definition The trades of nine kinds of annotation {#def-gongzhong}
**The *zhuàn* 传** (the two earliest forms: exegesis handed on — the *Mao Shi gu xun zhuan* 毛诗故训传 ('Mao's tradition of the old glosses on the *Songs*'); **the unfolding of historical events** — the three *zhuan* of the *Spring and Autumn*: the *Zuo Tradition* **attaches events to the classic** and explains the classic by historical fact, while the *Gongyang* and the *Guliang* explain the classic by norms and precedents (义例) — **one and the same classic opened two production lines, "history" and "precedent"**); **the *zhù* 注** ("*zhu*" is like water poured in, so that the meaning of the text runs through — **the definition of *zhu* comes with its own hydraulic works**; Zheng Xuan's 郑玄 commentaries, drawing on both New Text and Old Text, are the benchmark of the trade); **the *jiān* 笺** (Zheng Xuan's *Mao Shi jian* 毛诗笺: taking the Mao *zhuan* as the base, **supplementing where it is not yet clear, and marking the small points of difference** — **annotating a predecessor's commentary: where you agree, add light; where you differ, draw a line**); **the *shū* 疏 / *zhèngyì* 正义** (a commentary on the commentary: it explains both the classic and the commentary — **the Tang trade rule “疏不破注” ('a subcommentary does not break the commentary'): the child note may not overturn the parent note; you wrap the screw and give it one more turn**); **the *jíjiě* 集解** (He Yan's 何晏 *Lunyu jijie* 论语集解 **gathers the good explanations of the various schools** — **the genre of minutes of a meeting: the commentator himself does not appear**; Huang Kan's 皇侃 *Yishu* 义疏 of the Southern Dynasties and Xing Bing's 邢昺 *Shu* of the Song are stacked on, layer by layer); **the *zhāngjù* 章句** (**separating chapters and distinguishing sentences**: dividing into chapters, marking the pauses, and adding a running explanation — a book for beginners to read aloud; Zhao Qi's 赵岐 *Mengzi zhangju* 孟子章句 preserves the scene of Han *judou*); **the *yīnyì* 音义** (“读如” 'read as', “如字” 'as the character itself' — marking the sound of characters with more than one reading and more than one sense; Lu Deming's 陆德明 *Jingdian shiwen* 经典释文 ('Textual explanations of the classics'): **one strike of the pronunciation, and the sense is settled — a note on sound is the exegesis of lowest cost**); **the *jízhù* 集注** (Zhu Xi's 朱熹 *Sishu zhangju jizhu* 四书章句集注 ('The Four Books, in chapter and verse, with collected commentaries'): **the anthologist enters the commentary, and meaning and principle (*yìlǐ* 义理) is the guiding thread** — it changes from "preserving the various explanations" to "adjudicating the various explanations"); **the *shūzhèng* 疏证 / new *shu*** (one Qing form: **commentaries and subcommentaries plus evidential checking** — Dai Zhen's 戴震 *Mengzi ziyi shuzheng* 孟子字义疏证 strikes at meaning and principle by way of explaining characters; Liu Baonan's 刘宝楠 *Lunyu zhengyi* 论语正义 replaces the old subcommentary with a new one: **“疏不破注” is changed into “疏必核注” ('a subcommentary must check the commentary') — one volume of a new subcommentary = one structural overhaul of the whole building**).
:::

::: definition Three methods of exegesis, and the commentator's discipline {#def-sanfa}
**Explanation by form** (*xíngxùn* 形训: stating the original meaning from the shape of the character — of the kind of the *Shuowen jiezi* (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*), “目，人眼，近目象形” ('目 is the human eye; approximating an eye, it is a pictograph') (Our translation.); **the premise is that this character's shape has not yet lied to you**: some of the explanations have been corrected by the oracle-bone and bronze inscriptions — for the case of the character 为, see [[classical-chinese-2/original-meanings]]); **explanation by sound** (*shēngxùn* 声训: two keys and two doors: the upward one is “**读为、读曰**” ('read as', 'read as saying'), which breaks phonetic loans (*tongjia*) — a science that can be checked; the downward one is “**之言、之为言**” ('the word for', 'what is spoken of as'), which infers an etymology — folk etymology of the type “天，颠也” ('天 is the crown of the head') (Our translation.) — the same technique, half a scalpel and half a fortune-teller's board); **explanation by sense** (*yìxùn* 义训: borrowing neither form nor sound, and giving a definition straight out: the commentaries and subcommentaries are full of pages of “**某，某也**” ('X is Y'); it includes one consummate skill, “**反训**” (*fǎnxùn*, contrary glossing) — **the one character 故 is glossed both as 古 ('ancient') and as 今 ('now'); 乱 is glossed also as 治 ('to govern'): one word, two opposites, the thorniest troublemaker in explanation by sense**). **Three disciplines for the commentator**: **do not alter characters** (is the base text corrupt? Keep a collation note in the commentary, and do not privately change the text of the classic — **collation stays with collation, annotation stays with annotation**); **do not write in its stead** (“疏不破注” — **a contract between storeys: you may hold it in, but you may not overturn the storey you depend on in order to be shelved**); **do not pretend to understand** (“**君子于其所不知，盖阙如也**” ('The gentleman, as to what he does not know, in general leaves a blank') (Our translation.) — **every “未详” ('not yet clear'), “义未闻” ('the meaning has not been heard') and “或曰” ('some say') in a commentary is a record of conscience**).
:::

::: example Dissecting one room: ‘学而时习之’ {#ex-xueer}
The opening chapter of the *Analects*, 'Xue er': “学而时习之，不亦说乎” ('To learn, and in due time to practise it — is that not a delight?') (Our translation.) — please take one piece of material from each of the five floor-slabs, and mark the trade: ① the Han explanation quoted in He Yan's *Jijie*; ② Huang Kan's *Yishu* / Xing Bing's *Shu*; ③ Zhu Xi's *Jizhu*; ④ Liu Baonan's *Zhengyi*; ⑤ a modern translation with commentary.
::: solution
**A storey-by-storey record (the gist, retold from the principal books)**: **① He Yan's *Jijie***: Ma Rong 马融 says “**时者，学者以时诵习之**” ('As for 时, the learner recites and practises it according to the time') (Our translation.) — **the *jijie* form: quote one school's words, and they enter the volume**; 时 = at the set times (**a timetable reading: recitation and practice follow the hours**); **② Xing Bing's *Shu***: beneath the commentary it adds “**正义曰**” ('The correct meaning says'), strings the text through sentence by sentence, supplies the characters and explains the clauses — **the trade of the *shu*: to set the commentary flowing again**; **③ Zhu Xi's *Jizhu***: “**学之为言效也**” ('学, as a word, means to imitate') (Our translation.) (explanation by sound reports for duty), and 时 is **re-glossed as “时时” ('time and again')** — **from attending class at the appointed time to reviewing at any moment: the system of education changed, and the exegesis moved house with it**; **④ Liu Baonan's *Zhengyi***: it combs out the Han Confucians' school method of “诵习” ('reciting and practising'), and **puts it back in the pot to prove, against the Song, that the claim of “时时” is without basis — Qing scholars use Han commentaries to strike Song commentaries; every storey uses an older authority to press down the opponent of its own day**; **⑤ modern translations with commentary**: most follow Master Zhu's “时时实习” ('practising it time and again') and generalise the sense — **the textbook is the last storey of the building, and also the thinnest (in the footnotes one can see the lift shaft)**. The final account of the storeys: **the same sentence of twelve characters has housed, in succession, three generations of tenants, "attending class at the appointed time — reviewing at any time — the awakening of mind and nature"; every generation claims that the title deed bears its own name.**
:::

::: intuition Commentaries and subcommentaries fold time {#int-zhedie}
The hidden pleasure of reading commentaries and subcommentaries: **four centuries live at once on the same page** — the classic is pre-Qin, the commentary is Han and Wei, the subcommentary is Tang and Song, the *jizhu* is Southern Song, the *shuzheng* is Qing. One sentence, “三十而立” ('At thirty, one stands') (Our translation.): commentators either explain it as **one's learning being established** (the Han Confucian wording “有所立”, 'having that on which to stand'), or as **not being shaken by affairs** (the mind-and-nature wording of the learning of the Way, *dàoxué* 道学) — **turn one page, and you are sitting in on a brush gathering that crosses dynasties**. The training value of this art of folding: **it immunises you for good against the word "original meaning"** — not that there is no original meaning, but that **original meaning can only ever be reached through layer upon layer of commentary, and every layer is one making of it anew** (“**诗无达诂，易无达占，春秋无达辞**” ('The *Songs* admit of no definitive gloss; the *Changes* admit of no definitive divination; the *Spring and Autumn* admits of no definitive wording') (Our translation.) — the *Chunqiu fanlu* 春秋繁露 ('Luxuriant dew of the *Spring and Autumn*'), **the Han birth certificate of hermeneutics**). So the right posture for reading a commentary is not "digging the classic out of the commentary", but **reading the classic and the commentary against each other as a pair of documents, one set above the other**: see what the commentary supplies to the classic (historical material), what it changes (a stance), and what it holds itself back from changing (the boundary of its age).
:::

::: history A history of extending the building {#sec-kuojian}
**Pre-Qin**: the "*zhuan*" founds the house — exegesis accumulated by mouth and ear from teacher to pupil (the *Zuo Tradition* attaches events to the classic; the Gongyang school's “解诂” ('explanatory glosses') is the norms-and-precedents edition of the same thing); **Han**: commenting on a classic = founding a school — **the dispute between New Text and Old Text made the "commentary" the constitution of a teacher's method and a school's method (*shīfǎ* 师法 and *jiāfǎ* 家法)** (a household-register system of "the learning of such-and-such a school": one who comments on a classic has disciples who pass it on beneath the crimson curtain); **Zheng Xuan** “**括囊大典，网罗众家**” ('He bagged the great canons and netted every school') (Our translation.), drawing on both New and Old (the *Mao Shi jian* takes Mao as its base and now and then sets down his own ideas — **a soft breach of “注不破传” ('a commentary does not break the tradition')**); Wang Su 王肃, commenting on the classics, **set himself apart from him and contended for a name** (the anti-Zheng marketing of "Wang Su learning" — **commenting on the classics can also be a war of brands**); **Tang**: Kong Yingda's 孔颖达 *Wujing zhengyi* 五经正义 ('Correct meanings of the Five Classics') — **the official fixed text = the ceiling of interpretation; the examinations selected men by “疏义” ('elucidating the subcommentary'): the apartment of interpretation was taken into state ownership**; **Song**: meaning and principle moves in — Zhu Xi's *Sishu zhangju jizhu* reorganises the meaning of the classics on the frame of "mind and principle", and after the Southern Song it became the set form of the examinations — **the Han and Tang commentaries and subcommentaries were accused of “寻行数墨” ('tracing the lines and counting the ink'), and the apartment welcomed a renovation crew**; **Qing**: the Qian-Jia "return to the Han commentaries" — Hui Dong's 惠栋 party held that the meaning of the classics should be rebuilt from the exegesis of Han Confucians, who were “去古未远” ('not far removed from antiquity'); Dai Zhen set up the assembly line “**由字以通其词，由词以通其道**” ('From the character one gets through to its words; from the words one gets through to its Way') (Our translation.); Liu Baonan's *Lunyu zhengyi* and Liu Wenqi's 刘文淇 *Zuozhuan jiu zhu shuzheng* 左传旧注疏证 ('An evidential subcommentary on the old commentaries on the *Zuo Tradition*') — **the new-subcommentary movement = a structural overhaul of the whole building**; **the modern age**: commentaries and subcommentaries become excavated texts — the bamboo slips and silk of Guodian 郭店 and Mawangdui 马王堆 move "collation + meaning and principle" forward to the stage **before the classics took shape**; the *Guxun huizuan* 故训汇纂 ('A compendium of old glosses') and the databases carry two thousand years of old glosses up into the cloud — **the age of looking up the storey number is over; the age of choosing a storey has begun**.
:::

::: warning Four maladies in using commentaries
**Reading only the translation and not the commentary** (a vernacular translation does all the training for you — **the value of commentaries and subcommentaries lies in "how they tie the knot"; a translation keeps only "the conclusion"**); **reading only one layer** (taking Zhu Xi's *Jizhu* for "what Confucius meant" — **taking the Southern Song tenant for the landlord**); **a superstitious faith in "the authoritative commented edition"** (**“注不破注” ('a commentary does not break a commentary') means that the commentary itself may be holding an error in** — Wang Su's anti-Zheng commentaries are a specimen of "the stance comes first" — a good commentary, too, must be checked against the household's own holdings); **letting the commentary stand in for the classic** (lost in the layered accumulation of old paper, and **forgetting to read the text of the classic twenty times first** — the entrance to commentaries and subcommentaries is always a close reading of the plain text (*báiwén* 白文): **first run into the problem yourself, then ask the storeys for help**).
:::

::: exercise Dissection by layers {level=1}
Choose any one sentence of the *Analects* (for example “克己复礼” 'restrain the self and return to the rites'), find three layers — the He Yan *Jijie* line (or the old Han explanations quoted in Yang Bojun's 杨伯峻 translation with commentary), Zhu Xi's *Jizhu*, and one modern translation with commentary — and set out a **table of divergences**: a difference in glossing a character → a difference in grammar → a difference in thought, with evidence for each.
::: solution
Marking: **locate the divergence at a character** (for example, whether “一” is read as a numeral or as “专” 'solely' — **if the commentators do not say this, you may not invent a further head**); the grammatical difference must be one you can write out (whether “克” is transitive or intransitive; the structure of “为仁”); **the difference in thought** summarised in one sentence (constraint versus mind and nature are two ethics); cite page numbers throughout — **the first lesson in the practice of commentaries and subcommentaries: every judgement must be open to checking**.
:::
:::

::: exercise The commentator takes up the post {level=2}
On one sentence of plain text from the *Analects* that you choose yourself (for example “**君子求诸己，小人求诸人**” 'The gentleman seeks it in himself; the small man seeks it in others' (Our translation.)), write a "commentary" of twenty to forty characters. **It must imitate two forms**: ① glossing in a single sentence (“某，某也” 'X is Y'); ② the form that cites a classic (draw on another book to attest this sentence). Append one collation note (if there is a variant text).
::: solution
Marking: the language of the commentary form is in order (**a commentary in Classical Chinese is not a vernacular note written short**); the glossing form can be looked up in a dictionary or a proof from the classics; the form that cites a classic is precise down to the *pian* (at the level of "the 'Wei Linggong' 卫灵公: ‘躬自厚而薄责于人’ ('Be thick in what you lay on yourself, and thin in the blame you lay on others') (Our translation.) — the sense appears by the texts illuminating each other"); the format of the collation note (“某，一本作某／类书引作某” 'for X, one edition writes Y / a *leishu* quotes it as Y' — a side note on a phonetic loan, of the type “诸，一本作之”); **self-assessment: does this commentary "pretend to understand"? — for a character you are not sure of, better to mark “未详”**.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: the property rights of interpretation {level=3}
Write six hundred to one thousand characters: “一部经典的意义，归经文还是归注疏？” ('Does the meaning of a classic belong to the text of the classic, or to the commentaries and subcommentaries?') (Our translation.) You must call on two historical facts from this lesson (choose any two of: Wang Su against Zheng; the official compilation of the *Wujing zhengyi*; Zhu Xi's changing the sense of “时”; the Qing new subcommentaries), discuss **the mechanism by which the right to interpret is transferred** (a teacher's method — the examinations — printing — databases), and answer: **the "original text" you read today — who chose the storey for you?**
::: solution
Marking points: the historical facts are used accurately (the Wang Su case must make clear "**contending for a brand by commenting on the classics**" and the interests of the family learning, and must not stop at the gossip layer of "a petty man ruined the state"); the chain of mechanisms is complete (Han school method → Tang official compilation → Song block-printing → Qing evidential research → today's databases — **every revolution of the medium changes the landlord once**); the "storey confession" is honest (write down the edition and the translator you use — **the starting point of academic integrity is to admit that you have been inside the building all along**); if the conclusion is "**a classic = the text of the classic + the sum of its history of interpretation**", that is an A — but this sentence must have been paid for with argument.
:::

::: summary
The apartment's meters have been read: nine trades (*zhuan*, *zhu*, *jian*, *shu*, *jijie*, *zhangju*, *yinyi*, *jizhu*, *shuzheng* — each keeps to one step of the work); three disciplines (do not alter characters, do not write in its stead, do not pretend to understand — **“未详” is conscience, and “阙如” is an honour**); three methods (form, sound and sense: form fears that the character's shape is deceiving you, sound fears a key that opens too much, sense fears laziness); three generations of tenants (Han commentary, Song principle, Qing evidential subcommentary: **the same sentence has housed three generations of tenants, and the title deed has always been on the move**). The method in one sentence: **read the plain text before you go upstairs; every judgement must be open to checking; every citation must reach a page number**. The next chapter fills in the store of common cultural knowledge: astronomy and the calendar, geography and the commanderies and counties, official posts and the examinations, rites, music and the names of things — the "manual to the world" for reading Classical Chinese.
:::
