---
title: "Synonyms in classical Chinese"
---

The previous chapter said that one word with many meanings is a net. This chapter looks at its twin: **one meaning, many words**. What is frightening in Classical Chinese is not that unfamiliar words are many, but that **familiar words are too much alike** — five "look"s, six "arrive"s, eight layers of "die". To use the wrong one is not a faulty sentence; it is **overstepping one's station**. Three kinds of resident live in the synonym forest: **immigrants from the dialects, householders of register, and enrolled households of the ritual order**.

## A map of semantic fields {#sec-yichang}

::: definition The field of sensory verbs: the posts of five "look"s {#def-ganguan}
**视** = the general-issue "look", taking extra posts as the context requires ("视其辙乱" is inspecting; "孰视之" is looking closely); **瞻** = looking with the head raised (the *Book of Songs*: "**瞻望弗及，泣涕如雨**" — "I gaze afar and cannot reach them with my eyes; my tears fall like rain." (Our translation.) Gazing up never lowers the head); **望** = looking into the distance, and also looking forward to a later time (望月, gazing at the moon; 望幸, hoping for an imperial visit — waiting is the adjectivalisation of 望); **观** = looking that carries a purpose and a method (观兵, reviewing troops; 观政, observing government; "观乎天文", "observing the patterns of heaven" — **观察, 观摩 and 观念, "observe", "watch in order to learn" and "concept", are all on its establishment**); **见** = the chop that stamps the arrival of the line of sight (looking, and having it — the 见 of "**见善如不及**" in the *Shuoyuan*, "On seeing the good, it is as if one cannot catch up." (Our translation.), is a result of battle); and add **睹** (what the eye has verified counts as fact), **睐** (a sidelong glance full of feeling; "明眸善睐", "bright eyes, adept at the sidelong glance") and **睨** (a sidelong look of disdain — Lin Xiangru "睨"s the pillar: clasping the jade, he glares at the pillar askance, ready at any moment to stage a collision for the King of Qin to see). One set of actions is cut along three axes: **direction (瞻/望/睐 and 睨), intention (观/视), result (见/睹)** — this is what is called a **semantic field**: **synonyms do not seize one and the same sense; they only divide the workstations of a single office**.
:::

::: definition The field of time nouns: a shift table for the day {#def-shijian}
**朝／旦** (clocking in at daybreak: "朝闻道", "hearing the Way in the morning"; "旦日飨士卒", "at dawn, feasting the officers and men" — 旦 is the one horizontal stroke of the sun rising over the horizon); **中日／昃** ("**日中不至，则是无信**" — "If one does not arrive at noon, then one is without good faith." (Our translation.) Yuanfang's anger is precise to the shift; 昃 is the sun slanting once it has passed noon, **and the shape of the character is the posture of the sun going off duty**); **昳／晡／哺** (several shifts from afternoon to the *shen* double-hour; "日昳" and "晡时" in memorials are all codes for the hour); **日入／夕／莫** (the *Book of Songs*: "**日之夕矣，羊牛下来**" — "The day is at its evening; the sheep and the cattle come down." (Our translation.) 夕 is half-light; **莫 = the sun setting into the grass, that is, the original character of "暮"**; 莫 was borrowed away by the negative, and the character 暮 came on overtime to take the shift — **ancient and modern forms working the night shift**); **昏** (the sun entering the earth; **"婚" comes from "昏" — a wedding was originally a thing of dusk**, a fossil of the hour of 亲迎, the groom's fetching of the bride). Stack on three further calendrical tables: **dating the day by the stems and branches, timing the night by the five watches, and recording the phases of the moon by 朔, 朏, 望 and 晦** (new moon, first crescent, full moon, and the last day of the month) — on reading a memorial in diary form, check the roster first: **"夜分", "未旦" and "既望" ("the night at its division", "not yet dawn", "after the full moon") are all shift codes, not adjectives**.
:::

::: definition Three household registers for why synonyms exist {#def-sange}
**① Transcription from dialects.** Yang Xiong's *Fangyan* 方言 (*Dialects*) records "**党、晓、哲，知也。楚谓之党，或曰晓；齐宋之间谓之哲**" — "党, 晓 and 哲 mean 'to know'. Chu calls it 党, or else 晓; between Qi and Song it is called 哲." (Our translation.) Three words for "know" immigrated from three dialect regions into one and the same book. Another entry, "**嫁、逝、徂、适，往也**" — "嫁, 逝, 徂 and 适 mean 'to go'." (Our translation.) — pins the local ways of saying "go" (逝 means "to go", the speech of Chu; 徂, the speech of Qi — in broad outline) to the face of the scroll like specimens: **a person writing picks goods from the prestige language (*yayan*) and from the dialects; to choose a word is to choose an accent**. **② Register and rhythm.** "之, 如 and 适" are all "go to …" ("吾欲之南海", "I wish to go to the South Sea." (Our translation.) "子之武城", "The Master went to Wucheng." (Our translation.) "适千里者，三月聚粮", "One who goes a thousand *li* gathers grain for three months." (Our translation.) — the "适" of the *Zhuangzi* later still lives inside the old shell of "适意", "suited to one's inclination"). Choosing one of them and following it is often labour done for **level and oblique tones, and for the number of characters**: a five-character line wants a light "之"; only a seven-character line grants a heavy "适". **③ Enrolment under ritual.** The table of ranks of death in the "Quli" of the *Book of Rites* — "**天子死曰崩，诸侯曰薨，大夫曰卒，士曰不禄，庶人曰死**" — "When the Son of Heaven dies one says 崩; when a feudal lord dies, 薨; when a great officer dies, 卒; when a gentleman dies, 不禄; when a commoner dies, 死." (Our translation.) — five words for "die" are not five words; they are **five barcodes of status**. Stack on the forced diversions of the **name taboo** (*bihui*) (three techniques: substituting a character, omitting a stroke, and leaving a blank), and the store of synonyms thereupon becomes **a roll-call of the history of institutions**.
:::

::: example The coup of "去" and the killing air of "购" {#ex-qu}
Explain the words in bold, and give a ruling on their "ancient and modern household registration": ① "逝将**去**女，适彼乐土"; ② "一狼径**去**"; ③ "**去**岁" and "**去**处" in Tang and Song notebook literature (*biji*); ④ "吾闻汉**购**我头千金" (*Records of the Grand Historian*, "Basic Annals of Xiang Yu"); ⑤ "燕王**买**马" (*Intrigues of the Warring States*, the first "Stratagems of Yan").
::: solution
**The case of "去".** ① In 'Shishu' ('Large Rat') of the *Book of Songs*, "逝将去女" = I shall **leave** you — the old sense of "去" is **the platform of farewell**, not **hurrying off to a destination**; ② in 'The Wolf', "一狼径去" = it **walked** straight **off** — Pu Songling writes with an archaising brush (**Classical Chinese is the antiquity-reviving cosplay of people of every age; in the examination hall, "去" is looked up under its pre-Qin household registration**); ③ in the colloquial layer of the Tang and Song, "去" has already turned towards "go to" ("去处") — **a coup in word-meaning that went on for two thousand years; the remnant faction of the old sense is stuck to this day in the buildings of idioms and of colloquial speech**. The golden sentence for marking: **the ancient and the modern senses of "去" point in precisely opposite directions — the most dangerous familiar word is an opposite-sense shift that is familiar and not understood**. **The case of "购".** In the Qin and Han, "购" is **a bounty posted in heavy money** (in sentence ④, "购我头千金", "a thousand in gold for my head", is the reward price posted on Xiang Yu's side); 买 is the general-issue word (⑤ "买马且五百金", "buying horses, and for as much as five hundred in gold" — 买 is the market price, 购 is the reward tariff) — translate "购" as "purchase", and one has lost **the killing air of a price set by the state**. **An attached case, "直 = 值":** **ancient and modern forms** of a word (价直 → 价值, "the straightness of a price" → "value"), not a phonetic loan (*tongjia*) — a phonetic loan has no blood-tie of original meaning; ancient and modern forms come out of one and the same house (for the genealogical method, see [[classical-chinese-2/original-meanings]]).
:::
:::

::: intuition Synonyms are ritual, and they are also music {#def-liyin}
Take two intuitions into a text. **The intuition of ritual** — on meeting a set of synonyms swapped one for another, first ask the three things "**status, occasion, name taboo**": why does this stele text use "殂" and not "死" (the 'Memorial on Dispatching the Army': "**先帝创业未半而中道崩殂**" — "The late emperor's enterprise of founding was not yet half done when, midway, he died." (Our translation.) A late emperor can only 崩; that Liang says "殂" is a respectful word by way of variation); why this character lacks its last stroke (the fingerprint of a name taboo; a free lunch for dating). **The intuition of music** — synonyms linked in a phrase often serve a **disyllabic rhythm** ("追亡逐北", "pursuing the fugitives and chasing the routed"; "**殚其地之出，竭其庐之入**" — "exhausting what their land produces, using up what their cottages take in." (Our translation.) 殚 and 竭 are synonyms set in parallel; 出 and 入 are antonyms set in parallel; one sentence holds two kinds of symmetry). **Inferring the sense from the matching text** (*duiwen jianyi*) is the most economical dictionary: two words standing in equivalent positions, set one against the other, **have senses that are either of the same kind or opposite** — "**贤不肖**" = the worthy and the unworthy; the parallel of 存 and 亡 in "**存亡之秋**" is a season of life and death (**inside "存亡", "亡" keeps the sense "perish" and "存" keeps the sense "live" — the parallel makes the two words gloss each other**). When you cannot read a word, **look at who is standing opposite it**.
:::

::: history A long queue in the study of synonyms {#sec-duichang}
**Pre-Qin.** The *Erya* 尔雅 (*Approaching the Correct*), in the section "Shigu" ("Explaining the old words"), opens the book — "**林、烝、天、帝、皇、王、后、辟、公、侯，君也**", "林, 烝, 天, 帝, 皇, 王, 后, 辟, 公 and 侯 mean 'ruler'." (Our translation.) Eleven "leading figures" of dialect and of period are cashed, at par, into the one character "君" — **the way humankind's first dictionary of synonyms paid out was simple and crude**, but it issued the classicists with a "correspondence table of ancient and modern language". **Han.** Yang Xiong's ***Fangyan*** interviewed on the spot for "the same word in different places". The very language of the explanations in Xu Shen's *Shuowen jiezi* (*Explaining Graphs and Analysing Characters*) is a **controlled network of synonyms** ("老，考也；考，老也" — "老 means 考; 考 means 老." (Our translation.) Mutual glossing is the marriage registration of synonyms). **Tang, Kong Yingda.** His subcommentaries often use the distinction "**浑言** and **析言**" (*hunyan*, "speaking in the round"; *xiyan*, "speaking by analysis"). Duan Yucai generalised the experience of the commentaries and subcommentaries into the formula "**浑言则……析言则……**" ("spoken of in the round, then …; spoken of by analysis, then …") — "鸟" and "隹", spoken of in the round, are both birds; spoken of by analysis, long tail and short tail are distinguished. **Qing, Duan Yucai.** He upgraded "浑言/析言" into a method (everywhere in the *Shuowen zhu* 说文注, his annotation of the *Shuowen*, "**浑言** and **析言**" are set in parallel — **the study of synonyms had the graduations of a microscope for the first time**). **Modern times.** Semantics fitted synonyms with **tables of semantic features**: lists of synonyms, and componential analysis (【±人】 and 【±敬】, "± human" and "± respectful", tabulated and set side by side — the ritual coordinates of "崩薨卒死" expressed by switching features). **The present.** Corpora issue synonyms with a **frequency balance** (the hour at which "视" and "看" change shift; the curves of the waxing and waning of sense-entries in historical dictionaries) — **the *Erya*'s synonym list, of the kind kept at a counter for exchange at par, has evolved into a table of moving exchange rates**.
:::

::: warning Four mines among synonyms
**Mine one, "translating the ancient by the modern".** See "去" and translate it "arrive"; see "购" and translate it "buy" — **the shift of a familiar word is more dangerous than an unfamiliar word** (cases that run in the opposite direction sit at the end of the previous chapter and in this chapter's worked example). **Mine two, "synonym = interchangeable".** Changing "崩" to "死" does not break the law, but it **changes the history** (a word of the ritual order is a barcode, not rhetoric). **Mine three, "a pair set in parallel must be antonyms".** See "A B" and split them as antonyms. In earlier Chinese, pairs such as "衣服" and "饮食" are mostly synonyms linked in a phrase — **not every AB is "出入" or "存亡"**. **Mine four, "overdoing the name taboo".** See an omitted stroke and date the piece (copies made in imitation also learn to omit the stroke — **a name taboo is evidence, not testimony**, and must be put in together with the bibliographical record of the paper, the ink and the verso).
:::

::: exercise Post descriptions {level=1}
For each of the five members of the family of words for "arrive", "之, 如, 适, 诣 and 造", write a post description (original meaning, usual objects, band of register, and one citation from a book), and then explain why the *Analects* says "子之武城" and not "子诣武城".
::: solution
Marking: **诣** carries the respectful-word band of "paying a call on an elder, or on a government office" ("及郡下，诣太守" — "On reaching the commandery seat, he went to the grand administrator." (Our translation.)); **造** has the solid feel of coming to the door in "造门" and "造阙" ("coming to the gate", "coming to the gate-towers"), and also the extended sense "造诣", together with this (the "造次" of "君子无终食之间违仁，造次必于是" in the *Analects* — "The gentleman does not depart from benevolence for the space of a meal; in haste, too, he must be thus." (Our translation.) — has a separate sense — **the same character, a different household registration; do not join the lines at random**); **如**, in "子之武城", is the lightweight model for level and oblique tones; "诣" is too heavy — Confucius taking up his post is not the presenting of a calling card.
:::

::: exercise The name-taboo detective {level=2}
Find three examples of a character changed for a name taboo, and date them. Hint cases — Emperor Wen of Han tabooed **恒** (恒山 was written "常山"); Emperor Gaozu of Han tabooed **邦** ("邦" was changed to "国" on entering Han books); Emperor Taizong of Tang tabooed **世民** ("民" lacks its last stroke — the free dating-code of Tang steles); Shengzu of the Qing tabooed **玄烨** (玄 → 元, and 烨 lacks a stroke). Explain the dating rule "avoid the present dynasty; do not avoid former dynasties", and its boundary.
::: solution
Marking: the correspondence "tabooed name — substitution — dynasty" is without error in the three examples (恒 → 常 = Emperor Wen of Han, Liu Heng; 玄 → 元 = Shengzu of the Qing, Xuanye — **cases of the 常山 and 元烨 type are fingerprints of textbook grade**); the rule is set out clearly: **one avoids only the reigning dynasty and those late emperors who are close kin (for the finer rules, such as not tabooing a two-character name by one character alone, and not tabooing the *Odes* and the *Documents*, state the edition)**; an honest declaration: **substituting a character, or carving in imitation, can be faked — a name taboo is supporting evidence, and is not to be treated as evidence that stands alone**.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: the politics of synonyms {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters. "The rise and fall of a set of synonyms is a political history in miniature." Choose any one group (崩薨卒死 / 征伐侵 / the downgrading of the forms of address 陛下, 殿下, 足下 and 执事 / the transitivity of "杀诛殂毙", and the avoidance of superiors), argue **how the choice of a word exposes status, stance and period**, and answer: **what constrains the "choosing among synonyms" that you are doing in modern Chinese (politeness? traffic? censorship?)**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the **original domain of use** of the members of the group is got right (the definitions of "征, 伐 and 侵" are **differently stated in the old books** — the *Rites of Zhou* and the *Sima fa* (*Methods of the Marshal*) differ from one account to another; do not force a cut. It is enough to write the core of the usual account, "the difference in the feel of the words between giving notice of arms and not giving notice, and between a superior striking an inferior", and to declare the disagreement); the **chain of downgraded address** in "陛下/殿下/足下" (from "not daring to point straight at the person, one calls the one below him to pass the meaning on" — **the semantics of respectful words is the study of distance**); the modern comparison lands on a mechanism (the assignment of occasions among "去世/死亡/走了/没了", "passed away / died / has gone / is no more", = **modern ritual**). If the conclusion can point out that "**synonymous in the dictionary, not equivalent in use**", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
Closing the accounts of the synonym forest: the three axes of the semantic field (direction/intention/result — workstations, not competition); the three household registers (dialect immigrants/register and rhythm/enrolled households of the ritual order); the three techniques of the name taboo, and the fingerprints used in dating; inferring the sense from the matching text (look at who is standing opposite); the mines of a shift in a familiar word (the two cases of 去 and of 购 are on the file). The method in one sentence: **synonymous in the dictionary, not equivalent in use — synonyms are different departments wearing the same uniform**. The next chapter takes apart the system of forms of address: personal name, courtesy name, sobriquet, posthumous title, temple taboo, and the matrix of humble and respectful words in "卿, 君, 公 and 足下" — the ancients' navigation system for relations between people.
:::
