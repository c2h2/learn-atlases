---
title: "Reading aloud and the Putonghua Proficiency Test"
---

The Putonghua Proficiency Test (PSC) is one of the largest language-standardisation examinations in the world. Since it was established in 1994, teachers, announcers, civil servants and ten million students in school have walked into the examination room and converted everything in the first eight sections of this chapter into a mark sheet on a scale of one hundred. For that reason it is also the best 'end-of-term simulator': each of the four main items checks one layer of the skill — monosyllables check the foundation of initials, finals and tones; word groups check whether the neutral tone, rhotacisation (*erhua*) and tone sandhi have become automatic; reading a passage aloud checks pausing and linking, and intonation, in connected speech; speaking on a set topic checks whether, once one is off the script, a dialect accent will 'come back'. This chapter sets the test out as a revision map one can follow and carry out, and along the way teaches you the craft of reading aloud itself.

## What the test is: the system and the marks

::: definition The Putonghua Proficiency Test {#def-putonghua-test}
The **Putonghua Proficiency Test** (Putonghua Proficiency Test) is organised and administered by the departments for language and script work, together with the testing bodies. It measures how far the candidate has mastered the **standard common language** of Putonghua — note that what is measured is the 'degree of standardness', not the dialect one comes from, and not eloquence. It was established jointly in 1994 by the former State Language Commission and other departments, and gained a basis in law after the *Law on the National Common Spoken and Written Language* came into force in 2000. The system and the syllabus have been revised several times (the *Syllabus of the Putonghua Proficiency Test* of 2003, and the *Implementation Outline of the Putonghua Proficiency Test* of 2021, which brought in computer-assisted, computer-based testing), while the framework of the mark values has not changed for more than twenty years. Posts in teaching, in broadcasting and presenting, in the service trades and the like must by regulation hold a certificate of the corresponding grade (a teaching qualification generally requires Grade 2 Class B or above; teachers of Chinese often require Grade 2 Class A or above; posts connected with broadcasting require Grade 1 — the local documents decide).
:::

::: theorem The structure of the paper (the framework now in force) {#rule-juanmian}
Four items, taken in one sitting, the whole about seventeen minutes:
(1) **Reading monosyllabic words**, 100 of them, 10 marks, time limit about 3 minutes — each character is tested only for its monosyllabic sound in the citation tone, and **no sandhi of any kind is applied**;
(2) **Reading polysyllabic words**, 50 groups, 20 marks, time limit about 2 minutes — the minefield of the neutral tone, rhotacisation, third-tone sandhi and the sandhi of “一” and “不”;
(3) **Reading a short text aloud**, 1 piece (chosen from the sixty that have been published), 30 marks, time limit 4 minutes — connected speech is what is checked: pausing and linking, stress, intonation, speed, and added characters, omitted characters and reading back, each deducted on its own account;
(4) **Speaking on a set topic**, 3 minutes, 40 marks, one topic drawn from the batch for that sitting — a monologue, checking 'whether, with nobody watching, one still speaks standardly or not'.
:::

::: theorem The score lines of the grades {#rule-dengji}
Grade 1 Class A: **97 marks and above** (to be confirmed by review at a national testing body); Grade 1 Class B: 92; Grade 2 Class A: 87; Grade 2 Class B: 80; Grade 3 Class A: 70; Grade 3 Class B: 60. Three grades and six classes; below the pass line of 60 marks, no grade is awarded. The mark is a composite of an initial assessment by the system and a human review. The 'review line' for Grade 1 Class A is designed as it is because, above 97, phonetic blemishes are already near the limit of what the ear can hear, and a stricter pair of ears is needed to keep the gate.
:::

::: example From the marks to a grade {#ex-pandeng}
A candidate: monosyllables 9.2, word groups 17.6, reading aloud 25.4, speaking 34.0. What grade is she? What does the pattern of marks lost say she most needs to make up?
::: solution
The total is 86.2, Grade 2 Class B (80 ≤ 86.2 < 87; 0.8 of a mark short of Grade 2 Class A — a common disaster of being 'stuck on the line'). Comparing the rates of marks lost: monosyllables 8%, word groups 12%, reading aloud 15.3%, speaking 15% — the loss grows as the 'freedom' grows, a typical portrait of **citation forms passed, connected speech not yet automatic**: the isolated characters can be read correctly, but in connected reading the tone values are discounted, pausing and linking are stiff, and the dialect accent comes back. The prescription is not to memorise the character list over again, but a timed recording of reading aloud every day plus two or three minutes of speaking in one's own words ([[#ex-shuohua]]), training 'correct' until it is 'natural'.
:::
:::

## Monosyllables and word groups: laying mines and clearing them

::: warning The first mine on the monosyllable item is 'thinking too much' {#warning-danzi}
In reading monosyllabic words, **always read the citation tone**: do not apply the sandhi of connected reading (“管理” is split into two cards, and each is read in its own 214), do not use the neutral tone, and do not retroflex when no *erhua* spelling is there to be seen. The card for “北” is read běi in the full tone; do not let it slide into bě·. The card for “儿” is read ér; do not read it as r. The root of the slip is too good a feel for words — neighbouring cards have been joined, in the mind, into a word. The line-covering method (a finger pressed over the next line) will undo it.
:::

::: theorem Where the mines are laid in the word-group item {#rule-ciLei}
The 50 word groups usually have three kinds of mine buried in them: **sandhi** (third tones in succession, and the sandhi of 一 and 不 — to read the original tone is already to be wrong), the **neutral tone** (a neutral-tone word that is on the test word list loses marks if read in a full tone), and **rhotacisation** (an *erhua* word on the list loses marks if not retroflexed; the other way about, a forced retroflex on something off the list, such as “*水儿”, also counts as wrong). There are also characters misread by habit (“档案 dàng” read as dǎng, “畸形 jī” read as qí, “气氛 fēn” read as fèn — mostly old readings surviving from before the pronunciation-review table of 1985 fixed them as single readings) and out-of-the-way initials and finals (characters of the “有、与” kind, r-, and characters beginning with a zero initial and u). There is no other way to clear the mines: **go through the list** — record a self-test of the syllabus word list, word by word, and pull out those one 'recognises but reads wrong'. That batch is usually only a few per cent of the list, yet it contributes most of the marks lost in the examination room.
:::

::: example A self-test of five words {#ex-wuge}
“奶奶、月亮、使劲儿、不仅、处理” — first record yourself reading them, then check: what does each one test?
::: solution
奶奶 nǎi·nai: tests the neutral tone (the second syllable is light and short, with a pitch at degree 4; see the neutral-tone diagram in [[sounds-of-mandarin/sandhi]]); 月亮 yuè·liang: tests a neutral-tone word as the dictionary lists it (to read the full tone liáng is to be wrong); 使劲儿 shǐjìnr: tests rhotacisation (the sounds fuse into one syllable; reading it as the two syllables “劲·儿” loses marks); 不仅 bùjǐn: tests “不” kept in its citation tone, 51 (“仅” is a third tone, so **there is no sandhi** — the reverse trap of the rule that “不” changes only before a fourth tone); 处理 chǔlǐ: tests the third-tone verbal reading chǔ of “处” (to read chù is to be wrong; 处分/处理/处置 each keep their own place). Five words cover five kinds of mine, which is as dense as a 'self-test sample' can be; real mine-clearance has to go through the whole word list.
:::
:::

## Reading a short text aloud: sixty pieces over forty years

::: definition The item of reading a set text {#def-langdu}
One piece is drawn on the spot from the **sixty texts for reading aloud** (a catalogue appended to the *Implementation Outline of the Putonghua Proficiency Test*; excerpts from essays by famous modern writers and from expository prose; a body of pieces on the order of about six thousand characters in all), and the whole text is read within four minutes (about four hundred syllables). Marking comes on four fronts at once: **accuracy of pronunciation** (errors of initial, final and tone are deducted syllable by syllable), **intonation, and pausing and linking** (failing to pause where one should, pausing at random where one should not, and the same tune on every sentence, are all deducted), **fluency** (speed out of control, and too many fractured pauses, lose marks; if you misread, **do not read back** — a read-back is counted as a blemish, and it still counts as wrong even when the correction is right), **added and omitted characters** (a slip of the tongue, a swallowed character or an added character is counted at each place). The sixty pieces are protected by copyright; for practice one must buy a formal publication or official materials. This atlas teaches only the method, and does not print the texts.
:::

::: theorem Four layers of work in reading aloud {#rule-langdu-si}
(1) **Character**: go through the unfamiliar words piece by piece (“伧俗”, “罅隙” and written words of that kind; the actual word list of the piece chosen is what counts), mark the pronunciation and read them yourself. (2) **Linking**: mark pausing and linking by the foot and by the syntax (the notation for reading aloud in [[sounds-of-mandarin/stress-intonation]]); function words take no beat. (3) **Intonation**: vary the direction at the end of the sentence (statements fall, questions rise; do not flick upward at the end of every sentence). (4) **Speed**: 220 to 260 syllables a minute is suitable (the ranges in the various broadcasting textbooks differ a little); **better slow than chopped** — slow and continuous stands higher than fast and cut to pieces. Check a recording yourself three times: the first time against the characters, the second against pausing and linking, the third listening only for 'whether it sounds like speech'.
:::

::: example A reading that carries its wounds {#ex-shang}
A student reads a long sentence made up for the occasion, “她轻轻地合上了那本厚厚的书” ('She gently closed that thick book.') (Our translation.), as “她轻／轻地／合上了那／本厚厚的书”, and slams the stress onto the character “那”. By the four layers of work in this section, point out the errors and give a corrected reading.
::: solution
Both places where the line is cut cut a word open. “轻轻” is a reduplicated stative, and the knife must not come down inside it; “那本” is a tight unit of a demonstrative plus a classifier (measure word), and likewise must not be cut at the waist. The correct pausing and linking is: 她轻轻地／合上了／那本厚厚的书 (“地” and “了” are parasitic; the beats all fall on the disyllabic groups; see the rules for the foot in [[sounds-of-mandarin/stress-intonation]]). As for stress: the information focus of this sentence is the action “合上” and the state “厚厚的”; “那” is only an empty demonstrative limiter, and slamming the stress onto it amounts to telling the listener 'I do not know what I ought to emphasise'. Listen back to the recording after the correction: if the breaks fall on structural boundaries and the stress falls on the focus in the content words, it passes. One discipline of the examination room, in passing: an error of this kind is **not read back on the spot** — putting it right would be too late anyway; read on. Fluency is worth more than the face of a single character.
:::
:::

## Speaking on a set topic: a three-minute demon-revealing mirror

::: definition The set-topic speaking item {#def-shuohua}
One topic is drawn from the batch for that sitting (each edition of the syllabus provides thirty, mostly topics in the manner of a composition on a short title, of the kind “我喜欢的季节、我的愿望、童年往事、学习方法” ('a season I like, a wish of mine, an incident from childhood, a way of studying')), and the candidate gives a **three-minute one-way monologue**. Marking has three main parts: **how standard the pronunciation is** (how thick the dialect accent is, and how systematic), **standardness of vocabulary and grammar** (dialect words, dialect sentence patterns, and too high a frequency of the pet phrases “然后” and “那个” ('and then', 'that') are all deducted), **natural fluency** (traces of a memorised script, long stalls, and leaving the topic are all deducted). It is worth forty marks, the heaviest item on the whole paper — and the item that best separates 'can read' from 'can speak'.
:::

::: warning Memorised scripts, and training in 'leaving the script'
In the era of computer-based testing, the speaking item comes with **monitoring of the repetition rate and of prosody**: reciting a model essay, or reciting a 'universal script', is lowered a band and loses marks (each testing station applies the rule on the strict side). The right road is to prepare, for each cluster of topics, an **outline** rather than a script, and to train the skill of 'the mouth opens onto the outline': a sentence of viewpoint, plus two examples, plus a sentence to close; think while you speak, drop a gear in speed, and allow repair (“或者说” ('or rather') is a legitimate natural repair, whereas a memorised script's total lack of repair is on the contrary suspect — a marker's experience is that **perfection is suspicion**). A recording, three times a week, of a three-minute account in one's own words, listened through once each for dialect accent, for grammar and for pet phrases, is, within three to four weeks, the curve of learning that rises most steeply for the speaking item.
:::

::: example The revision ledger for thirty topics {#ex-shuohua}
“我喜欢的动物、我的心愿、一件难忘的事、谈诚信、学会宽容……” ('an animal I like, a wish of my heart, a thing hard to forget, on integrity, learning forbearance…'). Faced with thirty topics, how does one cover the whole stock with the fewest pieces of preparation?
::: solution
**The building-block method**: merge the topics by their skeleton — the 'writing about a person' group (a person one respects, a friend, a teacher… share one 'person block': one sentence of who they are, plus two sentences of qualities, plus one paragraph of example, plus a close on what one feels); the 'narrative' group (a thing hard to forget, childhood, an experience… share an 'event block': one sentence of how it began, plus four sentences of the course of it, plus the mood at the time, plus looking back today); the 'argument' group (integrity, forbearance, the environment… share a 'viewpoint block': one sentence of where one stands, plus one example on each side, plus what one oneself does, plus one sentence that lifts the theme). Thirty topics reduce to five or six blocks, assembled to the topic in the examination room: **assemble the outline; do not memorise the sentences** — which both stays clear of the red line on repetition rate and keeps the naturalness of 'thinking and speaking at the same time'. On the pronunciation side, keep a further booklet: take the sounds already merged on the dialect-accent check sheet ([[sounds-of-mandarin/dialect-differences]]) and practise them towards 'speaking'; do oral substitution drills on proper names, numbers and rhotacised words, so that the correct sound wins in free connected speech as well.
:::
:::

::: quiz
When you misread a character while reading the short text aloud, the right strategy in the examination room is ( ).
- [ ] read back at once and correct it
- [x] read on, and do not read back
- [ ] skip that word
- [ ] slow down, to signal 'I know I got it wrong'
::: solution
A read-back is counted as a lack of fluency, and the original error is still recorded ([[#def-langdu]]); skipping adds a new debt under 'left unread'. The only course that does not make the loss heavier is to read on — one wrong character costs pronunciation marks, while a read-back in a panic pays fluency marks and rhythm on top. The same frame of mind applies to the speaking item: repair a slip of the tongue and go on speaking, which is precisely what 'natural' looks like.
:::
:::

::: quiz
The score line that lies between Grade 1 Class A and Grade 1 Class B is ( ).
- [ ] 92 marks
- [x] 97 marks
- [ ] 95 marks
- [ ] 99 marks
::: solution
97 marks ([[#rule-dengji]]). And Grade 1 Class A needs, besides, a review by a national testing body: a double threshold of 'a high mark plus a strict review'. Most occupational thresholds are set between Grade 2 Class B and Grade 1 Class B. In preparing, first see clearly which grade the local documents require, and only then decide which band of marks to push for.

::: history
The institutional road of 'testing a mouthful of Putonghua': after the standard for Putonghua was fixed in 1955, the teaching force and the broadcasting corps were examined first; in 1986 the National Conference on Language and Script Work proposed 'strengthening the work of spreading Putonghua', and in the early 1990s some provinces and cities piloted graded tests; in **1994** the State Language Commission, the State Education Commission and the Ministry of Radio, Film and Television jointly introduced the Putonghua Proficiency Test, and the grade standards and the procedures took shape stage by stage; from 1998, the 'Publicity Week for the Promotion of Putonghua' each September brought the test into public view; in 2000 the *Law on the National Common Spoken and Written Language* confirmed, in its legal provisions, the status of 'Putonghua and the standard characters' as the national common spoken and written language, and set a requirement to meet the standard for the several trades of teaching, broadcasting, official duties and public service; the two syllabuses, of 2003 and of 2021, pushed the examination onto the track of computer-based testing and assessment by artificial intelligence — the final form, after forty years, of a paper-and-pen oral test, which is to write 'a consensus of the ear' as 'a consensus of the mark'.
:::

The technical support for revision all lies upstream in this chapter: for the diagnosis of monosyllabic pronunciation, see [[sounds-of-mandarin/speech-sounds]] through [[sounds-of-mandarin/tones]]; the ledger of rules for the word-group minefield is in [[sounds-of-mandarin/sandhi]]; pausing and linking, and intonation, in reading aloud are in [[sounds-of-mandarin/stress-intonation]]; the base account of dialect accent, and the work of correcting it, are in [[sounds-of-mandarin/dialect-differences]]. Outside the test, speaking Putonghua so that it is 'pleasant to hear' belongs to the art of public speaking and of reading aloud, and this atlas has not yet opened that course; for the sociological answer to 'how standard one ought to speak', see the informant's two accents, one before the microphone and one beside the stove, in [[dialects/fieldwork]] — the standard is not shame under another name; it is the range of an ability.

::: summary
- The test is four items and seventeen minutes: monosyllables 10, word groups 20, reading aloud 30, speaking 40; three grades and six classes, Grade 2 Class A at 87, Grade 1 Class B at 92, Grade 1 Class A at 97 (with a review besides).
- Monosyllables are read in the **citation tone**, and sandhi is not applied; the word-group item buries four kinds of mine — sandhi, the neutral tone, rhotacisation and single standard readings — and going through the list is the only way to clear them.
- Reading aloud checks the four layers 'character — linking — intonation — speed': better slow than chopped, and an error is not read back.
- The speaking item is the heaviest and also the hardest: leave the script behind; prepare blocks of outline, not a model essay; as to naturalness, 'perfection is suspicion'.
- The weight of revision follows the marks: give half the time spent practising reading to 'speaking off the script', and the grade is at once a different one.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Judging the grade {level=1 check="87"}
A candidate's total is 88.5. Which class of Grade 2 is the grade awarded in the Putonghua Proficiency Test? (Enter the figure of the score line that corresponds to “二甲”, that is, the lowest mark of that grade.)
::: solution
Grade 2 Class A (87 ≤ 88.5 < 92, hence 二甲). Note that 87 marks is precisely the threshold at which 二乙 rises to 二甲; 88.5 is already securely inside the band.
:::
:::

::: exercise The isolated character or connected speech {level=1}
Judge whether the following readings lose marks in the item 'reading monosyllabic words': (1) “好” read in the full tone 214; (2) “们” read in the neutral tone; (3) on seeing the two neighbouring characters “管理”, reading “管” as 35.
::: solution
(1) No deduction — reading an isolated character in its citation tone is exactly what is required ([[#rule-juanmian]]). (2) Deduct — the card for “们”, read on its own, should also be read in its citation tone mén (the full tone); to read the neutral tone is 'sandhi of one's own making'. (3) Deduct — the sandhi of connected reading occurs only in the flow of speech, and does not occur between cards ([[#warning-danzi]]). This question is a specific treatment for people whose feel for words is too good.
:::
:::

::: exercise A list for clearing the mines {level=2}
For the word-group item, draw up for yourself a list of the 'five classes of minefield' (two words each for sandhi, the neutral tone, rhotacisation, old readings under a single standard reading, and initials and finals easy to confuse), and, by the method of [[sounds-of-mandarin/dialect-differences]], mark each one for yourself “已过” or “未过” ('cleared' / 'not yet cleared').
::: solution
A sample for reference: sandhi (水果, 一定); the neutral tone (月亮, 事情 — the word list is what counts); rhotacisation (小孩儿, 没准儿); old readings under a single standard reading (档案 dàng, 卓越 zhuó); initials and finals easy to confuse (“牛” n and “留” l, “更” eng and “根” en). Three steps of self-checking: record yourself reading, check the pronunciations in 《现汉》 (the *Contemporary Chinese Dictionary*), and enter the wrong words in a notebook — within three weeks, retest the wrong words for two rounds; the rate of passing is the progress bar of your revision.
:::
:::

::: exercise How to use the sixty pieces {level=2}
You have no official textbook for reading aloud, and have found only half a text copied from a copy a classmate borrowed. Following this chapter, give a two-week plan for preparing the reading aloud that is both within the rules and effective (materials, the task for each day, and the indicators for self-assessment).
::: solution
Getting the materials by a lawful route is the first step: the catalogue of reading pieces in the syllabus is public, and each piece is collected in a formal publication (a textbook, or a reader issued to go with the test). A fragmentary text copied from a borrowed copy is not enough to prepare on. The skeleton of the plan: on days 1–7, one piece a day — mark the pronunciation of new words (the character layer), mark pausing and linking in a reading notation (the linking layer), and with a stopwatch read twice and record (the speed layer: 220 to 260 syllables a minute); on days 8–12, a second round, turning each piece into a package of 'reading + speaking': when the reading is finished, leave the script and retell the gist for one minute (building a bridge to the speaking item); on days 13–14, a simulation: three pieces at random, the whole procedure taken in one sitting. Three indicators for self-assessment: the number of wrong characters, the number of pauses, and the rate at which sentence-final intonation repeats — keep a table day by day; a curve is more honest than a feeling.
:::
:::

::: exercise Building a skeleton for the speaking item {level=3}
From the 'argument' group choose any one topic (for example “谈诚信”, 'on integrity'), write a three-minute speaking skeleton that is **an outline only** (no more than ten lines), and state the time allotted to each part and the mechanism that prevents a memorised script.
::: solution
A sample skeleton: position (integrity is the default contract, 20 seconds) → a positive example: the experience of returning an umbrella (50 seconds) → the negative side: the cost of cheating in the examination (50 seconds) → one's own practice: returning borrowed books on time (20 seconds) → close: trust is a slow deposit (20 seconds). Three moves against a memorised script: write only key words, not sentences; while recording, allow yourself to 'say it another way'; practise the same skeleton three times with different examples — in the examination room, assemble parts; do not load a videotape. What the marking looks at: whether all five pieces of the skeleton are there; whether the time fills out the whole three minutes (better to leave a margin of ten seconds than to overrun and be cut off); whether you have written concrete examples of your own (grand words spinning with no load are deducted as 'empty content').
:::
:::

::: exercise A prescription for one mark sheet {level=3}
A candidate from a dialect area has two sets of marks. The first time, 79.5 (monosyllables 8.6 / word groups 16.0 / reading aloud 23.0 / speaking 31.9); the second time, 83.0 (9.2 / 18.4 / 25.8 / 29.6). Construct a short comment, coherent in itself, of 'the second set of marks + a diagnosis + a prescription for the next step' (within two hundred characters), explaining why 'the speaking mark falling instead of rising' deserves to be watched.
::: solution
A conclusion for reference: characters, words and reading aloud rise across the board while the speaking item falls, which almost always points to one and the same cause — **preparation turned into examination drill**: during revision one concentrates on drilling characters, words and the pieces for reading aloud, speech off the script falls into disuse, and in the examination room the dialect accent comes back in free connected speech, while pet phrases and stalls increase (the other face of 'perfection is suspicion' is 'stiffness is a dropped line'). The prescription: cut character-and-word practice down to ten minutes a day, to keep it warm; each week add three recordings of a three-minute account in one's own words, plus one package of three minutes of reading aloud together with a retelling; in the two weeks before the next examination, do three simulations under real conditions, and the gap between the simulated mark and the mark in the real examination should come inside a predictable interval of one mark — 'a stable mark' is itself the signal of being up to standard.
:::
:::
