title: "The nature of speech sounds"
---

Say “妈（mā）” once, and you have already done three things that usually cannot be stated clearly until one has studied as far as a university department of Chinese. Physically, the vocal folds vibrate periodically, and produce a sound wave that an instrument can record. Physiologically, the lungs send air out under control, the vocal folds come together and vibrate, the tongue body lies flat, the lips open, and the passage through the nasal cavity is closed. Socially, from the inventory of the sounds of Chinese we have picked out this one sound, [ma⁵⁵], and not [ma⁵¹] (骂), and not [ma³⁵] (麻). The first two things belong to the sounds of every language; only the third — selection, convention, and the distinguishing of meaning — belongs peculiarly to speech sounds. This course sets out from here: what the sound system of Putonghua (standard Mandarin) actually is, how the sounds are produced, how they are heard and told apart, and how they distinguish meaning. Native speakers can already do all of this, yet almost no one can say it; to **say** it is the whole of the work of this lesson.

## Speech sounds and their three properties

::: definition Speech sound {#def-yuyin}
A speech sound is the sound by means of which the meaning of language is expressed; it is the material shell of language. Like every sound, it is a wave transmitted through the air, and so it has **physical properties**; like every voluntary movement, it is produced by the organs of speech, and so it has **physiological properties**; and a sound becomes a speech sound because a community uses it as the carrier of meaning — and so it has, what matters most, **social properties**.
:::

The three properties are not three kinds of sound, but three ways in to one and the same sound. Physics enters from the sound wave: frequency, amplitude, duration, and the make-up of the vibration. Physiology enters from the movement: who is supplying the air, who is vibrating, and at what point in the oral cavity the airstream meets an obstruction. Social convention then lays it down which, of the many sounds the mouth can produce, are used to pair with meanings, which differences of sound count, and which do not. The sciences that take these three entrances, respectively, as their centre are **acoustic phonetics**, **articulatory phonetics** and **phonology**; add auditory phonetics, which studies the receiving end, and that is the territory of phonetics. This course takes the analysis of articulation as its frame, calls on acoustic data for evidence, and leaves the functional question of distinguishing meaning to the section on the phoneme and to [[linguistics/phonetics-phonology]].

Setting sounds that are not speech sounds alongside can serve as a touchstone. Thunder, and the noise of machines, have only physical properties; that goes without saying. A cough, a clearing of the throat and a sneeze come from the body's own organs of phonation, yet they have never been selected by the sound system of any language, they carry no conventional meaning, and they do not count as speech sounds. The babbling of an infant before the age of six months can produce almost the sounds of every language in the world; between the ages of one and two it gradually "shrinks", and only those few sets that belong to the mother tongue are left — the shrinking takes place at the social level, and is the process by which the phonology of the mother tongue takes over the organs of speech. The interjections “唉” and “哼”, prolonged, carry a certain amount of meaning, yet they do not enter the phonology of the various languages and do not take part in the making of words; they are marginal phenomena. As for the distinction between "language" and "speech" (language is the socially conventional system, and speech is an individual's use of it on a particular occasion), it reminds us of this: the Putonghua described in this lesson is the Putonghua of the system, not the Putonghua in any one person's mouth.

::: quiz
Which of the following is a "speech sound" as linguistics uses the term?
- [ ] The whistling made by wind blowing through a crack in a window
- [ ] The sounds "ba-ba-ga" of an infant in the thick of play
- [x] The syllable [ni²¹⁴] produced when a speaker says “你”
- [ ] Applause from the audience, expressing approval
::: solution
A speech sound must be a sound that carries the conventionally assigned meaning of a language. The whistle of the wind is not produced by a person. An infant's babble, though it uses the human organs of speech, has not yet entered the system of the mother tongue, and carries no conventional meaning (the ba he produces is not identical with any word). Applause is a gesture, not a sound. Only the syllable “你” is chosen from the sound system of Chinese and bears one determinate grammatical word — it is a speech sound.
:::
:::

## The physical properties of speech sounds: pitch, loudness, length and timbre

A sound wave is the propagation of the vibration of compressions and rarefactions in the air. It has four physical properties: **frequency** (the number of complete vibrations in each second, in hertz, Hz), **amplitude** (the extent of the change in air pressure), **duration**, and the **make-up of the vibration** (which simple vibrations are superimposed, and in what proportions, to form the waveform). What the human ear perceives of these four, in that order, is **pitch**, **loudness**, **length** and **timbre**: if the frequency is high, the pitch is high; if the amplitude is large, the sound is loud; if it goes on for a long time, it has length; if the waveform differs, the timbre differs. A guqin and a piano at one and the same pitch can be told apart, and in a crowd one can recognise the footsteps of one's own child: both depend on timbre.

::: definition Timbre {#def-yinse}
The distinctive character of a sound. It is determined by the structure of the harmonics superimposed on the fundamental frequency (acoustically, a number of bands of frequency that have been strengthened, called **formants**), and traced to its root it has three sources: the nature of the vibrating body, the shape of the resonator, and the manner of producing the sound (striking, rubbing, blowing, plucking). In speech sounds the third is relatively fixed, and differences of timbre fall mainly on the shape of the resonating cavity — which is precisely the subject of the next section.
:::

Pitch depends on fundamental frequency, but the two are not two sides of one thing: frequency is a property of the sound wave, and pitch is the sensation of hearing it. A person can hear sounds from about 16 to 20,000 Hz, but distinguishes pitch only inside one sensitive stretch of that range — the ear is especially exacting about differences of fundamental frequency (below about 500 Hz), and this happens to cover the whole range of the speaking voice. Loudness is determined mainly by amplitude, yet different frequencies at the same amplitude do not sound equally loud: both extremes (the very low and the very shrill) sound weak — so a 悄悄话 ('speaking in an undertone') is not "softer" than an 耳语 ('a whisper') of the same content at a normal loudness.

The "make-up of the vibration" also draws a line that is of great importance to the course: **voiced** sounds and **voiceless** sounds. When the vocal folds vibrate periodically, the sound wave carries a clear period, the musical component predominates, and the sound is voiced; when the vocal folds do not vibrate, and the airstream is forced through a narrow gap into noise, the wave is aperiodic, and the sound is voiceless. Press a hand on the Adam's apple and draw out “思——” and “么——”: in the former the larynx vibrates, and in the latter it does not. That is the physiological criterion of voicing. Almost all the initials of Putonghua are voiceless; only the four m, n, l and r are voiced — a fact that looks trivial, and that will appear again and again in the next chapter, and in the dictionaries of native speakers of English who are learning Chinese.

Finally, the "standing" of the four elements in Putonghua. Length does not distinguish meaning in Putonghua: whether the word “八” is said drawn out or said in a hurry, it is still 八; it is only a matter of the rate of speech. **Absolute** pitch does not distinguish meaning either: one and the same tone contour, said by a bass voice and by a soprano voice, is the same word. Loudness in general does not distinguish meaning on its own (the place where it comes into its own is stress; see [[sounds-of-mandarin/stress-intonation]]). Only the **rises, falls and bends** of pitch have been taken into service by the system — a different contour, and the word changes. That is tone, the protagonist of chapter [[sounds-of-mandarin/tones]] of this course. Tone is "the use of pitch to distinguish meaning", not pitch itself. Remember this restriction, and what follows will not fall into confusion.

::: example Where the difference among three vowels lies {#ex-sangeyuanyin}
The three sounds [i], [u] and [a], said as nearly as possible equally long, equally loud and equally high: why are they still, the moment one hears them, not the same? Analyse this from both the physical and the physiological side.
::: solution
Physically: once amplitude, duration and fundamental frequency have all been made "the same", the difference that remains can only be the waveform, that is, the timbre. Physiologically: timbre comes from the shape of the resonating cavity, and the cavity shapes of the three sounds differ as greatly as can be — when [a] is produced the mouth is wide open and the tongue position is low and back, and the pharyngeal cavity and the oral cavity join into one large, open cavity; when [i] is produced, the front of the oral cavity is pressed by the raised tongue body into a narrow channel, and the pharyngeal cavity is relatively spacious; when [u] is produced, the two lips are rounded and thrust forward, the body of the tongue is drawn back, and the oral cavity is both narrow and long. If the cavity shapes differ, the formants that are strengthened differ, and even with the same fundamental frequency the sounds are plainly not the same. One reminder, in passing, for training the ear: vowels are told apart by timbre, and pitch by tone — people new to transcription often mishear "the person in the recording has changed to another tone" as "has changed to another vowel".
:::
:::

## The physiological properties of speech sounds: the organs of speech and the airstream

The organs of speech fall naturally into three sections, and into four if the receiving end is counted. The first section is the **organs of power**: the lungs, the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles, which govern the airstream and are the engine of speech. The second section is the **organs of phonation**: the larynx, and the vocal folds within it. The slackness or tension of the two vocal folds, their length, and their manner of vibration decide "whether there is voice, and what kind of voice there is": drawn tight and thin, the pitch is high; relaxed, it is low. Above the vocal folds in the larynx there is also a pair of **false vocal folds** (ventricular folds). Ordinarily they do not take part in vibration; certain languages (and a person deliberately pressing the voice in order to chant scripture) bring them in, and they produce a hoarse, constricted timbre. The third section is the **resonating cavities and the articulators**: the pharyngeal cavity, the oral cavity and the nasal cavity act as resonators; in the oral cavity the lips, the teeth, the molars, the tongue, the hard palate, the soft palate and the rest can move and can block, and so shape the cavity. The tongue is the most active articulator, and is customarily divided into tip, blade, dorsum (front and central) and root; the upper incisors and the alveolar ridge are the "chopping block" against which the tongue presses; the soft palate and the uvula are the gate by which the oral cavity leads to the nasal cavity — raised, they close the nose, and lowered, they open it. There is, besides, a side path leading into the nasal cavity: if the airstream goes out through the nose, the sound is **nasalised**. One and the same airstream, one and the same source of sound, passed through different shapings, is all that variety of timbre.

::: definition Airstream mechanism {#def-qiliu}
A mechanism that sets air moving so as to produce sound. There are three: the **pulmonic airstream** (the lungs compress, and the air is breathed out or breathed in), the **glottalic airstream** (the glottis moves up or down and carries the air with it; implosives and ejectives belong here, and are found in many languages of Africa and of the Americas), and the **lingual airstream** (the body of the tongue compresses air inside the oral cavity and then releases it, as with clicks). The great majority of the sounds of the world's languages, and every speech sound of Putonghua, are **pulmonic egressive** sounds. Clicks in Chinese, and calls to a horse such as “嘁”, involve ingressive air, but none of them lies inside the language system.
:::

::: definition Consonants and vowels {#def-fuyin-yuanyin}
A division according to whether the airstream, as it leaves the vocal tract, is **obviously obstructed**. What is obviously obstructed is a **consonant** — the obstruction may be at the larynx, or it may be in the oral cavity; it may close the passage off completely (a stop), or it may leave only a narrow gap for the airstream to rub through (a fricative). What is not obviously obstructed is a **vowel**. Note that voicing does **not** enter into this dividing line: [m] and [n] are voiced, and yet they are consonants, because the two lips, or the tip of the tongue, completely seal the oral cavity; [s] is voiceless, and it too is a consonant, because the obstruction of the narrow gap is obvious. Do not forget the other side either: a vowel may also be devoiced (the neutral-tone “吗” at the end of a sentence in Putonghua often leaves only a voiceless [A]), and a vowel is not the same thing as a sonorant.
:::

::: example Drawing up a file for a sound: [m] {#ex-jianlang-m}
Analyse [m] (the initial of “妈”) from the three sides of airstream, vocal folds, and oral and nasal passage.
::: solution
Airstream: pulmonic and egressive. Vocal folds: in vibration — press a hand on the throat and draw out “妈——”, and it can be felt. Passage: the two lips are closed together, and the oral cavity is sealed off; the soft palate hangs down and opens the nose, and the airstream goes out carrying the resonance of the nasal cavity — [m] is a nasal. Check once more against [[#def-fuyin-yuanyin]]: there is an obvious obstruction in the oral cavity, so it is a consonant, although it is voiced and resonant. The three features taken together: voiced, bilabial, nasal. To "draw up a file" for a sound is to write each observable movement down as one feature. This course treats every sound in this way: a feature is not a label, and each one corresponds to something the body does, which you can verify for yourself.
:::
:::

Physiology describes what is **possible** in a speech sound, not what is **actual**. The sounds that the organs of speech are capable of producing outnumber, many times over, the sounds that any one language uses. That a person can produce only those few dozen sounds of his or her own mother tongue is because, already in infancy, the system has "customised" the habits of the muscles — when an adult learns a foreign language and "cannot hear it and cannot produce it", half the reason is that the ear and the mouth both have first to be customised over again. The social property once more reaches in a hand and disturbs the territory of the physiological.

## The social properties of speech sounds: the phoneme and commutation {#sec-yinwei}

Now we come to the level bound most tightly to language itself. In Chinese, “你 [ni²¹⁴]” and “李 [li²¹⁴]”: the tone values (pitch contours) are the same, the finals are the same, and the difference between the initials [n] and [l] replaces the whole word, and the meaning with it. A difference of this sort, "a difference of one sound, a difference of one word", is called in phonology an **opposition**. Sounds that stand in an opposition are, in that language, **different phonemes**.

::: definition Phoneme {#def-yinwei}
The smallest unit of speech sound, in a language, that can **distinguish meaning**. The way to decide is the test of **commutation**: place sound A and sound B in the same position and substitute each for the other; if the substitution produces a different word, A and B are in opposition, and are two phonemes; if it produces no word, or the word does not change and it is only that "the manner of saying it has changed", then this difference does not distinguish meaning in that language. Sounds that do not distinguish meaning may be grouped as **conditioned variants** of one and the same phoneme (allophones): either each has its own occasion of occurrence, and they are in **complementary distribution** one with the other, or else they may be freely interchanged, with no difference of meaning.
:::

::: theorem Deciding the phoneme: opposition and complementary distribution {#rule-phoneme-test}
In a language, if two sounds can occur in the same position and, after substitution, distinguish meaning, they belong to **different** phonemes; if substitution never distinguishes meaning, they **may** belong to the same phoneme, and in that case the distribution must be examined further — two sounds that never appear on the same occasion (complementary distribution) are the usual case of different conditioned variants of the same phoneme; if on the same occasion they are freely substituted one for the other and the meaning is unharmed, they are free variants.
:::

::: proof Why the commutation test can directly prove only "difference"
Opposition can prove "difference": replace the initial of “八 [pa⁵⁵]” with the initial of “趴”, and another word is obtained, so [p] and [pʰ] are in opposition in Putonghua, and are two phonemes. But "the substitution brings out no difference" cannot, in one step, be taken for "the same phoneme", because the two sounds may only happen to have no opportunity to appear in the same environment — the distribution still has to be examined before one decides to merge them. In English the difference of aspiration between the [pʰ] of *pin* and the [p] of *spin* is perfectly clear, yet if the p of *spin* is replaced by the aspirated sound, the meaning of the word does not move; it has only been "said strangely". As it turns out, English [pʰ] occurs only at the beginning of a stressed syllable, and [p] only after s; they never meet, they are complementary, and they are assigned to the same phoneme /p/. The phoneme, it can be seen, is a **functional** category, not a physical one; whether or not sounds are merged depends on that system's need to distinguish meaning. Every language in this way compresses the several hundred producible sounds into a few dozen units, and, with the suprasegmental units of tone, the neutral tone and the like added, that is enough to govern all that can be said — the economy of language is not inferior to that of any work of engineering.
:::

::: example The same difference, two treatments in two systems {#ex-qingzhu}
In the system of initials of old-style Shanghainese, and of most present-day localities of Wu, three series stand side by side: [p] (voiceless unaspirated), [pʰ] (voiceless aspirated) and [b] (voiced). Putonghua has only the first two series; [b] does not occur at all in the position of an initial. Using the notion of the phoneme, describe the difference between the two systems.
::: solution
In Wu, voicing can distinguish meaning: in a position with the same final and the same tone, exchanging voiceless unaspirated with voiced changes the word, so voiceless and voiced are in opposition, and are two phonemes. In Putonghua the voiced column is empty: if the initial of “八” is produced with voice, the hearer will not think of some other character — a difference that is not made use of does not amount to a phoneme. What does distinguish meaning, on the exchange of “八/趴”, is rather aspiration against the lack of it, and so /p/ stands in opposition to /pʰ/. The conclusion: one and the same physiological difference (whether voiced or not) is a phonemic feature in one system, and in another system is not even a distinctive feature. The "hearing but not registering" of foreign-language learning has its root just here: the phonology has long since done the filtering for the ear.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In Putonghua, which of the following pairs of sounds belongs to **different** phonemes?
- [ ] A shortly said [ma] and a drawn-out [ma\cdot]
- [x] The initials of “自 [tsɿ⁵¹]” and “次 [tsʰɿ⁵¹]”
- [ ] A man's [ma⁵⁵] and a woman's [ma⁵⁵]
- [ ] The [a] of the word “安”, said by the same person, clearly or muddily, at different rates of speech
::: solution
The meaning of a word in Putonghua is not borne by differences of length, of absolute pitch, or of clarity; none of these three substitutions changes the word. Only [ts] and [tsʰ] can, on the same final and the same tone value, substitute one word for another (自/次 and 紫/此 — it counts only when, inside the pair, the final and the tone value are entirely the same). "Aspiration" is a meaning-distinguishing feature in Putonghua, and the two sounds are two phonemes. One comparison of English and Chinese, appended: English distinguishes meaning by voicing (pin/bin), and Putonghua by aspiration (拼/宾); whether or not English aspirates does not distinguish meaning (the p of *spin* and of *pin* are variants of one and the same phoneme) — the features that each system "seizes" are not the same.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which of the following statements about the phoneme is **incorrect**?
- [ ] The number of phonemes in a language is finite
- [ ] Two sounds that are physically close do not necessarily belong to the same phoneme
- [x] So long as two sounds differ physiologically, every language will treat them as different phonemes
- [ ] Whether or not one and the same pair of sounds is in opposition may receive different answers in two different languages
::: solution
Whether a difference "counts" is decided by the system, and a system does not take delivery of every physiological difference just as it stands on the list: English does not make use of aspiration, Putonghua does not make use of voicing, and Chinese does not distinguish long from short. The third statement takes a physiological possibility for a social necessity, and stands precisely opposed to [[#ex-qingzhu]]. The other three are all correct: phonemes are arrived at by induction, and their number is finite; /p/ and [pʰ] are physically close, and yet they are in opposition in Putonghua — "closeness" is not the criterion of one and the same phoneme; not distinguishing meaning is.
:::
:::

## Phonetics and phonetic symbols

Transcription is the technique of setting a sound down on paper. The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) is the tool in general use: the International Phonetic Association has drawn it up from 1888 on, and has revised it time and again. The principle is "one symbol for a sound corresponds to one sound"; when the letters do not suffice, new letter-shapes are added, and diacritics are added (ʰ is added for aspiration, a tilde for nasalisation, a colon for length). Transcription has a broad form and a narrow form: a **broad** transcription records as far as the phonemic level, with slashes drawn on either side; a **narrow** transcription records the actual phonetic value, with square brackets drawn on either side. Wherever this course speaks of the detail of articulation, the transcription is narrow; wherever it speaks of the system, the transcription is broad; and each chapter, as far as possible, states which system it is using.

::: example How many sounds one letter covers {#ex-san-ge-i}
How many different sounds does the Pinyin letter i stand for in the three syllables zī, shī and bǐ? Give a narrow transcription.
::: solution
Three: [tsɿ⁵⁵], [ʂʅ⁵⁵] and [pi²¹⁴]. The [ɿ] after z, c and s, and the [ʅ] after zh, ch, sh and r, are vowels continued out while the narrowing of the friction is not released; they are customarily called **apical vowels** (belonging respectively to the front of the tongue tip and the back of the tongue tip). Only the [i] after b is a true high vowel of the front of the tongue. Pinyin writes them all alike as i; the orthography covers over the pronunciation — and this is one instance of "Pinyin is not a phonetic alphabet". The Huang–Liao textbook marks them with the two signs -i[ɿ] and -i[ʅ] (meaning "the i that occurs only after z c s / zh ch sh r"), and this course follows that use. The various authorities differ slightly in the detail of the notation; that is a technical disagreement, and not a matter of someone being wrong.
:::
:::

::: warning Square brackets and slashes must not be mixed, and a letter is not a sound
[ ] records an actual phonetic value, / / records a unit of the system, and a letter of Pinyin records **spelling** — each of the three levels has its own rules. ✗ "b is voiced, because the Pinyin b is like the English b" — the mistake is to take the letter for what is in front of one: the b of Pinyin records the voiceless unaspirated stop /p/, and the actual phonetic value is [p]. ✗ "i is one sound" — see [[#ex-san-ge-i]]. ✓ To speak of articulation, consult the square brackets; to speak of the system, consult the slashes; to speak of spelling, consult the *Scheme for the Chinese Phonetic Alphabet*. When the rest of this course mentions the initials b, p, m and f, what is meant is that set of oppositions among the phonemes /p pʰ m f/, and this will not be said again.
:::

::: history
Chinese historical phonology, in the line that begins with the *Qieyun* 《切韵》 ('Cut Rhymes'), has a fine-grained analysis of initial, final and tone. Rhyme tables set the syllables out in grids, and are among the most strictly ordered phonological descriptions in the pre-modern world; the framework of "initials, finals and the four classes" in this course (open, even-teeth, closed and round-mouth finals, *kaiqi hecuo*) is inherited from just that. But the exchange of the ear for instruments is a matter of the twentieth century. From the 1920s on, Zhao Yuanren (Chao Yuen Ren), at the Institute of History and Philology of Academia Sinica, directed experiments in the gramophone recording of dialects, and devised the tone letters and the five-level method of noting tone — the ruler with which this course notes tones is that system as it was handed down. In the 1950s the Institute of Linguistics and the Institute of Acoustics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences studied the sounds of Chinese with the sound spectrograph; vowel formants, the duration of the aspiration interval, and the other "data" cited in this chapter were measured on precisely that generation of instruments. From *fanqie* spelling to the spectrogram, the method has changed, but the question asked is the same one: what, after all, is the sound of a Chinese character.
:::

::: summary
- A speech sound has three properties, physical, physiological and social; the social property is the essential property, and the phoneme is its unit ([[#def-yuyin]], [[#def-yinwei]]).
- The four physical elements — frequency, amplitude, duration and waveform — correspond to the four elements of perception, pitch, loudness, length and timbre; Putonghua distinguishes meaning only by the contour of pitch (tone), and does not use length or absolute pitch.
- The organs of speech fall into three sections: power (the lungs), phonation (the vocal folds), and resonance and articulation (the pharyngeal, oral and nasal cavities, and the lips, teeth, tongue and palate). The airstream mechanism is chiefly a pulmonic egressive one ([[#def-qiliu]]).
- The dividing line between consonants and vowels is "whether or not there is an obvious obstruction", not voicing ([[#def-fuyin-yuanyin]]).
- The phoneme is decided by the commutation test: substitution in the same position that distinguishes meaning is an opposition; if it does not distinguish meaning, look next at complementary distribution, and group the sounds as conditioned variants ([[#rule-phoneme-test]]).
- One and the same physiological difference may in different systems be a phonemic feature in the one and nothing at all in the other (voicing in Wu; aspiration in English).
- Transcription is layered: [ ] is narrow, // is broad. Pinyin is spelling, not a phonetic alphabet, and behind the letter i there are three sounds.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Counting phones {level=1 check="4"}
The syllable zhuāng (庄) can be analysed along several lines: the initial zh, the medial u, the main vowel a, and the coda ng. Counting each unit as one phoneme, how many phonemes does this syllable contain in all?
::: solution
4: /tʂ/ (zh), /u/, /a/ and /ŋ/ (ng). In the analysis, note two points: zh is one initial (one phoneme), and is not to be split into the two letters z and h; the spelling of ng is two letters, and one nasal-consonant phoneme. The number of letters (6) and the number of phonemes (4) are not equal — another instance of Pinyin not being a phonetic alphabet.
:::
:::

::: exercise Telling apart a set of phonemes in opposition {level=1 check="3"}
In the three syllables “爸 bà, 怕 pà, 骂 mà”, the finals and the tone values are entirely the same, and the three initials stand in opposition to one another. How many initial phonemes do these oppositions involve in all?
::: solution
3: /p/, /pʰ/ and /m/. The opposition of 爸 and 怕 is whether or not there is aspiration; the opposition of 怕 and 骂 is a difference of manner between stop and nasal. All three, substituted one for another in the same position, change the word; they are opposed in pairs, and so they are three phonemes. If one also counts “那/辣” (/n/, /l/), still more phonemes can stand on one and the same final.
:::

::: exercise Judging voiced and voiceless {level=1}
Which of the following initials are voiced: f, s, r, m, n, l, h? State your method of testing.
::: solution
The voiced sounds are the four r, m, n and l. The method of testing is the criterion of this chapter: press a hand on the throat and prolong the sound; those in which the vocal folds vibrate are voiced. Among the initials of Putonghua the voiced ones are only m, n, l and r (and, in addition, ŋ, which is not used as an initial); the other seventeen are all voiceless — this fact will be counted over once more, from the two directions of place and of manner, in [[sounds-of-mandarin/initials]].
:::

::: exercise The commutation test in practice: ü and i {level=2}
Use the commutation test to decide whether [y] (ü) and [i] in Putonghua belong to different phonemes. You are required to give a concrete pair of words in opposition, and to state the agreement of final and of tone value.
::: solution
The opposition holds. Minimal pairs in opposition include “你 [ni²¹⁴] / 女 [ny²¹⁴]” and “里 [li²¹⁴] / 旅 [ly²¹⁴]”: the same initial, the same tone value, and merely replacing [i] with [y] replaces the word. Therefore [i] and [y] belong in Putonghua to two phonemes, /i/ and /y/. The essential point in doing this exercise is to fix every remaining condition before changing the sound: if the codas differ, or the tone values differ, the "pair" is not minimal, and the conclusion is not reliable either.
:::

::: exercise Stern and mild {level=2}
A teacher says “坐下！” sternly, and says “坐下吧” mildly. Apart from timbre, what does each of pitch, loudness and length do? Why say that here "not a single phoneme has changed", and yet the speech sounds are "saying something"?
::: solution
The stern version usually raises the pitch range, makes the shape of the tone steep, brings the stress out, and increases the contrast of syllable duration; the mild version lays the pitch range flat, draws the ending out, and often has a shift into the neutral tone. Through the whole course of it, not one **phoneme** of the initials, the finals or the four tones has changed — what has changed is the suprasegmental use: a redistribution of pitch over and above the tone (stress and intonation; see [[sounds-of-mandarin/stress-intonation]]), and the rhythmic arrangement of loudness and length. This shows precisely that the physical parameters fall into two layers: the layer of the system is responsible for lexical and grammatical meaning, and the layer of use for mood and feeling. "Saying an attitude" depends on the latter; "saying the word" depends on the former.
:::

::: exercise The English speaker's "voicing ailment" {level=2}
Native speakers of English learning Putonghua often produce b, d and g as [b], [d] and [ɡ]. Using the theory of the phoneme in this chapter, explain the source of this error, and explain why a Chinese listener often cannot hear where it has gone wrong.
::: solution
In English, voiceless and voiced are in opposition (pin/bin), and aspiration does not make a phoneme (*spin* and *pin* differ only as variants); Putonghua is exactly the reverse: aspiration is in opposition, and voicing does not distinguish meaning (there is no voiced stop among the initials). The English speaker transfers the meaning-distinguishing feature of the mother tongue onto the stops of Chinese, and supposes that the b of Pinyin ought to be read as the b at the end of an English word; yet the Pinyin letter b records the voiceless unaspirated stop /p/ — a letter is not a sound. A Chinese listener cannot hear the mistake, because his or her own system does not make use of the dimension of voicing — the ear, likewise, has been customised by the system. The "hearing but not registering" in the two directions is the phonology filtering what comes in, and it is also the first difficulty in the teaching of pronunciation.
:::

::: exercise Transcribing a set of syllables {level=2}
Transcribe the following syllables in a narrow phonetic transcription (including the tone values): 四, 十, 屋 and 鸭. Mark the tone values of the tones, and after the transcription state the physiological basis of each initial or vowel.
::: solution
“四” [sɿ⁵¹] (a voiceless front-apical fricative, plus a continued apical vowel); “十” [ʂʅ³⁵] (the second (rising) tone of Putonghua; a voiceless back-apical fricative followed by [ʅ]); “屋” [u⁵⁵] (a zero initial, beginning on a high back rounded vowel of the tongue body; if the labial rounding movement at the start is counted as well, it may be written [wu⁵⁵]); “鸭” [ia⁵⁵], or, with a palatal glide, [ja⁵⁵] (a zero initial, of the even-teeth class). Inside every pair of square brackets in a transcription, one ought to be able to point to a gesture that can be verified — a sound whose gesture one cannot state is a transcription one has not yet understood.
:::

::: exercise Convicting a "wrong sound" {level=3}
Someone reads the “主” of “主要” as [tsu] (an initial like that of “组”), and reads the “纸” of “报纸” as [tsɿ]. Judge, for each of the two, what place of articulation has been changed, and explain why in Putonghua they count as "wrong", while in certain dialects they do not.
::: solution
“主” is properly the back-apical (retroflex) sound [tʂu²¹⁴]; to read it as [tsu] is to move the blade of the tongue from the front of the hard palate onto the upper alveolar ridge — the manner has not changed, and the place has. “纸” is properly [tʂʅ²¹⁴] (a voiceless back-apical affricate as initial, followed by a back-apical vowel); to read it as [tsɿ] is to change the initial for one of the front-apical class (an affricate, or even a fricative), and to change the class altogether. In the system of Putonghua, the [tʂ] series and the [ts] series are in opposition (知/资 and 主/组, each pair changing the word for the other), so a misreading changes the meaning, and hence counts as "wrong". In dialects where the two classes of initial have merged (as in a good many varieties of Southwestern Mandarin, which do not distinguish flat from retroflex), they are one and the same phoneme, and to say them as the same sound does not count as wrong — right and wrong change with the system. This is the most everyday problem in applying "the social property of speech sounds".
:::

::: exercise A short essay: my sound system {level=3}
In three hundred to five hundred characters, write a passage of self-analysis. Choose one distinctive feature which your own dialect (or a dialect of your grandparents' generation) makes use of and which Putonghua does not — voicing, a merger of nasals and laterals, the entering tone (checked tone), vowel length, or a difference in the number of tones will all do. Give at least two pairs of examples in which "substitution changes the word", and judge the relation of the feature to the system of Putonghua: is it the addition or the loss of a phoneme, or a merger, or a split?
::: hint
First set out a three-column comparison of "initials, finals and tones" for your dialect and for Putonghua, and then look for minimal pairs with the same final and the same tone. If no pair can be found, then that feature does not distinguish meaning in your speech either, and cannot count as a phoneme.
:::
::: solution
Points for marking: (1) the description of the feature is observable (place, manner, voicing, tone value, rather than an evaluative word of the sort 土音, 'rustic accent'); (2) the examples of opposition meet the requirement of a minimal opposition, that "every other condition is entirely the same"; (3) the comparison with Putonghua falls at the level of the phoneme (which have merged, which are additional), and does not say "which is more standard than which"; (4) the source of the material can be stated (one's own speech, or a certain place and a certain age-group; see the hints on method in [[dialects/fieldwork]]). Common ways of losing marks: using the Pinyin of Putonghua as a phonetic alphabet (spelling the character “土” as tu, and so supposing that it is the same phoneme as in Putonghua — the spelling conceals the fact that, besides [tʰu] and [tu], there may be a third reading); and describing a difference of phoneme as "unclear enunciation" (咬字不清).
