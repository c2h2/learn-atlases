“我没说他拿了你的书。” ('I did not say that he took your book.' (Our translation.)) Move the stress in this sentence onto whichever word you will, and the meaning changes to that word's — move it onto “我” and you are making clear who the person concerned is; move it onto “书” and you are limiting the object. Six words sustain six sentences. Words have heavy and light; the sentence has pausing and linking; the whole sentence has an intonation of rise and fall. Tone governs the character; the set of three in this chapter governs all the music above the character. This chapter writes these 'designs one cannot see' out as rules and exercises one can work with, because they have two immediate uses: saying a thing clearly (stress misplaced is an accident of expression), and reading a piece of writing aloud so that it sounds well (reading aloud, and the third question of the test, depend on it entirely).

## Heavy and light in the word: the 'metronome' of Putonghua

::: definition Stress and the stress pattern {#def-zhongyin}
When several syllables join into a rhythmic group, some are pronounced long, strong and clear, and some short, weak and blurred; the strong one is called **stress**. At the level of the **word**, the habitual pairing of stronger and weaker is called the **stress pattern**. The usual pattern of disyllabic words in Putonghua (standard Mandarin) is **medium–heavy** (heavy on the second syllable): 学习 ('study'), 语言 ('language'), 伟大 ('great'). Besides this there is the **heavy–light** pattern (neutral-tone words: 妈妈 'mother', 桌子 'table'; see [[sounds-of-mandarin/sandhi]]), and a few cases in which both syllables are heavy, or either pattern will do. Words and fixed phrases of three or four syllables show a hierarchy of **secondary stress + primary stress** (北京大学 'Peking University' is read with 北 light, 京 lighter still, 大 the heaviest, and 学 brought to a controlled close — the ways of marking it differ slightly from one scholar to another; the medium–heavy skeleton, pair set against pair, does not change).
:::

::: remark The 'stress' of Chinese is a weak stress
Set beside the strong stress of English (the vowel of a stressed syllable is full, the vowel of an unstressed syllable is centralised, and the contrast is extreme), the difference of heavy and light in Putonghua is **much milder**. Chinese tells meanings apart by pitch (by tone), and the room for a syllable to stretch or shrink in duration and in loudness is locked by the tone, so that in teaching it is often said that Chinese is a 'syllable-timed' language — each syllable like a mark of equal length on a metronome (experiment finds Chinese lying between 'syllable-timed' and 'stress-timed', more regular than English and looser than French; the conclusions of the several schools vary with the material). This explains two things: why a foreigner speaking Chinese by 'bouncing one character out after another' is not, for that reason, hard to understand, and why the words of a Chinese song can fall square on the beat — a language of the metronome is in time by nature. The same holds in the echo of the streets. The hawker's tune “磨剪子来——戗菜刀” ('Scissors to grind — kitchen knives to sharpen!' (Our translation.)) draws the syllables out into equal lengths of tune and beat; let the rhythm once go disordered, and the tune slips and the characters slip with it. In the accent lessons of the assistants in an old shop, the first lesson has always been 'Tread the beat even.' (Our translation.)
:::

## The foot: the smallest wheel of Chinese rhythm

::: definition The foot {#def-yinbu}
The smallest unit that organises syllables in prosody. The typical foot of Putonghua is **disyllabic** (the standard foot); a single character forms a 'superfoot / degenerate foot', and three syllables form an extended foot. Words and strings of words alike have to be ranged into feet. The disyllable is the standard beat; a trisyllable is cut, according to the way the word is formed, as '1＋2' or '2＋1' (图书馆 'library' is 2＋1 — 图书 'books' modifies 馆 'building', and in the reading 图书 is tight and 馆 is loose). It is never permitted to cut it at the waist, through the middle. Prosodic grammar (the line of Feng Shengli 冯胜利) has used the foot to account for a batch of syntactic facts: for example, 进行 ('carry out'), 加以 ('apply') and 实行 ('put into effect') are matched only with a disyllabic verb after them (“进行调查” 'carry out an investigation' is acceptable; “*进行查” is not). For the detail, see *Hanyu yunlü jufaxue* 《汉语韵律句法学》 ('The prosodic syntax of Chinese').
:::

The favour shown to the four-character frame (the four-character idiom, *chengyu*, and the four-character slogan) and the four-and-six line of parallel prose (*pianwen*) are both the foot projected onto literary form: the 2＋2 beat is the steadiest, and reads 'like Chinese'. In writing, “他快速地跑” ('he runs quickly') does not come so smoothly off the tongue as “他飞快地跑” ('he runs at a flying pace'). Besides register, there is rhythm's account to settle — the three syllables of “快速地” knock the 2＋2 beat askew. Prosody is not an ornament. It is the basic skeleton by which Chinese organises information.

::: example Beating out one sentence {#ex-paizi}
With “／”, mark the natural pauses of the sentence below, and say which beat the function words belong to: “我们的学校坐落在风景优美的西郊” ('Our school stands in the western suburbs, where the scenery is fine.' (Our translation.)).
::: solution
The natural reading: 我们的学校／坐落在／风景优美的／西郊. The points of the beat all fall on disyllabic groups (学校, 坐落, 风景, 优美, 西郊). Function words of the kind 的, 在 and 了 never make a beat by themselves; they live as parasites after the character before them. Read them as a beat of their own, and the line is at once awkward in the mouth (awkwardness in the mouth is a breach, and the standard by which one judges is the habit of the speech you use every day). Function words are light and short; content words hold the beat up. This and the list of neutral-tone words ([[sounds-of-mandarin/sandhi]]) are the two faces of one coin.
:::
:::

## The three instruments of the sentence: pause, stress and intonation

::: definition Pause, and pausing and linking {#def-tingdun}
**Pause** is of two kinds: the **grammatical pause** (the grammatical pause, corresponding to punctuation and to the levels of structure) and the **rhetorical pause** (the logical pause, and a blank left for feeling — what is called “此时无声胜有声”, 'At this moment silence surpasses any sound.' (Our translation.)). Punctuation is not the scale of the pause but a prompt. At a comma the structure need not pause (“虽然……但是”, 'although … yet', is read in one breath), and when the sentence is long one must, even where there is no comma, steal a breath according to the levels. A wrong pause is a misreading. The famous game of sentence-division played on “下雨天留客天天留我不留” (“下雨天，留客天。天留我不？留！”, 'A rainy day; a day for keeping a guest. Does Heaven keep me? Keep!' (Our translation.)) proves it: where you pause, there the meaning is.
:::

::: definition Logical stress {#def-luoji}
Grammatical stress is the default way of playing (in general the predicate and the object are stronger than the subject, the adverbial and the complement (补语) stronger than the head, and the head stronger than what is attached by a function word). When, in order to give weight to some piece of information, a speaker **goes against the default** and moves the stress across, that is called **logical stress**. The instruments of stress are not volume alone. Lengthening the duration, widening the pitch range, and giving weight to the onset of the character can all do the work — but one **must not change the tone contour** (to lift “书 shū” up into “ú” is not emphasis; it is the wrong character. See the warning later in this chapter).
:::

::: definition Intonation {#def-yudiao}
The direction of pitch over the whole sentence, most evident at the end. The tradition of teaching Chinese divides it into four patterns: **falling** (statement, request, command: 我走了。 'I am going.' ／请坐。 'Please sit down.' (Our translation.)), **rising** (a question, surprise, an exhortation: 你叫什么名字？ 'What is your name?' ／真的吗？ 'Really?' (Our translation.)), **level** (hesitation, coldness, seriousness: 真的……不好说。 'Really … it is hard to say.' (Our translation.) (held level and straight)), **curving** (sarcasm, a pun, a lingering aftertaste: 哼，你“聪明”得很！ 'Hmph, you are very "clever"!' (Our translation.)). Intonation is laid on top of the tone of each character. It does not grind the tone contour away, but **shifts the pitch range as a whole and compresses the size of the movement** — a question raises the ceiling of the whole sentence, a fall sends the tail sliding down all the way, and the relative shape from character to character is kept (the division of labour between the two layers in [[sounds-of-mandarin/tones]] is made good here).
:::

::: example Six centres of weight in one sentence {#ex-liuge-zhongxin}
“我没说他拿了你的书。” For each of the six ways of placing the logical stress, write out the meaning that is emphasised.
::: solution
Stress on “我”: others said it; it was not I. On “没”: a denial with one's whole strength (“我可没说！” 'I certainly did not say it!' (Our translation.)). On “说”: he may only have made a sign, or written a note; he did not necessarily 'say' it. On “他”: others were spoken of. On “你的”: what was taken is your book, not someone else's. On “书”: what was taken is a book, not a pen. The whole sentence is like an instrument of six keys: the key you strike is the key that lights — and at every playing, of the tone contours of the six words, not one has changed. What stress moves is pitch range, duration and clarity. The 'shape' of the tone stands its watch on the layer below ([[#rule-liangceng]]).
:::
:::

::: example When a fourth tone meets a rising intonation {#ex-qusheng-sheng}
In “你去不去？” ('Are you going or not?' (Our translation.)), the final “去” is a fourth tone (falling; 51, a full fall), and yet the whole sentence is a rising intonation — is that a contradiction? Write the analysis down.
::: solution
It is not a contradiction; the two layers each give way by one step. The 'tail rises' of the intonation layer is realised in **the pitch range being raised as a whole, and the final character not collapsing to the bottom**. The relative contour of “去” is still the small shape of 'closing from high to low'; only, this shape has been set upon a large slope that is climbing, and the actual curve takes on a composite form. The listener still judges “去” to be a fourth tone, because the shape at the character layer is there, and at the same time hears a question, because the sentence layer is rising. The best way to take the two layers apart is an experiment in variation: put the same character into a statement (“我不去。” 'I am not going.' (Our translation.)) and into a question (“我不去？” 'I am not going?' (Our translation.)) and record them. The head of the fall in “去” is thorough the one time and restrained the other, and it will never turn over into the second (rising) tone “qú”. Character tone is the load-bearing wall, and intonation is the soft furnishing; in this example both have given themselves away.
:::
:::

::: theorem The two layers of pitch do not encroach on each other {#rule-liangceng}
The tone of the character layer and the intonation of the sentence layer are transmitted on separate tracks. Tone keeps its shape (the tone contour); intonation changes the pitch range (the height of the whole, and the direction of the tail). A question does not turn a first tone (high level) into a second; emphasis does not turn a fourth tone into a third (dipping). Conversely, if in writing verse, or in fitting words to a tune, one twists the 'direction of the character tone' against the melody of chanting (formerly called *dàozì* 倒字, 'inverting the character': singing “明月” in the direction of “名月”), what one hears at once runs the two into a single stream. Jingles, and the fitting of words to rap, keep this old rule to this day — **a melody may borrow the shape of a tone; it may not destroy it**.
:::

::: proof From the inverted character to telling meanings apart: evidence that shape comes first
A listener hears “来啊～” ('Come!' (Our translation.)), when it has a falling tail, as an invitation and not as a command, and what this rests on is a combined decoding of tone contour and direction of the tail. Read the same sentence “他来了” ('He has come.' (Our translation.)) once on a rise and once on a fall, and the meaning changes from narration to seeking confirmation, while the tone category of every character does not stir by a hair. Experimentally, if the curve of absolute pitch in the stream of speech is subjected to 'a shift plus a scaling', the judgement of tone category and the judgement of mood both stay stable (the transposition experiment of [[sounds-of-mandarin/tones]] is used again here). The other way about, if one only flattens the character tones (keeping the large wave of the intonation), a native speaker at once loses the power to tell meanings apart — **character tone is the load-bearing wall; intonation is the soft furnishing**. The reason that 'the melody follows the character' (依字行腔) became a rule of Chinese opera (*xiqu*) is precisely that the tune (the melodic line) must leave the rising and falling shape of the character tone audible. To match the first tone with a level tune, and the fourth tone with a tune that slides down, is in both cases this same principle.
:::

::: quiz
To read “他大概不来了吧。” ('He probably is not coming, I suppose.' (Our translation.)) with a tone of hesitation, half believing and half doubting, one should use ( ).
- [ ] falling, closed decisively
- [ ] rising, closed on a high flick
- [x] level in the main, the final character rather level and straight, and drawn out
- [ ] curving, the stress brought down hard on “大概”
::: solution
Hesitation and coldness belong to the territory of the level pattern: the pitch range narrows, and the set of the tail is level ([[#def-yudiao]]). A fall has the air of delivering a conclusion, a rise the air of seeking confirmation, and a curve turns towards sarcasm. If what is wanted is 'half believing and half doubting' and not 'a sneer', only the level pattern fits. (“吧” is itself a modal particle; a questioning rise and a hesitant level are both open to it, and the hesitant version that the question wants lies with the level.)
:::
:::

::: quiz
When “学习语法很重要” ('Studying grammar matters a great deal.' (Our translation.)) is read out in natural feet, which item is the correct cut?
- [ ] 学习语／法很重／要
- [x] 学习／语法／很重要
- [ ] 学／习语／法／很重／要
- [ ] 学习／语法很／重要
::: solution
The correct beating is “学习／语法／很重要”: the two disyllabic words each make one beat, and “很”, as an adverb of degree, is a parasite before “重要” (the logical stress falls on “重”). Every other cut either slices “语法” at the waist, or breaks “学习” into scraps, or binds “语法很” into one beat and cuts the structure open. A foot never crosses the boundary of a word, and a function word does not take the main beat: grammar first, and rhythm after.
:::
:::

::: warning Stress is not 'shouting the tone crooked'
The character one emphasises may be lengthened, may be brighter, and its pitch range may be wider; one **may not change the tone contour**. To weight “真 zhēn” until it becomes “zhén” (borrowing the rise of the second tone to pass for 'force'), or to shout a bend out of “不 bù”, is in either case to press the character tone into service as stress. The listener hears 'the wrong character' first, and only then may think of 'emphasis'. The proper method of practice: in one and the same sentence, emphasise each of the six words in turn, record it and listen back, and check whether the tone-value curve of the character emphasised is still the standard one ([[#def-luoji]]). And, in passing, a blacklist of manners of reading aloud: a habitual rise at the end of the sentence (the 'upturned voice of broadcasting'), the same speed and the same height in every sentence, and a 'robot rhythm' that must pause once every two characters. Their fault is one and the same. They take the intonation layer for a habit, and press it down over the information itself.
:::

::: application The telephone, the voice key, and 'the face one cannot see'
With expression and gesture lost, a large part of the information in speech has to be made good again by the intonation layer. On the telephone, one short rise on “嗯？” equals 'please say it once more'; a “好的” whose tail lies level and straight equals business done strictly as business; a “好——的” that rises and is drawn out equals warmth, or else a perfunctory courtesy (according as the pitch range is wide or narrow). The 'read and no reply' feeling of a voice message comes precisely from the other person's sentence intonation having been flattened. The other way about, in reading aloud 'speaking into empty air' is harder than 'speaking to a person', and the way to make up for it is to do the stress, and the pausing and linking, a little more largely than life. The 'sense of an addressee' in the training of broadcasters is, said plainly, **finding, in the mind, a listener for the intonation layer**.
:::

::: history
Chinese men of letters were alive to the music inside the sentence from very early on. The distinguishing of the function words “也者”, the system of parallel fours and matched sixes, and the study of tune titles (*cipai*) and of tune manuals (*cipu* 词谱) — there is not one of these that is not at work polishing the foot and the intonation of the sentence. In the private school of former times, 'reading' was particular about fall and rise, about taking in and letting out, and that was the teaching of intonation built into the daily round. On the side of science, in the 1930s Zhao Yuanren (Yuen Ren Chao) 赵元任 used instruments and pitch graphs to study the character tones of Chinese and the intonation of the sentence (a number of specialised papers in English), and laid the foundation of the analytical frame 'character tone + sentence intonation, the two layers'. Chen Wangdao 陈望道, in *Xiucixue fafan* 《修辞学发凡》 ('An introduction to rhetoric', 1932), listed 'the patterning of sound' as one figure of speech. From the 1950s on, the arts of reading aloud and of recitation distilled a four-element word of command — 'pausing and linking, stress, mood, rhythm' — which, enlarged through the teaching of broadcasting and through the Putonghua test, became the common knowledge of people today. The demonstration recordings of several generations of broadcasting artists at Central Radio (Qi Yue 齐越, Xia Qing 夏青, and others of their company) are to this day a living specimen of 'what the sentence intonation of Chinese ought to sound like'. One set of 'the music of the sentence': half a volume of Chinese aesthetics, plus half a volume of experimental phonetics.
:::

::: quiz
“这也行？” ('Will this do too?' (Our translation.)) and “这也行。” ('This will do too.' (Our translation.)) have exactly the same order of words. The difference lies only in ( ).
- [ ] the position of the logical stress
- [x] the direction of the tail on the intonation layer (rise and fall), and the modal meaning brought about by that
- [ ] the tone categories being different
- [ ] the cutting into feet being different
::: solution
The same words and the same character tones; it is all in the sentence layer. A rising tail rewrites the statement as an exclamation and a rhetorical question of 'hard to believe'; a falling tail is a cold acknowledgement. Of character tone, foot and stress, not one of the three has moved. Intonation by itself can change the pragmatic force of a sentence, and this is its independent seat as an 'instrument' ([[#def-yudiao]]).
:::
:::

::: summary
- Words have a stress pattern. Disyllabic words in Putonghua take 'medium–heavy' as the trunk; the neutral tone is the heavy–light pattern become fixed. The syllables of Chinese are even, and the stress is a weak one.
- The disyllable is the standard foot. The favouring of four characters in the four-character idiom, in parallel prose and in slogans is in every case a projection of the foot. A foot does not cross the boundary of a word.
- Places of pause divide into the grammatical and the rhetorical, and punctuation is only a prompt. 'Where one pauses, there the meaning is.' (Our translation.)
- Logical stress moves the focus of the information. Its instruments are duration, pitch range and clarity, and **the tone contour must not be altered**.
- Intonation divides into four patterns, falling, rising, level and curving, and coexists with character tone as two layers: intonation moves the pitch range, and tone keeps the shape. The inverted character and a foreign lilt are both accidents of crossing the layers.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Cutting into feet {level=1}
For “现代汉语很难” ('Modern Chinese is hard.' (Our translation.)), mark the reading by the natural foot and by the rule that function words are parasitic (divide the beats with “／”), and explain why it is not “现代／汉语／很／难”.
::: solution
The natural reading: 现代汉语／很难 (“现代汉语” pairs off two and two; in “很难”, “很” is a parasite on “难”, and the two are one beat). The cut into four beats sets the function word up as a beat of its own, and also breaks the meaning “很难” apart. Pauses many and broken are 'fragmented reading', a common fault of those beginning to learn to read aloud. The mnemonic for judging: one beat, two content words; a function word takes no beat.
:::
:::

::: exercise Finding the stress {level=1}
“他昨天借给我一本书。” ('Yesterday he lent me a book.' (Our translation.)) For six follow-up questions (Who lent it? / When was it lent? / Lent to whom? / What was it that was lent? / Was it lent, and not bought?), say which word the logical stress ought to fall on.
::: solution
Who lent it — “他”; when — “昨天”; lent to whom — “我”; what was lent — “书”; whether or not it was a loan — “借”; when, as between “给” and “我”, the direction of receiving is emphasised, the stress is on “给”. Six questions and six answers, each of them moving one word, and not one tone contour changes. This exercise is the method of [[#ex-liuge-zhongxin]] done over again; be fluent at it, and the point is gained.
:::
:::

::: exercise A pragmatic list of intonation {level=2}
Listen to twenty minutes of an interview or a podcast, and note eight sentence-endings. Which of the four patterns is each — falling, rising, level or curving? What effect is the speaker trying to bring about (seeking confirmation, closing the matter off, leaving more to be said, teasing)? Set the notes out as a table of two columns.
::: solution
Directions, by way of a sample to consult: when a guest states a fact, the tail is mostly a fall (closing the matter off, and leaving no opening for discussion); an example not yet finished uses level, or a slight rise (leaving more to be said: 'there is more to follow'); the host's follow-up question rises high (seeking confirmation); a joking remark is paired with a curve, or with an odd stress (a signal of teasing). The line for a pass: at every place one can report a pairing of 'direction of the tail + function'. If, in one stretch of talk, all eight sentences use the same set of the tail, congratulations — you have collected a specimen of 'reading by machine'. Go back and listen again. In most cases it is being read off a script.
:::
:::

::: exercise Matching the intonation {level=1}
Assign each of the following sentences one intonation (falling / rising / level / curving), and give the reason. (1) “这事我不同意。” ('I do not agree about this.' (Our translation.)) (the stand is firm) (2) “你们这是搞什么名堂呀。” ('What game is this that you are playing?' (Our translation.)) (a demand to know) (3) “说也说不完。” ('Say what one may, it cannot all be said.' (Our translation.)) (at a loss) (4) “您可真是个大人物！” ('You really are a great personage!' (Our translation.)) (sarcasm)
::: solution
(1) Falling: the backbone of conclusions and of commands. (2) Rising: the family of interrogative sentences. (3) Level: the emotional colouring of being without strength, and of dragging the matter out. (4) Curving: the letter of it praises, and the intent of it belittles; a double meaning belongs to the curve (and on “大人物” a logical stress is added besides). For each of the four a reading at the margin may be debated; the principal pattern is as above. That one can 'debate the margin' shows precisely that the rule is a tendency, and not a shackle.
:::
:::

::: exercise Diagnosing pausing and linking {level=2}
A presenter read “本市将新建五座 跨江 人行景观桥” ('This city is to build five pedestrian scenic bridges across the river.' (Our translation.)), and broke every attributive apart. Give, by level, the correct division of this attributive of several layers (where one may pause, where one must never pause, where one may steal a breath), and explain why the structural relation between “跨江” and “人行景观” determines the cut.
::: solution
The chain of attributives: 五座‖（跨江）‖（人行景观）桥. The levels: “跨江” modifies “桥” (a bridge that crosses the river), and “人行” and “景观” also modify “桥”. The two are coordinate attributives, not each limiting the other (a bridge across the river, for people to walk, and with a scenic function), so the cut falls between the attributives, and one must never cut into the inside of “人行景观桥”. “人行景观桥” is tightly bound, and ought to be read continuously. After “五座” is the boundary of the numeral and the classifier, where one may pause or not. Between “新建” and the object, and between the verb and “五座”, one may also steal a breath (a change of breath before a comma). A wrong division (reading “跨江 人行” continuously) makes the listener take it for “人行跨江” ('walking across the river'), and binds the modifier to the wrong thing. **Inside an attributive, do not bundle at random; between verb and object, do not leave a blank.** These are the two iron rules of pausing and linking.
:::
:::

::: exercise An instrumental task on stress and tone contour {level=2}
With any software on a mobile phone that can draw a curve of fundamental frequency (anything of the speech-analysis kind will do), record “我请他吃饭” ('I am treating him to a meal.' (Our translation.)) three times, emphasising “我”, “他” and “吃饭” respectively. Observe: does the shape of the fundamental-frequency curve of the emphasised character change, or not? Does the direction of the end of the sentence change, or not? Write a conclusion of two hundred characters.
::: solution
What one expects to observe: at the place of emphasis the fundamental frequency **grows in amplitude** (the curve is pulled steeper), and the duration grows longer, but the rising and falling shapes of “请 qǐng” and of “他 tā” are still kept. The direction of the tail of the whole sentence follows the mood (a statement falls slightly). Whenever anyone reads “请” as climbing all the way up so as to supply the emphasis, the shape of the third tone is crossed into a second tone — precisely the scene of a breach that [[#def-luoji]] warned of. The conclusion ought to land on the 'division of labour between the two layers': intonation changes the pitch range (the soft furnishing), and tone keeps the shape (the load-bearing wall) ([[#rule-liangceng]]).
:::

::: exercise The score of a passage {level=3}
Choose for yourself a passage of prose of not more than three hundred characters (to be safe on copyright: use a text in the public domain, such as the opening of 'Spring' by Zhu Ziqing 朱自清, or write one yourself). Mark for it a 'score for reading aloud': pausing and linking (∨∧), stress (·), the direction of the intonation (↘↗→), and changes of speed (marks for fast and slow). For three key marks, write one sentence of reason each.
::: hint
First read it silently and fix a 'map of the meaning' (in which few sentences the weight of the information lies), and only then put the marks on. When the marking is done, read it through yourself and record it, and test 'whether, listening to the recording, one can hear the levels without looking at the marks'.
:::
::: solution
The line for a pass: (1) the levels of pause agree with the levels of syntax (a word is not cut open, and attributives are not bundled amiss); (2) each paragraph has only one or two primary stresses, and logical stress is used where there is a contrast; (3) the intonation at the ends of sentences is varied (not the same tune in sentence after sentence); (4) fast and slow follow the content (narration fast, the expression of feeling slow, and parallelism growing stronger, yet able to be brought to a close). The three reasons must each cite one rule ([[#def-tingdun]], [[#def-luoji]], [[#def-yudiao]]) as their warrant. 'It sounds well by feel' can earn only half the marks.
:::

::: exercise The inverted-character detective {level=3}
Listen to a popular song you know, and find one place of 'inverted character' (a word which, once sung, is easily heard as the wrong character, or a mishearing, *kōng'ěr* 空耳, of the sort that circles of fans often turn into a joke). With the two-layer theory of this chapter, explain: what 'intonation' did the melody give, and which character's 'tone shape' did it destroy? Then consider: why do some songs (for example certain systems of *nányīn* 南音, or styles of singing modelled on Chinese opera) almost never invert a character?
::: solution
A sample frame for the analysis. Some phrase of melody sings “想你” ('missing you') on a descent in the high register; if “想” has been set to a melodic line that flicks upward all the way, the listener decodes it, by the second tone, as “翔你” ('soaring you') — the 'intonation layer' of the melody overrides the layer of character tone, the shape of the tone contour is rewritten, and with that the discrimination of meaning overturns. On the side that does not invert characters, the method is precisely 'the melody follows the character': the melodic line gives way to the shape of the character tone (the first tone level and straight; the third tone and the fourth tone paired with a descending tail; and so on), or else padding notes are used to cushion the tune across the transition. What the marking looks to is whether the analysis of how tone contour answers melody is of a professional standard, and whether the example can be checked (the piece and the passage to be named). Inventing the words that are sung is not allowed.
