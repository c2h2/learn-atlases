title: "Putonghua and the dialects"
---

The same character “人”: a Beijinger says rén［ʐən³⁵］, a person from Chengdu says something close to ［zən²¹］, a person from Wenzhou says something close to ［nan³¹］ — three pronunciations for three ears, and yet all of them are Chinese. The gap between the dialects and Putonghua (standard Mandarin) is not a disorderly mass of ‘mistakes’, but **a neat set-for-set correspondence**: the initials have the initials' way of merging, the finals have the finals' way of being brought together, and the tone categories have the tone categories' table of displacements. The whole of the learning of correction is built on that neatness: first find out which sounds of Putonghua your dialect has ‘merged’, and where it has merged them, then change them in batches by the rules of correspondence — ten times faster than rote memorisation, one character at a time, and ten times more secure. This chapter teaches you to give yourself one ‘dialect-accent check-up’, and to issue a first prescription for standard pronunciation.

## Why a dialect accent is a ‘system’ and not a ‘collection of errors’

::: definition Dialect accent and rules of correspondence {#def-fangyin}
The accent which a native speaker of a local dialect brings into Putonghua is called a **dialect accent**. The features of a dialect accent are almost never scattered slips. They are **a wholesale transfer of the phonological system of the mother tongue**: phonemes that have merged in the dialect merge again when one speaks Putonghua; a sound that the dialect lacks is replaced, when one speaks Putonghua, by a near sound; the tone values (pitch contours) of the dialect are fastened onto the characters of Putonghua like hats. Because what is transferred is a system, it can be counted, it can be predicted, and it can be corrected in batches — that is the foundation of the science of ‘correction’ (identifying the differences between the dialect accent and Putonghua item by item, and correcting them item by item).
:::

::: remark Correction is not a seating by rank
In linguistics, the Chinese dialects stand to one another as ‘brothers’, not as ‘the standard and a warped copy’: Wu keeps the fully voiced initials, and Yue keeps the codas of the entering tone (checked tone), which are precisely the stratum of older sounds that Putonghua has lost (see [[phonology/mc-initials]] and [[history-of-chinese/periods]]); and Putonghua's own split of the level tone into yin and yang, and its distribution of the entering tone among the other tones, are likewise the fruit of its own course of change. The whole premise of correction is one commonplace of sociolinguistics: **accents have no higher and lower; situations have a norm** — in the classroom, in the examination hall and before the microphone one uses the standard pronunciation of the common language, and at home one speaks one's hometown speech; the two things do not clash. To take the dialect accent for something ‘ugly’ and to shame it is the greatest historical detour in education in standard pronunciation; to take it for ‘transfer’, and to solve it as an engineering problem, is the position this chapter takes.
:::

## Three cases for initials: flat and retroflex, n/l, f/h

::: example Case one: a flat tongue standing in for the retroflex {#ex-pingqiao}
In the dialects of Wuhan, Chengdu, Nanjing and other places, the *jing* group (z c s) and the *zhi* series (zh ch sh) merged historically into a single series (“四=事” and “才=柴” are homophonous). What learners of Putonghua from these areas look up is not ‘which characters are read wrong’, but ‘**which characters ought to be retroflex**’.
::: solution
Three ways of working in batches. First, **analogy from the phonetic component**: families of retroflex phonetic components, such as “召” (招, 昭, 沼, 超 and 绍 are all read zh/ch/sh), “中” (忠, 盅, 衷), “少” (抄, 钞, 砂, 纱; blacklist: 妙 miào); flat-tongue families, such as “宗” (棕, 综, 踪), “则” (侧, 测, 厕), “曾” (曾, 增 and 缯 are all of the z group). Remembering one representative character for a family beats remembering ten scattered characters; exceptions (the blacklist) are entered on their own. Second, **rules of co-occurrence**: the three closed finals (*hekou*) ua, uai and uang in Putonghua **combine only with zh, ch and sh, and never with z, c or s** (抓 zhuā, 揣 chuāi, 双 shuāng) — in a flat-tongue dialect accent, a character read like the “杂” type, so long as its final is ua, uai or uang, is bound to be right if one retroflexes with one's eyes shut. Third, **sifting the list of common characters**: among the three thousand five hundred characters in general use, characters with a retroflex initial are about three tenths; read them yourself from the list, record yourself, and tick them off word by word — the clumsy method has the highest recall, and it serves for any dialect area.
:::
:::

::: example Case two: n and l of one family {#ex-nl}
Across great stretches of Southwestern Mandarin (*guanhua*), Xiang and Gan, “牛=刘” and “女=旅”. The difficulty is not pronunciation (both sounds can be produced in the dialects of every locality). It lies in remembering **which character belongs where**.
::: solution
Analogy from the phonetic component works best in this family. Phonetic-component families fixed as n: “乃” (奶, 耐, 衲), “尼” (泥, 怩, 昵), “农” (浓, 侬). Phonetic-component families fixed as l: “卢” (炉, 芦, 颅, 庐, 轳), “吕” (旅, 铝, 闾), “洛” (烙, 骆), “良” (浪, 狼, 廊; blacklist: 酿 niàng). Characters with the two readings n and l in the dialects mostly happen to fall into the families of these two strings of phonetic components, so the analogy is extremely efficient; what one is not sure of is taken back to the dictionary. The ultimate criterion is still the dictionary: **go once a day through the six common characters “内、年、暖、牛、你、女”**. The greater part of the marks which candidates from dialect-accent areas lose on the paper is lost on these six characters.
:::
:::

::: example Case three: the dispute over the jurisdictions of f and h {#ex-fh}
In the dialects of Xiang, Hakka, Yue and some subgroups of Jiang–Huai, “湖=服” and “花=发” are not the same sound, and yet they tangle with each other: some keep only h, some keep only f, and some divide the ground by the final (they mix before the u final).
::: solution
First find which camp you are in (test yourself by recording the four groups “湖服、花发、灰飞、方荒”). Then memorise Putonghua's ledger of the distribution: **the group “无、文、万、武、微、未、网” is often read f/v in the dialect accent, and is read in Putonghua with a zero initial (spelt w/u)** — “武” is not read fǔ; while “夫、服、福、防、分、飞” and other characters of the old *fei*, *fu* and *feng* initials are uniformly f in Putonghua, and never change to h. What is left, the character-by-character dispute over ‘f or h’ (“荒/方”, “混/份”), rests on the ledger of character origins: characters of the *fei* group are read f, and characters of the *xiao* and *xia* initials are read h — once one has made a start in Chinese historical phonology, one table is in hand (see the family of correspondence rules in [[phonology/to-mandarin]]); before that start, it is enough to mark them character by character from the list of common characters.
:::
:::

## Two cases for finals: front and back nasals, and ü

::: example The dispute of “根” and “更” {#ex-nng}
In most of Southwestern Mandarin, and in some areas of Jin, the front nasal -n and the back nasal -ng merge into one set (often ending in nasalisation or in -ŋ). Whoever has “陈=程”, “斤=经” and “宾=冰” as homophones belongs to this case.
::: solution
On the side of pronunciation: for -n the tip of the tongue meets the upper alveolar ridge, and for -ng the root of the tongue meets the soft palate. Let the native speaker, facing a mirror, place fingers on the chin and on the Adam's apple respectively, and feel the difference of place (the vowels are the same, and the places of release differ; see the section on nasal finals in [[sounds-of-mandarin/finals]]). On the side of assigning characters: en and eng, and in and ing, differ sharply in how many characters they contain, and the boundaries of the phonetic components are clear, so whole families can be moved across. Remember “生、成、名、平、明” as one back-nasal family (phonetic component “生”: 牲, 笙, 甥; “成”: 城, 盛, 诚), and remember “人、民、真、身” as one front-nasal family (the “真” family: 镇, 慎, 填 and 滇 are all front-nasal). Anyone for whom “根” and “更” are homophonous in the dialect must, when memorising word lists, put a red tick against the back-nasal family — it is not that you cannot learn the pronunciation; it is that **your ear has never been assigned a household registration for these two families**.
:::
:::

::: example The case of the missing ü {#ex-u-umlaut}
Quite a few dialect accents of the Central Plains and the southwest have no round-mouth finals (*cuokou*): ü is read as i (“鱼=疑”), read as u (“女=努”), or simply merged into the class of u.
::: solution
The shortest route for supplying ü is this: first produce i, then, keeping the tongue position, round the lips — one movement yields ü (the ‘single-phone account’ in [[sounds-of-mandarin/finals]] here becomes a gymnastic exercise). In the assigning of characters, the character-families of the ü-series finals are small and easy to count (“鱼、女、居、去、学、全、宣、军”, eight families governing the great majority of the characters), and can be memorised in half a day. The hard part is **orthographic discipline**: u written after j, q or x is in fact read ü (去 qù), and the boundary with “路 lù、粗 cū” is sharp — one rule of the orthography puts an end, at a single stroke, to the dispute of the two cases ([[sounds-of-mandarin/syllables-pinyin]]).
:::
:::

## The ledger of tone is the most complicated: the categories match, the values do not

::: theorem Correspondence of tone categories, and ‘changing hats’ {#rule-dulei-duizhi}
Between most dialects and Putonghua there is a roughly regular **correspondence of tone categories** (the first tone, yinping, high level; the second, yangping, rising; the third, shang, dipping; and the fourth, qu, falling, each go back to their own ‘historical household registration’; see the ledger of tone categories in [[sounds-of-mandarin/tones]]). The most obstinate layer of a dialect accent is often not the initials or the finals, but **a dislocation of tone values**: your ‘first tone’ may be a low level 22, and once it is set onto the first tone of Putonghua (55, high level) it becomes the chief culprit of a ‘洋腔’ ('a foreign-sounding accent'). The method of correction is called ‘changing hats’: the characters do not have to be learnt over again (the tone categories do not change); one only replaces, as a whole, the actual pitch curve of each category with the five-level values of Putonghua — once the four hats have been changed, seven tenths of the accent is gone.
:::

::: example Where the entering-tone characters go {#ex-rusheng}
Around Chengdu and Chongqing the entering tone, as a whole category, has gone over to the second tone (“食” is read like “时”); in Nanjing, Hefei, Yangzhou and other places an entering-tone category is kept of itself. For a speaker of a dialect which keeps the entering tone, or whose assignment of the entering tone differs from that of Beijing, from where should the account be made up when learning Putonghua?
::: solution
First draw up the general ledger: **Putonghua has no entering tone**. The old entering-tone characters have already undergone ‘distribution of the entering tone among the other tones’, and are scattered into the four tones of today; Beijing's scattering of them has no short method, and depends entirely on memory. But there is one reliable coarse sieve: **among second-tone characters, any with a nasal coda (-n, -ng) is in every case not an entering-tone character** (人, 忙 and 年 are all 舒声, 'unchecked'), and the batch that remains within the second tone, with no nasal coda, is then taken through the table character by character — coarse sieve plus a pass through the table, and the list of common characters suspected of the entering tone can be cut very short; then memorise, as a whole family, a batch of high-frequency entering-tone characters, “一六七八九十、白食节月雪足曲国”, and the account balances. The common entering-tone characters, several hundred of them, form a family of their own (一六七八九十、白食节月雪足曲国); memorising the whole family is the right path. Get one family learnt thoroughly, and it amounts to balancing the thousand-year ledger between the dialect and the pronunciation of Beijing (for the historical ledger of the entering tone see [[history-of-chinese/middle-chinese-language]], and for the map of the dialects today see [[dialects/mandarin-dialects]]).
:::
:::

::: intuition The ear before the tongue: the point of minimal pairs {#intuition-ting}
The first lesson of training in correction is often not in the mouth, but in the ear. In someone who merges n and l, flat and retroflex, or front and back nasals, the perceptual system has already gathered the two phonemes into one family — if the difference cannot be heard, it naturally cannot be produced (perceptual assimilation of phonemes; see perceptual categorisation in [[linguistics/phonetics-phonology]]). The remedy is listening discrimination by ‘minimal pairs’: 根/更, 男/蓝 and 四/事 are played at random and ticked on paper, and only when the errors stand at a certain proportion does one go on to pronunciation practice. Experimental psychology shows that an adult's rebuilding of a sense of opposition depends on **high-intensity feedback** (the answer checked at once); soaking in the audio and ‘grinding the ear’, without judging right from wrong, brings a return close to zero. Ten minutes a day, and in two weeks the decibels show it — first let the ear register the household records of the two families, and only then can the tongue receive its posting.
:::

::: warning Three things to avoid
First, avoid **shame of home**: treating the dialect as a stain, and ‘giving up’ one's hometown speech before speaking Putonghua — transfer of a system depends not on abstinence but on setting the two side by side, and bilingual ability (dialect and Putonghua) is itself an asset. Second, avoid **overcorrection**: filling the mouth with exaggerated retroflexes, and dragging a falling tone through every sentence, in order to sound standard; the impression on the ear is worse than a touch of dialect accent (‘矫枉过正’, 'bending the crooked past the straight', is, in sound change, a real social judgement). Third, avoid **reading a sound off the writing, and gags about old pronunciations**: forcing a reading by the phonetic component (“函” read close to “含” can still count as analogy; reading “昧” as “未” is coining a character), or bringing abolished old readings back as though they were ‘cultured’ (after the unified readings of the 1985 *Shenyin biao* 审音表 (*Table of Reviewed Pronunciations*), “确” is no longer read qiǎo and “质” is no longer read zhǐ, and so on of that kind) — the referees of the standard pronunciation are the *Shenyin biao* and the *Xiandai Hanyu cidian* 现代汉语词典 (*The Contemporary Chinese Dictionary*), not ‘what our teacher taught back then’.
:::

::: history
The correction of dialect accent is not a modern invention starting from nothing: in the Yuan, Zhou Deqing 周德清 wrote *Zhongyuan yinyun* 中原音韵 (*Rhymes of the Central Plain*) for 正语 ('rectifying the pronunciation'); the dispute in the Ming over *Hongwu zhengyun* 洪武正韵 (*Correct Rhymes of the Hongwu Reign*), and *Zhengyin shilüe* 正音释略 (*Brief Notes on Standard Pronunciation*) and *Zhengyin qieyun zhizhang* 正音切韵指掌 (*A Palm Guide to Standard Pronunciation and Qieyun*) in the official schools of the Qing, ran in one line as far as the Qing book bureau for the standard pronunciation of Mandarin, and ‘正音’ ('standard pronunciation') was throughout a prominent field in education in the general language. The turn to a scientific footing came in the twentieth century: the dialect surveys of Zhao Yuanren 赵元任 in the 1920s opened the age of transcription at the level of the phone; the national general survey of dialects of 1956—1960 took a full inventory, and each province compiled a *Fangyan gaikuang* 方言概况 (*General Accounts of the Dialects*); the first edition, in 1962, of *Hanyu fangyin zihui* 汉语方音字汇 (*A Compendium of Character Readings in the Dialects*) set the character readings of sixteen dialect sites out in one large comparative table — **the rules of correspondence turned from empirical mnemonics into a table one can look up**. Thereafter, the section ‘difficult points of the dialect accent of this area’ in local textbooks of the dialect accent and in test syllabuses has in every case been that table extended to the level of the district and the county. The correction work you do is the private edition of this learning of ‘surveying the country’.
:::

## What follows

The results of correcting a dialect accent have to be accepted by reading aloud and by speaking; for the points at which the test deducts marks, and for the self-check list, see [[sounds-of-mandarin/putonghua-test]]. For the grouping and the lineage of the dialect accents of each place, see [[dialects/mandarin-dialects]] and the courses [[dialects/wu]], [[dialects/cantonese]] and others. The causal ledger of the historical strata of sounds (why ‘distribution of the entering tone among the other tones’, and why ‘devoicing of voiced initials’) is in [[phonology/to-mandarin]] and [[history-of-chinese/lexical-history]]. The end-of-term work for this chapter has only one item: find a recording of your own Putonghua, and for each of the three layers ‘cases of initials — cases of finals — the hat of tone value’ draw up one list of problems; then, working from the lists, spend two weeks on correction in batches, and after two weeks record the same piece for comparison — letting the data speak is the way phonetics receives a guest.

::: summary
- A dialect accent is a wholesale transfer of the sound system: a merger follows a merger, a missing sound is stopped with a near sound, and tone values are fastened on like hats — and so it can be counted, and corrected in batches.
- Three cases for initials: flat and retroflex (analogy from the phonetic component + the co-occurrence method + sifting the character list), n/l (families of phonetic components + a blacklist), f/h (first decide which camp you are in, then keep the account by the old sound-groups).
- Two cases for finals: front and back nasals (gymnastics of place + assignment by families of phonetic components), ü (gymnastics of lip-rounding + the character list of the eight families + orthographic discipline).
- Tone is a project of ‘changing hats’: the correspondence of tone categories does not change, and the tone values are replaced as wholes according to the five-level chart; transfer of tone value is the most hidden, and the recorded curve is the only honest mirror.
- The position correction takes: accents have no higher and lower, and situations have a norm; avoid shame of home, avoid overcorrection, and avoid gags about old pronunciations.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise A self-test on six words {level=1}
Read aloud in Putonghua “临时、牛奶、黄蜂、根、更、机会” — following the three cases and two examples of this lesson, set one dialect test-point for each of the six words (hint: n/l, f/h, front and back nasals, flat tongue and retroflex tongue, and ü after j, q and x), and judge whether your own pronunciation lands on the point.
::: solution
The six test-points: 临 (l/n, and the front nasal -in), 牛 (the initial n), 蜂 (the back nasal -eng, against the front nasal of “分”), 更 (the back nasal -èng, against “根” -èn), 旅 (the initial l plus the final ü), 机 (i after j, set against the “资翘舌” family, testing the flat/retroflex confusion of “字/四”). The method of self-testing: read slowly and record, and compare the notation word by word with that in the *Xianhan* 现汉; wherever ‘you yourself cannot hear the difference’ corresponds exactly to a merged item on which ‘the ear has not been assigned a household registration’ — mergers of this kind are solved by listening lessons alone. First build minimal pairs in which ‘the opposition of the two sounds can be heard’ (根/更, 男/蓝), ten groups a day.
:::
:::

::: exercise A dialect-accent check-up form {level=1}
Draw up for yourself a ‘three-layer check-up form’: four items for initials (flat and retroflex, n/l, f/h, r-), three items for finals (front and back nasals, ü, er), and one item for tone (the tone value of the first tone). For each item test yourself with two words and record them, marking ‘merged/split’. What use does this form have in later study?
::: solution
Suggested test words: flat and retroflex (四—事), n/l (牛—刘), f/h (福—湖), r- (人—银), front and back nasals (根—更), ü (鱼—疑), er (whether “二” and “而” have a retroflex movement; a dialect accent with no er commonly reads them like the “阿、额” type), the first tone (高, testing whether its tone value is high or low). Every place marked ‘merged’ is the **whole** list of the problems in your Putonghua accent — later workbooks, the correction of reading aloud, and preparation for the test all set out from this form, and there is no need to drill, into the bargain, faults you do not have. It is also a miniature of the file opened for an informant in [[dialects/fieldwork]].
:::
:::

::: exercise Looking up the correspondence table {level=1}
In your hometown speech, are “吃” and “七” homophonous? And “短” and “党”? For each of the three groups “吃七、短党、人银”, write one line: how they sound in the hometown speech (same/different), how they sound in Putonghua (same/different), and one rule of correspondence inferred from that.
::: solution
A sample (the Wuhan type): 吃—七／dialect: same／Putonghua: different → the *zhi* series has merged into the *jing* group, and Putonghua must ‘assign the retroflex household registrations’ over again; 短—党／dialect: same／Putonghua: different → front and back nasals have merged, and -an/-aŋ need to divide into two families; 人—银／dialect: same／Putonghua: different (rén／yín) → characters of the *ri* initial and characters with a zero initial have flowed together, and the r- initial of Putonghua is a priority account to be made up. Three lines are one miniature table of correction — only when the table is right can the workbook be opened.
:::
:::

::: exercise The drill of changing hats {level=2}
Read straight through the four first-tone characters “高、开、山、青”: first with the tone value of the first tone in your dialect, then as a high level 55; record the two stretches, and compare the fundamental-frequency curves with how they sound. Write a report of two hundred characters: about what degree is your starting tone value? Where is the difficulty of changing hats (unable to get up to the height? unable to hold it level?)?
::: solution
Three elements of an acceptable report: ① state an estimated reading of the dialect's first-tone value (for example a low level 22, a mid level 33, or a high fall of the Sichuan–Chongqing type — the measurement decides); ② a comparative screenshot, or a description, of the two curves (the height of the starting point, the slope); ③ attribute the difficulty — those from a low-level area commonly ‘cannot hold it once it is raised to degree 5’, and those from an entering-tone area commonly have it ‘short and steep, like the fourth tone’. The teacher's line: wherever the data of a report are self-consistent (recording, curve and conclusion, the three matching), it counts as excellent, and a ‘tone value that is the standard answer’ is not required.
:::
:::

::: exercise Laying out minimal pairs {level=2}
For a learner whose mother tongue is the speech of Chengdu, design a ten-day practice of ‘separating an and ang into two families’: list a table of minimal pairs for each day (four groups a day), one tongue-twister or dialogue task a day, and the standard for the self-test.
::: solution
A reference skeleton: Day 1 班—帮, 关—光; Day 2 三—桑, 丹—当; Day 3 山—商, 翻—方; four groups every day, and the previous day reviewed in rotation; each day read aloud along with a recording, then, off the page, do ‘dictation’ (a reading-aloud application on the phone calls the words, and one writes them oneself), and eight tenths written correctly is a pass; on Day 7 move to a tongue-twister (the classic “扁担长，板凳宽” The carrying pole is long, the bench is wide. (Our translation.), aimed squarely at the overlap of an and ang); on Day 9 a dialogue for two (one asks “饭馆在哪儿” ‘Where is the restaurant?’ (Our translation.) and the other answers “广场东巷” ‘the east lane of the square’ (Our translation.)); on Day 10 read aloud a short text of three hundred characters, count the rate of misreading an and ang, and if the errors are three or fewer move on to the next case. The point: **concentrate on one case, set the oppositions in batches, and test yourself every day** — this is the general mould of training in correction; change the name of the case and it is ten days of n/l, or ten days of flat and retroflex.
:::

::: exercise The boundary of correction {level=3}
Someone says: “我普通话二甲都过了，回家还得被我妈说‘不好好说话’。” ‘I have already passed Second Class, Grade A in Putonghua, and when I get home my mother still says I “don't talk properly”.’ (Our translation.) From the two concepts ‘situational register’ and ‘identity’, write a commentary of three hundred characters: what does a ‘Putonghua with no accent’, fully assimilated in its pronunciation, mean in reality? Where is the reasonable end point of education in correction?
::: hint
Materials you may use: your own experience of code-switching between dialect and Putonghua; any popular piece on the sociolinguistic topic ‘accent and identity’; the life in two varieties of the informant in [[dialects/fieldwork]].

::: solution
Arguments for reference: ① ‘no accent’ is in essence the giving up of one of the two systems, dialect and Putonghua, and the cost is that the register is halved — family, fellow townsmen, and gags in the hometown accent all have to be abandoned; ② the aim of the norm is **intelligibility and fitness to the situation**, not ‘no such person found’: Second Class, Grade A is already enough for discharging one's duties in any public situation, and the dialect accent that remains is a mark of identity, not a defect; ③ the reasonable end point of education in correction = the ability in two varieties to ‘be standard when one ought to be standard, and carry an accent when one ought to carry an accent’ + metalinguistic consciousness (knowing on which layer one is making the choice). Marking looks at whether the argument has used the two concepts ‘register/identity’ and has given a criterion one can apply; sentimentality, and the view that the dialect is superior, each take off half the marks.
