The four everyday characters 姐, 略, 全 and 冬 are spelt jie, lue, quan and dong — and between the 'written form' of these four finals and the vowels actually pronounced there is a story at every point. The e in jie is in fact pronounced ê［ɛ］; the üe of lue hides a ü whose two dots are not written; the u of quan is round-mouth rather than closed; the o of dong is simply u with its face changed. The final is the stretch of the syllable that carries the most information: the medial it brings shows whether an initial can combine with it, the main vowel it brings shows whether the sound is bright, and the coda it brings shows whether a poem can rhyme. This chapter opens a three-layer file on the final — structure, category, and the four classes (*kaiqi hecuo*: open, even-teeth, closed and round-mouth) — and keeps apart two things that are constantly taken for one: the **final** (a constituent of the syllable) and the **rhyme** (a unit of poetic metre).

## The structure of the final: medial, nucleus and coda

::: definition The final, and medial, nucleus and coda {#def-yunmu}
Everything after the initial, and before the tone, is the **final**. A final has at most three layers: the **medial** (the on-glide, which can only be one of i, u or ü, and which stands at the very front), the **nucleus** (the main vowel, the strongest and the longest sound, and the one layer that cannot be missing), and the **coda** (the closing part, which may be the vowel i or u, or the nasal consonant n or ng). Thus 严 yán ［iɛn］ has medial i, nucleus ê and coda n; 哥 gē ［kɤ］ has only a nucleus; 太 tài ［tʰai］ has nucleus a and coda i.
:::

The nucleus is obligatory; medial and coda depend on the particular final. In the terminology one speaks separately of a 'vowel coda' and a 'consonant coda'. Putonghua (standard Mandarin) has only two vowel codas, i and u (the o written in ao in fact moves the tongue towards u), and only two consonant codas, n and ng — the stock of endings is far smaller than that of the initials, which is precisely the look of a Chinese syllable, 'broad where it opens, narrow where it closes'. For the story, on the historical level, of how the -p, -t and -k codas of the entering tone died out in the northern varieties, see [[history-of-chinese/middle-chinese-language]].

::: example Taking the structure of 关 guān apart {#ex-guan}
Analyse the final of 关, and say which of the four classes it belongs to.
::: solution
The final is uan: medial u (a closed medial), nucleus a, coda n (a front apical nasal; at the close the tip of the tongue meets the alveolar ridge — set it against the tongue-root closure of ng, and producing the two side by side will keep them apart). It carries a u medial, and so belongs to the closed class. The test by which a structure is taken apart: draw the syllable out, 'quick in front, bright behind, short at the tail' — first pass lightly over u (the medial is light and quick), let the sound settle on a (the nucleus), and finally lift the tongue tip once and finish (the coda n). If you can 'draw it out', you can take it apart.
:::
:::

## An account of the thirty-nine finals

Following the Huang–Liao textbook, Putonghua has 39 finals in all, divided by structure into three groups:

1. **Simple finals**, ten of them: a, o, e, ê, i, u, ü, er, -i［ɿ］, -i［ʅ］. The last two are the apical vowels of the previous chapter (appearing after z, c, s and after zh, ch, sh, r respectively). ê used on its own occurs only in the exclamation 欸, and appears chiefly inside ie and üe, where it serves as the nucleus. er is a phonemic 'retroflex gesture plus a central vowel', and serves as the whole final only of a few characters (儿, 耳, 二); it is also the source of rhotacisation (*erhua*; see [[sounds-of-mandarin/sandhi]]).
2. **Compound finals**, thirteen of them: ai, ei, ao, ou, ia, ie, ua, uo, üe, iao, iou, uai, uei. Compound finals are divided again into three kinds according to 'where the bright sound sits'. ai, ei, ao and ou begin with the main vowel and glide towards the coda, with the resonant stretch in front, and are called **front-resonant**. ia, ie, ua, uo and üe begin with a medial, with the bright sound behind, and are called **back-resonant**. iao, iou, uai and uei stack 'a medial plus a front-resonant final': light at both ends, bright in the middle, then closing towards the coda, and they are called **mid-resonant**. The boundary among the three can be tested with a single finger. Draw the pronunciation out, and see whether the instant at which the jaw is most open falls at the beginning, in the middle, or in the later stretch. The coda of a front-resonant final is light and short, and [i] and [u] retain only a direction; the medial of a back-resonant final is likewise light, and the main vowel carries the loudness of the whole syllable.
3. **Nasal finals**, sixteen of them: eight ending in -n — an, ian, uan, üan, en, in, uen, ün; eight ending in -ng — ang, iang, uang, ueng, eng, ing, ong, iong.

The three groups sum to 10 + 13 + 16 = 39. The number shifts from textbook to textbook. Some strike -i［ɿ］ and -i［ʅ］ off the account of simple finals (saying that they are 'harmonising vowels' and do not count as independent finals); some list er on its own as a 'special final'; and so one meets accounts of 37 and of 38. The Huang–Liao figure of 39 is the most current account. Remembering the number is not a matter of reciting it, but of keeping the books clear: every final is either among the simple, or among the compound, or among the nasal, and not one is left out. Vowels that the dialects have in extra (at some localities of Jin, 〔ʅ〕 stands as a syllable by itself; Wu has short vowels with a tightened throat) and vowels they lack (most Southwestern Mandarin keeps only the two classes i and u, the round-mouth class having merged into the even-teeth or the closed, so that 去 is read close to 气 and 出 is read in a coarsened form) show themselves the moment the two are set side by side.

The way to learn the table off this account, firmer than reciting straight across from any one corner, is 'location by coordinates'. Given a final, first ask about structure (simple? what coda does it take?), then ask about the four classes (which medial?), and once the two lines of its registration match, only one cell on the chart is left for it. The type of question that works best when classmates examine one another is 'I make the mouth-shape, you name the sound': one person rounds the lips and produces ü, the other must give the single answer from among i, ü, u and ê, and then the two check one another's 'registration'. How well the table of finals is mastered directly decides the speed of three things that come afterwards: learning metre (rhyming by level and oblique tones), learning the dialects (correspondences among simple vowels), and learning historical phonology (the merging of rhyme classes, *she*, and of rhyme groups). It is worth two extra evenings of private study on this chapter.

::: example The 'swallowed sound' of iou is not a swallowed sound {#ex-iou}
The finals of 牛 niú, 六 liù and 秋 qiū are iou, written iu; the final of 归 guī is uei, written ui. Has speech swallowed the o and the e, or has Pinyin omitted them?
::: solution
The spelling omits them (iou → iu, uei → ui, uen → un, and only after an initial); speech does not. Read 牛 slowly and it is plainly i, then o, then u; only when the stream of speech is fast and the tone is light does it weaken to something near ［niəu］ → ［niə］. At the level of analysis, therefore, 'iu' is the two phonemes i and u plus a nucleus that is not written — and within phonological theory there is a real disagreement about how iou is grouped (some analyses treat ou as nucleus plus coda, the nucleus o being an allophone of the phoneme /o/). Our structural analysis follows the shape of the spelling itself, 'medial i + nucleus o + coda u'. Remember two things. Omission in the spelling has a phonetic condition (it is omitted only after an initial; with a zero initial one writes you, wei, wen). The placing of the tone mark has a remedy (in 流 liú the tone mark falls on u, landing on the nearest vowel by the spelling after the omission).
:::
:::

## Why the spellings of 'ong' and 'ing' each hide one thing

::: definition Three places where spelling and sound value part company {#def-yunmu-pinyin}
In the spelling of the nasal finals there are three places where 'the letters have moved house'. (1) The nucleus of iong is a round-mouth sound of the ü kind, and the spelling records it as iong (the actual sound is near ［yŋ］, and it begins with lip-rounding). (2) The 'o' of ong and of iong is not read 喔; its tongue position is near u: the sound value of the final of 冬 dōng is near ［ʊŋ］, that is, 'an ng-rhyme of u'. (3) The e and the i of ing and eng weaken in a fast reading, and the central vowel in ［iŋ］［əŋ］ is short and indistinct. All of this is an arrangement made by the *Scheme for the Chinese Phonetic Alphabet* (《汉语拼音方案》) so that the system should be tidy and the spelt form short — **the Scheme is the product of a compromise between phonemic principle and practical use, not a transcript of the sounds** (in full at [[sounds-of-mandarin/syllables-pinyin]]).
:::

::: example Filing 东, 英 and 雍 {#ex-dong-ying-yong}
Write out the structure of the finals of the three characters 东 dōng, 英 yīng and 雍 yōng, and mark the 'device' in the spelling of each.
::: solution
东: the final is ong, and in structure it is 'a nucleus ［u］ plus an ng coda'; writing it as o is the Scheme's arrangement of letters; a zero-initial character is written dong. 英: the final ing = medial i + an ng coda (the nucleus weakened); with a zero initial it is written ying, and y is a device of the writing. 雍: the final is iong, and the actual sound is near ［yŋ］; with a zero initial it is written yong, and the round-mouth ü takes no dots after y; to analyse it as 'medial ü + an ng coda' fits the pronunciation best. Read one after another, the three characters are precisely three parallel lines of the 'monophthongisation' of the back-resonant nasal finals of Putonghua — the letters differ, and each part of the mouth does its own work.
:::
:::

::: note Distinguishing o and uo {#note-o-uo}
The main vowels of o and uo are of one origin, and their positions are complementary: o appears only after bilabials and labiodentals (波 bo, 坡 po, 摸 mo, 佛 fo), and uo appears after the remaining initials (多 duo, 郭 guo, 窝 wo〔zero initial〕). Some textbooks on that ground judge the two to be conditioned variants of a single phoneme, while the grammar used in teaching often counts two finals. This course, following the account of 39, counts two, and one should at the same time remember the disagreement in transcription, that 'the o in b o is not full, and listened to slowly is near 〔uɔ〕'. Both ways of putting it are found in the hands of noted scholars. In an examination, follow the textbook; in a survey, follow the mouth.
:::

::: warning A final that 'has i and has u' is not one with 'medial, nucleus and coda all present'
✗ Splitting 'bi' into b + i and then insisting that there is a medial; ✗ failing to see an e in 'ben' and calling it 'without a nucleus'. ✓ i, u or ü is a medial only when it stands in **medial position**: 边 biān is medial i + nucleus a + coda n; 逼 bī ［pi］ has only a nucleus. The nucleus is always there — not finding the letters 'a, o, e' does not mean that there is no main vowel. The e of ben is the nucleus, and the i of bing doubles as the nucleus (there is disagreement about how the vocalic part of ing is grouped; state which textbook you follow). A judgement always returns to two points: which sound is the loudest when the syllable is drawn out (the nucleus), and whether a light, quick i, u or ü stands before it (the medial).
:::

By the standard of [[#def-sihu]], what are the four classes of the four syllables 元 yuán, 炯 jiǒng, 翁 wēng and 尔 ěr, in that order?
- [ ] open, even-teeth, open, closed
- [x] round-mouth, even-teeth, closed, open

::: solution

The four-class table of the Huang–Liao textbook lists iong under round-mouth (following the sound value ［yŋ］); if your textbook lists it as even-teeth, follow the textbook and state which you follow. 翁, ueng, is closed. 尔, er, has no medial, and its main vowel is central: open. What this question really tests is the habit of 'stating the standard before stating the conclusion'. To avoid dispute, the account of 39 in the main text records iong under the round-mouth class.

::: quiz
家 jiā and 麻 má belong to the same Ma rhyme in the Pingshui rhymes, but the two are not the same in their (　).
- [ ] nucleus
- [x] medial
- [ ] coda
- [ ] tone category
::: solution
A traditional rhyme (a unit of poetic metre) **takes no account of the medial**. The main vowels of 家 ia and 麻 a belong alike to the Ma rhyme group, and so the two can rhyme with each other; but as finals, constituents of the syllable, one carries an i medial (even-teeth) and one does not (open). Rhyming is broad and Pinyin is strict, and the 'distinction of the broad and the strict' between the two systems is entered from here — for the rhymes of metre, see [[poetry-metrics/rhyme]].

::: remark Letter names and sound values
The vowel letters, read aloud in class: a is 啊, o is 喔, e is 鹅, i is 衣, u is 乌, ü is 迂. Each is a name given to the letter by way of a zero-initial syllable, convenient when one has to refer to it, and not a record of sound value (o is called 喔 [ɔ], while the o in 波 is again near [uɔ]). This is the same pedagogical design as the name-readings of the initials, 玻坡摸佛. The Scheme has itself declared that the name of a letter and the value of that letter inside a spelling are two different things. Whenever there is a dispute about 'how a letter is read', first make clear whether the question is the name or the value.
:::

## The four classes: households of finals by the medial

::: definition The four classes {#def-sihu}
Finals are divided into four grades according to whether there is a medial and of what sort. The **open class**: no medial, and the nucleus is not i, u or ü (a, ai, an, ang, e, ei, en, eng, o, ong, er, -i［ɿ］, -i［ʅ］). The **even-teeth class**: the medial or the nucleus is i (i, ia, ie, iao, iou, ian, iang, ing, and others). The **closed class**: the medial or the nucleus is u (u, ua, uo, uai, uei, uan, uen, uang, ueng, ong). The **round-mouth class**: the medial or the nucleus is ü (ü, üe, üan, ün, iong). The names of the four classes come from the study of the rhyme tables (*dengyun*). In the Qing they were subdivided, from the two classes 'open and closed', into four, and they have been in use ever since.
:::

::: definition Three articulatory features of vowels {#def-yuanyin-tezheng}
A vowel is fixed by three states of the mouth: **front and back** of the tongue (i front, u back, a central and somewhat back), **height** of the tongue (i and u close, a open, e and ê in between), and **rounding or spreading** of the lips (u and ü rounded, i spread). From this a simple vowel can be 'located'. i is a high front unrounded vowel; ü is only one degree more rounded than i; u is set further back than ü, and its tongue position is higher. Telling the three sounds of 姐, 举 and 居 apart lies entirely among these three cells. The 'trapezoid' of the vowel chart is the projection of these three features onto two axes (front–back, high–low). Draw one yourself, and each of the ten simple finals falls into its place.
:::

::: example 鱼 and 五 {#ex-yu-wu}
Learners from the dialects keep reading 鱼 yú as 无 wú. Using [[#def-yuanyin-tezheng]], draw up a prescription.
::: solution
The nuclei of the two sounds differ by a single feature. ü is a high front rounded vowel, and u is a high back rounded vowel: the difference is front against back, and the lip-rounding is the same. The prescription has three steps. ① First produce i (tongue front and high, lips spread), hold the tongue still, and only gather the two lips into a round — what comes out must be ü. ② Produce u beside it, and feel the difference as the tongue draws back and the lips come to a round. ③ Practise the minimal oppositions 鱼—无, 学—活 and 月—袜: first discriminate by ear, then repeat, in slow pairs, and only then in the stream of speech. This merger is commonest in the Southwestern Mandarin area (去 is read close to 裤). To correct 'ü read as u', first correct the action of 'round-mouth' itself — in many varieties an 'ü' whose lips are not round and whose tongue is not front has in fact slid towards i.
:::
:::

::: application The 'craft' of the final: medial, nucleus and coda in broadcasting and in singing
Traditional *quyi* (the storytelling and singing arts) speaks of 字正腔圆, 'the character correct and the melody round', and the secret lies precisely in the three-way division of this chapter. The head of the character (the initial) must be 'gripped and at once let go'; the belly of the character must be opened out and set firm; the tail of the character must 'bring the sound home to its place'. Sing 光 guāng, and the roundness of the vocal line is in the lengthening of a, while the coda ng must be closed off and held, or else 光 slides into 瓜. Reciting 安 ān is the same: if the tip of the tongue does not meet the alveolar ridge, the sound of the character 'scatters'. The three-character formula of training for the broadcast voice, 'grip, open, close', is a performer's manual to the medial, the nucleus and the coda. One does not learn the structure in order to do exercises. From this point you know on which segment 'a round vocal line' is round, and to which gesture 'closing on the rhyme' returns.
:::

The four classes are not merely a craze for classification. They lock most tightly into the rules by which initials combine. **The front-apical z, c, s, the back-apical zh, ch, sh, r, and the tongue-root g, k, h combine only with the open and the closed classes, and not with the even-teeth or the round-mouth** (the i of 资 is the apical vowel -i［ɿ］, and counts as open). **The tongue-surface j, q, x combine only with the even-teeth and the round-mouth** (基 and 居), exactly complementary to the group before. **The bilabials b, p, m and the labiodental f do not combine with the closed class that has a u medial** (f combined with ua, as in 花, occurs only in the dialects — dialect readings of the sort 'a person from Wuxi reads 花 as fā' arise from this restriction on the closed class). The detail of the rules is verified in full, against the syllable table, at [[sounds-of-mandarin/syllables-pinyin]]; here we first set up the registration of the four classes. On seeing a syllable, first look up which class it is, and the range of initials it may take narrows at once. The rules of combination also have the false appearance of 'exceptions'. Some people cite 翁 wēng and 嗡 wēng and say that f and w combine with the closed class — but 翁 is ueng with a zero initial, and 嗡 is an alternative reading of ong, and neither has anything to do with the labials. Others take 否 fǒu and 某 mǒu and say that the labials combine with ou — but ou belongs to the open class, and there is no breach of the rule. The right move, in checking an alleged exception, is to verify the character's registration under the four classes and then check it against the table: eight out of ten 'exceptions' die of a registration that one has oneself reported wrong.

::: example How many layers 雪 xuě really has {#ex-xue}
Analyse the final of 雪, and answer four questions. What is the medial? What is the nucleus? What has been omitted in the spelling? Which of the four classes does it belong to?
::: solution
The final is üe. The medial is ü. The nucleus is ê［ɛ］ — the spelling uses the letter e for it (ie and üe are alike in this, just as with the character 严 in def-yunmu). What is 'omitted' is the two dots on ü (the spelling rule by which ü, standing after j, q, x or y, drops its dots). There is a ü medial, so it belongs to the round-mouth class. Testing the trajectory: it glides from a round mouth towards half-open, the lips go from rounded to spread, and the jaw drops. What makes 雪 'pleasant to hear' lies precisely in this trajectory from close to open.
:::
:::

## Rhyme and final: how the two systems divide the work {#sec-yun-yunmu}

A 'rhyme' is a unit of poetic metre. Traditional rhyming asks only whether nucleus and coda belong to the same group, and takes no account of the four classes; level and oblique tones are a further layer of rules (regulated verse, that is recent-style poetry, rhymes in the level tone, while a song lyric, *ci*, may change its rhyme). 关 uan and 山 an have different finals (one has a u medial, and one has none), yet by the rhyming of the modern pronunciation they both end in -n and their main vowels are close, and so they count as one set. In the Pingshui rhymes, 家 ia and 麻 a both belong to the Ma rhyme, while 回 uei and 雷 ei have different finals but both belong to the Hui rhyme. Set over against 'rhyme', there is a further old term, alliteration: the same initial is called alliteration (*shuangsheng*; 参差 and 踌躇), while a shared rhyme (*dieyun*) is the same rhyme (窈窕 and 彷徨). The beauty of sound in *lianmian* words, the disyllabic binomes, was pointed out by the ancients in just the four characters 双声叠韵, and the modern translation of that is precisely the system of initials and the system of finals. How broad or how strict rhyming is changes with the genre: ancient-style verse is broad, regulated verse is strict, and the rhymes of *qu* (song or aria) are another book again. When you cite 'this character and that are the same rhyme', first say clearly which rhyme dictionary you are using. When this atlas speaks of the 'Pingshui rhymes', it takes as its standard the line of the *Peiwen shiyun* (《佩文诗韵》, 'Peiwen Poetic Rhymes'); see [[poetry-metrics/rhyme]].

::: history
The analysis of 'rhyme' has two thousand years of accumulation in China. The *Qieyun* (《切韵》) line, in its division of rhymes, runs from more than a hundred and ninety categories to the hundred and six groups of the Pingshui rhymes, and was the official measure for the rhymes of poetry and of the rhapsody (*fu*). Rhyme tables set the initials out in rows and the rhyming characters in the cells, and by the Qing, in Pan Lei's 潘耒 *Leiyin* (《类音》, 'Classified Sounds'), the names of the four classes, open, even-teeth, closed and round-mouth, had already been set out with every feature clear. In the 1920s the term 韵母 ('final') entered the language of modern teaching; Ma Xulun 马叙伦, Li Jinxi 黎锦熙 and others each gave a definition of their own, and the three-part division 'medial–nucleus–coda' of the Huang–Liao textbook today has become the received account. Between the traditional account and the modern one, what most needs to be held clear is the division of labour between 'rhyme' and 'final': one draws the boundary for rhyming, and the other operates on the syllable.
:::

::: summary
- A final divides into the three layers medial, nucleus and coda. The nucleus must be there; the only vowel codas are i and u; the only consonant codas are n and ng.
- The general account of the 39 finals in the Huang–Liao textbook: 10 simple (including ê, er and the two apical vowels), 13 compound (front-resonant, back-resonant and mid-resonant), 16 nasal (eight in -n, eight in -ng).
- Spelling and sound value are systematically out of line: iong is round-mouth, ong is near ［uŋ］, the e of ie and of üe is ê, and iou, uei and uen are abbreviated after an initial. Pinyin is a scheme, not a transcript.
- The four classes assign households by the medial, and are the inner and outer faces of the rules by which initials combine: g, k, h, z, c, s, zh, ch, sh and r do not combine with even-teeth or round-mouth, and j, q and x combine only with even-teeth and round-mouth. o and uo are in complementary distribution, and the dialects and the textbooks keep different accounts.
- The three articulatory features of vowels (front and back of the tongue, high and low, rounded and spread lips) are the coordinates for correcting a dialect pronunciation. The difference between ü and u is the single cell 'front'.
- A 'rhyme' takes no account of the four classes; a 'final' goes down to the fineness of the syllable. To speak of rhyming, first say 'which rhyme'.
:::

For the four characters 看 kān, 见 jiàn, 关 guān and 元 yuán, assign each its class among the four according to the final, and for each give the medial, the nucleus and the coda.
::: solution
看, an: open class, no medial, nucleus a, coda n. 见, ian: even-teeth class, medial i, nucleus a, coda n. 关, uan: closed class, medial u, nucleus a, coda n. 元, üan: round-mouth class, medial ü (written y), nucleus a, coda n. The four characters are identical in nucleus and in coda, and take one class each — the difference among the four classes is purely a difference of medial.
:::

::: exercise Finding the nucleus {level=1}
Point out the nucleus (the main vowel) of each of the following syllables: duo, lun, xia, gei, jin.
::: solution
duo［uɔ］ has the nucleus o［ɔ］; lun［luən］ has the nucleus e［ə］ (uen is abbreviated in writing to un: abbreviated, not swallowed); xia［ɕia］ has the nucleus a; gei［kei］ has the nucleus e; jin［tɕin］ has the nucleus i (in may, in the analysis, be taken as i plus a coda n; if you follow the view that 'the nucleus ê has weakened', please state which you follow). Drawing the pronunciation out and finding 'the loudest one' is the most reliable homely method.

::: exercise Analysing the rhyme {level=2}
The rhyme words of Li Bai's 李白 《早发白帝城》 ('Setting Off Early from Baidi City') are 间、还、山 (朝辞白帝彩云间，千里江陵一日还……轻舟已过万重山 At dawn I leave Baidi amid the coloured clouds; a thousand li to Jiangling, and the journey is done in a day … the light boat has already passed ten thousand folds of mountains. (Our translation.)). For the modern pronunciation and for the Pingshui rhymes separately, state the finals of the three and the rhyme groups they belong to, and judge whether 'the same final' and 'the same rhyme' both hold.
::: solution
In the modern pronunciation: 间 jian［iɛn］, 还 huan［uan］, 山 shan［an］, with different finals (even-teeth / closed / open). In the Pingshui rhymes: all three belong to the fifteenth rhyme of the upper level tone, 删, and so they are the same rhyme and may rhyme together. Thus 'the same rhyme' holds and 'the same final' does not — precisely an example of the boundary drawn in [[#sec-yun-yunmu]]: a rhyme group merges the four classes, while a final analyses them. Notice in passing that 还 huán, though it carries a u medial, is still the same rhyme as 间 and 山. Rhyming takes no account of the medial, and here that is at its plainest.
:::

::: exercise Diagnosing the combinations {level=2}
A learner writes five syllables on paper: fiao, guai, güan, xü (he has written the two dots of ü as well), and ziao. By the rules for combining initials with finals, and by the rules of spelling, judge them one by one. Which are legitimate syllables of Putonghua? For each that is not, what has it violated?
::: solution
guai is legitimate: it is closed, and the g group may combine with finals of the u kind (拐 guǎi). The sound of xü is legitimate, but the spelling is not: ü after j, q or x drops its two dots by rule, and the correct spelling is xu (叙 xù). fiao is not legitimate: f does not combine with the even-teeth class, and Putonghua has no such syllable. ziao is not legitimate: the front-apical group (z, c, s) does not combine with iao; for 小 there is xiao (a tongue-surface initial) and there is no ziao. güan is not legitimate: the tongue-root group does not combine with the round-mouth final üan (关 is the closed guan); and in any case ü is not written after g. Questions of this kind are done by checking two tables against each other: the registration table of the four classes × the table of the places of the initials. 'What can be produced physically' and 'what the system can spell' are two different things — once again the parting of 'the possible' and 'the actual' (see [[sounds-of-mandarin/speech-sounds#sec-yinwei]]).
:::

::: exercise Restoring the spellings {level=2}
Restore the four spellings niu, dui, lun and jiang to structural formulae that carry the sound values (the three items medial / nucleus / coda, with what the spelling omits marked), and find an example character for each.
::: solution
niu = iou: medial i, nucleus o, coda u (o omitted after the initial); the example is 牛. dui = uei: medial u, nucleus e, coda i; the example is 对. lun = uen: medial u, nucleus e, coda n; the example is 论. jiang = iang: medial i, nucleus a, coda ng; the example is 江. Note that the e of dui and the e of lun are alike abbreviated and not swallowed: distinct when read slowly, weakened when read fast (the same reasoning as in the entry on iou; see [[#ex-iou]]).
:::

::: exercise The disagreement over how to group them {level=3}
Should ing be analysed as 'i + ŋ' or as '(nucleus weakened) + ŋ'? Should ong be counted as 'a nasal rhyme of u' or as an independent final? Write two hundred characters, set out the evidence on each of the two sides, and give the treatment you adopt in this course.
::: hint
Evidence one may consider: the letters of Pinyin; the actual sound value (a weakened central vowel); correspondences in the dialects and in history (in Southern Min, 东 is read as of the tang type); and convenience in assigning the four classes. No position is the only one; the standard must be consistent.
:::
::: solution
A position for reference. This course treats ing as 'medial i + coda ng, with the nucleus weakened', and ong as 'a nasal final near ［uŋ］, spelt with o'. The reasons are (1) that in sound value the main vowel really is both short and central, and (2) that putting ong with the closed class lines up neatly with the row of u. The opposing position can be set out just as well: treating ing and ong as independent finals of a monophthongal kind avoids the awkwardness of 'having no nucleus', and also makes them easier to read off against the dialects (Southern Min tang and tok show that ong once had a main vowel of the a kind; see [[phonology/to-mandarin]]). The marking looks at whether the evidence is set out rather than the loudness of the voices compared, and whether one states of one's own accord which account one follows. 'The textbook says there is a nucleus, and therefore there is' scores nothing.
:::

::: exercise A vowel account of one's own dialect {level=3}
Read each of the four characters 诗, 知, 鸡 and 资 in the speech of your home town and in Putonghua, and check whether the finals are the same. Write a paragraph of analysis: in your dialect, how many phonemes are the three vowels -i［ɿ］, -i［ʅ］ and i? Give evidence from substitution.
::: solution
In Putonghua the finals of the three characters divide three ways (知 -i［ɿ］ / 诗 -i［ʅ］ / 鸡 i), each appearing after its own initial. Strictly speaking this is a question of allophones in complementary distribution (each of the two apical vowels has its own environment; a mainstream analysis may set up three phonemes, or else 'conditioned variants of /-i/', and both are tenable; textbooks mostly record -i on the latter account). A pattern common in the dialects: in Southwestern Mandarin, 诗, 师, 知 and 资 are the same rhyme; Wu keeps the apical vowels, and monophthongisation is widespread. A satisfactory answer must give a judgement of whether substitution in the same environment changes the word, and must transcribe the sounds as they are (if you are not sure, give two schemes and write the disagreement out clearly). This question has no standard answer in sound values; it has a standard form of argument.
:::
