title: Initials
---

To read the three characters 的, 哥 and 可 correctly, a foreigner must first learn three things: they are voiceless; they are unaspirated, or else strongly aspirated; and what their tongue root meets is the soft palate. These three pieces of information are exactly what any textbook of Putonghua (standard Mandarin) assumes you "know by nature" — because you are a native speaker, and your ear had this system installed long before you could speak. The task of this chapter is to take what has been installed and pull it apart: the 21 initials of Putonghua (plus the "zero initial"), the two coordinates of every articulatory gesture — place and manner — and why these two coordinates should happen to be just these. When you have finished this chapter, you should be able to give the "file" of any initial (for example ch: back-apical, aspirated, voiceless, affricate), to say which feature you are dropping when you cannot hear a difference, and to set the initials of your dialect against this table, one for one.

## What an initial is

::: definition Initial {#def-shengmu}
The traditional three-way division of the Chinese syllable is initial, final and tone: the sound at the beginning of the syllable (or the slot for a group of sounds) is the **initial**, the stretch from there to the end of the syllable is the final, and the pitch contour carried by the whole syllable is the tone. Putonghua has 21 initials: b, p, m, f, d, t, n, l, g, k, h, j, q, x, zh, ch, sh, r, z, c and s, every one of them a consonant. In addition, a beginning "with no consonant" is recognised, and called the **zero initial**.
:::

::: definition Zero initial {#def-lingshengmu}
The case in which the final stands directly at the beginning of the syllable. In the four syllables 安 an, 欧 ou, 一 yi and 五 wu there is no consonant at the start, the initial slot is empty, and that empty slot is written as a zero initial. Pinyin spells it with a y or a w at the head (a zero-initial syllable beginning with i, u or ü is rewritten yi, wu, yu and so on; the details are in [[sounds-of-mandarin/syllables-pinyin]]), but y and w are **not** two initials: they are spelling devices at the level of the letters, not new units of pronunciation.
:::

::: note Distinguishing initial and consonant {#note-shengmu-fuyin}
The initial is a traditional Chinese notion of **structure** (the constituent at the beginning of the syllable); the consonant is a phonetic notion of **articulation** (a sound in which the airstream is plainly obstructed; [[sounds-of-mandarin/speech-sounds#def-fuyin-yuanyin]]). The two are not the same thing. Putonghua has about 22 consonants, but the initials take in only 21 — ŋ (the coda of 英) is a consonant and yet is not used as an initial; the other way about, the consonants that are not initials, -n and -ng, stand in the coda slot. One notion asks "which cell of the syllable it occupies"; the other asks "what happened in the mouth". In a dialect survey the two kinds of question often come up together, and only if they are asked apart do they stay untangled.
:::

::: quiz
Which of the following syllable-onsets **has a consonantal initial**?
- [ ] 安 ān
- [x] 堆 duī
- [ ] 鹅 é
- [ ] 羊 yáng
::: solution
The initial of 堆 is d, a mid-apical unaspirated voiceless stop [t]. The other three have no consonant at the start: 安 and 鹅 are examples of the zero initial in the open-mouth class (开口呼, one of the four classes of final, open, even-teeth, closed and round-mouth, *kaiqi hecuo*), and 羊 begins with y, a spelling device, so the initial slot is empty. Note that words of the sort 歪, 娃 and 野 raise the same doubt: they too are written with y or w, and they too have the zero initial — no syllable that begins with y or w carries a consonantal initial. y and w are the orthography of Pinyin, not a fact about pronunciation.
:::
:::

## The first coordinate: place of articulation

Place of articulation is where the obstruction is made: once the airstream has left the lungs, the stretch above the larynx at which it is pinched, narrowed or sealed off. The names of the places differ a little from one author to another. This course follows the Huang–Liao textbook and divides them into seven, from front to back:

::: definition Places of articulation of the Putonghua initials (the seven-way division of the Huang–Liao textbook) {#def-buwei}
**Bilabials** b, p, m — the upper and lower lips make the obstruction; **labiodentals** f — the lower lip meets the upper teeth; **front-apicals** z, c, s — the tongue tip meets, or nears, the back of the upper teeth (popularly called flat-tongue sounds, 平舌音); **mid-apicals** d, t, n, l — the tongue tip meets the upper alveolar ridge; **back-apicals** zh, ch, sh, r — the tongue tip is raised to meet, or near, the front of the hard palate (popularly called retroflexes, 翘舌音); **tongue-surface sounds** j, q, x — the front of the tongue meets, or nears, the hard palate; **tongue-root sounds** g, k, h — the tongue root meets, or nears, the soft palate. Some textbooks merge front-apical and mid-apical into one class and speak of a six-way division; Hu Yushu's textbook and others use the names 舌面前 and 舌面后 ("front of the tongue surface" and "back of the tongue surface") in place of 舌面 and 舌根 ("tongue surface" and "tongue root"). The names differ; the place in the mouth is the same — when you report a file, state the scheme first, and then the conclusion.
:::

Try each place for yourself. For 巴, the two lips close and then open. For 佛, the lower lip meets the upper front teeth: notice that it is not the upper and lower lips meeting each other, nor the upper teeth biting down hard on the lower lip (that is the direction of a lip-bitten [v], just where old-style Putonghua with a "Shanghai flavour" is often laughed at). For 资, the tongue tip stretches out flat against the back of the upper teeth, and does not reach the alveolar ridge. For 知, the tongue curls up and the tip reaches the front of the hard palate — this "curl" is not optional; eight parts in ten of the sound of zh, ch, sh and r come from where the tip reaches. For 得, the tongue tip meets the upper alveolar ridge flat, exactly between the two stations 资 and 知. For 基, the front of the tongue is set against the hard palate, while the tip, by contrast, hangs behind the lower teeth. For 哥, the tongue root rises and meets the soft palate, and the back wall of the pharynx tightens with it.

Two places are the easiest to mix. One is **front-apical and back-apical** (z, c, s against zh, ch, sh, r): quite apart from the narrow channel sitting a centimetre or two further forward or back, the centre of the noise on the spectrum shifts a long way, so that in the dialects the distinction between 四 and 十 rests entirely on this one lift. The other is **tongue-surface and back-apical** (j, q, x against zh, ch, sh): two neighbouring stretches of the dam along the hard palate, close in what one hears, and yet each under a strict limit on what it may combine with — j, q and x go only with finals that begin with i or ü (基, 居), while zh, ch and sh are not combined with i or ü in the even-teeth or round-mouth classes (the final of 猪 is u, not ü; pronouncing 去 and 居 as retroflexes is the famous *jiānyīn* 尖音, "sharp sounds"; [[sounds-of-mandarin/finals]] and [[history-of-chinese/modern-standard]]).

## The second coordinate: manner of articulation

Place of articulation says "where it is blocked"; manner of articulation says "how it is blocked". Four questions, asked in order:

1. **How the obstruction is made.** Sealing it off completely and then releasing it is a **stop** (b, p, d, t, g, k). Sealing it off and then leaving a gap to squeeze the air out is an **affricate** (z, c, zh, ch, j, q). Never sealing it off, and only leaving a narrow gap, is a **fricative** (f, s, sh, r, x, h). Sealing off the mouth and lowering the soft palate so as to open the nasal cavity is a **nasal** (m, n, ŋ). Leaving the two sides open along the centre line of the mouth, with the tongue tip touching lightly, is a **lateral** (l).
2. **Whether the vocal cords vibrate.** If they vibrate, the sound is **voiced**; if they do not, it is **voiceless**. Among the initials of Putonghua the voiced ones are only the four m, n, l and r; the other seventeen are all voiceless.
3. **The force of the airstream** (for stops and affricates). A clear stretch of aspiration after the release (noise of the h kind) is an **aspirated** sound; without it, the sound is unaspirated. Putonghua has five pairs: b/p, d/t, g/k, z/c and zh/ch (and, with j/q added, six pairs in all; when one says "five pairs", j/q is folded into the count of the affricate group).
4. **Where the airstream comes out.** Out through the mouth is oral; out through the nose is nasal — already included under item 1 above.

::: theorem The distinctive feature of Putonghua stops and affricates is aspiration, not voicing {#rule-songqi}
Within each of the sets b p, d t, g k, z c, zh ch and j q in Putonghua, the feature that distinguishes meaning is **whether there is aspiration or not**: p, t, k, c, ch and q are aspirated, and b, d, g, z, zh and j are unaspirated. In neither set do the vocal cords vibrate; all of them are voiceless. On the initials, the dimension of voicing does not take part in distinguishing meaning.
:::

::: proof Why we conclude that b, d and g are not voiced
Three pieces of evidence. First, instruments can check it. The mark of a voiced stop is that the vocal cords are already vibrating before the release (voice onset time, VOT, is negative or near zero). Measure the initial of Putonghua 八, and between the release and the vibration of the vocal cords there is a short stretch of silence or of noise: the pattern of a voiceless sound. Only the b at the beginning of an English word carries prevoicing. Second, a native speaker "cannot hear" this dimension. Pronounce 八 as a voiced [b], and a native speaker of Putonghua only feels that it is "a bit of a foreign accent, or a Henan accent", and does not suppose that the character has been changed — if voicing had a place in the system, the speaker would necessarily be able to tell the two apart. Third, an echo from history. The fully voiced initials (全浊) of earlier Chinese (并, 定, 群, 从, 澄, 邪 and others) were devoiced in northern speech, and the split in today's northern dialects, "aspirated in the level tone, unaspirated in the oblique" (同 → tóng, 洞 → dòng), is precisely the fossil of that devoicing — voicing was lost, and aspiration was not, from which one can see that, as between "aspiration" and "voiced or not", the system chose the former. Note, in passing, a cross-linguistic lesson: students who speak Korean or Wu often hear both Putonghua b and p as voiceless (they each use a dimension of "lax sound / aspiration", 松音/送气), while students who speak English, French or Wu hear b and p as voiceless against voiced (they have a voicing dimension) — the file is not wrong; it is only that each ear is uneven in a different part of the work.
:::

::: example Making a file for chàng {#ex-jianlang-chang}
Give a complete articulatory description of the initial of 唱 chàng, and explain why, on the point of manner, it is an "affricate" rather than a "stop" or a "fricative".
::: solution
Back-apical, aspirated, voiceless, affricate. It is called an "affricate" because, in the phase when the obstruction is made, the tongue tip and the front of the hard palate seal completely (as with a stop), and then, instead of opening with a jerk, they withdraw into a narrow gap and squeeze the air through with friction (as with a fricative) — one and the same obstruction, "stop first, fricative after", and hence an affricate. The initial ch of 唱 has nothing to do with the final -ang of 唱; this file records the initial only. Written out in full, tone value and all, it is [tʂʰaŋ⁵¹]. A check on yourself: draw 唱 out long and sing it — what you are holding is the fricative stretch (the fricative portion of [tʂʰ] can be prolonged; the stop portion cannot), and this is also the knack for hearing an affricate apart from the pure fricative r [ʐ].
:::
:::

::: example Making a file for ràng {#ex-jianlang-rang}
Authors do not agree on how to transcribe the initial r of 让 ràng: [ʐ], [ʐ̞], [ɻ]. Which should you use in class?
::: solution
This course follows the Huang–Liao textbook and writes [ʐ]: voiced, back-apical, fricative (some textbooks write the approximant [ɻ], because the friction is in fact often very slight). On the rest of the file there is no disagreement: the place is back-apical (the same as zh, ch and sh), and the sound is voiced — r is the only voiced fricative among the initials of Putonghua. The "variant readings" of r in the dialects (in Nanjing speech 人 is read with [l-], in Jinan speech it is a zero initial beginning with [ɻ], and in quite a few southern dialects it is read [z] or [j]) come about precisely because, in the phonology of Putonghua, it is both voiced and a fricative and goes its own way, while historically it comes from several sources; [[dialects/mandarin-dialects]].
:::
:::

| Place＼manner | Stop | Nasal | Fricative | Affricate | Lateral |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bilabial | b (unaspirated) / p (aspirated) | m | — | — | — |
| Labiodental | — | — | f | — | — |
| Front-apical | — | — | s | z / c | — |
| Back-apical | — | — | sh, r | zh / ch | — |
| Tongue-surface | — | — | x | j / q | — |
| Tongue-root | g / k | (ŋ, not used as an initial) | h | — | — |
| Mid-apical | d / t | n | — | — | l |

The "mid-apical" cell in the table, where d, t, n and l sit, is a place the Huang–Liao textbook sets up on its own; on the six-way account it is joined with the front-apicals into a single class, "apical". The difference between the two accounts is only how fine the cut is: the point at which d, t, n and l make the obstruction (the alveolar ridge) is indeed a little behind z, c and s (the back of the teeth) and a little in front of zh, ch and sh (the front of the hard palate). Listing it separately has its grounds, and merging it also works. Wherever this course counts the places, it declares what it is following — if a reader meets an older text that says "six places", there is no need to change one's wording; check the scheme first.

::: warning Do not pronounce b, d and g as voiced — and do not write them as voiced either
✗ A native speaker of English reading 八字 puts prevoicing, [b], on the initial, and the impression is at once "pidgin"; ✗ in a narrow transcription 八 is written [ba⁵⁵] — that is a different sound. ✓ The voiceless unaspirated stop of 八 is written [pa⁵⁵]; the Pinyin letter b records the phoneme /p/. The same point the other way round holds as well: a French student is apt to read 哥 as a voiced [ɡɤ], and to vibrate the vocal cords before 得 — three faces of one and the same error. There is only one test: whether or not the vocal cords vibrate before the release. A hand on the larynx is enough; no instrument is needed.
:::

## What a zero-initial syllable is when the mouth actually opens

To say that the zero initial "has no consonant" is to say that the slot is empty in the system. At the actual instant the mouth opens, the oral cavity is not a vacuum. 安 often begins with a touch of glottal stop [ʔ]. The beginning of 一 is a movement in which a high vowel at the front of the tongue slides towards the phoneme [i], and some textbooks mark the start with the glide symbol [j]. Syllables of the 五 and 乌 type carry a rounded glide [w]. All of these are fine phonetic description (the layering in [[sounds-of-mandarin/speech-sounds#def-yinwei]]); at the phonological level none of them is a phoneme. The Pinyin spellings y and w are there precisely to "line up a beginning" for syllables of this kind: a final of the i-row standing as a syllable on its own is written yi, ya, ye, the u-row is written wu, wa, wo, and the ü-row is written yu, yue (the two dots on ü are omitted, because after y there can be no clash with anything other than ü). The detail of the rules is set out in [[sounds-of-mandarin/syllables-pinyin]].

::: example 乌鸦 and 鸭 {#ex-wuya}
Are both syllables of 乌鸦 wūyā zero-initial? Why is the y of 鸭 yā not an initial, and why is the y of 牙 yá not one either?
::: solution
Both are zero-initial: 乌 [u⁵⁵] begins with a closed-mouth glide, and 鸦 [ia⁵⁵] with an even-teeth glide. The Pinyin rule: where there is no initial and the final begins with i, write y plus the final (ia → ya); where u stands as a syllable on its own, write wu — y and w are "written in", not "an extra consonant added". The test is at the level of the system: no native speaker of Putonghua can set y against some other initial and get a word out of the opposition (there are not two different sounds, "ia" and "ya"), so it is spelling only. What is of interest is that in speech 呀 ya often carries quite solid palatal friction, and once the stream of speech quickens it sounds again like a real consonant — the system is empty, and the mouth is not. That one sentence checks once more the "social level and the physical level" of the previous chapter.
:::
:::

- [x] 床: back-apical, aspirated affricate; 树: back-apical, voiceless fricative; 四: front-apical, voiceless fricative; 聚: tongue-surface, unaspirated affricate
- [ ] 床: tongue-surface, aspirated; 树: back-apical, voiced fricative; 四: front-apical, voiceless affricate; 聚: tongue-surface, aspirated
- [ ] 床: back-apical, aspirated affricate; 树: back-apical, voiced fricative; 四: front-apical, voiceless fricative; 聚: tongue-surface, unaspirated
::: solution
床 chuáng = ch [tʂʰ], an aspirated affricate; 树 shù = sh [ʂ], a voiceless fricative; 四 sì = s [s], a front-apical voiceless fricative; 聚 jù = j [tɕ], a tongue-surface unaspirated affricate. In the second of them, 树 is often mis-taken for a voiced sound — sh is a voiceless fricative, and the only voiced fricative initial in Putonghua is r. The right move on a question of this kind is not to recall the examples, but actually to pronounce the four sounds and let the tongue tip answer for you.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which of the following is **not** a manner class of the initials of Putonghua?
- [ ] affricate
- [ ] nasal
- [x] voiced stop
- [ ] lateral
::: solution
Putonghua has affricates (z, c, zh, ch, j, q), nasals (m, n) and a lateral (l), and it has no voiced stop as an initial — b, d and g are voiceless unaspirated stops ([[#rule-songqi]]). The "voiced stop" is a device of other languages (Wu, the b and d at the beginning of an English word, and the like).

## Training the ear: initials that are right and initials that are not

Setting confusable pairs to listen to, with final and tone value the same, is the "practical class" of this chapter. 四 sì and 是 shì differ in place (front-apical against back-apical); 错 cuò and 绰 chuò are both aspirated affricates, and differ again as flat-tongue against retroflex; 粗 cū and 出 chū are an affricate on one side and a fricative on the other, and the places differ too, so the listening has to be taken in two steps; 老 lǎo and 脑 nǎo differ in the route the air takes (along the two sides of the mouth, or through the nasal cavity); 鱼 yú and 驴 lú differ as zero initial against initial, and test the round-mouth vowel along the way. The tongue-twister 四是四，十是十 ("Four is four, ten is ten." (Our translation.)) trains not the lips, but the link between control of the tongue position and feedback from the ear. For a speaker of a dialect, the first step in putting a pronunciation right is always **telling the sounds apart by ear**: someone who does not separate n and l must be able to hear them apart before there can be any talk of being able to say them. How to listen, how to record and how to note it down: [[sounds-of-mandarin/dialect-differences]] and [[dialects/fieldwork]] each give half of the method.

::: history
The Chinese tradition of classifying initials is a thousand years older than modern phonetics. At the end of the Tang, Shouwen's thirty initials (as they come down in the Dunhuang documents) were already ordering the initials by matching the five sound-classes 唇、舌、牙、齿、喉 ("lips, tongue, molars, teeth and throat") with "voiceless and voiced". In the Song they were expanded to the thirty-six initials (见溪群疑、端透定泥、知彻澄娘、帮滂並明、精清从心邪、照穿床审禅、来日、影喻晓匣), and the horizontal rows of the rhyme tables are this table. The five-way division of today's textbooks, bilabial / labiodental / apical / tongue-surface / tongue-root, is a refining of those "five sound-classes". The old names *píngqiào* 平翘 ("flat-tongue and retroflex") and *jiāntuán* 尖团 ("sharp and round") are still alive in research on the dialects and in the profession of Chinese opera. In 1918 the Zhuyin alphabet (注音字母) was promulgated, and b, p, m and f were read as 玻, 坡, 摸 and 佛: calling pronunciations (呼读音) made for the teaching of the initials. The name of a letter is not its phonetic value (ㄅ is not pronounced as the sound of 玻; it is only easier to teach). In 1958 the Hanyu Pinyin scheme carried this set of calling pronunciations on, and only changed over to a string of Latin letters.
:::

## What comes next

The initial is only half of the syllable. The four classes of the final, and their structure, are in [[sounds-of-mandarin/finals]]; the table of syllables formed by joining the two, and the orthography of Pinyin, are in [[sounds-of-mandarin/syllables-pinyin]]. The historical layers of the initials (how 帮滂並明 became today's b, p, m and f, and the local pronunciations) are the course content of [[phonology/mc-initials]]. For the different choices the dialects make along the dimension "aspiration / voicing", used over and over in this chapter, see [[dialects/wu]] and [[dialects/mandarin-dialects]]. In the review questions of the next section the name of your home dialect will appear — write it into the table, and only then does this chapter count as learnt.

::: summary
- The initial is the constituent at the beginning of the syllable. Putonghua has 21, all of them consonants. The zero initial is an empty slot in the structure, not "some consonant left out".
- Two coordinates fix every initial: place of articulation (bilabial, labiodental, front-apical, mid-apical, back-apical, tongue-surface, tongue-root — the seven-way division of the Huang–Liao textbook; for the six-way account, check the textbook) and manner of articulation (stop, nasal, fricative, affricate, lateral; voiceless or voiced; aspirated or not).
- Stops and affricates in Putonghua distinguish meaning by **aspiration**. b, d, g, z, zh and j are voiceless unaspirated sounds, not voiced sounds. The only voiced initials are m, n, l and r.
- The limits of sharp-and-round and of flat-and-retroflex are a question of how the final combines, but the difference one hears arises in the place. To put a pronunciation right, put the ear right first.
- Initial and consonant, calling pronunciation and phonetic value, and the "empty slot in the system" against the "actual onset" of the zero initial, are three groupings of levels that must be kept apart.
:::

For each group of characters below, find which feature the "difference of initial" falls on: (1) 粗 cū / 苏 sū; (2) 招 zhāo / 交 jiāo; (3) 班 bān / 攀 pān; (4) 兰 lán / 南 nán.

(2) zh [tʂ] / j [tɕ]: both unaspirated voiceless affricates; a difference of place (back-apical against tongue-surface). (3) b [p] / p [pʰ]: the same place and the same manner; aspirated or not. ::: exercise Three pieces of evidence {level=2}
Of which character does the sentence “妈 m、飞 f、喝 h 是浊音” ("妈 m, 飞 f and 喝 h are voiced." (Our translation.)) actually hold? For each of the other two characters, what bodily evidence, and what evidence from instruments, rebuts it?
::: solution
The sentence holds only of 妈: m is a voiced nasal, and the vocal cords vibrate throughout the closure. Both f and h are voiceless fricatives, and the vocal cords do not vibrate. The bodily evidence is to press a hand to the throat and draw out 佛—— and 喝——: there is no vibration at the larynx. The evidence from instruments is that, on a spectrogram, the fricative stretch shows no low-frequency voice bar (the nasal stretch of m does). Of the 21 initials, the only voiced sounds that stand on the test "vibrate or not" are the four m, n, l and r.
:::

::: exercise Correcting a foreign accent {level=2}
An English-speaking friend of yours speaks Chinese, and 弟弟 comes out like 提提, 哥哥 like 科科. Explain, in terms of the phoneme: which feature has he in fact failed to learn? Why does his own language "not have" this feature?
::: solution
He has heard the Putonghua opposition of "voiceless unaspirated stop against voiceless aspirated stop" as the English opposition of "voiced against voiceless": in his ear, d answers to the [d] of *did* and t to the [tʰ] of *top*, and so both 弟 and 提 come out sounding like English aspiration or voicing. What he has not learnt is the place, in the system of Chinese, of the meaning-distinguishing feature **aspiration** — in English aspiration has no such place (*spin* and *pin* are allophones). His native system has never required him to distinguish "aspirated or not", and has required him to distinguish "voiced or not". The two systems have taken different cuts through one and the same physical dimension. The order for putting it right: first train the ear (set b, p and d, t, g, k, the six pairs, out as a discrimination table), then train the speaking (a slip of paper in front of the mouth, to watch the airstream), and only then words and sentences.

::: exercise Transcribing six initials {level=2}
Rewrite the initials of 非, 知, 才, 西, 日 and 五 as phonetic values in square brackets (for a zero initial, mark it as such), and add to each sound one sentence of articulatory description.
::: solution
非 [f], a labiodental voiceless fricative; 知 [tʂ], a back-apical unaspirated voiceless affricate; 才 [tsʰ], a front-apical aspirated voiceless affricate; 西 [s], a front-apical voiceless fricative; 日 [ʐ], a back-apical voiced fricative (writing [ɻ] is also acceptable, if you state your system); 五 [u], a zero initial (it begins with a rounded glide, and at the level of the system there is no consonant). Whether a transcription uses phonemes or phonetic values depends on what you mean to talk about; [[sounds-of-mandarin/speech-sounds#def-yinwei]].

::: exercise Picking the fault in 玻坡摸佛 {level=3}
Someone says: "b 的读音就是‘玻’，所以玻 [po] 才是声母的音值。" ("The reading of b just is 玻, so only 玻 [po] is the phonetic value of the initial." (Our translation.)) Using the three levels of calling pronunciation, phonetic value and phoneme, point out item by item what this sentence mixes together, and what in it is not wrong.
::: solution
What is not wrong: in class, b is indeed read 玻 — this is the calling pronunciation, a "reading with a vowel attached" given to the initial so that the letter can be named and so that spelling-out is easy. The textbooks say plainly that it is not the phonetic value. What gets mixed are two levels. (1) As a phonetic value, b is the voiceless unaspirated bilabial stop [p], and [po] has a central-vowel tail added. (2) As a phoneme, b is the unit /p/, opposed to /pʰ/ (坡) — read the calling pronunciation as [pʰo] and it has become another phoneme. The conclusion: there is no harm in using 玻坡摸佛 to indicate the pronunciation; using them as phonetic symbols is entirely wrong. An extension: why does a calling pronunciation generally have to be fitted with a vowel? (After a pure consonant is released there is no stretch that continues; it cannot be drawn out, and it will not answer when it is called.)

::: exercise A comparison table for the home dialect {level=3}
Draw up a table comparing the initials of your home dialect (or of a dialect locality you know) with those of Putonghua. List only the rows that "do not match" (a merger, something extra, a different reading). Give two example characters for each row, and mark which rows will produce a "regional accent" in Putonghua. If you do not know how to write it, you may first read the common correspondences in [[dialects/mandarin-dialects]], or, by the method of [[dialects/fieldwork]], note down two lists of single characters for yourself.
::: hint
Deal first with the three commonest groups: flat-tongue and retroflex (z, c, s / zh, ch, sh, r), n and l, and f and h; then check the voiced sounds of the Wu area, the "light labials returning to heavy labials" (轻唇归重唇) of the Min area, and f and h in the Hakka area; and last look at an extra [ŋ] or [n], or a nasalised onset, in front of zero-initial syllables.
:::
::: solution
An answer that will pass has a shape like this: "Pingshui speech: Putonghua n and l merge as [l] (南北 are homophones, both read with an onset [l-]); Putonghua hu- is often read f- (湖 is read [fu]); there are extra voiced stops b, d and g (the initial of 大 carries voice). Of the three rows, the first two directly produce a regional accent; the third is a feature of the dialect, and in learning to speak Putonghua it is turned into a voiceless unaspirated sound." The marking looks at whether the correspondence can be checked again (the phonetic values of the example characters have to be writable), whether one talks only of the system and not of better and worse (the norm in [[sounds-of-mandarin/dialect-differences]]), and whether one has mixed, in the notation, the phonemes and the phonetic values of one's own dialect ([l], or /l/?).
