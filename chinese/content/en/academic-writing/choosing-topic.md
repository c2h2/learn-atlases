title: "Choosing a topic"

The last chapter gave a test for whether a question is a question. **This one asks whether it is yours — a different test, and the one that decides more careers, because the answer changes with your library card, your income, your language and your deadline**.

## Three circles and two riders {#def-sangeyuan}

::: definition Reachable material, finishable work, somebody waiting {#def-yuan}
**The model**: a doable topic lies in the intersection of three circles. **① Reachable material** — not "what exists" but **which borrowing card you hold, which database your institution buys, which interviewees will see you, whether you can read the hand**. **② Finishable time** — estimate the work first and only then cut the claim to fit. **③ Somebody waiting** — a result that nobody would be disappointed to miss. **Two riders this course adds, because the three circles admit too much. Rider ④: the skill.** A topic inside your reach in every other respect may require paleography, statistics or a language with four years of lead time; skills are not borrowed at the last moment, and **the rider is the one students discover in month nine.** **Rider ⑤: can you bear to fail it?** A topic whose failure would cost you your funding, your visa or your relationship with your informants will not be pursued honestly — you will find yourself reading the material as though the answer were already safe (chapter eight). **Note the asymmetry: ① and ④ are auditable by anybody; ⑤ is not, which is exactly why it should be written down in the proposal where somebody may ask.**
:::

::: definition Fallibility {#def-shibai}
**The real criterion for a good topic is not importance but this: if your hypothesis were false, would anyone want to know why?** Three corollaries. **① A design that can only succeed is not research**: if every possible result can be read as confirming the claim, you have finished already and only the typing remains. **② Fallibility is the other face of defensibility**: the topic that can be falsified can also be defended in the viva, because you stated in advance what would overturn you. **③ Importance is not a criterion** — it is what people say when they have none, and the biggest questions are usually the least falsifiable (**the one advantage of a huge question is that it never needs to be refuted**). **The operational form: put in your proposal the sentence "if X turns out to be the case, my argument fails". A proposal that cannot contain that sentence is a project plan, not a research design** — and this course will keep asking for it in chapters five, six and ten.
:::

## Three sources {#def-sanyuan}

::: definition A gap in the material {#def-fengxi}
**The most respectable source**: find material nobody has used — a newly catalogued archive, an unpublished manuscript, a local collection, a set of court opinions, an oral history, a corpus only you can read. **Its virtue: the contribution is hard to take away from you, because you hold the thing itself; opponents can only argue about what it means, not whether it exists.** **Its risk: the material may not answer the question**, and worse, the seduction of new material produces a specific pathology — **the annotated discovery**, in which a paper proves that something exists and calls the proof an argument. **The guard rail: write the sentence the material makes possible before you write the sentence that it exists.** A test worth memorising: **if a rival scholar found the same material tomorrow, what would your paper still have that hers would not?**
:::

::: definition Misplacing a method, and the third source {#def-cuozhi}
**The second source**: carry a mature method from field A to material B — a quantitative technique onto an uncounted corpus, a narratological tool onto a court judgement. **Virtue: the contribution is easy to state. Risk: methods arrive with their own question-list, and one tends to carry the tool without the target it was aimed at.** Two mandatory questions: **what was this method invented to refute**, and **does that refutation still matter on material B** — a method whose target has vanished is a costume. **The third source is the least noticed and the most common in the humanities: a shared premise you can test. Two rival schools have argued for forty years about an author's intentions, and both assume that a certain edition is his final word.** Test the assumption and you may find that the debate was conducted on a text that did not exist in the year both sides were quoting — **a result that angers everyone and is very hard to refute.** **Choose your opponent accordingly: an opponent who shares your premises gives you the shortest path to a claim, which is why chapter three tells you to hunt for adversaries before hunting for literature.**
:::

::: definition Four widenings-in-miniature {#def-shoushai}
**Four narrowing steps, each of which must deliver an object.** **① Interest to subject**: deliver **one hundred words containing one concrete noun and one date-range** — "I like Song lyrics" delivers nothing; "the prefaces attached to ci collections of the late Southern Song" delivers. **② Subject to question**: the three-sentence test of chapter one. **③ Question to design**: deliver the list of material, the sampling frame, and the analyses you intend to run. **④ Design to proposal**: deliver the reverse-engineered calendar of chapter ten and the two surrender clauses below. **Why deliverables and not reflections: because a topic that exists only in your head has no failure condition, and failure conditions are the point.** If any step cannot produce its object, the work of that step has not been done, and no amount of further reading substitutes for it.
:::

::: definition Reverse calendar and surrender clauses {#def-daotui}
**The reverse calendar**: begin from the submission date and write backwards — marking, defence, revision, first full draft, complete draft of the worst chapter, data locked, fieldwork window, archive applications, pilot coding. **Whatever exceeds what remains has to be cut — and the cutting happens now while your topic is still a paragraph, not in month eleven while it is three chapters of prose.** **The two surrender clauses, which belong in the proposal where a supervisor may read them. ① "I abandon this question if…"** — some material is unavailable by some date, some coding yields no more than chance agreement. **② "I reduce the claim to…"** — the smaller claim you will defend if the larger fails. **Why put them in writing: because an unwritten condition is a mood.** Students abandon good topics at 2 a.m. and cling to dead ones for four years, and both are failures of the same pre-committed clause. **A supervisor who objects that writing down the conditions of failure invites failure has misunderstood the instrument: the clause is what allows you to keep working after a bad week.**
:::

::: example Four proposals {#ex-sige}
**A** "The imagery of the whole of Tang poetry." **B** "Interviews with twenty judges in one province; I will ask about how they use guiding opinions." **C** "Applying a model from memory studies to a Ming local gazetteer." **D** "Whether dialect use is declining — a survey." **For each, name the missing circle or rider, and write one sentence of repair.**

::: solution
**A**: no bound on material and no opponent — **fix by picking two decades, one genre, one image-cluster, and a claim someone could deny.** **B**: the healthiest of the four: material reachable, work bounded; **its riders are access and consequence** — whether twenty judges will speak candidly about a sensitive object, and what you will do if they all say the same thing (**decide in advance which answer would count as evidence against you**). **C**: method misplacement — **fix by stating what the model was built to refute and whether the gazetteer can bear that question**; if not, the paper becomes "this model does not fit, and here is what the misfit shows", which is a legitimate and underrated result. **D**: enormous plus unfalsifiable — **"is declining" needs a comparable earlier sample, and where none exists the honest version is a visibility statement: "among my respondents, X speaks it less often than their parents did"** (the same operation the dialect course prescribes: turn a strong claim into a statement about what you can observe). **Marking: whoever supplies for B the sentence "if all twenty say the same, my inference is that the interview setting produced it, not that the practice is uniform" takes an A** — that is fallibility applied to a qualitative design, and it is rarer than in quantitative ones.
:::
:::

::: intuition The remaining life of a hot topic {#int-redian}
**Three indicators of late arrival. ① Someone has started writing the survey article** — a survey means the field considers the question closed enough to summarise. **② A journal announces a special issue** — editors have decided it is a field rather than a provocation. **③ Criticism has shifted from conclusions to method** — when people begin saying "the quantitative work in this area is not rigorous", the easy positions are taken. **Joining at indicator ③ means your contribution will be a correction, and corrections are the least visible kind of paper** — this does not make them worthless, but do not expect the attention that greeted the original wave. **The compensation for late arrival: the tools are better and the material is catalogued.** The rule of thumb: **if you are first, check that you are not merely early; if you are late, write the correction on purpose and title it as one.** And one honest motive check: the reason a hot topic feels safe is that the literature, the positions and the audience are already there — **which is a statement about your anxiety, not about the question.**
:::

::: history One dissertation's institutional history {#sec-zhidu}
**Nineteenth-century Germany**: the research seminar trains philologists by assigning each student a fragment of a bounded problem, and the dissertation becomes proof of method rather than of learning (**standard account**). **Its export**: the American and Japanese doctoral models, and with them the expectation that a candidate can **delimit** — a word that means "choose a topic you can finish" in institutional language. **Mid-twentieth century**: the funded project replaces the lone scholar in some fields, **and the topic becomes a deliverable with a budget and a personnel plan** — which is when "reachability" and "time" become explicit criteria rather than private wisdom. **Late twentieth century**: ethics review, data management plans and pre-registration enter the proposal (**standard account**) — **the three documents that make the two riders of this chapter compulsory.** **Read the sequence as the gradual externalisation of what used to be judgement: each instrument is somebody else's way of asking "can you actually do this, and what will tell you that you cannot?"** That is why this chapter insists on deliverables: the institution will ask you for the calendar and the clause whether or not you have thought about them.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Hearing "too big" as a complaint about ambition** (**it is usually a material, time or skill problem**); **choosing the third kind of topic first** (**a question with shared premises is the hardest; read both camps before trying**); **writing the surrender clause only in your head** (**an unwritten condition is a mood**); **boarding a topic at its peak** (**surveys and special issues are closing signals**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: the politics of topic choice {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the advice "choose a topic you can finish" is class advice, and it is usually given by people who finished theirs when the same advice did not apply. A student who must work, who cannot travel, who is on a visa, whose language of competence is not the language of the archive, does not have a free choice of questions; and the syllabus that ignores this does not raise standards, it converts privilege into a degree mark.** Deploy at least six of: **the three circles and the two riders**, **fallibility**, **the three sources**, **the deliverables of the four narrowing steps**, **the reverse calendar**, **the remaining life of a hot topic**. Then adjudicate: **if the constraint is real, what does a supervisor owe a constrained student — and what does the constrained student owe the truth?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**must produce an obligation, not a sentiment**). **A passing shape: the supervisor owes the workable version of the student's question** — a route to the same claim through reachable material, which is a technical service and not charity: introducing the student to a colleague who has the corpus, or approving a comparative frame in which local material counts. **The student owes the supervisor the true constraint** ("I cannot go to Beijing for three months") rather than a disguised one ("the archives are unhelpful"), **because only the first is answerable** — the same alignment of claim with evidence required everywhere in this course. **Highest marks for the observation that the circles and riders are not obstacles to rigour but its guarantee: a student who chooses by reachability is choosing falsifiability**, since a bounded question can be answered; **whereas the "brave" choice of an enormous topic is often the unfalsifiable choice, and unfalsifiable topics are the true privilege — only the protected can afford a question that cannot fail.**
:::
