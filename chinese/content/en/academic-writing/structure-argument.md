title: "Structure and argument"
---

Style is the thing students ask about; structure is the thing that gets papers rejected. **This chapter's thesis: the function of a structure is not to be beautiful but to let a reader point at the step they reject — and a paper whose parts cannot be individually attacked cannot be individually defended either**.

## The function of a section {#def-gongneng}

::: definition One section, one business {#def-yijie}
**The rule: a section does one job, and states it.** Two tests. **① The deletion test**: if removing the section changes nothing, it was not doing a job; it was occupying space. **② The transposition test**: if you can swap two sections without anybody noticing, the order carries no argument, and your reader will conclude that you have none either. **Three common sections that fail both. The background chapter** — three thousand words nobody had to have — **passes the deletion test every time**; keep only the sentences your later sections actually use, and move the rest to a footnote. **The literature review that surveys by author** (chapter five). **The "significance of the study" section**, whose entire content is an assertion with no evidence attached. **And the rule for the honest survivor: one section may have one objection in it.** If you cannot name the objection your section is answering, it is decoration. **Structure is compression: a paper of five sections has five claims, and a paper of fifteen paragraphs about one claim has none, because no single paragraph was entrusted with anything.**
:::

::: definition Three ways to order a skeleton {#def-gujia}
**Only three orderings exist. ① Analytical**: from what is least contested to what you claim — the default of science writing, and the safest, because **it lets a hostile reader agree with you for three chapters before the quarrel**. **② Inverted**: verdict first, grounds after — the inverted pyramid of journalism, and the correct form for the abstract, the conclusion and the memorandum to a funder; **its risk is that a reader who rejects your verdict early has nowhere to go but out.** **③ Thematic**: organised by object — a corpus, a chronology, a set of cases; **the natural form for monographs, and the most dangerous for arguments**, because **the order of your material is not the order of your reasoning, and readers will find structure in the sequence you chose** (this is why thematic chapters end with "these cases show", which is a repair bolt). **Choose by your reader's disposition, not by your material's shape: an audience that expects a verdict wants ② in the abstract and ① in the body; an audience that distrusts you wants ① throughout with the verdict postponed to a place where it cannot be avoided.** Whichever you choose, **the skeleton must be visible in the headings alone** — that is what an examiner reads at eleven at night, and what she will quote when she says "the structure is unclear".
:::

## The four parts of a paragraph {#def-sijian}

::: definition Claim, warrant, evidence, qualification {#def-si}
**The four parts. ① The claim**: what this paragraph asserts. **② The warrant**: why the evidence supports that claim — the general licence that connects them. **③ The evidence**: the specific thing you found, with its location. **④ The qualification**: the conditions under which the claim does not extend.** **The most frequently omitted part is ②, and its omission is the commonest cause of a paragraph that "doesn't follow".** Example of the omission: "The preface of 1522 omits the dedication; **therefore** the printed edition was aimed at a different public." The warrant would read: "prefaces were rewritten when a text was republished for a new market, and a changed dedication is a reliable index of that" — a general statement that has to be true for the evidence to matter, and that is contestable, and is therefore where your argument really lives. **Marking rule of this course: a paragraph with only claim and evidence scores nothing, because it records a belief and a fact without the mechanism between.** **The fourth part is the one examiners praise and students skip**: **qualification is not timidity** — it is the guard rail that keeps a reviewer from disproving your whole chapter with one counter-example, and the writing course's rule that "a qualifier is a bumper" applies unchanged here. **Finally, treat every "therefore", "thus", "clearly" and "as is evident" as a customs duty unpaid until you have written the warrant in full.**
:::

::: definition Conceding without losing the position {#def-rangbu}
**Concession is a technique, not a mood.** Its form: **state the opposing claim in the version its best defender would recognise; name what your account cannot explain about it; then show what your account explains that it cannot.** Three rules. **① The concession must cost you something.** "Some scholars have expressed reservations" costs nothing and reads as manoeuvring. **"The strongest counter-example is X, and on my account X should not exist; I can only say that X is attested once, in a source compiled two centuries later"** — that is a concession with a price tag, and it buys credibility at the only place credibility is bought. **② Do not concede where two claims cannot both stand.** Conceding a point that would destroy your thesis is not generosity but incoherence, and reviewers find it. **③ Put your criterion early** — in the first chapter where it can be argued, not in the last section where it looks contrived: **a criterion introduced after the evidence has been read will be judged as special pleading** (this is the same requirement chapter seven will make of your citations: declare the rules before you show the receipts).
:::

::: example Take one paragraph apart {#ex-chai}
"**Guiding opinions are published by the Supreme People's Court, and interviews with judges confirm that they are read before adjudication; therefore judicial discretion in this area has narrowed.**" Label the four parts, supply the missing ones, and name the sentence in which a hostile reader stops believing you.

::: solution
**① Claim**: discretion has narrowed. **③ Evidence**: publication (a fact with a location) and interviews (a fact whose location is your own fieldwork — so the evidence is two items of different weight, which should be reported separately). **② Warrant, missing**: "a norm that is published and read is a constraint on decision-making". **④ Qualification, missing**: "narrowed" relative to what baseline, and in which of the decision's components (charging, characterising, sentencing). **A hostile reader stops at "confirm"**, because interviews confirming what the published document already says is not independent evidence; **the word begs the question that the section was supposed to settle** — namely whether reading amounts to following. **Repaired paragraph**: published guiding opinions (2019 no. X) set a range; **if judges treat a published range as binding, discretion over the ends of the range is reduced**; in sixteen of twenty interviews the range was named before the outcome was, and in three judges described departing from it with reasons given; **this supports a narrowing at the level of the sentence but says nothing about conviction.** **Marking: whoever supplies a qualification that a counter-example could falsify takes an A**, because that is the part the original paragraph could not survive without.
:::
:::

::: intuition The moral meaning of structure {#int-dao}
**Why this is not merely a matter of elegance**: **a structure is the record of who can check you.** **Claim-evidence paragraphs without warrants make checking impossible**: a reader can only say "I don't see it", which is a complaint about the reader. **Sections that fail the deletion test make blame impossible to allocate**: when a referee says "the argument is unclear" and cannot point, you cannot answer, and the exchange degenerates into authority — **exactly the failure the classical-criticism course diagnosed in "not awakened" style verdicts: an unverifiable judgement transfers its cost to the person judged.** And a conclusion that adds nothing but emphasis ("as is well known", "obviously") **asks the reader to accept the sum of steps she was never shown.** Three small remedies that make you a better citizen of your discipline: **write the objection your section answers; label the warrant whenever the evidence is more than one page away; and put the qualification in the same sentence as the claim, where it will be quoted with it** — the applied-writing course's rule about the sentence that gets screenshotted, in academic dress.
:::

::: history A set of drawings carried off the debating floor {#sec-tuzhi}
**Classical antiquity**: rhetoric supplies the parts of a speech — statement of the case, argument, refutation, peroration (**standard account**) — **and the academic section is their descendant by way of the university syllabus**. **Medieval Europe**: the disputed question, with objections, "on the contrary", responses and replies to objections in fixed order (**standard account**) — **the origin of the concession slot, and of the habit of stating your opponent better than he stated himself**. **Seventeenth century onwards**: scientific prose drops the disputation's costume and keeps its skeleton (**standard account**), adding the experimental section that the schools never had. **Twentieth century**: Toulmin analyses the layout of an argument into claim, grounds and warrant (**standard account**), and composition studies discover that first-year students write claim-evidence paragraphs and call them reasoning. **Read the sequence as a slow transfer of audience: from a live opponent who could interrupt, to a text that must contain the interruption in advance.** That is the whole of this chapter in one sentence, and it explains why the section on concession exists in a manual of structure: **the opponent is not a threat to your structure but one of its components, and a paper without her has one fewer part than it should.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**A background chapter that passes the deletion test** (**cut to what later sections use**); **"therefore" without a warrant** (**an unpaid customs duty**); **cost-free concessions** (**"some scholars have reservations" reads as manoeuvring**); **criteria introduced in the last chapter** (**it looks like special pleading, and is**).
:::

::: exercise Circle the connectives {level=1}
Take two thousand words of your own and circle every "therefore", "thus", "so" and "as is evident"; write the missing warrant under each.

::: solution
Marking: **a warrant that cannot be written is grounds for deleting the inference**, not for rewording it (the four parts).
:::

::: exercise Run the deletion test {level=2}
Delete one section of your draft entirely and read on; report what breaks, and whether anything does.

::: solution
Marking: **"nothing breaks" is a pass, provided you then cut the section** — the exercise fails only when the student restores the section and explains why it matters (one section, one business).
:::

::: exercise Essay: structure that can be hit {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the demand that a paper be "clearly structured" is not an aesthetic preference but an accountability device: it exists so that criticism can be localised, and a paper whose structure resists localisation is not harder to understand but harder to answer. This has a consequence that supervisors rarely state: writers who expect to be attacked cultivate visible structure, and writers who expect to be judged cultivate impressive structure — the second kind is longer, better footnoted, and unrefutable, which is why it gets marked highly and cited rarely.** Deploy at least six of: **the deletion and transposition tests**, **the three orderings and their audiences**, **the four parts of a paragraph**, **the unpaid "therefore"**, **the priced concession**, **the early criterion**, **the transfer of audience in the history section**. Then adjudicate: **if you could require one structural feature of every paper in your field, which one — and what would it make impossible?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**must name a feature and its casualty**). **Strong candidates: mandatory warrants** (every inference's general licence stated) — **casualty: the allusive, suggestive register that many humanists consider their best writing, which becomes non-compliant**; **mandatory objections** (each section names the objection it answers) — **casualty: the compilation paper, which cannot name an objection, and roughly a third of what gets published**; **mandatory visible skeleton** (headings alone must carry the argument) — **casualty: the long thematic chapter whose interest lies in accumulation.** **Highest marks for the systemic point: every one of these features converts a diffuse authority ("I don't see it") into a locatable claim, which shifts the cost of disagreement from the reader back to the writer — and that is why resistance to such rules is never about beauty.** If the required feature does not reduce output, it was not a device but a posture.
:::

::: summary
The chapter delivers: sections (**one job, one objection; two tests**), ordering (**analytical, inverted, thematic — chosen by the reader's disposition, not the material's shape**), paragraphs (**claim, warrant, evidence, qualification; only two of the four does everyone write**), concession (**price it, and do not yield on the impossible**), criterion (**early, or it is special pleading**), and one diagnosis: **invisible structure is a way of not being answerable.** Next chapter: citation, which this course treats as bookkeeping rather than etiquette.
