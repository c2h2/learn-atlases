title: "Academic integrity"
---

Most teaching on this subject takes the form of warnings to people who would not have cheated anyway. **This chapter's claim: integrity is not a binary of right and wrong but a scale of three positions, and the criterion on that scale is whether, before you wrote the sentence, there existed a check you were obliged to perform and did not.**

## Three positions, not right and wrong {#def-sandang}

::: definition Carelessness, conflict, knowledge {#def-san}
**Position ①, carelessness**: a wrong page number, an unmarked borrowing, a figure with an unlabelled axis, an overstatement you would not have made had you read your own draft aloud. **Position ②, interest**: you were paid, or reviewed, or promoted, or defending a supervisor's thesis, and the interest was not declared. **Position ③, knowledge**: you knew, and wrote it anyway — fabricating data, deleting a sentence from a quotation that changes its sense, submitting another's words as yours while knowing exactly what you were doing. **Why the distinction is not a softening: the three positions require different remedies.** Carelessness is answered by method — **a check that would have caught it** — and the response "I was careless" is a description of an absent procedure, not a confession. Interest is answered by disclosure, because interest is not removable and undisclosed interest corrupts everything downstream. Knowledge is answered by sanction. **The criterion, and it is the useful part of this chapter: before you wrote it, was there a check you were obliged to run?** If yes and you skipped it, that is a procedure failure; **if the check existed, you ran it, and the result still misleads, that is not misconduct but a limit of your method, and it must be published as such.** **The criterion is deliberately not "what did you intend", because intent is unobservable and is also the first thing a bad-faith actor can fake.**
:::

::: definition Eight familiar kinds {#def-ba}
**① Plagiarism**, including the structural kind: your own words, another's argument, order and examples, uncredited — **the hardest to see from the inside and the easiest to prove from the outside.** ② **Fabrication and falsification**, including the gentle variety: dropping two cases that "did not fit the sample frame". **③ Redundant publication** — the same result twice, with the second paper citing the first to look honest. **④ Salami slicing**, which is ③'s respectable cousin and the one that is usually a response to incentives rather than to dishonesty. **⑤ Authorship abuse**: gift, ghost, and the ordering fight; **the remedy is written down before the work starts, which is why this chapter belongs in your first year, not your last.** **⑥ Unauthorised or non-consensual use of data about people** (next section). **⑦ Undisclosed interest**, the most common breach in funded research and the most commonly thought not to count. **⑧ Bad citation as method** — citing work you know nothing about in order to enlist its authority, or burying the inconvenient reference in a footnote (chapter seven). **Note that three of the eight are cured by disclosure alone, two by pre-agreement, and only three require the language of guilt** — which is why a course built on exhortation addresses one category and leaves seven unmanaged.
:::

## Incentives determine the distribution {#def-jili}

::: definition The paper mill is a supply chain {#def-gongying}
**A mechanism, not a moral**: where output is counted, someone will supply units. **The paper mill is a supply chain with logistics**: order-taking, drafting, a stock of plausible data, images reused across journals, peer identities for hire, and a payment route (**standard account**: it is investigated as an industry, with revenue models and customers). **Two conclusions that follow and are rarely stated. ① The customer is usually not a criminal**: a person under a graduation or promotion rule that counts papers buys a unit, and the system that made the purchase rational is the same system that then punishes the buyer alone. **② Detection is expensive and asymmetric: verifying a paper means checking its sources, and the check costs more than the paper is worth to the journal that published it** (chapter seven's point that nobody pays for checking). **So the only defensible institutional response is not more exhortation but changing the counting** — reduce the number of counted units required for a degree, register study protocols before data collection, require deposit of materials (**standard account**: the registered-report format exists precisely for this). **A personal consequence worth acting on now: keep your raw material in a state you could hand over**, because in a supply-chain environment the burden of proof falls on whoever cannot produce a workbook.
:::

::: definition Consent, and four separate permissions {#def-tongyi}
**Research about people requires four permissions, taken separately, and they are routinely collapsed into one signature. ① To be approached** — that you may ask at all. **② To be recorded** — audio, notes, photographs, each a distinct trace. **③ To be quoted and identified**, including the small detail that makes a person recognisable in a small community (**anonymising a village by deleting names does not anonymise it if the story is unique**). **④ To be stored, shared and reused** — the question your data archive will ask in five years, when your interview is read by strangers you never mentioned. **Four rules: permission for one is not permission for the rest; permission may be withdrawn; consent given after seeing a draft of what will be said is worth infinitely more than consent given at the door; and you cannot grant on behalf of a community.** Then the part the ethics forms usually omit: **the cost falls on the researcher, who must decide whether the study is worth the relationship it consumes.** The applied-writing course's rule about writing what you would let others inspect is a consent rule in disguise, and the same goes for the interviews in your own thesis.
:::

::: definition After the error {#def-hou}
**Suppose you published something wrong. What you owe, in order: ① fix what can be fixed** — an erratum for a typo in a table, a corrected article where the argument stands; **② retract where the claim collapses** — **a retraction is an invalidation, not a verdict**, and treating it as a verdict is the belief that stops people correcting themselves; **③ name who is affected**, since downstream users cannot find your error by themselves; **④ say what the correction means for the conclusions that used it.** **The reason to publish the correction rather than quietly stop citing your own error is that the literature is a chain of payments and you are the only person who can void yours** (chapter seven). **The reason the norm fails is reputational and it fails predictably: the field punishes the visible self-correction more than the discovered concealment, which is one of the clearest cases I know of an incentive producing exactly the wrong behaviour.** If you ever edit a journal, this is the policy worth changing: **make the correction cheap and the concealment expensive** — which is also the logic of the three-position criterion at the head of this chapter.
:::

::: example Place them on the scale {#ex-fen}
**A**: a doctoral student quotes a passage from memory and does not check it; the page is wrong. **B**: a researcher writes a review of a competitor's grant proposal and cites her own unpublished work in the reviewer's comments. **C**: a team reports 12 of 15 sites and states in the method note that three were dropped for missing metadata; two of the three were dropped after seeing the results. **D**: an article's conclusion says "no study has examined X" while the author's own draft cites two studies of X. **For each, place the position on the scale and name the check that was missing.**

::: solution
**A: carelessness** — the check is the rule that you do not quote what you did not open (chapter seven), **and note that memory-quotation is how a false citation enters the literature without anyone intending it**. **B: interest**, and of the sharpest kind: using an asymmetrical position to advance yourself; **the check is a declaration made before the review, not a discovery afterwards.** **C: the reporting is good and the design is not**: the missing check is **pre-registration of the exclusion rule**, which is exactly what distinguishes a documented decision from a convenient one; **had the rule been written first, this would have been position "nothing at all"**. **D: knowledge**, arguably — a universal claim contradicted by the author's own apparatus is a claim she knew too much about; **the check is the search log and the discipline of writing the weaker sentence** (chapter three). **Marking: whoever says that C and D are the interesting pair takes an A** — **C is what procedural integrity looks like when the procedure is absent, and D is what it looks like when the incentive is present**, and neither involves a villain, which is the whole argument of the chapter.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why the integrity course never works {#int-meiyou}
**A recurring experience**: a session of warnings, slides of retractions, an honour code to sign; and the incidence does not change. **Four reasons. ① It targets the wrong distribution**: most breaches are carelessness and interest, and both are answered by procedures and disclosures rather than by guilt; exhortation addresses position ③, which is rare. **② It arrives after the habits**: the authorship agreement, the file discipline and the search log must exist in month one, and integrity courses run in month ten. **③ It names the wrong audience**: the people under the counting incentive are often the ones who cannot afford to comply, and a rule that ignores that is not ethics but privilege (chapter two's argument, applied here). **④ It treats the reader as the judge instead of the method**: the student learns that integrity means being caught. **The better course is this one, and it is not modest about the claim: chapters one to seven are integrity education conducted by other means** — a claim a stranger can verify, a dated search log, five columns of notes with locations, warrants written out, citations you opened, consent as four permissions. **No lecture on honesty comes close, because none of them hands you the procedure that would have caught the error.**
:::

::: history The age of the penalty system {#sec-chufa}
**Seventeenth century**: attribution becomes normal with the journal, and with it the possibility of taking another's words (**standard account**: chapter seven's history). **Nineteenth century**: the modern research university installs priority as the currency of the discipline, and with priority comes the charge of appropriation (**standard account**). **1970s–1980s**: human-subjects review and disclosure rules arrive after documented abuses in medicine (**standard account**) — **the first time the check, rather than the virtue, was made mandatory**. **1990s**: journals begin retraction notices; **research-misconduct offices appear in several countries** (**standard account**). **2010s**: paper mills, peer-ring fraud and mass withdrawals of thousands of articles at once (**standard account**). **Present**: detection tools, machine-generated text, and the machine-readable lineage that made checking possible and still unprofitable. **Two readings of the sequence. Optimist: the check has steadily replaced the oath, which is why the three-position criterion works. Pessimist: every new check raises the cost of compliance for the young and lowers it for the institutions that employ them, which is why the course keeps failing.** This chapter prefers the first reading with the second as a constraint: **build the procedure, and write down who bears its cost** — that sentence is also the answer to every version of "but everyone does it".
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Asking what the author intended** (**ask what check was owed**); **treating a retraction as a verdict** (**it is an invalidation, and stigma is why people do not correct**); **one consent signature for four permissions** (**recording is not quoting is not archiving**); **learning integrity as not being caught** (**the procedure, not the feeling**).
:::

::: exercise Write your own check list {level=1}
List the five checks you are obliged to run before submitting, in the order they can be run, each with the evidence it produces.

::: solution
Marking: **each check must produce an artefact** (a dated log, a completed consent form, an annotated draft); **a check that produces nothing is a resolve** (the three-position criterion).
:::
:::

::: exercise Split one consent {level=2}
Take one piece of fieldwork you have done or plan to do and write the four permissions separately, with what you will do if each is withdrawn.

::: solution
Marking: **the withdrawal plan must state what happens to data already collected**, which is the clause nobody has ready (the four permissions).
:::

::: exercise Essay: integrity as infrastructure {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a research culture's misconduct rate is set by its counting rules, and exhortation cannot move it. This does not excuse the individual, because the individual is the only place where a check can actually be performed. The two propositions look contradictory and are not: institutions change the distribution, people change the sample. Which is why the honest sentence about a young researcher who bought a paper is not "she is corrupt" nor "the system made her do it" but "the system priced honesty above her means and then punished her for the price" — and that sentence can only be written by someone willing to name the price.** Deploy at least six of: **the three positions**, **the eight kinds**, **the supply-chain analysis**, **the four permissions**, **the duties after an error**, **the four reasons the course fails**, **the history of the check replacing the oath**. Then adjudicate: **if you were made responsible for integrity in your department, what is the one rule you would change first, and who would you ask to pay for it?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**a rule and a payer, named**). **Passing: cut the required number of counted papers** — **and the payer is the ministry, the university's funding formula or the journal whose ranking depends on volume**, which is why the change never happens from below. **Stronger candidates: make registered protocols the condition of internal grant money** (payer: the funder, offset by fewer wasted projects); **pay for data and code curation as work, not as a favour** (payer: the grant, and this is the only rule that attacks the carelessness column rather than the fraud column); **put authorship agreements in the first-year contract** (payer: nobody; the cost is a conversation, which is why the refusal to do it is a preference, not a constraint). **Highest marks for the sentence that connects this chapter to the whole course: integrity rules are expensive for the person with the least time and money, so an integrity reform that adds procedures without adding staff converts ethics into a wealth test** — and this course's earlier claim, that the smallest unit of scholarship is a claim a stranger can verify, is then revealed as a claim about **who can afford to be verifiable**, which is the honest ending for a manual of academic writing.
:::
