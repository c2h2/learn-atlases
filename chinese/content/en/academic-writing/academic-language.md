title: "The language of scholarship"
---

Style advice almost always means "write more plainly", which is true and useless. **This chapter gives one operative rule: the strength of a sentence must match the strength of its evidence, and the two errors — writing up and writing down — are not equally punished, which is why almost everyone writes up**.

## Keeping the balance {#def-peiping}

::: definition Three registers of strength {#def-sandang}
**Grade your claims in three registers and use the wording of the register you are in. Register ①: the material directly supports it** — "the table shows", "in edition A the line reads", "eleven of the twenty interviews name". **Register ②: the material supports it but allows other readings** — "this is consistent with", "may be explained by", "suggests". **Register ③: you are inferring** — "I argue", "the present paper proposes", "on these grounds". **Three reasons the discipline demands this wording, none of them aesthetic. ① A sentence is a promise about what you did**: "X demonstrates that" promises a demonstration; when the demonstration is one suggestive case, the sentence is not inelegant but false. **② Asymmetry of punishment: writing up is penalised, writing down is not** — nobody refuses a paper for excessive modesty, which makes overclaiming individually rational and explains why the literature keeps needing corrections. ③ **The register is where your supervisor cannot help you**: only you know which register your evidence is in. **A useful habit from evidence-based medicine, where these markers are called hedges or evidentials and their function is to state where the knowledge came from** (**standard account**): **treat each verb of showing, suggesting and proving as an invoice, and price it against the material behind it.**
:::

::: definition What nominalisation costs {#def-mingci}
**The mechanism**: Chinese and English both allow an event to be compressed into a noun, and academic prose loves it, because it converts a doing into a thing you can discuss — "the introduction of the reform" instead of "the ministry introduced the reform". **That is the benefit and it is real; the price is that the nominalisation deletes the agent and the time.** "**The emergence of the error**" does not say who produced it; "**the slowdown of growth**" does not say whose; "**the absence of responsibility**" does not say who failed. **Three checks worth running mechanically on any draft: ① who did this? ② when? ③ is the noun hiding a verb you would have to defend?** Where the answer to ① is "I do not know and prefer not to say", you have found the sentence that will be quoted back to you. **This is not a complaint about Europeanised Chinese or about Latinate English: it is a claim about accountability, since a sentence without an agent is a sentence without a person answerable for it** — the same argument as the previous chapter's, arriving by way of grammar.
:::

## Person and the Europeanised sentence {#def-rencheng}

::: definition Who is doing the action {#def-zhuci}
**Three conventions, three costs. ① First person singular** ("I argue") — **clear and accountable; expensive in fields where the convention reads as self-assertion**, and cheapest in English, where it has been ordinary for two generations. **② The inclusive "we"** — used for the authorial voice, for the reader-and-author pair, and for the disciplinary community; **pick one and mean it, since a shifting "we" makes it unclear who is committed** (**standard account**: the editorial advice is consistent on this). **③ The impersonal** ("it is found that", "研究表明") — **looks objective, deletes the agent, and lies by omission when the thing "research" shows is one study you chose.** **The rule: match the pronoun to the responsibility.** Where the claim is yours, say so; **where it is the material's, name the material — "the two colophons date the block to 1522", not "it is clear that"**; where it is another's, cite; where nobody's, do not write it.
:::

::: definition Three questions for the Europeanised sentence {#def-ouhua}
**Long sentences with borrowed syntax are attacked constantly and analysed rarely. Three questions sort the useful from the damage. ① Is the length doing work?** A sentence that holds four qualifications in suspension may be the only way to state a complex claim without lying about it; **that length is an instrument.** A sentence that is long because each clause repeats the last is a failure of nerve. **② Can the reader hold the subject to its verb?** English and Chinese both parse left-branching material badly past about a dozen words; **if the subject arrives and its predicate has been buried under three layers of modification, the sentence has lost its own agent** — which is the grammatical twin of the nominalisation problem. **③ Is the abstraction necessary for the reader's sake or yours?** If a term is a technical instrument your field uses to make a distinction, keep it and define it once; **if it is a synonym you reached for to avoid an ordinary word, delete it.** **Note what these questions do not include: "is it native?" — a syntactic import is not a disease, and the complaint that academic Chinese is Europeanised has been made since 1920 and describes a completed change of register** (history section below).
:::

::: definition One term, one word; and the flow of information {#def-yizhi}
**Two rules of economy. ① One concept, one word.** Synonyms are for poetry; in scholarship they are noise, because **the reader reasonably infers a new concept from a new term**, and you have then introduced a concept you did not intend. **Keep the term that appears in your definition and in your headings; change the words only when you mean something different, and mark that you do.** **② Old information first, new information last.** Sentences begin with what the reader already has and end with what you are adding, because the end of the sentence is where attention lands; **a paragraph whose new claims keep arriving in the middle forces the reader to re-read, and readers experience re-reading as difficulty, then as your fault.** Two diagnostics: **read the first six words of each of your paragraphs in sequence** — they should form an argument by themselves; and **check that your paragraph-initial words are old information**, because a paragraph that begins with new information is either two paragraphs or has lost its connection. **Obscurity is not depth; it is the one technique available to a writer who wants to avoid being wrong.**
:::

::: example Revise six sentences {#ex-liuji}
Revise for register, agent and information flow: **1** "Studies have shown that the practice was widespread." **2** "The implementation of the policy resulted in the improvement of outcomes." **3** "This clearly proves that the hypothesis is correct." **4** "It is generally agreed that the novel is his masterpiece." **5** "We (the author) contend that the dating is mistaken." **6** "Due to the fact that the evidence was lacking, the conclusion could not be reached."

::: solution
**1**: name the studies and the finding, and drop to register ② unless the material forces ① — "**the three surveys of the 2010s report X in three provinces, which suggests the practice was common there**". **2**: restore agents and times — "**the ministry extended the subsidy in 1998; admissions rose the following year**"; nominalisations deleted, and note the register now follows. **3**: "clearly" plus "proves" is two overclaims in six words — "**this case supports the hypothesis; nothing here rules out the alternative**". **4**: name who agrees, and mark it as a claim about reception — "**since the 1980s the novel has usually been ranked first among his works (three anthologies, two textbooks)**"; **"it is generally agreed" is the sentence that will be falsified by one reviewer.** **5**: the parenthetical confession that "we" is lonely is honest; write "I contend" and mean it. **6**: "**because the evidence was lacking, no conclusion followed**" — or better, say whose conclusion and on what question. **Marking: whoever rewrites 4 rather than deleting it takes an A**, because the fix is not modesty but re-description: what was an assertion about the book becomes a documented claim about reception, which is both weaker and far more interesting.
:::
:::

::: intuition Affectation and fawning are the same fault {#int-zhuang}
**Two opposite styles, one diagnosis.** **Affectation**: piling abstractions to counterfeit depth, and borrowing certainty you do not have by dropping qualifiers. **Fawning**: counterfeit plainness — the idiom of intimacy ("we all know that"), the anecdote instead of the argument, the confession that replaces the method. **What they share: both take something from the reader and leave no entry in the ledger** — **respect extracted from abstraction, agreement extracted from familiarity**. The third path this course keeps recommending is neither style but a habit: **write sentences that can be checked, and let the reader see that you are standing behind them.** Two corollaries worth memorising. **① Difficulty is rarely an accident in a writer who can write plainly; it is a choice about who carries the risk** — an obscure sentence cannot be pinned down, so it cannot be refuted, which is comfortable and worthless. **② Plainness is not the opposite of difficulty: precision is.** Some claims are genuinely awkward to state, and the awkwardness must stay in the sentence; **the tell of a good writer is that she is plain where plainness is available and clumsy only where the subject is clumsy.**
:::

::: history Three changes of dress for one language {#sec-huanzhuang}
**Classical register**: written Chinese was a separate language from speech, with its own syntax and its own authority — a delay line between writing and speaking, which is also why "language" and "style" were separable objects for a thousand years (the history-of-chinese course's account of literary Sinitic). **1910s–1920s**: the vernacular becomes the language of scholarship, and with it the Europeanised sentence: new conjunctions, nominalisations, and the habit of the long qualified statement; **the same writers argue about whether this is decay or equipment, and both are right** (**standard account**). **1950s**: terminology standardisation and the Soviet-influenced textbook form a new academic register, with its own permitted connectives and its own permitted hedges (**standard account**). **1980s to present**: translation of Western theory floods in, English becomes the language of the top journals in several fields, and **Chinese-language scholars now write two academic registers, with the second learned from articles rather than from teachers** (**standard account**). **Why the history matters to your sentences: every norm you are told to obey about academic Chinese is roughly a hundred years old and was controversial when introduced.** That is liberating and disciplining at once — **liberating because the "natural" academic style is a recent invention and can be revised; disciplining because the invention was made to solve a problem you still have, which is saying things that can be false, and any new style that makes that harder is a regression no matter how elegant.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Register inflation — "proves" on second-hand material** (**the verb is an invoice**); **"research shows" meaning your one source** (**write "the material in this paper suggests"**); **swapping terms to avoid repetition** (**the reader infers a new concept**); **using obscurity as insurance** (**it protects you from being wrong, which is the point**).
:::

::: exercise Count your hedges {level=1}
Take a page of your draft, mark every strength marker, and grade each against your register; list the upgrades and downgrades you owe.

::: solution
Marking: **the revision must go in both directions**; a draft that only gets humbler has not been measured, only scolded (three registers).
:::

::: exercise Restore the agent {level=2}
Find ten nominalisations in your own text and rewrite the four that hide a decision you should be defending.

::: solution
Marking: **the four must name a person and a date**; "the committee's decision" fails until you say which committee and when (the nominalisation account).

::: exercise Essay: precision over plainness {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the demand for plain academic prose has a hidden cost: plainness is easy where the claim is simple, and a field whose claims are all simple is a field that has stopped asking hard questions. The alternative is not difficulty but precision, and precision often looks like clumsiness — a sentence with four qualifications is uglier than a slogan and far more useful. The real stylistic vice of scholarship is therefore not obscurity but fluency: the sentence that reads beautifully and commits to nothing, because fluency is what readers mistake for understanding, and what editors mistake for a claim.** Deploy at least six of: **the three registers**, **the asymmetry of punishment**, **nominalisation and agency**, **the three pronoun conventions**, **the three questions on Europeanised syntax**, **one concept one word**, **old-before-new information**, **the shared fault of affectation and fawning**, **the three historical registers**. Then adjudicate: **should a discipline correct its language by rule — a style guide with teeth — or by training, and what does your answer assume about who is being corrected?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**state the assumption about the person corrected**). **Rules** (mandatory hedges, banned unattributed universals, a checklist before submission) are cheap, transmissible and **they work on the careless, which is most breaches**; **the assumption is that the problem is inattention**. **Training** assumes incapacity, and its cost falls unevenly: **a rule applied to a non-native writer corrects a register she has not been taught, which is the real content of complaints that style guides are "linguistic discrimination"** — **and this chapter's history section gives the answer: academic Chinese's own norms were introduced by imitation, argument and teaching, not by a guide, and the periods when the register changed are precisely when the language was being argued about publicly.** **Highest marks for the position that the correct target is fluency, not error: require the sentence that commits — "my material supports register ② and not ①" — and style follows, whereas a rule against a word follows nothing.** **Bonus: noticing that this chapter's own prose is an argument about style and should be judged by its own test** — the passing grade requires one example from any draft submitted with the essay, and a specific place where the writer downgraded a verb.
:::
