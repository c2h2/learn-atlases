title: "Reading and notes"
---

You have found the literature. **This chapter is about the failure that follows most often: reading a great deal and producing nothing you can use — because the notes you took recorded the book, not the difference between the book and your thought**.

## One card, five columns {#def-wulane}

::: definition The five-column format {#def-wu}
**The compulsory format.** For each item you read, five columns. **① Location**: edition, chapter, page — the address you would hand to a stranger. **② The smallest quotable fragment**: not a summary, but the sentence in the author's words, copied exactly. **③ The author's claim**: what she asserts, in her terms. **④ Her strongest evidence**: not what she mentions, the thing that would actually decide the case for her. **⑤ Your reaction**: written last. Four rules. **The order is the method**: ⑤ comes last because anything written first gets defended rather than corrected. **② must be copied, not paraphrased** — a paraphrase silently imports your vocabulary into her argument, and six months later you cannot tell whose thought you are quoting. **③ must be a sentence the author would accept**; if she would deny it, you have written your own opinion under her name. **⑤ must pre-declare its use**: "usable as a counter-example to X", "usable for the periodisation in my chapter two", or "usable for nothing yet".
:::

::: definition The smallest citable unit {#def-danwei}
**The column that separates reading from scholarship is ②, and its rule is: the smallest unit you can cite is one sentence with its location.** Consequences. **① "I have read Foucault" is not a unit**, and neither is "his view is that…"; a unit is a sentence, a page, and a claim about what it shows. **② Plain copying (摘抄) merges two different states into one file: having read and having understood.** The evidence is embarrassingly direct: **open your own copy-file and try to reconstruct, for three passages at random, why you copied them.** Failure is not a memory defect; it means you never recorded the reason, which is column ⑤. **③ Understanding is measured by reconstruction, not recognition.** Read the passage, close the file, and write down what its author's argument was, what her best evidence was, and where she might be wrong; **if you cannot, you have recognised the text, and recognition is the feeling of having read.** **The operational rule of this course: anything without a written reaction does not enter your citation pool.** Harsh, cheap to apply, and it will cut your usable reading list by two thirds in the first month — which is what makes room for reading properly.
:::

## Three passes {#def-sanbian}

::: definition Each pass delivers a column {#def-jiaofu}
**Do not read an article once carefully; read it three times carelessly, each time for a different deliverable.** **Pass 1 — the map** (five minutes): title, abstract, conclusion, notes; **delivers the decision whether to continue**, and if you continue, column ③. **Pass 2 — the argument** (thirty minutes): body only, with a pencil; **delivers columns ② and ④**; you are reconstructing, not admiring, so the test is closing the file and writing the argument in three sentences. **Pass 3 — the quarrel** (later, once you know your own position): read only against your claim; **delivers column ⑤.** Why split: **each pass uses a different faculty, and doing them at once produces the illusion of depth** — you understand the sentence while reading it and cannot find it again in the archive. **One further economy: most papers deserve pass 1 only, and the time saved is what makes pass 2 possible for the twenty items that matter.**
:::

::: definition Reading against {#def-fandu}
**The technique for pass 3**: read the paper asking one question — **what would have to be true for this to be wrong?** Then check whether the author ever says it is true. Two results. Either **you find the unguarded assumption and a paper writes itself** (this is the "shared premise" source of chapter two), or **you find that the author guarded it, and you have learned how**, which is worth more than a criticism. **A caution: adversarial reading is a tool for finding gaps, not for feeling superior**, and it produces a specific pathology in graduate students — **the reflex of finding the flaw, which makes you useless at the thing scholarship mostly consists of, namely accepting a well-made argument and building on it.** So set a rule: **for every flaw you record in column ⑤, record one thing the paper does that you cannot yet do.** If you cannot, either the paper is trivial (then why are you citing it) or you are not yet equipped to read it (then read it again later).
:::

::: definition Three kinds of failed note {#def-shibai}
**① The copy-file**: beautiful quotations, no reactions; it records the author and loses you. **② The summary**: one paragraph per source in your own words — **which deletes the very thing you will need, since you will have to re-find the exact sentence to prove anything** (column ② exists to prevent that). ③ **The diary**: "chapter three interesting, reminds me of X" — affect without address, unusable because it names no location and no claim. **Three kinds of successful note, by contrast: the unit card** (all five columns filled, citable next week); **the bridge** (one sentence stating how two items disagree, with both locations, which is the raw material of the review in chapter five); **the debt** ("I used this author's periodisation in my section 2.3 and did not quote him") — **the note that keeps you honest when the prose is months old.** If your files contain only the first two, you are building an argument on material you cannot audit (chapter seven).
:::

::: example Rewrite one card {#ex-kapian}
Here is a card (**composed for this lesson**): "**Li: argues that popular drama shaped the way literati wrote about ghosts; good chapter on exorcism; interesting, similar to the Song tales chapter.**" Rebuild it in the five-column format, and mark which of the original content survives.

::: solution
**① Location**: need edition and page — **absent, so this card cannot be cited at all; that is not pedantry, it is the difference between a memory and a citation.** **② Fragment**: copy the sentence in which Li says drama "shaped" the literati account, since "shaped" is doing all the work and its precise sense is unknown. **③ Claim**: Li asserts a direction of influence from performance to text (**and note that "shaped" hides the mechanism, which is exactly what you would need to check**). **④ Strongest evidence**: presumably a comparison of play scripts with literati tale-collections in a stated period — **the card does not say, so pass 2 has not been done.** ⑤ **Reaction**: the original "similar to the Song tales chapter" is the only usable part, and it is a bridge note needing the other location. **Survives: a quarter of the content; and the failure is not the note-taker's ability but the format, which asked for none of the five.** **Marking: whoever notices that this card would have cost four minutes more to make properly, and that those four minutes are the whole of the difference between reading and research, takes an A** (see the intuition below for why the standard has to survive contact with your future laziness).
:::
:::

::: intuition The lazier you, one year from now {#int-yinian}
**Design rule: write your notes for a colleague who is a stranger; in fact write them for the lazier, hurried, less intelligent person you will be in twelve months.** Concretely: **assume that reader will not re-read the book, will not remember why the item mattered, has no tolerance for your abbreviations, and will paste your sentence into a draft.** Four consequences. **① Location before insight**, because the insight will seem fine at midnight and unverifiable in April. **② Full titles, not pet names** — "the 1602 preface" means nothing without its book. **③ Copy the language you will quote**, including the diacritics and the page of the edition you used. ④ **Pre-commit the use**, since the note you file under "maybe useful" will not be decoded again. **The extreme test this course recommends: could your notes survive you?** If your hard drive were read by a doctoral student from another country, could she tell which of these claims you verified and which you copied from a footnote? **A forty-year archive standard, borrowed from the data managers: if it cannot be understood without you, it is not an archive but a diary.** And one tool-warning: **anything you cannot restate without your note-taking software is not yet in your citation pool** — the tool's search box is not your memory, and the export button is not your argument.
:::

::: history From notebook to a reader that writes the summary {#sec-zhaiji}
**Pre-modern China**: reading was recorded as 札记 — short entries keyed to a classic, ordered by the text, with the reader's judgement appended (**standard account**); **the model of the second and fifth columns, arrived at independently and a millennium early**. **Early modern Europe**: the commonplace book, with extracts copied under headings and re-arranged by topic (**standard account**) — **the direct ancestor of column ②, and note that its purpose was re-use, not remembrance.** **Twentieth century**: the index card and the seminar paper (**standard account**); **the card's size enforced the smallest citable unit — a format that thought for you**. **Late twentieth century**: hypertext and the "second brain", with backlinks between notes (**standard account**): the promise that insight would emerge from the network. **Twenty-first century**: readers that summarise, tag and answer questions about a text. **This chapter's position, stated as a testable claim: the summariser writes pass-1 notes perfectly, which is precisely the kind of note that fails to distinguish having-read from having-understood — the device is excellent at deleting pass 2, and pass 2 is where column ④ comes from.** So the tool belongs at the beginning of the workflow and not at its end: **let it produce the map; you write the reconstruction, or you have learned nothing you can defend.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Paraphrasing in column ②** (**a paraphrase imports your vocabulary into her argument**); **writing the reaction first** (**it gets defended instead of corrected**); **believing recognition is understanding** (**close the file and rebuild the argument**); **keeping only notes that need you** (**could your notes survive you?**).
:::

::: exercise Rebuild five cards {level=1}
Take five cards you already have and fill any missing columns; note which cards could not be completed and why.

::: solution
Marking: **the incompletable cards must be scheduled for a second reading or dropped**; leaving them in the pool is how unverified claims reach a draft (the smallest citable unit).
:::
:::

::: exercise Write two bridges {level=2}
Choose two items in your pool that disagree; write one sentence naming the disagreement and both locations, and one sentence naming what they share.

::: solution
Marking: **the shared premise must be real** — if the two authors are not talking about the same thing, there is no bridge, only a pun (chapter five's matrix).
:::
:::

::: exercise Essay: notes as infrastructure {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a note-taking system is an argument about what your future self will be able to do, and every system in history has overestimated that person. The index card, the commonplace book and the networked second brain each promised that storage would become understanding; none of them could deliver column ⑤, because that column is the only one you cannot delegate. Which is why reading machines change the economics of reading without changing its unit: pass one becomes free, pass two becomes the whole cost, and the people who look busiest are the ones who have paid nothing.** Deploy at least six of: **the five columns**, **the smallest citable unit**, **the two states**, **the three passes**, **the three failed types**, **the archive standard**, **the tool warning**. Then adjudicate: **should a doctoral programme teach note-taking, and if so what exactly would be examinable?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**must name an assessable object**). **A passing shape: teach it, and examine the cards themselves** — **randomly sample five claims in a submitted chapter and require the card that supports each, with location**; that is not a test of writing but of whether the chain from material to sentence exists. **It is cheap, it is unanswerable, and it would eliminate most fabrication**, since a fabricated citation has no card. **Stronger answers notice the risk: examining notes examines the private workshop, and programmes that grade process tend to produce process theatre — formatted emptiness, which is what happened when teaching became publication counts** (chapter ten). **Highest marks for the compromise: assess the chain, not the notebook** — **the sample-and-produce exercise, with no mark for elegance in the cards themselves**; that is the same distinction this course has drawn throughout, between the existence of a record and its auditability.
:::
