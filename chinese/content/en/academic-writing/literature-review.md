title: "The literature review"
---

Most reviews fail in the same way, and the failure is invisible to the person writing it. **This chapter's first test is brutal and takes one minute: delete every name from your subheadings — if the headings still make sense, you have written a review; if they are blank, you have written a reading report**.

## The unit is the question {#def-danwei}

::: definition A shelf catalogue and a battle map {#def-dituzu}
**The first criterion: the organising unit of a review is the question, not the author.** A review organised by author is **a shelf catalogue**: it records who said what, and its implicit claim is that you have read them. **A review organised by question is a battle map**: it states what is at issue, who stands where, and what has been settled. **Three things follow. ① Two authors may occupy one cell** — and if your review has a paragraph each, you have described the shelf rather than the fight. **② One author may occupy three cells** — and you must split her across them, however unfair that feels to her memory. ③ **The order of cells is your argument**: whoever reads the map learns where you think the decisive ground is. **The practical form: your subheadings are propositions, not topics.** "Three accounts of the dating" is a topic; "the dating evidence points to two redactions, and only one account admits it" is a proposition. **A reviewer reading proposition headings can disagree with your review before reaching your own contribution, which is the point: your review is the first place you exercise judgement, and the last place where it is cheap.**
:::

::: definition Three columns and the matrix {#def-juzhen}
**The instrument**: a table whose rows are the live questions and whose columns are the positions, with each cell containing the strongest argument for that combination and its location. **Read across a row and you have the state of a question. Read down a column and you have a school. Look for the empty cell, because that is where your contribution will be claimed.** **Three columns of judgement beside it: settled, contested, unasked** — the last being the hardest to earn and the most valuable. **What the matrix is for: it fails informatively.** Filling it forces you to discover the cell you cannot populate, which is either a hole in the literature or a hole in your reading; **and the review's job description is precisely to hand the reader that hole with coordinates**. **If your matrix fills completely, either your topic is closed — or your rows are the wrong size, which they usually are: the question was too small to have a debate.** A good review also carries the **disagreement type** per cell: is this dispute about evidence, about definition, or about what would count? **Two-thirds of scholarly quarrels are of the third kind, and marking them as such is worth more than any summary of positions** (chapter six makes the same demand of your own argument).
:::

## Three failures {#def-shibai}

::: definition The chronology and the roll of honour {#def-biannian}
**Failure ①, the chronology**: "in the 1930s it was proposed, in the 1950s supplemented, in the 1980s revised". **Its defect: nobody is answerable for any judgement, because time has been made to look like a cause.** The style also smuggles in a progress story — later is better — which is frequently false and never argued. **The repair: put the date in parentheses and the claim in the main verb** — "the attribution (1931) is still orthodox (Zhang 1988), though the manuscript reads otherwise". **Failure ②, the roll of honour**: listing every important name for safety. **This is insurance, not scholarship**, and its cost is **citation inflation**: the more you cite defensively, the less each citation means, and readers learn to read your apparatus as noise. **The tell is a source you cite without ever stating what it shows.** **Failure ③, greed**: covering everything. **A review that covers everything has no thesis, because a thesis excludes.** The stopping rule is the same as for searching: **when consecutive items add nothing that changes the map, stop** — and say in the text what you excluded and on what grounds, which turns your coverage claim from a boast into a method.
:::

::: definition The gap, and the two-skins problem {#def-quekou}
**Every review must end by naming a gap; the commonest fatal flaw is that the gap does not match the review.** **The test: write your three sentences from chapter one, and place each against the matrix.** If the gap you announce is "no one has studied X" while your map shows six studies of X, you have contradicted yourself two pages earlier. **The two-skins failure (two layers that do not adhere): the review describes one debate, and the contribution opens another.** It is invisible to the writer because both parts are individually competent, and readers feel only a loss of momentum. **Repairs, in order of frequency: ① the gap is real but your map is wrong** — redraw the rows so that the empty cell appears in your table, and the review becomes an argument for why it is empty; **② the gap exists but nobody would care** — this is a topic problem, not a writing problem, and it must go back to chapter two's third circle; **③ the gap is filled by your own citation list**, in which case you have found something better: your contribution is a synthesis, and it must be claimed as one rather than disguised as a discovery.
:::

::: definition Three kinds of contribution {#def-gongxian}
**Say which you are making, in these words. ① New material**: something not previously used. **② New method**: something not previously done to this material. **③ New interpretation**: something not previously said about material everybody knows. **The reason for insisting on the taxonomy is that reviewers use it as a checklist, and the vaguer your claim the more likely they are to supply the answer for you — usually the unfavourable one.** Two rules of phrasing. **Write "differs from", not "innovates":** novelty is a verdict on other people's reading, difference is a fact you can point to (**this is the single most common fix in my editing**). **And never write "no one has studied" where you mean "I have not found"** — **a universal claim invites one counter-example and your entire review is then read as a failed search** (the search log of chapter three is your only defence). **One asymmetry of risk to remember: every generalisation about your field may be quoted back at you by someone who knows the field better than you do**, so keep field-wide statements in the same sentence as their evidence.
:::

::: example Reorganise one review {#ex-chongzu}
Here is a review's structure (**composed for this lesson**): "2.1 Hu Shi (1922). 2.2 Chen Yinke (1930s). 2.3 The 1950s debate. 2.4 Recent work." **Rewrite it as a map; then say what your new headings claim.**

::: solution
**Possible reconstruction. 2.1 What the two authors disagree about is not period but evidence** (Hu cites the preface; Chen cites the colophon; both were reprinted in 1935 and the difference collapsed). **2.2 The 1950s debate settled a definition nobody now accepts.** 2.3 **Recent work has split on whether the question is answerable at all** — **which is the real state of the field, and cannot be seen at all in the chronological version.** 2.4 **The empty cell: no one has asked what the 1935 reprint did to the dispute.** **What the new headings claim: each is a proposition that a specialist could deny**, and each denial would change your gap. **Marking: whoever notices that the chronological version concealed a fourth claim — that the 1950s debate is a dead end — takes an A.** That is the political content of the form: **a chronology is how a literature avoids taking a position**, and readers who are deciding whether to trust your judgement will notice that you did not exercise it (the intuition section states the audience problem).
:::
:::

::: intuition A review is written for three readers {#int-sange}
**Who is actually reading your review? Three people, wanting incompatible things.** **① The examiner or referee**, who is looking for a reason to stop worrying: she wants to see that you know the field's landmarks and that you can tell a settled question from an open one. **② The stranger from a neighbouring field**, who wants to know why anyone has been arguing about this for forty years, and who will use your review as her only source about the field for the rest of her life — **so every sentence you write about "the standard view" may be quoted back to you by someone who has read none of the primary work** (hence the phrasing rules). **③ Yourself in eighteen months**, who will have forgotten all of it and needs the map again. **Design consequences: put the landmarks in the first paragraph of each section, the disagreement type in the topic sentence, and the exclusion rule in a footnote; keep one paragraph addressed explicitly to reader ②** (the "why this was worth arguing" paragraph, which is the hardest thing in the chapter to write and the most reused when you give talks). **Notice what none of the three wants: completeness.** That is the empirical ground for the stopping rule — and if your supervisor asks for more coverage, ask which of the three she is serving, because the answer usually reveals that she is preparing to send it somewhere specific.
:::

::: history The provenance of a genre {#sec-laili}
**Nineteenth-century Germany**: the research seminar requires candidates to report prior work before proposing a solution, and the "Forschungsbericht" becomes a examinable genre (**standard account**). **Early twentieth century**: the disciplinary survey article appears with the specialised journal, and with it the review as a claim to have surveyed a field (**standard account**) — **a genre whose authority rests on the size of its bibliography and therefore inflates it** (failure ② is built into the form). **Mid-twentieth century**: the research grant proposal imports the "state of the art" section, funded by the promise that the applicant knows where the frontier is. **1970s onwards**: the systematic review is developed in clinical medicine, with protocol, search log, inclusion criteria and a risk-of-bias table (**standard account**) — **the only version of the genre that made its own method auditable, and it was invented because the older kind was demonstrably misleading**. **Humanities adoption is partial and late** (**standard account**), **and this chapter sides with the clinicians on one point and against them on another: adopt the search log and the exclusion criteria; refuse the pretence that a synthesis can be produced by counting**. **Why the history matters for you: your review is assessed by someone holding a genre convention, and the convention — "show that you know the field" — is what produces chronological reviews.** Once you see the convention as history you can satisfy it more cheaply: **give the examiner her landmarks in the first sentences and spend the rest of the chapter on the map** (chapter ten turns the same observation into viva tactics).
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Subheadings that are topics** (**make them propositions**); **chronology as structure** (**it makes time look like a cause**); **"no one has studied X"** (**a universal claim; one counter-example ends you**); **a gap in a different shape from your map** (**align the three sentences with the matrix, word for word**).
:::

::: exercise Convert to propositions {level=1}
Take four of your current review subheadings and rewrite each as a proposition a specialist could deny.

::: solution
Marking: **each must name a disagreement or a verdict**, not a subject ("views on X" fails; "the two datings rest on one colophon" passes).
:::

::: exercise Find the empty cell {level=2}
Build the matrix for one live question in your topic, mark the empty cell, and write two candidate gaps that cell would license.

::: solution
Marking: **each gap must name what would count as filling it** (chapter one's ③), otherwise it is an absence of reading, not of research.

::: exercise Essay: the review as instrument of power {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a literature review is the place where a field decides who is inside it, and the genre's conventions — the size of the bibliography, the language of the sources, the decades treated as live — are admission criteria disguised as scholarship. The systematic-review ideal does not remove this politics; it makes it auditable, which is different and better. But an audit trail can also be a way of looking rigorous while asking an unimportant question, since nobody audits the choice of question.** Deploy at least six of: **the shelf catalogue and the battle map**, **the matrix and its empty cell**, **the three failures**, **the gap and the two skins**, **the three contributions**, **the three readers**, **the genre history**. Then adjudicate: **should your field require search logs in reviews — and what would it start measuring that it does not measure now?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**name the new object of measurement**). **Passing shape: yes, and it measures thoroughness, which is the wrong thing to want to measure — the moment a review is graded on records, it inflates them** (failure ② plus chapter three's audit point). **Stronger: the log measures whether the authors can say what their method could not have seen, which is a claim about design, not about effort** — **and design is the only thing a review can be fairly judged on** (chapter three makes the same argument about the search itself). **Highest marks for noticing that the log also disciplines the politics**: **an exclusion criterion written in advance can be challenged, whereas "these are the important authors" cannot; the audit trail converts gatekeeping from taste into procedure — which is worse for the excluded few and better for everyone who comes after.**
:::
