---
title: "Citation and references"
---

Students treat citation as etiquette and formatting. **This chapter treats it as bookkeeping: a reference list is a ledger of debts, and the ledger is the one part of your paper that other people will audit** (chapter eight, on integrity, proceeds from that sentence).

## Two systems {#def-liangtao}

::: definition In-text notes and footnotes {#def-tixi}
**Two apparatuses, one job.** **Author-date in the text** suits fields where **the currency of a claim matters** and where readers navigate by who said it when. **Notes at the foot or end** suit fields where **the argument must run without interruption and the apparatus has to carry more than a pointer** — a variant reading, a translation, a qualification, a defence. **The job is identical: every borrowed element must be traceable to a specific location in a specific source.** Three consequences. **① Formatting is repairable; function is not.** A style guide can be fixed in an afternoon and a copy-editor will do it; **a citation that points to a book but not to a page cannot be repaired by anyone but you, because only you know which of its 400 pages you looked at.** So do not argue about styles in your proposal; argue about page numbers. **② Cite where the claim would fail without it, not where you feel exposed** — the defensive citation, sprayed to protect you, inflates the ledger and devalues every real entry (chapter five's roll of honour). **③ The reference is a payment, not a courtesy**, which is why the next section is a list of prohibitions rather than of tips.
:::

::: definition Six commandments {#def-liutiao}
**① Do not enter in the list an item you never opened.** The commonest way to acquire a false citation is to take it from a secondary work that took it from another (**standard account**: the mechanism of the citation loop, and of the fabricated reference). **② Mark a citation at second hand.** Write "cited in", and if the primary matters, go and get it; **if it does not matter, why is it in your argument?** **③ Give a page with the edition you used** — a page number without an edition is not a location, since pagination changes with every reprint and translation. **④ Quote what you checked in the source you used**, including its spelling; silently modernising a quotation is a change to somebody else's text and belongs in a note. **⑤ Distinguish your summary from the author's claim.** "X argues that…" is a reportable act; "as is well known" is unassignable and therefore uncheckable. **⑥ Log your own search.** A reviewer who can see what you did not find can discount your silences; a reviewer who cannot will assume the worst (chapter three). **Six rules, and only the second and third generate most of the complaints in real editorial work — because both are invisible to the writer at the moment of writing.**
:::

## Old books and editions {#def-guji}

::: definition Cite so that it can be found {#def-banye}
**For rare, manuscript or pre-modern material the rule changes: cite so that a stranger can find the thing, not so that a style guide is satisfied.** Priority order: **the identifier that survives re-binding and re-cataloguing** — a folio or leaf number, a scroll or juan division, a catalogue number, a database record identifier and access date, an archive fonds and box. **A page number of a facsimile helps only the reader who owns that facsimile.** Four practices. **① Name the edition in the first citation and abbreviate afterwards.** ② **For a text with stable internal divisions, cite the division** — juan, chapter, act, poem number — because it survives all editions, which is why classicists cite line numbers and not pages. **③ For anything online, record the date you saw it and the archival identifier if one exists**, since links die and, worse, quietly change content. **④ Say which tradition of the text you are quoting**: an edited critical edition, a popular reprint, or a recension produced by a seventeenth-century critic (the classical-criticism course's whole eighth chapter is the reason). **The test to apply to your own list: could a graduate student in another country, with no access to you, obtain these exact words on this exact page?**
:::

::: definition The citation chain and phantom entries {#def-lian}
**Two pathological objects. ① The single-source illusion**: when five recent works all say the same surprising thing, and their bibliographies reveal that all five cite one 1998 article, then **you do not have a consensus; you have a genealogy, and its root may be a misreading** (**standard account**: the mechanism is documented in scientometrics as citation chains and errors that propagate because checking is unprofitable). **The test is mechanical: trace the claim backwards through the references until you reach a work that reports the evidence rather than repeating the assertion. If you cannot reach one, the claim is not supported but inherited.** ② **The phantom entry**: a reference that is well-formed, plausible and nonexistent — now produced at industrial scale by machine-generated text, and occasionally caught in retracted papers (**standard account**). **Detection is cheap and humiliating: it consists of looking the thing up.** **This chapter's position, which will recur in chapter eight: because a reference list is the part of a paper a reader can check without agreeing with you, it is the part where a mistake is never forgiven as a mistake.** And the asymmetry is fair — **an error in your argument is a difference of view; an error in your citations is evidence that the argument was not built the way you said it was.**
:::

::: example Find five faults {#ex-wuchu}
Here is a reference entry and its in-text use (**composed for this lesson**): text — "Opinions differ on the dating (Li 2011; Wang 2008; Zhao 2015)"; list — "**Li, H. 2011. On the Dating. Journal of Text Studies.**" Identify five faults, of which at most two are formatting.

::: solution
**Formatting: ① no page range; ② no volume or issue.** Real faults: **③ the entry cannot be located from what is given** — with no pages you have not said which article in a monthly journal, and only you know; **④ the three citations are bundled to support "opinions differ" with no indication of who differs from whom, so the sentence is a bibliography pretending to be a finding** — **a reader cannot use it and a referee cannot check whether the three even disagree**; **⑤ the citation spans are unanchored: which claim of Li's is being invoked?** And a sixth if you look: **whether the author actually read all three is unknown, and bundling is the format that conceals not reading** — the same defect as the single-source illusion, but produced by laziness rather than by inheritance. **Marking: whoever finds ④ and ⑤ without mentioning the style guide takes an A**, because **the ledger's function is locatability and accountability, and formatting serves those two only.** Fixing ③–⑤ costs fifteen minutes and cannot be done by your editor.
:::
:::

::: intuition The list is a self-portrait {#int-zihua}
**Turn the apparatus into a diagnostic.** Take the bibliography of one of your field's influential recent papers and count: **the date distribution** — **a list ending in 2014 tells you the author stopped reading or considers the question closed**; **the language distribution** — **a list of one language tells you which language the argument was written for, and how much of the field it cannot see** (chapter three's language bias); **the type distribution** — **only monographs suggests a field slow to publish or an author avoiding live disputes; only recent articles suggests no historical sense**; **the direction of your own citations** — **who do you cite when you need an authority, and who never appears even when they disagree with you?** **This is why a reference list is both a self-portrait and a medical chart.** And it is why similarity-detection software, which measures your prose, measures the wrong thing: **the load-bearing part of a paper is not its wording but its dependencies, and nobody has built a plagiarism detector for a citation chain** — the one that would flag "this author is citing a work whose claim she repeats and which cites nobody". **Run the counts on your own list before you submit; the three distributions will tell you more about your draft than your supervisor's marginal comments will, because they cannot be argued with.**
:::

::: history Two hundred years of one footnote {#sec-zhushi}
**Nineteenth-century Germany**: the research article acquires the footnote, and with it the claim that a statement rests on identifiable evidence (**standard account**). **Same century, in Chinese scholarship**: the evidential school's kaozheng apparatus — sources named, editions named, variant readings listed — arrives at a comparable standard by a separate road, and Qian Daxing-style argument is often won by a citation (**standard account**; the history-of-Chinese course shows the method in practice). **Late nineteenth century**: Harvard-style author-date emerges with the mass production of journals, where editing by machine-sortable keys becomes possible (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: style manuals industrialise the apparatus, **and the apparatus becomes the place where graduate students are taught that scholarship has costs** (chapter eight opens with this). **Late twentieth century**: persistent identifiers, DOIs, digital repositories and the citation index — **the first time a claim's whole lineage became mechanically traceable, which is also the first time it became mechanically falsifiable.** **Twenty-first century**: machine-generated references. **Read the last two together: the instrument that made lineage checkable also made its absence conspicuous, and the detector's failure to keep up is not a technical lag but an economic one — checking other people's sources has never paid.** That is why this chapter's six commandments are obligations rather than best practices: **the system assumes you are the only person who will ever check them.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Citing a book without a page** (**only you know which page**); **bundling three references behind "opinions differ"** (**a bibliography pretending to be a finding**); **trusting a claim because several recent works make it** (**trace it back to the work that reports the evidence**); **relying on similarity software to protect you** (**it measures words, not dependencies**).
:::

::: exercise Audit ten entries {level=1}
Take ten entries from your current draft and apply the locatability test to each; delete or complete them.

::: solution
Marking: **each entry must be one you opened, with a page and the edition you used**; "I will check later" counts as not opened (the six commandments).
:::
:::

::: exercise Trace one claim {level=2}
Find a claim in your field that everyone repeats; trace it backwards through the references to the work that reports its evidence, or report that no such work exists.

::: solution
Marking: **the report must show the chain with dates and pages**, and a conclusion of "no origin found" is a publishable result if the chain is documented (the single-source illusion).
:::
:::

::: exercise Essay: why citations are unforgiven {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a citation is the only sentence in your paper that another scholar can verify without leaving her desk and without agreeing with you. That is why an error there is read as character while an error in your reasoning is read as a difference of view — and it is also why the apparatus, more than the argument, is the place where a discipline's trust is actually maintained. The new machines have not changed this; they have only made the checking of sources the cheapest form of criticism available, which is why careers now end in footnotes.** Deploy at least six of: **the two apparatuses and their one job**, **the six commandments**, **second-hand citation**, **edition-anchored page numbers**, **the single-source illusion**, **the phantom entry**, **the bibliography as medical chart**, **what similarity software cannot see**. Then adjudicate: **should a paper be required to state, for each citation, whether the author read the source or took it at second hand — and what would that rule do to scholarship?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**state the effect, not the virtue**). **A passing shape: the rule is already satisfied where it matters** — **scholars mark "cited in" when they are being careful — so the mandate would change nothing except to embarrass the careless, who do not read the requirement.** **Stronger answers find the real effect: it would make second-hand citation visible as a quantity, and the first thing a field does with a visible quantity is rank by it**, which would penalise fields whose whole material is transmitted in secondary works (**an editor of a documentary edition cannot read every source she prints**). **Highest marks for the institutional deduction: what the rule would actually fix is the bundle**, **since "read or quoted at second hand" must be declared per claim, not per source; and the bundle is where unverified claims enter a paper** (faults ④ and ⑤ of the worked example) — **which is also why chapter six's demand for stated warrants and this chapter's ledger are the same instrument seen from two ends.**
:::
