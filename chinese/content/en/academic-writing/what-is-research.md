---
title: "What research is"
---

This course does not teach you to write well; it teaches you to write what can be checked. **The first chapter therefore asks the one question that decides whether you are doing research at all — and the question is not "what is your topic" but "who disagrees, and what would make you stop"**.

## The smallest unit {#def-zuixiao}

::: definition A claim a stranger can check {#def-shuofa}
**A definition worth forcing through your head**: the smallest unit of scholarship is not a paragraph, a citation or a data set but **a claim that a stranger can verify** — and it has three parts. **① The assertion**: what you say is the case. **② The material**: what you looked at, in a form someone else could find. **③ The path**: how you got from the material to the assertion, in a form someone else could re-run. **A text containing only ① is opinion; ① plus ② without ③ is a catalogue; ① plus ③ without ② is a theory paper in the worst sense.** Three consequences worth stating plainly. **① It demotes "I read a lot"**: reading produces no scholarly unit until a three-part claim stands up. **② It promotes the footnote**: a sentence that names a page is a complete unit, and some careers consist of nothing else, honourably. **③ It explains why your supervisor asks "so what?"**: the question is not about importance but about ① — **you have presented material without an assertion, and I cannot tell what you want me to believe.** A practical test: **take any paragraph you wrote, cover it, and try to restate its point in one sentence that someone could deny.** If nobody could deny it, the paragraph has no claim in it.
:::

::: definition The three-sentence test {#def-sanju}
**The exercise this course uses all term.** Write three sentences: **① I argue that…** **② Someone who disagrees would point to…** **③ What settles it between us is…** **① is the assertion. ② is the opponent — and it is the sentence most people cannot write, because they have been trained to think that disagreement is rudeness. ③ is the criterion; it decides whether you are doing research or making a statement.** Three remarks on grading it. **The opponent must be a real position, not a fool**: if your objector says something no scholar has ever said, you have written a straw man and ③ becomes unnecessary, since nobody needs to settle it. **③ must not be "more research is needed"**, which is the standard way of refusing to state a criterion. **If you cannot fill in ② or ③, the topic is not difficult — it is not yet a topic.** Put the three sentences in your first paragraph, not in your conclusion: **a paper that hides its claim until the end is usually a paper that does not have one.**
:::

## Three fake questions {#def-weiwenti}

::: definition The compilation type {#def-huibian}
**Symptom**: your question contains no verb that anybody could resist — "A study of X", "An introduction to Y", "A comparative look at Z". **Structure**: the compilation-type question has **no opponent**: nothing in it can be answered wrongly, only incompletely. **How it arises**: you picked the topic by scanning titles and finding what no one has written, and you mistook an absence of literature for a presence of a problem. Two fixes. **① Name a thesis your own reading could have denied**: "X was influenced by Y" is a compilation; "readers of X attributed its imagery to Y, and the attribution first appears in a 1935 preface, not in the text itself" has an opponent who can say the preface is unreliable. **② Convert the absence into a claim** — "there is no work on X" is either a universal claim you must check exhaustively (chapter three) or a statement about databases, which is not a research question. **Note the institutional trap: the compilation question is safe**, and it is safe because it cannot fail; a genre of topic-choice that cannot fail is a genre for people being examined rather than for people finding out.
:::

::: definition The settled and the enormous {#def-yiding}
**The settled type**: the conclusion is fixed before you begin — "the importance of protecting local dialects", "the decline of reading". **Structure**: the conclusion has no opponent **because the audience is already in favour**, so ③ is never asked. **The tell-tale is that your argument consists of examples**: examples cannot accumulate into a conclusion that was assumed; the paper's real function is to make the reader feel correctly, which is the job of the essay course's other genre, the polemic — legitimate, but not research. Mark it as such and write it under that title. **The enormous type**: "what is literature", "how did language arise", "the relation between economics and art". **Structure**: no criterion can be stated for a question of that size, and this is the one case where "too big" is not a polite criticism — **the sole advantage of an enormous question is that it need never be refuted.** The fix is not to shrink the words but to add the two missing parts: **which material, in what period, and against which specific claim**. "What is literature" fails; "the word 文学 in 1902 editorials names a narrower set of texts than it does by 1925, and the change tracks a new school syllabus" has ② and ③ — **and the small version is not a fragment of the big question but a different kind of object: one that can be wrong.**
:::

::: definition Research, report, review, making {#def-sibie}
Four activities that share a desk. **Report**: what you found, addressed to somebody who needs to act; no opponent required, but the material must be complete and current. **Review**: what others have claimed, organised by question (chapter five); it becomes research the moment it rules between two claims. **Making** (a translation, an edition, an edition-with-translation, a corpus, a dataset, a work of creative writing): produces a public object rather than a claim; **but it can carry claims, and when it does they must be stated** — an edition silently asserts things about authorship all the time. **Research: something with ③.** The four are not a hierarchy; the confusion is what matters: **the commonest reason a chapter is returned is that it is a report submitted as research, which is to say it has no sentence that anyone could deny.** Ask a blunt diagnostic question of each section you write: **if I had found the opposite, would this section look different?** If not, it is a report — which is fine, provided you call it that and put it in the right place.
:::

::: example Five titles {#ex-wuge}
Say which of these are research questions, and for each failure name the type: **A** "A study of women in late-Qing fiction." **B** "Late-Qing fiction printed in Shanghai usually gave its heroines a death, and the practice follows judicial reporting more closely than it follows earlier fiction." **C** "Why did the May Fourth writers succeed?" **D** "The term X appears 4 times in edition 1 and 41 times in edition 3." **E** "An introduction to the rhetoric of online headlines."

::: solution
**A**: compilation — no assertion; "a study of" cannot be answered wrongly. **B**: research — it has an opponent (who can produce counter-examples or a different source of the convention) and a criterion (a coded sample of deaths and their causes of death against contemporaneous judicial narrative). **C**: settled plus enormous — "succeed" assumes the verdict, and "why" assumes one cause; **rewrite by splitting into who succeeded at what, measured how**. **D**: this is material with the assertion missing; it becomes research only with a "therefore" that someone could deny, e.g. "the increase tracks a publisher's change of series, not the author's politics" (**note the trap: the numbers feel like rigour and can carry a paper past a careless reader, which is precisely why the assertion must be demanded**). **E**: compilation, and worse, an advertisement for a review. **Marking: whoever notices that D is the most dangerous of the five takes an A** — it is the shape that most often passes examination, because effort is visible and the claim is absent. **The general form of the diagnosis: count the sentences in your draft that no one could deny; if the ratio is high, you have written a good report, and reports are paid work, not degrees.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Why "small" is a virtue {#int-xiao}
**A feeling**: students experience narrow questions as a demotion, as if one should be ambitious. **The opposite mechanics**. **① Small means the material is finite**, so you can plausibly claim to have looked at everything, and ③ becomes possible. **② Small means the opponent is specific**, so ② is writable. ③ **Small means an error is locatable** — **a thesis about one decade and one genre can be wrong in an identifiable place, while a thesis about "Chinese tradition" can only be disliked.** That is the whole difference: **falsifiability is not a stylistic preference but a consequence of size** — which is why this course calls "fallible" the criterion for a topic (chapter two). Two honest cautions. **A small question does not become important by being answered**, and most are not; what it buys you is **a known answer**, and the second-best thing about small questions is that nobody can pretend otherwise. **And "small" is not "trivial"**: the four sentences in example D would be trivial, while the same four sentences with a deniable "therefore" are the beginning of scholarship.
:::

::: history A vocabulary invented for being responsible {#sec-fuze}
**Seventeenth century**: the learned journal begins, and with it the practice of dating an opinion and naming where it came from (**standard account**). **Nineteenth century**: the research seminar and the doctoral dissertation spread from German universities; **the degree comes to certify not knowledge but the production of a checkable claim** (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: peer review, citation indexing and the ethics committee are built one after another (**standard account**). **Read the three inventions as one idea: an apparatus for saying who is answerable for what.** So when you are told to "state your contribution", the demand is not a ritual of self-praise; it is a **liability clause** inherited from a culture that had to replace the authority of a name with the authority of a procedure. **And this is why the vocabulary feels moral — "integrity", "responsibility", "acknowledge" are legal words in academic dress**: the sentence "I claim this" is a signature, and chapter eight treats it as one. **The practical consequence: you should experience a citation as a debt recorded rather than as a courtesy paid, and this chapter's three-part unit as the smallest assignable liability.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**"There is no literature on X" as a question** (**either an exhaustive universal claim or a database fact**, the compilation type); **examples in place of an argument** (**the settled type is a polemic, and should be titled as one**); **numbers without a deniable therefore** (**example D**; and this is the one that passes); **hiding the claim in the conclusion** (**put the three sentences first** — **a paper that conceals its assertion does not have one**).
:::

::: exercise Run the three-sentence test {level=1}
Write the three sentences for the topic you are working on now, and mark the one you cannot write.

::: solution
Marking: **the missing sentence must be named, not repaired by rewording**; if ② is absent, the exercise prescribes finding the strongest defence of the opposite before writing again (chapter two).
:::

::: exercise Catch one smuggling {level=2}
Find one paragraph in a published paper in your field where material is presented as though it were an argument; quote it and write the assertion that should have been there.

::: solution
Marking: **the written assertion must be deniable**; "the author shows that X is complex" fails the test.
:::

::: exercise Essay: the cost of being checkable {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a discipline's characteristic vocabulary is usually inherited from the institution that needed to assign blame. "Claim", "evidence", "support", "acknowledge", "cite" and "integrity" belong less to epistemology than to a legal habit of mind. This explains both the difficulty of scholarly prose and its peculiar morality: it is written by people who must be answerable sentence by sentence, and it is read by people whose job is to find the sentence that will not bear weight.** Deploy at least six of: **the three-part unit**, **the three-sentence test and its two failure modes**, **the compilation type**, **the settled and the enormous**, **the report diagnostic**, **falsifiability as a consequence of size**, **the liability reading of the vocabulary**. Then adjudicate: **does being checkable cost you anything you want to keep — and which one loss is the real one?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**name one loss and accept the cost explicitly**). **A passing shape admits the loss of rhetorical freedom**: a checkable sentence cannot be beautiful by being sweeping, and the temptation to sweep never goes away. **A strong answer adds the compensating gain: verifiability is the only property that lets a claim outlive your reputation** — a sweeping claim dies with you, a checkable one can be corrected by strangers and therefore cited for a century. **Highest marks for the institutional deduction**: if the vocabulary is a liability clause, then **every device that hides a claim (the passive without an agent, "it is often said", the review that rules on nothing) is an evasion of responsibility, and each has a checkable substitute** — which is the standing rule of chapters six, seven and nine, and this course's answer to "write nicely".
:::
:::

::: summary
The chapter delivers: the unit (**assertion, material, path**), the test (**argument, opponent, criterion; the two you cannot write are the diagnosis**), three fakes (**no opponent, verdict assumed, size that cannot fail**), four activities (**label them correctly and all four are honest work**), and one rule of thumb: **a small question can be wrong, which is the only kind of question that can be right.** Next chapter: choosing the topic you can actually finish.
:::
