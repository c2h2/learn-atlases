title: "Finding literature"
---

Everyone believes they are good at this. **The chapter's first claim is unpleasant: the only score you get from a search is reproducibility — whether somebody else, using your record, finds the same set you found**.

## Five parameters of one search {#def-wucan}

::: definition The search log {#def-rizhi}
**The compulsory format.** Every search gets five entries: **① the database** (which one, which run, which library catalogue); **② the query** (including every synonym and truncation you used); **③ the limits** (years, languages, document types); **④ the hit count** (how many records the query returned that day); **⑤ the date and the operator.** Why the fifth is not bureaucracy: **databases add and remove journals monthly, so the same query in June and in November returns different sets** — and a claim of comprehensiveness that names no date cannot even be checked for internal consistency. **Why the fourth: a hit count of 3 tells the reader that your query was wrong; a count of 4,000 tells them you stopped reading after the first screen. Both are results, and both are worth reporting.** The log belongs in your appendix or your methods section. **A reviewer who cannot reproduce your set cannot say whether your conclusion depends on what you missed** — and that is the only criticism of a literature review that cannot be waved away. **The excluded-list discipline follows from the log: keep what you threw out and why, because the exclusion list is more informative than the inclusion list** — **the difference between "I found nothing" and "I did not look" is invisible in the final text and visible only in the log** (which is also why chapter five says to publish the exclusions).
:::

::: definition Building the query {#def-jiansuoshi}
**Three sets, multiplied together. Set A: your object** — the thing you are claiming about, in every form other people used for it, including the obsolete term and the one your department stopped using in 1995. **Set B: the mechanism or property** you assert. **Set C: delimiters** — period, region, genre, population. **Query = (A1 OR A2 OR A3) AND (B1 OR B2) AND C.** Four practical rules. **① Search the synonym set first and the intersection later**, so that a zero result tells you which set failed. **② Include the term you disagree with**, since opponents use their own vocabulary and your adversary's words are the ones you must find. **③ Search both directions in citation indexes** (backward in its references, forward in its citations). **④ Stop by rule, not by fatigue** — **this course's rule: when thirty consecutive items add nothing you must cite, you have reached the boundary of the live debate** (**standard account**: systematic-review methodology uses comparable saturation rules). **Recall before precision, with a difference from the engineers: a missed paper in your field cannot be patched by re-ranking.** In a search engine, missing a document costs nothing; in a literature review, missing your strongest opponent costs the argument.
:::

## Five kinds of material, five biases {#def-wulei}

::: definition Journals, books, theses, primary and secondary {#def-piancha}
Each type of material fails in its own direction. **① Journal articles**: fast, reviewed — and subject to **publication bias**, since null and unsuccessful results are less likely to appear (**standard account**: quantified repeatedly in evidence-based medicine, with humanities examples emerging). **② Monographs**: systematic, slow, uneven in chapter status; **and they cannot be cited in pieces**, so you may cite three hundred pages for one sentence. **③ Theses and conference papers**: early, often not final, sometimes inaccessible; **check whether a published version supersedes before citing**. **④ Primary material** (archives, records, interviews, corpora): irreplaceable and governed by **archival luck** — what survived, what was catalogued, what was never considered worth keeping, which is a record of somebody's filing habits rather than of the past. **⑤ Secondary syntheses**: indispensable and subject to **the citation loop** — a claim circulates because it was repeated, and each repetition adds a footnote while none adds evidence. **The rule that follows: never let a claim enter your argument on the strength of a secondary source alone; go to the primary or mark the borrowing explicitly** (chapter seven on citing a source you have not seen).
:::

::: definition Language and database boundaries {#def-yuyan}
**An overlooked fact**: different databases cover different journals, the overlap between Chinese-language and foreign-language indexes is much smaller than intuition suggests, and **an English-dominant citation index systematically renders non-English work invisible** (**standard account**: systematic-review methodology calls this language bias and requires at least one non-English supplementary search). **Two equivalent operations for humanities work: ① run the same concept once in each writing system you can read, and ② read one foreign-language review of your topic and one Chinese-language review of the same topic, then compare their bibliographies — where the two lists fail to overlap is where the un-read literature is.** **Second-order effect: the corpus of what is cited in English becomes the field's agenda**, so a topic studied diligently in three other languages can be "under-researched" in the sense that matters for your proposal's significance paragraph. **Handle it explicitly: say which languages you searched and which you did not, and let the reader discount your universal claims accordingly.** That sentence is both honest and self-protecting.
:::

::: definition Snowballing and hinge literature {#def-xueqiu}
**Two directions of tracing**: backwards through a paper's references, forwards through its citations (**standard account**: citation indexes were invented precisely so that "who cited this" could be counted). Two uses and one trap. **Use ① find the hinges**: a field typically has a dozen repeatedly cited works, and missing one means repeating a step that was settled long ago — **this is what your supervisor means by "have you read so-and-so".** ② **Find the fault line**: two clusters that cite each other internally and a third paper cited by both are where the argument actually is. **Trap: citation counts measure visibility, not quality — they add approval and rebuttal to the same total.** **A refutation counts exactly like a tribute, and a famous error is therefore heavily cited.** **The repair is cheap: for your ten hinges, note whether each citation you have actually read is favourable or hostile, and report the split.** The split tells you whether a work is foundational or merely unavoidable, which is a distinction no metric makes for you.
:::

::: example Write the query {#ex-shi}
You want work on how judges in Chinese private-lending cases handle agreed interest rates above the protected ceiling. **Write the three sets A, B, C, the query, and the limits you will declare. Then name one thing the search cannot find.**

::: solution
**A (object)**: 民间借贷 ｜ 民间融资 ｜ 民间借款 ｜ 高利贷; **B (mechanism)**: 利率 ｜ 利息 ｜ 资金占用费 ｜ 综合费率; **C (delimiters)**: 裁判 ｜ 认定 ｜ 支持 ｜ 不予支持, plus a date range and "courts in province X" if the claim is provincial. **Query** = (A) AND (B) AND (C), run separately as (A) AND (C) if the intersection returns almost nothing. **Limits to declare**: databases used, years, languages, document types, and the date. **One thing it cannot find**: **any case that never entered a public collection, and every judgment the court chose not to publish** — which matters enormously, because publication decisions are not random (**standard account**: which disputes become public text is an institutional choice, and chapter one of the applied-writing course makes the same point about any dataset). **Marking: whoever writes the second half takes an A**: the point of a search log is not that it shows what you read but that **it shows what your method could not possibly have seen.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Why to read the notes first {#int-zhushi}
**A reading order**: title and abstract → conclusion → **notes and bibliography** → figures or the distribution of quotations → then the body. **Three reasons the notes come third. ① The notes expose the opponent**: whom a paper refutes, thanks, and cites at second hand is all in the footnotes, and this is more honest than the abstract, **because the abstract is a paper's self-portrait while the notes are its working record.** ② **The bibliography exposes the material boundary**: you learn immediately whether you possess the same access, and whether the author read the primary sources or the standard three summaries. ③ **The date distribution exposes the field's memory**: a bibliography ending in 2015 either stopped reading or is reporting a settled view; **a bibliography with no non-English sources tells you which language the argument was written in, and a bibliography of only books tells you the author does not know about a certain debate that has moved into journals.** This order also protects you: **you can abandon a bad paper in four minutes**, and the four minutes are the highest-yield in your research career.
:::

::: history From bibliography to hallucinated citation {#sec-xishou}
**Early period**: a dynastic bibliography lists the books then visible, becoming the starting point for later judgements of existence (**standard account**: Chinese bibliography begins as controlling knowledge by list). **Eighteenth century**: the great collectanea's critical notices state for each work its transmission, merits and authenticity (**standard account**) — **arguably the earliest large-scale annotated bibliography, functionally a literature review** (**standard account**). **Nineteenth century**: the scholarly journal and the citation index professionalise both the record and the counting (**standard account**). **Late twentieth century**: full-text search makes recall cheap and precision expensive; **anybody can now find everything and nobody can tell whether they did.** **Twenty-first century**: machine-generated text produces citations that are well-formed, plausible and nonexistent, and some journals have had to retract over them (**standard account**). **The last item is the strongest argument this chapter can make: a reference list has never been a decoration; it is a chain of payments, and the machine is the first author in history who does not have to pay — which is why a citation you have not opened is a liability, not a shortcut** (the whole of chapter seven, and chapter eight on integrity).
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Not recording the date** (**the same query returns different sets in different months**); **writing only what you included** (**"I found nothing" and "I did not look" have the same result**, exclusions); **relying on secondary sources for a key claim** (**go to the primary or mark the borrowing**); **using citation counts as merit** (**it adds approval and rebuttal together**).
:::

::: exercise Keep a log {level=1}
Run one search for your topic and produce the five-parameter record plus an excluded list with reasons.

::: solution
Marking: **the exclusion list must be longer than the inclusion list**, and each exclusion needs a reason a stranger could disagree with (the search log).

::: exercise Check one hinge {level=2}
Take the most cited work in your area and classify the ten citations you have read as favourable or hostile; then rewrite your sentence about its influence.

::: solution
Marking: **the rewrite must contain the words "cited as" rather than "showed that"** (the snowball trap).

::: exercise Essay: the search as an instrument {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a literature search is not a stage of research but an instrument whose design determines what can be found, and whose biases therefore belong inside your conclusion, not in a methods appendix you wrote once and forgot. The proposal claims "no study has addressed X"; that sentence is a claim about databases, languages, publication practice and your stopping rule. The honest form is a weaker sentence, and the weaker sentence is also more defensible, because universals are the first thing a hostile reader attacks.** Deploy at least six of: **the five parameters**, **the exclusion list**, **query construction and the saturation rule**, **the five material biases**, **language bias**, **the citation loop**, **hinge literature and the approval-hostile split**, **reading the notes first**. Then adjudicate: **should a research assessment system require a search log — and what does it start measuring that it does not measure now?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**name the new object of measurement**). **Passing shape: require the log, and it measures searchability rather than quality — the same trap that turned teaching into publication counts** (the argument of chapter ten). **Stronger answers name the actual object: not the search but its auditability** — "whether the authors can say what their method could not have seen"; **that sentence cannot be satisfied by padding**, so it resists the usual metric decay. **Highest marks for the corollary: requiring a search log shifts credit from coverage to design**, **which is the same transfer this course has argued for in every chapter: nobody can be graded on what they read, only on what they can account for.**
:::
