title: "Morphology"

The previous chapter cut continuous sound into units; this chapter cuts continuous speech into units — **and here Chinese sets linguistics the hardest problem in this course: the unit 'word' seems to exist by nature in the Indo-European languages, whereas in Chinese you have to draw a line with your own hand, and you are answerable for where that line is placed**.

## Definition: the morpheme and its identification {#def-yusu}

::: definition The smallest unit {#def-zuixiao}
**Standard account** (**a morpheme is the smallest unit pairing form with meaning; different realisations of one and the same morpheme are called allomorphs (**for example, distributional restrictions of the kind in which 们 and 每…都 do not combine with every noun, and the kind of case in which English un- becomes im- before lip); method of identification (segmentation + substitution: keep cutting, provided the meaning stays recognisable, until one further cut would leave only phonetic parts (once 学习 has been cut into 学／习, 习 on its own almost ceases to carry the meaning of 学习 (this lesson's reminder: this step already contains a judgement — you are deciding 'what counts as the same meaning' (how alike must meanings be before they count as one and the same morpheme? (this is a boundary dispute between morphology and semantics; this chapter enters it in the ledger and gives no judgement (Chapter 6 will reopen the court on polysemy and on the distinction between common origin and identical sound.
:::

::: definition Root, affix and 'free' {#def-ziyou}
**Standard account** (a free morpheme can be said alone; a bound morpheme must attach — what is characteristic of Chinese: a great many morphemes lie between the two (民, 语, 者, 水 and 雨 can be said alone in some sentences, and in others they absolutely cannot (so 'free' in Chinese is a distributional tendency to be said alone, not a logical possibility of being said alone (this has already turned the problem of the 'word' from a question of nature into a question of frequency (Lesson 1, the study of evidence: here, for the first time, evidence from a corpus becomes indispensable.
:::

## Word-formation {#def-gouci}

::: definition Four devices and two preferences of Chinese {#def-sizhong}
An inventory of devices (compounding, derivation, formal change (inflection), conversion — the distribution in Chinese: compounding holds an absolute predominance; derivation has a set of 'quasi-affixes' (子, 儿, 头, 们, 化, 性, 者, 员, 手); inflection is almost absent; conversion is extremely abundant in earlier Chinese (衣, 王 and 雨 used as verbs) and contracts sharply in the language of today (standard account); the traditional six types of compound structure (coordinate (道路, 买卖), modifier–head (火车, 周刊), verb–object (司机, 注意), subject–predicate (地震, 心疼), affixal (桌子, 石头) — this lesson's stance: the six types are not a discovery of taxonomy; they are a teachability device of Chinese word-formation (they allow a language without inflection to give a clear account, even so, of 'internal structure' (Chapter 5, syntax: these six types are almost phrase structure copied inside the word (**this is precisely the greatest puzzle in Chinese grammar, and its greatest opportunity:** the inner skeleton of the word and the outer skeleton of the sentence are the same in form (**the clause 'A fractal language' below deals with it**.
:::

## The core difficulty of Chinese: the word boundary {#def-cijie}

::: definition Four criteria, fighting one another {#def-sitiao}
**① Whether it can be said alone** (whether, when one answers, it can stand as a sentence by itself; ② Whether it is a minimal free form (the smallest combination that can be used independently; ③ Whether expansion is free (whether other material can be inserted in the middle: 说话 can become 说过话, whereas 东西 (物件, 'a thing') cannot become 东和西 (this is the famous expansion method); ④ Phonetic clues (stress, pause, and the domain of tone sandhi in connected speech and of rhotacisation — the difficulty is in the demonstration (火车 and 烧饼: the expansion method says that 火车 is a word (one cannot say 火和车? In fact 火和车 is grammatical, but the meaning has changed), and that 烧饼 is a word; whereas 走路 is split apart in 走了一条路 (so is it a word or a phrase after all? (this lesson's standard answer: in this sentence it is a phrase, and in that sentence it is a word: one and the same form belongs, under different uses, to different levels of unit; this is not a failure, it is a fact (we are used to treating 'is it a word?' as a question with a single answer, yet the very shape of that question comes from Latin grammar — there, changes of word-form write the answer on the word itself (Lesson 1, 'when a category comes in, it must pay distributional proof': now you know what tax that sentence is collecting.
:::

::: definition A fractal language {#def-fenxing}
**Standard account** (the structure of compounds in Chinese and the structure of phrases are highly of the same pattern ('看见' is verb–complement / '看完' is verb–complement; '黑色' is modifier–head / '黑的' is modifier–head) — three consequences (① a hundred years of difficulty in Chinese grammar lies here (if word and phrase are of the same structure, then 'delimit the words first, and only then discuss the sentence' is arbitrary force; if one discusses structure first, then the 'word' need not exist at all (Chapter 5 will show how construction grammar handles this step: it would rather talk of 'position, substitution and constructions' than issue the word a certificate (② that learners learn Chinese quickly lies just along this line (once you have the structure of 火车, you can guess 电车, 汽车 and 马车: transparency is the first gift of the Chinese vocabulary (the price is that the boundaries of word-formation are blurred; the boundaries in the Indo-European languages are clear, and the price is that the form of every word has to be memorised (transparency and boundary are the two sides of one and the same transaction (③ computational linguistics has to decide for you (Chinese word segmentation is the first operation in Chinese information processing; different standards of segmentation yield different word-lists, and an error is often not a bug in the program but a disagreement among standards (standard account: public evaluation has long taken a unified annotation set as its benchmark, and the field has also long granted that the definition of 'word' is part of this standard (this is an extremely clean case of 'theory decides engineering': every Chinese character you see on your phone is correctly gathered into one and the same word because, twenty years ago, a group of people signed a document) — the strongest contemporary version of Lesson 1's 'a list is a pair of glasses'.
:::

## Morphology in time and in society {#def-shijian}

::: definition Grammaticalisation and the cycle of morphology {#def-zhouqi}
**Standard account** (content word → grammatical word → clitic → inflectional part: a path observed again and again (the sources of items of the class of 了, 过, 着, 去 and 在; the English class of be going to → gonna) — this lesson's central proposition (morphology is not the original state of a language, but the sediment of grammaticalisation (in other words: an analytic language will grow inflection again; it is only slow (so the sentence 'Chinese lacks morphological change' is a thoroughly synchronic misreading (it takes the present state of a language's ledger for a trait of its species (Lesson 1, synchrony and diachrony: this is the sharpest application in this course of that dichotomy.
:::

::: definition The social stratification of morphology {#def-shehui}
**Standard account** (**one and the same grammatical device stands in sharply different proportions across registers:** ～性, ～化, ～度, 可～, 被～ and 非～ are highly concentrated in modern written Chinese and in the registers of official documents and of science and technology, and are of low frequency in speech (**this lesson calls this run of affixes 'the quasi-inflection of modern Chinese' (**their density measures directly how formal a register is (this is why 'archaistic', 'officialese' and 'academic tone' can be told apart at a hearing — Lesson 16, register: morphology is not a question of grammar; first of all it is a declaration of identity); the lifespan of new-style affixes (endings of the class of 门, 吧, 客 and 奴 were once highly productive (standard account) — a highly productive quasi-affix is hot money on the market of the vocabulary: within three years they are everywhere; after ten years most of them survive only in old news (a prediction that can be checked: any new affix imitated in bulk by the media will see its productivity curve peak within two years (this is the first testable prediction this course has offered (Lesson 3, refutability: if you can obtain corpora year by year, this claim can be falsified on the spot.
:::

::: example You draw the line {#ex-huaxian}
On the eight forms 火车, 烧饼, 走路, 开灯, 看见, 研究研究, 东西 and 国家, draw a boundary between word and phrase. The rule must be written in **no more than two sentences**; then answer: **on which form is your rule most strained, and why**.
::: solution
**A reference rule** — **A. If other material is inserted in the middle and the meaning does not change, judge it a phrase; B. If nothing can be inserted and it can answer a question on its own, judge it a word;** the strained places can be seen one by one: **under A, 走路 is sometimes a word and sometimes a phrase (**your rule must allow it to change with the use, otherwise you are drawing up the ledger for the language in advance**); 看见 does not allow insertion (×看和见), but it can be split into a complement structure of the 看／得／见 type (**this shows that what 'can something be inserted?' measures is the tightness of the complement relation, not 'wordhood'**); 研究研究 is reduplication of one and the same word, and reduplication is a morphological device (**on this account it looks, if anything, more like 'a word-form carrying grammatical meaning'**); 东西 cannot be split, whereas of 国家 one can say 国与家 (**but that is a rhetorical use, not ordinary syntax (**so you must stipulate that 'expansion is tested only in ordinary declarative sentences'**); **the point on which this lesson gives marks is not the line itself, but the column 'most strained':** it exposes what your rule is actually measuring (meaning? frequency? a feel for the language? a written norm? (**yet what the great majority of people's rules actually measure is 'whether I have seen it written solid in a textbook' — that is an orthographic standard, mistaken for a grammatical standard (Lesson 1, levels:** this is the straying into the wrong workshop that users of Chinese are most apt to commit).
:::

::: intuition A word is an invoice {#int-fapiao}
**Why this course refuses to define 'word'** (**because any definition is an invoice: it writes down clearly what you are buying, and it writes down clearly what price you pay (**'can be said alone' is cheap, but it misses a great many bound elements; 'minimal free form' is neat, but it fails for Chinese; 'nothing can be inserted' is practical, but it breaks down for complements and for reduplication; 'phonetic degree' is objective, but it has to be measured (**the habit this lesson wants you to form is this:** when a dispute over units arises, first name the invoice, then choose an invoice (this is an entirely different question from 'does Chinese have words?' — Chinese of course has a 'word-sense'; a speaker will without hesitation take 东西 as one word; only, that word-sense holds four kinds of evidence at once, meaning, frequency, writing and custom, and linguistics requires that they be taken apart — this is the real gain of this chapter: from now on you can no longer say 'Chinese has no morphology', nor can you say 'Chinese has words but no grammar' — the fault in these two sentences is one and the same: using a conclusion from one level to settle the account of another level.
:::

::: history A hundred and twenty years of drawing the line {#sec-huaxian}
**1898** ( *Mashi wentong* 《马氏文通》 (*Mr Ma's Grammar*): first posit word classes, then take the name 字类 ('classes of characters') from the Latin framework (its procedure was to treat the Chinese 字 as the word — this step was, at the time, extremely advanced (it made a grammar of Chinese possible), and it is precisely the source of every dispute thereafter (Lesson 1, 'when a category comes in, it must pay proof': at the time it did not pay (at the time it did not need to pay, either)); 1940s–1950s (structuralism enters China: the 'expansion method' and the 'substitution method' are introduced, and the division of word from phrase becomes a technical point of contention; at the same time, disagreement over 'whether morphology constitutes an independent level' begins to affect textbooks (standard account: the three levels 'morpheme–word–phrase' established in this period remain, to this day, the default structure of the teaching of Chinese (it is an institutional achievement, not a fact that has been proved)); 1980s (those of the Zhu Dexi 朱德熙 sort rebuild Chinese grammar on distribution and function (standard account: the approach that takes the phrase as the backbone of syntax moves the centre of gravity from 'word' to 'structure', and the 'fractal' clause of this chapter is its wake (this is another instance of what this course calls 'changing the object of study' rather than 'revising right and wrong' (Lesson 1, the four turns)); 1990s–the present (Chinese information processing forces the definition to land: standards of segmentation, word-lists and annotation sets become national standards or industry conventions; at the same time typology changes 'analytic / synthetic' from a ranking into a continuum (standard account: this change makes the sentence 'Chinese lacks morphology' lose its theoretical basis entirely (closing words of this chapter: engineering standards are more stable today than theoretical definitions, because nobody needs them to be correct; they only need them to be consistent.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**'Chinese has no morphology'** (**treating the synchronic ledger as a trait of the species:** Chinese has word-formation, has quasi-affixes, has reduplication, and has grammaticalisation under way (the clause above on the cycle of grammaticalisation)); deciding wordhood by 'whether the dictionary contains this word' (a dictionary is history and the market bound in one volume; it records success, and it does not record possibility (Lesson 3, the survivor clause)); taking the six types of compound structure for universal grammar (they are a teachability device of Chinese: put Turkish or Inuktitut in their place, and the inventory of word-formation devices will be entirely different (the typology lesson will supply the comparison)); demanding that a word boundary have a single answer (fractal structure is a fact, not a puzzle: the puzzle is 'why we expect it to have a single answer' (Chapter 5, constructions and position, will offer an alternative.
:::

'**Chinese makes most of its vocabulary transparent: 看见, 火车, 地震, 司机 — everyone can see the structure, and so everyone can guess a new word. ::: solution

::: summary
The lines, filed: the morpheme (**segmentation + substitution; a boundary dispute is entered in the ledger first**), the criteria (**four of them fight one another: name the invoice before you choose an invoice**), the fractal (**the inner skeleton of the word = the outer skeleton of the sentence: this is a puzzle and it is also a gift**), the cycle (**morphology is the sediment of grammaticalisation, not an original state**), stratification (**a quasi-affix is the thermometer of a register**), engineering (**a definition must land, and once it has landed there is a signatory**). The method in one sentence: **do not ask whether it is a word; ask which invoice you are using, and what price you pay**. Next chapter: syntax — position, constituents and structure.
