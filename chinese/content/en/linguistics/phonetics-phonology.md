title: "Phonetics and phonology"
---

The two preceding chapters discuss the general nature of systems; this chapter discusses the only material a system can use — sound (and its variants in gesture and in image) — **the whole technical difficulty of this chapter lies in one place: how physically continuous sound is cut into a string of units that can stand in opposition; and once the manner of cutting is fixed, the speaker can almost never again hear any other cut**.

## Definition: two disciplines, one material {#def-liangge}

::: definition The division of labour between phonetics and phonology {#def-fengong}
**Phonetics** (**studies the physical and physiological properties of sound: articulation (tongue position, airflow, manner of articulation), acoustics (spectrum, duration, formants), hearing (perceptual boundaries) —** its object is continuous quantity (**between consonants and vowels, and between [t] and [tʰ], there is physically only a difference of degree**); **phonology** (**studies which differences are treated as differences in this language —** its object is discrete opposition (**one verdict of this lesson:** phonetics measures, phonology keeps the books; **an account not opened on the ledger, and the speaker cannot see it (**this is also why the phonetics lessons of this course must always be read together with the historical-phonology course (Course 3): Chinese historical phonology is the earliest, and also the most thorough, phonological ledger in human history (Course 3, Chapter 2).
:::

::: definition Phonetic symbols are a three-line specification {#def-ipa}
**The received account** (**the International Phonetic Alphabet describes a consonant with the three coordinates "place of articulation + manner of articulation + voicing" (for example [pʰ]: bilabial, stop, aspirated, voiceless; [ʂ]: retroflex, fricative, voiceless) —** this lesson insists on two points of discipline in it (**①** one sound, one symbol (spelling is a four-line writing grid; phonetic symbols are coordinates: one and the same symbol must, in any language, refer to one and the same articulatory gesture, or else comparison is meaningless (Chapter 2, "Value": comparison across systems must first factor the segmentation out); ② brackets are a claim (square brackets [ ] claim to have described the actual pronunciation; slashes / / claim that the difference distinguishes meaning in this language (thus "how this sound should be symbolised" is not a question of format, but a falsifiable hypothesis: it is equivalent to "I claim that this difference does not bear an opposition in this language" (this sentence is the methodological core of this chapter (Lesson 3, refutability: a phonetic symbol is the cheapest falsifiable statement in linguistics.
:::

## How to find the units of a language {#def-zhaodao}

::: definition Minimal pairs {#def-weixiao}
**The operation** (**keep everything else unchanged, change only one sound, and see whether the meaning changes (**if the change changes the meaning, then that difference distinguishes meaning in this language); a demonstration in Chinese (change the initial: 八 [pa] — 趴 [pʰa] — 发 [fa]; change the final: 班 [pan] — 帮 [paŋ]; change the tone: 妈—麻—马—骂 — when the three lines are done, you have worked out for yourself the inventory of oppositions among initials, finals and tones in Putonghua (standard Mandarin) (this is a "discovery", very rare in this course, that a student can complete on the spot (at the same time it exposes a trap: [p] and [pʰ] are in opposition in Chinese, and are not in opposition in English (the difference in aspiration of the p in "spin" and in "pin" is automatic, and does not bear meaning) (so when a native speaker of English, beginning Chinese, cannot hear 八／趴, it is not that his ear is poor; it is that this account is not on his ledger (the categorisation of perception is a product of the native language (Lesson 9, "Accent and identity").
:::

::: definition Phonemes and allophones {#def-bianyi}
**The received account** (**different realisations of one and the same phoneme in different positions are called allophones; two sounds in complementary distribution usually belong to the same phoneme (**the classic case in Chinese (Putonghua /a/ is realised as different vowels before different codas ([a], [ɑ], [ɛ], [æ] and the like, the received description) — what shocks the beginner is that these four sounds, to their ear, "were the same sound all along" (how this lesson uses it: this is the principle, in the whole course of linguistics, that is easiest to verify in person: your hearing has already taken the rules in the system for physical fact (Lesson 1, "The inventory is a pair of glasses": and this pair of glasses was fitted before you were six; it is not inborn.
:::

::: definition Economy: a system keeps no superfluous opposition {#def-jingji}
**The received account** (**differences in a phoneme system must bear a function (distinguishing meaning, or, by distinctive features, bearing the consistency of word-formation and of history**); otherwise they are consumed — this lesson writes it as a piece of economics (opposition has a maintenance cost (every pair of phonemes will, in some year and some place, disappear because of some merger: in the history of Chinese, the devoicing of voiced initials, the disappearance of the entering tone (checked tone), and the *jian–tuan* merger (尖团合流), are each one merging of accounts on the ledger (the whole main line of the phonetics course, Course 3, and of the history-of-sounds course, Course 4); why a merger always happens where effort is saved (assimilation, weakening and deletion all run in the direction of "articulation that takes less trouble" (this is the tendency in language change closest to a natural law (but it is offset by another force: communication must be clear (so no theory of sound change that speaks only of "economy of effort" holds: only the point of equilibrium between economy of effort and clarity is the synchronic state of a language (Chapter 6, concession and equilibrium: this chapter is where it first appears.
:::

## Suprasegmentals and the sound of Chinese {#def-chao}

::: definition Tone is not "tone" {#def-shengdiao}
**The received account** (**tone is a pattern of pitch over time, with the function of distinguishing meaning; tone values are described on a five-level scale (the received view: the five-level values of Zhao Yuanren 赵元任) —** this lesson handles the matter in two ways (**①** tone is relative pitch, not absolute pitch (so when a man and a woman say the same sentence, the tone-value curves are similar in shape and wholly different in frequency: this explains why, in a tone language, "going out of tune" does not cause misunderstanding: the musicality of the tune is not on the ledger (Lesson 12, "Sound and music": when Chinese is sung, the melody covers the lexical tone and the listener can still identify the character, relying on relative relations and on context (this point is often taken as romantic evidence for "the musicality of Chinese"; in fact it is one piece of finely made informational redundancy); ② tone sandhi in connected speech is the eve of grammaticalisation (the sensitivity, to word boundaries, of third-tone (dipping-tone) sandhi in connected speech in Putonghua, of the neutral tone, and of rhotacisation (*erhua*) (the received view) — when a rule of pronunciation begins to depend on the boundaries of words and of structure, it is a living specimen at the border of phonology and morphology (material for the first lesson of the morphology course in Chapter 4.
:::

::: definition The syllable: the hidden protagonist of Chinese {#def-yinjie}
**The received account** (**Chinese syllable structure is regular (initial + final + tone); open syllables predominate; codas have, in history, merged in great numbers —** the inferences of this lesson (**①** the count of syllables equals the count of characters (this makes 字 ('the character') the most natural psychological unit in Chinese, and makes rhyme dictionaries, parallelism (the parallel couplet), parallel prose (*pianwen*) and poetic metre all count by the syllable — the whole apparatus of metre in Lesson 13 (level and oblique tones, parallelism, the number of lines) is built on this one phonological fact (so if one does not understand phonology one cannot truly read metre, just as if one does not understand the vocal folds one cannot truly read a way of singing (the conclusion of Course 1 is made good here); ② a preference for open syllables shapes the vocabulary (why a great many loanwords must add a vowel or drop a consonant ("咖啡", "巧克力" and the like: it is not that the transliteration is inaccurate; it is that the ledger accepts only accounts of this kind (the phonological source-text of the "loanwords" clause in Lesson 6.
:::

::: example Why four letters have to cover six sounds {#ex-zimu}
The Pinyin scheme uses one letter to stand for several different allophones (for example, i in the three positions [i], [ɿ] and [ʅ]; the differences of e in different finals). Answer three questions: **① Does this count as a flaw of design? ② What sort of user benefits because of it, and who loses? ③ If you were to design it again, what would you sacrifice in exchange for what?**
::: solution
**①** — **Viewed from phonology, it is not a flaw but a principle:** letters correspond to phonemes, not to phonetic values; one phoneme, one letter, can keep the written form of words stable (**that the finals of 知 and 诗 are written the same way shows precisely that the system claims they belong to one and the same phoneme (**this is a theoretical claim written into the primary-school textbook (**and this is also its most controversial point (**② Gains and losses (gains: children (few letters, few rules, consistent word-forms), and input methods and retrieval (one and the same phoneme is no longer split by phonetic value); losses: people learning a foreign language or a dialect (their ears have been trained so that "Pinyin is the phonetic value", while [ʅ] does not exist at all in English or in French, and so a systematic accent arises (Lesson 9: an accent is an outside guest of the native language's phonological ledger); ③ Redesigning it (every scheme exacts a price: if one switched to a narrow transcription, the alphabet would swell, word-forms would shift as the sounds shifted, and the costs of printing and of teaching would rise; if one kept the present state, then some years later one would have to patch it for "the phonetic values have already changed" (the conclusion of this lesson: a spelling system is phonology, politics, education and commerce bound in one volume; it is never only phonetics (Course 9, the choice of a "standard pronunciation": how the dialect of one place is chosen as the template for a nationwide ledger.
:::
:::

::: intuition Differences you cannot hear {#int-ting}
**Categorical perception** (**continuous differences within one and the same category are compressed, and differences at the category boundary are magnified (**this is a phenomenon confirmed again and again in the perception of speech (the received account) — its sociological consequences are very great (① the native language becomes a headset one cannot take off (what is called "a foreign language is hard to learn" is, at the level of speech sound, first a reorganisation of perceptual categories, and not a training of the muscles of the mouth (② for claims of the sort "some language has many words for snow/rice", the correct version is here (it is not that the words are many, but that that language cuts finely in your hearing (the perceptual version of Chapter 2, "Segmentation is sovereignty"); ③ this also explains why a person's judgement of his own accent is never objective (he cannot hear the boundaries of his own categories (so the exercise in "phonetic-symbol dictation" that this chapter requires has, as its real function, not the memorising of symbols, but the borrowing, for a while, of a pair of ears that have not been entered on the ledger (this is the only time, in an introduction to linguistics, that the student is made to experience the four characters 理论负载 ('theory-ladenness').
:::

::: history Four page-turns of the ledger {#sec-zhangben}
**Sixth to nineteenth century** (**the tradition of rhyme dictionaries and rhyme tables:** a matrix (initial × division and *hu* × tone) is used to set every reading out in a table (**that is a diagram of phonological structure from a thousand years ago, more orderly than descriptions of the same kind in Europe (Course 3, Chapters 1–2**); **the nineteenth century** (**the comparative method established that "sound laws have no exceptions" (the received account) —** sound change was for the first time written as a law that could be generalised; **the price was that exceptions were pushed onto analogy and borrowing (**this line of thought still, down to today, marks off spheres of influence between grammar and phonology (Course 5); the early twentieth century (phonemics is established: the feature theory of Prague, the American phoneme and distributionalism, and the great survey of Chinese dialects (the received account: dialect survey made "how one and the same phoneme splits from place to place" into data one can see (this is the infrastructure of the dialect course); mid-twentieth century to the present (distinctive features, generative phonology, autosegment-and-metre theory (writing "syllable—rime—nucleus" as a hierarchical structure), experimental phonetics and speech synthesis (the received account: from description by rules, to the satisfaction of constraints, and then to statistical modelling (the closing reminder of this chapter: every change of technical generation changes "what counts as an explanation" — when a model can learn oppositions out of the data and no longer needs the name "phoneme", the standing of this chapter in the textbook will have to be rewritten (the four turns of Lesson 1: this chapter stands at the door of the next turn.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**"Cannot be symbolised = does not exist"** (**failing to hear a difference is not the same as its not existing physically:** first do an acoustic test, or at least a minimal-pair test (**the converse holds as well:** what you think you hear is often what your ledger permits (the categorical perception above)); taking Pinyin for phonetic symbols (it is a scheme of writing, in the service of learning to read, of typing and of popularisation; it allows one letter to several phonemes, and phonetic symbols do not (the worked example)); explaining every sound change by "economy of effort" (economy of effort cannot explain why the pressure of clarity did not stop the change halfway: one must at the same time give the three of them — analogy inside the system, lexical diffusion, and social prestige (Course 4 and Lesson 9)); taking the "singing" of a tone language as a counter-example (that the character can still be identified when melody covers lexical tone shows redundant encoding; it does not prove that Chinese is "musical by nature" (① above.
:::

::: exercise Do it once on the spot {level=1}
In 100 characters, record one minimal-pair experiment: **what you changed, whether the meaning changed, and whether your sample contains two sounds that "sound the same" but are in fact different**.
::: solution
Marking: **a case of failure must be reported** (**the place of the failure is precisely the boundary of your phonology:** success proves only what you already know (the falsifiability clause of Lesson 3).
:::

::: exercise Settling the accounts of a spelling scheme {level=2}
For a notation you know well (a formula, names in code, a musical score, the five-line staff, or a phonetic notation for some dialect), write 120 characters: **which differences it enters on the ledger, which it merges, and whom the merger benefits and whom it costs**.
::: solution
Marking: **benefit and loss must fall on concrete groups of people** (**children, learners of a foreign language, specialists, retrieval systems:** if it does not fall on groups of people, a design has been spoken of as a matter of taste (question ② of the worked example).

::: exercise Short essay: the ear is a ledger {level=3}
Write from six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing "**Phonetics tells linguistics something rarely found elsewhere: we think we are perceiving the world, but in fact we are reading our own table of classification. That linguists can, on the strength of this, build an objective science is precisely because this table of classification is public, operable, and demonstrable on the spot by minimal pairs — it is not invisible in the way values are; it can make two native speakers mutually unable to hear one and the same difference**". You must call on **the division of labour between the two disciplines**, **brackets are a claim**, **the algorithm of the minimal pair, and its trap**, **allophones and complementary distribution**, **the equilibrium of economy of effort and clarity**, **the relativity of tone, and redundant encoding**, at least six items; and you must give a ruling: **the proposition "language affects perception" is verifiable at the level of speech sound, and why it weakens so quickly at the lexical and grammatical levels — you must give a mechanistic explanation, and say which experimental design can best distinguish the two**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**division of labour, claim, opposition, variant, equilibrium, redundancy**); the ruling (**an acceptable version of the mechanistic explanation:** phonetic categories are the part of perception that comes earliest, that receives the greatest amount of training, and that must be used online and in real time (**every sentence is fully cut within a few tens of milliseconds**), so categorisation acts on it with extreme force; whereas the use of lexical and grammatical categories can rely on context, allows a pause, and allows the question to be asked again, and the perceptual system need not answer for them (**the experiment that best distinguishes the two: take one and the same batch of differences and run them separately as an "online classification task (categorical perception: a boundary effect in discrimination)" and as an "offline naming task", and compare the distribution of the differences among native speakers of different languages (**when the paper is marked, look at whether the sources of confounding are pointed out: the confounding of length of training, experience of the written language, and the form of the experimental task (if the conclusion can be written "so this chapter is not teaching you to recognise phonetic symbols; it is teaching you to admit that objectivity is not the absence of glasses, but a pair of glasses that two people can inspect at the same time and that, once written down, can be reproduced", that is an A.
:::
