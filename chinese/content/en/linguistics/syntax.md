The previous chapter said that Chinese words and phrases are isomorphic; this chapter builds its house on that trouble — **syntax studies the hierarchy of combination, not the order of combination; and one of its greatest findings is this: order can be seen, hierarchy cannot, and yet it is precisely by the invisible hierarchy that we explain why some sentences have two layers of meaning**.

## Definition: hierarchy, not a queue {#def-cengji}

::: definition Why constituents are needed {#def-chengfen}
**The received account** (**“我昨天读了两本厚书” ('I read two thick books yesterday') is not a linear string of ten words, but constituents nested layer upon layer (**evidence one: the units that can be moved and replaced are not single words, but whole chunks (**“读了两本厚书” can be replaced as a whole by “读了它” ('read it')**); **evidence two: ambiguity is often structural ambiguity rather than ambiguity of word meaning (**“咬死了猎人的狗” has two understandings that use exactly the same words and the same order — **if there were only a queue, these two layers of meaning could not be separated (**this is the entrance to the chapter: **structural ambiguity is direct observational evidence that hierarchy exists (**Lesson 3, minimal opposition: evidence that can be demonstrated on the spot.
:::

::: definition Two ways of drawing {#def-lianghua}
**Immediate-constituent analysis** (**the received account: the binary cuts of American structuralism, and diagrams of cuts by layer; the Chinese analysis into "head — adjunct — complement" is highly isomorphic with it (a complement, 补语, is here the result, direction or potential that follows a verb, not an English complement of the sort in *she is a teacher*) (**the received account); dependency grammar (the received account: each word has at most one governing word, and a relation is a binary link between word and word — both are legitimate for one and the same sentence (this lesson's handling: the dispute over constituents is not a preference about how to draw a diagram, but a claim about "at which layer the relation exists": the constituent approach holds that there exist chunks larger than the word; the dependency approach holds that a chunk is only a derived phenomenon (marking point: in any one syntactic analysis, first declare which ontology you are using, or a later charge of being "wrong" has lost its object (the syntactic version of Lesson 1's "on which layer one is speaking".
:::

## Six structures Chinese commonly uses {#def-liujian}

::: definition Word order and function words {#def-yuxu}
The received account (the basic word order of Chinese is subject—verb—object, modifiers stand before the head, and relative clauses, possession and quantity all come before the noun — from this comes a famous difficulty in learning: the long attributive (“我昨天在书店碰到的那位先生的弟弟” ('the younger brother of that gentleman I came across in the bookshop yesterday'): English puts the relative clause after; Chinese puts the whole of it in front, so the noun arrives very late (this is not "Chinese is backward"; the place where information is compressed is different (Chapter 4, transparency: Chinese does its modifying in the open, and hides the structure inside long stretches placed in front (and function words are the real case-markers of Chinese (“的、地、得、了、着、过、把、被、给、让、对、关于” — the previous chapter said that Chinese has no inflection; this chapter adds one sentence: **it outsources the work of inflection to these little words, at the cost that the meanings of the little words are hard to teach, and are extremely easily omitted by learners (**the great majority of "grammatical errors" in teaching the language to non-native learners in fact occur at this layer (**Lesson 10, "Language and teaching"**).
:::

::: definition The dispute between topic and subject {#def-zhuti}
**The received account** (“那本书我看完了” ('that book, I have finished reading it'), “米饭我吃过了” ('rice, I have eaten it'): a noun stands at the beginning of the sentence, and what follows is a complete subject–predicate; typology has long called Chinese a topic-prominent language — the two schools each have their say (A. the sentence-based school: what stands at the beginning is the subject, and a Chinese subject need not be an agent (the cost is that "subject" loses its comparability across languages; B. the topic school: what stands at the beginning is the topic, and the subject lies separately, at a deeper layer (the cost is that a separate apparatus has to be set up for a topic position (this lesson's handling (① first record a fact that can be checked (the two analyses make different predictions for one and the same sentence: for example coordination, rhetorical questions, and the position of “都” ('all') are sensitive to which constituent — these are small experiments that can be designed (② then grant a theoretical fact (the concept "subject" comes from languages rich in morphology and clear in the nominative; to analyse Chinese with it is like opening a toothless lock with a toothed key: it can be opened, but you cannot turn it in certain directions (Chapter 4, "a category, on coming in, must hand in proof of its distribution"); ③ finally remember this chapter's hook (the construction-grammar clause below will say: perhaps one should not look for a "subject" at all; one should look for a "pattern".
:::

::: definition Patterns and constructions {#def-geshi}
**The received account** (argument-structure construction grammar takes the "pattern" as a unit pairing form and meaning; ditransitive, causative, locative and other patterns have been confirmed over and over across languages — this lesson uses three Chinese patterns to make its value plain (① the ditransitive (“他送我一本书” ('he gave me a book'): “送” ('give, present') does not of itself necessarily contain a "direction of giving"; it is the pattern that supplies the licence to combine it with a beneficiary argument (② the word “让” (“我让他打了” can express three things, a causative command, permission, and the passive: the three meanings cannot be deduced from the one word “让”, because one word does not have three kernels; it is three different patterns sharing one and the same word (③ the type “被自杀” (the previous chapter said that "被 + verb" is a construction; what is added here: that pattern brings with it a semantic contribution of "non-volition", and so it can take in a verb that is semantically out of the ordinary and be understood at once — this is the strongest argument of the chapter: patterns have a semantics of their own (its cost: patterns will be listed in ever greater numbers, until someone holds that this should no longer be called grammar, but a list) — this lesson's mark: a list is not a failure of grammar; it is the grammar of frequency of use and of degree of conventionalisation (this is also the syntactic version of the previous chapter's "hot money in quasi-affixes": patterns too can run hot, and can die.
:::

::: definition Result complements and the resultative {#def-buyu}
**The received account** ("V-result / V-directional / V-potential" (打伤 'hit and injure', 看见 'see', 走得动 'able to walk') is one of the most distinctive combinations in Chinese — three things worth notice (① a verbal element expresses the result, which is to press "causation + change" into a single word (this is a special interface between the Chinese verb and argument structure (it lets Chinese narrate an extremely complex causal chain with an extremely short string of verbs: “他推开门跑出去摔倒了” ('he pushed the door open, ran out, and fell') — the causation of five clauses compressed into one line (② it is hard for second-language learners to master (why not say “我看了累”? (because a complement requires that the result be directly caused by the action; this is a semantic constraint, and not a formal rule); ③ it is also an extreme case of the Chinese "isomorphism of word and phrase" (Chapter 4: can one insert? “打得过伤吗”? — the complement sits on that line, between word and phrase, which cannot be clearly drawn (the "fractal" of the previous chapter is cashed in once more here.
:::

::: definition Classifiers {#def-liangci}
The received account (a numeral does not in general modify a noun directly, and a classifier (measure word) is required; individual classifiers, collective classifiers, classifiers of weights and measures, temporary classifiers; some nouns have an inherent classifier — three observations of this lesson (① a classifier is one classification of the noun (the received account: shape and function are encoded into the classification (the sort of 条, 张, 面, 峰) (this is precisely the everyday exercise of the previous chapter's "to classify is to hold sovereignty": a Chinese child learns “一头牛” ('one head of cattle') before learning the gender of “牛” (② classifiers supply one measurable semantic projection (the temporary classifiers “一脸泥、一鼻子灰、一肚子主意” ('a faceful of mud, a nose full of ash, a bellyful of ideas') treat the noun as a container — between rhetoric and grammar there is no boundary, only a line of frequency (Lesson 12, "once a metaphor has been put on the payroll, it has to turn up for work": a great many of the metaphors of Chinese have already been paid their wages); ③it is also a marker of dialect identity (the classifier systems of the southern dialects (*fangyan*; topolects) differ enormously from Putonghua (standard Mandarin) (**one of the entrances to the dialect lessons of Chapter 10 (**the classifier is the smallest dialect feature, the one least easily noticed by a learner, and the most stubborn (**to get a classifier wrong gives away one's home town earlier than to get the grammar wrong**.
:::

## Three frameworks, one problem {#def-sange}

::: definition Three case histories of one sentence {#def-bingli}
The sentence: “他一场球打伤了观众。” ('he injured the spectators over one ball game') — the constituent approach (draw the hierarchy: the subject “他”, the predicate “一场球打伤了观众”, in which “一场球” is a topic, or a duration–condition constituent: its trouble is that the semantic relation of “一场球” to “打伤” is not a canonical argument (it will be treated as an adjunct or as a special topic; the dependency approach (the links: 打伤—他 (subject), 打伤—观众 (object), 打伤—一场球 (an adverbial dependency): it reduces the dispute to "who governs whom", at the cost that the semantic relations are flattened (the construction approach (this is a "causation—result" pattern plus an "event-frame" pattern: “一场球” is the scene of the source of causation, not the patient; the semantics of the pattern allows "a scene to serve as subject" (**this lesson's mark (**all three can describe; but the "explanations" they give are different: **the constituent approach says "the structure is so", the dependency approach says "the government is so", the construction approach says "this pattern carries this meaning to begin with" (**to judge which is better, one must find a sentence that only one of them can explain — **that is the task of the worked example**.
:::

::: example One sentence, two meanings {#ex-liangyi}
“鸡不吃了。” Please: **① list the two meanings; ② state, for each meaning, the semantic role of “鸡”; ③ explain how each of the three frameworks accounts for this ambiguity; ④ and give a sentence that makes one of the interpretations impossible, stating what you have changed**.
::: solution
① — A: 鸡 (the agent) is not eating any more (it is refusing food / it has been fed full); B: (I / we) are not eating chicken any more; ② — in A, “鸡” is the agent; in B, “鸡” is the patient (one and the same surface form, two semantic roles); ③ three explanations (the constituent approach: the deep structures differ (B has a subject that has been omitted or moved; A does not), and so this is structural ambiguity; the dependency approach: the government relations are the same and the semantic labelling differs, so strictly speaking this is not syntactic ambiguity but ambiguity of argument labelling; the construction approach: one and the same form is accepted by two patterns (a topic–patient pattern and a subject–predicate pattern), and each pattern carries a meaning (marking: note that the way the three treat this sentence extends to a whole class, “饭做完了” ('the meal is finished'), “车修好了” ('the car is repaired') — if a framework has to set up a further rule for every such class, its explanatory power is low (**this is how Ockham's razor is used in syntax**); **④ the changes that remove the ambiguity** (**“鸡不吃食了” (A**) and “这盘鸡我不吃了” (B**)**) — **what is changed is not the grammar but accessibility: give the agent an action that only an animal can perform, or give the patient a quantity that only food can have (**the value of this exercise: **it lets you feel for yourself that the boundary "syntactic ambiguity / semantic ambiguity / pragmatic ambiguity" is in fact very often only the layer to which we decide to charge the responsibility (**Lesson 1, five workshops: **one and the same phenomenon, and three workshops can each issue a bill, while the fees they charge differ**.
:::
:::

::: intuition Grammar is not logic {#int-luoji}
The received account (logicians often imagine an artificial language with no ambiguity; the grammar of a natural language, by contrast, systematically keeps ambiguity and redundancy (three reasons in this lesson (① redundancy is the price of robustness (the Chinese “我打了他” ('I hit him') and “他被我打了” ('he was hit by me') carry the same information in different patterns; if grammar served only to transmit propositions, the second ought to have been eliminated (it has not been eliminated, because grammar also does the work of discourse and of stance (② ambiguity is cheap, and removing ambiguity is cheap too (a single “哪个鸡？” ('which chicken?') can settle the worked example of this chapter: so a language does not need to be precise; it only needs the cost of repair to be lower than the cost of design (**this is the key sentence in the economics of language (**Chapter 4, the economy of paying a call: **the cost of speaking clearly is paid by the speaker, and the cost of understanding is paid by the hearer; both sides will act inside their budgets**); **③** therefore "the logic of Chinese is not rigorous" is a thoroughly mistaken thing to say (**ambiguity and omission are universal properties of natural language, not the defect of some one language (**what can really be compared is this: **on which kind of information this language forces you to speak clearly (**English forces you to mark tense and number; Chinese forces you to mark classifiers and result complements (**this is the concluding sentence of the chapter: grammar is a compulsion, and not an ability.**).
:::

::: history Syntax moves house three times {#sec-banjia}
The traditional period (it comes from the logical syllogism and from the teaching of grammar: a sentence = a subject term + a predicate term (this framework is still alive in the two words "subject / predicate" (its advantage is that it can be taught; its disadvantage is to take it for something real); the structuralist period (strip meaning away, and leave only distribution and layers; immediate-constituent analysis let syntax, for the first time, be carried out in the manner of a machine (the received account: this is precisely the forebear of today's syntactic annotation and automatic layering) — Chapter 4, the engineering constraint on word segmentation: the second time that syntax was taken over by engineering); the generative period (after 1957: syntax is an internal device, and its evidence includes intuitive acceptability and systematic restrictions; phrase-structure rules + transformations, later evolving into the kind of thing the Minimalist Program is (the received account: its greatest contribution to Chinese was to make "empty pronouns, movement, and long-distance dependencies" into objects one can study (it also turned "does Chinese have a subject or not?" from a complaint into a question that can be tested); the functional and constructional period (frequency, use, conventionalisation: grammar is the sediment of interaction (**the received account: typology, and the study of grammaticalisation, turned "patterns have a meaning of their own" into a cross-linguistic question (**this chapter's position: this is the most productive house-move of the last forty years, because it invited discourse, pragmatics and diachrony back into the house of syntax (**the rent it pays is that predictive power grows weaker: a functional explanation often comes after the fact (**Lesson 1, four turns: **this chapter happens to be standing inside the fourth turn**.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of this chapter
**Drawing the circles of Chinese with the sentences of English** (**the sentence "Chinese is an SVO language" has a dispute buried in every bracket (**the topic clause above: **the better way to put it is to give the sample and the criteria first, and the abbreviation afterwards**); **taking ambiguity for a defect** (**ambiguity is evidence that hierarchy exists, and it is also the price of language being cheap (**② above)); calling "ungrammatical" "illogical" (“打败了他” ('defeated him') and “赢了他” ('beat him') are both right, and only a native speaker knows which sentence is natural: acceptability is not the same as conforming to logic (the limit of evidence ① in Lesson 1); one framework explains everything (if the three frameworks give the same conclusion for one and the same sentence, then they do not differ, and so none of them can be falsified (worked example ③) — this lesson requires you to know at least one place where they fork: this is the concrete meaning of "change the object of study, rather than change right and wrong" (the four turns of Lesson 1.

::: exercise A short essay: the shape of compulsion {level=3}
Write from six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing "**In comparing the grammars of two languages, what carries the most information is not which of them is more rigorous, but the places where they do not allow you to be vague. Grammar is a compulsion carried out collectively by native speakers: it does not enlarge the range of what you can express; it lays down which form you must hand in first. Therefore so-called 'Chinese is flexible' is often only that Chinese sets no checkpoint on some of the cells, while on other cells it has set checkpoints that a foreigner cannot get through in a whole lifetime**". You must call on at least six items: **structural ambiguity as evidence**, **the ontological difference between the constituent approach and the dependency approach**, **the dispute between topic and subject**, **patterns have a semantics of their own**, **the causative constraint on result complements**, **redundancy and the cost of repair**; and give a verdict: **under what conditions the saying "Chinese is flexible" can be tested, and under what conditions it cannot — design one measurement, and point out its greatest threat**.
::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms (evidence, ontology, topic, pattern, complement, repair); the design of the measurement (a scheme that passes: take a set of equivalent communicative tasks (narrating a causative event, describing a picture, refusing a request), and measure, for two kinds of native speaker, the rate of answers left out and the number of self-repairs on the "items of information that must be accounted for" (the causer, the result, tense, number, classifiers, politeness markers); the greatest threat must be written concretely (the task itself has a preference of register (**a storytelling task forces a narrative structure, and forces "result complements" to appear, and so turns the design of the experiment into a salesman for Chinese**); marking looks at whether a control task is given (**the written and the spoken form of the same material, and parallel corpora of two languages from the same person**); if the conclusion can be written "**so the word 'flexible' can only be amended thus: the freedom of a language is that it has placed its compulsion somewhere else; only when that somewhere else has been found has one learned its grammar, and only then has one understood one's own accent**", that is an A.
:::
