title: "Languages of the world and of China"
---

The first nine chapters take Chinese as their object; this chapter pulls the lens back — **the languages within China do not belong to one family, nor do they share one script; and the hardest lesson of this chapter is not the classification table, but an admission at the level of definition: linguistics cannot draw, between "language" and "dialect" (*fangyan*; topolect), a line that is at once scientific and in keeping with people's intuition**.

## Definition: two classifications that do not inform each other {#def-liangtao}

::: definition Genealogy to genealogy, script to script {#def-lixian}
**Genealogy** (**Received account: the languages within China may be assigned to a number of families, each independent of the others — Sino-Tibetan, Altaic (whether this cluster holds has long been disputed in the discipline; this lesson takes only the "common name"), Miao-Yao, Zhuang-Dong (Kam-Tai), Austroasiatic, Indo-European, Austronesian and others —** Note that the column "Altaic" is itself a point of scholarly dispute (**Received account: the similarities may be explained as long contact rather than common origin (**This lesson's handling: **treat it as a convenient shelf, and in your mind fix to it a label reading "hypothesis" (the checklist of Lesson 1 is the spectacles:** the reliability of a classification table can differ from row to row (**② Script** (**Chinese characters, phonographic writing, syllabic writing, consonantal phonemic writing, and a number of scripts of indigenous origin (received account: scripts of the Shuishu (水书) kind, taken to contain a component of local origin, whose nature and age are still under study)**); **Why the two lines must be drawn apart** (**one speaks of kinship (it cannot be chosen**), one speaks of administration and education (it can be changed)**);** the sharpest contrast **(**Received account: Serbian, Croatian and Bosnian, with mutual intelligibility extremely high, are listed as three languages; several major groups of Chinese, with mutual intelligibility close to zero, have long been called "dialects" — **and so the answer to the question "language or dialect" is given by institutions, and not by the ear (Chapter 8, "the army and the navy"**).
:::

::: definition Typology and area: what the mainland shares {#def-leixing}
**Received account** (**the linguistic area of mainland Southeast Asia: a number of shared features among languages not of common origin, such as a tendency towards monosyllables, tone, classifiers (measure words), phase complements or serial-verb constructions, and relative clauses placed after the head; the languages of southern China stand at the core of this area —** this is the methodological climax of the chapter (**because it yields an elegant fact:** tone appears independently in families that have nothing to do with one another; often it is not a coincidence, but a contagious spread under long bilingual coexistence; **and so questions of the kind "why do Zhuang, Vietnamese and Chinese all have tone, while the written documents of Tibetan do not" now have two possible answers (common origin and contact**); **This lesson's criterion (** received account: to tell the two apart one must rely on systematic correspondence, not on similarity (**regular correspondence → common origin; similarity of type without strict correspondence → contact (** this is the spatial version of Chapter 9's "two-act structure": **variation can be lived into being by the neighbours; it does not have to be inherited from the ancestors**).
:::

## The status of the Chinese dialects {#def-fangyan}

::: definition How one script rewrites a taxonomy {#def-wenzi}
**Fact** (**Received account: among the major areas of Chinese (such as Mandarin (*guanhua*), Wu, Xiang, Gan, Hakka, Min, Yue and others; the precise division has always been disputed) the mutual intelligibility of speech can be lower than that between certain different branches —** and yet Chinese is still counted as one language (**three pillars** (**① one script shared for more than two thousand years (** the independence, across dialects, of the readings of Chinese characters, and the stability of their meanings, make "the same speech" an unnecessary premise; **② long-united administration and the imperial examinations (** the written language is the common language, and it need not be imitated by speech (the continuation to this day of Chapter 2's "separation of speech and writing") **; ③ the narrative of the modern nation-state (** received account: the construction, at the end of the nineteenth century, of the concept of *guóyǔ* 国语 ('national language'), and the standard-pronunciation policies of the twentieth (**This lesson's key judgement:** these three are not the cause of a "misnomer", but the source of the other half of the meaning of the word "language" ( within linguistics, too, one uses mutual intelligibility, and also identification with a norm, and also systematic correspondence ( so the next time someone says "they are independent languages" or "they are only dialects", what you should ask is not who is right, but which criterion he is using, and for which purpose (the jurisdiction table of Lesson 1: this sentence is worth more here than in any chapter).
:::

::: definition Several systems of writing {#def-xitong}
**Received account** (**Scripts used within China, historically and at present, include: Chinese characters, together with the two standards now in force, simplified and traditional; the Tibetan script; the scheme for a standardised Yi script; old Zhuang characters and square Zhuang characters; ancient Zhuang characters and the new Zhuang script (Latin letters); the Mongolian script (the traditional Uyghur-style Mongolian script, and the use of its Cyrillic variant in different regions); the Manchu script (borrowing the letters of the Mongolian script); Kyrgyz writing and Kazakh writing (of the Arabic alphabetic family); the Dai script; Sui writing; the Dongba script (pictographic–ideographic in nature); and Nüshu (女书, a system of phonetic symbols passed on among women in the Jiangyong area of Hunan) —** the three questions of this lesson (**①** why one language should have two standards in force **(** simplified and traditional ( the answer is political history, and not linguistics;  ② why newly created scripts commonly use the Latin alphabet (in the 1950s, help to a number of ethnic groups in creating or reforming scripts (received account) — because a direction for phonetic spelling was then being chosen for the whole country (Chapter 9, the policy clause: the choice of a scheme can decide a thousand years more than the merits and defects of the scheme); ③ why Nüshu matters (it is a rare practice of writing, organised by gender rather than by ethnic group or by a regime ( received account: its function, and the way it is handed on (between mother and daughter, and among sworn sisters), show that: script can be a community's private property, and need not be the infrastructure of the state ( this clause directly rebuts the default setting that "script = an appendage of a national standard").
:::

## Shift and endangerment {#def-weiji}
::: definition Not that no one speaks it, but that it is no longer taught {#def-jiaodai}
**Received account** (**The indicators in ordinary use for assessing the vitality of a language include intergenerational transmission, domains of use, use in new domains, and the attitudes of the community; what is called endangerment has, as its core signal, that children no longer take the language as a mother tongue —** three mechanisms (**①** opportunity structure **(** schooling, medical care, working away from home, military service, sitting examinations, platforms: **each of these is paying a subsidy to some other language (Chapter 8, "who pays the switching cost";** ② **the collapse of prestige** (**When parents of the group believe that "speaking this will put the child at a disadvantage", shift is at its most thorough:** a language does not die by being forbidden; it is let go by parents who love their children ( this is the saddest sentence of this chapter, and the one that matters most;  ③ narrowing of domains (A language first loses religion, the market and the screen, until only the family is left, and then not even the family ( received account: the narrowing of domains often precedes the fall in the number of users ( so statistics that look only at "how many people can still speak it" are always too late ( the case (Received account: the number of users of Manchu has fallen sharply since the Qing, and it is now one of the severely endangered languages — its shift was not accomplished by a single order, but pushed on, layer by layer, as bannermen moved into the cities, through the imperial examinations and official careers, and, after the Republic, through livelihood and the linguistic surroundings (the most complete specimen of the two-act structure in Chapter 9.
:::

::: definition Documentation, maintenance and bilingualism {#def-jilu}
**Two things often confused** (**①** documentation (documentation) **(** fieldwork, phonetic notation, grammatical description, corpus and dictionary, audio-visual archives ( limited cost, almost certain to succeed, of value to scholarship and to later generations;  ② maintenance (maintenance / revitalization) (changing the opportunity structure: immersion teaching before entry to school, the media and publishing, use in public business and in the courts, screens and input methods, teachers and their pay ( the cost is extremely high, failure is common, and the mother-tongue community must lead (the worst consequence of confusing the two ( using video in place of the classroom, and a "protection list" in place of an establishment of teachers:  this is the only strong judgement in this chapter ( because documentation can be done, while maintenance has to be paid for ( a middle path ( received account: bilingual or multilingual life is widely held to be an asset both to cognition and to employment; the dispute is in the carrying out:  which language is first, who compiles the teaching materials, whether examinations test it, and where the teachers come from ( this lesson stresses only the method:  any assessment of a bilingual scheme must state "who will learn less of what"; otherwise it is a budget that no one reconciles (Chapter 5, the three questions for a measure: who carries it out, who pays the cost, how side effects are monitored ).
:::

::: example A field note {#ex-tianye}
Suppose you are at work on a language with about eight thousand users, which children no longer learn as a mother tongue, which has no unified script, and which has four fluent speakers over seventy. Write: **① which five things you do in the first six months; ② what you cannot promise; ③ how you explain to the funder that your work is not "rescuing" this language**.
::: solution
**① The first six months** — **a. Settle, with the speakers and their families, consent and credit, payment, and the right to withdraw (** this is a question of method and also of ethics **); b. Build a basic corpus (** long narratives, dialogue, ritual, terms for parts of the body and for kin, orientation and counting **); c. Check the phonology and the tones (** minimal oppositions, that set of procedures in Chapter 3 which can be done on the spot **); d. A usable orthography or transcription, and have at least one native speaker write a letter in it (** it must not be readable only by the researcher **); e. A list of materials that may be made public and that may not (** received ethics: some content ought not to be made public **);** ② What cannot be promised **—** one cannot promise that this language will recover intergenerational transmission ( that is a decision of the community within the two-act structure of Chapter 9, not something a researcher can design ); one cannot promise a "standard way of writing" ( a community of eight thousand is diverse within itself to begin with ); one cannot promise anonymity ( a recording is enough to identify the speaker ); ③ How to explain — Write the products out clearly: a grammar, a dictionary, a corpus, a pack of teaching materials, and an archive that can be handed to the community ( the pack of teaching materials is a necessary condition of "if a school is willing to offer a class", not a sufficient one ); and at the same time state plainly: "The criterion of success for this project is that the knowledge is not lost, not that the language does not die; if the community decides to shift, the archive remains fully usable" ( Marking:  if this sentence cannot be written, one is not a fieldworker but a philanthropist ( this chapter's judgement:  what an honest linguist can give an endangered language is that its grammar can still be read a thousand years on —  keeping it alive today calls for other professions and another budget).
:::
:::

::: intuition How "our speech" becomes "a language" {#int-yizhong}
**Two faces of one concept** (**Received account: the modern trinity "nation–language–state" is a classifying apparatus imported in modern times; before that, what was usual within China was a distinction of level between "the speech we speak" and "the speech of the officials", while "the language of such-and-such a group" and "such-and-such a dialect" are the results of survey and of registration —** three inferences (**①** classification creates entities **(** one act of identification and registration places a population that already differed internally into one and the same "language" and one and the same "group", and institutions then build schools and textbooks around that name ( this is the weightiest example of Chapter 1's "classification is sovereignty" ( the "power" here is the power to be literate and to hold office ); ② a name fulfils itself ( once there is a norm, scattered local speeches begin to align with it ( the process by which the standard language swallows the dialects is often called "unification" (③ and the resistance is real as well ( there are people who contend for a television slot for a language of which only eight hundred speakers remain; this is not irrational:  language is, for many people, the only property that cannot be mortgaged ( this chapter decides neither of the two positions; it only requires:  when either side says "a language", it must state which ledger it is counting (mutual intelligibility, a norm, identity, or the state ).
:::

::: history How a map is drawn {#sec-ditu}
**Han to Tang** ( tribute records, local gazetteers, and works of the *Manshu* 《蛮书》 ('Book of the Man') kind record "the languages of the four quarters" ( received account: these records care about interpretation and about governance, not about kinship ( their value is being found again today: they are among the few materials that can prove that "a certain speech once existed in a certain place" ( the luck of the archive, Chapter 8 ); the end of the nineteenth century to the first half of the twentieth ( Western missionaries and scholars within China began, separately, to investigate: the survey of dialects and the phonetic notation of minority languages started at the same time ( Received account: the modern dialect surveys of Zhao Yuanren (Yuen Ren Chao) and others, and early phonetic research in the south-west ( for the first time Chinese linguistics had recordings and phonetic symbols ( technology once again rewrote the visibility of the object (Chapter 1, the format of distribution))); 1950s ( a nationwide survey of languages, ethnic identification, and the creation and reform of scripts ( three projects carried on in the same year, and citing one another ( this is the most important institutional fact of the chapter:  linguistics and administration met in the same fieldwork ); 1979–1990s ( summary works of the *Language Atlas* 《语言地图集》 kind, and trials of bilingual education (Received account: every new edition of the map has redrawn a number of lines ( the grounds for moving a line have often been a new survey and a new administrative need, and the two are not easy to separate ( this is why the third edition of a map is more accurate than the first, and also less certain ); the 2000s to the present ( projects for the protection of language resources, sound databases, the system of intangible cultural heritage, and urbanisation on a large scale (Received account: the capacity to record stands at its highest in history, and the speed of shift stands at its highest in history ( the conclusion of this chapter presses on this one sentence:  For the first time our generation can record completely what is disappearing, and for the first time we are making that disappearance so fast.).
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**"Fifty-six ethnic groups, five kinds, fifty-six languages"** ( ethnic groups and languages are neither one-to-one, nor convertible the one into the other:  one must speak from surveys and from figures for the population of users, and must attend to the year of a figure and to its scope (Lesson 3, a numerator with no denominator )); taking "dialect" as a slur or as a weapon ( in linguistics it is a technical term of relation; in politics it is a warhead:  the same word, two ledgers (the three pillars above)); putting sympathy in the place of method (the subject of endangered languages is the one on which an outsider most readily accepts a conclusion with no evidence:  romance does not raise the standard of argument; it only lowers it (example ③ is the compulsory constraint of this lesson )); taking contact for degeneration ( the spread of tone, loanwords and bilingualism are the normal state, not a pollution (Chapter 9, the contact clause:  a language has never been clean, nor has it grown worse on that account .
:::

::: exercise Short essay: who has the standing to say what a language is {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing "**The boundary of a language has never been drawn by linguists: what draws it is the school, the dictionary, the household register, the army and the television station. What linguistics can offer is three things — a hypothesis about kinship, a measurement of intelligibility, and a craft of documentation; what it cannot offer is the answer to 'how many languages this is', because half of that answer is data, and half is who people want to be. An honest text of language policy must therefore declare both its linguistic hypotheses and its political aims, and the most dangerous texts are precisely those that disguise a political aim as a linguistic conclusion**". You must call on: **the two lines of genealogy and of script**, **the spread of tone across the mainland Southeast Asian area**, **the three pillars by which Chinese characters rewrite the taxonomy**, **the political contrast of language and dialect**, **the three mechanisms of shift**, **the divide between documentation and maintenance**, at least these six; and you must decide: **whether linguists ought to refuse to take part in the work of "language identification" (because it necessarily serves administration) — you must take a position, and state the cost of refusing**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms ( the two lines, spread, the three pillars, the political contrast, shift, the divide ); the decision ( both positions can score, but the costs must be written in full —  refusing to take part: academic independence is kept, at the cost of handing the work of identification to people who do not care about evidence ( this is a real cost, because once a list has fixed a name it is extremely hard to change ); taking part: one can reduce the obvious errors and keep some investment in archives and in teaching, at the cost of supplying technique to an apparatus that may be used for assimilation ( the marking looks to whether the key line of defence is located in "revocability" and "revisability" : a responsible participation should leave the original materials, a record of internal difference, and a method made public, so that later people can overturn the conclusions of that year —  this constrains power better than "not taking part" ); if the conclusion can be written " so what this chapter really teaches linguistics is not classification, but a professional ethic: our tables will be taken up by others and used to draw lines; therefore every table must state which question it answers, and the half that it does not answer ", that is an A.

::: summary
Filed, with the lens pulled back: two lines ( genealogy cannot be chosen; script can be changed ), spread ( tone can live in homes that have nothing to do with one another ), Chinese characters ( they make "the same speech" an unnecessary premise ), the political contrast ( high mutual intelligibility need not be one language, and low need not be two ), shift ( let go by parents who love their children ), the divide ( documentation almost certainly succeeds; maintenance needs another budget ). Method, in one sentence:  when you hear someone say "this is a language", ask which weights he has just put on the scales . Ten chapters, read through to here: from sound to society, you already have a set of tools you can carry with you; putting them to use in writing, in reading and in argument is the business of the courses that remain.
:::
