title: "Language change"
---

The previous chapter said that a variety is a stratified social fact; this chapter says that variation is history in progress — **the least intuitive point in the study of language change is this: change does not begin from the collective, but from some one person's slip of the tongue, imitation or display of skill, and a group then decides whether to take it in; its regularity can therefore only be statistical, and it cannot be legislated**.

## Definition: two old propositions {#def-liangming}

::: definition Sound laws and lexical diffusion {#def-guilv}
**Received account** (Historical linguistics in the later nineteenth century proposed that "sound laws admit no exceptions": where the conditions are the same, the change is necessarily uniform; exceptions were assigned to analogy and to borrowing — this was the first time linguistics put forward a proposition in the form of a law — its achievement: it gave reconstruction and comparison an engine one can deduce with (its cost: it cannot explain why "under the same conditions, some words have changed and some have not" (Received account (the hypothesis of lexical diffusion holds that a sound change spreads through the lexicon one word at a time, and a language caught in the middle of the process presents precisely a stratification (received account: this hypothesis was put forward by scholars who study Chinese dialects, and their materials are its key evidence (This lesson's position: the two are not a matter of "right or wrong"; they differ in scale — what the account by laws states is the final state of the phonology; what the account by diffusion states is the process by which the change spreads (one speaks of the outcome, the other of the process; and most disputes of this kind in the history of linguistics have, in the end, been settled by "both are right, and each governs one layer" (Lesson 1, on levels: this chapter is the sharpest working of that method.
:::

::: definition Why change cannot be predicted {#def-buke}
**A two-act structure in the manner of evolutionary theory** (**①** variation is produced in quantity at the level of the individual (slips of the tongue, child speech, laziness, displays of skill, interference from a foreign language, performances of group identity — this layer is approximately random (② adoption has a direction at the level of the community (frequency, how easy a form is to say, the speaker's prestige, the structure of the network, and "whom it sounds like" (this layer is highly selective (Hence (the direction of a change can be explained after the fact, and is extremely hard to predict before the fact (because the second act depends on the structure of the social network (received account: an innovation often gains an underground prestige inside a close-knit group, and has to spread across groups by weak links (The name this lesson gives it: change is a kind of two-stage filtering, isomorphic with natural selection, and therefore it necessarily inherits the same fate — no purpose, no schedule of progress, and no end point (the clause on evolutionary theory in Chapter 11: this is the only scientific rebuttal of telling "the evolution of language" as "the progress of language".
:::

## An inventory of the mechanisms of change {#def-jizhi}

::: definition Sound change: economy of effort, the system, and borrowing {#def-yinbian}
**Common directions** (**assimilation, weakening, loss, dissimilation, metathesis, blending; and, in the opposite direction, strengthening (**received account); Four ledgers for Chinese (received account: devoicing of the fully voiced initials, the 尖团 merger, the level tone splitting according to voicing, and the entering tone (checked tone) disappearing in the northern Mandarin (*guanhua*) dialects and being distributed among the other three tones — each ledger shows the law and the exceptions at the same time (that the initials of 茶, 陈 and 除 change together is the law; that "certain common characters keep the old reading in a dialect" is diffusion caught midway (This lesson's further question: why economy of effort is always held back by the system (because if you rub it too clean, things collide — devoicing makes a batch of old oppositions disappear, and the language makes the distinctions back up by aspiration, by tone, and by disyllabification (the largest patching of a hole in the history of Chinese is disyllabification: monosyllabic words became disyllabic words in great numbers, and part of this was precisely so as to regain distinctiveness after phonemic oppositions had decreased (received account: this is one of the standard explanations in the history of Chinese (and so the most important mechanism of this chapter comes to the surface: change is not corrosion in a single direction, but a continual rebalancing between "distinctiveness" and "economy of effort" (the recurrence, on the diachronic layer, of Chapter 3's "the equilibrium of economy of effort and clarity".
:::

::: definition Reanalysis and analogy {#def-licheng}
**Reanalysis** (**the surface string stays the same; the underlying structure has changed: 公园 from "a garden held in common" to a word that cannot be taken apart; 怀疑 from two words to one verb (**its formal signature: the structural boundary moves while the pronunciation has not changed (the cleanest kind of change, because it can be demonstrated on the spot (Analogy (new forms are made on an existing pattern, or irregular forms are regularised (it explains "why exceptions disappear", and it also explains "why a rule comes to have new exceptions manufactured for it" (This lesson calls the two the two hands of change: reanalysis has altered the drawing, and analogy builds on according to the drawing (without the former, a language cannot change its structure; without the latter, a change, once made, does not get passed on.
:::

::: definition Grammaticalisation: how content words become little words {#def-yufahua}
**Received account** (content word → grammatical word → clitic → inflectional piece; accompanied by semantic erosion, phonetic weakening, an expansion of syntactic range, and subjectification — four Chinese samples (了 (a verb with the sense "finish" → a perfective marker), 把 (the sense "hold in the hand" → a marker of the disposal construction), 被 (a verb that carries a meaning → a passive marker), 们 (the sense "of the same kind" → a plural clitic) (received account: the detail of each item's source is still under discussion; this lesson takes only the direction "content → function" as the received account); Four accompanying symptoms (the meaning becomes empty, the sound becomes light, the range of what it can combine with grows wider, and the semantic centre of gravity shifts towards the speaker's attitude (the finest of them is subjectification (in 你看这事, the 你看 no longer requires that you actually look (this is the scene of something from the pragmatic layer settling into the grammar (the speech acts of Chapter 7 taking up residence in the morphology of Chapter 4.
:::

## Meaning, contact and the lexicon {#def-yuyi}

::: definition Four directions of change in word meaning {#def-sizhong}
**Widening, narrowing, amelioration, pejoration** (**received account**); **Three Chinese examples** (**江 and 河 changed from proper names to common nouns (widening); 金, 臭 and 谷 narrowed (金 originally referred to metal in general, 臭 originally to a smell, 谷 originally to the hundred grains); 爪牙 changed from warrior to accomplice (pejoration); 祥, from a sign of good or ill fortune, came to refer only to the auspicious (narrowing together with amelioration) (**received account); How this lesson uses it: the direction of a change of meaning is often written up as "a history of the development of word-meaning", while its real driving force is often on the social layer: taboo pushes a word downwards, politeness has a word replaced, and institutions make a word narrow (the euphemism cycle of Chapter 6: there is no semantic change that is purely semantic.
:::

::: definition Contact: loanwords, semantic borrowing and the substratum {#def-jiechu}
**Three kinds of influence** (**loanwords (the whole item carried over), semantic borrowing and calquing (native parts assembled on a foreign blueprint), substratum influence (the traces left after one language has been replaced by another) (**received account); Three layers of time in Chinese (① from the two Han dynasties to the Tang, contact with the Western Regions and with Sanskrit (received account: Old and Middle Chinese loanwords of the kind of 葡萄, 师子 and 琉璃, together with a batch of disyllabic words brought by the translation of the Buddhist scriptures; ② in the modern period, a large-scale importing, by way of Japanese, of words written in Chinese characters (received account: this layer is the most disputed, and the most widely used: the relation between words in modern Chinese of the kind of 经济, 社会, 革命, 文学 and 电话 and modern Japanese technical terms is a subject the field has long discussed (This lesson's reservation: all three of these relations — coined in Japanese; Japanese using an old word to translate a new sense; Chinese taking the word from a Japanese translation — have existed, so any account that cuts the matter with one stroke is unreliable (this is the recurrence, at the lexical layer, of Chapter 1's "stratification of the sources" (③ contemporary English loanwords and lettered words (received account); The most telling example (世界: in the Buddhist scriptures, a compound word in which 世 stands for time and 界 for space, and now the most ordinary word in general use — the widening of one word's meaning records one transplantation of a system of thought (this lesson calls words of this kind "everyday words of unclear provenance": they are used every day, and yet their grammar and their meaning were given by someone else (this is why the history of the vocabulary is always the most honest part of the history of thought.).
:::

::: example A snapshot of a merger in progress {#ex-kuaizhao}
Design a survey, and study a phonetic or lexical merger you know well (such as n/l, 尖团, some two finals, or some two pronouns or terms of address). It is required that: **① you choose ten high-frequency words and ten low-frequency words; ② you state which three social variables you will stratify by; ③ you state how you will distinguish "in change" from "the two sides mixed in use"; ④ you give a falsifiable sentence-frame for the conclusion**.
::: solution
**①** — **Word frequency is the independent variable of this chapter: the speed of change of high-frequency words may be the opposite of that of low-frequency words (**high-frequency words are more easily weakened, because they have been automatised, and they are also harder to replace, because they are heard over and over (this is precisely the key point on which research into lexical diffusion divides — so the word list must be ordered by a frequency table that can be checked, and cannot go by impression); ② — age (the first dimension, the least troublesome approximation to time), education and contact with the media (the second dimension, close to the "standard account"), length of local identification or years of residence (the third dimension)) — one must declare to what extent the three are confounded with one another; ③ — "in change" is a systematic stratification inside the community, and "mixed use" is one and the same person swinging between different registers: tell the two apart by the axis of style (Chapter 8): a large difference, for the same person, between reading aloud and casual chat = stratification by style; a small difference, but a large difference between age groups = generational replacement in progress (this is the most practical technique in this chapter); ④ — a sample falsifiable sentence-frame: "In this community, the merger is already complete in the group under 30 with the longer period of schooling, and high-frequency words merge before low-frequency words; if the group under 30 shows no difference, or if the low-frequency words change first, then this hypothesis does not hold" (Marking: without the half that says "if it does not hold", it does not count as a scientific conclusion (Lesson 3: the most expensive sentence in linguistics is the sentence "If I am wrong, it will show").
:::
:::

::: intuition Change does not take place in the collective {#int-jiti}
**A corollary often ignored** (**language is not an organism that changes, but the result of a group of people each speaking and adjusting to one another; what is called "a language has changed" is, strictly speaking, "the system one generation learned and the system the previous person used show a measurable difference" (**three consequences (**①** no proposition of the form "our language is being ruined" can stand (because what is ruined is only the norm currently in force at some one layer, and a norm has never been anything other than that layer's least troublesome way of writing (② language policy cannot change the direction; it can only change the speed (it can speed adoption up (textbooks, examinations, broadcasting), but it cannot designate the innovation: no one can legislate a word into popularity (the two-act structure above); ③ you are taking part at this moment (every person is at once the victim of the change and its agent (This chapter's final attitude: when a linguist hears "the Chinese of former times was more proper", they ought to remember that, once, some young person somewhere was the first to say it wrong, and that their companions found it pleasant to hear (that is the whole of the history).
:::

::: history Who has been in charge of the language over two thousand years {#sec-guan}
**Han–Tang** (**character dictionaries, rhyme dictionaries, and commentaries and subcommentaries on the Classics: linguistic norms were enforced by the study of the Classics and by the institutions for selecting officials (**received account: correct characters and pronunciations were a technical requirement of the civil-service examinations (this is the earliest and the most enduring "infrastructure of a standard language" in the world (the ancestral copy of Chapter 1's "a catalogue is a vetting")); Song–Qing (the rhyme-dictionary line and the sounds of speech parted ways: reading pronunciations and speaking pronunciations were recorded side by side over a long period (and so the dispute over "ancient and modern pronunciation" is really a dispute over "the ledgers of the refined and the vulgar" (the predecessor, in the sociology of language, of Chapter 3's "literary and colloquial readings"); the late nineteenth century to the early twentieth (the movement for phonetic characters, the dispute over schemes of phonetic spelling, the National Language (*guoyu*) movement: language planning became, for the first time, a project of the modern state (received account: in this period "language" and "nation" were bound together (This chapter's warning: the binding gave phonetic writing a moral driving force, and also gave discrimination against dialects a moral outer garment (Chapter 8, "the Army and the Navy"); 1949–1950s (the simplification of Chinese characters, the Pinyin scheme, the promotion of Putonghua (standard Mandarin), and the nationwide survey of dialects (the setting of norms and the survey got under way in the same year (the most exact footnote to that sentence of Chapter 8, "motives opposed, and yet each needing the other"); the 1990s to the present (under globalisation, bilingualism and multilingualism, the assessment of language vitality, and the documentation of endangered languages (received account: international organisations have proposed indicators such as intergenerational transmission (This chapter's close: the indicators of assessment will themselves also change policy — a definition of "vitality" written into an indicator decides which mixture counts as bilingual and which extinction counts as voluntary (measurement has never been neutral (the list in Lesson 1 is a pair of spectacles.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of this chapter
**"Ancient pronunciations were more scientific / more advanced"** (**there is no internal index that makes one state better:** Chinese losing the entering tone is not a loss, and gaining a merger of tones is not one either (the rebalancing clause above)); Piecing together a history of pronunciations out of documents (what a rhyme dictionary reflects is the reading pronunciation of some normative layer: it records "the pronunciation that had been approved", and it does not record the change in the street (Chapter 3, that an edition is an ideology: of whatever one reconstructs the sounds from, one must first make clear which layer it is ()); Taking "约定俗成" ("established by convention") as an explanation (it is a description, not a mechanism: one must answer who said it first, who adopted it, and in which groups it was adopted (② and ③ of the worked example are exactly this set of answers)); Calling contact, across the board, either "pollution" or "enrichment" (loanwords, calques and substratum are three different mechanisms, and the costs and the consequences of each differ (the contact clause above: words of moral approval can only block analysis; they cannot stand in for analysis.
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the evolution and the degeneration of language {level=3}
Write from six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing "**linguistics refuses two narratives that are equally tempting: degenerationism says that language once lived better days, and evolutionism says that it is moving towards a more perfect state; both narratives read a two-act process, one with no vector, as a journey that has a direction. Yet linguistics must also admit that the alternative picture it offers is harder to sell politically: because a change that has neither progress nor a fall amounts to declaring that the norm of the present is not an achievement but only one unfinished compromise — and this would put out of work everyone whose profession is the norm**". You must draw on: **the two old propositions and the two scales**, **the rebalancing of economy of effort and distinctiveness**, **reanalysis and analogy**, **subjectification in grammaticalisation**, **the three mechanisms of contact**, **the two-act structure and unpredictability**, at least six items; and adjudicate: **where the position "there is no progress" may slide into moral nihilism (if it does not matter whether language change is good or bad, does linguistic oppression also not matter?) — you must give one reason for opposing it that does not depend on a judgement of "better and worse"**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**propositions, rebalancing, the two hands, subjectification, contact, the two acts**); the adjudication (**the key to a satisfactory answer is to change the criterion of judgement, not to add one:** what is bad about oppression is not caused by an internal property of the language, but by its consequences — **it restricts who can reach which occasions, who pays the cost of switching, and whose accent is made into evidence (**so opposing linguistic oppression does not need the false premise that "a dialect is superior"; it needs only that apportionment table in Chapter 8 (the marking looks at whether the following argument is explicitly rejected: "because every variety is equally good, discriminating against a variety is wrong" — this step does not hold (equal complexity is not an equal right (and it looks at whether the answer points out the prescription common to degenerationism and evolutionism: in both, it is other people who come and change our speech); if the conclusion can be written "so the moral resource a linguist can really offer is very narrow, and very firm: he does not judge which way of speaking is better; he insists on only one thing — any change that shifts the cost onto those with the least say, whether it is called modernisation or called preservation, ought not to be called progress", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
Filed under the two acts: propositions (**the law governs the outcome, diffusion governs the process**), mechanisms (**economy of effort and distinctiveness continually rebalance**), the two hands (**reanalysis alters the drawing, analogy builds on according to the drawing**), grammaticalisation (**the little words are content words that have settled; subjectification is their signature**), contact (**three ledgers: loanwords, calques, and the substratum**), policy (**it can change the speed; it cannot designate the innovation**). The method in one sentence: **when you hear "the language is going bad", ask who was the first to say it wrong, who found it pleasant to hear, and who was forced to change what they say**.
