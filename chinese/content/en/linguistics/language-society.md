---
title: "Language and society"
---

The previous seven chapters treated language as a device; this chapter treats it as an asset — **the first lesson of sociolinguistics is not that 'dialects are interesting', but a method: find a set of regular differences in language, then find a set of regular differences in society, and then prove that their regularities are the same; once that step has been taken, a speaker's origin becomes a variable that can be counted**.

## Definition: variety and variable {#def-bianti}

::: definition Six nouns, distinguished in one go {#def-mingci}
**Received account** (**dialect (*fāngyán* 方言, a regional variety), social dialect (class, occupation, age, gender), register (usage switched according to the occasion), genre and style (*yǔtǐ* 语体) (usage shaped by the conventions of writing), personal usage (**the set of all the varieties one person has**) — **this lesson's mnemonic:** a dialect is divided by 'who you are', a register by 'what you are doing', and personal usage is the cell you occupy once those two axes have crossed (**many quarrels happen because someone presses a charge about a problem of register as if it were a problem of dialect (**"现在的年轻人不好好说话" ('Young people these days do not speak properly.' (Our translation.)) is a complaint about an age dialect, and yet it is often written up as a decline of grammar (Lesson 1, synchrony and diachrony; Chapter 9, the forerunner of style**)))).
:::

::: definition A language is a dialect with an army {#def-junui}
**Received account** (**a sentence often quoted: "一个语言就是拥有陆军与海军的方言" ('A language is a dialect that has an army and a navy.' (Our translation.)); it is not a definition, it is a sociological prompt — **this lesson reads it as an institutional statement (**'the standard language' is not a higher variety existing by nature, but a set of institutions: a phonological table, a dictionary, a set of textbooks, an examination, a broadcasting organisation (**take any one of them away, and it degenerates into yet another dialect (**the concrete ledger for Chinese** (**received account: Putonghua (standard Mandarin) is defined as "以北京语音为标准音、以北方话为基础方言、以典范的现代白话文著作为语法规范" ('taking the Beijing pronunciation as the standard pronunciation, northern speech as the base dialect, and exemplary modern vernacular writings as the grammatical norm.' (Our translation.)) — **note carefully that each of the three clauses of the definition has made a subtraction and a concession:** from the Beijing pronunciation a batch of local sounds has been subtracted, together with the neutral tone and rhotacisation (*erhua*); from northern speech, a great many words current only in northern colloquial have been subtracted; sentences in vernacular writings that do not meet the norm have been declared not to count (**so 'the standard' is not collected; it is cut out (**this is also the source of everything political in the chapter:** cutting needs someone to hold the knife, and the knife usually has the two characters 规范 ('the norm') written on it (Chapter 1, a catalogue is already censorship; Chapter 4, the standard for word segmentation: **putting a definition into effect always needs someone to sign**).
:::

## Method {#def-fangfa}

::: definition Labov's two moves {#def-labov}
**Received account** (**before the middle of the twentieth century, dialectology went to the countryside looking for 'the purest pronunciation of elderly men'; the local speech of the cities was treated as 'corrupted Putonghua' and not worth describing. The practice of urban sociolinguistics is: **①** the style axis** (**the same speaker is induced to produce a continuum of styles from 'attending to pronunciation' to 'chatting at ease': reading a word list, reading a passage, reading minimal pairs, free conversation, and a fourth and a fifth kind when one is hard pressed**) — received account: when reading aloud one is closest to the standard pronunciation, and when chatting idly one is closest to the local speech (**this proves that variation is not random noise, but is distributed along one observable style axis (**every speaker uses the whole of the axis, so 'non-standard' is not a defect of ability, but a choice of position**); **②** the embarrassment axis** (**use a 'correction study' to get round self-esteem: first have the person judge which pronunciation is 'correct', then, without their noticing, collect the pronunciation they actually produce (**received account: the result is a curve of 'self-esteem — actual usage': groups of the lower stratum, when they are not aware of it, use the local variety more often, and the moment they notice they draw towards the standard; the self-positioning of middle-stratum groups deviates the most**) — this one is linguistic evidence that sociology can use (**it turns 'vanity' into a curve (**this lesson requires you to remember the method and not the conclusion** (**the conclusion depends on the particular community and the particular variable, and if you change either one it may reverse; whereas the move 'first find the variable that speakers themselves dare not admit' can be replicated**) — the field methods of Lesson 10 and this chapter share one ethic: you do not know what you are observing until you ask the right question.
:::

::: definition Two kinds of prestige {#def-weiwang}
**Received account** (**'prestige' divides into overt prestige (the standard pronunciation, the returns of formal occasions) and covert prestige (the trust of the local group, and of being 'authentic') — **this distinction explains one thing that normative linguistics has long been unable to explain (**why, knowing perfectly well that the 读书音 ('reading pronunciation') is the more correct, many people still will not change (**because they are earning on another ledger (**what covert prestige converts into is this: being treated by one's own group as one of their own; its cost is: losing out in standardised examinations, in reading aloud, and in interviews**) — this is the first 'cost question' of the chapter (**the real difficulty of language policy is never 'which is better', but 'who is made to pay the switching cost' (the clause below, 'the cost of reviewing pronunciations', will reckon this account down to the yuan).
:::

## Three specimens of Chinese {#def-benbiao}

::: definition Reviewing pronunciations: the social cost of one change of reading {#def-shenyin}
**Received account** (**the sorting-out of variant readings in Chinese went on for several decades (the first draft, in 1963, of 《普通话异读词审音表》 (*Putonghua yidu ci shenyin biao*, 'Table for the review of variant readings in Putonghua'); a revision was promulgated in 1985; thereafter the readings of a number of characters remained in dispute in society, and a new draft for consultation, around 2016, was not put into effect, because of the reaction of public opinion (received account)) — **this lesson treats this series of events as one complete cost sheet (**①** the benefit side** (**teaching, reading aloud, dictionaries, speech recognition, communication across dialects: **a unified pronunciation is infrastructure that lowers transaction costs for the whole country** (**②** the cost side** (**every teacher's existing knowledge, every edition of the textbooks and the recordings, the lookup apparatus of every dictionary, the answers that every candidate in the senior-secondary and university entrance examinations (the *zhongkao* and the *gaokao*) has already memorised (**③** why 'following custom' angers people** (**because the pronunciation that is changed away usually belongs to a stock that has already been fully depreciated: to change the reading is to declare that, for a whole group of people, the pronunciation of half a lifetime was wrong (**the real content of the phrase in public opinion, "向错误妥协" ('compromising with error'), is: **I am unwilling to transfer my existing capital without compensation (**this is not a controversy in linguistics; it is a property-rights dispute wearing the shoes of linguistics (the aptest case of Lesson 2's point that the right to define is the right to allocate (**and if any review of pronunciations reckons only the account in phonetics, it is certain to lose in public opinion — **it must answer: who pays, who benefits, and whether there is compensation (Chapter 5, three questions of response: sociolinguistics has written that up as a compulsory question).
:::

::: definition Terms of address: a social grammar laid bare {#def-chenghu}
**Received account** (**the Chinese system of address carries information that other languages carry by morphology or by vocabulary: closeness and distance, above and below, insider and outsider, occasion (**four changes one can observe (**① 师傅 ('master', a skilled worker) spread from a term of address belonging to a trade into a general term for labourers one does not know, and then, in the contexts of schools and office buildings, gave way to 老师 ('teacher'); ② 小姐 ('Miss') passed through a complete life cycle, occupational term of address — general honorific — stigmatisation (**this is a circle of semantic disgrace that one word ran through within twenty years (the social version of the euphemism cycle above); ③ the use of 老板 ('boss'), 总 ('chief'), 某工 ('so-and-so' with 工) and 某处 ('so-and-so' with 处) grows and shrinks as the ownership of enterprises changes; ④ 哥 ('elder brother') and 姐 ('elder sister') have come back to life in the work unit and in online communities, used as a low-risk investment in closeness (**this lesson's reading:** address is the most expensive layer of pragmatics, because a wrong choice is immediately visible and cannot be repaired (Chapter 7, "称呼选错是语用失误而后果严重十倍" ('Choosing the wrong term of address is a pragmatic failure, and the consequences are ten times as serious.' (Our translation.))); **and it runs both ways:** you can use 老师 to raise the other person, but you cannot use it to raise yourself (**so the Chinese table of address is in fact a diagram of power relations filled in on the spot (**this is why 'what one ought to be called' is often the most important sentence before a meeting**.
:::

::: definition Language and gender: from 'difference' to 'stance' {#def-xingbie}
**Received account** (**research in the last thirty years of the twentieth century often reported that women stay closer to the standard pronunciation and make more use of politeness and of hedges; thereafter conclusions of this kind were revised in great number:** what are called female features are often 'the linguistic behaviour of a group of lower status', and they change with that once women acquire membership of a group of high status (**this lesson's way of handling it (**①** first, check the method** (**many early studies mixed 'the speaker's gender', 'the hearer's gender' and 'the formality of the occasion' together in one count (**this is a typical third-variable problem); ②** then, analyse stance** (**the vogue for evaluative words of the sort 娘娘腔 ('effeminate'), 少女感 ('a girlish air') and 夹子音 ('a pinched, cutesy voice') shows that what is being evaluated has never been language itself, but 'whether a person has crossed a line in gender' (**language is a stand-in for an accusation about identity**) — this is the judgement this chapter can give on 'language and gender', and it is the most useful one — **Lesson 10, on internet language, will meet it again: when one group of people begins to correct another group's tone, it is usually some other dispute that has found a safe bank of topics.
:::

::: example An accent on trial {#ex-kouyin}
Situation: an employee reports at a meeting in Putonghua coloured by dialect; colleagues imitate that pronunciation in the group chat and say, laughing, that they 'cannot understand'. Using sociolinguistics, write an analysis of 150 characters: **① who is offended, ② what social function the linguistic variety under attack was carrying, ③ what the demand 好好说话 ('speak properly') really amounts to in this incident, ④ two measures that can be carried out, and who benefits from each**.
::: solution
**①** — **what is offended is the dialect group, and membership of it;** mockery aimed at pronunciation almost never aims at 'clarity' (**the same pronunciation, in the mouth of 'one's own people', is no obstacle**); **②** — **that variety carries trust on the inside, and loyalty to one's origin (the covert-prestige account);** the substance of mocking it is to force the other party to transfer assets onto the overt account; **③** — **好好说话 is not, here, a demand for intelligibility, but a demand about identity:** if it really were a problem of intelligibility, the measure should be to allow a repetition, confirmation in writing, or a change of seat, and not imitation (**a check that can be verified:** turn the same report into a written text, and see whether the mockery disappears (if it disappears, the issue was never the content); **④** — **Measure A: impose one requirement in the name of 'norms for the language of meetings' (**who benefits: the party that needs cooperation across dialects; the cost: the switching cost is handed to the person with the least bargaining power, and enforcement necessarily depends on humiliation); Measure B: forbid only imitation and mockery, and allow intelligibility to be negotiated (**who benefits: the person attacked, and the order of the meeting; the cost: efficiency falls slightly, and the chair must bear it); **this lesson's marking:** if you can write the sentence 'A is cheap and B is expensive, so an institution always chooses A', that is this chapter's report card (**a standard is a kind of infrastructure, and not a kind of virtue**.
:::
:::

::: intuition The market for linguistic ability {#int-shichang}
**The value of a linguistic ability does not depend on how hard it is, but on who can convert it** (**four observable movements in the exchange rate (**①** a change in the official position of a variety** (**the promotion of Putonghua makes the overt returns to a dialect fall, and the covert returns rise (the two kinds of prestige above); ②** a change in the economic position of a written genre** (**official documents, promotional copy, voice-over for short videos: colloquial writing is suddenly worth more than a long written sentence; **and so the standard of 错别字 ('wrong and misused characters') gives way to the standard of the 钩子 ('hook') (Chapter 9 and Chapter 1, the format of distribution); ③** a change in how visible an accent is in the media** (**when the accent of some region appears again and again in comedy, in film and television, and in livestreaming, it acquires at the same time two kinds of capital, that of being recognisable and that of being mockable — **this is this chapter's explanation of the phenomenon that 'discrimination against accents instead increases':** visibility is double-edged; **④** a change in the policy status of a language** (**when the next chapter discusses the languages of the ethnic minorities and bilingual education, it will open the same table out by one further layer); **the reminder on which this chapter closes:** of any claim that 'language proficiency is falling', first ask which ability's conversion price it is that has fallen — **this question can usually turn a moral debate into an investigation that can be checked (Lesson 1, the study of evidence: **this is the one necessity that sociolinguistics teaches the ordinary reader**.
:::

::: history Three turns of a discipline {#sec-shehui}
**Before the 1940s** (**dialectology was cartography:** go to the countryside, record elderly men, draw isoglosses (**its presupposition was that the 'pure' language resides in the countryside and in the old, and that the city is pollution (**this presupposition guaranteed that it could not see its own object); **the 1960s–1970s** (**the study of variation:** the city, stratification, frequency, statistical significance (**received account: sociolinguistics gained the standing of a discipline from this, because it brought hypotheses that could be tested); in the same period, the debates over 'language and thought' and over the 'restricted code' (**received account: a claim of very great influence was later criticised for writing the language of the working class as a 'deficit', though the author had no such intention (**this is a lesson the chapter demonstrates again and again:** a descriptive distinction gets read as a judgement of rank, and often all it takes is one retelling in a newspaper); **the 1980s to the present** (**practice, identity, stance, networks and multilingualism (**received account: the centre of research turned from 'society determines language' to 'language takes part in constructing society'); **the parallel line in Chinese** (**received account: the dialect surveys and the National Language (*Guoyu*) movement of the 1920s, the academic conference on questions of norms for Chinese and the dialect surveys of the 1950s, the norms and Pinyin of 1955–1958, and, from the 1980s on, the journals of sociolinguistics and the discussion of protecting dialects (**a tension running through from beginning to end:** norms and survey set out at the same time and need each other, yet their motives are opposite (**the former wants to unify, the latter to record; every ethical problem in this chapter comes from this one sentence**.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**'The standard language is more logical'** (**a standard is an administrative choice; in pronunciation, in grammar and in vocabulary there is not one internal measure that makes some variety the more 'reasonable' (the definition clause above: **to call infrastructure rationality is the commonest single piece of rhetoric**)); **'Dialects are dying out, so they must be rescued'** (**the motive can be understood, but one must be clear: the driving force of language shift is the opportunity structure, not aesthetics; unless the opportunities change, to record is only to archive (the field-methods lesson in the next chapter will give the distinction between 'recording' and 'maintaining')); **reading a difference between groups as a difference in the abilities of groups** (**most of the early conclusions about gender, class and dialect came to grief on the third variable (gender, ①, above)); **defending 'what is fitting' or defending 'the rustic'** (**both defences are transactions on a moral account; **this chapter's stance is to write the costs and the benefits out clearly, and to leave the moral judgement to the parties concerned (Lesson 1, description and prescription).
:::

::: exercise An audit of origin {level=1}
List five linguistic items in your own mouth that 'would give your origin away' (any of: a word, a sound, a term of address, a modal particle), and write 80 characters: **which one of them you are paying for, and which one is making money for you**.
::: solution
Marking: **the five items must be spread across at least three levels** (**pronunciation, vocabulary, pragmatics:** if they are all on one level, it shows that you have thought only of accent); **'paying / earning' must name the occasion of conversion** (examinations, interviews, matchmaking meetings, livestreams that sell goods: **if there is no occasion, there is no market (the fourth movement in the exchange rate, above)).
:::
:::

::: exercise Design a study of variation {level=2}
Choose a linguistic item that is in dispute in your community (some word, some kind of tone, some reading), and design a research plan **that can be completed in two hours**. It must contain: **a dependent variable, two independent variables, one confounding variable and the control for it**.
::: solution
Marking: **the confounding variable must really exist** (**age and occasion, gender and topic, occupation and years of education are the commonest collinear pairs); **writing only 'the sample is too small' does not count as pointing out a confound; **the dependent variable must be countable** (**a proportion of occurrences, a reaction time, or a score:** for 'degree of authenticity' one must say who judges, and how (Lesson 1, the study of evidence).
:::

::: exercise A short essay: who pays the switching cost {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing '**Every language policy is a table apportioning switching costs. Unifying pronunciation, promoting a common language, reforming spelling, simplifying orthography, and even a platform's review of content, are all shifting one and the same expense between groups. Public texts always call this expense "convenience", because no one is willing to admit that they are charging other people. Therefore the only honest question in judging a language policy is: who changed the way they speak, who did not, and what the people who changed the way they speak have received**'. You must draw on at least six items: **the list of institutions behind the standard language**, **the three cuttings in the definition of Putonghua**, **the style axis and the embarrassment axis**, **the two kinds of prestige and the transfer between accounts**, **the cost sheet for the review of pronunciations**, **the on-the-spot diagram of power in terms of address**; and you must decide: **between 'ability in the common language' and 'maintenance of the local language', is there a policy instrument that allocates the cost more fairly — you must give one concrete instrument (one of: a subsidy, two-track teaching, a media quota, an examination's official line), and say which party would resist it**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**institutions, the cutting, the two axes, the accounts, the cost sheet, address**); the instrument (**an answer that passes must say where the money or the class hours come from, who carries it out, and how it avoids pressing the cost back onto the same person — **for example: adding dialect class hours to a timetable that is already full is the same as charging the teacher of Chinese and the parents (**the marking looks at whether you have pointed out the logic of the resisting party's interests, rather than 'backward ideas'**); if the conclusion can write '**so this chapter gives no one a reason to set a dialect down, and gives no one a reason to mock a dialect; it insists on one thing only: every policy that announces itself as "for everyone's convenience" must make that apportionment table public, because convenience is a kind of income, and an income must have a payee**', that is an A.
:::
