---
title: "Figures of meaning"
---

This lesson is the main hall of the gallery of figures: metaphor and simile and their kin (personification and its reverse, metonymy), the art of the boast (hyperbole), a trick of sound (the pun), and the right to say the opposite (irony). The six exhibits share one secret — **meaning can move house by sliding along three tracks: similarity, relatedness and contrariety**.

## Metaphor and simile: the king of the figures {#sec-biyu}

::: definition Tenor, vehicle and the three steps {#def-biyu}
Metaphor and simile join things of different kinds by a point of likeness: three pieces, the tenor (本体), the vehicle (喻体) and the point of likeness. The steps are three. **Simile** (A is like B; the markers 如 ('like'), 似 ('resembling'), 若 ('as') and 仿佛 ('as if') — “问君能有几多愁，恰似一江春水向东流”, 'I ask you: how much sorrow can you have? It is just like a river of spring water flowing to the east.' (Our translation.)). **Metaphor** (A is B: “这人是一堵墙”, 'This person is a wall.' (Our translation.)). **Implicit metaphor** (tenor and marker both leave the stage, and only the vehicle comes on: in 'My Old Home', Yang Ersao 杨二嫂 is given the nickname “圆规” ('a pair of compasses'), and thereafter the text calls her “圆规” directly wherever she appears). The gold standard for judging has only one clause: **tenor and vehicle must be of different kinds, and yet alike** — “他像他哥哥” ('He is like his elder brother.' (Our translation.)) is a comparison within the same kind, and is not a figure; “月亮像一只船” ('The moon is like a boat.' (Our translation.)) is what passes. Liu Xie 刘勰, in the chapter 'Comparison and *Xing*' of *The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons*, had already said it outright: 胡越之物可合为肝胆 ('Things of Hu and of Yue can be joined into liver and gall.' (Our translation.)) — the magic of metaphor and simile is that **distance produces lightning**: the further tenor and vehicle stand apart, the brighter the point of likeness when it is lit (“人生如旅馆”, 'Life is like a hotel.' (Our translation.), is ten times brighter than “脸如苹果”, 'a face like an apple', and ten times more dangerous — a risky figure easily slides into a crooked one).
:::

::: definition Dead metaphor and living thought {#def-si}
A **dead metaphor** (死喻): a metaphor used for a thousand years, whose picture nobody any longer "sees" — “手腕” ('wrist'), “桌面” ('desktop'), “文件夹” ('folder'), “挂钩” ('hook'), “瀑布流量” ('a waterfall of flow'). They are fossils of metaphor, and the dictionary enters them directly as senses. A **latent metaphor** (潜喻) picks out only one side: say “他钻进死胡同” ('He has squeezed into a dead alley.' (Our translation.)), and only the alley's "dead" is taken, not the grey bricks and the sound of cicadas. Modern cognitive linguistics (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980) pulled the cloth off the table: metaphor is first a **way of thinking**, not a rhetorical ornament — “时间是金钱” ('Time is money.' (Our translation.)) (spend, save, waste), “辩论是战争” ('Debate is war.' (Our translation.)) (smash through, gain the upper hand, a line of defence falls), “上是高兴、下是低落” ('Up is glad, and down is low.' (Our translation.)) (spirits running high / a mood dropped to the bottom of the valley) — we locate abstract concepts in the space of the body, and **beneath the inventory of figures lies a whole stratum of cognitive geology** (what this lesson used to call “比喻的连锁” ('chains of metaphor') and “系统隐喻” ('systematic metaphor') are both outcrops of it at the surface).
:::

## Personification and metonymy: two further rails {#sec-bini}

::: definition Personification and its reverse: getting by with A treated as B {#def-bini}
**Personification** (拟人): a thing is loaded with human words and deeds — “感时花溅泪，恨别鸟惊心”, 'Feeling the times, flowers splash tears; hating the parting, birds startle the heart.' (Our translation.). **Its reverse** (拟物): a person is made into some other thing — a proper example: “他夹着尾巴逃跑了” ('He ran off with his tail between his legs.' (Our translation.)) (a person made a beast). Telling personification and its reverse apart from implicit metaphor is an old examination question: **the one is "treating as"** (A's action is directly B's action; in the syntax there is no judgement of the form "B ="), **and implicit metaphor is "it is like, and only B is left"**; “海风在吼” ('The sea wind is roaring.' (Our translation.)) is personification (the sea wind is still on the scene; it has only borrowed a throat); of “恶霸登门” ('The local tyrant comes to the door.' (Our translation.)), the whole village does not say "local tyrant" but only “阎王到了” ('The King of Hell has come.' (Our translation.)) — the tenor has left for the whole length of the piece, and the reader decodes it without being asked: this is implicit metaphor. **Carried further**: the reverse figure can shift abstract things about as if they were solid objects — “把快乐打包” ('pack happiness up'), “忧愁超重” ('sorrow over the weight limit'). Modern copywriting earns its meals on this rail.
:::

::: definition Metonymy: relatedness stands in for similarity {#def-jiedai}
Metaphor and simile rely on **similarity**; metonymy relies on **relatedness** — tenor and substitute live next door in the same world. The part for the whole (“不拿群众一针一线”, 'Do not take from the masses so much as a needle or a thread.' (Our translation.)); a feature for the tenor (“圆规” is at the same time a specimen of metonymy: a feature-plate for a sharp, thin build); the concrete for the abstract (“烽火连三月”, 'Beacon fires running three months on end.' (Our translation.), = war); an instrument, or a place, for the person concerned (“白宫表态”, 'The White House states its position.' (Our translation.); “他把青春交给了讲台”, 'He gave his youth over to the platform.' (Our translation.)). The ledger of metonymy: **whatever is substituted must be something the reader can reckon** — “红领巾进场” ('The red scarves come on.' (Our translation.)) can be read (it sits in the stock of an age's common knowledge); an unfamiliar substitute is at once obscure. The boundary with implicit metaphor, in one sentence: **an implicit metaphor can be rewritten as a simile (with "like"); a metonymy cannot** (“针线”, 'needle and thread', is not like “财物”, 'property'; it already is a part of the property).
:::

## Hyperbole, the pun and irony {#sec-kuzhang}

::: definition Three exhibits with a sting {#def-kuzhang}
**Hyperbole**: the words go beyond the fact, and people know that they do — “飞流直下三千尺”, 'The flying torrent plunges straight down three thousand *chi*.' (Our translation.) (*chi* is the Chinese foot; its length was not the same in every age), which amplifies; diminishing hyperbole such as “一个汗珠摔八瓣”, 'One bead of sweat falls and splits into eight.' (Our translation.) (the weight of labour on the land); and again the cicada-like smallness of “室仅方丈，可以容膝”, 'The room was only a *zhang* square, with space enough for the knees.' (Our translation.) (*Xiangjixuan zhi*, 'Record of Xiangji Studio'; a *zhang* is ten *chi*). The legal licence for hyperbole is **a basis in the real + an intention out in the open**: white hair of “三千丈” ('three thousand *zhang*') — the sorrow is real; the ruler need not be. **Pun**: one sentence hangs two hooks — the homophone pun (“东边日出西边雨，道是无晴却有晴”, 'In the east the sun comes out, in the west the rain falls; one says there is no clear sky, and yet there is a clear sky.' (Our translation.); Liu Yuxi 刘禹锡, in folk-song style: 晴＝情, 'clear sky' = 'feeling') and the semantic pun (several senses of one word hung on together: “他这人挺有‘意思’”, 'This person is quite full of "sense".' (Our translation.); the context in which it comes on decides which layer develops). Crosstalk (*xiangsheng*), the two-part allegorical saying (*xiehouyu*) and the advertisement are the pun's three homes. **Irony**: the letter and the intent go opposite ways — in 'In Memory of Liu Hezhen', “几个所谓学者文人的阴险的论调”, 'the insidious line taken by certain so-called scholars and men of letters' (Our translation.); the two characters “所谓” ('so-called') are irony's signal lamp. In ordinary talk, “你可真行啊”, 'You really are something.' (Our translation.): slant the tone, and the sense flips. The vital point of irony is **a code that can be got open**: one and the same sentence, and one's own side can hear the satire, while the other side can hear a provocation — at this point rhetoric runs straight through into the study of public opinion.
:::

::: example The family sorting bench {#ex-fenjian}
Sort them, and state your grounds: ①问君能有几多愁，恰似一江春水向东流 ('I ask you: how much sorrow can you have? It is just like a river of spring water flowing to the east.' (Our translation.)) ②石狮子“醒”在桥头 ('the stone lion "wakes" at the bridgehead') ③不拿群众一针一线 ('do not take from the masses so much as a needle or a thread') ④“圆规”一副尖酸相 ('“圆规” ('compasses'), wearing a sharp, sour look') ⑤眼泪是心底的漏雨 ('Tears are rain leaking from the bottom of the heart.' (Our translation.)) ⑥“乖乖，这楼高得能碰着飞机！” ('Goodness, this building is so tall it could knock into an aeroplane!' (Our translation.)) ⑦“你居然是这种同学！” ('To think that you are this sort of classmate!' (Our translation.)) ⑧“春蚕到死丝方尽” ('The spring silkworm's silk is done only when it dies.' (Our translation.)).
::: solution
① Simile (sorrow like spring water: abstract → concrete, taking both quantity and drawn-out duration); ② personification (the stone lion has borrowed the throat of “醒” ('waking') — there is no word of judgement, so it is not a metaphor or a simile); ③ metonymy (the part for the whole: 一针一线 = property); ④ metonymy (a feature for the person; if the build has not been laid out above, the feel on reading here is that of an implicit metaphor — how full the stock is decides of which figure it is the specimen; see the entry on the metonymy ledger); ⑤ metaphor (the frame of a judgement sentence); ⑥ hyperbole (amplification; the tone of exclamation issues the licence); ⑦ irony (shock + a fake “居然” ('to think that') — blame staged as a performance); ⑧ pun (丝 = 思: the “丝” of silkworm silk and the “思” of yearning are the same sound and the same reel; mark only the pun). All eight right, and you may leave the hall.
:::

::: history From *bi* and *xing* to conceptual metaphor {#sec-lishi}
Chinese rhetorical discussion of metaphor and simile begins in the study of the Classics: **“赋比兴”** are three tools among the Six Principles in the study of the *Book of Songs* — exposition (*fu*), comparison (*bi*) and *xing* ('evocation'; also 'stimulus', 'affective image'). The preface to Zhong Rong's 钟嵘 *Shipin* ('Gradings of Poetry') defines “比” as “因物喻志” ('by a thing, to figure what one holds to' (Our translation.)). Liu Xie, in 'Comparison and *Xing*', uses “胡越合体、肝胆可照” ('Hu and Yue joined in one body, liver and gall able to shine' (Our translation.)) to say all that need be said of the principle by which different kinds are bridged — comparison and *xing* also serve as an instrument for explaining rule and moral instruction (“善鸟香草以配忠贞”, 'Good birds and fragrant herbs are matched with loyalty.' (Our translation.); the preface to Wang Yi's 王逸 *Chuci zhangju* ('Chapter and Verse Commentary on the *Songs of Chu*') notes “善鸟香草以配忠贞”, 'Good birds and fragrant herbs are matched with loyalty.' (Our translation.) — a manual for decoding fragrant herbs and the fair one). On the Western side, Aristotle's *Poetics* calls metaphor 'the mark of genius'; from Longinus (*On the Sublime*, under a name not his own, around the first century AD) to Pope, it was used to measure the dose of the sublime. The finest of it is the turnabout in the twentieth century: Richards (I. A. Richards, 1936) reduced metaphor to 'the universal vehicle of thought' (vehicles of thought); in 1980 Lakoff & Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* settled the note with one hammer-blow — metaphor lives in the conceptual system, and does not live in anthologies of poetry. The study of figures was thereby joined, mortise and tenon, to cognitive science: **you suppose that you are speaking; in fact it is your metaphor speaking for you.**
:::

::: warning Four prohibitions before leaving the hall
First, a ban on **comparing within the same kind**: “她像她妈妈一样爱花” ('She loves flowers just as her mother does.' (Our translation.)) — the registry of metaphor and simile does not enrol persons from inside the household. Second, a ban on the **mixed metaphor**: “这艘改革之船插上互联网的翅膀，在蓝海上乘风破浪疾驰如马” ('This ship of reform puts on the wings of the internet and, on the blue sea, rides the wind and cleaves the waves, galloping like a horse.' (Our translation.)) — one boat and three beasts, and the images trample one another. To revise the passage is to refit a cabin: **only one burner of incense to a page**. Third, a ban on **forcibly revitalising a dead metaphor**: make any further play with “桌面” or “挂钩”, and the reader's electric shock will be spent only on 'Why are you suddenly treating me as a foreign guest?' — a new metaphor must **be born already carrying its own charge**. Fourth, a ban on **irony that does not mind the occasion**: to say to a bereaved family “这下清静了” ('It will be quiet now.' (Our translation.)) is not called rhetoric; it is called an accident — the charter of aim and circumstance hangs high overhead, and however dazzling the figure, it first passes a constitutional review.
:::

Compose one sentence of each, as proof that you have it in hand: personification, diminishing hyperbole, the semantic pun, and irony — and with each sentence attach one sentence explaining the "mechanism that makes it work".

For reference: personification, “巷口的路灯咳了一声，天就亮了” ('The street lamp at the mouth of the lane gave a cough, and the sky grew light.' (Our translation.)) (the two senses, sincerity and lifelike truth, hung on together — context decides which layer develops); irony, “完美，钥匙又在门里隔着呢” ('Perfect: the key is shut on the far side of the door again.' (Our translation.)) (“完美” ('perfect'), at a moment of bad luck, = utterly awful; the modal particle is the licence). Full marks for inventing all four yourself and naming the mechanism.

::: exercise Surgery on a mixed metaphor {level=2}
Heal it, and explain: 我们的团队要插上腾飞的翅膀，在市场的蓝海里锚定赛道，跑出护城河的加速度。 ('Our team is to put on wings and soar, drop anchor on a racing lane in the blue ocean of the market, and run out the acceleration of a moat.' (Our translation.))
::: solution
The seat of the disease: wings (bird), blue ocean (ship), anchor (ship), racing lane (running on land), moat (war), acceleration (physics) — six source domains scrambling for one play. Treatment one (conservation): make the whole of it a ship — “在市场的蓝海里，我们的船调整帆位、避开暗礁，驶向新航线” ('On the blue sea of the market, our ship trims its sails, avoids the hidden reefs, and makes towards a new course.' (Our translation.)). Treatment two (by layers): changing the metaphor is allowed, but **one domain to a paragraph**, and where the domain changes, give a break in the sentence and a transition. Full marks for the reason for the operation, plus either one of the two treatments.
:::

Marking points: the original picture of all five metaphors correctly explained (no false source of the Baidu sort), and the reviving sentence really makes the picture "visible again" (it passes the test of defamiliarisation).

::: exercise A short essay: disaster metaphor and public opinion {level=3}
Write 800 to 1,200 characters. Collect five metaphors from public reports (for example 风暴 ('storm') / 防线 ('a line of defence') / 洪水 ('flood') / 摘桃 ('picking the peaches'); the words are yours to choose), and, in the framework of conceptual metaphor, analyse what script of response the source domain of each (the disaster kind / the war kind / the nature kind) has "framed". Answer this: when the media use “战疫” ('a war on the epidemic') and “风暴” ('storm'), are they describing reality, or conscripting emotion? Examples must note their source and their date.
::: solution
Marking points: five examples that can be checked, and the source domains classified; the analysis of "framing" in place (a disaster frame → a script of obedience and evacuation; a war frame → a script that mobilises one's own side against the enemy; the two frames can, each excluding the other, yield different implications for policy); the conclusion takes a stand and owns its own limit (metaphor is a necessity of cognition, and it is also ready ammunition for manipulation — the question is not whether one uses it or not, but the not knowing that one does).

::: summary
A physics lesson in figures of meaning: metaphor and simile depend on a likeness between different kinds (the further apart, the brighter, and also the more dangerous); personification is "treating as"; metonymy is "next door"; hyperbole requires "excess openly declared"; the pun is one rope and two hooks; irony drives the other way, and depends entirely on a code. Cognitive linguistics has moved the whole exhibition hall into the brain — metaphor is not a firework display of fine phrasing; it is a load-bearing wall of the conceptual system. Four prohibitions on leaving the hall (no comparison within one kind; no mixed metaphor in the cabin; no revitalising of a dead metaphor; no irony let into a place where it causes harm). Next it is the turn of the hall of form: antithesis, parallelism, repetition, climax and allusion — that half of the realm in which Chinese most insists on "physique".
:::
