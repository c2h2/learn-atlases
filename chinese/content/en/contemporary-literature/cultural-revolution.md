title: "The Cultural Revolution years"

The literature of the previous chapter had wages, had journals and had prizes; the literature of this chapter has no readers — **it has handwriting, has passing from hand to hand, and has the risk of confiscation; the method of this chapter is to take away the variable “publication” first, and to ask a clean question: when public publishing in an era almost stops, where does literature actually go, and why, when it comes back, does it carry an unfamiliar accent**.

## Definition: the closing of the distribution system {#def-fenfa}

::: definition Count the journals first, then talk about ideas {#def-guanbi}
**The thread of the usual account** (**From 1966, large numbers of literary journals ceased publication or were merged; over the several years that followed, creative work published in public was highly concentrated, and the list of authors shortened sharply —** this lesson cites no estimated figures, and gives only one observation that can be checked — **the ceasing of one journal equals the collective unemployment of a batch of professional writers, and also equals the extinction of a genre (** fiction becomes a novella or a novel only once it is published, and a *zawen* (polemical essay) becomes a *zawen* only once it has a position (the extreme experiment, in Chapter 1, of a distribution format deciding life and death: **this time even the “format” was gone**)); **the nature of the positions that remained** (**the gist of the usual account: thereafter the public stage consisted mainly of works of the “model” kind, together with a small amount of creative writing produced in step with the campaigns —** please note that this is not “only bad works”, but **a book market left with only one kind of supplier (** when the sole supplier monopolises the space on the page, “best-selling” becomes the only concept of sales (the box-office constraint of Chapter 9: **at this point the reader’s preference cannot be observed, because it has no form in which it can be expressed**.
:::

::: definition Writing moves into three places of concealment {#def-cangsuo}
**① The drawer** (**Writing for oneself, or for after one’s death:** this is the most expensive kind of commissioning party in literary history, because its remuneration is zero (the limit of the “Party A” clause in Chapter 1: **when the first reader does not exist, the author becomes his own editorial office (** at this point rhetoric no longer serves persuasion, and serves only memory and self-examination — **this chapter asks the reader to notice this rare controlled experiment**)); **② The hand-copied manuscript** (**the thread of the usual account: novellas, short stories and poems circulated in small circles as hand-copied manuscripts and as collections of hand-copied manuscripts —** the hand-copied manuscript reintroduces the problem of the edition: **every copying onward is an editing (Chapter 3, that an edition is an ideology, and Chapter 8, sixty years of hand-copied manuscripts, are replayed here:** a hand-copied manuscript is still social; only this time the social relation means prison)); ③ The spoken, and underground associations (the thread of the usual account: urban youth formed small circles by reading, writing and exchanging songs, and particular groups were convicted for associating and for copying texts onward (for the concrete circumstances of the cases, this lesson takes only the usual account) — literature entered the historical record for the first time in the form of a “charge”: its sociological meaning is that a group which wrote poems was dealt with as a political organisation ( when power takes your metaphor for a programme, your programme has already taken effect ( this is the most uncomfortable sentence in this chapter, and the most instructive.
:::

## The black market in reading matter {#def-heishi}

::: definition Banned books, grey books and foreign books {#def-jinshu}
**Three kinds of circulation** (**the thread of the usual account: ① old works singled out by name for criticism, and foreign works of “internal distribution” (** grey books: there is publication, and sale is restricted**); ② earlier modern literature in translation (translations of the 1930–40s); ③ handwritten and mimeographed copies of foreign works —** these made up a generation’s actual syllabus ( Chapter 1, “the object of influence is always the nearest textbook”: this time the textbook was passed on in secret, so its influence bypassed the whole training in the official style ( this is the sociological explanation of why the literature of the 1980s suddenly “looked like translationese”: not a worship of the foreign, but that that generation’s lyric grammar was learnt from translations)); why this point matters (literary history usually tells the new voices of the late 1970s as an outburst of genius; this lesson tells them as a change of syllabus: when a generation cannot read contemporary works in the mother tongue, they go and read translations, and then rewrite the mother tongue in the syntax of the translations (Chapter 4, the grammar of the vernacular (*baihua*) growing up in the pleasure quarters, played backwards: grammar can grow up in the gaps of a channel.
:::

::: definition Why “Today” matters {#def-jintian}
**Underground journals in vogue, 1978–1979** (**the thread of the usual account: from the end of 1978, unofficial journals of poetry and prose appeared in Beijing and Shanghai (of the kind of *Today*, 《今天》), and the works in them were mostly old pieces from between 1969 and 1978 —** note this lag in time: **what was “new” had in fact been in the drawer (** this is precisely the methodological gain of this chapter: **the literary level of an era can be measured by “how long the author had already been writing when it was published” (** the depth of the drawer is the depth of the censorship (the reverse of Chapter 1’s “the luck of the archive”: **some works were not unwritten; they were not permitted to be read in the year they were finished**.
:::

## The evidence of form {#def-xingshi}

::: definition Three formal techniques {#def-jishu}
**① Metaphor as encryption** (**the gist of the usual account: underground writing of this period made much use of symbols, subjectless short sentences and images of nature (** this is not “obscurity”, but a secrecy protocol: **it can be read aloud, it is hard to quote, and a sentence taken out of it cannot be used to convict (** the cost is an inflation of literariness: **when everyone has to encrypt, the first layer of meaning is never said by anyone, and the second layer of meaning cannot be said clearly either (** the right posture for reading these poems is not guessing a riddle, but noticing that they must speak on several layers)); ② The splitting of the lyric subject (the thread of the usual account: dialogue structures of the “I—you—night—stars” kind are common in this period — to set the person addressed as someone absent, or as a natural object, is a grammatical consequence of drawer literature ( there is no lyric poem with a public; what remains are letters written to oneself and to those who are not present (the mirror image of Liniang in Chapter 2, “speaking a situation as language”: here, people speak language as a situation)); ③ The rise of notes and autobiography (the thread of the usual account: a considerable batch of “belated fiction” published in the 1980s had base texts written in 1972–1978 — when fiction reappeared, the first subject it chose was personal memory ( because this is the only subject that does not need a commissioning party (the contemporary revival of Chapter 10’s “the accountancy of memory”: in an era that does not permit a public archive, personal memory became the only usable database.
:::

::: definition Model works: the accounts of an extreme craft {#def-yangben}
**The thread of the usual account** (**works of the model kind were, through these ten years, repeatedly revised, filmed, recorded and promoted, and came to cover every medium —** this lesson notes here the problem of their textual authority: **of a work repeatedly altered by the collective and by politics, the “author” is a committee, and the “established text” is a decision (the final version of Chapter 2, that the approved copy is the established text, and of the previous chapter’s “the situation exercises the rights of the author”**); **The technical legacy** (**the gist of the usual account: their music and their staging did indeed contain a great many innovations of craft —** the verdict this chapter passes on them agrees with the previous chapter: **extreme constraint can force out a density of craft; it cannot buy a good play (Chapter 8: constraint produces form, and does not produce quality**); **An irony** (**it was precisely this batch of highly refined works that trained a whole generation of audiences to an appetite for “a complete production” (the thread of the usual account) —** part of the aesthetic standard of China’s industry of literature and the arts after the 1990s is a legacy left by these ten years ( this is the sentence this lesson is least willing to say, and yet must say: a legacy is not an endorsement.
:::

## Return and retrospective recognition {#def-zhuiren}

::: definition Three identities of the “return” {#def-guilai}
**① Writers who survived come back** (**the thread of the usual account: from the late 1970s, writers who had been stopped from writing resumed, one after another, their writing and their publication —** what they faced was not a blank, but a break in the succession: **their readers had already been replaced by another generation, and the grammar had changed as well (the “absent author” clause of Chapter 9:** the generation literary history lost is the generation that the 1980s would otherwise have had)); ② Underground works come back (drawer manuscripts and hand-copied manuscripts were put in order and published in the 1980s — and so something worth dwelling on occurred: the first batch of classics of the new literature were old works ( critics read them as “the beginning of a new era”, while their language belonged to another era ( this is a misplacement that can be checked: a work’s date of production and its date in literary history differ by ten years (Lesson 15, retrospective recognition: what we thought of as an “outburst” was a clearing of stock.
:::

::: intuition What literature without readers did {#int-meiyduzhe}
It did something that public literature could not do (it made writing once again a personal capacity, rather than the duty of a post ( ten years later, when the journals opened their doors again, the people who poured in treated writing as something that had to be done, and not as work one might or might not do ( this is the sociological reason for the explosive recovery of literature at the end of the 1970s: repression did not destroy demand; it turned demand from an occupation into a habit (a distant cousin of Chapter 5’s “three teams”: **the same people wrote both a public style and a private style, and the accents of the two sides leaked into each other**); **it also left two debts** (**① a generational debt**: a generation that would have written was cancelled; ② **a debt of trust**: when a generation of writers, before setting pen to paper, first estimates “who might become the first reader”, this reflex does not disappear because censorship has ended (a restatement of the conclusion of the previous chapter: **after 1978 it changed its name, and was called the market, and later was called the platform**.
:::

::: history Another timeline of the ten years {#sec-shiyan}
**1966** journals ceased in large numbers (**it is not that inspection became stricter; inspection became the only mode of production**); **around 1968** cases of underground associations and of hand-copied manuscripts (**associating, and copying onward, were handled as a matter of organisation:** the sociological definition of writing was rewritten); 1969–1971 sending-down, dispersal, and writing in “places of exile” (one geographical fact: the centre of literature was dispersed to county towns and farms, and so the first subjects of the 1980s were the borderlands and the native soil (the nationwide version of Chapter 3, the place of banishment as invisible editor-in-chief)); 1971–1976 extremely narrow public publication, and a slow thaw (grey books of “internal distribution”, and a limited reprinting of foreign works: the loosening of the censorship organs changed the language of literature earlier than a manifesto did); 1976 the Tiananmen poetry copies (an outburst of popular hand-copied manuscripts and of oral transmission: this is the only event in these ten years in which “the reader reappeared” — people were not reading poems; they were using the copying of poems in order to assemble ( literature was for the first time taken as a lawful shell for a public assembly, because it looked the least like politics ( its lesson was afterwards studied, again and again, by both sides); 1978 official journals resumed publication, and unofficial journals of the *Today* (《今天》) kind came into circulation (in the same winter two kinds of “first issue” appeared: their authors may not have known one another, but each was the other’s reader ( this is the seed of all the contradictions of the next ten years); 1979 the Fourth Congress of Writers and Artists (the institution co-opts them again: and so this chapter ends, and the next chapter begins — scar, reflection, and a queue over “who has the right to be wounded”.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of the ten years
**Reading “the underground” as “the truer”** (**the drawer likewise has performance, temper and self-beautification:** to write for oneself is also to write for an imagined court (Chapter 8, independent sources: **underground texts need the same study of sources as public texts**)); **Putting “there are no works” in the place of research** (**most of the reliable statements about this period are negative (“unpublished”, “not issued”); bringing evidence for a negative statement is harder:** you must say which catalogues you have checked, and which you have not (Chapter 4, doing the accounts that other people leave out**)); The trap of spurious works and of retrospective recognition** (**after the 1990s a number of texts appeared which were said to have been written at the time; authenticity and date are both disputed (the thread of the usual account) —** there is only one way to handle them: **wherever the year of writing cannot be checked, cite them in every case as “works of the year of publication” (the edition clause of Chapter 2**)); **Taking craft for value** (**how completely a promotion across media was carried through is not weight in literary history:** otherwise these ten years ought to have produced the greatest number of masterpieces (Chapter 6, the false-depth clause: **abundance can be the expense claim of insufficient imagination, and refinement the expense claim of insufficient courage**).
:::

::: exercise A test of encryption strength {level=2}
Write a passage of 60 words: **a friend must be able to understand it when it is read face to face, but an anonymous excerpt must not be able to turn it into a sentence that is “problematic”**; then write down the two devices you used.
::: solution
Marking: **one must not rely on a witty pun** (a pun is the easiest thing to excerpt: the real skill is to hand the meaning over to tone of voice and to context ( this is precisely the technique of the underground poetry of this period: the image is not decoration; it is a load-bearing position — devices for reference: no subject, dialogue with natural objects, and leaving the judgement to the pause).

::: exercise A short essay: the years with no readers {level=3}
Write between six hundred and a thousand words, assessing “**to study an era that almost did not publish can wash away every coincidence in an explanation: when the market, reputation, prizes and readers’ letters no longer reward anyone, what the people still writing depend on is the oldest part of literature — a physiological need to speak experience as language; but it is precisely in such an era that literature pays its two heaviest debts: a generation that was cancelled, and a generation’s permanent conditioned reflex towards the first reader**”. You must draw on at least six items: **the closing of the distribution system**, **the three places of concealment**, **the translation syllabus**, **metaphor as encryption**, **the depth of the drawer (misplacement of period, and survivorship bias)**, **the Tiananmen poetry copies as “the reader reappearing”**; and give a ruling: **is this literature, after all, truer or falser than “literature that has readers” — you must give a criterion that can be checked, and you may not use “sincerity” as the criterion**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**closing, places of concealment, syllabus, encryption, misplacement, reappearance**); the ruling (**it is right to judge “sincerity” unusable:** it cannot be checked; the criteria that can be checked should be: ① the number of times the text was revised, ② the identity of the first reader, ③ the stability of the form in which it survives (in marking, see whether the following is inferred from these — that a drawer text is “truer” holds in only one sense: it has been altered less by an editor, and is therefore closer to the sentences the author wrote at the time; on the dimension “closer to the facts” it has no advantage ( the drawer of someone who lived through it will likewise contain self-justification, embellishment and later revision (the concession passage of Lesson 6: this is the hardest asymmetry this chapter can give: a system can control who is published, but it cannot control what a sentence becomes after it has been written down ( if the conclusion can be written, “so the real cost of the ten years is not what they published, but that they turned writing into a private reflex — later, when literature flourished, this reflex supplied an astonishing output and a continuous tension: that is precisely the set of signs of its best works”, that is an A.

::: summary
Closing, filed: distribution (**a market with no sales cannot observe the reader**), places of concealment (**the drawer, the hand-copied manuscript, the underground association: three commissioning parties whose remuneration is zero**), the syllabus (**translations take the place of contemporary works in the mother tongue: the accent comes from this**), encryption (**it can be read aloud, and is hard to excerpt: the image is a load-bearing position**), retrospective recognition (**how long the author had already been writing when it was published: the depth of the drawer is the depth of the censorship**). The method in one sentence: **first measure a work’s two dates — when it was written, and when it was read; the difference between them is the degree of freedom of this era**. :::
