title: "Morphemes and words"
---

“我喜欢她” is five characters, five syllables and five words — 'I like her.' (Our translation.) — and when luck holds, the three ledgers agree; but “我喜欢她**们**” adds one morpheme without adding one character — 'I like them.' (Our translation.) — the three characters of “**葡萄**牙” ('Portugal') are only one morpheme plus two, and the one word in “**洗了一个澡**” is chopped in two by a duration complement (a 补语 of duration, not a complement in the English sense). The most basic problem in Chinese grammar, and the most troublesome, sits in this ledger that will not balance. This lesson issues three rulers: what a **morpheme**, a **word** and a **phrase** each is, and how each is measured.

## Four “smallest” things: do not mix them {#sec-sige}

::: definition Morpheme, word and phrase {#def-sany}
A **morpheme** (词素) is the smallest **combination of sound and meaning** — a unit that cannot be divided any further and still have meaning: “人”, “民”, “罗” (the 罗 of 罗盘, 'compass', has no meaning looked at alone; “萝” and “锣” are each another morpheme). A **word** is the smallest combination of sound and meaning that **can be used independently**: it can be said alone (“谁来了？——人。” 'Who has come? — A person.' (Our translation.)), it can be used alone as a constituent, and it can carry an intonation and form a sentence. A **phrase** is a combination of sound and meaning temporarily assembled from words by rule (“白石桥” will do, “白苹果” is awkward — the making of a phrase is under a double constraint, of rules and of meaning). As for the **character**, that is a unit of writing; the **syllable** is a unit of pronunciation. Both the trouble and the charm of Chinese lie in this: “character, morpheme and syllable” **roughly** coincide, with exceptions at every turn, so that the “character” is often mistaken for the “word”.
:::

::: theorem Character ≠ morpheme ≠ word: three kinds of counter-example {#rule-budeng}
① **One character is not a morpheme**: linking words (*liánmiáncí* 连绵词) such as “蜘蛛、蜻蜓、窈窕”, disyllabic simple words of the linking type (联绵) such as “蝴蝶、鸳鸯”, and transliterated words such as “沙发、巧克力、麦克风” — the two characters of “沙发” refer to nothing each, and only together are they one morpheme. ② **One character is more than two morphemes**: the homographs “花₁（花草）／花₂（花钱）” ('flowers and grass' / 'to spend money') are separate entries in the dictionary — one grapheme has packed into it several morphemes that have nothing to do with one another. ③ **A morpheme is not always a word**: “语素”, “民”, “谓” and “第” are productive but **not free** (one does not say “我民” or “很第”). Once the three counter-example cards are stood up, it is plain that “3,500 common characters” and “having mastered 3,500 words” are not the same thing at all — half the accidents in teaching Chinese as a foreign language come from taking characters for words.
:::

::: example Three-step segmentation: constituent surgery on “他养了一头奶牛场的牛” {#ex-qiefen}
First cut the morphemes, then fix the word boundaries, and last mark the levels of the phrase. Note that the dispute between “奶牛场” and “奶 牛场” shows exactly what the problem of the word boundary really looks like.
::: solution
Morpheme layer: 他｜养｜了｜一｜头｜奶｜牛｜场｜的｜牛 (“了” and “的” are function-word morphemes; “一”, even under tone sandhi, is still one morpheme). Word layer: “奶牛场” is one noun (a place for feeding cattle); the cut “奶／牛场” will not stand semantically, and is ruled a mis-cut. Phrase layer: 他／养了／一头奶牛场的牛 — the classifier (measure word) “头” locks “奶牛” into one cluster of meaning, and that is just what forces the reading of “奶牛场” as a whole. In a single sentence the three layers, morpheme, word and phrase, each follow their own rules. This is precisely the beginner’s specimen of Chinese as “没有形态却层层有法” ('without morphology, and yet with rules at every layer').
:::

## Judging a word: substitution, expansion, solidification {#sec-panci}

::: definition The expansion method (insertion test) {#def-kuozhan}
Insert the necessary functional elements between two morphemes. What can take the insertion, with the meaning still related, is in most cases a **phrase** (“黑板” cannot take *“黑的板” and still refer to the same thing → a word; of “白饭” one can say “白米饭/很白的饭” → more strongly a modifier–head phrase). What cannot be inserted, or changes its meaning once inserted, is judged a **word**. This is called the “expansion method”, and it has been the operational core of the discussion of Chinese word boundaries since the 1950s. Its three weak points must be entered in the ledger. ① **Separable words** — “洗澡、睡觉、理发、游泳” can be expanded (“洗了个热水澡”) and yet linguistic intuition still judges them one word. Huang Borong 黄伯荣 and Liao Xudong 廖序东, in their *Modern Chinese* 《现代汉语》, treat them as “a whole in meaning + able to separate and to combine”, and grant that the Chinese word boundary has an **elastic zone**. ② **Fixed phrases** — nothing can be inserted into “车水马龙”, and yet it is still a 语 (a four-character idiom, *chengyu*); the expansion method only judges “whether it looks like a word”, not “whether it is a word”. ③ **Solidification by frequency** — “电脑” can be expanded to “大的电脑”, which no one finds unsayable, but no one on that account calls it a phrase: **wordhood is decided by how many times a form has been used, not by any one use**.
:::

::: example “南京市长江大桥” {#ex-nj}
Make two segmentations of this famous ambiguous string, and say what the machine (an input method, or a segmenter) and a person each rely on to resolve the ambiguity.
::: solution
南京市／长江大桥 (a modifier–head phrase of place names) or 南京／市长／江大桥 (an absurd reading). A person relies on **meaning and common sense** (“长江大桥” is a high-frequency proper name taken as a whole); statistical word segmentation relies on **bigram and n-gram word lists plus a language model**: if both “南京市” and “长江大桥” sit high in the word-frequency table, their combined score beats “市长江” outright. This is exactly why Chinese information processing must “segment words” first: **written Chinese has no spaces between words, and the word boundary is inferred** — the 1992 *Standard of Modern Chinese Word Segmentation for Information Processing* 《信息处理用现代汉语分词规范》 (GB 13715), and the successive international evaluations (the SIGHAN Bakeoff, from 2005 on), turned that inference into an industrial procedure.
:::
:::

::: intuition Native speakers cannot say clearly what a “word” is, and that fact matters
Ask subjects to mark word boundaries in sentences, and for Chinese the agreement in annotation is high (around 95%) but it is not 100%: the disagreements gather on **quasi-affixes** (the “者” and “手” of “读者” and “歌手”), on **separable words** (whether “见面” counts as one word or as a verb–object phrase) and on **proper names** (whether “北京大学” is cut in two or in four). Some conclude from this that Chinese simply has no such unit as the “word”; the fairer reading is that the Chinese “word” is a **prototype category** — core members (“山、水、电脑”) have clear boundaries, while marginal members (the complement in “看得见”, and “洗了澡”) shade off towards the phrase. The task of linguistics is not to declare that the category does not exist, but to draw the high-water line on this tidal flat of gradual change — normative dictionaries and the standards for word segmentation are that line.
:::

## The family of morphemes {#sec-yusu}

::: definition A classification that places them {#def-dingwei}
By **whether they can be used alone**: free morphemes (“人、跑、很”) / morphemes that are not free (“民、语、第、老-、-子”); morphemes that are not free divide again into **roots** (with a concrete meaning: “民”, “语”, “桌”) and **affixes** (meaning bleached, position fixed: “老-、第-、子、儿、头、们” and the like; derivation has its own treatment in the next lesson). By **number of syllables**: monosyllabic morphemes are overwhelmingly the main stock; then disyllabic linking forms and transliterations (“琵琶、逻辑、幽默”); from three syllables up, almost everything is a translated name (“巧克力、达尔文、奥林匹克”). By **the way meaning is produced**: native morphemes / survivals from Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) (“之、乎、其、所”, not colloquial, yet highly productive) / entrants from dialects (*fangyan*; topolect) (“搞、俺、啥”) / foreign morphemes (“咖啡、基因”).
:::

::: example A set piece: counting morphemes {#ex-shu}
Count how many morphemes there are in each of “玻璃葡萄架、旧博物馆、智能化、奥林匹克运动会”.
::: solution
“玻璃葡萄架”: 玻璃 (1, linking) + 葡萄 (1, linking) + 架 (1) = 3 morphemes and 5 characters. “旧博物馆”: 旧 + 博 + 物 + 馆 = 4 (“博” goes into “博识”, 物 into “物体”, 馆 into “展馆”, and substitution on all three sides holds). “智能化”: 智 + 能 + 化 = 3 (“智” and “能” each make a morpheme on their own, and “-化” is an affix). “奥林匹克运动会”: 奥林匹克 (one polysyllabic morpheme) + 运 + 动 + 会 = 4 (“运”, used alone, does not have this meaning; if one counts “运动” as one morpheme, taking it as a coordinate disyllabic root, then three morphemes is also acceptable — clear reasoning earns the mark). The whole value of the question is in **the reasoning**: see through one case each of linking, transliteration, affix and compound, and the standard is met.
:::
:::

::: history How the “word” entered the self-awareness of Chinese {#sec-cijinchu}
Traditional philology had the “character” and not the “word”: the *Shuowen jiezi* 《说文》 explains characters, and the *Erya* 《尔雅》 glosses by “词语” ('words and phrases') and does not discuss what a “词” is. In 1898 *Mashi wentong* 《马氏文通》 (*Ma's Grammar*) borrowed the Latin “ partes orationis ” and set up Chinese “字类” ('character classes'), and the “word”, as a grammatical unit, was named for the first time. The great debate of the 1950s on “the word and the boundary of the word” — the pedagogical grammar system crossing swords with the various authors of the 汉语汉语语法丛书 — established the definition “the smallest unit of meaning that can act independently”, and the standard standing of the expansion method. The national standard for word segmentation of 1992 cast the fruits of the debate as the foundation of information processing. In the large-corpus age of the twenty-first century, the word has turned from a concept of linguistics into a **computational entity** — inside the “feel for Chinese” of every input method and every machine-translation system there lives a set of word boundaries. A unit borrowed a hundred years ago has, all the same, been put to use by Chinese.
:::

::: summary
A morpheme is the smallest combination of sound and meaning, a word is the smallest unit that can be used independently, and a phrase is a rule-governed assembly of words; the character (writing) and the syllable (pronunciation) often line up with the morpheme and the word, and yet fail to line up everywhere — linking forms, transliterations and separable words are the three great sources of the mismatch. Judgement rests on three axe-strokes: substitution (replacement settles identity), independent use (sayable alone / able to form a sentence), and expansion (what can be inserted into tends to be a phrase), with solidification by frequency and the semantic whole added as a buffer. The “word” is a prototype category, and the border zone (lexicalised phrases, quasi-affixes, proper names) is left to norms and to corpora to draw the line. Morphemes are classed along several dimensions: free or bound, monosyllabic or polysyllabic, native, Classical Chinese, dialect or foreign. That a character is not equal to a word is the first lesson shared by the teaching of character recognition, by Chinese as a foreign language, and by information processing.
:::

::: quiz
The number of morphemes contained in “巧克力” is ( ).
- [ ] 3
- [x] 1
- [ ] 2
- [ ] 0
::: solution
A transliterated polysyllabic word is **one** polysyllabic morpheme: the separated “巧”, “克” and “力” have nothing to do with the meaning of this word (the “力” of “巧克力” does not mean “力量”, 'strength'; it only records the sound).
:::

::: quiz
Judged by the expansion method, the one most like a phrase is ( ).
- [ ] 火车
- [x] 白饭
- [ ] 黑板
- [ ] 领袖
::: solution
The expansion “白米饭/很白的饭” is natural and the relation is transparent, so the modifier–head phrase character is strong; “火车” is not “火 and 车”, and cannot be expanded; of “黑板” one does not say “黑的板”; “领袖” takes its meaning as a whole, and expansion breaks it (领 and 袖, taken apart, are parts of clothing, not the sense “带头人”, 'one who leads').
:::

::: quiz
Which of the following is **not** required for judging a morpheme? ( )
- [ ] Whether the segment can be divided again into smaller meaningful units
- [ ] Whether the segment can be used independently
- [x] How many Chinese characters the segment corresponds to
- [ ] Whether segments of the same phonetic form can substitute for one another
::: solution
A count of characters is only the surface of the ledger: in a linking word, one character to one morpheme does not hold, and one morpheme spread over several characters is commonplace. Judgement looks at three things: **the minimality of the meaning, the possibility of independent use, and identity within a frame of substitution**.
:::

::: exercise A dossier on separable words {level=1}
“见面、跳舞、帮忙、游泳” are often called “words that can be separated”. For each, make one expanded sentence, and under the expansion method and under linguistic intuition enter one line in the ledger apiece: which rule are they stuck on?
::: solution
见了一面／跳了三年舞／帮了他大忙／游了泳 (“游了个泳” is also acceptable). The snag: the expansion method judges them phrases, while intuition and the dictionary judge them words. The textbook’s way out is to recognise “separable words that can be used apart” as a transitional zone between word and phrase — recording this “the two ledgers do not balance” is itself the standard answer to the question.
:::
:::

::: exercise Taking homographs apart {level=1}
For each of the groups “花₁花钱／花₂开花” and “老₁老虎／老₂老人／老³老是”, give one frame for judging: are they one character with several morphemes, or one morpheme with several meanings?
::: solution
In everyday linguistic intuition the link between the sense 'flowers and grass' and the sense 'spend' has already broken, and it is safer to give them separate entries in the dictionary — judge them **same form, different morphemes** (同形异素). The “老” of “老虎” is a prefix (not free), the “老” of “老人” is a root that can be used alone, and the “老” of “老是” has bleached into an adverbial morpheme — three different morphemes (the same in sound and in shape, and each different in nature). That the *Contemporary Chinese Dictionary* 《现代汉语词典》 lists “老¹²³” separately is exactly this position. The watchword of the method: look at the two things **whether the link of meaning survives or dies, and whether each can stand independently**.
:::

::: exercise Word segmentation in practice {level=2}
In the spirit of the *Standard of Modern Chinese Word Segmentation for Information Processing* 《信息处理用现代汉语分词规范》 (dictionary segmentation first), segment the sentence below into words, and mark three places where you hesitate, with the reason for the choice you make: “他去年在北京大学东门外新开的那家奶茶店买过一杯珍珠奶茶。”
::: solution
他/去年/在/北京大学/东门外? 或 东门/外／新开/的/那/家/奶茶店? 或 奶茶/店／买/过/一/杯/珍珠/奶茶. Three sample places of hesitation: ① “北京大学” as a proper name taken whole, against 北京/大学 (GB tends towards the whole, and names of institutions are dictionary words); ② “东门外” as a compound of place (the tendency is 东门/外, with the localiser standing alone); ③ how far “奶茶店” has been lexicalised (in a new trade the whole is frequent, and may be taken as a whole; “珍珠奶茶” taken as a whole, as an entry for a dish name, is still less in dispute). The question does not ask for a single correct answer. It asks that **every place have a basis**.
:::

::: exercise Short essay: is there anything to the character-based approach? {level=3}
Some scholars hold that the grammar of Chinese ought to be “character-based” (*zìběnwèi* 字本位, 'the character as the basic unit') rather than “morpheme-based or word-based” (Feng Zhiwei 冯志伟, Lu Jianming 陆俭明 and others each have their own position in the discussion of the “character”; take any one scholar’s view). Write a short essay of eight hundred to twelve hundred characters. Taking as your material the three hard cases of judgement in this lesson (separable words, linking forms, affixes), assess what the character-based approach gains and what it loses. You must also give one example that the character-based approach cannot explain, and one example that the word-based approach cannot explain.
::: solution
Points for marking: take the character-based approach as **a choice of where to enter, for teaching and for analysis**, not as a position in the study of the Chinese script; be able to use the three hard cases “葡萄/洗澡/老-” to show what the word-based approach costs at the boundary; a good example of “what the character-based approach cannot explain”: linking words (the characters of “玻璃” have no meaning at all), and grammatical morphemes such as “们/第” that are not given on the face of the character; a good example of “what the word-based approach cannot explain”: the independent activity of characters inside four-character idioms and idiomatic phrases (the “是” of “唯利是图”). The position need not be uniform; the logic must be self-consistent. An essay that mixes up “汉字” ('Chinese character') and “语素” ('morpheme') is sent straight back to be rewritten.
:::
