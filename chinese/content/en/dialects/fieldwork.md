title: "Recording a dialect"
---

The last chapter presses the craft of the preceding nine chapters into one notebook — **this chapter's claim is mildly provocative: the whole difficulty of a dialect survey is not hearing the sounds, but deciding what to write down; every sound you write down is a claim that 'this is the same sound'**.

## Definition: sound and transcription {#def-jiyin}

::: definition Induction before etymological research {#def-guidna}
**The received account.** The first step of a survey is transcription, the second is inducing the phonological system, and only the third is finding the etymological character and discussing strata. This lesson explains why that order cannot be reversed. **① If you do not induce the system first, you will transcribe the 'right' sound.** Beginners habitually write an unfamiliar sound as a familiar character or a familiar letter, so that the transcription sheet reads as if it would do for any dialect — **this is material that has been screened by one's own phoneme inventory.** **② Inducing the phonology is a claim.** To write two sounds with one symbol is to claim that, within the same system, they do not distinguish meaning. The line from Course 3, 'a bracket is a claim', here takes its hardest form: **your square brackets and your slashes are the first claim in the paper, and they lie hidden in the notes, where no one checks them.** **③ Identification of the etymological character has to wait until the end.** Once you set a Chinese character against a sound, you begin to hear that sound according to the reading of the character. This is the commonest contamination in the work of transcription, and once it has happened it is irreversible. **This lesson's working protocol.** For the first two weeks, writing Chinese characters is forbidden. **First record the distinctions with arbitrary symbols; only when, on hearing them, you register the opposition do you go on to ask which Middle Chinese category it corresponds to, and which character.** The protocol can be tried on anyone; the cost is a week or two of discomfort. It is, at the same time, the concrete practice this course gives to the ethic 'describe first, judge afterwards'.
:::

::: definition Three tools {#def-gongju}
**① Tone value.** Use the five-level system to record the relative pitch of a tone. **The received account:** this notation comes from a linguist of the twentieth century, and the merit of the design is that it is cheap and comparable. **Three precepts.** The same tone value must be measured by the same listener, the same ear and the same room. **Change the person or the surroundings, and you change the scale.** ② You must measure several informants and several characters, because a tone value is the centre of a class, not the realisation on one occasion. ③ A recording is more reliable than listening on the spot, and less complete, because a recording loses rhythm and gesture, and gesture is often isomorphic with intonation. **The received account.** **② Minimal pairs.** Finding a pair of words that differ in only one component is the cheapest argument that a phoneme exists (Course 3). The difficulty is this: native speakers often do not feel that two sounds differ, and their difference has already disappeared in the system. **So you need a second thing — ③ extended natural speech.** A stretch of narration, a quarrel, a joke. **Only that will supply the patterns and the words you had not thought to ask for.** The six-item elicitation design of the previous chapter settles the completeness of the grid; extended speech settles the honesty of the grid. **This lesson gives one proportion.** If your notes contain only word lists and single sentences, then what you have recorded is a language you do not know. **The sentence sounds exaggerated. What it means is this:** grammar and pragmatics appear only in connected speech, and that is the only moment at which the informant is not watching the look on your face.
:::

::: definition Whose language, whose record {#def-chanquan}
**Four questions, which this lesson requires you to answer before you set out.** **① How is the informant's time to be paid for?** **The received practice is equivalent compensation.** **This lesson adds one sentence:** payment changes rate of speech and patience, and it also changes the other person's willingness to correct you, so the fact of payment is to be written in the notes. **② Who may hear the recording?** One village interview may be turned into a paper, a textbook, a database and a short video. **The scope of consent is not the same for these four uses, and they are usually swallowed by a single blanket consent form.** **In this chapter's view, this is the point in dialect work most in need of improvement:** **a generic consent form, in linguistics, amounts to no consent.** **③ How are the name and the place to be labelled?** Write 'an elderly person surnamed Zhang, of a certain village', and he can never be found again. Write '×× village, ×× county, Zhejiang, male, born in 1947', and he may be identified in the village. **The received account:** material that involves personal information usually requires anonymisation. **This lesson's position is this:** **what anonymisation protects is the person; it does not protect the scholar's chance of having the work rechecked —** **so keep a path that leads back to the field (through an intermediary), rather than delete the path.** **④ Can the record be of help?** **This is the hardest of the questions, because the answer is mostly that it cannot.** **This lesson opposes calling the record a rescue, but it supports doing three things that actually work** (see the next section).
:::

::: definition Three things that actually work {#def-sanjian}
**① Return the materials to the community.** A booklet that can be read, a recording that can be played, a set of audio for children to listen to. **The cost is extremely low, and the effect, among the indicators of academic evaluation, is zero; that is precisely why it matters.** **The received account:** quite a few results of dialect research have never gone back to the village that was studied. **② Train recorders from the place itself.** Twenty years set down by a teacher in the county outweigh two weeks set down by a scholar. **This is not rhetoric; it is the arithmetic of the quantity of data.** **③ Write the uncertainty out clearly.** In the report, mark which sounds you did not hear clearly, which utterances only one informant said, and which conclusions need more survey points. **This lesson holds that this is the chapter's only 'hard morality':** **a vague report will be used by later people as evidence, and you will by then be unable to answer for them.** The point from Chapter 8 closes the loop here. **The received account.** Both internationally and within the country there are norms of ethical review and of informed consent, and linguistic fieldwork is gradually being brought under them. **This lesson does not comment on how they are carried out; it only points to the consequence:** **standardisation makes a young recorder, for the first time, have to consider seriously the power gap between the recorder and the people studied.** That step took decades in other disciplines. **In dialectology it has gone slowly, because the informant is often a relative.**
:::

::: example Twelve minutes on one sound {#ex-yige}
You are transcribing in some county, and you note one character as an aspirated voiceless stop. Three days later, listening to the recording, you notice that in two words it seems unaspirated, while in a third word there is a clear laryngeal tightening. Write down the six things you can do in the next twelve minutes, and rank them by strength of evidence.

::: solution
**First lay down one principle.** What you are facing is not 'having transcribed it wrong', but a phoneme problem whose induction is not yet finished. **Six things.** **① Listen again to every recording that contains the word, and list every environment in which it occurs.** This is the recheck of lowest cost. **If the distribution is conditioned, it may be an allophone.** **② Ask the informant for a pair of words that might make a minimal pair.** **If he denies that there is a difference, while the recording goes on showing one, move to ③.** **Note:** denying a difference is a normal reaction, not an unreliable one. **③ Check whether other cues accompany it.** Vowel length, glottal tension, the onset of the tone. **An opposition of 'aspirated or not' has often already been moved onto another carrier.** **The received account:** this is the phenomenon of 'replacement' seen again and again in research on grammaticalisation and on sound change. **④ Have another informant repeat the same set of words.** A single observation of a single person is, in any discipline, the weakest evidence. The four strengths in Course 1. **⑤ Put the word into sentences, and have him make up sentences rather than say it on its own.** The feature of aspiration often appears only in a position of emphasis. **⑥ Write your uncertainty in the notes, and date it.** This is the item most easily omitted, and the most useful: **three months later you will have forgotten that you hesitated at the time.** **Ranked by strength of evidence.** Distribution across several people and several environments ＞ a stable accompanying feature within the same environment ＞ denial or admission by one person ＞ a single impression on the spot. **Marking:** **this chapter gives full marks to whoever writes ⑥ down** — **because the only product of the work of transcription that can be handed on is material carrying marks of hesitation;** **and a mark of hesitation is the licence under which a later person dares to overturn you.** **This sentence applies to all the writing in this course.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Transcription changes what you hear {#int-gai}
**A phenomenon every recorder meets.** Before you learn to transcribe, what you hear is one undifferentiated stretch; after you have learned, you hear a string of categories. **Three consequences.** **① This is a gain, and also a loss.** You can no longer protect yourself with 'I can't make it out'; and the same ability will lead you to overestimate your own reliability. **② Symbols are the mould of your senses.** Someone who has used a given transcription alphabet will hear a difference where that alphabet distinguishes, and will fail to hear one where it merges. The 'phoneme inventory' of Course 3, reproduced at the level of the person. **③ So, at regular intervals, do two-person back-to-back transcription with a partner.** The same stretch of recording, each of the two transcribes it, and then you compare sheets. **Where you diverge is a problem of your ear, not of the other person's accent.** **The received account:** this is the plainest, and the most effective, quality control in linguistic training. **This lesson requires you to do it at least three times, because within half an hour it cures one illness:** **treating 'I heard it clearly' as evidence.**
:::

::: history The history of a notebook {#sec-biji}
**① Around the first century BC.** A handbook of regional words collected local expressions by asking and by copying. **The received account:** **this lesson reads it as the first fieldwork.** Its difficulty is the same as ours: **the other person is saying words; what you want is a system.** **② The end of the nineteenth century to the beginning of the twentieth.** Dialect surveys and dialect maps arise in Europe. **The received account:** **this lesson takes from this only one methodological consequence:** a map requires uniform items of survey, and so 'asking the same questions' becomes the core of a craft. **The unified word lists of China in the 1950s are the continuation of this thread.** **③ 1928.** A study of Wu brings together transcription, a questionnaire and maps, and uses a new notation for tone values. **The received account.** **④ 1950s.** Large-scale survey teams and unified forms: this course's 'comparability comes from asking one and the same set of questions' comes from here. **The received account.** **⑤ The 1980s–2000s.** Magnetic tape and digital recording make rechecking possible, and extended speech data begin to be archived. **The received account:** **this lesson points out one quiet revolution in method:** once a recording can be listened to over and over, transcription changes from 'impression plus judgement' into 'auditable material'. **⑥ Now.** Mobile phones, cloud transcription and automatic annotation. A model can supply phonetic symbols, but it does not answer for what it has misheard. **The received account:** **this chapter leaves a last question to the reader:** when transcription can be done by a machine, where does the surveyor remain irreplaceable? **This lesson's answer:** in deciding what to ask, in detecting what one cannot hear, and in being willing to write the uncertainty in the notes — **none of the three lies within the model's scope of responsibility, because each of them is responsibility itself.** **This is the other half of the sentence that the twenty-five chapters of this course keep repeating:** **technology changes the working procedure; it does not change the value of checkability itself.**
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of this chapter
**Seeking the etymological character before transcribing** (the character will hear for you (③ in the order of induction above)); **using only word lists and single sentences** (only connected speech supplies the patterns you had not thought of (③ of the three tools above)); **one blanket consent form** (a paper, a database and a short video are not the same permission (② of ownership above)); **deleting the hesitation** (a vague report will be taken as evidence, and you will be unable to answer for it (③ of what actually works, above)): this is this chapter's summing precept for the whole course.
:::

::: exercise Twenty minutes of transcription {level=1}
Find someone who speaks a dialect other than Mandarin (*guanhua*), and in twenty minutes transcribe twenty words. Do not use Chinese characters. Afterwards write your phoneme hypothesis, and three points on which you are uncertain.

::: solution
Marking: **a point of uncertainty must be a specific question that can be tested** (for example, 'whether two particular sounds are in complementary distribution in the same environment'). **Writing 'I can't hear it accurately' scores nothing** (② in the order of induction above).
:::
:::

::: exercise Compare the sheets once {level=2}
With a partner, each of you transcribe once the same recording of three minutes. Mark every divergence, and divide the divergences into three classes: auditory impression, habit of transcription, and analytical claim.

::: solution
Marking: **for each class you must give one example, and propose a way to remove it** (auditory impression, by listening again and by changing the equipment; habit, by settling on one alphabet; claims, by discussing the conditions of the distribution (③ of the intuition above)).
:::
:::

::: exercise Short essay: responsibility and technology {level=3}
Write six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing the following: 田野工作的伦理之所以难以处理，是因为它的伤害通常不是恶意造成的：一位好心的记录者带回一批珍贵的材料，把它们放进一个村里人永远打不开的数据库，用村里的名字换取期刊的认可，然后在告别时说感谢配合。这里的每一步单独看都无害。因此方言调查真正需要的不是更多守则，而是把一件本来隐形的东西变成可核对的义务：材料的去向、同意的层级，以及研究者本人对错误的可追索性 'The ethics of fieldwork are hard to handle because the harm is usually not done out of malice: a well-meaning recorder brings back a body of precious material, puts it into a database the people of the village can never open, exchanges the village's name for a journal's recognition, and then, on taking leave, says thank you for your cooperation. Each of these steps, looked at alone, is harmless. What a dialect survey really needs is therefore not more codes of rules, but to turn something that had been invisible into an obligation that can be checked: where the materials go, the levels of consent, and the researcher's own traceability where error is concerned. (Our translation.)' You must draw on **induction before etymological research**, **the three tools**, **the four questions of ownership**, **the three things that work**, **the six steps of the twelve minutes**, **machine transcription**, at least six items; and you must rule on **how 'traceability' is to be achieved without scaring researchers off** (give one concrete institution, and say what it will miss).

::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms; the design of an institution (candidates: ① a **layered consent form**, with paper, database, teaching, return to the community and the open web each ticked separately, and with the stipulation that a secondary use requires consent afresh; ② a **page describing the material**, one page attached to each body of data, stating the collector, the time, the scope of consent, the points of uncertainty already known, and a path of contact (through an intermediary); ③ an **error register**, a journal or a database allowing the author to append a correction without retracting the paper (isomorphic with 'a correction is not a stain' in the previous chapter, and with 'citation inflation' in the course on argumentative essays)). **What it will miss must be stated.** An acceptable answer: **it cannot govern what has already been made public** (once the material is on the internet, withdrawal is impossible); it cannot govern misuse passed from mouth to ear; nor can it govern 'well-meaning over-disclosure' — a young scholar, for the sake of reputation, makes the material public of his own accord, and the community instead loses control; the last line of defence is therefore **to give the right of making it public to the community, and not to leave it with the researcher** (**marking looks at whether this point is touched**). If the conclusion can be written 所以本章的终点不是技术而是可追索：一个愿意被找到的研究者，比一份更长的守则更值得引用；而在机器能记音之后，人的贡献将主要是这种可追索性——它既是伦理，也是方法论 'So the end of this chapter is not technology but traceability: a researcher who is willing to be found is more worth citing than a longer code of rules; and once a machine can transcribe, a person's contribution will be chiefly this traceability — it is both an ethic and a method. (Our translation.)', that is an A.
:::

::: summary
Checklist for the last chapter: order (transcription, induction, etymological research; the character will hear for you); tools (**tone value, minimal pairs, extended speech**); ethics (**the layering of consent, the dilemma of names, where the materials go**); three things that work (**returning the materials, local recorders, writing the uncertainty clearly**); quality control (**transcribing the same stretch of recording back to back**); and one sentence with which to carry off the whole course: **you cannot control how others quote what you say; you can only control whether you yourself have a base text you can show — so for a person, and so for a dialect.**
