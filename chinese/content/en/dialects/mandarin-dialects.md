title: "Mandarin dialects"
---

The table of splits and mergers from the previous chapter receives its content in this one — **Mandarin is the block of Chinese that covers the greatest area, and the one whose internal differences are relatively the smallest, and these two things are one thing: its homogeneity is not innate; it was levelled flat by roads, armies and migration**.

## Definition: two features of one speech area {#def-gd}

::: definition Loss of the entering tone and devoicing of the fully voiced initials {#def-tezheng}
**Received account.** **The two most widely used diagnostic features of Mandarin.** **①** **Characters of the old entering tone (checked tone), for the most part, no longer have a stop coda** (**they have been distributed into the other tones; which tone they are distributed into is precisely the main basis for dividing Mandarin internally**). **②** **The fully voiced initials have devoiced, and have split into two paths according to the tone condition** (**received view: level tone aspirated, oblique (deflected) tones unaspirated**). **This rule is carried through the most thoroughly in Mandarin, and it is exactly the model of the previous chapter's 'degree of completion':** **under the same devoicing, Wu did not carry it through, and a number of border dialects carried through only half.** **This lesson adds two features that are often overlooked.** **③** **The rhyme categories of the Shan, Xian, Shen, Zhen, Zeng and Geng rhyme classes (*she*) have merged to a high degree** (**at many northern sites the vowel system is simpler than in the south, but, by way of compensation, more compound vowels have developed, together with 对转 ('corresponding alternation') of nasal codas**). **④** **Rhotacisation (*erhua*) and the neutral tone are abundant** (**this is the feature by which Mandarin depends most on prosody;** **it interacts with the morphology:** **suffixes of the 头, 子 and 儿 kind have a high productivity in the north, and their pronunciation in turn affects tone**). **Received account.** **A reminder on the reading.** **Mandarin is not 'simple because it lacks the entering tone';** **it has only moved the burden from the coda onto tone and prosody.** **This is the application, at the level of dialect, of Course 3's 'the balance of saving effort and being clear':** **no dialect is one that has been simplified; there are only dialects that have put their complexity somewhere else.**
:::

::: definition How the eight blocks are divided {#def-bakuai}
**Received account.** **Mandarin is often divided into several large areas, commonly eight: Beijing, Northeastern, Ji–Lu, the Central Plains, Lan–Yin, Southwestern, Jiang–Huai and Jiao–Liao.** **Three clues for the division.** **① Which tone the entering tone is distributed into** (**most of Southwestern Mandarin merges the entering tone, as one whole batch, into a single tone category** (**this is the reason it is surprisingly uniform internally**); Jiang–Huai Mandarin, by contrast, keeps the entering tone, and thus becomes 'the exception inside Mandarin'). **② The splits and mergers of several sets of rhyme categories** (**differences in the reading of the two rhyme classes Zeng and Geng, and of Shen and Liu, for example, are often used to cut the Central Plains off from Ji–Lu**). **Received account:** **these splits and mergers are all rows in that table of the previous chapter;** **change the batch of sites, and the same table can be redrawn.** **③ Migration history** (**the wide distribution of Southwestern Mandarin is the result of two great migrations, in the Ming and the Qing** (**received view: in the early Ming, population from the Nanjing area was moved into the various *weisuo* (卫所, garrison colonies) of Yunnan and Guizhou; in the early Qing, '湖广填四川' ('Huguang filled Sichuan')**). **These two events explain why the accents of cities a thousand *li* apart are similar, while the accents of their countryside differ from one another** (the length of the *li* changed from period to period). **This lesson calls this the enclave effect of dialects** (**cities speak migrant speech, the countryside speaks the old speech;** **and so, inside the Mandarin area, a peculiar two-layer pattern appears:** **the administrative network is one accent, the agricultural hinterland is another**). **The difference between city and countryside in the Wu of Chapter 4 is played out again here, at the largest scale.**
:::

::: definition How the homogeneity was levelled flat {#def-junzhi}
**Three mechanisms.** **① Post roads and canals** (**a form of speech spreads along the lines of transport, and the result is a belt-shaped distribution rather than a block-shaped one:** **that great stretch of 'Southwestern Mandarin' on the map is in fact the thickened projection of a few waterways and post roads**). **② Garrisons and military farm-colonies** (**received account: the Ming *weisuo* system settled northern population, in batches, in places across the south, forming dialect islands and varieties of the *junhua* (军话, 'military speech') kind.** **Sites of this kind will be met again in Chapters 7 and 10 of this course; they are the ugliest blotches on a map of the divisions —** **and an ugly blotch is precisely historical evidence:** **a village that stands alone speaking something close to northern speech is the stub of a migration that can be checked in the archives**). **③ The civil examinations and officialdom** (**the word 官话 (*guānhuà*) itself means the spoken language used among officials.** **Received account: in the Ming and the Qing, '官话' was the name for a cross-regional common spoken language, and officials had to be able to communicate with one another in official business.** **This lesson stresses its sociological meaning.** **Mandarin was first a spoken lingua franca of administration and commerce, and only two centuries later was it made into phonological tables and examinations.** **Chapter 7 will give this timeline;** **record here, first, this chapter's verdict:** **a common language is never produced by being laid down; it is first used, and afterwards recognised after the fact as a standard;** **what the document of that recognition does is make people believe that it had always existed.** **This sentence is to be read straight on into Chapter 8's 'a standard language is infrastructure'.**
:::

::: definition Why its internal differences are small {#def-chai}
**One fact and two explanations.** **The Mandarin area spans an extremely large geographical range, and yet the degree of mutual comprehension is high; this is rare anywhere in the world.** **① The strata are comparatively recent** (**received account: most of the internal divergences of the northern dialects arose after late Middle Chinese, and the time of differentiation has been short.** **Note that this point is hard to separate from 'levelling by migration' —** **they are two ways of saying the same thing:** **every great migration reset the clock of divergence**). **② Contact and reunification** (**repeated warfare, migration and administrative integration have continually wiped the differences away;** **in dialectology an area of this kind is called a 'levelling' area: features are pulled flat over a wide range**). **Received account.** **③ There are also exceptions, and the exceptions are the most valuable** (**the southwest of Shanxi, the Jiang–Huai line, and a number of garrison dialect islands are all 'non-homogeneous patches' inside Mandarin**). **This lesson offers one suggestion for research:** **in studying the Mandarin dialects, do not go first to the centre; go to the edges:** **the edges have kept the strata from before the flattening, and the edges are precisely the places under dispute in the classification.** **This is this chapter's concrete use of the sentence 'a dispute is data'.**
:::

::: example One word across the Mandarin area {#ex-ci}
For the state of affairs '完蛋了、没有了' ('done for', 'gone'), the ways of saying it differ greatly from one Mandarin site to another. Please investigate, or conjecture, the forms at the following places: **Beijing, Jinan, Xi'an, Chengdu, Nanjing, Yinchuan**; then answer: **why the differences in words of this kind are much greater than the differences in words of the kind 山, 水 and 人 ('mountain', 'water', 'person')**.

::: solution
**A prompt for the investigation.** **Words of this kind are basic colloquial words, and they are common in dialect dictionaries and in local corpora;** **differences of form may include a different verb, a different negation, and a different aspect.** **The point does not lie in a standard answer, but in the explanation.** **Why the differences are large.** **First,** **words for basic actions and states are used at a high frequency in daily life, and they change quickly** (**high-frequency words are the zone where forms wear away**). **② Second,** **words of this kind are not in the written language, and so they have no orthographic anchor** (**once a character has been written into textbooks and examinations, its pronunciation has a guardian;** **spoken words do not**). **Course 2's 'the script is the night watchman of the standard' here receives a corollary from the other side.** **③ Third,** **words of this kind are the easiest to borrow from one another and then to replace, because they carry a regional identity** (**people will deliberately say the local form in order to show who they are**). **The item 'stance' in Chapter 8.** **Marking:** **to be able to write the point 'there is no written anchor' counts as an A.** **It explains, at the same time, a larger phenomenon:** **the divergence, among the Chinese dialects, in grammar and in common words runs much deeper than the divergence in vocabulary in the language of documents;** **so, when you read dialect materials, whenever you meet a character borrowed casually to record a sound, that is precisely evidence that there is no standard —** **and that casualness is exactly the chronological layer this chapter is looking for.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Which line your accent is on {#int-ni}
**An exercise you can put to yourself.** **Run your own accent, or an accent in your family, once through the four features of this chapter:** **what entering-tone characters are pronounced as, whether fully voiced characters are aspirated, whether rhotacisation is used, and how the three words 给, 看 and 拿 ('give', 'look', 'take') are said.** **After the four questions you will know two rather unpleasant things.** **First, your accent is not 'Putonghua (standard Mandarin) plus impurities', but a line that has a source; second, what you took for your own mistake may be a consistent feature of a whole local area.** **This lesson's explanation of the second:** **what the school teaches is an abstract standard, and what is passed on at home is a feature of a local area;** **when the two conflict, the side recorded as 'wrong' is always the latter.** **This is why people who study dialects often go through a small shame and then a relief:** **the shame is because it turns out that it was not a defect, and the relief is because it has a name, it has a map, and several tens of millions of other people speak it.** **I write this here because the readers of this course are, for the most part, standing just between the two.** **The line from Course 1, 'to describe is not to approve', here becomes a sentence that applies to a person:** **describe first, and then judge — of others, and of yourself.**
:::

::: history Three renamings of a lingua franca {#sec-gai}
**After Middle Chinese.** **Northern speech grew outside the system of the rhyme dictionaries;** **received account: at the Song–Yuan transition, northern pronunciation already had a number of Mandarin features, and the *zhugongdiao* (诸宫调, 'medley in all modes') and Yuan *qu* used it.** **Books of the *Zhongyuan yinyun* (中原音韵, *Rhymes of the Central Plain*) kind kept a ledger for it.** **The history of Chinese, in Chapter 3, will develop this.** **② The Ming and the Qing.** **'官话' became the general name for cross-regional speech;** **it was the lingua franca of administration and commerce, the Mandarin spoken in each place differed from the others, and there was not one person who spoke '标准官话' ('standard Mandarin').** **Received account:** **this lesson calls this stretch 'the period of the nameless standard':** **there was a direction, and there was no centre.** **③ 1913／1932.** **A commission settled the national pronunciation (*guoyin*), character by character, and after the failure changed to taking the Beijing pronunciation as the standard.** **Received account.** **④ 1955／1956.** **The common language was defined as taking the Beijing pronunciation for its standard pronunciation, northern speech for its base dialect, and model writings in the modern vernacular for its grammatical norm;** **Mandarin, as a dialect area, from then on shared a name with a national language and not the substance.** **This lesson's reminder:** **this is the largest reorganisation of name and substance in the history of the Chinese language:** **formerly, to say '官话' was to name a tool; now, to say 'Putonghua' is to name an identity.** **The account metaphor of Chapter 8 here acquires a subject.** **⑤ The 1980s to the present.** **Dialect maps have been drawn finer, and the internal differences of Southwestern Mandarin have been described again; at the same time a rising rate of popularisation has drawn the features of local areas towards the standard.** **Received account:** **a process that is under way, which this lesson calls 're-levelling':** **the force of this flattening is not migration but schools and screens.** **Every word you are saying at this moment is inside it —** **this is not a lament; it is an observation: a homogeneity is forming, and its price is that local features become a personal taste.**
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of this chapter
**Calling Mandarin 'the simplest Chinese'** (**it is only that the complexity has changed its place** (features ① and ② above)); **taking a wide homogeneity for an absence of history** (**homogeneity is usually a product of migration and administration** (the mechanisms above)); **ignoring enclaves and dialect islands** (**they are the stubs of migration, and they are also the bones of the division** (mechanism ② above: **study the edges, not the centre**)); **explaining Mandarin by way of Putonghua** (**Putonghua is the standard recognised after the fact, and Mandarin is the lingua franca in use;** **the two stand several centuries apart** (history ③ and ④ above)). **This is the question in this chapter that is easiest to get wrong.**
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the politics of homogeneity {level=3}
Write six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing '**the internal uniformity of a large speech area is usually not a natural state, but the result of repeated levelling by transport, garrisons, markets and administration; and precisely because of this, the boundary of a speech area is often a fossil of an old empire's administrative boundary. When the force that does the levelling is changed for schools and screens, the boundary of the speech area stops moving, and turns instead into a boundary of attitude: people begin to divide one another by accent, and not by comprehension**'. You must call on: **the two diagnostic features**, **the division into eight blocks**, **the enclave effect**, **the period of the nameless standard**, **re-levelling**, and **the edges keep the strata** — at least six items; and you must rule: **whether the levelling is reversible (if it is not, what dialectology is doing — you must give an answer that does not take nostalgia as its reason)**.

::: solution
Points for the mark: the six mechanisms; the ruling (**do not answer with a circularity of the kind 'protecting the heritage is itself the reason';** **candidates for an answer that passes:** ① **scientific use** (**the differences among dialects are material for the study of language change and of human migration; homogenisation amounts to destroying the laboratory —** **this is the only strong argument that does not rest on emotion** (**this lesson favours this one the most**)); ② **functional redundancy** (**one variety carries functions that another does not:** **an intimate register, jargon, local arts, and a set of names for fine-grained feeling;** **their loss cannot be made up by the standard language**); ③ **the irreversibility can itself be questioned** (**in a number of areas, a revival of pride in accent appeared after the 1990s** (**received account**), which shows that 'levelling' is not one-directional, and that the variable of attitude can reverse (**this item keeps the answer from falling into determinism**)); if the conclusion can be written '**the modern reason for dialectology is not to preserve the past, but to maintain a kind of comparability: a world with only one accent will still have change, but it will no longer keep records that can be checked; we record so that those who come later have an original base ledger awaiting them — this is the reason of the study of documents, not the reason of nostalgia**', that is an A.
:::

::: summary
The Mandarin file: features (**the entering tone distributed into tones, the two paths of aspiration from the voiced initials, a complex prosody**), the division (**beneath the eight blocks it is, in every case, the same table of splits and mergers**), causes (**roads, the *weisuo*, two great migrations**), the double layer (**cities speak migrant speech, the countryside speaks the old speech**), the present (**re-levelling, carried through by screens**). One sentence of method: **to see the history of a speech area, go to its edges; do not go to its centre.** Next chapter: Wu — voiced initials, tone sandhi in connected speech, and a city that grinds flat the countryside around it.
