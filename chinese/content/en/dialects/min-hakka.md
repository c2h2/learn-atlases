---
title: "Min and Hakka"
---

The dialects of the previous two chapters each have one signature feature; the two kinds of speech in this chapter each have something more troublesome — **Min carries, in one and the same person's mouth, the pronunciations of several eras, while Hakka writes into the dialect map a migration story that a population tells to itself**.

## Definition: literary and colloquial readings {#def-wenbai}

::: definition Two household registrations for one character {#def-liangdu}
**Received-account thread.** **Min is noted for how fully developed its literary and colloquial readings are:** one and the same character often has a reading pronunciation and a spoken pronunciation, and the correspondence between the two sets of readings is systematic rather than occasional. **The crux is the three characters 成系统 ('forming a system').** If it is only that a few characters have two readings, that is an exception; **yet in Min the divide between the two readings can be described in whole batches, by classes of finals and by classes of initials, and this shows that** there were once two routes of input here. **The first is the natural transmission of the local spoken language; the second is pronunciations learned from the system of reading aloud and from a more recent common language.** **This lesson offers a more usable way of putting it:** the literary reading is an official standard from outside, absorbed generation by generation; the colloquial reading is the local way of saying the word that was already there before that standard arrived — **and so the sound system of Min is like what lies under an old city:** each layer corresponds to the common language of one era and to one kind of institution. **The "stacking of layers" (层次叠加) of Chapter 1 acquires, in this chapter, a concrete stratigraphy.** And its verifiability is extremely high: **the same character is pronounced differently in 学生 ('student') and in 教书 ('to teach'), and these two places are not allotted at random.** What the law of distribution in the next section has to deal with is precisely this.
:::

::: definition The colloquial reading is the earlier layer {#def-baidus}
**Received-account thread.** **A number of colloquial-reading layers in Min carry features held to be older. Examples often cited include phenomena of the kind in which a batch of characters with the ancient *fei* initial (非母) are read as bilabial stops.** This phenomenon is cited again and again under theses of the kind put forward by scholars of the Qing, “古无轻唇音” ('in antiquity there were no light-labial initials'). **This lesson handles the matter in three layers; this is the method of layering sources which this curriculum uses from beginning to end.** ① Verifiable layer. At a number of Min points such readings can indeed be recorded, and they appear in batches. ② Inferential layer. It shows that the colloquial-reading layer is earlier than some split of initials. **This step requires contact and borrowing to be ruled out, and the ruling-out cannot always be done. ③ Legendary layer.** “闽语保存了唐宋古音” — Min has preserved the ancient pronunciation of the Tang and the Song. (Our translation.) “衣冠入闽带来某朝洛阳音” — The entry of the gentry into Min brought the Luoyang pronunciation of one dynasty or another. (Our translation.) **Whole sets of formulations of this kind set linguistic layers and the migrations of populations in one-to-one correspondence, and correspondences of this kind are almost never verifiable.** This lesson's verdict: **the third layer has the most life in popular writing, and is also the one most often used to advertise an identity.** Your job is not to mock it, but to write down which sentence belongs to which layer — **the thing this course practises in the literature course is, here, the same craft.**
:::

::: definition Diglossia: one speech, two registers {#def-shuangyan}
**Received-account thread.** **In sociology there is a concept called diglossia (双言): within one language a high variety and a low variety each govern a set of occasions, and a speaker will not mix them on the same occasion.** This lesson holds that Min is the case, within Chinese, closest to this model. **① The law of distribution.** **The literary reading governs what is written, formal, abstract, and new words; the colloquial reading governs the body, food, kin, and everyday actions.** Received-account thread: **Second.** **A breach of the distribution will be heard:** to read a compound of Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) with a character's colloquial reading at once makes the speaker's level of education look suspect. **This judgeability on the spot shows that the boundary between the two layers is not a problem of the lexicon but a problem of register.** Third. **Both layers are shrinking, but they shrink at different speeds:** the literary-reading layer is replaced as it draws towards Putonghua (standard Mandarin) and towards the written language, while the colloquial-reading layer disappears because everyday speech shifts to Putonghua — **the result is that the two systems most characteristic of Min are stripped out at the same time, and what replaces them is a Putonghua with a Min accent.** This is the definition this chapter gives of "language shift" (语言转用): **not that one speech has been exchanged for another, but that two systems have been exchanged for one system plus a layer of accent.** Chapter 9 will take this sentence as the skeleton of the whole chapter.
:::

::: definition Five subgroups and one sea {#def-wupian}
**Received-account thread.** **Min is often divided into a number of subgroups (片), such as Southern Min, Eastern Min, Northern Min, Central Min and Puxian, and mutual intelligibility between the subgroups is low.** Three consequences. **① "Min", as a unit, rests on shared innovations and on shared layers, not on mutual intelligibility within it.** **This is exactly what the previous chapter meant by “分合平行” ('parallelism of splits and mergers') rather than “像不像” ('whether they look alike').** ② Crossing the sea means dividing the household. Southern Min went with the migrants into Taiwan and into the Nanyang. **A number of accents already different from one another inside Fujian itself became, outside the territory, the languages of different communities.** Received-account thread: **This lesson offers a comparison:** Min is the branch, among the Chinese dialects, that went "transnational" earliest, and its going transnational was a hundred years earlier than Putonghua's — **this is not an honour; it is a record of Min as a way for a poor coastal population to make a living.** ③ The Min of Hainan and Leizhou. Min that went south with garrison troops and with migrants formed a large dialect area on Hainan. **On the island there are, besides, a number of varieties called *junhua* (“军话”, 'military speech'), and the legends of how they formed have to do with the Ming *weisuo* (卫所).** Received-account thread: **Note that the mirror image of the previous chapter appears here:** areas of Mandarin (*guanhua*) were carried by migrants into the south, and Min areas were carried by migrants into the tropics. **Dialect islands and "exclaves" (飞地) are two sides of one and the same coin; the difference is only who keeps the accounts.**
:::

::: definition Hakka: a self-designated speech area {#def-kejie}
**Received-account thread.** **Hakka is distributed across the mountains and hills of Guangdong, Fujian, Jiangxi, Guangxi and elsewhere; the internal differences are not small, and yet the sense of identity is strong.** Three lines. **① The name.** **“客” ('guest') is a category on the register, set over against “土” ('native').** In the conflicts between natives and migrants in the southeast during the Qing, this designation was fixed, and after the conflicts of the mid-nineteenth century it entered public language. **Received-account thread:** this lesson's first-layer judgement: **an exonym (他称, 'a name given by others') produced by tax and corvée and by household registration later became a self-designation (自称), and later still the name of a division in scholarship —** this path is not rare in the dialect geography of the world, and on the Hakka it is the clearest. ② **The narrative.** **In the 1930s a work of research integrated scattered genealogies, legends of migration and the distribution of the dialect into one systematic narrative of migration.** Received-account thread: **This lesson does not judge whether this narrative is true or false in its details, and only points out its function:** it enables a language group to answer "who we are", and it makes later dialectological division bear a pressure which that division cannot refuse. **Please read this sentence again:** when a population insists that it is "one kind of person", linguistics can hardly, on the strength of the splits and mergers of sound classes alone, say that it is not. ③ **The Hakka–Gan dispute.** **On the relation between Hakka and Gan there have long been two views, "combine them into one group" and "let each form its own group".** Received-account thread: **This lesson's way of reading it:** each of the two views has evidence from sound classes, and the real focus of the disagreement is "whether one history of migration is to be taken as a ground of classification" — **the previous chapter said that the weight given in a division is often decided by migration history; this chapter is the best specimen of that claim.**
:::

::: example Which layer to use {#ex-ceng}
Below are several kinds of word. At a Min point which preserves literary and colloquial readings, judge which kinds are more likely to take the colloquial reading, which are more likely to take the literary reading, and state your grounds: **① basic words of number and of direction; ② abstract nouns of the modern period; ③ kin terms; ④ names of institutions and official posts; ⑤ everyday actions and food; ⑥ newly coined written compounds.** (　)

::: solution
**Colloquial-reading group** — **①③⑤.** **Number, direction, kin, the body, food and high-frequency actions belong to the domestic register; they are precisely the territory of the layer transmitted by speech.** Received-account thread. ② Literary-reading group — ②④⑥. Abstract nouns, official titles and institutions, and new-style written compounds entered with the reading system and with the common language of the modern period, and therefore carry the pronunciation that came in with them. **The ground is not the meaning of the word itself, but the institutional occasion on which this word was first spoken:** what is said at home uses the colloquial reading; what is said in the school, the yamen and the newspaper uses the literary reading. **This lesson presses one step further, and this question is the core of the chapter.** "On the ground of the institutional occasion" means that the literary and colloquial layers are not a phonetic phenomenon but the fossil of a social phenomenon; **so when you record someone using the literary reading for one word and the colloquial reading for another, while both words belong to food, you should suspect: ① this word is a borrowing of later origin; ② the speaker has changed the habit under the influence of schooling; ③ or he is in the middle of merging the two layers.** The three explanations correspond to three actions in a survey: **check the etymology, ask age and education, and then look at his consistency within one and the same stretch of speech.** For the mark: **whoever can list these three suspicions scores an A** — **what this chapter has ever taught is not "what literary and colloquial readings are", but** "when you find a crack in a system, which explanations you have in hand, and what evidence each of them requires". **This sentence applies to every course in this curriculum.**
:::

::: intuition *Qiaopi*: the balance sheet of a speech {#def-qiaopi}
**Received-account thread.** **People of southern Fujian and eastern Guangdong in the modern period who went abroad to make a living regularly sent home a document that "joins a letter and a remittance slip in one", commonly called *qiaopi* (侨批);** a number of the related archives have been entered on the international register of memory heritage. **This lesson takes *qiaopi* as the close of the chapter, because it is at once three faces of one thing.** ① It is the practical use of a speech. The remittance letters are written in the spoken dialect, and they contain forms of address, amounts, promises and complaints. **Here this speech is not heritage; it is a contract.** ② It records institutions outside language. **Remittance offices, couriers, native banks and rates of exchange: a dialect's ability to survive often depends on whether there is a trade that keeps its books in that dialect.** This is the most solid paying-out of the economic metaphor used in this curriculum. ③ It explains a kind of shift. When a remittance can be sent by mobile phone, and a contract must be written in the common language, *qiaopi* disappears, and the dialect's use in writing is withdrawn with it. **The line in Chapter 5, “先撤销书写用途，然后才无人会说” — First the use in writing is withdrawn, and only then does no one remain who can speak it. (Our translation.) — here acquires an archive.** This lesson requires you to read this section together with the previous two chapters: **the writtenness of Yue comes from the entertainment industry, and the writtenness of Min comes from the remittance industry.** Both industries gave up a speech earlier than the schools did — **so the conclusion of this chapter is a cold one:** the fate of a dialect does not lie in the classroom and the radio, but in whether it can appear on a document that other people also have to read.
:::

::: history Legend, contract and the textbook {#sec-min}
**Early.** **The dialect layers of the Min region are almost without any direct record in the documents.** Divisions made later can only be inferred indirectly, from reconstruction and from documents. **Received-account thread:** this lesson marks every formulation of the type “某朝移民带来某音” — Migrants of such-and-such a dynasty brought such-and-such a pronunciation. (Our translation.) — as the legendary layer, and requires the grounds to be written out. ② **Song and Yuan.** **The civil-service examinations and the community schools brought reading pronunciations into the lineage society of the coast.** Received-account thread: **this lesson holds that the crux of the split into literary and colloquial layers is not which dynasty's standard pronunciation it was, but that "book study" (读书) became an institution:** only where there is a teacher of a private school is there a literary reading. ③ **Ming and Qing.** **The maritime ban, the coastal evacuation and seagoing trade repeatedly changed the structure of the coastal population.** Hakka groups took shape in the mountains along the border of Jiangxi, Fujian and Guangdong, and the designation hardened in the conflicts between 土 and 客 in the southeast during the Qing. **Received-account thread.** ④ **The mid-nineteenth century.** Conflict along the southeastern coast and going away to make a living reached a peak at the same time. **The churches and the colonies devised romanisations for a number of coastal accents, and Southern Min became one of the earliest Chinese dialects to have printed reading matter on a large scale.** Received-account thread: **what this lesson insists on is this:** once a script that spells sounds is circulating in a community, it is harder to abolish than any policy. ⑤ **1933.** **A work of the *Kejia yanjiu daolun* 《客家研究导论》 ('An introduction to Hakka studies') type integrated the narrative of migration.** Received-account thread. ⑥ **The 1980s to the present.** **Linguistic links between Fujian and Taiwan, and Southeast Asia, became an object of research; in the Taiwan region, after the lifting of martial law, Southern Min entered mother-tongue courses in the schools; at the same time *qiaopi* archives were entered on the international register of memory, and the project for the protection of language resources set up sites densely along the coast.** Received-account thread. **Closing words of this chapter:** a speech raised by seafaring and by remittances now depends on courses and museums to commemorate it — **this is the sentence this chapter is most unwilling to write down, and it is also the sentence for which the evidence is fullest.**
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**“闽语保存唐宋古音” — Min preserves the ancient pronunciation of the Tang and the Song. (Our translation.)** **It has to be taken in layers: what is verifiable is correspondence in batches; what is inferred is the date; what is legend is the origin of the group** (see above, the three layers of the colloquial reading). **Treating literary and colloquial readings as free variants.** **Mixing them at once gives away age and education** (see above, diglossia, ②). **Measuring the unity of Min by mutual intelligibility.** **It holds by shared innovations and by layers** (see above, the five subgroups, ①). **Treating Hakka as a unit of bloodline.** **It is a process in which a category of household registration became a self-designation and then the name of a division** (see above, Hakka, ① and ②): **this is the hardest item in the chapter, and the most valuable.**
:::

::: exercise Short essay: self-designation and splits and mergers {level=3}
Write from six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing "**Dialect division claims to recognise only the splits and mergers of sound classes, but it has never been able to deal with one variable: what speakers insist that they are. The case of the Hakka is the clearest — a category produced by household registration and by conflict has, on the strength of genealogical records and a narrative of migration, lived through a hundred years of scrutiny by linguistics, and in the end has forced the map to draw a piece for it. Linguistics can take two attitudes to this: strip it out as noise, or admit that the units by which language is classified have all along been decided jointly by populations and by institutions. The latter is the more uncomfortable, because it requires the scholar to say clearly on whose behalf the line is being drawn.**" You must call on at least six items: **systematic correspondence**, **the three layers of the colloquial reading**, **diglossic distribution**, **the five subgroups and crossing the sea**, ***junhua* and exclaves**, ***qiaopi***; and give a ruling: **if you were in charge of compiling a dialect atlas, would you list "self-designation" (自称) as one of the grounds of the division? (State clearly the consequences you take on.)**

::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms; the ruling (**either of the two positions is acceptable, but the cost must be written out:** the cost of listing it as a ground. The map will split into a number of small units that cannot be compared with one another, and will give identity politics a scientific seal; **the most serious version is this: once scholars endorse some one self-designation, the groups that oppose it will take themselves to have been excluded by science.** This sentence is the point of the question. **The cost of not listing it as a ground.** The map will systematically underestimate the real social units of language, and every time it is applied to policy it will be accused of "not understanding the actual situation"; and policy is precisely the buyer of the map. This is what the chapter says repeatedly: "use decides the division".) **The third way, in an answer that passes:** set two layers side by side on the map: **one page for the splits and mergers of sound classes, one page for self-designation and administrative affiliation, and write in the notes where the two do not agree.** The highest mark on the paper goes to schemes of this kind, because they leave the disagreement where it can be seen, instead of burying it with a single line — of the same construction as the previous chapter's "classification should leave a seam" and "on the map one should write 'undecided'". If the conclusion can be written, "So the lesson of this chapter is not that linguistics is unscientific, but that its object refuses to be only sound; a dialect is at once a geography of change and the name of a group of people, and a map can draw the former, and can only label the latter", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
Two layers and two groups: literary and colloquial readings (**only a systematic correspondence is a layer; the occasion decides the reading**); the method of layering (**the verifiable, the inferential and the legendary each go to their own place**); Min (**the subgroups do not understand one another, and the group is formed by shared innovations; to cross the sea is to divide the household**); Hakka (**an exonym of household registration → a self-designation → the name of a division; the focus of the Hakka–Gan dispute is whether migration history counts as a ground**); *qiaopi* (**the fate is written on the documents**). Next chapter: Xiang, Gan, Hui and Pinghua — four places "lying between the two", and whether a transitional belt can become a group.
