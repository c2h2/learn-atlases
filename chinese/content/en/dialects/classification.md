title: "Classifying the dialects"
---

The previous chapter showed that the three rulers fight one another; this chapter gives the one this craft has actually used — **classification does not look at whether two kinds of speech 'look alike', but at whether the splits and mergers of their sound categories run in parallel; that is, what dialectology compares is not a word list, but the chart of accounts**.

## Definition: three methods of classification {#def-fenzoning}

::: definition Genealogy, typology and area {#def-sanfa}
**① Genealogical** (it asks 'which one split off first'; it gives a tree). **② Typological** (it asks 'whether the features are the same'; it gives a feature table). **③ Areal** (it asks 'which features turn up together in the same stretch of country'; it gives a bundle of isoglosses). **Received account.** The mainstream practice for Chinese dialects lies between the second and the third, and its concrete tool is the comparison of splits and mergers of sound categories: **one does not compare 'how do you read this character', but compares whether 'this class of characters and that class of characters are split or merged where you are'**. **Why use this tool? This chapter gives three reasons.** First, similarity of pronunciation may be coincidence or borrowing, whereas **a whole set of splits and mergers is unlikely to come out the same by chance**. Second, relations of split and merger can be audited, character by character, across dialects, and **they can be checked again**. Third, the degree to which one split or merger has been completed itself records the time of the change (**this one joins on to 'two degrees of completion under a common origin'**). **One sentence to remember:** words are the weather; sound categories are the climate of splits and mergers.
:::

::: definition Why the sound system rather than the vocabulary {#def-yinlei}
**The problem with vocabulary is that it can be borrowed.** A word can travel the whole of East Asia without any relationship of kinship at all (the loanword ledger in Chapter 9). Grammar and sound categories are relatively stable, while word lists move, so similarity of vocabulary cannot prove a common origin; at most it proves contact. ② **The split and merger of sound categories is a kind of 'negative evidence'.** Two dialects make the same merger on one and the same batch of characters, and keep a distinction on one and the same batch of characters: that consistency is hard to explain by borrowing, because loanwords do not neatly fill only the one cell that is left after the merger. ③ **Received account:** taking the splits and mergers of sound categories, rather than similarity of pronunciation, as the basis of classification was the methodological consensus of dialectology in the late twentieth century. **This lesson adds one restriction, a restriction often ignored in examinations.** What the splits and mergers of sound categories prove is that 'they went through the same change'; they cannot prove that 'they did not part afterwards'. **So every block on a classification map is the range over which a set of changes has been completed, not an inheritance from a common ancestor.** **This is the claim (thesis) of this chapter:** a dialect map is a geographical map of change, not a family tree.
:::

::: definition Seven groups, ten groups, and redrawing the lines {#def-shiqu}
**Received account.** The national dialect survey of the 1950s was accompanied by a seven-group draft: Mandarin (*guanhua*), Wu, Xiang, Gan, Hakka, Min and Yue. A language atlas of the 1980s changed this to ten groups: **Jin, Hui and Pinghua were added.** The second edition moved a number of lines again. **This lesson reads that revision item by item.** **① Jin is split off from Mandarin.** The received account attributes this to a proposal by one scholar of Chinese historical phonology, and the criterion is so simple as to be almost wilful: **the dialects of this belt keep the entering tone (checked tone).** And one of the defining features of Mandarin is precisely that the entering tone has disappeared. What one such revision shows: a single feature is enough to change one piece of the map, so long as that feature is chosen as a defining term. ② **Hui stands on its own.** This is the most disputed of the groups. **It lies between Wu and Gan; its features are in part like Wu, in part like Gan, and it has besides some innovations of its own.** Opponents say it is a transition zone rather than an independent unit. **This lesson's reading:** whether a transition zone is a 'group' depends on what the classification is for — **for compiling a dictionary, a transition zone deserves a page to itself;** **for drawing a policy map, someone will wipe it off.** ③ **Where Pinghua belongs.** Several varieties in Guangxi; their relations to Yue, to Mandarin, and to one another have long been unsettled. **This is a case not yet closed, and it is exactly what this chapter wants you to look at:** while a classification is still moving, you can see its bones. **Received account:** on the relation of Hakka and Gan there are likewise two views, 'Hakka and Gan as one' and 'each forming its own group'. **The point on which the two views diverge does not lie in the facts, but in 'which migration ought to be treated as the separating event' —** a historical hypothesis enters the criteria of classification directly. This is what this chapter calls the dispute over weighting.
:::

::: definition The internal classification of Mandarin {#def-guanhua}
**Received account.** Mandarin is often divided again into several large groups. A common way of putting it is eight blocks: Northeastern, Beijing, Ji–Lu, Zhongyuan, Lan–Yin, Southwestern, Jianghuai and Jiao–Liao. **The main clue for the classification is which tone the entering-tone characters are assigned to.** The same Middle Chinese source merges into the departing tone in area A and into the second (rising) tone in area B: that is hard evidence that the splits and mergers do not run in parallel. **Received account:** Southwestern Mandarin covers the middle and upper reaches of the Yangtze, and Yunnan and Guizhou, and is the result of migration in the modern period. **Jianghuai Mandarin has two features at once: a residue of the entering tone, and a number of rhyme categories that differ from the north.** **It is 'the southernmost block inside Mandarin', and it is the front line of contact between Mandarin and Wu, Gan and Hui.** **This lesson sets a map-reading exercise.** Look at a map of the Mandarin classification and ask three questions. **① Are provincial boundaries respected again and again?** If the line always coincides with a provincial boundary, suspect that the classification has been influenced by administrative materials. ② Does the density of the lines equal the density of the railways and the rivers? ③ Large areas with no difference inside them: is the survey insufficient, or are they truly homogeneous? **The answer to the third question is often that the survey was insufficient —** please use this rule of suspicion from this lesson whenever you look at any map. **The resolution of the map equals the resolution of the funding. This is the only sentence of economics in this chapter.**
:::

::: example Classifying by splits and mergers {#ex-fenhe}
The table below gives the way six survey points treat three sets of character classes ('split' means that the two classes of characters differ in pronunciation; 'merged' means that they are the same):

| Point | Old voiced-obstruent level tone vs departing tone | Entering tone independent? | Shan rhyme class (*she*), open (unrounded) divisions (grades) I and II |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | split | independent | split |
| B | split | independent | split |
| C | split | assigned to the other three tones | merged |
| D | merged | assigned to the other three tones | merged |
| E | merged | assigned to the other three tones | merged |
| F | split | assigned to the other three tones | split |

Classify by 'parallel splits and mergers', and say how your classification might differ from one made by 'similarity to the ear'.

::: solution
**Step one: choose the defining terms.** Of the three items, independence of the entering tone has the most power to discriminate, because it is **the degree of completion of a single change**, whereas the other two may be gradual typological differences. **Hence: A and B form a group of their own** (**entering tone independent**); C, D, E and F form one group. ② **The second layer.** Inside the large groups, use the other two items again: **D and E (both items merged)**, and **C and F (the items do not agree; they are transitional)**. **F is especially awkward: the entering tone has already been assigned elsewhere, while the other tones are still the same as in A and B —** this is the way of reading that this chapter wants to teach: F is not a 'mixed dialect'; it is three changes passing through the same place at different speeds. **The lexical diffusion of Chapter 9 is directly visible here:** a change is not completed all at once, so the number of boundaries on the map depends on which changes you have chosen. ③ **How this differs from similarity.** If one classifies by 'sounding alike', F might be assigned to A and B, because its tonal pattern sounds close; **whereas parallel splits and mergers place it on the side of the large group: it has already completed the loss of the entering tone, the most important entry in the ledger.** **How this lesson would have the conclusion written.** **Any scheme of classification should state three things:** which splits and mergers of sound categories were used, what weight each one carries, and which points, because they fall in the transition zone, will object no matter how the line is drawn. **The third is this chapter's honesty clause:** a classification report that does not write down the objections is not to be trusted.
:::
:::

::: intuition One county, three classifications {#int-san}
**The phenomenon.** The dialect of one and the same place is put in different groups in different works; the speakers themselves say 'everyone can understand the speech here'. **Three explanations.** **① The feature tables differ.** The list each scholar brings is different: **some look at the entering tone, some at voiced sounds, some at splits and mergers of rhyme classes.** ② **The weights differ.** On one and the same feature table, the weights can still be political: **if one takes 'explainable by a history of migration' as a weight, then a group will be merged or split apart because of a migration legend.** ③ **The density of the points.** A dialect map is made by interpolating from a limited number of survey points; **the line drawn between two thinly covered areas is in fact the mark 'nobody surveyed here'.** **This lesson gives a rule for reading a paper:** look first at where its points are, and then at where the lines are; when a line crosses a blank area, all it expresses is the author's hesitation. **These three hold in linguistics, and they hold for any classification in the social sciences as well.** **That sentence from the logic course, 'classification is sovereignty', receives a technical version here:** the way sovereignty is exercised is by choosing the feature table.
:::

::: history Three redrawings in sixty years {#sec-gai}
**1930s–1940s.** Modern dialect survey gets under way, and the points are concentrated in the east and along the river. **Received account:** **this lesson's first reminder:** the western outline of maps of Chinese dialects was for a long time extrapolated. ② **1955–1956.** In step with the promotion of the common language and with script reform, the national dialect survey was launched and a seven-group draft was proposed. **Received account:** the fact that the classification and the policy fell in the same year is not a defect but the context: **the survey teams took on two tasks at once, 'to get a clear picture of the language situation' and 'to assess the difficulty of promoting the common language'.** ③ **1960.** An outline of the dialects was written as a textbook on the seven-group framework, and it shaped the classificatory intuitions of several generations. **Received account.** ④ **1980s.** Discussion that took the splits and mergers of sound categories as its standard pushed forward the proposal of Jin and of Hui; on the relation of Hakka and Gan there is, besides, a view that unites them in one group. **Received account.** ⑤ **1987/2012.** The first edition of the language atlas fixed ten groups; the second edition redrew the lines. **Received account:** the redrawing of the lines is itself the best teaching material: **set the two editions side by side, and you will see which points were filled in by further survey in between.** ⑥ **From 2015.** The project for the protection of language resources greatly increased the number of survey points. **This lesson's prediction** (**and it is a sentence that can be tested**): once the points are dense to a certain degree, the 'groups' we have now will all fail, and what takes their place will be independent distribution maps of a number of features. **This is precisely the refutable form of this chapter's claim:** if classification is a geographical map of change, then in the end it will become an animation. **A scholar of the old school would object to that sentence, and the objection has reason on its side —** because a classification people can communicate by is also knowledge.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**Classifying by whether they 'look alike'.** Similarity may be borrowing; look at splits and mergers (① above). **Treating the boundary as a fact.** On a transition zone, however you draw the line, someone will object (intuition ③ above). **Ignoring the distribution of the points.** When a line crosses a blank area, it expresses the author's hesitation (intuition ③ above: this one applies to every map and every chart). **Forgetting to ask what it is for.** Maps drawn for a dictionary, for the history of sounds, and for policy ought to differ (Use, above: this is this lesson's return to the point, in logic and in argumentative writing, that 'for a definition, look at who is signing').
:::

::: exercise Reclassifying one case {level=1}
The six points in the table above are fictitious. Using materials from the area where you are (a dictionary, a dialect survey, or an interview), make a table of three features and four to six points, and classify by splits and mergers.

::: solution
Marking: **you must write which three features you chose, and why**, and at least one line on 'how the conclusion would change if one feature were swapped' (the refutable form, above). **If you do not write the test of swapping a feature, that is a fail.**
:::

::: exercise Reading two editions of the map {level=2}
Find a language atlas (or a publicly available facsimile). Compare your home town's affiliation, and the boundaries of the classification, in the first edition and in the second; point out what changed, and conjecture how the changes relate to the points added by further survey.

::: solution
Marking: **you must distinguish 'the line was changed' from 'the name was changed'**, and write down the part you cannot confirm (intuition ③, above). **Attributing the change to one school replacing another, without mentioning a change in the data, is where this chapter deducts marks.**
:::

::: exercise A short essay: discovery or invention {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand characters, assess the following: '**Classification is an invention, not a discovery, but it is not an arbitrary invention: data can only reject a scheme; they cannot generate one. A good dialect atlas is worth believing not because it has drawn the lines correctly, but because it makes public all three steps — choosing the features, fixing the weights, and setting the points — so that an opponent can point precisely to which step they oppose. A bad classification hides its first three steps and shows people only the final line, and so it looks like nature.**' You must invoke at least six items: **the three methods of classification**, **parallel splits and mergers**, **the Jin example**, **the eight blocks of Mandarin**, **the resolution of the lines and the funding**, and **the transition zone**. And adjudicate: **whether 'classification is sovereignty' and 'classification must be refutable' conflict (if a classification is adopted by policy, what happens to its refutability)**.

::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms (**the three methods, parallelism, the Jin example, the eight blocks, resolution, the transition zone**); the adjudication (**what you write must be a mechanism, not an aphorism:** candidate: once a classification has been adopted, the cost of objecting shifts from scholarship to interests — **a scholar who objects to a scheme need only cite another feature, whereas once a group of people who have been placed in some area objects, the objection pulls on textbooks, funding and identity, so that objectors grow fewer and 'silent agreement' grows;** this is the sociological condition of refutability (**whether a classification is refutable depends on whether anyone can afford the cost of refuting it**); institutional remedies should also be given (**candidate: on the hierarchy of the classification, keep a class called 'undetermined', and require that a redrawn line be accompanied by an explanation; publish the original phonetic notes; and decouple classification from the allocation of resources, so that objecting to a classification is no longer the same as objecting to a grant of funds —** marking looks at whether these three were thought of, and not at which side you take**). If the conclusion can say '**so this chapter's conclusion is not that classification is useless, but that classification should leave a seam; one mark of a mature discipline is that its maps have "undetermined" written on them**', that is an A.
:::

::: summary
What this chapter delivers: **the tool** (**what is compared is splits and mergers, not pronunciations**), **the reasons** (**splits and mergers are hard to borrow, and their degree of completion records time**), **the history** (**from seven groups to ten, and then to redrawn lines: three times in sixty years**), **a way of reading the map** (**look at the points first, then at the lines, and last ask what they are for**), **one testable prediction** (**the denser the points, the more the groups scatter**).
