---
title: "Wu"
---

Mandarin (*guanhua*) is the continent after it has been levelled flat; Wu is the first piece of ground in this chapter that has 'not been levelled flat' — **it keeps a manner of phonation that other places have already lost, and it also keeps something more troublesome: a set of tones that exists only when one speaks in a string**.

## Definition: voiced initials {#def-ze}

::: definition Voiceless with voiced airflow {#def-qingyin}
**Received account.** **The feature of Wu most often cited is that it preserves the ancient fully voiced initials: when the north reads a batch of initials as aspirated or unaspirated voiceless sounds, many places in Wu still read them as sounds with a voiced colouring.** **And the first correction this course makes is to that claim itself.** **The 'voiced sounds' of many localities are not pure voiced stops as in English, but a kind of slack voice (弛声) called 清音浊流 ('voiceless with voiced airflow'):** during the closure the vocal folds do not vibrate, and after the release there is breathy voice. **Received account:** this lesson asks you to read the matter as a methodological lesson. **A classificatory formulation used for a hundred years can be correct in the sense of 'different from its opposite', and imprecise in the sense of 'what it is';** dialectology is full of such formulations, and the rule for handling them is: **first ask by what criterion this feature was measured, then ask at which localities it holds.** **The second correction matters more.** **The voiced sounds of the speech of the Shanghai urban area are weakening, and are even merging into the voiceless opposition.** **Received account: a fall in distinctiveness among younger speakers has been recorded again and again.** **So the first timeline of this chapter begins:** the most famous feature of Wu is disappearing in its most famous city — **and the direction of the disappearance is the same as in Mandarin.** **This is not a coincidence; it is the subject of the next section.**
:::

::: definition Tone is a property of the unit {#def-biantiao}
**The second difficulty in Wu is tone sandhi in connected speech.** **Received account: most Wu localities have seven or eight isolation tones, yet in actual speech one almost never hears the isolation tone:** when syllables form a word or a phrase, the whole string is assigned a fresh set of tone shapes. **This lesson gives it a precise formulation.** **In Wu, tone is not a property of the character, but a property of the prosodic unit.** **This sentence should be remembered together with the line from Course 3, 'brackets are a claim':** any textbook that marks the tone on the isolated character is teaching a formulation that does not exist; **what it teaches is a set of abstract oppositions, and not a speech.** **Received account.** **The two patterns of sandhi are often called first-syllable-dominant and last-syllable-dominant:** in the former the tone shape of the whole string is determined by the first syllable, in the latter by the final syllable. **Their theoretical significance has been discussed again and again among linguists, because they show that** phonological rules can operate across word boundaries, and that such a rule is not sensitive to grammatical boundaries. **This is why Wu has a special place in modern linguistic theory.** **It supplies a phonological counterpart to the line in the syntax course, 'structural ambiguity is evidence of hierarchy':** the behaviour of sandhi can be explained only by a larger unit, and that unit often does not equal any grammatical constituent.
:::

::: definition The six subgroups and the differences among them {#def-liupian}
**Received account.** **Wu is usually divided into a number of subgroups; the common statement puts them at six: the Taihu subgroup around Lake Tai, Taizhou, Oujiang, Shangli, Chuqu and Xuanzhou.** **Three readings.** **① The Taihu subgroup is the centre, and the rest are the periphery.** **And the periphery keeps features the centre does not have:** the splits and mergers of rhyme categories in the Oujiang and Chuqu subgroups, their vocabulary and their types of sandhi are all far from the Taihu subgroup. **The line from the previous chapter, 'study the periphery, not the centre', can be used here at once.** ② **Mutual intelligibility inside the group is lower than outsiders imagine.** **Within the Taihu subgroup one can still, just barely, understand and be understood; between the Oujiang subgroup and the Taihu subgroup communication is close to failing altogether.** **This reminds us:** **'Wu' is a scholarly unit, not a vernacular of everyday mutual understanding;** **when you say 'a user of Wu', you are naming a classification, not a group of people who could hold a meeting.** ③ **Administrative boundaries are very conspicuous on the map.** **A received phenomenon: a number of boundaries coincide with old prefectural boundaries, with mountain country and with river systems.** **This lesson's attitude is both to acknowledge the coincidence and not to be superstitious about it:** **coincidence shows the geography of migration and of markets; it does not show bloodline.** **This sentence will be required to be developed in the short essay at the end of the chapter.**
:::

::: definition Shanghainese is the newest Wu {#def-shanghai}
**Received account.** **The modern speech of the Shanghai urban area took shape in the large-scale migration after the port was opened; it mixed features of several localities of the Taihu subgroup and underwent a marked levelling.** **Four consequences.** **① weakening of the voiced sounds, ② merger of tones, ③ simplification of sandhi, ④ vocabulary drawing towards the written language and Putonghua (standard Mandarin).** **This lesson's verdict.** **Shanghainese is not 'the most typical Wu'; it is** **the branch of Wu that formed latest and has been levelled the most.** **Hence a common misalignment:** **by the impression of people from elsewhere, Shanghainese represents Wu; by the number of features, what can best represent Wu are the townships and the mountain districts to its west and its south.** **This misalignment is itself what the chapter teaches:** **the representativeness of a variety comes from its economic and media status, not from its typicality;** **Chapter 8, 'the prestige account of language', here receives a case from inside a single dialect.** **What is more worth remembering is the mechanism:** **the city draws in the differences around it and then presses them flat;** **so the largest city of a speech area is often the place where that speech area has the fewest features.** **The sentence holds for Mandarin, for Wu, for Yue (Cantonese for the speech of Guangzhou and Hong Kong) and for any language that is urbanising —** **you may take it as this chapter's portable conclusion.**
:::

::: definition The Wenzhou dialect and the measure 'hard to understand' {#def-wenzhou}
**A popular saying.** **Public opinion often ranks the Wenzhou dialect as 'the hardest dialect to understand'.** **This lesson's handling has two steps.** **① First, acknowledge that it cannot be quantified.** **'Hard to understand' is not a property, but a relational quantity: it equals the distance between the listener's mother tongue and the target speech, minus the listener's amount of contact.** **One and the same Wenzhou dialect is a neighbour to a person from Qingtian, and a foreign language to a person from Harbin.** **Rankings of the 'hardest to understand' kind are therefore zero information in any linguistics.** ② **Then ask why it is popular.** **Because the difference between the Oujiang subgroup and the Taihu subgroup really is large** (tone categories are many, the splits and mergers of rhyme categories differ, everyday words are another set, and the sandhi rules form a system of their own); and clan networks and commercial networks in the Wenzhou area are dense, with high internal consistency and closure to the outside. **Received account:** **this item thus becomes an application of Chapter 8:** **a ranking of 'hard to understand' is in fact the reverse projection of a ranking of amount of contact;** **and that it catches on so smoothly shows that people are willing to restate 'I am not familiar with it' as 'it is mysterious'.** **An alternative wording offered by this lesson.** **Never say that some speech is hard to understand; say 'for a given kind of speaker, mutual intelligibility is low, because such-and-such features differ' —** **this is a very small act of linguistic hygiene, and it is the initiation gift of the whole dialect course.**
:::

::: example Fifty-four readings and one table {#ex-biaoge}
Suppose a certain Wu locality has five isolation tones, numbered 1 to 5. The tone sandhi in connected speech of two-syllable groups can be summed up in the table below (on the left, the tone category of the first syllable; along the top, the tone category of the second syllable; in the cell, the actual tone shape of the whole string):

| first＼second | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 55-31 | 55-31 | 55-31 | 55-31 |
| 2 | 12-44 | 12-44 | 12-34 | 12-34 |
| 3 | 33-53 | 33-53 | 33-44 | 33-44 |
| 5 | 13-33 | 13-33 | 13-33 | 13-33 |

Please answer three questions: **① Is this first-syllable-dominant or last-syllable-dominant? ② How many regularities can be read off the table? ③ Why can looking this table up not replace listening?**

::: solution
**① Judgement.** **The output tone shape of each row depends only on the row of the first syllable: the three columns with the same first syllable are almost completely identical;** **it is therefore the first-syllable-dominant type.** **If one changed to another dialect and the outputs in the same column agreed, it would be last-syllable-dominant.** ② **Regularities.** **First, information about the tone category of the second syllable is almost wiped away:** a slight difference appears only once, in column 3 of rows 2 and 3; **this shows that** a sandhi system will sacrifice oppositions in exchange for prosodic neatness. **Course 3's 'the balance of economy of effort and clarity' is a big-character poster here;** **second, the tone shape of the whole string in row 1 is like one stress pattern for the whole:** the two-syllable group is here more like a single word. ③ **Why the table cannot replace listening.** **Because the table leaves out three things that exist only in time:** the length of the syllable, the habit of pausing at a boundary, and the unit on which the speaker decides to start the tone afresh; **and the third is precisely the core mechanism of sandhi.** **Received account: the sandhi domain usually corresponds to the prosodic word or the foot.** **This is why this chapter asks you to do the exercise rather than to recite:** **sandhi is not a question of memory; it is a question of rhythm;** **a transcriber who can only consult the table will record two different words as the same string of tones —** **this kind of error has a name in this course:** **taking a claim for a fact.** **The brackets line in Course 3.**
:::
:::

::: intuition The pronunciation problem of a novel {#def-xiaoshuo}
**Received account.** **In the late Qing there was a long novel written with its dialogue in the Suzhou dialect, and it became an example often cited of literature in dialect; before that, too, there were books collecting the mountain songs and the speaking-and-singing (*shuōchàng* 说唱) of the Wu region.** **This lesson uses it to raise three questions.** **① How does the author spell words that have no orthography?** **Borrowed characters, popular characters, characters used to note the sound, and characters of the author's own making are used together;** **the text thus preserves a continuous experiment in spelling.** **A miniature of Course 1's 'taxonomy of character-rewriting'.** ② **Who is the reader?** **Writing the dialogue in *Sūbái* 苏白 ('Suzhou vernacular') assumes that the reader can read it and cannot speak it: the dialect is here a marker of character and a local colour.** **This is the legitimate place of dialect in literature:** **it is a voice that is quoted, not a written language.** **Please read this sentence against Chapters 21 and 22:** **a writer of the northern Mandarin area need not add a note when writing dialect, while a writer of the Wu area must —** **the same technique has different costs in different speech areas, and this explains why the proportion of literature in dialect tilts along speech areas.** ③ **What kind of material does it count as today?** **A linguist can use it as evidence for vocabulary and for grammar, but must handle it in layers:** **the novel's conventions of spelling may continue those of the block-printed editions of an earlier time, and they may also have been standardised by the author.** **This returns to the layering that this course repeats:** **the textual layer, the layer of habit and the spoken layer — the three do not coincide;** **and if any conclusion about the grammar of a dialect has only one literary work as evidence, it can be entered only as the 'legend layer'.**
:::

::: history From one survey to public opinion {#sec-wu}
**Six Dynasties to Tang and Song.** **The spoken language of Jiangnan left a number of layers in the documents.** **Received account:** **this lesson takes only one reliable reading:** **a number of words and pronunciations in Wu today can be explained only by historical layers, and cannot be explained by 'a living fossil of ancient pronunciation'.** **The word 'living fossil' is forbidden in this course, for the same reason as the previous chapter's item 'who preserves the ancient pronunciation'.** ② **Ming and Qing.** **The phonology of southern drama (*nánxì* 南戏) and of the Kunshan style (*Kūnshān qiāng* 昆山腔), and the manuscripts of mountain songs and of *táncí* 弹词, gave Wu a written existence over a long time.** **Received account.** ③ **The 1890s.** **A novel written with its dialogue in the Suzhou vernacular was published.** **Received account.** ④ **1928.** **A study of Wu described many localities by transcription and by questionnaire, and became the opening for business of what this book calls 'the laboratory of Chinese dialectology'.** **The received account attributes it to Zhao Yuanren (Yuen Ren Chao).** ⑤ **After the 1980s.** **Dialect dictionaries, county-by-county surveys and studies of grammar accumulated; at the same time urbanisation changed the direction of the difference between city and countryside:** **formerly the countryside preserved the old features; now the older stratum of the cities preserves them.** **Received account:** **this lesson calls this 'age replacing geography', and takes it to be the most important structural change in the Chinese dialects in this century.** ⑥ **From 2008 to the present.** **Public discussion of the protection of dialects, trials of local media and of interest classes in schools, and the transcriptions of the project for protecting language resources.** **Received account.** **This lesson's judgement:** **once the transmission of a speech changes from a question of geography into a question of the timetable, its fate is handed to administration and to budgets;** **Chapter 9 sets this out.**
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**'Wu preserves the ancient pronunciation, and is therefore ancient.'** **What is preserved is different layers** (above: voiceless with voiced airflow, and the ban on 'living fossil'). **Taking Shanghainese as representative.** **It is the branch that has been levelled the most** (above: Shanghainese). **Saying that some speech is 'hard to understand'.** **Being hard to understand is the reverse projection of amount of contact;** **rewrite it as 'for whom mutual intelligibility is low, and because of which features'** (above: the Wenzhou item). **Using a sandhi table in place of listening.** **The three things left out all lie in time** (above: example ③): **this point holds for any work of transcription.**
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the economics of a written form {level=3}
Write six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing '**Whether a dialect can be given a written form depends mainly not on its literary gifts, but on whether there is an industry that charges in it: distribution, records, radio, subtitles and textbooks. In the twentieth century Yue had this whole set, and Wu had only some of the parts; as a result the former developed a usable convention of transcription, while the latter remained local colour inside dialogue. What is called the decline of a dialect is often, first, that its uses in writing are withdrawn, and only afterwards that no one can speak it**'. You must call on at least six items: **voiceless with voiced airflow, and the method of measuring it**, **tone sandhi in connected speech**, **the levelling of Shanghai**, **rankings of 'hard to understand'**, **the three questions about the dialect novel**, **age replacing geography**; and you must rule: **does giving a speech a writing system speed its dying out, or slow it?** (You must give the mechanism and one counter-example.)

::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms; the ruling (**a passing answer must have both sides.** **The side on which it speeds the dying-out.** **An orthography needs a standard, and a standard needs one locality chosen as its base;** **this choice strips the other localities of their qualification to be written, and turns internal differences into 'a problem of wrong characters' —** **and so the writing system becomes yet another levelling force.** **This is the answer this lesson most hopes to see, because it is isomorphic with Chapter 3's 'orthography is a tax'.** **The side on which it slows the dying-out.** **A speech that has a way of being written can be taught, can be cited and can be taken as a badge of identity, and children will be willing to learn it because it can appear on a screen;** **a candidate counter-example:** **a number of dialects that have been standardised, and for which grammars and dictionaries have been published, still shift to the common language within two or three generations** (**because writing does not change the structure of opportunity**); and one supporting counter-example. **Hebrew is cited as the stock example of 'writing and education bringing a spoken language back to life';** **the reason it is rare is precisely that it needs a budget at national level and immigration —** **marking looks at whether this example is used to show 'conditions, not will'.**) If the conclusion can be written '**so, for a speech, writing is not oxygen but a shop window: it can change how other people see that speech, and it rarely changes who will use it to support a family; the real lever lies in the structure of opportunity, and recording and writing are two things we can still do outside the lever**', that is an A.
:::
