The previous chapter inferred a sound system from poems. **This one works from a book that was itself an attempt to record a sound system — and the book is a list of categories with no description, which is why a thousand years of scholars have been reading it as a code.**

## One book and its accounts {#def-qieyun}

::: definition Rhimes, classes, tables {#def-lei}
**The standard account**: a rhyme dictionary of 601 was compiled by a group of men who stated that they were reconciling southern and northern practice, and it survived because the examination system needed a standard; its categories were re-ordered into articulatory diagrams some centuries later. **Three layers of evidence must be kept apart. ① The dictionary's own units — 193 rhyme categories, arranged by the four tones**, whose names are mnemonic and whose boundaries are the primary datum. **② The initial classes**, recovered later by the method below. **③ The rhyme tables**, medieval charts that arrange syllables in rows and columns for teaching, and that distinguish "open" and "closed" and a four-fold vowel division whose meaning was debated before it was forgotten. **The methodological rule of this chapter: the first layer is a document, the second and third are interpretations of it; the tables in particular are a theory, not a transcript.** And one warning the course repeats: **the categories are real and the sounds are proposals** — a chapter of the phonology course is devoted to what a rhyme category is not.
:::

::: definition The reverse-fanqie chaining {#def-xilian}
**The dictionary spells every character with two others** — the initial of one plus the final and tone of another. **The method, formalised by Chen Li in the nineteenth century, is to chain these**: if A is spelled with X and B is also spelled with X, then A and B share an initial, and the set of all spelling characters partitions itself (**standard account**). **The result is a set of initial classes, forty-odd by the usual counting, whose traditional labels are taken from a mnemonic verse.** **The method is a beautiful piece of structural reasoning and its limits are equally instructive. ① It yields classes, not sounds**: the analysis cannot tell you whether two classes differ by place, by manner, or by an element that later dialects lost. **② It depends on the compiler's consistency**: two spelling series that a modern scholar thinks "must" be one may have been kept apart for a reason that was orthographic, and the chaining cannot see the difference between a phonological distinction and an editorial habit. **③ Exceptions are not noise but evidence**, since the residual links record either scribal transmission or the dialect of the scribe (**standard account**: the collation of received editions shows spelling drift). **This lesson's generalisation: any method that recovers contrast from distribution gives you the shape of the system, and the substance must come from elsewhere — from dialects, from loans, from transcriptions.**
:::

::: definition Four foreign copies {#def-fuguang}
**Four independent transcriptions of Chinese dating from the seventh to the tenth centuries and made outside China: Sino-Japanese readings, Sino-Korean, Sino-Vietnamese, and the Tang-period transcriptions of Sanskrit dhāraṇī** (**standard account** — the last are the most explicit, because the foreign source script had letters). **Their value is that they were made by people who wrote down what they heard in a notation they controlled; their weakness is that each one is a restructuring of Chinese into another phonology.** A Sino-Vietnamese layer preserves distinctions of final that Middle Chinese lost; the Sanskrit transcriptions distinguish the Chinese nasal codas with care, and sometimes add a vowel to break a final cluster that the transcriber's own language disallowed. **Two working rules. ① Use them for contrasts, not for values**: if a transcription distinguishes two Chinese categories, Chinese probably distinguished them, and the actual segments are constrained, not given. **② Date and stratify the layer**: a Sino-xenic reading is a fossil of some stage, and the "some" is the whole argument (the third course's borrowing account, which is the same problem in miniature). **The most valuable single item: the transcriptions of the Tang capital, made by a monk who recorded his own corrective remarks on how a syllable should be pronounced — near-direct evidence about a standard, nine hundred years before a recording device.**
:::

::: definition An interface is not an accent {#def-jiekou}
**The standard account**: the same book served the examinations for centuries, and later periods read its categories as a pronunciation of their own speech, which they were not. **This chapter insists on the distinction: a literary and reading tradition (a standard interface) is not an accent. ① It can be shared by people who cannot speak alike** — two men from different provinces reading the same categories correctly and sounding entirely different, which is the structure of the rhyme-book tradition in Chinese, Latin and Sanskrit. **② It changes more slowly than speech**, so a text like the tables records a compromise between an old standard and a living one, which is why it seems to describe impossible syllables. **③ It can be analysed without knowing its speaker's native dialect**, which is what makes the whole field possible — and what makes the sentence "this is how it sounded" a category error, since there was no single "it." The phonology course's slogan returns: **the rhyme book is a spelling standard for a class of readers, not a transcript of anyone's breakfast.** **This lesson adds: if you find yourself reconstructing the speech of the Tang capital from the examinations, you are reconstructing an orthography.**
:::

::: definition Tone and initial voicing {#def-qingzhuo}
**Middle Chinese has three tones in the entering type and four in the category names; and its initials are conventionally arranged in a scheme of voiceless, voiced and aspirated sets, plus the "clear" and "muddy" labels of the tables. Two relations are essential to everything after this chapter. ① The tone splits according to the initial: a voiced initial and a voiceless one send the same syllable to different later tones** — this single correspondence explains most of the modern dialect tone inventories (the next two chapters cash it out). **② Entering-tone syllables end in a stop** (-p, -t, -k), which is what makes them a class. **Three cautions. The traditional labels map onto phonetic properties whose exact values are argued — the "muddy" initials, for example, may have been prenasalised, or implosive, or simply voiced** (**standard account**: the three have all been proposed). **The number of vowel categories in the tables is the most contested question in the field** (**standard account**), and **the tables' four-fold division may be a property of the vowel, of the medial, or of the arrangement convention.** **Where you must not compromise: the relation between initials and tone splits is a correspondence, and correspondences are not hypotheses.**
:::

::: example From four spellings, two conclusions {#ex-fanzqie}
Four fanqie spellings (**invented for this lesson, modelled on real patterns**): 冬, 都宗; 彤, 徒冬; 桃, 徒刀; 高, 古刀. **What can you conclude, and what cannot you conclude?**

::: solution
**Chaining**: 冬 is spelled with 都, so they share an initial; 彤 is spelled with 徒 and rhymes on 冬 — 徒 is voiced where 都 is voiceless, so the two initials are in complementary distribution with respect to tone behaviour… **Conclusions available: the contrast between the 都-type and 徒-type spellers is real; 冬 and 彤 belong to the same final group; 桃 and 高 share the 刀-spelled rhyme, so a distinction between 冬-type and 刀-type finals exists in the system.** **Not available: whether the 都/徒 contrast is voicing, aspiration or a prefix; whether the two rhyme groups differed in vowel, coda or length; whether any of these spellings reflects the speech of the compiler or his book tradition.** **And a trap in the data: real fanqie spellings occasionally "cross" categories in ways the system forbids; a beginner smooths them, a scholar publishes them, because those exceptions are where a scribe's own dialect leaks through** (**standard account**: the study of spelling drift is a real subfield). **Marking: whoever ends the answer with the sentence "the method yields contrasts, and the values come from elsewhere" has understood both this chapter and the previous one.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Why still read a book that cannot be used {#int-buneng}
**A fair question: the rhyme book does not tell you how to speak, and no one has spoken its language for a thousand years. Four answers. ① It is the hinge of all the evidence: the older material (rhyme groups, phonetic series) and the later material (modern dialects, the tone splits) can be connected only through it**; the next two chapters are nothing but this hinge opened. **② It is a record of a standard, and a standard is a social fact** — who was required to read this, and what it cost them, is a real history of the examination state (the applied phonology of the earlier phonology course). **③ It trained a method**: chaining spellings to recover contrast is an elegant piece of structural reasoning, and it is the earliest Chinese example of an argument that modern linguistics would recognise. **④ It is where the vocabulary lives** — the words for initial, final, medial, "clear" and "muddy", the rhyme names that every dialect and poetry discussion still uses. **The practical reason this matters to a student: nine hundred years of Chinese writing about sound are written against this book, and if you cannot read it you cannot read the tradition's own metalinguistics, which is half of what the classical-criticism course called "whoever controls the text controls the interpretation" applied to pronunciation.**
:::

::: history A millennium of footnotes {#sec-zhujie}
**601**: the dictionary is compiled, its preface stating the reconciliation of regional practice (**standard account**). **Eighth to tenth centuries**: additions and revisions absorb new characters, and foreign transcriptions record the living reading of the capital (**standard account**). **Tenth to eleventh centuries**: the rime-spelling of a monastic tradition and then a national rhyme standard for the examinations fix the categories in use (**standard account**). **Twelfth to thirteenth centuries**: rhyme tables reorganise the material articulatorily; a Mongol-era rhyme book reorganises it again to fit the speech of the north — **the two books disagree, and the disagreement is the best evidence we have for what changed** (next chapter). **Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries**: scholars recover the initial classes by chaining spellings, and the analysis is completed in the 19th century (**standard account**: Chen Li's result). **Twentieth century**: the foreign transcriptions, the Dunhuang materials and the dialect surveys are brought to bear; the notation of Swedish and Li Fanggui becomes standard; and the vowel question remains open (**standard account**). **Present: databases of character readings allow the whole system to be tested against thousands of modern reflexes at once** (**standard account**). **The arc: a practical examination book becomes, by successive re-readings, the object of a science — and each science was invented to answer a question its predecessors had left in the orthography.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Confusing the three layers** (**document, recovered classes, tables-as-theory**); **reading a class as a sound** (**chaining yields contrasts**, chaining); **treating the standard as an accent** (**an interface, not a breakfast**); **ignoring the spelling exceptions** (**they are the leak of a living dialect**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: the science that grew on a book {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a discipline can be organised around a single document for a thousand years, and the document's silence is what does the organising: every method in the field — chaining, tables, comparison with foreign transcriptions — was invented to make the book say something it does not say. This is not a deficiency. A shared silence is what lets generations of scholars disagree productively, because they are disagreeing about the same object.** Deploy at least six of: **the three layers**, **the chaining method and its three limits**, **the four copies and their two rules**, **the interface argument**, **the tone-initial correspondence**, **the contested vowel division**, **the millennium sequence**. Then adjudicate: **is a field better served by a canonical document with an ambiguous text, or by a clear description of a lost language that nobody agrees on anymore?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**must be an argument, not a taste**). **For the canonical document**: it anchors disputes, disciplines vocabulary, and keeps the object stable long enough for methods to accumulate — **and it can be re-dug, since each generation finds new information in an ambiguous text**. **For the clear description**: it makes claims falsifiable and teaches faster; **the cost is that the next generation's data will contradict it, and a field whose canonical text is refuted spends its energy defending it.** **Highest marks for the observation that Middle Chinese's canonical book has both properties, and that this is why it worked: the categories are unambiguous and the sounds are not, so the field has a stable object and an inexhaustible problem** — **compare the fate of a fully explicit reconstruction, which becomes either a dogma or a museum piece within a generation.**
:::
