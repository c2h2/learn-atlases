title: "Old Chinese"
---

Everything before this chapter is inferred from rhyme books; here the evidence is older than any book about it. **The subject is the language of the Odes and of the bronze inscriptions, roughly the first millennium before the common era, and the methodological problem is that we have no dictionary, no grammar and no recording — only characters, which are not phonetic transcriptions, and poems, whose rhymes we must first decide how to read**.

## Rhyme groups {#def-yunbu}

::: definition Reconstructing a dead sound system by statistics {#def-tongji}
**The base data are rhyming practice**: which characters rhyme in the Odes, and how often. **The procedure is an analysis of co-occurrence**: count how often each pair of candidate rhyme words occurs in the same rhyme position, then group characters whose co-occurrence is high and whose gaps to other groups are systematic. **Qing scholars did this by hand and arrived at a set of rhyme groups whose boundaries are still broadly the ones used** (**standard account**: the divisions of Gu Yanwu, Jiang Shaoqu and Duan Yucai are the ancestors of modern tables). **Two results and one caution. Result ①: the groups are not the rhyme categories of later books** — the Odes rhyme what the rhyme books separate and separate what they unite, so the difference is evidence about sound, not about licence. **Result ②: the groups can be split by evidence from the inscriptions and from the Odes' own uneven practice, and how finely to split is still argued.** The caution: **co-occurrence measures a convention, not a pronunciation** — what we recover is a poet's partition of the sound system, and any statement about actual vowels is an inference from that partition plus other evidence. **This lesson's methodological rule: the rhyme groups are the strongest thing we have about Old Chinese phonology and the weakest about its phonetics; state which of the two you are talking about whenever you say "they pronounced it…"**
:::

::: definition Phonetic series and initials {#def-xiesheng}
**Characters were built from phonetic components, so characters sharing a component were somehow similar in sound when the character was made. Duan Yucai's formulation — "those sharing a phonetic belong to the same group" — is the starting point for the consonants** (**standard account**). **Three cautions that are the actual content of the method. ① The series span centuries of sound change**, so a modern reading like *gāo* beside *jiǎ* is not an anomaly but the record of a change; **the series is a set of relations of varying age, not a snapshot.** **② The component may be semantic or decorative**, so a series must be filtered against form and against other evidence (**standard account**: commentators on the dictionary tradition note that not every stated phonetic was phonetic). **③ The characters do not tell you what the sound was, only that two were close — which is why Old Chinese reconstruction is a system of contrasts and not a pronunciation.** Where the method shines is in the clusters: because series mix materials such as *t-* with *rt-* type analyses, most recent systems postulate **consonant clusters and pre-initials** for Old Chinese (**standard account**), **which also explain the odd behaviour of some 反切 materials later** — a hypothesis with explanatory breadth and independent motivation is a good hypothesis, and this is the strongest argument in the chapter.
:::

::: definition The shape of a hypothesis {#def-jiazh}
**Take one claim and see what makes it a hypothesis at all: that the tone system of later Chinese grew out of lost final consonants — the departing tone from a final *-s, and on one account the rising tone from a glottal stop** (**standard account**: the mechanism is Haudricourt's tonogenesis, established for Vietnamese and extended to Chinese; the details of the rising tone remain argued). **① Motive: it explains an irregularity** — why certain characters rhyme or share phonetics across categories that later tones separate, and why loan words in neighbouring languages behave as if the endings existed. **② Mechanism: an ending that is reduced still leaves a trace on the preceding segment, so its loss is not neutral but registers as pitch.** **③ Independent test: the same change should be datable in the Sino-xenic layers of Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese, and the predictions differ from those of an innate-tone account.** **④ What would falsify it: a well-established Old Chinese rhyme in which the two categories mix freely and the account forbids it.** The lesson's rule: **for any reconstruction claim, ask what it was invented to explain, whether the mechanism is known to operate elsewhere, and what it forbids.** Three questions, and a claim that answers none of them is a table of correspondences with a story attached.
:::

::: definition The morphology debate {#def-xingtai}
**Old Chinese is widely reconstructed with derivational apparatus: prefixes and infixes that mark, among other things, causatives, nominalisation and reciprocity** (**standard account**: associated with the comparative method and with Sino-Tibetan comparison; the mainstream recent systems differ in details). **Two reasons this belongs in a course on the history of Chinese rather than in a historical-comparative seminar. ① It explains pairs that are visible inside the transmitted language**: an alternation of initial and of tone in pairs like "die / kill" type verb pairs, where a tonal difference correlates with a valency difference — **the morphology of a word used to be its syntax, and the residues remain in the modern language as register fossils.** ② **It makes Chinese comparable**: a language usually described as isolating turns out to have had, at one stage, something like the family's device of marking the verb. **The honest state of the art: the individual affixes are far less secure than the general claim that there was morphology; the general claim is contested less by new data than by the difficulty of the data.** So when you write about it, use the three-layer method of this course: **mechanism, mainstream account, disputed detail**, and never let a student come away with the impression that a form with an asterisk is a word somebody said.
:::

::: definition Five syntactic patterns and a warning {#def-wuzhong}
**Five features of the older written language that survive as recognisable alternatives in the classical register: ① object before verb in questions and negations** (interrogative and indefinite pronouns in pre-verbal position); **② the sentence-final and mid-sentence particles, whose function is still debated**; **③ predicate use of numerals and adjectives without a copula; ④ the absence of a definite article and the massive use of bare nouns; ⑤ serial verb constructions and the "by-verb" before the subject** (**standard account**). **The warning: the corpus is written, and probably partly literary rather than spoken**, and one genre — the inscriptions — is formulaic to the point of being a different register from the Odes. **So a "rule of Old Chinese syntax" is usually a statement about a genre's written conventions, and the safest claims are those that hold across genres.** Two more cautions. **The writing does not mark dialect**, so a feature may be regional; **and the corpus is small enough that rarity is not evidence of ungrammaticality** (**standard account**: an argument that recurs in the methodology of ancient corpus work). **This is the chapter's humility clause, and it is the reason to prefer the negative result: a construction never attested in a genre may be a property of the genre.**
:::

::: example Five steps to one phonetic conclusion {#ex-xie}
A phonetic series contains a modern *zh-* initial beside a modern *n-/l-* initial. **Lay out the five steps from the data to a reconstructive conclusion, and mark what each step costs.**

::: solution
**① Establish the series**: check that the shared component is genuinely phonetic and that the characters are old enough to matter — **cost: excluding the semantic and late cases removes much of the apparent mess, and some of the sample.** **② Collect the rhyming behaviour** of the same characters in the Odes and the inscriptions. **③ Compare the family**: cognates in other Sino-Tibetan languages, since the cluster analysis is stronger with external comparison — **cost: the comparanda are themselves reconstructive; you now depend on two inferences at once.** **④ State the co-occurrence regularity**: every character in this series, and no characters outside it, show the alternation — **a regularity, not a sound.** **⑤ Propose a source** (a cluster, a prefix, a following medial), then run the two tests of the hypothesis section: does the mechanism operate elsewhere, and what does it forbid? **Cost: if the proposal explains your series but forces changes in three others, you have bought one item with a hundred.** **Marking: whoever marks step ④ as the last place where the argument is safe takes an A**, and step ⑤ is where the discipline's disagreement actually lives, which is why this chapter says the reconstruction is a system of contrasts rather than a pronunciation.
:::
:::

::: intuition Three claims hidden in "Old Chinese had no tones" {#int-sanzhong}
**The sentence is heard constantly and means three different things. ① A statement of evidence**: the tone categories of later Chinese are not distinguishable in the oldest material, because the categories postdate the loss of the endings that conditioned them — **the strongest of the three, and close to orthodox** (**standard account**). **② A statement of structure**: syllables then had no contrastive pitch — **much harder, since pitch can be phonemic without leaving the traces we look for, and the claim is argued** (**standard account**). **③ A statement about experience**: that speakers "did not hear" tones — **unfalsifiable, and not a linguistic claim at all.** Three questions to run on anyone who says it: **which of the three do you mean; what evidence would change your mind; and are you describing a sound system or a listening ability?** The general form of this course's method, again: **distinguish mechanism, mainstream claim and contested detail, and refuse to let a strong sentence stand on weak footing.** The same drill applies to "Old Chinese was monosyllabic" (a claim about the average word, not the syllable), and to "Classical Chinese is Chinese" (a claim about a written register, dealt with in the classical-chinese course).
:::

::: history Two centuries of division, fifty years of reconstruction {#sec-fenbu}
**Seventeenth century**: a scholar reads the Odes against later rhyme practice and begins the division of Old Chinese rhyme groups, arguing that the ancients rhymed by their own system rather than by distortion (**standard account**: this is the decisive methodological move, against the older "leafing the rhyme" practice of changing readings to make rhymes work). **Eighteenth century**: the divisions are refined by hand to something close to the modern picture, and Duan Yucai states the phonetic-series principle (**standard account**). **Early twentieth century**: excavated inscriptions and the comparative method add evidence that no one had in 1700 (**standard account**). **Mid-twentieth century**: systematic reconstructions are proposed, and the reconstruction of a tone-origin mechanism follows (**standard account**). **Late twentieth to twenty-first century**: dense databases of character readings, dialect and cognate forms support quantitative comparison, and current published systems differ in the number of vowels, in the inventory of clusters and in how much morphology to posit (**standard account**). **The moral of the sequence: two hundred years were spent establishing which characters belong together, and only the last few decades attempt sounds; the slow part was not the technology but the discipline of not over-claiming.** Which is why this chapter ends on method rather than on a sound list: **what you can defend is a partition and a set of contrasts; a pronunciation is a proposal you make about them, and it should be labelled as one.**

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Reading a reconstruction as a recording** (**a system of contrasts**, phonetic series); **treating a rhyme group as a pronunciation** (**co-occurrence measures a convention**, rhyme groups); **citing an unattested form as a word** (**mark the asterisk's layer**, morphology); **generalising a genre's syntax to a language** (**five patterns and the warning**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: what a partition can prove {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the most secure results in the history of a language are negative and structural: that two sets of items were treated apart, that a contrast existed at one time and not later. Pronunciations are the least secure and the most photogenic, and every popular account of a dead language is built from them. The honest style of the field is therefore awkward to read: it says "the two categories do not mix" when what the reader wants is a sound, and it should not be improved.** Deploy at least six of: **co-occurrence and its limits**, **the phonetic series and its three cautions**, **the three-question test of a hypothesis**, **the morphology debate's layers**, **genre and the syntax warning**, **the three claims hidden in "no tones"**, **the two-century sequence**. Then adjudicate: **is a field better served by publishing reconstructions in the popular voice, with their confidence elided, or by refusing to summarise?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**must give a policy, not a preference**). **A passing shape: publish with the confidence marked**, and the standard is technical — **the asterisk, the question mark, the words "on one account"; a summary that drops them is not simpler but false**. **Stronger answers note what the popular voice is for: it recruits, and a field that refuses to recruit loses funding and students, which is a real cost of honesty — so the policy should be two registers by the same author, not one register by a journalist.** **Highest marks for the observation that the negative-structural style is not a weakness of the field but its actual result: knowing that a partition existed without knowing what was partitioned is a substantive discovery about a language two and a half thousand years old**, and it is exactly the kind of claim that survives the next fifty years of data — **whereas pronunciations, this chapter's history section shows, have been rewritten in every generation since the 1940s.**
:::
