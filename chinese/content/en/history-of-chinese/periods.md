---
title: "Periods of Chinese"
---

Dialectology divides space; this chapter divides time. The first claim of this chapter is to put the two courses into a single sentence — **periodisation is division carried out in time: its criteria, its disputes, and the look of its atlas being redrawn once every thirty years are all exactly the same**.

## Definition: three blocks or four {#def-fenqi}

::: definition Periodisation anchored to books {#def-mao}
**Received account.** **The history of Chinese is usually divided into four periods: Old Chinese, Middle Chinese, Early Mandarin and Modern Chinese.** Their representative sound systems are often spoken of as three anchors: **Old Chinese on the evidence of the rhyme groups of the *Book of Songs*, Middle Chinese on the evidence of the *Qieyun* 切韵 ('Cut Rhymes'), Early Mandarin on the evidence of rhyme dictionaries for *qu* 曲 (song or aria) of the *Zhongyuan yinyun* 中原音韵 ('Rhymes of the Central Plain') type.** This lesson lays that string of titles out side by side so that you can see one thing: **all three anchors are books compiled, or composed, so that someone writing verse could look a rhyme up.** Three thousand years of one language are thus lit by three occasions of 'the need to rhyme'. **The sociology of periodisation, in this lesson:** (1) the actual basis of a periodisation is not that a change has been completed, but that texts are available. The reason a given stretch 'looks like the language of one age' is that there happens to be a body of material one can count. (2) Change itself does not happen by periods. **The devoicing of the fully voiced initials, the loss of the entering tone (checked tone), disyllabification and the rise of the disposal construction each keep a timetable of their own.** The periodisation lines in a textbook are nine curves cut at one and the same place. (3) So, in reading a table of periods, one has to carry a question: **in this stretch, who wrote my evidence, and for whom was it written?** **The line from Course 1 and the dialect course, 'first ask where the measuring-stick comes from', becomes here the first lesson of the history of Chinese.**
:::

::: definition Why the boundaries never cease to be disputed {#def-bianjie}
**Received account.** **The several periodisations do not agree on the boundaries.** Three typical disputes. **(1) The lower limit of Old Chinese.** **Some bring it down to the Han, some to the Jin and the Liu-Song (晋宋).** The disagreement is not about the phonetic facts, but about 'which change is enough to end a period' — **and 'enough' is a value-word for which no argument has been given.** (2) **The upper limit of Early Mandarin.** **Some fix it in the thirteenth century (with a *qu* rhyme dictionary as the anchor), some push it back to the Song dynasty (with Chan records and storyteller's scripts, *huaben* 话本, that stand close to speech, as the anchor).** **Received account:** **this dispute is where method shows most clearly.** **Take a rhyme dictionary as the anchor, and you get an abstract sound system;** **take recorded sayings as the anchor, and you get an approximation to speech in which literary forms are mixed.** **Both can justify themselves, because they are not answering one and the same question.** (3) **The starting point of the modern period.** **The year 1919, the year 1949, or the 1950s answer to three different stories:** the Literary Revolution, a change of regime, or language planning. **This lesson's comment on (3):** **this is the most naked case, in the whole course, of 'to periodise is to hold a view of history'.** **Whoever sets modern Chinese in 1919 is telling the story of a revolution in the written language;** **whoever sets it around 1956 is telling the story of the institutionalisation of a common language.** **The supporters of the two periodisations read one and the same body of material, and yet on 'what Chinese is' they hold a further view** — **and that sentence applies to every dispute over periodisation in history, including the dialect map in the previous chapter.**
:::

::: definition Nine kinds of material and the bias of each {#def-jiu}
**(1) Rhyme groups in verse.** **They can prove that 'these characters rhymed at the time'.** Bias: the literary form is conservative, the position of the rhyme word is restricted, and layers of archaism are piled one on another. **(2) Phonetic series** (*xiesheng* 谐声). **They can prove that the sounds were close when the characters were made.** Bias: the layers stack up over several centuries, and dialects are mixed in. (3) **Phonetic loan and variant texts.** **Phonetic substitution (*tongjia* 通假) gives the same sound or a near sound.** Bias: the copyist's period is unknown, and an association by the shape of the character can go wrong. (4) ***Duruo* 读若 ('read as') glosses and sound glosses** (*shengxun* 声训). **Han scholars explained the meaning of a character by a character of similar sound, and so produced approximations a modern person would not guess.** Bias: the glossator is often playing at etymology. (5) ***Fanqie* spelling and rhyme dictionaries.** **They can give the splits and mergers of categories.** Bias: compromise, abstraction, and possibly not anyone's accent. (6) ***Dengyun* rhyme tables** (等韵图). **They give a system of structure and of places of articulation.** Bias: simplified for teaching; the diagram may override the facts. (7) **Transcriptional matches** (*duiyin* 对音). **Phonetic spellings in translation among Sanskrit, Tibetan and Chinese; the Phags-pa script (八思巴字); Latin spellings by the Jesuits.** Bias: the foreigner's ear, and the limits of the script. (8) **Comparison of dialects and of related languages.** **It gives the branches of the split.** Bias: a loanword can disguise itself as an inherited word. (9) **Character usage in excavated texts and in transmitted texts.** **It gives the writing habits of one time.** Bias: the norms of copying may keep on an older standard. **This lesson compresses the table into a single rule of discipline.** **A single kind of material can only propose a hypothesis; a conclusion holds only where several lines of evidence point the same way, and where the evidence conflicts we ought to report the conflict rather than pick one.** **The four degrees of strength from Course 1 are here upgraded to nine kinds of bias.** And the practical consequence is the most useful sentence in this chapter: **before you read any work of reconstruction, first check how many rows of this table it cites.** **A conclusion that cites only rhyme dictionaries, and does not use transcriptional matches — its reliability you can already judge for yourself.** **That sentence is the first tool this lesson gives you for writing a literature review.**
:::

::: definition What can be known, and what cannot {#def-buneng}
**What can be known.** **Splits and mergers of categories: at a given time and place, these characters were read the same or not the same.** **This is the most reliable kind of proposition in the whole of research on Chinese, because it can be counted.** **Received account.** (2) **What can only barely be known.** **Relative relations: class A is closer than class B, or a given class has only just merged in this period.** It depends on your measure of 'nearness in sound'. (3) **What cannot be known.** **Precise phonetic values, and any person's actual accent.** **A symbol in a reconstruction is a placeholder for a category.** **To take it as 'people at the time spoke just like this' is to over-read it.** **This lesson offers a comparison here:** **a reconstruction is a diagram of the pipes underground, not a photograph of the street.** **It can tell you whether two pipes are connected; it cannot tell you whether there is a smell on that street.** **The comparison is at once this chapter's consolation to the beginner and its warning:** **in the textbooks you will see many symbols that look like photographs; they are drawings.** **And the person who takes them for photographs tends, in the next class, to ask a question that cannot be answered** — **for instance, 'how exactly was a certain character pronounced three thousand years ago?'** **The right answer is: first ask what you want to use it for.** **This is the historian's version of the line from the logic course, 'first ask the truth-conditions of the question'.**
:::

::: example Five phenomena, five periods {#ex-wujian}
Judge which period each of the following phenomena roughly belongs to, say what evidence you use to judge, and say how strong your judgement is: **(1) fronting of the pronoun object in a negative sentence; (2) the *ba* 把 disposal construction; (3) the plural marker 们; (4) the modal particle 吗; (5) 着 marking continuation** (　　).

::: solution
**(1) Old Chinese.** **In transmitted texts the syntax is productive, and there is a great mass of examples set in pairs.** **By Middle Chinese it is already a prosodic rule of Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*), not of speech.** Strength of the evidence: high, because the synchronic texts are many. (2) **From late Middle Chinese to Early Mandarin.** **Received account: the maturation of the disposal construction is usually placed at the turn of the Tang and the Song; an embryonic form is already seen in the Tang.** Strength of the evidence: middling. It depends on texts that stand close to speech, and texts of that kind are themselves stratified. (3) **Early Mandarin.** **Received account: the usual form of the plural marker is established in Song and Yuan vernacular (*baihua*) materials.** Note that the ages before it have, separately, a number of other forms. (4) **From late Early Mandarin to the modern period.** **Received account: most modal particles of the present come, by grammaticalisation, from earlier negatives or from sentence-final elements; an Old Chinese source for the 吗 type is discussed separately in the field.** **This item is the weakest, because a modal particle appears in the texts later than it appears in speech.** (5) **Middle Chinese.** **Received account: the durative use, and the bleaching of verbs meaning 'to attach', is generally held to have advanced between the Six Dynasties and the Tang.** The mark does not turn on right and wrong, but on whether you can write this sentence. **The conclusion in (1) is strong and the conclusion in (4) is weak, not because the former is more important, but because the former has a countable corpus and the latter has none.** **Modal particles disappear into speech and leave no position in which anything could rhyme, whereas content words can be preserved by verse and by phonetic series.** **This is what, under the title of this chapter, is really to be taught:** **the history of Chinese is not periodised by importance, but by visibility.** **From this follows a precept for writing:** **on such items as modal particles, function words and intonation, any wording of the form 'in such-and-such an age it was simply like this' should be downgraded to 'the surviving materials tend this way'** — **and this sentence can be carried straight across into literary history and into any social history.** **A return visit to the stratification of sources in Lesson 1.**
:::
:::

::: intuition A city that does not exist {#int-chengshi}
**A thought experiment.** **Suppose a man of the seventh century held office in Luoyang: if the accent in which he spoke were recorded, would it agree completely with the categories of the *Qieyun*?** **Answer:** **it is almost impossible that the agreement would be complete.** **Received account: in the long discussion, within the field, of what the *Qieyun* is, one school insists that it is a composite system of reading pronunciations, not the speech of one place.** **This lesson joins none of the schools. It states only the consequence for teaching:** **the most precise body of material we have for Middle Chinese sounds records a pronunciation for reading that crosses regions and that serves the writing of poetry.** **The four characters 中古汉语 thus have two meanings: (1) a system of categories; (2) the accents of people at a given time and place.** **This course uses the term, throughout, only in the first sense.** **It also suggests that, as you read, you do this exercise in substitution:** **whenever you meet 'the pronunciation of people of the Sui and the Tang', change it in your mind to 'the distinctions of category which literati of the Sui and the Tang made when they needed a rhyme'.** **That substitution will make your judgement of the reliability of the whole literature of Chinese historical phonology more accurate, and it will make you all the more sceptical of those claims in literary history that 'a certain poet used a local pronunciation'.** **Received account: claims of that sort often rest on counts of rhyme words, and the rhyme words themselves may be archaising, or a convention of cross-rhyme (*tongya* 通押).** **This is the fourth degree of strength, to which this course keeps returning:** **the most probable explanation, and not a proof.**
:::

::: history Who first cut the three thousand years {#sec-qie}
**The seventeenth to the eighteenth century.** **Qing scholars gathered the characters of Old Chinese verse into a number of rhyme groups, and the fineness of the division gave the history of Chinese a taxonomy before any sound recording existed.** **Received account:** **this lesson insists that this is a rare case in the history of linguistics in the world:** **a dead sound system was reconstructed by counting in texts.** (2) **The 1910s–1920s.** **A Swedish sinologist applied the comparative method and modern phonetics to Chinese, reconstructed Middle Chinese, and carried the reconstruction back to Old Chinese.** **Received account:** **the consequence was to turn the history of Chinese from 'evidential research' (*kaozheng* 考证) into 'a system'.** **It also left a bad habit —** **reading the symbols of a reconstruction as if they were photographs (the 'pipe diagram' comparison above).** (3) **The 1930s–1950s.** **Research on the history of Chinese was divided by field: sound, grammar and the lexicon each came to have a history.** **Received account:** **this lesson points out in particular that the subject of grammatical history came to be established on the strength of a grammatical work whose materials were Japanese medieval texts (日本中古文献) and the Early Mandarin vernacular** (**the received account attributes it to Ōta Tatsuo 太田辰夫**), **because it laid down a rule:** **historical grammar must rest on corpus material that can be dated, and cannot rest on example characters.** (4) **1957.** **A comprehensive work of the *Hanyu shi gao* 汉语史稿 ('Draft history of Chinese') type wrote the four-period framework, and three kinds of change, into the textbooks, and the influence continues to this day.** **Received account.** (5) **The 1970s–1990s.** **Excavated texts appeared in quantity, and the study of Old Chinese sounds and of Old Chinese grammar gained material of a new stratum; and, at the same time, the turning of the comparison of dialects and of related languages into data.** **Received account.** (6) **Now.** **Historical corpora, automatic dating, and quantitative methods.** **At the same time there is a pressure the other way:** **the reliability of a quantitative method depends on how coarse the periodisation is —** **the testable prediction of this chapter:** **over the next twenty years, tables of periods will not grow finer; they will grow less certain.** **For once we can measure more precisely, we shall see that the nine curves were each going their own way all along.** **This sentence and the line from the dialect course, 'the denser the points, the more the regions scatter', are two directions of one and the same theorem.**
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**Treating a periodisation as a real break in the changes** (the nine curves each go their own way; anchor (2) above); **citing only one kind of material** (a conclusion holds only where several lines agree; the nine kinds above); **reading a reconstructed symbol as an accent** (it is a placeholder for a category; 'what cannot be known', (3) above); **writing 'such-and-such an age was so' on an item that is a function word** (modal particles leave no rhyme word; **it should be downgraded to 'the surviving materials tend this way'** (the worked example above)). **This is the only hard requirement about writing in this chapter.**
:::

::: exercise Short essay: periodisation explains historical facts {level=3}
Write between six hundred and a thousand words, assessing the following: '**On the surface, periodisation is description; in fact it is the preparation for explanation. Once you call some fifteen hundred years "Middle Chinese", you have already committed yourself as to which changes belong to this period, which are exceptions that come late, and which phenomena are worth explaining. What is called "it appears only in Early Mandarin" means, most of the time, really "it is visible only in the Early Mandarin corpus". The difference between the two is often nothing but literary form, and the luck of preservation. The virtue that historical linguistics needs most is therefore the courage to turn time-labels back into statements of visibility.**' You must draw on at least six items: **anchored to books**, **the three disputes over boundaries**, **the nine kinds of material and their biases**, **what can be known and what cannot**, **the five phenomena ranked by strength**, **a city that does not exist**. And give a ruling: **if you could draw up the periodisation of Chinese once more, would you keep the period names or abolish them** (say how researchers would communicate once the names were abolished).

::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms; the ruling (**either road will do, but the 'cost of communication' must be dealt with.** **The side that keeps the names** (**an acceptable reason: a period name is a compressed format of citation. To say 'Middle Chinese' is to say 'inside the *Qieyun* system', and for efficiency it has no substitute.** **The cost is that a 'visibility declaration' must be attached** — **marking looks to see whether this patch is proposed.** **The side that abolishes them** (**an acceptable reason: every table of periods is lending authority to the unargued value-word 'enough to end a period'.** **The alternative must be one that can be carried out:** **for example, change to the names of materials (the *Qieyun* layer, the *Zhongyuan yinyun* layer) or to a span of dates.** **The cost is an explosion of terminology and a break in the indexes, and the new terms will soon become new period names in their turn.** (**This sentence is the best answer to the question, because it shows that the pull of naming cannot be resisted.** (**The answer this lesson admires most is a third kind:** **keep the period names, but require that a name be defined at its first appearance:** **'In this essay, Middle Chinese means the period to which applies the system of categories describable from the rhyme dictionaries of the *Qieyun* line, roughly such-and-such centuries, and it does not include a dating judgement at the level of speech.'** (**This is the same rule as 'classification must leave a seam' in the previous chapter, and as 'every number must carry its scope' in the practical-writing course: write the definition in the place where it is used, and not in a glossary.**)); if the conclusion can be written as '**So periodisation is not the frame of the map but the scale of the map: it does not change the terrain, but it decides what terrain you can see. An honest historian will restate it once in the conclusion: the part I have not spoken of is, for the most part, the part where no one wrote a rhyme word down**', that is an A.
:::

::: summary
This chapter hands over four things: **periodisation is division** (**the criteria, the disputes and the redrawing of the lines are isomorphic**), **anchors** (**three books for rhyming; one field of learning about verse**), **nine kinds of material** (**a conclusion holds only where several lines agree**), **drawings and photographs** (**a reconstruction is a category, not an accent**). The method, in one sentence: **read every time-label back into a statement of visibility.** Next chapter: Old Chinese — phonetic series, rhyme groups, and the ages that had no tones.
