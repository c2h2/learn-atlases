---
title: "The formation of Mandarin"
---

The previous chapters asked how a language changed; this one asks how one of its varieties became the thing people used across the country — **and the argument is that it happened mostly without being planned, and that the planning we can document is the part that failed**.

## What "the formation of Mandarin" means {#def-xingcheng}

::: definition Three different questions {#def-sanwen}
**A single dispute is usually three. ① When were the structural changes of the northern varieties completed?** — a question of sound history, answered with rhyme books and transcriptions. **② When did a cross-regional spoken koiné appear, and who used it?** — a question of social history, answered with travelers' remarks, embassy records and schoolbooks. **③ When was it recorded, prescribed and made a standard?** — a question of institution, answered with dictionaries, edicts and examinations. **Each has different evidence, and the literature is full of answers that switch between them mid-paragraph.** This lesson's division of labour: **the first gives the structure, the second gives the distribution, the third gives the name** — and the chapter's thesis is that **in the history of Chinese these three run on separate clocks, which is why any single date is misleading.** If you take one sentence away: **"Mandarin formed in the Ming" is a sentence that can be true, false and meaningless depending on which of the three questions is being answered.**
:::

::: definition One timeline, four anchors {#def-sigeman}
**Anchor ①, Tang to Song**: the earliest signs of the devoicing of the fully voiced initials are inferred from indirect materials, and the entering tone still exists (**standard account**). **② Thirteenth century**: a rhyme book compiled for the drama shows the level tone divided by initial voicing and the entering-tone characters distributed among the other tones; at the same time a script invented for a new empire leaves tablets and wordlists recording the northern reading and official layer of the day (**standard account**). **③ Fourteenth to seventeenth centuries**: the final -m merges into -n in the north; the stop codas disappear there; and the term "official speech" (官话) becomes common in the literature (**standard account**). **④ Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries**: missionary grammars and textbooks, the accent academies of the 1720s, and at the end of the dynasty a textbook of the capital's speech that has been read as a description of the standard (**standard account**). **Note what the anchors are: four kinds of evidence, not four events.** The structure runs ahead of the distribution, the distribution ahead of the name, and the name ahead of the standard — **and every one of them is datable only within its own kind of source.** That is why this chapter prefers the four-anchor form to a period.
:::

::: definition A rhyme book that tried to roll the language back {#def-hongwu}
**A state-commissioned rhyme book of the fourteenth century took the older reading categories as correct and set itself against "vulgar" pronunciation; it was used for examinations and literary work for centuries and never described anyone's speech** (**standard account**). **This lesson calls it the first documented attempt in Chinese history to roll a language back — and asks where it failed. ① It changed nobody's interests**: what an examination candidate needed was an acceptable format and an authority for rhyme, not a new accent; the book supplied the first two, so it survived as a format tool and its phonetic programme was simply unused. **② It had no teachers and no tests**, so nothing carried it into the mouth. **③ Its standard was a text, and a text can be cited without being spoken** — the interface problem of chapter three, arriving in policy form. **The general rule, which the sociolinguistics course states for modern standardisation and this chapter states for the 1370s: a prescription that costs its users something and rewards them with nothing becomes a citation, not a habit.** Compare the next section, which understood this.
:::

::: definition A smarter attempt {#def-zhengyin}
**In the 1720s the throne, troubled by officials whose accent obstructed the business of judging, ordered the training of official speech in several southern provinces, and academies and readers with a pronunciation aim appeared locally** (**standard account**). **This lesson finds this attempt far more interesting than the previous one because it got three things right. ① It aimed at a specific, checkable occasion** — not "speak more elegantly" but "be understood by both parties in the courtroom". **② It used local literate people as teachers, with readers built for speakers of that particular variety, compared word by word** — which is cheaper than an abstract command and harder to evade. **③ It did not try to change the local language at home; it added a second variety for official use** (**standard account**: the policy is usually assessed as a failure in the long run, and the assessment depends on whether the goal was assimilation or additivity). **The lesson generalises: this is the earliest Chinese case I know of bilingual education as language policy, and its modern descendants — any "add a standard variety, keep the dialect" programme — still face the same two fates, additivity or replacement, decided not by method but by what the second variety is good for.** Where the Qing attempt failed is where every such programme fails: **the incentive had to be renewed each generation, and the examination content, not the accent, was what candidates optimised for.**
:::

::: definition When a kind of speech has no name {#def-wuming}
**In Ming and Qing sources "official speech" is a common noun for the spoken medium of cross-regional business; it does not pick out one city's accent, regional versions of it differed, and nobody claimed to speak "the standard official speech"** (**standard account**). **Three observations. ① An unnamed standard is efficient**: precisely because it did not declare itself some city's accent, a man speaking Nanchang official speech and a man speaking Nanjing official speech each conceded nothing — **a name is exclusivity, and exclusivity recruits resistance** (chapter one's "classification is sovereignty", here in reverse: while sovereignty is unsettled, circulation is fastest). **② The variety was portable because it was under-specified**, admitting local phonetic habits within a shared lexicon and grammar — **the same tolerance that makes a lingua franca work makes it impossible to standardise later, since there is no specimen to point at.** ③ **Its speakers were not proud of it**: it was the medium of yamen errands, market and travel, while prestige attached to the classical register and to one's native city (**standard account**), and this is why the sources that mention it are usually complaining about it. **The chapter's methodological note: a variety with no name leaves no self-description, so its history must be written from its users' enemies — manuals, prescriptions, and the jokes.**
:::

::: definition Layers inside official speech {#def-cengci}
**Official speech was not a homogeneous system; it had a reading layer and a spoken layer, and differences of venue between the yamen and the market** (**standard account**). **Three workable readings. ① Read the correspondence materials in layers**: the transcriptions made by sixteenth- and seventeenth-century visitors often record a "literary reading" and an "official-speech colloquial" side by side, and **the difference in their spellings is itself the evidence that two systems existed** — the most underrated corpus in the field, because the compilers did not know they were doing sociolinguistics. **② The literary/colloquial split exists inside official speech too, only shallower than in Min**, so a handful of characters carry two readings whose distribution is a social map (the dialect course's 文白异读, in a northern key). ③ **Register and layer explain most apparent contradictions in the sources**, where one author reports a distinction another says has merged; **they were describing different layers of the same koiné, not disagreeing about the language.** **The operational rule: when two historical witnesses contradict each other, first ask which layer and which venue each was describing, and only then consider that one of them is wrong** — the same rule this course applies to any corpus, including the interview transcripts in your own thesis.
:::

::: example Four materials, one question {#ex-sizhong}
You want to know whether the entering tone had disappeared in the speech of a given city in 1600. **Four sources: a local rhyme manual; a missionary's transcription of words dictated to him; a collection of local-language jokes printed for outsiders; and a family instruction book complaining that young people no longer speak properly. What can each show, and what is the one conclusion none of them can?**

::: solution
**The rhyme manual**: prescriptive and category-bound, it can show that a distinction was still being taught, and its merging of categories would show the opposite; **it cannot show anyone's speech, only a project.** **The missionary transcription**: strongest, because the transcriber had a notation to fill and often remarks on difficulty — **but it is a transcription into a foreign system, so an absent distinction may be an absent letter, and you must check what his script can and cannot write** (chapter three's foreign copies, now as primary material). **The joke book**: printed for outsiders, hence probably pitched between the local variety and the koiné, and its rhymes and puns are evidence about what the audience heard — **puns are the closest thing historical linguistics has to a comprehension test.** **The complaint**: evidence that something changed, with no way of knowing how much (**a prescription reports a deviation**, previous chapter). **The conclusion none supports: that "the people of the city" had lost the distinction — since none of the four records a population, and none distinguishes the reading layer from the market layer.** **Marking: whoever proposes a fifth source — today's dialect, compared systematically — and states the assumption that makes the comparison legitimate takes an A**; that assumption is the comparative method's whole burden, and the chapter ends by refusing to let it be unloaded.
:::
:::

::: intuition A speech expands fastest with no dictionary {#int-wucidian}
**A generalisation this chapter earns the right to make: a variety with no fixed dictionary, no examiner and no specimen accent can spread faster than a standardised one, because adopting it costs the adopter nothing in identity.** Four mechanisms. **① No gatekeepers means no entrance exam**, so adoption is a matter of utility. ② **Under-specification allows local realisation**, so a speaker keeps his accent and still counts as competent — **the tolerance that offends later purists is the engine of the spread.** **③ No name means no allegiance demanded**: learning it is not declared as switching sides. ④ **Its norm is statistical, located in the frequency of its use in a set of venues — the court, the army, the canal, the treaty port** — which is exactly where the historical evidence for it is found and why it is found in other people's complaints. **The corollary is the sad one: standardisation is the point at which a variety stops expanding, because a specimen creates impostors, and a language with impostors develops a market in credentials** (the sociolinguistics course's account of the "correctness industry", and this chapter's answer to anyone who asks why the eighteenth century suddenly produced so many pronunciation books: **the answer is that the koiné had begun to acquire a name, and a name is a claim on other people's speech**).
:::

::: history One road, two cities, one phonograph {#sec-lu}
**Fourteenth to fifteenth centuries**: a unified examination system and the military-post relay network spread a northern-based official speech; the capital moves, and the prestige of a variety moves with the administration rather than with the literature (**standard account**). **Sixteenth to seventeenth centuries**: printed encyclopedias, commercial readers and the missionary presses produce a mass of near-spoken text, and Nanjing and Beijing are both named as the reference cities of acceptable speech — **two cities, one koiné, and the sources disagree by design** (**standard account**). **Eighteenth century**: the accent academies, and the first serious northern phonetic treatises; **the beginning of the transition from an unnamed medium to a named standard** (**standard account**). **Nineteenth century**: the treaty-port pidgins and the vernacular textbooks of the missions put Chinese into writing for people who could not read classical, and the capital's speech becomes the reference point of the northern variety (**standard account**). **Around 1900**: the phonograph and the school recitation make an accent reproducible without a teacher present (**standard account**) — **the technical precondition of the next chapter's story.** **The reading of this sequence: the road (institutions), the two cities (a contested specimen), the phonograph (a technology that ends the argument by changing the medium). A standard is not chosen; it is what is left when the cost of imitating one place falls below the cost of maintaining a koiné.** Next chapter: what happens when the state decides that the written language should follow the spoken one at last.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Answering three questions with one date** (**structure, distribution, institution**); **reading a prescriptive text as a description** (**a complaint reports a deviation**); **treating transcriptions as recordings** (**check what the notation can write**); **assuming that a named standard is older than the koiné** (**the name came last, and it cost the variety its growth**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: standards and their costs {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a spoken koiné spreads because it is cheap to join, and a standard exists because it is expensive to fail. The history of Mandarin is the history of that substitution, and the moment of substitution — when the specimen is chosen, the name is fixed, and the pronunciation becomes examinable — is also the moment the variety stops growing and begins to stratify socially, since now there are people who speak it wrongly, and the mark of their having been educated is the very cost that earlier made the language easy to join.** Deploy at least six of: **the three questions**, **the four anchors**, **the rollback failure**, **the smarter attempt's three right moves**, **the unnamed standard's four efficiencies**, **layers within official speech**, **the four materials of the worked example**, **the road, the cities, the phonograph**. Then adjudicate: **should a language-planning authority prefer an under-specified norm or a specified one, and who pays the difference?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**name the payer**). **Under-specified**: cheap to adopt, wide reach, and no way to certify competence — **the payer is the stranger who cannot tell who is fluent, i.e. the cost is in coordination.** **Specified**: coordination is cheap and identity is at stake — **the payer is everyone whose first variety is not the specimen, and the bill arrives as schooling, employment and self-respect.** **Highest marks for the two-part conclusion: specify late and only for the occasions that need it** — **courtroom and air-traffic control rather than literature and the family table — which is the technical content of the eighteenth century's better instinct and the practical reading of the modern standard's chapter that follows**; **and for the honesty that "specify late" is a slogan the authority cannot actually implement, since the moment the specimen is chosen, some region's children begin paying.**
:::
:::

The chapter delivers: questions (**three, on separate clocks**), anchors (**four kinds of evidence, not four events**), policy (**a rollback fails on interests; a smarter attempt aims at one occasion**), the koiné (**unnamed, under-specified, layered**), method (**read the layer and the venue before you accuse a witness**), and one mechanism: **the standard is what remains when imitation becomes cheaper than negotiation.** Next chapter: the decision to make writing follow speech, and what it cost.
