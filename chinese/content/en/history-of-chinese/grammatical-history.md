title: "History of grammar"
---

Sounds change below the level of decision; grammar changes in public, and somebody writes a complaint about it. **This chapter's argument: the major changes in Chinese grammar are a small set of reanalyses, each of which needed an ambiguous construction to start from, and the reason they look slow is that a literary register was available to catch every form that speech dropped.**

## Record and dating {#def-jilv}

::: definition Datable corpora, and datable manuscripts {#def-duandai}
**The asymmetry that governs everything after this point: a sound change can be inferred from a single book's categories, but a grammatical change needs a sequence of texts whose dates are independent of their language.** **Three hazards. ① The transmitted classics were copied for a thousand years, and a copyist modernises a function word as easily as he modernises a graph, so an "early attestation" in a received text may be a medieval spelling** (**standard account**: collation against excavated manuscripts repeatedly shows this, and the manuscript discoveries of the twentieth century are the field's most important event after the dictionaries). **② Genre determines what is recorded**, so the absence of a construction from a genre is a statement about the genre. **③ dating by the text's claimed date rather than by the manuscript's date** confuses a work with a witness. **The discipline's answer is boring and absolute: cite the corpus, the edition, and the manuscript, and give the date of the witness.** A syntactician writing "in Old Chinese" without naming a text has written about a school's view of a text (the same three-column habit — mechanism, mainstream, dispute — that this course demands of everything else).
:::

::: definition Reanalysis needs an ambiguity {#def-chongfen}
**The engine of grammatical change is reanalysis: a form is reinterpreted, and the new reading then spreads by analogy.** The prerequisite is a construction that legitimately has two parses, and Chinese history is unusually well supplied with them because the lack of inflection leaves the surface string unchanged when the structure changes. **Worked pattern**: a verb of completion in a chain ("he finished the rice left") is a second predicate in one reading and an aspect marker in another; **the string does not change, the grammar does.** Then three rules of evidence. **① An ambiguity in the corpus is not proof of reanalysis**, only a possible site — **the change is proven when the new reading appears in a context the old one forbids**, e.g. when the element begins to attach to verbs it could not formerly follow. ② **The rate of spread is not the rate of the change**, since a reanalysis can sit latent for centuries in one genre and then be recorded within decades of another (the previous chapter's register point, used here as method). **③ Two changes may share a site and be confused**, which is why a history of a single function word often turns out to be two histories. **This lesson's summary of the mechanism: reanalysis redraws the blueprint; analogy builds to the new drawing, and only the building leaves datable evidence.**
:::

::: definition Aspect: a new machine {#def-timao}
**Where Old Chinese signalled time largely by lexical means and context, the later language acquires a post-verbal apparatus for the phase of an event — the perfective, the durative, the experiential, and a set of resultative and directional elements.** **Three points worth teaching. ① The sources are ordinary verbs**: completion, arrival, obtaining, "holding" — so the apparatus is transparently a second-hand kit, and its pieces retain their old shapes in other uses, which is how a learner (and a historian) can see the joints. **② The machine was assembled at different dates for each piece**, and a text from any one year shows a partly built system — **a strong argument against dating "the change to analytic" as an event.** ③ **The constructional meaning is not the sum of the parts**, which is the aspect system's standing lesson for the syntax-semantics boundary: a durative marker inherits its sense from a verb of contact or attachment (**standard account**, with details argued). **And a warning against the temptation of the field: to write "the language became more analytic" as if analyticity were a destination.** The apparatus is a set of separate solutions to separate problems — sequencing, duration, experience, result — and the fact that they all ended up post-verbal is a property of the language's phrase structure, not a tendency of language in general.
:::

::: definition The disposal, the passive, and the copula {#def-chuzhi}
**Three constructions whose history is the best-documented in the language. ① The disposal construction**, in which an agent takes an object and hands it to a second verb — **grammaticalised from a verb meaning "handle", attested in the near-spoken record and completing in the vernacular sources** (**standard account**). **② The passive**, built from verbs of suffering or of giving, with a regional stratigraphy that survives in the modern dialects — **one variety's passive marker is another's benefactive**. **③ The copula**, which is the longest case study in the language: a demonstrative and an ending word, then a nominal "is", acquire connective function in the centuries around the common-era beginning, with the negative and the locative forms taking over this job in different places (**standard account**). **Two generalisations. First, every one of these sources is a full verb with its own career still running, so the older use is evidence you must exclude token by token — a frequency count over an undifferentiated corpus is a count of two constructions.** Second, **the modern language keeps several markers of the same function side by side, distributed by dialect, register and verb class; the history is therefore not the replacement of one system by another but the accumulation of alternatives, which is the opposite of how textbooks present it.**
:::

::: definition Classifiers: a late grammaticalisation {#def-liangci}
**Numerals in the oldest texts count nouns freely, and the modern requirement that a classifier stand between numeral and noun is a later development, reaching its present generality late** (**standard account**). **Why the case is instructive. ① It is gradual and uneven**: classifiers first appear for some nouns — long objects, flat objects, people — and extend by analogy over centuries, and the extension to abstract nouns is recent. **② It is semantically motivated and therefore unpredictable**: a classifier records a shape, a function or a cultural grouping, and the set of classifiers in a variety is a small ethnography (**the vocabulary course's observation that the classifier system makes the language classify the world, seen in the making**). **③ It shows the direction of the whole field's caution: an absent construction in an old text may be absent because the text's genre avoided the contexts where it occurs**, and for classifiers the counting contexts are precisely the ones the transmitted classics under-represent. **Add the social fact: the classifier inventory of a learned register is partly a bookish inheritance, so the same speaker may use a traditional classifier in writing and another in speech** — the register split again, now inside a single word choice.
:::

::: example Put them in order {#ex-xulie}
Five constructions, undated, from the same broad tradition: **a** a post-verbal durative marker; **b** a disposal construction with a verb of handling; **c** a classifier obligatory with a counted noun; **d** a sentential negation before the verb; **e** a copula in equative sentences. **Put them in the order of their generalisation and name the criterion you used, the one you would reverse first, and the item that cannot be dated from the classics at all.**

::: solution
**Defensible order: d is inherited and applies at the start; e is the earliest of the innovations (attested in the early centuries of the common era in near-spoken and translated material); then a; then b; then c, whose full generality is the latest** (**standard account**: the details, and the relative order of a and b, are argued). **Criterion: the date at which each is obligatory in the genres that record speech, not the date of its first occurrence.** **The one to reverse first: a before b, since the aspect markers and the disposal construction both grow out of verb serialization and their relative chronology depends on which serial pattern you take as prior** — **a real disagreement, not a matter of taste.** **The item that cannot be dated from the transmitted classics: c**, because the counting contexts are rare in that corpus and the received texts were copied by people who modernised (**standard account**). **Marking: whoever states the criterion as a rule about genres rather than about centuries takes an A** — this is the chapter's method in one line, and it is the same answer as the previous chapter gave about first attestations.
:::
:::

::: intuition The literary register is a delay device {#int-yanshi}
**The recurring surprise of Chinese grammatical history: speech changes and the written language keeps the older forms, and this is not conservatism but infrastructure.** Four consequences of treating the classical register as a delay line. **① Two grammars coexist and each is complete**: a writer chooses a construction the way a modern writer chooses a tense, and the choice is meaningful. **② Change is recorded late and then suddenly**, because the moment a genre admits the spoken form the historian sees a "rapid" shift. **③ The corpus must be ranked by its proximity to speech** — which is a claim about genre, and the ranking is contestable, and it must be stated. ④ **The result is the peculiar texture of the modern standard, which uses forms of three ages side by side: a classical conjunction in a bureaucratic phrase, a vernacular aspect marker, and an imported pattern from translation. A learner feels this as "style"; a historian sees three datations in one sentence** (the next two chapters and the applied-writing course on register all cash this out). **The rule this lesson wants: whenever the dates of a grammatical change look wrong, ask what was holding the old form up, and name the institution.**
:::

::: history From a grammar to a corpus {#sec-wentong}
**Late nineteenth century**: a grammar of the classical language is written on the model of Latin, and is mocked for the model while establishing the questions — what are the parts of speech of Chinese, and how is their order to be stated (**standard account**: the book is conventionally the opening event of Chinese grammar as a discipline). **1920s–1940s**: studies of the vernacular and of the recorded lectures apply the new descriptive methods to near-spoken corpora, and a school of grammatical history is founded (**standard account**). **1950s**: the state language programme standardises grammar teaching, and school grammar becomes a second force shaping what counts as a construction (**standard account**). **Late twentieth century**: dialect surveys and then parsed corpora allow change to be measured rather than illustrated; **the function-word frequency lists and the dating of the classifiers become quantitative projects** (**standard account**). **Twenty-first century**: digitised full-text corpora with claimed dating, and a reaction against them by scholars who point out that the corpora are undated at the level of the witness (**standard account**: the hazard of the first section, arriving with better computers). **The arc: from a foreign model to a national curriculum to a database, and the same three questions each time — what is the date of this text, what did its genre permit, and is this one construction or two.** That is what this chapter has been teaching, and it is why the history of Chinese grammar is a good training in method rather than a body of results.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Trusting a received text's date** (**cite the witness**); **reading an ambiguity as a change** (**find the context the old parse forbids**); **"the language became analytic"** (**a set of separate solutions**); **frequency counts across constructional homonyms** (**exclude token by token, or report two curves**).
:::

::: exercise Find the site {level=1}
Take a function word of your own language and identify the construction where two parses were once both available; write the two readings.

::: solution
Marking: **the two readings must both fit the surface string**; a paraphrase difference fails (reanalysis needs an ambiguity).
:::

::: exercise Split a curve {level=2}
Count one form in a corpus you can access, then split the counts by genre; report whether you have one change or two.

::: solution
Marking: **two curves are the expected result**, and a single curve must be defended by showing the genres move together (the classifier warning).
:::

::: exercise Essay: why Chinese grammar looks slow {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the standard story — that Chinese grammar changed little for two thousand years — is an artefact of the survival of a written register, and that the correct story is a language whose speech ran ahead of its writing by a millennium. The two narratives are empirically indistinguishable if you read only the classics, which is why the field's real turn came with new corpora and not with new theory.** Deploy at least six of: **the three dating hazards**, **reanalysis and its proof**, **the partly built aspect machine**, **the three constructions and their verb sources**, **the late classifier**, **the delay device**, **the four-question history section**. Then adjudicate: **if you had to teach the history of Chinese grammar from one principle, which would it be, and what would you refuse to teach in the same course?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**the refusal must be named**). **A passing principle: the date of a form in a text is not the date of the change.** **Stronger versions are methodological: "genre is the first dating evidence", or "there is no grammar without a register".** **The refusal that earns the highest marks is the one about the modern standard: do not present the present language as the outcome of this history as though the outcome were a resolution**, since **the twentieth century's standardisation re-opened the register question rather than closing it (next two chapters), and a course that ends on "modern Chinese has these features" implicitly asserts a settlement that the sources of the 1920s and the 1980s both dispute.** **Bonus: noticing that the same claim about delay applies to legal, medical and religious Latin, and that the comparative question — what happens to a language with a trans-regional written standard — is the one Chinese historical linguistics is best placed to answer and least often asked.**
:::
