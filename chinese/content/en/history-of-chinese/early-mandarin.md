title: "Early Mandarin"
---

Up to here the evidence was books about sound. **From this chapter the evidence changes character: textbooks for foreigners, libretti, primers, and the accidental records of people who had to write down how words were actually said — and the change of material is why the thirteenth century is treated as a boundary**.

## A change of material {#def-cailiao}

::: definition Five kinds of text that had to write speech {#def-wulei}
**① Language-teaching texts for speakers of other languages**, including those produced under Mongol and later rule, where the target was spoken Chinese and the notation had to be explicit. **② Drama libretti and their performance manuals**, which must respect the singing syllable: a treatise on stage pronunciation of the fourteenth century already sorts rhyme categories for the stage (**standard account**). **③ Primers and common-letter manuals**, whose vocabulary is daily life. **④ Rhyme books that describe living practice**, above all a 1324 work that re-divides the old categories and states that the entering tone has been redistributed (**standard account**). **⑤ Transcriptions of Mongol and other foreign words into characters**, which are experiments in using Chinese letters as a phonetic script and are unusually frank about what sounds the transcriber could hear (**standard account**). **Why the difference matters: the older corpus was produced by people who did not think they were describing speech.** Here we have people whose job was to make speech reproducible, which is why this chapter's evidence is better and its scholarship is more careful about what "better" means: **these texts are prescriptive, regionally placed, and often written for outsiders who over- or under-distinguish. Every one of them is a witness with an interest.**
:::

::: definition Three changes: devoicing, tone redistribution, the -m coda {#def-sanbian}
**① The voiced obstruents of the medieval standard devoice**, so that a series of initials collapses — **the date and order differ by region, which is why modern Wu keeps what the north lost and why the tonal split of the previous chapter's correspondence is now visible in the data.** **② The entering tone is redistributed**: syllables ending in a stop lose the stop and their tones fall in with other categories, unevenly — **a single irregular correspondence which becomes the sharpest tool in dialect classification, since which category an entering-tone character landed in is nearly a signature of a variety** (**standard account**). **③ The -m nasal merges with -n in most northern speech while southern varieties keep the two apart** (**standard account**: a change that can be watched in the rhyme materials and in transcriptions). **Note what the three changes have in common: they are all losses of a distinction, and the losses are not simultaneous, so the evidence has to be ordered item by item rather than dated as a system.** **And note the argumentative rule: a change is attested when the sources stop distinguishing, and the source may be silent for reasons other than silence of the ear. The safe form is a statement about the text: "in this 1324 work the two categories are merged", and only then, with other witnesses, a statement about speech.**
:::

::: definition The sharp-flat question and one open case {#def-xuana}
**The standard account**: medieval sibilant series distinguished "sharp" and "round" groups, and in much of the modern north the two have merged before the same vowels — a change still unfinished in some varieties, still absent in others, and datable in the stage manuals. **Why the case matters methodologically: the merger is a chain shift in miniature, and its stages leave different traces in different texts, so the same change can be "already complete" in one source and "in progress" in another, without contradiction.** **The open question the chapter leaves open: how far the change had spread in the capital area at a given date, and whether the standard of the court and the speech of the city had partly come apart; the sources are elite, prescriptive and reluctant to record what they disapprove of** (**standard account**: this is the state of the question). **Two lessons. ① A single isogloss is not a language; a change spreads item by item and region by region, so a history of a sound change is a set of maps, not a date.** ② **Prescriptive silence is evidence of a different kind**: a grammar that forbids a merger tells you the merger was alive among the people the grammar was written against (the rhetoric course's rule that a prescription is a report of a deviation, three centuries early).
:::

::: definition Six new formats {#def-liujian}
**Grammar shows the change more dramatically than sound, because the function words were replaced wholesale. Six formats that become visible in the near-spoken record: ① the perfective 了 after the verb; ② the durative 著/着; ③ the determiner use of 底/的 growing out of a possessive; ④ sentence-final mood particles stacking; ⑤ the new classifiers; ⑥ the ba-construction, in which a verb takes a second object-like argument whose treatment is still the subject of argument** (**standard account**: the sources for these include the recorded lectures of Song thinkers, which are the closest thing we have to transcripts). **Two rules for reading them. First, do not date a construction by its first appearance in writing**; the written record follows the spoken one by an unpredictable interval, and the interval is genre-dependent, so a "first attestation" is a statement about the corpus. **Second, do not assume that a new form replaces the old one: the older literary construction continues in the literary register, and the two coexist as a stylistic pair, which is exactly the modern situation of "the same grammar in two registers" that the modern-grammar course treats as synchronic variation.** **This is the deep point of the chapter: the history of Chinese syntax is largely the history of a register split, and most "change" you will read about is a form moving from the spoken column to the written one, or being pushed out of it.**
:::

::: definition Panel data from a textbook {#def-mianban}
**A language-teaching book of the later period is the closest thing historical linguistics gets to a panel study: its categories are explicit, its comparisons are between varieties, and its examples are sentences someone had to be able to say.** **Three uses. ① It records what a contemporary thought was difficult**, which is nearly always an interface between two systems, and difficulty is a better diagnostic of difference than elegance. **② It lists the vocabulary of a domain** — marketplace, law office, letters — **which is where the lexical history of the next chapter gets its dating evidence.** ③ **Its errors and its commentary betray the author's own variety**, since a writer correcting a mistake has just produced two forms. **The limits: a textbook is a claim about a standard, its examples may be traditional, and it addresses readers whose speech we do not know.** **The general method, which the whole course has been building: treat a source as a witness with a purpose, extract from it what it could not help revealing, and keep its assertions in a separate column from its evidence.** You did this with inscriptions and with rhyme books; here it is easier, because the author is trying, and failing, to be explicit.
:::

::: example Six sentences, four questions {#ex-liuju}
Six constructions from the near-spoken record (**invented here in the shape of real ones**): **1** "ate the rice, then left." **2** "he is sleeping on the bed" (durative). **3** "the one who came is my brother." **4** "take that letter and post it." **5** "three sheets of paper." **6** "did he go or not?" **For each: which of the six new formats is at work, and then answer four questions — when was it spoken, when was it written, what replaced what, and who is the source's audience?**

::: solution
**1 perfective 了; 2 durative; 3 nominalising 的 (from a possessive); 4 the ba-construction; 5 a numeral-classifier system extended to a new noun; 6 the A-not-A question with a sentence-final particle.** **The four questions: the spoken date is unknown and earlier than the written one; the written date is the corpus's date, and only that; the replacement is partial — the older literary means of nominalisation or perfect continues in literary prose alongside the new one, so the pair is a register contrast and not a succession;** and **the audience determines reliability: a text teaching northerners to read southern documents preserves southern syntax as a foreign object, and a text teaching foreigners preserves northern speech as a standard.** **Marking: whoever answers that the four questions have different answers for each of the six sentences takes an A** — **there is no single "time when the language became vernacular", which is the assumption the chapter exists to break** (see the history section for how that assumption was installed).
:::
:::

::: intuition Hearing the person, not the style {#int-ting}
**A phenomenon worth testing on yourself: read a recorded lecture of a Song thinker and a formal parallel-prose memorial of the same decade, and notice that one of them has a person in it.** The difference is not that one is "closer to speech" in any measurable sense; it is that the genre allowed hesitations, restarts, address to a listener, and the first person. **Three things to listen for in old texts. ① The address forms and their shifts**, which record hierarchy more honestly than any institutional history. **② The discourse particles**, which are almost never written unless the genre permits, and which are where a speaker's attitude survives. **③ The unfinished sentence**, rare in print and common in the recorded-lecture genre. **This is not a method but a sensitivity, and the course asks for it for a specific reason: the entire argument of this chapter is that the register split does most of the work that historians of language used to attribute to time, and that argument is easier to misuse than to apply.** The guard rail: **never write "this shows that people said" — write "this genre admits a feature that the other genre forbids", and let two or three genres converge before you say anything about mouths.**
:::

::: history Why the thirteenth century is a boundary {#sec-bianjie}
**Early period**: the rhyme-book system describes a literary reading standard, and the classical register is fixed (the classical-chinese course's account of literary Sinitic). **Eighth to tenth centuries**: the new function words appear in the near-spoken genres. **Tenth to thirteenth**: the north is under non-Han rule for centuries, contact with Mongolic and Turkic languages is administrative as well as popular, and the teaching literature for spoken Chinese begins — **a change in what was recorded, not only in what was spoken** (**standard account**). **1324**: a rhyme book redistributes the old categories and states the loss of the entering tone (**standard account**) — **the date most textbooks give for "Middle Chinese ends".** **Fourteenth to seventeenth**: the vernacular novel, and with it a large corpus that no longer needs apology; stage manuals refine the standard of singing (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: scholars write the history of the change, and a school of grammatical study of the new corpus is founded by men who read the recorded lectures as data (**standard account**). **The last item is the chapter's point: the boundary is drawn where the corpus changes and where a generation of scholars needed it to be, since a period is a claim about discontinuity and evidence of that kind was only assembled in the twentieth century.** **This does not make the boundary arbitrary — the sound changes above really are completed in this window — but it makes the boundary a claim about evidence, and you should cite the corpus when you use it, not the century.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Dating a construction by first attestation** (**a statement about the corpus**); **reading merger as replacement** (**the pair is a register contrast**); **treating a textbook as a transcript** (**a claim about a standard, with the author's variety leaking**); **assuming one boundary date** (**1324 is a book, and the book is the evidence**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: the boundary problem {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **periods in the history of a language are decisions about evidence, and they are argued on grounds that are partly linguistic and partly institutional: what corpus a generation of scholars could work with, and what the examination system needed a period to be. Once a boundary is textbooked, it becomes a fact about the past, and the work of showing that the change was gradual, uneven and register-bound has to be redone by each generation against the same dates.** Deploy at least six of: **the five kinds of text**, **the three sound changes**, **the unfinished merger and its maps**, **the six formats and the register split**, **the textbook as panel data**, **the listening exercise**, **the 1324 case**. Then adjudicate: **if you were writing a grammar of "Early Mandarin", what would you call the object, and what would you refuse to say about it?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**the refusal must be explicit**). **A naming**: something like "the northern spoken registers recorded in the sources of the twelfth to seventeenth centuries", and the **refusal** that follows from this chapter is the point: **no single variety, no completion date for any change, and no statement that writing reflects speech without naming the genre that permits it**. **Highest marks for adding the second refusal: do not present the literary register as the same language at an earlier stage**, since **the two were available together and chose between them stylistically, which is a sociolinguistic claim about a historical corpus and is the part that most textbooks still omit** — and this is also why the modern-standard chapter will have to say that the "victory" of the vernacular was in fact a re-collapsing of a split that had lasted a millennium.
:::
