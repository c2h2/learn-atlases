title: "History of the lexicon"
---

Sounds change and nobody notices; words change and somebody writes a complaint. **This chapter is about the largest and least datable change in the vocabulary — the growth of disyllabic words — and about the four things a historian of words must refuse to say.**

## Disyllabification {#def-shuangyin}

::: definition Four explanations, weighed {#def-sizhong}
**The fact: over the first millennium and a half of the record, the share of disyllabic words in prose rises steadily, and in many domains the monosyllabic form disappears from ordinary use.** Four explanations are on offer, and this course weighs them rather than choosing. **① Phonological compensation**: as final consonants and clusters were lost, homophony increased, and a second syllable restores distinctness — **strong, because it correlates with the sound changes of the previous two chapters; weak because disyllabification continues long after the changes stop.** **② A stylistic drive toward parallel pairs**, native to the rhetoric of balanced prose — **strong for literary vocabulary; weak because the trend shows in near-spoken genres too.** **③ Lexical competition and idiom freeze**: pairs that began as two words or as synonyms hardened into one unit — **well attested item by item, and the mechanism one can actually watch.** ④ **A change in the morphology of word-formation**, with the compound becoming the default means of making a new word — **the least testable and the most explanatory.** **The rule: a change with four adequate causes is not a mystery but a signal that the four causes operate on different corpora; separate them by genre and period before you add them.**
:::

::: definition Three tools {#def-sange}
**① Frequency over time in a bounded corpus** — and the first lesson is that the number is meaningless until the corpus is bounded: **a "rise" of a word may be the survival of a text type**, and the record thickens arbitrarily (the applied-writing course's dictum that statistics need a sampling frame, and the dialect course's "the resolution of the map is the resolution of the funding"). **② Diagnostic contexts**: a pair is a word when it stops allowing internal modification, when a particle cannot be inserted, when it takes an object as a unit — **criteria you must state, because the answer "is this one word or two?" is only as good as the test used.** **③ Stratigraphy of the sources**: Buddhist translations, technical writing, official documents and the near-spoken genres each import and coin at different rates, and **a word's first appearance in a religious translation may be its first appearance anywhere, which is an argument about networks of translators, not about the thing named.** **Three tools, and all three are weaker than they look; that is the honest condition of the field, and it is why the next section is a list of conflicts.**
:::

::: definition Four criteria that fight {#def-sitiao}
**① Position** (does the element stand where words stand?), **② independence** (can it occur alone?), **③ cohesion** (can anything come inside?), **④ meaning** (is the whole not compositional?). **They disagree constantly, and the disagreement is not confusion but the record of a change in progress: an element can be semantically fused before it is syntactically bound, or bound while still analysable to some speakers.** **Two rules. First: name your criterion in the sentence that makes the claim** — "in the third century this pair counts as a word under criterion ③ but not ②" is a statement that can be checked, while "it had become a word" is a wish. **Second: expect layering, because the criteria resolve at different dates for different items, and the modern dictionary's treatment of a form often preserves a decision made for another criterion** (**standard account**: the word-division rules of modern dictionaries are the subject of a standing argument, and the classical-chinese course's segmentation problem is the same problem read backwards). **This is the chapter's transferable lesson: a category boundary in a historical corpus is not vague, it is a two-dimensional map of where each criterion has reached.**
:::

::: definition Old words do not retire {#def-tuichang}
**A monosyllable displaced in speech survives in compounds, in set phrases, in surnames, in place names and in the standard expressions of the written register — so "replaced" is nearly always wrong, and "restricted to other functions" is nearly always right.** **Three consequences. ① A history of words is not an account of deaths but of register migrations**: the old form moves from free use to bound use, and its new habitat is a clue to the date of the change (**the fossil in the compound is the evidence; the vanished word in the dictionary is an artefact of the dictionary's genre**). ② **Idiom freeze is the mechanism that preserves the old meaning**, which is why a modern reader of set phrases meets grammar two thousand years old (the idiom chapter of the vocabulary course, read historically). ③ **Frequency counts mislead in exactly this place**: a form may fall out of prose and rise in commentary, because commentary is where the old language is being explained. **The rule this course prefers: never write "X was replaced by Y" without saying in which genres, for which meanings, and by which corpus you know.**
:::

::: definition Three layers of borrowing {#def-jieceng}
**The vocabulary has acquired its foreign layers in three distinguishable waves. ① Early contact vocabulary with the languages of Inner Asia, mostly nouns for things that arrived — animals, implements, titles, office terms** (**standard account**: some items are secure, many proposed etymologies are not, and the debate is a literature in itself). **② Buddhist translation**, the largest and best documented: **transliteration** (sounds rendered by characters chosen for their sound), **semantic translation** (an existing word loaded with a new sense), and **native coinage** (a new compound made to hold a foreign concept) — **three techniques that are visible in the translation colophons, which discuss the choice** (**standard account**). Its reach goes far beyond religion: abstraction, aspect and a large part of ordinary discourse vocabulary belong to this stratum (**standard account**). **③ Modern Japanese-mediated terms** of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — a layer of the peculiar kind, since **the coinages were made in Chinese characters by Japanese writers using Chinese materials, so they are simultaneously native and borrowed, and the argument about which ones are "loans" is an argument about the direction of the traffic and the awareness of the user** (**standard account**). **A fourth layer is current and unstudied at the level of daily practice: the untranslated English of interfaces, brands and office speech.** **Two things to notice. The techniques of layer ② are the techniques of layer ③, which is why the modern layer is invisible to native speakers — it uses the native machinery. And a loanword is never only a word: it arrives with a domain, a prestige and a set of collocates.**
:::

::: example One word or two? {#ex-ci2ci}
A pair in a text of the fifth century occurs in these environments: **a)** as a sequence with nothing between the elements, 120 times; **b)** with an element of the pair fronted for focus, 4 times; **c)** as the second element taking its own object, 9 times; **d)** in parallel position against a known disyllabic word, 60 times. **Decide, and write the sentence you would actually put in a paper.**

::: solution
**a** favours cohesion (criterion ③); **b** is a genuine counter-argument, since internal fronting is what two separate words allow — four tokens against a hundred and twenty is a real ratio to report, not a nuisance; **c** favours two words, or an analysable compound with a verb-object origin; **d** is evidence about the metre of the genre, not about the lexicon, and should be excluded from the argument though reported. **The sentence: "under criterion ③ the pair is largely bound by the fifth century, with residual analysability visible in four focus contexts and in nine object-taking uses; under criterion ② the elements remain independent; I treat it as a compound in the process of formation, and the process is not datable from this corpus."** **Marking: whoever writes the residual cases into the conclusion rather than into a footnote takes an A**, because **that is the only way the next scholar can use the number** — and the whole chapter's tool section reduces to one duty: **report what your criterion could not classify.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Your word list is your curriculum vitae {#int-lixian}
**Take any ten words you use for your field's technical business and ask when each entered the language, in what kind of text, and who introduced it.** You will find that **a surprising number are twentieth-century coinages, that several are translations of foreign terms made by a small number of people in a short decade, and that a few are Buddhist.** **What the exercise teaches: the vocabulary you experience as neutral and permanent is a set of decisions with dates, and you inherited the dates from a curriculum.** Three consequences for a historian of words. **① Anachronism is not a matter of old words but of new ones**: reading a modern abstraction back into a classical argument is the commonest error in the field, and it is invisible because the modern word is transparent to you. **② Your own vocabulary is a source**: the words you cannot avoid using in a definition are the ones your discipline has not examined. ③ **Terminology debates in your field are usually disguised history-of-lexicon arguments** — about whether a term's etymology should constrain its use (the rhetoric course's "whoever controls the name controls the argument", applied to your own desk).
:::

::: history An account that is never closed {#sec-zhang}
**First centuries**: the earliest wordlists — a glossary arranged by topic, and later commentaries glossing archaisms — establish the genre of explaining old words (**standard account**). **Seventh to ninth centuries**: classical exegesis and its glossaries accumulate the evidence that Qing scholars will systematise (**standard account**). **First to tenth centuries**: the Buddhist translation programmes run for centuries with colophons recording terminology decisions (**standard account**) — **the longest documented terminological engineering project in the tradition.** **Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries**: evidential scholarship applies phonetic and textual method to old words and reconstructs meanings by systematic comparison (**standard account**). **Late nineteenth century**: the Japanese-mediated layer and the first "term" dictionaries, and public argument about which terms a national language should use (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: historical dictionaries built on dated citations, and with them the rule that a word's history is a sequence of attestations; then word-frequency corpora (**standard account**). **Twenty-first century**: full-text databases make the citation-search nearly free, and **the errors have migrated accordingly — from missing evidence to unbounded corpora and unexamined criteria** (**standard account**). **The moral of the chapter is in the last line: the tools improved and the class of mistakes moved one step down the chain, which is what happens in every empirical field, including the one you are about to write your thesis in.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Counting frequency in an unbounded corpus** (**a rise may be a survival rate**); **"X was replaced by Y"** (**say the genres and the meanings**); **choosing a criterion silently** (**name it in the sentence**); **reading your own technical vocabulary as timeless** (**the anachronism is in the new words**).
:::

::: exercise Date ten words {level=1}
Take ten words of your own field, find their earliest use you can document, and classify the evidence for each as bounded or unbounded.

::: solution
Marking: **the bounded ones are the findings**; an unbounded "first appearance" must be rewritten as "earliest in this corpus" (the three tools).
:::

::: exercise Apply two criteria {level=2}
Take a pair in a text you know and test it under two criteria that disagree; write the two verdicts and the sentence that reports both.

::: solution
Marking: **the reporting sentence must contain the word "criterion"**, or it has not reported anything (four criteria).
:::

::: exercise Essay: the lexicon refuses periods {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the vocabulary does not cooperate with the periods of a language, because words change for reasons that belong to trade, religion, administration and fashion rather than to the internal life of a sound system. A history of words is therefore the most social and the least elegant branch of the discipline, and the least quotable — which is exactly why it is the best check on the periodisations that the phonologists and the grammarians agree among themselves.** Deploy at least six of: **the four explanations**, **the three tools**, **the fighting criteria**, **register migration**, **the three borrowing layers**, **the anachronism of new words**, **the migration of errors in the history section**. Then adjudicate: **should a periodisation of Chinese be drawn primarily on phonological, grammatical or lexical criteria — and what does your choice cost?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**the cost must be stated**). **Phonological**: datable, measurable, and the criteria are the ones the tradition itself invented — **cost: it periodises a reading standard rather than speech, and is silent about the vocabulary a speaker actually commands**. **Grammatical**: closest to what a language is — **cost: the register split means the "change" is often a migration between written genres, as the previous chapter argued**. **Lexical**: richest in social evidence and best dated by documents — **cost: no boundaries, because the layers arrive at different rates in different domains, which is the subject of this chapter and the reason the adjudication cannot end well**. **Highest marks for the answer that picks a criterion per question — phonology for the history of the standard, grammar for the history of the register, lexicon for the history of concepts — and refuses the single periodisation, since that is the only position that does not require pretending the register split does not exist.**
:::
