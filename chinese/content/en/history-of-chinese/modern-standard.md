title: "Modern standard Chinese"
---

The previous chapter's change happened by argument; this one happened by definition, catalogue and machine. **Its thesis: a standard language is not a corpus of texts but a set of institutions, and the interesting question about Putonghua is not what its rules are but who executes them, at what cost, and to what tolerance.**

## Three phrases, three traditions {#def-dingyi}

::: definition What the definition says {#desange}
**The standard definition, current since 1955–1956, has three clauses: the phonology is based on the Beijing dialect, the vocabulary on the northern dialects, and the grammar on modern exemplary vernacular writings** (**standard account**). **Read it as a settlement of three arguments rather than as a description. ① The Beijing clause** — after decades in which a standard was sought above regionalism, the capital's speech was adopted with the qualifier "based on", which keeps the right to exclude local items; **the phrase is a legal instrument, not a linguist's statement.** **② The northern-dialects clause** concedes that vocabulary is wider than one city. **③ The grammar clause is the most remarkable: it names no dialect at all and instead delegates the norm to a class of texts, "exemplary writings" — which makes the standard of correctness a canon, and the canon a matter for editors and examiners.** **Three consequences worth spelling out: a standard defined partly by models is more conservative than one defined by rules; a learner acquires it by reading the approved set; and disputes are settled by citation rather than by elicitation** (compare the grammar course's complaint that "exemplary usage" is a circular criterion; this chapter says the circularity is deliberate and cheap).
:::

::: definition Who executes it {#def-zhixing}
**A rule nobody applies is a wish. The instruments of Putonghua are: the school timetable and the teacher's certificate; the broadcast and now the streaming licence; the broadcaster's pronunciation and the character of the subtitle; the proficiency test that a graduate in a language-related field may need to sit; the orthography lists and the pronouncing standards revised repeatedly; and the input method on your phone** (**standard account**). **This lesson calls the last two the most important and least studied: the printed standard and the software default.** **Three observations. ① Enforcement is thickest exactly where it is cheapest** — broadcasting, school, official documents — **and thinnest where speech is intimate, which is why the sociolinguistic map of competence is a map of institutions, not of attitudes.** ② **The standard's vocabulary is fixed by lists that are revised**, so a teacher's authority on a doubtful form is a function of which year's list she was trained on (the pronouncing-standard revisions, with their public controversies, are the best evidence of how unsettled the "standard" is below its headline). **③ Execution requires mistakes to be identifiable**, so the standard produces a class of forms labelled wrong — and many are simply the living variety, which is the dialect course's argument from the other side. **The rule: to know what a standard is, find its examiners; if you cannot name a person whose job depends on it, you have found a norm of etiquette.**
:::

::: definition The one that worked: pinyin {#def-pinyin}
**A Latin-letter phonetic alphabet was designed by a committee, formally approved in 1958, and has outlived every expectation its designers stated, including its designers' own modest one** (**standard account**: the original long-run hopes about a phonetic script were abandoned in practice within a generation). **Why it succeeded. ① It was made useful before it was made ideological** — literacy classes, dictionary ordering, telegraphy and place-name spelling, and later the input method. **② It never had to defeat the characters; it was positioned as an aid to them**, which removed the objection that it would cut readers off from the archive. ③ **It was written with a native speaker's tolerance for two-to-one sound-to-letter mappings and with an eye to what a beginner could learn**, and its choice of spellings is a set of compromises that mostly please northerners — **a national instrument designed with northern bias and received as neutral** (compare the dialect course's argument that a standard is a dialect with an army and a navy). **The unintended consequence: it became the interface through which hundreds of millions type Chinese, so that a phonetic alphabet designed to teach children to read now mediates most writing in the language — the most consequential script change of the twentieth century, achieved by a keyboard and never voted on.**
:::

::: definition The half-done one: simplification {#def-jianhua}
**Simplified characters were standardised in 1956 with later lists and a 1986 reprint that withdrew some newly simplified forms** (**standard account**). **Four things to hold apart. ① The technique is old**: most simplifications are received cursive or popular variants, so the reform legislated what was already written rather than inventing shapes. ② **The goal was literacy, and the evidence on whether fewer strokes teach faster is weak** (**standard account**: the pedagogical argument was never settled by the kind of study that would settle it). **③ The cost fell on continuity**: a reader trained on one list reads pre-1956 printing with friction, and across the region boundary the same word is written with a different set of forms — **a cost that is not paid by the reform's authors and is paid for ever, which is the signature of a half-done language plan.** ④ **The 1986 withdrawal is the most instructive event in the whole programme: a state reversed part of its own standardisation after a consultation**, which is close to unprecedented, and which is why the current list is treated as settled when the process that produced it demonstrably was not. **The rule: script reform is judged not by elegance but by the number of readers it strands per year of literacy gained, and nobody has published that accounting for any reform, anywhere.**
:::

::: definition The pending one: pronunciation review {#def-shenyin}
**Pronunciation review — deciding, character by character and word by word, which reading is standard — has been carried out in campaigns since the 1920s and reopened repeatedly, with public controversy each time, because the committee's recommendations often move a reading that teachers, broadcasters and dictionaries had fixed** (**standard account**). **Why the item can never be closed. ① Speech moves faster than a list**, so any review is obsolete before its print run ends. ② **Every decision transfers value**: the family name, the place name and the song lyric have vested interests in a pronunciation, and the reading a committee prefers is often the one a dictionary publisher has already printed. **③ It is a property dispute** — the same character may have a literary and a colloquial reading tied to different registers and different regions, so a ruling is not a discovery but an allocation (**the phonology course's point that reviewing sounds is a dispute over property, in its policy form**). **The observed pattern is instructive: a recommendation is published, teachers protest, some items are revised or delayed, and the actual pronunciation in the population moves regardless — usually toward the form the medium uses.** **The lesson generalises to any standard whose enforcement is a test: the standard converges on what the most imitated channel does, and the committee ends up ratifying the broadcasters.**
:::

::: definition From the ear to the algorithm {#def-suanfa}
**In the last decade the arbiter of the spoken standard has begun to shift from the broadcasting academy to an automatic scoring system: pronunciation tests now grade by machine, subtitles are generated by speech recognition, and corrections of handwriting and usage come from a model** (**standard account**). **Four consequences and one warning. ① The algorithm needs a defensible norm to score against, and it inherits the published lists — so a review decision that teachers could resist in a staff room becomes a score that a candidate cannot argue with.** ② **It also fixes usage at scale**: recognition systems are trained on what is said most, and what is said most is what the standard variety produces, so the model raises the cost of the deviant realisation and accelerates what the dialect course calls language shift. **③ It is more consistent than a human examiner and less able to hear an intention**, and a test that cannot credit an understandable near-miss is a different instrument from one that can: **the criterion of a good test changes when the examiner cannot be appealed to.** ④ **It is not neutral about region**, since training data are an accident of who publishes audio. **The warning, which closes the course's long argument about auditability: an automatic judge is the first standard in this history that keeps records, and that is an advantage — provided the norm it scores against, and the data that trained it, are published; otherwise the standard is no longer a set of institutions but a proprietary object with a hidden cost function.**
:::

::: example Three characters, one deferral {#ex-sange}
Three items of the kind that appear in every pronouncing controversy (**invented here as shapes; the pattern is common**): **a)** a character in a place name with a local reading that the list prefers differently; **b)** a word with an old literary reading and a widespread colloquial one, the latter now dominant on television; **c)** a grammatical function word whose "correct" value is a textbook convention nobody uses in speech. **For each, name the type of dispute, who pays for a change, and what a committee should do.**

::: solution
**a) A property dispute over a name**: the local community holds the reading, and the standard list holds the printable form; **the cost of change falls on signposts, maps, and the identity of people whose name is pronounced differently by officials** — the correct policy is almost always a dual reading with the local form listed first. **b) A register dispute**: the reading that the medium uses will win, since imitation is the enforcement mechanism; **a committee can delay the recognition of victory and pay in credibility, or ratify it and pay in the embarrassment of the textbooks already printed** (the honest third option is to list both with a register label — the option committees dislike because it looks like weakness). **c) A convention with no speakers**: here the only defensible ruling is to stop testing it, **since a norm that nobody's speech violates cannot be enforced and exists only to sort schools.** **Marking: whoever says that the committee should be measured by how much argument it leaves unresolved, rather than by how many it settles, takes an A** — **the review campaigns of the last hundred years show that settlement is temporary and the record of decisions is the actual contribution.** And the chapter's point: **the "standard" at any moment is the sum of these thousands of deferrals, not the printed list.**
:::
:::

::: intuition How old is the word "Putonghua" {#int-nianji}
**Test yourself: in what year did people begin to say "the national common speech"? The term is old and its institutional meaning is young — as a school subject it belongs to the 1950s, and universal classroom use is a much later fact** (**standard account**: this chapter's caveat, since the date depends on whether you count the word, the policy or the practice). **Three things the exercise does for you. ① It breaks the illusion of depth** — a term's age is not its hold on the population, and a language is not old because its name is. **② It exposes what you are really measuring: competence, or its documentation?** A society may have a standard in the sense that its courts require one and in the sense that its children speak one, and those two states can be a century apart. **③ It makes the present legible: the claim that Putonghua is "now universal" and the claim that dialects are "now dying" are both claims about a very short interval, and the survey evidence for each is better than the anecdote and worse than the slogan** (the dialect course's language-shift chapter, where the age-grading data live). **A rule of thumb for anything with a name and a policy: ask what your grandparents' generation called the thing, and what they would have had to do differently if it had already existed.**
:::

::: history Making a standard into infrastructure {#sec-jichu}
**1909–1913**: a late-Qing and early-republic commission works on a national pronunciation, and the "national language" concept becomes policy vocabulary (**standard account**). **1920s–1930s**: the romanisation contests, the mass-literacy alphabets, and the Republic's promotion campaigns, which reach cities and schools and not the countryside (**standard account**). **1949–1958**: the two decisive instruments are produced in quick succession — the character simplification list and the phonetic alphabet — **with the standard definition of 1955–1956 between them; in nine years the state built more language infrastructure than the previous forty had planned** (**standard account**). **1982**: the constitution names the promotion of the national common speech (**standard account**). **2000**: a law on the spoken and written language (**standard account**). **2010s onward**: proficiency testing at scale, and machine scoring and recognition entering both the test and the subtitle (**standard account**) — **the algorithmic turn of the previous section.** **The reading of the sequence: three regimes, one continuous programme, and an acceleration whenever the state acquired a new delivery channel — the school, the broadcast licence, then the phone. This is the whole chapter's argument in one line: a standard is infrastructure, and it advances at the speed of the infrastructure, while the language itself keeps moving somewhere else.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Reading the definition as a description** (**three clauses, three settlements**); **confusing the list with the practice** (**find the examiners**); **counting strokes as evidence of teachability** (**the pedagogical case was never settled**); **trusting a machine judge whose norm and training data are unpublished** (**a proprietary standard with a hidden cost function**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: infrastructure and the language {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the success of a language standard should be measured not by how many people speak it but by how few people need it in order to be understood, and by how cheap it is for a stranger to acquire. By that measure the twentieth century's Chinese language programme is the most successful in the world, and by the same measure it has produced a class of speakers whose first language is now officially a rustic version of the standard they were taught to prefer — which is not a failure of the programme but its normal by-product, and the thing a policy should be judged on is whether it pays for the by-product.** Deploy at least six of: **the three clauses as settlements**, **the executors**, **pinyin's three conditions**, **the four points on simplification**, **why pronunciation review cannot close**, **the algorithmic judge**, **the age of the term**, **the infrastructure reading of the history section**. Then adjudicate: **your city introduces machine-graded pronunciation in its teacher-licensing exam. Vote — for, against, or for with conditions — and state the condition you would not trade.**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**an untradeable condition**). **For**: consistency, no examiner fatigue, no regional sympathy, records that can be audited. **Against**: it converts the local variety into a permanent liability on a person's pay, which is a redistribution by software; **and a scorer cannot credit a near-miss, so it measures fidelity to a specimen rather than intelligibility**. **For-with-conditions, and the strongest answers pick one of these: publish the norm and the training data; score only items where the machine and human examiners historically agree; cap the weight of pronunciation in the total; or run it as feedback rather than as a gate for the first five years, with the disagreement rate published.** **Highest marks for the condition this course would not trade: no candidate may be failed by a system whose cost function is not disclosed — a standard that has become a proprietary object has to be licensed into the public domain before it can be used to take away a livelihood.**
:::

::: summary
The chapter delivers: definition (**three clauses, three settlements, one deliberate circularity**), execution (**find the examiners; the input method counts**), success (**useful before ideological**), half-measure (**stranded readers, no accounting**), the pending item (**pronunciation review as an allocation that never closes**), the new judge (**consistent, record-keeping, and possibly private**), and one line of history: **the standard advances at the speed of the infrastructure while the language moves elsewhere.** This closes the history-of-Chinese course: from bronze and bone to a scoring machine, with the same question throughout — **who decided, and who paid?**
