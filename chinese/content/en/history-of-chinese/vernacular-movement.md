---
title: "The vernacular movement"
---

For a thousand years the language had two written layers and everyone knew which was which. **In a period of about five years around 1919 the upper layer lost its official position, and this chapter is about how that happened, why the opponents were not fools, and which part of the programme is still unfinished.**

## Which layer? {#def-na-yi-ceng}

::: definition The grammar layer of the written language {#def-yufacing}
**Be precise about what changed, because three different things are usually conflated: the script, the vocabulary, and the grammar. The change was in the grammar layer of the standard written language — the function words, the word-order norms and the sentence length ceased to be those of the classical register and became those of a spoken koiné** (**standard account**). **Why this precision matters. ① The vocabulary had already been mixed for centuries**: administrative and near-spoken vocabulary had been entering written Chinese all along, so "it began to use spoken words" is not what happened. **② The script was not changed**, despite a generation's plans, so the visual surface of the language is the least informative thing about the period. **③ A change at the grammar layer is the most conservative kind of revolution and the most visible to a contemporary**: the same characters, the same topics, and a reader instantly knows in which language a sentence is written — which is why the period's quarrels read as aesthetic and were in fact constitutional. **The chapter's rule: whenever you are told that "the language changed", ask which layer; in this case the answer determines the date, since the script changed in 1956, the grammar layer between 1917 and 1921, and the vocabulary continuously.**
:::

::: definition Three things {#def-sanyang}
**The change required three things at once, and histories that credit one of them are wrong. ① A proposal, with a programme and a name**: essays of 1917 argued for a literature in the living language, listing concrete requirements rather than a mood, and a well-known formula of "eight essentials" was offered (**standard account**). **② An institution to adopt it**: the state school system switched the language of primary composition to the vernacular within a few years of 1919, and the examiners, textbooks and teacher-training colleges followed (**standard account**) — **this is the item that actually converted a pamphlet into a fact.** ③ **A supply of models**: magazines, translations, and prose by the advocates themselves, sufficient that a student could imitate something. **Compare the failed programs of the previous chapter: the 1375 rhyme book had ② and lacked ③; the accent academies had ① and lacked ②.** **Three legs, and the stool falls without any one of them — which is the reusable result of this chapter, and the reason it is a chapter about institutions rather than about writers.**
:::

::: definition The opponents were not weak {#def-fandui}
**The standard account of the controversy treats the defenders of the classical register as antiquarian; their arguments were three, and each was sound in some degree. ① The precision argument**: classical prose was extremely compact, and a technical or legal register that had been written in it for two thousand years carried distinctions that the vernacular of 1918 did not yet have words for — **an argument about the immaturity of the new medium, and the next section shows it was correct about one thing**. **② The common-language argument**: the koiné was not anybody's mother tongue; making a written standard of it meant every child in the country learned a second variety, whereas classical had at least been a shared written language of the educated — **an argument about equity that this course concedes has never been adequately answered, and which the previous chapter's account of the "smarter attempt" already frames as additivity rather than replacement**. **③ The continuity argument**: the whole commentary tradition, and much of the vocabulary of abstraction, was stored in the classical register, and cutting the tie would make the archive unreadable to educated people within three generations. **It happened, and it is the plainest fact on the ledger of this period.** **The lesson of listing them: a reform that wins may still have lost the argument, and the loss is paid later, by readers.**
:::

::: definition Europeanisation: who changed Chinese syntax {#def-ouhua}
**The standard account**: translationese mattered as much as speech. Translating European and Japanese texts required long qualified sentences, explicit connectives, passive-like constructions, and nominalisations, and **the translators of the period adopted a deliberately literal method on the theory that the syntax as well as the words had to be imported**; a famous formulation held that translation should preserve the foreign manner and even its sentence structure (**standard account**). **Three consequences. ① The grammar of the new written language is partly a translation artefact — its average sentence length, its use of "被" for passive, its "关于…" preposing, its habit of stacking attributive modifiers — which is why the modern-grammar course's complaints about Europeanisation and this chapter's history are the same subject seen from two ends** (the academic-writing course's three questions about the Europeanised sentence belong here as a historical observation). **② The change was not degenerate but functional**: it made explicit distinctions the classical register had left to inference, and it made possible the paragraph as a unit of argument rather than of parallel arrangement. ③ **It is not finished**: **the layer is still being deposited now, by interfaces and by English-medium business speech**, and the same three complaints are being made about it (**standard account**: each generation repeats them and each is describing a completed change).
:::

::: definition The unfinished parts {#def-weiwancheng}
**Three items on the 1910s programme did not happen, and their absence is the shape of the present language. ① Script reform — abolition or heavy phoneticisation of the characters — was argued by nearly everyone who mattered in 1918 and remains unimplemented**; what arrived instead was simplification (next chapter), which is a different project (**standard account**). **② A spelling-and-pronunciation instrument for the whole population**: a phonetic alphabet was designed in the 1950s and is used daily, but as an aid to literacy and to input methods rather than as a replacement — **the greatest unintended success of a failed programme**, since it became the interface through which most of a generation writes. **③ A literature in the actual spoken varieties rather than in the koiné**: the 1930s debate on a "mass language" asked whether the vernacular had simply installed a new educated elite's speech in place of the old one, and the question is a live one in the sociolinguistics course (**standard account**). **This lesson's verdict: the movement succeeded so completely at the grammar layer that its two unfinished items — script and dialect — look like omissions rather than like defeats; and it matters which, because a defeat can be revised and an omission can be resumed.**
:::

::: example Two passages, five differences {#ex-liangduan}
Two sentences about the same event, the first from before 1918, the second from the early 1920s (**composed for this lesson**): **A**: "The province being flooded, the relief Commissioner memorialised for open-grain relief, and the court ordered it so." (in classical) **B**: "Because the river broke in the south and the water did not go down, the people of several counties had nothing to eat, and the official who was sent to relief work wrote a long report to say that the public granaries ought to be opened at once." **List five differences and then say which of them are grammar-layer, which are rhetorical, and which one is evidence about audience.**

::: solution
**Five differences: ① function words (因为…而/的/了 in place of the classical set); ② sentence length and clause count; ③ explicit causal connectives where the classical uses juxtaposition; ④ the noun phrase "the people of several counties had nothing to eat" as a subject with a predicate, where the classical uses a nominal sequence; ⑤ redundancy and concreteness ("a long report", "at once"), which are rhetorical, not grammatical.** **Grammar-layer: ①②③④; rhetorical: ⑤; and ③ is also a Europeanisation trace, since explicit subordination of this pattern is likelier in translation than in native writing.** **Audience evidence: ⑤** — a writer who explains what a granary relief does has stopped writing for readers who already know; **redundancy is a marker of a widened audience, and it is the single most reliable index of a change of readership in any corpus** (the applied-writing course's rule that a document's length is a theory of its reader). **Marking: whoever names redundancy rather than vocabulary as the audience evidence takes an A** — **vocabulary changes with fashion; the amount of explanation a text supplies changes with its public.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Transitional style always looks immature {#int-guodu}
**A feeling with a history: prose of the 1920s strikes most readers as awkward — over-explained, uneven, sometimes infantile, sometimes strangely dense. Three reasons this is not a verdict on the writers. ① The resources were new**: a connective that is a calque feels clumsy to everyone, including the author, who is watching her own sentence work for the first time. **② Two registers were both available, so writers alternated**, and the shifting is what we register as inconsistency — a bilingual or diglossic stage always produces this texture, and the same can be heard in Chinese prose of the 1990s under English influence (**standard account**: the complaint recurs on a cycle shorter than a generation). **③ The reader's competence is the moving part**: we read the 1920s badly because we were not trained in its conventions, while its own readers found some of it lucid and some of it obscure. **The methodological use: never date a style change by when it starts to look natural; date it by when it becomes possible — the first essay a critic calls "not yet literature" may be the first that could have been written at all.** And one caution for your own writing: **the sentence you think is clumsy because it is new to you may be the sentence the change needed.**
:::

::: history From phonetic alphabets to national language textbooks {#sec-qieyin}
**Late nineteenth century**: men under the shock of defeat design phonetic alphabets for Chinese, each intended for a specific variety and aimed at mass literacy, and they argue that a country cannot win with a script only the few master (**standard account**: the phonographic movement, and the point that its programmes were designed for the spoken varieties, which the 1910s solution quietly abandoned). **1902–1911**: the school system is rebuilt, a national language is defined in the last years of the dynasty, and a commission argues for a standard pronunciation with northern and southern positions both represented (**standard account**). **1915–1920**: a magazine, a student movement, the essays on literary reform, and the switch of the school composition language (**standard account**). **1920s–1930s**: textbooks, a grammar of the vernacular written by a man who had compiled school readers, and the "mass language" debate about whose vernacular was being installed (**standard account**). **1949 and after**: the state implements what the earlier decades had planned — a standard, a phonetic alphabet, character simplification — **in the order of priority that the 1890s reformers would have recognised and the 1917 writers would have regretted** (**standard account**). **The chapter's closing reading: one long reform movement, and the language question resolved by institutions rather than by argument, which is why this chapter ends with textbook policy and not with a writer.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Saying "the language changed"** (**name the layer and the date**); **treating the defenders as relics** (**three arguments, one conceded here**); **blaming Europeanisation** (**a functional deposit, still being laid**); **assuming the vernacular was the people's language** (**the mass-language debate is still open**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: revolutions keep their promises {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a language revolution is judged by what it made possible and paid for by what it made unreadable. The vernacular programme gave a mass school system a medium children already half commanded, and in the same act cut three generations of readers off from their own archive; it made argumentative prose possible in a language whose brilliance lay in compression, and thereby lost something that cannot be recovered by learning the classics late. No accounting of the two is available to us, since we are the people who inherited both the gain and the loss, and any verdict we reach is a report on our own position in the sequence.** Deploy at least six of: **the layer precision**, **the three legs**, **the three arguments of the opponents**, **Europeanisation as deposit**, **the unfinished items**, **the audience index in the worked example**, **the transitional style**, **the phonographic movement's abandoned variety-orientation**. Then adjudicate: **was the cost avoidable — and if so, by which specific policy, at which date, and who would have had to pay?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**a policy, a date, a payer**). **Passing candidates: bilingualism as the official policy** (classical as a second written language for those who needed the archive, taught from 1923 in the middle years, **paid for by extending schooling by one year — the cost falling on the state's budget and on the rural families whose sons were needed at home, which is why it was never adopted**); **the abandoned variety-orientation** (a standard built on several regional vernaculars with local media, which the phonographic movement had implied and the 1920s school system refused, **paid for by printing costs and teacher training in each variety**); **a script reform instead of a grammar revolution**, which was the actual preference of many 1910s reformers and whose cost falls on everyone over thirty and on every existing book. **Highest marks for the observation that all three were available and all three required the state to pay in the 1920s, whereas the solution actually adopted — switch the grammar, keep everything else — cost the state nothing and the archive everything, and the archive does not send invoices.**
:::
