---
title: "Ming and Qing theories of poetry"
---

The critics of the last chapter were fighting for a class's right to exist; these have settled the rank of poetry and argue about something else — **which age to take as a model, and whether a poem's merit lives in its metre, its talent, its learning or its resonance. This chapter argues that this is not a factional melee but the record of a self-correcting system running for four hundred years**.

## One cycle {#def-xunhuan}

::: definition Four schools and their diagnoses {#def-sipai}
**The standard account**: one Ming school took the high Tang as the law ("poetry must model the high Tang" is their slogan); a second, associated with three brothers, answered with "utter the nature of the singularity, unbound by fixed moulds" (in translation: speak from your own nature, refuse the templates); a third sought a remote, difficult beauty; Qing critics then ran through several programmes in turn, named after metre and tone (格调), spirit and resonance (神韵), nature and spontaneity (性灵), and texture (肌理). **This lesson rearranges them into a chain of diagnoses: ① the archaists diagnose their present as a decline of grandeur, hence the demand for a high model; ② the spontaneity school diagnoses the archaists with having no people left, only imitations, hence taking everything from the heart; ③ the remote school diagnoses the spontaneity people with vulgarity that runs out at once, hence depth and asperity; ④ Qing critics diagnose the Ming with empty talk without roots, hence one school's remedy of learning and another's of flavour.** This is a standard thesis-antithesis structure except that it is not a Hegelian ascent: **after four hundred years none of the problems is solved.** The first analysis: **each school's programme is correct, because it is an exact description of the previous school's actual defect; and once implemented it manufactures a new defect.** That is why the chapter reads uneasily: **you cannot avoid error by picking the right camp; you can only choose which error you are willing to commit.** Apply that sentence, unchanged, to every methodology war you attend.
:::

::: definition Four direction-words {#def-fangxiang}
**The standard account**: the four terms — metre and tone, spirit and resonance, nature and spontaneity, texture — became the basic vocabulary of Qing poetics. Put them through a semantic test. **① Each can be instantiated**: every school can produce ten poems "with resonance", "with texture". **② None can be tested**: no school prohibits any specific procedure. **The key: the four schools share the same corpus, and when the corpus is shared, examples cannot prove rules.** ③ **Their semantics is negative**: "resonance" really means "not this kind" — it excludes the bare, the exhausted, the solid, the learned. **So they are indicators of direction rather than machines of decision.** The verdict: **these four words are the normal product of a discipline that cannot verify; they convert a criterion into a taste signal so that initiates can recognise one another.** That reading does not abolish their value: the last chapter's "the function of a list is recruiting" upgrades here to **the function of a category is recognising one's own kind** — at the price of outsourcing argument to taste. One exercise, then: **every time you meet an unverifiable term of praise, translate it into a prohibition; if you cannot, the word is functioning in your hands as a proof of birth.**
:::

## One attempt at system {#def-tixi}

::: definition Ye Xie's table {#def-yexie}
**The standard account**: a seventeenth-century treatise proposes two sets of elements — on the side of the object, principle, occasion, and feeling (理, 事, 情); on the side of the writer, talent, courage, judgement, and force (才, 胆, 识, 力) — with judgement as the root of the four. Read it as the only genuine system-building attempt of the period. **① It splits the unanalysable "talent" into four**, and judgement can be got from reading and travel — **so "talent" becomes educable for the first time**. ② **It supplies the object's side as well**, so that a work is a function of two sets of variables and not a gift of inspiration. **③ Its weakness is that the table has no weights and no interaction terms**: what does "high judgement, weak courage" write? What does "great talent, shallow judgement"? **The table yields vocabulary, not prediction.** The division of labour with the last chapter: the *Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons* was an encyclopedia of a trade; this is the sketch of a scoring sheet — **and both lack the same thing, weights and interactions. Weights are the hardest thing any empirical discipline can hand over, because to publish them is to write your taste as a cost function.** That sentence will be repaid, under the heading of auditability, in the last three chapters of this course.
:::

::: definition Categories and power {#def-quanli}
**The standard account**: an eighteenth-century critic who championed metre and tone held high office, and his anthologies and pronouncements dominated both the literati and the examination world; after his death a literary inquisition caused his posthumous honours to be stripped. Three institutional implications. **① Once a category is bound to political honour, opposing it carries risk, so its currency no longer requires argument.** **② Its adherents turn into opportunists**, because command of the vocabulary is a regional accent on the road to office. **③ Its later fall is equally not a scholarly event**: the same poems are read as "grand manner" in one reign and "the breath is spent" in the next, and the difference often lies in the examiners' affiliations. Hence the hardest sentence of the chapter: **once a critical category is co-opted by power, it will be liquidated as part of the old regime in the next reorganisation of power — and the scapegoat is poetry.** If you ever sit on a committee, remember three devices: **publish the criteria, fix the weights in advance, allow appeals.** That is the whole available technology for keeping a category from becoming a birth certificate.
:::

::: definition Two counter-theses, two hundred years later {#def-fanzheng}
**The standard account**: a late-eighteenth-century poet wrote the famous couplet that each age produces its own talent and leads the tradition for a few centuries; separately, another school insisted that poetry must be rooted in learning, and the "poetry of the Song model" flourished after the 1840s. Put the two together. **① "Each leads for a few centuries" is a historical optimism**: it grants each age its own poetry, so no high-Tang standard is needed. **② The Song-school practice is its cashing-out**: writing with learning, with prose force and with loose syntax produces something that "resonance" can no longer judge. **So a category often fails not by being refuted but because new writing appears that it cannot describe.** That is the chapter's main theoretical conclusion: **the life of a category depends on its coverage of new work** — consistent with the last chapter's warning about standards fixed in a past period. Practical form: **do not ask whether an evaluative framework is correct; ask how many works of the last five years it can describe.** Try that on literature, architecture, software and on research assessment, and you will find it uncomfortable everywhere.
:::

::: example One poem of twenty characters {#ex-ershi}
Take the transmitted quatrain: "Empty hills, no man in sight; / only the sound of voices echoing. / Returning light enters the deep forest / and shines again upon the green moss." Write one verdict in each of the four vocabularies, then answer: **can those four verdicts refute one another?**

::: solution
**The four verdicts (composed here from each school's positions — not quotations; each school's doctrine is documented in its own collected works)**: metre and tone — *clear in manner, far-carrying in sound, but the air is short and craggy, not the highest grade*; resonance — *not a word imposed, and the idea outside the words; sound and colour both dissolved*; spontaneity — *language spoken at the moment, true delight without seeking merit in learning*; texture — *the phrases on returning light and shining again rest on physics one can check; alas the poem offers little book-learning to hold*. **Can they refute each other? No, for three reasons: each uses only features the poem already has, and no feature belongs to one school alone; none prohibits any rewriting — none says "delete line 3 and it collapses", so all four tolerate the poem exactly as it is; and their objects are different questions — grandeur, flavour, sincerity, knowledge.** Conclusion: **a perfect case of shared corpus, unshared criteria**, which shows that **in a dispute between incommensurable criteria the text never decides**; therefore the right way to study these quarrels is not to adjudicate them but to record from what institutional position each side spoke and to whom. That sentence turns the chapter into a sociology and hands the next chapter its hinge — **for Wang Guowei brings a criterion borrowed from quite elsewhere.** **Marking: an A for the sentence "they evaluate different questions rather than giving different answers to one question"** — much harder than "each has a point", since it converts a quarrel into an agenda. The logic course's "find another variable" appears here for the third time.
:::
:::

::: intuition Old coins still in circulation {#int-jiubi}
**A fact about language**: people who write old-style verse, edit anthologies and teach poetry still use metre-and-tone, resonance and spontaneity. Three observations. **① They no longer carry argument**: nobody excludes a poem from an anthology for want of resonance; **the words are used to provide a decent sentence for a choice already made**. ② **They are proofs of birth**: users recognise each other, and a misuse is heard at once — **that function is unchanged in two hundred years.** ③ **They are occasionally efficient**: when you must choose among three equally defensible poems, "this one has resonance" compresses a judgement for you. **Old coins are still useful for small change, and useless for bulk trade.** The pedagogic advice: **learn the vocabulary, for the same reason you learn a foreign language — your objects speak it; but translate it into checkable statements in your own writing, unless you and your reader share the criterion** — and in cross-disciplinary writing you never do. Consider that a preview of the next chapter on academic language.
:::

::: history Four centuries of changing standards {#sec-koujing}
**Late fifteenth to sixteenth centuries**: the slogan "model on the high Tang" is issued and becomes the common language of the literati (**standard account**). **Late sixteenth to early seventeenth**: spontaneity is propounded by three brothers, and a school of the remote and obscure splits off (**standard account**); **add one material cause: woodblock printing and anthologies let a contemporary manifesto reach every province within a few years** — chapter fourteen's "printing is the invisible patron" re-enacted. **Later seventeenth century**: poet-loyalists reopen the nature of poetry with terms like "situation-and-feeling"; a treatise publishes the two-set table (**standard account**). **Late seventeenth to early eighteenth**: "resonance" becomes the politest word in gentry sociability (**standard account**). **Eighteenth century**: three programmes stand against one another, poetry-talks revive, and **anthologies become the physical existence of a school — to be selected is to be recognised**, the poetry version of the last chapter's "whoever controls the text controls the interpretation". **Nineteenth to twentieth centuries**: the Song model and its late echoes; then, after the new literature, all these categories are installed in textbooks of criticism (**standard account**). **Conclusion: in four hundred years what changed was the standard, while what was inherited was the premise that there must be an orthodoxy.** That premise is what this chapter asks you to interrogate — and the next chapter shakes it for the first time with a small book written in borrowed categories.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Arranging the four schools in a correct order** (**a chain of diagnoses, not a ladder**); **using direction-words as argument** (**translate into a prohibition, or delete**); **treating an anthology as an objective assessment** (**an anthology is a school's body**); **concluding that "each has a point"** (**rewrite as "they evaluate different questions"** — this chapter's only hard grading rule).
:::

::: exercise Essay: the premise of orthodoxy {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the real consensus of Ming and Qing poetics was neither the high Tang nor spontaneity but this: there must be an orthodoxy, and it must be sayable. The four schools disputed its content; nobody questioned the sentence. Correspondingly, all of them treated criticism as legislation — fix one style and make it the standard. Which is why Chinese theory could switch to foreign categories so quickly in the twentieth century: not entirely under compulsion; the new categories satisfied the same sentence with different contents.** Deploy at least six of: **the chain of diagnoses**, **the semantics of direction-words**, **Ye Xie's table and its gap**, **categories and power**, **the anthology as a school's body**, **new work voiding a category**. Then adjudicate: **can the premise "there must be an orthodoxy" be abandoned, and if so what does the evaluative system become — name one you have actually seen?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**you must name a real system without an orthodoxy**). **Candidates**: **juried contemporary art** — several uncoordinated lines instead of one standard; the cost is that outcomes depend on the composition of the jury, so "who was invited" replaces "what is good" — **exactly this lesson's point: when an orthodoxy is abandoned the dispute does not vanish, it moves upstream into procedure**, which is the auditability demand of the earlier chapters; **peer review** (single versus double blind produces different distributions of what gets published, unrelated to quality); **the league-table era** (abandoning one orthodoxy yields dozens of opaque algorithmic orthodoxies). **Highest marks** for the deduction that **an orthodoxy cannot be cancelled, only replaced, and every replacement at once breeds a less presentable one that does not publish its criteria — Ming critics at least knew what they were legislating for, which is our only chance of being more honest than them, and the chance we usually decline.** A conclusion able to write that earns an A.
:::
