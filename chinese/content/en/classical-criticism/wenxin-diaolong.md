The last two chapters were essays; this one is a book of fifty chapters — **its ambition was not to advance a thesis but to arrange everything a literate man of its day knew about writing into a catalogue with entries. This chapter says what the book achieved, and what completeness costs**.

## What kind of book it is {#def-shishenme}

::: definition An encyclopaedia of a trade {#def-quanshu}
**The standard account**: near the end of the Southern Qi an author completed a work of fifty chapters, and in his preface explained the arrangement: the upper half treats genres, the lower half treats making and judgement; the last chapter is an autobiography of the book. **This lesson offers one modern analogy and then withdraws it**. What the book most resembles is a trade manual: it has a product catalogue (**thirty-odd categories of writing with their origins, vocabularies and proper manner**), a production process (**from conception through arrangement to cutting and polishing**), a quality checklist (**six things to look at when appraising**), an industrial history (**how the age and the writing change together**), and a professional ethics (**talent and conduct**). Its greatness comes from that ambition — completeness — and so do its weaknesses. A word on the author's circumstances: he spent years attached to a monastery arranging its collection of texts, and when the book was finished he showed it to an influential writer of the day, who praised it (**standard account, from the dynastic biography**). This lesson draws only one institutional inference: **from the moment a book is praised by somebody, its circulation contains an element of luck**, and "who praised it" is a question this chapter will keep asking.
:::

::: definition Five pillars and a two-way division {#def-zhu}
**The standard account**: the opening chapters establish the ground of writing — literature originates in the Way, must be verified against the sages, must take the classics as model, must have its names set right, and must deal with inheritance and change. Two readings. **① This is a structure of authorisation**: authority is installed before any recipe is given, and in the literary world of the day that was a necessary opening, not a conservative one. **This lesson rejects the usual complaint that these chapters are "pedantic": every reforming manual has to prove first that its author is qualified to speak.** ② **The division into *wen* and *bi***: the book sorts all writing into the rhymed and the unrhymed and treats them by different rules (**standard account**). The modern significance this lesson insists on: **the boundary of "literature" was drawn at sound, not at fiction**. Chinese criticism therefore never developed a system of genres built on imitation — this is the strongest internal evidence available. The question our first course on literary theory asks, "what is literature", receives here a historical answer: **asked in sixth-century China it comes out as a different question**.
:::

## The parts you can use {#def-kexing}

::: definition Four steps for the study of a genre {#def-sibu}
**The standard account**: the book states its own procedure for handling each genre — trace the origin in order to set out the later course; explain the name in order to make the meaning clear; select pieces in order to fix the exemplars; unfold the principles in order to state the norm. **Translate the four steps into their modern functions**. **① History**: for what problem did this form first arise? **② Definition**: how was its name used at the time? **③ Examples**: which works count as its models? **④ Norm**: what teachable rules follow from the models? **You will recognise the four parts of a modern literature review — and the formulation is some thirteen centuries earlier.** This lesson asks you to use the four steps as the checklist for every piece of writing in this course: **each of your sections should be able to answer all four. Without step 1 you are writing in an eternal present; without step 2 you are deploying an undefined keyword; without step 3 your norm has no instance; without step 4 you have only stacked material.** The four steps recur in each of the last three chapters. **They are not literary theory; they are method.**
:::

::: definition Six things to look at: a checklist {#def-liuguan}
**The standard account**: near the end the book discusses the difficulty of judging — the world rates the ancient above the modern and itself above others — and offers a set of observation points: the arrangement of the whole, the placing of words, inheritance and change, the orthodox and the strange, the use of matter and allusion, and the tonal pattern (**standard account**). This lesson treats the list as the most usable object in the book, and adds three remarks on use. **① It decomposes "good" into six separately gradable questions**, so disagreement can be located: "I do not dislike this poem; I think it fails on point four." That is what it means for criticism to be usable. **② It implies a stance**: formal items and substantive items stand side by side without rank (**standard account**) — the place where it is more generous than much later moralising criticism. **③ It gives no weights.** How much do the six count for? It does not say, **and a list without weights turns out to depend on whoever fills it in**. Call this the blanks in the system; the chapter will keep pointing at them.
:::

::: definition Three pairs of categories {#def-fanchou}
**The standard account**: the lower half organises its problems in pairs. **① Feeling and ornament (情・采)**: ornament attaches to substance, and substance waits for ornament; and there is the famous contrast — those who write out of feeling are spare and deliver what is real, those who write out of ornament are lavish and flabby. This lesson's translation: **a criterion of motive that is not a moral verdict but an efficiency judgement — writing for the sake of writing pays its costs somewhere else**. **② Inheritance and change (通・變)**: inheritance keeps the road open, change keeps the life going — an almost trivial piece of trade wisdom that has been written as a law of history. **③ Hidden and outstanding (隱・秀)**: the hidden is the surplus of sense beyond the words; the outstanding is the single line that stands up in a piece. Note that the two are separated — **a poem may lack both** — and that later Chinese criticism raised the hidden far above the outstanding, which is the Daoist licence of the last chapter cashing in. **The verdict on the three pairs together: every one of them is a pair, because the author wrote in parallel prose. See the next section.**
:::

::: example Grading a couplet on six points {#ex-liuguan2}
Take the transmitted couplet "If within the four seas there is a known heart, though Heaven and Earth be apart, the neighbour is next door" (**the line is transmitted and readable**). Write one sentence of assessment under each of the six observation points, assign a weight to each, and then state the position implied by your weights.

::: solution
**A possible fill-in**: **① Arrangement**: the couplet stands at the turn of a farewell poem, moving the piece from parting to refusal of grief; **② Diction**: the characters are plain, no colouring words; **③ Inheritance and change**: to bring argument into a farewell is a change against the routine of the parting-lament (伤别); **④ Orthodox and strange**: the language is orthodox, the thought strange — the strangeness lies in unhooking distance from affection; **⑤ Matter and allusion**: no allusion, and the sense rests outside the words; **⑥ Tonal pattern**: the couplet is steady (**standard account**: the standing of the line within the formation of regulated verse is separately debated; this lesson makes an exercise judgement only). **Weights**: you may give thirty per cent each to ③ and ④ and forty between the rest — that makes you a critic who values change; give thirty each to ② and ⑥ and you value the texture of language. **The last step is the assignment**: state the position your weights take. **A critic who weights change writes the history of literature as a history of problem solving, and will systematically undervalue works that solve nothing; a critic who weights texture writes it as a catalogue of styles, and cannot explain why one style ruled a century.** Marking: **whoever can write "my weights have this blind spot" takes an A**. That is the chapter's whole attitude to systems: **a list without weights cannot decide for you; a list with weights lets you see your own prejudice — and both beat "instinct", the difference being auditability**. The last chapter's rule, that criticism is tested by whether it can be executed, receives here its upgrade: **by whether it can be called to account**.
:::
:::

::: intuition How parallel prose shaped a theory {#int-pian}
**An overlooked fact**: the book is written in parallel prose — four-six couplets, allusion, tonal regulation. Three consequences. **① The couplet forces propositions into halves**, so the world appears in theory as feeling-ornament, inheritance-change, hidden-outstanding. It does not necessarily make thought binary; **it makes the binary the least effortful shape**. **② The couplet forbids fine subordination**: a sentence of the form "because p, therefore in the exceptional case q" does not parallelise, **so the book lacks conditionals and lacks quantified statements — that is the stylistic origin of its blanks**. **③ Allusion lends authority to assertion**: to state a judgement as a likeness to two prior events dispenses with argument; this is parallel prose's strongest device and its most dangerous. **Generalisation: to understand why any theoretical work does not say something, first ask whether its genre could say it.** The question travels to present-day papers: **why does a sociology paragraph always give method before results, and a psychology paragraph always lead with significance? Not disciplinary logic — the grammar of the genre.** The rhetoric course and the applied-writing course will say it again.
:::

::: history The reception of one book {#sec-jieshou}
**About 501**: the book is completed, and the author shows it to a man with a voice in letters, who praises it (**standard account, from the biography**). **Seventh to tenth centuries**: its chapters on genre are quoted by classbooks and by bibliographic treatises; **how far it was honoured in Tang times is unsettled among scholars** (**standard account**). This lesson's handling: **where there is no consensus, no argument may be built on it** — the discipline this chapter teaches most persistently. **Song: printed editions appear, and the text acquires a comparatively fixed shape; surviving early manuscripts make collation a speciality** (**standard account**). **Sixteenth to seventeenth centuries**: annotation and reprinting multiply, and readers use it as a composition manual and a register of styles (**standard account**). **This lesson notes a common downgrade: once a systematic work enters the commercial press it is read as a handbook.** **Eighteenth century**: a standard variorum appears (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: new editions, commentaries and monographic studies under the heading of "literary theory" install the book in a discipline (**standard account**). The closing observation: **for nine hundred years the same book has been read as a manual, a register, and a theory, and each reclassification corresponds to a new set of readers with a new job. Chapter one's "a formula survives by being rewritten" becomes here: a book survives by being re-shelved.** When you write any literature review, ask who needed which shelf, and when — **that question will save you a third of the pointless argument you were going to have**.
:::

::: exercise Essay: the price of completeness {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a systematic work owes its force to its refusal to leave anything out, and fails at exactly that point: when every criterion stands in parallel with every other, the reader always finds the chapter that supports his own taste. Such books are therefore remarkably long-lived and remarkably easy to hollow out — posterity quotes their sentences while discarding their arrangement. To inherit a system properly one must supply the one thing it refused to supply: a statement of weights and its reasons.** Deploy at least six of: **the trade-manual analogy and its withdrawal**, **the authorising opening**, **the four-step procedure**, **the six points and their missing weights**, **the three pairs of categories**, **parallel prose as the shape of theory**. Then adjudicate: **would you rather write a system or a thesis (bounded by what you could actually produce), and what is the merit of the one you gave up?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**you must state the merit of the option you abandoned**). **A system**: it supplies later people with vocabulary and classification, so it remains in use even after being refuted; **its cost is that it must tolerate blanks, and therefore hands the final judgement to its readers**. **A thesis**: it can be falsified, so it is hard currency in the scholarly market; **its cost is that its life depends on whether opponents still find the question important**. The third answer this lesson likes: **write a small system with a named opponent** — for instance, four standards for one genre, with weights stated. **Marking looks for the two qualifiers "small" and "with an opponent", because they are the prescription this course offers to systems** (isomorphic with the last chapter's rule that unexecutable advice fails). A conclusion able to write "**so the lesson is not whether Liu Xie was right, but that an author must choose the way he is willing to be misread: a system will be broken into a handbook, a thesis will be taken for a school. Having chosen, fill in your own blank and mark the filling-in as provisional — this is the only homage three writing courses can pay to a book of the year 501**" earns an A.
:::
