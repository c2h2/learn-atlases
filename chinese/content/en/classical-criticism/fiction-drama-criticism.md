title: "The criticism of fiction and drama"
---

The previous seven chapters dealt with classes that were already admitted; the object of this one had no place in the bibliography — **so its critics did a thing rarely said of them: they not only defended fiction, they set about rewriting it into a shape that deserved defence**.

## Three things {#def-sanjian}

::: definition Building a lineage {#def-fatong}
**The standard account**: a late-Ming thinker famous for heterodoxy proposed "childlike heart" (童心) as the ground of literary value, and there are extant commentated editions of *Water Margin* and *The Chamberly* bearing his name — **whose authenticity is still disputed**; at the same time another critic ranked a handful of works as "books of genius" (才子书). Both episodes are lineage-building. **① "Childlike heart" is an argument from qualification**: it asks nothing about the rank of a genre and only whether the utterance is unclouded by borrowed doctrine — **so a novel may take a seat beside poetry and prose on the strength of a property that belongs to no genre**. (**standard account**: here for the first time in the course is the strategy of **substituting a universal property for a hierarchical standard**.) ② **"Books of genius" is a catalogue**: to be listed together is to be lifted, and the act of placing the classics, histories, poetry, prose, a novel and a drama on one list is itself a revolution in the taxonomy of writing. **This lesson's precise judgement on ②: the power of the list comes from its not being finished** (**standard account**: the project of six such books was never completed). **A finished list is checkable; an unfinished one is a promise. Promises recruit followers; audits only recruit objections.** That is the full case for the last chapter's closing line about excluded classes founding their own lineage, and chapter nine will show it being absorbed into scholarship.
:::

::: definition Building a method {#def-fangfa}
**The standard account**: a seventeenth-century critic wrote for the novel and the play he edited a series of "ways of reading" and prefaces; there are remarks before each chapter and remarks between the lines, and he discusses structure, foreshadowing, point of view and the handling of characters. **Call this apparatus the closest thing Chinese criticism produced to close reading, and note its three parts: ① the "ways of reading", a list of instructions that first tells you what to attend to; ② the commentary before and inside the text, which turns reading into dialogue; ③ the vocabulary of structure**, in which expressions such as "the grass-snake and the grey thread" name伏 and its answering light (**standard account**). **The three parts correspond, one by one, to the modern textbook, the footnote, and the term-list of narrative theory, three hundred years earlier.** What this lesson values most is where they appear: **methodological innovation happens at the bottom of the hierarchy, because at the top the method has already hardened into the format of the examination.** The ways of reading poetry and prose were fixed by the canonical syllabus; fiction had no agreed way of being read, so ways of reading could be invented. **Generalise it: every field's methodological flowering precedes its canonisation; once the classics are settled, people write commentary** — you have met this in the chapter on the publishing contract and will meet it again in the chapter on network literature.
:::

::: definition Building a text {#def-wenben}
**The standard account**: these critics often did more than comment; they edited. One issued a seventy-chapter recension, deleting everything after the bandits' amnesty and revising the wording; a father and son in the next generation produced a recension of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* which became the standard text; a commentator of the 1690s declared *Jin Ping Mei* the first of strange books and wrote commentary on it by the hundred thousand words. **Call this criticism by editing, and give it four reasons and one consequence. ① To change is to assert**: to delete a passage is to claim that it should not exist, and the claim is stronger than any remark because it cannot be withdrawn. **② The edited text becomes the standard text**, so that what later readers call "the novel" is the critic's edition — and most of them do not know it. **Here is the sharpest sentence of the chapter: in China, whoever controls the text controls the interpretation** (the last chapter's "whoever may write the catalogue"). **③ It makes the author a contested object**: commentary claims to know the author's device and intention, while the author is absent and the text has been altered (**standard account**: the authenticity of editions bearing a commentator's name remains a special study). **④ It fuses collation with criticism**, which is the first difficulty this course hands over to the final chapter on scholarship. **Our stance: we do not need to condemn their editing; we require that every change be entered in an appendix** — which is the whole of what this course calls textual awareness, and chapter twenty's business.
:::

## The exception of drama {#def-xiqu}

::: definition A theory written by a playwright {#def-bianju}
**The standard account**: a seventeenth-century author who combined playwriting, staging and hack-work left a treatise on the theatre that puts structure first, insists that a play must have a "main brain" (主脑) and that its threads should be reduced, and discusses dialogue, comic business and the ending. **This is the most underrated book in Chinese criticism, for three reasons. ① Its reader is somebody who works**, not somebody who sits an examination — so every recommendation in it is executable, which is this course's grading standard three centuries ahead of itself. **② It admits that it is selling.** Writing and staging are a livelihood, so the book is the first to treat literature as an industry; **what the previous chapter called "the publishing cause" becomes here the author's own subject-matter.** ③ **Its criterion is the audience's attention**: the question it asks is "at this moment nobody in the house is watching the stage", not "does this line approach the high Tang". **Call this the theoretical point of the section: a criticism whose standard is "does anyone keep watching" is by nature closer to an empirical science than one whose standard is "does it match the model", because its verdict is testable at the next performance.** The writing courses' executability criterion finds, in a book of stage directions, the best case in the Chinese tradition.
:::

::: definition One novel and a crowd of commentators {#def-pizhe}
**The standard account**: an unfinished eighteenth-century novel circulated in manuscript; several manuscripts carry commentary over a signature associated with the author's household, whose identity and relation to the author, and the layered date of the remarks, are still matters of argument; the notes reveal traces of revision and hint at plots planned for the second half; from the 1790s printed editions made the book available. **Take the case as the chapter's conclusion, because four kinds of uncertainty meet in it.** ① **The author is uncertain**, and the ending of the transmitted text was completed by somebody else. ② **The commentator is uncertain** — a signature may be a pseudonym, may cover several hands, and the notes may be the deposit of repeated copying. ③ **The text is uncertain** — the manuscripts differ, and there is no author-set edition. ④ **The interpretive community is uncertain** — the same notes have been read as poetics, as family history and as political allegory. **The methodological sentence**: with material like this, any claim of the form "what the author really meant" must also state **which edition you are using, which layer of commentary, and on what basis that layer is dated**; if you cannot, then declare that your sentence belongs to interpretation rather than to evidence — **and interpretation, too, must state its rules**. This is the standing requirement of the last three chapters of this course. **The consolation this lesson offers is the opposite of pessimism: these uncertainties are not obstacles to our knowledge but its content.** A novel preserved for two centuries by copyists and bookshops, with no official recension, has in the very state of its text the record of its social history.
:::

::: example Commentary and close reading {#ex-xidu}
Two passages commenting on the same kind of material (**abstract forms invented for this lesson**). **A**: "Reading this far I could not help crying aloud. The author's pen here was planted three chapters back; the reader has forgotten it, but the author did not dare to forget." **B**: "This passage answers a sentence earlier in the work and thereby creates an expectation whose effect depends on the reader's familiarity with a convention." **Write three methodological agreements and one fundamental difference.**

::: solution
**Agreements**: **① both attribute effect to structure rather than to diction; ② both presuppose an ideal reader** — A's "the reader forgets, the author dares not", B's "the reader's familiarity with the convention"; both are claims about reading time; **③ both prove the whole out of a part**: a single piece of foreshadowing is used to assert that the work is designed. **The difference**: A is simultaneously interpretation and praise, and asks not to be checked, because it assumes an author who is present and can be divined — **its grammar is "the author did not dare forget", an assertion about a man's mind. B places the cause on the reader's side, in a mechanism that could be surveyed in other texts, so it does not rely on the author's intention.** The verdict: **this is not an ancient-modern contrast but a distribution of the burden of evidence. A assigns the burden to genius, B to convention; and convention can be looked up while genius cannot** (**standard account**: which is precisely what modern scholars mean when they distinguish influence from coincidence). **Note the asymmetry that makes the exercise worth doing: A is closer to how readers actually experience a poem; B is closer to what a paper is required to say.** **Marking: whoever states the rewriting rule takes an A — if you write in a paper that "the author plants a foreshadow", you must have material on the author planting it; otherwise move the sentence to the reader's side of the ledger.** That rule is the executability criterion of the writing courses fused with the logic course's demand for an alternative variable, and it is the grading standard of the remaining three chapters of this course.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why the list is never completed {#int-mingdan}
**A phenomenon**: from the "books of genius" to today's "ten bests" and "must-reads", lists are rarely completed and rarely revised. Four explanations. **① An unfinished list stays open**: every latecomer believes he could add a name, **which turns the list into a public utility rather than a private judgement**. **② Completion imports liability**: once your list is full, every absence becomes your oversight — the sociological insight of the section. **③ Revision demands new discrimination**, and discrimination depreciates with taste; **the holding cost of a list far exceeds its founding cost.** ④ **The true function of a list is recruiting**: it makes its holder findable. **Hence the reading discipline: whenever you meet a canonical bibliography, ask first whether it was made for teaching, for collecting, for exclusion, or for recruiting.** Four causes produce four very different lists that look identical on the page (**standard account**). **The rule applies to your own discipline's "ten books every graduate must know", which are mostly the sediment of generations of recruiting and self-justification, and not the minutes of a meeting — because they were many meetings, none of which was recorded.** Which is why the next chapter begins with archives.
:::

::: history From banned to studied {#sec-jin}
**Fifteenth to sixteenth centuries**: printed fiction and staged drama expand; occasional remarks attach themselves to prefaces and postfaces (**standard account**). **Late sixteenth to seventeenth centuries**: commented editions multiply in dependence on commercial publishing, **and several rival commentary editions of one novel compete for readers** (**standard account**). **This lesson stresses the line: criticism becomes a component of the book trade, and the critic's reputation is part of the sales pitch** (chapter fourteen's billing-and-patronage, arriving early in the wrong century). **Mid-seventeenth century**: a recension becomes the standard text, and a systematic dramaturgy appears (**standard account**). **Late seventeenth to eighteenth centuries**: commentary continues, while the throne repeatedly orders lists of prohibited books and the destruction of blocks (**standard account**). **Read the bans and the commentaries side by side: a prohibition is a reverse bibliography, and it did the critics' marketing for them** — said once in chapter two, evidenced here. **Late eighteenth to nineteenth centuries**: manuscripts and their commentary circulate, printed editions appear, and the centre of gravity of judgement moves from "how a piece is written" to plot and character (**standard account**). **Early twentieth century**: a history of theatre assembles the material by the new scholarly method, and one essay reads a novel through a foreign philosophy; then evidential research starts, and authorship and text become the questions of the field (**standard account**). **The last stroke: a class that had been excluded gained status at the same moment as the discipline that judged it was replaced**; **from an argument about how to read into an argument about evidence, and once evidence became the orthodoxy, taste lost its seat again** — which rounds off the "four uses" of the last chapter and opens the next.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Treating a commented edition as the author's intention** (**authenticity and layering are fields of study**, the text-building section); **treating a "books of genius" list as an achieved canon** (**an unfinished list is a promise**, lineage-building); **writing "the author meant" without material** (**rewrite on the reader's side**, the worked example); **equating prohibition with quality** (**a ban is a reverse bibliography**, history section 4 — **and do not treat "it was banned" as evidence of merit**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: status and discipline {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **the ascent of a class is almost always paid for by a change of track: first its standards are raised, then the institution that applies them is replaced. Fiction passed from being called a triviality to being required to enlighten and to reflect reality in the space of twenty years, and the second demand was more thorough than the first, because henceforth fiction had to prove its right to exist by somebody else's criteria. Critics build a lineage for a work, scholars build a pedigree; the services are the same and the price is the same.** Deploy at least six of: **the argument from qualification (childlike heart)**, **the function of the unfinished list**, **the three parts of close reading**, **criticism by editing**, **the empirical standard of the dramaturgy**, **prohibition as reverse bibliography**. Then adjudicate: **should an excluded class seek admission to the existing standards or found new ones — and name the real constraint on your choice.**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**you must name the constraint rather than a preference**). **Admission to existing standards**: the cost is the rewriting of the class's nature (the novel must be "as good as a poem"), the gain is immediate resources and a place in the syllabus; **the constraint to state is whether any of the existing standards is commensurable with what the class actually does.** **New standards**: explanatory fit is the gain; the cost is non-recognition — no funds, no posts, no students, **and nobody lives on explanatory fit alone**; the constraint is whether you can find a new funder or a new readership. **The historical evidence of this chapter: the rise of fiction and drama ran on three lines — critics raised its status, the market consolidated it, scholars ratified it last — and the order cannot be reversed.** **Highest marks for that three-line analysis.** **Bonus for noting that the institution also protects**: manuscripts, data, funds and networks are held by institutions, and the lone scholar's predicament is precisely that nobody pays for her archive; **so "compliance" is better described as working in exchange for infrastructure, which is a bargain and not a surrender.** A conclusion able to write "**so this chapter offers no choice but a diagnosis: a new standard proposed before its community exists decays into private vocabulary; an old standard accepted without resistance decays into an examination. The critic's whole job is to recruit and to change the standard simultaneously, in the narrow seam between the two — one man did the first, another the second, and nobody we have a name for has done both**" earns an A.
:::
