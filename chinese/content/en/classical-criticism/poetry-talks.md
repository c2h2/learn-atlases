---
title: "Tang and Song poetry talks"
---

The last chapter ranked poets; the material of this one is the thousand-year accumulation of notes that people kept dropping into one another's drawers — **hundreds of "poetry talks" (诗话) form the ordinary genre of Chinese criticism. This chapter argues that their casualness is an epistemology, and that their scatter brought about an evidentiary regime nobody intended**.

## A genre {#def-wenti}

::: definition The entry form and its four parts {#def-sibujian}
**The standard account**: the name "poetry talks" comes from an eleventh-century work that described itself as material for idle talk, and imitators were numerous; several dozen Song examples survive (**standard account**). A typical entry contains four parts. **① The occasion (本事)**: who wrote which line, and when, and before whom. **② The line**: the couplet or single verse extracted. **③ The verdict**: a judgement, usually delivered as a simile or analogy. **④ The appendix**: notes on a word's sense, on an official's career, on a variant reading. **This lesson weights the fourth part highly: it is the earliest citation habit in Chinese criticism** — alongside the judgement, the writer supplies something checkable. **And its reliability must be assessed separately, because the occasions are largely hearsay** (**standard account**: commentators warn constantly that the anecdotes in poetry-talks cannot be trusted and must be collated against history and collected works). The handling in this chapter: **parts 2 and 3 are criticism; parts 1 and 4 are evidence. Confusing them is the first way to read a poetry-talk wrongly** — that sentence is the whole historical method of the chapter, and you will need it for every "it is said" source you ever use.
:::

::: definition Why not write a system {#def-tixi}
**An obvious question**: Song writers had the capacity for systematic treatises, so why did the discussion of poetry stay in entries? Four answers that do not exclude each other. **① Epistemic**: if the excellence of poetry lies mainly where it cannot be stated, then to systematise is a betrayal — this line runs from the "flavour" of the last chapter to the Chan of the next. **② Social**: the genre's implied reader is someone who can answer back; **the entry form is the transcript of a conversation, not a lecture**. **③ Practical**: what men at table needed was quotable items, not demonstrations. **④ Publishable**: note-books could be issued in separate juan and circulate independently, **so a genre that survives for a thousand years is usually one that fits how texts were produced**. **The conclusion of this lesson: the four answers each explain a part, and the fourth — publishing — is the one habitually missed because it is the most uncomfortable: it says that the prevalence of a form can be explained by distribution rather than by spirit.** The chapter on billing and the patron occurred eight hundred years earlier; here it happens again.
:::

## Three Song moves {#def-banfa}

::: definition Turning iron into gold, stealing the胎 and exchanging the bone {#def-diantie}
**The standard account**: a poet and his followers left two sets of phrases — one says that making verse is like turning iron into gold with a single pellet of elixir, another distinguishes "steosing the胎" from "exchanging the meaning", though the exact sense of these formulas is still disputed (**standard account**). Read this as the most important Chinese theory of rewriting. **① The problem it solves**: in a society where everyone has read the same predecessors, every fresh line must resemble someone; the question is therefore not how to be original but **how to inherit lawfully**. **② The answer it gives**: the legitimacy of inheritance lies not in your having read, but in your having improved — take the other man's meaning and make it better, or keep the meaning and change the words. **This lesson calls it a re-use licence: it specifies the conditions under which another's sentence may appear in your poem without being theft.** ③ **Its opponent** is "direct pursuit" from the last chapter: fine lines are not got by borrowing. **So Chinese poetics stages its most useful two-thousand-year argument: is writing an encounter or a rewrite?** This lesson's ruling: **a pseudo-question, because the two sides describe different stages — material comes from encounter, finished work comes from rewriting; the real disagreement is about where the weight of evaluation falls.** State that in the essay and then attack my ruling; this chapter does not accept a both-are-right conclusion.
:::

::: definition Living rules {#def-huofa}
**The standard account**: Song critics speak of "living rules" (活法) — the learner must first take in the rules and then be able to stand outside them; and they borrow from Chan the distinction between a "live phrase" and a "dead phrase" (**standard account**). Read as pedagogy: **① it ranks rules in two orders** — teachable and unteachable; **② it concedes that teaching has a side-effect** — rules held too tightly become constraint, hence the need for a second step; **③ and it is a retrospective ratification**: "living" is what is called of those who succeeded, and "lawless" of those who did not. Here is the sharpest sentence of the chapter: **every craft invents a word like "living" at the moment it has to explain why its teaching fails some pupils** — it works for writing, investing, medicine and Go. **Whenever training material tells you to "apply the method flexibly", translate it as "we are not responsible here", and then decide whether to pay.**
:::

::: definition Three Tang theses {#def-tangren}
Go back to the Tang for comparison. **①** A poet's long letter to a friend maintains that writing should be composed for the age and song for the occasion (**standard account**) — the extreme form of the job theory, and its most honest, because it admits that its purpose is remonstrance and accepts a downgrade of style in exchange. **②** Another writer, composing a preface, says that words of joy are hard to make good and words of hardship easily good (**standard account**) — the writing courses have already supplied the sociological reading; add here that **it converts a claim about circumstances into a claim about quality, and so can be misused into an praise of deprivation**. **③** Yet others pursue "a charm beyond the rhyme" and "a flavour beyond the taste" (**standard account**); and a work that sorts poetic moods into twenty-four kinds is transmitted under the name of one of them, with the authorship still unresolved (see chapter one). **The lesson of the three: our notices must record the setting in which each was uttered — ① and ② are letters and prefaces, private explanations; ③ is a reply to a correspondent, a demonstration.** None of them is a paper, which is why none of them tries to be consistent. **That is the fundamental difference between Tang criticism and Song talk: the Tang insight is mostly incidental, the Song mostly methodological; the incidental is deeper, the methodological is transmissible.**
:::

::: example Dismantling one entry {#ex-chaotiao}
Here is an invented poetry-talk entry (**composed for this lesson**): "**When a certain minister was young he passed a monastery, saw a couplet on the wall — 'such and such, such and such' — and admired it. Enquiring, he found it was by a contemporary poet. Later the poet came to court, and the minister recommended him. The world admired the minister's judgement of men.**" Split it into the four parts, decide what it can prove about the couplet and about the minister's discernment, and state what else you would need.

::: solution
**Parts**: the **occasion** (passing the temple, seeing the wall, asking, recommending); the **line** (quoted); the **verdict** ("admired", "the world admired"); the **appendix**: none — **which is precisely why this is an anecdote and not a piece of scholarship**. **Evidential grade.** For the attribution of the couplet, the entry supplies only one hearsay rank: a single narrator relaying a signed act at two removes, and writing on a wall is the class of attribution most easily mis-recorded, post-written later, or forged (**standard account**: commentators on wall inscriptions and matched verses constantly warn of this). For "the minister had judgement", **that is a moral claim supported by a causal chain inside the same anecdote**: the link between "recommended" and "admired his poetry" is arranged by the teller, not recorded — **this is what the chapter means by mixing the columns: a story uses an aesthetic act to certify an ethical one**. **What you would still need**: independent attribution of the couplet (another collection, another anthology, a local gazetteer); an independent record of the recommendation (a draft edict, a memorial, a chronological biography); and whether contemporaries also reported the story — **several independent accounts agreeing is what lifts a claim to the second rank** (**standard account**, and the same four-grade scale used since chapter one). **Marking: whoever writes "the story uses an aesthetic act to certify an ethical one" takes an A.** That judgement moves out of the eleventh century: it holds for celebrity interviews, corporate prospectuses, and every "he is very modest, they say". **This is also why the next chapter needed a man who would set a standard for all Tang poetry at once: where the evidence is thin, people turn to authority.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Poetry-talks are the manuscript age's social feed {#int-pengyouquan}
**One analogy, two limits.** Where they resemble a feed: short, fast, centred on extracted lines, circulated by re-sharing, author and reader interchangeable, full of inside jokes; low information density, high social density. Where they differ. **① They accumulate across a lifetime**: one man notes one thing a year, and a hundred men's notebooks become a body of material; **the life of a social post is measured in hours**. That is the crucial difference, and it is a cost: **poetry-talks sediment because copying and carving were expensive, and cost filters — what the filter kept is not always the better half.** ② **They do not demand instant reply**, so an error can slowly propagate and, slowly, be corrected: **a wrong attribution in one talk is amended by a later talk** (**standard account**). Against the last chapter's "no recension, no history", note that the genre has a slow self-correction precisely because it is a thousand-year continuous act of writing. **The method this lesson draws: judge the knowledge value of a genre not by the quality of a single item but by its accumulation and correction mechanisms.** That sentence applies to comment sections, to wiki histories, and to the literature review you are writing.
:::

::: history A thousand years of annotations {#sec-tianzhu}
**Ninth to tenth centuries**: letters on poetry, prefaces, and a few specialist works; by the end of the Tang there are already works arranging poets in grades (**standard account**). **Around 1070**: a notebook that calls itself idle talk is completed, and the genre receives its name (**standard account**). **Eleventh to twelfth centuries**: the population of authors widens; occasions, textual notes and questions of phrase are mixed freely; and much of what we know of the Jiangxi school's account of its own transmission is preserved in notebooks (**standard account**). **Thirteenth century**: a comparatively systematic work on poetry written in the idiom of Chan appears — the subject of the next chapter — **and this chapter stresses that it stands inside an ocean of notebooks and that its form expresses dissatisfaction with that genre** (**standard account**). **Eighteenth century**: a collected edition of the great poetry-talks is compiled, so that chatting acquires canonical status; in the same decades a huge imperial bibliography installs a category for "prose and poetry criticism" (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: new editions of the texts and, alongside them, the charge that the genre is fragmentary (**standard account**). **This lesson's closing point: "fragmentary" is a verdict issued by a discipline that takes the system as its default; by the criterion this chapter proposed — accumulation and correction — the poetry-talks are the most effective infrastructure Chinese criticism ever built. They preserve almost every writing occasion we still know about, and without them we should not even know the dates of many famous lines. That is the floor on which all the theory stands, and it was laid by small talk.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Treating the occasion as source material** (**grade the evidence first**, the four parts and the worked example); **reading the entry form as carelessness** (**it is epistemic, social, practical and publishable at once**, why not a system); **using "living rules" to explain failure** (**"living" ratifies the successful**, living rules); **accepting a sweeping verdict such as "Tang is skilful, Song is dull"** (**this chapter's judgement is that the incidental is deeper and the methodological transmissible** — the three Tang theses: a verdict of that kind is the first thing an examiner will ask you to evidence).
:::

::: exercise Dismantle three entries {level=1}
Take three entries from any poetry-talk (or any book of artists talking about their work), split each into four parts, and mark the evidential grade of each part.

::: solution
Marking: **each entry must be reported with its missing part**, and you must say what would have to be added for it to count as evidence (the four parts above).

Write a two-hundred-word poetry-talk entry in the four-part form about a contemporary work you admire, then dismantle it: name the part you cannot check.

::: solution

::: exercise Essay: depth against transmissibility {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **Tang criticism is mostly a side-remark made by geniuses while doing something else; Song criticism is mostly a worksheet written by craftsmen for apprentices. The side-remark is deeper, because it has no pedagogic aim; the worksheet is transmissible, because it assumes someone who must get to work. A civilisation usually gets the side-remarks first and the worksheets afterwards, and once the worksheets exist the side-remarks are reinterpreted as their ancestors — the commonest inversion in the history of a discipline.** Deploy at least six of: **the four parts and the historical method**, **the four causes of the entry form**, **iron into gold against direct pursuit**, **the pedagogy of living rules**, **the settings of the three Tang theses**, **accumulation and correction**. Then adjudicate: **is your own discipline writing side-remarks or worksheets — on the evidence of what it actually publishes, with no "both"?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**you must choose and defend one**). **Worksheets**: a passing argument cites the two-section ritual of method and data, the reproducibility requirement, and the "future work may…" ending — all of them addressed to someone who is going to do something; **so what we produce is procedure, not insight, and this lesson counts that as honest self-description** — **with its cost stated: side-remark criticism has nowhere to publish, and the next generation's depth will have no source.** **Side-remarks**: a passing argument, more scathingly true, notes that criticism lives in reviews, podcasts and teaching, where its business is立场 and taste, and that the journal article is only its citation voucher; **accepted, provided three living examples are named**. **"Both" is not accepted, because it evades the adjudication.** The preference of this lesson: **if you notice the inversion actually happening** — in recent years the worksheet genres themselves have become research objects, with studies of methods, formats and evaluation regimes multiplying — **that is a discipline examining its own production rather than its objects, which is the method of this chapter turned on the course itself** (the same operation as the last chapter's "earliest is a title awarded by a discipline"). **Highest marks.** A conclusion able to write "**so the lesson is not that the Tang were better than the Song, but that each genre delivers only what it can deliver; and that a discipline grows up when it learns to ask, of the format I am using, what it can deliver and what it is bound to miss**" earns an A.
:::
