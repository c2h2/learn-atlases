title: "Yan Yu and Chan"

The last chapter ended inside an ocean of notebooks, in which one book was dissatisfied with the form itself — **this book says that poetry is not to be reached by reason and does not deal in reasons, yet it is among the most systematically organised Chinese books about poetry. This chapter explains the paradox, and explains why the vocabulary was borrowed from another religion**.

## The shape of the book {#def-xingzhuang}

::: definition Five divisions and one thesis {#def-wumen}
**The standard account**: a treatise on poetry is arranged in several divisions, conventionally listed as "On the Discrimination of Poetry" (诗辨), "On Styles of Poetry", "On Methods of Poetry", "On Criticising Poetry" and "On Evidential Research", with a letter to a friend appended to state its intent; it was written in the first half of the thirteenth century. The thesis sentences are among the most quoted in the tradition: the Way of Chan lies in awakening, and the way of poetry also lies in awakening; poetry has a special material, which has nothing to do with books, and a special flavour, which has nothing to do with principle; it does not travel by the road of reason and does not fall inside the trap of words. **Begin with the form**. The five divisions are a sequence of setting a standard, sorting the styles, giving a curriculum, delivering verdicts, and finally handling matters of evidence — **a textbook contents page, not a string of notes** (**standard account**). **The first paradox is therefore this: a book that insists on "not travelling by the road of reason" has chosen the form most able to reason**. The solution offered here: **Yan Yu's claim is not that poetry cannot be said but that it cannot be proved**. What cannot be proved cannot be handed over by argument; it can only be indicated by order and by contrast. **The five divisions are the gesture of indicating**. That reading rescues the book from being called mysticism against intellect, and equally prevents it from being called a coherent system: **it is a guide to recognising, not a theory of what poetry is**. Keep this sentence as the ballast of the chapter.
:::

::: definition Awakening and thorough acquaintance {#def-miaowu}
**The standard account**: two sets of statements stand in the same book — on awakening (妙悟), and on thorough acquaintance (熟参), on beginning from the highest vehicle and possessing the correct eye of the law. Their relation is the most mistreated crux in Chinese criticism. **① Awakening is a conclusion**: it describes how discriminative competence arrives — suddenly one can tell. **② Thorough acquaintance is the method**: read and compare enough that you can rank without deliberation (**standard account**). **The genuinely executable sentence in Yan Yu is therefore the second, whose operational meaning is that judgement comes from the volume and the ordering of a comparison set, not from a rule-book** — the epistemology of every apprenticeship and every peer-review system, and the senior version of the writing course's dictum that selection is editing. **③ Confusing the two levels produces two misreadings**: take the conclusion as the method and you get the poet who need not read; take the method as the conclusion and you get the scholar for whom reading much is being good (**standard account**: both misreadings have historically occurred, and both claimed Yan Yu as their author)**. The tool this lesson wants you to keep: a doctrine is cited less for what it says than for what it leaves out**.
:::

## Borrowed words {#def-jieci}

::: definition Why Chan {#def-weishenme}
**A methodological question**: the author could have borrowed from the classics, from philosophy, or from the schools of names; why did he take the vocabulary of Chan? The answer is one general law: **when a field borrows terms, it borrows the liveliest question currently running in the other field**. **① Inside Chan the argument of the day was where authority comes from** — the patriarch, the canonical text, sudden awakening, the dharma lineage, orthodoxy and heterodoxy. **② In poetics the very same thing was in dispute** — Han Wei or high Tang or the present dynasty; whom to study counts as initiation, whom counts as going astray (**standard account**). **The two disputes have the same shape, so the words travel.** And what travels is not a term but a whole grammar of doctrine-classification, which gave Yan Yu's criticism three things it had not had: **a lineage** (a descent chart), **a curriculum** (an order of stages from shallow to deep), and **a verdict of heterodoxy** (a critic may pronounce a man unawakened without having to identify a single wrong character). **The third is the point of this chapter: it made judgement unfalsifiable, which is why posterity loved it and why it deserves reproach.** The previous chapter required of every borrowed category that it state what it was originally used to refute; **here is the demonstration: Yan Yu borrowed Chan in order to refute the reduction of poetry to a quantity of reading** (**standard account**: his targets include writing by talent-for-learning and by argument). **That borrowing was honest, because he carried over the thing being refuted along with the words.**
:::

::: definition The price of the first meaning {#def-diyiyi}
**The standard account**: the book urges the learner to begin from the supreme vehicle and to awaken to the "first meaning", and installs the high Tang as a standard that cannot be exceeded. Four consequences, two gains and two costs. **Gain ①: it creates a historical sense** — to arrange the poets of the ages in a gradable sequence is the closest Chinese criticism comes to writing literary history. **Gain ②: it creates a teachable course** — what to study first, then, and what must not be touched. **Cost ③: it fixes the standard in a past segment of the literature**, after which any new work can only be read as approaching or departing from the Tang; **a system whose absolute criterion is a period must judge its own contemporaries either degenerate or presumptuous** — which is the practical effect inherited by the archaists of the Ming (**standard account**). **Cost ④: it banishes the opponent from discussion**, since "not awakened" is an unfalsifiable charge: you may call it unreasonable, but you cannot produce a case that refutes it. **A term that can explain every failure while supplying no checkable criterion has stopped being an instrument of criticism and become a badge of identity.** That is the critical peak of the chapter: chapter five's "a refutable form is the invention of criticism" has here its counter-example. And cost ③ reminds us that a standard fixed in a past period is usually advanced by people who need a canon to support a faction today — **the open secret of every classicising movement**. It will be answered in chapter eight, where the excluded classes build a lineage of their own.
:::

::: definition The four similes {#def-biyu}
**The standard account**: the book contains the famous string in which the good poem is likened to a sound in empty air, a colour in a mirror image, a moon in water, an elephant in a mirror, the words ending while the idea has no term. Technical analysis, item by item. **① What the four share is the structure "visible but not graspable"**: sound cannot be held, an image cannot be touched, the moon in water cannot be scooped up, the elephant in the mirror has no body. **They are not four different perfections but one thesis proved four times.** ② **They are all handling the same semantic problem**: the meaning of a poem is neither inside the words (else it could be exhausted) nor outside them (else it would be indeterminate) — precisely the mediation problem that the meaning triangle poses, which Yan Yu declines to state and instead images. **③ Are they argument or ornament?** The judgement here: **they are a weak form of argument. Setting four unrelated domains side by side supplies four analogical securities for a proposition that cannot be proved** (**standard account**: four-fold similes are among the most persuasive standard moves of parallel prose), and their force depends on the reader's familiarity with all four domains. **Readers today feel their beauty and miss their argument, which is not our loss but the expiry date of that argumentative form.** Refute me in the essay if you like; do not pretend the sentence is neutral.
:::

::: example Find today's grammar of doctrine {#ex-panjiao}
Examine a contemporary evaluative system you know — a literary prize, a canonical reading list in your discipline, a "where to start" list circulated by a critic. **Show whether it carries Yan Yu's three-piece kit: a lineage, a curriculum, and a verdict of heterodoxy.** If it does, name its "first meaning" and ask how people placed outside respond.

::: solution
**A possible answer**: **① Lineage** — "serious fiction descends from two or three modernists" narratives; **② Curriculum** — "read these twenty before you talk about contemporary writing", issued by reviews and podcasts; **③ Verdict of heterodoxy** — "that is not literature, it is genre fiction / self-published / online serial". **The first meaning** is usually not a property at all but a stance of taste — **and any formulation that makes the judgement unverifiable belongs to the technology of declaring heterodoxy.** **Three possible responses by the excluded**: accept the standard and rewrite your work to fit it; **build a second lineage**, inventing a descent chart and two dead champions for the excluded class — **the commonest successful strategy, and chapter eight will stage it in full for fiction and drama**; or attack the criterion itself, exposing "unverifiable judgement" as an identity badge, which requires of the audience exactly the refutability standard taught in chapter five — **the rarest of all audiences**. **Marking: whoever can see the second response as lineage replication rather than as progress takes an A.** It shows that a category wins status not by overthrowing the standard but by founding another, **so the whole history of canonisation is a history of who is entitled to write the catalogue** — the same thread that ran from the anthology pedigree of chapter three to the last chapter's "earliest is an award". **It will be the theme of the closing chapters.**
:::
:::

::: intuition Awakening in the examination room {#int-dayan}
**A daily test**. Have you ever heard a verdict such as "this one has the feeling", "this passage is wrong", "the breath is not correct"? **In current usage these do exactly what "awakening" does: they are not evaluation but the termination of discussion.** Three consequences. **① They favour whoever speaks**: an unverifiable judgement transfers its cost to the person judged. **② They are sometimes true**: the speaker may genuinely have seen enough cases to tell without stating a criterion — **the product of thorough acquaintance.** ③ **They are therefore an honest predicament**, not a fraud: a person with discrimination often cannot hand over his grounds. **The treatment this course prescribes: do not demand a rule; demand a pair.** *If this passage is wrong, take another passage by the same author that is right, put them side by side, and tell me at which sentence you hear the difference.* That question almost always forces a checkable ground out of the speaker; and if it does not, **he has reason to withdraw the verdict**. **Use paired samples to extract the criterion** — which is also precisely what Yan Yu himself recommended (熟参). **His real legacy is not the word "awakening" but a drill.** The whole chapter has existed in order to say so.
:::

::: history The four uses of one book {#sec-sipai}
**Thirteenth century**: the book is written while the influence of the Jiangxi school and its "living rules" revision are still being argued, and the market question is "how does one learn?" (**standard account**). **Thirteenth to fourteenth centuries**: its theses are cited and contested; **Yuan critics of the "learn from the ancients" and the "principled nature" camps each take what they need** (**standard account**). **Sixteenth century**: the Later Archives proclaim that poetry must be Tang, and the grammar of their claim is the grammar of this book; **historians of criticism have made the book their herald** (**standard account**). **This lesson's caution: heralds are always appointed afterwards**; the story elevates one book into a founder and deletes its other Song sources. **Seventeenth century**: the theorists of spirit-resonance pick up the thread, while scholars of evidential learning write a refutation of Yan Yu's misuse of Chan terminology (**standard account**: Feng Ban is the name to look up). **This is a rare "terminology audit" in the history of the field: what is examined is not the poetic thesis but whether the borrowed words were used correctly**, and its procedure is more instructive than its conclusion. **Eighteenth century**: the great imperial bibliography assigns the book a category and a notice (**standard account**), **and the shelf it was put on determined the next two hundred years of reading**. **Twentieth century**: it is read as a forerunner of "aesthetic autonomy", "imagistic thinking" and "pure poetry" (**standard account**). **The closing observation: this sequence of uses shows that the "influence" of a book is mostly later readers taking from it the sentences that match their own agenda** — and Yan Yu's book happened to supply mystical, textbook, factional and anti-evidential raw material all at once. **Chapter six's "re-shelved for nine hundred years" upgrades here to "quoted by four camps without any of them minding the others"; that is not the success of the book but the number of blanks in its wording.** You have now seen the formula from chapter one applied seven times; you should be able to run it yourself.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Treating awakening as a method** (**it is a conclusion; the method is thorough acquaintance**); **treating "does not deal in reason" as anti-intellectual** (**it rejects exhaustive explanation, not understanding**); **using "not awakened" style verdicts** (**unverifiable judgement transfers its cost to the judged**, the examination-room section); **accepting a "herald" story** (**heralds are appointed afterwards**, history section 3 — **be ready with a second example in your viva**).

::: exercise Essay: unfalsifiable judgement {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **awakening is the ideal form of judgement: it describes expert experience exactly — the expert sees and cannot tell — and it is simultaneously criticism's worst language, because it exempts the judge from accounting. The growth of a critical discipline can be written as the extent to which it translates awakening into checkable operations while honestly admitting what the translation loses. Yan Yu did both: he spoke of awakening and taught acquaintance; his posterity remembered only the first sentence.** Deploy at least six of: **the paradox of form and thesis**, **the two levels of awakening and acquaintance**, **the borrowing mechanism and the three-piece grammar**, **the two gains and two losses of the first meaning**, **the argumentative status of the four similes**, **the four uses of the book**. Then adjudicate: **are there judgements that cannot in principle be translated, and if so how should a discipline handle them without turning them into power?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**you must give an institution, not a moral**). **A passing shape: admit that some states of mind are hard to hand over** (for instance "this line is true") **but impose three conditions: ① every such judgement must come with a paired sample** — the claimant must produce the pair on which he hears a difference, the drill of this chapter and its only demand; **② the samples, not the verdict, must be open to colleagues' inspection**; **③ an appeal channel must exist, meaning the judge's own past verdicts are on record, so that "what has he ruled before" is knowable** — **that is the conversion of doctrinal judgement into case law**, isomorphic with the last course's rule that "the executive of a standard is hundreds of thousands of ears". **Missing condition ① degenerates into private taste; missing ② into a vote; ③ is the constraint on the judge himself, and this chapter's only answer to "awakening becomes power".** Highest marks go here. **Bonus credit for the institutional deduction that ③ is how scholarly communities already work and never write down in methodology** — the reliability of peer review rests on reviewers being identifiable, and editorial double-blindness weakens exactly that. A conclusion able to write "**so we do not demand that Yan Yu explain himself, which his age and his genre could not do; we demand that today's authors stop using his inexplainability as a shield. You may say you see a difference you cannot state, provided the pair on which you see it goes in the footnote. That one requirement will eliminate a third of all judgements, which is its whole value**" earns an A.
:::
