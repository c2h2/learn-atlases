---
title: "Poetry Expresses Intent"
---

From here this course turns to what Chinese writers said about literature in their own words, and the first piece of evidence is three characters long — **诗言志, "poetry voices intent." The argument of this chapter is that Chinese literary criticism begins not with "how should one write" but with "who may say what, on poetry's authority"; its first chapter is therefore not aesthetics but a history of permission**.

## The provenance of a programme {#def-gangling}

::: definition Three characters, and the setting they occur in {#def-sange}
**The standard account**: the formula 诗言志 is found in transmitted literature of high antiquity, and Warring States masters use similar turns of phrase when talking about the *Odes*; modern historians of criticism have called it China's "foundational programme" of literary thought (朱自清). This lesson begins with a tedious task, namely finding out *in what setting* the sentence was uttered. It surfaces in two kinds of context: passages on ritual, music and instruction, and records of the *Odes* being quoted in diplomacy and in convivial exchange. **Note the second kind**. Aristocrats of the Spring and Autumn period would have a piece of the *Odes* performed or recited at a banquet in order to convey to those present something they could not say in their own names; one much-quoted remark says, in effect, that those who quote the *Odes* each take what they need (**standard account**). **So the first sentence of Chinese literary theory describes an act of quotation rather than an act of composition**. From this the chapter's thesis follows: **the earliest problem of poetics is a problem of pragmatics**. When a sentence sits in someone else's mouth and is put to use here and now, who answers for its meaning? That question is still the question of every theory of citation two thousand three hundred years later. The ethics of quotation taught in the rhetoric course and in the course on evidence has its pre-Qin beginning here.
:::

::: definition What a 志 is {#def-zhi}
**Three layers in one word**: in early texts 志 carries at least the sense of what the heart goes towards (inclination and purpose), of recording (记, what is noted down and held in mind), and of marking or discerning (识, a sign and a judgement) (**standard account**). This lesson declines any of the etymologies and points instead to a structural fact: **the word 志 binds personal intention to moral weight from the outset**. Saying "my intent" is more creditable than saying "my feeling", because it implies something directional, something that can be inspected and something for which someone can be held answerable. That is the mechanism by which the intent theory held the field for two millennia: **it granted private feeling a public status, at the price of making it liable to questioning**. The lesson states the transferable judgement: whenever a theory defines expression as a declaration, it issues a licence and erects a censor in the same breath. This will be re-enacted in later chapters on "writing as the exposition of the way" and on song and voice, and it applies to any present-day quarrel about having to answer for one's words. The idea that face is a transferable account, familiar from the logic and argument courses, has here an antique version: **to quote a poem is to spend private money from a public account**. That point is needed again in the next chapter on Confucian and Daoist views of literature.
:::

::: definition The two columns of a preface {#def-lianglan}
**The standard account**: the *Great Preface* to the *Odes*, handed down from the Han, states that intent lodged in the heart is 志 and uttered in speech it is 诗; that feeling moves in the breast and issues in words, and when words fall short it continues into sighs and song; and that the poems of the "changed airs" are "born of feeling and stopped by ritual and right" (发乎情，止乎礼义). This lesson reads the passage as a two-column table. **The first column is genetic**: the ascent from feeling to speech to sighing to song, which explains why poetry carries musicality — because language is not enough. This matters very much: **it is the rare "insufficiency of medium" argument in Chinese criticism. Poetry exists because prose cannot do the job**; the study of metre and the use of allusion later in this atlas are both descendants of that argument. **The second column is normative**: feeling is the material, ritual and right are the shape, and the two are not opposites but consecutive operations. How this lesson reads it: the line is often cited as evidence of repression, but it is closer to a workshop specification. **It concedes that feeling is the only raw material, and prescribes a deliverable shape for the finished article**. Repression, if it comes, is not in this sentence but in the question of who gets to fix where "stopping" falls. Hence the sentence "gently and generously: this is the teaching of poetry" (温柔敦厚，诗教也) (**attested in the ritual literature**), which turns an aesthetic requirement into a register rule: **criticism stops asking whether you were honest and starts asking whether you kept to an intensity that can be tolerated**. The aphorism that a hedge is a bumper — used throughout the rhetoric course — has here a precedent two thousand years old, and the exercises below will ask what that hedge is worth today.
:::

::: definition Praise, satire, and observation {#def-mei}
**The standard account**: the old vocabulary of *Odes* criticism contains three functional terms — 美 (praise), 刺 (satire), 观 (inspection of customs). This lesson singles out 观 because it is the design of an information system. **① Who is doing the observing?** Nominally the ruler; hence the legitimacy of criticism derives from its usefulness to government rather than from its correctness. **② What is observed with?** Ballads and poems, precisely because they circulate locally without passing through the memorial system. This is an insight about channels of information: **the ruler knows that official reports lie, so he installs an informal channel dressed as aesthetics** (**standard account**: the legend of officials who collected folk songs). This lesson reads the legend as institutional imagination: **what it envisages is a form of evidence that a subordinate cannot forge**. **③ The price.** Legitimacy is bought with aesthetics: once a poem is required to be "inspectable", it begins automatically to dress itself up. Here is the hardest sentence of the chapter: **a system of inspection gives poetry a status and gives it a task, and the task slowly turns poems into briefing material**. What was said about observation in the writing courses is exactly isomorphic: experience that is harvested stops being experience once it is on the harvesting sheet.
:::

::: definition The second pair: writing from pent-up resentment {#def-fafen}
**The standard account**: a Han historian, in a letter that is also a confession, lists several works composed in distress, saying in effect that their authors had something knotted inside and so set down the past in the hope of later readers; later ages condensed this into phrases such as "writing from anger" (发愤著书). This lesson introduces it as the rival of the intent theory. **Intent**: expression is a directional, deliverable declaration. **Resentment**: expression is a discharge after long pressure; its readership may not exist, and its first function is survival. The fault line between them recurs across two thousand years: **poetry as public act against poetry as evidence of having endured**. A criterion for the student: when you read a confession claiming "I wrote so as not to go mad", and that same text was in fact carved, printed and circulated, do not imagine a contradiction. **In practice the resentment topos is often a publishing strategy: it uses the author's suffering as surety for the text** — and suffering is the hardest surety to rebut (**standard account**: the later critical commonplace that "poverty makes the verse fine" (穷而后工) belongs here; the writing courses have already supplied its sociological reading: **to describe a predicament as a quality is criticism's most charitable misunderstanding**). This chapter adds one sentence only: **the theory of resentment outlives the theory of intent because its surety never needs replacing**.
:::

::: example One line, three settings {#ex-sange}
Take the line from "Without Clothes" (《无衣》) that asks "Who says we lack clothes? I share my cloak with you" (**the line is transmitted and readable**). Suppose you quote it in three settings: **A**, rallying people for a joint enterprise; **B**, commemorating a dead companion afterwards; **C**, reproaching a friend for not being allied enough. Write down, for each, what the hearer understands and what the speaker takes on, and then answer: **which of these three meanings is "the meaning of the line"**?

::: solution
**A** takes "shared cloak" as shared equipment and shared fighting, a summons: **the hearer understands "we are bound together", and the speaker assumes the obligation "on this basis you owe something"**. **B** takes it as "we once shared a cloak": the same sentence moved into the past tense, and what the speaker assumes is "I acknowledge that this relationship existed". **C** takes the rhetorical question "who says we lack clothes": **the sharpest use, because it reproaches the other for claiming scarcity as an excuse**; what the hearer receives is "your difficulties do not count as difficulties", which is where quotation does its easiest wound. Which meaning is the line's? **This lesson answers in three layers**. First, the historical-language layer: the poem's own setting in the transmitted text differs from all three (**the consensus is that it belongs to the songs of Qin, something like a camp song, but even its occasion is unsettled**). Second, the pragmatic layer: all three meanings are real, because they were understood and moved people to act (the meaning triangle of the logic course: meaning lies between reference and effect, not inside the word). Third, the institutional layer: the three meanings do not have equal standing — **which one gets into the commentary tradition depends on which reading was most useful to government and instruction in that age**. Marking: **whoever writes the third layer gets an A**. It tells us that quarrels over the meaning of classical texts were never only quarrels about interpretation but about who has the right to stipulate what a sentence stands for in our tradition; every subsequent chapter of this course fights that fight, and the next chapter on Confucian and Daoist views makes it visible at once.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why this programme is so hard to refute {#int-nantui}
**The nature of the theory**: "poetry voices intent" is a theory about speakers, not about texts. **It does not ask what a poem looks like; it asks what was in the mind of the person who made it**. Three consequences. **① Its evidence lies otherwhere in other people's heads**, so it is almost unfalsifiable: any counter-instance can be absorbed as "he really had a different intent". The modern quarrel about authorial intention has here its three-thousand-year-old exemplar. **② It can absorb opponents without limit**. When "poetry springs from feeling" arrives, feeling is declared a species of intent; when imitation arrives, the imitator has intent; when form arrives, the choice of form reveals intent. **A theory that can absorb every opponent is not thereby true; it has merely transferred its falsifiability to its users**. **③ Its real vitality lies in the institutions that use it**: so long as education takes "know the person, discuss the age" as its principal method of reading, and so long as criticism ends with "what does this tell us about the author", the formula keeps running. The pedagogic requirement of this lesson: **every time you write "the author here expresses", you should know that you are deploying a three-thousand-year-old formula, and you should be able to say how you know he had that intent** — from chains of evidence to the rhetoric of citation, this course has already said it four times. That is why a course of theory is taught here as a course in logic.
:::

::: history Three rewrites of a programme {#sec-gaixie}
**Spring and Autumn to Warring States**: the practice of quoting the *Odes* at missions and banquets; the masters quoting them to support their own claims (**standard account**). **This lesson's first rewrite**: 志 here is a public, inspectable disposition, not the modern private interior. **Han**: the *Odes* are enrolled as a classic, and its prefaces and commentaries constitute an interpretive system, while the Music Bureau collects ballads which commentators say are "moved by grief and joy, issued in response to events" (**attested in the dynastic treatises on literature**). An important juxtaposition: **collection and interpretation are taken over by the state at the same moment**. **Song**: Neo-Confucian literary thought re-reads "voicing intent" as the outcrop of moral cultivation, so the standard of judgement shifts towards the author's character and his effort (**standard account**). **This lesson calls it the internalisation of moral surety**: formerly one asked whether the poem fitted ritual and right; now one asks whether the author is sincere within. **Ming to Qing**: the arguments between archaism and individual inspiration keep calling up the old pair 志 and 情, each side accusing the other of having no intent or of drowning in feeling (**standard account**). **The 1940s**: a study titled after the formula arranges this material as a history of criticism (**the book is conventionally assigned to Zhu Ziqing**). This lesson's verdict: **it did two things at once — it established the formula as the programme, and it made us see that it had been established**. **After the 1950s**: the dichotomy "realist versus romantic" is used to rearrange the history of Chinese literature (**standard account**). The chapter's closing observation: **the lifespan of a formula is determined by how often it can be rewritten without changing its name** — which is also a tool for judging any theory: count how many times it has been rewritten without dying, and you have measured the blanks left in its wording. That sentence can be applied to any institutional history you will ever be asked to write.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Reading 志 as a modern "ideal"** (**in early usage it is an inspectable public disposition**, the entry on the word above); **reading "stopped by ritual and right" as repression alone** (**it simultaneously concedes the material and prescribes the shape**, the two columns above); **treating quotation as misreading** (**cutting the verse to suit the occasion was the normal use of the period**, the programme above); **writing "the author expresses" without producing evidence** (**that is an unfalsifiable construction unless you attach a chain of evidence**, intuition sections 1 and 2 above — **all the marks lost in this chapter are lost on these two points**).
:::

::: exercise Find one act of cutting to the occasion {level=1}
Find a present-day instance of quoting a classic — a speech, an official document, an advertisement, a paper — and set the quotation's original setting against its current use, then judge whether the citation is honest.

::: solution
Marking: **the original setting must be stated**, and **you must state whether the argument would still stand if the line were read in its own sense** (the three layers above); **writing only "aptly used" earns nothing**.
:::

::: exercise Essay: a history of permission {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **to write the opening of Chinese literary theory as a history of aesthetics is a translation accident of the nineteenth century; in its own shape it is closer to a history of licensing: what may be said through the mouths of the ancients, to whom, at what intensity, and who answers when it goes wrong. Most quarrels about genre are quarrels about the rank of a licence: poetry was qualified to voice intent, the ci lyric was fit only to say love, and fiction had to wait for critics to manufacture a defensible reason for it**. You must deploy at least six of: **the quotative rather than productive beginning**, **the moral weight of 志**, **the two columns of genesis and norm**, **inspection as an information channel**, **resentment as surety**, **the three rewrites of the programme**. Then adjudicate: **are permission and aesthetic judgement related as external constraint to free creation, or is aesthetic judgement itself a product of permission — and argue from one example in this chapter**.

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**no fifty-fifty split allowed**. A passing answer usually takes the second option and supplies a mechanism: **candidate — once "the tolerable intensity" is internalised as taste, the author no longer needs an external censor**. This explains why "gently and generously" could long be experienced as something other than oppression, and how "reticence" migrated from strategy to aesthetic category; the dictum that reticence is pressure, familiar from the writing courses, receives here its theoretical version: **formal categories are often sedimented historical constraints**). A passing answer must also produce counter-evidence: **candidate — some of the most admired works are famous precisely for excess**. This shows that the line of permission is moved retrospectively by ratification: **a line of permission that never moved could never generate praise words like "breakthrough"** — the best insight available in this question). A conclusion able to write "**so learning classical criticism is not learning an opinion about beauty but a technology of permission: it presses what may be said into form, and form, after several generations, reports that pressure as style. We do the same thing over and over today, except that our line of permission is called platform rules and ranking algorithms**" — where the chapters on online literature and on new media shake hands with this course — earns an A.
:::
