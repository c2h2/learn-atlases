title: "Cao Pi and Lu Ji"
---

The last two chapters asked what literature was for and where language stops; the two texts of this chapter speak about the craft itself for the first time — **in the third century criticism changed the question: no longer only what a poem should do, but how a poem is made, and why I cannot make one**.

## Cao Pi's "On Literature" {#def-dianlun}

::: definition Four famous sentences, doing three kinds of work {#def-siju}
**The standard account**: this is a short essay appraising living writers; the whole book is lost and the essay survives because an anthology took it in. Four sentences are quoted constantly, and each is doing a different job. **① "Writers belittle one another; it has been so since antiquity"** — a sociological opening: **it moves the object of criticism from the work to the critic, and inoculates the reader against prejudice before a single judgement is made**. **② "Memorials should be dignified; letters and arguments, lucid; inscriptions and elegies, truthful; poetry and rhapsody, lovely"** — four genres, each with its proper manner: the first sketch of a taxonomy of genres. Note the grammar: **it does not describe, it prescribes; it is not aesthetics but a job description**. **③ "Writing is governed by breath (气); breath has its kinds, clear or turbid, and cannot be reached by effort"** — talent is stated to be innate and untransferable, illustrated by music: the same score, the same tempo, and yet one man's breathing differs from another's, so that fathers and elder brothers cannot hand it to sons and younger brothers (**standard account**: this is the closest Chinese criticism comes to a theory of genius). **④ "Writing is the great business of statecraft, the splendid affair by which one does not perish"** — the two dignities of office and immortality. **Read ③ and ④ together, because together they make a structure: talent cannot be transferred, yet the work is a transferable immortality; writing is therefore an enterprise that converts the untransferable into the transferable.** Here is this chapter's first sentence: **this is the first instruction manual in Chinese criticism that describes literature as an asset** — and it was written by a man about to become emperor, which is not a coincidence; see the next section.
:::

::: definition Who is judging whom {#def-shui}
**The standard account**: in the Jian'an years a group of writers inside an aristocratic circle composed on common topics, presented poems to one another, and graded each other's work; this essay was written inside that circle. Three institutional readings. **① When texts circulate inside a circle, describing the craft becomes more useful than pronouncing on conduct**: you have to say "this line is better than yours, and here is where" — and "his character is sound" is useless in a same-topic contest. **That is the social condition of the technical turn, and this lesson's formula for it: the granularity of criticism is set by the frequency of exchange** — the principle that the chapter on publishing would state four hundred years later as "format changes content". **② Appraisal is a position.** To grade living writers is to exercise criticism as a form of power; a future emperor ranking the poets of his own day is not, in genre, a literary act (**standard account**: this is not a conspiracy; it is closer to a norm of the circle, where judgement is the dearest social currency). **③ The text we read is a product of selection.** The book is lost; one essay survives because an anthology chose it. So the first famous essay in the history of Chinese criticism is in fact the residue of another act of choice. **Whatever weight we give to the claim "the taxonomy of genres begins with Cao Pi" depends on what the editors of the *Wenxuan* thought worth keeping** — a point that becomes sharper in chapter four, since the man who compiled that anthology lived within a generation of the man who wrote the *Wenxin diaolong*.
:::

## Lu Ji's "On Literature" {#def-wenf}

::: definition The triangle of thing, idea, and words {#def-sanjiao}
**The standard account**: a rhapsody on the making of literature whose preface contains a much-quoted confession: the author always fears that the idea will not match the thing, and the words will not catch up with the idea; and adds that the difficulty is not in knowing but in being able. This lesson reads it as a three-layer model. **① The thing (物)**: external objects and encounters — the seasons move one to sigh, one looks on the ten thousand things and one's thoughts are tangled. **② The idea (意)**: what has taken shape in the mind. **③ The words (文)**: what is written down. **Loss occurs on each leg, and the two losses differ in kind: "the idea fails to match the thing" is a problem of knowledge; "the words fail to catch the idea" is a problem of technique** (**standard account**). The value of the triangle is that it hands criticism a diagnostic procedure, which we use as a fault-location table in the worked example below. One comparison: the modern triangle of thought, reference and symbol has a similar shape and a different purpose — **Lu Ji's triangle is built for the writer, the semioticians' for the interpreter** (**standard account**: the two are habitually paired in the literature; the requirement of this course is that when you make the comparison you state the difference in direction, otherwise you have written a contentless resemblance). The semantics course and this chapter shake hands here, and part company here.
:::

::: definition "Poetry arises from feeling and is elaborate" {#def-yuanqing}
**The standard account**: in a passage sorting the genres the same work says that poetry arises out of feeling (缘情) and is elaborate and lovely (绮靡). For two thousand years this line has been read as a declaration opposed to "poetry voices intent". Treat it in three layers. **① The textual layer**: in its own setting it is one of some dozen short parallel clauses describing the look of a genre; the register is not legislative (**standard account**). **② The categorical layer**: what matters is that 缘情 and 绮靡 are yoked, binding feeling and beauty of manner to the same genre; **so that "being beautifully written" becomes for the first time an obligation of the lyric rather than a luxury**. **③ The reception layer**: to read it as a manifesto is a later reading, produced by later quarrels in which such a manifesto was needed. Here is the most important lesson of the chapter: **what a sentence does in the history of thought is often larger than what it thought, and studying the former explains much better why some sentences are remembered** (chapter one's "the life of a formula depends on the blanks in its wording" has an instance: intent and feeling could stand opposed for two thousand years precisely because neither was defined). This lesson sets the two side by side without a verdict: **they answer different questions — one asks who is granted the licence to express, the other what a genre owes**. To oppose them is to force a political frame onto a list of genres — **and that forcing was standard practice in twentieth-century Chinese scholarship** (**standard account**). Note that the observation is itself the last chapter's lesson: you think you are reading a third-century author; you are reusing a textbook's two columns.
:::

::: definition A vocabulary of making {#def-cizuo}
**The standard account**: this work leaves behind a set of expressions that became common sense: one line must be set at the hinge of a piece, so that a short sentence carries the whole (what later critics called the "bracing line", 警策); jade hidden in a stone makes the mountain shine, a pearl in water makes the river lovely; one queries being out of nothing and knocks sound out of silence (**课虚无以责有，叩寂寞而求音**). Read all of this as an invention of vocabulary. **① They are all metaphors of space and hearing** — "set", "lie at", "store up", "shine", "knock" — so making is imagined as placing and striking. This is decorationless conceptual apparatus: when later Chinese criticism says a poem has fine lines but no whole, or that one line lights up a piece, it is spending the currency minted here. **② They concede that making contains chance**: to knock at silence for a sound is to admit that no sound may come — the most honest thing a technical text can say. **③ They are teachable**, in contrast to breath, which cannot be forced: every step here is an operation. **Thus the two texts of the chapter form a pair: Cao Pi speaks of untransferable endowment, Lu Ji of transferable operations** (**standard account**: all later argument between "heaven-made" and "crafted", including Song verses about marvellous hands hitting on what was already in nature, swings between this pair). This lesson's judgement: **any teaching of writing has to speak about both at once — which operations can be taught, and which cannot. Teach only the first and you produce workmanship; teach only the second and you produce excuses.** That requirement is this course's standing demand on every writing class, and you will see it discharged three more times in the final courses.
:::

::: example Locating a fault with the triangle {#ex-guzhang}
Three failures of your own writing. **A**: you have a pile of material and what comes out reads like an inventory. **B**: you know perfectly well what you want to write, sit down, and cannot get past the first sentence. **C**: you finish, read it once, and feel that the idea has come out smaller than it was. **For each, say which layer has failed — thing, idea, or words — and give one remedy that can be carried out today.**

::: solution
**A fails at the idea**. Nothing is missing from the material; what is missing is the judgement that would organise it. The symptom is inventory prose: every paragraph on the same level, no sentence carrying a claim. **Remedy**: write in one sentence what you want to assert, then delete every item of material that cannot testify for that sentence — the sieve, as the writing courses put it. **B fails at the thing**. It is not a failure of writing but of having something to write: the head contains category words ("autumn", "home") rather than one observed occasion. **Remedy**: stop writing; go and make one observed visit with notes, and lay down that you must return with three details nobody else could have written. This deliberately cuts against the writing course's advice that observation means collecting: **collect in order to know; when you write, tear the sheet back open**. **C fails at the words.** Experience and idea are both present; the language has too small a capacity — and it is usually a syntactic rather than a lexical problem, since in Chinese a shrunk idea is almost always a complex relation flattened into a run of coordinated short clauses. **Remedy**: find the sentence that has shrunk, give it a structure (concession, condition, gradation), and read again (**standard account**: this is the sense of "words fail to catch the idea"). **Marking**: all three remedies must be actions performable today. **Write "read more, write more" or "accumulate material" and you fail this chapter** — not as a kindness but because it is the chapter's only grading rule: **the value of a piece of criticism is tested by whether it can be executed**.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why write a treatise on poetry as a rhapsody {#int-fu}
**A formal question**: if the subject is the difficulty of making, why choose a rhetorical, parallel, tonally fussy verse form rather than an argument? Three answers. **① The logical status of the thesis**: to demonstrate by argument that a thing cannot be fully transmitted is to contradict yourself; to describe it is not. **② The taste of the living reader**: the rhapsody was the form that could command attention; a prose treatise had no market (**standard account**). **③ Demonstration is the argument**: a beautifully written essay on the difficulty of writing beautifully both instants the difficulty and refutes it — it is evidence and counter-evidence in one object. **The generalisation: whenever a genre looks unsuitable for its subject, ask first whether it is carrying a load for the argument**. The question works on papers, official documents and advertisements alike: **form is almost never the outcome of a choice; it is the place where the necessary part of an argument has gone to live**. That isomorphic with the rhetoric course's opening chapter and with the structural course; **this chapter hands it over to the third century, and to you**.
:::

::: history Two births of two essays {#sec-chusheng}
**The 220s**: a short essay appraising contemporaries is written; **standard account**: for long it was not specially honoured, and survives because an anthology admitted it. **Late third to early fourth century**: a rhapsody on the craft of writing circulates, and is read both as a model of prose and as material for theory (**standard account**). **Sixth century**: a systematic work of criticism, tracing the changes of genre in its chapters on form, fits the two essays into a genealogy — the next chapter. **Eleventh to nineteenth centuries**: "arises from feeling" and "voices intent" are set against each other repeatedly and used as old evidence in new factional quarrels (**standard account**). This lesson's insistence: **most of that opposition never took place in the age it claims to explain**. **Eighteenth century**: a theory of "rhyme-resonance" traces its own origin back to these two (**standard account**; chapter nine). **After the 1930s**: the first systematic histories of Chinese literary criticism are published, and the "age of literary self-consciousness" is proposed and inherited (**standard account**). How this lesson handles the last item — this is the most teachable thing in the chapter: **"the discovery of literature" is a twentieth-century thesis (its author is a particular person, and this course names no one because you should go and read him), which organised a body of material brilliantly and systematically concealed another. It made third-century writers look like the forerunners of modern literary men**. You will meet the construction again: **every nomination of a "discovery" or "awakening" is a retrospective adoption**, and adoptions work because they meet a need felt at the moment of adopting — a sentence that applies to the history of any discipline.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Reading "poetry arises from feeling" as a manifesto** (**in its setting it is one item in a list of genres**, the three layers above); **treating the four genres as an aesthetic classification** (**it is a job description**, sentence ② above); **forgetting that the text is a survivor** (**famous essays live inside anthologies**, section on who judges whom, item 3); **giving advice that cannot be executed** (**"read more, write more" fails this chapter** — the worked example, and the same rule as the argument-writing course's executability test).
:::

::: exercise Essay: endowment and operation {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **to call talent a native breath is an eminently useful theory: it explains why teaching has a limit, and it hands the unsuccessful a creditable story; at the same time it explains to the successful why their success was not the fruit of effort — two comforts from one sentence. To call making a set of operations is useful in a different way: it makes writing teachable, priceable, and sellable to outsiders, and it turns writers into a trade. Chinese criticism bought both sets of words in the third century and has never let either go.** Deploy at least six of: **what each of the four sentences does**, **appraisal as a position**, **the thing–idea–words triangle**, **the yoking of feeling and beauty**, **the spatial metaphors of the craft vocabulary**, **the anthology pedigree of the "famous essay"**. Then adjudicate: **where should a school of writing put its weight, and what do you do with the other set of words?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**you must dispose of the other set of words, not declare victory**). **A passing skeleton: teach the operations, speak about the endowment**. Operations are what can be graded, so teaching must be built on them; endowment must be spoken about with an explicit statement of its function — **it explains differences, not merits**. Concretely: **① in written feedback use only executable items** ("no concession here", never "lacking gift"); **② allow the vocabulary of talent when discussing why some writers achieve more, but require it to describe rather than to adjudicate**; **③ publish the ceiling of your teaching** — say what the course guarantees and what it does not. **This third item is the top-mark point, consistent with this course's standing refusal to mislead**. **What happens if you drop the other set**: without endowment, writing instruction slides into the moral charge that bad writing proves insufficient labour; without operation, no one can be charged tuition. **This sentence is the sociological definition of every writing workshop** — it must appear for full marks. A conclusion able to write "**so those two sentences of eighteen centuries ago describe our present predicament exactly: a person must make a living from operations and live with the differences between people; the whole honesty of criticism lies in saying which of the two you are talking about at the moment, and in not lying between them**" earns an A.
:::
