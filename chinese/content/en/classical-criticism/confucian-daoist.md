---
title: "Confucian and Daoist views of literature"
---

The previous chapter left one word behind: permission. This chapter names the two offices that issued licences — **Confucianism assigned literature a job, Daoism drew a boundary around language; for two thousand years Chinese criticism asked only two questions: what is literature for, and why must it concede that it cannot say everything**.

## Confucianism {#def-ru}

::: definition Four job descriptions {#def-zhiwu}
**The standard account**: the Analects contain several passages on the *Odes*, and they form the ground plan of every later theory of literature's duties. **① A general verdict**: one formula sums up three hundred poems in the assertion that thinking is free of evil — note that this converts an aesthetic question into a question about motives, and it is the ancestor of a great many later quarrels. **② A list of functions**: rouse, observe, group together, complain; near at hand it serves the father, at a distance the ruler, and one learns many names of birds, beasts, plants and trees. Translated into modern duties: rousing is mobilisation and empathy; observing is knowledge; grouping is association; complaining is protest; the names are the transmission of information. **This is a remarkable list, because it concedes that protest and association are legitimate functions of literature while registering them in the catalogue of the permitted. See the move clearly: to licence is to register, and a protest that has been registered henceforth has an official address.** ③ **A faculty of speech**: "not having learned the Odes, one cannot speak" — in the setting of missions and replies, a man who cannot quote is a man who cannot talk; the quotation system of chapter one has here become an educational system. **④ The formation of character**: where substance outweighs manner one gets vulgarity, where manner outweighs substance one gets pedantry; manner and substance in balance make the full man. **This lesson takes the fourth item as the most important, because it is not a judgement about a work but a proportion within a person. Chinese criticism therefore treats "the literary" from the start as a component of a personality rather than a property of a product** — which explains a persistent later phenomenon: to say a man writes badly is heard as a comment on the man. Chapter 16 of this atlas had its quarrel about intention; here it has a moral version.
:::

::: definition Two clauses that pull against each other {#def-liangtiao}
**The standard account**: two much-quoted sentences coexist in the same tradition. One says that words which suffice to carry the meaning are enough; the other that words without ornament travel no distance. Critics commonly call this a tension. **This lesson refuses that reading: their scopes differ. The first governs personal honesty — do not exaggerate in order to move people. The second governs public circulation — do not be crude because you are honest**. Together they are a complete technical regulation: no inflation of content, no shabbiness of form. And the real sting of the regulation is that **it supplies no measure**: how much sufficing counts as sufficing? how much ornament counts as outweighing? Most of the space in the history of criticism is spent filling in these two blanks. Here is this chapter's methodological sentence: **the staying power of a theory often comes from the blanks in its wording rather than from the answers it gives** — chapter one's "a programme rewritten four times without dying" said exactly this. If you ever design an assessment standard, remember it: **blanks will be filled by the inheritors' taste, and taste will call itself principle**.
:::

## Daoism {#def-dao}

::: definition Three limit propositions {#def-xianzhi}
**The standard account**: transmitted Laozi and Zhuangzi texts contain three kinds of claim about language — the way that can be spoken is not the constant way; trustworthy words are not elegant, elegant words are not trustworthy; what a book carries is the lees, and the men of old perished together with them. Note their genre: **these are not proposals about literature but a cap placed on the efficacy of language**. And the capping had historical consequences. This lesson's argument: **only once it is conceded that language cannot reach the core does "whatever cannot be reached" stop being a failure and become something worth pursuing**. Hence the sentence this chapter most wants you to keep: **Daoism is not the negation of Chinese literature but the licence for the ideal of "meaning beyond the words"; without it, reticence is merely inarticulacy — with it, inarticulacy becomes a grade**. The rhetoric course's dictum that reticence is pressure finds here its metaphysical ground, and chapter seven's use of Chan to talk about poetry is nothing but the renewal of the same licence. Note also the symmetry that is usually missed: **Confucianism turns literature into a post to be filled; Daoism turns the limit of literature into a resource to be priced. Both are supremely practical; one regulates production, the other sets values**.
:::

::: definition The debate on words and meaning {#def-yanyi}
**The standard account**: the Commentaries on the Changes contain the assertion that writing does not exhaust speech and speech does not exhaust meaning; in the third century a commentator on the Changes argued that to grasp the meaning one forgets the images, and this became a programme of xuanxue method. This lesson narrates it as the foundation of a methodology. **Step one is to concede defeat**: language cannot reach the core. **Step two is to turn the concession into a procedure**: borrowing words to reach meaning, then throwing the words away — "forgetting words" is not forgetting the sentences but refusing to treat them as the terminus. **Step three is institutional**: if sentences are not the terminus, then neither commentary lineages nor school orthodoxies can hold final authority, **and a door opens for the private judgement of the critic**. Call this the licence Daoism issued to critics: those later verdicts delivered in a breath of phrase ("such a poet is clear and easy, wanting to let himself go") are methodologically indebted to this step (**standard account**). And this is also why Chinese criticism writes like poetry and not like definition: **a critic who does not concede that his medium is poetic has not read step two**. The semantics course observed that frames outweigh feature-lists; the Chinese critic's frames are "realm" and "flavour", not checklists.
:::

::: definition Two houses in one person {#def-bujia}
**A standard phenomenon**: the same author writes for the court and for hills and water, and describes himself in one passage as leaning on the Northern Dipper and in another as finding a true meaning he no longer wishes to define. The old description is "Confucian and Daoist complement each other". This lesson demands a rewrite: **they are not complementary, they are separate ledgers**. The Confucian ledger holds public texts — memorials, stele inscriptions, edifying writing — and its criterion is diligence in office. The Daoist ledger holds private writing and self-description, and its criterion is unwillingness to strain. Between the ledgers a permanent transfer payment runs: **public texts borrow the guarantee of private honesty, private writing borrows the elevation of public concern**. Critics often use the formula "in adversity, care for one's own body; in success, care for the realm" to describe the switch (**standard account**). This lesson's verdict: **the history of Chinese criticism is not a struggle between two schools but two sets of self-reports made by one man in two situations. To classify any writer as a Confucian or a Daoist theorist of literature is therefore an error committed for the convenience of teaching — worth committing, provided you confess the convenience in a footnote**. And chapter one's question, "who has the right to stipulate what this sentence stands for in our tradition", can now be restated: who has the right to stipulate which school a dead man belonged to? **The answer is whoever edits the textbook**.
:::

::: example Two readings, one poem {#ex-liangzhong}
Read the line "picking chrysanthemums beneath the eastern hedge, calmly I catch sight of the southern mountain". **Reader A** says: here is a man's intent; though in the fields he has not abandoned his grief for the world, therefore his words are pale and his meaning far. **Reader B** says: this state cannot be spoken of; to speak of it is already to fall to a second order, and the marvel lies in forgetting words. **Answer three questions**: which category from the last chapter or this one is each reader using; which reading is more correct; and is there a third question better than both.

::: solution
**The categories**: A uses the Confucian category of motive (intent, grief for the world) and the Confucian grammar of evaluation (pale in form, weighty in conscience). B uses the Daoist limit proposition (unspeakability) and the order of quality derived from it (forgetting words, the second order). **Which is more correct**: neither, as a judgement about what the author meant. But as evidence about the text's own self-description there is a plain fact: at the end of the same poem the speaker says there is a true meaning here, and that wanting to sort it out he has forgotten the words (**standard account: these are lines of the poem itself**). **That is to say, the poem supplies the permission for reading B and withholds it from reading A**, so B has a slight advantage in being compatible with the text. Keep the word "slight": **the poem's self-report is also rhetoric and cannot serve as notarised intention**. **The better question**: this lesson offers three. First, in which periods did each reading become possible — A depends on a commentary tradition that ties poem to character, B on a context in which "words do not exhaust meaning" was already accepted; **interpretation history turns into category history**. Second, which lines each reading makes conspicuous and which it makes redundant: A must hunt for traces of grief and so skips several lines; B must declare the unsayable and so turns the whole poem into a gesture — this is the cost sheet of an interpretation. Third, which is worth teaching, a normative question that requires you to state your purpose first. **Marking**: whoever writes the sentence "interpretation history turns into category history" takes an A. It is this course's key to the whole of literary history: **we imagine we are reading the ancients when most of the time we are reading the readings the ancients prepared for us — and readings have birthdates, patrons, and fee schedules**. The next chapter, on the third-century turn, will use the key immediately.
:::
:::

::: intuition Why Chinese critics use phrases rather than definitions {#int-duanyu}
**A formal observation**: the mainstream genres of indigenous criticism are the phrase, the couplet and the simile — one poet is like wind over snow, another like lotus buds first opening. Three explanations, not mutually exclusive. **① Methodological honesty**: if "words do not exhaust meaning" is conceded, a definition is a bluff. **② A social function**: a handsome phrase is remembered, quoted, and fastened upon by later people; a definition is not; criticism thus becomes a prestige object in circulation. The observation that clichés rise in circulation as their meaning falls to zero applies exactly — the sense of these phrases was worn thin in the wearing. **③ A training effect**: memorise forty grades of evaluative phrase and a man can pronounce on poetry at once, though he has never offered a single checkable criterion. This lesson's stance is not to condemn the genre but to add one operation to it: **translate each phrase into a claim that could be falsified, and only then decide whether to keep the phrase**. "Clear" might be rendered as: few allusions, few heavy-coloured words, short sentences, sparse rhyme. After translating you will find the phrase was a useful cluster, or you will find it does not fit this particular poem at all. **That operation is this course's homework assignment for the whole of classical criticism, and the preparation for chapter six on the poetry-talks**. It turns aesthetic verdicts into auditable verdicts without destroying them — the only methodological promise this chapter is prepared to make.
:::

::: history How two schools became one skeleton {#sec-gujia}
**Sixth to third centuries BC**: Confucius' remarks on the *Odes* are written down by disciples; Mencius proposes reading by meeting the mind through the words and knowing the man by discussing his age (**standard account**); Xunzi writes on music and on rhapsody; the ritual essays on music and on the lessons of the classics supply the state's account of why poetry matters. **Third century**: language-scepticism is fully formed in Laozi and Zhuangzi, though its object is not literature. **Second to first centuries BC**: the Confucian canon is institutionalised; the teaching of poetry and the legend of song-collection enter official narrative, while the commentary tradition on the songs of Chu begins to borrow Confucian categories of motive in order to defend writing that is obviously not "free of evil" (**standard account**). **This lesson calls that the first great transplant: Confucian categories used to protect non-Confucian works, and thereafter it becomes the standard defence format**. **Third century**: the words-and-meanings debate becomes xuanxue method, and it is in this air that works describing the craft of composition are written — the next chapter. **Eleventh to twelfth centuries**: the Learning of the Way debates writing and the way, while categories grown in a Daoist and Chan air — "rhyme-resonance", "flavour" — enter the discourse on poetry (**standard account**). **Twentieth century**: Chinese literature is sorted into "realist" and "romantic" camps, and the Confucian–Daoist distinction is translated into a foreign vocabulary; the sorting still lives in several textbooks (**standard account**). The closing criticism of this chapter: **the frame "Confucian and Daoist complement each other" is itself a twentieth-century invention, and it rewrote a practice of separate ledgers as an opposition of two -isms**. Hence the chapter's whole lesson in one sentence: **you think you are describing two sides of your object; in fact you are reusing two columns of somebody's textbook** — the semantics course's "frames outweigh feature-lists" turns out to apply to the history of criticism as well.
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Treating "thinking free of evil" as an aesthetic standard** (**it is primarily a judgement of motive**, job description 1 above); **reading "words that suffice" and "words without ornament" as a contradiction** (**each has its own scope**, the two clauses above); **calling Daoism anti-literary** (**it is the licence for the ideal of meaning beyond words**, the limit propositions above); **pinning a Confucian or Daoist label on a writer** (**two ledgers in one man; the label is a textbook's column**, the two houses above and history section 6 — **this is the examinable point of the chapter**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: the birthmarks of categories {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a history of criticism which records only what was said is a scrapbook of opinions about literature; it becomes a discipline when it records by what categories speakers spoke, who issued those categories, and in which settings they had to be used. Almost every category of Chinese criticism carries a birthmark: intent comes from the mission, rhyme-resonance from tonal regulation, flavour from the vocabulary of tasting, Chan from borrowed religious authority. Categories are not neutral instruments; they are inheritances of power, parcel by parcel.** Deploy at least six of: **the four job descriptions**, **the blanks in the two clauses**, **the limit as licence**, **the three steps of the words-and-meanings debate**, **separate ledgers**, **the three functions of the critical phrase**. Then adjudicate: **what differs in the cost of inheriting an old category and borrowing a foreign one (intention, image, focalisation, the typological)?**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**a passing answer gives the symmetrical defect of both borrowings**). **Old categories**: the cost is a moral and social baggage that drags aesthetic judgement into judgement of character; the advantage is a thousand years of use, which makes them mutually checkable. **Foreign categories**: the cost is that they arrive without the set of questions they were meant to answer — "typology" arrives with Engels' quarrel behind it, "focalisation" with a grammar behind it — and **a term imported without the problem it was invented to solve yields a criticism that can classify but not argue**. That sentence is the point of the question; **its advantage is that it carries no examination baggage and can speak to living writing**. This lesson's prescription: **every borrowing incurs one obligation — state what the borrowed category was originally used to refute**. The instruction is exactly isomorphic with chapter one's "every quotation must carry its original setting", so the true subject of this chapter has all along been: **to quote is to incur debt**. A conclusion able to write "**so we do not propose abandoning old categories or adopting new ones; we propose attaching a birth certificate to each: from which setting it came, for which sort of people it once spoke, and for whom it still speaks today. This makes criticism no more beautiful, but it makes criticism answerable — and answerability is the only courtesy this discipline has**" earns an A.
:::

::: summary
The two dossiers: Confucianism (**four job descriptions, a regulation with two blanks, licence as registration**), Daoism (**three caps on language, defeat turned into a procedure, a licence issued to critics**), the person (**separate ledgers, not complementarity**), method (**phrases must be translated, and the translation must be falsifiable**), and one sentence: **you think you are reading the ancients; most of the time you are reading the readings the ancients prepared for you**. Next chapter: Cao Pi and Lu Ji — criticism begins to describe the craft, and what "writing is governed by breath" actually promises.
