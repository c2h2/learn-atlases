---
title: "Lu You and the Southern Song poets"
---

The previous chapter said that Song poetry re-signed a long-term contract; this chapter writes the man who signed that contract to the full — **the more than nine thousand poems that survive from Lu You 陆游 are the largest continuous archive the Southern Song has left us: he did not write nearly ten thousand poems; he used poetry to keep the accounts of a whole life: military expenditure, the price of rice, rain, dreams, and a Central Plain that was never paid out**.

## Definition: output as a poetics {#def-chanliang}

::: definition The party who performs diary-form literature {#def-riji}
**The *Jiannan shigao* 《剑南诗稿》 ('Draft Poems from Jiannan'), eighty-five juan (the number of juan in the edition in circulation; more than nine thousand poems survive; the order of magnitude in the usual account)** (**Why it did not crash:** because it was never, in the first place, a "collected poems"; it was a running account: several pieces a day, nothing left unwritten, and nothing deleted once it had been written (the extreme performance of the contract in Chapter 6, "poetry has changed its tense": **Lu You turned the lyric poem into a verse edition of prose that keeps accounts**)); **What he says of himself** (lines of the sort in which he counts his own abundance, such as "**六十年间万首诗**" — Across sixty years, ten thousand poems. (Our translation.) (the usual account) — **the counting itself becomes the theme: beside Song poetry's "talent and learning", one further item is added, "hours worked"** (**quantity is a kind of argument: it proves that "there has not been a day on which I gave up", and this is precisely the only form of patriotism that can be checked**)); **The poet of "being stationed"** (**all his life he rotated among posts as a local official, staff appointments, and shrine stipends (the career as usually told: eight years at the Sichuan–Shaanxi military headquarters were the high point),** he never commanded a single campaign, and he never stopped writing of the northern expedition (the contrast with Xin Qiji 辛弃疾 in Lesson 4: Xin had achievements in war but no authority over troops; Lu You had a brush but no achievements in war: the Southern Song "restoration" was, in the main, left to be written by two men who had no authority over troops**.
:::

::: definition A toponymy of the Central Plain {#def-zhongyuan}
**'Writing My Indignation' (*Shufen* 《书愤》)** — "**楼船夜雪瓜洲渡，铁马秋风大散关**" — Towered ships, night snow, the Guazhou crossing; iron horses, autumn wind, the Dasan Pass. (Our translation.) — **one couplet, two pictures, no person, no verb:** a memory of himself drawn up in ranks; the one who reviews them is the self who writes the poem (parallelism (a parallel couplet) presses two battlefields into the two lines of one claim for expenses: this is what Song poetry calls "allusion", and here there is no allusion, only a service record**); **A list of place names** (**Guazhou 瓜洲, the Dasan Pass 大散关, Chang'an 长安, the Central Plain 中原, Shangcai 上蔡, mountains and rivers 山河 —** the place names in Lu You's poems are not geography; they are frequency: he writes, over and over, the same "Central Plain" not yet recovered, until the word changes from a goal into a habit (**"repetition is a poetic device": patriotism is not a theme; it is a frequency: writing it several dozen times a year proves that a person is in earnest more than one masterpiece does (the interest statement on Chapter 6's "long-term contract"**)); **Dreams** (**"iron horses" and "towered ships" enter his dreams again and again (the usual account: the collection contains a great many poems that record dreams; a famous line is "**铁马冰河入梦来**", in 'Wind and Rain Raging on the Fourth Day of the Eleventh Month' (*Shiyiyue siri fengyu dazuo* 《十一月四日风雨大作》) — Iron horses and a frozen river come into the dream. (Our translation.) — **when the channel of reality is closed, the dream becomes the only permitted exit: the most honest technique in Song poetry is to write "cannot" as "dreamed" (the reverse of Chapter 5's clause on *jìtuō* 寄托 ('entrusting intent elsewhere'): it is not that he does not dare to speak; it is that, apart from speaking, there is nothing to be done**).
:::

::: definition 'To My Son': the will as a genre {#def-shier}
"**死去元知万事空，但悲不见九州同。王师北定中原日，家祭无忘告乃翁。**" — I have always known that, once I am dead, the ten thousand affairs are empty; I grieve only that I shall not see the Nine Provinces made one. On the day the king's armies settle the Central Plain in the north, at the household sacrifice do not forget to tell your old father. (Our translation.) — **four lines complete a document: informed consent (the ten thousand affairs are empty), an exception clause (I grieve only), an item to be done (the day the north is settled), an executor (the old father's son)** (**a will occupies the strongest position in rhetoric: it does not permit the reader to answer back (the heroic version of Lesson 6's "a way of dying": he even wrote out his own way of dying, and he pushed the date of acceptance onto the enemy**); **the final form of patriotic poetry is household business** (**a descent from the language of court and temple (the Nine Provinces made one) to the language of the stove (the household sacrifice) —** this demotion is its strength: he does not want a monument; he wants one follow-up message (the limiting case of Chapter 9's "a recommendation is a prophecy": this is a prophecy that cannot be honoured; the author is already dead, and the honouring is the son's responsibility: **faith held in trust**).
:::

## The Lu You who is quoted {#def-beiyinyong}

::: definition The couplet the emperor chose {#def-xinghua}
**'Spring Rain Just Clearing in Lin'an' (*Lin'an chunyu chu ji* 《临安春雨初霁》)** — "**小楼一夜听春雨，深巷明朝卖杏花**" — In a small upper room, all night I listen to the spring rain; in a deep lane, tomorrow morning, they sell apricot blossom. (Our translation.) — **according to records left by Song writers, this couplet was once praised from above (the thread of the usual account: Emperor Xiaozong 孝宗 recited it and passed it on) —** yet the second half of the whole poem is self-admonition and complaint of the kind in "**素衣莫起风尘叹**" — In plain clothes, do not start a sigh over wind and dust. (Our translation.): **the mechanism of careful selection always chooses the mildest couplet (the reverse of Chapter 8's "irascible slice": a domesticating slice — quoting a protester so that he becomes a man at his ease (to quote is to rewrite; an old case of the three virtues of quotation in Chapter 3**); **'An Outing to the Village West of the Mountain' (*You Shanxi cun* 《游山西村》)** — "**山重水复疑无路，柳暗花明又一村**" — Mountains repeat and waters turn back, and I doubt there is a path; willows darken, flowers brighten, and there is another village. (Our translation.) — **a positive specimen of the *lǐqù* 理趣 ('the interest of principle') of Song poetry (the acceptance procedure of Lesson 6: this "principle" grows out of the bodily experience of a mountain path, and can be inferred from the images (*yìxiàng*) themselves) —** but note the job it holds today: it has been raised to a general-purpose phrase of encouragement, detached from the farming household of the original poem ("**箫鼓追随春社近，衣冠简朴古风存**" — Flutes and drums follow one another, and the spring *shè* sacrifice is near; clothes and caps are plain, and the old ways remain. (Our translation.): the original poem is writing a village; it is not teaching you how to behave as a person). The price of turning into an idiom: the sentence's rate of circulation rises, and the meaning of the work returns to zero (the site, in an old poem, of Lesson 7's "three seconds for an idiom"**).
:::

## The Four Masters and their ranking {#def-sijiada}

::: definition The long life of the list and the short life of the texts {#def-mingdan}
**The various accounts of the "Four Masters of the Restoration" (You, Yang, Fan and Lu; the order differs from one account to another; the usual account)** (**the list is stable, and the order is often quarrelled over —** the dispute over ranking is itself the self-awareness of Song poetry (Chapter 6); **The lesson of You Mao 尤袤** (**the *Liangxi ji* 《梁溪集》 ('The Liangxi Collection') was put together by later hands gathering what survived, and the collection of poems was lost early (the usual account):** the first of the Four Masters to fall out of the ranks is the one worst preserved — half the luck of literary history is the luck of the archive (the reverse of Chapter 5's "a list is a state that refuses to come apart": if the list is lost, the state becomes a legend**)); **Fan Chengda 范成大** (**what he saw as an envoy to the Jin (the *Lanpei lu* 《揽辔录》 ('A Record of Taking the Reins')) and the sixty poems of 'Miscellaneous Stirrings on Fields and Gardens through the Four Seasons' (*Sishi tianyuan zaxing* 《四时田园杂兴》) — "**童孙未解供耕织，也傍桑阴学种瓜**"**: The little grandson does not yet know how to take his part in ploughing and weaving; even so, beside the shade of the mulberries, he learns to plant melons. (Our translation.): "fields and gardens" are changed from a symbol of withdrawal into a place of work: the child is not enjoying nature; he is in training before he takes up the job (the best result of Chapter 6's expansion of subject matter: **farm work is written as a set of reportage, and a set that comes with a tax bill (his poems write, as well, of official rent, of weaving and of wheat; poems of fields and gardens keep accounts for the first time**)); **Yang Wanli 杨万里** (**"*huófǎ*" 活法 ('the living method') and "opening the door and looking at the flowers" are already on file in Chapter 6 — this chapter adds only one fact of a career:** among the Four Masters his reputation as an official was the cleanest, his span of life the longest, and his name the most like a pen-name ("Chengzhai" 诚斋 is the studio name he was commanded to take: **the people of the Song treated their own study as a second identity: the separation of the institution from the person is something the people of the Song had long practised**.
:::

::: definition The calling-card economics of the rivers-and-lakes petitioners {#def-jianghu}
**The Four Lings (Xu Zhao 徐照, Xu Ji 徐玑, Weng Juan 翁卷, Zhao Shixiu 赵师秀) and the Rivers and Lakes school** (**the Four Lings of Yongjia 永嘉 took Jia Dao 贾岛 as their patriarch, carved their characters extremely small, and made a point of avoiding "large" language (the usual account); the men of the rivers and lakes sought patronage with their poems (Dai Fugu 戴复古 remained a commoner all his life and travelled the length of north and south; the usual account) —** in the late Southern Song, a poem is a brick for knocking at a door, a calling card, and a gift offered when paying a visit (**the petitioner's economy: one five-character regulated poem costs three days, and the return is one banquet and one bolt of silk: when poetry can no longer keep a person, the person writes short pieces (the lowered version of Chapter 5's economy of the patron's hall: from guest-clerk in a military headquarters to petitioner in the street, the price quoted by the man of letters is marked down all the way**)); **Liu Kezhuang 刘克庄** (**the *Houcun shihua* 《后村诗话》 ('Houcun's Remarks on Poetry') and a vast quantity of matching poems; the gathering-point of the Rivers and Lakes school, and its critic (the usual account) —** a man who took both livelihoods, "writing poems" and "judging poems", to the limit:** the division of labour in the literature of the late Southern Song is already complete: some produce, some grade, some compile catalogues (the process, in the same period, of Chapter 5's "age of cataloguing" (poetry and the song lyric (*cí* 词) enter the period of putting things in order at the same time: a civilisation's attention turns from breaking new ground to taking stock (the market evidence for Lesson 15's "the life cycle of genres"**).
:::

## The fall of a state: two deathbed genres {#def-wangguo}

::: definition Deathbed poems and battlefield reporting {#def-lingshi}
**Wen Tianxiang 文天祥, 'Crossing the Lingding Sea' (*Guo Lingding yang* 《过零丁洋》)** — "**人生自古谁无死，留取丹心照汗青**" — From ancient times, in a human life, who is there that does not die? Leave a loyal heart to shine on the sweated slips. (Our translation.) — **taken under guard past the old sites of battlefields, he wrote a "statement refusing surrender" (the usual account: composed on the way to the Lingding Sea after he had been detained):** "sweated slips" (汗青) are a ledger that outsources judgement to the future (the limiting case of Chapter 9's "rate of honouring": the readers are people a thousand years on, and he gave them no means of answering back, only one fact that can be checked: he was still alive, and he would not**); **'Song of the Upright *Qi*' (*Zhengqi ge* 《正气歌》)** (**written in prison; the preface and the poem use the cosmology of "**天地有正气**" — Heaven and earth have an upright *qi* (vital energy). (Our translation.) — as surety for one man's imprisonment (the usual account) —** the technical core of a deathbed genre: rewriting a military defeat as a moral victory (the work that caps Lesson 4's "giving an accident a new title": change the title as far as death, and it can be changed no further**); **Wang Yuanliang 汪元量, the poem sequence 'Songs of Huzhou' (*Huzhou ge* 《湖州歌》)** (**in his capacity as a musician accompanying the party, he records the removal of the three palaces to the north; the sequence records events (the usual account) —** the news dispatch of a fallen state: a poem sequence is battlefield reporting issued in instalments (the 1279 edition of Chapter 8's commentary on the times: sentences of the kind "**一夜北兵南渡江**" — In a single night the northern troops crossed the river to the south. (Our translation.) — are that day's bulletin, and also the cheapest archive: **when the historian is not on the scene, rhyme is notarisation**).
:::

::: example Two genres for the same Central Plain {#ex-liangti}
'Writing My Indignation' (楼船夜雪瓜洲渡 — Towered ships, night snow, the Guazhou crossing. (Our translation.)) and 'To My Son' (*Shi'er* 《示儿》; 死去元知万事空 — Once I am dead, I have always known, the ten thousand affairs are empty. (Our translation.)) both point to "the Nine Provinces made one". Compare them in the three columns **① tense, ② reader, ③ falsifiability**, and answer: **why the same sentence is recollection in 'Writing My Indignation' and a will in 'To My Son'**.
::: solution
**① Tense** — **the tense of 'Writing My Indignation' is the past (two photographs of battlefields, plus the sense of having been cheated in "**早岁那知世事艰**" — In my early years, how was I to know that the world's affairs were hard? (Our translation.)); the tense of 'To My Son' is the future ("the day the north is settled …":** a future which he knows he himself will not witness — the grammatical mark of a will is that it uses an adverbial of time which only other people can arrive at**); ② **Reader** — **the reader of 'Writing My Indignation' is himself and his colleagues (the indignation is a verdict passed on himself); the reader of 'To My Son' is his son (**a will has no addressee for rhetoric, only an addressee who is to carry it out: this is why it does not need images (the four lines are almost entirely function words and nominal sentences: Song poetry's "using argument as poetry" is granted an exemption in a household scene**)); ③ **Falsifiability** — **'Writing My Indignation' can be rebutted ("you never, in those years, fought it to a decision":** recollection can be checked**); 'To My Son' cannot be falsified (it pushes the verification into the time after death, and into the fate of the enemy state) —** and so it does not stand in the court of argument, but in the court of promise (the exception clause to Lesson 2's "a claim must have a way of losing": some sentences refuse to enter the contest, because their use is not to win, but to entrust**).
:::
:::

::: intuition The other half of Song poetry's readers were outside the border {#int-jingwai}
**The Jin and the Yuan were a parallel market for Song poetry** (**Yuan Haowen 元好问, under the Jin, passed judgement on the school of Su and compiled the *Zhongzhou ji* 《中州集》 ('Anthology of the Central Land'), preserving the poetry of a generation (the usual account) —** a civilisation's "same age" is larger than its borders: what envoys of the Southern Song saw in the Jin was a political enemy state, yet what the people of the Jin wrote poetry with was precisely the technique of the Song (**culture has no customs house, but it has an accent (the mirror image of Lesson 4's "his experience of the north is an accent, not a fault": the northern and the southern accounts of Song poetry were kept separately in the same period; after the Southern Song fell, the consolidated statements were drawn up by men of the Yuan (the continuation of Chapter 8's "the archive and the fire": after the fire, the editors come on duty**)); **Three uses of "Lu You" in later ages** (**anthologies want his apricot blossom, textbooks want his Central Plain, and commentators want his dreams (the thread of the usual account: the history of anthologies of Lu You's poetry diverges sharply) —** after a writer dies he becomes a public shareholder: each age, according to its own need, draws a different heading (the author's version of Chapter 3's "inflation of citation": **the same name is drawn on by different ages, and the interest rate is the way literary history moves**.
:::

::: history A list for one century {#sec-qingdan}
**1125–1210** Lu You (**eight years in Sichuan and Shaanxi (the 1170s) → local official → more than ten years living at leisure in Shanyin 山阴 → summoned in the Kaixi years (the usual account: when Han Tuozhou 韩侂胄 undertook the Kaixi northern expedition, Lu You was summoned to compile history and to draft writings, and wrote for him 'A Record of the Southern Garden' (*Nanyuan ji* 《南园记》) (the usual account: in his late years he composed a record for Han Tuozhou, which later ages took as a handle against him) —** his years past eighty also carry a case over a political stain:** when we read Lu You, we have to allow him to be at once a sincere advocate of war and a staff guest who had miscalculated (Chapter 9, "a person and a book are two sets of accounts"); **1127–1194** Yang Wanli; **1126–1193** Fan Chengda (**embassy to the Jin in 1170:** the dispute over the rite of kneeling and bowing was written up as a diary: a diplomatic failure is often only a victory of ritual, and ritual was the battlefield the people of the Song cared about most**); **the 1200s** the Four Lings and the Rivers and Lakes school are active; **1276** Lin'an 临安 surrenders, and the three palaces are moved north (**the poem sequence Wang Yuanliang wrote in attendance:** a journey arranged by the winning side produced the best reporting of the side whose state had fallen**); **1282** Wen Tianxiang dies for his cause (**'Song of the Upright *Qi*' and the inscription on his belt (the usual account: a line of records whose encomium says "**孔曰成仁，孟曰取义**" — Confucius said, fulfil humaneness; Mencius said, choose rightness. (Our translation.)) —** the right to edit a deathbed text belongs to the dead: this is the last right held by the literature of the Song**); **the 1290s—** the gathering of lost texts, and the memoirs, after the fall of the Song (**notebooks of recollection, of the kind of Wu Zimu 吴自牧's *Mengliang lu* 《梦粱录》 ('A Record of Dreaming of Millet') and Zhou Mi 周密's *Wulin jiushi* 《武林旧事》 ('Former Events of Wulin') (the usual account):** the city had fallen, and so it was written as a catalogue: the whole of the Song's work of closing up is completed by lists (the final performance of the clauses in Chapter 5**).
:::

::: warning Four pits in this chapter
**Making a set subject of the "patriotic poet"** (**reading Lu You as a specimen in which the theme is decided first, so that his rain, his sleep, his medicines and his grandsons all become "minor works":** to simplify a person into one word is the routine work of an anthology, not a fact of literature (Chapter 9, a person and a book as two sets of accounts**)); **Citing the apricot blossom and cutting the complaint** (**the separate circulation of "**小楼一夜听春雨**" — In a small upper room, all night I listen to the spring rain. (Our translation.) — manufactures a Lu You at his ease:** to select with care is to cut and alter, and to cut and alter is to rewrite (Chapter 3's clause on the neighbours of a quotation**)); **Judging rank by output, or judging inferiority by output** (**"nearly ten thousand poems must contain much dross" and "nearly ten thousand poems, how abundant" are two faces of the same mistake:** neither asks what institution the output is — a running account is not a failure; it is the choice of a genre (the difficulty of the subject "nothing happening" in Chapter 6**)); **Explaining the idioms of Song poetry by their present meaning** (**"柳暗花明" ('willows dark, flowers bright') and "山重水复" ('mountains repeat, waters turn back') are, in the modern language, phrases of encouragement, and in the original poem a village path and the spring sacrifice:** once they have become idioms, the meaning of the sentence gives way to the use of the sentence (Lesson 7, the pragmatic layer of the vocabulary: look up what it is now, and also look up what it was then**).
:::

::: exercise Measuring frequency {level=1}
Count (or estimate by a sample) how visible words of the kind 中原 ('the Central Plain'), 楼船 ('towered ships'), 铁马 ('iron horses') and 梦 ('dream') are in Lu You's *Jiannan shigao* (spot-check one juan in a searchable electronic text), and write a conclusion of 100 characters: **what frequency can prove, and what it cannot prove**.
::: solution
Marking: **distinguish the strength of a theme from the quality of a work** (**frequency proves the strength of keeping on; it does not prove how high any single piece stands:** the two are different quantities, and to mix them is a common offence committed by statistics (Chapter 8's three checks on numbers**)); **the method of sampling must be stated by you** (**one juan, or fifty poems: make your sampling frame plain**.

::: exercise Pricing a petitioner {level=2}
Suppose you are a commoner poet in Lin'an in 1205. Write a "poem plus visiting card" (in no more than 60 characters) and present it to a prefect newly arrived in his post; then, by the logic of the petitioner's economy, assess yourself: **which characters are the cost of the compliment, which characters are the price quoted, and which sentence leaves a way out**.
::: solution
Marking: **the price quoted must be so veiled that it cannot be entered in the accounts** (**the contract of the genre for a poem that seeks patronage is to want it both ways —** its aesthetic difficulty comes precisely from this transaction, which cannot be stated outright (Law 8, the residual liability of the personal voice**)); **a way out** (**self-belittling of the kind 献丑 ('I make bold to show my clumsiness') and 刍荷 ('my crude gift, and I am obliged') is a device for leaving a way out:** first speak of yourself as of no weight, and then, if you are refused, it does not count as a refusal (the petitioner's version of Lesson 4's "say the self-mockery through to the end, and the mockery loses its force"**).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: frequency and the masterpiece {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing this claim: "**Literary history prefers masterpieces, while a life is made of frequency: by nearly ten thousand repetitions Lu You did a thing that no single quatrain (*juéjù*) could do — he made a Central Plain not yet recovered into a chronic pain of the whole people; the cost was that he gave up selection, and the gain is that we have received an archive with nothing blotted out**". You must draw on at least six items: **output = hours worked**, **the frequency of place names**, **the technique of the dream**, **the genre of the will**, **to select with care is to rewrite**, **the petitioner's economy**; and you must decide: **which is more useful to later ages, "the complete archive" or "the canon selected with care" (you must write one paragraph standing as a historian of the thirteenth century and one standing as the editor of an anthology in the twenty-first century, and then state your final preference and its cost)**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**hours worked, frequency, the permitted exit, entrusting, cutting and altering, pricing**); both standpoints (**the historian: the archive is more useful, because an anthology will lie, while a running account keeps the tax rate, the rain, and one person's weakness; the editor of an anthology: the canon is more useful, because the reader's time is the scarce resource, and frequency cannot be translated into a class of two minutes (marking looks at whether you admit that your own preference has a cost:** the party of the archive has to pay for the reader's attention, and the party of the anthology has to pay for the completeness of the truth: **no one can choose a side free of charge**); if the conclusion can be written, "**so I would rather close the case of Lu You like this: what he left is not an answer, but a duty roster from which nothing has been deleted — if patriotism is to become a virtue that can be checked, it first has to be a habit of coming to work on time**", that is an A.
:::
