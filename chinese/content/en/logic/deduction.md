title: "Deductive reasoning"
---

The previous chapter put sentences on the scales; this chapter works on **transport**. Deduction is the only trade in logic that issues a **guarantee**: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true, and there is no 'usually'. Its cost is equally plain: **it produces no new content**.

## Definition: validity = the truth-preserving conveyor {#def-baozhen}

::: definition Form decides the fate {#def-xingshi}
**Valid = it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false** (**the quality-control standard of the conveyor: it does not guarantee that the goods are genuine — whether the premises are true is not form's business — it only guarantees that transport loses nothing. 'If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true': that is the whole of the promise**). **There are two instruments of testing.** **The counter-example machine** (**to judge invalidity you need only one possible world in which the premises are true and the conclusion false: build it, and the case is closed — 'if it rains, the ground is wet; the ground is wet; therefore it rained' — a watering cart passes: counter-examples are cheap, and validity is expensive**). **The truth table** (**to judge validity you must look through every row: n atomic sentences, 2ⁿ rows — exhaustion is proof. The clumsy method is logic's virtue. It does not win by examples; it wins by looking at every example**). **Deduction's two birth certificates.** **Analyticity** (**'all bachelors are unmarried' is true by its meaning: you need not go out, need not sample, and need not disturb any bachelor — the Fregean route, the grand programme of reducing mathematics to logic (the front in Chapter 1)**). **Syntheticity** (**'the sun will rise tomorrow' has to be collected by experience taking delivery — Hume's problem (the fuse of Chapter 7: how does the habit of experience obtain an official approval for 'necessity'? Deduction does not take this job. It stakes the whole of necessity on form, and so its safety and its poverty are one and the same thing)**). **The overall verdict: a deductive conclusion is never better entitled than its premises. If you want something new, go to induction; if you want absolute safety, you can accept only empty talk. The whole adventure of knowledge takes place between the two poles.**
:::

## Connectives: five knives and two ambiguities {#def-lianjie}

::: definition ¬ ∧ ∨ → ↔ {#def-wulian}
**Negation, ¬** (**stacked negation in Chinese has to be reckoned up. Does “不是不同意” equal agreement? Not necessarily — “不是不同意，是还没想好” ('It is not that I do not agree; it is that I have not yet thought it through.' (Our translation.)): in speech a double negative is often a delaying action, not arithmetic (grammatical negatives stack, while pragmatics keeps a position in reserve — the auditor's way of writing: turn “不是不” directly into a formal wording, or write 'undecided' in plain words)**). **Conjunction, ∧** (**“且/但/却” are all ∧ in truth-value — “他很穷但很诚实” ('He is very poor, but very honest.' (Our translation.)) has the same value as “他很穷且很诚实” ('He is very poor, and very honest.' (Our translation.)): 'but' additionally registers a surprise (an adversative is a pragmatic complaint; it does not alter the accounts: logic cannot see your disappointment)**). **Disjunction, ∨ (inclusive)** (**the “或” on an application form is exclusive (a single choice); the “或” in contracts and in dictionaries is inclusive (“具备驾驶执照或同等资质”, 'holding a driving licence or an equivalent qualification' — if one has both, one still qualifies). Logic chooses the inclusive reading unless one writes expressly that exactly one of the two holds — pairings of the excluded-middle sort, such as “去或者不去” ('go, or not go'), are rhetoric, not a connective**). **Implication, →** (**treated in a section of its own below**). **Equivalence, ↔** (**if and only if: a two-way street of necessary and sufficient conditions — the legislator's favourite and the reader's minefield (“本合同自双方签字之日起生效”, 'This contract takes effect from the day on which both parties sign.' (Our translation.): is that necessary, or necessary and sufficient? Signing → taking effect: that cell is iron. If they do not sign, does it then fail to take effect? The clause does not write the second arrow. The second arrow is the interpreter's imagination)**).
:::

::: definition Material implication: a minimal contract {#def-yunhan}
**'If p then q' is false only when p is true and q is false** — **and so 'if 2＋2＝5, then the moon is made of cheese' is true: logic has not gone mad; the contract is written thin. '→' promises no connection of content between p and q. It only cancels one case (true antecedent, false consequent).** **A consequence of this thin contract (the Lewis-style 'paradoxes'): when the antecedent cannot be true, the conditional is true in every case ('if the sun goes out tomorrow morning, I shall stand you dinner' is eternally true — because it never has a chance to be in breach).** **The everyday 'if' carries a thick expectation of relevance and of causation — the two must be kept in separate accounts.** The position in class (**when you assess an everyday or a legal argument, require '→' to count as 'strong relevance'. Of a sentence such as 'if you do not sign up for the card, that shows you do not care about your health', ask 'have you thought of the case in which the antecedent is true and the consequent false?' — sophistry is lawful on the thin contract and in breach on the thick one: your refutation has to point at the thick contract as it speaks**). **Counterfactual conditionals** (**“早如此，竟不用今日来了” — 'Had it been so earlier, there would have been no need, after all, to come today.' (Our translation.) — words of regret spoken by Wu Jinxiao 乌进孝 in *Dream of the Red Chamber*: the antecedent is contrary to a known fact, and the truth table crashes on the spot (p is false, so the whole sentence is eternally true — but a person's regret plainly has content: what it compares is a world in which 'it had been so earlier'). Neither strict implication nor possible-world semantics can put this account fully right. Conditionals are a frontier region of logic. This lesson leaves a blank at the boundary stone, and asks only that you recognise this: a sentence whose antecedent is already known to be false has no force as a refutation (it has not even been entered on the books)**).
:::

## Four famous instruments and two ways to die {#def-mingqi}

::: definition MP and MT: the only two fast lanes {#def-mpmt}
**Modus ponens, MP, affirming the antecedent** (**if p then q; p; ∴ q — insert a coin and the goods come out**). **Modus tollens, MT, denying the consequent** (**if p then q; not q; ∴ not p — nothing came out, so no coin was inserted / the access control did not open, so the card is invalid. Proof by contradiction in mathematics, and in court 'if it were true it would have left a trace; there is no trace', are all MT wearing different uniforms ('MT is the stance in logic that can fight best: it lets the enemy's prophecy dig the grave')**). **The two ways to die.** **Affirming the consequent** (**if p then q; q; ∴ p — the ground is wet, so it rained: the vending machine has been treated as a one-way street. An everyday disaster zone: 'he said that because he had a guilty conscience; he said it; therefore he has a guilty conscience' — nerves, a slip of the tongue and being in a hurry are all other fathers of the consequent**). **Denying the antecedent** (**if p then q; not p; ∴ not q — no coin inserted, so no goods come out? If the access card is not swiped one cannot get in, but the door may have been open already: a letter of self-pardon when one's effort is not sufficient ('effort is useless anyway': the depressive logic of denying the antecedent — psychologically true, formally illicit)**). **The key model (a precise match in Chinese)** (**只要…就… = sufficient (insert a coin and the goods must come out); 只有…才… = necessary (no card, and one necessarily does not get in). The two connectives are the double yellow lines of the logic of Chinese — “只有努力，才能毕业” ('Only if one works hard can one graduate.' (Our translation.)) = graduation → hard work (the contrapositive: no hard work → no graduation: MT's track); “只要努力，就能毕业” ('So long as one works hard, one can graduate.' (Our translation.)) = hard work → graduation (MP's track, which a single failed course can punch through) — look at the guarantees the people around you issue: nine-tenths of them are written on the wrong track**).
:::

::: definition Transformations: obversion, conversion and contraposition {#def-bianxing}
**The square of opposition keeps the accounts of 'all / some / not'; the logic of the connectives keeps the accounts of 'if'; the two meet in the standard form of the categorical proposition.** **Three lawful transformations.** **Obversion** (**'all S are P' = 'no S is non-P': the cosmetic art of double negation**). **Conversion** (**'all S are P' permits only the weakened conversion 'some P are S' (“所有人都会犯错” ('All humans make mistakes.' (Our translation.)) can be converted only into “有些会犯错的是人” ('Some who make mistakes are human.' (Our translation.)), and cannot be converted into “所有会犯错的都是人” ('All who make mistakes are human.' (Our translation.))); 'no S is P' → 'no P is S': full conversion; 'some S are P' → 'some P are S': full conversion — particulars and negatives are not allowed offside: conversion is a dance of extensions; step wrong, and you tread the empty ground of 'some P are not S'**). **Contraposition (the golden equivalence)** — **'if p then q' = 'if not q then not p': the golden equivalence of the conditional, and the twin of MT — “若及格须满四十学时” ('If one is to pass, one must complete forty class hours.' (Our translation.)) = “不满四十学时则不及格” ('If one has not completed forty class hours, one does not pass.' (Our translation.)): two accents of the academic registry, one meaning.** **A joint exercise in natural deduction (the square + negation):** “并非所有都合格” ('It is not the case that all are qualified.' (Our translation.)) = “有些不合格” ('Some are not qualified.' (Our translation.)); “并非（甲且乙）” = 'not (Jia and Yi)' = “非甲或非乙” ('not Jia, or not Yi') (**De Morgan: sending a negation inside the brackets means changing the sign — “不是又贵又难用” ('It is not both expensive and hard to use.' (Our translation.)) = “要么不贵，要么不难用” ('Either it is not expensive, or it is not hard to use.' (Our translation.)): two grounds for a refund in a complaint, and only one of them needs to hold**).
:::

::: example The dormitory case of 'only one sentence is true' {#ex-zhenhua}
Before the end-of-term results are posted. Jia 甲: 'The whole class can pass.' Yi 乙: 'The class monitor cannot pass.' Bing 丙: 'Someone cannot pass.' Ding 丁: 'Someone can pass.' It is known that **only one of the four sentences is true**. Who told the truth? What is the situation of the whole class?
Then answer a second question: if the given condition is changed to 'only two sentences are true', does the answer change?
::: solution
**First step: find the relations on the square.** Jia (A: all are) and Bing (O: some are not) are **contradictories** — **necessarily one true and one false: the true sentence must lie between Jia and Bing** (the handle that the law of non-contradiction gives you: among the six sentences, find one contradictory pair, and the rest are all false — this is the 'master switch' for problems of this kind). Thus **Yi and Ding are both false**: Yi false → the monitor can pass; Ding (I: some are) false → its contradictory (E: none are) is true → **the whole class fails**. Check back: when the whole class fails, Bing ('someone cannot pass') is true and Jia is false — **this matches 'only one sentence is true'**. Conclusion: **the true sentence is Bing's; the whole class fails, and the monitor is among them** (the teaching point: **the contradictory pair is the hinge of the problem: find it, and the other propositions disarm themselves**). **The second question.** When two sentences are true, the hinge is still between Jia and Bing — **Jia and Bing are still one true and one false, so exactly one of Yi and Ding is true. Suppose Yi is true (the monitor fails): Ding ('someone can pass') is not settled by that, and the stock of cases must be divided — if the others pass, then Yi is true, Bing is true, Ding is true and Jia is false: three truths, over the quota; if everyone except the monitor fails, then Yi is true, Bing is true, Ding is false and Jia is false: exactly two true sentences, and this solution holds.** Suppose Yi is false (the monitor passes); then Ding must be true, and one further sentence among Jia and Bing must still be true (the monitor passes, and someone fails: Bing true, Jia false) — again three truths, over the quota. **After the exhaustion there is only one solution: everyone except the monitor fails** ('only one true sentence', and the truth is Bing's; 'two true sentences', and the truths are Yi's and Bing's — the two questions have the same shape and different fruit: one change in a sentence of the given conditions reshuffles the whole solution space. The 'eye' of a logic problem is that half-line (the value of a truth table: do not use cleverness, use patience: 2⁴＝16 rows are enough, but the handle on the square takes only ten seconds)).
:::
:::

::: intuition Tautology: between the safest and the most useless {#int-chongyan}
**'Tomorrow it either rains or it does not rain' is eternally true — and it says nothing.** **A sentence that is true on every row of the truth table runs no risk, because the set of worlds it rules out is empty** (**Popper's inverse law registers here: the lower the degree of falsifiability, the safer the content, and the poorer. Why the empty talk of 'if it is not A, then it is B' is infuriating: it spends the risk-budget of speaking on a bet at zero odds, and still asks you to applaud its 'clear-headedness'**). **Corollary one (the proper use of an eternally true sentence).** **A tautology is the scaffolding of logic, not the building: validity itself is not for use ('if the premises are true, then the conclusion is true; the premises are true; ∴ the conclusion is true' is eternally true — and its use lies in setting an argument out in a shape that can be checked: first there is MP, and only then is there a theorem of MP).** **Corollary two (beware the sentence that pretends to be insurance).** **'Investing carries risk; does not investing carry none?' — the questions kick the topic back into an unsettled region, and neither question carries falsifiable content: a double eternal truth in the language of policy ('possible' and 'necessarily possible' have the same value: when you take out insurance on a conclusion, first read off the sum insured on the policy. Between “争取完成” ('strive to complete') and “完成” ('complete') there stands an entire sheet of accountability (the official-document version of the account of qualifiers in Chapter 3)**).
:::

::: history A three-generation genealogy of the art of deduction {#sec-jiapu}
**4th century BC.** Aristotle's *Prior Analytics* (**the syllogism = the first deductive machine in history: the exhaustion of figure and mood is the ancestor of a 'decision procedure' — inference was brought down from eloquence to arithmetic**). **The Stoics** (**five 'argument-forms' of propositional logic: MP and MT are entered on the register here — the second school of ancient logic, and what it taught was 'if', not 'all'**). **The Middle Ages.** The doctrine of consequentiae (consequentiae: the scholastic high period of the study of rules of deduction, plus **the continued settling of accounts in the truth-value dispute over 'the necessary and the contingent'**). **1879–1910.** From Frege to *Principia Mathematica* (**modern logic is switched on: quantifiers, rules of inference, and the deriving of arithmetic from logic. The certainty of deduction was pushed to its limit, and was also frozen on the spot by Gödel in 1931 (the incompleteness theorem: in any sufficiently strong system, truth cannot all be gathered into what is provable — the metamathematical seal on 'safety and poverty are one and the same thing')**). **The 1930s.** The formalist programme is set back (**certainty acquired a boundary stone: what deduction can guarantee stops here — an honest map draws its own coastline first**). **From 1965.** Natural deduction enters the computer (**the Curry–Howard correspondence: 'a proof is a program'. Deduction had a result of running for the first time — people who can write a compiler put Aristotle's product online**). **On the Chinese-department side** (**the entry of *yinming* into China (因明, the Buddhist science of reasons): the 'three characteristics of a reason' (因三相) of Dignāga (陈那) and Dharmakīrti (法称), transmitted in the translations of Xuanzang 玄奘. The three-part form 'thesis — reason — example' (宗—因—喻) = an Indian hybrid of deduction and analogy. Qing scholars reread the *Mobian* 《墨辩》 ('the Mohist dialectical chapters') through *yinming*: a new lens for the study of *mingbian* (名辩, names and disputation). A special dossier is kept for the final lesson of this chapter: the three skeletons — the syllogism, *yinming* and the *Mobian* — may X-ray one another, but do not set one another's bones**).
:::

::: warning Four pits of deduction
**Addiction to affirming the consequent** (**the consequent may have a hundred households (sprinkling, pouring, rain) — when you see 'so it must be', first check whether the consequent still has another father**). **Taking validity for the conclusion's being correct** (**'valid' and 'true' are two different firms (Chapter 1): a perfectly valid argument can carry water across from false premises to the far bank — form does not produce the water**). **Misreading “只有／只要”** (**the guarantee is written on the wrong arrow: the whole school supposes that “只有平时分合格才能考试” ('Only if the mark for work over the term is satisfactory may one sit the examination.' (Our translation.)) equals 'satisfactory → may sit' (in fact it is 'may sit → satisfactory'): read one character the wrong way round, and the appeal is lost in full**). **Using an eternally true sentence to fake depth** (**'every decision has a cost, so make no decision': the antecedent is eternally true, and the consequent is illicit. Smuggling a risky conclusion out of a safe sentence is the standard production line of chicken soup**).
:::

::: quiz
The logical form of “只有年满十八岁才能投票” ('Only if one has reached the age of eighteen may one vote.' (Our translation.)) is ( ).
- [ ] eighteen or over → vote (the direction has been read backwards)
- [x] if one votes, then one has reached eighteen (voting → eighteen or over: the necessary condition stands as the consequent)
- [ ] eighteen or over → vote
- [ ] the two are unrelated
::: solution
'Only if p, q' = q→p: no card, and one necessarily does not get in. Having reached the age of eighteen is a necessary condition.
:::

If Jia's “如果及格须满四十学时” ('If one is to pass, one must complete forty class hours.' (Our translation.)) is true, then what must be true is ( ).
- [ ] Complete forty class hours and one is sure to pass
- [x] Fail to complete forty class hours and one does not pass
- [ ] None of those who fail have completed forty class hours
- [ ] Someone does not pass
::: solution
Equivalence by contraposition: the twin of MT. Option A is an attempted affirming of the consequent.

::: quiz
The sentence 'tomorrow it may rain, and it may also not rain' ( ).
- [ ] carries a great deal of information
- [x] is logically eternally true, and pays zero odds in experience: it rules out no weather
- [ ] is a logical contradiction
- [ ] cannot be judged true or false
::: solution
The set of worlds ruled out by an eternally true sentence is empty: safety and poverty have the same price.

::: exercise Reasoning from a case note {level=1}
The doctor says: 'If the test shows an infection, I shall prescribe an antibiotic.' Afterwards the nurse says: 'The doctor prescribed an antibiotic.' ① Can one infer 'the test showed an infection'? Write the form. ② If the nurse corrects herself to 'the doctor did not prescribe an antibiotic', what can be inferred? Is that inference valid, and what does its soundness still lack? ③ Fill the doctor's words out to 'if and only if', and answer ① again.
::: solution
① **No (affirming the consequent):** p→q, q ∴ p is invalid — the doctor may have prescribed from experience (the consequent has another father). ② **MT is valid:** ¬q→¬p, 'the test did not show an infection' (on the premise that what the doctor said is true) — validity scores full marks, and soundness depends on the premise: whether the doctor's sentence was iron at the time (is there an exception clause? The moment 'except in a severe case' appears, the contrapositive is no longer iron: one exception eats the occasion of the whole implication — the 'sufficiency' of a sufficient condition is priced by the list of exceptions (the contract version of the qualifiers in Chapter 3)). ③ **Once it is rewritten as a two-way street, ① becomes valid** (q→p holds — the art of putting it in a written bond: the whole of a contract lawyer's work is to supply the second arrow).
:::

::: exercise A screwdriver for the connectives {level=2}
Rewrite the five Chinese sentences into **symbolic form**, and judge each valid or invalid. ① “不撞邪就不会这么倒霉；他这么倒霉；所以他撞邪了” ('If he had not run into an evil influence, he would not be this unlucky; he is this unlucky; therefore he has run into an evil influence.' (Our translation.)); ② “只要努力就能学会；他没学会；所以他没努力” ('So long as one works hard, one can learn it; he has not learned it; therefore he did not work hard.' (Our translation.)); ③ “除非你有通行证，否则不能进；他进去了；所以他有通行证” ('Unless you have a pass, you cannot go in; he went in; therefore he has a pass.' (Our translation.)); ④ “要么甲是主犯，要么乙是主犯；甲是主犯；所以乙不是” ('Either Jia is the principal offender, or Yi is; Jia is the principal offender; therefore Yi is not.' (Our translation.)); ⑤ “不是不想去，是去不起” ('It is not that I do not want to go; it is that I cannot afford to go.' (Our translation.)). (Treat “除非…否则…” uniformly as “除非 p 否则不 q” = “只有 p 才 q” = q→p.)
::: solution
① **The MT form is legitimate, but the premise contains a false implication** (the consequent 'bad luck' has ten million fathers: validity ≠ soundness (on site at pit 3)). ② **MT:** hard work → learning it; not learning it ∴ not hard work — valid. Soundness depends on whether “只要…就…” has been written too full (a guarantee on which every variable of the method of teaching has been flattened) — valid, and almost sound: this is the full-marks posture of everyday argument. ③ Entering → a pass; he entered ∴ he has a pass (MP): valid — the standard taming of an 'unless' sentence. ④ **An illicit use of inclusive disjunction** ('Jia or Yi' allows both to be principal offenders: affirming one cannot negate the other. To judge it valid, one must first stamp 'or' with an exclusive seal (a retrial of the ambiguity case in the section on connectives)). ⑤ **Not ¬go, but ¬able:** a model case of a pragmatic adversative plus a shifted negation — the symbolic form: ¬想去 ∧ ¬(负担得起) → 实际¬去, a coordinate structure. What is negated in “不想去” is the reason, not the action: in speech, conjunction and excuse divide the work.
:::

::: exercise A short essay: the humility of deduction {level=3}
Write six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing the following. “演绎是逻辑里的守财奴：它一个铜板也不多给你，因此你永远可以相信它的花账——想发财（新知识）的人必须去归纳、去试验、去冒休谟的险；而所有发财史的最后一步，仍是把账本交给守财奴审计” ('Deduction is the miser of logic: it never gives you one copper coin extra, and therefore you can always trust the accounts it keeps — anyone who wants to get rich (new knowledge) must go to induction, go and make experiments, and run Hume's risk; and the last step of every history of getting rich is still to hand the books to the miser for audit.' (Our translation.)). You must draw on at least five items: **the truth-preserving conveyor**, **MP/MT and the case of affirming the consequent**, **the key model (只有／只要)**, **the thin contract of material implication**, and **the safety zone of tautology**. And give a verdict: does 'the feeling of discovering a mathematical theorem' refute 'a deductive conclusion does not exceed its premises'? (You may stand with Frege, stand with Peirce's 'abduction' (溯因), or stand with the intuitionism for which 'proof is invention' — choose any one, and give the conditions under which it fails.)
::: solution
Points for marking. The five mechanisms (**truth-preservation adds no value; MT makes the enemy's prophecy dig the grave; the connective is the track; the thin contract indulges false guarantees; an eternally true sentence pays zero odds**). The verdict on mathematics (**three reference lines. The Fregean line — the surprise comes from 'logic and meaning not having been seen clearly'; discovery = coming to recognise one's own premises anew: the principle of not exceeding is kept, and psychology yields. The abduction school — the process of guessing a theorem is induction and abduction (Peircean 'hypothetical inference'): only the proof is deduction: award the right of invention to the venture, and the right of audit to the miser, and both are secured. Intuitionism — 'to be true is to have been constructed': deduction really does add value, because it produces mathematical objects themselves — the cost: the law of excluded middle is out, and some classical theorems have their tickets refunded. All three roads must pay tax; marking looks at the tax bill**). If the conclusion writes that **'what deduction teaches is humility plus ambition: humility in "your conclusion is never better entitled than your premises", ambition in "then establish premises worthy of the conclusion" — there is not much human certainty, but transport never loses a load: all the rest is a loan from induction, and the next chapter begins the repayment of principal and interest'**, that is an A.
:::
