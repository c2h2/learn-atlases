---
title: "The structure of arguments"
---

Logic textbooks usually begin from "what is logic". This lesson begins from a harsher line: **"I feel that what I say is reasonable" is not an argument — a tape repeater can also say "I feel"** — **an argument is the finished product of reasoning, and this lesson teaches three things: take it apart, test it, and rebuild it**.

## Definition: the minimal skeleton of an argument {#def-gujia}

::: definition Premises, conclusion and inference-markers {#def-qiant}
**An argument = a set of statements: some premises claim to give support to another statement (the conclusion).** Three tools for the dissection: **① the conclusion-locator** (**因此 ('therefore'), 可见 ('it can be seen'), 这就是为什么 ('this is why'), 综上所述 ('to sum up') — the house numbers of the conclusion; note that 因为 ('because') always points to a premise and 所以 ('so') always points to a conclusion — the disaster zone of Chinese faulty sentences is the chain 之所以……是因为…… ('the reason why … is because …') ("我迟到的原因，是因为闹钟坏了" — 'The reason I was late is because the alarm clock broke.' (Our translation.) — 的原因 ('the reason') and 因为 ('because') issue the same ticket twice: logical laziness hides inside a grammatical error**); **② premise-indicators** (**由于 ('owing to'), 根据 ('on the basis of'), 鉴于 ('in view of'), 既然 ('since') — and the most insidious "zero marker": two sentences side by side, the relation of support left for the reader to supply in the mind ("他平时用功，这次没考好" — 'He is usually diligent; this time he did badly in the exam.' (Our translation.) — is it an explanation? a rebuttal of "effort is bound to bring good results"? or pure narrative? a zero-marker sentence requires you first to supply "他认为……所以……" ('he holds that …, so …')**); **③ inferential relation ≠ psychological order** (**whether a person states the conclusion first or the reasons first follows mood; logic recognises only the direction of support: premises → conclusion, and it has nothing to do with time, word order or volume ("我反对，因为我不接受" — 'I object, because I do not accept it.' (Our translation.) — the two sentences are echoes of each other: this is not an argument, it is a wheel marking time where it stands — learning to spot 同语反复 ('tautology') is the spectacles issued in the first lesson**). **A blacklist of what is not an argument** (**imperatives ("把证据摆出来！" — 'Put the evidence on the table!' (Our translation.)), questions ("这难道不是撒谎吗？" — 'Is this not lying?' (Our translation.) — a rhetorical question is an argument's cosplay), and a bare stack of assertions ("他不诚实，政策也失败，媒体还腐败" — 'He is dishonest, the policy has failed too, and the media are corrupt as well.' (Our translation.) — three claims and zero support: a list is not an argument, a slogan is not reasoning**).
:::

::: definition Argument, explanation, persuasion and quarrel {#def-biejia}
**Argument vs explanation** (**the same fact, two questions: "why" asks for a cause (explanation) or for a reason (argument) — "the iron bridge broke because it was overloaded" is an explanation; "this bridge ought to be repaired, because it has been overloaded" is an argument: an explanation sets out from what is known and looks back for a cause; an argument sets out from what is known and establishes a conclusion ahead (Lesson 15's "what was said vs what was done" comes to earth here: an explanation does not require you to believe anything new; an argument does**); **argument vs persuasion** (**rhetoric persuades by character and by circumstance (Aristotle's three appeals, ethos/pathos/logos), and logic inspects only the logos branch — this lesson does not forbid a moving close; it only forbids using what is moving to smuggle a premise through ("if you can be moved, then you have been persuaded: tears are the receipt for persuasion, not the invoice for an argument"**); **argument vs quarrel** (**the test a quarrel must pass is winning; the test an argument must pass is that "both sides may be wrong": each round, a quarrel eliminates evidence; each round, an argument registers evidence ("a person who puts the position first is not arguing, but going to law: every one of his premises was hired in"**). **The steel-man principle** (the correct Chinese for charity ought to be 原情原则 ('the principle of going back to the circumstances'), or one may simply call it the **"steel man"**: first build the other side's position up to its strongest, and only then set about refuting it — knocking down a straw man is pleasant, but the return is zero: every weakened version you overturn has done nothing to help your opponent improve his weapon).
:::

::: example Dissecting a group-chat screenshot {#ex-qunliao}
Take a common dispute apart into standard form (number the premises, and mark the conclusion with "∴"), and answer the three questions that follow.
A: "**抽烟导致肺癌，我爷爷抽到九十岁好好的，所以‘导致’是骗人的。**" ('Smoking causes lung cancer. My grandfather smoked until ninety and was perfectly well, so "causes" is a fraud.') (Our translation.)
B: "**你不能这么说，医生都不抽烟。**" ('You cannot say that. Doctors don't smoke.') (Our translation.)
C: "**你不就是烟厂的吗，别洗了。**" ('Aren't you from the cigarette factory? Stop the whitewash.') (Our translation.)
① Of A's argument, what are the premises and what is the conclusion? **Which step has gone wrong** (the sample? or the meaning of "causes"?); ② Once the implicit premise is supplied, does B's argument hold?; ③ What has C given (an argument? or something else?); ④ If A changes his wording — "**我认识两个人抽到九十没事——所以抽烟‘必然’致癌说不通**" ('I know two people who smoked until ninety and came to no harm — so it will not do to say that smoking "necessarily" causes cancer.') (Our translation.) — by how much does his chance of winning change?
::: solution
① **A's standard form**: P1 Smoking causes lung cancer (on the strong reading, as "everyone who smokes is bound to fall ill"); P2 My grandfather smoked until ninety and is healthy; ∴ It is not the case that it "causes". **There are two lesions, and they must be judged separately**: **the sample level** (a single case can rebut a universal proposition — if P1 is read as "whoever smokes must die young", the grandfather, as one sample, is enough (the logical force of a counter-example is at full strength — **an important standing order of this lesson: a single case cannot confirm a regularity, but it can perfectly well falsify a universal: the asymmetry of induction**); **the semantic level** (A's real target is in fact the popular misunderstanding that "causes = must bring a person to illness" — **he has beaten a straw man: "causes" in the statistical sense (a rise in relative risk) has not been knocked down at all**) — **an argument can be at once "valid" (if read in the strong way, the form is correct) and "irrelevant" (what it strikes is a version nobody asserted): validity and relevance are two independent inspections**; ② **B's elliptical syllogism**: the implicit premise "doctors (all, or most) do not do what harms health" + "they do not smoke" → smoking is harmful; **the implicit premise must itself come into court** (doctors' not smoking ≠ smoking causes disease? what is relied on here is the appeal of "the expert's own example" — an appeal to authority, but it falls within a legitimate domain of authority: medicine's relevant authority on lung cancer; legitimate and illegitimate differ only in the "domain"**); ③ C has given nothing at all** (**an attack on the person (ad hominem): to question a source's interest is not to rebut the content of the claim — but mind the measure: as a "prompt about the weight of evidence" C's remark has a weak use (look into his interest), and as a rebuttal it is not worth a penny (a worker in a cigarette factory may also say something true) — this lesson's term: an appeal to the person is "relevance at zero; the weight may be set at 0.1"**); ④ **The revised version is stronger** (the conclusion is lowered from "'causes' is a fraud" to "'necessarily causes cancer' does not hold" — **the conclusion shrinks, and the premises can hold it up: this is "the inverse law of an argument's strength and the boldness of its conclusion": an argument willing to concede is often harder to knock down**).
:::
:::

::: intuition An argument is a narrative that can be audited {#int-shenji}
Think of an argument as **a ledger with its vouchers**: premises = the vouchers entered on the books, rules of inference = the regulations for keeping the books, the conclusion = the balance on the statement — **an audit looks at only two things: whether the vouchers are genuine (the truth of the premises), and whether the bookkeeping is in order (the validity of the inference)** (**a wrong balance may be false vouchers (false premises) or a mis-entry (invalidity): the two criticisms must not be used one for the other ("your premises are not true" and "it does not follow" are the business of two different firms)**). **From this comes the master formula of the lesson: a good argument = sound = valid + true premises** — **validity only guarantees that the conveyor of "if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true" is intact; the conveyor does not produce truth, it only transports it (the definition in Chapter 4: validity is truth-preservation)** — **this is also the difference between a logic class and a "class in having a point": logic does not mind whether your starting point stands high; it only minds whether your road is broken**.
:::

::: history Four fronts in the study of argument {#sec-sixian2}
**The 4th century BC**: Aristotle's *Organon* (**the legislation of the art of debate: *Sophistical Refutations* is the first "casebook of fallacies" — humanity's earliest "round-up of bad reviews"**) + the Chinese Mohist *Xiaoqu* 《小取》 ('Choosing the Lesser') ("**辟也者，举他物而以明之也；侔也者，比辞而俱行也；援也者，曰：子然，我奚独不可以然也？推也者，以其所不取之同于其所取者，予之也**" (*Xiaoqu*): 'To *pi* is to bring forward some other thing and by that make the matter clear; to *mou* is to set expressions side by side so that they advance together; to *yuan* is to say: you count it as so — why am I alone not allowed to count it as so?; to *tui* is to hand over the point on which what he will not accept is the same as what he does accept.' (Our translation.)) **— four forms: giving an example (辟 *pi*), analogy (侔 *mou*), turning his own spear on him (援 *yuan*), and extending within the same kind (推 *tui*) — the Warring States already had a complete study of argument-forms, only a generation later than the *Organon***; **the Middle Ages**: logic as the first of the university's three arts (**the scholastic gymnastics of disputation: the ancestor of the "objection–reply" structure of the modern essay**); **1958**: Perelman's *The New Rhetoric* (**"the theory of argument returns from the realm of proof to the realm of the probable" — the proposal of sorting hearers into a "universal audience": fitting a rational prosthesis onto persuasion**); **the 1970s**: the informal-logic movement + the Toulmin model (**six items: claim — data — warrant — backing — qualifier — rebuttal (Toulmin 1958): a jurist's scalpel for dissecting arguments — why a textbook must teach "qualifiers": because the strength of everyday argument is almost entirely hidden in "usually", "probably", "on condition that …" (the modality of propositions in Chapter 3, previewed here**); **the 1940s to the present**: argument and artificial intelligence (**defeasible logic: reasoning in law and in common sense permits "overthrow by an exception" — machines begin to learn that "a principle is not clear until it is argued", and also to learn "not to speak in absolutes"**); **the hidden line on the Chinese-department side** (**from the *Mobian* 《墨辩》 to the "wenli" of the Qing scholars (文理; Liu Dakui 刘大櫆 and Yao Yongpu 姚永朴 on "how to bring a sentence to rest, and how to turn away from it") — logic asleep inside the study of prose: one task of this lesson is to wake it, but not to call its ghost back for it: logic is anatomy, the eight-legged essay is a dance; both are wanted; do not certify one as the other**).
:::

::: warning Four pits on the way into argument
**Taking "the feeling of being reasonable" for an argument** (**reading smoothly ≠ valid: the Chinese 因为……所以…… ('because … therefore …') joins two clauses that may both be illusions — a feel for the language is a product of statistics; logic is the auditor of the statistics**); **taking refutation for a quarrel** (**after "what you say is wrong" there must be content: a turn that only denies and does not rebuild is a roadblock, not a work of engineering (Chapter 6: refutation is skilled work — first build the other side's position up to its strongest, then strike)**); **finding "logic" for everything** (**"she doesn't like me, because she didn't reply today" — the form is complete, the premises are rotten: logic cannot save the raw material (a conveyor in good order does not mean the goods are genuine — do not mix up the two firms of Section 1)**); **wrecking the occasion by abusing terms** (**"That's a slippery slope!" "Equivocation!" — pinning on a label is a pseudo-refutation that costs nothing: a charge of fallacy itself needs an argument (it must first restate the other side's argument correctly — the steel-man principle binds the accuser too**).
:::

::: exercise Rewriting into standard form {level=1}
Rewrite three everyday assertions as a **standard argument form** (P1, P2, … ∴ conclusion), and **supply the implicit premises that were left out**: ① "Don't trust his review; he took a fee for the advertisement"; ② "The teacher has explained this problem, so it cannot but be that you are stupid"; ③ "This shop has so many good reviews — let's go."
::: solution
Marking: **two or more premises on each question** (including the implicit ones supplied — the implicit premises of ① are "taking money affects the objectivity of a review" + "this review is of that kind"); **the conclusion and the premises agree in the direction of support** (the hidden chain of premises in ②: "it was explained → one ought to be able to do it; one can't → one ought to go over it; you got through none of that → the only attribution left is 'stupid'?" — once it is filled in, one naturally sees where the chain breaks (**the "method of exhaustive attribution" is not in fact exhaustive: the problem is hard, time, carelessness — see "elimination" in the next lesson**)); the implicit premise of ③ is "number of good reviews = quality" (**a regime of fake orders exists, so the weight of this premise is marked down in the e-commerce setting of the 2020s — an implicit premise has a shelf life**).
:::
:::

::: exercise A pre-trial of fallacies {level=2}
Collect three real remarks from the internet (a screenshot, or an imitation you write yourself), and **exchange them and hold a "pre-trial"**: reconstruct the standard form → name the type of problem (false premise / invalid / irrelevant) → write one "steel-man refutation" (first restate the other side's strongest version in a single sentence, then strike).
::: solution
Marking: **the restatement step is compulsory** (anyone who cannot restate the other side's position is not admitted to the refutation — **the coach's rule: if you cannot state your opponent clearly, you do not take the field**); more than one type may be chosen, but a "main problem" must be given; if the steel-man refutation contains the pattern "even on the strongest way of putting what you say …, still …", full marks (**this is the set pattern of this lesson**).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: is logic courtesy or violence? {level=3}
Write six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing "**logic teaches people to be reasonable, but the sentence 'let us be reasonable about this' is itself often the beginning of not being reasonable: it pretends that the two sides stand on the starting line, pretends that the judge is neutral, and pretends that the conclusion is open**". You must draw on at least five items: **argument ≠ persuasion ≠ quarrel**, **the steel-man principle**, **sound = valid + true premises**, **the shelf life of implicit premises**, **the pit of abusing terms**; and give a verdict: **in the three settings of an intimate relationship, of mourning, and of the aesthetic, is "demanding logic" a breach of courtesy — and who draws the boundary within which logic applies?**
::: solution
Marking points: the five mechanisms (**the illusion of a starting line = "argument ≠ persuasion": the three appeals of rhetoric have never left the stage; the illusion of a judge = a position is already embedded in the premises; "the look of you being reasonable" is also a performance of ethos; the steel man = reverse training, in which the opposing side requires you to be honest; shelf life = the entrance, through logic, into historical sociology**); the verdict (**reference routes: the discourtesy thesis — in mourning and in love there is "legitimate expression that is not an argument" (an embrace supplies no premise), and to demand logic = to drag the relationship into the wrong genre (Chapter 8: the genre-contract has been misapplied); the internalist thesis about the boundary — a so-called "moment of not being reasonable" is precisely the surface on which logic works: identify which contract the other person has signed at this moment (a contract of companionship?), for to misuse logic is itself to fail at logic ("the boundary within which logic applies" is a logical question — the meta-level is not exempt)**); if the conclusion is written "**the real courtesy of logic is not to open a hearing at any moment, but to know when the gavel ought to be set down — and to be clear whether one has set it down, or has not set it down at all**", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
The skeleton, filed: three tools (**locating the conclusion, indicating the premises, direction of support ≠ psychological order**), telling things apart (**an explanation looks back, an argument establishes what lies ahead; tears are the receipt for persuasion, not the invoice; a quarrel eliminates evidence, an argument registers evidence**), the audit formula (**sound = valid + true premises; do not mix up the two firms**), the *Mobian* (**pi, mou, yuan, tui: the Warring States already had a study of argument-forms**). The method in one sentence: **first reconstruct, then accuse; first the steel man, then draw the sword**. The next chapter sets up the target: what a "claim" is — how a single sentence counts as "something worth proving".
:::
