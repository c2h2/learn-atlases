---
title: "Causal reasoning"
---

The five methods of the previous chapter produced a list of suspects; this chapter opens the court — **"A causes B" is the most expensive assertion in logic: other sentences ask for evidence; what this one asks for is a mechanism, a comparison and an exclusion. Its treatment is also the most exceptional: the whole of the media, handling a myriad of affairs each day, carries out the death penalty every day without a trial**.

## Definition: why this sentence is the most expensive {#def-zuigui}

::: definition The three policies of a causal assertion {#def-baodan}
**What "causes" claims is not merely "appearing together", but "move it, and it will change" — it puts up three policies**: **① temporal order** (**the cause comes before the effect — note that this is only a necessary condition: among the things that arrive earlier are also queue numbers and rumours**); **② the method of concomitant variation** (**move the cause and the effect moves: dose, time point, and removal of the intervention must all answer, at all three places (the formal appearance in court of the method of concomitant variation in Chapter 6**); **③ no confounding** (**apart from it, no other explanation can square the accounts equally well — this policy is the most expensive: it requires you to know every suspect, including those who never appeared in court**). **How the burden of proof is assigned** (**the one who judges causation takes out the policies; the one who doubts points to the suspects: in everyday discussion, whoever first says "causes" is the one who first pays the deposit — a media headline that promotes "correlation" to "causes" is the usual driving without a licence, and the reader's repost is the free driver in the structure of joint offence (the full brother of the persuasive definition in Chapter 2: "causes" is the causal edition of the trickery of definition**)).
:::

## The four exits of correlation {#def-siku}

::: definition Four cases in which A and B co-occur {#def-chukou}
**①A→B** (**one of the correct readings**); **②B→A** (**reverse causation — "people who regularly drink sugar-free drinks are fatter": it is people who want to lose weight who are changing their drinks: the causal arrow is often taken in by the steering wheel of the data (predicting "body weight" from "drinks sugar-free" is like predicting a downpour from the traffic in umbrellas: the umbrella is not the cause of the weather; it is the weather's effect — and, in passing: making the reverse direction speak coherently is itself a causal assertion: trying the case means trying the direction, not only whether there is a relation**)); **③ C causes A and B at once** (**a confounding variable — "children who eat breakfast get better marks": the family's daily round, the parents' level of education, and the very existence of a breakfast table are all accomplices ("the child whom someone wakes every day and for whom food is got ready" — it is his "being looked after" that is the principal offender: the weak point of the method of difference in Chapter 6 comes to court here: if you cannot find that "same field", you can only admit that you are watching a play, not running an experiment**)); **④ pure coincidence** (**once the sample size throws a party, someone is bound to share the frame: ice cream and drowning, Nike sales and the stock index, number of pages and the length of a book title — coincidence is not shameful; writing it down as causation is ("correlation is cheap: while it is cheap one treats it as free, and only when it is expensive does one discover that it carries a charge (take a coincidence into a theory, and the theory has to pay the tax for you: one wrong causal chain can deceive for decades — the autobiographical argument 'married early, therefore happy' is an offender permanently on the books**))). **An interrogation template** (**when they co-occur, first ask four questions: which came first, which after? if you move it, will it change? is there a third "it"? might it be nothing but chance? — once the four questions have been asked, most clickbait is closed on the spot**).
:::

::: definition A checklist of doubts: the defendant's lawful speech in defence {#def-huaiyi}
**Regression to the mean** (**after an extreme score, a natural return to the middle — the lifelong illusion of the flight instructor: praise a trainee and the next time they fall back; criticise them and the next time they improve; and so one comes to believe firmly that "criticism works, and praise is harmful" (the received account of the air-force instructor, as Kahneman tells it) — the truth is that the scores were going to drift toward the middle anyway: the pendulum's _return_ was taken for a force one had pushed oneself (education, management, Chinese medicine "taking effect", and a team's "change the manager and you change the knife" all belong on this list: any object screened out by an "extreme value" is, at the next step, very likely to become ordinary — "the share that a master was asked to exorcise then rose" — whose is the credit, the master's or regression to the mean? it takes a control group to divide the spoils**)); **selection bias** (**every restaurant one has been to slips easily onto the "must-eat list": the threshold is itself the cause (the cousin of the survivors in Chapter 6: the sample put itself forward, so of course the results play along**)); **publication bias** (**experiments that work go into the archive of papers; experiments that do not go into the drawer: a literature review is counting people under a spotlight — "the meta-analysis has produced a definite conclusion" calls for the question: how many corpses are in the drawer (negative results are not published, so the effect size naturally runs fat — the sunk-cost accounting of the research world**)); **placebo and expectation effects** (**"I took it and I feel lively": even the colour of the box is administering the drug — the philosophical point of the double-blind: even the subject's self-report cannot be believed, because a self-report will fall in with what is expected ("'I feel that it works' is the softest link in the chain of evidence — not a lie; a person simply falls in with his own expectation: this is the original sin of self-reported data, not a moral failing of the person who reports**).
:::

::: example Three news headlines: the court sits on the spot {#ex-kaiting}
**Court 1: "A study finds: people who exercise regularly are less depressed."** (the media's conclusion: exercise fights depression) **Court 2: "In cities where the number of people doing postgraduate study has risen, cafés have also increased — the knowledge economy is driving the consumption of coffee."** **Court 3: "After this vaccine came onto the market, reports of adverse reactions rose; it should therefore be withdrawn."** — For each, point out where the four exits are, which policy is missing, and what the strongest judgement is that can be handed down.
::: solution
**Court 1** (**the correct reading may really hold (the mechanism of exercise is credible: nerves, social contact, the daily round — several pathways — but in observational studies "people who are depressed do not exercise" is the standard reverse channel: depression first strips away the motive, and then strips away the running shoes; the strongest judgement: "exercise is negatively correlated with depression; the mechanism is reasonable; randomised controlled trials (some of which have already been done) show a moderate effect" — one may hand down "effective for some people", and one may not hand down the all-purpose sentence "exercise fights depression"; what is missing: the no-confounding policy has not been signed in full (people who exercise often also do not smoke, and have higher incomes)**); **Court 2** (**the co-occurrence is real, and the arrow is at its weakest: the size of the city, the universities, and the structure of the population are a ready-made C; a café is the shadow of a university town, not the direction in which a wallet flows — judgement: "the correlation holds; for the causation there is no evidence"; the case to be added: a comparison in the same city and the same stretch of time (the difference in the rate of growth of cafés around universities as against around industrial parks — difference-in-differences: economists who evaluate policy use this one move to borrow "the same field" back**)); **Court 3** (**the hardest of the three: the rise in reports of adverse reactions may be entirely ③ + intensified surveillance (only after it is on the market does anyone keep watch on the people who are given the vaccine: reporting rate ≠ incidence; this is the original sin of passive surveillance — the classic recipe for "vaccine hesitancy": use "reports have increased" to prove "the vaccine is harmful", and then use the control data on "not being vaccinated" to prove that it is harmless — both sets of data are true, and the judgements are the opposite: what is missing is the denominator**); **the strongest judgement: "active surveillance and age-matched controls are needed; the evidence at present does not support withdrawal, and it does support closer follow-up" — note the character of the language of this kind of judgement: it does not adjudicate feeling; it adjudicates only what data should be gathered next (the end of causal reasoning is often not a conclusion, but a better plan for obtaining evidence**).
:::
:::

::: intuition An experiment is an artificial future {#def-shiyang2}
**Why a randomised control is the gold standard** (**randomisation is the only machine that "makes the same field with dice": before they enter, the two groups are statistically equivalent — and so a difference after they enter can come only from the one variable that was fiddled with — "aspirin and heart attacks, the experiments with small cash grants, the randomised pilots of educational interventions: in the second half of the twentieth century, 'random' became the banknote-printer of causal knowledge (and, at the same time, a machine for giving offence: where an experiment cannot be done (race, poverty and wealth, climate), causal knowledge can walk only on legs borrowed from quasi-experiments and natural experiments**). **When you can experiment, experiment; when you cannot, learn to label** (**call an observational conclusion "correlation + a conjecture about the mechanism", and call an experimental conclusion "causation + a limit on the scope" — the schedule of tax rates for language: the closer you come to causation, the more tax of caution you must pay (the only thing exempt is mathematics)**). **An ancient near relation** (**the tradition of 效案 ('cases of efficacy') in Chinese medicine and Mill's method of difference look at each other across the ocean: without randomisation, the comparative wisdom of 同病异治、异病同治 ('different treatments for the same illness, the same treatment for different illnesses') is still struggling its way toward the control of variables — the lesson is not to laugh at the ancients, but to see clearly: the idea of control is to be found everywhere; what has always been lacking is only an institutionalised means of carrying it out (today's A/B test has institutionalised it down to every time you click "next page"**).
:::

::: history The modernisation of the aesthetics of causation {#sec-xiandaihua}
**1748** Hume, *An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (**causation is not discovered by reason; it is a habitual expectation: necessity is taken back out of the object and stored in the mind — the causal branch of the customarism of Chapter 6 (this judgement forced out Kant's a priori counter-attack, and a hundred years of litigation in the philosophy of causation in the twentieth century**); **1747** Lind's scurvy trial (**twelve patients at sea, divided into six groups and given different additions — known to history as the earliest recorded controlled comparison by groups: the birth certificate of causal knowledge came not much later than its philosophical defence, and in its whole life it never won the philosophical lawsuit: the experiment wins all the same, because its judge is whether the patients lived**); **1854** Snow's map of cholera (**the Broad Street pump: without relying on germ theory, without relying on a randomised trial, the case was settled by a map and by "the water that never appeared in court" — a textbook performance of the natural experiment and of the method of residues (microbiology hurried in afterwards and supplied the mechanism for the judgement: epidemiology solves the case first, and the laboratory obtains the evidence afterwards — the division of labour between the two legs, causation and mechanism, on the spot**); **1948** the randomised controlled trial enters clinical trials (**the received milestone of the streptomycin trial: from then on the double-blind is the coming-of-age rite for the approval of a drug — the twentieth century's "bureaucratisation of causation": if you want to judge causation, first get through the forms**); **1950s–60s** the Hill criteria and the revival of the philosophy of causation (**the trial of the century, "smoking causes lung cancer": there was no randomised trial (one cannot have people draw lots for who is to smoke), and the mechanism was incomplete at the time; Toulmin and Hill each gave standards for "settling the case amid the probable" (the Hill criteria: nine questions — strength, consistency, dose, time, mechanism, and the rest — like the score-sheet of a detective novel, not a theorem; but tick enough boxes on the sheet, and the tobacco industry loses the case**); **1970s–the present** **structural causal models and the "causal revolution" (the received account of Pearl's *Causality*: correlation, intervention and counterfactual, three levels, each assigned a mathematics of its own; instrumental variables made the "borrowed leg" in observational data into a proper prosthesis) — "the 'method of difference' in the logic textbook grew a do-operator: a question from agronomy became a Nobel Prize (the causal-inference territory of the economics prize of 2021**).
:::

::: warning Four pits of causation
**The slippery causal chain charges a fee** (**"permit A, and there will be B, C and D, all the way to the end of the world" — the strength of the chain = the weakest link, while the rhetoric charges a ten-link fee on the first link: the way to try it is to ask the probability step by step and to mark the price in the open ("0.9 at each step, 0.35 at the tenth: a slippery slope is not impossible; it is not free: compound interest has to be calculated**)); **the craze for a single cause** (**the investigation of an accident, a fall in marks, the fall of a dynasty: the human brain prefers to have it finished in one sentence — "the root cause" is often another name for "we cannot be bothered to draw up a list" (the family of sufficient causes, the family of necessary causes, the difference-making cause: one and the same event, three ways of asking, three answers (the temptation of the simplified version of the "thousand-year ledger" of Lesson 15 comes to court here**)); **the denominator gone missing** (**"reports of deaths have increased", "complaints have soared", "some disease is highly prevalent": a numerator with no denominator is rhetoric, not data (a preview of the lesson on charts in Chapter 8: first ask for the total, the base, and the intensity of surveillance**)); **superstition about mechanism** (**the pit in the other direction: "if one cannot state the mechanism, then it is ineffective" — the mechanism by which aspirin relieves pain arrived only decades later, and when Snow settled the case he did not know the germ: a mechanism is an item that adds marks and an item that explains, not a necessary condition of causation; likewise, "the mechanism sounds reasonable" cannot rescue reverse causation either: both have to be guarded against**)
:::

::: exercise A post-mortem on three sentences {level=1}
For each of the following assertions write a **post-mortem report of one hundred words** (direction of the arrow / candidate confounders / the policy that is missing / the strongest conclusion that can be handed down): ① "the longer one spends scrolling short videos, the worse one's attention"; ② "cities that use our App handle complaints faster"; ③ "people who keep up a diary are happier".
::: solution
A sample for marking: ① **both directions are reasonable (people whose attention is poor are fonder of low-resistance stimulation; it is also possible that the rhythm of the editing does the harm — the direction is not settled; confounders: sleep, age, depression; the strongest: "a negative correlation related to dose; the direction waits on a longitudinal study")**; ② **sales-page causation (the customers select themselves: only a city that is well managed can afford to buy / is willing to buy; the denominator is missing: what is the definition of handling things fast or slowly? the strongest: "the decision to purchase is correlated with fast cities")**; ③ **self-selection by the happy + recall bias (happy people remember good things better: the measuring instrument is itself a confounder; keeping a diary is the only intervention that is "almost free", and a small pilot can be run, but the control is hard to blind (one knows whether one wrote or not: the honesty tax on a psychological trial**).
:::
:::

::: exercise Design the cheapest judgement {level=2}
Turn ① into **an experiment or a quasi-experiment that you can afford**: the ethical and technical obstacles to random assignment, a natural experiment one might borrow (a version update? the rhythm of the term?), the traps of pre-registration and of multiple comparisons — write the design, and the "upper bound of the conclusion it can support".
::: solution
Marking: **assignment to groups, and the dose, can be controlled** (**one cannot ban mobile phones; one can limit the time slots, or switch push notifications off — the intervention has to be capable of being carried out**); **blinding of the measurement** (**attention is measured by scores on a task, not by a self-rating: a self-report will fall in with what is expected (item 4 of the checklist of doubts, put into practice**); **pre-registration** (**declare the outcome measure first, so as to prevent the drawer effect (the personal edition of publication bias: a person will quietly report only the one that came out significant**); **the upper bound of the conclusion** (**a short-term intervention in a single population: "switching push notifications off for two weeks can improve scores on an attention task" — extrapolating to "short videos have ruined a generation" is still taking out a loan: the upper bound of a conclusion is written on the sample's household register (the causal branch of the public-square law of Chapter 6**).
:::
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the tax on causation {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand words, assessing "**modern society has turned 'causes' into an assertion on which tax has to be paid: randomised trials, the denominator, pre-registration and the scrutiny of mechanism are the tax office — the evader settles the tax with 'everyone thinks so', and so the arena of opinion becomes an underground bank for causation**". You must call on at least six items: the **three policies**, **at least three uses of the four exits**, **regression to the mean**, **the denominator gone missing**, **the place of the Hill criteria**, and **"experiment when you can / label when you cannot"**; and adjudicate: **whether it is fair that "only people who have run a randomised trial are fit to speak about causal knowledge" — between the experts' monopoly and the public's right to know, how the tax rate of language ought to be set**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**policies = a checklist for producing evidence; exits = the interrogation template; regression to the mean = the antidote to the superstition of management; the denominator = the household register of the data; Hill = a checklist for settling a case amid the probable, not a theorem; labelling = the tax rate of language**); the adjudication (**for reference: the argument for a professional right to speak — a causal assertion is highly technical, and a non-expert is easily carried along by the beat of "the number of reports" (the scene in Court 3); the argument for a democratic right to know — policy must be tried by the public; set the tax rate too high and it = an experts' monopoly; in history, closure of the "vaccine safety committee" type has likewise overturned; the marking sits with a compromise (translating the conclusion into language the public can examine = the duty of communicating science; open data and pre-registration = turning the tax office into a transparent workshop: "certification cannot take the place of accountability; it can only change the currency of accountability"**)); if the conclusion can be written "**to say 'I do not know' is not failure; it is making out the bill for the tax on causation in the right place — what a logic course teaches, by the time it has taught through to the end, is a restraint of language: knowing which sentence has to pay tax, which sentence is evading tax, and which sentence does not deserve to be entered on the books at all**", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
Filed from the hearing: the three policies (**temporal order, concomitant variation, no confounding — the third is the most expensive**), the four exits (**forward, reverse, common cause, coincidence: once the four questions have been asked, the clickbait is closed on the spot**), the speech for the defence (**regression to the mean, selection bias, publication bias, the placebo**), the experiment (**dice make the same field: when you cannot experiment, learn to label**), the criteria (**Hill's nine questions: like the score-sheet of a detective novel, not a theorem**). The method in one sentence: **before you judge causation, first take out the policies; if you cannot afford them, write in plain words "it is only a correlation"**. The court of causation adjourns; the next chapter goes outside the scene of the crime: how to assess that share of information in which you never took any part.
:::
