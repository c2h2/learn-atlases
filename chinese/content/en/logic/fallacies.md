This chapter is a **dictionary**, not a lesson text — every accident from the previous eight chapters is entered here under its heading, and the regulars who never came on stage are filled in as well. How to use it: **first learn to describe, then learn to name; a name is useful only when a repair has to be called up** (**being able to recognise it without being able to name it is a skill; being able to name it without being able to repair it is a catchphrase**).

## Definition: two kinds of defect {#def-leibie}

::: definition Machine defects and content smuggling {#def-lianglei}
**Formal fallacies = machine defects** (**deductive derailments: affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, an undistributed middle term, the four-term syllogism — their errors have nothing to do with the content; put any words in their place and they still crash: the machine-inspection items of Chapters 4 and 5**); **informal fallacies = content smuggling** (**the format shows no error; the error is in the material: false premises, irrelevance, vagueness, presuppositions carried in by stealth — the list is much longer, because the varieties of smuggling equal the varieties of language (**the main battlefield of the teaching is here: machine defects have a decision table one can consult, while the smuggler depends on the experience of the patrol — this chapter is the patrol manual**)**). **One master principle** (**most fallacies are not evils invented out of nothing, but counterfeits of good methods: giving an example is a good method, and the straw man and hasty generalisation are high-grade imitations of it; analogy is a good method, and false analogy is its relabelled imitation; inside the appeal to authority there lives legitimate expert opinion — the greatest gain from the study of fallacies: to recognise, beside every scam, the shop that is trading honestly (the negative lesson corresponding to Lesson 15's "the way a school of thought is laid off is by becoming common sense": the way a fallacy takes up its post is by passing itself off as a technique)**).
:::

## The roster {#def-mingdan}

::: definition The relevance family (evidence divorced from the thesis) {#def-xiangguan}
**The three brothers of personal attack** (**the abusive type ("What does he even amount to?"), the circumstantial type ("You are the landlord, so of course you oppose a rise in the rent" — note: an interest affects only the weight, and does not overturn the proposition: a landlord, too, can be right about rent), the you-too type (tu quoque — "You smoke, so don't tell me to give it up": that the person urging is unworthy is not the same as the content of the urging being false)**); **the red herring** (**muddy the water and change the quarry: "Why don't you mention XX, then?" = an application to adjourn of the "and what about you?" kind (an old account from Chapter 8)**); **appeal to fear / pity / intimidation** (**sympathy advertisements, threatening warnings, "Raise a child to provide for your old age — have you the heart?" — tears are the receipt for persuasion, not the invoice for an argument (Chapter 1); how to tell them apart: does it change the inventory of facts, or your heartbeat?**); **appeal to renown / toil / hardship** (**"He hasn't had it easy either", "I have been doing this for thirty years": what has been invested is not a deposit of correctness (the rhetorical branch of Chapter 7's sunk cost)**); **appeal to nature** (**"purely natural = harmless": aconite is purely natural too; "only the man-made is harmful" — not one poisonous mushroom is man-made (the health-preservation edition of the naturalistic fallacy: taking "comes from" for "is better than" — a case of the same kind as Lesson 9's ethics, which teaches that an "is" does not yield an "ought")**); **appeal to tradition / novelty** (**"used for a thousand years" and "the latest technology" are two faces of the same counterfeit coin: time does not cast a vote, and length does not inspect for quality**); **appeal to the crowd** (**descriptive public opinion ("a majority of voters support the policy") is legitimate evidence; the prescriptive kind ("three hundred million people are using it, so it is good") is treating the size of the print run as a certificate of authenticity (what sales prove is sales)**); **appeal to authority out of bounds** (**the legitimate and the illegitimate differ by only one "domain" (Chapter 1): a nuclear physicist endorsing mineral water — the range of the professional title has gone outside its circle**); **appeal to ignorance** (**"cannot be proved safe = dangerous" and "cannot be proved harmful = safe" are two directions of the same contrarian quibble: no one may move into a vacuum of evidence (in common use for the two vaccine cases and the two cases of illness caused by staying up late)**); **shifting the burden** (**"You cannot refute it, so what I say is right": the burden of proof travels with the assertion, not with the volume of the voice**).
:::

::: definition The presupposition family (a secret stash hidden in the premises) {#def-yushe}
**Complex question** (**"Have you stopped cheating?" — answer "yes" or answer "no", and either way you have first admitted the guilt: the presupposition has been smuggled through the security check (the repair: do not answer; first hand the paper back: "This question presupposes X; I reject that presupposition" — in court it is called "objection: the question itself contains an assumption")**); **false dichotomy** (**"either raise the price or lower the quality" — the third option has merely not been printed on the menu (first ask: who drew the list up, and who was left off it — the marketing edition of Chapter 2's "excluded-middle juxtaposition")**); **begging the question / circular argument** (**"this shop is trustworthy because it has many good reviews, and it has many good reviews because the shop is trustworthy": a loan at zero interest (Chapter 2); but note that a good hermeneutic circle ("understanding the parts depends on the whole, and understanding the whole depends on the parts") is not begging the question: the viciousness lies in ensnaring itself on the spot and refusing any test from outside (the narrow gate of Lesson 15's hermeneutics registers here)**); **fallacy of accident** (**"the rule is that animals may not be brought into the station; a guide dog is an animal; ∴ a guide dog may not enter the station": the purpose of a rule is not written in its letter — the articles of a law are the skeleton, and the purpose is the joints: a "strictness" that has only the skeleton is a mannequin imitating the human form**); **shift of centre** (**ignotum per ignotius: explaining what is unfamiliar by something still more unfamiliar — "he is asleep because the '寐性' ('sleep-nature') in his body has been activated" (the joke of the doctor in Molière: disguising a mechanism with a noun — twins with circular definition: passing a synonym off as an explanation)**).
:::

::: definition The strength and probability family (loans issued in excess) {#def-qiangdu}
**Hasty generalisation** (**a default on the law of the square (Chapter 6): "my neighbour smoked and lived to ninety" — buying at the price of an anecdote and selling at the price of a law**); **the three-piece set of biased samples** (**survivors, self-selection, volunteers (the joint wanted notice of Chapters 6 and 8)**); **slippery slope** (**pricing by compound interest: 0.9 at each step, 0.35 at the tenth — a slippery slope is not impossible; it is not free (Chapter 7)**); **false cause** (**post hoc: the cock's crow and the sunrise; and a fraudulent claim upon "regression to the mean" (the instructor illusion, Chapter 7)**); **the gambler's fallacy** (**the coin comes up heads seven times running, and the eighth "is due to be tails": independent events do not keep accounts — note that it and regression to the mean are twins, and that they face opposite ways: the gambler thinks the short run will revert (roulette), the instructor thinks a long-run trend has been rewritten by personal effort (marks) — one adds drama to randomness, the other steals the credit from randomness**); **the hot-hand fallacy** (**three heads in a row, "this coin is in good form today": treating the random as a character (the shared dialect of the playing field and the stock forum)**); **base-rate neglect** (**"positive = has the disease", forgetting the prevalence (Chapter 7's quotation for the medical examination)**); **composition** (**each person saving money = the whole country richer: Keynes's paradox of thrift — the macro is not a photocopier of the micro (outgoings and income are one another's accounts: what you save is precisely what someone else receives less of)**); **division** (**"everyone in the best line-up is the best player, so the best line-up is the best at each position bolted together"? Not necessarily — "every chapter of this work is good, so the whole work is good" does not hold either: good parts do not make a good machine — structure is itself a property: the whole is not the addition table of the sum of the parts (a return visit to Lesson 15's "hierarchy of levels")**); **the perfect-solution fallacy** (**"your plan has problems too, so it is useless": a covert swap of comparative rank and an absolute standard — the condition on which plan A wins is often merely "one fewer way of dying than B" (the perpetual-motion machine of political debate: how to see through it — ask the other side "what cost is your alternative willing to pay?", and level the comparison table again)**).
:::

::: example A set of contrarian quibbles: eighteen in a row, to classify and repair {#ex-shibalian}
An extract from a comments section (numbered 1–12): ① "The professor supports raising tuition fees? Of course he supports it; he doesn't pay them." ② "You cannot prove that the price rise is harmless, so that shows it is harmful." ③ "Either tuition fees go up, or the school closes." ④ "Tuition fees went up last year, and the employment rate rose instead, which shows that raising the price promotes employment." ⑤ "Everyone else's children pay; why shouldn't our family pay?" ⑥ "This study is from twenty years ago; who still believes it?" ⑦ "This plan of yours is too ideal; if it cannot reach one hundred per cent, it is waste paper." ⑧ "Someone like me on three thousand a month — what fairness in education is there for me to discuss with you?" ⑨ "So long as the school is given autonomy, it can be run well; once autonomy is given the marks will be good, and once the marks are good the students will come." ⑩ "People who oppose the price rise are all people who do not care about rural children." ⑪ "The data show a rise of 'as much as' 120%. Terrifying." ⑫ "You say the basis of counting is faulty? Then you first prove that the data have no fault." — **Task: for each item state the charge (a number from the roster, or one you coin yourself, will do), and give one sentence of "wording after repair" (the steel-man version)**.
::: solution
① **Circumstantial personal attack** (repair: "His identity affects the weight; it does not overturn the argument. Ask him to publish the itemised budget, and let the data speak"); ② **appeal to ignorance** (repair: "The burden of proof is on our side: our evidence is …; absence of a counter-proof is not confirmation"); ③ **false dichotomy** (repair: "There are further options: structural adjustment, targeted subsidies, compressing costs — assess the cost of the three, then talk"); ④ **false cause + no denominator** (repair: "What was the employment rate, over the same period, of institutions in the same region that did not raise their fees?"); ⑤⑤ **appeal to the crowd** (repair: "That everyone has paid does not constitute a reason why one ought to pay; please state the cost per student"); ⑥ **appeal to tradition (the reverse: time does not vote)** (repair: "If an old study's method can be replicated, the conclusion is still valid; please look at replication studies of recent years"); ⑦ **the perfect-solution fallacy** (repair: "This plan's coverage is 70%, and its cost is X; the coverage and the cost of the plan compared with it are …"); ⑧ **circumstantial nickname + shift of centre** (repair: "My income has nothing to do with whether the price rise is right or wrong; please bring the discussion back to the budget table"); ⑨ **one-sided chain (the optimistic edition of the slippery slope)** (repair: "What is the conditional probability of each link? Where is the weakest link?"); ⑩ **stigmatising personal attack + false dichotomy** (a specimen of the compound fallacy: "defining opponents as bad people" is the cheapest art of solidarity — repair: "A plan that cares about rural children can have a version that does not raise the price; please look at the subsidy clauses"); ⑪ **missing base** (repair: "From 5 yuan to 11 yuan: the absolute figure, the proportion, and a comparable basis of counting"); ⑫ **shifting the burden** (repair: "Our side has already published the basis of counting and the raw data (link); let the doubting side point out the anomalous points: the duty to check cannot be contracted out as a whole package"); **overall comment** (**of the twelve sentences, compound fallacies account for three — in reality fallacies do not offend alone; habitual offenders gang up ("a personal attack packaged as a dilemma", "an appeal to ignorance wearing the rhetoric of data"): the list is a dictionary, and the faulty sentence is a text: one consults the dictionary in order to read the text**).
:::

::: intuition Instructions for the use of fallacies (the part on oneself) {#int-duiji}
**The first danger of the study of fallacies is enforcing the law upon other people** (**the person who has memorised the roster is the first to catch "the pleasure of accusing": a label on every post, a format-audit of every person — an accusation of fallacy itself needs an argument (Chapter 1): it must first restate the other side's argument correctly; if the restatement cannot be produced, the accusation is void**). **The second danger is exempting oneself** (**"I was only drawing a comparison" — rhetoric is not excused because it calls itself a figure: the harm to policy done by a false analogy never consults the author's note (Lesson 15: "consequences are not the dressing room of intentions")**). **The third, which is also the method of proper use**: **treat the list as a check-up package and not as a weapon** (**ask yourself four sentences: might my conclusion be only what I hope is true (confirmation)? Which square is my sample from (geography)? How many policies has my causation taken out (causation)? Can I restate the strongest version of the side opposite me (steel man)? — speak only after all four have passed: the most honest use of the study of fallacies is on one's own late-night draft**).
:::

::: history Two thousand years of the study of bad reviews {#sec-chaping}
**4th century BC**, Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations* (《辩谬篇》) (**humankind's first list of fallacies: thirteen kinds, divided into two great classes, "related to language / not related" — the pioneering act of turning "losing without knowing why" into a catalogue**); **the Warring States in China**, Xunzi 荀子, 'Rectifying Names' (《正名》) (**the "three confusions": 惑于用名以乱实、惑于用实以乱名、惑于用情欲以乱是非 — 'confused by using names to disorder realities, confused by using realities to disorder names, and confused by using feelings and desires to disorder right and wrong' (Our translation.) — a study of fallacies divided three ways, into name, reality and feeling; the eastern version of the same age as *Sophistical Refutations* (entered on the shadow side of Chapter 2's "rectification of names")**) + the Mohist discriminations along the line of *Xiaoqu* 小取 ('Choosing the Lesser') (**the common account: the Mohists could already point out the trap in the *móu* 侔 form (the trap in inference by analogy and by coordination: the scene where a *móu* form of the type “白马，马也；乘白马，乘马也” fails — 'A white horse is a horse; to ride a white horse is to ride a horse.' (Our translation.)) — the Warring States intuition about "trickery by sentence pattern"**); **1690**, Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (《人类理解》) (**the "four methods of argument": appeal to authority, to reason, to emotion, to private interest (the embryonic naming of ad verecundiam / ad rationem / ad ignorantiam / ad hominem) — the household-registration office of the modern family of "appeals"**); **1841**, Bentham, *Principles of Logic* (《逻辑学原理》) (**an industrial inspection of "letting a name stand in for an argument": slogan-words of the "virtue" and "nature" kind — first check what work it did for you, then ask what truth it spoke**); **1851**, Schopenhauer, *The Art of Argument* (《论证的艺术》) (**"fifty tricks": a dark textbook written specially for "even if refuted, still do not admit defeat" — the first person to read the study of fallacies as a wrestling guide: he grants that people contend for face, and not only over true and false**); **the later 19th century** (**textbooks taking "fallacies" as their title come onto the market: the list turns from an appendix of philosophy into an independent commodity — the first lesson of critical thinking in the age of self-study**); **the 1950s–70s**, the informal-logic movement (**the teaching of fallacies returns to student essays, editorials and advertisements (the front of Chapter 1) + "critical thinking" is institutionalised as a general-education course**); **the present** (**the debate over the "recontextualisation" of fallacies: many a "fallacy", in argumentation theory, is a legitimate probing move (an appeal to authority, in ordinary life, is often a rational choice) — the wake of Toulmin and Perelman: to say, absolutely, that it is wrong is often to blame it unjustly; under what conditions it is legitimate is what the maturity of knowledge consists in (the latest edition of the list carries the small print of "conditions of application")**).
:::

::: warning Four pits in using the list
**Bombing with names** (**"Slippery slope! Straw man! Personal attack!" — sticking on a label is a pseudo-refutation at zero cost (the iron rule of Chapter 1: restate first, then accuse)**); **the illusion of the universal** (**memorise the list and suppose that you can think? What the study of fallacies gives you is the format of an autopsy report, not a method for rearing a living creature: a good argument does not hold by "being without fallacy"; it holds by its premises and its support (the list is a blacklist and produces no work (a warning of the same kind as the eight prohibitions of Lesson 15)**)**); context-blindness** (**the same move has a different degree of legitimacy in different genres: "hasty generalisation" in literature is called typification ("a novel uses one case to write the situation of a group": in literature it is a craft, in statistics it is a fallacy — ask the genre first, then issue the ticket)**); **over-defence** (**treating all rhetoric as fraud: rhetoric is the art of persuasion, and a fallacy is a counterfeit of persuasion — telling them apart needs aesthetic judgement, not only a list (Lesson 15's "theory is spectacles, not a filter": the list, too, is spectacles; wear them too long and do not come to see the world as a workbook of wrong answers)**).
:::

::: exercise The fallacy double act {level=1}
In pairs: **A, using an assigned fallacy (drawn by lot), writes three sentences of contrarian quibble; B is allowed to do only one thing — restate, as a steel man, the strongest version of what the other said**; then swap attack and defence: A admits or adds something, and B accuses only then. After three rounds write a review of fifty characters: **when it has been "correctly restated", does your quibble still smell sweet?**
::: solution
Marking: **the restatement must be more persuasive than the original wording** (**the coach's standard: make a bystander feel that "the other side, in fact, has a point" — until the restatement is finished, the accuse button is disabled**); what the review tests (**a contrarian quibble generally loses its value at the moment it is understood: the fuel of a straw man is being misunderstood, not being understood — this is field evidence for the steel-man principle**).
:::
:::

::: exercise An autopsy on one's own piece of writing {level=2}
Dig out a passage of argument you wrote three months ago (homework, a post, or a script for a debate will all do), and **carry out a full autopsy with the roster**: mark at least five places (including at least one from the strength family and one from the presupposition family), and write a repaired draft that **does not change the conclusion, and changes only the reasoning**.
::: solution
Marking: **keep the autopsy separate from the repair** (**for many people a "repair" is in fact an overturning of the conclusion — what this lesson tests is this: the conclusion may have been right, and only the wrong rope was used at the time — "a right conclusion + the wrong reasoning" is the commonest private stock (it makes a person lose, the next time, on a thing that is right)**); **exemption from public execution** (**execute sentence only on yourself: this lesson does not require old pieces to be exchanged — the first lesson of the study of fallacies is the management of permissions**).
:::
:::

::: exercise Short essay: a fallacy is a counterfeit of persuasion {level=3}
Write six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing "**beside every kind of fallacy a legitimate shop stands open: next door to giving examples is exemplification, next door to authority is expert opinion, next door to intimidation is the assessment of risk — the cost of human learning is too low, and only that gives counterfeits a market**". You must call on: **the dichotomy of machine defects and content smuggling**, **the principle that "a fallacy is a counterfeit of a good method"**, **complex question and false dichotomy**, **the twin relationship of the gambler's fallacy and regression to the mean**, **the fallacy of composition (the paradox of thrift)**, **at least three of the four pits of the list**, and **the principle that "the study of fallacies is for oneself"** — at least seven items; and adjudicate: **does teaching a list of fallacies mass-produce "contrarian quibblers who can only pull the stage down"? — a list of the side-effects of education in critical thinking, and the remedies**.
::: solution
Marking points: the seven mechanisms (**the two kinds of defect; the counterfeit principle = the greatest gain; the security check on presuppositions; the mirror-image relation of the twins; the macro does not photocopy the micro; context-blindness and genre; the check-up on oneself**); the adjudication of side-effects (**a reference route: the side-effects really do exist — bombing with names, enforcing the law on others, a slide into cynicism ("every argument is a set routine" → trusting no argument); the remedy is not to delete the list, but to change the way it is taught: from "identifying fallacies" to "constructing the strongest argument + giving a list of conditional legitimacy" (the Perelman route: first explain under what conditions an appeal to authority is rational, then explain when it goes out of bounds — "to teach the gun you must first teach the rules of the range: issuing the gun and not issuing the rules is precisely what produces contrarian quibblers")**); if the conclusion can be written "**a fallacy is not stupidity, but a shortcut abused: the brain's heuristics save electricity in ordinary times and leak electricity at the critical point — the use of the list is not to make you clever, but to have you, in the most expensive place, switch into a manual free-gear, and remember: the next time you slip into neutral, first check whether you yourself are on a slope**", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
The manual, filed: the dichotomy (**formal = machine defects, informal = content smuggling**), the counterfeit principle (**that legitimate shop beside every scam**), the four jurisdictions (**relevance, presupposition, strength, probability: habitual offenders gang up, and offend in company**), the part on oneself (**the four-sentence check-up: confirmation, geography, the insurance policy, the steel man**), the history ( *Sophistical Refutations* and Xunzi's "three confusions" are of the same age; Schopenhauer's wrestling guide; the small print, in our own time, of "conditions of application"). One sentence of method: describe first, then name; restate first, then accuse; inspect yourself first, then sign the ticket. The dictionary is closed; the last chapter goes back two thousand years: *míngbiàn* 名辩 ('names and disputation') — China's own tradition of logic.
:::
