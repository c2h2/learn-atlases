---
title: "Evaluating information"
---

The first seven chapters examined "the argument in your hands". This chapter examines **words you had no part in producing** — **99% of an adult's knowledge comes from testimony (what other people say), so the last lesson of logic is not a technique of doubt, but the economics of trust: you cannot check everything, and must therefore learn when to check, and how much credit to extend**.

## Definition: reliance is the default, and doubt is a budget {#def-yuzhang}

::: definition The subcontracting of epistemology {#def-fenbao}
**"Doubt everything" is, epistemologically, a failing mark, not a virtue** (**most of your "truths" about the world — that Antarctica exists, the principles of vaccines, last year's GDP — can rest only on subcontracts with credible sources** (**testimony is one legitimate source of knowledge: in the history of philosophy it was for a long time buried alongside the fantasy of "reduction to first-hand experience"; in reality it is the load-bearing wall of civilisation** (**"all knowledge must be verified for oneself" = an ascetic epistemology in which everyone reinvents the wheel: the real lesion is not trust, but choosing the wrong object of trust and the wrong amount**))); **and so the true question of this chapter**: **doubt is an expensive asset, to be allocated by "the cost of getting it wrong"** (**a stock tip that is wrong loses money, and is worth checking to the bottom; the inside story of a celebrity divorce, if wrong, costs only a topic of conversation, and to take it seriously is to be taken in** (the cognitive version of Chapter 7's "missing denominator": your attention is that total budget)). **The honest posture of a society with a division of labour** (**is neither "I believe all of it" nor "I believe none of it", but "I know which grade I am trusting, and at which link I can escalate the check" — make trust a gearbox with gears, not a switch that is fully on or fully off**.
:::

::: definition Four questions for a source {#def-siwen}
①**Who said it** (**a named institution, a verified individual, an anonymous "informed source", or "netizens say" — anonymity is not an original sin; what matters is where the cost of anonymity is assigned: "an informed source says" shifts the cost of the assertion onto the reader and leaves the benefit with the source — whoever is let off the bill is the one who benefits**); ②**on what grounds it is said** (**first-hand research, an official bulletin, or "a friend of mine at the hospital" — Chapter 6's law of the square here has an institutional version: every source has its own "square" — the National Health Commission's square is full of patients, and the gym's square is full of muscle**); ③**to whom it is said** (**a thesis defence, a financial report, an obituary and a job advertisement are all true speech; they have only put on a uniform: genre decides the filter (a return to the rhetoric of Chapter 8) — "the thanks in a paper's acknowledgements" and "the risk section of a prospectus" are two texts of one origin, not equally to be trusted**); ④**what was not said** (**silence is the second document a source hands you: a share-price flash that reports the rise and not the base, a promotional film that shows only the champion's trophy — "selective truth is, of all lies, the kind that best survives checking: every word of it is true"**).
:::

## Evidence grades and citation inflation {#def-dengji}

::: definition The pyramid and its use in Chinese {#def-jinzi}
**The evidence pyramid of evidence-based medicine** (**apex: systematic reviews / meta-analyses (the trials of the world analysed over again as data); the middle: randomised controlled trials; the base: expert opinion, case reports, experiments in vitro** (the philosophical content of this scale: the further down, the fewer people will sign a policy against confounding — the marker of Chapter 7 has changed uniform and appeared in the hospital)). **Everyday use in Chinese is not reciting the pyramid, but learning its principle of "re-analysis first"** (**"there is a study" and "a review of the studies" are not first-grade evidence; the distance from "animal experiments show" to your mouth = four jumps, in vitro → mouse → clinical → dose** (the metamorphosis of science news: each jump is eaten by the headline — the standard death of frauds of the "chocolate helps you lose weight" kind: the ethanol extract from a mouse is translated into a snack for humans)). **Citation inflation and circular citation** (**"according to foreign media" on three platforms is in fact one news agency disguised as three people, a plural hallucination; a reposted figure grows as it travels ("preliminary statistics" to "official data show" is only three reposts apart) — the act of checking: walk back along the citations until you touch the first-hand source** (SIFT's "trace back to the original material": the truth is often on the first page; it is only that no one gets through to the first page)).
:::

::: definition Three checks on numbers, and the accent of charts {#def-shuzi}
**First, check the denominator** (**"death reports have increased", "complaints have soared" — with no denominator, this is rhetoric** (an old debt from Chapter 7, paid anew)); **second, check the base** (**"up 200%" is from 1 to 3; "down by half" is from 4 sets to 2 — a percentage is a relative number, and a relative number cut loose from its base is running naked** ("experts say a certain new method cuts the recurrence rate by 50%": from two in ten thousand down to one in ten thousand, or from six tenths down to three tenths? the same 50%, two different lives)); **third, check the scope** (**the twin fraud of percentage points vs per cent: "support fell by 2 percentage points" is broadcast as "fell by 2%"; the statistical scope of the "unemployment rate" (who counts as "currently looking for work") is itself a policy variable** (the stipulated definitions of Chapter 2, enforced on the spot: the scope is the law)). **The study of chart accents** (**a truncated vertical axis makes a slope of 3% look like a cliff; a dual-axis chart lays two unrelated curves one over the other into "divine synchrony"; drawing bubble area by the radius produces area × π²** ("a chart is a translation of the data: a translation may have an accent, and it may also be a miscarriage of justice — three steps in examining a chart: see where the axis starts, see the units, and see whether the legend is telling a second story")).
:::

::: example A full autopsy of a long group-chat post {#ex-qunliao}
Take a long piece in the manner of 《震惊！九成专家呼吁废除英语高考》 ("Shocking! Nine out of ten experts call for abolishing English in the gaokao." (Our translation.)) and give it a **five-step autopsy**: ① check the title against the body; ② trace the sources back; ③ the three checks on numbers; ④ check the pictures and the data files; ⑤ lateral reading (open two further tabs). Produce an **autopsy report**, and for each step state "what disease it is most likely to find" — and **which disease is enough to abandon the whole piece, and which only deducts marks for impression**.
::: solution
①**Title and body out of joint** (**the body says "a survey of teachers at a certain school shows"; the title upgrades this to "nine out of ten experts call for": the sample goes from 46 people to "the world of experts": the square swells to a province (Chapter 6) — the disease: an upgrading title = a credit warning for the whole piece; one disease counts as three**); ②**the trace back is broken** (**"according to foreign media", nested three layers deep, disappears into a local education forum: the far tip of citation inflation; "experts say", with no quotation marks, no work-unit and no name, = a naked quotation** (the on-the-spot version of "anonymity shifts the cost")); ③**a disease of the numbers** (**"people who hold that English is useless make up 78%": the denominator is "voters", and the voters are "people who saw this post" — the sampling frame caves in; "average wages rise after abolition": correlation and reverse causation take off together** (all four exits of Chapter 7 are hit)); ④**an old wound in the graphic** (**the picture used is an old image from another news item in 2014: a picture's function as "proof of being present" outweighs its function as information — an old picture put to a new use = emotion grafted across**); ⑤**lateral reading** (**open two further pages: search the key sentence of the title and it turns out to be a 2019 rumour in a new skin; search 英语 高考 废除 ("English", "gaokao", "abolish") and the authoritative scope is entirely different — the verdict: the factual layer is scrap (broken chain + numbers + old picture, three diseases at once); the layer of opinion is kept as "a sample of what one group of people demands": as evidence of an opinion the piece will do; as evidence of fact it is waste** ("it cheated you of one fact, but it exposed a real emotion: the last column of an autopsy report must record how much truth the deceased had — that is exactly why it was so good at cheating")).
:::
:::

::: intuition The platform age: the industrialisation of relevance {#int-pingtai}
**Recommendation systems replace "important" with "you will look" as the standard for taking something on** (**gatekeeping in the age of editors was a triple filter (commercial, political, professional); the platform age replaces the filter with your past — and so the public agenda is sliced into 800 million private agendas** (the social version of Chapter 7's law of relevance: the platform does not care about true or false; it cares about dwell time: true and false things happen, sometimes, to sell well, and that is luck for the facts, not an institution of the facts)); **the transmission coefficient of anger** (**the agreed received view of research across platforms: high-arousal emotions (anger and fear) are forwarded more readily than a calm statement — it is not that human nature has changed; the distribution channels of human nature have got cheaper** ("what you scroll onto is not the world, but the world's anger-prone slice")); **the half-life of a debunking, and the dispute over "backfire" —** the dispute over the backfire effect has to be put honestly: the early experimental conclusion that "debunking instead strengthens the false belief" has not replicated stably in larger samples (a regular of the replication crisis) — the conclusion one can hold for now is weaker, but it is enough to use: debunking works, but slowly, and the structure of the telling has to be right (give an alternative explanation first, then negate the original rumour: to stick on only a label reading "untrue" is to leave the vacuum for the rumour to fill by itself — "you cannot fill a story with 'not so'; you can only fill it with a better story"**). A self-check on the motive for searching** (**the search version of confirmation bias: we search for "why X is right" rather than "what could overthrow X" — the technical prescription: search for the contrary on purpose, and close the page only after you have read** ("a search engine is your echo wall: shouting at it 'Does anyone oppose me?' is the only spell this course teaches")).
:::

::: history From the "uncrowned king" to "everyone an editor" {#sec-wumian}
**1922**, journalism is established as a discipline (**the American press examines itself over the age of "yellow journalism": objectivity is invented as a professional norm — "objectivity is a trade's warranty, not a person's virtue"** (it rests on procedures of reporting: balanced quotations, a grading of sources, a corrections column — the first edition of institutionalised doubt)); **the 1960–70s**, agenda-setting and gatekeeping research (**the received view in McCombs and Shaw's "agenda-setting": the media cannot decide how you think, but they can decide what you think about; the supplementary version, the "attribute agenda", goes further: they can also decide the angle from which you think**); **1843**, Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (**the "pseudo-environment": what we deal with are the pictures in the head — the word "stereotype" enters the study of public opinion** (the communication-studies branch of Chapter 2's "inventory": a stereotype is an associative definition that has never been audited)); **the 2000s**, the wiki age (**bottom-up collaborative editing turns the "corrections column" into "version history": an open process becomes, for the first time, a resource of credibility — "trust migrates in part from 'the authority of the source' to 'traceability': this is the most underestimated change of dynasty in the history of evaluating information"**); **the 2010s**, fact-checking becomes a profession (**professional bodies and a scale of "fact ratings" (true / basically true / half-true / false) — the marking sheet of the logic class is made into a news product; in the same period, social bots and deepfakes demote "where there is a picture, there is the truth" to "where there is a picture, there is doubt": techniques for securing evidence (reverse image search, metadata) become part of a new civic literacy**); **the Chinese setting** (**from a chain of re-quotation in the manner of the *Global Times*, "according to Xinhua", to hierarchical re-quotation, "according to the CCTV News client" — a system of administrative rank for sources: Chinese readers live, as a matter of course, inside an evidence pyramid; only the apex has been replaced by an official seal — the task of this chapter is not to overthrow this tower, but to add Question 4 to it: what the people at the top of the tower did not say**).
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in evaluating information
**Taking "can be checked" for "has been checked"** (**do the three characters 有出处 ("a source is given") exempt the whole piece? The source given may itself be the first link in a circular citation: being checkable is a qualification, not a conclusion**); **symmetrical scepticism** (**believing every item on your own side, and running the five-step autopsy only on the other side, = you are not a sceptic, you are a fan** (a tool for self-audit: take out the piece you most want to forward, and test it once by the same standard: if it hurts, the method is right)); **diversion of the "and what about you?" kind** (**once a fault is pointed out, throw up another, larger problem: issue inflation is an old craft for shifting attention — "close this case first, then hear a different one: a sentence of 'and what about XX?' is not an argument; it is a motion to adjourn"**); **anti-intellectual cynicism** (**"none of the media can be trusted" and "therefore look at none of it / believe all of it" are two sides of one coin: cynicism is not clear sight; it is giving up the allocation of the budget: it saves the most thinking, and it is also the most easily harvested by "the only person who dares to tell the truth"** (the reserve of Chapter 9's populist rhetoric)).
:::

::: exercise Build your whitelist {level=1}
For one issue you care about (education, health, or house prices — choose one), draw up a **table of source gears**: three gears (accept by preference / must cross-check / a sample of emotion only), and for each write two real examples and a one-sentence rule for admission; and set **the conditions that trigger an escalated check** (in what circumstances you would move up from "accept" to "go and look up the original source").
::: solution
Marking: **the standards for the gears are written as rules one can apply** (**"priority = the publishing party and the researching party are independent of each other, and the data can be downloaded", not "I feel it is authoritative"**); **the column "a sample of emotion only" must have something in it** (**that is where you place "things I particularly want to forward" — a quarantine zone for your own preferences** (self-audit starts from the cell that hurts most)); **the trigger is specific** ("a percentage is involved, and the conclusion supports the position I already hold = check the original scope at once: that is when the alarm of bias sounds").
:::
:::

::: exercise Anatomy of a rumour {level=2}
Choose one item that has spread widely of late (health or education), write a report by the five-step autopsy of the worked example, and **write for it a "debunking that comes up to standard"** (it must contain: an alternative explanation, the figure under the correct scope, and an acknowledgement of the emotion — the four characters 纯属谣言 ("nothing but a rumour") must not stand as a paragraph on their own).
::: solution
Marking: **tell the story before the conclusion** (**"where did the number you saw come from?" changes belief more than "this is fake" — the half-life rule in practice: the explanation must occupy the vacuum the rumour leaves**); **grant that the emotion is legitimate** (**"the worry about a child's eyesight is real; it is only that this document cannot carry that worry" — correcting the fact and catching the emotion are done together** (how this logic course divides the account of Lesson 1's three appeals in persuasion: *logos* belongs to this lesson; do not hand *pathos* back to the rumour-maker)); **give a better thing to do** (**"what is to be done is two hours outdoors every day, not buying this device" — a debunking ends by replacing the behaviour, not by winning the argument**).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the economics of trust {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing "**post-truth is not 'people no longer tell the truth', but 'the wholesale price of truth is lower than the retail price of rumour' — when the marginal cost of production is zero, and the cost of checking falls entirely on the buyer, the market is occupied by poor-quality goods: this is not a moral slide; it is a failure of pricing**". You must draw on at least six items: **testimony is the load-bearing wall**, **the budgetary allocation of doubt**, **circular citation / citation inflation**, **the scope is the law**, **the platform's "relevance replaces importance"**, and **lateral reading**; and give a ruling: **ought a platform to answer for the quality of information — the clash between the account of algorithmic neutrality and the paternalist account; write the conditions under which your proposal fails**.
::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms (**the outsourcing system is not to be abolished; the budget is allocated by the cost of getting it wrong; the plural hallucination; definition is governance; dwell time makes the agenda; leaving the current page**); the ruling on platforms (**the neutrality account — gatekeeping is power, power must be called to account, and the platform has no democratic legitimacy; the paternalist account — the market has already failed, and not acting is itself a choice; the marking looks at the conditions of failure** (for example "transparency in place of a censor's review: label the source and publish the ranking parameters, rather than delete the post outright — when 'labelling' sinks into a new ornament of trust (everyone confers a 'verified' badge on themselves), the transparency scheme fails; the dispute then comes back to who has the right to issue the certificate: which is back at Chapter 2's right to define — this lesson and the whole book close the loop here")); if the conclusion is written "**taught to its deepest point, a logic course in the information age is one sentence: leave doubt for the assertions that can hurt you, leave trust for sources that can be traced, and leave anger for the things that deserve it — skimp on none of the three; all three are costly**", that is an A.
:::
