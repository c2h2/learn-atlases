title: "The syllogism"

The previous chapter's MP/MT handles the transport of "if"; this chapter handles the transport of "all / some" — **Aristotle's first machine: three terms, three pairwise appointments, a go-between presiding over the whole occasion**.

## Definition: the appointment schedule of three terms {#def-yuehui}

::: definition Standard form and the middle term {#def-zhongxiang}
**A syllogism = major premise (major term—middle term) + minor premise (minor term—middle term) ⇒ conclusion (minor term—major term)** (**"All humans err; Socrates is a human; ∴ Socrates errs" — each of the three terms appears on stage twice, and the conclusion keeps only two: the middle term is the matchmaker, and once the match is made it leaves the stage ("a matchmaker appearing in the conclusion" is a breach of form: the middle term must disappear into the product, like sugar dissolving into water**)). **Three steps for taming Chinese**: **① find the conclusion and so fix the two ends; ② find the one that shows its face twice and never enters the conclusion, and take that as the middle term; ③ press the two sentences into A/E/I/O standard form (the admission ticket from Chapter 3)**. **The office of the middle term is a wharf** — **both times the freighter must come alongside before it can unload and transship: if both times it fails to come alongside (both undistributed), the cargo never learns whether the other party exists**.
:::

::: definition Distribution: does one roll-call count {#def-zhouyan}
**Distributed = the premise has given an account of the whole extension of that term** (**an A proposition governs the subject and not the predicate: "All humans err" has finished with the whole clan of "human", and has not dared to say who else is among "those who err"; an E governs both; an I governs neither; an O governs the predicate and not the subject — the "table of jurisdiction" of the four sentences is this chapter's declension table**). **The three great violations**: **① undistributed middle** (**"Dogs are animals, cats are animals, ∴ cats are dogs" — both times the middle term only showed the face of "animal": this is a wharf with no shore; dog and cat each moor in a corner and never meet — a Chinese case: "All who love reading are kind; Xiao Wang loves reading; ∴ Xiao Wang is kind" — again the same sick machine — for the full case see the worked example**); **② expansion of the major (or the minor)** (**"All cheating is a breach of the rules; all of this handling is a breach of the rules; ∴ all of this handling is cheating" — the major premise only says that cheaters all lie inside the circle of "breaches"; it does not say that the circle holds only cheaters: the conclusion draws other people's property onto one's own map as well — "the premises rented only one room; the conclusion announces title to the whole building": expansion is the forging of a balance sheet**); **③ two negative premises** (**"Cats are not birds, dogs are not birds" — both ships have collected only a returns slip for "not in the bird zone", and where each lies relative to the other is still unknown: information that refuses cannot be used as evidence; two pieces of bad news cannot be gripped into one piece of good news**. **One money-saving rule to add**: **two affirmative premises cannot yield a negative conclusion (an optimistic conveyor cannot carry pessimistic cargo); if the conclusion is negative, a negative must already be hidden in the premises: pessimism does not produce, it only smuggles**).
:::

::: definition The four figures, and "the first figure is the most respectable" {#def-sige}
**Figure = the four ways of arranging where the middle term stands** (**first figure: the middle term is the subject of the major premise and the predicate of the minor (the Barbara type — "all M are P, all S are M, ∴ all S are P": the chain S→M→P is as straight as algebraic substitution; second figure: the middle term is the predicate both times (in charge specifically of negative distinctions — "all cheats tell lies; no honest person tells lies; ∴ no honest person is a cheat": drawing a boundary by a property; third figure: the middle term is the subject both times (its special product is particulars — "all whales are mammals; all whales live in the water; ∴ some aquatic animals are mammals": the specialist who breaks a universal with a counter-example; the fourth figure is a medieval household registration (filling in the symmetrical accounts)**). **Aristotle's project of "completion"** (**he declared two moods of the first figure "self-evident", and the remaining valid moods counted as hired only once reduced back to the first figure — the first "standardisation" in the history of logic: the other figures are only the first figure in a change of costume; the implication for teaching: the store of parts need keep only one mainframe, and the rest count as peripherals**).
:::

::: example Reconstruction, diagrams and testing for invalidity {#ex-chongjian}
① Press "No mammal is oviparous; ducks are oviparous; so ducks are not mammals" into standard form, judge the figure and the mood, and explain what makes it valid; ② use a Venn diagram to check why "All cats fear water; some animals that fear water are very cute; ∴ some cats are very cute" is invalid — what the diagram lacks; ③ complete the elliptical form: "He actually did not vote in favour, and yet he is a committee member." — list at least two major premises that could be supplied, and give one comment on each.
::: solution
① **Standard form**: no M (mammals) are P (oviparous) [E]; all S (ducks) are P [A]; ∴ all S are not M [E] — **second figure, of the Camestres type: the middle term (P) is distributed both times (the object of an A is not distributed! Note: the second, middle proposition "ducks are oviparous" is an A, and the predicate "oviparous" is not distributed — but this mood is valid by relying on the symmetrical conversion of the E proposition: no M are P ⇔ no P are M: first convert the E, and the middle term has then "come alongside twice" (the rule-check of a valid mood will wave it through: the middle term is distributed in the E; the major term M is distributed in the conclusion, and after conversion it is distributed in the premise as well — the suspicion of expansion is cleared**) — **teaching point: testing a mood for invalidity is a dead machine; it knows even better than you whether you have broken a rule (the machine knows better than its driver whether it has run off course: it tests the syllogism; the person is only responsible for setting the cargo into the right shape**); ② **Venn diagram** (**draw "猫⊂怕水" ('cats ⊂ fear water') as inclusion with a solid line; "有些怕水的很可爱" ('some that fear water are very cute') is an I proposition: the marker-dot must fall in the region "怕水∩可爱" ('fear-water ∩ cute'), but the diagram gives no guarantee whether that region intersects the circle of cats: the dot may lie outside the cat circle — the conclusion demands "有的猫可爱" ('some cats are cute'), yet there is nowhere in the diagram for a dot to land safely: the definition the other way round is clearer — valid = a counter-example has nowhere to lodge; the value of a diagram is precisely that it makes invalidity visible**); ③ **Elliptical syllogism** (**candidate major premises: A, "All committee members vote in favour" (he has broken the organisation's rule); B, "All committee members are sensible people" (he is unwise); C, "His not voting shows that he has an objection" (reading an action as an attitude) — three ways of completing it yield three charges: the elliptical form is where a stance hides; completing it is turning the hiding-place into a hall open for business (a principle of rhetoric: Aristotle says the elliptical form is "the complete individual of rhetoric" — because the listener supplies the premises himself, being persuaded looks like having thought it through himself: ellipsis is courtesy, and it is also hiding stolen goods**).
:::

::: intuition The machine and the mother tongue: the hard points of taming Chinese {#int-muyu}
**The syllogism assumes that every sentence has one determinate subject term** — **the topic sentence of Chinese just will not comply ("这本书看了三遍了，很好" ('This book, [I have] read it three times already; very good.' (Our translation.)) — subject term? patient? the agent is absent; does "很好" ('very good') appraise the book, or the experience of "看" ('reading')? If the subject term is not locked, there is no talking about distribution: the focus of remedial logic for students in a Chinese department is not the rules, but the judgement of "who is being spoken of" (rewriting a topic sentence as a subject–predicate form is a joint venture of modern Chinese and logic — "这篇课文我教了三遍" = "我教这篇课文教了三遍" ('This lesson, I have taught three times' = 'I have taught this lesson, taught it three times.' (Our translation.)): only once the subject term is supplied does it count as being put on the scale**). **Quantifiers hide inside the adjectives** ("**年轻人爱冲动" ('Young people are impulsive.' (Our translation.)) looks like an A but is really an I: making the common saying into a universal is the violation: reading a generic as a universal, and one contraposition overturns the cart: "年轻人爱冲动；他不是年轻人；∴他不会冲动" ('Young people are impulsive; he is not a young person; ∴ he will not be impulsive.' (Our translation.)) — denying the antecedent comes back to life by way of the generic sentence (a joint case of the ways of dying in Chapter 4 and the distribution of this chapter)**). **The teaching conclusion**: **the rules are dead, Chinese is alive: the whole technique of this chapter can be condensed into one translation — translate what a living person says into an appointment schedule of three terms; once the translation is done, right and wrong show their shape of themselves; the places that cannot be translated (ambiguity, topic-drift, generics) are the questions really worth quarrelling over**.
:::

::: history Two refits of one machine {#sec-gaixie}
**The 4th century BC**, the *Prior Analytics* (**an exhaustive enumeration of figures and moods, plus "reduction to completion": the first time "correctness of inference" became something one could count item by item**); **Theophrastus** (**the usual account: relational inference ("if A is to the left of B, then B is to the right of A") was entered in the ledger of logic: the first batch of plug-ins outside the system of the syllogism**); **the Middle Ages** (**the mnemonic verses Barbara, Celarent… the names of the six moods remain in use to this day: the scholastics took the machine apart into rules and proved the completeness of the theorems — both hands were firm, "the machine of rules" and "the rules of the machine"; the parallel line of *yīnmíng* 因明 ('Buddhist logic of the reason'): Dignāga 陈那 and Dharmakīrti 法称 on the "three aspects of the reason" (the reason must be certainly present in the similar instances, and entirely absent from the dissimilar instances) — the same effect by a different tune from the theory of distribution, and yet a different path: Greece governs the jurisdiction of terms; India governs the for-and-against inventory of properties**); **1772**, Lambert, *Logic* (**the herald of axiomatising the syllogism: reducing the several moods to a few basic laws; in the same period Kant left the famous assertion "from Aristotle on, not a single step forward" (the commonly quoted gist, as later people summarise it) — the sentence itself became the backdrop of the nineteenth-century revolution**); **1847—54**, Boole (**algebraisation: equations of classes swallow the syllogism — "all S are P" is written S＝SP: the machine is taken apart into arithmetic; people of the nineteenth century used symbols to perform a heart-transplant operation on the old machine**); **1879**, Frege (**quantifiers come online: the syllogism is demoted to one small block of predicate logic — but the small block became the shopfront of secondary-school textbooks: the narrowest logic was taught to the most people (teaching method often travels in the opposite direction from the history of the subject)**); **the present day** (**the syllogism lives on in the genealogy of database queries (the grammar of intersection, subset and complement is its dialect), and it also lives on in every charge of "hasty generalisation" — the charge of "taking a single case for the whole" = a modern trial that prosecutes an undistributed middle**).
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of the syllogism
**Taking the machine for a worldview** (**most everyday arguments are not categorical syllogisms: probability, causation and value each use a logic of their own — this chapter is the frog of an introductory dissection, not the whole life of the frog (Chapters 6—8 each have a machine of their own)**); **memorising distribution as a jingle and not memorising the reason** (**the jingle asks "did it govern the whole, or not?"; the reason is only one sentence: are you speaking of its whole family, or of a few mouths in its household — a person who understands the reason can check himself on the spot; a person who recites the jingle has the power cut the moment the sentence pattern changes**); **using a collective term to substitute, by stealth, for an individual term** (**"Lu Xun's works cannot be finished in a day; 'My Old Home' is a work by Lu Xun; ∴ 'My Old Home' cannot be finished in a day" — the middle term seems to show its face twice, but in fact the first "Lu Xun's works" is the collection as a whole, and the second is one book among them: two middle terms are hiding in one machine — a four-legged syllogism is a deformity (an outbreak, inside the grid, of the inventory problem of Chapter 2: a four-term syllogism = ambiguity caught in the act)**); **taking "all valid" for "all true"** (**only a valid mood plus true premises is reliable: a syllogism cannot smelt false premises into a true conclusion; it does not produce so much as one gram of fact (Chapter 4: form does not produce water)**).
:::

::: exercise A banknote checker {level=1}
Press five inferences into standard form and test them for invalidity by the rules (name the specific one that is broken): ① "Whales are not fish; sharks are not fish; so sharks are not whales" (note: the conclusion happens to be true); ② "All who love reading are kind; Lao Li does not love reading; so Lao Li is not kind"; ③ "Some civil servants are Party members; some Party members are scholars; so some civil servants are scholars"; ④ invent one faulty sentence of a "four-legged middle term"; ⑤ write a valid mood of the **second figure** and translate it into one Chinese sentence.
::: solution
Marking: ① **two negative premises, invalid — a conclusion that happens to be true is not the same as an inference that is valid: validity governs transport, truth governs the cargo, and one firm signs only one slip**; ② **valid: "loves reading → kind" + "Lao Li is not kind": MT put into syllogistic form (the categorical version of denying the consequent, of the Camestres family in the second figure: valid, and the premises can be checked)**; ③ **two particulars (I＋I) yield nothing at all: the necessary consequence of the middle term being undistributed both times — "some" paired with "some", and the matchmaker never comes ashore**; ④ an invented four-term sentence is marked "**ambiguity of the middle term = two middle terms conspiring: the number of legs is wrong, and the machine falls apart**"; ⑤ the second figure (the middle term is the predicate both times, and there must be one negative premise), a Chinese specimen: “所有合同都要签字；这份条子不要求签字；∴它不是合同” ('Every contract must be signed; this slip does not require a signature; ∴ it is not a contract.' (Our translation.)) — **the marks fall in the two columns "the mood is valid" and "the Chinese is natural"**.
:::

::: exercise Translating topic sentences {level=2}
Rewrite five Chinese flowing sentences (*liushuiju*) into **subject–predicate standard form on which distribution can be judged**: “这瓜吃了不闹肚子” ('Eat this melon and the stomach will not play up.' (Our translation.)), “论文写了三稿还是乱” ('The essay has been through three drafts and is still a mess.' (Our translation.)), “这件事处理得不合适” ('This matter has been handled improperly.' (Our translation.)), “内容没问题，排版太挤了” ('The content is no problem; the layout is too cramped.' (Our translation.)), “孩子大了就说不得” ('Once a child is grown, one may no longer tell them off.' (Our translation.)). And mark: who the subject term actually is, and what quantifier the original sentence was hiding.
::: solution
A specimen for marking: "**这瓜吃了不闹肚子" = "任何人吃了这瓜都不会闹肚子" ('Eat this melon and the stomach will not play up' = 'Anyone who has eaten this melon will not have an upset stomach.' (Our translation.)) (the subject term slides from "瓜" ('melon') to "the person who eats": a patient-topic sentence hides the agent inside the verb — the quantifier "任何" ('any') appears out of nowhere: the distribution table has to be redrawn at once**); "**孩子大了就说不得" = "对所有孩子：一旦其成年，则不可劝" ('Once a child is grown, one may no longer tell them off' = 'Of every child: once he or she comes of age, they cannot be admonished.' (Our translation.)) (the universal hides where there is no marker — the dispute over how to read a generic sentence is the dispute over the deposits in the determiner account of Chapter 3**); for each sentence point out **what the rewriting lost** (the intimacy of the colloquial, and its waiver of liability: locking the subject term = claiming the responsibility — the vagueness of Chinese is not a defect; it is a strategy of leaving the right to sign until one is on the spot (the division of labour between pragmatics and logic: logic wants a signature; Chinese speaks of a tacit understanding)).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the syllogism is the Latin grammar of logic {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing "**the syllogism is like Latin grammar: the rules are fiddly and useless for a whole lifetime, yet they taught later ages one thing — the correctness of inference is written in the format, not in the content; this 'compulsory course' was later taken over by Frege and remade into the programmer's mother tongue**". You must draw on: **the wharf of the middle term, and distribution**, **at least one case of each of the three great violations**, **figure and reduction (standardisation)**, **the rhetoric of the elliptical syllogism**, **the Boole–Frege line of refitting** — at least five items; and give a verdict: **should the syllogism still be taught today — is it gymnastics for thinking, cultural heritage, or a fossil of a wrong classification (give your proposed timetable, and the conditions under which it fails)**.
::: solution
Points for marking: the five mechanisms (**the wharf = a metaphor for relation; a violation = an audit of the balance sheet; reduction = standardisation, which is power; the elliptical form = the self-delusion of the one who is persuaded; algebraisation = a heart transplant for the machine**); the timetable verdict (**for reference: the gymnastics view — the rules train an intuition of "jurisdiction", which transfers to probabilistic reasoning and to legal reasoning; the heritage view — it is a world heritage of rationalism; the meaning of a museum ≠ the meaning of a workshop; the fossil view — in natural language the hit rate of what can directly be called a syllogism is extremely low, and teaching time ought to give way to probability and to statistical fallacies — all three cases must state conditions of failure (a sterner way of marking: a course that only examines "recognising the mood" is cultural heritage; only a course that requires "reconstruction and testing for invalidity" is gymnastics — cultivate only the former, and this course is a fossil)**); if the conclusion can be written "**the real legacy of the syllogism is not those nineteen moods, but that astonishing idea: correctness can leave content unasked and look only at the arrangement — this idea later grew mathematics, grew logic, and finally grew the computer: the grammar died; the format lives**", that is an A.

::: summary
The machine, archived: the appointment schedule (**three terms, six appearances on stage; once the middle term has finished the match it must leave the stage**), distribution (**governing the whole family or a few mouths: the middle term must come alongside, expansion is forgery, negatives do not shake hands**), figure (**the first figure is the mainframe, and the rest are all peripherals: standardisation is power**), diagrams (**invalid = the reverse of a counter-example having nowhere to lodge: there is nowhere the point can be drawn**), mother tongue (**if the subject term is not locked, then nothing can be discussed: translate first, then test for invalidity**). The method in one sentence: **translate what is said into a format, and let the machine quarrel for you**. The two machines of deduction (connectives + the syllogism) are complete; the next chapter changes the fuel: the probable — induction and analogy.
:::
