---
title: "Translation and reception"
---

The first ten chapters dealt with how works are produced; this chapter deals with how a work is transferred — **the master ledger of the whole lesson is one sentence: world literature is not a batch of works, but a set of techniques of transfer; what a work "loses" in translation is precisely the receipt for what has been paid by the part of it that is allowed to remain**.

## Definition: three changes of object {#def-sandui}

::: definition From the original, the translation, to the system {#def-duixiang}
**Received account.** Translation studies changed its object of study three times in the twentieth century: first it studied how to translate (the linguistic area, the 1950s–60s), then the effects and norms of translated works (the descriptive school, the 1970s), and last the position of translations inside the literary system, and cultural power (the cultural turn, after the 1970s). **Three concluding sentences from the three changes of object.** **①** **"Fidelity" is not a criterion; it is rhetoric.** It writes a technical choice up as a moral attitude; **and so every dispute about fidelity ends in a quarrel, and ends in who has the right to define "fidelity".** This chapter is to give an operable substitute criterion (the worked example will use it). **②** **Changing the object matters more than changing the conclusion.** A linguistic question (whether this word can be exchanged for that word) can never answer a sociological question (why this book is translated and another book is not). **Chapter 1's theory of levels, recurring inside translation.** **③** **One regularity of the polysystem.** **Received account: some scholars have proposed that, when a literature stands in a position of crisis, of being young, or of being flooded by literatures in other languages, translated literature occupies the centre of that system; when it is stable and confident, translation withdraws to the margin.** **This lesson regards that as the single most useful testable hypothesis in the whole course,** **because it predicts two things:** **one is that the years when translation flourishes most are precisely the years when domestic genres fail; the other is that, after the flourishing, people forget that these translations were once models for original work.** **The second will, at the end of this chapter, become a verdict on the present.**
:::

::: definition Three kinds of untranslatability, and their real prices {#def-buke}
**Received account.** The translation world has long used "untranslatable" to describe three kinds of difficulty: form (sound and rhyme, metre, the shape of the characters), pragmatics (the pun, honorifics, register), and institutional proper names (official posts, measures, articles of law). **This lesson's reading.** **①** **First split "untranslatable" into the untranslatable and the not worth the cost.** In form, there are indeed things of which, under any way of translating, one of the two must be lost; far more often, what the translator is saying is this: **to translate this place well would take extra hours of work, footnotes, and an editor's patience, and this contract does not pay that sum.** **This is the sharpest sentence in the chapter:** **"untranslatable" is often the diplomatic wording for a price that could not be agreed.** **②** **An old list.** **Received account: the tradition of translating the Buddhist scriptures keeps an itemised saying about "what kinds of words it is better not to translate", later summed up as several kinds of "not to be translated".** — **This lesson's reading of it:** **it is not an admission of failure, but a table of costs and benefits:** **for some words, keeping the original's pronunciation is both cheaper and safer than forcing a local word.** **Chapter 2's "three-layer assembly of the text" has, here, a version done by transliteration.** **③** **Its counterpart is over-translatability.** The same difficult place is often translated too far: a pun is turned into an explanation, a dialect into the written language, and honorifics are changed, without exception, into ordinary forms of address. **This lesson calls this "translatability inflation":** **when everything has been translated into smoothness, the social layering of the text is levelled, and the layering is often precisely the content of the original.** **The loss, inside the translation, of Course 7's "register is not politeness but admission" is the interest on this inflation.** **This is why many translations "read better", and yet are no longer a novel of class.**
:::

::: definition The exchange rate, relay translation and self-translation {#def-huivil}
**The exchange rate.** **Received account: the flow of translation is highly asymmetrical; for a few languages, the volume translated out has long been far higher than the volume translated in.** **This lesson refuses to tell "a certain country reads only itself" as national character:** **it is a fact of the exchange rate: in a central language, a book can sell without being translated; in a minor language, if an author is to be read, he must often write directly in a central language.** **This is where the whole cost of the previous chapter's "exiting a language" lies.** **②** **Relay translation (a book's third language).** **Received account: in modern East Asia a great many texts passed through Japanese or English; the very material of which some modern terms are made is itself Chinese-character words that flowed back from Japanese.** **This lesson's three points.** **First, relay translation is not laziness but the economics of channels:** **if two languages have no direct relation of publishing between them, a third party's intermediary fee must be paid.** **Second, relay translation leaves structural traces:** **from syntax to the order of the text, the habits of the intermediary language remain in the text of the terminal language.** **Received account: certain "Europeanised" sentence patterns in written modern Chinese were introduced by the translation of scriptures and by translational style, and the field has long discussed them.** **Third, the easiest to overlook:** **relay translation makes the "nationality" of the original cease to matter.** **In a book that has been relay-translated twice, the author in person is no longer the evidence that is required.** **Chapter 1's author-function here completes its voyage round the world.** **③** **Self-translation.** **Received account: some writers rewrite, as English, works from their early years in their own national language, or revise with their own hand the translations of themselves.** **This lesson reads this as one transfer of title:** **by publishing himself over again, the author changes his own position in the literary system —** **from "the alien who has been translated out" to "a writer inside the central language".** **Its gain is a column that can be checked: the currency of the fee, the scale of the readership, and eligibility for prizes; its loss is a column that nobody checks: the rhythm that belongs to that language alone.** **Chapter 6's "the writing workshop changes the links of production" has a more extreme version here:** **the language itself has changed production line.**
:::

::: definition Three ledgers of cutting and alteration {#def-shanchu}
**Received account.** Cuts and alterations in a translation come, roughly, from three kinds of pressure: censorship in the importing country, the publisher's constraints of length and readability, and the translator's judgement that "readers at home cannot understand". **The three ledgers are kept differently.** **①** **The censorship ledger.** It can be looked up; there are documents; there are rulings. **Its consequences are the easiest to mis-record as "the original never had this passage".** **Chapter 1's "the luck of the archive" here becomes a requirement that evidence be taken:** **comparing the first printing, the abridged edition and the unabridged edition with commentary is the homework this chapter sets the reader.** **②** **The length ledger.** **Received account: the earliest translations into foreign languages of a number of nineteenth-century classics of the world once left out the passages of argument, the lines of secondary characters, and the descriptions of landscape.** **This lesson calls this "the dismantling of the hall":** **the part cut away is often the part by which the original took part in the public debate of its own country, and in the target language that part has no hall to correspond to it.** **The long arguments in the Russian works of the previous chapter are of this kind.** **And so the impression that "the original is not so fond of arguing" is the product of a decision about the binding.** **③** **The judgement ledger.** The translator's estimate that "the reader will not understand" is the hardest to check, because it has no documents. **This lesson's way of handling it:** **do not lay blame, but do distinguish:** **what censorship cuts is what has been forbidden; what judgement cuts is what has been valued at zero;** **the former leaves a precedent, the latter leaves only one tactful sentence in the preface.** **What Course 9, on argumentative writing, calls "the concession put first" is most often met, in a translator's preface, in its commercial embodiment.**
:::

::: definition A mistranslation can be an asset of the home literature {#def-wuyi}
**Received account.** Two cases often cited: a modernist poet rewrote ancient Chinese poems from another person's notes, and the result entered anthologies of poetry in his own country; a British poet rewrote Persian quatrains, and made their original author famous outside his own country. **Four judgements.** **①** **A literary system asks only about usability.** These rewritings are often pointed out as errors in the scholarship of the original's language, while in English poetry they are effective works; **this is not a moral question, but a difference between the criteria of two systems:** **the academic system checks truth-value, and the literary system checks usability.** **Yet another split in Chapter 1's "description and prescription".** **②** **It makes "influence" into a suspect thing.** Once the person rewritten has become an asset of the home literature, what readers at home read in it is their own modernity; **and so the often-cited judgement that "Chinese poetry influenced modern American poetry" in fact refers to the productivity of a misreading.** **Chapter 10's "convergence rather than influence" gets a more extreme version here:** **sometimes not even the text is the same work.** **③** **Flowing back.** **Received account: an author who becomes famous by being rewritten has a fame at home which is, on the contrary, constituted in part by the fame abroad.** **This is the most important mirroring in the whole chapter:** **the structure of the readership of world literature can move a work's "homeland" away.** **The previous chapter's "the name on the shelf comes before the work", together with this chapter's "the translation comes before the original", is the whole of this lesson's critique of "national literature".** **④** **It raises the price and lowers it at the same time.** The rewriter gains reputation and fees; **the person rewritten gains a name, and this name no longer belongs to his language.** **Chapter 11's question "who pays the bill" reaches its end here:** **the profit statement of translation never balances, and the headings of the accounts are set on the side of the target language.**
:::

::: definition The translator's grades of pay, and invisibility {#def-baochou}
**Received account.** Most systems of publishing pay the translator once, by the character, and pay the author an advance plus royalties; where the translator's name sits on the cover, whether a review mentions it, and whether prizes set aside a separate credit for the translator, differ enormously from country to country. **This lesson's four points.** **①** **"The translator's invisibility" is not that the idea is backward; it is the structure of pay.** A one-off payment means that the translator does not bear the risk of sales, and does not share the return on sales; **a person who does not share the return is naturally not asked to sign.** **This is the cleanest variant of Chapter 4's "quotation sheet".** **②** **A signed name is a luxury that can be bought.** **Received account: certain languages, and certain series, place the translator's name in a position equal to the author's, and discuss the two side by side in reviews.** **This lesson requires of the reader:** **every time you recommend a translation, say the translator's name aloud;** **this is a transfer of value at almost no cost.** **③** **Who decides the way of translating.** Disagreements over terminology among the four parties — editor, author, translator and reader — are in the end often decided by "who bears the readers' complaints". **Received account: revised translations of scholarly classics are often delayed for years by disputes over terminology.** **This lesson's reading:** **terminology is a constitutional clause of literature, and the procedure for amending a constitution has always been more political than legislation.** **Chapter 3's "for a definition to be put into effect, someone always has to sign" has, here, one piece of evidence from a translation.** **④** **The machine's new place.** **Received account: public experiments in machine translation began in the middle of the 1950s; over several decades their funding and their reputation went through one sharp contraction and one explosive recovery.** **This lesson records only one institutional fact:** **when translation turns from a craft into water and electricity, the question "who pays" does not disappear; it only becomes "who maintains the model, whose corpus is used for training, and whose name does not appear in the acknowledgements".** **Chapter 14's online literature and this chapter meet here:** **the next step for translation is not to translate better, but to translate to the point where it need not be noticed —** **and the pay for all labour that is not noticed is borne by the people who do not sign.**
:::

::: example Audit a translation {#ex-shenji}
Choose a translation you have read, and in 200 characters complete four items: **① list three places where it cuts, merges or rewrites (you may compare them with the original, or with another translation); ② for each place, decide which of the three ledgers it belongs to; ③ name the beneficiary and the payer of these three entries; ④ give one falsifiable criterion of a "good translation"**.

::: solution
**① The order of taking evidence** — **first compare the first edition with the later edition, then the abridged edition with the complete translation, and last a relay translation in another language;** **if the original cannot be obtained, use two translations to corroborate each other: wherever the two translations agree and yet some place is jarring, it is often a problem of the base text or of the conventions of presentation; wherever the two translations diverge, it is often a real difficulty in the original book.** **Marking:** **one must distinguish "I have checked" from "I infer".** **Chapter 1's strength of evidence:** **this chapter gives this distinction full marks, and confusing the two is a fail.** **② Posting to a ledger** — **a deleted passage that touches the political taboos of the home country → the censorship ledger; long description and secondary characters that have been merged → the length ledger; puns, dialect and honorifics that have been rewritten as smooth set phrases → the judgement ledger.** **If all three places are posted to "the translator's level", the mark is zero:** **this makes an institutional problem into a private one, and it is the very thing this chapter opposes from beginning to end.** **③ Beneficiary and payer** — **the beneficiary is usually the party of the channel: a shorter length of copy, a lower cost of printing, fewer complaints, and a "cleaner" experience for the reader;** **the payer is the author of the original and the readers of that author's language, because what they lose is something that cannot be claimed back.** **This lesson calls this "the negative externality of translation":** **the cost falls on the party that does not sign the contract.** **The final form, inside the translation, of Chapter 8's "who pays the conversion cost".** **④ A falsifiable criterion** — **An example that passes: "Of two translations of the same original, if at some place version A keeps the original's layering by register and version B flattens it, then version B is an inferior translation at that place; this judgement can be checked again by marking the register of the original."** **An example that fails: "A good translation ought to be as natural as the original" —** **it cannot be tested by any procedure.** **Lesson 3's ranking by refutability:** **"natural" is the word this lesson hears for the last time in Chapter 11, and the word it rejects for the last time.**
:::
:::

::: intuition The original is an archive; the translation is a work {#int-dang}
**A proposition that is excessive on purpose.** The great majority of the readers of world literature are reading a translation; therefore, as far as actual circulation is concerned, the form in which a work exists is its group of translations, and not its manuscript. **Three arguments.** **①** **The functional argument.** What is cited, adapted, attacked, banned and put into textbooks is, in every case, some translation; **the original bears another function: it is the voucher for checking and for tracing a claim back, which is to say, the archive.** **②** **It does not deny the value of the original.** **On the contrary:** **without the archive, a translation cannot be corrected;** **so what this chapter wants is a stronger archival awareness (editions, commentaries and subcommentaries, collation, philology), and not a stronger worship of the original.** **All the techniques of Chapter 1 and of Chapter 6 here become necessities of the age of the translation.** **③** **Its contemporary form.** In the age of machine subtitles and automatic summaries, the foreign text that most people "read" for the first time is a layer of transcription with no name; **it does not even claim that it is itself translating.** **This lesson leaves its last paragraph for this sentence:** **we spent ten chapters learning to say "whatever is literature has conditions of production"; the conclusion of this chapter only applies that sentence to translation:** **a translation is not the shadow of the original; it is another work, with its own patron, its own censorship, its own table of pay and its own silence —** **and world literature is the total of these silences added together; to call it "leakage" is our only honest unit of measure.**
:::

::: history Six changes of hands {#sec-liuci}
**Third century BC to second century AD.** The Septuagint made a translation into Greek into the "original" of the West; in the same period, the assemblies for translating Buddhist scriptures in South Asia and East Asia turned translation into an official enterprise with a staff establishment, a language of translation, and disputes. **Received account:** **this lesson sets the two things side by side:** **translation became a project of the state for the first time, and for the first time produced a list of rules for "which words cannot be translated".** **②** **Fourth to sixteenth centuries.** Copying by hand, commentaries and subcommentaries, and the shift into dialects; a number of religious translations took part in shaping the standard languages that came later. **Received account: a reformer's translation into the vernacular, and its influence on grammar, have long been discussed.** **Chapter 8's "a standard language is cut to shape": the knife, here, is the translation of scripture.** **③** **Sixteenth to seventeenth centuries.** Vernacular Bibles, and the dispute over Catholic translations of scripture (received account: a long dispute about terminology, over whether words that already existed could be used to translate the supreme concept). **A dispute over terms is a dispute over sovereignty;** **this lesson has already demonstrated this point once each, in the previous chapter, with Chinese characters, with Arabic and with Swahili.** **④** **The nineteenth century.** The negotiations over international copyright turned translation from taking possession into authorisation; at the same time, colonial administration needed grammars, word-lists and translations of law codes. **Received account:** **this lesson's verdict: the two faces of the modern profession of translation were born at the same moment —** **one pays royalties to writers, one makes indexes for an empire.** **⑤** **The first half of the twentieth century to the 1960s.** War and exile made "writing in a second language" into the ordinary case; public experiments in machine translation appeared, and afterwards their funding contracted sharply. **Received account.** **A technological surge was broken off by one assessment report, and for the twenty years after that the field was carried on by a few laboratories.** **This is the most important precedent this lesson gives for "AI and literature":** **the failure of a technology is often not a failure; it is funding withdrawn.** **⑥** **The 1970s to the present.** The cultural turn, postcolonial translation studies, "the travel of theory" becoming a phrase in common use, regional prizes requiring that a work on the shortlist have a translation, and the spread of translation by models. **Received account.** **The close of this chapter:** **a way of putting it that took shape in the early 1980s — theory travels too, and on the journey changes its use — has now become the everyday working language of world literature;** **and a discipline's highest achievement is to make its terms so ordinary that no one any longer remembers that they were once a claim.** **Chapter 4's "becoming an idiom = the rate of circulation rises, and the meaning goes to zero" here completes the last lesson it gives to the whole course.**
:::

::: warning Four pits in this chapter
**Treating three characters from the end of the nineteenth century as a tool of analysis.** **Received account: the three-character maxim that is often cited is a wish about proportion, and it contains no operable criterion;** **it is serviceable because everyone can agree to it, and it is useless for just that reason** (above, clause ① on "fidelity"). **Writing a technical choice up as a moral question.** **"Betrayal", "fidelity", "the beauty of treason": these words rewrite a negotiation as a test of loyalty** (above, ①: **every moral word that makes the discussion shorter deserves suspicion**). **Replacing analysis with a rate of leakage.** **Figures of the sort "only a few per cent of the books are translated" are often cited without stating the basis of the count, the year, or the range of languages** (Lesson 3, a numerator with no denominator: **the number is not wrong; the people who use it to draw a conclusion have often not done the division**). **Forgetting that a translation also has authors.** **The translator, the editor, the person in charge of the series and the funder make up the real group of authors of a foreign work;** **however, in the first ten chapters, we treated the word "literature", so, in this chapter, we should treat the three characters 某某著 ('by So-and-so')** (above, grades of pay, ②).

::: exercise Short essay: the accounts of world literature {level=3}
Write eight hundred to twelve hundred characters, assessing "**the way a work becomes world literature is that it becomes useful in a succession of other people's hands: useful to a reform of a language, useful to the market position of a series, useful to the syllabus of a course, useful to the outward image of a policy. What is called universality is the sum of these concrete uses; and what is left after the sum is called its literariness — which is nothing but that small remainder which, after every use has been exhausted, still cannot be used up**". You must draw on at least six of: **the three changes of object**, **the polysystem regularity**, **the untranslatable and the not worth the cost**, **relay translation and self-translation**, **the three ledgers of cutting and alteration**, **mistranslation as a home asset**, **the structure of the translator's pay**; and you must decide: **whether this description in the form of accounts cancels the concept of "value"** (if all greatness is produced by being used, can we still say that some work is better — you must give a formulation that can keep the accounts and yet does not take the accounts for the whole, and say in the face of what kind of counter-example it would fail).

::: solution
Marking points: seven mechanisms (**object, system, the untranslatable, relay translation, the three ledgers, mistranslation, pay**); the decision (**the core of an answer that passes is to keep two things apart:** "being used" explains circulation, and "the remainder" explains why, after its uses are exhausted, it is still used again; **so a formulation that is neither naïve nor cynical should meet two conditions: ① it grants that any work which gets read has accounts; ② it keeps a criterion that history can test —** for example: after a work has left its original uses (a reform of language, the positioning of a series, a political image), it is still taken up again, and each time it is taken up it produces a new conflict of interpretation; **this criterion can be checked, and so it is not mysticism** (**what the marking looks for is whether the student has written the operable form "still taken up after its uses have been left behind", and not something equally impossible to test, of the kind "true art transcends its age"**); a counter-example on which it fails (**one must be given:** for example, some bestselling text that is used only in its original context and that thereafter nobody at all reprints — **the method of accounts can explain its popularity and cannot explain its disappearance, unless features internal to the text are brought in; conversely, a manuscript that no institution has ever used, and that an individual reader has preserved, exposes the blind spot of the method of accounts** (**to give either kind is full marks**); if the conclusion can be written "**so the end of this course is not to set a price on any work, but to require that every pricing come with an itemisation: who translated it, who cut it, who taught it, who cited it — once the itemisation is public, we can still say that a work is better, but what we are saying is no longer 'everyone says so', but 'after its uses are exhausted it still cannot supply an answer', and this is a thing that can be checked together**", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
The master ledger, filed: object (**changing the object of study matters more than changing the conclusion**), system (**it asks only about usability, and not about truth-value**), the untranslatable (**first ask whether it is not worth the cost, or whether it is impossible**), relay translation (**a book's third language**), the three ledgers (**censorship leaves a precedent; judgement leaves only one tactful sentence**), mistranslation (**it can be a home asset, and it moves a work's homeland away**), pay (**a person who does not share the return is not asked to sign**). One sentence for the whole lesson: **from Homer to today, literature has never travelled alone; all along the road it has carried the names of translators, editors, book lists, censors and funders, and our task is not to celebrate this journey, but to fill these names back, one by one, into the line for the signature.**

:::
