title: "The Bible and the Middle Ages"
---

This course cannot assume you have met either of its objects. **So the chapter starts with two pieces of bookkeeping — how one library became "the Bible", and how a thousand years got named — and only then looks at the literary techniques that medieval Europe invented and still uses on us.**

## Text, canon, translation {#def-zhengdian}

::: definition Three layers of assembly for one book {#def-sancao}
**Start with the word: "book" is singular in English and wrong. The Bible is a library assembled in three layers, and the layers are the first thing to learn. ① Composition**: texts written over centuries in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek, many of them compiled from earlier sources and none of them by a single hand in the modern sense. **② Canonisation**: communities selected, ordered and closed lists — Jewish circles with differing boundaries, Christian canons including a Greek translation, the Septuagint, whose books the Reformation later moved to an appendix — **so "the Bible" is a decision with a date, and the dates differ by community** (**standard account**: canon lists and councils are the evidence; the process is long and not uniform). **③ Translation**: from Hebrew into Greek, into Latin (the Vulgate, late fourth century), then into the vernaculars, each step changing what can be said (**standard account**). **Three consequences worth the space: ① a "quotation" from the Bible is a translation's quotation, and much theological argument is an argument about a word in one language pretending to be a word in another.** ② **The ordering of books is an interpretation**: putting a gospel fourth, or the epistles before the Acts, is a claim about the story. **③ Canons close and are reopened — and every reopening is a literary event**, since it decides which texts will be read for the next four hundred years. **The rule for this course: whenever you meet a sacred text, ask first which list it belongs to.**
:::

::: definition Inspiration and the copyright of a text {#def-qishi}
**The standard account**: tradition attributes the Pentateuch to Moses, the Psalms to David, the Pauline letters to Paul; modern scholarship denies single authorship for most, and the debate over the letters is technical. **Read the attributions as a theory of authorship rather than as bad attribution. ① The name attached to a text is a warrant, not a receipt**: naming Moses authorises a law code; naming David authorises a hymnbook; naming an apostle authorises a letter in a dispute about who speaks for the community. **② Anonymity is not a loss but a genre**: many works are composed in the voice of an earlier authority (the scholarly term is apocalyptic or pseudonymous writing), **which is a claim about the text's use, not an attempt to deceive a bibliographer** (**standard account**: the phenomenon is well documented and its ancient ethics are not ours). **③ Inspiration relocates the author** — if the text is breathed by God through a writer, then the writer's biography is a secondary object, and the reader's job is to explicate rather than to explain. **This is the deepest difference between the tradition this chapter opens with and the one the Chinese courses taught: not "influence" but "inspiration", and the second removes the author from the chain of responsibility and installs the interpreter in her place** (the criticism course's whole argument about who controls the text, arriving with a different metaphysics).
:::

## The technology of interpretation {#def-jieshi}

::: definition Four senses, three senses {#def-sizhong}
**The standard account**: medieval exegesis distinguishes the literal sense from the spiritual, and the spiritual into allegory (what you believe), tropology or the moral sense (what you do) and anagogy (where you are going); a couplet usually cited in the schools says the letter instructs events, allegory faith, the moral sense conduct, anagogy the end. **Do not treat this as a quaint filing system; it is a machine with four outputs and one input. ① It makes any detail significant** — a worm, a borrowed cloak, a wrong-side-of-the-bed detail must mean something, because the text cannot be idle. **② It makes the text a single book**: the link between an event in one century and a teaching in another is not similarity but a plan, which is what typology does (next section's example). **③ It allocates authority**: to say which sense you are reading in is to say who may decide — the literal sense can be discussed by a grammarian, the allegorical requires doctrine, so a dispute about method is a dispute about jurisdiction (**standard account**: the same mechanism operates in any commentary culture, including the classical tradition of the Chinese courses). **④ It generates literature**: because the machine demands correspondences, it produced a thousand years of vision, dream-vision, emblem and allegorical narrative — the tradition whose end this course will reach in the next chapter.
:::

::: definition Two consequences of one method {#def-liangguo}
**The method that made everything meaningful had two long-run results, one creative and one destructive. ① Creative: a culture of commentary and gloss**, in which a text is printed surrounded by its interpretation, and in which writing new work means working inside an inherited set of figures; **the page as a community across time is a visual fact about medieval books and not a metaphor.** ② **Destructive: the authority problem.** If the text has several senses, then somebody must say which is meant, and that somebody is a church; **when the identification of that authority becomes disputed, the remedy offered by reformers — the text alone, in its plain sense, read by the individual — collapses the interpretive machinery that had produced a literature, and does so permanently** (**standard account**: the Reformation's relation to the literal sense, and the Wars of Religion that followed, belong in any literary history, not only in doctrinal ones). **Three secondary effects that this course will keep meeting: a vernacular bible creates standard literary languages; a protestant reading public creates mass literacy and therefore censorship systems; and the abandonment of allegory leaves the nineteenth century to invent symbolism all over again.** **The lesson's generalisation: a method of interpretation is an institution, and when the institution falls the literature loses a set of available forms — which is why you cannot explain a change of style without a change of authority.**
:::

## Genres {#def-wenlei}

::: definition Hagiography, chanson de geste, romance {#def-sanlei}
**Three genres of the Latin and vernacular Middle Ages, each with an audience, a venue and a standard of the real. ① Hagiography, the saints' life** (**standard account**: collections and their Bollandist editors are the modern infrastructure): its purpose is not biography but proof of sanctity, so its plots are conventional — renunciation, trial, miracle, death, relics — and **its evidence is repetition: a wonder that happens to three saints is more credible, not less, because it shows the same power at work.** A modern reader's instinct to check facts is a category error; the correct critic's question is what the pattern claims. **② Chanson de geste** (**the Song of Roland is the canonical case): a long assonanced narrative for public performance, built from formulaic half-lines, whose subject is the loss of a good man and the guilt of a king** (**standard account**: the oral-formal argument from the last chapter is at its home here). **③ Romance (roman)**: verse adventure in octosyllables, with a courtly audience, written by named poets, and organised around the knight's desire and its obstruction rather than around the loss of a hero. **Compare the three: the first proves sanctity, the second mourns a community, the third educates a court; and note that only the second was thought to be about history — the claim "these were real events" attaches to the genre least able to survive it.**
:::

::: definition Courtly love: an invented grammar of feeling {#def-gongting}
**The standard account**: in the twelfth century, poetry in southern France addresses a married lady at length, praises service to her, describes a love that is unfulfilled, secretive, ennobling and obsessive; critics have argued ever since whether this describes a social practice, a literary convention, or an allegory of religious love. **The chapter's three claims. ① It is an invention in the strong sense — a new syntax for a feeling, and syntax is what makes a feeling sayable**: before it, a poem could praise beauty or mourn separation, but not the specific structure of desiring someone whose status makes the desire impossible and who improves you by refusing you. **② The scholarly argument about whether anyone actually lived it is answerable and has mostly been answered: the poetry itself is the evidence, and its audience learned the grammar from the poetry** (**standard account**: this is now close to the mainstream and still controversial at its edges). **③ Its descendants are uncountable and unacknowledged: the novel's love plot, the pop song's addressee, and the professional courtesy of a service industry that calls you "my lady" once in a while.** **The methodological payoff: this is the cleanest case in the course for the claim that literature does not express feelings but supplies them** — and it is worth holding beside the last chapter's finding that the Chinese tradition's lyric voice is likewise a set of forms the poet enters.
:::

::: example Typology and xing {#ex-yubiao}
Two pairings. **A**: a flood in one book and a ritual washing in another are read as one pattern, the first "prefiguring" the second. **B**: a poem sets mulberry leaves in spring beside a wasted field and a woman's face, without saying why (**the technique the Book of Songs calls xing, as the pre-Qin course described it**). **Compare the two operations and say which one can be wrong.**

::: solution
**Both are associations that generate meaning by juxtaposition: A across time (an event in one century and an ordinance in another), B within a scene (a plant and a feeling). Both presuppose a world in which such pairs are not accidental.** **The difference is in the account given for the pairing: A claims a plan — a history written by an author, in which the earlier event was designed to point forward — so A is a strong claim about reality, and it can be wrong** (the pairing either is or is not part of the design; and the design is what the community says it is, which is how the method becomes jurisdiction). **B claims nothing: the leaf does not signify the face, it is set beside it, and the poem's force is that the reader has to make the connection without instructions — which is why it cannot be refuted, and why the Chinese commentator tradition spent two thousand years deciding that some of these pairings were political allegories after all** (**standard account**: that is the xing-interpretation debate of the Odes, and it is the same machine as A, applied in reverse — a figurative reading imposed on a non-figurative form). **Marking: whoever writes that the two are the same operation judged by different institutions takes an A**, and the course's comparative point is here: **what looks like a difference between "symbolism" and "imagery" is often a difference between a culture with a single authorised reading and one with a commentary tradition that never quite closed the list.**
:::
:::

::: intuition The Middle Ages are an invention {#def-faming}
**The period was invented by the people who came after it, and the word is a confession. Three facts. ① The name is a periodisation with a value attached**: Petrarch and his successors spoke of a time of darkness between the ancients and themselves, and the noun "media aetas" is a Renaissance coinage — **so a medievalist teaching "the Middle Ages" is teaching a Renaissance argument** (**standard account**). ② **The period is far too long and too various** — a thousand years, several hundred languages, and no self-description shared across them; a scholar of the eleventh century would not recognise the century before the Black Death as her kin. **③ The invention was necessary, and this is why the field cannot simply discard it: a literature needs a before and after, and the "dark" age supplied early modern Europe with the contrast that made its own self-description work.** **Three other inventions of this chapter belong in the same file: "the Renaissance" (a nineteenth-century historian's interpretation of a period), "the feudal age", and "the Christian century" of any nation's history.** **The chapter's transferable test: whenever a period name does double duty as an evaluation, ask who benefits from the darkness — and note that the answer is usually the period after.**
:::

::: history Four handings-over of one book {#sec-zhuanshou}
**First transfer, Hebrew into Greek** (from the third century before the common era): the Septuagint, made in Alexandria for readers who no longer had the original languages, **and it is this version, not the Hebrew, that most New Testament writers quote** (**standard account**) — **a decisive literary fact: the vocabulary of Christian Europe was set by a translation made for immigrants.** **Second, Greek into Latin** (late fourth century): the Vulgate, whose revisions and printings then govern the Western text for a millennium (**standard account**). **Third, Latin into the vernaculars** (twelfth to sixteenth centuries): Wycliffe's circle, then print and the reformers' bibles, which fix national literary languages partly because translating the Bible requires deciding what ordinary words mean in the highest register (**standard account**). **A fourth transfer must be recorded, and it is not Christian: Arabic and Syriac scholarship preserved, translated and commented on the Greek philosophical inheritance, and Latin Europe recovered Aristotle through that route in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries** (**standard account**: the transmission routes are complex and partly contested; the fact of dependence is not). **The chapter's closing observation: the "tradition" this course is describing was assembled by four acts of translation and one of borrowing, each of them a loss and a gain, and each of them resented by somebody in the receiving language — which is the last chapter's argument about translation, stated at the scale of a civilisation.**
:::

::: warning Four traps in this chapter
**Treating "the Bible" as one book** (**ask which list**); **correcting a medieval attribution as though it were a modern one** (**the name is a warrant**); **reading hagiography for facts** (**repetition is its evidence**); **forgetting that your period names are inherited arguments** (**who benefits from the darkness?**).
:::

::: exercise Essay: authority and the available forms {level=3}
In six hundred to a thousand words, discuss the claim that **a literary culture's stock of available forms is regulated less by taste than by the authority that decides what a text may mean. When that authority is secure, allegory is free — everything can mean more than it says, and literature flourishes in the margins of commentary. When it fractures, the plain sense becomes the only safe sense, and a thousand years of figures go unreadable within two generations, to be revived later as decoration rather than as method.** Deploy at least six of: **the three layers of assembly**, **the warrant theory of authorship**, **the four senses and their jurisdiction**, **the two consequences of the method**, **the three genres and their standards of the real**, **the invented grammar of courtly love**, **typology and xing**, **the four handings-over**. Then adjudicate: **is any of this visible in your own reading present — and name one form currently available only because some authority is still secure.**

::: solution
Marking: six mechanisms; the adjudication (**the named form must be an argument, not an example**). **Strong answers look for a genre that depends on an unchallenged interpretive authority and is therefore free to be allusive: for instance the national or constitutional narrative, whose literal sense nobody disputes and whose figures are therefore available** — **or the sacred text read in a still-devotional community, whose allegorical use remains live** — **or the research-paper form itself, whose "method section" is a jurisdiction claim that very few readers are entitled to contest, which licenses the ironies and citations the genre permits.** **Highest marks for noticing the asymmetry: the allegory that survives in a secular age is the kind that no institution can veto, which is why it migrated to fiction, to the novel, and then to the advert** — **and for the closing corollary, which is the chapter's: a form's availability is a political fact about reading, and a literary history that ignores authority is a list of tastes.**
:::
