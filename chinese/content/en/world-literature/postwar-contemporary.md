---
title: "Post-war and contemporary literature"
---

The modernism of the previous chapter survived on little magazines and residence abroad; the literature of this chapter runs on prizes, paperbacks, agents, university chairs and the mimeograph—**the argument of this chapter is that, after 1945, the institutions which decide how far a work can go outweighed, for the first time, the kind of tradition in which it was written; this is also the age in which "world literature" turned from a wish into a budget**.

## Definition: rupture is a narrative {#def-duanlie}

::: definition "Year zero" and the ledger of continuity {#def-lingnian}
**Received account** (**In postwar Central Europe there appeared an extremely powerful claim: everything starts from zero; it has long been counterbalanced by another body of research: the same editors, the same publishing house, the same paper, and the same set of rules for academic posts often carried on** (**this course's three readings** (**①**the narrative of rupture is an application for qualification (to say "we have gone back to zero" is so as not to have to explain what one was doing yesterday; its beneficiaries are precisely the people who profited yesterday (this is the first lesson of this chapter, and also the whole course's ordinary way of handling claims of the "new era" sort (the opening of Chapter 12, on contemporary Chinese literature, will show the same table (②it is at the same time true (cities, readerships and the organs of censorship did indeed change; so the right question is not "whether year zero is true or false", but "which column broke and which did not": equipment and personnel did not break; international copyright and the status of languages did (any assertion of "a literary mutation in such-and-such a year" must be put through this column-by-column audit (Lesson 3: a change in the total, with no columns, is meaningless); ③its opponent is memory (received account: for the decades that followed, half of the important literary work was argument over how this war was to be handled (this course's reading: when neither the law nor the church of a society assigns responsibility, literature is pushed forward to serve as the court—the previous chapter's Russian "the journal as parliament", and this chapter's "the novel as a court": the same mechanism in a variant elsewhere (Chapter 3, "the family as a court", is contemporary China's third model).
:::

::: definition Three new institutions {#def-sanjigou}
**① Paperbacks** (**Received account: they had already appeared before the war, and after the war they burst out because of the price of paper, the channels, and readers coming home from the army; books began to be sold at kiosks, stations and supermarkets** (**three consequences** (the fall in the unit price made "one-off consumption" possible; channels outside the bookshop require the cover to persuade a person within three paces; and "bestseller" became, for the first time, a ranking that can be counted week by week (this course calls this "the birth of the ranking": from the moment it appeared, the field in which literature is judged gained a figure that no one can deny (the modern starting point of Chapter 1's "sales as evidence" (②the writing workshop and the degree (Received account: in the 1930s American universities brought creative work into the curriculum, and after the war this spread across the world; "creative writing" became the name of a degree (its far-reaching consequence is not that more writers appeared, but the invention of a technical vocabulary that can be taught and graded (point of view, suspense, arc, scene-unit (this course's judgement: when a craft can be graded, it converges; this is the first variable in explaining why the forms of the novel in different countries today resemble one another—it is more direct than "the influence of translation", because what it changes is the stage of production (the writing templates of Chapter 14, and the "golden three chapters", are the automated version of the same logic); ③the agent and international copyright (Received account: in the middle of the twentieth century there appeared in Europe literary agents who handled copyright for many countries, and a number of authors using lesser-used languages thereby gained a position from which to negotiate across borders (this is the perfect tense of the whole course's "the author as an asset": the patron gives alms, the publisher buys outright, the agent takes a percentage—the third relation, for the first time, gave the author a motive to run the works as a long-term portfolio (this course adds one reminder: the rate of the percentage is itself an inequality; it is only more transparent than alms (the contemporary version of Chapter 5's "price list").
:::

::: definition The shelf-name precedes the work {#def-huojia}
**Received account** (**The label called "magical realism" today was, at the start, a word coined in the 1920s by a German-language art critic for a style of painting; thirty years later it was applied to Latin American narrative, and in the 1960s, by way of critical essays and publishing marketing, it was fixed as a shelf-name for the whole world** (**a four-step trace** (**①**a word borrowed across fields (the language of painting reviews entered literary criticism, at the cost that its original meaning (the New Objectivist manner of painting) is almost never looked up again; ②travel across languages (German → Spanish → English, and each step widened the extension; ③acceptance by those who are named (a number of writers and critics used it as a programme—this is the same type as Chapter 7's "the opponent coins the word, and the person claims it"; ④adoption by the market (publishers found that the word could locate readers, and so it gained immortality on the back cover and in reviews (this course's verdict (not one Latin American writer writes according to this word; it is the result of the European market's need for a drawer (this is not to say that those works are bad, but to say: their global position is decided by a borrowed label, and the power to define that label lies on the side of the language translated into (Chapters 11 and 18 will write this up as a general rule: writers in lesser-used languages, in order to enter world literature, mostly have to accept a name simpler than their own works.
:::

::: definition A book and its opening sentence {#def-kaipian}
**Received account** (**A Latin American novel of the 1960s is known for a famous long opening, and its time is folded between three layers; it first appeared in a newspaper in fragments, then became a book, and was quickly translated into many languages** (**this course's two columns, technique and institution** (**① on the technical side** (to set "many years later" beside "before one's eyes" is to replace a causal relation with a relation of memory; the reader is forced to give up "what happens next", and to turn instead to "how all this has always been so" (the reverse of Chapter 5's "system of advances": *Dream of the Red Chamber* gives the ending first; this book gives, first, the tone of an ending (② on the institutional side (serialisation in a newspaper gave it a testing ground, European publishing houses gave it a shelf, and a transnational system of literary prizes gave it, some years later, the standing of a classic (the direction of causation this course requires: not "it was so good that it spread across the world", but "it met, at the same time, what each of three channels needed"—the newspaper wanted fragments that could be read, the publisher wanted a name that could explain a whole continent, and the prize wanted an author who could be translated (this sentence may stand as the whole course's standard answer to "how a classic becomes global".
:::

::: definition The short story as a philosophical device {#def-duanpian}
**Received account** (**The same Argentine writer established his standing in the world with short stories, essays and fictional book reviews; in his later years he went blind, and he served as director of the National Library** (**this course's three techniques** (**①**the pseudo-annotation form (to write a review of a book that does not exist: this is the purely formal version of modernism's "inversion of the annotation"—the annotation no longer explains a text; the annotation is the text, and the object it explains does not exist—it pushes the privatisation of citation to the limit: a writer's learning can be entirely self-certifying); ②length as an ethical choice (he does not write long novels, and he has texts that defend brevity; this course refuses to read this as a limit of ability, and refuses to read it as lofty aloofness: it is a decision about production: a short story can circulate through journals and anthologies, and it does not ask a reader to put in two weeks without a break (in the translation market the apportioned cost of translating a short story is higher, so its circulation depends on anthologies and on universities (this sentence explains why he is stronger in the academy than on the bestseller list); ③the library and the labyrinth (his central images are two infinities: a catalogue that can be exhausted, and a branching that cannot; this course's use of them: they are the twentieth century's earliest imaginings of the "database", and they are also this chapter's hook for the reader of the twenty-first (Chapter 1, that the catalogue is censorship, and Chapter 14, algorithmic recommendation, are joined into one line here: what he wrote is exactly the thing we live inside today.
:::

::: definition Treating the classic as the accused {#def-beigao}
**Received account** (**In the second half of the twentieth century there appeared a group of novels that "write back" to famous works: a prehistory for the woman shut in the attic in a nineteenth-century British novel** (**three judgements** (**①**writing back is an audit, not a homage (it does not deny the greatness of the original; it demands that the original's schedule of assets be checked: whose house, whose property, whose silence (Chapter 6's "cash-flow statement" becomes a political tool here); ②it must be a parasite, and so it admits subordination (its title and its characters all come from the book written back to; this is exactly the source of its power, and its ceiling: a perfectly parasitic work cannot exist apart from its host—this course therefore requires the reader always to read the two books side by side); ③it turns world literature into a closed arena of debate (one way into world literature is to comment on a book already inside it (and so a classic is not brought out by being read; it is kept at body temperature by being rewritten again and again (the strongest variant of Chapter 16's "retrospective recognition": the original becomes a quotation in the sequel.
:::

::: definition Withdrawing from a language {#def-tuichu}
**Received account** (**An East African writer, after the works in English that made his name, announced that he was giving English up, turned to writing in the language of his own country, and turned towards popular theatre; over the decades that followed he wrote in English again, and explained why** (**this course reads this as one sanction and one accounting** (**①**the sanction worked (the withdrawal of an author who had made his name made the question of "writing in the colonial language", for the first time, an object of discussion across the world; it shows that the choice of a language is not only a convenience, but a question of which hall one belongs to (the other side of Chapter 8's "residence abroad exempts one from an audience": to remain inside an imperial language is to set the review bench in someone else's city); ②the cost can be calculated (to withdraw is to give up the readers of world literature, the advances on translations, and eligibility for prizes (his experiment succeeded in the theatre, and was limited on the book market (this course's verdict: a language is a kind of pass; to give it up is to cancel one's own visa; and the issuer of that pass is elsewhere); ③the return is not a defection (explanations from the later period are often reduced to a contradiction; this course reads them as a plainer discovery: in a world made of translations, one person may belong to two readerships at once, and neither of the two is complete (Chapter 18, on Chinese-language writing abroad, will take this as its entire premise).
:::

::: definition The output of the mimeograph {#def-youyin}
**Received account** (**After the war, unofficial channels of publication appeared in a number of countries: "self-printed copies", handwritten or typed and then carbon-copied; publishing houses in exile beyond the border; and underground presses. Their output is counted in tens and in hundreds** (**this course's three points** (**①**the print run decides the literary form (a work that can be printed in only two hundred copies cannot carry a long work in serial; hence the composite form "short story + notes + reading aloud at a gathering" (Chapter 6's "first ask by what it is sold" has an extreme form here: not sold, only lent (and "lending" requires that the text can be copied, remembered and passed on); ②what is inspected is the equipment (Received account: prosecutions in cases of this kind are mostly brought on the ground of "illegal printing", and not of the content (this course's observation: when a regime would rather charge the typewriter than charge the sentence, it is already admitting that the former is far more provable than the latter (Chapter 6's "what can be located can be convicted", used the other way round); ③what it means for the digital age (the mimeograph is the first "copying device with a low threshold"; every discussion today of "a platform changes the review bench" is a rerun of this section (Chapter 14 will run this account: when the cost of copying falls, first the genre shortens, and then authority moves house).
:::

::: example A famous sentence that is misquoted {#ex-mingyan}
A proposition of the twentieth century is often quoted, to the effect that "after a certain massacre, to write poetry is barbaric"; those who quote it do not usually read the original context, and the original has something further to add. Complete three tasks: **① set out the verifiable part of this sentence, and the layer of legend; ② explain why this misquotation has more historical force than the original; ③ point out two common functions, in literary study, of this sort of "quotation turned into a maxim"**.

::: solution
① The layers—the verifiable part is this: a philosopher, in a work in the form of notes from a certain period, set down a judgement on the relation between culture and barbarism, and in a later text revised it or set limits to it; the layer of legend is this: it is often retold as a prohibition, and used to require that writers be silent (Received account: what this course does is to quote it, but to note who has quoted it, and in what context (the layering of sources in Chapter 1 comes down, here, onto one famous sentence (②why the misquotation is more effective—because it is a tool that can be used at once: it is short, it has authority, and it cannot be rebutted (the original argument takes a hundred pages to read; the misquotation needs only one silence (this course's formula: the historical force of a maxim has nothing to do with how accurate it is, and everything to do with what it can fend off, and for whom (the colloquial version of Chapter 4's "review bench" (③two functions—the first is a release from responsibility (quote one authoritative sentence, and you need not judge the text); the second is admission (people who know how to use the ellipsis of this sentence recognise one another as members of the same trade (Marking: **to have written the second function is a pass—**because the whole institutional analysis of this chapter points at this one sentence: **most of what is said in literary circles is not there to make a work clear, but to confirm who is inside** (**this is also this course's most honest account of "theoretical cant". It does not mean to abolish these quotations. It only asks the reader, each time one is heard, to ask first: is this sentence, here, an argument, or a password?**).
:::

::: intuition If that book were published today {#int-jintian}
**Do a chapter's exercise once more, and change the object to a book from this chapter** (**an author who uses a lesser-used language writes a long novel whose time is folded, on a subject that touches the history of a continent; submit it to four channels of today: a large English-language publishing house, a translation-subsidy programme, a series from one independent house, and a multilingual audiobook platform** (**four replies that can be expected** (**the large house: first have a translator with a reputation, and a list of reviewers** (**that is, the risk must be borne on your side**); the subsidy programme: it can be funded, but no promise is made about the print run (that is, it pays money but does not pay for a channel); the independent house: a slot in September, and translations are already scheduled into next year (that is, a queue); the audio platform: a hook is required every six minutes (that is, the text has to be remade (this course's conclusion (the success of that book of the 1960s depended on a combination of institutions that has today partly gone: newspapers willing to serialise, publishers willing to gamble on a translation, prizes willing to give one continent one name; today's combination is this: a translation must have a prize first, a platform must have data first, and "a name for one continent" has already been used up (this sentence is not nostalgia. It is an observation that can be checked: the marginal cost of entering contemporary world literature is rising, while the terms for authors already in the stock are improving—this matches the change in the structure of income from copyright, and it also explains why "the belated discovery" has become the commonest literary news of our time (Chapter 16, retrospective recognition: it is now the rule, not the exception.
:::

::: history Six segments of institutional history {#sec-jigou}
1945–1949 (the founding of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organisation, the war trials, and a number of international conferences of writers (Received account: "peace" and "propaganda" appear side by side in the documents of one and the same institution; this chapter gives no motives, and records only facts: much of the infrastructure of postwar world literature was built in this period out of funds that had both a cultural-department background and an intelligence background (this is a subject scholars have long discussed; this course lists it as an account still to be checked); ②the 1950s (the expansion of the paperback, a number of works tried in English-speaking countries over sexual description and successful in those cases, and the theatre of the absurd staged in Paris (Received account: the name of one school comes from the title of a work of criticism, and that work, taking drama in French as its object, was written in English (naming after the fact, for the fourth time); ③the 1960s (the wave of Latin American books and of translation, the rise of the agent, the publication of novels that write back, and a public argument about "which language to write in" (Received account: this year is also the age in which, in a number of countries, systems of literary censorship and policies of export subsidy expanded together (this course: censorship and subsidy are two valves on the same machine); ④the 1970s (informal publishing in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union, a playwright from Eastern Europe becoming a head of state, and a novel about totalitarianism becoming a secondary-school text in the English-speaking world (Received account (this course asks only one question: when a dissident writer is elected, what happens to the review bench of dissident literature?—this is an open question the chapter leaves to the reader; Chapter 12 will give the case of the same type in contemporary China); ⑤the 1980s (the expansion of the politics of memory and of the market for memoirs, the establishment of "postmodernism" as a category on the curriculum, and one controversy about reality (Received account: the classic texts of postmodern theory come, for the most part, out of French, and by way of the English-language academic system are turned into a curriculum for the whole world (this is one more demonstration of how terms travel); ⑥the 1990s to the present (further international prizes, e-books and self-publishing, the institutionalisation of subsidies for translation, and text generated by models coming onto the market (Received account: the literary "contemporary" includes, from here on, a new category of author: the non-human (the close of this chapter: we do not judge whether this is a crisis or a convenience; we only point out that the method of this whole chapter still applies—first ask who pays, who makes the record, and who has not been recorded. The question is just as good for what a machine writes; only the answer has become simpler.).
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**Treating "world literature" as a bookshelf rather than as a question** (**its books are arranged by translation, by prize and by series** (the clause on the opening sentence, above: **first ask whose wall that shelf is**)); **treating writing back as plagiarism or as homage** (**it is an audit, and it has its own rules of evidence** (writing back ①, above; **to read it as a study of influence is exactly to miss its political content**)); **putting "literature has dealt with memory" in the place of analysis** (**it may only be filling a gap left where, somewhere else, responsibility is not assigned** (rupture ③, above: **when literature takes on the work of a court, first ask why the court is not there**)); **putting the difficulty of getting in today down to the quality of the work** (**not one of the four letters of reply in the intuition above is judging the text** (**they are all channels; this is the most important reminder in the chapter, and it is also this course's answer to the question "why does no one read the book I like"**).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: who makes world literature possible {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing "**From the second half of the twentieth century on, a work's standing in the world has been decided by three services: someone translates it, someone bears for it the risk of distribution across borders, and someone gives it a name in a room it cannot hear. All three services are supplied by institutions, and all of them are paid for by other people; so-called world literature is therefore not so much the worldwide reach of works as the worldwide reach of services—it copies, exactly, every inequality of the other service trades: the centre supplies the standard, the periphery supplies the raw material, and the fee for translation is always put last**". You must call on at least six of these: **the column-by-column audit of the rupture**, **paperbacks and the ranking**, **the writing workshop**, **the agent and international copyright**, **the travel of the shelf-name**, **writing back or withdrawal**; and you must rule on this: **whether the judgement that "the periphery supplies the raw material" is too cynical** (you must give a counter-example, show how the centre is rewritten by the periphery, and state what trace that rewriting left in the institutions.

::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (audit, ranking, workshop, copyright, label, writing back or withdrawal); the counter-example (what a counter-example must share, if it is to count, is that it changes the form of the centre, and does not merely hand the centre a subject: fragments in a newspaper, together with a book made across borders, brought a syntax of folded time into the ordinary practice of the novel in English; one author's withdrawal from an imperial language made "language policy" an issue for the English-language literary world itself; mimeograph copies and publishing houses in exile made "literature of dissent" into a kind of course taught across the world, and into a translation industry (the institutional trace must be written in concrete terms**: series, grants for translations, university courses, the statutes of prizes, and the division of labour in the copyright-agency business** (**the marking looks at whether you stop on the word "influence"**—**to say only "it influenced" scores nothing; you must say which layer was changed: the stage of production, the stage of evaluation, or the stage of distribution** (**this is exactly the tool that all eight chapters of this course train over and over**); if the conclusion can say "**so this chapter does not define world literature, but only opens a service list for it: who translates, who advances the money, who gives the name, who pays, and whose name is printed on the cover at the end—fill this table in, and you will never again call an unequal result universality**", that is an A.

::: summary
The service list, filed: rupture (**a column-by-column audit; do not trust the total**), paperbacks (**the birth of the ranking**), the workshop (**it can be graded, and so it converges**), the agent (**a percentage is more transparent than alms**), the label (**the market wants a drawer first**), writing back (**treat the classic as the accused**), the mimeograph (**a literary form that is not sold, only lent**). The method in one sentence: **when you read a work that is "of the world", first count the three services that have paid for it**.
