title: "Romanticism"

In the previous chapter literature learned to hold a meeting; in this chapter it declares that holding a meeting strangles the soul — **and the sentence that matters most in this chapter is this: the Romantics' struggle against institutions ended in the founding of new institutions; today we call that set of institutions 'creation' and 'copyright'**.

## Definition: a word's triple jump {#def-santiao}

::: definition From a literary form to a temperament to an industry {#def-ciyi}
**The received account.** The original sense of this word is 'in the manner of a romance', and it names a property of a literary form; at the end of the eighteenth century it was used to name a kind of character and a temper of the age; after the middle of the nineteenth century it became a product category of the culture industry. **The three consequences of the triple jump.** **①** The meaning of a critical term can drift across into its opposite. 'romanesque' was originally a word of blame, 'absurd and baseless'; it jumped to a word of praise, and then jumped back to a label on a commodity. The two-way cycle, in Chapter 6, of lowering and raising in meaning is completed here in units of fifty years. **②** To name a movement is to issue it a programme. The received account: a controversy in fragments, carried on in a periodical, completed the Romantics' naming of themselves. Before this, no one was a Romantic; **this sentence applies to every chapter in this course that carries an '-ism'**. **③** The industrialisation of a temperament has a fixed route. A way of life set against the market is packaged as an image that can be copied, and sold: **long hair, black clothes, melancholy, insomnia, an early death**. This lesson calls this 'the wholesale of poses': the Romantics invented the image of the artist, and the image earns more than the work — **this is the chapter's only answer to 'why the Romantics won'**.
:::

::: definition Reversing the stigma: selling an illness as an asset {#def-wei}
**The received account.** In the medical–theological classifications of the Middle Ages and the early modern period, melancholy was an illness, and even a hotbed of vice; in the eighteenth century it was revalued as the mark of genius. This lesson writes that process out as an asset-valuation sheet. **①** The original reading. Melancholy = an imbalance of the humours = a near relation of laziness and despair. Its costs: failure in society, loss of one's post, worry from one's family. **②** The mechanism of the reversal. It cannot be overcome by the will, and therefore it proves that this person 'is not under his own command' — **and 'not being under one's own command' is precisely the definition of genius**. The received account: in the same period there appeared talk of the sort 'genius is not acquired; it is unconscious, and given'. **③** The new obligation after the reversal. You must look as if you are in pain, and you must not look as if you are making an effort. Out of this comes the strangest professional discipline of the Romantics: **one must not appear skilled**. This lesson's verdict: **every field that prizes the 'untrainable' produces this discipline;** today's 'gifted player', 'playing oneself', 'not open for business' and 'an air of ease' are the same bookkeeping. Lesson 10 and Lesson 14 will meet it again, **and every time it creates rent for a few**.
:::

::: definition The fragment, the symphony, and 'the unfinished' {#def-duanpian}
**The fragment.** The received account: periodicals of the early nineteenth century took numbered fragments, dialogue and stray thoughts as their form, and held that 'being in the course of formation' is itself higher than the finished work. Three reasons. **①** The unfinished compels the reader to take part. The meaning of the work is no longer inside it, but between it and the reader; **this is the ancestor of twentieth-century reception theory** (Chapter 16 will settle this account). **②** It is a solution to censorship. A fragment has no thesis one can point to; **a deleted fragment is harder to plead for than a deleted essay**. The sister technique of Chapter 4's 'the footnote's two sets of books'. **③** It is a new tempo of publication. The periodical comes out month by month, and the fragments are arranged by number: this format gave philosophy a serial for the first time. This lesson's blow at the present: **a sentence of a hundred and forty characters, deliberately left unfinished, is the same form as the numbered fragment of two hundred years ago; the difference lies in the manner of settlement —** the fragment circulated through the salon and the periodical, and the repost circulates through platforms that count. **The same form, a different settlement:** this sentence may be used once each in Chapter 1, Chapter 9 and this chapter, and each time it is new. **②** 'The infinite' and the sublime. The received account: a word which until then had belonged to rhetoric became a core category of poetics, and landscape thereby changed from background into protagonist. This lesson's technical analysis: once 'the infinite' becomes the standard, description must keep raising the stake — **this is the financial cause of the self-exhaustion of later Romanticism**. An inflation of the imagination: every work must be more sublime than the last, until the whole category goes bankrupt (Chapter 1's 'outbreak of rhetorical species' is given here a complete demonstration of a winding-up in bankruptcy).
:::

::: definition The footnote: a scholarly annex in the lyric {#def-jiaozhu}
**The received account.** Certain long poems of the nineteenth century are famous for a mass of notes by the poet himself, who explains his own imagination in a scholar's voice. This lesson reads this as one case of 'mixing the accounts'. The lyric belongs to the account of 'what cannot be verified'; the footnote belongs to the account of 'what can be verified'; to bind the two together is to declare that the poet holds both authorities at once. Three consequences. **①** Humour is institutionalised ahead of time. The poet's own notes often trample the seriousness of the poetry to pieces: **this is an anti-romantic technique invented by the Romantics themselves**. **②** It supplies copyright with a chain of evidence. The notes record the sources of the inspiration, what was seen and heard on the spot, and the list of books read. How does a person prove that a long poem was not copied? **By the annexes.** The cleverest case of Chapter 1's 'a signature is a contract which has to be kept'. **③** It exposes the crack in the Romantics. Genius ought to find its own way, with no teacher; the footnote shows that he has books. This lesson's conclusion: **the Romantics' real contradiction is not 'feeling and form', but 'inspiration' and 'property rights': one requires the work to have no source, the other requires the work to have a source —** and our law, to this day, stands on the side of property rights.
:::

::: definition A little book that set off imitation {#ex-vierte}
**The received account.** At the end of the eighteenth century an epistolary novel gave rise, in Europe, to rumours that the young were imitating its dress and its ending, and it was banned in a number of regions. This lesson's treatment. **①** The strength of the rumour as evidence has to be discounted. 'It taught people to kill themselves' is the standard sentence-form of a moral panic, and the part of it that can be checked is very small. Lesson 3: count the survivors at eight-tenths, and count the accusation at eight-tenths as well. **②** But it did create a precedent. From then on, 'the novel does harm' became a claim on which one could legislate: **literature was recognised as a social variable with causal force —** this is the paradox of this chapter: the censor's vote raised the standing of literature more solidly than any book review. Another making-good of Chapter 1's 'literature comes to set its seal'. **③** It invented 'the reader as a dangerous group'. What had been feared before was a large audience of listeners, and a large audience of spectators; what is feared here is one person, alone, late at night, and a book. This lesson's judgement: **'private reading', in the modern sense, was for the first time treated as a public problem —** because it really is a new freedom, and every new freedom receives its first summons.
:::

::: definition A vote in a theatre {#ex-ounani}
**The received account.** In the Paris of the 1830s, on the night before the premiere of a Romantic play and inside the house, supporters and critics stood against one another in a long confrontation. This lesson's three readings. **①** This is a public re-election of the judges' bench. The previous chapter said that rules are the discipline of a meeting; here someone rushes into the meeting room and seizes the seats. **The quarrel between the Romantics and the classicists is, in the end, an armed debate about 'who counts as the judge'.** **②** It divides the audience into camps that have names. The media for the first time reports 'who is in the box': literary factions become an antagonism in the manner of sport, and the antagonism itself can be ticketed. This lesson calls this move 'confrontational release': when a work will not sell, turn its opponents into the show. **The ancestral text of Lesson 16's 'a slanging match is traffic'.** **③** It proves the price of victory. Once the seats have been changed, the new judges at once begin to lay down new rules; **in the very year the Romantics won in the theatre, their anti-institutional pose became an institution**. **This is the concrete year of that sentence at the opening of this chapter, 'ended in the founding of new institutions'.**
:::

::: intuition The factory of inspiration {#def-gongchang}
**The received account.** The Romantics' theory of genius, and the copyright law running from the end of the eighteenth century into the nineteenth, were established in the same period. This lesson sets the two out on one sheet. **①** 'Inspiration' and 'property' are a pair of partners. To prove that a work belongs to you, the strongest thing to say is not 'I wrote it', but 'only I could have written it' — **an inalienable uniqueness is precisely the best collateral for a property right**. And so the Romantics' mysticism and the bourgeois law of contract complete one another. **②** Today's structure of payment exposes this story. The content industry pays for 'capacity', and does not pay for 'inspiration': daily updates, weekly updates, quarterly assessments, indicators. **The story writers tell about themselves is still the Romantics'; the pay sheet is still the copyist's.** This lesson's verdict: **this is the everyday tool the chapter leaves the reader:** whenever someone explains an allocation of pay by 'innate gift', ask what the other nine people did. **③** And we cannot, for that reason, mock the Romantics. Only after they had raised the word 'imagination' to the standing of an organ did people have a legal language in which fiction resists reality. Liniang in Chapter 2, *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* in Chapter 7, and this chapter share one charter here: **imagination is not an escape; it is a claim of jurisdiction**.
:::

::: history A polycentric movement and one merging of the accounts {#sec-hebing}
**1770s—1790s.** Germany: the second edition of a tragedy, a periodical, and a band of literati of an undergraduate sort. The 'Jena group' existed for an extremely short time, and yet it is the first case in Europe of 'a literary movement whose form of organisation is a periodical'. **1798—1830.** England: the preface to a collection redefined poetry as 'the natural outflow of intense feeling', and added a further clause, 'feeling recollected in tranquillity'. The received account: this limiting half-sentence is often left out of the textbooks. This lesson's reading: **Romanticism's first programme came with its own brake;** **every text that writes 'the spontaneous' up as theory will, in the second half of the sentence, fetch discipline back**. **③ 1800—1830.** France: a critic translated a German word into French and was besieged for it; a confrontation in a theatre. The received account: when a literary concept travels from one country to another, it always brings a foreign enemy with it. **This is the general exchange rate of world literature:** **half the value of an imported concept lies in its power to make the locals quarrel**. **④ 1800—1860.** Russian Romanticism and narrative of the nation, the Polish poets in exile, and the collecting of folk songs in the various countries. The received account: the idea of 'the voice of the nation', in the line of Herder, supplied the motive for gathering folk songs, fairy tales and epics. This lesson's judgement: **this is the weightiest item in the whole course:** **the commonplace that 'every nation has its own literature' is a product of this collecting movement;** **it made literature into evidence of a qualification for nationhood**. Chapter 11's work on translation, and Chapter 18's 'who is qualified to speak a language', both draw their wages from here. **⑤ After 1830.** 'Romanticism' became a historical period; realism lives on its failure. The received account: **every successor movement needs a body of Romantics to oppose, and what it opposes is the version it needs** — this lesson's closing: **so we cannot 'finish the telling of Romanticism'; we can only tell of those who have used it, generation by generation**. **This is precisely the core technique of Chapter 16's 'retrospective ratification'.**
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**Treating 'Romanticism' as a kind of mood.** It is a set of arguments about genius, imagination, the nation and property rights (① and ② above: **a mood cannot explain why the copyright law and the theory of genius appeared at the same time**). **Confusing the Romantics with nature.** What it believes in is the imagination, not the natural world: **landscape is an object to be read, not an object to be studied** (the sublime clause above). **Taking 'every country has a Romanticism' as evidence of a world phenomenon.** This lesson reads that as the consolidated bookkeeping of later scholarship (④ in the history above: **the neater the consolidated statement, the more one must look at which subsidiaries it wrote off**). **Using the Romantics against the Romantics.** Irony and the poet's own notes are techniques of its own (① under the footnote, above: **to take its mockery of itself as evidence that 'it had already negated itself' is a cheap combination in two moves**).
:::

::: exercise Pricing a pose {level=1}
List three poses of language or of behaviour which are valued at present as 'not trying', 'at ease' and 'not open for business', and in 100 characters point out: **who collects rent by means of it, and who pays the training fee for it**.

::: solution
Marking: **the party that collects the rent and the party that pays must be two kinds of person**. If you write 'everyone benefits', you are treating a valuation as a description (③ of reversing the stigma, above).
:::

::: exercise Write a fragment {level=2}
Write a text of no more than sixty characters, deliberately left unfinished, and attach one sentence of explanation: **which half you want the reader to supply for you, and what you would lose if it were retold in full**.

::: solution
Marking: **you must set down 'what is lost'**. If the answer is 'the sense of mystery is lost', that is too abstract: you must name a kind of gain, such as being pressed with further questions, being quoted, being required to explain (① of the fragment, above: **the unfinished compels the reader to take part**).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: imagination and property rights {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing '**The doctrine of genius is a chapter of the doctrine of property rights: when a thing is to become property, it must first be proved to be unique, and the proof that saves the most trouble is not to explain how it was made, but to explain that it cannot be made. The Romantics' fear of mechanical reproduction, their worship of inspiration, and their pursuit of authenticity are therefore not a remnant of the pre-modern, but an advance in the technique of describing assets; the same technique is still running today, only its name has changed from "inspiration" to "original content" and "personal brand"**'. You must call on at least six items: **the word's triple jump**, **the asset-valuation sheet of reversing the stigma**, **the double function of the fragment**, **the footnote's chain of evidence**, **the contemporaneity of the theory of genius and the copyright law**, **the collecting movement of national literatures**; and you must rule: **if 'uniqueness' is a technique of property rights, ought the study of literature to give this concept up? (what giving it up costs, and what keeping it costs — you must take a side)**.

::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**the triple jump, reversing the stigma, the fragment, the footnote, contemporaneity, collecting**); the ruling (**Abandoning it: one gains a sociology that can be checked, at the cost of being unable to deal with differences of experience inside the text —** two people receive one and the same education; why does only one of them write this book? (**this question cannot be answered by a theory of property rights**); keeping it: one can explain the first-hand facts of reading, at the cost of taking a historical product for a natural object (**the marking looks at whether a third workable path is pointed out —** treating 'uniqueness' as a result that waits to be explained, rather than as a tool of explanation: **ask which mechanisms allow a given text to resist copying, and set the answer out in three layers: technique, institution and syntax**) — this is what the chapter really teaches; if the conclusion can be written '**so this chapter is not closing the books on the Romantics, but checking the notes of a currency that is still being printed: whenever someone claims "this is a thing that cannot be taught", ask what it is making capable of being owned**', that is an A.
:::
