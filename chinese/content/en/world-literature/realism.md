The people of the previous chapter demanded the infinite; the people of this chapter demand accounts — **but the first sentence of this chapter is a negation: realism is not a technique of 'describing things as they are', because no age has lacked people who describe things as they are; it is a declaration newly appeared, a public pledge of a contract between the novel and the real, and a political programme that was still being cited into the twentieth century**.

## Definition: three phrasings of a declaration {#def-shengming}

::: definition The mirror, the ledger and the court {#def-jingzi}
The received account (in the France of the mid-nineteenth century a set of claims bound up with one another appeared: the novel should write contemporary manners, should write the nameless, should study society by a scientific method, and should furnish evidence as history does (three phrasings (① the mirror (a novel is said to be a mirror walking along the road (the received account: a metaphor of the Stendhal kind) — its emphasis does not lie in 'reflecting', but in 'who has the right to say that the mirror has reflected wrongly' (the mirror does not accept opinions; it accepts only facts) — this is a coup in rhetoric: it moves literary dispute from taste to evidence (② the ledger (a multi-volume monument styles itself 'historian of society', and claims that it will write out manners, occupations, property and articles of law (the received account: the programme of the preface to Balzac's *The Human Comedy*) — this lesson's reading: this is the first time in literary history that someone made quantity of output into an argument: more than ninety books completed, characters appearing again and again, names forming an index — a collection of novels becomes a database that can be queried (Course 3's 'a list is a state that refuses to fall apart' here becomes an inventory of assets (③ the court (the novelist on trial (the received account: in the Paris of the 1850s a novel and its editor were prosecuted for 'corrupting public morals' (the received account: Flaubert and the *Revue des Deux Mondes*) — the prosecution requires the text to have a meaning that can be pointed to; the defence holds that meaning lies within the style, and that style cannot be excerpted) — this lesson's core verdict: this trial invented the technical conditions of the modern novel: when a book can be located down to one particular sentence, it can be convicted; and so a great generation began to write sentences that cannot be located (Lesson 9's 'the author's silence is the legal condition of irony' here receives its European written judgement).
:::

::: definition Free indirect speech: the sentence no one claims {#def-zhiyou}
**The received account** (**a narration which, without adding an introductory word and without changing the tense, mixes in a character's tone of voice; the nineteenth-century French novel made it routine** (**this lesson takes it apart into three devices** (**①** the transfer of the cost (direct speech is cheap and clear: over a stretch, one person is speaking; free indirect speech is expensive and blurred, and the price is that the reader must judge for themselves who is thinking this (so the novel, for the first time, outsources 'the labour of judgement' to the reader (this is a milestone of narrative technique: it promotes the reader from listener to co-author (② a structure of exemption (no character signs this sentence, and therefore no one can be prosecuted); ③ it makes 'many voices' the defining experience of the novel (the received account: the line that follows Bakhtin reads the essence of the novel as the coexistence and the clash of many social voices (this lesson's use of it: the reason realism seems 'deep' is not that it writes psychology, but that it prints a social opinion and one person's belief into the same sentence, and does not mark the source — this sentence could be used as the thesis title for this chapter).
:::

::: definition Serialisation: the reader as a co-author with a vote {#def-lianzai}
**Structure** (**the received account: serialisation in the monthlies and the dailies of nineteenth-century Britain and America sold the novel by the segment, and authors revised their drafts according to circulation and to readers' responses** (**three consequences** (**①** suspense is a commercial apparatus (a hook is left at the end of each instalment: this is the same mechanics as the storyteller's script (*huaben*) and its 下回分解 ('to be continued in the next chapter'), and as the golden three chapters of online literature (Chapter 9 and Chapter 14: instalments change the sense of time); ② characters come back to life (the received account: a certain best-selling novel, because readers protested at a shipwreck ending, had its ending rewritten when it was issued as a book — this event is often cited, while the strength of the causal claim is often exaggerated (this lesson's handling: it does not deny the author's will; it only points out an institutional fact: the serial system turned 'changing one's mind' into a commercial decision that could be defended); ③ length is a function of price (the number of pages in the three-volume novel is fixed by the format of the subscription contract (this lesson's formula: do not ask why the nineteenth-century novel is long; ask by what unit it was sold (Course 2's 'length is a physiological quantity' and Chapter 14's 'payment by the thousand characters' are here joined into one line: the first-ranking explanatory variable of literary form has always been the way of charging).
:::

::: definition A new role: the humanities scholar as witness {#def-zhuren}
**The received account** (**in the later nineteenth century there appeared a group of writers who intervened in public cases by means of literary reputation: a novel about child labour and the poor laws pushed discussion of legislation forward; a novelist published an open letter in the period of a wrongful case** (**the received account: a long text which Zola addressed to the head of state at the height of a military miscarriage of justice**); **three levels** (**①** literature for the first time possesses moral capital that can be transferred (a novelist's name can add to a petition something other than a signature (this is the most important sociological fact of this chapter: 'the intellectual' is an office, and this office was first held by the novelist); ② the rate at which the capital is cashed varies with the medium (the same article does not have the same effect in a newspaper and in a pamphlet: and the author immediately discovers that the most effective use is to print it as a separate volume and sell it (the moral stance and the market act are completed in one and the same operation: this lesson does not call this hypocrisy; it calls it an exchange rate); ③ the bonus added by legend (the received account: the causal relation between an anti-slavery novel and a war is often fixed in place by a joke said to have come from a president's mouth (this is the exercise in layering historical material which the whole lesson uses again and again: the layer of fact is the sales and the record of speeches in parliament; the layer of legend is that half-sentence of anecdote; and textbooks have tended to keep only the anecdote).
:::

::: definition A door and a corpse {#ex-men}
Two pieces of stage and narrative apparatus: **the first**, a modern northern European play ends on an accusation about 'responsibility' and on the sound of a door closing; **the second**, a French novel opens with a panoramic description of an apartment building, and writes out, storey by storey, the residents' class and their smell. Explain: **what argument each of these two physical details completes, and why both are more effective than a character's confession**.

::: solution
**The first (the closing of the door** — **the ending of the play hands the power of verdict to a sound event:** she does not defend herself; she leaves (**the stage version of Lesson 9's 'the author's silence':** the verdict is not announced by the author; it is announced by a slam of the door; **the received account: the anecdote that this slam of the door is said to have once drawn cheers or whistles outside the theatre** (this lesson treats it only as the layer of legend, but points out its function: it proves that the audience had already taken a play as a social resolution and voted on it (the second (the apartment — the opening description does one piece of sociological work: it puts the characters inside prices (the rent, the storey, the wear of the furniture, the smell of the stairs: before a character speaks, the reader already knows what he can afford (this lesson's core criterion: the 'depth' of realism is often the depth of accounting: a character's tragedy is seldom proved; it is usually priced (why more effective than a confession — a confession is one person stating; a room is a system stating; and a system will not be prosecuted (this one sentence at the same time explains why the three metaphors of this chapter, the mirror, the ledger and the court, are one thing: realism invented a narrative authority of the kind 'the impersonal witness' — it is more credible than any character, because no one claims it).
:::
:::

::: definition The marriage market: where realism's sense of the real comes from {#def-hunyin}
**The received account** (**in nineteenth-century Europe there appeared a view of marriage whose ideal was feeling and personal choice, while women's rights in property and the possibility of divorce were for a long time restricted; the main line of a great many realist novels is inheritance, annuities, the dowry and a respectable income** (**three readings** (**①** the real engine of the plot is the transfer of cash flow (who has a dowry, who inherits, who must work: this lesson suggests that the reader draw up a cash-flow statement for a realist novel — you will find that the turns of the plot coincide closely with the changes in income (② 'love' as an institutional vocabulary (the received account: in this period it became the principal language of legitimacy for marriage (this lesson's judgement: the rise of a language of legitimacy often happens at the same time as the old standard which it replaces: when property is no longer openly a condition, it becomes a condition that must be present and that cannot be spoken (this point can be used directly in analysing contemporary discourse of courtship and marriage (③ it determines the choice set of the female characters (the women in the novels are more clear-headed than the women in social history, because their situation is an income risk: Madame Bovary, Anna, and a number of 'improper' heroines, the charge against whom is for the most part financial: they spent that sum which was not theirs — this lesson's definition of the 'novel of adultery': a narrative about property held in common and the lack of a right to dispose of it).
:::

::: definition Naturalism: borrow the method and spoil the conclusion {#def-ziran}
**The received account** (**a school of the later nineteenth century which held that the novel should be written by the methods of observation and experiment, represented by a cluster of novels about alcohol, labourers and mining districts** (**this lesson's three parts** (**①** what it borrowed was authority, not method (the physiology of the time supplied it with the word 'experiment'; and a novel cannot run a controlled experiment: it can only manage 'sufficient detail' (and so method became a kind of rhetorical guarantee (the modern version of Chapter 5's 'making poetry out of learning'); ② its real invention is a quota of subject matter (washerwomen, miners, drunkards and shop apprentices enter the centre of the long novel (this lesson's verdict: what literary history calls a revolution in taste is, most of the time, a revolution in quotas: the objects described did not change; the list of objects that can be enumerated grew (Chapter 6, 'style', and Chapter 8, 'variants', join on here); ③ its failure, too, can be taught (a determinist narrative requires the environment to explain everything, while the plot requires the character to make a choice: so the naturalist novel often, at the most crucial point, covertly abandons the premise, and lets the character 'suddenly awaken' — this lesson calls this 'the theory's missing entry': any critical theory which guarantees itself by science will, in its own works, expose the one item it cannot pay).
:::

::: example An audit of the 'real' {#ex-shenji}
Choose a passage of description famous for the 'real' (realist or naturalist, either will do), and complete three audits: **① list the details in it that have a narrative function and the details that have no narrative function; ② suppose the latter are deleted, and say how much information and how much 'sense of the real' then remain in the text; ③ point out by which kind of authority this description obtains trust**.

::: solution
**① Two columns** — **functional details: they advance the action, they limit the conditions, and they expose a character's power of disposal**; details without a function: colour, smell, objects, unrelated parts of the body (the point is not to delete them, but to discover the form in which they exist: they are usually replaceable (this is the source of the 'sense of the real': a replaceable detail is written as irreplaceable, and the reader then reads 'reality'); ② the remainder — information is almost not lost, while the sense of the real collapses sharply; from this follows the technical conclusion of this chapter: 'the real' is not an attribute of content; it is the way detail is presented (the full proof of Chapter 4's 'detail without a function is the fingerprint of a genre'); ③ types of authority — there are three candidates: seeing it oneself (witnessing of a journalistic kind), method (observation of a scientific kind), and the impersonal (a systematic statement in the manner of free indirect speech); this lesson requires you to judge which of them it is, and to state its cost: one who appeals to having seen it must answer for the facts, and therefore can be overturned by the facts; one who appeals to method must answer for the procedure, and therefore can be reviewed by peers; one who appeals to the impersonal answers for no concrete thing, and therefore can hardly be refuted (marking: whoever can write the sentence 'irrefutability is precisely its advantage in the market' has an A for this chapter — this sentence is at once the achievement of realism and its danger).
:::
:::

::: intuition One line of arithmetic about disillusion {#def-huanmie}
**Why every succeeding movement takes realism as its target** (**the received account: the modernism of the twentieth century, the New Novel and postmodern criticism attacked it one after another under the names 'reflection theory', 'verisimilitude' and 'the hegemony of realism'** (**this lesson gives three pieces of arithmetic** (**①** its success made it the default (any praise that is called 'objective', 'daring to write' or 'facing reality' is using the vocabulary it invented; the mark of a default is that it cannot see itself); ② its contract is revocable (the reader can, on the same shelf, change to another contract: this is the technical precondition on which the experimental literature of the twentieth century is able to exist); ③ it has never stopped producing new unreality (in the most successful works of realism there is often an intensity that cannot be believed: Balzac's exaggeration, Dickens's caricature, Flaubert's lyricism (this lesson's conclusion: realism did not abolish rhetoric; it successfully concealed rhetoric — so the work of each later generation is not 'to return to the real', but 'to expose an old concealment' — this is also the sixth general law which this lesson gives to the whole course in world literature: the way literary history advances is to turn the manner of writing that was the default last time into an active choice this time.
:::

::: history A movement named posthumously {#sec-mingming}
1820s—1840s (the rise of the philosophy of history and of social statistics, and the ambition of 'the study of manners': the novel for the first time declares that it is a kind of knowledge (the received account: the first batch of reports of social investigation, appearing in the same year, shared their readers with the novel); ② 1850s (a label was put into use in Paris by one periodical; a novel was put on trial (the received account: the term and the trial fell in the same year: this is not a coincidence; it is two sides of the same thing — when a way of writing is summoned by a court, it has to have a name); ③ 1860s—1880s (the expansion of serialisation, the renting of printing plates and popular books; naturalist manifestos and polemics; the problem play of northern Europe (the received account: Europe came to have a mass reading market in the true sense (this lesson's reminder: the canonisation of realism did not depend on its being high; it depended on its occupying the high end and the low end at the same time); ④ 1890s—1930s (theoretical disputes about 'realism' reached their peak in the intellectual circle of a political exile (the received account: the polemic of Lukács and of the Brecht camp concerning expressionism (the most important historical fact of this lesson: the golden age of 'realism' as a combat concept came several decades after the death of its so-called masters; the name of every literary period is the product of the disputes of later ages (Chapter 1 and Chapter 5 are here joined into one line); ⑤ 1950s—the present (official policies on literature and art, in one country after another, which take 'realism' as their guiding principle, and the Third World's re-requisition of it (the received account: a label from the Paris of the nineteenth century became the common currency of global literary policy in the twentieth century) — the closing of this lesson: this is both the proof of its power and its bankruptcy as a concept for analysis — so this chapter teaches you to use its techniques (quotas, the impersonal, cash flow), and not to use its name).
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**Taking realism as the mark of 'maturity'** (this is an evolutionary narrative, and this lesson has already dealt with its logic in Chapter 11; it writes a contract of genre as a grade of ability (history ④ above)); taking the density of detail as evidence of objectivity (the worked example is the counter-evidence: replaceable detail, written as irreplaceable, produces a sense of the real, not the real) ②); using 'it reflected society' in place of analysis (it leaves out the whole of the work: quotas, the format of serialisation, the trial, the exchange rate (the three places above are all set up for this)); forgetting that its contract is still in force (the three characters 太假了 ('too fake') in a film review are still a nineteenth-century contract being performed (disillusion ① above) — the default cannot see itself.
:::

::: exercise Short essay: the real is a way of presenting {level=3}
Write from six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing '**realism's greatest invention is not what it wrote, but that it made a kind of trust possible: the reader is willing to believe that the words before them are manipulated by no one. In law this trust is called an "unclaimed statement"; in narratology it is called objectivity; its name in the market is called true. Every question, therefore, which asks "how does literature reflect reality" is asking in the wrong direction; the right question is: under what conditions does a group of people agree to read a certain artefact as evidence**'. You must call on at least six items: **the mirror and the ledger**, **the trial and the defence by style**, **free indirect speech**, **the serial system**, **the marriage market and pricing**, **the posthumousness of the naming**; and you must rule: **whether the habit of 'reading the text as evidence' also governs today's non-fiction writing and the 'shot for real' of short videos — you must give an example that is happening now, and state its difference from the nineteenth-century contract**.

::: solution
Points for the mark: the six mechanisms (the declaration, the trial, the impersonal, serialisation, pricing, naming after death); a contemporary example (examples that qualify: a handheld shot and its shake as proof of the 'impossible to manipulate', a long take that has not been edited, screenshots and original files, the irrevocability of a livestream; the difference from the nineteenth-century contract must be written out: **the impersonal of the nineteenth century is obtained by syntax, and today's is obtained by 'the absence of traces of making'; the former lets the author disappear into the rhetoric, the latter lets a machine testify in the author's place**) — the marking looks at whether this layer is pointed out — **this is the end point of this lesson: the witness is changed from the narrator to the recording device, while the structure of trust is exactly the same**); if the conclusion can be written as '**so what realism has left us is not a style, but a trust that can be withdrawn at any time: once someone asks "is this true?", they are already performing a contract signed in the nineteenth century**', that is an A.
:::

::: summary
The contract, filed: the declaration (**the mirror turns a dispute over taste into a dispute over evidence**), the trial (**what can be located can be convicted**), the impersonal (**the labour of judgement is outsourced to the reader**), serialisation (**first ask by what unit it is sold**), pricing (**tragedy is often arrived at by calculation**), naming (**canonisation happens after the funeral**). The method in one sentence: **do not ask whether it is true or not; ask under what conditions it is read as evidence**.
