---
title: "Asian, African and Latin American literature"
---

The previous chapter said that world literature is a list of services; this chapter folds the list back to where it stands, and looks at the literatures **not covered by that same batch of services** — and the sharpest sentence of this chapter is: **"Asian literature" and "African literature" are not objects of study; they are shelves; to judge that a region's literature "arose late" is, most of the time, only because we take the novel as the measuring-rod**.

## Definition: the region is an administrative unit {#def-quyu}

::: definition Who drew this continental boundary {#def-zhoujie}
**Received account** (**Cutting the literatures of Eurasia and of Africa into four or five "regions" was the joint work of the comparative disciplines of the nineteenth century and the publishers' lists of the twentieth; before that, local people had only "the poems we write", "the documents of the yamen", "the scriptures of the monks" and "what is sung when the market meets" (**Three pieces of evidence (**①** **In the textbooks, literary history by period and literary history by region are of the same year** (Received account: the two kinds of syllabus, "national literatures" and "regional literatures", appeared together at the end of the nineteenth century, and the latter was often got by adding the former up; addition needs a common denominator, and that denominator is the administrative table of the colonial bureau (Chapter 10's "ethnic identification and the language survey were carried out in the same year" is the Chinese model of the same machine (② A prize will invent a region out of nothing (Received account: since the end of the twentieth century, a number of literary prizes taking a language or a continent as their unit have been founded; their shortlists are cited by reviews, and the reviews are then adopted by courses (This lesson's formula: a shortlist is a regional census: before it is nominated, "the novel in Arabic" is only a pile of national publications that do not deal with one another (Chapter 9's "the shelf-name precedes the work" is here upgraded to "the shelf precedes the work" (③ The problem of the measuring-rod (If one takes the short lyric or the oral clause as the standard, the "antiquity" of most regions far exceeds the lineage of the European novel; if one takes the long novel as the standard, they look young as a group (The whole method of this chapter lies in this one clause: ask which measuring-rod is being used, and only then ask early or late (This is this chapter's only criterion for every syllabus of world literature, and the worked example will make it good.
:::

::: definition Two Latins: the East Asian character and Western European Latin {#def-erlading}
**Received account** (**East Asia long had one written common language across dialects and across states: the Chinese character and the Classical Chinese (Literary Chinese; *wenyan*) canon; Western Europe long had another: Latin and the church–university system (**Four points of comparison (**①** **The same function** (Both sides make "the same speech" an unnecessary premise: two communities of speech that cannot understand each other can hold a brush-talk (*bitan*), just as two monasteries can read one scripture together (The strongest evidence for that sentence of Chapter 10: here not even "language" is the common term; writing is (② The same apparatus (Both sides are kept up by examinations, commentaries and subcommentaries, and the copying of scriptures: the canon is enforced by the system for selecting officials (Chapter 2's "whatever is examined is a classic" here obtains two independent cases (③ Opposite outcomes (The Latin of Western Europe withdrew from writing after literatures in the dialects arose; the Classical Chinese of East Asia, after the vernacular (*baihua*) had gained a written status, was still kept for a long time as an educational stratum (This lesson refuses to tell this as a difference of cultural character: the real cause is print and politics: an administrative system of one unified currency, and a movement of script reform directed towards alphabetic spelling, gave different answers in the two places (Chapter 9, Chapter 10 and Chapter 3 of this course join into one line here (④ The account left outstanding (Every difficulty in discussing "East Asian literature" today comes from this account: Japan, Korea and Vietnam historically wrote in Chinese characters and did not speak Chinese; so "in what language is it written" is, inside these texts, simply not a usable question (This is why this chapter treats the region as an administrative unit: when language, script and politics do not coincide, any map is a compromise (And the reason the map of Europe looks natural is that it has hidden this lesson better.
:::

::: definition India: an epic that does not close, and the theory of "suggestion" {#def-yindu}
**Received account** (**The two great epics were continually supplemented within the manuscript tradition, and never had a fixed text; at the same time, Sanskrit poetics developed a theory with "suggestion" at its centre: the beauty of a poem lies not in what the letter states, but in what the letter arouses and does not say (**Three points (**①** **"Not closing" is a mode of production** (**Continuations, local rewritings and translations into the dialects run side by side; the story of Rama became, in Southeast Asia, a stock of plots for court dance-drama and shadow theatre (**This lesson's reading: this is not many versions of one and the same book, but one book cited separately by several regimes: a cross-civilisation case of Course 2's "reuse licence"; here translation is not a derivative, and the form in which the text exists is itself translation (② The theory of suggestion is isomorphic with "implicitness is pressure" (The poetics on both sides place the value of the work on "what is not said"; but the institutional roles differ: Sanskrit poetics is the knowledge-trade of courts and religious orders, while the Chinese theory of "beyond the image" (*xiangwai* 象外) is bound to the literati's identity as connoisseurs (The same proposition finds its place in each of two structures of occupation (Chapter 8's "the elegant is the loser's dividend" has an Indian counterpart here (③ The measuring-rod breaks here (By the standard of the epic, the South Asian lineage is extremely ancient; by the standard of the long realist novel, it is "recent"; what actually happened is this: a poetics centred on "a stock of stories + suggestion" was measured over again by another measuring-rod centred on "originality + credibility" (This is the substance of the educational documents of the colonial era: they did not evaluate the works; they changed the measuring instrument (Chapter 11 will give the consequences of this document.
:::

::: definition Persia and Arabia: the signature set inside the form {#def-bosi}
**Received account** (**The opening of the Arabic long poem often stops on the remaining traces of an old camp or of ruins; the short lyric in the Persian form has a fixed opening line and closing line, and the closing line often places in it the author's pen-name or a wish (**Three (**①** **Evocation from ruins is an isomorphism across civilisations** (To begin while standing before the traces of a former dwelling has the same structure as the grief of "Drooping Millet" (*Shuli* 黍离), and as climbing the walls to brood on the past: first point out a place that does not recognise you, then speak of feeling (This lesson's explanation of this isomorphism is not "a feeling common to humankind", but a very banal one: in nomadic societies and in societies of the post road, the only warrant of memory is the landmark; the landmark is an archive (Chapter 8's "place-name = frequency", and place-name capital of the kind of the previous chapter's "poem-pillow" (歌枕) (② The pen-name in the closing line (The signature is not on the title page, but inside the verse form; this is the cleanest demonstration, in world literature, of the "author-function": you are allowed to be yourself only in the position the form permits (Chapter 1 and Chapter 3's "writing unemployment into the literary name" (*hao* 号) has a Persian model here (③ How an anthology becomes a classic (Received account: a number of important lyric anthologies were fixed by editors of later ages, and chronology and authenticity were long unclear; the "complete works" is the editor's work (Another recurrence of Chapter 1's "whom the fixed text is for" and of Chapter 6's "naming after death" (This chapter requires the reader, every time they read "the collected poems of so-and-so", to ask one question: who edited it, and for which controversy was it edited.
:::

::: definition Japan: one script, one place-name, one verse meeting {#def-riben}
**Received account** (**Literary Chinese (*hanwen* 汉文) was for a long time the writing of officials and of the canon; a syllabic script was current at court, and was used for diaries, informal essays (*suibi*) and one pioneering long novel; later ages trace the one genre of the "informal essay" back to these notes from the palace (**Three institutional lines (**①** **The gendered division of writing manufactured masterpieces** (Received account: men for the most part studied literary Chinese, and court women wrote in the syllabic script; thus the earliest and the most famous works of Japanese literature came from a group of authors excluded from the official script (This lesson's formula: when a genre is opened to those who are excluded, it becomes the technique of people of that kind; Lesson 20's Shen Qiongzhi (沈琼枝), "an income, but not a status", is the Chinese version of the same proposition; and this formula is the antidote to every claim of "so-and-so the genius" (② 歌枕 (*utamakura*): the place-name as an inheritable asset (Received account: a number of celebrated sites took their names from old poems, and later *waka* (和歌) had to mention them before they counted as proper to the form (This is wholly isomorphic with "Twenty-Four Bridges" (二十四桥) and "Yellow Crane Tower" (黄鹤楼): the place-name is not a backdrop; it is an account not yet written off (Chapter 3's "the list is a state that refuses to fall apart" here gets its tourist version (③ The verse meeting is an institution of review (Received account: gatherings of linked verse (*renga* 连歌) and *haikai* (俳谐) set topics by order of seating, comment on them, and keep the names of the topics; such a gathering is at once salon, journal and prize (The three venues of Chapter 4 find a compact model in Edo; and the travel diary of Matsuo Bashō (松尾芭蕉) and his company moved this kind of gathering onto the road, binding "style" to "route" (This sentence can be used directly to analyse writers' residencies and literary tour groups of the present — the same line of business, three hundred years earlier.
:::

::: definition Africa: the oral as method, the paperback as a continent {#def-feizhou}
**Received account** (**In the second half of the twentieth century, research on African oral art split into two schools: one recorded it as text so that it could circulate; one held that, once you leave the occasion of performance, there is no work; at the same time, a series of cheap paperbacks was selected and edited in London, and turned African writers who wrote in European languages into one world course (**Four readings (**①** **The dispute between the two schools is a dispute over copyright** (**The recording school makes the work able to be cited and sold; the performance school makes it able to be owned only by those who are present (**Chapter 10's "writing can be a community's private property" is restated here (This lesson does not adjudicate; it only points out: what is taught in universities today is the result of the recording school, while the cost of recording is borne by African scholars, and the return sits on the booklists of the North (② One series creates a continent (Received account: a certain paperback series took the campus as its distribution network, with the general editor and the editorial board in London (This lesson's verdict: this is the most blunt fact in the whole course: the literary canon of a continent can be finished, within twenty years, by the page-space decisions of a few publishing houses (It is not a conspiracy; it is a channel; Chapter 9's "three services" appears here in its purest form (③ One language decides the writing (Received account: the written alphabet of a certain East African language was officially adopted at the end of the 1970s, and within a generation after that a large written literature appeared (This is the limiting experiment this lesson offers for "the relation between script and literature": it is not that literature waits for an orthography, but that, once an orthography is in place, literature at once fills it (Chapter 10's "the making of a script" here gets a contemporary case that can be checked in full (④ Somalia and oral resistance (Received account: in the same region, before the alphabet was adopted, political mobilisation relied mainly on oral poetry and radio (This lesson's technical observation: when a society already has an efficient system of oral distribution, written literature is not a substitute but a competing product — this clause can be turned round to explain, for many regions, "why the novel is late": it is not that there are no stories; it is that the stories already had another means of distribution (This is this chapter's final answer to the problem of "genre as measuring-rod".
:::

::: example Change the measuring-rod {#ex-chizi}
For a region you know (you may choose any region often said to have had the novel "arise rather late"), do one thing: **① write the received line of dates that takes the long novel as its standard; ② taking instead the short lyric, the oral clause or drama as the standard, rewrite the same chronology; ③ list which works each of these two chronologies would render of no account**.

::: solution
**① The first chronology** — **it is usually written like this: antiquity is religious texts and epic texts; the modern period is newspapers and translation; the novel appears in the twentieth century; after that come realism, national literature and modernism (**Note its three implicit criteria: **written, original, credible (**② The second — if one takes the oral clause as the standard, the "history of texts" of the same region is at once pushed several centuries earlier, and its nature changes completely: there is no first edition, no author, no fixed text, and therefore no place for the concept of "influence" (If one takes the lyric as the standard, then it may be as exact as any European lineage; only, its criterion of judgement is whether it can be sung (The way of writing this lesson requires: you must give years and the names of genres, and mark the type of the source (manuscripts, stone inscriptions, sound archives, survey reports of the colonial period (Chapter 1, the layering of sources (③ Who is deleted — the first chronology deletes: reciters, women who write, authors who write in another script, and everything that never entered print; and the so-called "rise of the literature of some region" is precisely the process by which these several groups leave together (Marking (You must write a sentence that can be refuted: for example, "if somewhere there exists a recorded early long narrative, then this claim does not hold"; expressing sympathy alone scores nothing (Lesson 3, the ranking by refutability: to change the wording, and to change the measuring-rod, are both operations that can be tested; they are not the expression of an attitude.
:::

::: intuition If that script had not entered the court {#int-gongting}
**The rule for one counterfactual inference** (This lesson does not accept fancies of the kind "if there is no genius there is no masterpiece", but it does accept institutional counterfactuals: if that syllabic script had been used only for commentaries and subcommentaries on Buddhist scriptures and for account books, and if court women had not obtained a means of writing of their own, then the oldest long narrative of Japan would not exist in the form it now has (Three inferences (① It can measure out the real cause (We discover from this that the formal features of that work — long, inward, carried forward by letters and by *waka* — all come from a writing that was not used for official documents and did not have to answer to the court (The cause of a genre is its use, not the author's temperament (② At the same time it prevents romanticising (To be excluded is not to be free: the status of these authors was still a court post, and their writing still needed patrons and a circle of readers; to cancel the myth of the "court lady of genius" is also to cancel the myth of "writing because one is oppressed" (What remains is the institution (③ It can be used generally (Of any year in which "some group suddenly produces masterpieces", it is worth asking: did they happen just then to obtain a new means of writing, or a new channel of distribution? The answer this lesson gives is the same in East Asia, in East Africa and in eighteenth-century Britain: a new genre usually belongs to new readers, and new readers are usually the people whom the old genres excluded (This is the high point of this chapter, and the gift it gives to the next course.
:::

::: history Six changes of measuring-rod {#sec-liuci}
The first millennium (The two great epics circulated while being supplemented; Sanskrit poetics and the Chinese tradition of commentaries and subcommentaries each established a theory of "beyond the words" (*yanwai* 言外) (Received account: two critical traditions at the eastern ends of Eurasia arrived at the same conclusion at nearly the same time, and between them there is no path of transmission that can be shown (This is this lesson's exemplar of "convergence rather than influence" — the methodological discipline that matters most in comparative literature is here (② The seventh to the fifteenth century (The East Asian network that took the Chinese character as its common written language: brush-talk documents of envoys, monks and merchants; the circulation of lyric and of narrative, in the Arabic–Persian linguistic sphere, from Iberia to Southeast Asia (Received account: this lesson reminds us: these two systems are "world literatures" of the same age and of equal scale, and they meet mainly along the trade routes, rarely entering one another's shelves (Where the textbook fails is not that it ignores them, but that it pretends there is only one centre (③ The fifteenth to the seventeenth century (Literature in the court script, the maturing of the theatre, and the canonisation of one novel; the oral lineages of the Horn of Africa and of West Africa continue to expand (Received account (The right way to tell this stretch is in parallel, not as earlier and later (④ The eighteenth to the nineteenth century (Colonial administration, printing houses, missionaries translating the scriptures, and the local press (Received account: the first things printing produced in these regions were religious and administrative texts; the novel comes after the grammar book, and the grammar book comes after the orthography (This lesson's causal chain: script → grammar → newspapers → the novel → national literature; every step of this chain needs an institution, and no step needs a genius (⑤ The nineteenth century to the middle of the twentieth (The nationalist intelligentsia rewrote the literary history of its own country with Europe's list of genres: first look for an epic, then look for realism (Received account: this is the most decisive self-colonial change of measuring-rod in the whole course: local traditions of criticism were demoted to "aesthetics", and the measuring instrument from outside was taken as a universal standard (Take care not to read this as passive: these scholars at the same time invented a powerful modern literature; their error is only in the chronology (⑥ The second half of the twentieth century to the present (Independence, official language policy, regional prizes and series of translations; and a dispute not finished to this day: in whose language, classified by whose genres, translated by whom (Received account: This chapter's closing: of the six changes of measuring-rod, the first five were other people coming to change it, and the sixth is at last proposed by ourselves — and the proposal itself costs money, so we are still inside the same course.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**Defining "literature" as the long novel** (The measuring-rod clause of this chapter is set up exactly for this; this one definition shuts out two-thirds of humankind's oral and lyric making, and trains their descendants to be illiterate in their own culture (example ③ above ()); Explaining every isomorphism by "influence" (Evocation from ruins, and the theory of suggestion, arise independently on two sides between which there is no path of transmission (history ① above: half the work of comparative literature is to prove that there is no connection ()); Reading oral art as material for folklore (Its form of existence is the performance; a record is a rewriting, not a preservation (Africa ① above: every time you say "rescue", you are casting a vote for the recording school ()); Assuming that a boundary of language is a boundary of literature (The Chinese character, the Arabic alphabet and the Latin alphabet each crossed a number of spoken languages that cannot understand one another (the two Latins above: a phrase of the form "the literature of such-and-such a language" does not hold in East Asia from the very start (the several language lessons of Chapter 10 converge here.

::: exercise Short essay: who has the right to define literature {level=3}
Write from six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing "**To take the list of genres of one civilisation as a universal standard is the most successful institutional export of the colonial era: it does not even need to be enforced; it only needs to be written into textbooks and into the tables of academic posts. From then on a people can announce, in its own language, that its own past is not literary enough; and this announcement looks objective because the measuring-rod does not look like an artefact. The real task of literary study in the postcolonial era is therefore not to defend indigenous traditions, but to refuse to accept that question: not 'do we have epics and novels', but 'why do you think I need them**'". You must call on at least six items: **the region as an administrative unit**, **the two Latins**, **the Sanskrit theory of suggestion**, **the isomorphism of evocation from ruins**, **kana and the court authors**, **the oral as method**; and you must rule: **whether refusing that question is possible (inside an academic system in which one must publish, must apply for funds and must offer courses — you must give one real example of an institutional change, and say whether it only puts another measuring-rod in place of the first**.

::: solution
Marking points: the six mechanisms (**administrative unit, common language, the theory of suggestion, isomorphism, the division of writing, the oral**); the ruling (**a passing answer must acknowledge that "total refusal" is not feasible:** disciplines, journals, funds and posts all take classification as their premise; this is the consistent position of this chapter: classification is a pair of spectacles and is also a tariff (Lesson 1; **the feasible institutional change is to lower the compulsory force of a single measuring-rod**: multilingual bibliographies, with a requirement to read against the original; bringing oral clauses into the norms of citation; treating performance and text as two copyrights; and teaching two chronologies at once within a course (**the criterion of whether one has "changed to another measuring-rod" is here:** if the new institution still requires that each text can be placed in only one class, then it is only a change of rod; if it allows the same text to occupy one place in each of two chronologies, and both places count, then it is reducing the compulsion (**what the marking looks for is whether this distinction is written** — **this is the end point of this chapter, and the whole course's final answer to "what comparative literature is"**); if the conclusion can be written "**so this chapter does not propose that every region have its own literary history, but proposes that every measuring-rod be signed: which institution is using it, and which sum one can pay less by using it — once the rod has been signed, the question 'do we have any' lapses of itself, because it was never a question of fact**", that is an A.
