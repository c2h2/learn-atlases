title: "Modernism"
---

The novels of the previous chapter dressed arguments up as characters; the novels of this chapter begin to doubt whether words themselves can hold anything — **and the first thing this chapter sets out to oppose is telling it as a revolt of genius: for every breakthrough of modernism, one can find a sum of money, a periodical, a publishing house and a court judgement**.

## Definition: a name sealed only after the event {#def-mingzi}

::: definition 'High modernism' is a mid-twentieth-century course {#def-gaoji}
**Received account.** The authors of these works called themselves Imagists, Symbolists, Futurists and Expressionists, and attacked one another; the critical category that merged them into a single movement was established after the Second World War in the universities of the English-speaking world; from the 1970s onwards there were, further, people who used 'another modernism' to add back the authors who had been excluded. **Three readings.** **① The merger took place after the authors' deaths, or after they had gained the upper hand.** The polemics of the time were rewritten as the character of a period; **what a textbook needs is a subject, not a heap of disagreements.** The 'naming after the event' of Chapters 5 and 6 appears again here, for the third time, and this time the scale of the merger is the whole of Europe. **② Canonisation has an institutional location one can check.** Received account: a criticism whose method was close reading, a university syllabus and an anthology together completed this merger. **This lesson's formula:** the most effective way to make a book that is hard to read into a classic is not to praise it, but to open a course on which it must be read. 'Literature comes to affix the seal' in Chapter 1 and 'the review bench moves house' in Chapter 4 are here joined into one. **③ The supplementary entry called 'another' is not the correction of an error but a new budget.** Received account: the modernisms of women, of the colonies and of popular periodicals were taken in one after another. **This lesson does not lay blame for it:** it only requires that every expansion state clearly whose chair, whose series and whose foundation put up the money — Chapter 18 will measure the 'rediscovery' of Chinese-language literature abroad with the same ruler.
:::

::: definition Little magazines, bookshops and printing for oneself {#def-xiaozazhi}
**Received account.** In the Europe of the early twentieth century there appeared a batch of periodicals with tiny circulations, bookshops that specialised in selling poetry, and household presses for which writers set up hand-operated printing presses of their own. **Four institutional facts.** **① The reversal of the threshold.** Commercial publishers screened out works that were hard; little magazines screened out people who did not want to read; **the same kind of screening, the direction reversed.** This lesson calls this 'inverted peer review': **it does not test for qualification; it recruits believers.** The four venues of Chapter 4 are here reduced to a single editorial office. **② The subscription system keeps experiment going.** A few dozen subscribers plus one wealthy patron were enough to bring out a whole year of a periodical (received account: some periodicals were kept up by the taking of small shares, and the shareholders were the readers). **This is the latest version of 'the patron has changed':** **from one bishop to a hundred subscribers.** **③ One bookshop's experiment.** Received account: a bookshop in London that dealt specially in volumes of poetry, and was also a club, tried to create demand at the retail end. **A result worth remembering:** it was able to survive for twenty years, and yet it could not turn poetry into a business. **This lesson's verdict:** innovation in the channel can prolong a life; it cannot change a fate — **this point still applies to people who, today, make literary podcasts and keep independent bookshops.** **④ Printing it oneself.** Received account: a couple installed a hand-operated printing press in the living room of their own home, printed their own books first, and also printed the manuscripts of their own time that were hardest to get out. **This is the finest case of 'means of production' in the whole course:** when a printing press was cheap enough to be carried into the parlour, inspection lost its only throat — contrast Chapter 12: when the printing press returned to the hands of the state, the same sentence at once became impossible.
:::

::: definition Residence abroad is the default position {#def-qiaojv}
**Received account.** The biographies of this group of writers are highly alike: leaving their own countries, gathering in two or three cities, making a living by translations and the expatriate press, writing the dialect of their own country in a flat abroad. **This lesson's core proposition.** **① Residence abroad dispenses with an audience.** To write at home is to have to answer to a group of people; writing in London or Paris, one has, of the audience at home, only a dozen or so friends left: **'being hard to understand' is first a sociological consequence of residence abroad, and only afterwards an aesthetic choice.** The mirror image of Chapter 7's 'oblique writing': the Russian concealment was in order to get it said at home; modernism's concealment is because at home there was already no one to say it to. **② Residence abroad turns language into a tool rather than a home.** A writer who writes in a second language; a journalist who earns a wage between two languages. Received account: a considerable proportion of the writers in this group worked in a foreign-language environment. **And so 'whether words can grasp the real' is no longer a philosophical oddity, but an everyday experience** — this chapter's explanation of 'linguistic scepticism' lies entirely here. **③ Residence abroad produced the body of world literature.** Received account: the two world wars and the revolutions sent large numbers of authors into exile, and their manuscripts, translations and agents for the estate were thereafter scattered across several countries. The sentence at the end of Chapter 7, 'on its list of readers there is a long string of translators' names', this chapter will set down as a legal fact: **the nationality of the copyright and the nationality of the text can differ** — **this sentence will become the crux of translation studies in Chapter 11.**
:::

::: definition Four new devices {#def-sizhong}
**① The flow of inner speech without a lead-in.** Received account: in the same period, psychology described thinking as a 'stream of consciousness'; the technique of fiction, by contrast, is to set inner language directly into the paragraph, without a lead-in of the form 'he thought'. **This lesson's mechanism:** leave out the lead-in word, and you leave out the narrator's approval; **the reader has no way to judge whether this sentence is memory, hallucination or the present.** Chapter 6's 'outsourcing the labour of judgement' is here pushed to the limit: **even tense and ontology no longer stand as guarantees.** Set against Course 2's 'the dream is a jurisdiction': **Liniang's dream has deities to witness it; in the modernist dream even the witnesses are suspect.** **② The mythical method and the device of quotation.** Received account: some critics use 'the mythical method' to describe a structural technique: taking the skeleton of a set of old myths, and stringing scattered contemporary material upon it. **This lesson reads it as one act of 'outsourcing':** **handing the burden of organisation to a system the reader already knows.** **What is more worth noticing is another thing often mentioned — received account: an author's own notes to a famous poem; the author himself once stated that they were there in order to fill out the pages.** **This lesson's reading:** the notes lead the reader to attribute being hard to understand to the insufficiency of the reader's own learning; **this is the most successful shifting of responsibility in literary history.** Lesson 15 will name it 'the inversion of the note'. **③ Juxtaposition and montage.** Received account: newspaper headlines, advertisements, statistical figures and song lyrics are embedded directly in the narrative. **Its technical definition is a single sentence:** cancel the connectives. **The modern version of Course 3's 'density = omitting the connectives':** density is not writing a great deal; it is not explaining. **④ The reversal of the quota of images.** The realism of the previous chapter expanded the list of objects that could be written about; Imagism cut the list down until only a single object was left. Received account: its programme requires that the object be treated with the whole of one's attention, and it opposes redundancy. **This lesson's table of comparison:** realism says 'anything can be written'; modernism says 'write only one thing at a time'; **both changed the quota; it is only that one enlarged it and one shrank it.** **This is the whole course's answer to the question 'how does literary history progress':** **it does not advance; it redistributes.**
:::

::: definition Difficulty is a ticket {#du-nandu}
**Received account.** In the same period, elementary education became general in Western Europe, the readership of the press expanded, and popular magazines and cheap fiction formed an enormous market. **This lesson's economics.** **① Being easy to understand became the specification of the mass market.** Elite literature therefore set difficulty as the threshold of entry: **it is not that it happens to be hard to read, but that it must be hard to read if it is to be told apart from its rivals.** This is one kind of product differentiation; its cost is paid by the reader. **② Difficulty can be measured, shown off and inherited.** Once someone has read it, that person has gained a kind of capital that cannot be transferred. Chapter 8's 'two kinds of prestige' has a high-priced version here: **underground prestige became academic prestige, and what it is exchanged for is an academic qualification.** **③ Its lifespan.** Received account: by the second half of the twentieth century, the canonisation of modernism had turned the hard into the easy — **in the classroom there are notes, selections and standard answers.** A new generation of writers therefore had to invent difficulty over again. **This lesson's formula:** difficulty is an asset that depreciates, and the speed of its depreciation depends on the speed with which it is entered on the teaching syllabus. **This point serves equally well to explain the experimental literature of the later twentieth century and today's 'writing that drives the reader off'.**
:::

::: example A book that did not wish to be published {#ex-yizhu}
A writer, in his last words, asked that every unpublished manuscript be burnt; a friend of his did not carry this out, and published them one after another. Write 180 words: **① what this event proves at the level of institutions; ② how, on this basis, we should lower the weight given in interpretation to 'the author's original intention'; ③ if the friend had done as asked at the time, what part of world literature would change**.

::: solution
**① Institutional proof** — **it proves that the production of a text and its distribution are two things that can be entirely separated:** one person's intention cannot control the circulation of an object; **so long as there is an editor, a manuscript and a sum of money, the intention that it be destroyed will be overturned.** This lesson calls this 'the weakest case of authorial intention'. **Chapter 1's 'author function' here receives its sharpest piece of evidence.** **② Weight in interpretation** — **not to abolish the author, but to demote the author to one piece of evidence:** set alongside the manuscripts, the correspondence, the timetable of the post (by day he held a post at an insurance company, and wrote at night), and the responses of readers of the same time. Received account: this author long held a post in an insurance institution, and the writing was done in spare time. **The causal link this lesson requires to be written out:** writing in one's spare time explains the uneven length of the fragments and the unevenness of their degree of completion — **this is not gossip; it is a condition of production.** **③ If the friend had done so** — **what would change is not 'a few books fewer', but the standard by which the fiction of the whole twentieth century is judged:** because these novels later became the common reference for 'institutional narrative' and 'allegory', and almost every successor took them as coordinates; **delete them, and the coordinates change, and the coordinates put in their place will redefine who the forerunner is.** **Marking:** being able to write the sentence 'the chain of retrospective recognition will be rearranged' counts as an A. The definition of 'retrospective recognition' in Chapter 16 is here fulfilled in full: **a canon is not the set of the best works; it is the set of the works most able to be cited by those who come after.**
:::
:::

::: intuition If these works were submitted today {#int-tougao}
**A thought experiment.** Submit a modernist masterpiece, anonymously, to today's mainstream channels: platforms for long works in serial, literary journals, publishers' boxes for submissions, and a company that makes scripts for short videos. **Four reasons for rejection that one can expect:** 'the pace is too slow', 'there is no hook', 'the characters are unlikeable', 'no idea what it is saying'. **The aim of this lesson is not to mock editors,** but to point out that all four are the correct criteria of their respective channels: completion rate, space on the page, the cost of empathy, and three-minute retention. **From this comes the last point of this chapter, and the most practical point of the whole course:** **a work's 'being hard', inside its own system of distribution, has never been a fault but a function; change the system of distribution, and it at once becomes a defect** — the online literature of Chapter 14 will give the proof the other way: **where one is paid by the thousand characters, simplicity is a virtue, because the reader's attention is the only cash flow** — modernism and online literature are thus the two sides of one coin: **one makes difficulty into a ticket; the other makes ease of reading into a contract; both answer, with precision, the party that pays them.**
:::

::: history A line blown in two {#sec-xian}
**1857.** In the same year, a novel and a collection of poems were each put on trial in Paris. Received account. The opening of the previous chapter is this case, and the opening of this chapter is the same: **modernism's first piece of equipment was not a technique; it was the dock.** **② 1890s–1910s.** Symbolism's turning into manifestos; the Expressionists' abolition of plot in the theatre; and the founding, in Vienna, of a philosophical circle concerned with language. **Received account:** this lesson sets them side by side and does not claim a cause: the same set of restless people did the same thing in different disciplines — **they no longer asked what language had got right, and asked why language is able to speak.** **③ 1912–1917.** An Imagist group launched a reform with a rule in three lines, and then expelled one another from within. Received account. **How this lesson uses it:** this is the ordinary form in which a literary movement is organised: the programme extremely short, the membership extremely small, the splits extremely fast; **what later ages call a 'movement' was, at the time, only a few periodicals attacking one another.** **④ 1914–1918.** Total war; trench periodicals run by the soldiers themselves; a number of writers killed. **Received account:** this lesson refuses the lazy sentence 'the war caused modernism'; **what it maintains is a narrower claim, and one that can be checked:** the war for the first time made large numbers of people who had not come from the literary profession into writers and readers, and after the war left a group of people who did not trust the rhetoric already in place. **What this explains is the composition of the readership, not the syntax.** **⑤ 1922.** Two epoch-making works appeared in the same year. **Received account:** this lesson reminds us that the reason this year is famous is that later textbooks chose it; the other books published in the same year were just as many. **Selection effect, Lesson 3.** **⑥ 1933 / 1940s–1950s / 1970s–the present.** A banned book won its case in another country and was released from the ban; a university course merged the movement; an expansion entered the excluded. Received account. **The close of this chapter:** the three key events of modernism all took place outside the text — one trial, one course of teaching, one revision of the syllabus; **this does not belittle it; this is precisely the process by which it became world literature.**
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls of this chapter
**Speaking of the 'rupture' too absolutely.** These authors relied heavily on traditional forms: the sonnet, commentaries and subcommentaries, myth and parody. **Received account:** what they ruptured was the manner of joining, not the material. **② Explaining syntax by the 'spirit of the age'.** This lesson's explanation is residence abroad, little magazines and the subscription system (① and ② above): **whatever can be explained by the number of venues should not be explained by a national psychology.** **Taking difficulty for depth.** Difficulty is a marketing asset that depreciates (the ticket clause above): **the real question is what it requires the reader to pay, and what the reader gets from it.** **Forgetting that expansion too has a bill.** The supplementary entry of 'another modernism' depends on chairs, series and funds (③ above): **before cheering for justice, first write down who paid; otherwise the next revision of the edition will again forget the same people.**
:::

::: solution

::: exercise Short essay: difficulty as an institution {level=3}
Write 600 to 1,000 words assessing '**Whenever a kind of writing becomes easy for the majority to consume, a part of writing will take being hard to consume as its trade; this is neither a fall nor a lofty aloofness, but the only workable strategy of survival for elite literature once a mass market has formed: it gives up sales, and prices instead by the reader's time. Therefore "why is modernism so hard" is the wrong question; the right question is: in that age, who could pay this price, and what did those who could afford it want to buy with the money**'. You must call on at least the six items **the after-the-event character of naming**, **little magazines and inverted review**, **residence abroad dispenses with an audience**, **cancelling the connectives**, **the inversion of the note**, **the depreciation of difficulty**; and give a ruling: **if difficulty is only a strategy of pricing, is algorithmic distribution today in the process of destroying the economic basis of elite literature? (You must give an observable indicator, and propose a way of keeping it alive that does not depend on a subsidy.**

::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**naming, little magazines, residence abroad, juxtaposition, the note, depreciation**); the ruling (**an acceptable indicator must be one that can be looked up in public data**: the print run and the years in print of single volumes of literary criticism, the number of varieties of poetry in translation, the subscription figures of literary journals, and the appearance of the indicator 'completion rate' in categories other than web fiction. **The trap in this lesson must be noted:** the algorithm has not wiped out elite writing; it has changed the funder from the market to foundations, teaching posts and prizes — **that is, the form of the funding has changed, and not difficulty itself.** **To be unable to answer this step is to fail.**); an acceptable answer for a way of keeping it alive must give a concrete mechanism: **for example, pricing by 'discussability' rather than by 'consumability'** (accompanying courses, reading together, annotated reissues, a grant towards the translation), and must say who is to pay (**because the whole argument of this chapter is precisely that every innovation of form must in the end make out a bill**); if the conclusion can run '**so what modernism teaches the reader is not how to appreciate works that are hard, but how to see through the price list behind any age's "naturally easy reading"**', that is an A.
:::

::: summary
Filed by venue: naming (**the merger took place after the authors' deaths**), little magazines (**recruiting believers rather than testing for qualification**), residence abroad (**the audience disappears, and clarity with it loses its necessity**), devices (**cancel the connectives; hand the burden of organisation to the reader**), the note (**the shifting of responsibility**), the ticket (**difficulty depreciates, and the speed of depreciation depends on the teaching syllabus**). The method in one sentence: **ask what fee a work that is hard charges the reader, and what the money taken in buys**. Next chapter: after the war, and the contemporary — prizes, translations, and the literature of exile.
