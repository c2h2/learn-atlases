---
title: "Classicism and the Enlightenment"
---

In the previous chapter literature learned to defend itself; in this chapter it learned to hold meetings — **seventeenth-century France invented a thing that had not existed before: a standing body of review made up of the academy, the salon, the periodical and the auditorium; and the eighteenth century invented its successor: the public**.

## Definition: classicism is not rules; it is an institution {#def-jigou}

::: definition Three venues {#def-sange}
**The academy** (**the received account:** in the Paris of the 1630s there appeared a standing institution of language and literature, made up of forty members and supported by a protector; it received plays for judgement, ruled on vocabulary and issued opinions (**the received account:** in the quarrel over *Le Cid*, the official body criticised a work that had already made its name, and a public war of words followed)); **the salon** (**the received account:** regular gatherings presided over by aristocratic women; the writer had to be present, had to improvise and had to be decorous; criticism took place here, and not afterwards); ③ **the auditorium** (**the received account:** the commercial public theatre made ‘applause and hisses’ into a vote that could be counted (**this lesson's formula:** the ‘rules’ of classicism are not a law written in a book; they are the meeting discipline of these three venues (**so ‘to break the three unities’ is not an aesthetic error; it is an error of procedure (**this sentence turns the chapter from a ‘history of style’ into a ‘history of institutions’, and is this lesson's foremost correction of the textbook (the strongest European case of Course 3's ‘form is a seating plan of who may speak’))))).
:::

::: definition Decorum, the sublime and what may not be watched {#def-deti}
**The received account** (**the core taboo of classical tragedy is not ‘death’ but ‘loss of composure’: a king may die, but may not cry out; blood must be shed offstage (**three points (① constraint produced psychological description (because one cannot do it, one can only say it; that is: a character's greatness lies in whether he speaks, and in how he speaks (Course 7's ‘register is not politeness; it is admission’ reaches its extreme here: a class is forbidden to lose its composure, and so loss of composure becomes the only plot (② ‘greatness’ is a speech act (characters of the Corneille kind create facts by declaration (what he is to become, he first declares himself to be (this lesson calls this ‘self-notarisation’: in a society with no notary, the sentence is the notarial deed (③ it taught Europe a way of reading (the received account: later ages read lines of this sort as ‘character’, and the nineteenth century, turning the other way, read them as ‘rhetoric’ (and so the quarrel between greatness and affectation went back and forth for two hundred years (another turn of the cycle, in Chapter 1, of ‘description and prescription’))).
:::

::: definition The craft of polemic {#def-lunzhan}
**The received account** (**the philosophical notes of a physicist, and nineteen pseudonymous letters by a parish priest in the provinces: two of the most beautiful pieces of seventeenth-century prose, and both of them weapons of polemic (**the received account: works of the Pascal kind were published anonymously, under a pseudonym, and in the form of a letter ‘to a friend’, so as to evade the censorship and the judgement of the university (**three techniques (**① **the pseudonym is a publishing strategy** (**not moral cowardice:** in a dispute the right to sign one's name is a target; to hang it out first is to lose a city first (the practical version of Chapter 1's ‘author-function’ (② humour is the safest argument (write the opponent down as laughable rather than wrong, and the reader's assent comes from the laugh; laughter does not need to be refuted (this lesson calls this move ‘degradation’: it does not refute the other party; it moves the other party out of the respectability that debate requires (here the art of satire in Course 4 has a European founding master (③ style is the pledge of a position (polemical prose is deliberately unacademic: it uses a living language to strike at the Schools that use Latin (this lesson's reading: this is a public run, as between ‘language accounts’ (the Western parallel to Chapter 3's ‘taking the vulgar as the elegant’))).
:::

::: definition Two books, two projects {#def-liangshu}
One is a historical and critical dictionary (the received account: at the beginning of the eighteenth century, a great dictionary in the form of entries, the main text mediocre and the footnotes elaborate (this lesson calls it ‘subversion by the footnote’: the main text hands the work in; the footnotes tell the truth; the censor reads the former, the clever person the latter (this is an upgrade of the device in Chapter 4's ‘drawers and prefaces’: two sets of accounts, kept in one and the same text (② one is an encyclopaedia (the received account: in the middle of the eighteenth century, a multi-volume compilation of the crafts and of knowledge, opening with a ‘diagram of human reason’, and, because of disputes over entries, long locked in manoeuvres with the censorship (three consequences (① the taxonomy of knowledge is made public for the first time (the most barefaced confession, in the age of the Enlightenment, of Chapter 1's ‘the catalogue is censorship’: the diagram of classification is not the shape of the world; it is the shape the editorial office is willing to answer for (the received account: entries of the ‘authority’ class in it became the focus of protest from the Church and the government); ② ‘the crafts’ enter respectable literature (forging, weaving and mining were written into solemn copperplate engravings (**this is the class history of the present chapter:** **literature admits, for the first time, that there is oil on its hands** (③ **the contributor becomes a new profession** (**living by commissions and by subscription, not by patronage (**here the economy of authorship in Chapter 1 changes its client (**and the new client is the ‘subscriber’, which is to say the market in the earliest sense**).
:::

::: definition The judgement of taste: a discipline is born in the eighteenth century {#def-quwei}
**The received account** (**in the middle of the eighteenth century, ‘aesthetics’ was named as the discipline that studies sensuous cognition; the discussion of that same generation was concerned with whether ‘taste’ can have a standard, and whether it can be argued over (**this lesson's three consequences (① the discipline is later than its object (‘literature’, as a category that can be studied on its own, is itself a product of this period (before this there were poetics, rhetoric, the study of the Classics, and history (so this chapter's first reminder to a course in ‘world literature’: the piece we cut out with ‘literature’ is a very new cut (Chapter 6 and Chapter 11 will both pay this bill (② ‘beyond dispute’ is a social design (if a judgement of taste is at once subjective and requires universal agreement, then it has to be underwritten by an institution — salons, periodicals and critics acquire their posts from this (Kant's ‘communal sense’ is a hard problem in philosophy, and a meeting in sociology (③ so ‘aesthetic autonomy’ is a clause of a constitution, and not a fact (it provides that literature is not under the jurisdiction of cognition or of morality; and every dispute about ‘whether this piece corrupts the young’ is a constitutional review of that clause (Chapter 1, Chapter 10 and Chapter 8 of this course join here into a single line).
:::

::: definition A manual for one genre was not written until the eighteenth century {#def-shuoming}
**The received account** (**in eighteenth-century Britain there appeared a body of texts later retrospectively recognised as ‘the rise of the novel’, and a classic study of the twentieth century; the claim of the latter is that the novel moves in step with the legitimation of the middle class, of personal experience and of everyday detail (**this lesson's three moves (① first, read it as a manual (the novel's early plea for itself — “这是真实书信” (‘These are real letters.’ (Our translation.)), “这是道德的” (‘This is moral.’ (Our translation.)), “这是历史” (‘This is history.’ (Our translation.)) — precisely exposes the instability of its legal standing (the received account: early novelists often appeared in the role of editor, translator or compiler (Chapter 2's ‘the preface is the lawyer; the main text is the confession’, made good in full (② ‘formal realism’ is a technique (the received account: that researcher uses the word for an emphasis on a concrete time and place, on the individual object and on minute description (it can be taken apart into three things one can check: personal names are to be ordinary, time is to be precise, detail is to have no function (this lesson's criterion: functionless detail is the fingerprint of a genre: the epic does not write shoes; the novel does (because the novel's contract is ‘this world has a cost’ (③ the chain of retrospective recognition (the twentieth century retrospectively recognised a jesting work of the seventeenth century as ‘the first postmodern novel’, and an experimental text of the eighteenth century as ‘metafiction’ (the received account) — this is not a discovery; it is a reissue (Chapter 16's concept of ‘retrospective recognition’ is put to use in earnest here for the first time: every extension of a lineage of classics happens because someone in the present needs an ancestor).
:::

::: definition Travel, satire and a style that will not be reconciled {#def-lvxing}
**The use of the travel form** (**the received account:** the travel account was the best-selling form of the time; by way of ‘afar’, the author obtained an exemption for commenting on home (**an operation of the Swift kind is:** to take a fictional country and turn the reliability of the travel account into a weapon (**you cannot refute a people that does not exist, but you can refute its description of human beings (**this lesson calls this ‘jurisdictional arbitrage’ (Lesson 1: which court you sue in decides which law takes effect (② the two faces of satire (the received account: satire is often ‘attack’ and ‘accusation’ at once; it requires the reader to share a set of values which it does not itself write down (so satire has one permanent weakness: it cannot be fully understood without cancelling itself (Lesson 15, on argumentative writing, sets this down as a rule: irony needs an audience; otherwise it is only coarse talk (③ the resurrection of the storyteller (the received account: one novelist repeatedly interrupts the narrative, confers with the reader, and calls himself something of the order of 在下 (‘your humble servant’) (this is the same device as the 说话人 (‘speaker’) of the storyteller's script (*huaben* 话本): to admit that someone is telling it is more believable than to pretend that the world is happening by itself (the echo, in this lesson, of Chapter 9 (the *huaben*): do not pretend to be a camera; pretend to be a person).
:::

::: example The venues move {#ex-huichang}
One and the same work passes in turn through four venues of judgement: a patron at court, the academy, the salon and the readers of a periodical. Explain: **① which features each place makes important; ② after the move from the ‘academy’ to the ‘periodical’, what inflation befalls the ‘merits’ of a work**.

::: solution
**① Venues and merits** — **the patron** (**flattery and the dedication have a price; the merits are ‘timely’ and ‘fit for use’;** **② the academy** (**the rules, and purity of language, have a price; the merit is ‘beyond reproach’ —** because its censorship is public, and can be consulted in advance (**this is the real dividend of the academy system:** **the rules can be looked up, and so one can comply in advance (**③ the salon (manner of speaking, improvisation and decorum have a price; the merits are ‘good to read, and able to be retold’ (this is the first time that ‘retellability’ becomes a literary value (this lesson's hook: this is the previous chapter's well-water study of transmission, recurring inside an aristocratic room (④ the readers of the periodical (views, novelty and a stance have a price; the merit is ‘being cited’ (the definition of the inflation (when the number of those who judge grows from forty persons to ten thousand subscribers, ‘good’ no longer means ‘beyond reproach’, but ‘someone is willing to go on talking’; and so the judgements passed on one and the same work in two ages can be entirely opposite, and yet not contradictory (Marking (one must name one concrete thing that loses its value as the venue moves: for example a letter of flattery, a Latin epigraph, or the article ‘conforming to the ancients’ (to write only ‘there are more readers’ does not count as analysis (Lesson 3, a numerator with no denominator: the number of readers must be converted into ‘how many characters can be read per minute, and how much money each person pays each year’).
:::
:::

::: intuition A quarrel over whether the moderns can surpass the ancients {#def-zhengming}
**The received account** (**from the end of the seventeenth century into the eighteenth, the ‘Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns’ broke out in Paris and in London, one after the other: one side maintained that antiquity had already attained perfection; the other maintained that science and the crafts made it necessary that the moderns advance (**this lesson's way of reading (① the real result of this quarrel is the profession of criticism (both sides needed judges, a standard and periodicals; once the quarrel was over, the judges found that they were more indispensable than the contestants (this is the European detail of Chapter 1's ‘criticism and literature come into their institutions at the same time’ (② ‘progress’ is the grammar this quarrel left to the world (it made ‘the contemporary is superior to the classical’ into something that can be maintained, and thereby also made ‘backward’ into an adjective for an entire civilisation (the ancestor of Chapter 11's clause on evolution is here (this lesson's judgement: the narrative of progress is a spillover from a literary polemic, and not a scientific fact (③ and both sides were wrong in a way that was very necessary (imitating the ancients and surpassing the ancients both presuppose that ‘the ancients are a standard’; the truly new thing is that the standard itself became a public issue (this is the ‘reason’ in the title of this chapter: reason is not a content; it is the very act of handing a dispute over to a venue where it can be debated in public).
:::

::: history The establishment table of the reviewing bodies {#sec-bianzhi}
1630s—1680s (the academy, official censorship, standing theatres and three kinds of audience take shape (literature has rules of procedure for the first time)); 1680s—1750s (periodicals are born, copyright is first established, the reading population expands, coffee-houses and salons (the received account: in this period ‘public opinion’ was written as one word (this lesson: a thing that can be named must already have been put to use by some institution); ③ 1750s—1790s (the encyclopaedia and dialogue of the Diderot kind, the sublime and taste in Kant, and a great controversy over recitation and performance (criticism turns from the essay into a system)); 1790s (a revolution at one stroke takes over language, the theatre and copyright (the received account: the revolutionary regime tried to unify national education by a language policy (the hint planted at the end of this chapter: when ‘the people’ becomes the new reviewing bench, literature's problem of the venue will not disappear; it will only change chairs (the thirty-seven departments of Chapter 12, and the first Literature and Art Congress of 1949, will be read here as later models of the same machine)); the nineteenth century (newspapers, serialisation, critics and seats in the academy (the patron of the previous chapter becomes an editor-in-chief); ⑥ the twentieth century—now (university departments, literary prizes, review pages and algorithms (**the reviewing bench moves for the fourth time, and each sitting declares the sittings before it to have been ‘laymen’** — **this lesson's ironic close:** **the dispute over ‘who is qualified to judge’ is precisely the most stable product of this institution**.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
**Taking classicism for ‘sticking to the old’** (**it is a new institution's takeover of literature:** the rules are a by-product of administration, not the conservatism of taste (the three venues above)); **measuring every narrative from before the eighteenth century by ‘realism’** (**functionless detail is a term of the contract, and not a want of ability (② above;** **to take ‘written so as to seem true’ as a universal standard is to take one genre's local habit for nature**)); **writing the Enlightenment as ‘the victory of reason’** (**it is an editorial office, a body of subscribers and a long negotiation with the censorship (the two books above:** **reason here is a workflow, not the spirit of the age**)); **ignoring that the venues of this chapter move** (**after the academy comes the salon, after the salon the periodical, after the periodical the university and the prizes:** each move redefines what counts as good, and no one has ever announced that this was so (history, ⑥ above).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: a history of the reviewing bench {level=3}
Write from six hundred to a thousand characters, assessing ‘**Every literary fashion can be translated into a seating plan: who is in the meeting room, who is waiting in the corridor, and whose words are entered in the minutes. A study of “changes of style” that describes only syntax and subject matter is therefore only describing the decoration of the meeting; what really has to be reconstructed is the change in the order of seats — because every change in the order of seats leaves traces in the works within a single generation, and the people concerned almost never admit that they are looking for a seat**’. You must draw on: **the three venues**, **decorum and self-notarisation**, **the three techniques of polemic**, **the encyclopaedia's diagram of classification**, **the judgement of taste and the birth of the discipline**, **the novel's manual**, at least six items; and give a ruling: **if every evaluation is a function of a seat, is this essay's own judgement also only another seating plan (you must give a way of writing that can escape this charge, and say where its cost lies**.

::: solution
Points for marking: the six mechanisms (**venues, decorum, polemic, classification, taste, the manual**); the ruling (**a passing answer does not claim to be neutral, but writes the seats into the text —** that is, ‘institutional reflexivity’: it declares which institutions this essay depends on (a teaching chair, periodicals, translations, the course budget**) and gives evidence that other seats can check (**the cost is that it cannot obtain the complete trust of any side: **the cost of neutrality is a sense of belonging, and the cost of belonging is refutability**); marking looks for whether a symmetrical statement of these two costs appears, and whether a third, lazy answer is refused (‘I stand on the side of literature’ — **literature is precisely the office this table sends out**); if the conclusion can write ‘**so what this chapter teaches is to translate value into a post: do not ask “is it good?”; first ask “who is present, who keeps the record, whose objection was not written down”**’, it is an A.
:::
