title: "The Renaissance"
---

The order of the previous chapter was kept up by "a text must have some further, deeper meaning"; this chapter looks at how the machine changes its God — **and this chapter has first to hand in a reduced note: even the name "Renaissance" is an invention of the nineteenth century; we are not speaking of an age, but of how a group of people decided to paint themselves as the children of a second age**.

## Definition: the origin of a term of periodisation {#def-fenqi}

::: definition Who invented "rebirth" {#def-bukehardt}
**Received account.** Talk of "rebirth" is much older, but what made it into a whole historical interpretation was a work of the middle of the nineteenth century (**received account: Burckhardt**). Its claim can be compressed into one sentence: **the world, the art and the spirit of adventure of the Italian city-states are the birthplace of the modern "individual"**. **This lesson's three operations.** **① Read it as a thesis, and not as a fact.** Its evidence is portraits, diaries, autobiographies and biographies of artists. The problem is not that the evidence is false, but the inference: these genres had long existed in monasteries and at courts. **② The discovery of "the individual" is a historical claim open to counter-evidence.** Received account: later research has pointed out, in great quantity, that the so-called features of modern individualism are not rare in earlier texts. The result a discipline arrives at is, then, this: a slogan has been studied until it is a bill that needs an invoice. **③ At the same time, refuse another cheap saying.** **"The Renaissance did not exist" is also wrong:** patronage structures, the circulation of manuscripts, vernacular publishing and education in rhetoric did change. **This chapter's position:** periodisation is neither a discovery nor a fiction; it is a classificatory apparatus in the service of administration and of the curriculum; it is both true and false. **This sentence applies to every "awakening of so-and-so" in the history of Chinese literature.**
:::

::: definition The two ledgers of the same age {#def-zhang}
**Received account.** In the very century when "the discovery of man" was written into the textbooks, Europe saw large-scale expulsions and forced conversions (**received account: the expulsion orders in Iberia at the end of the fifteenth century, and the practice of the Inquisition that followed**). **This lesson's treatment.** This is not "overturning the verdict"; it is one combined statement. The routine technique of a narrative of progress is to move other things that happened at the same time into another column. **Lesson 16 will name this move "the two ledgers of the same age", and apply it to every saying of the form "the age of so-and-so".** It is also this chapter's only moral judgement on "humanism": **humanism is both a learning that liberates and a course about the qualification for being "human"** — **and the question "who counts as human" was, in that same hundred years, given the worst answer**.
:::

::: definition The patron changes, and the boundary of the work changes with it {#def-bianjie}
**Structure.** Received account: a multi-headed patronage market in which the papal court, the city-states, merchant clans and royal houses exist side by side. Compared with the previous chapter's orders from a single Church, this is a competition among sellers — **three visible consequences**. **① The range of subjects widens.** Mythological subjects acquire a legitimate standing: **it was not that thought was liberated; it was that a second house was willing to pay.** This is exactly the criterion used again and again in Chapter 1, and in the lesson on *The Peony Pavilion*. **② Signing one's name, and praising oneself, are made legitimate.** Lives of artists are written, names are signed, self-portraits are painted; Vasari's biographical system writes the creator as a hero (**received account**) — a complete contrast with the previous chapter's anonymous authority, "the Five Books of Moses assigned to the prophet". **Authority moves from the text onto the person.** **③ Opportunism becomes a genre.** Prefaces offered to patrons, dedicatory inscriptions and letters of flattery make up a vast sub-genre. Received account: Milton wrote Latin gift-poems for the grandees of various countries. **This is a whole layer of work outside the "work"** (Chapter 7's "a dedicatory poem is a job application": the general European case). This lesson's formula: **an age's image of itself is written on its quotation sheet, not in its manifesto**.
:::

::: definition Print: amplifying heresy and the classics together {#def-yinshua}
**Received account.** In the middle of the fifteenth century, movable-type printing took shape in the Rhine region, and within a few decades made the number of printed books in Europe grow exponentially. **Four consequences.** **① The established text, and the list of errata.** For the first time an error becomes enumerable, and therefore someone can be held to account. **② Copyright as a scarce good.** Received account: charters and the booksellers' guilds kept a long hold on reprinting, and what the author received was meagre; not until the law of the eighteenth century was the right returned to the author (Chapter 11's "translation is piracy" is what followed from this legislation). **③ The vernacular is fixed by the printed book.** One vernacular, because it is printed in quantity, becomes "a language"; the rest become "local talk". This is the hardest evidence for Chapter 8's "a standard language is cut to shape": the knife is the printing press. **④ Cheap folios, and an enlarged readership.** Received account: religious controversy is carried on in popular pamphlets. Lesson 16 compares printing to "issuing a loudspeaker to one's opponent" — **in this chapter, this metaphor is cashed for the first time**.
:::

::: definition Utopia: a promise that will not hand in the manuscript {#def-wutuopang}
**Received account.** At the beginning of the sixteenth century, a work that wrote an ideal polity by way of a "fictional journey" established a genre (**received account: the line of More's *Utopia***). Its three rules of grammar. **① "Somewhere".** Geographical impossibility is the text's immunity in law: **I do not say that it ought to be so here; I only say that it is so there.** This is the spatial version of "the dream is a jurisdiction" in the Liniang lesson of Chapter 2. **② Description instead of argument.** The force of a utopia lies in writing, in detail, the daily round, the meals, the public offices and the permits to travel. **It gives no reasons; it only gives you a plan one could live in** — and so it by nature requires the reader to supply the missing words, "what does this cost" — **and the author refuses to supply them**. **③ The title is the disclaimer.** Received account: the book's title and its preface sway, over a long time, between "a joke" and "in earnest". **This lesson's verdict:** utopia is the genre's system of "payment in advance". **It deposits in advance the whole of the reader's discontent with things as they are into an account that is not altered, and lives on the interest.** Lesson 10 will meet it again in science fiction and in the "root-seeking" of the 1980s, **and will find that utopia and anti-utopia share one layout, and that only the signatory's attitude is changed**.
:::

::: definition The essay: making "I am thinking" into a form {#def-suibi}
**Received account.** In the later sixteenth century a writer invented a genre of his own, under the title "attempt" (**received account: Montaigne**). **Four formal features:** the topics are small, the structure follows the train of thought, verse is quoted in quantity without the edition being noted, and the conclusion often overturns itself. **This lesson's methodological reading.** **① This is the first time that "the scene of admitting a mistake" is sold as the point of the genre.** The previous chapter's art of commenting on the classics hides the deeper meaning behind the text; the essay places the hesitation in the middle of the sentence. **② Its honesty is a privilege.** The condition for being able to write "I was wrong" is a reader who will not pursue the matter. **The essay is a genre of peacetime.** This clause lets us measure every kind of high-pressure writing by it: however much self-overturning can be kept, that much freedom there is (the drawer literature of Lesson 10 acquires here an indicator one can use). **③ It leaves later ages a weapon.** The sentence-form "a fragment on …" is to this day the format in which prose has the greatest freedom. **The cost is that it allows the author to stop anywhere** (Course 1, "the division of labour between the essay and persuasion").
:::

::: definition The sonnet, the scenario and the breaking of mixture {#def-xingshi}
**The sonnet.** Received account: from the octave and the sestet of the Petrarchan form to the three quatrains plus a couplet of the Shakespearean form; a difference in the division into stanzas corresponds to a different rhythm of argument. This lesson, using a term from Course 3, calls this "the physical form of argument": **the first eight lines pose the question and the last six turn it over, or three parallel groups plus one cold close** — **metre is not packaging; it is the temporal arrangement of reasoning**. **② Scenario theatre.** Received account: Italian improvised comedy is performed from role-formulas and an outline of the plot, and the lines are produced on the spot. This is the same kind of fact as the acoustic constraint of 一人主唱 ('one person leads the singing') in the Yuan variety play (*zaju*), and as the 得胜头回 ('the winning opening round') of the storyteller's script (*huaben*): **before a written established text appears, the role is more stable than the script**. It is the theatre version of Chapter 1's "accumulation across generations". **③ Breaking the mixture.** Received account: classicism requires that tragedy and comedy not be mixed, while modern theory of drama argues for mixing them. **This is the cleanest "experiment in changing the rules" in world literature:** what is changed is only which juxtapositions are allowed, and only after the change does the inner life of a character become something one can write (**because "a person of high station speaking coarsely" turns from a breach of the rules into a resource**). The section on "register" in Chapter 7 gets its historical weight here: **register is not politeness; it is admission**.
:::

::: example "A version" or "an author" {#ex-banben}
Of a stretch of text from the drama of the early modern period, scholarship has long drawn a distinction between "an earlier variant text" and "a later edited text", and there is a claim of "an addition written by a certain contemporary". In 180 characters, write: **① if you adopt the "author" frame, which differences you would explain as slips of the hand; ② if you adopt the "troupe text" frame, which you would explain as performance and revision of the script; ③ what each of the two frames would miss**.

::: solution
**① The author frame** — **reads the cuts, the simplifications, the changes of rhyme word and the conveniences for the stage as a copyist's error.** Its advantage is that it keeps the explanation consistent (**one personality as the model of error**). What it loses: **it cannot explain those changes which are "plainly there to make things easier for a given actor", and can only call them "debasement".** **② The troupe frame** — **reads those same differences as adjustments of role, official cutting and rewriting, scenes added and scenes removed, and the replacement of actors.** Its advantage is that it grants the text to be something made in collaboration. **What it loses: it has trouble explaining why some of the changes are plainly better as art, while "the troupe" does not take art as its objective function.** This lesson's ruling: **the difference between the two frames does not lie in "whether one respects the work", but in what you take to be the unit in which the text is produced.** Chapter 1's "author hypothesis" and this chapter's patronage market meet here: the concept of the author is a product of copyright law and of an education through biography; the concept of the troupe is a product of account books and of sheets of performance. **Marking:** to be able to write "the frame decides which differences count as errors" is a pass for this chapter — **this is the first sentence of the study of editions, and it is this chapter's one desensitising of "collation is morality"** — and "the edition is an ideology", from Course 1 and from Course 2, becomes here an operable technical rule.
:::
:::

::: intuition A humanist's bookshelf {#def-shujia}
**Shrink "the discovery of man" down to a list of books.** Received account: the educational programme of humanism is grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy. **Note what it takes and what it leaves.** **① Rhetoric comes before argument.** To be able to speak beautifully, and as the occasion requires, matters more than the truth of the proposition. **This is an education designed for public life.** It assumes that truth needs to be persuaded, and not only proved — and so the dispute of Lesson 15 acquires an ancient ancestor here: oratory is the technique of the city-state. **② Copying comes before observation.** Knowledge of nature, too, is passed on by the authority of texts: **this explains why the same people could expose a forged document by collation, and yet do no experiment.** **③ The Latin republic and the vernacular market exist side by side.** A humanist writes in Latin for the whole of Europe, and in the vernacular for the locality: **two circles of readers, two scales of payment for a manuscript, two kinds of reputation** — **and this is the most useful sentence in the chapter:** **any "literary revolution" can begin by asking to whom the author writes in Latin (the common language), and to whom in the vernacular (the local language)** — **every revolution that has only one readership succeeds faster.** Chapter 17, and Chapter 11 of this course, will both use this.
:::

::: history From the study to the museum {#sec-bowuguan}
**The fourteenth century.** After the Black Death: a structural change in population, in wages and in piety; a Boccaccio-style frame narrative. Received account: **after the catastrophe, literature for the first time admits in public that it is a diversion.** This sentence is repeated once in each of the sixteenth, the eighteenth and the nineteenth centuries in Europe, and each time a fee has to be paid for a moral defence. **The fifteenth century.** Scholars come west from Constantinople; manuscripts are hunted; the art of printing. **A political catastrophe is read by another civilisation as a cultural dividend, and the dividend is captured by a small number of families** — **this is the footnote from fact that this chapter sets under the word "revival".** **The sixteenth century.** Vernacular Bibles, an explosion of genres, utopia and the essay, and the Index system of the Counter-Reformation. Censorship and publishing expand at the same time. **This is the standard consequence of the age of print: there is more that can be looked up, and there is more that can be banned.** **The seventeenth century.** Classicism laying down the law, the taste of the court, and the birth of learned academies and of journals. **For the first time, criticism has a standing institution.** The previous chapter is the Church's established text; this chapter is the academy's established text. **This change decides the concept of "literature" for the three hundred years that follow.** **The nineteenth century.** Burckhardt's book, the museum and the university, and the systematic compiling of histories of national literatures. **Here the Renaissance becomes a course.** The ground on which this chapter turns back on itself: the Renaissance we discuss today is a work of the nineteenth century. **The twentieth century to now.** Social history, material culture, the global turn and postcolonial criticism. Received account: **"the Renaissance" is turning from an age into a claim.** **This lesson's judgement:** by the time this course has been taught as far as this, the reader ought already to be able to do one thing: **on hearing "the awakening of so-and-so", go and check its bill, its bookshelf, its printing press, and what it entered in the other column in that same hundred years.**
:::

::: warning Four pits in this chapter
**Treating "humanism" as a movement of ideas, and not as a curriculum.** It is grammar schools, chairs and textbooks: **ideas are passed on through class hours** (the bookshelf clause above). **Using the two-stage account in which "the Middle Ages repressed, and the Renaissance liberated".** This chapter has already given the counter-example: censorship and the Index expanded in the same hundred years (③ in the history above: **a narrative of liberation always finances itself by moving the cost into another column**). **Reading the text of a play as a literary work.** Cuts, errors in the line, adjustments of role and replacements of actors are all flattened by the concept "work" (the worked example: **first ask whom this copy is for** — the European version of Course 3's "whom the established text is for"). **Automatically reading writing in the vernacular as an affinity with the people.** A written defence of the vernacular is often in Latin (③ above: **"for whom one writes" is to be judged by the language in which the letter of apology is written**).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: periodisation is administration {level=3}
Write from six hundred to one thousand characters, assessing "**Cutting history into ages is an act of administration: it first marks out the frontiers and the kinds of tax, and only then goes looking for evidence; it writes certain things that happened at the same time into one period's budget, and pushes others into the deficit. Every reform of periodisation is therefore a fiscal reform — the Middle Ages becoming shorter, the Renaissance becoming wider, the Enlightenment moving from Paris to Edinburgh and to Suzhou, are none of them the result of new sources, but a new list of taxpayers. A method of history that is truly reliable is the readiness, at any moment, to point out whose account this period's budget has entered in the wrong column.**" You must call on at least six items: **the nineteenth-century origin of the term of periodisation**, **"the individual" as a historical claim open to counter-evidence**, **the structure of patronage deciding the boundary of the work**, **the four consequences of print**, **the three rules of grammar of utopia**, **the essay as a privileged genre**; and you must rule on this: **if periodisation is only administration, can literary history still have any concept of time at all — you must give a substitute unit which neither depends on a "spirit of the age" nor falls back into a chronicle, and you must say how it is falsified**.

::: solution
Points for the mark: the six mechanisms (**origin, open to counter-evidence, patronage, print, utopia, the essay**); the substitute unit (**acceptable candidates: a circulation network (the node graph of a given translation — a book list — a journal), an institutional apparatus (copyright, censorship, a chair), the lifespan of a form (the rise and fall of some formula)**); the condition of falsification must be set down — **for example: if the curve of the rise and fall of some form has nothing to do with the curve of income and expenditure of the institution that carries it, then this explanation fails.** Marking looks to see whether this half appears: "if …, then this account does not hold" (Lesson 3: **a concept of time that has no refutability is only fatalism with its name changed**). If the conclusion can be written so that it says "**what this chapter teaches is, therefore, a bookkeeping of time: not that 'there are no ages', but that any age must produce its consolidated statement — and the losses on that statement which were consolidated away**", that is an A.
:::

::: summary
The machine changes its God, and the file is closed: periodisation (**an invention of the nineteenth century, both true and false**), the two ledgers (**a combined statement, not an overturned verdict**), patronage (**the self-image is written on the quotation sheet**), print (**the established text, copyright, the vernacular, the loudspeaker**), utopia (**a promise that will not hand in the manuscript**), the essay (**however much self-overturning can be kept, that much freedom there is**). The method in one sentence: **on hearing "some age", first ask its budget year and its list of taxpayers**. Next chapter: the seventeenth century — reason, classicism, and the politics of drama.
