title: "Greece and Rome"

This course does not teach 'the origin of Western literature', because an 'origin' is a consequence rather than a cause — **the Greece we hold in our hands is what two thousand years of manuscripts, textbooks, translations and commentaries and subcommentaries have left after picking it over; the real object of this chapter is not Homer, but the machine that has all along been choosing for him**.

## Definition: the technique of oral poetry {#def-kouTou}

::: definition Formula and 'measure' {#def-chengshi}
**The received account** (**In the early twentieth century Parry and Lord argued from field recordings (singers of oral epic in Yugoslavia) and from statistics internal to the text: the long verse of a society without writing is generated on the spot by 'formulaic phrase + metre' (**the received account); '捷足的阿基里斯' ('swift-footed Achilles'), '玫瑰色手指的黎明' ('rosy-fingered dawn'), '灰色的海' ('the grey sea') (the received account cites the gist of these examples) — three conclusions of this course (① an epithet is not a choice but an obligation (at a given position in the metre a word of a certain shape is needed, and the formula delivers the word for you: the aesthetic effect is a by-product of constraint (the metre of Course 3, and the 'three prominences' of the model opera in Chapter 8: the same mechanics (constraint produces form; it does not produce quality; but it does produce form); ② memory is not storage but regeneration (the singer 'makes a poem at every performance' (this is the very opposite of the 'fixed text' as manuscript culture understands it: and so the question 'who is Homer?' is almost meaningless in that context); ③ the moment of writing-down is the moment loss begins (to write the sound down is necessarily to drop gesture, tune, occasion and the audience's response; what is gained in exchange is a book that can be annotated, set in examinations and translated (all the 'canonisation' in the rest of this chapter takes place on top of this exchange.
:::

::: definition The economics of epic {#def-jingji}
**What a long text needs** (verse of more than six thousand lines needs: a library of formulae, an audience willing to listen all night, and an occasion of patronage that can pay (the received account: banquets, contest-festivals, religious festivals); set against the earlier courses (the four acts of Yuan variety plays (*zaju*) are a physiological quantity, the storyteller's script (*huaben*) is the length of one episode, and online fiction is paid by the thousand characters (the 'unit of length' of Greek epic is one night: so its structure is juxtaposition and repetition, not suspense and recovery (the antithesis of Chapter 9's 'the cycle is the greatest promise of reunion': oral narrative does not hold you by the ending; it holds you by your already being here.
:::

## A heritage that has been selected {#def-yichan}

::: definition Why only three tragedians remain {#def-sanwei}
**The layer of fact** (**the received account: complete plays survive from the three great tragedians; of the rest, several hundred works mostly survive as fragments and titles; and the lists of playwrights recorded in antiquity are far longer than what we get to read**) — two lines of cause (① the language textbook (the received account: the school anthologies of the Byzantine age determined the core repertoire transmitted to the present (the Nth time 'an anthology is censorship' pays out, and this time the censor is a ninth-century teacher (Chapter 1: a bibliography (catalogue) is censorship); ② the luck of materials (papyrus survived in the dry places of Egypt and rotted in the damp places of Europe; parchment was costly, so only what was useful was copied (Chapter 8, the luck of the archive: inside the so-called 'glory of Greece' there is an inventory of losses we shall never see (this course asks you to keep one blank cell for it in your mind (this is the most necessary discipline of the course in world literature: we cannot study only the grammar of the survivors.
:::

::: definition The time Aristotle was misread {#def-sanheyi}
**The received account** (the *Poetics* discusses the unity of action, and discusses imitation and 'catharsis'; sixteenth-century Italian commentaries and subcommentaries extended 'unity' into the 'three unities' of time, place and action, which then became an iron rule of classicism, and were broken by the Romantics — this course's way of reading (① this is the cleanest thing an influence study can do (a misreading has a concrete commentary, a concrete year and a concrete consequence on the stage; it is not a 'misunderstanding'; it is production (② the concept that is misread is precisely the one with the most power (the 'three unities' were never written in this form in the *Poetics*, yet they ruled the European theatre for a hundred and fifty years — the authority of a theory often comes from the wrong version of it); ③ it joins another line of this course (the same *Poetics* entered the Islamic world through an Arabic translation, and was read there as poetics and rhetoric (the received account: commentaries and subcommentaries of the kind written by al-Farabi and Avicenna) — one book can become two different disciplines in two civilisations (this is the prelude to the chapter on 'translation and reception'.
:::

## Rome: the creditor of influence {#def-luoma}

::: definition Translation as a conquest {#def-fanyi}
**The received account** (**Roman literature begins in the translation and rewriting of Greek; Horace has a sentence often quoted, the gist of which is “被俘的希腊俘获了她的野蛮征服者” — Captured Greece captured her savage conqueror. (Our translation.) (**the received account cites the gist); Virgil's *Aeneid* at once follows and surpasses Homer; Catullus and the school of the New Poets advocate the short piece, fine workmanship and the taste of the learned — three theoretical points (① 'the anxiety of influence' takes shape here (the starting point of Latin literature is a debt, and so its system of honour is 'to surpass the original': rendering across, juxtaposition, competition — this supplied later Europe, as a whole, with the precedent for a 'right to rewrite' the classics (② the scholar's poetry (the received account: the Alexandrian scholars' tradition of collation and of commentaries and subcommentaries nurtured the school of 'taking learning as poetry' (this and the Song-dynasty '以才学为诗、以议论为诗' ('taking erudition as poetry, taking argument as poetry') are comparable phenomena in the sociology of literature (the condition they share is that the books are already many, and a new work must show its place by intertextuality (the cross-cultural isomorphism of Chapter 3's '以故为新', 'making the new from the old'); ③ Rome invented the apparatus of 'the curriculum of the classics' (the received account: the textbooks, practice exercises and model texts of the schools of rhetoric (these explain 'how a classic takes effect' better than any masterpiece does: a classic is not read; it is examined (the institutional prototype of Chapter 1's 'literature comes to set the seal'.
:::

::: definition Two outward routes {#def-lianglu}
**The Latin route** (**the received account: medieval western Europe received Greek writing through Latin translations, summaries and quotations by the Church Fathers; Plato was absent for a long time, and returned only gradually in the twelfth to the fifteenth centuries (**a curve of break and continuation: what western Europe lost was in fact the Greek language itself (② the Arabic–Syriac route (the received account: the translation movement in Baghdad in the eighth to the tenth centuries translated Greek philosophy and science into Arabic, and it was then translated back into Latin; the *Poetics* was read here as a book about 'embellishment and exhortation' (the methodological gain of this course: the 'Greek legacy' is not one river but two irrigation systems; any narrative that takes 'the West' as a single receiver shows its flaw here (this is also why this chapter objects to the word 'origin': the so-called origin is the result of several mutually unrelated histories of reception being entered on one combined account in the twentieth century.
:::

::: example Three fates of one formula {#ex-chengs}
For a formulaic phrase of the type '玫瑰色手指的黎明' ('rosy-fingered dawn'), explain separately what it is in ① oral performance, ② the manuscripts of the Alexandrian scholars, and ③ a modern Chinese translation (technical component / artefact / rhetoric), and answer: **why modern readers often find Homer's comparisons 'too beautiful', while researchers hold that in the original context they are hardly beautiful at all**.
::: solution
**① In performance** — **it is a spare part:** at this position in the metre the singer needs 'dawn + a disyllabic epithet', and the formula is taken up and used; **its 'beauty' is the beauty of reliability, like a good spanner (**② In the manuscript — it becomes a fingerprint of the text: commentators and subcommentators begin to ask why the dawn is called '玫瑰色手指' ('rosy-fingered'), and the formula is then retrospectively acknowledged as an image (this is the first misplacement in literary criticism: reading stock as if it were choice (③ In the translation — it becomes a label of style: if the translator keeps it, the Chinese reader meets 'a poetic flavour from elsewhere'; if the translator cuts it, the text at once becomes 'fluent, and like something we wrote ourselves' (the marks in this course fall on the last question (the modern reader's 'too beautiful' is a product of distance: formulaic phrases have an extremely high rate of repetition, and in an oral context repetition is not rhetoric but efficiency; we find it beautiful precisely because we cannot hear the scene in which it was not found beautiful (this matches the course's treatment of every 'classical solemnity': solemnity is often the result of deafness (the ancient Greek version of Chapter 8's 'becoming an idiom = the circulation rate rises, and the meaning goes to zero'.
:::
:::

::: intuition 'The classical' is a kind of copyright {#def-gudian}
**Why this label keeps moving** (**the received account: the so-called classical age (Athens in the fifth century BC) is itself a later construction: the Romans upheld it as the model, the Renaissance upheld it as the adversary, and nineteenth-century German universities upheld it as the model for national education (**this course takes 'the classical' apart into three components (**① a curriculum (**it decides who must read; **② a set of editions (**it decides which copy is read; **③ an authority that may not be questioned (**it decides which questions one is not permitted to put to it (**and so 'returning to the original text' is always an act of legislating again:** each time, the three things from the time before are replaced (**set beside this the Chinese case of the same discipline, in this chapter: the three shifts of position of the *Book of Songs* through the New Text and the Old Text, the Han–Song controversy, and modern anthologies are the local model of the same machine (Course 3, 'an edition is an ideology': Greece and the Han dynasty prove, at the same time, that the exact wording of a book depends on who has the right to fix the text**.
:::

::: history Two thousand years of goods received {#sec-shouhuo}
The fifth to fourth centuries BC (the Athenian theatre and festivals: one-off events, with manuscripts circulating in small numbers ('literature' at this point is a public craft, not private property); the third to first centuries BC (collation at Alexandria and Roman imitation: the first 'fixed text', the first 'list of classics' (the received account: this is at once the childhood of modern editorial studies and of comparative literature); the ninth to twelfth centuries (Byzantine textbooks and the Arabic translation bureaus: the two lines of receipt each go into store (the same batch of goods, two tax bills); the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries (print turned the collated edition into a textbook; the three unities passed from commentaries and subcommentaries into law (an error of scholarship acquired an administrative lifespan (this is the strongest case the chapter gives for the relation between scholarship and power); the eighteenth to nineteenth centuries (Winckelmann and the 'noble simplicity' of Greek aesthetics, the classical curriculum of German schooling, Nietzsche's counter-attack (the received account: *The Birth of Tragedy* launches, in the name of Greece, a war on Greek education (a fine operation: use an earlier Greece to cancel the Greece of the present (this move was used again and again afterwards; the course will meet it again in the next batch of chapters); the twentieth century to the present (the decipherment of Linear B, Parry's field recordings, papyrus fragments, and translation and film adaptation (**the received account:** 'Greece' changes masters for the last time: from philology and humanistic education, it is handed over to archaeology, cognitive science and the culture industry (**the close of the chapter:** we are still reading Greece, only the machine that does the selecting has been changed for a shape we cannot fully recognise — **prizes, databases, and a buyer who decides which translation gets into the bookshop**.
:::

::: warning Four pitfalls in this chapter
Taking 'Homer' as an author (in an oral context this is not a question that can be asked (② above; to require a text to have an author is a reflection of modern copyright (this course's compromise: read 'Homer' as an editorial hypothesis, not as a biography); using 'we' to inherit the Western classics (this line took one detour each through Byzantium, the Arabs and the German university of the modern period; 'inheritance' is a rhetorical choice, not a heritage handed down (the two routes above)); taking the *Poetics* as common sense (even the 'three unities' are the product of a misreading (the clause above: **whenever you meet a rule of literature that claims to have been 'so since antiquity', first check the history of its commentaries and subcommentaries (**)); **taking a fragment as silence** (**the title and the quotations are there; the work is not:** we cannot on that ground judge that it was no good, only that it was not copied down (the inventory of losses above: **the first lesson of world literature is to learn to record the empty place**).
:::

::: exercise Short essay: an origin is a consequence {level=3}
Write between six hundred and one thousand characters, assessing the claim that '**our calling Greece the origin of Western literature is the result of several mutually unrelated histories of reception being entered on one combined account in the modern period: the Byzantine textbook, the Arabic translation bureau, the Italian commentaries and subcommentaries, the German humanistic secondary school and the archaeology of the nineteenth century each took delivery, each levied a tax, and each decided which text was the authorised one. ::: solution
Points for the mark: the six mechanisms (**formula, luck, commentary, debt, the two routes, the three components**); the ruling (**both lines must be followed to the end:** ① 'necessarily carries a politics' is easy to argue, but one must grant that, without the narrative of an 'origin', classical texts cannot enter any budget of public education, which is to hand them back to the studies of a few (**this is a real cost**); ② an example of a reading 'without an origin' that qualifies: **read some one text as a cross-linguistic event of translation (**for example, read only the relation between the *Aeneid* and the *Odyssey*, or read only the history of modern translations of some epic**); its cost is that it cannot explain the conditions of the text's first production (**the oral, the festival, patronage**); the marking looks at whether the third, lazy route is refused ('both are right', without paying the bill**); if the conclusion can be written, '**so the conclusion of this chapter is a sentence of method: do not ask where a tradition comes from; ask by whom, at what time, and on what accounts it was merged into "one" tradition; every merger can be traced to an invoice, and what is written on the invoice is mostly not literature**', that is an A.
