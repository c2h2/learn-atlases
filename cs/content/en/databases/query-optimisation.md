SQL says what to compute, and [[databases/query-processing]] showed that there are many ways to compute it, with costs differing by orders of magnitude. Somebody must choose. The **query optimiser** takes the algebra expression of a query, generates equivalent expressions, assigns each a physical implementation, estimates the cost of every candidate, and picks the cheapest. For the four-table query of this chapter's main example, the best join order costs about $10^4$ units of work and the worst about $2 \times 10^7$, and a typical optimiser must make such a choice for every query in a few milliseconds, without running any of the candidates.

The optimiser has three ingredients, and this chapter treats each in turn. A **search space**: the plans equivalent to the query, generated by algebraic **equivalence rules**. A **cost model**: the formulas of the previous chapter, fed with **cardinality estimates** — predicted sizes of intermediate results, computed from statistics about the data. A **search algorithm**: here, the dynamic programming of IBM's System R, still the core of most relational optimisers. The theory is clean; the practice is hard, because cardinality estimates are often badly wrong, and we will see an optimiser choose a plan fifty times slower than the alternative.

## Equivalence rules

Two relational algebra expressions are **equivalent** if they return the same relation on every database instance ([[databases/relational-model]]). The optimiser rewrites expressions only by rules that are proved to preserve equivalence.

::: theorem Equivalence rules of the relational algebra {#thm-equivalences}
For relations $R, S, T$, conditions $\theta, \theta_1, \theta_2$ and attribute lists $L, L_1, L_2$, the following hold whenever both sides are defined.
1. **Cascade of selections**: $\sigma_{\theta_1 \wedge \theta_2}(R) \equiv \sigma_{\theta_1}(\sigma_{\theta_2}(R))$; hence selections commute.
2. **Cascade of projections**: $\pi_{L_1}(\pi_{L_2}(R)) \equiv \pi_{L_1}(R)$ if $L_1 \subseteq L_2$.
3. **Selection and product**: $\sigma_\theta(R \times S) \equiv R \bowtie_\theta S$ (definition of the theta join).
4. **Commutativity of joins**: $R \bowtie_\theta S \equiv S \bowtie_\theta R$ (up to the order of columns, which is immaterial with named attributes).
5. **Associativity of joins**: $(R \bowtie S) \bowtie T \equiv R \bowtie (S \bowtie T)$ for natural joins; for theta joins, $(R \bowtie_{\theta_1} S) \bowtie_{\theta_2 \wedge \theta_3} T \equiv R \bowtie_{\theta_1 \wedge \theta_3} (S \bowtie_{\theta_2} T)$ if $\theta_2$ mentions only attributes of $S$ and $T$.
6. **Pushing selections**: $\sigma_\theta(R \bowtie S) \equiv \sigma_\theta(R) \bowtie S$ if $\theta$ mentions only attributes of $R$; and $\sigma_{\theta_1 \wedge \theta_2}(R \bowtie S) \equiv \sigma_{\theta_1}(R) \bowtie \sigma_{\theta_2}(S)$ if $\theta_1$ mentions only $R$ and $\theta_2$ only $S$.
7. **Pushing projections**: $\pi_L(R \bowtie_\theta S) \equiv \pi_L\bigl(\pi_{L_1}(R) \bowtie_\theta \pi_{L_2}(S)\bigr)$, where $L_1$ ($L_2$) consists of the attributes of $R$ ($S$) that occur in $L$ or in $\theta$.
8. **Selections and set operations**: $\sigma_\theta(R \cup S) \equiv \sigma_\theta(R) \cup \sigma_\theta(S)$, and likewise for $\cap$ and $-$; also $\sigma_\theta(R - S) \equiv \sigma_\theta(R) - S$.
:::

::: proof
We prove rules 5 (natural joins), 6 and 8 for difference; the others are similar direct checks.

*Rule 6.* Let $U$ and $V$ be the attributes of $R$ and $S$. A tuple $t$ over $U \cup V$ is in $\sigma_\theta(R \bowtie S)$ iff $t[U] \in R$, $t[V] \in S$ and $\theta(t)$. Since $\theta$ mentions only attributes of $U$, $\theta(t) = \theta(t[U])$, so the condition is equivalent to $t[U] \in \sigma_\theta(R)$ and $t[V] \in S$, i.e. $t \in \sigma_\theta(R) \bowtie S$. The second form follows by applying the first twice, with rule 1.

*Rule 5.* With attributes $U, V, W$, both sides consist of the tuples $t$ over $U \cup V \cup W$ with $t[U] \in R$, $t[V] \in S$ and $t[W] \in T$: on the left, $t \in (R \bowtie S) \bowtie T$ iff $t[U \cup V] \in R \bowtie S$ and $t[W] \in T$, iff $t[U] \in R$, $t[V] \in S$, $t[W] \in T$; the right side is symmetric.

*Rule 8, difference.* $t \in \sigma_\theta(R - S)$ iff $t \in R$, $t \notin S$, $\theta(t)$, iff $t \in \sigma_\theta(R)$ and $t \notin S$. And since $t \in \sigma_\theta(R)$ implies $\theta(t)$, "$t \notin S$" can be replaced by "$t \notin \sigma_\theta(S)$" without changing the condition, giving $\sigma_\theta(R) - \sigma_\theta(S)$ too.
:::

Most rules also hold for SQL's bag semantics ([[databases/sql#def-bag]]), with care for duplicates: projection does not remove them, and $\sigma_\theta(R - S)$ for bags needs the bag difference. With nulls, rule 6 holds for inner joins but *not* in general for outer joins: pushing a condition on the padded side of a left join below the join changes the result ([[databases/sql#ex-on-where]]). Optimisers have separate, more restricted rules for outer joins and for subqueries.

The rules justify the classic **heuristics**: push selections as far down as possible (they shrink inputs early), push projections down to drop unneeded columns, and combine a selection with a product into a join so that a join algorithm can be used. The order of joins, however, is not a matter of heuristics; it needs costs.

::: example Rewriting a query tree {#ex-rewrite}
The query "names of students with an A in a CS course" is parsed as $\pi_{\text{name}}\bigl(\sigma_{\theta}(\text{Student} \times \text{Enrolled} \times \text{Course})\bigr)$ with $\theta = (\text{S.sid} = \text{E.sid}) \wedge (\text{E.code} = \text{C.code}) \wedge (\text{E.grade} = \text{'A'}) \wedge (\text{C.dept} = \text{'CS'})$. Rewrite it with [[#thm-equivalences]].
::: solution
1. Split $\theta$ into its four conjuncts (rule 1).
2. Push $\text{grade} = \text{'A'}$ down to Enrolled and $\text{dept} = \text{'CS'}$ down to Course (rule 6).
3. Combine each join condition with the product it belongs to (rule 3): $\text{S.sid} = \text{E.sid}$ with Student $\times$ Enrolled, and $\text{E.code} = \text{C.code}$ with the product of that result and Course (rules 5 and 6 let us regroup).
4. Push projections (rule 7): from Student we need only sid and name; from Enrolled sid and code; from Course only code.

The result is

$$
\pi_{\text{name}}\Bigl(\bigl(\pi_{\text{sid},\text{name}}(\text{Student}) \bowtie \pi_{\text{sid},\text{code}}(\sigma_{\text{grade}=\text{'A'}}(\text{Enrolled}))\bigr) \bowtie \pi_{\text{code}}(\sigma_{\text{dept}=\text{'CS'}}(\text{Course}))\Bigr).
$$

On the ten-row instance, the product had $5 \cdot 10 \cdot 4 = 200$ tuples; after rewriting the largest intermediate result has $4$ (the A-grades). The rewritten tree is still only a *logical* plan: which join to do first, and with which algorithm, remains to be decided.
:::
:::

## Estimating cardinalities

Every cost formula of [[databases/query-processing]] depends on the sizes of its inputs, and for all operators except the leaves these are sizes of intermediate results that do not exist yet. The optimiser estimates them from **statistics** kept in the system catalogue: for each relation $R$, the number of tuples $T(R)$ and pages $B(R)$; for each attribute $A$, the number of distinct values $V(R, A)$, the minimum and maximum, and often a histogram and a list of the most common values. Statistics are gathered by a command such as `ANALYZE` and become stale as data changes.

::: definition Selectivity {#def-selectivity}
The **selectivity** of a condition $\theta$ on $R$ is $\mathrm{sel}(\theta) = \lvert \sigma_\theta(R) \rvert / T(R)$, the fraction of tuples that satisfy it. The estimated size of $\sigma_\theta(R)$ is $\mathrm{sel}(\theta)\cdot T(R)$.
:::

Without further information, optimisers assume **uniformity** (values are equally frequent and evenly spread between min and max) and **independence** (conditions on different attributes are statistically independent). Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{sel}(A = c) &= \frac{1}{V(R, A)}, &
\mathrm{sel}(A \le c) &= \frac{c - \min A}{\max A - \min A}, \\
\mathrm{sel}(\theta_1 \wedge \theta_2) &= \mathrm{sel}(\theta_1)\,\mathrm{sel}(\theta_2), &
\mathrm{sel}(\theta_1 \vee \theta_2) &= s_1 + s_2 - s_1 s_2, \qquad \mathrm{sel}(\neg\theta) = 1 - \mathrm{sel}(\theta).
\end{aligned}
$$ {#eq-selectivity}

For joins, one more assumption is needed.

::: proposition Join size estimate {#prop-join-estimate}
Assume that the values of $A$ are uniformly distributed in $R$ and in $S$, and that the set of $A$-values of the relation with fewer distinct values is contained in that of the other (**containment of value sets**). Then

$$
\lvert R \bowtie_{R.A = S.A} S \rvert = \frac{T(R)\,T(S)}{\max\bigl(V(R, A), V(S, A)\bigr)}.
$$ {#eq-join-estimate}
:::

::: proof
Suppose $V(R, A) \le V(S, A)$, so every $A$-value of $R$ occurs in $S$. By uniformity, each value of $A$ that occurs in $S$ occurs in $T(S)/V(S, A)$ tuples of $S$. Each tuple of $R$ has a value occurring in $S$, so it joins with $T(S)/V(S, A)$ tuples, and the join has $T(R)\,T(S)/V(S, A)$ tuples. The other case is symmetric, and in both the denominator is the larger number of distinct values.
:::

For a foreign-key join — $R.A$ references the key $S.A$ — we have $V(S, A) = T(S) \ge V(R, A)$, and the formula gives $T(R)$, which is exact ([[databases/relational-model#prop-join-size]]). Estimates for a join of several relations multiply the selectivities of all join and selection conditions, by independence: for relations $R_1, \dots, R_k$ with conditions $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_m$,

$$
\lvert R_1 \bowtie \dots \bowtie R_k \rvert \approx T(R_1) \cdots T(R_k) \cdot \mathrm{sel}(\theta_1) \cdots \mathrm{sel}(\theta_m),
$$

which does not depend on the order of the joins — only the sizes of the *intermediate* results do.

::: example Estimating a three-way join {#ex-estimate}
With $T(E) = 100\,000$, $V(E, \text{grade}) = 5$, $T(S) = 40\,000$, $T(C) = 500$ and $V(C, \text{dept}) = 20$, estimate the size of $\sigma_{\text{grade}=\text{'A'}}(E) \bowtie S \bowtie \sigma_{\text{dept}=\text{'CS'}}(C)$, joining on sid and code, and the intermediate sizes for the orders "$E'$ with $S$ first" and "$E'$ with $C'$ first".
::: solution
Selections: $E' = 100\,000/5 = 20\,000$ enrolments, $C' = 500/20 = 25$ courses. Join selectivities: $1/\max(V(E, \text{sid}), V(S, \text{sid})) = 1/40\,000$ (sid is the key of $S$) and $1/500$ for code. Final estimate:

$$
20\,000 \cdot 40\,000 \cdot 25 \cdot \frac{1}{40\,000} \cdot \frac{1}{500} = 1000.
$$

Order 1: $E' \bowtie S$ has $20\,000 \cdot 40\,000/40\,000 = 20\,000$ tuples (a foreign-key join keeps every enrolment), then joining $C'$ gives $1000$. Order 2: $E' \bowtie C'$ has $20\,000 \cdot 25/500 = 1000$ tuples, then joining $S$ keeps $1000$. The final size is the same; the intermediate results are $20\,000$ against $1000$, so the second order does less work. Note the hidden assumption that grade and dept are independent: if CS courses give more A grades than average, the true answer is larger.
:::
:::

### Histograms

Uniformity is often false: a few customers place most orders, most tickets are closed. A **histogram** divides the range of an attribute into buckets and records the number of tuples in each. An **equi-width** histogram has buckets of equal width; an **equi-depth** (equi-height) histogram has buckets holding equal numbers of tuples, so that frequent values get narrow buckets and are estimated more precisely. Within a bucket, uniformity is assumed.

::: example Estimating a range with a histogram {#ex-histogram}
A relation holds $1000$ exam marks between $0$ and $100$, summarised by an equi-width histogram with counts $50$, $150$, $400$, $300$, $100$ for the buckets $[0, 20)$, $[20, 40)$, $[40, 60)$, $[60, 80)$, $[80, 100]$. Estimate the number of marks $\ge 70$, with and without the histogram. Then estimate $\lvert \sigma_{\text{mark} \ge 70 \,\wedge\, \text{year} = 2}\rvert$ if $V(\text{year}) = 4$.
::: solution
With the histogram: $[70, 80)$ is half of the bucket $[60, 80)$, so it contributes $300/2 = 150$; the bucket $[80, 100]$ contributes $100$. Estimate $250$, selectivity $0.25$.

Without it, uniformity over $[0, 100]$ gives $\mathrm{sel}(\text{mark} \ge 70) = (100 - 70)/100 = 0.3$, estimate $300$.

With year: $\mathrm{sel}(\text{year} = 2) = 1/4$, and by independence the estimate is $1000 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 0.25 = 62.5$. If, in fact, second-year students score systematically higher, the true number could be twice that: independence is an assumption, not a fact.
:::
:::

::: quiz
$R$ has $T(R) = 10\,000$, $V(R, A) = 50$ and $V(R, B) = 200$. Under uniformity and independence, what is the estimated size of $\sigma_{A = 3 \,\wedge\, B = 7}(R)$?
- [ ] $250$
- [ ] $50$
- [x] $1$
- [ ] $0.0001$
::: solution
$\mathrm{sel} = \frac{1}{50}\cdot\frac{1}{200} = \frac{1}{10\,000}$, so the estimate is $10\,000/10\,000 = 1$ tuple. The fourth option is the selectivity, not the size. If $A$ and $B$ are correlated (say $B$ determines $A$), the true size is $10\,000/200 = 50$, fifty times the estimate.
:::
:::

::: warning Independence and error propagation
The independence assumption is the weakest link in optimisation. Real columns are correlated — city and postcode, car make and model, order date and ship date — and for correlated conditions the product of selectivities can underestimate the result by orders of magnitude. Errors also **compound through joins**: if each of five estimates in a plan is off by a factor of $3$, the final estimate may be off by $3^5 = 243$, and Ioannidis and Christodoulakis showed in 1991 that errors can grow exponentially with the number of joins. An underestimate makes nested-loop and index plans look cheap when they are disastrous. Systems mitigate this with multi-column statistics (`CREATE STATISTICS` in PostgreSQL), sampling, and adaptive execution that re-plans when observed sizes differ from estimates; but no estimate is reliable for every query, so the plan is always a bet.
:::

### A wrong estimate in SQLite

SQLite's statistics, gathered by `ANALYZE` into the table `sqlite_stat1`, record for each index the number of rows and the *average* number of rows per distinct key. (A build option, `SQLITE_ENABLE_STAT4`, adds samples of the key distribution; the standard build used here, SQLite 3.46.1 as shipped with Python 3.14, does not have it.) Consider $200\,000$ support tickets, of which about $1\%$ are open, with indexes on status and on customer:

```python
import sqlite3, random
random.seed(5)
con = sqlite3.connect("sk.db")
con.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE Ticket (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, status TEXT, customer INTEGER, opened INTEGER);
CREATE INDEX ticket_status ON Ticket(status);
CREATE INDEX ticket_customer ON Ticket(customer);""")
con.executemany("INSERT INTO Ticket VALUES (?,?,?,?)",
    [(i, "open" if random.random() < 0.01 else "closed", random.randrange(5000), i)
     for i in range(200_000)])
con.commit()
```

There are $1945$ open tickets, and $100\,108$ tickets of customers $0$ to $2500$. For the query `SELECT * FROM Ticket WHERE status = 'open' AND customer BETWEEN 0 AND 2500` the status index is clearly the right access path. Before `ANALYZE`, SQLite uses it; after `ANALYZE` it changes its mind:

```console
$ sqlite3 sk.db "EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN SELECT * FROM Ticket WHERE status = 'open' AND customer BETWEEN 0 AND 2500"
QUERY PLAN
`--SEARCH Ticket USING INDEX ticket_status (status=?)
$ sqlite3 sk.db "ANALYZE; SELECT * FROM sqlite_stat1;"
Ticket|ticket_customer|200000 40
Ticket|ticket_status|200000 100000
$ sqlite3 sk.db "EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN SELECT * FROM Ticket WHERE status = 'open' AND customer BETWEEN 0 AND 2500"
QUERY PLAN
`--SEARCH Ticket USING INDEX ticket_customer (customer>? AND customer<?)
```

The statistics say that a status value matches $100\,000$ rows *on average* — true, since there are two values — so `status = 'open'` looks like half the table, and the range on customer looks better. Timing the three variants on this machine (an observation, the mean of $20$ runs):

```console
$ python3 timing.py
planner choice                963 rows   126.3 ms
INDEXED BY ticket_status      963 rows     3.0 ms
INDEXED BY ticket_customer    963 rows   133.8 ms
```

The plan chosen with statistics is about forty times slower than the one chosen without. The cure is better statistics about *skew* — the most-common-values lists and histograms that PostgreSQL keeps by default, or SQLite's STAT4 — not fewer statistics.

## Choosing a join order

With selections pushed down, the remaining big decision is the order of the joins. The number of possibilities is large.

::: theorem Number of join orders {#thm-join-orders}
For $n$ relations, there are $n!$ **left-deep** join trees (each join has a base relation as its right input) and $\dfrac{(2n-2)!}{(n-1)!}$ join trees in all, counting $R \bowtie S$ and $S \bowtie R$ as different.
:::

::: proof
A left-deep tree is determined by the order in which the relations are joined, a permutation: $n!$. A general join tree is a full binary tree with $n$ leaves, labelled by the relations. The number of full binary tree shapes with $n$ leaves (left and right children distinguished) is the Catalan number $C_{n-1} = \frac{1}{n}\binom{2n-2}{n-1}$, since such a tree is a root with a left subtree of $k$ leaves and a right subtree of $n - k$, giving the Catalan recurrence. The leaves can be labelled in $n!$ ways, all giving different trees, so the total is

$$
n! \cdot \frac{1}{n}\binom{2n-2}{n-1} = (n-1)!\,\frac{(2n-2)!}{(n-1)!\,(n-1)!} = \frac{(2n-2)!}{(n-1)!}.
$$
:::

| $n$ | $2$ | $4$ | $6$ | $8$ | $10$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| left-deep trees $n!$ | $2$ | $24$ | $720$ | $40\,320$ | $3\,628\,800$ |
| all trees $(2n-2)!/(n-1)!$ | $2$ | $120$ | $30\,240$ | $17\,297\,280$ | $17\,643\,225\,600$ |
| subsets $2^n$ | $4$ | $16$ | $64$ | $256$ | $1024$ |

Enumerating all trees is hopeless beyond a handful of relations. The insight of System R is that it is not necessary: the best plan for a set of relations can be built from best plans for its subsets — the problem has optimal substructure, and **dynamic programming** ([[algorithms/dynamic-programming]]) applies, with one table entry per *subset* rather than per tree.

### The System R algorithm

We use a simple cost model to keep the arithmetic transparent: the cost of a plan is $C_{\text{out}}$, the **sum of the sizes of all intermediate and final join results**. This is the work of producing them, and it captures the key fact that plans with small intermediate results are cheap; real optimisers use the I/O and CPU formulas of [[databases/query-processing]] instead, and the algorithm is the same.

::: algorithm Dynamic programming for left-deep join orders {#alg-selinger}
Input: relations $R_1, \dots, R_n$ (after pushing down selections), their estimated sizes and the selectivities of the join conditions. Output: a cheapest left-deep join plan.
1. For each $i$: $\mathit{best}[\set{R_i}] \gets$ the cheapest access path for $R_i$ (scan or index), cost $0$ in the $C_{\text{out}}$ model.
2. For $k = 2, \dots, n$: for each subset $Q$ of $k$ relations and each $R \in Q$ such that $R$ is connected to $Q - \set R$ by a join condition: let $c = \mathit{cost}(\mathit{best}[Q - \set R]) + \lvert \bowtie Q \rvert$; if $c$ is the smallest found so far for $Q$, set $\mathit{best}[Q] \gets \mathit{best}[Q - \set R] \bowtie R$.
3. Return $\mathit{best}[\set{R_1, \dots, R_n}]$.
:::

::: theorem Optimality of the dynamic program {#thm-selinger}
If the cost of a plan $P \bowtie R$ is $\mathit{cost}(P) + f(Q)$, where $f$ depends only on the set $Q$ of relations joined (as for $C_{\text{out}}$), then [[#alg-selinger]], without the connectivity test, returns a cheapest left-deep plan. It examines $O(n\,2^n)$ candidate joins.
:::

::: proof
By induction on $\lvert Q \rvert$ we show that $\mathit{best}[Q]$ is a cheapest left-deep plan for $Q$. For one relation it holds by step 1. For $\lvert Q\rvert = k$, any left-deep plan for $Q$ has the form $P \bowtie R$ for some $R \in Q$, with $P$ a left-deep plan for $Q - \set R$, and costs $\mathit{cost}(P) + f(Q)$. Replacing $P$ by $\mathit{best}[Q - \set R]$, which is no more expensive by induction, gives a plan no more expensive. The algorithm considers $\mathit{best}[Q - \set R] \bowtie R$ for every $R \in Q$ and keeps the cheapest, so its choice is at least as cheap as every left-deep plan for $Q$. There are $2^n$ subsets and at most $n$ choices of $R$ for each: $O(n 2^n)$ candidates, each costed in constant time given the size estimates.
:::

The hypothesis matters. With real cost formulas, the cost of joining a subplan with $R$ depends on more than the set of relations: a sort-merge join is cheaper if its input is already sorted on the join attribute, and a plan that is not the cheapest for $Q - \set R$ might produce its output in a useful order. System R therefore keeps, for each subset, the cheapest plan for each **interesting order** — an order on an attribute used by a later join, a `GROUP BY` or an `ORDER BY` — plus the cheapest plan overall. The connectivity test in step 2 is the heuristic of **avoiding Cartesian products**; it shrinks the search, usually without losing the best plan, but not always ([[#ex-dp]]).

::: example Optimising a four-way join {#ex-dp}
Find the best left-deep order, in the $C_{\text{out}}$ model, for the join of Student $S$ ($40\,000$ tuples), Enrolled $E$ ($100\,000$), Course $C$ ($500$) and the selection $D$ of the one department in building Turing (from $20$ departments), with join conditions $S.\text{sid} = E.\text{sid}$, $E.\text{code} = C.\text{code}$ and $C.\text{dept} = D.\text{dept}$ (all foreign keys to keys).
::: solution
The selectivities from [[#prop-join-estimate]] are $1/40\,000$ for $S$–$E$, $1/500$ for $E$–$C$ and $1/20$ for $C$–$D$. Estimated sizes of the joins of connected subsets:

| subset | estimate |
|---|---|
| $SE$ | $40\,000 \cdot 100\,000 / 40\,000 = 100\,000$ |
| $EC$ | $100\,000 \cdot 500/500 = 100\,000$ |
| $CD$ | $500 \cdot 1 / 20 = 25$ |
| $SEC$ | $100\,000$ |
| $ECD$ | $100\,000 \cdot 500 \cdot 1 / (500 \cdot 20) = 5000$ |
| $SECD$ | $5000$ |

Dynamic programming: $\mathit{best}[SE] = 100\,000$, $\mathit{best}[EC] = 100\,000$, $\mathit{best}[CD] = 25$. For $ECD$: extend $EC$ by $D$ ($100\,000 + 5000$) or $CD$ by $E$ ($25 + 5000$); best $5025$. For $SEC$: $SE$ by $C$ or $EC$ by $S$, both $200\,000$. For $SECD$: extend $ECD$ by $S$ ($5025 + 5000 = 10\,025$) or $SEC$ by $D$ ($200\,000 + 5000$); best $10\,025$, the plan $((C \bowtie D) \bowtie E) \bowtie S$.

```python
from itertools import combinations

card = {"S": 40_000, "E": 100_000, "C": 500, "D": 1}         # D after the selection
sel = {frozenset("SE"): 1 / 40_000, frozenset("EC"): 1 / 500, frozenset("CD"): 1 / 20}

def size(rels):                                  # estimate with independence
    n = 1.0
    for r in rels:
        n *= card[r]
    for pair, s in sel.items():
        if pair <= rels:
            n *= s
    return n

def connected(rels, r):
    return any(frozenset((r, q)) in sel for q in rels)

def best_left_deep(names):
    best = {frozenset([r]): (0.0, r) for r in names}           # cost C_out, plan
    for k in range(2, len(names) + 1):
        for subset in map(frozenset, combinations(names, k)):
            for r in subset:                                     # r joins last
                rest = subset - {r}
                if rest in best and connected(rest, r):          # no Cartesian products
                    cost = best[rest][0] + size(subset)
                    if subset not in best or cost < best[subset][0]:
                        best[subset] = (cost, f"({best[rest][1]} ⋈ {r})")
    return best[frozenset(names)]

print(best_left_deep("SECD"))
```

```console
$ python3 dp.py
(10025.0, '(((C ⋈ D) ⋈ E) ⋈ S)')
```

Enumerating all $24$ orders confirms it: the best costs $10\,025$ (also as $D, C, E, S$), the worst order without Cartesian products costs $205\,000$ (starting with $S \bowtie E$, which produces all $100\,000$ enrolments before the department filter applies), and the worst with them $21\,005\,000$ (starting with $S \times C$, $2 \times 10^7$ tuples). One Cartesian product is harmless here: $S \times D$ has only $40\,000$ tuples because $D$ has one, and the order $S, D, E, C$ costs $145\,000$ — better than several product-free orders. Avoiding products is a heuristic, not a theorem.
:::
:::

The join graph of a query — relations as vertices, join conditions as edges — shows which orders avoid products. A cheap alternative to dynamic programming for very large queries is to be **greedy**: start from the smallest relation and repeatedly join the neighbour that gives the smallest result.

### Beyond left-deep plans

Left-deep plans suit pipelining: each join's right input is a base relation (which can be an index for index nested loops, or the build side of a hash join), and intermediate results flow up without being stored. **Bushy** plans, such as $(R \bowtie S) \bowtie (T \bowtie U)$, can be much cheaper when two selective joins are independent, and dynamic programming over pairs of disjoint subsets finds the best one in $O(3^n)$ time. For queries with more than about $10$–$15$ relations, optimisers switch to heuristics — greedy, randomised or genetic search (PostgreSQL uses a genetic optimiser by default from $12$ relations upward, as of version 17).

Optimisers also rewrite queries at a higher level. A correlated `EXISTS` subquery ([[databases/sql]]) becomes a **semijoin** and `NOT EXISTS` an **anti-join**, which can then be implemented by any join algorithm rather than by re-running the subquery for each row. Views are merged into the query that uses them. Constraints help: a join to a table referenced by a foreign key whose columns are not otherwise used can be removed entirely, since by [[databases/relational-model#prop-join-size]] it neither adds nor removes rows.

::: history
The System R optimiser was described by Patricia Selinger, Morton Astrahan, Donald Chamberlin, Raymond Lorie and Thomas Price in "Access path selection in a relational database management system" (SIGMOD 1979), which introduced cost-based optimisation with catalogue statistics, selectivity formulas, dynamic programming over left-deep plans and interesting orders; it remains one of the most influential papers in the field. Ingres took a different route, decomposing queries into single-variable pieces (Wong and Youssefi, 1976). Equi-depth histograms were proposed by Piatetsky-Shapiro and Connell in 1984; Ioannidis and Christodoulakis analysed error propagation in 1991. Goetz Graefe's Volcano (1993) and Cascades (1995) frameworks introduced rule-based, extensible optimisers used in Microsoft SQL Server and elsewhere. Viktor Leis and colleagues' "How good are query optimizers, really?" (VLDB 2015) showed on a realistic benchmark that cardinality estimation errors, rather than cost models or search, dominate plan quality.
:::

## Where this leads

The optimiser connects the whole course: it reads SQL ([[databases/sql]]), applies the algebra's laws ([[databases/relational-model]]), uses keys and foreign keys from design ([[databases/normalisation]]), chooses among the access paths of [[databases/storage-indexing]] and the algorithms of [[databases/query-processing]]. Distributed query optimisers add the cost of moving data between machines ([[distributed-systems]]), and learned cardinality estimators and cost models are an active research area at the meeting point with machine learning. The dynamic program is a classic instance of [[algorithms/dynamic-programming]], and the counting of join trees uses the Catalan numbers of [Maths Atlas: generating functions](/learn/maths/lesson.php?c=discrete&l=generating-functions).

::: summary
- An optimiser needs a **search space** (plans generated by equivalence rules), a **cost model** fed by **cardinality estimates**, and a **search algorithm**.
- Proven **equivalences** — cascading and pushing selections and projections, commutativity and associativity of joins — justify pushing selections down and reordering joins; outer joins need more restrictive rules.
- Selectivity estimates assume **uniformity** and **independence**: $1/V(R,A)$ for equality, products for conjunctions; a join on $A$ has about $T(R)T(S)/\max(V(R,A),V(S,A))$ tuples.
- **Histograms** and most-common-value lists capture skew; without them, an optimiser can pick a plan many times slower, as SQLite did after `ANALYZE` on skewed data.
- There are $n!$ left-deep and $(2n-2)!/(n-1)!$ total join trees; **System R's dynamic programming** finds the best left-deep plan in $O(n 2^n)$ steps, keeping the best plan per subset and per interesting order.
- Avoiding Cartesian products and greedy ordering are heuristics: usually good, sometimes not.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Equality selectivity {level=1 check="200"}
$R$ has $T(R) = 50\,000$ tuples and $V(R, \text{city}) = 250$. Estimate $\lvert\sigma_{\text{city} = \text{'Leeds'}}(R)\rvert$ under uniformity. (Enter the number of tuples.)
::: solution
$\mathrm{sel} = 1/250$, so the estimate is $50\,000/250 = 200$ tuples. If Leeds is one of the largest cities in the data, the true number may be far larger — the reason for most-common-value statistics.
:::

::: exercise A join estimate {level=1 check="30000"}
$T(R) = 3000$, $T(S) = 20\,000$, $V(R, A) = 100$ and $V(S, A) = 2000$. Estimate $\lvert R \bowtie_{R.A = S.A} S\rvert$. (Enter the number of tuples.)
::: solution
By [[#eq-join-estimate]], $3000 \cdot 20\,000 / \max(100, 2000) = 6 \times 10^7 / 2000 = 30\,000$. Each tuple of $R$ matches $20\,000/2000 = 10$ tuples of $S$ on average.
:::
:::

::: exercise Counting join trees {level=1 check="1680"}
How many join trees (left-deep or not, ordered children) are there for $5$ relations? How many left-deep ones? (Enter the total number.)
::: solution
$(2 \cdot 5 - 2)!/(5 - 1)! = 8!/4! = 40\,320/24 = 1680$ trees in all, of which $5! = 120$ are left-deep. ([[#thm-join-orders]]; the Catalan number $C_4 = 14$ times $5! = 120$ gives $1680$.)
:::
:::

::: exercise A disjunction and a negation {level=2}
With $T(R) = 10\,000$, $V(R, A) = 20$ and $A$ uniformly distributed on $\set{1, \ldots, 20}$, estimate the sizes of $\sigma_{A = 3 \vee A = 5}(R)$, $\sigma_{A \ne 3}(R)$ and $\sigma_{A \le 5}(R)$. Why is the independence formula for $\vee$ wrong for the first?
::: solution
The conditions $A = 3$ and $A = 5$ are mutually exclusive, not independent: the correct selectivity is $\frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{20} = \frac{1}{10}$, estimate $1000$. The formula $s_1 + s_2 - s_1 s_2 = 0.0975$ (estimate $975$) subtracts an overlap that cannot exist. $\sigma_{A \ne 3}$: $1 - \frac1{20}$, estimate $9500$. $\sigma_{A \le 5}$: five of twenty values, $2500$ (the interpolation formula with min $1$ and max $20$ gives $(5 - 1)/(20 - 1) \approx 0.21$, estimate $2105$ — for integer values, counting values is more accurate than interpolating).
:::
:::

::: exercise Pushing a selection through a left join {level=2}
Give a small instance showing that $\sigma_{S.b = 1}(R \mathbin{\bowtie_{\mathrm{L}}} S) \ne R \mathbin{\bowtie_{\mathrm{L}}} \sigma_{S.b = 1}(S)$, where $\bowtie_{\mathrm{L}}$ denotes the left outer join on $R.a = S.a$ (SQL's `R LEFT JOIN S ON R.a = S.a`). Which side of a left join may a selection always be pushed into?
::: solution
Let $R = \set{(a{=}1)}$ and $S = \set{(a{=}1, b{=}2)}$. Then $R \bowtie_{\mathrm{L}} S = \set{(1, 2)}$ and the selection $b = 1$ leaves nothing. But $\sigma_{b=1}(S) = \emptyset$, and $R \bowtie_{\mathrm{L}} \emptyset = \set{(1, \text{null})}$: one row. A selection on attributes of the *preserved* side $R$ can always be pushed: $\sigma_\theta(R \bowtie_{\mathrm{L}} S) = \sigma_\theta(R) \bowtie_{\mathrm{L}} S$ if $\theta$ mentions only $R$, because each tuple of $R$ either appears (matched or padded) in the output with its own values or not at all, and $\theta$ depends only on those values.
:::
:::

::: exercise Histogram estimate {level=2 check="250"}
An equi-depth histogram on the age of $1100$ customers has $11$ buckets of $100$ customers each, with boundaries $18, 22, 25, 28, 31, 35, 40, 46, 54, 60, 70, 95$. Treating ages as continuous and uniform within each bucket, estimate the number of customers with age in $[35, 50]$. (Enter your estimate.)
::: solution
The range covers the bucket $[35, 40)$ completely ($100$ customers), the bucket $[40, 46)$ completely ($100$), and half of the bucket $[46, 54)$, since $(50 - 46)/(54 - 46) = 1/2$ ($50$). The estimate is $100 + 100 + 50 = 250$. Uniformity over the whole range $[18, 95]$ would give $1100 \cdot 15/77 \approx 214$; the histogram knows that middle ages are more common here than the extremes.
:::
:::

::: exercise Interesting orders {level=2}
Explain with an example why [[#alg-selinger]] with realistic costs must keep more than one plan per subset of relations, and what System R keeps.
::: solution
Suppose for $\set{R, S}$ the cheapest plan is a hash join costing $1000$, with unordered output, and a sort-merge join costs $1200$ but outputs tuples sorted on $S.b$. If the next step joins with $T$ on $S.b$, a merge join with $T$ (already sorted on $b$ through a clustered index) costs only a scan of the sorted inputs, say $300$, after the sort-merge plan, while after the hash plan the intermediate result must first be sorted, say $+800$. Then $1200 + 300 < 1000 + 800 + 300$: the cheaper subplan leads to the dearer full plan, violating the hypothesis of [[#thm-selinger]]. System R therefore keeps, for each subset, the cheapest plan for each interesting order (each order on an attribute that a later join, grouping or `ORDER BY` can use) and the cheapest unordered plan, and compares them when extending.
:::
:::

::: exercise Best plan with a product {level=3}
In [[#ex-dp]], replace the selection on departments by a selection on students that keeps $40$ of them ($S'$ with $T = 40$), and let $D$ be all $20$ departments. Find the best left-deep order in the $C_{\text{out}}$ model, and decide whether allowing Cartesian products could help.
::: solution
The estimates are: $S'E = 40 \cdot 100\,000/40\,000 = 100$, $EC = 100\,000$, $CD = 500 \cdot 20/20 = 500$; $S'EC = 100$, $ECD = 100\,000$; the whole join $100$; and for products $S'D = 800$, $S'C = 20\,000$, $S'ED = 2000$, $S'CD = 20\,000$. A left-deep plan pays for one two-relation result, one three-relation result and the final $100$. The smallest two-relation result is $S'E = 100$ and the smallest three-relation result is $S'EC = 100$, so no plan costs less than $300$, and $((S' \bowtie E) \bowtie C) \bowtie D$ (or the same with $E$ first) achieves it without products. The modified program agrees: `(300.0, '(((S ⋈ E) ⋈ C) ⋈ D)')`. Here products cannot help; in [[#ex-dp]] they were not needed either, but a product with a one-tuple relation was cheaper than some product-free orders. The general lesson: start from the most selective part of the query.
:::
:::

::: exercise Bushy dynamic programming {level=3}
Modify `best_left_deep` to consider bushy plans: for each subset $Q$, try every split of $Q$ into two non-empty disjoint parts $Q_1, Q_2$ connected by a join condition, with cost $\mathit{cost}(Q_1) + \mathit{cost}(Q_2) + \lvert \bowtie Q \rvert$. Show that the number of (subset, split) pairs examined is $O(3^n)$, and run it on [[#ex-dp]].
::: solution
```python
def best_bushy(names):
    best = {frozenset([r]): (0.0, r) for r in names}
    for k in range(2, len(names) + 1):
        for Q in map(frozenset, combinations(names, k)):
            items = sorted(Q)
            for m in range(1, k):                        # every split Q = Q1 + Q2
                for Q1 in map(frozenset, combinations(items, m)):
                    Q2 = Q - Q1
                    if Q1 in best and Q2 in best and any(frozenset((a, b)) in sel
                                                         for a in Q1 for b in Q2):
                        cost = best[Q1][0] + best[Q2][0] + size(Q)
                        if Q not in best or cost < best[Q][0]:
                            best[Q] = (cost, f"({best[Q1][1]} ⋈ {best[Q2][1]})")
    return best[frozenset(names)]

print(best_bushy("SECD"))
```

```console
$ python3 bushy.py
(10025.0, '(S ⋈ (E ⋈ (C ⋈ D)))')
```

Each element of $\set{R_1, \dots, R_n}$ is, for a given pair $(Q, Q_1)$, in $Q_1$, in $Q - Q_1$, or outside $Q$: three choices, so there are at most $3^n$ pairs, and the work is $O(3^n)$ (up to the cost of the connectivity test). On this chain query the best bushy plan is the left-deep plan already found, written with the operands of each join swapped (the splits are enumerated in a different order, and commutativity does not change $C_{\text{out}}$): a bushy plan such as $(S \bowtie E) \bowtie (C \bowtie D)$ costs $100\,000 + 25 + 5000$, worse, because $S \bowtie E$ is large. Bushy plans win when the query has two independent selective parts.
:::
