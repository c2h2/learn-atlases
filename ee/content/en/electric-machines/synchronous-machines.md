Almost every kilowatt-hour you have ever used was generated by a **synchronous machine**. Steam turbines in coal, gas and nuclear plants drive two- or four-pole synchronous generators of up to $1.7\,\mathrm{GW}$ each, water turbines drive slow, many-pole ones, and all of them, across a continent, turn in exact step with the frequency of the grid. Run as motors, synchronous machines drive large compressors, mills and ship propellers at a precisely constant speed, and, because their power factor can be adjusted at will, they can also supply reactive power to a plant or a network. Permanent-magnet synchronous motors in electric vehicles and robots are close cousins, treated in [[electric-machines/special-machines]].

This chapter analyses the synchronous machine in the balanced steady state. The stator is the three-phase winding of [[electric-machines/rotating-magnetic-fields]]; the rotor carries a DC field winding and turns at exactly synchronous speed. We derive the per-phase model, an internal emf behind a **synchronous reactance**, and show how to measure it by open- and short-circuit tests. Phasor diagrams give the generator's excitation and voltage regulation. The **power–angle characteristic** shows how real power depends on the load angle and how it limits stability, and the behaviour of a machine connected to an **infinite bus** explains how operators control real power with the turbine and reactive power with the field. We end with the synchronous motor, power-factor correction, and the two-reaction theory of salient-pole machines.

## Construction

The stator of a synchronous machine is a laminated core with a distributed three-phase winding, called the **armature** winding in this machine because it carries the load current. The rotor carries the **field** winding, supplied with direct current through slip rings or, in most large machines, from a **brushless exciter**: a small AC generator on the same shaft whose output is rectified by diodes rotating with the rotor.

There are two rotor types. **Round-rotor** (cylindrical, non-salient) machines have the field winding distributed in slots on a solid steel cylinder, giving a uniform air gap and a nearly sinusoidal field. They are used for high speeds, $3000$ or $3600\,\mathrm{r/min}$ (two-pole) and $1500$ or $1800\,\mathrm{r/min}$ (four-pole), because a long, slender rotor withstands the enormous centrifugal forces; such **turbo-generators** may be $1.2\,\mathrm m$ in diameter and $8\,\mathrm m$ long. **Salient-pole** machines have concentrated field coils on projecting poles. They are used at low speeds, typically water turbines at $75$ to $500\,\mathrm{r/min}$, which by $n = 120f/P$ need many poles, giving a short rotor of large diameter. Because the air gap is non-uniform, a salient-pole machine has different reactances along and across the poles.

## The equivalent circuit

### Internal emf and armature reaction

With the rotor turning at synchronous speed and field current $I_f$, the rotor field sweeps the stator winding and induces in each phase the **internal** or **excitation emf**

$$
E_f = 4.44fk_wN_{\text{ph}}\Phi_f,
$$

by [[electric-machines/rotating-magnetic-fields#thm-ac-emf]], where $\Phi_f$ is the flux per pole produced by the field alone. At no load, $E_f$ is the terminal voltage, and its dependence on $I_f$ is the machine's **open-circuit characteristic** (OCC), linear at first (the **air-gap line**) and then bending as the iron saturates.

When the armature carries balanced currents, they produce their own rotating field at synchronous speed, the **armature reaction**, which adds to the rotor field. The resultant gap field, which is what induces the actual air-gap voltage, is therefore not the field of the rotor alone.

::: definition Synchronous reactance {#def-sync-reactance}
In a round-rotor machine with linear iron, the armature-reaction field is proportional to the armature current and induces in each phase a voltage $-jX_{\text{ar}}\mathbf I_a$ that lags the current by $90^\circ$. Together with the leakage reactance $X_\ell$ of the armature winding, this defines the **synchronous reactance**

$$
X_s = X_\ell + X_{\text{ar}},
$$

and the **synchronous impedance** $Z_s = R_a + jX_s$, where $R_a$ is the armature resistance per phase. Typical unsaturated values are $1$ to $2.5\,\mathrm{pu}$ for turbo-generators and $0.6$ to $1.5\,\mathrm{pu}$ for salient-pole machines; $R_a$ is usually below $0.01\,\mathrm{pu}$ and often neglected.
:::

::: theorem Per-phase model of a round-rotor synchronous machine {#thm-sync-model}
In balanced steady state, each phase of a round-rotor synchronous machine with linear iron is equivalent to the excitation emf $\mathbf E_f$ (whose magnitude is set by the field current and whose phase is fixed by the rotor position) in series with the synchronous impedance. With the generator convention (current $\mathbf I_a$ leaving the terminal),

$$
\mathbf V_t = \mathbf E_f - (R_a + jX_s)\mathbf I_a,
$$ {#eq-sync-gen}

and with the motor convention (current entering), $\mathbf V_t = \mathbf E_f + (R_a + jX_s)\mathbf I_a$. The angle $\delta$ by which $\mathbf E_f$ leads $\mathbf V_t$ is the **load angle** (torque angle, power angle); $\delta > 0$ for a generator and $\delta < 0$ for a motor.
:::

::: proof
With linear iron the gap mmf is the sum of the rotor mmf and the armature mmf, and the gap flux, and hence the induced voltage, is the sum of the contributions of each. The rotor contributes $\mathbf E_f$. The armature mmf of [[electric-machines/rotating-magnetic-fields#thm-rotating-field]] is in space phase with the current of the phase whose axis it is crossing; its flux induces in each phase a voltage proportional to $\omega$ times the flux linkage, so $90^\circ$ behind the current that produced it, i.e. $-jX_{\text{ar}}\mathbf I_a$. The resultant **air-gap voltage** is $\mathbf E_r = \mathbf E_f - jX_{\text{ar}}\mathbf I_a$. The terminal voltage is the air-gap voltage less the drop in the leakage reactance and the resistance: $\mathbf V_t = \mathbf E_r - (R_a + jX_\ell)\mathbf I_a$. Combining gives [[#eq-sync-gen]]. Reversing the current direction gives the motor form.
:::

The model says that a synchronous machine looks, from its terminals, like a voltage source behind a large reactance. The reactance is mostly armature reaction, not leakage: it is a fiction that represents the change of the gap field by the load current, and it is valid only when the iron is linear, or approximately linear around a given operating point.

### Measuring the synchronous reactance

Two tests suffice. The **open-circuit test** drives the machine at synchronous speed with the armature open and records the terminal voltage against $I_f$ (the OCC). The **short-circuit test** shorts the three terminals and records the armature current against $I_f$ (the short-circuit characteristic, SCC). With the armature shorted the armature reaction nearly cancels the field, so the iron is unsaturated and the SCC is a straight line. Then

$$
X_s \approx \frac{V_{\text{ph,oc}}(I_f)}{I_{\text{sc}}(I_f)}
$$

at the same field current, with the open-circuit voltage taken from the air-gap line for the **unsaturated** value or from the OCC at rated voltage for an approximate **saturated** value. The **short-circuit ratio** (SCR), the field current for rated open-circuit voltage divided by the field current for rated short-circuit current, is approximately $1/X_s$ in per unit (saturated). Machines with a high SCR (low $X_s$) are larger and more expensive but more stable.

::: example Synchronous reactance from tests {#ex-tests}
A three-phase, star-connected, $20\,\mathrm{MVA}$, $11\,\mathrm{kV}$, $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ generator gives rated open-circuit voltage at a field current of $200\,\mathrm A$, on the linear part of the OCC. The same field current drives $1300\,\mathrm A$ in the short-circuit test. Find $X_s$ in ohms and per unit, and the SCR.
::: solution
The phase voltage is $11\,000/\sqrt3 = 6351\,\mathrm V$, so $X_s = 6351/1300 = 4.885\,\Omega$ per phase. The base impedance is $Z_b = 11^2/20 = 6.05\,\Omega$, so $X_s = 0.807\,\mathrm{pu}$. Rated current is $20\times10^6/(\sqrt3\times11\,000) = 1050\,\mathrm A$, which on the straight-line SCC needs $200\times1050/1300 = 161.5\,\mathrm A$ of field current, so $\mathrm{SCR} = 200/161.5 = 1.24 = 1/0.807$. Since the test point is on the air-gap line, this is the unsaturated reactance.
:::
:::

## Phasor diagrams and voltage regulation

For a generator supplying current $\mathbf I_a$ at terminal voltage $\mathbf V_t$, [[#eq-sync-gen]] gives the excitation needed: $\mathbf E_f = \mathbf V_t + (R_a + jX_s)\mathbf I_a$. Drawing $\mathbf V_t$ horizontally, the drop $jX_s\mathbf I_a$ is perpendicular to the current, so for a lagging current it points up and to the right and $\mathbf E_f$ is much larger than $\mathbf V_t$; for a leading current it points up and to the left, and $\mathbf E_f$ can be smaller than $\mathbf V_t$. The **voltage regulation** of a generator is $(E_f - V_t)/V_t$, the rise in terminal voltage when the load is removed at constant field current and speed.

::: example Excitation and regulation of the 20 MVA generator {#ex-regulation}
The generator of [[#ex-tests]] supplies rated current at rated voltage. Neglecting $R_a$ and saturation, find $E_f$, the load angle and the voltage regulation at power factors (a) $0.8$ lagging, (b) $1$, (c) $0.8$ leading.
::: solution
With $\mathbf V_t = 6351\angle 0^\circ\,\mathrm V$ and $X_s = 4.885\,\Omega$:

(a) $\mathbf I_a = 1050\angle -36.87^\circ\,\mathrm A$; $jX_s\mathbf I_a = 5128\angle 53.13^\circ = 3077 + j4102\,\mathrm V$; $\mathbf E_f = 9428 + j4102 = 10\,282\angle 23.5^\circ\,\mathrm V$ ($17.8\,\mathrm{kV}$ line). Regulation $(10\,282 - 6351)/6351 = 62\,\%$.

(b) $\mathbf E_f = 6351 + j5128 = 8163\angle 38.9^\circ\,\mathrm V$, regulation $29\,\%$.

(c) $\mathbf I_a = 1050\angle 36.87^\circ$; $\mathbf E_f = 6351 - 3077 + j4102 = 5249\angle 51.4^\circ\,\mathrm V$, regulation $-17\,\%$: removing a leading load **lowers** the voltage.

The regulation of synchronous generators is large because $X_s$ is large; no generator is operated at constant field current. An **automatic voltage regulator** adjusts $I_f$ continuously to hold $V_t$. Because the iron saturates, the actual $E_f$ in (a) would need somewhat more field current than the linear model suggests.
:::
:::

::: warning Phase and line, generator and motor
$E_f$, $V_t$ and $X_s$ in the per-phase model are **phase** quantities of the star equivalent; quote line voltages only at the end, multiplied by $\sqrt3$. Three-phase power is three times the per-phase power. Also keep the convention straight: in the generator convention $\mathbf E_f = \mathbf V_t + jX_s\mathbf I_a$ and $\mathbf E_f$ leads $\mathbf V_t$; in the motor convention $\mathbf E_f = \mathbf V_t - jX_s\mathbf I_a$ and $\mathbf E_f$ lags. Mixing them flips the sign of $\delta$ and turns a generator into a motor on paper.
:::

## The power–angle characteristic

::: theorem Power and reactive power of a round-rotor machine {#thm-power-angle}
For a round-rotor synchronous generator with $R_a = 0$, phase voltages $V_t$ and $E_f$ and load angle $\delta$, the three-phase real and reactive power delivered at the terminals are

$$
P = \frac{3V_tE_f}{X_s}\sin\delta,\qquad Q = \frac{3V_t}{X_s}\left(E_f\cos\delta - V_t\right).
$$ {#eq-power-angle}

The real power is greatest, $P_{\max} = 3V_tE_f/X_s$, at $\delta = 90^\circ$: the **steady-state stability limit**. The torque is $T = P/\omega_s$.
:::

::: proof
With $\mathbf V_t = V_t\angle 0^\circ$ and $\mathbf E_f = E_f\angle\delta$, [[#eq-sync-gen]] gives $\mathbf I_a = (\mathbf E_f - \mathbf V_t)/(jX_s)$. The complex power per phase is

$$
\mathbf V_t\mathbf I_a^* = V_t\,\frac{E_f\angle -\delta - V_t}{-jX_s} = \frac{jV_t}{X_s}\bigl(E_f\cos\delta - jE_f\sin\delta - V_t\bigr) = \frac{V_tE_f\sin\delta}{X_s} + j\frac{V_t(E_f\cos\delta - V_t)}{X_s}.
$$

Multiplying by $3$ gives [[#eq-power-angle]]. With no losses the terminal power equals the air-gap power, and the rotor turns at $\omega_s$, so $T = P/\omega_s$.
:::

Three facts follow. **Real power depends on $\delta$**: to deliver more power, the turbine must push the rotor further ahead of the terminal voltage. **Reactive power depends mainly on $E_f$**: for small $\delta$, $Q\approx3V_t(E_f - V_t)/X_s$, so a generator whose $E_f$ exceeds $V_t$ (**overexcited**) supplies reactive power, and one with $E_f < V_t$ (**underexcited**) absorbs it. And there is a **stability limit**: beyond $\delta = 90^\circ$ more angle gives less power, so if the turbine's input exceeds $P_{\max}$ the rotor accelerates, slips poles and loses synchronism.

The slope of the curve, $\mathrm dP/\mathrm d\delta = (3V_tE_f/X_s)\cos\delta$, is the **synchronising power coefficient**. It measures the restoring power when the rotor is pushed ahead of its equilibrium by a disturbance, and so acts like the stiffness of a spring connecting the rotor to the grid. Together with the rotor's inertia it sets a natural frequency of oscillation of the load angle, typically $0.5$ to $2\,\mathrm{Hz}$, studied with the **swing equation** in [[power-systems]].

## Operation on an infinite bus

A generator connected to a large grid sees a bus whose voltage and frequency are fixed by thousands of other machines: an **infinite bus**. Its speed is locked to the grid, so the operator cannot change it. Two controls remain.

- **Prime-mover torque (governor).** Opening the steam or water valve raises the shaft torque. The rotor advances momentarily, $\delta$ increases, and the electrical power rises until it balances the new mechanical input. Real power is controlled by the turbine.
- **Field current (exciter).** Changing $I_f$ changes $E_f$. At constant real power, $E_f\sin\delta$ is fixed by [[#eq-power-angle]], so the tip of $\mathbf E_f$ moves along a horizontal line in the phasor diagram, $\delta$ adjusts, and the current's reactive component changes. Reactive power is controlled by the field.

::: theorem Constant-power locus and V curves {#thm-v-curves}
For a round-rotor machine delivering constant real power $P$ to an infinite bus of phase voltage $V$, the armature current varies with excitation as

$$
I_a = \frac1{X_s}\sqrt{\left(\sqrt{E_f^2 - c^2} - V\right)^2 + c^2},\qquad c = \frac{PX_s}{3V},
$$ {#eq-v-curve}

which is smallest, $I_a = P/(3V)$, at unity power factor, $E_f = \sqrt{V^2 + c^2}$, and rises for both higher (lagging, overexcited) and lower (leading, underexcited) excitation. Synchronism is lost when $E_f$ falls to $c$, where $\delta = 90^\circ$.
:::

::: proof
By [[#eq-power-angle]], $E_f\sin\delta = PX_s/(3V) = c$, so $E_f\cos\delta = \sqrt{E_f^2 - c^2}$ for $\delta \le 90^\circ$. Then $jX_s\mathbf I_a = \mathbf E_f - \mathbf V = (E_f\cos\delta - V) + jc$, whose magnitude divided by $X_s$ gives [[#eq-v-curve]]. The current is smallest when the real part vanishes, $E_f\cos\delta = V$, i.e. when $jX_s\mathbf I_a$ is vertical and $\mathbf I_a$ is in phase with $\mathbf V$. At $E_f = c$ the square root vanishes and $\delta = 90^\circ$; below it no steady solution exists.
:::

::: example Reducing the excitation on the bus {#ex-infinite-bus}
The generator of [[#ex-tests]] is connected to an infinite $11\,\mathrm{kV}$ bus and delivers $16\,\mathrm{MW}$ at $0.8$ power factor lagging, so $E_f = 10\,282\,\mathrm V$ and $\delta = 23.5^\circ$ (from [[#ex-regulation]]). (a) Find the maximum power at this excitation and the synchronising power per degree. (b) The field current is reduced by $20\,\%$, with the turbine power unchanged. Find the new load angle, armature current and power factor. (c) What is the smallest excitation at which it can still deliver $16\,\mathrm{MW}$?
::: solution
(a) $P_{\max} = 3\times6351\times10\,282/4.885 = 40.1\,\mathrm{MW}$. Synchronising power: $P_{\max}\cos23.5^\circ = 36.8\,\mathrm{MW/rad} = 0.642\,\mathrm{MW}$ per degree.

(b) With linear iron, $E_f = 0.8\times10\,282 = 8225\,\mathrm V$. From $P = 3V_tE_f\sin\delta/X_s$: $\sin\delta = 16\times10^6\times4.885/(3\times6351\times8225) = 0.499$, so $\delta = 29.9^\circ$. Then $\mathbf I_a = (8225\angle 29.9^\circ - 6351)/(j4.885) = 855\angle -10.7^\circ\,\mathrm A$. The power factor is $\cos10.7^\circ = 0.982$ lagging; the reactive output has fallen from $12\,\mathrm{Mvar}$ to $3.0\,\mathrm{Mvar}$, and the current from $1050\,\mathrm A$ to $855\,\mathrm A$, while the real power stays $16\,\mathrm{MW}$.

(c) The limit is $\delta = 90^\circ$: $E_f = PX_s/(3V_t) = 16\times10^6\times4.885/(3\times6351) = 4103\,\mathrm V$, that is $0.65$ of rated phase voltage. Generators are never run so close to the limit; protection and the voltage regulator keep the excitation well above it, and the **capability chart** of the machine marks the safe underexcited region.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A synchronous generator on an infinite bus is delivering power at unity power factor. The operator increases the field current, leaving the governor setting unchanged. What happens?
- [ ] The real power output increases
- [ ] The rotor speeds up
- [x] The load angle decreases and the generator begins to supply lagging reactive power to the bus
- [ ] The generator begins to absorb reactive power from the bus
::: solution
Real power is fixed by the turbine, so $E_f\sin\delta$ is constant: as $E_f$ rises, $\delta$ falls. The speed is locked to the grid. By [[#eq-power-angle]], $Q$ rises from zero: an overexcited generator supplies reactive power (it behaves like a capacitor as seen by the grid, delivering current that lags the bus voltage).
:::

## The synchronous motor

A synchronous motor runs at exactly synchronous speed at any load up to its pull-out torque, $T_{\max} = 3V_tE_f/(X_s\omega_s)$. Like a generator, its reactive power is set by its excitation: underexcited it draws lagging current like an induction motor, overexcited it draws **leading** current and supplies reactive power to the plant. A synchronous motor run with no mechanical load purely to supply reactive power is a **synchronous condenser**.

A synchronous motor has no starting torque of its own: at standstill the stator field sweeps past the rotor at full speed and the torque averages to zero ([[electric-machines/energy-conversion#thm-average-torque]]). Motors are started either from a variable-frequency drive or as induction motors, using **damper** (amortisseur) **windings**, cage bars in the pole faces, and the DC field is applied when the rotor is close to synchronous speed. Damper windings also damp oscillations of the load angle in normal operation.

::: example Power-factor correction with a synchronous motor {#ex-pf-correction}
A plant supplied at $6.6\,\mathrm{kV}$ has a load of $2\,\mathrm{MW}$ at $0.75$ power factor lagging. A new compressor needs a $520\,\mathrm{kW}$ (electrical input) synchronous motor, rated $1\,\mathrm{MVA}$, $6.6\,\mathrm{kV}$, with $X_s = 1.0\,\mathrm{pu}$ and $R_a$ negligible. Choose the motor's excitation so that the plant's overall power factor becomes $0.92$ lagging. Check that the motor is within its rating.
::: solution
**Reactive power budget.** The existing load draws $Q_L = 2\tan(\cos^{-1}0.75) = 1.764\,\mathrm{Mvar}$. With the motor the real power is $2.52\,\mathrm{MW}$, and at $0.92$ lagging the total reactive power must be $2.52\tan(\cos^{-1}0.92) = 1.074\,\mathrm{Mvar}$. The motor must therefore **supply** $0.690\,\mathrm{Mvar}$ while absorbing $0.520\,\mathrm{MW}$. Its apparent power is $\sqrt{0.520^2 + 0.690^2} = 0.864\,\mathrm{MVA}$, within its $1\,\mathrm{MVA}$ rating.

**Excitation.** Per phase, $V_t = 6600/\sqrt3 = 3811\,\mathrm V$ and $X_s = 1.0\times6.6^2/1 = 43.56\,\Omega$. The motor's complex power absorbed is $\mathbf S = 0.520 - j0.690\,\mathrm{MVA}$, so

$$
\mathbf I_a = \left(\frac{\mathbf S}{3\mathbf V_t}\right)^* = \frac{(0.520 + j0.690)\times10^6}{3\times3811} = 75.6\angle 53.0^\circ\,\mathrm A,
$$

leading. In the motor convention, $\mathbf E_f = \mathbf V_t - jX_s\mathbf I_a = 3811 - j43.56\times75.6\angle 53.0^\circ = 6739\angle -17.1^\circ\,\mathrm V$, that is $11.7\,\mathrm{kV}$ line, $1.77\,\mathrm{pu}$: strongly overexcited, with the rotor lagging the terminal voltage by $17.1^\circ$.

The field current must be large enough to produce $E_f = 1.77\,\mathrm{pu}$ on the OCC, which the field winding must be designed for. The plant's line current changes from $233\,\mathrm A$ to $240\,\mathrm A$: despite the added $520\,\mathrm{kW}$, it barely rises, because the reactive current has fallen.
:::
:::

::: application Capability chart
A generator's operating limits form its **capability chart**, drawn in the $P$–$Q$ plane. The armature heating limit is a circle of radius equal to the rated MVA; the field heating limit is a circle centred at $Q = -3V_t^2/X_s$ with radius $3V_tE_{f,\max}/X_s$, which restricts operation at lagging power factors; the turbine limits $P$; and in the underexcited region the steady-state stability limit and stator-end heating restrict how much reactive power can be absorbed. Grid operators dispatch generators within these charts, and the rated power factor (often $0.85$ or $0.9$ lagging) marks where the armature and field limits meet.
:::

## Salient-pole machines

In a salient-pole machine the air gap is short over the pole faces (the **direct axis**, $d$) and long between the poles (the **quadrature axis**, $q$). An armature mmf along the $d$ axis meets less reluctance than one along the $q$ axis, so a single synchronous reactance cannot describe the machine. André Blondel's **two-reaction theory** resolves the armature current into components along the two axes, each with its own reactance: the **direct-axis synchronous reactance** $X_d$ and the smaller **quadrature-axis synchronous reactance** $X_q$ (typically $X_q\approx0.6X_d$ to $0.7X_d$).

::: theorem Two-reaction model and power–angle equation {#thm-two-reaction}
Resolve the armature current into $\mathbf I_d$ (along the $d$ axis, perpendicular to $\mathbf E_f$) and $\mathbf I_q$ (in phase with $\mathbf E_f$). With $R_a = 0$, in the generator convention,

$$
\mathbf E_f = \mathbf V_t + jX_d\mathbf I_d + jX_q\mathbf I_q,
$$ {#eq-two-reaction}

and the three-phase power delivered at load angle $\delta$ is

$$
P = \frac{3V_tE_f}{X_d}\sin\delta + \frac{3V_t^2}2\left(\frac1{X_q} - \frac1{X_d}\right)\sin2\delta.
$$ {#eq-salient-power}
:::

::: proof
Each current component produces a sinusoidal armature mmf along its own axis, where the reluctance is constant, so each induces a voltage $90^\circ$ behind it with its own reactance; superposition gives [[#eq-two-reaction]]. Take $\mathbf E_f$ along the $q$ axis at angle $\delta$ from $\mathbf V_t = V_t\angle0^\circ$. Resolve $\mathbf V_t$ into components along and perpendicular to $\mathbf E_f$: $V_t\cos\delta$ along $\mathbf E_f$ and $V_t\sin\delta$ perpendicular to it (pointing in the $-d$ direction). Along $\mathbf E_f$, the only drop is $X_dI_d$ (since $jX_d\mathbf I_d$ is parallel to $\mathbf E_f$), so $E_f = V_t\cos\delta + X_dI_d$, giving $I_d = (E_f - V_t\cos\delta)/X_d$. Perpendicular to $\mathbf E_f$, the only drop is $X_qI_q$: $X_qI_q = V_t\sin\delta$, so $I_q = V_t\sin\delta/X_q$. The power per phase is the sum of the products of voltage and current components along each axis: $P/3 = I_qV_t\cos\delta + I_dV_t\sin\delta$, with $I_d$ taken positive in the direction that makes the generated power positive (the component of $\mathbf V_t$ along $-d$). Substituting,

$$
\frac P3 = \frac{V_t^2\sin\delta\cos\delta}{X_q} + \frac{V_t\sin\delta(E_f - V_t\cos\delta)}{X_d} = \frac{V_tE_f\sin\delta}{X_d} + V_t^2\sin\delta\cos\delta\left(\frac1{X_q} - \frac1{X_d}\right),
$$

and $\sin\delta\cos\delta = \tfrac12\sin2\delta$.
:::

The second term is **reluctance power**: it exists even with no field current, because the salient rotor tends to align with the armature field ([[electric-machines/energy-conversion]]). It makes the power–angle curve steeper at small angles and moves the peak below $90^\circ$. To find $\mathbf E_f$ from terminal conditions, first form $\mathbf E_Q = \mathbf V_t + jX_q\mathbf I_a$: it lies along $\mathbf E_f$ and so gives $\delta$; then $E_f = E_Q + (X_d - X_q)\abs{I_d}$.

::: example A salient-pole generator {#ex-salient}
A salient-pole generator with $X_d = 1.0\,\mathrm{pu}$ and $X_q = 0.6\,\mathrm{pu}$ delivers rated current ($1\,\mathrm{pu}$) at rated voltage and $0.8$ power factor lagging. Find $E_f$ and $\delta$, and compare with a round-rotor calculation using $X_s = X_d$. Find the maximum power at this excitation.
::: solution
$\mathbf I_a = 1\angle -36.87^\circ = 0.8 - j0.6$. Then $\mathbf E_Q = 1 + j0.6(0.8 - j0.6) = 1.36 + j0.48 = 1.442\angle 19.44^\circ$, so $\delta = 19.44^\circ$. The current makes the angle $19.44^\circ + 36.87^\circ = 56.31^\circ$ with $\mathbf E_f$, so $\abs{I_d} = \sin56.31^\circ = 0.832$ and $I_q = \cos56.31^\circ = 0.555\,\mathrm{pu}$. Hence $E_f = 1.442 + (1.0 - 0.6)\times0.832 = 1.775\,\mathrm{pu}$.

Check with [[#eq-salient-power]] in per unit (dropping the $3$): $1.775\sin19.44^\circ + 0.5(1/0.6 - 1)\sin38.88^\circ = 0.591 + 0.209 = 0.800\,\mathrm{pu}$. Correct.

A round-rotor model with $X_s = 1.0$ would give $\mathbf E_f = 1 + j(0.8 - j0.6) = 1.6 + j0.8 = 1.789\angle 26.57^\circ$: almost the same excitation but a larger angle, $26.6^\circ$ instead of $19.4^\circ$. Maximising $P(\delta)$ numerically gives $P_{\max} = 1.884\,\mathrm{pu}$ at $\delta = 72.2^\circ$, against $1.775\,\mathrm{pu}$ at $90^\circ$ for the field term alone. Saliency makes the machine stiffer and slightly stronger.
:::
:::

## Synchronising a generator

Before a generator is connected to a live bus its voltage must match the bus in four respects: the same **phase sequence**, the same **frequency** (in practice slightly higher, so that it picks up a little load on connection), the same **voltage magnitude**, and the same **phase angle** at the instant the breaker closes. A mismatch causes a surge current limited only by the machine's small subtransient reactance and a violent torque on the shaft. Synchronisation was once done by watching lamps and a **synchroscope**; today automatic synchronisers close the breaker when all four conditions are within tolerance.

::: history
The early AC stations, such as Sebastian Ziani de Ferranti's Deptford power station in London (designed in 1887 and supplying $10\,\mathrm{kV}$ from 1890), used single-phase alternators. The three-phase transmission from Lauffen to Frankfurt in 1891 was fed by a three-phase generator designed by Charles E. L. Brown of Oerlikon, and in 1895 the Adams station at Niagara Falls began operating Westinghouse two-phase generators of $5000$ horsepower. Brown, by then a founder of Brown, Boveri & Cie, developed the cylindrical slotted rotor for high-speed turbo-generators around 1900, as steam turbines replaced reciprocating engines. André Blondel published the two-reaction theory of salient-pole machines in 1899, and Robert H. Park of General Electric introduced the $dq$ transformation and the general dynamic model of the synchronous machine in 1929.
:::

## Where this leads

The synchronous machine is the heart of [[power-systems]]: the per-phase model, extended by **transient** and **subtransient** reactances that describe the machine's response in the first cycles after a disturbance, is the basis of fault calculations, and the power–angle curve and the swing equation are the basis of stability studies. The excitation system and automatic voltage regulator, and the governor, are the subject of power-system control. In [[electric-machines/special-machines]] the field winding is replaced by permanent magnets, and with an inverter and a rotor-position sensor the synchronous machine becomes the brushless motor of modern drives, controlled by the same $dq$ ideas Park introduced.

::: summary
- A synchronous machine has a DC-excited rotor turning at exactly $n_s = 120f/P$; round rotors are used at high speed, salient poles at low speed.
- Per phase it is an emf $E_f$ (set by the field current) behind a synchronous reactance $X_s$, mostly armature reaction; $X_s$ is found from open- and short-circuit tests, and the SCR $\approx 1/X_s$ (pu).
- Generator: $\mathbf E_f = \mathbf V_t + jX_s\mathbf I_a$, with $\mathbf E_f$ leading $\mathbf V_t$ by the load angle $\delta$. Regulation is large, so the field is controlled automatically.
- $P = 3V_tE_f\sin\delta/X_s$ and $Q = 3V_t(E_f\cos\delta - V_t)/X_s$: real power is set by the prime mover through $\delta$, reactive power by the field through $E_f$; the steady-state limit is $\delta = 90^\circ$.
- On an infinite bus, V curves show the armature current against excitation at constant power, with a minimum at unity power factor; overexcited machines supply reactive power.
- Synchronous motors run at constant speed and, overexcited, correct a plant's power factor; they start as induction motors on their damper windings or from a drive.
- Salient-pole machines need $X_d$ and $X_q$; their power includes a reluctance term $\propto(1/X_q - 1/X_d)\sin2\delta$.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Synchronous reactance {level=1 check="(6600/sqrt(3))/420"}
A star-connected, $6.6\,\mathrm{kV}$ generator gives rated open-circuit voltage and, at the same field current on the air-gap line, a short-circuit current of $420\,\mathrm A$. Find the unsaturated synchronous reactance per phase in ohms.
::: solution
$X_s = (6600/\sqrt3)/420 = 3811/420 = 9.07\,\Omega$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Maximum power {level=1 check="3*6351*8000/5"}
A round-rotor generator with $X_s = 5\,\Omega$ per phase and $E_f = 8000\,\mathrm V$ per phase is connected to a bus of $6351\,\mathrm V$ per phase. Find the steady-state maximum power in watts.
::: solution
$P_{\max} = 3V_tE_f/X_s = 3\times6351\times8000/5 = 30.5\,\mathrm{MW}$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Load angle {level=1 check="asin(0.5)*180/pi"}
A generator delivering half of its maximum power at constant excitation operates at what load angle, in degrees?
::: solution
$\sin\delta = 0.5$, so $\delta = 30^\circ$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Excitation for unity power factor {level=2 check="sqrt(1+0.9^2)"}
A round-rotor generator with $X_s = 0.9\,\mathrm{pu}$ delivers $1\,\mathrm{pu}$ current at unity power factor and $1\,\mathrm{pu}$ terminal voltage. Find $E_f$ in per unit and the load angle.
::: solution
$\mathbf E_f = 1 + j0.9\times1 = 1.345\angle 42.0^\circ\,\mathrm{pu}$. $E_f = 1.345\,\mathrm{pu}$, $\delta = 42.0^\circ$.
:::
:::

::: exercise A synchronous motor's pull-out torque {level=2 check="3*(400/sqrt(3))*300/(2*2*pi*1500/60)"}
A $400\,\mathrm V$, four-pole, $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ synchronous motor has $X_s = 2\,\Omega$ per phase and is excited to $E_f = 300\,\mathrm V$ per phase. Find its pull-out torque in N·m.
::: solution
$P_{\max} = 3\times230.9\times300/2 = 103.9\,\mathrm{kW}$; $\omega_s = 157.1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$; $T_{\max} = 661\,\mathrm{N\,m}$.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reactive power from excitation {level=2}
The generator of [[#ex-infinite-bus]] is delivering $16\,\mathrm{MW}$. Find the excitation $E_f$ (phase) for which it delivers zero reactive power, and the armature current then.
::: solution
At unity power factor, $E_f = \sqrt{V^2 + c^2}$ with $c = PX_s/(3V) = 4103\,\mathrm V$ ([[#thm-v-curves]]): $E_f = \sqrt{6351^2 + 4103^2} = 7561\,\mathrm V$, and $\delta = \arctan(4103/6351) = 32.9^\circ$. The current is $P/(3V) = 16\times10^6/(3\times6351) = 840\,\mathrm A$, the minimum of the V curve.
:::
:::

::: exercise Synchronous condenser {level=2 check="3*(6600/sqrt(3))*(9000-6600/sqrt(3))/43.56"}
A synchronous condenser on a $6.6\,\mathrm{kV}$ bus has $X_s = 43.56\,\Omega$ per phase (losses neglected). Find the reactive power it supplies, in var, when $E_f = 9000\,\mathrm V$ per phase.
::: solution
With $P = 0$, $\delta = 0$ and $Q = 3V_t(E_f - V_t)/X_s = 3\times3811\times(9000 - 3811)/43.56 = 1.362\,\mathrm{Mvar}$. Underexcited ($E_f < 3811\,\mathrm V$) it would absorb reactive power instead.
:::
:::

::: exercise Reluctance motor from a salient-pole machine {level=3}
A salient-pole synchronous motor with $X_d = 1.0\,\mathrm{pu}$ and $X_q = 0.6\,\mathrm{pu}$ loses its field excitation ($E_f = 0$) while connected to a $1\,\mathrm{pu}$ supply. (a) What is the largest per-unit power it can still convert, and at what angle? (b) Could it continue to drive a load of $0.3\,\mathrm{pu}$? (c) What would happen to a round-rotor machine in the same situation?
::: solution
(a) With $E_f = 0$, [[#eq-salient-power]] in per unit gives $P = \tfrac12(1/0.6 - 1)\sin2\delta = 0.333\sin2\delta$. The maximum is $0.333\,\mathrm{pu}$ at $\delta = 45^\circ$ (magnitude; for motoring $\delta = -45^\circ$).

(b) Yes, at $\abs\delta = \tfrac12\arcsin(0.3/0.333) = 32.1^\circ$; the machine runs on as a reluctance motor, drawing a large lagging current to magnetise itself.

(c) A round-rotor machine has $X_d = X_q$, so the reluctance term vanishes, and with $E_f = 0$ it has no synchronous torque at all: it falls out of step and runs (if at all) as a poor induction motor on its damper windings. Loss-of-field protection trips such machines.
:::
:::

::: exercise Designing the excitation range {level=3}
A $100\,\mathrm{MVA}$, $13.8\,\mathrm{kV}$ round-rotor generator has $X_s = 1.8\,\mathrm{pu}$ and is rated at $0.85$ power factor lagging. (a) Find the per-unit excitation needed at rated output. (b) The operator also wants to absorb $30\,\mathrm{Mvar}$ while delivering $60\,\mathrm{MW}$. Find the excitation and load angle then, and decide whether this point respects a practical stability margin of $\delta \le 70^\circ$. (c) Find the excitation range the field system must cover between these two points.
::: hint
Work in per unit on $100\,\mathrm{MVA}$ with $V_t = 1$; then $P = E_f\sin\delta/X_s$ and $Q = (E_f\cos\delta - 1)/X_s$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\mathbf I = 1\angle -31.79^\circ = 0.85 - j0.527$. $\mathbf E_f = 1 + j1.8(0.85 - j0.527) = 1.949 + j1.53 = 2.478\angle 38.1^\circ\,\mathrm{pu}$.

(b) $P = 0.6$, $Q = -0.3$: $E_f\sin\delta = 0.6\times1.8 = 1.08$ and $E_f\cos\delta = 1 + (-0.3)(1.8) = 0.46$. So $E_f = \sqrt{1.08^2 + 0.46^2} = 1.174\,\mathrm{pu}$ and $\delta = \arctan(1.08/0.46) = 66.9^\circ$, inside the $70^\circ$ margin, but only just; absorbing much more reactive power at this output would breach it.

(c) The field must cover $E_f$ from about $1.17$ to $2.48\,\mathrm{pu}$ (on the air-gap line), a ratio of $2.1$; allowing for saturation at the upper end, the field current range is wider still. This is typical of large generators, whose exciters are rated for roughly twice the no-load field current at rated output, plus a ceiling margin for transient forcing.
:::
:::
