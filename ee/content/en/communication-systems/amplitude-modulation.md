The first radio broadcasts of speech and music, in the 1900s and 1910s, varied the amplitude of a radio-frequency carrier in step with the sound. A century later the same method still fills the medium-wave band from $531$ to $1602\,\mathrm{kHz}$ (to $1700\,\mathrm{kHz}$ in the Americas), and a receiver for it can be built from a coil, a capacitor, a diode and an earphone, with no power supply at all. The simplicity has a price — conventional AM wastes most of its power on a carrier that carries no information, and its bandwidth is twice the message bandwidth — and the variants developed to remove those costs (suppressed-carrier, single-sideband and vestigial-sideband modulation) are still at the heart of radio, television and the design of every modern quadrature transmitter.

This lesson builds each form of **amplitude modulation** from its complex envelope, derives its spectrum, power and bandwidth, and analyses the two ways of demodulating it: **coherent detection**, which multiplies by a locally generated carrier, and **envelope detection**, which simply follows the peaks. It ends with the **superheterodyne receiver**, the architecture that made selective, sensitive radio receivers possible and that survives in almost every receiver built today. Throughout, the message $m(t)$ is a real low-pass signal of bandwidth $W$ with no DC component, and the carrier is $A_c\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ with $f_c \gg W$. We use the complex envelopes and the Hilbert transform of [[communication-systems/signals-spectra-channels]].

## Double-sideband suppressed-carrier modulation {#sec-dsb}

The most direct way to put a message on a carrier is to multiply them.

::: definition DSB-SC modulation {#def-dsb}
A **double-sideband suppressed-carrier** (DSB-SC) signal is

$$
s(t) = A_c\,m(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct), \qquad \tilde s(t) = A_cm(t) .
$$ {#eq-dsb}

Its spectrum is $S(f) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}\big[M(f - f_c) + M(f + f_c)\big]$, which consists of an **upper sideband** ($f_c < \lvert f\rvert < f_c + W$) and a **lower sideband** ($f_c - W < \lvert f\rvert < f_c$). Its bandwidth is $B_T = 2W$ and its average power $P_s = \tfrac12A_c^2P_m$, where $P_m = \overline{m^2(t)}$.
:::

The spectrum follows from the modulation theorem [[communication-systems/signals-spectra-channels#eq-mod-thm]], and the power from $P = \tfrac12\overline{\lvert\tilde s\rvert^2}$. The lower sideband is the mirror image of the upper one: the positive and negative frequencies of $M(f)$ have both been moved to the positive-frequency axis. There is no discrete carrier component (no impulse at $f_c$) unless $m(t)$ has a DC component — hence "suppressed carrier".

DSB-SC is generated by a **multiplier**. At radio frequencies true analog multipliers are rare; instead, **balanced modulators** exploit symmetry. A diode ring or a Gilbert-cell mixer switches the polarity of $m(t)$ at the carrier rate, which multiplies $m(t)$ by a square wave $\operatorname{sq}(t)$ of fundamental frequency $f_c$. By the Fourier series of a square wave, $\operatorname{sq}(t) = \tfrac4\pi[\cos(2\pi f_ct) - \tfrac13\cos(6\pi f_ct) + \dots]$, so the product contains the wanted DSB signal at $f_c$ plus copies at $3f_c$, $5f_c$, …, which a band-pass filter removes. The balanced structure cancels the carrier and the message itself from the output.

### Coherent detection

To recover $m(t)$, the receiver multiplies the received signal by a local carrier and low-pass filters, exactly as in the quadrature demodulator of [[communication-systems/signals-spectra-channels]]. The local oscillator must match the carrier in frequency and phase, which is why this is called **coherent** (or synchronous) detection. What happens if it does not?

::: theorem Coherent detection with a phase or frequency error {#thm-coherent}
Let the DSB-SC signal [[#eq-dsb]] be multiplied by a local carrier $2\cos(2\pi(f_c + \Delta f)t + \varphi)$ and the product low-pass filtered to bandwidth $W$. The output is

$$
y(t) = A_c\,m(t)\cos(2\pi\Delta f\,t + \varphi) .
$$ {#eq-coherent}

With a phase error only ($\Delta f = 0$), the message is scaled by $\cos\varphi$; with a frequency error the message is multiplied by a slow sinusoid at $\Delta f$.
:::

::: proof
Using $2\cos a\cos b = \cos(a - b) + \cos(a + b)$,

$$
A_cm(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)\cdot2\cos(2\pi(f_c + \Delta f)t + \varphi) = A_cm(t)\big[\cos(2\pi\Delta f\,t + \varphi) + \cos(2\pi(2f_c + \Delta f)t + \varphi)\big] .
$$

The second term is a DSB signal at $2f_c + \Delta f$, far above $W$, and is removed by the filter. The first is low-pass, provided $\lvert\Delta f\rvert$ is small compared with $f_c$, and passes. In complex-envelope terms: the receiver computes $\operatorname{Re}\{\tilde s(t)e^{-j(2\pi\Delta ft + \varphi)}\}$.
:::

A phase error of $90^\circ$ makes the output vanish: the local carrier is then in quadrature with the received one — the orthogonality used for quadrature multiplexing in [[communication-systems/signals-spectra-channels]]. A frequency error of even $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$ makes the output swell and fade once a second, which is intolerable for speech. DSB-SC receivers must therefore **recover the carrier** from the received signal. Squaring $s(t)$ gives $A_c^2m^2(t)\cos^2(2\pi f_ct) = \tfrac12A_c^2m^2(t)[1 + \cos(4\pi f_ct)]$; since $m^2(t)$ has a non-zero mean, the squared signal contains a discrete component at $2f_c$, which a narrow filter or a phase-locked loop ([[communication-systems/angle-modulation]]) can lock onto and divide by two. The **Costas loop** does the same job with an I/Q demodulator whose product of outputs, $\tfrac12A_c^2m^2\sin 2\varphi$, steers the oscillator phase towards $\varphi = 0$ (or $\pi$: the recovered carrier has a sign ambiguity).

::: example The cost of a phase error {#ex-phase-error}
A DSB-SC receiver's recovered carrier lags by $30^\circ$. By how many decibels is the output signal power reduced? If the received signal also contains an independent quadrature signal of equal power (a second message on $\sin(2\pi f_ct)$), what is the ratio of wanted to unwanted output power?
::: solution
By [[#eq-coherent]], the output amplitude is multiplied by $\cos30^\circ = 0.866$, so the power falls by $20\log_{10}0.866 = -1.25\,\mathrm{dB}$.

A quadrature message $A_cm_2(t)\sin(2\pi f_ct)$ gives, by the same calculation with $\cos$ replaced by $\sin$, an output $A_cm_2(t)\sin\varphi$ — zero when $\varphi = 0$, but $0.5A_cm_2$ at $\varphi = 30^\circ$. The ratio of wanted to unwanted power is $\cos^2\varphi/\sin^2\varphi = \cot^230^\circ = 3$, only $4.8\,\mathrm{dB}$: the two messages are badly mixed. Quadrature systems are far more sensitive to phase errors than single-channel DSB, which is why digital QAM receivers track phase to within a few degrees.
:::
:::

## Conventional AM {#sec-am}

Carrier recovery was beyond the technology of the 1920s. Conventional AM avoids it by transmitting a large carrier along with the sidebands, so that the message appears directly in the envelope.

::: definition Conventional AM {#def-am}
Let $m_n(t)$ be the message normalised so that $\min_t m_n(t) = -1$. A **conventional AM** (DSB with large carrier) signal is

$$
s(t) = A_c\big[1 + \mu\,m_n(t)\big]\cos(2\pi f_ct), \qquad \tilde s(t) = A_c\big[1 + \mu m_n(t)\big],
$$ {#eq-am}

where $\mu > 0$ is the **modulation index**. Its spectrum is $S(f) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}[\delta(f - f_c) + \delta(f + f_c)] + \tfrac{A_c\mu}{2}[M_n(f - f_c) + M_n(f + f_c)]$, a carrier plus DSB sidebands, and its bandwidth is $2W$. For $\mu \le 1$ the envelope $\lvert\tilde s(t)\rvert = A_c[1 + \mu m_n(t)]$ is a faithful, shifted copy of the message; $\mu > 1$ is **overmodulation**.
:::

For a single tone $m_n(t) = \cos(2\pi f_mt)$, the envelope swings between $A_{\mathrm{max}} = A_c(1 + \mu)$ and $A_{\mathrm{min}} = A_c(1 - \mu)$, so $\mu = (A_{\mathrm{max}} - A_{\mathrm{min}})/(A_{\mathrm{max}} + A_{\mathrm{min}})$, which is how the modulation index is measured on an oscilloscope. The spectrum then consists of three lines: the carrier at $f_c$ with amplitude $A_c$, and two side-frequencies at $f_c \pm f_m$, each of amplitude $\mu A_c/2$.

For $\mu \le 1$ the two coincide. Push $\mu$ above $1$: the carrier phase reverses where $1 + \mu m_n < 0$, the envelope folds upwards, and an envelope detector's output is a distorted, rectified version of the message.

::: theorem Power efficiency of conventional AM {#thm-am-power}
The average power of the AM signal [[#eq-am]] splits into carrier and sideband power,

$$
P_s = \underbrace{\tfrac12A_c^2}_{\text{carrier}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12A_c^2\mu^2P_{m_n}}_{\text{sidebands}}, \qquad \eta = \frac{\mu^2P_{m_n}}{1 + \mu^2P_{m_n}} ,
$$ {#eq-am-power}

where $P_{m_n} = \overline{m_n^2}$ and $\eta$, the **power efficiency**, is the fraction of the power in the sidebands. For tone modulation $P_{m_n} = \tfrac12$ and $\eta = \mu^2/(2 + \mu^2)$, at most $\tfrac13$ (at $\mu = 1$).
:::

::: proof
$P_s = \tfrac12\overline{\lvert\tilde s\rvert^2} = \tfrac12A_c^2\,\overline{(1 + \mu m_n)^2} = \tfrac12A_c^2\big(1 + 2\mu\overline{m_n} + \mu^2\overline{m_n^2}\big)$. The message has no DC component, $\overline{m_n} = 0$, which gives [[#eq-am-power]]. For a tone, $\overline{\cos^2} = \tfrac12$.
:::

For speech, whose peaks are far above its average, $P_{m_n}$ is typically $0.1$ or less, so the efficiency is a few per cent: nearly all the power of a broadcast transmitter goes into the carrier. Broadcasters use audio compression to raise $P_{m_n}$, and some modern transmitters vary the carrier level with the programme (**modulation-dependent carrier level**) to save electricity.

::: example An AM broadcast transmitter {#ex-am-tx}
A transmitter radiates an unmodulated carrier of $1\,\mathrm{kW}$ into a $50\,\Omega$ antenna. It is modulated by a tone with $\mu = 0.8$. Find the total power, the power in each side-frequency, the efficiency, the peak envelope voltage, and the peak envelope power.
::: solution
The carrier power is $\tfrac12A_c^2/R = 1000\,\mathrm{W}$, so $A_c = \sqrt{2\cdot1000\cdot50} = 316\,\mathrm{V}$. The sideband power is $1000\cdot\mu^2/2 = 1000\cdot0.32 = 320\,\mathrm{W}$, split equally: $160\,\mathrm{W}$ in each of $f_c \pm f_m$. The total is $1320\,\mathrm{W}$ and the efficiency is $\eta = 320/1320 = 24.2\,\%$, in agreement with $\mu^2/(2 + \mu^2) = 0.64/2.64$.

The peak envelope is $A_c(1 + \mu) = 316\times1.8 = 569\,\mathrm{V}$. The **peak envelope power** (the power of a carrier with that amplitude) is $\tfrac12(569)^2/50 = 3240\,\mathrm{W}$, $(1 + \mu)^2 = 3.24$ times the carrier power. The output stage must handle the peak power without distortion, although it delivers only $1320\,\mathrm{W}$ on average — another reason why amplitude modulation is expensive in power.
:::
:::

::: warning Overmodulation
With $\mu > 1$ the expression $1 + \mu m_n(t)$ becomes negative at the message troughs. The signal is still a valid DSB-plus-carrier signal, and a coherent detector would recover the message, but the *envelope* is $\lvert1 + \mu m_n\rvert$, not $1 + \mu m_n$, and an envelope detector produces severe distortion. Transmitters that overmodulate, for example by clipping the carrier to zero at loud peaks, also splatter energy far outside their channel, because the sharp corners have wide spectra. Broadcast transmitters limit negative peaks to $\mu = 1$ (positive peaks are often allowed somewhat higher).
:::

### The envelope detector

The beauty of conventional AM is its receiver. A diode followed by a parallel $RC$ load conducts on each positive carrier peak, charging the capacitor to the peak value; between peaks the diode is off and the capacitor discharges slowly through $R$. The capacitor voltage follows the envelope, with a small ripple at the carrier frequency, and a series capacitor removes the DC (the $1$ in $1 + \mu m_n$).

The time constant $RC$ must satisfy two conflicting requirements. It must be **long compared with the carrier period**, $RC \gg 1/f_c$, so that the voltage does not sag much between peaks (the ripple is roughly a fraction $1/(f_cRC)$ of the envelope). And it must be **short enough to follow the envelope** where it falls fastest; otherwise the voltage decays more slowly than the envelope and the output misses the troughs (**diagonal clipping**).

::: theorem Envelope-detector time constant {#thm-envelope-rc}
For tone modulation $A_c[1 + \mu\cos(2\pi f_mt)]$, an ideal diode envelope detector follows the envelope without diagonal clipping if and only if

$$
\frac{1}{f_c} \ll RC \le \frac{\sqrt{1 - \mu^2}}{2\pi f_m\,\mu} .
$$ {#eq-envelope-rc}
:::

::: proof
When the diode is off, the capacitor voltage $v$ decays at the rate $\dd v/\dd t = -v/RC$. Starting from the envelope $e(t) = A_c(1 + \mu\cos\theta)$, $\theta = 2\pi f_mt$, the voltage keeps up with the envelope only if the envelope falls no faster than this: $-\dd e/\dd t \le e/RC$ wherever the envelope is falling, i.e.

$$
2\pi f_m\mu\sin\theta \le \frac{1 + \mu\cos\theta}{RC} \quad\text{for } 0 < \theta < \pi,\qquad\text{so}\qquad RC \le \frac{1}{2\pi f_m\mu}\,\min_\theta\frac{1 + \mu\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} .
$$

Setting the derivative of $g(\theta) = (1 + \mu\cos\theta)/\sin\theta$ to zero gives $-\mu\sin^2\theta - (1 + \mu\cos\theta)\cos\theta = 0$, i.e. $\cos\theta = -\mu$. There $\sin\theta = \sqrt{1 - \mu^2}$ and $g = (1 - \mu^2)/\sqrt{1 - \mu^2} = \sqrt{1 - \mu^2}$, giving the upper bound. The lower bound is the ripple requirement.
:::

The bound tightens as $\mu \to 1$: at full modulation the envelope touches zero with a finite slope, and no finite time constant can follow it there perfectly. Designs use the highest modulation frequency $f_m = W$ and a typical maximum index such as $\mu = 0.8$.

::: example Designing an envelope detector {#ex-envelope-design}
An AM receiver has an intermediate frequency of $455\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and audio bandwidth $W = 5\,\mathrm{kHz}$. Choose $R$ and $C$ for its envelope detector to handle $\mu$ up to $0.8$ at all audio frequencies.
::: solution
By [[#eq-envelope-rc]] with $f_m = 5\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and $\mu = 0.8$:

$$
RC \le \frac{\sqrt{1 - 0.64}}{2\pi\cdot5000\cdot0.8} = \frac{0.6}{25\,130\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}} = 23.9\,\mathrm{\mu s} .
$$

The carrier period is $1/455\,\mathrm{kHz} = 2.2\,\mathrm{\mu s}$, so we want $RC$ much larger than that. Choosing $R = 10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ (large compared with the diode's forward resistance, so the capacitor charges fully at each peak) and $C = 2.2\,\mathrm{nF}$ gives $RC = 22\,\mathrm{\mu s}$, just inside the upper limit and ten carrier periods long. The ripple fraction is about $1/(f_cRC) = 1/(455\times10^3\times22\times10^{-6}) = 0.10$ of the envelope, a ripple at $455\,\mathrm{kHz}$ that the following audio amplifier ignores (a small extra RC filter removes it). Had the carrier been $1\,\mathrm{MHz}$, the same component values would give a ripple of $4.5\,\%$; the IF of a superheterodyne receiver is a compromise between this and image rejection, discussed below.
:::
:::

::: quiz
An envelope detector is used on a DSB-SC signal $A_cm(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$, where $m(t) = \cos(2\pi f_mt)$. What is its output?
- [ ] $A_c\cos(2\pi f_mt)$
- [x] $A_c\lvert\cos(2\pi f_mt)\rvert$, a full-wave rectified tone with strong harmonics at $2f_m, 4f_m, \dots$
- [ ] Zero, because there is no carrier
- [ ] $A_c\cos^2(2\pi f_mt)$
::: solution
The envelope of $A_cm(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ is $\lvert\tilde s\rvert = A_c\lvert m(t)\rvert$. The detector follows it faithfully — it is the envelope that is wrong for this purpose. Its fundamental is at $2f_m$, so the output sounds an octave too high and badly distorted. Only adding a carrier at least as large as the peak of $A_cm(t)$ makes the envelope equal to $A_c[1 + \mu m_n(t)]$.
:::
:::

## Single-sideband modulation {#sec-ssb}

The two sidebands of a DSB signal carry the same information: for real $m$, $M(-f) = M^*(f)$, so the lower sideband is determined by the upper. Transmitting just one of them halves the bandwidth, and with suppressed carrier it puts all the power into information.

::: definition SSB modulation {#def-ssb}
The **single-sideband** (SSB) signals with message $m(t)$ are

$$
s(t) = \frac{A_c}{2}\big[m(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct) \mp \hat m(t)\sin(2\pi f_ct)\big], \qquad \tilde s(t) = \frac{A_c}{2}\big[m(t) \pm j\hat m(t)\big],
$$ {#eq-ssb}

with the upper signs for the **upper sideband** (USB) and the lower signs for the **lower sideband** (LSB); $\hat m$ is the Hilbert transform of $m$. The bandwidth is $B_T = W$.
:::

::: theorem The SSB spectrum {#thm-ssb}
The USB signal in [[#eq-ssb]] has spectrum

$$
S(f) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}\big[M(f - f_c)\,u(f - f_c) + M(f + f_c)\,u(-f - f_c)\big],
$$ {#eq-ssb-spectrum}

where $u$ is the unit step: exactly the upper sideband of the DSB-SC signal $A_cm(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$. The LSB signal is likewise the lower sideband.
:::

::: proof
The complex envelope of the USB signal is $\tilde s = \tfrac{A_c}{2}(m + j\hat m) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}m_+$, where $m_+$ is the analytic signal of $m$. By [[communication-systems/signals-spectra-channels#thm-hilbert-props]], $M_+(f) = 2M(f)u(f)$ (with no DC component). Then by [[communication-systems/signals-spectra-channels#thm-bandpass-spectrum]], the positive-frequency part of $S$ is $\tfrac12\tilde S(f - f_c) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{A_c}{2}\cdot2M(f - f_c)u(f - f_c) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}M(f - f_c)u(f - f_c)$, which is the part of the DSB spectrum $\tfrac{A_c}{2}M(f - f_c)$ above $f_c$. The negative-frequency part follows by conjugate symmetry. For the LSB, $\tilde s = \tfrac{A_c}{2}(m - j\hat m)$, whose spectrum $A_cM(f)u(-f)$ keeps only negative baseband frequencies, which appear below $f_c$.
:::

With tone modulation $m = \cos(2\pi f_mt)$, $\hat m = \sin(2\pi f_mt)$ and the USB signal is $\tfrac{A_c}{2}[\cos(2\pi f_mt)\cos(2\pi f_ct) - \sin(2\pi f_mt)\sin(2\pi f_ct)] = \tfrac{A_c}{2}\cos(2\pi(f_c + f_m)t)$: a single tone at the upper side-frequency, as it must be.

### Generating and detecting SSB

There are two classical methods of generation.

- The **filter method** forms DSB-SC with a balanced modulator and removes one sideband with a band-pass filter. The filter must pass the wanted sideband from $f_c$ (more precisely from $f_c + f_{\mathrm{low}}$) and reject the other, which starts at $f_c - f_{\mathrm{low}}$; the transition band is only $2f_{\mathrm{low}}$ wide. Speech has little energy below $f_{\mathrm{low}} \approx 300\,\mathrm{Hz}$, leaving a $600\,\mathrm{Hz}$ gap. At a carrier of $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$ that is a fractional transition width of $6\times10^{-5}$, which needs crystal or mechanical filters, so SSB is generated at a low frequency and then translated up. Messages with significant energy near DC (video, data) cannot be sent by SSB at all.
- The **phasing method** (Hartley modulator) implements [[#eq-ssb]] directly: one mixer multiplies $m(t)$ by $\cos(2\pi f_ct)$, another multiplies $\hat m(t)$, produced by a wideband $90^\circ$ phase-shift network, by $\sin(2\pi f_ct)$, and the outputs are subtracted (USB) or added (LSB). Imbalance between the two paths leaves a residue of the unwanted sideband. Modern transmitters do exactly this digitally: a DSP computes $\tilde s(t) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}(m \pm j\hat m)$ and an I/Q modulator puts it on the carrier.

SSB requires coherent detection. A local carrier with a frequency error $\Delta f$ shifts every frequency of the recovered message by $\Delta f$ (exercise), so speech sounds high- or low-pitched ("Donald Duck"), but an error of $10$–$20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is tolerable for speech; a phase error produces a phase shift of all components, which the ear does not notice. Amateur and military HF radio, and the long-haul analog telephone network before digitisation, relied on this tolerance.

::: example SSB filter requirements {#ex-ssb-filter}
A USB transmitter generates DSB-SC at $f_c = 100\,\mathrm{kHz}$ from speech in $300$–$3400\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and must suppress the lower sideband by $40\,\mathrm{dB}$. Specify the sideband filter. If the same filter shape were scaled to a carrier of $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$, what would its fractional transition width be?
::: solution
The wanted sideband occupies $100.3$–$103.4\,\mathrm{kHz}$; the unwanted one $96.6$–$99.7\,\mathrm{kHz}$. The filter must pass $100.3$–$103.4\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and attenuate everything below $99.7\,\mathrm{kHz}$ by $40\,\mathrm{dB}$. The transition band is $600\,\mathrm{Hz}$ wide at $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$, a fractional width of $0.6\,\%$ — demanding but realisable with a mechanical or crystal filter. At $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$ the same $600\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is $0.006\,\%$, a hundred times harder, which is why SSB transmitters filter at a low frequency and then translate the signal to the operating frequency with one or two mixers.
:::
:::

## Vestigial-sideband modulation {#sec-vsb}

SSB fails for messages with energy near DC, and DSB wastes bandwidth. **Vestigial-sideband** (VSB) modulation is the compromise: transmit one sideband almost completely, plus a small *vestige* of the other, with a gradual filter transition centred on the carrier. The question is what shape that transition must have.

::: theorem VSB filter condition {#thm-vsb}
A DSB-SC signal $A_cm(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ is passed through a band-pass filter $H(f)$, and the result is coherently detected with $2\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ and low-pass filtered to $W$. The message is recovered without distortion if and only if

$$
H(f + f_c) + H(f - f_c) = \text{constant}\quad\text{for } \lvert f\rvert \le W .
$$ {#eq-vsb}
:::

::: proof
The filtered signal has spectrum $S(f) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}[M(f - f_c) + M(f + f_c)]H(f)$. Multiplying by $2\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ shifts it by $\pm f_c$, giving $S(f - f_c) + S(f + f_c)$. Near baseband ($\lvert f\rvert \le W$), the terms that survive the low-pass filter are

$$
\tfrac{A_c}{2}M(f)H(f - f_c) + \tfrac{A_c}{2}M(f)H(f + f_c) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}M(f)\big[H(f + f_c) + H(f - f_c)\big],
$$

the other terms lying near $\pm2f_c$. This equals a constant times $M(f)$ for every message exactly when the bracket is constant on $\lvert f\rvert \le W$.
:::

The condition says that the filter's response, folded about $f_c$, must add up to a constant: the attenuation of the partially removed upper frequencies near the carrier is made up exactly by the vestige of the lower sideband. A filter whose magnitude falls through $\tfrac12$ at $f_c$ with odd symmetry about that point (and linear phase) satisfies it. SSB is the limiting case of a vestige of zero width, DSB of a filter that passes everything.

Change the vestige width $v$: the sum stays exactly $1$ whatever $v$, because the roll-off is antisymmetric about the carrier. Small $v$ approaches SSB; $v = 1$ is DSB with half the amplitude.

::: application Analog and digital television
Analog television (NTSC, PAL, SECAM) sent the picture by VSB because video extends almost to DC and needs several megahertz: NTSC's $4.2\,\mathrm{MHz}$ of luminance would need $8.4\,\mathrm{MHz}$ in DSB, but VSB with a $1.25\,\mathrm{MHz}$ vestige fitted picture and FM sound into a $6\,\mathrm{MHz}$ channel. The receiver's IF filter, not the transmitter's, provided the precise **Nyquist slope** of [[#eq-vsb]]. The first-generation US digital-television standard, ATSC 1.0, kept VSB for digital data (8-VSB, $19.4\,\mathrm{Mbit/s}$ in the same $6\,\mathrm{MHz}$), and the same "folded response must sum to a constant" condition reappears as the Nyquist criterion for pulse shaping in [[communication-systems/baseband-transmission]].
:::

## Frequency translation and the superheterodyne receiver {#sec-superhet}

Multiplying a bandpass signal by a sinusoid shifts it in frequency — that is all a modulator does. Multiplying a signal at $f_{RF}$ by a **local oscillator** (LO) at $f_{LO}$ produces components at $\lvert f_{RF} - f_{LO}\rvert$ and $f_{RF} + f_{LO}$, and a filter selects one of them. The circuit is a **mixer**, and the operation **frequency conversion** or **heterodyning**.

An early radio receiver amplified and filtered at the station's own frequency, with several tuned stages that all had to track each other as the dial was turned. Their bandwidth, roughly $f/Q$ for a fixed component quality factor $Q$, changed across the band, and high-gain amplification at radio frequency was unstable. Armstrong's **superheterodyne** receiver solves all three problems by converting every station to one fixed **intermediate frequency** (IF), where a fixed filter provides the selectivity and most of the gain.

::: definition Superheterodyne receiver and image frequency {#def-superhet}
A **superheterodyne receiver** mixes the received signal at $f_{RF}$ with a tunable local oscillator so that the wanted station appears at a fixed intermediate frequency $f_{IF} = \lvert f_{LO} - f_{RF}\rvert$, where it is filtered, amplified and demodulated. With **high-side injection**, $f_{LO} = f_{RF} + f_{IF}$. The signal at the **image frequency**

$$
f_{\mathrm{image}} = f_{LO} + f_{IF} = f_{RF} + 2f_{IF}\quad\text{(high-side injection)}
$$ {#eq-image}

is also converted to $f_{IF}$ and cannot be separated from the wanted signal after the mixer; a **preselector** filter before the mixer must reject it.
:::

The choice of IF is the central design trade-off. A *high* IF puts the image far from the wanted signal ($2f_{IF}$ away), so a simple RF filter rejects it. A *low* IF makes a narrow, sharp channel filter easy to build (bandwidth is roughly $f_{IF}/Q$). Standard values are $455\,\mathrm{kHz}$ for medium-wave AM broadcast receivers (channels of $9$ or $10\,\mathrm{kHz}$) and $10.7\,\mathrm{MHz}$ for FM broadcast receivers ($200\,\mathrm{kHz}$ channels). Receivers that need both use **double conversion**: a high first IF for image rejection, then a low second IF for selectivity.

::: example Frequency plan of an AM broadcast receiver {#ex-superhet}
A medium-wave receiver covers $540$–$1600\,\mathrm{kHz}$ with $f_{IF} = 455\,\mathrm{kHz}$. (a) Find the LO frequency and the image when tuned to $1000\,\mathrm{kHz}$. (b) Compare the LO tuning ranges for high-side and low-side injection and explain which is used. (c) The preselector is a single tuned circuit with $Q = 50$, $\lvert H\rvert = 1/\sqrt{1 + Q^2(f/f_0 - f_0/f)^2}$. Find the image rejection at $1000\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and at the two band edges.
::: solution
(a) High-side: $f_{LO} = 1000 + 455 = 1455\,\mathrm{kHz}$. By [[#eq-image]], the image is at $1000 + 2\cdot455 = 1910\,\mathrm{kHz}$: a station there also produces $1910 - 1455 = 455\,\mathrm{kHz}$.

(b) High-side injection needs $f_{LO}$ from $995$ to $2055\,\mathrm{kHz}$, a ratio of $2.07$; low-side needs $85$ to $1145\,\mathrm{kHz}$, a ratio of $13.5$. A tuning capacitor changes the resonant frequency in proportion to $1/\sqrt C$, so a ratio of $2.07$ needs a capacitance ratio of $4.3$, which a standard variable capacitor provides, whereas $13.5$ would need $182$. Moreover the RF ($540$–$1600$, ratio $2.96$) and LO sections of a ganged capacitor can be made to track each other with a fixed offset. High-side injection is therefore universal in AM receivers.

(c) At $f_0 = 1000\,\mathrm{kHz}$, $f = 1910\,\mathrm{kHz}$: $f/f_0 - f_0/f = 1.910 - 0.524 = 1.386$, $Q\times1.386 = 69.3$, so $\lvert H\rvert = 1/\sqrt{1 + 69.3^2}$, i.e. $-36.8\,\mathrm{dB}$. At $540\,\mathrm{kHz}$ (image $1450\,\mathrm{kHz}$): $-41.3\,\mathrm{dB}$. At $1600\,\mathrm{kHz}$ (image $2510\,\mathrm{kHz}$): $-33.4\,\mathrm{dB}$. The image rejection is worst at the top of the band, where the image is proportionally closest; a second tuned stage would roughly double these figures in decibels.
:::
:::

::: quiz
A superheterodyne FM receiver with $f_{IF} = 10.7\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and high-side injection is tuned to $98.1\,\mathrm{MHz}$. A strong transmitter at which frequency could cause image interference?
- [ ] $87.4\,\mathrm{MHz}$
- [ ] $108.8\,\mathrm{MHz}$
- [x] $119.5\,\mathrm{MHz}$
- [ ] $196.2\,\mathrm{MHz}$
::: solution
$f_{LO} = 98.1 + 10.7 = 108.8\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and the image is $f_{LO} + f_{IF} = 119.5\,\mathrm{MHz}$, which is in the aeronautical band. Because $2f_{IF} = 21.4\,\mathrm{MHz}$ exceeds the $20\,\mathrm{MHz}$ width of the FM band, no FM broadcast station can be an image of another — the reason for choosing $10.7\,\mathrm{MHz}$.
:::
:::

::: application Frequency-division multiplexing in the telephone network
Before digital transmission, long-distance telephone cables and microwave links carried thousands of calls by SSB frequency-division multiplexing. Twelve voice channels, each limited to $300$–$3400\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and allotted $4\,\mathrm{kHz}$, were placed by SSB on carriers $4\,\mathrm{kHz}$ apart to form a **group** in $60$–$108\,\mathrm{kHz}$; five groups formed a $60$-channel supergroup, and so on up to systems carrying over ten thousand calls on one coaxial pair. The $900\,\mathrm{Hz}$ guard band between channels made the channel filters realisable. Digital time-division multiplexing ([[communication-systems/pulse-code-modulation]]) replaced these systems from the 1960s onward.
:::

## Where this leads

The complex-envelope descriptions here — $A_cm$ for DSB, $A_c(1 + \mu m_n)$ for AM, $\tfrac{A_c}{2}(m \pm j\hat m)$ for SSB — are the basis for the noise analysis of [[communication-systems/noise-in-analog-systems]], which shows that DSB and SSB achieve the same output SNR for the same transmitted power while AM loses at least $4.8\,\mathrm{dB}$. [[communication-systems/angle-modulation]] puts the message into the phase of the carrier instead, and introduces the phase-locked loop that also recovers carriers for coherent AM detection. Quadrature multiplexing returns in digital form as QAM in [[communication-systems/digital-modulation]], and the VSB condition as the Nyquist criterion in [[communication-systems/baseband-transmission]]. The mixers, filters and oscillators of the superheterodyne are designed in [[rf-microwave]].

::: history
Reginald Fessenden, who had argued that radio could carry continuous waves rather than sparks, is reported to have broadcast speech and music from Brant Rock, Massachusetts, on Christmas Eve 1906 using an alternator transmitter (the details rest on his later accounts). John Renshaw Carson of AT&T analysed the sidebands of AM mathematically and filed a patent for single-sideband suppressed-carrier transmission in 1915 (granted 1923); AT&T used SSB on the first transatlantic radiotelephone service in 1927. Ralph Hartley patented the phasing method of SSB generation in the 1920s. Edwin Howard Armstrong devised the superheterodyne receiver in 1918 while serving with the US Army Signal Corps in France, building on the heterodyne principle Fessenden had introduced in 1901. Regular commercial broadcasting began in 1920 (KDKA in Pittsburgh among the first), and vestigial-sideband transmission was adopted for television when the NTSC standard was set in 1941.
:::

::: summary
- DSB-SC: $s = A_cm(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$, bandwidth $2W$, no carrier; coherent detection gives $A_cm\cos(2\pi\Delta ft + \varphi)$, so the carrier must be recovered (squaring loop, Costas loop).
- Conventional AM: $s = A_c[1 + \mu m_n]\cos(2\pi f_ct)$; for $\mu \le 1$ the envelope is the message, and a diode envelope detector recovers it. Efficiency $\eta = \mu^2P_{m_n}/(1 + \mu^2P_{m_n}) \le \tfrac13$ for a tone.
- The envelope detector needs $1/f_c \ll RC \le \sqrt{1 - \mu^2}/(2\pi W\mu)$ to avoid ripple and diagonal clipping.
- SSB: $\tilde s = \tfrac{A_c}{2}(m \pm j\hat m)$ occupies only $W$; generated by filtering (needs a spectral gap near DC) or by phasing; detected coherently, tolerating small frequency errors for speech.
- VSB: a filter whose response, folded about the carrier, sums to a constant, $H(f + f_c) + H(f - f_c) = \text{const}$, allows messages extending to DC in little more than $W$.
- A superheterodyne receiver mixes every station to a fixed IF; the image at $f_{RF} + 2f_{IF}$ (high-side LO) must be removed before the mixer, and the IF trades image rejection against selectivity.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Measuring the modulation index {level=1 check="0.6"}
An oscilloscope shows a tone-modulated AM wave whose envelope varies between $2\,\mathrm{V}$ and $8\,\mathrm{V}$. What is the modulation index?
::: solution
$\mu = (A_{\mathrm{max}} - A_{\mathrm{min}})/(A_{\mathrm{max}} + A_{\mathrm{min}}) = (8 - 2)/(8 + 2) = 0.6$.
:::
:::

::: exercise AM efficiency {level=1 check="0.5^2/(2+0.5^2)"}
Find the power efficiency (as a fraction) of a tone-modulated AM signal with $\mu = 0.5$.
::: solution
$\eta = \mu^2/(2 + \mu^2) = 0.25/2.25 = 0.111$: only $11\,\%$ of the power is in the sidebands.
:::
:::

::: exercise Transmission bandwidths {level=1}
A message has bandwidth $W = 4\,\mathrm{kHz}$. State the transmission bandwidth for DSB-SC, conventional AM, SSB, and VSB with a vestige of $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$. How many SSB channels fit in $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$ with no guard bands?
::: solution
DSB-SC and AM: $8\,\mathrm{kHz}$. SSB: $4\,\mathrm{kHz}$. VSB: $W + 0.5 = 4.5\,\mathrm{kHz}$. Twenty-five SSB channels fit in $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$, compared with twelve DSB channels.
:::
:::

::: exercise SSB with a frequency error {level=2}
A USB signal $\tfrac{A_c}{2}[m\cos(2\pi f_ct) - \hat m\sin(2\pi f_ct)]$ with $m = \cos(2\pi f_mt)$ is demodulated with $2\cos(2\pi(f_c + \Delta f)t)$ and low-pass filtered. Find the output. Generalise to an arbitrary message, and contrast with DSB-SC in [[#thm-coherent]].
::: solution
The USB signal is $\tfrac{A_c}{2}\cos(2\pi(f_c + f_m)t)$. Multiplying by $2\cos(2\pi(f_c + \Delta f)t)$ and keeping the difference frequency gives $\tfrac{A_c}{2}\cos(2\pi(f_m - \Delta f)t)$: the tone is shifted down by $\Delta f$. Every component of a general message is shifted by the same $\Delta f$, so the message spectrum is translated — harmonically related tones lose their relation, which makes music sound wrong but leaves speech intelligible for $\lvert\Delta f\rvert$ up to a few tens of hertz. In DSB-SC the same error multiplies the message by $\cos(2\pi\Delta ft)$, a periodic fading that is much more objectionable.
:::
:::

::: exercise Power of an SSB signal {level=2}
Show that the average power of the SSB signal [[#eq-ssb]] is $\tfrac14A_c^2P_m$, half that of the DSB-SC signal $A_cm\cos(2\pi f_ct)$. Is SSB at a disadvantage?
::: solution
$P = \tfrac12\overline{\lvert\tilde s\rvert^2} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{A_c^2}{4}\overline{m^2 + \hat m^2} = \tfrac18A_c^2(P_m + P_m) = \tfrac14A_c^2P_m$, using $\overline{\hat m^2} = \overline{m^2}$ ([[communication-systems/signals-spectra-channels#thm-hilbert-props]]). DSB has $\tfrac12A_c^2P_m$. The SSB signal is one of the two DSB sidebands, so it has half the power — but also half the bandwidth and so half the noise. For equal *transmitted* power the two give the same output SNR, as shown in [[communication-systems/noise-in-analog-systems]].
:::
:::

::: exercise The image of a shortwave receiver {level=2}
A single-conversion shortwave receiver with $f_{IF} = 455\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and high-side injection is tuned to $15\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Find the image frequency. Its preselector is one tuned circuit with $Q = 60$. Find the image rejection, and explain why shortwave receivers use a high first IF.
::: solution
$f_{\mathrm{image}} = 15 + 0.91 = 15.91\,\mathrm{MHz}$. $f/f_0 - f_0/f = 1.0607 - 0.9428 = 0.1179$; $Q\times0.1179 = 7.07$; rejection $20\log_{10}\sqrt{1 + 7.07^2} = 17.1\,\mathrm{dB}$ — poor, since the image is only $6\,\%$ away in frequency. With a first IF of, say, $45\,\mathrm{MHz}$, the image is at $105\,\mathrm{MHz}$, seven times the tuned frequency, and even a fixed low-pass filter rejects it; a second conversion to $455\,\mathrm{kHz}$ then provides selectivity.
:::
:::

::: exercise Envelope of an SSB signal {level=2}
Find the envelope of a USB signal whose message is $m(t) = \cos(2\pi f_1t) + \cos(2\pi f_2t)$, with $f_2 > f_1$. Explain why adding a large carrier and using an envelope detector, as broadcasters once proposed for "compatible SSB", gives only approximate recovery.
::: solution
$\tilde s = \tfrac{A_c}{2}(m + j\hat m) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}\big(e^{j2\pi f_1t} + e^{j2\pi f_2t}\big)$, with magnitude $\tfrac{A_c}{2}\lvert1 + e^{j2\pi(f_2 - f_1)t}\rvert = A_c\lvert\cos(\pi(f_2 - f_1)t)\rvert$. The envelope varies at the *difference* frequency and bears no simple relation to $m(t)$. Adding a carrier $A_0$ gives the envelope $\lvert A_0 + \tfrac{A_c}{2}(m + j\hat m)\rvert = \sqrt{(A_0 + \tfrac{A_c}{2}m)^2 + \tfrac{A_c^2}{4}\hat m^2}$, which is close to $A_0 + \tfrac{A_c}{2}m$ only when $A_0 \gg A_c\lvert\hat m\rvert$; the quadrature term causes distortion unless the carrier dominates, which throws away SSB's power advantage.
:::
:::

::: exercise Carrier recovery by squaring {level=3}
A DSB-SC signal with message $m(t) = \cos(2\pi f_mt)$ is squared. Find all frequency components of $s^2(t)$, and design (state the centre frequency and maximum bandwidth of) the band-pass filter that extracts a carrier reference. What ambiguity remains after the divide-by-two, and does it matter for speech?
::: hint
Expand $\cos^2(2\pi f_mt)\cos^2(2\pi f_ct)$ into a sum of cosines.
:::
::: solution
$s^2 = A_c^2\cos^2(2\pi f_mt)\cos^2(2\pi f_ct) = \tfrac{A_c^2}{4}[1 + \cos(4\pi f_mt)][1 + \cos(4\pi f_ct)]$. Expanding: components at DC ($\tfrac{A_c^2}{4}$), $2f_m$ ($\tfrac{A_c^2}{4}$), $2f_c$ ($\tfrac{A_c^2}{4}$), and $2f_c \pm 2f_m$ ($\tfrac{A_c^2}{8}$ each). A band-pass filter centred on $2f_c$ must pass the line at $2f_c$ and reject those at $2f_c \pm 2f_m$, so its bandwidth must be less than $4f_m$ — and, for a general message whose lowest frequency is $f_{\mathrm{low}}$, less than $4f_{\mathrm{low}}$ (in practice a phase-locked loop with a narrow loop bandwidth). Dividing the frequency by two gives $\cos(2\pi f_ct + \theta)$ with $\theta = 0$ or $\pi$: the recovered carrier may be inverted, giving $-m(t)$. A sign inversion of audio is inaudible, but for digital data (BPSK) it inverts every bit, which is why such systems use differential encoding ([[communication-systems/digital-modulation]]).
:::
:::

::: exercise Designing a VSB filter {level=3}
A message of bandwidth $W = 1\,\mathrm{MHz}$ is sent by VSB on a carrier $f_c$. The transmit filter has a linear magnitude roll-off from $0$ at $f_c - 0.2\,\mathrm{MHz}$ to $1$ at $f_c + 0.2\,\mathrm{MHz}$, is flat to $f_c + 1\,\mathrm{MHz}$, and zero above. (a) Verify [[#eq-vsb]]. (b) Find the transmission bandwidth. (c) Find the complex envelope's quadrature component for a test tone $m = \cos(2\pi f_mt)$ with $f_m = 0.1\,\mathrm{MHz}$, and explain why an envelope detector would then produce distortion unless a large carrier is added.
::: hint
For a tone at $f_m$ the filter passes the upper side-frequency with gain $H(f_c + f_m)$ and the lower with $H(f_c - f_m)$.
:::
::: solution
(a) For $0 \le f \le 0.2\,\mathrm{MHz}$: $H(f_c + f) = \tfrac12 + f/0.4$ and $H(f_c - f) = \tfrac12 - f/0.4$ (in MHz), which sum to $1$; the filter is real and even about $f = 0$, so $H(f - f_c) = H(f_c - f)$ for the negative-frequency image and the condition holds. For $0.2 < f \le 1$: $H(f_c + f) = 1$, $H(f_c - f) = 0$, sum $1$. So $H(f + f_c) + H(f - f_c) = 1$ on $\lvert f\rvert \le W$.

(b) From $f_c - 0.2$ to $f_c + 1\,\mathrm{MHz}$: $B_T = 1.2\,\mathrm{MHz}$.

(c) At $f_m = 0.1\,\mathrm{MHz}$, the upper side-frequency has gain $H(f_c + 0.1) = 0.75$, the lower $H(f_c - 0.1) = 0.25$. The DSB tone $A_c\cos(2\pi f_mt)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ has complex envelope $\tfrac{A_c}{2}(e^{j2\pi f_mt} + e^{-j2\pi f_mt})$, and filtering gives $\tilde s = \tfrac{A_c}{2}(0.75e^{j2\pi f_mt} + 0.25e^{-j2\pi f_mt}) = \tfrac{A_c}{2}[\cos(2\pi f_mt) + j\,0.5\sin(2\pi f_mt)]$. The quadrature component $\tfrac{A_c}{4}\sin(2\pi f_mt)$ is non-zero, so the envelope is $\sqrt{x_I^2 + x_Q^2}$, not $\lvert x_I\rvert$; a coherent detector recovers $x_I = \tfrac{A_c}{2}m$ exactly, but an envelope detector does so only approximately, after a large carrier is added to make $x_I$ dominate. Analog television accepted this small distortion.
:::
