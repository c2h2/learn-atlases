Knowing how many carriers a semiconductor contains is only half of the story; a device works because those carriers move. They move for two reasons. An electric field pushes electrons one way and holes the other, which is **drift**, the mechanism behind Ohm's law in a resistor. And wherever the concentration of carriers varies in space, their random thermal motion carries more of them from the crowded region to the sparse one, which is **diffusion** — the mechanism behind the current of every forward-biased pn junction and every bipolar transistor, which a purely resistive picture cannot explain at all. On top of that, carriers are created and destroyed: electron–hole pairs are generated by heat and light and recombine, with a characteristic lifetime.

This chapter turns those three processes into equations. We define mobility and conductivity, see how mobility depends on doping, temperature and field, introduce the diffusion coefficient and prove the Einstein relation that ties it to mobility, describe recombination and the minority-carrier lifetime, and finally combine everything into the **continuity equations** and the minority-carrier diffusion equation. That equation, with its characteristic **diffusion length**, is the tool from which the diode and transistor equations of later chapters are derived. Carrier concentrations, the Fermi level and the silicon constants at $300\,\mathrm K$ are taken from [[semiconductor-devices/carrier-statistics]].

## Drift and mobility

Even with no applied field, carriers are not at rest. An electron in the conduction band has an average kinetic energy of $\tfrac32kT$, so with the conductivity effective mass $m_n^* = 0.26\,m_0$ its root-mean-square **thermal velocity** is

$$
v_{th} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m_n^*}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(0.02585)(1.602\times10^{-19})}{0.26\times9.109\times10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.3\times10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.3\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{cm/s}.
$$

The motion is random: the electron travels in a straight line until it is **scattered** — by a lattice vibration (phonon), an ionised impurity, or a defect — and sets off in a new direction. Averaged over many electrons the velocity is zero, and no current flows.

Apply a field $\mathcal E$. Between collisions each electron accelerates at $-q\mathcal E/m_n^*$. If the mean time between collisions is $\tau_m$, and each collision randomises the direction, the electrons acquire an average **drift velocity** $v_d = -q\tau_m\mathcal E/m_n^*$ superimposed on their random motion. (We write $\mathcal E$ for the electric field to keep $E$ free for energies.)

::: definition Mobility and drift velocity {#def-mobility}
At low fields the average velocity of carriers is proportional to the field:

$$
v_{dn} = -\mu_n\mathcal E, \qquad v_{dp} = +\mu_p\mathcal E,
$$ {#eq-drift-velocity}

where the **mobilities** $\mu_n = q\tau_{mn}/m_n^*$ and $\mu_p = q\tau_{mp}/m_p^*$ are positive, measured in $\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$. Electrons drift against the field and holes along it.
:::

For lightly doped silicon at $300\,\mathrm K$, $\mu_n \approx 1360$ and $\mu_p \approx 490\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$. Electrons are about three times as mobile as holes, mainly because of their smaller conductivity mass; this is why n-channel MOSFETs are faster than p-channel ones of the same size and why npn bipolar transistors are preferred to pnp. The electron mobility corresponds to a mean time between collisions $\tau_m = \mu m^*/q = (0.136)(0.26)(9.109\times10^{-31})/(1.602\times10^{-19}) = 2.0\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm s$ and a mean free path $v_{th}\tau_m \approx 46\,\mathrm{nm}$, about $85$ lattice constants. Modern transistors have channels only a few times longer than this, which is why their behaviour departs from the drift picture.

A drift velocity of $1360\,\mathrm{cm/s}$ per $\mathrm{V/cm}$ sounds large, but at $1\,\mathrm{V/cm}$ it is $14\,\mathrm{m/s}$, ten thousand times smaller than the thermal velocity: the field produces a tiny bias on top of a violent random motion. That is why the linear relation [[#eq-drift-velocity]] holds over a wide range of fields.

### Drift current and conductivity

A concentration $n$ of electrons with charge $-q$ moving at $v_{dn}$ carries current density $-qnv_{dn} = qn\mu_n\mathcal E$; holes carry $qp\mu_p\mathcal E$. Both are in the direction of the field.

::: theorem Drift current and conductivity {#thm-conductivity}
The drift current density in a semiconductor is

$$
J_{\text{drift}} = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)\,\mathcal E = \sigma\mathcal E, \qquad \sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p), \qquad \rho = \frac1\sigma,
$$ {#eq-sigma}

which is Ohm's law in point form, with conductivity $\sigma$ (in $\mathrm{S/cm}$) and resistivity $\rho$ (in $\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$). In doped material only the majority carriers matter: $\rho \approx 1/(qN_D\mu_n)$ for n-type and $1/(qN_A\mu_p)$ for p-type.
:::

::: example Resistivity of doped and undoped silicon {#ex-resistivity}
Find the resistivity at $300\,\mathrm K$ of (a) intrinsic silicon, (b) n-type silicon with $N_D = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, (c) p-type silicon with $N_A = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Use the mobilities from the fit [[#eq-mobility-fit]] below: $1360$ and $495$ at negligible doping; $1248$ and $406\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$ at $10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.
::: solution
(a) $n = p = 10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$:

$$
\rho_i = \frac{1}{q n_i(\mu_n + \mu_p)} = \frac{1}{(1.602\times10^{-19})(10^{10})(1360 + 495)}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm} = 3.4\times10^{5}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}.
$$

(b) $n = 10^{16}$, $p = 10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$; the hole term is negligible:

$$
\rho = \frac{1}{(1.602\times10^{-19})(10^{16})(1248)}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.50\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}.
$$

(c) $\rho = 1/[(1.602\times10^{-19})(10^{16})(406)] = 1.54\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$, three times higher than (b) because holes are less mobile. Doping at $10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ — two parts in ten million — has lowered the resistivity by a factor of $7\times10^{5}$. (With the older value $n_i = 1.5\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ one finds the often-quoted $2.3\times10^{5}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$ for intrinsic silicon.)
:::
:::

### What limits the mobility

Two scattering mechanisms dominate in silicon. **Lattice (phonon) scattering** grows with temperature as the atoms vibrate more; it gives a mobility roughly proportional to $T^{-3/2}$ in simple theory (closer to $T^{-2.4}$ for electrons in silicon). **Ionised-impurity scattering** is the Coulomb deflection of a carrier by a donor or acceptor ion; it is weaker for faster carriers, so it gives a mobility proportional to roughly $T^{3/2}/N_I$, where $N_I = N_D^+ + N_A^-$ counts **all** ionised impurities, including compensating ones. When several independent mechanisms act, their scattering rates add, and since $\mu \propto \tau_m$:

$$
\frac1\mu = \frac{1}{\mu_{\text{lattice}}} + \frac{1}{\mu_{\text{impurity}}} \qquad\text{(Matthiessen's rule)}.
$$

The resulting dependence on doping at $300\,\mathrm K$ is well described by an empirical fit of the Caughey–Thomas form,

$$
\mu(N) = \mu_{\min} + \frac{\mu_{\max} - \mu_{\min}}{1 + (N/N_{\text{ref}})^\alpha},
$$ {#eq-mobility-fit}

with $\mu_{\min} = 92$, $\mu_{\max} = 1360\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$, $N_{\text{ref}} = 1.3\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $\alpha = 0.91$ for electrons, and $\mu_{\min} = 47.7$, $\mu_{\max} = 495\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$, $N_{\text{ref}} = 6.3\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $\alpha = 0.76$ for holes. $N$ is the total ionised impurity concentration.

### High fields: velocity saturation

The linear law [[#eq-drift-velocity]] assumes the field barely disturbs the random motion. When the drift velocity approaches the thermal velocity — at fields of order $10^4\,\mathrm{V/cm}$, or $1\,\mathrm V$ across a micrometre — the carriers gain energy from the field faster than they can lose it to the lattice. They then emit optical phonons (energy about $63\,\mathrm{meV}$ in silicon) almost as soon as they can, and the velocity stops increasing. In silicon it saturates at

$$
v_{\text{sat}} \approx 1.0\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{cm/s}\ \text{(electrons)}, \qquad \approx 8\times10^{6}\,\mathrm{cm/s}\ \text{(holes)},
$$

and an empirical form that fits measurements is $v = \mu\mathcal E/[1 + (\mu\mathcal E/v_{\text{sat}})^\beta]^{1/\beta}$ with $\beta \approx 2$ for electrons and $\beta \approx 1$ for holes.

::: application Resistors in integrated circuits
A resistor on a chip is a strip of doped silicon or polysilicon. For a layer of thickness $t$ and uniform resistivity $\rho$, a strip of length $L$ and width $W$ has $R = \rho L/(Wt) = R_s\,(L/W)$, where $R_s = \rho/t$ is the **sheet resistance**, quoted in **ohms per square** ($\Omega/\square$): any square of the layer, whatever its size, has resistance $R_s$ between opposite edges. Designers count squares. Typical values are $5$–$10\,\Omega/\square$ for silicided layers, $100$–$200\,\Omega/\square$ for doped polysilicon and $1$–$2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}/\square$ for a lightly doped well. Absolute values vary by $\pm20\,\%$ between wafers, but the **ratio** of two resistors on the same chip is matched to $0.1\,\%$ or better, so analogue circuits are designed to depend on ratios.
:::

::: example Designing a diffused resistor {#ex-resistor}
A p-type layer $0.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thick, uniformly doped with $N_A = 10^{18}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, is used to make a $10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ resistor of width $2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Find the sheet resistance and the required length. How does the resistance change between $25$ and $85\,^\circ\mathrm C$ if the hole mobility at this doping varies as $T^{-1}$?
::: solution
At $10^{18}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ the fit gives $\mu_p = 96.5\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$, so

$$
\rho = \frac{1}{qN_A\mu_p} = \frac{1}{(1.602\times10^{-19})(10^{18})(96.5)} = 0.0647\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}, \qquad R_s = \frac{\rho}{t} = \frac{0.0647}{0.5\times10^{-4}} = 1294\,\Omega/\square.
$$

We need $L/W = 10\,000/1294 = 7.73$ squares, so $L = 7.73\times2 = 15.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Since $R \propto 1/\mu_p \propto T$, heating from $298$ to $358\,\mathrm K$ raises the resistance by the factor $358/298 = 1.20$, a temperature coefficient of about $+3300\,\mathrm{ppm/K}$. That is why precision analogue designs use thin-film or polysilicon resistors with much lower coefficients, or rely on ratios.
:::
:::

## Diffusion

Release a drop of ink in still water and it spreads, although nothing pushes it: molecules move randomly, and since more of them start in the dense region, more cross any plane from the dense side than from the sparse side. Carriers do exactly the same.

::: definition Diffusion current {#def-diffusion}
A gradient of carrier concentration produces a particle flux $-D\,\mathrm dn/\mathrm dx$ down the gradient (Fick's law), where $D$ is the **diffusion coefficient** in $\mathrm{cm^2/s}$. Multiplying by the carrier charge, the **diffusion current densities** are

$$
J_{n,\text{diff}} = +qD_n\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm dx}, \qquad J_{p,\text{diff}} = -qD_p\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dx}.
$$ {#eq-diffusion}
:::

::: warning Signs of diffusion currents
Both electrons and holes diffuse **down** their concentration gradients. But the electron flux and the electron current point in opposite directions, because electrons are negative. So an electron concentration that decreases to the right ($\mathrm dn/\mathrm dx < 0$) sends electrons to the right and current to the **left**: $J_n = qD_n\,\mathrm dn/\mathrm dx < 0$. For holes, flux and current point the same way, hence the minus sign in $J_p$. Keeping a sketch of the concentration with arrows for flux and current next to every diffusion calculation avoids sign errors in diode and transistor problems.
:::

A simple argument gives the size of $D$. In one dimension, carriers moving with thermal speed $v$ and mean free path $\ell = v\tau_m$ cross a plane from each side, and those crossing from the left come on average from a distance $\ell$ away, where the concentration is $n(x - \ell)$. The net flux is about $\tfrac12 v[n(x-\ell) - n(x+\ell)] = -v\ell\,\mathrm dn/\mathrm dx$, so $D \sim v\ell = v^2\tau_m$. With $\tfrac12m^*v^2 = \tfrac12kT$ per dimension, $D \sim kT\tau_m/m^* = (kT/q)\mu$. The careful version of this estimate is exact.

### The Einstein relation

::: theorem Einstein relation {#thm-einstein}
For non-degenerate carriers in thermal equilibrium,

$$
\frac{D_n}{\mu_n} = \frac{D_p}{\mu_p} = \frac{kT}{q} = V_T.
$$ {#eq-einstein}
:::

::: proof
Consider a non-uniformly doped semiconductor in equilibrium. The Fermi level $E_F$ is constant, but the band edges, and with them $E_i$, vary with position. The electrostatic potential energy of an electron is $-q\psi$, and all band energies move with it, so $\mathrm dE_i/\mathrm dx = -q\,\mathrm d\psi/\mathrm dx = q\mathcal E$. From [[semiconductor-devices/carrier-statistics#eq-ni-form]], $n = n_ie^{(E_F - E_i)/kT}$, hence

$$
\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm dx} = -\frac{n}{kT}\frac{\mathrm dE_i}{\mathrm dx} = -\frac{qn\mathcal E}{kT}.
$$

In equilibrium the net electron current must vanish (otherwise there would be a steady flow of energy with no source). Adding drift and diffusion,

$$
J_n = qn\mu_n\mathcal E + qD_n\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm dx} = qn\mathcal E\left(\mu_n - \frac{qD_n}{kT}\right) = 0,
$$

which requires $D_n = (kT/q)\mu_n$. The same argument with $p = n_ie^{(E_i - E_F)/kT}$ gives the hole relation.
:::

Although the proof uses equilibrium, $D$ and $\mu$ are properties of the scattering, not of the distribution, so the relation holds whenever the carriers are near thermal equilibrium with the lattice — which covers almost all low-field device operation. At $300\,\mathrm K$, $V_T = 25.85\,\mathrm{mV}$: lightly doped silicon has $D_n = 35.2$ and $D_p = 12.8\,\mathrm{cm^2/s}$.

::: intuition Band diagrams show the field
The proof contains a rule you will use constantly. The electric field is proportional to the slope of the bands: $\mathcal E = \frac1q\,\mathrm dE_c/\mathrm dx = \frac1q\,\mathrm dE_i/\mathrm dx$. Where $E_c$ **rises** to the right, $\mathcal E$ points to the right; electrons, which roll downhill on the band diagram, are pushed to the left, against the field, as negative charges should be, while holes float uphill on $E_v$, to the right, along the field. Draw the bands, and the forces on both carriers can be read off at a glance.
:::

::: example Built-in field of a graded base {#ex-graded}
In a p-type region the acceptor concentration falls exponentially, $N_A(x) = N_0e^{-x/\lambda}$ with $\lambda = 0.1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, and $N_A \gg n_i$ throughout. Find the electric field in equilibrium, and the potential difference across $0.3\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Which way does the field push electrons?
::: solution
In equilibrium $J_p = 0$ with $p = N_A(x)$:

$$
qp\mu_p\mathcal E - qD_p\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dx} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathcal E = \frac{D_p}{\mu_p}\frac{1}{p}\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dx} = V_T\,\frac{\mathrm d\ln N_A}{\mathrm dx} = -\frac{V_T}{\lambda}.
$$

So $\abs{\mathcal E} = 0.02585\,\mathrm V/(0.1\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{cm}) = 2585\,\mathrm{V/cm}$, uniform, and pointing in the $-x$ direction (from low to high doping). Across $0.3\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ the potential changes by $2585\times0.3\times10^{-4} = 77.6\,\mathrm{mV} = 3V_T$, as it must, since the doping changes by $e^3$. The field pushes holes towards $-x$, balancing their diffusion towards $+x$, and pushes electrons towards $+x$. Minority electrons injected at $x = 0$ are therefore swept across the region by drift as well as diffusion: this is the **drift transistor**, whose graded base speeds up bipolar transistors ([[semiconductor-devices/bipolar-transistors]]).
:::
:::

### The drift–diffusion equations

The total electron and hole current densities are the sums of their drift and diffusion parts:

$$
J_n = qn\mu_n\mathcal E + qD_n\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm dx}, \qquad J_p = qp\mu_p\mathcal E - qD_p\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dx}, \qquad J = J_n + J_p.
$$ {#eq-dd}

These, together with Poisson's equation and the continuity equations below, are the **drift–diffusion model**, the set of equations solved by every device simulator.

::: quiz
In a region of n-type silicon the electron concentration is uniform and there is a field of $100\,\mathrm{V/cm}$ in the $+x$ direction. The hole concentration decreases in the $+x$ direction. Which statement is true?
- [ ] The electron current is zero because $n$ is uniform.
- [x] The electron current is a drift current in the $+x$ direction; the holes carry both a drift current in $+x$ and a diffusion current in $+x$.
- [ ] Electrons drift in $+x$, so the electron current is in $-x$.
- [ ] The hole diffusion current is in $-x$ because $\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx < 0$.
::: solution
A uniform $n$ gives no electron diffusion, but there is drift: electrons move in $-x$, so their current $qn\mu_n\mathcal E$ is in $+x$. Holes drift with the field, current in $+x$. They also diffuse down their gradient, towards $+x$, and since they are positive their diffusion current $-qD_p\,\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx$ is positive, also in $+x$.
:::
:::

## Generation and recombination

Thermal equilibrium is dynamic. Electron–hole pairs are constantly being generated by thermal energy and destroyed by recombination, at equal rates. When the balance is upset — by light, or by injection at a junction — the system relaxes back towards $np = n_i^2$, and the time it takes is one of the most important parameters of a device.

::: definition Excess carriers, low-level injection and lifetime {#def-lifetime}
Write $n = n_0 + \Delta n$ and $p = p_0 + \Delta p$, where $n_0$, $p_0$ are the equilibrium values and $\Delta n$, $\Delta p$ the **excess carrier concentrations**; in a neutral region $\Delta n \approx \Delta p$. **Low-level injection** means the excess is much smaller than the majority concentration (e.g. $\Delta p \ll n_0$ in n-type material). Then the net recombination rate (recombinations minus thermal generations per unit volume per second) is proportional to the excess minority concentration,

$$
U = \frac{\Delta p}{\tau_p}\ \ \text{(n-type)}, \qquad U = \frac{\Delta n}{\tau_n}\ \ \text{(p-type)},
$$ {#eq-lifetime}

where $\tau_p$, $\tau_n$ are the **minority-carrier lifetimes**.
:::

Three recombination mechanisms operate in parallel, and their rates add:

- **Radiative (band-to-band)** recombination: an electron falls directly into a hole, emitting a photon. The rate is $B(np - n_i^2)$. It is fast in direct-gap materials and negligible in silicon, where $B \approx 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{cm^3/s}$.
- **Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH)** recombination through **traps**, defect or impurity levels deep in the gap (gold, iron and lattice defects in silicon). A trap captures an electron, then a hole, and the pair is gone; each step needs to release only part of the gap energy, as phonons, which makes it far more likely than direct recombination in an indirect-gap material. This dominates in silicon at normal doping.
- **Auger** recombination: the energy of the recombining pair is given to a third carrier, rate proportional to $n^2p$ or $np^2$. It dominates at very high carrier densities, in heavily doped emitters and concentrator solar cells.

::: theorem Shockley–Read–Hall recombination rate {#thm-srh}
For traps at energy $E_t$ with electron and hole lifetimes $\tau_{n0}$ and $\tau_{p0}$ (the inverse capture rates when the traps are all empty or all full), the net recombination rate in steady state is

$$
U = \frac{np - n_i^2}{\tau_{p0}(n + n_1) + \tau_{n0}(p + p_1)}, \qquad n_1 = n_ie^{(E_t - E_i)/kT},\quad p_1 = n_ie^{(E_i - E_t)/kT}.
$$ {#eq-srh}
:::

The derivation balances the four capture and emission rates at the trap and is in Pierret's or Sze's text. Three consequences matter here. The rate is proportional to $np - n_i^2$, so it vanishes in equilibrium and is negative (net generation) when $np < n_i^2$, as in a reverse-biased junction. Traps near midgap ($n_1 \approx p_1 \approx n_i$) are the most effective, because a level near either band edge re-emits the captured carrier before the other carrier arrives. And in n-type material under low-level injection, $n \approx n_0 \gg p, n_1, p_1$, so $U \approx (n_0\Delta p)/(\tau_{p0}n_0) = \Delta p/\tau_{p0}$, which is [[#eq-lifetime]]: the lifetime of the minority holes is set by how fast the traps capture holes.

Typical minority-carrier lifetimes in silicon range from more than $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ in the best float-zone crystal to about $1$–$10\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ in device-grade material and $1\,\mathrm{ns}$ in fast-switching diodes that are deliberately doped with gold or platinum. In direct-gap GaAs radiative recombination gives lifetimes of a few nanoseconds.

At a surface, the broken bonds form a dense set of traps. Their effect is described by a **surface recombination velocity** $S$, defined by: recombination per unit area per second $= S\,\Delta p(\text{surface})$. A bare silicon surface has $S \sim 10^{4}$–$10^{5}\,\mathrm{cm/s}$; a well-oxidised one $10$–$100\,\mathrm{cm/s}$.

### Quasi-Fermi levels

Out of equilibrium a single Fermi level can no longer describe both carriers, because $np \neq n_i^2$. But electrons within the conduction band thermalise among themselves in about $10^{-13}\,\mathrm s$, far faster than they recombine, and similarly for holes. Each population is therefore in equilibrium with itself, and each can be given its own Fermi level.

::: definition Quasi-Fermi levels {#def-quasi-fermi}
The **quasi-Fermi levels** $F_n$ and $F_p$ are defined by

$$
n = n_ie^{(F_n - E_i)/kT}, \qquad p = n_ie^{(E_i - F_p)/kT},
$$ {#eq-quasi-fermi}

so that $np = n_i^2e^{(F_n - F_p)/kT}$. In equilibrium $F_n = F_p = E_F$; the separation $F_n - F_p$ measures how far the material is from equilibrium.
:::

Substituting [[#eq-quasi-fermi]] into [[#eq-dd]] and using the Einstein relation collapses drift and diffusion into a single expression (you will prove this in an exercise):

$$
J_n = \mu_n n\,\frac{\mathrm dF_n}{\mathrm dx}, \qquad J_p = \mu_p p\,\frac{\mathrm dF_p}{\mathrm dx}.
$$ {#eq-j-quasi}

A current flows only where a quasi-Fermi level has a slope, and where the carrier concentration is large the slope needed is small. In a forward-biased diode, $F_n - F_p = qV_A$ across the junction region: that is the origin of the factor $e^{V_A/V_T}$ in the diode equation.

## The continuity equations

Count the electrons in a thin slab between $x$ and $x + \mathrm dx$. Their number changes because current flows in at one face and out at the other, because pairs are generated (by light, say, at rate $G_L$ per unit volume per second), and because they recombine at the net rate $U$.

::: theorem Continuity equations {#thm-continuity}
In one dimension,

$$
\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = \frac1q\frac{\partial J_n}{\partial x} + G_L - U, \qquad \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\frac1q\frac{\partial J_p}{\partial x} + G_L - U.
$$ {#eq-continuity}
:::

::: proof
The electron particle flux is $J_n/(-q)$. The number of electrons per unit area in the slab, $n\,\mathrm dx$, increases at the rate (flux in at $x$) minus (flux out at $x + \mathrm dx$) plus $(G_L - U)\,\mathrm dx$:

$$
\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}\mathrm dx = \frac{J_n(x)}{-q} - \frac{J_n(x + \mathrm dx)}{-q} + (G_L - U)\,\mathrm dx = \frac1q\frac{\partial J_n}{\partial x}\mathrm dx + (G_L - U)\,\mathrm dx.
$$

Dividing by $\mathrm dx$ gives the electron equation. Holes carry charge $+q$, so their flux is $J_p/q$ and the sign of the current term is reversed.
:::

The two equations are statements of conservation of particles; adding $q$ times the hole equation and subtracting $q$ times the electron equation gives conservation of charge, $\partial\rho/\partial t + \partial J/\partial x = 0$.

### The minority-carrier diffusion equation

In the quasi-neutral regions of a device — everywhere outside the narrow depletion layers — the field is small and the majority carriers barely change. Consider minority holes in n-type material under low-level injection. If the field is small enough that drift of the *minority* carriers is negligible, $J_p \approx -qD_p\,\partial\Delta p/\partial x$, and with $U = \Delta p/\tau_p$:

::: corollary Minority-carrier diffusion equation {#cor-mcde}
For minority holes in a uniform, quasi-neutral n-type region under low-level injection,

$$
\frac{\partial\Delta p}{\partial t} = D_p\frac{\partial^2\Delta p}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\Delta p}{\tau_p} + G_L,
$$ {#eq-mcde}

and the same with $n$ for minority electrons in p-type material. In steady state with $G_L = 0$, the general solution is

$$
\Delta p(x) = Ae^{-x/L_p} + Be^{x/L_p}, \qquad L_p = \sqrt{D_p\tau_p},
$$ {#eq-diffusion-length}

where $L_p$ is the **diffusion length**: the average distance a minority carrier diffuses before it recombines.
:::

Why may we neglect minority drift while the majority carriers obviously drift to carry current? Because the minority concentration is tiny, the drift current $qp\mu_p\mathcal E$ it carries in a small field is negligible compared with its diffusion current, while the much larger majority concentration carries an appreciable drift current in the same small field. The field adjusts itself to whatever value keeps the region neutral, a point made precise by the **dielectric relaxation time** in an exercise.

::: example Injection from a surface {#ex-injection}
Light absorbed in a thin layer at the surface $x = 0$ of a thick n-type silicon sample ($N_D = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $\tau_p = 1\,\mathrm{\mu s}$) holds the excess hole concentration there at $\Delta p(0) = 10^{12}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Find $\Delta p(x)$, the diffusion length, and the hole current density at the surface. Is low-level injection satisfied?
::: solution
At $N_D = 10^{16}$, $\mu_p = 406\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$ so $D_p = V_T\mu_p = 0.02585\times406 = 10.5\,\mathrm{cm^2/s}$, and

$$
L_p = \sqrt{D_p\tau_p} = \sqrt{10.5\times10^{-6}}\,\mathrm{cm} = 3.24\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm} = 32.4\,\mathrm{\mu m}.
$$

In a thick sample $\Delta p$ must stay finite as $x \to \infty$, so $B = 0$ in [[#eq-diffusion-length]] and $\Delta p(x) = 10^{12}e^{-x/L_p}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. The diffusion current is

$$
J_p(0) = -qD_p\frac{\mathrm d\Delta p}{\mathrm dx}\Big|_0 = \frac{qD_p\Delta p(0)}{L_p} = \frac{(1.602\times10^{-19})(10.5)(10^{12})}{3.24\times10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{A/cm^2} = 0.52\,\mathrm{mA/cm^2},
$$

flowing into the sample. Low-level injection holds: $\Delta p = 10^{12} \ll n_0 = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Note the pattern $J = qD\Delta p/L$, "charge times concentration times an effective velocity $D/L$" ($D_p/L_p = 3240\,\mathrm{cm/s}$ here); it reappears in the diode equation.
:::

::: example Photoconductivity {#ex-photoconductor}
A bar of n-type silicon ($N_D = 10^{15}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $\tau_p = 10\,\mathrm{\mu s}$) is uniformly illuminated, generating $G_L = 10^{19}$ electron–hole pairs per cubic centimetre per second. Find the steady-state excess concentration and the fractional change in conductivity. If the light is switched off at $t = 0$, how does the conductivity recover?
::: solution
With uniform illumination there are no gradients, so [[#eq-mcde]] in steady state gives $0 = -\Delta p/\tau_p + G_L$, hence

$$
\Delta p = \Delta n = G_L\tau_p = 10^{19}\times10^{-5} = 10^{14}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}},
$$

which is $10\,\%$ of $n_0$: low-level injection holds approximately. Both excess electrons and excess holes conduct, so with $\mu_n = 1345$ and $\mu_p = 477\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$ at this doping,

$$
\frac{\Delta\sigma}{\sigma_0} = \frac{q\Delta p(\mu_n + \mu_p)}{qn_0\mu_n} = \frac{10^{14}(1345 + 477)}{10^{15}\times1345} = 0.135.
$$

The conductivity rises by $13.5\,\%$. After switch-off, $\mathrm d\Delta p/\mathrm dt = -\Delta p/\tau_p$, so $\Delta p = 10^{14}e^{-t/\tau_p}$ and the excess conductivity decays exponentially with time constant $10\,\mathrm{\mu s}$. Measuring this decay is the standard way of measuring lifetime (the photoconductive-decay method).
:::

::: quiz
The minority-carrier lifetime in a p-type region is made four times longer by gettering out iron contamination. What happens to the electron diffusion length?
- [ ] It becomes four times longer.
- [x] It doubles.
- [ ] It is unchanged, because $L$ depends only on the mobility.
- [ ] It halves.
::: solution
$L_n = \sqrt{D_n\tau_n}$, so multiplying $\tau_n$ by four multiplies $L_n$ by $\sqrt4 = 2$. Diffusion is a random walk: the distance covered grows with the square root of the time.
:::

::: history
Paul Drude's 1900 model of conduction by free electrons undergoing collisions is the origin of the mobility $q\tau/m$. Albert Einstein derived the relation between diffusion and mobility in 1905, in his paper on Brownian motion; the same relation had been found for ions in solution by Walther Nernst in 1888. Minority-carrier injection, drift and diffusion were demonstrated directly in 1949 by J. Richard Haynes and William Shockley at Bell Laboratories, who injected a pulse of holes into a germanium bar and watched it drift and spread on its way to a collector, measuring mobility and diffusion coefficient in the same experiment. The theory of recombination through traps was published in 1952 by Shockley and W. T. Read and, independently, by Robert N. Hall. The saturation of drift velocity at high fields was studied in silicon in the 1950s and 1960s, notably by Ryder (1953) and later by Canali, Jacoboni and co-workers, whose 1975 measurements underlie the empirical fits still in use.
:::

## Where this leads

The minority-carrier diffusion equation with its exponential and hyperbolic solutions is the engine of the next three chapters. In [[semiconductor-devices/pn-junction]] it gives the ideal diode equation, with saturation current proportional to $qn_i^2D/(NL)$. In [[semiconductor-devices/diodes-photonic-devices]] the SRH rate gives the generation and recombination currents of real diodes and the photocurrent of solar cells. In [[semiconductor-devices/bipolar-transistors]] the short-base limit of the last figure gives the transistor's collector current and its current gain. Mobility and velocity saturation set the current of the MOSFET in [[semiconductor-devices/mosfets]].

::: summary
- Carriers drift in a field with velocity $\mu\mathcal E$ at low field; the drift current is $q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)\mathcal E$, so $\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$. In lightly doped silicon $\mu_n \approx 1360$ and $\mu_p \approx 490\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$.
- Mobility falls with doping (ionised-impurity scattering) and with temperature (phonon scattering); at fields above about $10^4\,\mathrm{V/cm}$ the velocity saturates near $10^{7}\,\mathrm{cm/s}$.
- Concentration gradients cause diffusion currents $qD_n\,\mathrm dn/\mathrm dx$ and $-qD_p\,\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx$; the Einstein relation $D/\mu = kT/q$ links the two transport coefficients.
- The electric field equals $\frac1q$ times the slope of the bands; out of equilibrium each carrier has a quasi-Fermi level, and current flows only where it slopes.
- Excess carriers recombine with lifetime $\tau$ (SRH through midgap traps in silicon, radiative in direct-gap materials); the net rate is proportional to $np - n_i^2$.
- The continuity equations express particle conservation; in quasi-neutral regions they reduce to the minority-carrier diffusion equation with diffusion length $L = \sqrt{D\tau}$.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Drift velocity and current {level=1 check="1.602e-19*1e16*1248*500*1e4"}
N-type silicon with $N_D = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ ($\mu_n = 1248\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$) carries a field of $500\,\mathrm{V/cm}$. Find the electron drift velocity and the current density, the latter in $\mathrm{A/m^2}$.
::: solution
$v_d = \mu_n\mathcal E = 1248\times500 = 6.24\times10^{5}\,\mathrm{cm/s}$, still well below $v_{\text{sat}}$. $J = qn\mu_n\mathcal E = (1.602\times10^{-19})(10^{16})(6.24\times10^{5}) = 1000\,\mathrm{A/cm^2} = 1.0\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{A/m^2}$.
:::

Find the electron diffusion coefficient in silicon with $10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ ionised impurities at $300\,\mathrm K$, using $\mu_n = 801.3\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$. Give the answer in $\mathrm{m^2/s}$.
::: solution
$D_n = V_T\mu_n = 0.02585\times801.3 = 20.7\,\mathrm{cm^2/s}$ (that is, $2.07\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$).

A polysilicon layer has $R_s = 250\,\Omega/\square$. What length of a $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ wide strip gives $5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$? Give the answer in metres.
::: solution
$L/W = 5000/250 = 20$ squares, so $L = 20\,\mathrm{\mu m} = 2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm m$. (In practice the contacts at each end add roughly half a square or more, which the layout must allow for.)

::: exercise Compensated resistivity {level=2}
Silicon contains $N_D = 1.1\times10^{17}$ and $N_A = 1.0\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Find its resistivity, and compare with uncompensated material with $N_D = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.
::: solution
The net doping is $n = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, but the ionised impurity concentration that scatters is $2.1\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. The fit gives $\mu_n(2.1\times10^{17}) = 92 + 1268/(1 + 1.615^{0.91}) = 92 + 1268/2.547 = 590\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$. Then $\rho = 1/(1.602\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times590) = 1.06\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$, more than twice the $0.50\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$ of the uncompensated sample with the same electron concentration.
:::

::: exercise Dielectric relaxation {level=2 check="11.7*8.854e-14/(1.602e-19*1e16*1248)"}
Suppose a small net charge density $\rho_c$ is created inside uniform n-type silicon ($N_D = 10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$). Combining Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}) = \rho_c$, Ohm's law $\mathbf J = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}$ and charge conservation, show that $\rho_c$ decays as $e^{-t/\tau_d}$ with $\tau_d = \varepsilon/\sigma$, and evaluate $\tau_d$ in seconds.
::: solution
$\partial\rho_c/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf J = -\sigma\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal E} = -(\sigma/\varepsilon)\rho_c$, so $\rho_c \propto e^{-t/\tau_d}$ with $\tau_d = \varepsilon/\sigma$. Here $\sigma = qN_D\mu_n = 2.0\,\mathrm{S/cm}$ and $\varepsilon = 11.7\times8.854\times10^{-14} = 1.036\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/cm}$, so $\tau_d = 5.2\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm s$. Any charge imbalance is neutralised by majority carriers in half a picosecond, a million times faster than minority carriers recombine. That is why the regions outside depletion layers can be treated as neutral, and why it is the minority carriers' slow return to equilibrium that governs device dynamics.
:::

::: exercise Current from quasi-Fermi levels {level=2}
Show that the drift–diffusion expression [[#eq-dd]] for $J_n$, combined with $n = n_ie^{(F_n - E_i)/kT}$, the Einstein relation and $\mathcal E = \frac1q\,\mathrm dE_i/\mathrm dx$, gives $J_n = \mu_nn\,\mathrm dF_n/\mathrm dx$.
::: solution
$\mathrm dn/\mathrm dx = \frac{n}{kT}\left(\frac{\mathrm dF_n}{\mathrm dx} - \frac{\mathrm dE_i}{\mathrm dx}\right)$. Then $J_n = qn\mu_n\frac1q\frac{\mathrm dE_i}{\mathrm dx} + q\frac{kT}{q}\mu_n\frac{n}{kT}\left(\frac{\mathrm dF_n}{\mathrm dx} - \frac{\mathrm dE_i}{\mathrm dx}\right) = \mu_nn\frac{\mathrm dF_n}{\mathrm dx}$. The drift term cancels the part of the diffusion term due to band bending, leaving only the gradient of the quasi-Fermi level.
:::

::: exercise A thin sample with a recombining back surface {level=3}
The sample of [[#ex-injection]] is only $W = 20\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thick, and its back surface at $x = W$ is an ohmic contact where $\Delta p = 0$. (a) Find $\Delta p(x)$. (b) Find the hole current density entering at $x = 0$ and the fraction of injected holes that reach the back contact rather than recombining in the bulk.
::: hint
Use the hyperbolic form $\Delta p = A\sinh[(W - x)/L_p] + B\cosh[(W - x)/L_p]$; the back boundary condition fixes $B$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\Delta p(W) = 0$ gives $B = 0$, and $\Delta p(0) = A\sinh(W/L_p)$, so $\Delta p(x) = \Delta p(0)\sinh[(W - x)/L_p]/\sinh(W/L_p)$, with $W/L_p = 20/32.4 = 0.617$.

(b) $J_p(0) = -qD_p\,\mathrm d\Delta p/\mathrm dx|_0 = \frac{qD_p\Delta p(0)}{L_p}\coth(W/L_p)$. Now $\coth 0.617 = 1/\tanh0.617 = 1/0.549 = 1.82$, so $J_p(0) = 0.52\times1.82 = 0.95\,\mathrm{mA/cm^2}$. At $x = W$, $J_p(W) = \frac{qD_p\Delta p(0)}{L_p}\frac{1}{\sinh(W/L_p)} = 0.52/0.657 = 0.79\,\mathrm{mA/cm^2}$. The fraction reaching the contact is $J_p(W)/J_p(0) = 1/\cosh(W/L_p) = 1/1.197 = 0.835$: $84\,\%$ of the holes cross the region, $16\,\%$ recombine on the way. A transistor base works in this regime, but with $W/L$ about $0.01$, so that more than $99.99\,\%$ cross.
:::

::: exercise Velocity saturation in a short resistor {level=3}
A $0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ long bar of lightly doped n-type silicon ($\mu_n = 1360\,\mathrm{cm^2/(V\,s)}$, $v_{\text{sat}} = 10^{7}\,\mathrm{cm/s}$, $\beta = 2$) has $1\,\mathrm V$ across it. (a) What would the low-field drift velocity be? (b) What is the actual velocity predicted by the empirical law? (c) By what factor is the current lower than Ohm's law predicts? (d) What voltage would give $90\,\%$ of the saturated velocity?
::: solution
(a) $\mathcal E = 1/(0.2\times10^{-4}) = 5\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/cm}$, so $\mu_n\mathcal E = 6.8\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{cm/s}$, faster than the thermal velocity — impossible. (b) $v = 6.8\times10^{7}/\sqrt{1 + 6.8^2} = 6.8\times10^{7}/6.873 = 9.89\times10^{6}\,\mathrm{cm/s}$. (c) The current is lower by the factor $6.87$. (d) $v/v_{\text{sat}} = u/\sqrt{1 + u^2} = 0.9$ with $u = \mu\mathcal E/v_{\text{sat}}$ gives $u^2 = 0.81/0.19$, $u = 2.065$, so $\mathcal E = 2.065\times10^{7}/1360 = 1.52\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/cm}$, i.e. $0.30\,\mathrm V$ across $0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. (This simple model ignores velocity overshoot, which in very short devices lets carriers briefly exceed $v_{\text{sat}}$.)
:::
