Say the English words *pin*, *spin* and *bin*, holding the back of your hand close to your lips. In *pin* you feel a small puff of air after the /p/; in *spin* there is almost none; in *bin*, none either. Now consider what a speaker of English *hears*. The /p/ of *pin* and the /p/ of *spin* sound like "the same sound" to an English speaker, though they are physically different, while the /p/ of *spin* and the /b/ of *bin*, which are physically very close, sound like different sounds. A speaker of Mandarin hears the matter the other way round. For her, the puff of air is what separates 怕 *pà* ‘fear’ from 爸 *bà* ‘dad’, so the /p/ of *pin* and the /p/ of *spin* are clearly two sounds, and the English *spin* sounds as if it were spelled *sbin*.

Both speakers are right about their own language. The physical facts are the same for everyone; what differs is how each language organises them. That difference is the boundary between the two subjects of this chapter. **Phonetics** studies speech sounds as physical events: how they are made, what they are as sound waves, and how the ear and brain receive them. **Phonology** studies how a particular language uses sounds as a system: which differences make words different, how sounds combine into syllables, and what predictable changes they undergo. The practical lessons of the atlas — [[sounds-of-english/how-speech-is-made]], [[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives]], [[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters]] and the *Connected Speech* course — teach how to pronounce English. Here we look at the theory behind them.

## Phonetics: three ways to study a sound

::: definition Articulatory, acoustic and auditory phonetics {#def-phonetics}
**Articulatory phonetics** describes how speech sounds are produced by the organs of speech: the lungs, the vocal folds in the larynx, the tongue, lips, teeth, palate and nasal cavity. **Acoustic phonetics** describes speech as sound waves, measured with instruments: frequency, intensity and duration. **Auditory phonetics** (with speech perception) studies how listeners receive and interpret those waves.
:::

Articulatory description is the basis of the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), whose principles were set out by the International Phonetic Association, founded in 1886. A consonant is described by three properties, which [[sounds-of-english/how-speech-is-made#rule-voice-place-manner]] introduced: **voicing** (whether the vocal folds vibrate), **place of articulation** (where the airflow is narrowed or stopped) and **manner of articulation** (how much it is narrowed and how the air escapes). The English /p/ is a voiceless bilabial plosive, /z/ a voiced alveolar fricative, /ŋ/ a voiced velar nasal. A vowel is described by the height of the tongue, how far forward or back it is, and whether the lips are rounded: /iː/ is close, front and unrounded; /uː/ is close, back and rounded (in GB today it is often quite central).

These labels are a classification, not a list of separate facts. A small set of properties, combined, describes all the consonants of English — and of every other language. Mandarin has places and manners that English does not use, such as the retroflex affricates of 知 *zhī* and 吃 *chī* and the alveolo-palatal ones of 鸡 *jī* and 七 *qī*, but they are described with the same grid.

### Sound as a wave

Every voiced sound starts with the vibration of the vocal folds. They open and close many times a second — on average roughly 100–150 times for adult men and 200–250 times for adult women, with wide variation — and each opening lets out a small burst of air. The rate of vibration is the **fundamental frequency** (F0), heard as pitch. But the wave that leaves the larynx is not a simple sine wave. It is a complex periodic wave, which can be analysed as the sum of many sine waves: the fundamental and its **harmonics** at two, three, four … times its frequency, each weaker than the one before.

Increase the number of terms and watch the sum approach the sawtooth. The figure is an illustration of the mathematics and makes no sound. In speech, the shape of the mouth and throat then strengthens some bands of harmonics and weakens others, which is what makes one vowel sound different from another.
:::

::: definition Source, filter and formants {#def-formant}
In the **source–filter** account of speech (developed by Gunnar Fant in *Acoustic Theory of Speech Production*, 1960), the vibrating vocal folds are the **source** of a wave rich in harmonics, and the air spaces of the throat, mouth and nose act as a **filter** that strengthens the harmonics near their natural resonances. The bands of strengthened frequencies are the **formants**, numbered from the lowest: F1, F2, F3. Different vowels are different filter shapes, and so have different formant frequencies.
:::

Two formants do most of the work of distinguishing vowels. **F1** is lower for close vowels and higher for open ones: the tongue is high for /iː/ and low for /ɑː/. **F2** is higher for front vowels and lower for back ones, and lip-rounding lowers it further. In the classic study of American English vowels by Peterson and Barney (1952), the average values for men were about F1 270 Hz and F2 2290 Hz for the vowel of *heed*, about 730 Hz and 1090 Hz for the vowel of *hod*, and about 300 Hz and 870 Hz for the vowel of *who'd*. If you plot F2 on a horizontal axis running from high to low and F1 on a vertical axis running downwards, the vowels fall roughly where they sit on the traditional vowel chart. That correspondence is why phoneticians say the vowel chart describes acoustic space at least as well as tongue positions.

### Timing: voice onset time

The acoustic record also shows timing that the ear hardly notices. In a syllable such as *pa*, there is a moment when the lips open (the release) and a moment when the vocal folds start to vibrate. The interval between them is the **voice onset time** (VOT). Lisker and Abramson (1964) measured it across eleven languages and found that languages divide the VOT continuum into two or three categories in a few common ways:

| VOT | description | English | Mandarin |
|---|---|---|---|
| voicing starts before the release (negative VOT) | **prevoiced** | /b d ɡ/ for some speakers, especially between vowels | — |
| voicing starts at or just after the release (short lag) | **voiceless unaspirated** | /b d ɡ/ at the start of a word for most speakers; /p t k/ after /s/ | *b d g* (爸 *bà*) |
| voicing starts well after the release (long lag) | **voiceless aspirated** | /p t k/ at the start of a stressed syllable | *p t k* (怕 *pà*) |

The table explains the puzzle at the start of the chapter. English contrasts short-lag /b/ with long-lag /p/ at the start of a word, as Mandarin contrasts *b* with *p*: the two languages place their boundary in a similar region. What differs is what each language calls the categories (English "voiced" and "voiceless", Mandarin "unaspirated" and "aspirated") and where else the categories appear. In English, /p/ after /s/ is short-lag, and that is why *spin* sounds like *sbin* to a Mandarin speaker; in English the spelling, the native speaker's intuition and the phonology all say it is /p/.

::: quiz
In Peterson and Barney's measurements, which vowel had the **lowest F2**?
- [ ] the vowel of *heed*
- [ ] the vowel of *hod*
- [x] the vowel of *who'd*
- [ ] they had the same F2
::: solution
*Who'd* (about 870 Hz). F2 is lowest for back vowels and is lowered further by lip-rounding, and the vowel of *who'd* is close, back and rounded. *Heed* has a front vowel with a high F2 (about 2290 Hz). *Hod* has a back vowel too, but unrounded and open, so its F2 is higher (about 1090 Hz) and its F1 much higher.
:::
:::

### Hearing in categories

Listeners do not hear the VOT continuum as a continuum. In experiments begun at Haskins Laboratories by Alvin Liberman and his colleagues (1957), listeners heard synthetic syllables that varied in small equal steps from *ba* through *da* to *ga*. They did not hear gradual change: they heard *ba*, *ba*, *ba*, then suddenly *da*, and they were much better at telling apart two syllables on opposite sides of the boundary than two equally different syllables within one category. This is **categorical perception**. Later work showed that it is less absolute than first thought — listeners can detect some differences within a category, and vowels are perceived less categorically than stops — but the main finding stands: we hear speech through the categories of our language. Those categories are the subject of phonology.

## Phonology: sounds as a system

::: definition Phoneme and allophone {#def-allophone}
A **phoneme** is a unit of the sound system of a particular language that distinguishes words from one another: English /p/ and /b/ are different phonemes because *pin* and *bin* are different words. An **allophone** is one of the physical realisations of a phoneme, chosen by its context: [pʰ] in *pin* and [p] in *spin* are allophones of English /p/. Phonemes are written between slashes; allophones, and any transcription with phonetic detail, between square brackets.
:::

[[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#def-phoneme]] introduced phonemes for practical use: one symbol for each sound that matters in English. The linguistic question is how to decide which sounds are separate phonemes and which are allophones of one phoneme. There are two kinds of evidence.

::: theorem Contrast and distribution {#rule-distribution}
1. **Contrast.** Two sounds belong to **different phonemes** if replacing one with the other in the same position can change one word into another. The proof is a **minimal pair**: *pin* /pɪn/ – *bin* /bɪn/.
2. **Complementary distribution.** Two phonetically similar sounds are **allophones of one phoneme** if they never occur in the same position — each appears where the other cannot — so that they can never distinguish words. English [pʰ] occurs at the start of stressed syllables, [p] after /s/; neither occurs in the other's position.
3. **Free variation.** Two sounds that can replace each other in the same position *without* changing the word are also allophones, in free variation: a final /t/ in *cat* may be released, unreleased or glottalised in many accents, and it is still the same word.
4. **Phonetic similarity.** Sounds in complementary distribution are grouped only if they are similar. English [h] occurs only at the start of syllables and [ŋ] only at the end, so they are in complementary distribution, but no analysis treats them as one phoneme, because they share almost nothing phonetically.
:::

::: proof
The criteria come from the structuralist phonology of the 1930s and 1940s — Nikolai Trubetzkoy's *Grundzüge der Phonologie* (1939) in Europe, Bloomfield (1933) and his followers in America. Their logic is that a phoneme is defined by the work it does in the system: a difference that can change meaning is part of the system, and a difference that is predictable from context is not, because a speaker supplies it automatically. That is why native speakers are usually unaware of allophones and find them hard to hear. The criteria do not always give one answer. Yuen Ren Chao showed in a famous paper, "The non-uniqueness of phonemic solutions of phonetic systems" (1934), that the same facts can often be analysed into phonemes in several equally valid ways; his examples included Mandarin. Criterion 4 is part of the reason: "similar" is a matter of degree. Modern phonology still uses minimal pairs and distribution as its first tools, while adding evidence from speakers' behaviour (slips, word games, loanword adaptation) and from acquisition.
:::

::: example Finding allophones: English /l/ {#ex-l}
In GB, /l/ has two main allophones: a "clear" [l], with the front of the tongue raised, and a "dark" [ɫ], with the back of the tongue raised towards the velum. Here are some words with the allophone each one has:

*leaf* [l], *look* [l], *silly* [l], *feel* [ɫ], *milk* [ɫ], *help* [ɫ], *fall* [ɫ], *fall over* [l].

State the distribution and show that the two are allophones of one phoneme.
::: solution
1. **Look for contrast.** Is there a pair of words that differ only in [l] versus [ɫ]? There is none: no English word changes into another when its clear /l/ is made dark.
2. **Look at the contexts.** Clear [l] appears in *leaf*, *look*, *silly* and *fall over* — in each case **before a vowel**. Dark [ɫ] appears in *feel* and *fall* (before a pause) and in *milk* and *help* (before a consonant) — that is, **not before a vowel**.
3. **Check the boundary case.** *Fall* has dark [ɫ] alone but clear [l] in *fall over*, where the next word begins with a vowel. So the choice depends on the sound that follows, not on the word.
4. **Conclusion.** Clear and dark /l/ are in complementary distribution and phonetically similar (both laterals), so they are allophones of one phoneme /l/. A rule states it: /l/ is dark when no vowel follows. Many GA speakers have a darker /l/ in all positions, and in some accents of south-east England dark /l/ is often vocalised, so that *milk* sounds like [mɪʊk]. The details are in [[sounds-of-english/nasals-approximants#rule-dark-l]].
:::
:::

::: example A Mandarin problem: *j q x* {#ex-jqx}
Pinyin writes three series of Mandarin consonants that are in an unusual relationship:

- *z c s* (as in 字 *zì*, 次 *cì*, 四 *sì*) and *zh ch sh* (知 *zhī*, 吃 *chī*, 是 *shì*) occur before many vowels, but never before *i* [i] or *ü* [y];
- *g k h* (哥 *gē*, 可 *kě*, 喝 *hē*) also never occur before [i] or [y];
- *j q x* (鸡 *jī*, 七 *qī*, 西 *xī*) occur **only** before [i] or [y].

What does this distribution suggest, and why do linguists disagree about the answer?
::: solution
1. *j q x* are in complementary distribution with *z c s*, with *zh ch sh* **and** with *g k h*: wherever *j q x* occur, none of the others can, and vice versa.
2. By criterion 2, *j q x* could therefore be analysed as allophones of one of the other series, appearing before high front vowels. Each choice has some phonetic support: *j q x* are palatal-like, made with the tongue front, and so share features with *z c s* (sibilants) and with *g k h* (whose place is moved forward before front vowels in many languages).
3. Because there are three equally good candidates, the facts do not settle the analysis. This is exactly the situation Chao (1934) described as the non-uniqueness of phonemic solutions. History adds evidence of a different kind: in Mandarin, *j q x* come partly from earlier velars and partly from earlier dental sibilants, both palatalised before high front vowels — which is why some place names are spelled with *k* or *ts* in older romanisations (*Peking* for 北京, *Tsingtao* for 青岛).
4. Many descriptions keep *j q x* as separate phonemes for practical reasons, as Pinyin does. The lesson for phonology is that phonemic analysis is a set of decisions, and the decisions need to be justified, not assumed.
:::
:::

### Features and natural classes

Sounds that behave alike in a language's rules usually share phonetic properties. English /p t k/ are all aspirated in the same position; /p t k f θ s ʃ tʃ/ are all followed by the /s/ form of the plural, not the /z/ form ([[word-formation/morphemes#rule-s-ed]]). Phonologists capture this with **distinctive features**: binary properties such as [±voice], [±continuant] (air flows continuously, as in fricatives) or [±nasal]. The idea was developed by Roman Jakobson, Gunnar Fant and Morris Halle in *Preliminaries to Speech Analysis* (1952), and Chomsky and Halle's *The Sound Pattern of English* (1968) used it to state the rules of English phonology.

::: definition Natural class {#def-natural-class}
A **natural class** is a set of sounds that can be picked out by fewer features than any one of its members needs: [−voice, −continuant] picks out the voiceless plosives /p t k/ (and the affricate /tʃ/, depending on the analysis). Phonological rules typically apply to natural classes, not to random sets of sounds: a language may aspirate all its voiceless plosives, but no known language aspirates exactly /p/, /s/ and /m/.
:::

With features, a rule can be stated compactly. Linguists write a rule as *A → B / C ___ D*, read as ‘A becomes B when it comes after C and before D’. The GA tapping rule ([[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet#rule-rhotic]]) is:

/t, d/ → [ɾ] / stressed vowel ___ unstressed vowel

so *water* is [ˈwɑːɾər], *city* [ˈsɪɾi], *ladder* [ˈlæɾər], and in GA *latter* and *ladder* can sound the same. The rule applies across word boundaries too (*get it* [ɡeɾɪt]) and does not apply before a stressed vowel (*attack* keeps its [tʰ]), which is exactly what the statement predicts.

::: theorem Three allophonic rules of English {#rule-allophonic}
1. **Aspiration.** The voiceless plosives /p t k/ are aspirated [pʰ tʰ kʰ] at the start of a stressed syllable (*pin*, *attack*), less so at the start of unstressed ones, and unaspirated after /s/ (*spin*, *stop*, *skin*).
2. **Pre-fortis clipping.** Vowels and sonorants are noticeably shorter before a voiceless consonant in the same syllable than before a voiced one: the vowel of *beat* is shorter than that of *bead*, the /n/ of *bent* shorter than that of *bend*. Roach calls voiceless consonants **fortis** and voiced ones **lenis**.
3. **Dark /l/** in GB where no vowel follows (as in [[#ex-l]]); **tapping** of /t, d/ in GA between a stressed and an unstressed vowel.
:::

::: proof
These rules are well established in the standard descriptions of GB and GA (Roach 2009; Cruttenden 2014; Ladefoged and Johnson 2015). Rule 2 is especially important for listeners: at the end of a word, English /d/ and /t/ are often both partly or wholly voiceless, so the main cue to *bead* versus *beat* is the length of the vowel before it. This is why learners whose first language has no voiced final consonants — which includes Mandarin — are helped more by lengthening the vowel of *bag* than by trying to voice the /ɡ/ ([[sounds-of-english/stops-fricatives#rule-final]]). The rules are tendencies of normal speech, and their details vary between speakers and accents; in some northern English and Scottish accents, for instance, /p t k/ are much less aspirated than in GB.
:::

::: warning Transcribing phonemes, not allophones
A phonemic transcription does not show allophones, and a phonetic one should not leave out the ones it claims to show. ✗ /pʰɪn/ mixes the two: aspiration belongs in square brackets. ✓ /pɪn/ (phonemic) or ✓ [pʰɪn] (phonetic). Equally, ✗ [spʰɪn] is a wrong phonetic transcription: /p/ after /s/ is unaspirated, ✓ [spɪn]. Use slashes when you are talking about which word it is, brackets when you are talking about how it is said.
:::

## Syllables and phonotactics

Phonemes do not combine freely. English allows /bl/ at the start of a word (*blue*) but not /bn/; it allows /ŋ/ at the end (*sing*) but not at the start. A made-up word such as *blick* could be English; *bnick* could not. These restrictions are called **phonotactics**, and most of them are stated in terms of the syllable.

::: definition Syllable structure {#def-syllable-structure}
A **syllable** consists of an optional **onset** (the consonants before the vowel) and an obligatory **rhyme**, which contains the **nucleus** (usually a vowel) and an optional **coda** (the consonants after it). The structure of *strengths* /streŋθs/, an English syllable with a very heavy onset and coda, is:

- onset /str/, nucleus /e/, coda /ŋθs/ — the pattern CCCVCCC.

[[sounds-of-english/syllables-clusters#def-syllable]] gives the practical side.
:::

Evidence for the rhyme as a unit comes from verse (words rhyme when their rhymes match — *strengths* and *lengths*) and from the fact that many phonotactic restrictions hold between nucleus and coda but not between onset and nucleus. The final /s/ of *strengths* looks like a plural ending, and in fact it is one: *strength* + *-s*. Mandarin syllables are much simpler: at most one consonant in the onset, an optional glide, a vowel, and a coda that can only be *n* or *ng* (or the *r* of *érhuà*). Drag the nodes to compare other words.

::: theorem English onsets {#rule-onsets}
1. An English onset has at most three consonants, and three-consonant onsets always have the form /s/ + voiceless plosive + approximant: *spr-* (*spring*), *str-* (*street*), *skr-* (*screen*), *spl-* (*split*), *skw-* (*square*), and *spj-*, *stj-*, *skj-* before /uː/ in some words and accents (*spew*, GB *stew*, *skew*).
2. Two-consonant onsets are mostly an obstruent followed by an approximant (*pl*, *br*, *tw*, *kj* in *cute*, *fl*, *θr*), or /s/ followed by a plosive or a nasal (*sp*, *st*, *sk*, *sm*, *sn*).
3. Some combinations are excluded even though both consonants are possible: no English onset has /tl/ or /dl/, and /ŋ/ never begins a syllable.
:::

::: proof
These are the restrictions described for English by Gimson and his successors and by Roach (2009). Many of them follow a general principle, the **sonority sequencing principle**: in most languages, sounds within a syllable rise in sonority (roughly, loudness and openness) towards the nucleus and fall after it. Plosives are least sonorous, then fricatives, nasals, liquids (/l/, /r/), glides and vowels. *Blue* rises from /b/ to /l/ to /uː/; ✗ *lbue* would fall then rise. English /s/ + plosive clusters break the principle (/s/ is more sonorous than /t/, yet it comes first), and many phonologists therefore treat the /s/ as a special appendix outside the ordinary onset. Restriction 3 shows that a language also has gaps that sonority does not explain: /tl/ is a rising cluster, possible in other languages, but English happens not to use it.
:::

::: example Possible English words {#ex-phonotactics}
Which of these invented words are possible English words, and which break a phonotactic rule? Say which rule. *blick*, *bnick*, *ngop*, *sprell*, *tlaim*, *glorp*, *fsat*.
::: solution
- *blick* /blɪk/: possible (obstruent + approximant onset). It is an accidental gap: the language allows it but does not happen to have it.
- *bnick* /bnɪk/: impossible; plosive + nasal is not an English onset (rule 2).
- *ngop* /ŋɒp/: impossible; /ŋ/ does not begin a syllable (rule 3).
- *sprell* /sprel/: possible (rule 1).
- *tlaim* /tlaɪm/: impossible in English (rule 3), though /tl/ occurs in other languages, for example in Nahuatl.
- *glorp*: possible — /ɡlɔːp/ in GB, /ɡlɔːrp/ in GA, with /rp/ in the GA coda.
- *fsat* /fsæt/: impossible; two fricatives do not make an English onset.

The distinction between an **accidental gap** (*blick*) and a **systematic gap** (*bnick*) is a classic test of phonotactic knowledge: speakers accept the first as a possible new word and reject the second, without having heard either.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
Mandarin syllable structure is much more restricted than English: an optional initial consonant, an optional medial glide (*i*, *u*, *ü*), a vowel, and an optional final that can only be a vowel glide, *n* or *ng*; the *r* of words like 一点儿 *yìdiǎnr* is a suffix with limited use. No clusters occur in either onset or coda. When Mandarin speakers learn English, the repairs they make to English clusters follow from this: adding a vowel (*street* with a vowel inserted between /s/ and /t/, or after the final /t/), or dropping a final consonant (*desk* as *des*). Speakers of Cantonese have more codas — the unreleased stops /p t k/ of 十 *sap6*, 七 *cat1*, 六 *luk6* and the nasal /m/ of 心 *sam1* — and find some English codas easier but tend to leave final stops unreleased. Each first language transfers its own phonotactics, so a Shanghainese, Cantonese and Sichuanese learner may need different practice ([[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]]).

The deeper difference is in pitch. In Mandarin, pitch is a property of each syllable and changes the word: 汤 *tāng* ‘soup’ (high level, 55 in Chao's tone numbers), 糖 *táng* ‘sugar’ (rising, 35), 躺 *tǎng* ‘lie down’ (low dipping, 214), 烫 *tàng* ‘hot, scalding’ (falling, 51). This is **lexical tone**. In English, pitch belongs to the phrase and carries meaning such as statement, question, contrast or attitude — **intonation** — while the word has a **stress** pattern realised by pitch, length, loudness and vowel quality together. Mandarin also has intonation, but it must work around the tones, mainly by raising or widening the pitch range of a whole phrase ([[connected-speech/tones-and-intonation]]).
:::

## Stress, tone and intonation

Features that extend over more than one segment — stress, tone, length and intonation — are called **suprasegmentals** or **prosody**.

::: theorem How English marks stress {#rule-stress}
1. English stress is **lexical**: each word has a fixed stress pattern, which must be learned or worked out from its morphology, and which can distinguish words: *ˈimport* (noun) and *imˈport* (verb), *ˈinsight* and *inˈcite*.
2. A stressed syllable is made prominent by several cues together: a pitch movement or a pitch level different from its neighbours, greater length, greater loudness and a full vowel quality.
3. Unstressed syllables often have a **reduced vowel**, especially /ə/: *photograph* /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ (GB), *photography* /fəˈtɒɡrəfi/. Vowel reduction is one of the most important differences between English and Mandarin rhythm.
:::

::: proof
Rule 2 is supported by perception experiments since Dennis Fry's in the 1950s, which varied each cue separately in synthetic words such as *object* and *digest* and found pitch change and duration to be stronger cues than intensity. The combination matters for learners: a Mandarin speaker who marks stress only by loudness, or who keeps a full vowel in every syllable, produces words that native listeners find hard to recognise even when every consonant and vowel is correct. Rule 1 holds for English but not for every stress language: in Czech and Finnish stress is fixed on the first syllable, in Polish mostly on the second-to-last, so it cannot distinguish words. [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]] gives the patterns English words follow and their exceptions.
:::

::: example Transcribing a phrase two ways {#ex-transcribe}
Give a phonemic and a phonetic transcription of the GA phrase *a little pot of tea*, said at normal speed, and explain each allophone you mark.
::: solution
**Phonemic:** /ə ˈlɪtl ˈpɑːt əv ˈtiː/. This shows which words are said: the phonemes and the stresses. (*Pot* has the LOT vowel, /ɑː/ in GA.)

**Phonetic:** [ə ˈlɪɾɫ̩ ˈpʰɑːɾ əv ˈtʰiː].

- [ɾ] in *little*: /t/ between a stressed vowel and an unstressed syllable is tapped in GA.
- [ɫ̩]: the final /l/ is syllabic (it forms the second syllable on its own) and dark, because no vowel follows.
- [pʰ] in *pot*: /p/ at the start of a stressed syllable is aspirated.
- [ɾ] in *pot of*: the /t/ is followed by the unstressed vowel of *of*, so it is tapped across the word boundary.
- [tʰ] in *tea*: aspirated at the start of a stressed syllable.

*Of* is in its weak form /əv/ ([[connected-speech/weak-forms]]). A GB version would differ: /ə ˈlɪtl ˈpɒt əv ˈtiː/, phonetically perhaps [ə ˈlɪtɫ̩ ˈpʰɒt əv ˈtʰiː], with the /t/ of *little* often glottalised in casual speech, [ˈlɪʔɫ̩].
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which pair shows **complementary distribution** in English?
- [ ] /p/ and /b/ in *pat* and *bat*
- [x] aspirated [kʰ] in *kit* and unaspirated [k] in *skit*
- [ ] /ɪ/ and /iː/ in *ship* and *sheep*
- [ ] /s/ and /z/ in *sip* and *zip*
::: solution
[kʰ] occurs at the start of stressed syllables and [k] after /s/; neither appears where the other does, so they cannot distinguish words: they are allophones of /k/. The other three pairs are minimal pairs, which prove that the sounds are separate phonemes.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Lexical tone means that pitch …
- [ ] shows whether a sentence is a question
- [x] is part of the identity of a word, so that changing it changes the word
- [ ] is always higher on stressed syllables
- [ ] is the same in all languages
::: solution
In a tone language such as Mandarin, Cantonese, Thai or Yoruba, the pitch pattern of a syllable is part of the word: 汤 *tāng* and 糖 *táng* are different words. English uses pitch for intonation and as one cue to stress, but changing the pitch of *cat* does not make it a different word.

::: history
The modern study of speech sounds grew from two traditions. One was practical: spelling reform, the teaching of languages and of the deaf, and the description of accents, which led Henry Sweet (*A Handbook of Phonetics*, 1877) and Daniel Jones (*An Outline of English Phonetics*, first published 1918) to the careful description of English pronunciation that Gimson, Roach, Cruttenden and Wells continued. The International Phonetic Association, founded in Paris in 1886, published the first version of its alphabet in 1888. The other tradition was theoretical: the phoneme, worked out by Jan Baudouin de Courtenay in the late nineteenth century and by the Prague School and the American structuralists in the 1920s and 1930s. Acoustic phonetics became possible with the sound spectrograph, developed at Bell Laboratories in the 1940s, which turned speech into a visible picture of frequency against time. *The Sound Pattern of English* (Chomsky and Halle 1968) treated English phonology as a system of ordered rules over features; later theories, such as autosegmental phonology (Goldsmith 1976) and Optimality Theory (Prince and Smolensky 1993), were shaped partly by tone languages and by languages with very different syllable structures.
:::

## Where this leads

Phonology meets morphology in the allomorphs of *-s* and *-ed* and in stress shifts such as *photograph* – *photography*, which [[linguistics/morphology]] takes up. The rules of connected speech — assimilation, elision, weak forms — are phonological processes on a larger scale: see [[connected-speech/elision-assimilation]], [[connected-speech/weak-forms]] and [[connected-speech/linking]]. Sound variation and change, from the *r* of New York to the Great Vowel Shift, are in [[linguistics/variation-change]] and [[history-of-english/great-vowel-shift]]. How infants learn the phonemes of their language, and why a second language's phonemes are harder later, is in [[linguistics/language-acquisition]].

::: summary
- **Phonetics** studies speech sounds physically (articulatory, acoustic, auditory); **phonology** studies how one language organises them into a system.
- Consonants are classified by voicing, place and manner; vowels by height, backness and rounding; acoustically, vowels differ in their **formants** (F1 for height, F2 for backness and rounding), while F0 gives pitch.
- **Voice onset time** distinguishes English /p/ and /b/ and Mandarin *p* and *b* in similar ways; English /p t k/ after /s/ are unaspirated.
- A **phoneme** is a contrastive unit, proved by minimal pairs; **allophones** are its realisations, in complementary distribution or free variation. Phonemic analyses are not always unique (Chao 1934).
- Rules apply to **natural classes** defined by features, and are written *A → B / C ___ D*: aspiration, pre-fortis clipping, dark /l/, GA tapping.
- **Phonotactics** restricts how phonemes combine; syllables have an onset and a rhyme (nucleus + coda). English allows up to three onset and four coda consonants; Mandarin allows no clusters.
- English has lexical **stress** with vowel reduction and phrasal **intonation**; Mandarin has lexical **tone** and an intonation that works around it.
:::

How many phonemes are there in *strengths* /streŋθs/? (It has nine letters.)

Give voicing, place and manner for each consonant: /f/, /ð/, /ŋ/, /dʒ/, /w/.
::: solution
- /f/: voiceless labiodental fricative.
- /ð/: voiced dental fricative.
- /ŋ/: voiced velar nasal.
- /dʒ/: voiced postalveolar (palato-alveolar) affricate.

::: exercise Contrast or allophone? {level=2}
For each pair of sounds, give a minimal pair if they are separate English phonemes, or state their distribution if they are allophones.

1. /θ/ and /s/
2. [l] and [ɫ] (GB)
3. /n/ and /ŋ/
4. [tʰ] and [ɾ] (GA)

1. Separate phonemes: *thin* – *sin*, *thick* – *sick*.
2. Allophones of /l/: clear before a vowel, dark elsewhere.

::: exercise Accidental or systematic gap? {level=2}
For each invented word, say whether it is an accidental gap (possible but not used) or a systematic gap (impossible in English), and why: *plim*, *mlop*, *strag*, *zbek*, *thrimble*.
::: solution
- *plim*: accidental (plosive + approximant onset).
- *mlop*: systematic (nasal + /l/ is not an English onset).
- *strag*: accidental (/s/ + plosive + approximant).
- *zbek*: systematic (/z/ + plosive does not occur in English onsets; only voiceless /s/ begins such clusters).
- *thrimble*: accidental (/θr/ as in *three*; /mbl/ splits between syllables, *thrim.ble*, like *thimble*).
:::

::: exercise Explain an accent feature {level=2}
A Mandarin speaker says that English *spy* sounds to her like *sbai* and asks whether native speakers are saying /b/. Write three or four sentences that explain what is happening, using the terms *aspiration*, *allophone* and *voice onset time*.
::: solution
A model answer: *English /p/ has two main allophones. At the start of a stressed syllable, as in* pie, *it is aspirated, with a long voice onset time; after /s/, as in* spy, *it is unaspirated, with a short voice onset time — physically close to Mandarin* b *and to English /b/ at the start of a word. Native speakers still hear it as /p/, because in English aspiration is predictable and does not distinguish words, so the difference between the two allophones goes unnoticed. In Mandarin, aspiration is contrastive (怕* pà *versus 爸* bà*), so a Mandarin listener hears the unaspirated sound as* b.
:::

::: exercise A phonological rule from data {level=3}
In many varieties of English, the vowel of PRICE has two pronunciations, here written [ʌɪ] and [aɪ]. Study the data and state a rule that predicts which one appears.

*write* [rʌɪt], *ride* [raɪd], *ice* [ʌɪs], *eyes* [aɪz], *life* [lʌɪf], *lives* (plural) [laɪvz], *bike* [bʌɪk], *tie* [taɪ], *tribe* [traɪb].
::: hint
Look at the consonant that follows the vowel in the same syllable, and at what happens when no consonant follows.
:::
::: solution
[ʌɪ] appears before a **voiceless** consonant (*write* /t/, *ice* /s/, *life* /f/, *bike* /k/); [aɪ] appears before a voiced consonant (*ride*, *eyes*, *lives*, *tribe*) and at the end of a word (*tie*). The rule: the PRICE vowel begins with a higher, more central first element before a voiceless consonant. In the notation of this chapter: /aɪ/ → [ʌɪ] / ___ voiceless consonant.

This is the pattern known as **Canadian raising**, found in Canada and in parts of the United States (with the MOUTH vowel too: *house* [hʌʊs], *houses* [ˈhaʊzɪz]). The data here are idealised; in real speech the pattern has exceptions and lexical complications, and it interacts with tapping in words like *writer* and *rider*, where the following consonant is the same tap in both.
:::

::: exercise Tone and stress compared {level=3}
Write a paragraph of 150–200 words comparing the use of pitch in Mandarin and in English for a reader who knows neither linguistics nor Mandarin. Use one Mandarin example and two English examples (one of word stress, one of intonation), and say what this means for a Mandarin speaker learning English.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*In Mandarin, the pitch of each syllable is part of the word. Said with a high level pitch,* tāng *means ‘soup’; with a rising pitch,* táng *means ‘sugar’. Changing the pitch is like changing a vowel in English: you get a different word. This is called lexical tone. English uses pitch in two other ways. First, it helps to mark the stressed syllable of a word, together with length, loudness and a full vowel: the noun* IMport *and the verb* imPORT *differ in where the prominence falls, not in a fixed pitch pattern. Second, pitch spread over a whole phrase — intonation — shows what kind of utterance it is and what the speaker means by it: “She’s coming” with a fall is a statement; with a rise, it can be a surprised question. For a Mandarin speaker learning English, this means learning to let pitch move freely across a phrase rather than fixing it syllable by syllable, and to mark stress by length and vowel quality as well as by pitch, reducing unstressed vowels to /ə/.*

A good answer distinguishes tone from stress and intonation, uses accurate examples and draws a practical conclusion.
:::
