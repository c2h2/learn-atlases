In 1958 Jean Berko showed young children a drawing of a small bird-like creature and said: *This is a wug.* Then she showed two of them: *Now there is another one. There are two of them. There are two …* Most of the children, aged between about four and seven, said *wugs* — with the /z/ form of the plural, the right one after /ɡ/. None of them had ever heard the word, because Berko had invented it. They could not have been repeating a form they had memorised; they were applying a rule. The same children told her that a man who *ricks* every day *ricked* yesterday, and that a dog covered with *quirks* was *quirky*.

That experiment, published as "The child's learning of English morphology" in the journal *Word*, is the clearest demonstration that speakers know the structure of words as a system and not just as a list. This chapter asks what that system contains. The practical side — what morphemes are, how to split *unhappiness* into *un-* + *happy* + *-ness*, the pronunciation of *-s* and *-ed*, and the main prefixes, suffixes and compounds of English — is taught in [[word-formation/morphemes]] and the rest of the *Word Formation* course, and is taken as known here. We go further: what a word is, how inflection differs from derivation in theory, why some affixes make new words freely and others do not, how words are structured inside, and how English and Mandarin sit among the world's languages.

## Words, lexemes and morphemes

The word *word* is used in at least three senses, and morphology needs to keep them apart.

::: definition Word-form, lexeme and morpheme {#def-lexeme}
A **word-form** is a particular form as it appears in a text: *sings*, *sang*, *singing* and *sing* are four word-forms. A **lexeme** is the abstract dictionary word that they are all forms of, written in small capitals in linguistics books and here simply named: the lexeme SING. A **morpheme** is the smallest unit with a meaning or grammatical function ([[word-formation/morphemes#def-morpheme]]); a **morph** is the actual piece of sound or spelling that expresses it, and the different morphs of one morpheme are its **allomorphs** ([[word-formation/morphemes#def-allomorph]]).
:::

With these terms, the familiar distinction can be stated precisely. **Inflection** makes the different word-forms of one lexeme (*sing*, *sings*, *sang*, *sung*, *singing*); **derivation** makes a new lexeme from an existing one (*sing* → *singer*). A learner's dictionary is a list of lexemes; its entry for *sing* gives the irregular word-forms *sang* and *sung*, and *singer* has an entry of its own.

Not every morpheme has its own morph. Three kinds of case show that the relation between meaning and form can be indirect:

- **Zero.** *Sheep* is singular in *one sheep* and plural in *two sheep*, with no change of form. Some analyses posit a "zero" plural morph; others say simply that the lexeme has the same form for both.
- **Suppletion.** *Went* is the past of *go*, *better* the comparative of *good*, *worse* of *bad*: the inflected form is a different root altogether. *Went* was originally the past tense of *wend* ‘go, turn’, and replaced the old past of *go* in late Middle English.
- **Internal change.** *Sang*, *feet*, *mice* express past or plural by changing the vowel of the root (historically, by the processes called ablaut and umlaut).

There is also the opposite case: a piece that seems to be a morpheme but has no meaning of its own. The *cran-* of *cranberry* occurs in no other English word, yet *cranberry* is clearly a kind of berry; such pieces are called **cranberry morphemes**. *Huckle-* in *huckleberry* and *-kempt* in *unkempt* are others. They show that the morpheme is a unit of structure as much as of meaning.

::: quiz
How many **lexemes** are represented by the word-forms *run*, *ran*, *runs*, *runner*, *runners*, *running* (as in *She is running*)?
- [ ] one
- [x] two
- [ ] three
- [ ] six
::: solution
Two: RUN (*run*, *ran*, *runs*, *running*) and RUNNER (*runner*, *runners*), which is derived from RUN by *-er*. *Running* as a verb form is inflection; if it were used as a noun (*Running is good for you*), many analyses would treat it as a further derived lexeme, which is a point on which grammars differ.
:::
:::

## Inflection and derivation in theory

[[word-formation/morphemes#rule-four-tests]] gives four practical tests: derivation may change word class, inflection never does; inflection applies to almost every member of a class; inflection comes outside derivation; inflection is required by the syntax. Morphologists have tried to say what lies behind these tests.

::: theorem What makes an affix inflectional {#rule-inflection}
1. **Inflection is what the syntax needs.** An inflectional category is one that grammatical rules outside the word refer to: agreement (*she work**s***), tense in a finite clause, case on pronouns (*I*/*me*). Derivation is invisible to the syntax except for the word class it produces. (This is the definition proposed by Stephen Anderson in 1982.)
2. **Inflection is paradigmatic.** Inflectional forms come in sets — a **paradigm** — with one cell for each combination of categories, and every lexeme of the class has the whole set, even if some cells are irregular or empty.
3. **Inflection is semantically regular.** The plural adds ‘more than one’ to any noun; *-er* in *teacher*, *printer*, *diner* and *sleeper* (a railway carriage) means rather different things.
4. **The line is a cline, not a wall.** Some affixes have properties of both: *-ly* turning adjectives into adverbs is almost fully productive and regular, but it changes word class and is not required by any agreement rule.
:::

::: proof
Each property explains one of the practical tests. If inflection is what the syntax needs, it must be available for every word that can appear in that syntactic position (hence the range test), it must not change the word class that the syntax has already selected (hence the class test), and it must be at the edge of the word, where the syntax can "see" it (hence the order test). The paradigm explains why gaps in inflection are felt as gaps: speakers who are unsure of the past participle of *stride* (*strode*? *stridden*?) feel that the cell must have a form, whereas nobody feels that the lexeme KIND must have a derived adjective in *-ish*. Property 4 is widely accepted: Bauer, Lieber and Plag (2013) and Huddleston and Pullum (2002) both note borderline cases, and several languages have categories (such as aspect in Russian) that linguists classify differently. English, with only eight inflectional affixes, is a language in which the line is unusually easy to draw.
:::

::: example Three borderline cases {#ex-borderline}
Use the properties of [[#rule-inflection]] to decide how each ending should be classified, and say what makes it difficult.

1. *-ly* in *quickly*, *carefully*, *happily*.
2. *-ing* in *an interesting book* and *-ed* in *a worried look*.
3. *-'s* in *the woman's car* and *the woman next door's car*.
::: solution
1. *-ly* is regular and nearly fully productive with adjectives, like an inflection (property 3). But it changes the word class from adjective to adverb, and no agreement rule requires it — *She ran quick* is non-standard but not a breach of agreement. So most grammars, and learner's dictionaries, treat it as derivation; Huddleston and Pullum (2002) note that it is the most inflection-like of the derivational suffixes.
2. *Interesting* and *worried* began as verb forms (inflection) but here behave as adjectives: they can be modified by *very* (*a very interesting book*), they have comparatives (*more interesting*), and they can follow *seem* (*She seemed worried*), which verbs cannot. A form made by inflection has been converted into a new lexeme, an adjective. The test of class shows which use is which: ✓ *very interesting* (adjective) versus ✗ *She is very interesting her students* (verb).
3. *-'s* looks like an inflection on nouns, but in *the woman next door's car* it attaches to *door*, which is not the owner. It attaches to the end of a whole noun phrase, *the woman next door*. An element that is pronounced as part of a word but attaches to a phrase is called a **clitic**. So the English possessive is better analysed as a clitic than as a case inflection — which is why some lists of English inflections (like the one in [[word-formation/morphemes]]) include it with a note, and others leave it out.
:::
:::

## Productivity and blocking

Some affixes can be added to almost any suitable base, including brand-new words; others are found only in a fixed list of old words.

::: definition Productivity {#def-productivity}
A morphological process is **productive** to the extent that speakers use it to form new words. *-ness* is highly productive: it attaches to almost any adjective, including new ones (*Instagrammable-ness* would be understood at once). *-th* (*warmth*, *width*, *depth*, *truth*) is unproductive: no new nouns are formed with it, and a coinage such as *coolth* is heard as a joke. Productivity is a matter of degree, and it can change over time.
:::

How can productivity be measured? A simple count of how many words contain an affix is misleading: *-ment* appears in many old words (*government*, *movement*) but is now used for new formations much less than *-ness* or *-ization*. A better clue is the number of words with the affix that occur only once in a large corpus — **hapax legomena**, from the Greek for ‘said once’. Rare words are mostly new or occasional formations, so an affix with many hapaxes among its words is one that speakers are still using to make words. Harald Baayen developed measures of this kind in the early 1990s, and corpus studies of English suffixes since then have confirmed what intuition suggests: *-ness* is far more productive than *-ity*, which is far more productive than *-th*.

::: definition Blocking {#def-blocking}
**Blocking** is the failure of a regular formation to be used because a word with the same meaning already exists. *Stealer* is not used for ‘a person who steals’ because *thief* exists; ✗ *gloriosity* is blocked by *glory*, although *curious* → *curiosity* is regular (Mark Aronoff discussed this pair in *Word Formation in Generative Grammar*, 1976); *goed* is blocked in adult English by *went*.
:::

Blocking is not absolute. *Stealer* is possible in compounds and fixed phrases (*a scene-stealer*), and two words can coexist when they develop different meanings: *cooker* is a kitchen appliance in British English, while *cook* is the person. Children are the best evidence for blocking: before they have learned *went*, they say *goed*; once *went* is secure, the regular form disappears ([[linguistics/language-acquisition]]).

::: example Productivity and blocking {#ex-productivity}
For each form, say whether it is an existing word, a possible but unused word, or blocked, and explain.

1. *bakery*, *brewery*, ✗ *teachery*
2. *unhappy*, ? *unsad*
3. *driver*, *swimmer*, *cooker* (‘a person who cooks’)
4. *broadness*, *breadth*
5. *happiness*, *happity*
::: solution
1. *Bakery* and *brewery* are existing words: *-ery* makes nouns for a place where something is done. *Teachery* would be a possible formation but is blocked by *school*, which fills the meaning — and *-ery* is now of limited productivity in any case.
2. *Unhappy* exists. ? *Unsad* is possible in principle but rare, because *un-* tends to attach to adjectives with positive meanings (*unkind*, *unwell*, *unhealthy*), and the opposite of *sad* is usually expressed by *happy*. This is a tendency first described by Otto Jespersen, not an absolute rule: *unselfish* has a negative base.
3. *Driver* and *swimmer* exist. *Cooker* for a person is blocked by *cook* (a case of conversion: the verb *cook* used as a noun); *cooker* survives with another meaning in British English.
4. Both exist. *Breadth* is an old *-th* noun and the normal one for measurement (*length*, *breadth* and *height*); *broadness* is a newer *-ness* formation, used for other senses (*the broadness of his accent*). Blocking is weaker when the meanings can diverge.
5. *Happiness* exists. ✗ *Happity* is impossible: *-ity* attaches mainly to Latinate bases (*curious*, *able*, *real*), not to native words like *happy*. This is a restriction on the base, not blocking.
:::
:::

## The structure of complex words

Morphemes in a word are not just strung in a row. They are built up in layers, as [[word-formation/morphemes#ex-unlockable]] showed for *unlockable*, and each layer has a word class.

::: theorem Heads and layers in English words {#rule-heads}
1. **Words have hierarchical structure.** Each affix attaches to a base of a particular word class and produces a word of a particular class: *-ness* attaches to adjectives and produces nouns; *un-* ‘not’ attaches to adjectives.
2. **The rightmost element is usually the head.** In English compounds and suffixed words, the last element determines the word class and the basic meaning: a *blackbird* is a bird (noun), *sweeten* is a verb because *-en* makes verbs, *kindness* is a noun because of *-ness*. Prefixes usually do not change word class (*kind*/*unkind*, *write*/*rewrite*), though a few do (*en-* in *enlarge*, *be-* in *befriend*). Edwin Williams (1981) called this the **righthand head rule**.
3. **Compounds have a stress pattern of their own.** Noun compounds are usually stressed on the first element (*a ˈblackbird*, *a ˈgreenhouse*), while the phrase has its main stress on the noun (*a black ˈbird*, *a green ˈhouse*). There are many exceptions (*apple ˈpie*, *Oxford ˈStreet* but *ˈOxford Road*), described in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]].
:::

### Two kinds of suffix

English has two groups of derivational suffixes that behave differently in sound and in combination. The first group, mostly from Latin or French, includes *-ity*, *-ic*, *-ion*, *-ous* and *-al*. These often move the stress of the base and change its vowels: *proˈductive* → *producˈtivity*, *ˈatom* → *aˈtomic*, *sane* /seɪn/ → *sanity* /ˈsænəti/. The second group, mostly native, includes *-ness*, *-less*, *-ful*, *-ly* and *-er*: they leave the stress and vowels of the base alone (*proˈductiveness*, *ˈatomless*, *ˈsaneness*).

::: definition Class I and Class II affixes {#def-affix-classes}
**Class I** (or **level 1**) affixes, such as *-ity*, *-ic*, *-ion* and the negative prefix *in-*, are stress-shifting or vowel-changing, attach to bound roots as well as words (*elect-ric*, *nat-ion*), and occur closer to the root. **Class II** (or **level 2**) affixes, such as *-ness*, *-less*, *-ful* and *un-*, are stress-neutral, attach to whole words, and occur further out. The distinction was proposed by Dorothy Siegel (1974) and developed in Paul Kiparsky's **lexical phonology** (1982).
:::

The theory makes a prediction: a Class I affix should not attach outside a Class II affix. *Hopelessness* (II inside II) is fine; ✗ *hopelessity* (*-ity*, Class I, outside *-less*, Class II) is impossible. The prediction is largely right, but there are well-known counterexamples. *Ungrammaticality* seems to need *un-* (Class II) inside *-ity* (Class I), because *-ity* changes the stress of *grammatical* but *un-* attaches to adjectives: the bracketing [ [un- grammatical] -ity ] makes sense of the meaning, and [ un- [grammatical -ity] ] makes sense of the sound. Such cases are called **bracketing paradoxes**, and they are one reason morphologists still debate how much of word structure is fixed by the phonology and how much by the meaning.

::: quiz
Which pair shows a **Class I** suffix, one that moves the stress of its base?
- [ ] *ˈcareful* → *ˈcarefulness*
- [x] *ˈpersonal* → *ˌpersoˈnality*
- [ ] *ˈhope* → *ˈhopeless*
- [ ] *ˈkind* → *ˈkindly*
::: solution
*-ity* is a Class I suffix: it puts the main stress on the syllable before it, so *ˈpersonal* becomes *ˌpersoˈnality*, and the vowel of the *-al* changes from /ə/ to the full vowel /æ/. *-ness*, *-less* and *-ly* are Class II suffixes and leave the stress of the base where it was.
:::
:::

::: example A bracketing paradox {#ex-unhappier}
*Unhappier* means ‘more unhappy’. Show why its structure is a puzzle, using two facts: (a) *un-* attaches to adjectives; (b) the comparative *-er* attaches only to short adjectives (one syllable, or two syllables ending in *-y*, *-ow*, *-le*), so that we say *happier* but *more comfortable*, not ✗ *comfortabler*.
::: solution
1. **The meaning** is ‘more [un-happy]’: someone who is *unhappier* than before is more unhappy, not *not happier* (which would mean ‘no happier than’). So by meaning, the structure should be [ [un- happy] -er ], with *-er* added last.
2. **The form**: *-er* attaches only to short adjectives. *Unhappy* has three syllables, so by fact (b) ✗ *unhappy* + *-er* should be impossible, like ✗ *uncomfortabler*. By sound, the structure should be [ un- [happy -er] ], with *-er* added to the two-syllable *happy* first.
3. **The paradox**: the meaning requires one bracketing and the form requires the other. Neither structure satisfies both.
4. **Proposed solutions** differ. Some linguists allow the structure to be different at different levels — one bracketing for meaning, one for sound. Others note that *-er* can attach to longer adjectives that begin with *un-* (*untidier*, *unluckier*) because the restriction really concerns the base *happy*, *tidy*, *lucky*, and so restate fact (b). The example shows that word structure, like sentence structure, can be studied by testing hypotheses against data.
:::
:::

## How words are made: a summary

The processes of English word formation are each the subject of a lesson in *Word Formation*. In theoretical terms they divide into those that add material, those that change nothing but the word class, and those that shorten.

| process | example | adds material? | lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| **affixation** (derivation) | *kind* → *kindness*, *rewrite* | yes | [[word-formation/prefixes]], [[word-formation/suffixes]] |
| **compounding** | *black* + *bird* → *blackbird* | yes (a second root) | [[word-formation/compounds]] |
| **conversion** | *email* (noun) → *to email* (verb) | no | [[word-formation/conversion]] |
| **clipping** | *laboratory* → *lab* | no (shortens) | [[word-formation/shortening]] |
| **blending** | *breakfast* + *lunch* → *brunch* | no (merges) | [[word-formation/shortening]] |
| **acronyms and initialisms** | *NATO* (said as a word), *BBC* (letter by letter) | no (shortens) | [[word-formation/shortening]] |
| **back-formation** | *editor* → *edit*, *television* → *televise* | removes an apparent affix | [[word-formation/suffixes]] |
| **borrowing** | *tofu*, *kowtow* (from 叩头), *lychee* | yes (from another language) | the course *History of English* |

Back-formation is a good example of morphology driving change: *editor* (from Latin) looked like *edit* + *-or*, so speakers created the verb *edit*, which did not exist before. The same happened with *burgle* from *burglar* and *babysit* from *babysitter*.

::: quiz
Which word was formed by **back-formation**?
- [ ] *unhappy*
- [x] *donate*, from *donation*
- [ ] *brunch*
- [ ] *lab*
::: solution
*Donation* is the older word in English; *donate* was formed from it in the late eighteenth century, in the United States, by removing what looked like the suffix *-ion*. *Unhappy* is prefixation, *brunch* a blend, *lab* a clipping.
:::
:::

## Languages compared: morphological types

Languages differ greatly in how much work they do inside words. Nineteenth-century linguists such as August Schleicher sorted languages into types, and the classification is still used, though now as a set of tendencies rather than boxes.

::: definition Morphological types {#def-typology}
- An **isolating** (or **analytic**) language has few morphemes per word, and expresses grammatical meaning mainly with separate words and word order. Vietnamese and Mandarin are standard examples.
- An **agglutinating** language builds words from many morphemes, each with one clear meaning and an easily separated form. Turkish *ev-ler-im-de* ‘house-PLURAL-MY-IN’ = ‘in my houses’.
- A **fusional** (or **inflecting**) language has affixes that express several meanings at once in a single form. Latin *amō* ‘I love’: the *-ō* means first person, singular, present, indicative and active, all together.
- A **polysynthetic** language can combine many roots and affixes into one long word that corresponds to a whole sentence in English; many languages of North America, such as the Inuit languages and Mohawk, are described in this way.

Joseph Greenberg (1960) proposed measuring these tendencies with indices, such as the average number of morphemes per word in a sample of text (the **index of synthesis**).
:::

English is a mixture. It has become much more analytic since Old English, which had case endings on nouns, adjectives and articles (see the course *History of English*): today it uses word order where Old English used case (*The dog bit the man* versus *The man bit the dog*), auxiliaries where other languages use inflection (*will go*, *has gone*), and prepositions where Latin used case (*of the king*). But it is fusional in its remaining inflections: the *-s* of *works* means third person, singular and present at once. And its derivational morphology, borrowed largely from Latin and French, gives long words such as *denationalisation* that an isolating language would express as a phrase.

::: example Classifying by type {#ex-typology}
Say what each example shows about the morphological type of its language.

1. Mandarin 我明天去北京 ‘I go to Beijing tomorrow’; 我昨天去北京了 ‘I went to Beijing yesterday’.
2. Turkish *ev* ‘house’, *evler* ‘houses’, *evlerim* ‘my houses’, *evlerimde* ‘in my houses’.
3. English *took*, *mice*, *went*.
4. English *will have been working*.
::: solution
1. **Isolating.** The verb 去 does not change. Time is expressed by separate words (明天, 昨天), and completion by the separate particle 了, whose position after the verb or at the end of the sentence is decided by syntax. Each word here is one morpheme except 北京 (北 + 京, two morphemes in one compound).
2. **Agglutinating.** Each suffix adds one meaning (*-ler* plural, *-im* ‘my’, *-de* ‘in’), and each can be cut off cleanly.
3. **Fusional** at its extreme: a single form expresses root and past or plural together, with no separable affix (internal change in *took*, *mice*; suppletion in *went*).
4. **Analytic**: four separate words express future, perfect, progressive aspect and the verb. Latin or Turkish would put most of this into one word.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
It is sometimes said that Chinese "has no morphology". That is true only of inflection. Mandarin word formation is rich, and most of it is compounding. 朱德熙, in 《语法讲义》 (1982), classified compounds by the grammatical relation between their parts, the same relations found in phrases: **联合** (coordinate, 道路 ‘road + way’), **偏正** (modifier–head, 白菜 ‘white + vegetable’, 火车 ‘fire + vehicle’), **述宾** (verb–object, 司机 ‘control + machine’ = ‘driver’, 动员 ‘move + personnel’), **述补** (verb–complement, 提高 ‘lift + high’ = ‘raise’, 说明 ‘say + clear’ = ‘explain’) and **主谓** (subject–predicate, 地震 ‘earth + shake’, 心疼 ‘heart + ache’). Most English compounds, by contrast, are modifier–head (*blackbird*, *bookshop*), with the head on the right — the same order as 偏正 compounds.

Mandarin also has **reduplication** (看看 ‘have a look’, 高高兴兴 ‘very happily’), a few affixes (老- in 老师, 第- in 第三, -子 in 桌子, -们 for human plurals), and a growing set of **affix-like morphemes** (类词缀) such as -化 ‘-isation’ (现代化), -性 ‘-ity, -ness’ (可能性), -者 ‘-er’ (读者) and 非- ‘non-’ (非正式), several of which became productive partly through translation from European languages. Jerome Packard's *The Morphology of Chinese* (2000) is a linguistic study of these patterns.

The word itself is harder to define in Mandarin than in English. Writing has no spaces, and some verb–object compounds can be split by other material: 睡觉 ‘sleep’ becomes 睡了一个好觉 ‘slept a good sleep’. These **separable words** (离合词) behave like words in some ways and like phrases in others — a reminder that "word" is a theoretical notion, to be defined by criteria, even in English (is *ice cream* one word or two?).
:::

## Morphology in the mind

Berko's experiment showed that children apply rules to new words. What happens with irregular forms? Two families of theory have competed since the 1980s, mostly over the English past tense.

::: theorem Two accounts of regular and irregular forms {#rule-dual-route}
1. **Dual-route** accounts (Steven Pinker, *Words and Rules*, 1999) hold that regular forms such as *walked* are built by a rule (‘add *-ed*’) while irregular forms such as *went* and *sang* are stored in memory, and that a stored form blocks the rule.
2. **Single-mechanism** (connectionist) accounts, beginning with Rumelhart and McClelland's model of 1986, hold that both regular and irregular forms are produced by one associative network trained on the forms a speaker has heard; regularity is the effect of many similar forms, not of a rule.
3. Both accounts agree on the data to be explained: the productivity of *-ed* with new verbs (*She googled it*); the existence of small irregular families (*sing–sang*, *ring–rang*) that occasionally attract new members (*bring–brang* in some speakers and children); and the fact that frequent irregulars survive while rare ones become regular over time (*help–holp* became *helped*).
:::

::: proof
Evidence has been offered for both. Supporters of the dual route point to the fact that regular inflection applies even to words that sound unlike any existing verb (*He out-Gorbachev'd Gorbachev*), and to some patient studies in which damage affects regular and irregular forms differently. Supporters of single-mechanism models point out that networks also generalise to unusual words, and that regularity in some languages is a matter of degree. Studies of language change agree with both on one point: Lieberman and colleagues (2007), counting the irregular verbs of Old English that have become regular, found that the rate of regularisation is strongly related to frequency — rare irregulars regularised much faster than frequent ones. The debate is not settled, and many current theories combine stored forms with generalisation over stored forms.
:::

::: warning Morphology is not etymology
To analyse a word's morphology today, ask what speakers can recognise in it now, not where it came from. *Disaster* comes from Latin *dis-* + *astrum* ‘star’ (‘ill-starred’), but for a present-day speaker it is one morpheme. ✗ *Disaster has two morphemes because it means ‘bad star’.* ✓ *Disaster is one morpheme today; historically it was formed from* dis- *and* astrum. History can help a learner remember a word ([[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]), but it is diachronic knowledge, not part of the synchronic analysis ([[linguistics/what-linguistics-studies#def-synchronic]]).
:::

::: history
Morphological analysis is older than any other part of linguistics. The Sanskrit grammar of Pāṇini (usually dated to around the fourth century BCE) derives the words of the language from roots and affixes by nearly four thousand compact rules. European grammar until the nineteenth century worked instead with whole-word paradigms, learned by heart — *amo, amas, amat*. The structuralists of the twentieth century made the morpheme the basic unit: Bloomfield's *Language* (1933) defined it, and American linguists in the 1940s and 1950s developed procedures for finding morphemes in unfamiliar languages from field data. Generative grammar at first left morphology to the syntax and phonology; Chomsky's "Remarks on nominalization" (1970) and Aronoff's *Word Formation in Generative Grammar* (1976) argued that words have their own structure and their own rules. Jean Berko's wug test (1958) became one of the best-known experiments in the study of language, and it is still used, in many languages, to test children's knowledge of morphology.
:::

## Where this leads

The practical lessons on English word formation are in *Word Formation*: [[word-formation/prefixes]], [[word-formation/suffixes]], [[word-formation/compounds]], [[word-formation/conversion]], [[word-formation/shortening]] and [[word-formation/greek-latin-roots]]. The interaction of morphology and stress is in [[connected-speech/word-stress-patterns]]. Children's learning of inflection — the wug test, overregularisation and the order in which English morphemes appear — returns in [[linguistics/language-acquisition]]. The next chapter, [[linguistics/phrases-constituents]], applies the idea of hierarchical structure to sentences.

::: summary
- A **word-form** is a form in a text, a **lexeme** an abstract dictionary word, a **morpheme** the smallest unit of meaning or function, realised by **morphs** and **allomorphs**; forms can involve zero, suppletion, internal change and cranberry morphemes.
- **Inflection** makes the forms of one lexeme for the syntax, in **paradigms**; **derivation** makes new lexemes. The line is a cline.
- **Productivity** is the degree to which a process makes new words (*-ness* high, *-th* none); **blocking** stops a regular formation when a word with its meaning exists (*thief*, *went*).
- Words have **hierarchical structure**; in English the rightmost element is usually the **head**. **Class I** affixes shift stress and sit inside **Class II** affixes, with **bracketing paradoxes** as exceptions.
- Languages range from **isolating** (Mandarin) to **agglutinating** (Turkish), **fusional** (Latin) and **polysynthetic**; English mixes analytic syntax with fusional inflection and Latinate derivation.
- Mandarin has little inflection but rich **compounding** (联合, 偏正, 述宾, 述补, 主谓), reduplication and affix-like morphemes; the word is hard to define in both languages.
- **Dual-route** and **single-mechanism** theories compete to explain regular and irregular forms; frequency protects irregulars.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Word-forms and lexemes {level=1 check="3"}
How many lexemes are represented by: *happy*, *happier*, *happiest*, *happiness*, *unhappy*, *unhappier*?
::: solution
**3**: HAPPY (*happy*, *happier*, *happiest*), HAPPINESS and UNHAPPY (*unhappy*, *unhappier*). The comparative and superlative are inflection; *-ness* and *un-* derive new lexemes.
:::

::: exercise Zero, suppletion or internal change? {level=1}
Say how each form expresses its grammatical meaning: *geese*, *worse*, *deer* (plural), *was*, *drove*, *fish* (plural).
::: solution
- *geese*: internal change (vowel).
- *worse*: suppletion (comparative of *bad*).
- *deer* (plural): zero.
- *was*: suppletion (past of *be*, a different root from *be* and *is*).
- *drove*: internal change.
- *fish* (plural): zero (*fishes* is also used, especially for different kinds of fish).
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the process {level=1}
Name the word-formation process: *smog*, *to text* (from the noun), *flu*, *to self-isolate*, *UN*, *to housekeep*.
::: solution
*smog*: blend (*smoke* + *fog*). *to text*: conversion. *flu*: clipping (*influenza*). *self-isolate*: compounding (or prefixation with the combining form *self-*). *UN*: initialism. *to housekeep*: back-formation from *housekeeper*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Draw the layers {level=2}
Show the layers of *unreadability* and *rethinkable* in the order the affixes are added, giving the word class at each stage, as in the figure for *denationalisation*. Is either one a bracketing paradox?
::: solution
- *unreadability*: *read* (V) → *readable* (A, *-able*) → *unreadable* (A, *un-*) → *unreadability* (N, *-ity*). The class is clear at every step, but *-ity* (Class I) is added outside *un-* (Class II), as in *ungrammaticality*, so in the Class I/Class II theory it is a bracketing paradox: the meaning needs [ [un- [read -able] ] -ity ], while level ordering would require *-ity* inside *un-*.
- *rethinkable*: *think* (V) → *rethink* (V, *re-*) → *rethinkable* (A, *-able*). *Re-* attaches to verbs and *-able* to verbs, so no paradox; the other order, ✗ *re-* + *thinkable*, would fail because *re-* does not attach to adjectives.
:::
:::

::: exercise Productive or not? {level=2}
Rank these suffixes from most to least productive in present-day English and give one piece of evidence for each: *-th* (*warmth*), *-ness*, *-ity*, *-ise/-ize*.
::: solution
Roughly: *-ness* (attaches to almost any adjective, including new ones), *-ise/-ize* (forms many new verbs, *prioritise*, *weaponise*, *monetise*), *-ity* (still forms new words, but mainly on Latinate bases in *-able*, *-al*, *-ic*: *walkability*), *-th* (no new formations; *coolth* is jocular). The ranking of *-ness* and *-ize* could differ by register and corpus; the clearest evidence would be the proportion of hapax legomena among words with each suffix in a large recent corpus.
:::
:::

::: exercise Chinese compounds {level=2}
Classify each Mandarin compound using 朱德熙's types (联合, 偏正, 述宾, 述补, 主谓) and give its nearest English equivalent: 毕业, 黑板, 改善, 买卖, 头疼, 打破.
::: solution
- 毕业 ‘finish + course of study’: 述宾 — *graduate*.
- 黑板 ‘black + board’: 偏正 — *blackboard* (also modifier–head in English).
- 改善 ‘change + good’: 述补 — *improve*.
- 买卖 ‘buy + sell’: 联合 — *business, trade*.
- 头疼 ‘head + ache’: 主谓 — *headache* (in English a modifier–head compound: a kind of ache).
- 打破 ‘hit + broken’: 述补 — *break, smash*.

Note that 毕业 is also a separable word: 毕了业.
:::

::: exercise A wug test of your own {level=3}
Design four test items, in the style of Berko (1958), to find out whether a child (or an adult learner) knows (a) the /ɪz/ allomorph of the plural, (b) the past tense *-ed*, (c) the derivational suffix *-er* for agents and (d) the possessive *-'s*. Write each item as the tester would say it, and give the expected answer with its pronunciation.
::: hint
Invent words that obey English phonotactics, and choose their final sounds so that the right allomorph is predictable.
:::
::: solution
Model items (any equivalent set is acceptable):

(a) *This is a tass. Now there are two of them. There are two …* — *tasses* /ˈtæsɪz/. The base ends in /s/, so /ɪz/ is required. (Berko found children much less reliable on this form, many repeating *tass*.)

(b) *This man knows how to spow. He is spowing. He did the same thing yesterday. What did he do yesterday? Yesterday he …* — *spowed* /spəʊd/ (GB), /spoʊd/ (GA), with /d/ after a vowel.

(c) *This woman zibs every day. She's a very good …* — *zibber* /ˈzɪbə/ (GB), /ˈzɪbər/ (GA).

(d) *This is a niz who owns a hat. Whose hat is it? It is the …* — *niz's hat* /ˈnɪzɪz/.

Each item uses a nonsense base so that the answer cannot be remembered, and a sentence frame that makes the required form natural.
:::
:::

::: exercise Is it one word? {level=3}
Write 150–200 words discussing whether English *ice cream* and Mandarin 睡觉 are single words. Use at least three criteria (for example spelling, stress, separability, meaning, whether parts can be modified).
::: solution
A model answer:

*Neither case is decided by one criterion. English* ice cream *is written as two words, but spelling is a convention: we write* bookshop *as one word and* book club *as two, though their structure is the same. In stress,* ice cream *is variable — many speakers stress* ice*, as in a compound, and some stress* cream*. Its parts cannot be modified separately: ✗* icy cream *and ✗* ice delicious cream *do not mean ‘ice cream’, and its meaning is more specific than ‘cream with ice’. By these criteria it behaves as one word, a compound noun. Mandarin* 睡觉 *passes some tests for a word: its meaning ‘sleep’ is not simply ‘sleep + feel’, and it is listed in dictionaries. But it can be separated:* 睡了一个好觉 *puts an aspect marker and a modified object between its parts, as a verb–object phrase would. Linguists therefore call such forms separable words (*离合词*): they are words in the lexicon and phrases in the syntax. The comparison shows that "word" must be defined by explicit criteria, which may disagree.*
:::
