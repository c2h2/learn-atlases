Here are two strings of English words, made famous by Noam Chomsky in *Syntactic Structures* (1957):

> *Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.*
> *Furiously sleep ideas green colorless.*

Neither means anything sensible. Ideas have no colour, nothing can be both colourless and green, and ideas do not sleep, furiously or otherwise. Yet every speaker of English feels at once that the two are different. The first sounds like an English sentence: it has the rhythm of one, it could be read aloud with a natural intonation, and if you replace the words one by one with sensible ones — *Young American students work hard* — you get an ordinary sentence with the same shape. The second is a heap of words. Chomsky's point was that speakers know something about the *form* of sentences that is independent of their meaning and of whether they have ever heard them before. Neither string had appeared in print before 1957, so speakers could not be recognising them from experience.

What is that knowledge, and how could we find out what it contains? That is the central question of linguistics. Speakers of a language know thousands of things they cannot put into words: that *blick* could be an English word but *bnick* could not; that *the cats* has a different ending from *the dogs* when you say it aloud; that *She said that she would come* can be about two different women but *She said that Mary would come* cannot be about Mary twice. This chapter sets out how linguists study that knowledge: what they mean by a rule, what the levels of analysis are, which properties make human language unusual, and what counts as evidence. The rest of the course applies these ideas to each level of English in turn.

## Description and prescription

Most people meet "rules of English" first at school, as instructions: do not end a sentence with a preposition, do not begin one with *and*, say *fewer books* and not *less books*. Linguists use the word *rule* in a different sense, and the difference is the first thing to understand about the subject.

::: definition Descriptive and prescriptive {#def-descriptive}
A **descriptive** statement about a language says what its speakers actually do: *Most speakers of English put the adjective before the noun* (*a red car*), or *In informal speech, many speakers use* less *with plural nouns*. It is true or false, and it is tested against evidence.

A **prescriptive** statement says what speakers *should* do, according to some authority or standard: *Use* fewer, *not* less, *with plural nouns*. It is a recommendation, and it can be judged as useful, unhelpful or arbitrary, but not as true or false about the language.

Linguistics is descriptive. A **rule** in linguistics is a generalisation about what speakers do — usually without being aware of it — and a grammar is a hypothesis about the knowledge behind their behaviour.
:::

A descriptive rule can be stated with great precision. Here is one that no school teaches, because every native speaker already follows it: when several adjectives come before a noun, opinion comes before size, size before age, age before colour, colour before origin and origin before material. So *a lovely little old red Italian leather bag* sounds natural, and ✗ *a leather Italian red old little lovely bag* does not. Speakers follow this order with high reliability, although almost none of them could state it.

Description does not mean that "anything goes". Speakers of every variety follow rules, and a description records them all, including the social facts: that *I ain't seen nothing* is normal in many spoken varieties and is not used in formal writing; that examiners expect *fewer* with plural nouns in an essay; that *between you and I* is used by many educated speakers and condemned by many style guides. Those facts about expectations are themselves part of what linguists describe.

::: theorem Linguistic rules describe; style rules prescribe {#rule-describe}
1. A linguistic rule states a regularity in what speakers of a variety produce and accept, together with its scope (which speakers, which registers, which contexts).
2. A prescriptive rule states a norm that some institution or tradition recommends. It may describe the usage of one register (*fewer* with plural nouns is the norm of formal written English) or it may describe nothing at all (no variety of English has ever avoided splitting infinitives).
3. When the two conflict, the linguist does not say that speakers are "wrong" about their own language; the linguist records the norm, records the usage, and asks why they differ.
:::

::: proof
The reason is the purpose of the enquiry. A physicist who found that stones did not fall as the textbook said would revise the textbook, not blame the stones; a linguist who finds that speakers of standard English say *Who did you meet?* rather than *Whom did you meet?* in conversation revises the description. The comparison is imperfect, because the norms of a language are real social facts — an applicant who writes *less applicants* in a formal letter may be judged for it — which is why rule 3 asks the linguist to record the norm as well. Huddleston and Pullum (2002) make exactly this distinction when they describe standard English, the variety of formal writing and of most public speech, as the object of their grammar, while treating non-standard varieties as systematic in their own right and setting aside the prescriptions that no variety follows. The zombie rules mentioned in the atlas's content guide — never split an infinitive, never end a sentence with a preposition, never begin with *And* — belong to the second kind of rule 2: careful writers of every century have broken them.
:::

::: example Testing a prescriptive rule {#ex-preposition}
A style guide says: "Never end a sentence with a preposition." Is this a rule of English? Set out how a linguist would answer.
::: solution
1. **Restate the claim descriptively.** The claim would be: in English, a preposition does not appear at the end of a clause without its object (*Who did you talk to?*), and speakers instead put the preposition before the question word (*To whom did you talk?*).
2. **Check usage.** Ordinary speech and writing are full of stranded prepositions: *Who did you talk to?*, *That's the shop I was telling you about*, *It's worth looking into*. Some sentences cannot be rephrased without a stranded preposition at all: *She was looked after by her aunt* has no natural alternative with *after* elsewhere, and *What are you looking at?* becomes stiff as *At what are you looking?*
3. **Check judgments.** Speakers accept both orders in formal writing, prefer the stranded order in speech, and find the fronted order odd with some verbs: ✓ *the problem we came across*, but ? *the problem across which we came*.
4. **Find the source.** The idea is usually traced to John Dryden, who in 1672 criticised Ben Jonson for a sentence ending in a preposition, and to eighteenth-century grammars that took Latin, where a preposition cannot be separated from its noun, as a model. It is a style preference transferred from another language.
5. **Conclusion.** As a descriptive rule the claim is false. What is true is narrower: in formal registers, with a relative pronoun, writers often choose the fronted order (*the committee to which the report was sent*), and the choice is available. [[complex-sentences/relative-clauses]] gives the practical advice.
:::
:::

::: warning "Ungrammatical" does not mean "non-standard"
Learners sometimes call any form their teacher dislikes "ungrammatical". In linguistics a form is **ungrammatical** for a variety when speakers of that variety would not produce it and reject it when they hear it. *She don't know* is ungrammatical in standard English but grammatical in many dialects of English, where it follows the dialect's own rule for the verb *do*. ✗ *Don't know she* is ungrammatical in every variety. So say which variety you mean: ✓ *"She don't know" is non-standard*; ✓ *"She don't know" is not used in formal writing*; ✗ *"She don't know" is bad English.*
:::

## Competence, performance and judgments

People do not always say what their grammar allows. They start a sentence, change their mind and finish it differently; they lose track of a long subject and make the verb agree with the nearest noun (*The quality of the photos are good*); they produce sentences that are perfectly grammatical but too complicated to understand. A theory of what speakers know must separate the knowledge from the use.

::: definition Competence and performance {#def-competence}
**Competence** is a speaker's unconscious knowledge of the system of their language. **Performance** is the actual use of that knowledge in speaking and understanding, which is also affected by memory, attention, tiredness, distraction and the situation. The terms are Chomsky's (*Aspects of the Theory of Syntax*, 1965).

Ferdinand de Saussure, in the *Course in General Linguistics* (1916), drew a related line between **langue**, the language as a shared social system, and **parole**, individual acts of speaking. The two pairs are not the same: *langue* is social, *competence* is individual and mental.
:::

Because competence cannot be observed directly, linguists infer it, and one of their main tools is the **grammaticality judgment**: asking speakers whether a sentence is possible in their language. Judgments are graded. Some sentences are clearly out; some are slightly odd; some are grammatical but strange in meaning.

::: definition Grammaticality and acceptability {#def-acceptability}
A sentence is **grammatical** if it conforms to the rules of the grammar of the variety. It is **acceptable** if speakers judge it natural and usable. The two usually go together, but not always:

- grammatical but hardly acceptable: *The rat the cat the dog chased killed ate the malt* (each clause is built correctly, but two clauses are buried in the middle of others, and memory gives up);
- grammatical but meaningless: *Colorless green ideas sleep furiously*;
- grammatical but misleading at first: *The horse raced past the barn fell* (a "garden-path" sentence, discussed by Thomas Bever in 1970: *raced past the barn* is a reduced relative clause, ‘the horse that was raced past the barn’, but the reader first takes *raced* as the main verb).
:::

Linguists have a set of marks for judgments, which you will meet in every book on syntax. **They put an asterisk — the star symbol — before a sentence that is ungrammatical**, a question mark before one that is doubtful, two question marks before one that is very doubtful, and the hash sign (#) before one that is grammatical but odd in meaning or in context. Historical linguists use the same star for a different purpose: before a word that is not recorded but **reconstructed**, such as the ancestor of *father* in Proto-Indo-European. On this site the star cannot be used, because it starts italics, so we write ✗ for ungrammatical, ? for doubtful and ✓ for acceptable, and we say in words when a form is reconstructed. When you read linguistics books, translate the star into ✗ as you go.

::: example Four kinds of oddness {#ex-judgments}
Say what is wrong, if anything, with each sentence for a speaker of standard English, and mark it ✗, ? or ✓.

1. *Ate the cake the children.*
2. *The doctor married her own mother's twin brother.*
3. *My brother is an only child.*
4. *The editor who the journalist who the lawyer advised hired resigned.*
5. *What did you buy the book and?*
::: solution
1. ✗ Ungrammatical: English declarative clauses put the subject before the verb (*The children ate the cake*). The words are fine; the order is not.
2. ✓ Grammatical and meaningful, though socially unusual and in many places illegal. Strangeness of content is not a matter of grammar.
3. Grammatical but contradictory: *brother* implies that someone has a sibling, *only child* says that he has none. A linguist would mark it with the hash sign; on this site, ✓ for grammar, with the note that it cannot be true.
4. ? Grammatical by the rules (each relative clause is correctly built), but hardly acceptable, because two relative clauses are centre-embedded. Unpacked, it says: the lawyer advised a journalist, the journalist hired an editor, and the editor resigned.
5. ✗ Ungrammatical: a question word cannot be taken out of one half of a coordination (*You bought the book and a pen* → ✗ *What did you buy the book and?*). This constraint is discussed in [[linguistics/clauses-movement]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which statement is **descriptive**?
- [ ] *You should say* It is I, *not* It is me.
- [x] *In conversation, most speakers of standard English say* It's me *rather than* It is I.
- [ ] It is I *is the correct form, because* be *takes the subject case.*
- [ ] *Good writers avoid* It's me.
::: solution
The second statement says what speakers do and can be checked against a corpus of conversation. The first and fourth recommend; the third presents a recommendation as a fact, with a reason taken from Latin grammar, where the verb 'be' has the same case on both sides. In English the object form *me* after *be* has been normal for centuries.
:::
:::

## The levels of linguistic analysis

Language is organised in layers. Sounds combine into syllables and morphemes, morphemes into words, words into phrases and clauses, clauses into texts and conversations, and at each layer meaning is added. Linguistics divides the work accordingly.

::: definition The levels of analysis {#def-levels}
| level | studies | an English example | a Mandarin example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **phonetics** | how speech sounds are made, transmitted and heard | the puff of air after /p/ in *pin* | the pitch movement of the third tone |
| **phonology** | how a language organises sounds into a system | /p/ and /b/ distinguish *pin* and *bin* | tone distinguishes 妈 *mā* and 马 *mǎ* |
| **morphology** | the structure of words | *un-* + *kind* + *-ness* | 电 + 脑 → 电脑 |
| **syntax** | how words combine into phrases and sentences | *the cat that I saw* (relative clause after the noun) | 我看见的猫 (relative clause before the noun) |
| **semantics** | meaning in words and sentences | *All dogs bark* entails *Some dogs bark* (if there are dogs) | 三本书 'three books', with the classifier 本 |
| **pragmatics** | meaning in context | *Can you pass the salt?* as a request | 你吃了吗? as a greeting |
| **discourse** | the structure of texts and conversations | *however* linking two paragraphs | a topic carried through a sentence with no repeated subject |
:::

The levels are not sealed off from each other. The plural ending of *cats* /s/ and *dogs* /z/ is a fact of morphology realised by phonology; the stress of *record* (noun, Oo) and *record* (verb, oO) is phonology serving morphology and syntax; the meaning of *some* depends both on semantics (‘at least one’) and on pragmatics (‘not all’, in most contexts). The places where two levels meet are called **interfaces**, and much current research is about them.

Around these core levels are fields that study language in relation to something else. The figure arranges them.

::: example Which level? {#ex-levels}
Each of these misunderstandings or errors arises at one level of analysis. Name the level and explain.

1. A listener hears *I scream* when the speaker said *ice cream*.
2. A learner writes ✗ *two informations*.
3. A learner writes ✗ *I very like it.*
4. A visitor is asked *Would you like to come in for a coffee?* and says *No, thank you, I don't drink coffee*, missing that the invitation was to come in.
5. A reader takes *Visiting relatives can be boring* to mean that it is boring to visit them, when the writer meant that relatives who visit are boring.
::: solution
1. **Phonetics/phonology**: the two phrases have the same phonemes, /aɪskriːm/; they differ only in small timing and aspiration details at the word boundary, which the listener missed. [[connected-speech/linking]] has more examples.
2. **Morphology** (with the semantics of countability): *information* is an uncountable noun and has no plural inflection in standard English, though some other varieties use *informations*.
3. **Syntax**: *very* modifies adjectives and adverbs, not verbs; the verb needs *really* or *very much* in a different position: ✓ *I really like it*, ✓ *I like it very much*. The error reflects 我很喜欢, where 很 goes before the verb.
4. **Pragmatics**: the literal meaning was understood; the intended meaning — an invitation, with coffee as the occasion — was not.
5. **Syntax**: *visiting relatives* has two structures — *visiting* as a verb with *relatives* as its object, or *visiting* as a modifier of *relatives*. This is a **structural ambiguity**, the subject of [[linguistics/phrases-constituents]].
:::
:::

::: quiz
At which level do the English words *bit* and *beat* differ?

- [x] in phonology: they are a minimal pair for the vowels /ɪ/ and /iː/
- [ ] only in spelling
- [ ] in pragmatics
::: solution
*Bit* /bɪt/ and *beat* /biːt/ differ in one phoneme, so the difference is phonological. They also differ in spelling, but spelling is not a level of the spoken language; *beet* and *beat* differ in spelling and not at all in sound. [[sounds-of-english/short-vowels]] practises the pair.

## What makes human language special

All animals communicate, but human language has a combination of properties that no other known system has. The American linguist Charles Hockett listed them as "design features" in a 1960 article in *Scientific American*; four of them matter most for this course.

::: theorem Four properties of human language {#rule-design-features}
1. **Arbitrariness.** The link between the sound of a word and its meaning is, in most words, a convention: nothing about the sounds of *tree* makes them suit a tree, and French *arbre*, German *Baum* and Mandarin 树 *shù* do the job equally well.
2. **Duality of patterning.** Language has two levels of structure: a small set of meaningless units (about 44 phonemes in GB English) combines into thousands of meaningful units (morphemes and words), which combine again into sentences.
3. **Productivity and recursion.** Speakers can make and understand sentences they have never met, and structures can be put inside structures of the same kind without limit: *the friend of the sister of the teacher of …*; *She said that he thought that they knew that …*
4. **Displacement.** Language can be about things that are absent, past, future, hypothetical or imaginary.
:::

::: quiz
A bee's dance tells other bees the direction and distance of food it has found. Which property of human language does it share?
- [x] displacement, in a limited way: it is about food that is not present
- [ ] duality of patterning
- [ ] unlimited recursion
- [ ] productivity in every domain
::: solution
Karl von Frisch's studies of the honeybee showed that the dance communicates the direction and distance of a food source the other bees cannot see — a limited form of displacement. But the dance has no small set of meaningless units recombined into meaningful ones, it cannot embed one message in another, and it can be about food sources (and nest sites), not about anything at all.
:::
:::

::: proof
Arbitrariness was made central by Saussure (1916), who described the linguistic sign as the union of a **signifier** (the sound pattern) and a **signified** (the concept), linked by convention. It has limits. **Onomatopoeic** words imitate sounds, but even these are shaped by each language: English cocks say *cock-a-doodle-doo*, Chinese ones 喔喔喔, French ones *cocorico*. There are also weaker, statistical links between sound and meaning, called **sound symbolism**: many English words for light begin with *gl-* (*gleam*, *glow*, *glitter*, *glisten*), and in experiments going back to Wolfgang Köhler's *Gestalt Psychology* (1929), speakers of many languages match a rounded shape with a made-up word like *maluma* and a spiky one with *takete* (Köhler's first version of the rounded word was *baluma*). None of this overturns the principle: most of the vocabulary of any language could have been otherwise. Duality explains how a small set of sounds can make an open set of words; recursion explains how a finite grammar can make an unlimited set of sentences. Whether recursion is unique to humans, and whether all languages use it, is debated: Hauser, Chomsky and Fitch (2002) proposed that it is the core of the human language faculty, and Daniel Everett (2005) argued that the Amazonian language Pirahã lacks embedding — a claim others have disputed. The phonemic figure of about 44 is for GB in the analysis used in this atlas (24 consonants and 20 vowels, [[sounds-of-english/phonemic-alphabet]]); analyses differ slightly.
:::

## What counts as evidence

A description of a language is a set of claims, and claims need evidence. Linguists use several kinds, and they are strongest in combination.

::: theorem Kinds of evidence in linguistics {#rule-evidence}
1. **Intuitions and judgments** of native or expert speakers: quick and sensitive to rare structures, but subjective. Best collected from several speakers, with controlled sentences, and checked by other methods.
2. **Corpora**: large collections of real texts and transcribed speech, searched by computer ([[linguistics/corpus-linguistics]]). They show what is frequent and in which registers; they cannot show directly that something is impossible, since a missing form may simply be rare.
3. **Experiments**: reaction times, eye movements while reading, brain responses, rating studies with many participants. They show how language is processed.
4. **Acquisition data**: what children say at each age, including their errors (*goed*, *mouses*), which reveal the rules they are building ([[linguistics/language-acquisition]]).
5. **Fieldwork and sociolinguistic interviews**: recordings of how people in a community actually speak ([[linguistics/variation-change]]).
6. **Historical records**: written texts from earlier centuries, rhymes, spellings and comparisons between related languages.
7. **Errors and slips of the tongue**: *a pack of lies* said as *a lack of pies* shows that the onsets of syllables are units the speaker plans and can move.
:::

::: proof
No single source is enough because each has a blind spot. A linguist's own intuitions can be shaped by the theory he or she hopes to confirm; a corpus can show that a structure is rare, but not that it is ungrammatical; an experiment can measure a small effect but only for the few sentences it tests. When the sources agree — when speakers reject a sentence, it never appears in a large corpus, children do not produce it and readers slow down when they meet it — the claim is secure. Sprouse, Schütze and Almeida (2013) tested a large random sample of acceptability contrasts published in a leading syntax journal with naive participants and found that the great majority of the published judgments were replicated, which suggests that careful intuitions are good evidence; they are still best checked.
:::

::: example Planning the evidence {#ex-evidence}
A student wants to know whether *different than* (*This is different than that*) is acceptable in British English, or only in American English. Which kinds of evidence would answer the question, and what would each show?
::: solution
1. **Corpora.** Compare frequencies of *different from*, *different to* and *different than* in a British corpus (such as the British National Corpus) and an American one (such as the Corpus of Contemporary American English), normalised per million words, and look at registers: speech, newspapers, academic writing. This shows how common each is and where. [[collocation-and-register/using-a-corpus]] explains normalised frequency.
2. **Judgments.** Ask a group of British and a group of American speakers to rate sentences with each preposition, in contexts where it is followed by a noun (*different than the last one*) and by a clause (*different than it was*). Studies and usage guides report that *than* is more acceptable before a clause, where *from what it was* is longer.
3. **Usage guides and dictionaries.** These show the norm: British guides have tended to criticise *different than* and *different to*; American dictionaries record *different than* as standard, especially before a clause.
4. **History.** Dictionaries such as the *Oxford English Dictionary* record all three prepositions since the sixteenth or seventeenth century, which shows the variation is old, not a recent error.

The answer would combine these: usage in each variety, by register, and the norms readers will apply.
:::
:::

## Two ways of looking at a language

::: definition Synchronic and diachronic {#def-synchronic}
A **synchronic** study describes a language as a system at one point in time: English as spoken today, or English of 1600. A **diachronic** study describes how a language changes through time. Saussure (1916) insisted on the distinction, because the reasons that a form exists (its history) are different from the reasons that it works as it does in the present system.
:::

The two views can give different answers to the same question. Diachronically, *nice* comes from Latin *nescius* ‘ignorant’; synchronically, that fact plays no part in what *nice* means to a speaker today, and a learner who argues that *nice* "really" means ‘foolish’ has confused the two. Most of this course is synchronic. [[linguistics/variation-change]] and the course *History of English* are diachronic.

::: note English and Chinese
Chinese has one of the longest traditions of language study in the world, but it was built around the written character. The traditional disciplines of 小学 were 文字学 (the structure of characters), 音韵学 (the sounds of characters, including the rhyme tables and the reconstruction of earlier pronunciations) and 训诂学 (the meanings of words in classical texts). Grammar as a separate subject came later: the first systematic Chinese grammar, 马建忠's 《马氏文通》 (1898), took Latin grammar as its model, in the same way that early grammars of English had done. Twentieth-century linguists then described Chinese on its own terms — Yuen Ren Chao (赵元任) in *A Grammar of Spoken Chinese* (1968), 朱德熙 in 《语法讲义》 (1982), and Charles Li and Sandra Thompson in *Mandarin Chinese: A Functional Reference Grammar* (1981). If you have studied Chinese grammar at school, you have seen the same move from prescription and Latin models to description that this chapter describes for English, and you will meet those authors again in the notes of later chapters.

A practical consequence for learners: the terms of Chinese school grammar (主语, 谓语, 宾语, 定语, 状语, 补语) do not line up exactly with English ones. 补语 in particular covers resultative and directional complements (看完, 走进来) for which English uses particles or other structures, and it is not the same as the English "complement".
:::

::: history
The scientific study of language in Europe began with comparison. In 1786 William Jones told the Asiatic Society in Calcutta that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin must have "sprung from some common source", and in the nineteenth century scholars such as Rasmus Rask and Jacob Grimm worked out the regular sound correspondences between the Indo-European languages. The study was historical until Ferdinand de Saussure, whose Geneva lectures were published after his death as the *Cours de linguistique générale* (1916), argued that a language at one moment is a system whose parts are defined by their relations to each other. In the United States, Leonard Bloomfield's *Language* (1933) made linguistics an empirical science built on observable forms, partly through fieldwork on Native American languages. Noam Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* (1957) and *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965) moved the focus to the speaker's mind: a grammar should generate all and only the grammatical sentences of a language and so model the speaker's competence. Since the 1960s the field has also grown in the other direction — sociolinguistics, corpus linguistics and functional and usage-based theories study language as it is used. Most linguists today use both kinds of evidence.
:::

::: summary
- Linguistics is **descriptive**: its rules are generalisations about what speakers do, tested against evidence; **prescriptive** rules are norms, which a description records as social facts.
- "Ungrammatical" is relative to a variety: *She don't know* is non-standard, not random; ✗ *Don't know she* is impossible in every variety.
- **Competence** (unconscious knowledge) is distinct from **performance** (use, limited by memory and attention); a sentence can be **grammatical** but hardly **acceptable**.

- The levels of analysis are phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics and discourse, with **interfaces** between them.
- Human language is largely **arbitrary**, has **duality of patterning**, is **productive** and **recursive**, and allows **displacement**.
- Evidence comes from judgments, corpora, experiments, acquisition, fieldwork, history and errors; claims are secure when several sources agree.
- **Synchronic** study describes a system at one time; **diachronic** study describes change.
:::

Which level of analysis does each fact belong to?

1. English *sing* ends in one consonant, /ŋ/, though it is spelled with two letters.
2. *Unbelievable* contains three morphemes.
3. In English, adjectives normally come before the noun they modify.
4. *Bachelor* and *unmarried man* are close in meaning.
5. *It's cold in here* can be a request to close the window.
::: solution
1. Phonology (with spelling as a separate system). 2. Morphology. 3. Syntax. 4. Semantics (sense relations; [[linguistics/semantics]]). 5. Pragmatics.
:::

::: exercise Marks for judgments {level=1}
Rewrite each sentence with the mark this site uses (✗, ? or ✓) for standard English, and say whether a linguist would use a star, a question mark or a hash sign.

1. *Book the on table is the.*
2. *The square circle was drawn in red.*
3. *I wonder what who bought.*
4. *She has been being followed.*
::: solution
1. ✗ — a star: the word order of the noun phrases is impossible.
2. ✓ for grammar, but contradictory — a hash sign.
3. ✗ — a star: in standard English only one question word moves to the front of a clause. (Compare ✓ *I wonder who bought what.*)
4. ? — many speakers find the combination of perfect, progressive and passive *has been being* awkward, though it occurs; a question mark.
:::
:::

::: exercise Arbitrary or not? {level=2}
For each word, say whether the link between form and meaning is arbitrary, onomatopoeic or sound-symbolic, and give a reason. *cuckoo*, *table*, *glimmer*, *moo*, *seven*.
::: solution
- *cuckoo*: onomatopoeic — the bird is named after its call, and many languages name it similarly (German *Kuckuck*, French *coucou*, Mandarin 布谷 *bùgǔ* imitates the call in its own way).
- *table*: arbitrary.
- *glimmer*: sound-symbolic — one of the *gl-* words for light (*gleam*, *glow*, *glint*); the link is a tendency, not a rule.
- *moo*: onomatopoeic (Mandarin 哞 *mōu*).
- *seven*: arbitrary. Its similarity to Latin *septem* and Sanskrit *saptá* is not imitation but common descent: the words are cognates.
:::
:::

::: exercise Competence or performance? {level=2}
Say whether each observation tells us more about competence or about performance, and why.

1. A speaker says *The keys to the cabinet is on the table*, then corrects herself: *are on the table*.
2. All native speakers asked reject ✗ *Who did you see Mary and?*
3. Readers slow down on the word *fell* in *The horse raced past the barn fell*.
4. A tired lecturer says *a lack of pies* when she means *a pack of lies*.
::: solution
1. Performance: agreement with the nearest noun (*cabinet*) is a known slip; the correction shows that her competence requires *are*, agreeing with *keys*.
2. Competence: a judgment shared by all speakers, about a sentence they have probably never heard, reflects a rule of grammar (the constraint on coordinate structures).
3. Mainly performance: the sentence is grammatical, but the processor first builds the wrong structure and must revise it. It also tells us something about competence — the reduced relative reading exists.
4. Performance: a slip of the tongue, in which the onsets /p/ and /l/ swap places. Slips are evidence about how speech is planned — the units that move are syllable onsets, not random letters — but the slip itself is not part of her grammar.
:::
:::

::: exercise Synchronic or diachronic? {level=2}
Classify each claim as synchronic or diachronic.

1. *Silly* once meant ‘happy, blessed’.
2. In present-day English, *silly* is gradable (*very silly*, *sillier*).
3. The *k* of *knee* was pronounced in Old English.
4. *Knee* and *knell* begin with the same phoneme, /n/, in present-day English.
::: solution
1. Diachronic. 2. Synchronic. 3. Diachronic. 4. Synchronic — and the contrast between 3 and 4 shows why spelling is no guide to the present system.

::: exercise A usage claim examined {level=3}
A blog post says: "*Less* with plural nouns, as in *less cars*, is a modern error caused by careless speakers. The rule is *fewer* for things you count and *less* for things you do not." Write a paragraph of 150–200 words that examines this claim as a linguist would, separating the descriptive and prescriptive parts and saying what evidence would test each.
::: hint
Think about three questions: is it modern (history), who uses it and where (corpus, register), and what do usage guides say (the norm)?
:::
::: solution
A model answer:

*The post combines a descriptive claim with a prescriptive one. The descriptive claim is that* less *with plural count nouns is recent. This can be tested historically: the* Oxford English Dictionary *records* less *with count nouns from Old English onwards, and the rule that restricts it is usually traced to Robert Baker's* Reflections on the English Language *(1770), who suggested that* fewer *would be better and more elegant. So the use is old and the rule is the innovation. A second descriptive claim, that "careless speakers" use it, can be tested in a corpus by comparing registers: we would expect* less *with plurals to be common in conversation and rare in edited academic prose, used by speakers of every social group. The prescriptive part — use* fewer *with count nouns — is a real norm of formal writing, which editors and examiners apply, and learners should follow it in essays. But it is a convention of a register, not a law of English, and some uses with* less *are standard everywhere:* less than ten miles, *one less thing to worry about,* 25 words or less.

Answers may differ in wording; they should separate the claims, name evidence for each, and record the formal norm without calling the other usage an error.
:::
:::

::: exercise Your own language as evidence {level=3}
Think of a sentence of Mandarin (or another language you speak) that is grammatical but very hard to understand because of its structure, and one that is ungrammatical although its meaning is clear. Write both, translate them into English, and explain in 100–150 words what each shows about the difference between competence and performance.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A model:

*Hard but grammatical:* 我昨天在书店遇到的那个借给你那本你一直想看的书的朋友回国了 — ‘The friend I met at the bookshop yesterday, who lent you the book you have always wanted to read, has gone back to his country.’ Every modifier is correctly placed before its noun with 的, but the long chain of prenominal relative clauses forces the listener to hold many words before finding the head noun 朋友. It is a problem of performance, like English centre-embedding.

*Clear but ungrammatical:* ✗ 我吃饭了已经 — ‘I have already eaten.’ The meaning is clear, but 已经 must come before the verb: ✓ 我已经吃饭了. Speakers reject it at once, which is a fact about competence: understanding a sentence and accepting it as grammatical are different abilities.

Any pair is acceptable if the first follows the rules but strains memory and the second breaks a rule while remaining understandable.
:::
