In November 1962, a young researcher walked through three department stores in Manhattan and asked shop assistants where he could find an item that he knew was on the fourth floor. When an assistant answered *Fourth floor*, he leaned forward and said *Excuse me?*, and the assistant repeated, more carefully, *Fourth floor*. Then he walked away and noted down whether each *r* in *fourth* and *floor* had been pronounced. The researcher was William Labov, and the result, published in *The Social Stratification of English in New York City* (1966), showed something that changed the study of language: the pronunciation of a single consonant was distributed through the city's population in an orderly way, by social class, by the care with which people spoke, and by age — and this orderly variation was a sign of a language change in progress.

Until then, most linguists had treated variation within a community as noise: "free variation" between forms that mean the same thing, of no interest to the description of the system. Labov and the sociolinguists who followed him showed that variation is **structured**, that it can be measured, and that it is the link between the language of today and the language of tomorrow. This chapter introduces the linguistic variable, the classic studies of variation by social class, style, gender, network, region and ethnicity, and then turns to change: how it is detected, how it spreads through a community and through the vocabulary, and how sounds and grammar change over time. It builds on the phonology of [[linguistics/phonetics-phonology]] and the corpus methods of [[linguistics/corpus-linguistics]]. The varieties of English around the world are the subject of the course *World Englishes*, and the history of English of the course *History of English*.

## The linguistic variable

::: definition Linguistic variable and variant {#def-variable}
A **linguistic variable** is a point in the language where speakers have a choice between two or more forms with the same referential meaning. The forms are its **variants**. Sociolinguists write variables in round brackets:

- (r): the pronunciation or absence of /r/ after a vowel, as in *car*, *fourth*;
- (ing): the ending of *walking* as [ɪŋ] or [ɪn] (often written *walkin'*);
- (th): /θ/ in *think* as [θ], [t] (in some Irish and New York speech) or [f] (*fink*, in many British cities);
- (h): /h/ pronounced or dropped in *hat*, *house*;
- (t/d): a final /t/ or /d/ after a consonant pronounced or deleted, as in *last night*, *old man*;
- grammatical variables, such as *was* and *were* in *we was* / *we were*, or the quotatives *say*, *go* and *be like* (*She said* / *She goes* / *She was like, "No way"*).
:::

A variable is studied by counting. The researcher records speakers in natural conversation, identifies every occasion on which the variable could occur, and calculates the proportion of each variant for each speaker or group. The proportions, not single tokens, are the data: a speaker who says *walkin'* 40 per cent of the time and *walking* 60 per cent of the time is not inconsistent but is using a variable rule.

::: theorem Variation is constrained {#rule-constraints}
The choice between variants is not random. It is influenced, as a matter of probability, by:

1. **Linguistic factors**: the surrounding sounds, the grammatical status of the form, the word class. Final /t, d/ after a consonant is deleted more often before another consonant (*last night*, *old man*) than before a vowel (*last Easter*), and less often when it is the past tense ending than when it is part of the root: *missed* /mɪst/ loses its /t/ less often than *mist*, though the two sound the same.
2. **Stylistic factors**: the same speaker uses more prestige variants when paying more attention to speech, or when speaking to a more distant or formal audience.
3. **Social factors**: social class, gender, age, ethnicity, region and the speaker's social network.
:::

::: proof
The pattern in rule 1 is one of the best-replicated findings in sociolinguistics, found in many varieties of English, including African American English, varieties in England and in the United States (research by Labov, Gregory Guy and others since the late 1960s). Its explanation is debated: perhaps a past tense morpheme resists deletion because it carries information; perhaps forms such as *mist* are stored with their /t/ less firmly. The constraint shows that speakers' variable behaviour is sensitive to grammar, which is why variation belongs in linguistics. Rules 2 and 3 are the subject of the rest of the chapter. All three are probabilistic: they describe tendencies in groups, measured over many tokens, and they do not predict what an individual will say on one occasion.
:::

## Social class and style

Labov's department-store survey was designed to test a hypothesis: that if the speech communities of New York were stratified by social class, salespeople in stores of different social standing would differ in the same way.

::: example The department-store survey {#ex-labov}
Labov chose three stores: Saks Fifth Avenue (expensive, high-status), Macy's (middle) and S. Klein (cheap, low-status). He asked a question whose answer was *fourth floor*, then asked for a repetition. Explain (a) why he asked for *fourth floor*, (b) why he asked for a repetition, (c) why he studied shop assistants rather than customers, and (d) what the results showed.
::: solution
(a) *Fourth floor* contains two chances for the variable (r) in one short answer: one before a consonant (*fourth*) and one at the end of a word (*floor*). It is a natural answer to a natural question, so the speaker pays little attention to pronunciation.

(b) The repetition after *Excuse me?* gives a second, more careful version of the same words from the same speaker. Comparing the two shows the effect of attention to speech — **style** — while everything else is held constant.

(c) Shop assistants in each store were broadly similar in background, but Labov argued that they tend to "borrow prestige" from their customers and adapt their speech to them. The stores therefore stood for social levels without the researcher needing to interview anyone about class.

(d) Pronunciation of /r/ was most frequent among the assistants at Saks, less frequent at Macy's and least frequent at S. Klein: (r) was stratified by the social standing of the store. Pronunciation of /r/ also increased in the careful repetition. Since the use of /r/ after vowels in New York City was at that time increasing — it had been largely absent from the prestige speech of the city earlier in the century — the pattern suggested a change in progress led from above, towards a new prestige norm. The survey took only a few hours and was anonymous; its findings were confirmed by Labov's much larger interview study in the Lower East Side.
:::
:::

In the main interview study, Labov recorded speakers in several **styles**: casual speech (for example while telling a story about a time they were in danger of death), careful interview speech, reading a passage aloud, reading word lists and reading minimal pairs (*guard*/*god*, which differ only in /r/ in New York speech). The proportion of /r/ rose in every social group from casual speech to minimal pairs. One group behaved unexpectedly: in the most formal styles, the **lower middle class** used more /r/ than the upper middle class. Labov called this **hypercorrection** — overshooting the prestige norm under attention — and interpreted it as a sign of linguistic insecurity in a socially mobile group.

Later research added a second view of style. Allan Bell (1984) argued that speakers shift style mainly in response to their audience — **audience design** — and showed that newsreaders on New Zealand radio stations, reading the news in the same studio, used different proportions of prestige variants depending on the station's audience. Both attention to speech and audience shape style; more recent work also treats style as a way of presenting identity.

## Gender and social networks

::: theorem Gender patterns in variation {#rule-gender}
1. In many communities studied in Western Europe and North America, **women use fewer stigmatised variants** than men of the same social group, in stable variation such as (ing).
2. In changes from above (towards a prestige norm), women tend to adopt the new prestige form earlier.
3. In many changes from below (below the level of social awareness), women also lead, often by about a generation (Labov 1990, *Language Variation and Change*).
4. These are tendencies of particular communities at particular times, not universal properties of women's and men's speech, and they interact with class, age and network.
:::

Peter Trudgill's study of Norwich, in the east of England (*The Social Differentiation of English in Norwich*, 1974), showed the first pattern clearly for (ing): in every class, women used more [ɪŋ] than men. He also tested how speakers perceived their own speech. Speakers heard words pronounced in different ways and said which pronunciation they normally used. Many women claimed to use the prestige form more than they actually did — **over-reporting** the standard — while many men claimed to use the local, non-standard form more than they actually did. Trudgill (1972) interpreted the men's answers as evidence of **covert prestige**: the local, working-class forms carried positive values of toughness and local loyalty for men, though nobody would call them "correct".

::: example Interpreting self-reports {#ex-trudgill}
Explain why the self-report test in Norwich was important, and what it adds to recordings of actual speech.
::: solution
1. **Recordings** show what speakers do: the proportions of each variant by class, sex and style.
2. **Self-reports** show what speakers *think* they do, which is a window on the values attached to each variant. A speaker who claims to use a form more than she does is oriented towards it.
3. **The finding**: women's errors went one way (towards the standard) and men's the other (towards the local form). The difference cannot be explained by what they said, only by what they valued.
4. **What it adds**: it explains *why* the gender difference in speech exists — not because of any natural difference between the sexes, but because the variants carry different social meanings for different groups — and it shows that a "non-standard" variant can carry prestige of its own. This is why the theory of language change needs social evaluation, not just social categories.
:::
:::

Lesley Milroy's study of three working-class communities in Belfast (*Language and Social Networks*, 1980) added a different factor. She measured each speaker's **social network**: how far the people they knew also knew each other, and how many of their ties were with kin, neighbours and workmates. Speakers in dense, multiplex networks used more local vernacular variants, whatever their sex, and the networks acted as norm-enforcing mechanisms. Where the men had dense networks and the women did not, the usual gender pattern appeared; where the women's networks were dense, the pattern changed. The lesson is that categories such as gender and class work partly through the patterns of contact they create.

## Region and ethnicity

Regional variation is the oldest object of study. Traditional **dialectology** mapped the words and pronunciations of rural speakers, usually older men who had lived all their lives in one village. The Survey of English Dialects, directed by Harold Orton, collected such data from over three hundred localities in England between 1950 and 1961. Dialect maps show **isoglosses** — lines separating areas that use different forms, such as the line between northern English accents that have the same vowel in *foot* and *strut* and southern accents that distinguish them. Isoglosses rarely coincide exactly, and between neighbouring areas there is usually a **dialect continuum** rather than a border.

Modern studies of urban speech show both continuing regional differences and **levelling**: the loss of very local features in favour of regional ones, through mobility and contact. At the same time, some features spread fast across regions. In Britain, TH-fronting — [f] for /θ/ and [v] for /ð/ in non-initial positions, as in *fink*, *bruvver* — was associated with London in the mid-twentieth century and is now heard among young speakers in many cities in England and in Scotland. Glottal replacement of /t/ (*wa[ʔ]er*) has spread in a similar way.

Ethnicity can also define a variety. **African American English** (AAE) is the most studied, and Labov's "The logic of nonstandard English" (1969) was an influential demonstration that its grammar is systematic. Two of its features show this clearly. Where standard English can contract the copula (*She's working*), AAE can omit it (*She working* ‘she is working now’); where standard English cannot contract it (*That's what she is*), AAE cannot omit it either (✗ *That's what she*). And AAE has an **habitual** *be*, invariant, which marks a repeated or habitual situation: *She be working* means ‘she usually works, she is often working’, a distinction standard English expresses only with adverbs. These are rules, not errors; children who speak AAE at home and learn standard English at school are acquiring a second dialect, a fact with consequences for education.

::: warning Calling a dialect "bad grammar"
✗ *People who say* We was *don't know grammar.* The form *we was* is part of the grammar of many dialects of English in England and the United States, where *was* is used with all persons; standard English uses *were* with *we* and *you*. ✓ *We was is non-standard; in formal writing and exams, use* we were. The difference is one of variety and register, not of knowledge or logic. [[linguistics/what-linguistics-studies#rule-describe]] explains the descriptive view; the course *World Englishes* treats varieties as systems.
:::

## Detecting change in progress

How can we see a change while it is happening? There are two methods.

::: definition Apparent time and real time {#def-apparent-time}
A study in **apparent time** compares speakers of different ages recorded at the same time. If younger speakers use a variant more than older ones, and if each speaker's usage is assumed to stay fairly stable after early adulthood, the age difference reflects a change across generations. A study in **real time** compares the same community (a **trend study**) or the same speakers (a **panel study**) at two or more points in time. Real-time studies are the stronger evidence, because apparent-time patterns can also arise from **age grading** — features that speakers use at one stage of life and abandon later, as many adolescents do with slang.
:::

Labov's first major study, on the island of Martha's Vineyard in Massachusetts ("The social motivation of a sound change", 1963), used apparent time. He found that the first element of the diphthongs in *right* and *house* was becoming more centralised ([əɪ], [əʊ]) among some groups of islanders — especially among those, including young men who intended to stay on the island, who most strongly identified with it against the summer visitors. The change was spreading not because it was easier to say but because it meant something: being a Vineyarder.

::: example An apparent-time pattern {#ex-apparent-time}
A study in a city in 2025 finds that the quotative *be like* (*She was like, "Really?"*) is used for about half of all quotations by speakers aged 15–25, much less by speakers aged 40–55 and very rarely by speakers over 70. (The pattern is typical of what has been reported in many English-speaking communities; the figures here are illustrative.) What can be concluded, and what further evidence would make the conclusion secure?
::: solution
1. **Apparent-time interpretation.** The age gradient suggests a change in progress: each generation uses *be like* more than the last.
2. **Alternative: age grading.** Perhaps people use *be like* in youth and abandon it as they get older. If so, today's 20-year-olds would use less of it at 50, and there would be no change in the community.
3. **Real-time evidence.** Two kinds would decide. A trend study: recordings of 15–25-year-olds in the same city in, say, 1995 should show much less *be like* if the change is real — and the form was in fact first noticed by linguists in American speech in the early 1980s, which supports a recent change. A panel study: recording the same speakers again after twenty years would show whether individuals keep their usage.
4. **Further detail.** Linguistic constraints (which kinds of quotation *be like* introduces — for example, inner thoughts and attitudes rather than exact words) and their development over time would show how the change is spreading through the grammar.
:::
:::

## How changes spread

::: theorem The S-curve of change {#rule-s-curve}
A new form typically spreads slowly at first, then rapidly, and slowly again as it approaches completion: plotted against time, its frequency follows an S-shaped (logistic) curve. Anthony Kroch (1989) showed that when the rise of periphrastic *do* in Early Modern English questions and negatives was modelled in this way, the curves for different contexts rose at the same rate, which he took as evidence that they were one change in the grammar (the **constant rate hypothesis**).
:::

::: widget plot
title: A model of the S-curve
f: 100/(1+exp(-k*(x-m)))
x: 0, 100
y: 0, 100
sliders: k=0.12:0.04:0.4:0.01; m=50:20:80:1
labels: percentage of speakers (or contexts) using the new form
caption: A logistic curve, the standard model of how a change spreads. The horizontal axis is time in years from the first appearance of the new form; the vertical axis is the percentage of use. *k* sets how fast the change moves through its middle phase, and *m* the year at which it reaches 50%. This is a model, not data: real changes are measured from dated texts or recordings, and they often stall, reverse or end before 100%. With *k* = 0.12 the middle half of the change (25% to 75%) takes about 18 years; with *k* = 0.4, under 6.
:::

::: proof
The S-curve has a simple intuitive basis: a new form spreads by contact between speakers who use it and speakers who do not, and contact between the two groups is greatest when they are of similar size, in the middle of the change. The same curve describes the spread of innovations of many kinds. In linguistics it fits many documented changes reasonably well, though not all, and the fit is often judged by eye on limited data. Kroch's analysis used the counts of *do* in texts from 1400 to 1700 collected by Alvar Ellegård (1953). The constant rate hypothesis remains influential and has been tested on other changes; its claim that contexts change at one rate has been disputed for some cases.
:::

Within the vocabulary, sound changes can spread in two ways. The **Neogrammarians** of Leipzig, in a famous statement of 1878 (Hermann Osthoff and Karl Brugmann), held that sound laws operate without exceptions: a change affects every word with the relevant sound in the relevant environment at once. William S.-Y. Wang (王士元), in "Competing changes as a cause of residue" (1969), argued from Chinese dialect data that some changes instead spread **word by word** — **lexical diffusion** — so that at any moment some words have changed and others have not, and a change may stop half-way, leaving irregular "residue". Labov (*Principles of Linguistic Change*, vol. 1, 1994) proposed that both happen: fine changes of phonetic detail, such as vowel shifts, tend to be regular, while changes that replace one phoneme with another, sensitive to word frequency and grammatical category, tend to diffuse through the lexicon.

::: quiz
A sound change has affected some words with the relevant sound but not others, and the frequent words changed first. Which model fits this best?
- [ ] Neogrammarian regularity
- [x] lexical diffusion
- [ ] age grading
- [ ] hypercorrection
::: solution
Lexical diffusion (Wang 1969) describes a change spreading through the vocabulary word by word, leaving some words unchanged for a time. Neogrammarian change would affect every word with the sound in the same environment at once. Age grading and hypercorrection concern speakers, not words.
:::
:::

::: example Grimm's law {#ex-grimm}
Compare these English words with their cognates in Latin. What regular correspondences do they show, and why are the English words not borrowings from Latin?

| English | Latin |
|---|---|
| *father* | *pater* |
| *foot* | *pēs, pedis* |
| *three* | *trēs* |
| *hundred* | *centum* (pronounced with /k/) |
| *heart* | *cor, cordis* |
::: solution
1. **Correspondences.** Latin /p/ ↔ English /f/ (*pater*–*father*, *pēs*–*foot*); Latin /t/ ↔ English /θ/ (*trēs*–*three*); Latin /k/ ↔ English /h/ (*centum*–*hundred*, *cor*–*heart*). Voiceless plosives in Latin correspond to voiceless fricatives in English.
2. **Interpretation.** These correspondences are part of what is known as **Grimm's law**, described by Rasmus Rask (1818) and Jacob Grimm (1822): in the prehistory of the Germanic languages, the voiceless plosives of Proto-Indo-European became fricatives. Latin kept the plosives; English, German and the other Germanic languages show the fricatives (German *Vater*, *Fuß*, *drei*, *hundert*, *Herz*, with later changes of their own).
3. **Not borrowings.** Borrowed words do not show the shift: English *paternal*, *pedal*, *trio*, *century* and *cordial* were borrowed from Latin or French much later and keep the Latin consonants. The pairs *father*/*paternal* and *heart*/*cordial* in one language show two layers of vocabulary — inherited and borrowed — from the same Indo-European roots.
4. **What it shows.** Sound change is regular enough that correspondences can be stated as laws and used to reconstruct ancestors; the regularity is what makes historical linguistics possible. [[history-of-english/english-and-its-relatives]] continues the story.
:::
:::

## Grammatical change

Grammar changes too, often by **grammaticalisation**: the process by which a word or construction with a full lexical meaning becomes a grammatical marker (Paul Hopper and Elizabeth Closs Traugott, *Grammaticalization*, 2nd edition, 2003). Three English examples:

- *will* was a lexical verb meaning ‘want’ in Old English (as German *will* ‘wants’ still is); it became the marker of the future.
- *be going to* began as a verb of motion with a purpose (*I am going [to the market] to buy fish*) and became a marker of future time (*It's going to rain*), with a reduced form *gonna* ([[connected-speech/elision-assimilation#rule-gonna]]). Changes of this kind typically involve **semantic bleaching** (the motion meaning is lost), **phonetic reduction** and a widening of the contexts of use.
- the *-ly* suffix of *friendly* and *quickly* comes from an Old English noun *līc* ‘body, form’; its present-day status is discussed in [[linguistics/morphology#ex-borderline]].

Corpora show grammatical change over short periods. Comparing the Brown family of corpora from 1961 and 1991/2, Geoffrey Leech, Marianne Hundt, Christian Mair and Nicholas Smith (*Change in Contemporary English*, 2009) found that the core modal verbs, especially *must*, *shall* and *may*, became less frequent in both British and American English, while semi-modals such as *have to*, *need to* and *want to* became more frequent; the progressive was used more, and the passive less. They interpreted several of these trends as **colloquialisation**: written English moving towards the norms of speech.

::: note English and Chinese
The Chinese "dialects" (方言) — Mandarin, Wu, Yue (Cantonese), Min, Hakka, Xiang, Gan and others — are as different from each other as many European languages: Cantonese and Shanghainese speakers who know no Mandarin cannot understand each other's speech. Linguists often call them **Sinitic languages** or varieties; the Chinese term reflects a shared writing system and political and cultural unity rather than mutual intelligibility. Within each group there is variation of the kind this chapter describes, and Putonghua (普通话), defined in 1956 as based on the pronunciation of Beijing, the grammar of the northern dialects and the norms of modern vernacular literature, is a standard in the same sense as standard English: a variety with institutional support, not the "correct" form of the others.

Chinese gives classic evidence on change. The **entering tone** (入声) syllables of Middle Chinese, which ended in /p/, /t/ or /k/, keep those endings in Cantonese — 十 *sap6* ‘ten’, 八 *baat3* ‘eight’, 六 *luk6* ‘six’ — and in Hakka, while in Mandarin the stops were lost and the syllables were redistributed among the other tones, in a way that is not fully regular (Wang's data for lexical diffusion came from such cases). Many varieties also show **literary and colloquial readings** (文白异读) of the same character, especially in Min, where one character has a reading used in reading aloud and another in everyday words — two historical layers in one system, like English *father* and *paternal*. And familiar regional features of Mandarin, such as the merger of *n* and *l* in some south-western and southern varieties or of *zh ch sh* with *z c s* in much of the south, are linguistic variables in exactly Labov's sense: they vary with region, style and the influence of the standard ([[sounds-of-english/sounds-for-chinese-speakers]] discusses how they affect English pronunciation).
:::

::: history
Language change was the main subject of nineteenth-century linguistics: Rask (1818) and Grimm (1822) on the Germanic sound shift, the Neogrammarians' regularity hypothesis (1878), and Hermann Paul's *Prinzipien der Sprachgeschichte* (1880). Dialect geography began at the same time, with Georg Wenker's postal questionnaire survey of German dialects (from 1876) and Jules Gilliéron's *Atlas linguistique de la France* (1902–10), whose maps showed that every word seemed to have its own history — a slogan that later supported lexical diffusion. Saussure's separation of synchronic from diachronic study (1916) put change outside the description of the system, and it was Uriel Weinreich, William Labov and Marvin Herzog's "Empirical foundations for a theory of language change" (1968) that argued for studying the two together, through "orderly heterogeneity" in the speech community. Labov's New York study (1966), Trudgill's Norwich (1974) and Milroy's Belfast (1980) established the methods, and corpora of historical and present-day English have since made it possible to measure change in writing over centuries and decades.
:::

::: summary
- Variation is **structured**: a **linguistic variable** has **variants** whose proportions are influenced by linguistic, stylistic and social factors (t/d deletion is less frequent when the /t, d/ is a past-tense ending).
- Labov's **department-store survey** (1962, published 1966) found (r) stratified by social status and by style; the lower middle class showed **hypercorrection** in formal styles.
- **Style** reflects attention to speech and **audience design**; **gender** differences (Trudgill's Norwich, **covert prestige**) and **social networks** (Milroy's Belfast) show how variants carry social meaning.
- Regional variation forms **dialect continua** with **isoglosses**; ethnic varieties such as **African American English** are rule-governed (copula omission, habitual *be*).
- Change is detected in **apparent time** and confirmed in **real time**; **age grading** is the alternative to rule out.
- Changes spread along an **S-curve**; sound change can be **regular** (Neogrammarians) or spread by **lexical diffusion** (Wang 1969); **Grimm's law** shows the regularity of historical correspondences.
- **Grammaticalisation** turns lexical words into grammatical markers (*will*, *going to*); corpora show recent shifts such as the decline of core modals.
- Chinese varieties show the same processes: entering-tone stops kept in Cantonese, lost in Mandarin; literary and colloquial layers; regional variables.
:::

::: exercise Hypercorrection {level=2}
Explain in three or four sentences what Labov meant by the hypercorrection of the lower middle class, and why it suggested linguistic insecurity.
::: solution
In casual speech the lower middle class used less /r/ than the upper middle class, as their position in the social order would predict. In the most formal styles (word lists, minimal pairs) they used more /r/ than the upper middle class — they overshot the prestige norm. Labov interpreted this as linguistic insecurity: a socially mobile group, aware of the prestige form and anxious to use it, overcorrects when paying close attention. Speakers in this group also tended to judge their own speech harshly in Labov's attitude tests.

::: exercise Grammaticalisation {level=2}
Explain how *gonna* illustrates semantic bleaching, phonetic reduction and widening of contexts, with an example of each.
::: solution
- **Bleaching**: *I'm going to buy fish* once required motion; *It's going to rain* has no motion — only future meaning remains.
- **Reduction**: *going to* /ˈɡəʊɪŋ tə/ becomes *gonna* /ˈɡɒnə/ (GB), /ˈɡʌnə/ (GA), possible only in the future sense (✗ *I'm gonna the shop*).
- **Widening**: the construction now combines with subjects and verbs that cannot move (*The bridge is going to collapse*; *You're going to love it*), and with *be going to* itself (*I'm going to go*).
:::

::: exercise Design a variation study {level=3}
Design a study of whether TH-fronting (*fink* for *think*) is spreading among young people in a British city. Say whom you would record, in which styles, how you would count, which social factors you would record, and how you would distinguish change from age grading.

Count every token of /θ/ and /ð/ in non-initial positions and in initial /θ/ (*think*, *three*), coding the variant ([θ], [f], [t], or other) and linguistic factors: position in the word, neighbouring sounds, word frequency. Report proportions of [f] by speaker group and style, with a statistical model of the factors. An age gradient (more [f] among the young) suggests change in progress; to rule out age grading, compare with earlier recordings of the same city (a trend study) or re-record the same speakers later (a panel study). Also collect attitudes: do speakers see [f] as local, youthful, "lazy"? That evaluation helps explain why the variant spreads.*

::: exercise A standard and its varieties {level=3}
Write 150–200 words comparing the position of standard English among English dialects with that of Putonghua among Chinese varieties. Mention at least one similarity and one difference, and use the terms *standard*, *variety* and *mutual intelligibility*.
::: solution
A model answer:

*Standard English and Putonghua are both standard varieties: forms of the language given institutional support — in education, publishing, broadcasting and government — and expected in formal writing. Neither is more logical or "correct" than the other varieties, and both were shaped by the speech of a centre of power: south-east England and the court for English, Beijing for Putonghua, which was defined in 1956. A difference is mutual intelligibility. English dialects, however different in accent and some grammar, are mostly mutually intelligible, so standard English is one variety among closely related ones. Many Chinese varieties — Cantonese, Shanghainese, Hokkien — are not mutually intelligible with Mandarin in speech, so learning Putonghua is, for many speakers, closer to learning a second language, while the shared writing system makes the written standard available to all. A second difference is that standard English has no single standard accent, while Putonghua defines a standard pronunciation, tested in the national proficiency test (普通话水平测试). In both cases the standard and the local varieties coexist, and speakers shift between them by situation.*
