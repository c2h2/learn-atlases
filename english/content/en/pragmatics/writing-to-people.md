Professor Helen Carter receives about sixty emails a day. On one Monday morning, three of them come from students she has not met.

The first:

*Subject: (no subject)*

*Dear teacher, I am Liu Yang. I want to join your project. Please tell me how to apply. Waiting for your reply.*

The second:

*Subject: question*

*hey, is the reading for week 3 online?? cant find it thx*

The third:

*Subject: Possible MSc dissertation supervision: urban heat and green roofs*

*Dear Professor Carter,*

*My name is Oscar Lindgren and I am a first-year MSc student in Environmental Science. I'm writing to ask whether you might be willing to supervise my dissertation next year. I read your 2023 paper on green roofs in Sheffield, and I would like to build on its method for a study of school buildings. I have attached a one-page outline.*

*I know this is a busy time of year, so please don't feel you need to reply in detail. If you think it could be a good fit, would you have fifteen minutes to meet in the next few weeks?*

*Best wishes,*
*Oscar Lindgren*

All three writers want something. Only the third has made it easy to say yes. The first is clear but sounds like a demand; the second is so casual that it sounds careless, and a lecturer may feel that she is being treated as a search engine. Neither was intended that way — Liu Yang was being direct and respectful, as he would be in many Chinese emails, where 老师 and 您 carry the respect; the second student writes to everyone like that. But in a written message the reader has nothing except the words to judge by, and the words create the **tone**.

This chapter applies the course to writing: emails and messages to teachers, colleagues and people you do not know. The basic structure of emails — subject lines, greetings, purpose, request and sign-off — is taught in [[sentences-and-paragraphs/emails-messages]], and the modal forms of polite requests in [[modality/modality-politeness]]; they are assumed here. This chapter is about the judgements behind them: who the reader is, what the email asks of them, and how to diagnose and repair tone.

## Tone and the reader

::: definition Tone {#def-tone}
The **tone** of a message is the attitude towards the reader that the reader infers from it: respectful, friendly, demanding, careless, cold, anxious, warm. Tone is built from many small choices — the greeting, the request form, the presence of reasons and thanks, the length, punctuation, the subject line — and it is judged against the reader's expectations for that relationship and purpose. Writers often know what tone they intended; only the reader knows what tone arrived.
:::

Writing removes most of the signals that soften speech: the smile, the voice, the chance to notice confusion and repair it. A spoken *Could you send me the slides?* is shaped by intonation; the written version is judged on its words alone. This is why emails tend to need *more* explicit politeness than speech for the same request, and why irony and teasing are risky in writing ([[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]).

Research on emails from students to university teachers shows how much tone matters. Stephens, Houser and Cowan (2009) gave American instructors versions of a student email that differed only in how casual they were — with or without a greeting, abbreviations, and careful spelling — and found that the overly casual versions led instructors to like the student less, to see them as less credible, and to be less willing to do what was asked. Economidou-Kogetsidis (2011) analysed email requests that Greek Cypriot students wrote to their lecturers in English, and found that many were very direct, with little softening and sometimes without greetings or closings; when British English-speaking lecturers rated them, they often judged them abrupt or impolite, although the writers had not intended this. Biesenbach-Lucas (2007), comparing native and non-native English-speaking graduate students in the United States, reported that the non-native speakers used fewer of the softening devices native speakers used in requests to faculty. None of these studies suggests that the students were rude people; they show readers interpreting **pragmalinguistic** choices as personal attitudes ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures#def-pragmatic-failure]]).

::: theorem Four questions before you write {#rule-four-questions}
Before writing to someone you do not know well, ask:

1. **Who is the reader to me?** Their **power** (teacher, manager, official, client), the **distance** between you (stranger, acquaintance, colleague) and the conventions of their setting ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]).
2. **What am I asking them to do?** The size of the **imposition**: reading a two-line question is small; writing a reference, reading a draft or giving an extension is large.
3. **What do they need to know to do it?** Who you are, what exactly you want, why, and by when — in that order of importance.
4. **How will they read it?** Probably quickly, among many others, perhaps on a phone. Can they see the point in the first two lines, and reply in one?

The answers decide the greeting, the amount of softening, the order of information and the length.
:::

::: proof
The four questions are Brown and Levinson's variables — power, distance and imposition — plus the reader's practical situation. The practical question matters because in email, efficiency is itself a form of politeness: a message that makes the reader work to discover who is writing and what they want imposes on them, however many softeners it contains. Professor Carter can act on Oscar's email in a minute; she would have to write back to Liu Yang to ask which project he means and what he has done so far. Clarity and courtesy are not alternatives. The best emails to busy people are both.
:::

## Names and openings

::: theorem Addressing people in English emails {#rule-address}
1. **Use the title and surname for a first email** to a teacher, official or senior person: *Dear Dr Okafor*, *Dear Professor Carter*, *Dear Ms Romano*. Use the title they use themselves (*Dr*, *Professor*); if unsure, *Dr* is safe for university lecturers in most English-speaking countries, and *Professor* in the United States for any faculty member.
2. **Use the surname, not the given name, after a title**: *Dear Professor Li* (for Li Wei), never ✗ *Dear Professor Wei* or ✗ *Dear Professor Li Wei*. Check which name is the family name: many Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese and Hungarian names put it first, many others last.
3. **Follow the reader's lead.** If they reply and sign with their first name (*Best, Helen*), you may use it next time (*Dear Helen* or *Hi Helen*); if they sign with their full name or title, keep the title.
4. **Avoid titles as names**: ✗ *Dear Teacher*, ✗ *Dear Professor* (without a name), ✗ *Hello Manager*. With an unknown reader, use a role or the organisation: *Dear Admissions Team*, *Dear Hiring Manager*; *Dear Sir or Madam* is formal and somewhat old-fashioned but still used.
5. **Avoid guessing gender**: if you do not know whether to write *Mr* or *Ms*, use the full name: *Dear Sam Taylor*.
6. **Opening lines**: *I hope you are well* is conventional and harmless; in a first email, a sentence saying who you are and why you are writing matters more.
:::

::: warning Names and titles
✗ *Dear teacher,* ✓ *Dear Professor Carter,* / *Dear Ms Romano,*

✗ *Dear Professor Helen,* ✓ *Dear Professor Carter,* (or, once invited, *Dear Helen,*)

✗ *Dear Mr Zhang Wei,* ✓ *Dear Mr Zhang,*

✗ *Hi!* (to a stranger, with no name) ✓ *Dear Dr Mensah,* / *Hello Dr Mensah,*

The pattern *title + given name* (*Professor Helen*, *Mr John*) is a common transfer from languages and varieties where it is respectful; in British and American English it sounds odd, though it is normal in some other Englishes ([[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes]]).
:::

## Requests to people with power

The request email is the one learners write most often and get wrong most often. Its conventions follow from the requests chapter ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]): a clear head act, conventional indirectness, supportive moves, and an easy way to say no — with the addition, in writing, that the purpose should appear early.

::: example Diagnosing and repairing tone {#ex-repair}
Diagnose the tone of Liu Yang's email at the start of the chapter, then rewrite it.
::: solution
**Diagnosis.**
- *(no subject)*: the reader cannot tell what the email is about or file it.
- *Dear teacher*: a title used as a name ([[#rule-address]]); it suggests the writer does not know who the reader is.
- *I am Liu Yang* gives the name but not who he is (a student? which course? which university?).
- *I want to join your project*: a want statement — among the most direct request strategies ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals#rule-ccsarp-levels]]); *your project* does not say which project.
- *Please tell me how to apply*: *please* + imperative, which in English is closer to an instruction than a favour.
- *Waiting for your reply* (a translation of 期待您的回复 or 等您回复): in English it can sound impatient, as if the reader is late.
- No reason, no thanks, no closing; the reader must write back to find out the basic facts.

**Repair.**

*Subject: Enquiry about joining the coastal erosion project*

*Dear Professor Carter,*

*My name is Liu Yang, and I am a second-year undergraduate in Geography. I am very interested in your coastal erosion project, which I heard about in Dr Ahmed's lecture last week, and I would like to ask whether there might be opportunities for undergraduate students to take part — for example as a research assistant over the summer.*

*I have taken modules in GIS and statistics, and I have attached my CV. If you are able to tell me whether places are available, and how I could apply, I would be very grateful.*

*Thank you for your time.*

*Best wishes,*
*Liu Yang*

The repaired email identifies the writer, names the project and how he heard of it, uses conventionally indirect, hypothetical requests (*I would like to ask whether there might be…*, *If you are able to…, I would be very grateful*), gives the reader what she needs to decide, and closes with thanks. It is longer, but quicker to answer.
:::
:::

::: theorem Requests by email to someone with power {#rule-email-request}
1. **Say who you are** in one line, with what the reader needs to place you (course, year, student number, where you met).
2. **State the purpose early** — in the first or second sentence: *I'm writing to ask whether…*
3. **Make the head act conventionally indirect** and suitably distant: *Would it be possible…?*, *I was wondering whether you might…*, *I would be grateful if you could…*.
4. **Give a reason** briefly and **make the request specific**: which document, which date, how long a meeting.
5. **Make compliance easy**: attach the file, suggest times, ask a question that can be answered in a line.
6. **State a deadline with its reason**, softened: *…by Friday, if possible, as the form is due on Monday.*
7. **Offer an easy way out** for large requests: *I quite understand if this isn't possible.*
8. **Close with thanks** that do not presume: *Thank you for considering this* rather than *Thanks in advance* ([[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments#rule-thanks]]).
:::

::: quiz
Which closing line is least likely to sound presumptuous in a first email asking a professor for a reference?
- [ ] *Waiting for your reply.*
- [ ] *Thanks in advance for the reference.*
- [x] *Thank you very much for considering this.*
- [ ] *Please reply as soon as possible.*
::: solution
*Thank you for considering this* thanks the reader without assuming the answer is yes. *Thanks in advance* assumes she will write the reference; *Waiting for your reply* and *Please reply as soon as possible* put pressure on her.
:::
:::

## Writing to strangers

A first message to a stranger — a researcher, a possible employer, someone recommended by a friend — has a special difficulty: the reader owes the writer nothing. The imposition is as large as it can be in relation to the relationship, so the message must justify itself quickly.

::: theorem The first message to a stranger {#rule-cold-email}
1. **Explain the connection**: how you found them or who suggested you write (*Dr Patel suggested I contact you*; *I read your article on…*).
2. **Show that you have chosen them for a reason**: something specific about their work, not a general compliment.
3. **Ask for something small and specific**: a short call, one piece of advice, a pointer to a resource — not *any opportunities* or *help with my career*.
4. **Keep it short**: one screen at most; put details in an attachment or a link.
5. **Make refusal easy**, and do not take silence as hostility. If there is no reply, one polite follow-up after a week or two is acceptable; after that, let it go.
:::

::: example A message to a stranger {#ex-cold}
Sun-hee, a final-year student of journalism, wants advice from Tomasz Nowak, a data journalist whose work she admires and whom she has never met. Write her message.
::: solution
*Subject: Question from a journalism student about learning data skills*

*Dear Mr Nowak,*

*I'm a final-year journalism student at the University of Leeds, and I've been following your data stories on housing for the past year — the interactive map of rents last spring was what made me want to learn data journalism.*

*I'm trying to decide between two short courses this summer, one in Python and one in R. Would you have a moment to tell me which you'd recommend for a beginner, or whether there is something else you wish you'd learned first? Even a one-line answer would be very helpful.*

*Thank you for your time, and for your work.*

*Best wishes,*
*Kim Sun-hee*

**Why it works.** The subject line says what is inside. The first sentence gives who she is and a specific, sincere reason for choosing him (positive politeness, [[pragmatics/politeness-face#rule-strategies]]). The request is small, specific and easy to answer (*Even a one-line answer*), with a conventional indirect head act (*Would you have a moment to…?*). The thanks do not presume. Her name is given in the order she uses in English (*Kim Sun-hee*); if he replies *Dear Sun-hee*, he has understood which is the given name.
:::
:::

## Follow-ups, apologies and refusals

Not every email is answered, and not every answer is yes. Three further kinds of message need care.

**Following up.** Silence usually means that the reader is busy, not that they are offended or have refused. A follow-up should therefore be friendly and easy to answer.

Reply in the same thread so that the original is below, restate the request in one line, and give the reader an easy reply. A second channel might be a phone call to an office, or asking at the end of a class; for a stranger who has not replied twice, letting it go is usually best. The waiting times are rough conventions, not rules. You can drag the boxes.
:::

::: theorem Following up, apologising and refusing in writing {#rule-follow-up}
1. **Follow-ups** reply in the same thread, restate the request briefly, and assume good faith: *I'm just following up on my email below about the reference — I know it's a busy time. The deadline is 20 March, so if you're able to let me know by then whether you can write it, I'd be very grateful.* Avoid reproach (✗ *I still haven't received a reply*) and urgency you cannot justify.
2. **Apologies for a slow reply** are short: *Apologies for the slow reply* / *Sorry for not getting back to you sooner.* A long account of why you were busy turns the message towards you; one clause is enough (*— it's been a hectic week*). For a real mistake, use the apology set of [[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments#def-apology-set]]: responsibility and repair.
3. **Refusals in writing** follow [[pragmatics/requests-refusals#rule-refusal]], with extra care for clarity, since the reader cannot ask what you meant: thanks, regret, a clear refusal, a reason, and an alternative if possible.
4. **Confirming what was agreed** after a call or meeting is courteous and practical: *Thanks for the call this morning. Just to confirm what we agreed: …* — a short list of actions, names and dates, ending with *Let me know if I've missed anything* ([[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]]).
:::

::: example A refusal and a confirmation {#ex-refuse-confirm}
(a) Your colleague Marco has asked you by email to give a talk at his department's seminar next month. You cannot, because you will be at a conference. Write your reply. (b) After a video call with a client, Ms Haddad, you agreed that you would send a revised quotation by Thursday, she would send the floor plans by Wednesday, and you would meet again on the 18th. Write the follow-up email.
::: solution
(a) *Hi Marco, thanks so much for thinking of me — I'd have loved to. Unfortunately I'll be at a conference in Porto for the whole of that week, so I won't be able to make it. Would a date in the spring work instead? If not, my colleague Aiyana Begay works on very similar questions and might be interested — I'd be happy to introduce you. All the best, Ruth* — appreciation, regret, a clear refusal with a specific reason, and two alternatives.

(b) *Subject: Follow-up to today's call — quotation and floor plans*

*Dear Ms Haddad,*

*Thank you for your time this morning. Just to confirm what we agreed:*

*- You will send us the floor plans by Wednesday 9th.*
*- We will send you a revised quotation by Thursday 10th.*
*- We will meet again by video on Tuesday 18th at 10 a.m.*

*Please let me know if I've missed or misunderstood anything.*

*Kind regards,*
*Daniel Osei*

The confirmation is neutral and precise; it lists both parties' actions (so it does not read as a list of demands), and its last line invites correction.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which follow-up is most likely to get a helpful reply from a busy lecturer?
- [ ] *Why haven't you answered my email?*
- [ ] *I sent you an email last week and still haven't received a reply.*
- [x] *Just following up on my email below — I know it's a busy time. Would you be able to let me know by Friday whether the extension is possible?*
- [ ] *URGENT!!! Please reply.*
::: solution
The third assumes good faith, refers to the original (*below*), restates the request in one line and gives a date. The first two reproach the reader; the fourth creates pressure without a reason.
:::
:::

::: example An apology by email {#ex-email-apology}
You promised to send your group your part of a joint presentation by Monday; it is now Wednesday and you have sent nothing, because you misjudged how long it would take. Write a short email to the group, and explain your choices.
::: solution
*Subject: My slides — sorry for the delay*

*Hi all,*

*I'm really sorry — I said I'd send my slides by Monday and I haven't. I underestimated how long the data section would take, which is my fault. They'll be in the shared folder by 6 p.m. tomorrow (Thursday). If that leaves too little time before Friday's rehearsal, I'm happy to stay on afterwards to help put everything together.*

*Sorry again for holding things up.*

*Kofi*

**Choices.** The subject line announces both the topic and the apology, so the group knows at once. The apology uses the set of [[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments#def-apology-set]]: an intensified expression (*really sorry*), acknowledgement of the broken promise and of responsibility (*which is my fault*), a one-clause account that explains without excusing, a specific repair with a time, and an extra offer to reduce the harm. It is short — a long account of how busy he has been would turn the message towards himself — and it ends with a brief second apology rather than a string of them.
:::
:::

## Messages and chat at work

Workplace chat is closer to speech than email: messages are short, greetings are often skipped after the first message of the day, and emoji and reactions do the work of back-channelling. The conventions vary widely between workplaces, so the best guide is the style of the people you write to ([[everyday-english/phone-messages]]). Three points apply widely. First, a message that says only *Hi* or *Are you there?* and waits for an answer before giving the question makes the reader wait; put the question in the same message (*Hi Priya — quick question: is the meeting at 2 or 3?*). Second, very short replies (*OK.*, *Fine.*) can read as cold in some communities; *OK, thanks!* or a reaction is warmer. Third, chat is easily forwarded and searched: write nothing you would not say in the open office.

::: note English and Chinese
- **老师 and *Dear Teacher*.** 老师 is a respectful form of address in Chinese; English uses the title and surname instead (*Dear Dr Wang*, *Dear Ms Evans*). Teachers in English-speaking schools may be called *Miss* or *Sir* by children, but not in university emails.
- **尊敬的 and *Respected*.** *Respected Professor* is a word-for-word version of 尊敬的教授; in British and American English it sounds unusual, though *Respected Sir/Madam* is normal in Indian English. *Dear* is the standard neutral opening in both formal and informal emails.
- **期待您的回复 and 等您回复.** *Waiting for your reply* can sound impatient; use *I look forward to hearing from you* (formal) or simply *Thank you for your time*.
- **Reasons first.** If you tend to give the background before the request, keep the background but move the request to the first lines; busy readers decide from the start what an email wants ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures#rule-dimensions]]).
- **此致敬礼 and closings.** Chinese letter closings have no direct English equivalent. *Best wishes*, *Kind regards* and *Best regards* are safe in most professional emails; *Yours sincerely* is formal and mainly British; *Cheers* is informal.
- **收到 and *Received*.** A one-word acknowledgement such as 收到 is efficient in Chinese chat; in English, *Received* alone can sound like a machine. *Got it, thanks!* or *Thanks — will do* is friendlier.
- **Varieties of English.** Some forms common in emails in South Asian and other Englishes — *Kindly revert* (‘please reply’), *Please do the needful*, *Respected Sir* — are standard in those varieties, not errors ([[world-englishes/south-asian-englishes]]). Readers elsewhere may not know them, so in international emails *Could you let me know…?* is safer.
:::

::: history
Model letters for every occasion are older than modern English: Renaissance handbooks of letter-writing followed Latin models, and in English the novelist Samuel Richardson's *Letters Written to and for Particular Friends* (1741) was one of many collections of model letters for ordinary people. The modern email was developed on ARPANET in the early 1970s, and its etiquette was first written down by its users: the "Netiquette Guidelines" published by the Internet Engineering Task Force as RFC 1855 (1995) advised, among other things, against writing in capitals, which "looks as if you're SHOUTING". Research on email politeness grew in the 2000s with the spread of email in universities, much of it on students' requests to teachers (Biesenbach-Lucas 2007; Stephens, Houser & Cowan 2009; Economidou-Kogetsidis 2011), applying the request categories of the Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project to a new medium.
:::

::: summary
- **Tone** is the attitude the reader infers from many small choices; in writing it rests on the words alone, so emails often need more explicit politeness than speech ([[#def-tone]]).
- Research shows that teachers judge overly direct or casual emails as less polite and are less willing to help — readers interpret pragmalinguistic choices as attitudes.
- Before writing, consider the reader's **power** and **distance**, the **imposition**, what they need to know, and how they will read ([[#rule-four-questions]]).
- Address strangers with **title + surname**, never a title alone or title + given name; follow the reader's lead afterwards ([[#rule-address]]).
- Request emails to people with power: who you are, purpose early, an indirect specific request, a reason, ease of compliance, a softened deadline and non-presumptuous thanks ([[#rule-email-request]]).
- To strangers: a connection, a specific reason, a small request, brevity and an easy way out ([[#rule-cold-email]]).
- Follow up politely in the same thread; apologise briefly for slow replies; refuse clearly; confirm agreements in writing ([[#rule-follow-up]]).
:::

A model:

*Subject: Broken heating — Room 214, Elm House*

*Dear Housing Office,*

*I'm writing to report that the heating in my room (Room 214, Elm House) has not been working since last Thursday, 3 November. The radiator stays cold even when the thermostat is on its highest setting. As the temperature at night has been below freezing, I would be very grateful if someone could come and look at it as soon as possible — ideally today or tomorrow. I'm in the room after 4 p.m. every day this week.*

*Thank you for your help.*

*Best regards,*
*Tran Minh Anh (student ID 21058834)*

*Dear Minh Anh,*

*Thank you for letting us know, and I'm sorry you've been without heating. I've logged a repair request (reference 7731) and marked it as urgent; a technician should contact you within 24 hours. For future repairs, you can also use the form on our website, which goes straight to the maintenance team.*

*If the room gets too cold tonight, please call the night porter (the number is on the back of your door) — they can provide a portable heater.*

*Kind regards,*
*Sam, Housing Office*

Changes to the student's email: a specific subject line; a greeting; identifying details (room, building, student ID) that make the request easy to act on; the date the problem began, with *since* used correctly (✗ *since 5 days* → *for five days* or *since 3 November*); the reason for urgency stated as fact (*below freezing*) instead of *This is not acceptable* and *You must*; a conditional request with a time (*I would be very grateful if… ideally today or tomorrow*); availability; thanks. The complaint remains firm — the facts do the work.

Changes to the office's reply: thanks and an apology (sympathy); action taken, with a reference number; what will happen next; the form mentioned as information for the future rather than as a refusal to help; a practical offer for tonight; a name, so that the reply comes from a person.
