Lucía and her flatmate Chen Jie are in the kitchen on a Thursday evening.

**Lucía:** *Are you coming to Martin's party on Saturday?*

**Chen Jie:** *I've got an exam on Monday.*

**Lucía:** *Oh, that's a shame. Good luck with it!*

Chen Jie never said *no*. Her answer was about an exam, not a party, and *I've got an exam on Monday* is perfectly compatible, logically, with going to a party on Saturday. Yet Lucía understood at once that Chen Jie was not coming, and she was right. Chen Jie, for her part, chose this answer on purpose: it refuses and explains the refusal in one sentence, and it does so more gently than *No, I'm not coming*.

Now a second exchange, a week later, between Chen Jie and her tutor, Dr Hartley, about a draft essay:

**Chen Jie:** *Did you have a chance to look at my draft?*

**Dr Hartley:** *I did. The references are very nicely formatted.*

Chen Jie went home pleased. Her tutor had praised her work. Her flatmate, when she heard the story, was less sure: a tutor who reads a whole essay and comments only on the formatting of the references may be saying, politely, that she has nothing good to say about the argument.

Both exchanges show the gap between **what is said** and **what is meant**. Every competent speaker uses that gap all the time, in every language, mostly without noticing; this chapter makes it visible. It explains how listeners work out meaning that is not in the words, using a theory proposed by the philosopher Paul Grice, and how a later theory, relevance theory, refines it. You have already met inference in reading ([[reading-skills/inference]]) and, in [[listening-and-conversation/opinions]], disagreement that is announced by *Well…* and a delay rather than by the word *no*. Here we look at the mechanism behind both.

## What is said and what is meant

::: definition Pragmatics {#def-pragmatics}
**Pragmatics** is the study of meaning in use: what a speaker means by an utterance in a particular context, as opposed to what the sentence means in itself. **Semantics** studies the meaning of words and sentences (*bank* ‘the side of a river’ or ‘a financial institution’); pragmatics studies how a particular use of them is understood (*I'm going to the bank* means the financial one when said by someone holding a cheque).
:::

The distinction matters because the same sentence can do very different jobs. *It's ten o'clock* states the time. Said by a parent to a teenager still watching television, it means ‘go to bed’. Said by a host as guests linger at the door, it may mean ‘please leave’. Said by a colleague who is waiting for you at a meeting that began at half past nine, it is a complaint. The sentence has not changed; the context has, and with it the meaning.

Context includes at least four things: the **physical situation** (who is present, what can be seen); the **preceding talk** (what has just been said); the **relationship** between the speakers (friends, strangers, teacher and student); and **shared knowledge** — what both people know, and know that the other knows. Chen Jie's answer works only because she and Lucía both know that people study before exams and that exams and late parties do not mix.

::: definition Implicature {#def-implicature}
An **implicature** is something a speaker means, and expects the hearer to understand, without saying it. Grice (1975) distinguished:

1. **Conventional implicature**, attached to particular words regardless of context: *She is poor **but** honest* says that she is poor and honest, and implicates, through *but*, a contrast between the two.
2. **Conversational implicature**, which arises from the context and from the assumption that the speaker is cooperating: *I've got an exam on Monday* implicates ‘I'm not coming to the party’ only as an answer to Lucía's question.

In everyday use, and in this course, *implicature* without a qualifier means conversational implicature. The verb is **implicate**; the hearer **infers** what the speaker implicates.
:::

Two related terms help to keep the picture clear. What a sentence logically guarantees is its **entailment**: *Chen Jie has an exam on Monday* entails *Someone has an exam on Monday*, and no context can change that. A **presupposition** is what an utterance takes for granted as background: *Have you stopped working at weekends?* presupposes that you used to work at weekends, which is why it is hard to answer either *yes* or *no* if you never did. Implicature is different from both: it is not guaranteed by the words, and the speaker can deny it without contradiction.

## The Cooperative Principle

Why does Lucía conclude that Chen Jie is not coming? Grice's answer, first given in his William James Lectures at Harvard in 1967 and published as "Logic and Conversation" (1975), was that conversation is a cooperative activity, and that hearers assume, unless they have reason to think otherwise, that speakers are trying to cooperate.

::: theorem The Cooperative Principle and the maxims {#rule-cp}
Grice (1975: 45) stated the **Cooperative Principle**: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged." He divided it into four groups of **maxims**:

1. **Quantity**: make your contribution as informative as is required; do not make it more informative than is required.
2. **Quality**: try to make your contribution one that is true — do not say what you believe to be false, and do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence.
3. **Relation**: be relevant.
4. **Manner**: be perspicuous — avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief, and be orderly.
:::

::: proof
The maxims are not rules of good behaviour that speakers are told to follow, and Grice did not claim that people always follow them. They describe the **assumptions a hearer makes** in order to interpret what a speaker says. Because Lucía assumes that Chen Jie's answer is relevant to her question (Relation), she looks for a connection between an exam on Monday and a party on Saturday, and the obvious one is that Chen Jie will be studying. The implicature is the extra assumption that makes the answer cooperative. That is why it is **calculable**: a hearer can, in principle, spell out the reasoning step by step, as in the figure below. And it is why the same words implicate nothing at all in another context — *I've got an exam on Monday* in answer to *What's new?* is simply news.
:::

::: widget graph

That assumption is what the speaker implicated. In real conversation this happens in a fraction of a second and is rarely conscious. You can drag the boxes.

## Four ways of not following a maxim

Speakers do not always follow the maxims, and Grice distinguished several ways of failing to do so. Only one of them is the main source of implicature.

::: theorem Observing, violating, opting out and flouting {#rule-flouting}
A speaker can:

1. **observe** the maxims — say what is true, relevant, clear and as informative as needed. Implicatures still arise, because the hearer assumes the maxims are being observed: *There's a pharmacy round the corner*, said to someone with a headache, implicates that it is open and sells painkillers.
2. **violate** a maxim quietly, intending the hearer not to notice: a lie violates Quality; a misleading half-truth violates Quantity. No implicature is intended — the aim is to deceive.
3. **opt out**, saying openly that they will not cooperate in the usual way: *I'm afraid I can't comment on that*; *My lips are sealed.*
4. face a **clash**, when observing one maxim means breaking another: *Where does Aiko live? — Somewhere in the south of France* is less informative than asked (Quantity) because the speaker does not know more and must not say what she lacks evidence for (Quality). The hearer infers: ‘she doesn't know exactly’.
5. **flout** a maxim — break it so obviously that the hearer is meant to notice, and to look for another meaning. Flouting is the main way speakers create implicatures on purpose.
:::

::: proof
A violation and a flouting may look alike — both say something untrue, or too little, or irrelevant — but they differ in what the speaker intends the hearer to recognise. A liar hopes the hearer will believe the literal content; a speaker who says *Lovely weather!* in a downpour wants the hearer to see at once that the literal content is false, and to infer an ironic meaning ([[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]). Dr Hartley's comment on the references is a flouting of Quantity: she says far less than a tutor's comment on a draft is expected to say, and she says it in a way that draws attention to what is missing.
:::

Each maxim can be flouted, and each kind of flouting has typical uses in English:

| maxim flouted | example | what is implicated |
|---|---|---|
| Quantity (too little) | *How was the concert? — The venue was nice.* | ‘the music was not good’ |
| Quantity (too little, tautology) | *Rules are rules.* / *It is what it is.* | ‘nothing can be done, so stop arguing’ |
| Quantity (too much) | *I did my homework, and I washed my hands, and I didn't touch the biscuits.* | ‘I'm protesting too much — perhaps I did touch them’ |
| Quality | *Oh, brilliant* (after a coffee is spilt); *He's a walking dictionary.* | irony; metaphor |
| Relation | *How did the interview go? — Do you want a cup of tea?* | ‘I don't want to talk about it’ |
| Manner | *Shall we get the children an I-C-E C-R-E-A-M?* | ‘I don't want the children to understand’ |

::: example Calculating implicatures {#ex-calculate}
For each exchange, say which maxim seems to be broken, whether it is flouted, and what the second speaker implicates.

(a) **Kemal:** *Is Ingrid a good manager?* **Ayumi:** *She's always very punctual.*

(b) **Marta:** *Where are my keys?* **Kofi:** *Have you looked in your coat?*

(c) **Customer:** *Is the fish fresh?* **Waiter:** *The chicken's very popular tonight.*

(d) **Parent:** *Did you finish your homework and tidy your room?* **Teenager:** *I finished my homework.*
::: solution
(a) Quantity is flouted: asked for an overall judgement, Ayumi gives one small positive fact. Assuming she is cooperative, the most likely reason is that she has nothing more positive to say. Implicature: ‘as a manager she is not very good’. The minimal praise is a polite way of avoiding open criticism.

(b) Relation is apparently broken — a question answered by a question — but not really flouted: Kofi's question is relevant if he thinks the keys may be in the coat. Implicature: ‘your keys may be in your coat’. This is an implicature arising when the maxims are **observed**.

(c) Relation is flouted: the waiter answers a question about fish by talking about chicken. Implicature: ‘I'd recommend the chicken rather than the fish’, and probably ‘the fish is not very fresh’ — something the waiter cannot say openly to a customer.

(d) Quantity: the parent asked about two things and the teenager answers about one. Since the teenager would surely mention a tidy room if the room were tidy, the parent infers ‘I didn't tidy my room’. This is a **scalar**-type reasoning: saying less implicates that the more did not happen (see the next section).
:::
:::

::: quiz
Ji-ho asks his colleague: *Do you think the new software is any good?* She answers: *Well, the logo's lovely.* What does she most probably mean?
- [ ] She is interested only in design.
- [ ] She has not tried the software yet.
- [x] She does not think the software is good.
- [ ] She thinks the software is excellent, including the logo.
::: solution
She flouts Quantity and arguably Relation: asked about the software as a whole, she praises only its logo, a detail that does not matter to whether software is good. Assuming she is cooperating, Ji-ho infers that she has nothing better to say about it. The *Well* at the start, typical of a dispreferred answer ([[listening-and-conversation/opinions]]), supports that reading.
:::

## Implicatures without special context

The implicatures so far depend on a particular situation. Grice noticed another kind, which arises almost whenever certain words are used, unless something blocks it. He called these **generalised** conversational implicatures, as opposed to the **particularised** ones that need a special context.

::: definition Scalar implicature {#def-scalar}
Some words form **scales** of strength: *some* < *most* < *all*; *possible* < *likely* < *certain*; *warm* < *hot*; *sometimes* < *often* < *always*; *good* < *excellent*. When a speaker uses a weaker word on a scale, the hearer usually infers that the stronger words do not apply — because if they did, the maxim of Quantity would have required the speaker to say so. This is a **scalar implicature** (Horn 1972). *Some of the students passed* implicates ‘not all of them passed’.
:::

The same reasoning explains two other common implicatures.

- ***Or*** is usually understood as ‘one or the other but not both’: *You can have tea or coffee* implicates that you cannot have both. In logic, *p or q* is true when both are true; in conversation, the speaker would have said *and* if both were on offer.
- ***And*** between events is usually understood as ‘and then’, following the maxim of Manner (*be orderly*): *She got a job and moved to Glasgow* and *She moved to Glasgow and got a job* tell different stories, though they are logically equivalent.

::: widget truthtable
title: Logical *or* and conversational *or*
formula: p | q
compare: p xor q
caption: The first formula is logical, inclusive *or*: *You can have tea or coffee* is true if you have tea (p), coffee (q) or both. The second is exclusive *or*, which is how most hearers understand the sentence in conversation. The two differ in exactly one row — when p and q are both true. That row is ruled out not by the meaning of *or* but by a scalar implicature: if both were allowed, a cooperative speaker would have said *and* ([[#def-scalar]]).
:::

::: theorem Implicatures can be cancelled {#rule-cancel}
A conversational implicature can be **cancelled** — denied or suspended by the speaker — without contradiction: *Some of the students passed — in fact, all of them did.* *You can have tea or coffee, or both if you like.* *I've got an exam on Monday, but I'll come for an hour.* An entailment cannot be cancelled: ✗ *All of the students passed — in fact, none of them did* is a contradiction.
:::

::: proof
Cancellability follows from what an implicature is: an inference the hearer draws by assuming cooperation, not something the words guarantee. If the speaker adds information that makes the inference unnecessary, the inference disappears. This is a useful test for learners and analysts alike. If you can add *…but not really* or *…in fact, more than that* without sounding self-contradictory, the meaning you are testing was implicated, not said. It also explains why implicature is a useful tool for politeness: because an implicated refusal or criticism can be denied (*I didn't say it was bad!*), it is less of a threat than a stated one ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]).
:::

::: warning Saying *some* when you mean *all*
✗ *Some of the participants completed the survey* (when all of them did). ✓ *All the participants completed the survey.*

✗ *The results are possibly significant* (when the statistics show they are). ✓ *The results are significant.*

Because of scalar implicature, a weaker word is not a "safe" or "modest" version of a stronger one: it tells the reader that the stronger one is **not** true. Learners who hedge everything to sound polite or academic can mislead readers in this way. Hedge when you are genuinely uncertain ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]), not as a reflex.
:::

::: example Testing implicature and entailment {#ex-cancel}
For each sentence, decide whether the meaning in brackets is entailed or implicated. Use the cancellation test.

(a) *Most of the tickets have been sold.* (‘not all of them have been sold’)
(b) *Most of the tickets have been sold.* (‘some of the tickets have been sold’)
(c) *Ravi tried to open the window.* (‘Ravi did not succeed’)
(d) *It's possible that the train will be late.* (‘it's not certain that the train will be late’)
(e) *Hana ate three of the dumplings.* (‘Hana ate some of the dumplings’)
::: solution
(a) Implicated: *Most of the tickets have been sold — in fact, all of them have* is not a contradiction.
(b) Entailed: ✗ *Most of the tickets have been sold, but none of them have* is a contradiction.
(c) Implicated: *Ravi tried to open the window, and he managed it in the end* is fine. *Try* suggests difficulty and possible failure without stating it.
(d) Implicated: *It's possible that the train will be late — actually, it's certain; I've just seen the board* is acceptable.
(e) Entailed: eating three entails eating some. (Whether *three* implicates ‘exactly three’ — *She ate three, in fact four* — is debated among linguists, but the ‘some’ part cannot be cancelled.)
:::
:::

## Relevance theory: one principle instead of four

Grice's maxims have been criticised from several directions: they are hard to keep apart (is Dr Hartley's comment a flouting of Quantity or of Relation?), and they say little about *how* hearers choose between the many assumptions that might make an utterance fit. The most influential alternative is **relevance theory**, proposed by Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson in *Relevance: Communication and Cognition* (1986; second edition 1995).

::: definition Relevance and explicature {#def-relevance}
In relevance theory, an input is **relevant** to the degree that it produces worthwhile **cognitive effects** (new conclusions, strengthened or abandoned beliefs) for a small **processing effort**. Sperber and Wilson's **communicative principle of relevance** holds that every utterance communicates a presumption of its own optimal relevance: the hearer is entitled to expect that it is worth the effort of processing, and that the speaker chose the most relevant wording compatible with her abilities and preferences. What is communicated explicitly, after the hearer has filled in what the words leave open, is the **explicature**; what is communicated beyond that is the implicature.
:::

Relevance theory stresses that even the "explicit" meaning needs pragmatic work. *I haven't eaten* is literally true of nobody over the age of a few hours; a hearer understands ‘I haven't eaten **today**’ or ‘**since breakfast**’. *It'll take some time* is literally true of everything; the hearer understands ‘longer than you might expect’. *The park is near* needs a standard of nearness: near for a walk, or near for a drive? Filling in such gaps — reference, time, degree — gives the explicature, and it uses context as much as implicature does.

::: example Explicature and implicature {#ex-explicature}
Hamid has just moved into a shared house. He asks his housemate Nell: *Is there a supermarket near here?* Nell replies: *There's a Lidl on the high street, but it closes at eight.* It is 8.15 p.m. Separate the explicature from the implicatures.
::: solution
**Explicature** (what Nell communicates explicitly, once the gaps are filled): ‘There is a Lidl supermarket on the high street of this neighbourhood, and it closes at eight o'clock in the evening, every day or at least today.’ Hamid has to fill in *the high street* (the one near the house), *eight* (p.m., not a.m.) and *closes* (habitually, so also tonight).

**Implicatures**: (1) ‘The Lidl is near enough to count as *near here*’ — otherwise the answer would not be relevant. (2) ‘You can't go there now’ — the *but* clause is relevant only because it is already after eight. (3) Possibly: ‘If you need something tonight, you'll have to try somewhere else, or ask me.’

The reasoning that gets Hamid from the words to (2) is the search for relevance: of all the things Nell could have said, she chose the closing time, so the closing time must matter.
:::
:::

::: remark Grice or relevance?
Both theories are still used. Gricean and "neo-Gricean" accounts (Horn; Levinson, *Presumptive Meanings*, 2000) are convenient for describing types of implicature and for teaching, because the maxims give names to what is going on. Relevance theory is more often used in psychology and in the study of how understanding happens in the mind. For a learner, the practical lesson is the same: when a reply seems not to answer the question, assume that it does, and ask yourself what would make it an answer.
:::

## Implicature across languages

Implicature is not an English speciality. Every language community relies on hearers to infer more than is said, and Chinese has a rich vocabulary for the phenomenon: 言外之意 and 弦外之音 for meaning beyond the words, 话里有话 for words with something behind them, and 听话听音 for the advice to listen to the tone and not only the words. What differs between communities, and between people, is **which** meanings are usually left implicit, and in which situations.

::: note English and Chinese
- **The same mechanism, different conventions.** A Chinese speaker who answers an invitation with 我那天可能有点事 relies on exactly the implicature that Chen Jie used: an answer that seems not to answer is a polite refusal. English listeners usually understand such answers too. Problems arise more often with **conventional** formulas whose implicature is specific to one language: *We should get coffee sometime* (especially in American English) often means ‘I'd be happy to see you again’, not a concrete invitation, much as 改天请你吃饭 often does not fix a date.
- **Faint praise.** In British and American academic and professional settings, praise of a minor feature in place of an overall judgement (*The references are nicely formatted*) is a recognised way of implicating criticism. Some learners, used to more explicit feedback, miss it. If you are unsure, ask a direct, polite question: *Thank you — is there anything in the argument you think I should work on?*
- **Answering the literal question.** *Do you know what time it is?* is not a question about knowledge. Answering *Yes* (and nothing else) is a joke in English; answer with the time. The same applies to *Have you got a minute?* and *Is there any more coffee?* — the expected answer addresses the implicated request ([[pragmatics/speech-acts]]).
- **Over-reading.** The opposite risk is inferring a hidden meaning where none was intended. Some English speakers, especially in casual talk, mean exactly what they say: *I'm fine with either* usually really means ‘I don't mind’. Individual speakers differ, and context is the best guide.
:::

::: example A reference letter {#ex-reference}
Professor Adeyemi is asked to write a reference for a former student, Mr Tan, who has applied for a research post. The whole letter reads:

*Dear Dr Wilson,*

*Mr Tan attended my course on data analysis in 2024. He was always polite in class, and his handwriting is very clear.*

*Yours sincerely,*
*Olufemi Adeyemi*

What does the letter implicate, how, and why might Professor Adeyemi write it this way?
::: solution
The letter **flouts Quantity**: a reference for a research post is expected to discuss ability, knowledge and reliability, and this one says nothing about them. Professor Adeyemi clearly knows Mr Tan, so the gap cannot be explained by lack of knowledge (that would be a clash with Quality). It also seems to **flout Relation**: politeness and handwriting are irrelevant to research. A reader who assumes the writer is cooperative concludes: ‘I have nothing positive to say about his academic ability.’

Why write this way? Saying *He is a weak student* openly would be hard to justify if challenged, would hurt the student if he saw it, and could be denied less easily. The implicature gives the reader the necessary information while leaving the literal text inoffensive — an example of how implicature serves politeness. (In practice, most referees who cannot support a candidate decline to write; a letter like this is itself a strong signal.)
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which sentence **cancels** an implicature without contradiction?
- [ ] *All my friends came — in fact, none of them did.*
- [x] *Some of my friends came — in fact, they all did.*
- [ ] *My sister is a doctor, but she isn't a woman.*
- [ ] *I've stopped smoking, though I've never smoked.*
::: solution
*Some* implicates ‘not all’, and the implicature can be cancelled: the second sentence is fine. *All…none* contradicts an entailment; *sister* entails ‘female’; *I've stopped smoking* presupposes that the speaker used to smoke, so *I've never smoked* clashes with it.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Tasha says: *I'm not saying the plan is bad.* Her colleague replies: *But you're not saying it's good, either.* What is the colleague drawing attention to?
- [ ] An entailment of Tasha's sentence
- [x] A possible implicature of Tasha's sentence
- [ ] A grammatical mistake in Tasha's sentence
- [ ] A presupposition that the plan exists
::: solution
*I'm not saying the plan is bad* does not entail anything about the plan being good, but saying only the weaker thing can implicate reservations (Quantity). The colleague makes the implicature explicit so that Tasha must either confirm or cancel it.
:::
:::

::: history
The philosopher H. P. Grice (1913–1988) gave the William James Lectures at Harvard in 1967. Part of them was published as "Logic and Conversation" in *Syntax and Semantics, Volume 3: Speech Acts*, edited by Peter Cole and Jerry Morgan (1975), and the lectures were later collected in Grice's *Studies in the Way of Words* (1989). His aim was partly philosophical: to show that words such as *and*, *or* and *if* mean in ordinary language what they mean in logic, with the extra meanings explained by conversational principles. Laurence Horn's doctoral dissertation (UCLA, 1972) developed the theory of scales behind scalar implicature. In 1986 Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson published *Relevance: Communication and Cognition*, which replaced the maxims with a single principle grounded in cognitive psychology; Stephen Levinson's *Presumptive Meanings* (2000) defended a neo-Gricean theory of generalised implicatures. Grice's ideas were quickly taken up by linguists, and by teachers of English, who recognised in the maxims a vocabulary for what learners often find hardest: hearing what is not said.
:::

## Where this leads

The next chapter, [[pragmatics/speech-acts]], asks what speakers *do* with utterances — request, promise, apologise — and why *Can you pass the salt?* is a request. Implicature is one of the main tools of politeness, the subject of [[pragmatics/politeness-face]], and of refusals in [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]. Flouting the maxim of Quality gives irony, metaphor and understatement ([[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]). Inference in reading is the subject of [[reading-skills/inference]], and stance and hedging in academic texts, where scalar words matter a great deal, of [[academic-reading/stance-and-hedging]]. The theory is placed in the wider field of linguistics in [[linguistics/pragmatics-discourse]].

::: summary
- **Pragmatics** studies what speakers mean in context; the same sentence can state, request, complain or dismiss depending on the situation ([[#def-pragmatics]]).
- An **implicature** is meant but not said; conversational implicatures depend on context and on the assumption that the speaker is cooperating ([[#def-implicature]]).
- Grice's **Cooperative Principle** has four maxims — Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner — that describe what hearers assume ([[#rule-cp]]).
- Speakers can observe, violate, opt out of or **flout** a maxim; flouting, an obvious breach meant to be noticed, is the main source of deliberate implicature ([[#rule-flouting]]).
- **Scalar implicature**: a weaker word (*some*, *possible*, *warm*) implicates that the stronger one does not apply; *or* implicates ‘not both’, *and* ‘and then’ ([[#def-scalar]]).
- Implicatures can be **cancelled** without contradiction; entailments cannot ([[#rule-cancel]]).
- **Relevance theory** explains interpretation as a search for the most relevant reading; even the explicit meaning (**explicature**) needs context ([[#def-relevance]]).
- All languages use implicature; what differs is which meanings are conventionally left unsaid. When unsure, a polite direct question is better than a guess.
:::

::: exercise Scales {level=1}
Write the stronger word or words on the scale, and the implicature of the sentence.

(a) *The soup is warm.* (b) *I sometimes cycle to work.* (c) *It's possible we'll finish by June.* (d) *The essay was good.* (e) *I like her.*
::: solution
(a) *hot*; ‘not hot’. (b) *often*, *usually*, *always*; ‘not often / not always’. (c) *likely*, *certain*; ‘it's not certain, and maybe not even likely’. (d) *excellent*, *outstanding*; ‘not excellent’. (e) *love*; in some contexts (*Do you love her? — I like her*) ‘I don't love her’.
:::
:::

::: exercise Cancel it {level=2}
Add a clause that cancels the implicature of each sentence without contradiction.

(a) *Some of the guests stayed until midnight.* (b) *You can pay by card or in cash.* (c) *Liang tried to call you.* (d) *It's quite warm today.*
::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *…in fact, all of them did.* (b) *…or partly by each, if you prefer.* (c) *…and he got through, actually — didn't he tell you?* (d) *…in fact, it's hot — thirty degrees.*
:::
:::

::: exercise Explicature {level=2}
Say what the hearer has to fill in to understand each utterance explicitly.

(a) *I've had enough.* (said by a guest when offered more rice) (b) *Everyone was there.* (said about a colleague's leaving party) (c) *It's not far.* (said by a local to a tourist on foot) (d) *She's ready.* (said by a parent at the school gate)
::: solution
(a) ‘enough **rice / food**, **for now**’. (b) ‘everyone **from our team / who was invited**’, not everyone in the world. (c) ‘not far **to walk**’ — perhaps ten or fifteen minutes. (d) ‘ready **to go home / for the trip**’, depending on what the parent and teacher are waiting for.
:::
:::

::: exercise Faint praise {level=2}
Your supervisor writes on your report: *Thanks for this. The charts look very professional.* Nothing else. (a) What might be implicated? (b) Is the implicature certain? (c) Write a polite email reply that finds out what she really thinks.
::: solution
(a) Possibly ‘I have reservations about the content, the analysis or the conclusions’: praise of the presentation alone can flout Quantity. (b) No. Implicatures can be cancelled, and the supervisor may simply be busy or brief by habit. (c) *Thank you for reading the report — I'm glad the charts work. Do you have any comments on the analysis or the conclusions? I'd be very happy to revise anything before it goes to the client.* This invites criticism without suggesting that the supervisor was being evasive.
:::
:::

::: exercise Analyse a conversation {level=3}
Identify every implicature in this conversation, and say which maxim or principle gives rise to each.

**Rosa:** *Have you seen the new Thai place on Mill Road?*
**Dev:** *I went on Friday.*
**Rosa:** *And?*
**Dev:** *Well, the decor's fun.*
**Rosa:** *Ah. I was going to suggest it for Sunday.*
**Dev:** *There's always the Lebanese place.*
::: hint
Look at what each speaker could have said and did not.
:::
::: solution
- *I went on Friday* — answers more than *yes* and implicates ‘so I can tell you about it’ (relevance to the question behind Rosa's question: *what is it like?*).
- *And?* — Rosa asks for the evaluation that Dev's answer left out; she treats his reply as incomplete for her purpose (Quantity).
- *Well, the decor's fun* — *Well* marks a dispreferred answer; praising only the decor flouts Quantity, implicating ‘the food was not good’.
- *Ah* — Rosa shows she has received the implicature. *I was going to suggest it for Sunday* implicates ‘but I won't now’ (the past progressive *was going to* signals an abandoned plan) and also, by Relation, ‘we have plans for Sunday — where shall we go?’
- *There's always the Lebanese place* — a statement of fact that, by Relation, is a suggestion: ‘let's go to the Lebanese place on Sunday’. *Always* implicates that it is a reliable, familiar option.

Neither speaker says *The food was bad*, *I won't suggest it* or *Let's go to the Lebanese place*, but both understand all three.
:::
:::

::: exercise Say it without saying it {level=3}
Write a reply for each situation that conveys the message in brackets by implicature only, and name the maxim you flout or exploit. Then write a second, explicit reply, and say when the explicit one would be the better choice.

(a) A friend asks whether you liked the cake she baked. (You didn't.)
(b) A colleague asks if you can help move furniture on Saturday. (You can't — you're working.)
(c) A classmate asks what you got in the exam. (You don't want to say.)
::: solution
Model answers:

(a) Implicit: *The icing was really pretty!* (Quantity — praise of one minor feature). Explicit: *Honestly, it was a bit dry for me — but the icing was lovely.* The explicit version is better if she has asked for honest feedback, for example before baking it for a competition.

(b) Implicit: *I'm at work all day Saturday, unfortunately.* (Relation — the hearer infers ‘so I can't help’). Explicit: *Sorry, I can't — I'm working all day.* Here both are fine and the difference is small; for a refusal, a reason is often enough ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]).

(c) Implicit: *Oh, it went OK. How about you?* (Quantity and Relation — a vague answer and a change of direction). Explicit: *I'd rather not say, if that's all right.* The explicit version is better if the classmate keeps asking and the implicature has failed.

In general, implicature protects relationships but can be missed; explicit wording is clearer but more face-threatening. Choose by how important it is that the message arrives.
