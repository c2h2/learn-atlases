Lin Xiaoyu is a doctoral student in Melbourne. One Tuesday, three small things happen.

In the morning, rushing into a café, she bumps into a man coming out.

**Lin Xiaoyu:** *Oh — sorry!*

**The man:** *Sorry!*

Both apologised, although only one of them could have been at fault, and neither expected anything more.

At lunchtime she gives her first seminar paper. Afterwards her supervisor, Professor Nguyen, comes up to her.

**Professor Nguyen:** *Xiaoyu, that was an excellent talk. Really clear.*

**Lin Xiaoyu:** *No, no, it was not good. I made many mistakes.*

**Professor Nguyen:** *Oh… well, I thought it went very well.*

Professor Nguyen looked slightly put out, as if her judgement had been questioned.

In the evening her flatmate, Josh, comes home to find she has cooked for both of them.

**Josh:** *Oh, wow, thanks so much! You didn't have to do that.*

**Lin Xiaoyu:** *You're welcome.*

That exchange went smoothly — but if Josh had apologised for being late instead, *You're welcome* would have been the wrong answer.

Apologies, thanks and compliments are **expressives** ([[pragmatics/speech-acts#rule-five-classes]]): they express the speaker's attitude to something that has happened. They are among the most frequent acts of everyday life, and among the most formulaic, which makes them easy to learn in one sense and easy to get slightly wrong in another. This chapter describes how each is done in English, how the other person usually responds, and how responses to compliments differ — and have changed — between English and Chinese speakers. Everyday phrases for thanking and compliments in small talk are in [[everyday-english/small-talk]]; the politeness theory behind them is in [[pragmatics/politeness-face]].

## Apologies

An apology repairs a relationship after an offence. In Brown and Levinson's terms it threatens the speaker's own positive face — it admits a fault — in order to restore the hearer's ([[pragmatics/politeness-face#def-pos-neg-face]]). Its conditions, set out in [[pragmatics/speech-acts#rule-searle-conditions]], require a past act of the speaker that harmed the hearer, and regret for it.

::: definition The apology speech act set {#def-apology-set}
Olshtain and Cohen (1983) showed that an apology is not a single formula but a **speech act set** — a group of strategies, any one of which can count as an apology, and which are often combined:

1. an **expression of apology** (IFID, ‘illocutionary force indicating device’): *Sorry*, *I'm sorry*, *I apologise*, *Excuse me*, *Forgive me*;
2. an **explanation or account**: *The bus broke down*;
3. an **acknowledgement of responsibility**: *It was my fault*, *I should have checked*, *I completely forgot*;
4. an **offer of repair**: *I'll pay for the cleaning*, *Let me get you another one*;
5. a **promise of forbearance**: *It won't happen again*.

The Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project (Blum-Kulka et al. 1989) used the same categories to compare apologies across languages.
:::

::: theorem Matching the apology to the offence {#rule-apology}
In English, the more serious the offence and the more distant or powerful the person offended, the more strategies are usually combined:

1. **Minor, accidental offences** (bumping into someone, interrupting): a bare expression is enough — *Sorry!*, *Oops, sorry*, *Excuse me*.
2. **Moderate offences** (arriving late, forgetting something you promised): expression + intensifier + account or responsibility — *I'm really sorry I'm late — the train was cancelled.*
3. **Serious offences** (damaging something, letting someone down badly, hurting their feelings): expression + responsibility + repair, often with a promise — *I'm so sorry. I broke your camera — it was completely my fault. I'll pay for the repair, of course.*

Intensifiers grow with the offence: *sorry* < *really sorry* < *so sorry* / *terribly sorry* (BrE) < *I can't tell you how sorry I am*. *I apologise* is more formal than *I'm sorry*, and common in writing and in service settings.
:::

::: proof
The strategies match the conditions of the act. Acknowledging responsibility makes clear that the speaker accepts that the act was theirs; an account explains it without denying it; an offer of repair gives practical redress for the harm; a promise of forbearance addresses the future of the relationship. A minor accident needs no account because nobody thinks the speaker intended it; a serious offence with only *Sorry* sounds as if the speaker has not recognised how serious it was. The **account** is the riskiest part: an explanation that sounds like an excuse (*I'm sorry, but the instructions were really unclear*) can shift the blame and turn the apology into a defence.
:::

Not every *sorry* is an apology. English, especially British English, uses *sorry* for many acts that are related to apology but are not apologies in Searle's sense:

- **Sympathy**: *I'm so sorry to hear about your grandmother* — the speaker is not responsible.
- **Requests for repetition**: *Sorry?* / *Sorry, what was that?* ([[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]).
- **Prefacing a request or interruption**: *Sorry to bother you, but…*; *Sorry — can I just say something?*
- **Prefacing disagreement or refusal**: *Sorry, but I don't think that's right*; *I'm sorry, I can't.*
- **Getting attention**: *Sorry — excuse me — is this the queue for tickets?*

This is why two strangers who bump into each other both say *Sorry*: it acknowledges the moment and shows that neither is going to make a fuss. It is not an admission of fault.

::: theorem Responding to an apology {#rule-accept-apology}
For a minor offence, accept the apology lightly and quickly: *That's OK*, *No problem*, *Don't worry (about it)*, *No worries* (AusE, NZE, and now widespread), *Never mind* (BrE), *No harm done.* For a serious offence, acknowledge the apology itself: *Thank you for saying that*, *I appreciate the apology*, *It's OK — these things happen.* Do not answer an apology with *You're welcome*, which answers thanks, or with *It doesn't matter* when it clearly does — say *It's OK* and, if needed, what you would like to happen next.
:::

::: warning Non-apologies and over-apologies
✗ *I'm sorry if you were offended.* / *I'm sorry you feel that way.* (as an apology for something you did) ✓ *I'm sorry — what I said was unkind.*

✗ *Sorry for my poor English* (at the start of every email). ✓ Leave it out, or once, if really necessary: *English isn't my first language, so please let me know if anything is unclear.*

✗ *Sorry, sorry, sorry, I'm so sorry, I'm really so sorry…* (for a small delay). ✓ *Sorry for the delay!*

The first pattern expresses regret about the hearer's reaction, not about the speaker's act, and is widely heard as a refusal to take responsibility. The second draws attention to a problem the reader may not have noticed, and lowers your own standing before you have begun. The third makes the hearer reassure you, which reverses the purpose of the apology.
:::

::: example Building apologies {#ex-apologies}
Write an apology for each situation and name the strategies you used.

(a) You step on someone's foot on a crowded bus. (b) You arrive 25 minutes late for a meeting with your thesis supervisor because you misread the time. (c) You borrowed your friend Nadia's laptop and spilt coffee on the keyboard; it no longer works.
::: solution
(a) *Oh, sorry!* — expression only. A minor accident needs nothing more; more would sound odd.

(b) *I'm really sorry I'm late. I misread the time in your email — that was my mistake. I hope it hasn't messed up your afternoon; if it helps, I'm happy to keep this short or come back another day.* — intensified expression; acknowledgement of responsibility (*my mistake*) instead of an excuse; concern for the hearer; an offer of repair. Note that *I misread the time* is an account that takes the blame rather than shifting it.

(c) *Nadia, I'm so, so sorry. I spilt coffee on your laptop and now it won't turn on. It was completely my fault — I shouldn't have had a drink next to it. I'll take it to be repaired today, and if it can't be fixed I'll replace it. I'll be much more careful in future.* — intensified expression; a clear account of what happened; responsibility; repair; forbearance. Telling her at once, before she discovers it, is itself part of the apology.
:::
:::

## Thanks

Thanking is usually simpler than apologising, but it is not as simple as it looks. Eisenstein and Bodman (1986), whose study of American English is titled after one learner's phrase, *"I very appreciate"*, found that advanced learners often knew the formulas but differed from native speakers in when they thanked, how much they elaborated, and how they responded — and that in more complex situations, such as thanking for a large favour, the learners' expressions often sounded too short or oddly worded.

::: theorem How thanks are expressed {#rule-thanks}
1. **Formulas**, from light to strong: *Thanks!* / *Cheers* (BrE, informal) — *Thank you* — *Thanks very much* / *Thanks a lot* — *Thank you so much* — *I really appreciate it* — *I'm very grateful* (formal) — *I can't thank you enough.*
2. **Thanking for something**: *Thank you **for** the flowers* / ***for** helping me*. *Appreciate* takes the thing as a direct object: ✓ *I really appreciate your help*; ✗ *I very appreciate*; ✗ *I appreciate you for your help* (in standard written English).
3. **Elaboration** for larger favours: say what the help made possible (*I couldn't have finished without you*), compliment the giver (*That was so thoughtful*), or acknowledge the cost to them (*You didn't have to do that*; *I know how busy you are*).
4. **Responses**: *You're welcome*; *No problem*; *Not at all* (BrE, formal); *My pleasure* / *It was a pleasure*; *Any time*; *No worries*; *Don't mention it*; *Glad I could help.* For thanks after a large favour, add warmth: *It was the least I could do*; *Honestly, I was happy to.*
:::

Some uses of thanks need care. **Thanking in advance** (*Thanks in advance for your help*) is common in emails, but some readers feel it takes their help for granted; *Thank you for considering this* or *I'd be grateful for any help* avoids the problem ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]). **Thanking for small services** is frequent in English — to a bus driver, to someone who holds a door, to a waiter who brings water — and its absence can be noticed more than its presence. And **thanking people close to you** is common in many English-speaking families: partners, parents and children often thank each other for small acts.

Thanks and apologies are closely related, because both respond to what someone has done. Florian Coulmas (1981) compared them across languages and observed that in Japanese, a gift or a favour is often acknowledged with an expression of apology (*sumimasen*), recognising the trouble caused to the giver, where English speakers would usually thank. Chinese 不好意思 and 麻烦你了 can do similar work. English has the pattern too, in a smaller way: *Sorry to have kept you waiting — thank you for your patience* combines the two, and *Thank you for your patience* is often used where *Sorry for the wait* would also be possible.

::: example Thanks that fit {#ex-thanks}
Write thanks suited to each situation, and a response.

(a) A stranger picks up a glove you dropped. (b) Your colleague, Ewa, stayed late to help you finish a report for a client. (c) Your former teacher wrote a reference that helped you get a scholarship.
::: solution
(a) *Oh, thank you!* — *No problem.* A light formula is enough.

(b) *Ewa, thank you so much for staying last night — I really appreciate it. I'd never have got it done on my own. Can I buy you lunch this week?* — *Ah, don't worry, it was fine! But I won't say no to lunch.* The thanks are intensified, the help is described, the cost is recognised and a small return is offered.

(c) A written message: *Dear Dr Adebayo, I wanted to let you know that I have been awarded the Kingsley Scholarship, and to thank you for your reference. I know how much time references take, and I am very grateful for your support, both in writing it and during your course. I'll let you know how my studies go. With best wishes, Arjun Mehta.* — formal thanks, news that shows the help mattered, acknowledgement of the cost, and a future-oriented close. The likely response: *Congratulations, Arjun — that's wonderful news, and well deserved.*
:::
:::

## Compliments

::: definition Compliment {#def-compliment}
A **compliment** attributes credit to someone, usually the hearer, for something that both speaker and hearer value — a possession, an appearance, a skill or an achievement (after Holmes 1986). Compliments are a classic **positive-politeness** strategy: they attend to the hearer's want to be approved of, and they build solidarity ([[pragmatics/politeness-face#rule-strategies]]).
:::

Compliments in English are surprisingly formulaic. Manes and Wolfson (1981), analysing a large collection of compliments heard in everyday American conversation, found that most followed one of three syntactic patterns, and that a handful of adjectives (*nice*, *good*, *beautiful*, *pretty*, *great*) and verbs (*like*, *love*) appeared in a large share of them:

1. *NP is / looks (really) ADJ*: *Your garden looks great.* *That jacket is really nice.*
2. *I (really) like / love NP*: *I love your earrings.* *I really like what you've done with the kitchen.*
3. *PRO is (really) (a) ADJ NP*: *That's a great idea.* *That was a really good talk.*

Formulas make compliments easy to recognise and easy to give. The usual **topics** are appearance and possessions (*I love your bag*), and performance and ability (*Great presentation!*, *This is delicious*). Wolfson (1988) observed, in her "bulge" theory, that compliments and other solidarity-building talk were most frequent between people of roughly equal status who know each other but are not intimate — friends, acquaintances, colleagues — where the relationship is still being negotiated, and less frequent between strangers and between intimates.

::: example Giving a compliment {#ex-giving}
Each comment was meant kindly but may not be heard as a compliment. Say why, and rewrite it with one of the three formulas.

(a) To a colleague after her talk: *You were not as nervous as last time.* (b) To a friend: *You've lost a lot of weight!* (c) To a host: *This is quite good for homemade bread.* (d) To a classmate: *Your essay is better than I expected.*
::: solution
(a) It praises by comparison with a past failure, so it also reminds her of it. ✓ *That was a really good talk — you seemed so relaxed!* (pattern 3, with a positive comment on her manner).

(b) It comments on the body, implies a judgement of how she looked before, and may touch on illness or a private struggle. ✓ Choose another topic: *I love that colour on you* (pattern 2), or say nothing about appearance.

(c) *Quite good* and *for homemade* are downgraders: by scalar implicature ([[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words#def-scalar]]) they suggest ‘not very good’. ✓ *This bread is delicious — did you make it yourself?* (pattern 1, with a question that invites the host to talk about it).

(d) *Better than I expected* reveals that the speaker's expectations were low. ✓ *I really liked your essay — the argument about transport was really convincing* (pattern 2, with a specific detail, which makes a compliment sound sincere).
:::
:::

### Responding to a compliment

The response is harder than the compliment. Anita Pomerantz (1978) noticed that it places the recipient between two pressures.

::: theorem Two constraints on compliment responses {#rule-compliment-response}
Pomerantz (1978) described two constraints that pull in different directions:

1. **agree with the speaker** — accepting the compliment agrees with the complimenter's judgement;
2. **avoid self-praise** — accepting it fully risks praising oneself.

Responses can be ordered by how they balance the two:

- **accept**: *Thank you!* / *Thanks — I'm really pleased with it.* (agreement)
- **accept and downgrade**: *Thanks — it's quite old, actually.* / *Oh, it was nothing special.* (partial agreement, modest)
- **shift the credit**: *Thanks — my sister chose it.* / *Thanks, but it was a team effort.*
- **return**: *Thanks — yours is lovely too!*
- **question or reject**: *Really? I thought it was terrible.* / *No, no, it's not good.* (modest, but disagrees)

In most contexts in English, a response that includes an appreciation token (*Thanks*, *Thank you*) and then, if wished, a downgrade, a shift or a return is expected and polite. An outright rejection can sound as if the complimenter's judgement or taste is being questioned.
:::

Type *agrees with the complimenter - avoids self-praise* to shade the plain acceptances.
:::

::: proof
Pomerantz's two constraints are close to Leech's maxims of Agreement and Modesty, which conflict in exactly this situation ([[pragmatics/politeness-face#rule-leech]]). Every community has to resolve the conflict somehow, and the solutions differ by situation, by relationship and by person, not simply by language. Robert Herbert (1989), comparing American and South African English, found that the American speakers accepted compliments outright much less often than the American "rule" of *say thank you* would suggest: many responses downgraded, shifted, returned or questioned the compliment. The *Thank you* rule is a useful guide for learners, not a description of everything English speakers do.
:::

The most striking evidence of change comes from Chinese. Rong Chen (1993) compared compliment responses of American speakers and of Chinese speakers in Xi'an and found a clear difference: the Americans mostly accepted, often with a downgrade or return, while the Chinese speakers mostly rejected compliments, consistent with Leech's Modesty maxim and Gu's Self-denigration Maxim ([[pragmatics/politeness-face#rule-gu]]). Rong Chen and Dafu Yang (2010) later repeated the study in the same city and found that acceptance had become the most common response, and rejection much less frequent — a change they connected with social change and wider contact with other conventions. Spencer-Oatey and Ng (2001), comparing evaluations by speakers in Hong Kong and mainland China, found that the Chinese participants did not, as the stereotype would predict, rate rejection as the most appropriate response. Modesty is still valued — but *哪里哪里* is no longer the only, or even the usual, reply.

::: example Diagnosing compliment responses {#ex-compliment-responses}
Classify each response with Pomerantz's categories, and say how an English-speaking complimenter would probably hear it.

(a) *I love your scarf!* — *Thanks! It was a present from my mum.*
(b) *That was an excellent talk.* — *No, no, it was not good. I made many mistakes.*
(c) *Your English is so good!* — *Thank you — I'm still working on it.*
(d) *Great goal!* — *Cheers! Lucky, though — the keeper slipped.*
(e) *This soup is amazing.* — *Yes, I'm a very good cook.*
::: solution
(a) **Accept and shift the credit** (to the giver). Heard as warm and modest.

(b) **Reject**. Heard as modest by some, but many English speakers would feel slightly contradicted, and might wonder whether the speaker was fishing for more praise or genuinely upset. A better response: *Thank you! I was really nervous — I'm glad it was clear.* (accept, then downgrade).

(c) **Accept and downgrade**. Heard as natural and modest. (The compliment itself can be slightly patronising when the hearer has lived in an English-speaking country for years; a gracious response accepts it anyway.)

(d) **Accept and downgrade** (*Lucky, though* shifts credit to chance). Very typical between friends.

(e) **Accept and upgrade**. Grammatically fine, but in most contexts it sounds boastful unless said with an obvious smile, as a joke.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **对不起, 不好意思, 抱歉.** 对不起 is a fairly strong apology; 不好意思 covers light apologies and embarrassment, and often prefaces a request (不好意思，请问…). In English, *Sorry* and *Excuse me* cover most uses of 不好意思; *I'm sorry* covers 对不起; *I apologise* and *Apologies for…* are close to 抱歉 in writing.
- **没关系 and 不客气.** 没关系 accepts an apology: *That's OK*, *No problem*, *Don't worry*. 不客气 and 不用谢 answer thanks: *You're welcome*, *No problem*, *My pleasure*. Keep the two pairs apart: *You're welcome* never answers *Sorry*.
- **应该的.** Answering thanks with 应该的 ‘it's what I should do’ is modest in Chinese. A literal translation, *It's my duty*, can sound stiff, or as if the help was given only from obligation. *Happy to help!*, *Not at all* or *Glad I could help* carry the modesty without that implication.
- **Compliments.** The traditional response 哪里哪里 rejects the compliment; as Chen and Yang (2010) show, many Chinese speakers now accept compliments with 谢谢, and in English *Thank you* plus a modest comment is the safe choice. You keep your modesty in the comment, not in a denial.
- **Topics.** Compliments on appearance are common in English (*I love your haircut*), but comments on weight — even positive ones such as *You've lost weight!* — are risky, because they imply a judgement about the body ([[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]]). Compliments on work, choices and possessions are safest.
:::

::: history
Apologies, thanks and compliments were studied intensively from the late 1970s, as linguists turned from philosophical analysis to collecting what people actually said. Anita Pomerantz's analysis of compliment responses appeared in 1978, in a volume of conversation-analytic studies edited by Jim Schenkein. Joan Manes and Nessa Wolfson collected compliments in the United States and published "The compliment formula" in Florian Coulmas's collection *Conversational Routine* (1981), which also contained Coulmas's comparison of thanks and apologies. Elite Olshtain and Andrew Cohen described the apology speech act set in 1983, and the Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project compared apologies, with requests, across languages (Blum-Kulka, House and Kasper 1989). Janet Holmes studied compliments and apologies in New Zealand English in the 1980s, and Rong Chen's two studies in Xi'an (1993, 2010) became one of the clearest examples of pragmatic norms changing within a single community over a generation.
:::

## Where this leads

Apologies in emails — *Apologies for the delay*, *Sorry for the slow reply* — and thanking in writing are treated in [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]. Compliments as a way into small talk, and the topics they can safely touch, are part of [[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]]. Ironic thanks and apologies (*Thanks a lot!*, *Well, excuse me!*) are explained in [[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]. How norms for these acts vary between communities, and why individual differences matter as much as group tendencies, is the subject of [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]].

::: summary
- An apology is a **speech act set**: an expression of apology, an account, an acknowledgement of responsibility, an offer of repair and a promise of forbearance ([[#def-apology-set]]).
- Match the apology to the offence: a bare *Sorry* for accidents; responsibility and repair for serious offences; avoid accounts that sound like excuses ([[#rule-apology]]).
- Many uses of *sorry* are not apologies: sympathy, requests for repetition, prefaces to requests or disagreement.
- Accept an apology with *That's OK*, *No problem*, *Don't worry*; never with *You're welcome* ([[#rule-accept-apology]]).
- Thanks are graded (*Thanks* → *I can't thank you enough*) and elaborated for larger favours; respond with *You're welcome*, *No problem*, *My pleasure* ([[#rule-thanks]]).
- Compliments are formulaic positive politeness; responses balance **agreeing** with the speaker and **avoiding self-praise** ([[#def-compliment]], [[#rule-compliment-response]]).
- *Thank you* plus a modest comment is the safe English response. Chinese compliment responses have shifted from rejection towards acceptance (Chen 1993; Chen & Yang 2010).
:::

Using the concepts of this chapter, analyse the three exchanges at the start (the café, the seminar and the flat). For each, say which act was performed, whether the response fitted English conventions, and, where it did not, what alternative you would suggest and why.
::: solution
**Café**: both said *Sorry* — a minor, accidental offence where the bare expression is enough; neither admits fault, and the mutual *sorry* closes the incident. Conventional; nothing to change.

**Seminar**: Professor Nguyen paid a compliment (performance; formula *that was an excellent talk*). Lin Xiaoyu **rejected** it (*No, no, it was not good*), satisfying the constraint of avoiding self-praise but breaking the constraint of agreeing with the speaker (Pomerantz 1978). For a supervisor, whose role is to judge, rejection can sound like questioning her judgement. Alternative: *Thank you so much! I was really nervous — I'm glad it came across clearly.* (accept and downgrade). Modesty is kept in the second sentence.

**Flat**: Josh thanked her, with elaboration (*You didn't have to do that* recognises the effort). *You're welcome* fits; a warmer response might add something (*No problem — I made too much anyway!*, a downgrade that minimises the favour). Had Josh apologised, the right response would have been *That's OK* or *Don't worry*, never *You're welcome*.
:::

::: exercise A letter of apology {level=3}
You organised a study group trip to a museum but booked the wrong date; eight people arrived to find the museum closed. Write a message of 120–180 words to the group that apologises appropriately, without over-apologising, and proposes a repair.
::: hint
Use expression, responsibility, explanation (briefly), repair and forbearance. One intensified apology is enough.
:::
::: solution
A model:

*Hi everyone,*

*I'm really sorry about yesterday. I booked the tickets for the 14th instead of the 4th — that was my mistake, and I should have double-checked the confirmation email. I know several of you travelled a long way, and I'm sorry you had a wasted trip.*

*I've spoken to the museum, and they have agreed to move our booking to Saturday the 21st at 10 a.m., at no extra cost. If that date doesn't work for you, let me know and I'll ask them about a refund for your ticket. For anyone who paid for a train, I'd like to cover the cost — just send me the amount.*

*From now on I'll send the booking details to the group before confirming, so that more than one person can check them.*

*Sorry again, and thanks for being so understanding.*

*Mateus*

The message apologises clearly once (with a brief *Sorry again* at the end), takes responsibility without excuses, acknowledges the harm, offers concrete repair and promises a change of practice.
:::
:::
