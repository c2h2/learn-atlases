It is Wu Hao's second week as an intern in a small design studio in Bristol. On Monday morning his manager, Grace, stops by his desk on her way to a meeting.

**Grace:** *Morning! It'd be great to have those sales figures by lunchtime.*

**Wu Hao:** *Yes, that would be great.*

Wu Hao agreed with her — it *would* be great — and went back to his poster. At half past twelve Grace came back and asked, a little coldly, where the figures were. She had not said *Please send me the figures*; she had made a remark about what would be good. But her remark was a request, and from a manager to an intern a request is very close to an instruction.

Later that week Wu Hao's flatmate, Kalinda, asked him a question at dinner:

**Kalinda:** *Can you reach the soy sauce?*

**Wu Hao:** *Sure* (passing it).

This time there was no misunderstanding, although Kalinda's words were literally a question about his arm.

What Grace and Kalinda did with their words — asking someone to do something — is a **speech act**. This chapter introduces the theory of speech acts, created by the philosopher J. L. Austin and developed by his student John Searle. It explains how saying something can be doing something, what has to be true for a speech act to succeed, how acts can be classified, and why so many requests in English look like statements or questions. The previous chapter, [[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words]], explained how hearers infer what is not said; here we ask what kind of act a speaker performs when they say it.

## Saying and doing

Most sentences in a grammar book describe the world — *Lagos is in Nigeria*, *My sister lives in Chengdu* — and can be true or false. Philosophers long assumed that this was the central job of language. In a series of lectures given at Harvard in 1955 and published after his death as *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), Austin pointed out a class of ordinary sentences that are not true or false at all, because in saying them the speaker is not describing an action but performing it.

::: definition Performative and constative {#def-performative}
A **performative utterance** is one in which saying the words is (part of) doing the act they name: *I promise to call you tomorrow*; *I apologise for the delay*; *I name this ship* …; *I bet you five pounds it rains*; *I resign*. Austin contrasted performatives with **constatives**, utterances that describe a state of affairs and can be true or false: *She promised to call me*; *He resigned last week*. A performative is not true or false; it can only be **happy** or **unhappy** — successful, or failed in some way.
:::

An **explicit performative** names the act with a verb in the simple present, with *I* (or *we*) as subject, addressed to the hearer: *I **promise**…*, *I **warn** you…*, *We **invite** you…*, *I **advise** you…*. Austin proposed a simple test: such verbs can take the word *hereby*. *I hereby resign* is a resignation; ✗ *I hereby walk to work* is nonsense, because walking is not done by saying so. The test also shows why the same verb in other forms is constative: *I promised to call you* reports a past promise and can be true or false.

Austin's examples came largely from ceremonies, but explicit performatives are common in everyday English when the speaker wants to make the act unmistakable: *I'm warning you — don't touch that.* *I suggest we take a break.* *I'd like to thank everyone who came tonight* — grammatically a statement about a wish, heard as the thanks itself.

::: theorem Felicity conditions {#rule-felicity}
Austin (1962) argued that a performative is successful — **felicitous** — only when certain conditions hold. In outline:

1. There must be an **accepted procedure** with a conventional effect (a wedding ceremony, a bet, a resignation), and the **persons and circumstances** must be the right ones for it.
2. The procedure must be carried out **correctly and completely**.
3. The participants must have the **thoughts, feelings and intentions** the procedure presupposes (a person who promises must intend to keep the promise), and must behave accordingly afterwards.

If conditions 1 or 2 fail, the act is a **misfire**: it does not take place at all. A guest at a wedding who shouts *I now pronounce you husband and wife* has married nobody. If condition 3 fails, the act is an **abuse**: it takes place, but insincerely. A promise made with no intention of keeping it is still a promise — that is why the speaker can be blamed for breaking it.
:::

::: proof
The two kinds of failure match our ordinary reactions. Of a misfire we say the act is void: *That doesn't count — you can't resign from a club you never joined.* Of an abuse we say the act was done badly: *You promised!* is a complaint that assumes the promise exists. Most felicity conditions are invisible until they fail.
:::

## Three acts in one utterance

Austin's most important discovery was that his own distinction did not survive. *I warn you that the bull is dangerous* is an explicit performative, but *The bull is dangerous*, said in the same field, is just as much a warning — and it is also true or false. Constatives turn out to do things as well: stating, reporting and describing are acts too. So Austin replaced the contrast between two kinds of utterance with a contrast between three things that every utterance does at once.

::: definition Locution, illocution, perlocution {#def-three-acts}
In saying something, a speaker performs:

1. a **locutionary act** — producing a meaningful utterance with a certain sense and reference: saying the words *It'd be great to have those figures by lunchtime*;
2. an **illocutionary act** — the act performed *in* saying it, recognised by convention and context: requesting, promising, warning, thanking, complaining, informing. The type of illocutionary act is its **illocutionary force**;
3. a **perlocutionary act** — the effect achieved *by* saying it on the hearer's feelings, thoughts or actions: persuading, annoying, reassuring, getting someone to send the figures. The effect itself is the **perlocutionary effect**.
:::

The illocution is the centre of the theory, because it is the part that speakers and hearers share by convention. Grace's illocution was a request whatever Wu Hao made of it; its intended perlocutionary effect — the figures on her desk by lunchtime — did not happen, because he did not recognise the illocution. Perlocutions are not under the speaker's control in the same way: you can *argue* (an illocution) without *convincing* (a perlocution), and you can *annoy* someone without meaning to.

English **speech-act verbs** name illocutions: *She **promised** to come*, *They **warned** us about the ice*. In reported speech ([[complex-sentences/reported-speech]]) the right verb often reports an act better than the words do: *Grace asked me to send the figures by lunchtime*, though she never said *Please send*.

::: example Separating the three acts {#ex-three-acts}
Describe the locution, the most likely illocution and a possible perlocution for each utterance.

(a) Ana, a tour guide, to a group of tourists near a cliff edge: *The rock here is very loose.*

(b) Mr Danjuma to his neighbour, whose dog has barked all night: *Some of us have to get up at six.*

(c) A student, Sun Li, to her lab partner, who has just fixed their broken experiment: *You're a genius.*
::: solution
(a) **Locution**: the statement that the rock near the speaker is very loose. **Illocution**: a warning (‘stay back from the edge’), though it has the form of a description. **Perlocution**: if it works, the tourists move away from the edge; it might also frighten some of them.

(b) **Locution**: a statement that some people, including the speaker, must wake at six. **Illocution**: a complaint, and an indirect request (‘please keep your dog quiet at night’). The general *some of us* makes it sound pointed rather than polite. **Perlocution**: the neighbour may apologise and act — or feel criticised and become defensive.

(c) **Locution**: an assertion that the partner is a genius. It is obviously not meant literally (it flouts Quality, [[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words#rule-flouting]]). **Illocution**: thanking and praising. **Perlocution**: the partner feels pleased and appreciated.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Which of these is an explicit performative?
- [ ] *I promised to help him.*
- [x] *I promise I'll be there by eight.*
- [ ] *She always keeps her promises.*
- [ ] *I'll probably be there by eight.*
::: solution
*I promise I'll be there* names the act (*promise*) in the simple present with *I* as subject, and saying it is the act of promising; you could add *hereby*. *I promised* reports a past act and is true or false. The third is a description. The fourth is a prediction; it may function as a weak commitment, but it does not name the act.
:::
:::

## Searle's conditions for a speech act

Austin died in 1960, before he could develop his lectures into a full theory. His student John Searle did so in *Speech Acts: An Essay in the Philosophy of Language* (1969). Searle asked what exactly has to be true for a particular speech act — a promise, a request, a thanks — to count as performed, and stated the answer as four kinds of condition.

::: theorem Searle's four conditions {#rule-searle-conditions}
For each type of illocutionary act, Searle (1969) distinguished:

1. the **propositional content condition** — what the content must be about (a promise is about a future act of the speaker; a request about a future act of the hearer; thanks about a past act of the hearer);
2. **preparatory conditions** — what must be true beforehand (for a request: the hearer is able to do the act, and would not obviously do it anyway; for a promise: the hearer would like the act done);
3. the **sincerity condition** — the psychological state the speaker expresses (a request expresses a want; a promise an intention; thanks gratitude; an apology regret);
4. the **essential condition** — what the utterance counts as (a request counts as an attempt to get the hearer to do the act; a promise as the speaker's taking on an obligation to do it).
:::

The conditions for the commonest acts can be set side by side:

| condition | request | promise | thanks | apology |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| propositional content | future act A of hearer | future act A of speaker | past act A of hearer | past act A of speaker |
| preparatory | H can do A; H would not do A anyway | H wants A done; S would not do A anyway | A benefited S | A harmed or inconvenienced H |
| sincerity | S wants H to do A | S intends to do A | S is grateful for A | S regrets A |
| essential | counts as an attempt to get H to do A | counts as S taking on an obligation to do A | counts as an expression of gratitude | counts as an expression of regret for A |

::: proof
The conditions are not a checklist that speakers go through; they analyse what competent speakers already know, and so explain why some utterances sound wrong without being ungrammatical. *Please breathe* is an odd request because the hearer would do it anyway. *I promise I'll tell your parents* is heard as a threat because the hearer does not want the act. *Thank you for the terrible meal* fails the preparatory condition of thanks and is heard as irony ([[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]). Most importantly for this course, the conditions give speakers something to talk *about* when they make a request indirectly, as the section after next shows.
:::

::: example Is it an apology? {#ex-apology-conditions}
English *sorry* appears in many utterances. Use the conditions for an apology in the table to decide which of these are apologies, and what the others are.

(a) Yusuf, who has stepped on a stranger's foot: *Oh, I'm so sorry!* (b) Priya to a colleague whose father has died: *I'm so sorry for your loss.* (c) A manager to an employee who has complained about a decision: *I'm sorry you feel that way.* (d) Lena, who did not hear a question: *Sorry?*
::: solution
(a) **An apology.** The content is a past act of the speaker (stepping on the foot), it harmed the hearer, and the speaker expresses regret. All the conditions hold.

(b) **Not an apology but an expression of sympathy** (condolence). The death is not an act of the speaker, so the propositional content condition fails for an apology; *sorry* here expresses sadness on the hearer's behalf. No one would answer *That's OK, it wasn't your fault*.

(c) **Not really an apology.** The regret is about the hearer's feelings, not about an act of the speaker, so the speaker takes no responsibility. This is why *I'm sorry you feel that way* is often heard as a refusal to apologise, and can make the hearer angrier ([[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]]).

(d) **A request for repetition** (a directive), with a trace of apology for the trouble caused. It is a conventional formula, like *Pardon?* or *Sorry, what was that?* ([[listening-and-conversation/clarification]]).
:::
:::

## Five kinds of speech act

English has hundreds of speech-act verbs, but many name variations of the same basic act (*ask*, *request*, *beg*, *urge*, *order*, *command*; *state*, *claim*, *assert*, *report*). Austin proposed five broad classes — verdictives, exercitives, commissives, behabitives and expositives — but did not give clear criteria for them. Searle's "A classification of illocutionary acts" (*Language in Society*, 1976) replaced them with five classes based on what the speaker is trying to do and on the relation between the words and the world.

::: theorem Searle's five classes {#rule-five-classes}
1. **Representatives** (also called **assertives**) commit the speaker to the truth of something: stating, claiming, reporting, describing, concluding, predicting. *The meeting starts at ten.*
2. **Directives** are attempts to get the hearer to do something: requesting, ordering, inviting, advising, asking questions (a question asks the hearer to supply an answer). *Could you send me the figures?*
3. **Commissives** commit the speaker to a future action: promising, offering, threatening, refusing, vowing. *I'll call you back in five minutes.*
4. **Expressives** express the speaker's feelings or attitude about a state of affairs: thanking, apologising, congratulating, welcoming, complaining, condoling. *Congratulations on the new job!*
5. **Declarations** change the world simply by being successfully performed, usually by a person with an institutional role: declaring a meeting open, sentencing, naming, firing, resigning, pronouncing a couple married. *I declare the meeting open.*
:::

::: proof
Searle's main criterion was the **direction of fit** between words and world. A representative tries to make the words fit the world — if they do not, the statement is false. Directives and commissives try to make the world fit the words — if the hearer does not do what was asked, or the speaker does not do what was promised, the world has failed to match. Expressives have no direction of fit: thanks presuppose that something happened but neither describe nor change it. Declarations have both at once: *You're fired*, said with authority, makes itself true. Some acts sit between classes — a complaint is expressive but often also directive (‘stop doing it’).
:::

::: example Classifying speech acts {#ex-classify}
Classify the main illocutionary act in each utterance with Searle's five classes, and name it with a speech-act verb.

(a) *I'm so sorry I forgot your birthday.* (b) *Leave it on the table — I'll deal with it later.* (c) *I now declare this library open.* (d) *If you touch my bike again, you'll regret it.* (e) *The train to Leeds has been cancelled.* (f) *What time does the film start?*
::: solution
(a) **Expressive** — an apology; it expresses regret for a past act of the speaker. (b) Two acts: *Leave it on the table* is a **directive** (an instruction or a request, depending on the relationship), and *I'll deal with it later* is a **commissive** (an undertaking). (c) **Declaration** — opening; said by an authorised person at the right ceremony, it makes the library officially open. (d) **Commissive** — a threat: the speaker commits to a future act that the hearer does not want. (e) **Representative** — an announcement or report; it can be true or false. (f) **Directive** — a question, which asks the hearer to provide information.
:::
:::

## Indirect speech acts

English sentences come in three main grammatical types, and each has a typical use: **declaratives** (*You are closing the window*) for statements, **interrogatives** (*Are you closing the window?*) for questions, and **imperatives** (*Close the window*) for directives. When the type matches the act, the speech act is **direct**. But the match is often broken. *Can you reach the soy sauce?* is an interrogative used as a request; *It'd be great to have those figures by lunchtime* is a declarative used as one.

::: definition Indirect speech act {#def-indirect-act}
An **indirect speech act** is one in which one illocutionary act is performed by way of another (Searle 1975, "Indirect speech acts"). In *Can you pass the salt?* the speaker performs the **secondary** (literal) act of asking a question about the hearer's ability, and through it the **primary** act of requesting. An indirect speech act is **conventional** when the form is a standard way of performing the primary act in a language (*Can you…?*, *Would you mind…?*); it is **non-conventional** when it depends on the particular context (*It's cold in here* as a request to close the window).
:::

Searle (1975) noticed a pattern: an indirect request usually works by **asking about or stating one of the felicity conditions** of a request. If the conditions for requesting are that the hearer *can* do the act, that the speaker *wants* it done, and that the hearer is *willing*, then talking about any of them can serve as the request itself.

::: theorem How indirect requests are built {#rule-indirect-requests}
In English, a request can be made indirectly by:

1. **asking whether the preparatory condition holds** (ability): *Can you…?* *Could you…?* *Are you able to…?* *Is it possible to…?*
2. **stating the sincerity condition** (the speaker's want): *I'd like you to…*, *I'd be grateful if you could…*, *It'd be great if…*, *I need you to…*
3. **asking about the hearer's willingness** or future act: *Will you…?* *Would you…?* *Would you mind…?* *Are you going to…?*
4. **asking about or stating reasons for the act**: *Why don't you…?* *Shouldn't you…?* *It might be a good idea to…* *It's very hot in here.*

Types 1–3 are **conventional** in English: they are understood as requests almost without inference. Type 4 is often **non-conventional** and depends on context.
:::

::: proof
Two tests show that conventional indirect requests are a special case. First, they accept *please* in the middle: *Can you **please** pass the salt?* is natural, but ✗ *Do you have the ability, please, to pass the salt?* is not, although it asks the same literal question. *Please* marks a request, so the conventional form is already understood as one. Second, a literal answer is a joke or an act of resistance: *Can you pass the salt? — Yes* (and nothing else) is either humour or a teacher's lesson to a child. Conventionality belongs to each language, not to logic: English uses *Can you…?* but not ✗ *Are you capable of passing the salt?* as a routine request, so the forms must be learned ([[modality/modality-politeness]]).
:::

::: warning Answering the literal question
✗ *Do you know where the station is? — Yes.* ✓ *Yes — it's straight on, second left.*

✗ *Have you got a minute? — Yes, I have.* (and then waiting) ✓ *Sure — what's up?*

✗ *Would you mind turning the music down? — Yes.* (meant as agreement) ✓ *Oh, sorry — not at all.* / *Sure, no problem.*

A conventional indirect request is answered by responding to the **request**: doing the thing, or saying you will, or refusing politely. The *Would you mind…?* case is special because *Yes* answers the literal question and means ‘I do mind’ — a refusal. The safest replies are *Not at all*, *Sure* or *Of course* ([[everyday-english/requests-offers#rule-would-you-mind]]).
:::

Not every indirect speech act is a request. Many everyday acts in English are typically done indirectly:

- **Offers** as questions about the hearer's wants: *Would you like a lift?* *Do you want me to carry that?*
- **Suggestions** as questions about reasons: *Why don't we stop for lunch?* *How about the Lebanese place?*
- **Refusals** as statements of a reason: *I've got an exam on Monday* ([[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words]]).
- **Complaints** as questions: *Do you know what time it is?* (to a flatmate coming in at 3 a.m.); *Who left the milk out?*
- **Thanks and praise** as exclamations about the act: *You didn't have to do that!* *That was so kind of you.*
- **Orders** as statements of the speaker's expectation: *I'll need that on my desk by five.* *We don't run in the corridors.*

::: example Reconstructing the request {#ex-reconstruct}
For each utterance, give the primary illocution, say which condition of [[#rule-indirect-requests]] it works through (if any), and decide whether it is conventional or non-conventional.

(a) Erik, a teacher, to a student at the window: *It's getting rather stuffy in here.*

(b) Mei Ling to her colleague: *Would you be able to cover reception for ten minutes?*

(c) Hassan to his housemate, pointing at the dishes in the sink: *Are you planning to wash those at some point?*

(d) Grace, to Wu Hao: *It'd be great to have those sales figures by lunchtime.*
::: solution
(a) **Primary act**: a request to open the window. It states a **reason** (type 4) and needs context — the student is by the window — so it is **non-conventional**: a hint.

(b) **Primary act**: a request. It asks about the hearer's **ability** (type 1) with the conventional, distant form *Would you be able to…?*. **Conventional**: no one would answer *Yes, I would be able to* and not move.

(c) **Primary act**: a request, and also a complaint. It asks about the hearer's **future act** (type 3); *planning to… at some point* adds an ironic edge, so it may be resented more than a direct *Could you wash those, please?*

(d) **Primary act**: a request (from a manager, effectively an instruction). It states the **sincerity condition** — the speaker's want — in a softened, impersonal form (type 2): *It'd be great* rather than *I want*. It is a **conventional** way for a manager to give a task politely; Wu Hao treated it as a representative and agreed with it.
:::
:::

::: solution
The question is about a preparatory condition (is coffee available?), and in context it usually means ‘could I have some more?’. *I'll make a fresh pot* responds to the request with an offer. The first two answer only the literal question; the fourth changes the subject — unless it is a refusal disguised as advice.

## Speech acts across languages

Speech-act theory was built on English examples, and some of its assumptions were later questioned. Anna Wierzbicka (1985) argued that the English preference for interrogative requests (*Could you…?*) reflects a cultural value placed on personal autonomy, and that in Polish polite requests are often made with imperatives softened in other ways: the impoliteness of the bare imperative is a fact about English, not about language. The Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project (Blum-Kulka, House and Kasper 1989) found conventionally indirect requests in all the languages it studied, but with different forms and frequencies. The basic acts seem to be found everywhere; the ways of performing them, and when each is expected, vary ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]).

::: note English and Chinese
- **Greetings that look like questions.** 你吃了吗？ and 去哪儿啊？, used as greetings, are formally questions but function as greetings (expressives) or as phatic talk, and no detailed answer is expected. English *How are you?* and *How's it going?* work the same way ([[everyday-english/greetings-introductions]]). The difficulty comes in translation: *Where are you going?* in English is taken as a real question, and to some listeners a nosy one ([[pragmatics/small-talk-norms]]).
- **好的 and *OK*.** In English, *OK* or *Sure* in reply to a conventional indirect request is heard as agreeing to do it. If you are not sure whether a task is meant, ask: *Sorry — do you need them from me?*
- **Expressives without equivalents.** 辛苦了 acknowledges effort and has no exact English counterpart; English usually turns it into thanks (*Thanks for all your hard work*) ([[collocation-and-register/false-friends-chinglish]]).
- **Indirect requests.** Chinese has conventional indirect requests too — 能不能…？, 可不可以…？, 你方便…吗？ — and they map well onto *Could you…?* and *Is it possible to…?*. Hints such as 这里有点热啊 are understood in both languages. The difference is less in the existence of indirectness than in which forms are the routine ones in which situations.
:::

::: history
John Langshaw Austin (1911–1960), an Oxford philosopher of "ordinary language", gave the William James Lectures at Harvard in 1955. After his death they were edited by J. O. Urmson and published as *How to Do Things with Words* (1962); a second edition, edited by Urmson and Marina Sbisà, followed in 1975. John R. Searle (born 1932), an American who had studied with Austin at Oxford, turned the lectures into a systematic theory in *Speech Acts* (1969). His paper "Indirect speech acts" appeared in 1975 in the same volume of *Syntax and Semantics* as Grice's "Logic and Conversation", and "A classification of illocutionary acts" in *Language in Society* in 1976. The theory soon influenced language teaching: the "functional" syllabuses of the 1970s organised courses around acts such as requesting and apologising.
:::

## Where this leads

[[pragmatics/politeness-face]] explains *why* speakers so often prefer indirect acts. Requests and refusals are the subject of [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]]; apologies, thanks and compliments of [[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]]. The modal forms of indirect requests are in [[modality/modality-politeness]] and [[everyday-english/requests-offers]], and speech-act theory in the wider field of linguistics in [[linguistics/pragmatics-discourse]].

::: summary
- A **speech act** is an action performed by saying something: requesting, promising, apologising, warning, naming ([[#def-performative]]).
- **Explicit performatives** name the act (*I promise…*, *I apologise…*) and pass the *hereby* test; they are not true or false but felicitous or not ([[#rule-felicity]]).
- Every utterance is at once a **locutionary**, an **illocutionary** and a **perlocutionary** act; the illocutionary force is what the speaker does *in* speaking ([[#def-three-acts]]).
- Searle's **propositional content, preparatory, sincerity and essential conditions** define each type of act and explain why some utterances sound odd ([[#rule-searle-conditions]]).
- Searle's five classes are **representatives, directives, commissives, expressives and declarations**, distinguished by direction of fit ([[#rule-five-classes]]).
- In an **indirect speech act** one act is performed by way of another; English requests are often made by asking about ability or willingness or by stating a want ([[#def-indirect-act]], [[#rule-indirect-requests]]).
- Respond to the request, not the literal question: *Have you got a minute? — Sure, what's up?*
- The basic acts seem universal, but which forms are conventional, and when each act is expected, differs between languages.
:::

## Exercises

::: exercise Performative or not? {level=1}
Which of these are explicit performatives? Use the *hereby* test.

(a) *I apologise for the confusion.* (b) *I apologised for the confusion.* (c) *I bet you ten pounds they lose.* (d) *I walk to work every day.* (e) *We invite you to our wedding on 14 June.* (f) *I think it will rain.*
::: solution
Explicit performatives: (a) *I hereby apologise…* — yes; (c) *I hereby bet you…* — yes; (e) *We hereby invite you…* — yes (formal, but it works).

Not performatives: (b) reports a past apology; (d) describes a habit; (f) states an opinion — *I hereby think* is nonsense, because thinking is not done by saying so.
:::
:::

::: exercise Name the class {level=1}
Classify each utterance as a representative, directive, commissive, expressive or declaration.

(a) *Welcome to Edinburgh!* (b) *You're under arrest.* (said by a police officer) (c) *I'll send it first thing tomorrow.* (d) *Please take a seat.* (e) *The museum is closed on Mondays.* (f) *Could you spell your surname for me?*
::: solution
(a) Expressive (welcoming). (b) Declaration (an authorised person changes the hearer's legal status by saying it). (c) Commissive (an undertaking). (d) Directive (an invitation or polite instruction). (e) Representative (a statement of fact). (f) Directive (a request for information).

::: exercise Three acts {level=1}
Kaveh says to his flatmate on a winter evening: *The heating's been on all day, you know.* Describe (a) the locution, (b) a likely illocution, (c) two possible perlocutionary effects.
::: solution
(a) The statement that the heating has been on all day. (b) Most likely a complaint and an indirect request: ‘please turn the heating down / stop leaving it on’ (the *you know* shows it is addressed to the flatmate's responsibility). (c) Possible effects: the flatmate turns the heating off; the flatmate feels criticised and annoyed; the flatmate apologises. Any reasonable pair is acceptable.
:::

::: exercise What went wrong? {level=2}
Each utterance fails as the speech act it seems to be. Say which condition of [[#rule-searle-conditions]] or [[#rule-felicity]] fails, and what the hearer will probably understand instead.

(a) A passenger to a bus driver who is already driving to the next stop: *Please drive to the next stop.* (b) *I promise I'll tell your parents everything.* (said to a child who misbehaved) (c) *Thank you for keeping me waiting for an hour.* (d) A guest at a dinner: *I hereby declare this dinner over.*
::: solution
(a) A **preparatory condition** of requesting fails: the driver would do it anyway. The driver may hear it as a joke, as impatience (‘hurry up’), or as a sign that the passenger is confused.

(b) A **preparatory condition** of promising fails: the hearer does not want the act done. It is heard as a **threat** (a commissive the hearer does not want).

(c) A **preparatory condition** of thanking fails: keeping someone waiting does not benefit them. It is heard as **ironic** — a complaint.

(d) Austin's first condition fails: a guest is not the right person to end the dinner by declaration, so the act **misfires**. It will be heard as a joke, or perhaps as a hint that the guest wants to leave.
:::
:::

::: exercise Make it indirect {level=2}
Write four different indirect requests for each situation, one for each type in [[#rule-indirect-requests]]. Mark which are conventional.

(a) You want a classmate, Femi, to lend you his notes from Tuesday's lecture. (b) You want your landlord to fix a dripping tap.
::: solution
Possible answers:

(a) Ability: *Could you lend me your notes from Tuesday?* (conventional). Want: *I'd be really grateful if I could borrow your notes from Tuesday* (conventional). Willingness: *Would you mind lending me your notes from Tuesday?* (conventional). Reason: *I missed Tuesday's lecture and I don't have any notes* (non-conventional hint).

(b) Ability: *Would you be able to send someone to fix the kitchen tap?* (conventional). Want: *I'd be grateful if the kitchen tap could be fixed this week* (conventional; the passive avoids telling the landlord what to do). Willingness: *Would you mind arranging a repair for the kitchen tap?* (conventional). Reason: *The kitchen tap has been dripping since Sunday and it's getting worse* (a hint, though in a message to a landlord it will usually be understood as a request).
:::

::: exercise Report the act {level=2}
Report each utterance with a suitable speech-act verb rather than the speaker's words. Use *suggest*, *warn*, *offer*, *remind*, *apologise*, *complain* or *invite*.

(a) Lucy to Ravi: *Don't forget your passport!* (b) Ming to his boss: *Shall I take the minutes?* (c) Ana to Tom: *Why don't we leave early and avoid the traffic?* (d) Mrs Osei to her neighbour: *I'm so sorry about the noise last night.* (e) Pablo to Hana: *Would you like to come to dinner on Friday?* (f) A customer to a waiter: *This soup is cold.*
::: solution
(a) *Lucy reminded Ravi to take his passport.* (b) *Ming offered to take the minutes.* (c) *Ana suggested leaving early to avoid the traffic.* (or *suggested that they leave / left early*) (d) *Mrs Osei apologised to her neighbour for the noise.* (e) *Pablo invited Hana to dinner on Friday.* (f) *The customer complained that the soup was cold.*
:::

::: exercise Analyse an exchange {level=3}
Identify every speech act in this exchange, give its class and say whether it is direct or indirect.

**Rahel:** *Oh, you're back! Did you manage to get bread?*
**Jonas:** *The bakery was shut.*
**Rahel:** *Typical. Well, there's always toast from the freezer.*
**Jonas:** *I'll go to the supermarket after lunch, I promise.*
**Rahel:** *You're a star.*
::: hint
Some turns contain more than one act. Look at what each turn does, not only at its grammar.
:::
::: solution
- *Oh, you're back!* — an expressive (a greeting), in the form of a statement: indirect.
- *Did you manage to get bread?* — a directive (a question), direct. *Manage* presupposes that getting bread might have been difficult.
- *The bakery was shut.* — a representative, and indirectly the answer ‘no, I didn't’ (by Relation: [[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words]]).
- *Typical.* — an expressive (a mild complaint about bad luck, not about Jonas).
- *Well, there's always toast from the freezer.* — a representative used indirectly as a suggestion (a directive), and perhaps as reassurance (‘it doesn't matter’).
- *I'll go to the supermarket after lunch* — a commissive (an offer or undertaking); *I promise* makes it an explicit performative.
- *You're a star.* — an expressive (thanks and praise) in the form of a representative that flouts Quality: indirect.
:::
:::

::: exercise Write a misunderstanding {level=3}
Write a short dialogue (6–10 turns) in which one speaker misunderstands an indirect speech act, as Wu Hao did, and the misunderstanding is then noticed and repaired. Then explain, using the terms of this chapter, what went wrong and how it was repaired.
::: hint
Choose a conventional indirect form that has a plausible literal reading — *Do you know…?*, *It'd be nice if…*, *Are you using this?*, *I wonder if…*.
:::
::: solution
A model:

**Ines (at a library desk):** *Are you using this chair?*
**Kenji:** *No, I'm just resting my bag on it.*
**Ines:** *Right…*
*(she waits)*
**Kenji:** *Oh — sorry! Did you want to sit here?*
**Ines:** *If that's OK.*
**Kenji:** *Of course* (moving the bag).
**Ines:** *Thanks!*

Explanation: *Are you using this chair?* asks about a reason for the request (type 4) and in context is a conventional way of asking for the chair. Kenji answered the secondary, literal question but not the primary act. Ines's *Right…* and her waiting flouted Quantity, signalling that something was still expected. Kenji then made the request explicit (*Did you want to sit here?*, softened by the past tense) and apologised; Ines accepted with a minimal *If that's OK*.

Answers will vary; a good answer uses a conventional form, shows the literal answer, a signal that something is missing, and a repair.
:::
