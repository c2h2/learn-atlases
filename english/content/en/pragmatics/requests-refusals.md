Two messages arrived on the same morning in the staff chat of a hotel in Dublin. The first was from Aoife, a receptionist, to her colleague Hiroshi:

*Hi Hiroshi! Sorry to ask at short notice — I've got a hospital appointment on Thursday afternoon. Is there any chance you could swap your morning shift for my afternoon one? Totally fine if not, I can ask someone else.*

Hiroshi replied an hour later:

*Hmm, Thursday is a bit difficult… I'll see what I can do.*

Aoife thanked him and waited. On Wednesday evening she asked again, and Hiroshi apologised: he had never been able to swap, because he had a class on Thursday afternoons. He had meant *no* from the start, and had chosen *a bit difficult* and *I'll see* to say it gently. Aoife had heard *maybe*, and had stopped looking for someone else.

Aoife's request was carefully built: an apology, a reason, a hedged question, and a sentence that made refusal easy. Hiroshi's refusal was gentle, but so gentle that it failed to refuse. This chapter is about building both acts. It uses the descriptions of requests developed in the largest comparative study of them, the Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project, and of refusals in the research that followed. The theory comes from [[pragmatics/speech-acts]] and [[pragmatics/politeness-face]]; the modal forms (*could*, *would*, *I was wondering if*) are taught in [[modality/modality-politeness]] and the everyday request and refusal phrases in [[everyday-english/requests-offers]], and are assumed here.

## The anatomy of a request

In the 1980s a group of researchers led by Shoshana Blum-Kulka, Juliane House and Gabriele Kasper collected requests and apologies from speakers of several languages and varieties, including English, German, Hebrew, Danish and Canadian French, and from learners of those languages. They used a **discourse completion task**: participants read a short description of a situation and wrote what they would say. The results, published as *Cross-Cultural Pragmatics: Requests and Apologies* (1989), were analysed with a shared coding scheme that is still the standard way to describe requests.

::: definition Head act, alerter, supportive move {#def-request-parts}
In the CCSARP scheme (Blum-Kulka et al. 1989), a request sequence has up to three kinds of part:

1. the **head act** — the minimal unit that does the requesting on its own: *Is there any chance you could swap your morning shift for my afternoon one?*
2. **alerters** — attention-getters and address terms before it: *Hi Hiroshi!*, *Excuse me*, *Dr Rahman*;
3. **supportive moves** — external modifiers before or after the head act that soften (or sometimes strengthen) it: *Sorry to ask at short notice*; *I've got a hospital appointment on Thursday afternoon*; *Totally fine if not.*
:::

The head act itself can be modified **internally**, by words and grammar inside it. Most of the softening in English requests is done this way, with the tools of [[modality/modality-politeness]] and [[modality/hedging-boosting]].

::: theorem Modifying a request {#rule-modifiers}
Following Blum-Kulka et al. (1989), the main ways of modifying a request in English are:

1. **Supportive moves** (external): a **preparator** that announces the request (*Can I ask you something?*); a **pre-commitment** (*Could you do me a favour?*); a **grounder**, giving the reason (*I've got a hospital appointment*); a **disarmer** that anticipates an objection (*I know you're busy, but…*); an **imposition minimiser** (*only if you're going that way anyway*); a **promise of reward** (*I'll buy you lunch*).
2. **Syntactic downgraders** (internal): interrogative (*Can you…?*), negation (*You couldn't…, could you?*), past tense and progressive aspect (*I was wondering…*), conditional (*if you could*), embedding (*I'd be grateful if…*).
3. **Lexical and phrasal downgraders** (internal): the politeness marker *please*; understaters (*a bit*, *a quick look*); hedges (*kind of*, *somehow*); subjectivisers (*I think*, *I'm afraid*); downtoners (*possibly*, *perhaps*, *just*); consultative devices (*Would you mind…?*, *Do you think…?*); appealers (*…, OK?*, *…, would you?*).
4. **Upgraders** strengthen the request: intensifiers (*really*, *urgently*), time markers (*right now*, *today*), commitment markers (*I'm sure you'll…*). They are not impolite in themselves — *I'd really appreciate it* is warm — but they increase pressure.
:::

::: example Taking a request apart {#ex-anatomy}
Identify the alerter, the head act, the supportive moves and the internal modifiers in this email from a student, Tendai, to a lecturer.

*Dear Dr Lindqvist,*

*I hope you're well. I'm in your Tuesday seminar on urban geography. I was wondering if you could possibly recommend one or two readings on housing policy in Scandinavia — I'm planning my dissertation on this and I'm finding it hard to know where to start. I know it's a busy time of term, so please don't worry if you don't have time.*

*Best wishes,*
*Tendai Moyo*
::: solution
- **Alerter**: *Dear Dr Lindqvist* (title and surname).
- **Supportive moves**: *I hope you're well* (a conventional opener — positive politeness); *I'm in your Tuesday seminar on urban geography* (self-identification, which makes the request easier to grant); the **grounder** *I'm planning my dissertation on this and I'm finding it hard to know where to start*; a **disarmer** *I know it's a busy time of term*; and an **imposition minimiser** *please don't worry if you don't have time*.
- **Head act**: *I was wondering if you could possibly recommend one or two readings on housing policy in Scandinavia.*
- **Internal modifiers**: past progressive (*I was wondering*), embedding and conditional (*if you could*), the downtoner *possibly*, and the understater *one or two*.

The request is heavily modified, which suits a large power difference and a first contact. With a supervisor Tendai already knows well, *Could you recommend a couple of readings on Scandinavian housing policy?* would be enough.
:::
:::

## Choosing the strategy

The head act can take many forms. The CCSARP coders sorted them into nine **strategy types**, which fall into three levels of directness.

::: theorem Three levels of directness in requests {#rule-ccsarp-levels}
Blum-Kulka et al. (1989) distinguished:

1. **Direct** strategies: the imperative (*Swap with me on Thursday*); explicit and hedged performatives (*I'm asking you to…*, *I'd like to ask you to…*); obligation statements (*You'll have to swap with me*); want statements (*I want / I'd like you to swap with me*).
2. **Conventionally indirect** strategies: suggestory formulas (*How about swapping with me?*); **query preparatory** — questions about ability, willingness or possibility (*Could you…?*, *Would you mind…?*, *Is there any chance…?*).
3. **Non-conventionally indirect** strategies: **strong hints**, which mention part of what is wanted (*Are you working on Thursday morning?*), and **mild hints**, which do not (*Thursdays are always so busy*).
:::

::: proof
One might expect the most indirect request to be the most polite, since it threatens face least. Blum-Kulka (1987) tested this with speakers of English and Hebrew, asking them to rate requests for politeness, and found that it was not so: in both groups, **conventionally indirect** requests were rated most polite, and hints were rated less polite than one would predict. Her explanation was that politeness needs **pragmatic clarity** as well as indirectness. A hint makes the hearer do the work of inferring the request, and can seem manipulative, because the speaker can deny having asked. A query preparatory combines the protection of indirectness with a message that cannot be missed. This is one reason *Could you…?* is the backbone of English requests.
:::

A second choice is the **perspective** of the request — whose action the head act mentions.

::: definition Request perspective {#def-perspective}
A request can be **hearer-oriented** (*Could **you** lend me your notes?*), **speaker-oriented** (*Could **I** borrow your notes?*), **inclusive** (*Could **we** go through the notes together?*) or **impersonal** (*Would it be possible to borrow the notes?*; *The notes need to be returned by Friday*). Speaker-oriented and impersonal requests avoid naming the hearer as the one who must act, and so are often felt to be less imposing (Blum-Kulka et al. 1989).
:::

::: example Changing the perspective {#ex-perspective}
Dr Okonkwo runs a shared laboratory. Students keep leaving equipment on the benches. Write the request to clean up in four perspectives, and say which suits (a) a notice on the wall, (b) an email to all users, (c) a word with one student she knows well.
::: solution
- Hearer-oriented: *Could you put the equipment away when you've finished?*
- Speaker-oriented: *Can I ask you to put the equipment away when you've finished?*
- Inclusive: *Let's all try to put the equipment away when we've finished.*
- Impersonal: *Equipment should be returned to the cupboards after use.* / *Please make sure equipment is put away after use.*

(a) A notice addresses no one in particular, so the **impersonal** form is usual; its directness is acceptable because no individual is being told off. (b) To all users, the **inclusive** form avoids implying that every reader is guilty, or an impersonal one softened with *please*. (c) With a student she knows, a **hearer-oriented** query preparatory is clear and friendly: *Could you put the microscopes away when you're done, Leila? Thanks!*
:::
:::

::: warning Word order in embedded questions
✗ *Could you tell me where is the station?* ✓ *Could you tell me where the station is?*

✗ *Do you know what time does the film start?* ✓ *Do you know what time the film starts?*

✗ *I was wondering could you help me.* ✓ *I was wondering if you could help me.*

Indirect questions are among the most common polite requests for information in English, and the question form is already in *Could you tell me* or *Do you know*: the embedded part keeps statement order, with no *do*-support, and a yes/no question is introduced by *if* or *whether* ([[complex-sentences/noun-clauses]]). In informal speech, *I was wondering, could you help me?* (with a pause) is also heard.
:::

::: quiz
According to Blum-Kulka (1987), which kind of request did English and Hebrew speakers rate as most polite?
- [ ] imperatives with *please*
- [ ] mild hints, because they are the most indirect
- [x] conventionally indirect requests such as *Could you…?*
- [ ] want statements such as *I'd like you to…*
::: solution
Conventionally indirect requests were rated most polite. Hints were less highly rated because they lack pragmatic clarity: the hearer has to work out the request, and the speaker can deny it.
:::

## Refusals

A refusal is the dispreferred answer to a request, an invitation, an offer or a suggestion. It threatens the other person's positive face (their wish is rejected) and can threaten the relationship, so in most languages refusals are among the most carefully managed acts — and among the most likely to go wrong between speakers of different backgrounds.

::: definition Refusal strategies {#def-refusal}
Beebe, Takahashi and Uliss-Weltz (1990), in a study of refusals by American English speakers, Japanese speakers and Japanese learners of English, classified the parts of a refusal as:

1. **direct** refusals: performative (*I refuse*) or non-performative (*No*; *I can't*; *I won't be able to*);
2. **indirect** refusals: statements of **regret** (*I'm sorry*, *I'm afraid*), **wishes** (*I wish I could*), **excuses, reasons and explanations** (*I've got a class on Thursday afternoons*), **alternatives** (*Could you ask Niamh?*), **conditions for future acceptance** (*If you'd asked me last week…*), **promises of future acceptance** (*Next time, definitely*), statements of **principle** (*I never lend money to friends*), attempts to **dissuade** (*I'd only slow you down*), and **avoidance** (silence, changing the subject, *I'll see*);
3. **adjuncts**, which accompany a refusal but do not refuse: positive opinion (*I'd love to*), empathy (*I know you're stuck*), pause fillers (*Oh*, *Well*), gratitude (*Thanks for thinking of me*).
:::

::: theorem The shape of a clear, polite refusal {#rule-refusal}
In English, a refusal that protects the relationship without leaving the other person unsure usually contains:

1. an **adjunct** — appreciation, empathy or a delay marker: *Oh, thanks for asking —*;
2. a **regret**: *I'm really sorry, but…* / *I'm afraid…*;
3. a **clear refusal**, often softened with modality: *I can't*, *I won't be able to*;
4. a **reason** specific enough to be believed: *I've got a class on Thursday afternoons*;
5. where possible, an **alternative** or a **future-oriented** move: *Could Niamh do it?* / *I could do next Thursday if that helps.*

The order varies, and short refusals between friends may contain only 3 and 4. What matters is that the refusal itself is said or unmistakably implied, and early enough for the other person to act on it.
:::

::: proof
Refusals are **dispreferred** responses, and conversation analysts have shown that dispreferred responses have a recognisable shape in English: they are delayed, prefaced (*Well…*, *Oh…*), softened and accompanied by an account (Pomerantz 1984; Levinson 1983). The shape itself signals the refusal before it arrives, which is why Hiroshi's *Hmm* would already have warned many listeners. But a delay and a preface are only the frame: the listener still expects the refusal to come. Hiroshi gave the frame (*Hmm, … a bit difficult…*) and then, instead of the refusal, an avoidance strategy (*I'll see what I can do*), which in English workplaces is commonly used and understood as a real, if weak, promise to try. The reason — the strongest signal that a refusal is final — was missing. Beebe et al. (1990) reported that the Japanese speakers in their study tended to give vaguer reasons than the American speakers, and that this pattern carried over into the learners' English; the same may happen with speakers of any language in which a vague reason is enough to refuse.
:::

::: example Three refusals {#ex-refusals}
Write refusals for each situation and explain your choices.

(a) Your classmate Bilal invites you to his birthday party on Saturday; you are flying home that day. (b) Your manager, Ms Achebe, asks if you can take on an extra project; you are already working on three. (c) Your neighbour Mrs Petrov offers you a lift to the station; you prefer to walk.
::: solution
(a) *Oh, thanks so much for inviting me! I'm really sorry — I'm flying home on Saturday morning. Have a brilliant time, though, and let's celebrate when I'm back?* — gratitude, regret, a specific reason that makes the refusal obviously not personal, a good wish and a future-oriented suggestion. Between classmates this is warm and clear.

(b) *Thank you for thinking of me. I'd like to help, but I'm worried I won't be able to give it proper attention at the moment — I've got the Harbour, Eastgate and Linden projects all due this month. Would it be possible to start it in June, or could I take on a smaller part of it?* — refusing a manager is risky, so the refusal is presented as concern for the quality of the work (*I'm worried I won't be able to*), the reason is concrete, and two alternatives make it a negotiation rather than a flat *no*.

(c) *That's really kind of you, but I'll walk, thanks — I like the exercise!* — refusing an offer is a small threat; appreciation, a short refusal and a light reason are enough. Note that in English a single refusal of an offer is usually taken as final ([[pragmatics/politeness-face]]), so do not refuse if you would like the lift.
:::
:::

::: warning Refusals that sound like *maybe*
✗ *It's a bit difficult.* / *I'll try.* / *I'll see what I can do.* / *Maybe.* / *I'll think about it.* (when you have already decided *no*)

✓ *I'm afraid I can't on Thursday — I've got a class.* / *I'd love to, but I won't be able to make it.*

In English, these vague phrases are often taken literally, as a possible *yes*. If you mean *no*, include a clear refusal or a reason that makes it obvious. If you genuinely need time, say so and give a time: *Can I let you know by tomorrow?*
:::

### Ritual and substantive refusals

Not every refusal is meant to be final. Chen Xing, Ye Lei and Zhang Yanyin (1995), in a study of refusals in Chinese, distinguished **substantive refusals**, which really decline, from **ritual refusals**, which are expected by politeness and are made with the intention of finally accepting — the guest's *不用了，不用了* to a host's offer, or the declining of a gift before taking it. The host is expected to insist, and the guest to give way. English has ritual refusals too (*Oh, you shouldn't have!* when given a present), but they are fewer, and with offers of food, drink and help a first refusal is usually treated as substantive. The difficulty for learners in both directions is to recognise which kind of refusal they are hearing — and, when making one, to make it clear which kind it is.

::: example Ritual or substantive? {#ex-ritual}
Decide whether each refusal is probably ritual or substantive, and what the other speaker should do next.

(a) Zhou Min's aunt hands her a red envelope at New Year. Zhou Min says: *哎呀，不用不用！* (b) Gabriel offers his colleague Sophie a biscuit. She says: *No thanks, I've just had lunch.* (c) Mr Haddad insists on paying for his guest's taxi. The guest says: *Oh, you shouldn't — really, I can pay.* Mr Haddad insists again, and the guest says: *Well, if you're sure. That's very kind.*
::: solution
(a) **Ritual**, in many families: declining a gift from an elder before accepting it shows modesty. The aunt is expected to insist, and Zhou Min to accept with thanks.

(b) **Substantive**: the refusal comes with a specific reason, and in an English-speaking workplace a first refusal of food is normally taken at face value. Gabriel should accept it: *Fair enough!*

(c) The first refusal is **ritual** (*you shouldn't* is a formula), and both speakers seem to share the convention: Mr Haddad insists once, and the guest accepts with *if you're sure*, a standard English way of yielding to an insistent offer. A host who is unsure can ask once, lightly — *Are you sure?* — and then believe the answer.
:::
:::

::: quiz
Kemal asks his colleague: *Could you look over my report before I send it?* She is too busy. Which reply is clearest without being rude?
- [ ] *Hmm… I'll see.*
- [ ] *No.*
- [x] *Sorry, Kemal, I can't today — I've got a deadline at five. Could Ying have a look?*
- [ ] *Maybe later, if I get time.*
::: solution
The third reply has regret, a clear refusal, a specific reason and an alternative. *I'll see* and *Maybe later* leave Kemal waiting; *No* alone is likely to be heard as unfriendly between colleagues.
:::
:::

## Answering requests and refusals

The other side of each act also needs care. Agreeing to a request is easy, but English has some conventions: answer the request, not the literal question (*Could you…? — Sure!*, not *Yes, I could*); after *Would you mind…?* agreement is *Not at all* or *Of course not* ([[everyday-english/requests-offers#rule-would-you-mind]]); and a warm agreement may add something: *Of course — happy to help*, *No problem at all*, *With pleasure* (formal).

When you are refused, the polite response accepts the refusal quickly and lightly, so that the other person does not feel guilty: *No worries — thanks anyway!*, *Of course, don't worry about it*, *That's fine, I'll ask Niamh.* Pressing again after a clear refusal with a reason (*Are you sure? It would only take an hour…*) threatens the other's negative face a second time, and in many English-speaking settings is felt as pushy, especially at work.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Reasons first or request first.** Chinese requests often put the reasons and background first and the request at the end (因为…所以…), and English requests often state the request early and the reason after it. Kirkpatrick (1991), analysing Mandarin letters of request, and Zhang Yanyin (1995), studying spoken requests, describe this tendency. Both orders are possible in both languages, and both are polite. In English emails, though, a request delayed to the last paragraph can leave the reader wondering what is being asked; in a long message, state the purpose early ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]], [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).
- **帮我个忙 and *Could you do me a favour?*** Both are pre-commitments. Some English speakers are uncomfortable agreeing before they know what the favour is, and may answer *Depends what it is!* (as a joke, but meaning it). Saying the request at once is often easier: *Could you do me a favour and water my plants while I'm away?*
- **我考虑一下 and 再说吧.** Used as gentle refusals in Chinese, their English translations — *I'll think about it*, *We'll see* — are often taken literally. Add a reason or a clear signal if you mean *no*.
- **不好意思.** Its English counterparts at the start of a request are *Sorry to bother you*, *Excuse me* and *Sorry, could I…?* — British English in particular uses *sorry* very freely here ([[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]]).
- **Insisting.** A host's repeated offers and a guest's ritual refusals, described by Chen, Ye and Zhang (1995) and by Gu (1990), work well when both people share the convention. With someone who does not, accept what you want the first time, and accept their first refusal.
:::

::: history
Requests were the first speech act to be studied on a large scale across languages. After Searle's "Indirect speech acts" (1975), Ervin-Tripp ("Is Sybil there? The structure of some American English directives", *Language in Society*, 1976) described the forms of directives in natural American English talk and how they varied with the relationship between speakers. Blum-Kulka and Olshtain (1984) set out the design of the Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project in *Applied Linguistics*, and the full results appeared in 1989. The discourse completion task the project used was quick and comparable across languages, but critics pointed out that written answers to imagined situations are not the same as what people say in real conversation — they tend to be shorter and to lack the negotiation over several turns that real requests and refusals involve. Later research therefore combined questionnaires with role plays and recordings of real talk. Beebe, Takahashi and Uliss-Weltz's classification of refusals (1990) became the standard scheme for that act, and Chinese refusals and requests were studied in the collection *Pragmatics of Chinese as Native and Target Language*, edited by Gabriele Kasper (1995).
:::

## Where this leads

The research on directness and how it varies between speakers and communities is the subject of [[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]. Requests and refusals in emails, including follow-ups and chasers, are in [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]. Apologies, which often accompany both acts, are analysed in the next chapter, [[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]]. In meetings and negotiations, refusing and making counter-proposals are practised in [[presentations-and-discussion/meetings-negotiation]].

::: summary
- A request has a **head act**, often with **alerters** and **supportive moves** (preparators, grounders, disarmers, imposition minimisers) ([[#def-request-parts]]).
- The head act is softened internally by **syntactic** and **lexical downgraders** — *could*, *I was wondering*, *possibly*, *just*, *a bit* — and strengthened by **upgraders** ([[#rule-modifiers]]).
- Requests are **direct**, **conventionally indirect** or **non-conventionally indirect** (hints); in English, conventional indirectness is the main polite form, and hints are not rated most polite because they lack clarity ([[#rule-ccsarp-levels]]).
- Speaker-oriented and impersonal **perspectives** (*Could I…?*, *Would it be possible…?*) reduce the imposition ([[#def-perspective]]).
- Refusals combine direct refusals, indirect strategies (regret, reasons, alternatives) and adjuncts ([[#def-refusal]]).
- A good English refusal is delayed and softened but **clear**, with a specific reason; vague phrases such as *I'll see* are often taken as *maybe* ([[#rule-refusal]]).
- Distinguish **ritual** from **substantive** refusals; in English a first refusal of an offer is usually substantive.
:::

::: exercise Same request, different people {level=2}
You need someone to feed your cat for a week. Write the request (a) to your brother, (b) to a neighbour you have spoken to twice, (c) in a message to a pet-sitting service. Explain the differences in terms of distance, power and imposition.
::: solution
Possible answers:

(a) *Can you feed Mochi for me next week? I'm away from the 3rd. I'll bring you something back!* — small distance; the imposition is real, so there is a reason and a light promise of reward.

(b) *Hi, sorry to bother you — I'm Yuki from number 14. I'm away from the 3rd to the 10th, and I was wondering if you might possibly be able to feed my cat once a day? It only takes five minutes, and I'd be very happy to return the favour. Please say no if it's not convenient!* — large distance and a large imposition, so many supportive moves: self-identification, grounder, minimiser, reward, an easy way out.

(c) *Hello, I'd like to book daily cat-feeding visits from 3 to 10 May. Could you let me know if you have availability, and the price?* — a service relationship: the provider is paid, so the imposition is low and a want statement (*I'd like to book*) and a simple query preparatory are appropriate.
:::

::: solution
Possible answers: (a) *No worries! Hope it goes well — next time.* (b) *I understand, thank you. I'll get a certificate from my doctor and send it as soon as I can.* (c) *Of course — the stop's just round the corner. Safe journey!* In each case the refusal is accepted at once; pressing again would be felt as pushy.

::: exercise Diagnose a misunderstanding {level=3}
Reread the messages between Aoife and Hiroshi at the start of the chapter. (a) Analyse Hiroshi's reply with Beebe et al.'s categories. (b) Explain why Aoife misunderstood. (c) Write the reply Hiroshi should have sent, and a short apology for the one he did send.
::: solution
(a) *Hmm* is a pause filler (adjunct); *Thursday is a bit difficult* is a very vague reason, close to avoidance, with the understater *a bit*; *I'll see what I can do* is avoidance in the form of a commitment to try. There is no direct refusal, no regret and no specific reason.

(b) In English, *I'll see what I can do* is commonly a weak but real promise. Without a clear refusal or a specific reason, Aoife had no reason to think the answer was *no*, and her own message had invited him to say no easily (*Totally fine if not*), so she assumed he would have done so.

(c) *Hi Aoife, sorry — I can't swap on Thursday, I've got a class every Thursday afternoon. I hope the appointment goes OK! Maybe ask Niamh?* Apology: *Aoife, I'm so sorry — I should have said straight away that I couldn't swap. I've got a class on Thursdays. I hope you can still find someone; I'll ask around too.* The apology admits the fault, gives the reason and offers repair ([[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]]).

::: exercise Role play and reflection {level=3}
With a partner, act out the three situations below twice: once following the conventions of this chapter, and once following the conventions you would use in your first language. Record or write down what you said, then compare the two versions: their length, the order of the reason and the request or refusal, and the number of turns. (a) Asking a classmate to share their notes. (b) Refusing an invitation to dinner from your landlord. (c) Asking a stranger to swap seats on a train so that you can sit with your friend.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A good reflection describes concrete differences, for example: in the first-language version the reason came before the request; there were more turns of insistence and ritual refusal; address terms were used where English used none; or the English version was shorter and stated the request or refusal earlier. It avoids generalising from two speakers to whole cultures, notes where the two versions were the same, and comments on which version each partner felt more comfortable with — and why.
