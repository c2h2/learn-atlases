Fang Yu has invited her new colleague Oliver to dinner at her flat in Glasgow. She has cooked far too much, on purpose, and as soon as his bowl is half empty she offers him more.

**Fang Yu:** *Have some more fish!*

**Oliver:** *Oh, no thanks, I'm fine — it was delicious.*

**Fang Yu:** *Come on, just a little more. You've hardly eaten anything!*

**Oliver:** *Honestly, I'm full. Thank you.*

**Fang Yu** (putting a piece of fish in his bowl): *Don't be polite!*

Oliver ate the fish, but he left feeling slightly pushed: he had said *no* twice, and his *no* had not been accepted. Fang Yu, for her part, wondered whether he had really enjoyed the meal, because he had refused so quickly and so firmly. In her family, a guest refuses once or twice out of modesty, and a host who takes the first refusal at face value seems not to care. Both of them were being polite. They simply had different ideas of what politeness required.

This chapter is about those ideas. It explains the concept of **face**, the most influential theory of politeness — Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson's — and the alternatives and critiques that followed, including Geoffrey Leech's maxims, Helen Spencer-Oatey's rapport management, and Chinese accounts of 礼貌, 面子 and 脸. The previous chapter, [[pragmatics/speech-acts]], showed that requests and other acts are often performed indirectly; this one explains why. The grammar of polite forms — *could*, *would*, *I was wondering if* — is in [[modality/modality-politeness]], and is not repeated here.

## Face

::: definition Face (Goffman) {#def-face}
The sociologist Erving Goffman defined **face** as "the positive social value a person effectively claims for himself by the line others assume he has taken during a particular contact" (Goffman 1967: 5, first published as "On face-work", 1955). Face is not something a person owns privately: it is given by others during an interaction, and it can be lost, saved or given. **Face-work** is what people do to keep their own and others' face — avoiding embarrassing topics, accepting an excuse, pretending not to notice a mistake.
:::

Brown and Levinson took Goffman's idea and made it the basis of a theory of language. Their essay "Universals in language usage: politeness phenomena" (1978), reissued as the book *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987), proposed that every competent adult — their "Model Person" — has two kinds of face want.

::: definition Positive and negative face {#def-pos-neg-face}
In Brown and Levinson's theory (1987):

1. **Positive face** is the want to be liked, approved of and appreciated — for one's wants, possessions and qualities to be thought desirable by at least some others.
2. **Negative face** is the want to be unimpeded — to have freedom of action and freedom from imposition.

An act that by its nature runs against one of these wants is a **face-threatening act** (FTA).
:::

The word *negative* does not mean ‘bad’: negative face is the wish to be left alone, and positive face the wish to be included. Almost every act that matters threatens someone's face in some way:

| whose face | positive face threatened by | negative face threatened by |
|---|---|---|
| the hearer's | criticism, disagreement, complaints, insults, raising a taboo topic, ignoring the hearer | requests, orders, advice, reminders, warnings; offers and promises (they pressure the hearer to accept) |
| the speaker's | apologies, confessions, accepting a compliment (risking immodesty), losing control in public | accepting thanks or an offer (incurring a debt), making a promise, giving an excuse |

## Strategies of politeness

::: theorem Brown and Levinson's five strategies {#rule-strategies}
When a speaker decides to perform a face-threatening act, Brown and Levinson (1987) describe five broad options, from the least to the most protective:

1. **Bald on record** — say it directly, with no softening: *Close the door.* *You're late.*
2. **Positive politeness** — attend to the hearer's positive face: show liking, interest, common ground, solidarity. *Close the door, love, it's freezing.* *We're all a bit tired — shall we finish this tomorrow?*
3. **Negative politeness** — attend to the hearer's negative face: be indirect, hedge, apologise, minimise the imposition, show deference. *Sorry to bother you — could you possibly close the door?*
4. **Off record** — do the act in a way that can be denied: hint, understate, be ironic or vague. *It's a bit chilly in here.*
5. **Don't do the FTA** — say nothing; put up with the cold.
:::

::: widget graph

nodes: do the FTA@-2,8; on record@-4,6; off record (4)@1,6; baldly without redress (1)@-6,4; with redressive action@-2,4; positive politeness (2)@-4,2; negative politeness (3)@0,2; don't do the FTA (5)@4,8

caption: The five strategies of [[#rule-strategies]], after the diagram in Brown and Levinson (1987). The numbers run from least to most protective of face: the greater the estimated risk of the act, the higher the number a speaker is predicted to choose. *Redressive action* means wording that "repairs" the threat in advance — a compliment, an apology, a hedge. Real utterances often mix strategies: *Sorry, love, could you close the door?* uses an in-group term (positive) and an apology and a modal question (negative). You can drag the boxes.
:::

How does a speaker decide? Brown and Levinson proposed that the seriousness, or **weightiness**, of an act depends on three social variables, which they summed:

$$W_x = D(S,H) + P(H,S) + R_x$$

Here $D$ is the social **distance** between speaker and hearer, $P$ the **power** of the hearer over the speaker, and $R_x$ the ranking of the **imposition** of act $x$ in that culture. The formula is not meant to be calculated with real numbers; it says that each variable independently raises the risk. A small favour from a close friend calls for little redress; the same favour from a stranger, or a large favour from a friend, calls for more ([[modality/modality-politeness#rule-directness]]).

::: proof
The theory explains a great deal with a small apparatus. It explains why indirect requests exist at all: a request threatens negative face, and indirectness leaves the hearer an escape route. It explains why disagreement is softened with partial agreement and hedges ([[listening-and-conversation/opinions]]): disagreement threatens positive face. It explains why close friends can be blunt and why over-polite requests between friends sound cold: they treat a short distance as a long one. And it explains off-record speech, including hints and irony, as the safest way to do something risky, because the speaker can deny having done it ([[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words#rule-cancel]]). The theory also makes testable predictions, which is why so much research — and criticism — followed.
:::

::: example Distance, power and imposition {#ex-weightiness}
Shirin needs to borrow a phone charger. Rank the three situations by the weightiness of the request, say which variable changes, and suggest a suitable wording for each.

(a) from her sister, for an hour; (b) from a passenger she does not know, on a long train journey, for an hour; (c) from her sister, for the whole of a two-week holiday.
::: solution
(a) is the lightest: small **distance**, no **power** difference, small **imposition**. *Can I borrow your charger for a bit?* or even *Chuck me your charger?* — bald on record or light positive politeness.

(b) raises **distance**: the same small favour from a stranger. *Sorry to bother you — could I possibly borrow your charger for a few minutes? My phone's about to die.* — negative politeness with a reason.

(c) raises **imposition** while distance stays small: *Would you mind if I took your charger on holiday? I'll buy you a new one if mine doesn't turn up* — more redress, and an offer, even to a sister.

(b) and (c) are both weightier than (a), for different reasons; which is weightier depends on the people. That is the point of the formula: the variables add up, and each can be enough on its own to call for more politeness.
:::
:::

Each strategy has many sub-strategies; Brown and Levinson listed fifteen for positive politeness, ten for negative politeness and fifteen for off record. Some of the most frequent in English are:

- **Positive politeness**: notice the hearer (*You've had your hair cut — it looks great*); use in-group markers (*mate*, *guys*, first names, shared jargon); seek agreement and avoid disagreement (*Yeah, I know what you mean, but…*); joke; include both speaker and hearer (*Let's…*, *Shall we…?*); give reasons (*…because otherwise we'll miss the train*).
- **Negative politeness**: be conventionally indirect (*Could you…?*); hedge (*I was sort of hoping…*); be pessimistic (*I don't suppose you could…?*); minimise the imposition (*just a quick question*, *a moment*); give deference (*sir*, *Professor Okafor*); apologise (*Sorry to bother you*); impersonalise (*It would be appreciated if…*); go on record as incurring a debt (*I'd be eternally grateful*).
- **Off record**: hint (*I've left my wallet at home*); understate (*It's not the best essay you've written*); use tautology (*A deadline's a deadline*); be ironic (*Lovely weather for a picnic*); ask rhetorical questions (*Who knows?*); be vague (*Some people have been leaving the kitchen in a mess*).

::: example Naming the strategy {#ex-strategy}
Name the main strategy in each utterance (bald on record, positive politeness, negative politeness, off record) and the sub-strategy.

(a) Paola, to her housemate: *You're a better cook than me — any chance you could do the rice tonight?*
(b) A sign in a lift: *Do not use in case of fire.*
(c) Dr Mensah, to a visiting professor: *I'm sorry to impose, but would you possibly have time to look at a short draft?*
(d) Ibrahim, to his brother, who has borrowed his car: *Funny — the tank was full when I parked it.*
(e) Jae-won, to his team: *Right, let's get this finished and we can all go home.*
::: solution
(a) **Positive politeness** (a compliment that attends to the hearer's wants and qualities), followed by a fairly informal request (*any chance you could*), itself slightly hedged.
(b) **Bald on record**. In an emergency, or where efficiency matters more than face, Brown and Levinson predict bald on record; written notices and instructions also use it, because no particular hearer's face is addressed.
(c) **Negative politeness**: an apology (*I'm sorry to impose*), a conventional indirect question with *would*, a hedge (*possibly*) and a minimiser (*short*).
(d) **Off record**: a hint, made with irony. Ibrahim says nothing about his brother or the petrol, so he could deny complaining — but the message is clear.
(e) **Positive politeness**: including speaker and hearers in the activity (*let's*, *we*), and giving a reason that serves everyone (*we can all go home*). It is also a directive from a team leader.
:::
:::

::: quiz
In Brown and Levinson's terms, a request mainly threatens the hearer's
- [ ] positive face, because it suggests the hearer is unpopular
- [x] negative face, because it limits the hearer's freedom of action
- [ ] positive face, because the hearer may be criticised
- [ ] negative face, because it shows the speaker is weak
::: solution
A request asks the hearer to act, and so impinges on the want to be unimpeded: negative face. That is why most request softeners — *could*, *possibly*, *just*, *sorry to bother you* — are negative-politeness strategies.

## Leech: maxims of politeness

Geoffrey Leech approached politeness from Grice's side. In *Principles of Pragmatics* (1983) he argued that a **Politeness Principle** works alongside the Cooperative Principle ([[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words#rule-cp]]): people are often less informative or less truthful than the maxims require because they are being polite. Why say *I'm afraid I'm busy that evening* rather than *I don't want to come*? Because the Politeness Principle overrides Quantity and perhaps Quality.

::: theorem Leech's maxims of politeness {#rule-leech}
Leech (1983: 132) stated the Politeness Principle in six maxims, each with a main part and a less important second part:

| maxim | minimise | maximise | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tact** | cost to other | benefit to other | *Could I borrow it for just five minutes?* |
| **Generosity** | benefit to self | cost to self | *Let me get this — you paid last time.* |
| **Approbation** | dispraise of other | praise of other | *What a lovely garden!* |
| **Modesty** | praise of self | dispraise of self | *Oh, it's just something I threw together.* |
| **Agreement** | disagreement between self and other | agreement | *Yes, definitely — though I wonder about the cost.* |
| **Sympathy** | antipathy between self and other | sympathy | *I was so sorry to hear about your exam.* |
:::

::: proof
Leech's maxims make politeness a matter of balancing values between self and other: what is polite is to give the other person a slightly larger share of benefit, praise and agreement than oneself. The maxims also explain why the parts can conflict. Accepting a compliment satisfies Agreement but strains Modesty; refusing it satisfies Modesty but strains Agreement — a conflict that different communities resolve differently ([[pragmatics/apologies-thanks-compliments]]). In *The Pragmatics of Politeness* (2014) Leech reorganised the maxims under a single **General Strategy of Politeness**: in order to be polite, a speaker expresses or implies meanings that place a high value on what concerns the other person and a low value on what concerns the speaker. He also distinguished **pragmalinguistic** politeness (the politeness of a form, such as *Could you…?* versus *Do it*) from **sociopragmatic** politeness (what counts as appropriate in a given social situation), a distinction that matters greatly for learners.
:::

::: example Leech's maxims at work {#ex-leech}
Analyse this exchange with Leech's maxims. Which maxims does each speaker observe, and where do they conflict?

**Ruth:** *These dumplings are amazing — did you make them yourself?*
**Kenta:** *I did, but the wrappers are a bit thick. My grandmother's are much better.*
**Ruth:** *Well, I've never managed to make a single one. Can I take one more?*
**Kenta:** *Take as many as you like — I made far too many.*
::: solution
- Ruth's first turn observes **Approbation** (praise of other) and shows interest in Kenta's work.
- Kenta observes **Modesty** (dispraise of self: *the wrappers are a bit thick*; praise of a third party, his grandmother). He accepts the factual part (*I did*) but downgrades the praise. Agreement and Modesty are in mild conflict, resolved by accepting the fact and rejecting the evaluation.
- Ruth's *I've never managed to make a single one* observes **Modesty** for herself and implicitly restores the praise (Approbation); asking permission for *one more* observes **Tact** — she minimises the cost (*one*) and asks rather than takes.
- Kenta observes **Generosity**: *Take as many as you like* maximises benefit to the other, and *I made far too many* minimises the apparent cost, so that Ruth does not feel she is taking something valuable.
:::
:::

## Spencer-Oatey: managing rapport

Brown and Levinson's theory is about protecting face from threats. Helen Spencer-Oatey argued that it says too little about the social expectations people bring to an interaction, and too little about relationships, not only individuals. Her framework of **rapport management** (Spencer-Oatey 2000; 2nd edition 2008) is widely used in research on intercultural communication.

::: definition Rapport management {#def-rapport}
In Spencer-Oatey's framework (2008), the harmony or disharmony between people — **rapport** — depends on three things:

1. **Face**, concerned with people's sense of worth: **quality face** (personal qualities such as competence or appearance), **social identity face** (one's standing as a member of a group — a teacher, a family, a company) and **relational face** (how one is valued in a particular relationship).
2. **Sociality rights and obligations**, concerned with what people feel entitled to: **equity rights** (to be treated fairly, not to be exploited or unduly imposed on) and **association rights** (to an appropriate degree of involvement with others — neither too much nor too little).
3. **Interactional goals**, both transactional (getting a task done) and relational.

Speakers may aim to **enhance**, **maintain**, **neglect** or **challenge** rapport.
:::

The advantage of the framework for cross-cultural pragmatics is that it separates a **threat to face** from an **infringement of rights**. When Oliver felt "pushed" by Fang Yu's insistence, his face was not threatened — he was being honoured — but his equity rights, as he understood them, were infringed: his *no* was not respected. When Fang Yu wondered whether he had enjoyed the meal, she was responding to a possible threat to her relational and quality face as a host. Seeing the two concerns separately makes it easier to understand that neither person intended offence.

## Chinese politeness: 礼貌, 面子 and 脸

Brown and Levinson subtitled their book *Some Universals in Language Usage*, and claimed that their face wants and strategies are found in all cultures, though weighted differently. Some of the strongest critiques came from researchers working on Chinese and Japanese.

::: definition 面子 and 脸 {#def-mianzi-lian}
The anthropologist Hu Hsien Chin, in "The Chinese concepts of 'face'" (*American Anthropologist*, 1944), distinguished two Chinese terms that English translates with one word:

1. **面子** (*miànzi*) — prestige or reputation achieved through success, position and display; it can be gained, increased, given (给面子) and lost (没面子), and its loss is embarrassing.
2. **脸** (*liǎn*) — the respect of the community for a person of decent moral character; its loss (丢脸) is a matter of shame, and to be called 不要脸 is a severe condemnation, because it suggests the person no longer cares about moral judgement.

Both are social: they exist in the eyes of others, as Goffman's face does, but 脸 is tied to moral standing in a way that neither of Brown and Levinson's face wants is.
:::

::: theorem Gu Yueguo's maxims of Chinese politeness {#rule-gu}
Gu Yueguo, in "Politeness phenomena in modern Chinese" (*Journal of Pragmatics*, 1990), traced modern 礼貌 to the Confucian concept of 礼 and identified four underlying notions — **respectfulness**, **modesty**, **attitudinal warmth** and **refinement**. He described Chinese politeness with maxims including:

1. the **Self-denigration Maxim** (贬己尊人): denigrate self and elevate other — 寒舍 for one's own home, 贵姓 in asking someone's name;
2. the **Address Term Maxim** (称呼准则): address the hearer with an appropriate term, reflecting relative status and relationship — 王老师, 李总, 师傅, 阿姨;
3. the **Refinement Maxim** (文雅准则): choose refined language and avoid the coarse;
4. the **Agreement Maxim** (求同准则): seek harmony and agreement, and soften disagreement;
5. the **Virtues–Words–Deeds Maxim** (德、言、行准则): in deeds, minimise the other's cost and maximise the benefit received; in words, maximise the benefit to the other and minimise the cost to oneself.
:::

::: proof
Gu's critique of Brown and Levinson had two parts. First, he argued that Chinese politeness is **normative**: speakers are polite because society expects certain behaviour and sanctions its absence, not mainly because they are calculating threats to individual wants. Second, he argued that Brown and Levinson's **negative face** does not explain Chinese offering and inviting. In their theory an offer threatens the hearer's freedom; in Gu's account, a host who insists — as Fang Yu did — is showing attitudinal warmth and generosity, and the guest's first refusals are a ritual display of modesty, so neither act is felt as an imposition. LuMing Mao, in "Beyond politeness theory: 'face' revisited and renewed" (*Journal of Pragmatics*, 1994), built on Hu's distinction and proposed that face is oriented differently in different communities: towards an **ideal social identity** — standing in the eyes of the group — in Chinese, and towards an **ideal individual autonomy** in the Anglo-American model Brown and Levinson took as their base. Similar critiques came from Japanese: Matsumoto (1988) argued that Japanese face is about one's place in relationships rather than freedom from imposition, and Ide (1989) that much Japanese politeness is **discernment** (*wakimae*) — the socially obligatory choice of forms — rather than strategy.
:::

These critiques did not make Brown and Levinson's theory useless; their strategies still describe much of what speakers of Chinese, Japanese and English do. What the critiques showed is that the *reasons* for politeness, and the acts that count as threatening, are not the same everywhere. A second wave of research went further. Gino Eelen (*A Critique of Politeness Theories*, 2001) and Richard Watts (*Politeness*, 2003) distinguished **first-order politeness** — what ordinary people in a community call polite or rude — from **second-order politeness**, the scholar's theoretical model, and argued that theories had too often imposed the second on the first. Jonathan Culpeper ("Towards an anatomy of impoliteness", 1996) showed that **impoliteness** — deliberate attack on face — has its own strategies and deserves study in its own right.

::: warning Refusing an offer you would like to accept
✗ *Oh no, please don't go to any trouble* (when you would like a drink, expecting the host to insist). ✓ *Oh, yes please — that would be lovely.*

✗ (as a host) accepting a guest's *No thanks, I'm fine* as final when you think it is only modesty, and then feeling hurt. ✓ Offer once more, lightly — *Are you sure? There's plenty* — and then accept the answer.

In many English-speaking settings, a first refusal is taken at face value, so ritual refusals can leave you without the thing you wanted. Equally, a guest's *no* in English is usually meant. Neither convention is more polite than the other: they are different ways of protecting the same relationship.
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- ***Lose face*** **and *save face***. English *lose face* is generally explained in dictionaries as a translation of a Chinese expression, adopted by English speakers in China in the nineteenth century; *save face* was formed later in English on the same model. So the English word *face* in this sense partly comes from Chinese. In English, *face* in ordinary speech mostly means 面子 — public standing — rather than 脸.
- **Address terms.** Gu's Address Term Maxim has no exact English parallel. Brown and Gilman ("The pronouns of power and solidarity", 1960) showed how languages such as French and German use a pair of pronouns (*tu*/*vous*, *du*/*Sie*) — like 你/您 — to mark power and solidarity. Modern English has only *you*, and uses names and titles instead: *Dr Lee*, *Sally*, *Mrs Ito*. Occupational titles used as address terms in Chinese (老师, 经理, 师傅) mostly have no English equivalent as address terms: ✗ *Hello, Teacher* ✓ *Hello, Ms Ito* ([[collocation-and-register/register]]).
- **Self-denigration.** 寒舍 and 拙作 have no routine English equivalents; *my humble home* is jocular or old-fashioned. English speakers display modesty more often by downgrading specifics (*It's only a small flat*) than by fixed formulas.
- **客气.** 别客气 / 不要客气 urges the hearer not to stand on ceremony. *Don't be polite!*, a word-for-word version, can sound in English as if politeness were a fault; *Help yourself!*, *Make yourself at home* or *Please, go ahead — there's plenty* express the warmth instead.
- **Variation.** Politeness norms differ between regions, generations, cities and the countryside, and families, in China as in every English-speaking country; urban professionals may follow conventions closer to the English ones described here, and many English speakers insist warmly when offering food. Describe what a particular person does, not what "the Chinese" or "the British" do ([[pragmatics/directness-across-cultures]]).
:::

::: example Two views of one incident {#ex-two-views}
At the end of a project, Ms Kowalska, a British manager, thanks one team member publicly: *I want to say a special thank you to Zhao Lin, whose analysis saved the project.* Zhao Lin looks uncomfortable and replies: *No, no — it was the whole team. I didn't do much.* Afterwards Ms Kowalska wonders whether she embarrassed him. Analyse the incident with (a) Brown and Levinson's theory, (b) Leech's maxims, and (c) Spencer-Oatey's rapport management.
::: solution
(a) **Brown and Levinson.** Ms Kowalska's public praise is positive politeness: it attends to Zhao Lin's positive face. But accepting a compliment threatens the speaker's own positive face (the risk of seeming immodest), so Zhao Lin protects his face by denying it.

(b) **Leech.** Ms Kowalska observes Approbation (praise of other). Zhao Lin resolves the conflict between Agreement and **Modesty** in favour of Modesty: he disagrees with the praise of himself (*I didn't do much*) and redirects it to the group.

(c) **Spencer-Oatey.** This framework adds the most. Singling out one person may have threatened Zhao Lin's **social identity face** as a member of the team, and his relations with colleagues who were not named — perhaps an **association rights** concern (being set apart from the group). His reply restores the group's standing and his place in it. The praise was meant to enhance rapport, and probably did, but the public form had a cost Ms Kowalska had not expected.

None of the analyses requires a claim about national character: many people in many cultures would feel as Zhao Lin did, and many would enjoy the praise. A manager who knows the person can ask beforehand, or thank them privately as well.
:::
:::

::: history
The study of politeness took shape in the 1970s. Erving Goffman's essay "On face-work" appeared in the journal *Psychiatry* in 1955 and was reprinted in *Interaction Ritual* (1967). Robin Lakoff's paper "The logic of politeness; or, minding your p's and q's" (1973) proposed three rules — don't impose, give options, make the other person feel good. Penelope Brown, who had done fieldwork on Tzeltal in Mexico, and Stephen Levinson, who had worked on Tamil in India, published their long essay in 1978 and reissued it, with a new introduction, as a book in 1987. Geoffrey Leech's *Principles of Pragmatics* appeared in 1983. Hu Hsien Chin's article on 面子 and 脸 had been published in 1944; it was rediscovered by pragmatics in the debates of the late 1980s and 1990s, when Matsumoto (1988), Ide (1989), Gu (1990) and Mao (1994) questioned the universality of Brown and Levinson's model. Helen Spencer-Oatey's *Culturally Speaking* (2000; 2nd edition 2008), Eelen's critique (2001), Watts's *Politeness* (2003), Leech's *The Pragmatics of Politeness* (2014) and Culpeper's *Impoliteness* (2011) mark the later stages of the debate, which continues in the *Journal of Politeness Research*, founded in 2005.
:::

::: exercise Positive or negative face? {level=1}
Which face, positive or negative, does each act mainly threaten, and whose — the speaker's or the hearer's?

(a) *Can you lend me your car this weekend?* (b) *I think your conclusion is wrong.* (c) *I'm so sorry — I broke your mug.* (d) *You really should see a doctor about that cough.* (e) *Thank you, that's very kind* (accepting a favour).
::: solution
(a) Hearer's negative face (a request). (b) Hearer's positive face (disagreement, criticism). (c) Speaker's positive face (an apology admits a fault). (d) Hearer's negative face (advice limits freedom of action); also slightly positive face, since it suggests the hearer has not been sensible. (e) Speaker's negative face (accepting a favour incurs a debt).
:::
:::

::: exercise Which strategy? {level=1}
Label each version of the same request as bald on record, positive politeness, negative politeness or off record.

(a) *Give me a hand with these boxes.* (b) *I'm so sorry to ask, but could you possibly help me carry these boxes?* (c) *These boxes are really heavy…* (d) *Hey, you're strong — help me with these boxes and I'll buy you a coffee!*
::: solution
(a) Bald on record. (b) Negative politeness (apology, conventional indirectness, hedge). (c) Off record (a hint). (d) Positive politeness (compliment, promise of reward, friendly tone), with a bald imperative.
:::
:::

::: exercise Which maxim? {level=1}
Which of Leech's maxims does each utterance observe?

(a) *No, no, let me pay.* (b) *Your English is excellent.* (c) *My essay's not great, but here it is.* (d) *I completely agree — that's a really good point.* (e) *Could I possibly borrow just one chair?*
::: solution
(a) Generosity (maximise cost to self). (b) Approbation (praise of other). (c) Modesty (dispraise of self). (d) Agreement. (e) Tact (minimise cost to other: *possibly*, *just one*).
:::

::: exercise Raise the redress {level=2}
Rewrite each bald-on-record act twice: once with positive politeness and once with negative politeness.

(a) *Send me your slides.* (to a colleague) (b) *You've got the date wrong.* (to your supervisor) (c) *Move your bag.* (to a stranger on a train)
::: solution
Possible answers:

(a) Positive: *Your slides looked brilliant — could you send them over? I'd love to borrow a couple of ideas.* Negative: *Sorry to bother you, but would you mind sending me your slides when you have a moment?*

(b) Positive: *I think we've both been looking at the old timetable — isn't it the 14th now?* (inclusive *we*, shared blame). Negative: *Sorry, I may be wrong, but I think the deadline might be the 14th rather than the 12th?* (apology, hedges).

(c) Positive: *Hi — mind if I squeeze in here? Busy today, isn't it!* (friendly, common ground). Negative: *Excuse me, I'm sorry — would you mind if I sat here?* (apology, conventional indirectness). Note that both versions avoid naming the bag at all; the request is clear from context.
:::
:::

::: exercise 面子, 脸 and *face* {level=2}
Translate or explain each expression in English, and say whether English *face* would work in the translation.

(a) 给我个面子 (b) 他丢脸了 (c) 这件事让她很没面子 (d) 他真不要脸
::: solution
(a) ‘Do it for my sake’ / ‘Do me this favour’ — *face* does not work naturally (*give me face* is not English). (b) ‘He disgraced himself’ / ‘He was humiliated’; *He lost face* works if the loss is of public standing rather than moral character. (c) ‘This was very embarrassing for her’ / ‘She lost face over this’ — *lose face* works well here. (d) ‘He's utterly shameless’ / ‘He has no shame’ — *face* does not work; English expresses the moral judgement with *shame*.
:::
:::

::: exercise Gu or Brown and Levinson? {level=2}
For each situation, explain how Brown and Levinson's theory and Gu's (1990) account would interpret it.

(a) A host refills a guest's glass three times although the guest says *Enough!* each time. (b) A student addresses her tutor as *Professor Wang* in every email although he signs his emails *Jian*.
::: solution
(a) **Brown and Levinson**: the offers threaten the guest's negative face; insistence is an escalating imposition, and the guest's *Enough!* should end it. **Gu**: insistence shows attitudinal warmth and generosity (Virtues–Words–Deeds Maxim), and the guest's refusals may be a ritual display of modesty; the exchange may be polite on both sides.

(b) **Brown and Levinson**: the title is negative politeness (deference), suitable to a large power difference; continuing after the tutor signals a shorter distance (by signing *Jian*) keeps the distance he has tried to reduce, and may be felt as slightly cold. **Gu**: the Address Term Maxim makes the respectful term the correct, normative choice regardless of the tutor's signature. In practice, many English-speaking academics expect students to follow their lead; a middle course is to ask: *Would you prefer me to call you Jian?*
:::

::: exercise Analyse a dinner {level=3}
Reread the dialogue between Fang Yu and Oliver at the start of the chapter. Write a short analysis (150–200 words) using at least three concepts from this chapter, explaining why both felt uncomfortable. Then rewrite the exchange so that it goes smoothly, keeping both speakers' values.
::: hint
Think about what *no* means to each of them, Gu's view of insistence, and Spencer-Oatey's equity rights.
:::
::: solution
A model analysis: Fang Yu's offers are, for her, expressions of warmth and generosity, as Gu (1990) describes Chinese hospitality: a guest's first refusal is ritual modesty, and a good host insists. Oliver follows a different convention, in which a refusal is meant literally and a host who accepts it respects the guest's negative face — his freedom to decide how much to eat. In Spencer-Oatey's terms, her insistence infringed what he felt were his **equity rights**, though his face was not threatened; his quick refusals put her **quality face** as a host at risk, because they could mean that he did not like the food. *Don't be polite!*, translating 别客气, made matters worse by suggesting that his politeness was the problem. Neither intended offence.

A smoother version:

**Fang Yu:** *Have some more fish! There's plenty.*
**Oliver:** *It's delicious — I'd love a little more, actually, but just a small piece. I'm getting full!*
**Fang Yu:** *Good! I'm glad you like it.*

Here Oliver accepts first, praising the food (Approbation), and limits the amount; Fang Yu shows warmth without needing to insist. Other good versions are possible, for instance Fang Yu explaining the custom with a smile: *In my family we always offer three times — so say no if you really mean it!*
:::

::: exercise Collect and classify {level=3}
Over a few days, collect five requests or refusals from conversations, emails or messages you take part in (in English or Chinese). For each one, write down the words, the relationship between the people, and the strategy (bald on record, positive politeness, negative politeness, off record). Then write a paragraph on what your examples suggest about the influence of distance, power and imposition.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A good answer gives exact wording; notes the relationship (friend, family, teacher, stranger, colleague) and the size of the request; labels strategies accurately, often more than one per utterance; and draws a cautious conclusion — for example, that requests to close friends were mostly bald on record or positively polite, that requests to teachers and strangers used negative politeness, and that large requests were softened more than small ones even among friends. It should also note any examples that do not fit the predictions (a manager being very indirect to a junior colleague, for instance) and suggest why, since one person's examples are not a representative sample.
:::
