It is Mariam's first week in an office in Leeds. On Thursday afternoon the printer jams for the third time in an hour. Her colleague Colin opens the side panel, pulls out a crumpled sheet and says, without smiling:

**Colin:** *Brilliant. Just what I needed today.*

**Mariam:** *Oh good — is it working now?*

**Colin** (after a pause): *…No. No, it's not.*

On Friday, Mariam asks another colleague, Bridget, how her weekend camping trip had gone; Bridget had told her it rained the whole time and the tent leaked.

**Bridget:** *Well, it wasn't the best weekend of my life.*

Mariam nodded politely, thinking that the weekend had been fairly good but not perfect. Bridget meant that it had been miserable.

Colin said the opposite of what he meant; Bridget said much less than she meant. The first is **irony**, the second **understatement**, and both are common in everyday English — in conversation, in feedback, in reviews and in humour. They are also among the hardest things for any listener to interpret in a second language, because the words, taken literally, point the wrong way. This chapter explains how they work, the cues that help listeners recognise them, and the place of humour and teasing in conversation. The mechanism behind both — flouting a maxim and leaving the hearer to infer the meaning — was introduced in [[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words]].

## Verbal irony

::: definition Verbal irony and sarcasm {#def-irony}
**Verbal irony** is the use of an utterance whose literal meaning is clearly at odds with the speaker's evident attitude, in order to convey that attitude: *Lovely weather!* in a storm; *Well, that went well* after a disaster. **Sarcasm** is irony directed at a target — usually a person — in order to criticise or mock: *Thanks for your help* to someone who did nothing. Not all irony is sarcastic: *Lovely weather!* criticises no one.
:::

The traditional definition, going back to classical rhetoric, is that irony means **the opposite** of what it says. Grice (1975) fitted this into his theory: an ironic utterance **flouts the maxim of Quality** — it is obviously false — so the hearer, assuming the speaker is cooperating, looks for a related meaning, and the most obvious is the opposite ([[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words#rule-flouting]]). This explains Colin's *Brilliant*, but not everything. Irony can be literally true: *I love people who signal before they turn*, said as a car swerves without signalling, is presumably true of the speaker, yet ironic. Ironic questions (*Are you always this punctual?* to someone an hour late) and ironic politeness (*Would you mind awfully if I had my pen back, at some point this year?*) are not false at all.

::: theorem Irony as echo {#rule-echo}
Sperber and Wilson (1981), and Wilson and Sperber (1992), proposed that verbal irony is **echoic**: the speaker repeats, or alludes to, a thought, an expectation, a norm or an earlier utterance — someone else's or her own — and at the same time expresses a **dissociative attitude** towards it, typically mockery, scepticism or disappointment.

1. *Lovely weather for a picnic!* echoes the hope (or forecast) that the weather would be good, and shows how wrong it was.
2. *Brilliant. Just what I needed* echoes the kind of thing one says about good news, in a situation that is bad news.
3. *I love people who signal before they turn* echoes the norm that drivers should signal, to mock the driver who has broken it.

A related account, the **pretence theory** of Clark and Gerrig (1984), says that the ironic speaker pretends to be someone who would sincerely say the words — an unaware or foolish person — and invites the hearer to see through the pretence.
:::

::: proof
The echo theory explains the cases that the "opposite" theory cannot. An ironic utterance need not be false; it needs to recall something — an expectation, a norm, a remark — that the situation shows to be wrong or absurd. It also explains why irony is **asymmetrical**: positive words are used ironically to criticise far more often than negative words are used ironically to praise, because norms and hopes are usually positive, and there is more often a positive expectation to echo when things go wrong. Kreuz and Glucksberg (1989) found experimental support for this asymmetry: listeners understood sarcastic positive statements (*Great game!* after a bad one) more easily than sarcastic negative ones (*Terrible game!* after a good one), unless the negative one clearly echoed an earlier prediction (*You said we'd lose*). For the learner, the theory gives a practical question: *what is this echoing?* If an utterance sounds like what one would say in a quite different situation, it may be ironic.
:::

Geoffrey Leech (1983) placed irony within his theory of politeness. His **Irony Principle** says, in effect, that if you must cause offence, you can do it in a way that does not openly conflict with politeness, leaving the hearer to reach the offensive point by implicature. *Thanks for your help* to a colleague who did nothing is polite on the surface and critical underneath, and the speaker can retreat to the surface (*I was only joking!*). This is why irony can be both gentler and more cutting than open criticism.

### Recognising irony

::: theorem Cues to irony {#rule-irony-cues}
Listeners infer irony from several cues together; none is decisive on its own.

1. **Mismatch with the situation**: the words are positive and the situation is bad (or the reverse). This is the main cue.
2. **An echo**: the words repeat an earlier remark, a forecast, a promise or a norm (*"It'll only take five minutes," he said* — after two hours).
3. **Exaggeration**: piled-up or extreme positive words (*Oh, wonderful, fantastic, just perfect*), or extreme politeness in a casual setting.
4. **Ironic formulas** that are usually ironic in context: *Yeah, right*; *Thanks a lot* / *Thanks a bunch*; *That's all I need*; *Big deal*; *Charming!* (BrE); *Tell me something I don't know*; *What a surprise*.
5. **Voice and face**: a flat, slow or exaggerated voice, a "deadpan" face, a pause, a sigh, rolled eyes. Bryant and Fox Tree (2005) found that there is no single "ironic tone of voice" that listeners can recognise without context; voice helps, but it is not reliable on its own.
6. **In writing**: scare quotes (*his "help"*), exclamation marks after something negative, emoji, and in some online communities explicit markers such as */s*.
:::

::: warning Taking irony literally — and using it with strangers
✗ *Brilliant. Just what I needed.* — *Oh good, is it working now?* ✓ *Oh no — is it still jammed?*

✗ (to a new colleague, by email) *Thanks SO much for sending the file so quickly* (when it was late). ✓ *Thanks for the file. Could we aim for Thursday next time? I needed it for a meeting.*

When the situation is bad and the words are good, consider irony before replying to the words. When *you* are the speaker, be careful with irony in writing and with people who do not know you: without voice and shared history, it is easily missed — or taken as sarcasm aimed at the reader.
:::

::: example Recognising irony {#ex-irony}
For each utterance, decide whether it is probably ironic, explain the cues, and say what it means. If it is sarcastic, say who the target is.

(a) Sipho, as his train is cancelled for the second time: *Well, this is turning into a lovely day.*
(b) Hana, after her friend has fixed her bike in two minutes: *You're a genius!*
(c) Mr Ito, to a student who arrives forty minutes late: *Ah, good of you to join us.*
(d) Elif, reading a long list of new rules from her landlord: *Oh, great — I can't even put a picture on the wall now.*
::: solution
(a) **Ironic**, not sarcastic. Cues: the situation is bad and the words are positive; *turning into* echoes the hopeful expectation of a good day; *Well* frames a comment on the situation. Meaning: ‘this is a terrible day’. No person is the target — the irony is directed at the situation.

(b) **Probably not ironic.** The situation is good and the words are positive; *genius* is an exaggeration (a flouting of Quality), but it is hyperbolic praise, not irony. Meaning: ‘thank you, that was impressive’.

(c) **Ironic and sarcastic.** The words are polite and welcoming; the situation (very late arrival) makes them obviously inappropriate. The utterance echoes the norm of thanking people for coming, and the target is the student. Meaning: ‘you are very late’. Teachers' irony of this kind is often gentle, but it is a public reprimand.

(d) **Ironic.** Cues: *Oh, great* is an ironic formula; the second clause states a bad consequence. Meaning: ‘this is a bad rule’. The target is the landlord, who is not present — sarcasm about an absent person, shared with a listener, is a common way of building solidarity.
:::
:::

## Understatement

::: definition Understatement, litotes and hyperbole {#def-understatement}
**Understatement** (meiosis) presents something as less — less bad, less good, less important — than it obviously is: *It's a bit chilly* at −20 °C; *We've had a slight problem with the roof* when it has fallen in. **Litotes** is understatement by negating the opposite: *not bad* (‘good’), *not unhappy*, *not the cleverest idea*, *not exactly cheap* (‘expensive’). Its opposite is **hyperbole**, deliberate exaggeration: *I've told you a million times*; *I'm starving*. Both flout the maxim of Quantity or Quality in a way the hearer is expected to notice.
:::

Understatement is used in English for several purposes: to be modest about oneself (*I did all right in the exam* after a top mark); to criticise gently (*It's not quite what we were looking for*); to show calm or toughness in difficulty (*It's been a bit of a week*); and for humour, where the gap between the words and the situation is the joke (*The party got slightly out of hand* — the police arrived). It is a form of off-record politeness ([[pragmatics/politeness-face#rule-strategies]]): the speaker says less than is meant, and the hearer completes it.

::: theorem Common understatement in English {#rule-understatement}
These patterns are often understood as stronger than their literal meaning, especially in evaluation and feedback:

| pattern | example | usually means |
|---|---|---|
| *not* + positive word | *It's not ideal.* / *Not great.* | ‘it's bad’ |
| *not exactly* / *not the most* | *It's not exactly cheap.* / *Not the most exciting film.* | ‘expensive’ / ‘boring’ |
| downtoner + negative word | *It's a bit of a problem.* / *slightly disappointing* | ‘a real problem’ / ‘disappointing’ |
| *not* + negative word (litotes) | *Not bad at all!* / *That's no small achievement.* | ‘good’ / ‘a big achievement’ |
| hedged doubt | *I'm not entirely convinced.* / *I'm not sure that's quite right.* | ‘I disagree’ / ‘it's wrong’ |
| *could be better* | *Sales could be better.* | ‘sales are poor’ |
| *interesting* (in evaluation) | *That's an interesting choice.* | often ‘strange’ or ‘not good’ ([[collocation-and-register/connotation]]) |

How strong the real meaning is depends on the speaker, the relationship and the situation; when in doubt, ask.
:::

::: proof
Understatement works by scalar implicature in reverse ([[pragmatics/meaning-beyond-words#def-scalar]]). A weaker word normally implicates that the stronger one does not apply — *It's warm* implicates ‘not hot’. But when the situation obviously calls for a strong word, choosing a weak one flouts Quantity so clearly that the hearer infers the opposite: the speaker is holding back, and the holding back is itself a message. *Not bad* works because *bad* is negated rather than *good* asserted, and the positive meaning is reached by inference. The effect depends on both speakers recognising the gap, which is why understatement can be missed by listeners who take the words at face value, as Mariam did.
:::

One word deserves particular attention. Swan (*Practical English Usage*, 2016) notes that ***quite*** before a gradable adjective usually means ‘fairly, rather’ in British English (*The film was quite good* — ‘reasonably good, not very’), but is often closer to ‘very’ in American English. With non-gradable or extreme adjectives it means ‘completely’ in both (*quite right*, *quite impossible*, *quite amazing*). A British listener told *Your essay is quite good* may hear moderate praise; an American listener may hear stronger praise. The stress and intonation also matter: in British English *quite **good*** with the stress on *good* sounds more positive than ***quite** good* with the stress on *quite*.

::: example Decoding feedback {#ex-feedback}
A supervisor writes these comments on Mariam's report. Say what each probably means, and how firm it is.

(a) *The introduction is a bit long.* (b) *I'm not entirely convinced by the second argument.* (c) *The conclusion isn't quite there yet.* (d) *Not bad at all — a real improvement.* (e) *It's an interesting approach to the data.*
::: solution
(a) ‘The introduction is too long — shorten it.’ Fairly firm; *a bit* softens but does not cancel the criticism.

(b) ‘I think the second argument is weak, or wrong.’ Firm: hedged doubt from a supervisor usually signals that the argument needs serious work.

(c) ‘The conclusion needs revising’ — *not quite there yet* is an encouraging way of saying it does not work yet. Firm.

(d) ‘This is good, and much better than before.’ Litotes for praise; *a real improvement* confirms the positive reading.

(e) Ambiguous. It may be genuine interest; it may mean ‘unusual, and I'm not sure it's right’. The next sentence usually settles it; if not, ask: *Thank you — do you think the approach works, or should I reconsider it?*

In written feedback from teachers and managers, softened criticism usually still requires action ([[modality/modality-politeness#rule-suggestions]]).
:::
:::

::: example Using understatement {#ex-use-understatement}
Rewrite each blunt remark with mild understatement, so that the meaning is still clear. Say whom each version suits.

(a) *Your plan is too expensive.* (to a colleague) (b) *The hotel was awful.* (in a story to friends) (c) *I failed my driving test badly.* (to a neighbour who asks) (d) *This draft has serious problems.* (to a student)
::: solution
(a) *It might be a bit expensive for this year's budget.* — a downtoner (*a bit*) and modality; suits a colleague whose work you are commenting on. Still clear: the cost is a problem.

(b) *Let's just say it won't be getting five stars from me.* — understatement with humour; suits friends, who will enjoy the story and hear ‘awful’.

(c) *Not my best day — I'll try again next month!* — modest understatement that closes the topic without detail; suits a neighbour.

(d) *There are a few things to work on before this is ready — let's go through them.* — understatement (*a few things*) followed by a concrete plan; the plan makes the seriousness clear without discouraging. With a student who tends to miss softened criticism, add one plain sentence: *The argument in section 2 needs to be rewritten.*

Understatement is a choice, not an obligation: when the stakes are high or the hearer may miss it, plain words are kinder.
:::
:::

## Humour and teasing in conversation

Irony and understatement often serve humour, and humour does serious work in conversation. Janet Holmes (2000), analysing recorded talk in New Zealand workplaces, found humour used to build solidarity (shared jokes about the workload), to soften criticism and requests (*If you could get that to me before I retire…*), and to manage power — including what she called **repressive** humour, in which a superior makes a critical point in a joking frame that is hard to object to. Teasing among friends and colleagues — mock insults, ironic complaints — is a common sign of closeness. Leech (1983) described it with a **Banter Principle**: in order to show solidarity, say something that is obviously untrue and obviously impolite, so that the hearer understands it as friendly. Culpeper (1996) called such talk **mock impoliteness**.

::: theorem When humour and teasing are safe {#rule-humour}
In English-speaking settings, humour and teasing tend to be safer when:

1. **The relationship is established**: teasing assumes closeness; with strangers and new colleagues, self-mocking humour (*I'm hopeless with printers*) is safer than humour about the other person.
2. **The target is not vulnerable**: joke about the situation, the weather, the printer, or yourself — not about someone's appearance, accent, background or a known sensitivity.
3. **The hearer can respond in kind**: banter between equals is more comfortable than teasing from a superior, which the junior person cannot easily return.
4. **The channel carries the tone**: face to face, a smile marks the joke; in an email to someone you do not know, irony and teasing are easily misread.

When a joke falls flat, a quick repair restores the relationship: *Sorry — I was only joking!* / *That came out wrong — I meant…*
:::

::: example Banter or offence? {#ex-banter}
Decide whether each remark is likely to be heard as friendly banter or as an offence, and explain using [[#rule-humour]].

(a) Two old friends, after one beats the other at tennis: *You only won because I let you.* (b) A manager to a new employee in front of the team: *Ah, the expert arrives — perhaps you'll explain the spreadsheet to us all.* (c) Amara, after spilling coffee, to her colleague: *This is why they don't let me near the important documents.* (d) A colleague to Mariam: *Did you learn English from watching cartoons?*
::: solution
(a) **Banter.** An established relationship, a playful obvious untruth, between equals who can reply in kind (*Of course you did*). Leech's Banter Principle.

(b) Probably **offensive**, though meant as a joke. The irony (*the expert*) targets a new, junior person in public, who cannot easily answer back — close to Holmes's repressive humour.

(c) **Safe**: self-mocking humour about a small accident invites a laugh and makes the colleague's response easy (*Ha — don't worry, I'll get a cloth*).

(d) **Offensive**: the target is the hearer's English, which is a vulnerable point, and the relationship is new. Even if the speaker meant it as a compliment on her American accent, it implies a judgement of how she learned.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Irony exists in Chinese too.** 反话 (saying the opposite) is common: 你可真行啊！, 好极了！ after a mistake, 谢谢你啊 said coldly. The mechanism is the same; what differs is how often irony is used, by whom and where, and individuals vary greatly in both languages.
- **Online signals differ.** In Chinese online chat, 呵呵 and some smiley emoji can read as cold or sarcastic, while in English chat a smiley is usually friendly. Signals do not translate reliably; when writing to someone whose conventions you do not know, state the meaning in words.
- **Understatement and modesty.** Chinese modesty often uses self-deprecation (我做得不好); English modesty often uses understatement (*It went OK*, *I did all right*). Both say less than the speaker might, but understatement in English is also used for criticism of *others* (*It's not ideal*) — a use that may be missed if you expect criticism to be stated.
- **幽默.** The word 幽默 was coined as a transliteration of English *humour* by the writer Lin Yutang in 1924. Chinese has long traditions of comic dialogue (相声) and wordplay; puns based on homophones, which are frequent in Chinese, rarely survive translation, and English puns rarely survive the other way.
- **Asking is not a failure.** If you are not sure whether someone is joking, a smile and *Are you being serious?* or *Is that a joke?* is a normal question in English and usually received well.
:::

::: history
The term *irony* comes from Greek *eirōneia*, the feigned ignorance of the "dissembler", and the Roman rhetorician Quintilian (first century CE) defined it as a figure in which the opposite of what is said is to be understood. That definition held for nearly two thousand years and survives in many dictionaries. Grice (1975) explained it as a flouting of Quality. Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson's "Irony and the use–mention distinction" (1981) proposed the echoic account, refined in Wilson and Sperber's "On verbal irony" (*Lingua*, 1992); Herbert Clark and Richard Gerrig's "On the pretense theory of irony" (1984) offered the main alternative. Psycholinguists then tested how irony is understood: Kreuz and Glucksberg (1989) on the asymmetry of sarcasm, and Bryant and Fox Tree (2005) on the ironic tone of voice. Janet Holmes and her colleagues in the Language in the Workplace Project at Victoria University of Wellington made humour at work a major topic of research from the late 1990s.
:::

## Where this leads

Understatement in critical feedback connects with the modal softeners of [[modality/modality-politeness]] and with hedging in academic writing ([[modality/hedging-boosting]]). Irony in literature, where whole texts can be ironic, is studied in [[reading-literature/figurative-language]], and irony and other figures in persuasive writing in [[style-and-rhetoric/figures-of-speech]]. Reading irony in texts is part of [[reading-skills/inference]], and idioms used for evaluation (*It's not my cup of tea*) are in [[collocation-and-register/idioms]]. Tone in emails, where irony is easily misread, is the subject of the last chapter, [[pragmatics/writing-to-people]].

::: summary
- **Verbal irony** conveys an attitude at odds with the literal meaning; **sarcasm** is irony aimed at a target ([[#def-irony]]).
- Grice explained irony as flouting Quality; Sperber and Wilson's **echoic** account explains ironic questions and true ironic statements: irony echoes a thought or norm with a dissociative attitude ([[#rule-echo]]).
- Irony is **asymmetrical**: positive words for criticism are far more common than the reverse (Kreuz & Glucksberg 1989).
- Recognise irony from a **mismatch** with the situation, echoes, exaggeration, formulas, voice and face — no single cue is enough ([[#rule-irony-cues]]).
- **Understatement** and **litotes** say less than is meant (*not ideal*, *a bit of a problem*, *not bad*); in feedback they usually require action ([[#def-understatement]], [[#rule-understatement]]).
- *Quite* + gradable adjective is often ‘fairly’ in BrE and nearer ‘very’ in AmE (Swan 2016).
- **Humour and teasing** build solidarity but need an established relationship and a non-vulnerable target; be careful with irony in writing ([[#rule-humour]]).
:::

Each sentence was written in an email to a colleague the writer does not know well. Explain the risk, and rewrite it so that the meaning is clear without irony or sarcasm.

::: solution
(a) The irony may not be noticed — or may be noticed and resented. ✓ *Thanks for the instructions. Could you tell me a bit more about the format you need? I want to make sure I get it right.*

(b) Sarcasm in writing to someone you do not know is very likely to offend. ✓ *I noticed the report hasn't come in yet — is everything all right? We need it by Friday for the client meeting.*

(c) The exaggerated patience is passive-aggressive, and the reader may also take *no rush* literally. ✓ *Could you send it by Friday 14th? Let me know if that's difficult.*

The general point: in writing, especially to new contacts, state the problem and the request plainly and politely ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).

::: exercise Analyse a conversation {level=3}
Identify every instance of irony, understatement, hyperbole and humour in this conversation, and explain what each speaker means.

**Ngozi:** *How was the job interview?*
**Kwabena:** *Oh, fantastic. I spilt water on the CEO.*
**Ngozi:** *You didn't!*
**Kwabena:** *Just a little. Mostly on his notes. He was very understanding — he only sighed about forty times.*
**Ngozi:** *So… not your finest hour.*
**Kwabena:** *Not exactly. But the second half went OK, actually. They want me back next week.*
**Ngozi:** *What? That's brilliant! See — you worry too much.*
::: hint
Look at the situation behind each remark. Not every positive word is ironic.
:::
::: solution
- *Oh, fantastic* — **irony**: an exaggerated positive word for a bad experience, at once explained by the next sentence.
- *Just a little* — **understatement**, humorous; *Mostly on his notes* makes it worse, which is the joke.
- *He was very understanding* — **irony**, echoing the polite thing one would hope to say; *he only sighed about forty times* combines **hyperbole** (*forty times*) with ironic *only*.
- *not your finest hour* — **understatement** (litotes), gently humorous: ‘it went badly’.
- *Not exactly* — **understatement**: agreement that it went badly.
