Lars and Shu Fen work in the same international team, writing a report together over a shared document and a chat channel. On Tuesday, Lars leaves a comment on Shu Fen's section:

*This paragraph doesn't work. The argument is in the wrong order. Move the conclusion to the start.*

Shu Fen reads it three times and feels that she has been told off in public. On Wednesday she sends Lars a long message: she explains that the data from the Lisbon office arrived late, that two figures had to be recalculated, that her manager has asked for a new version by Friday, and — in the last sentence — that it would be very helpful if Lars could check the figures in his section again. Lars replies *Thanks for the update!* and does nothing about the figures. He had read the message as information and missed the request at the end.

By Thursday, each has formed a view of the other. Shu Fen thinks Lars is rude. Lars thinks Shu Fen is vague. Neither view is about language, and both may be wrong. Lars was being efficient in the way he gives feedback to everyone, including his manager; Shu Fen was being considerate, giving the background before asking. What they did differently was choose different levels of **directness** — and each judged the other's choice as a fact about character.

This chapter asks what research actually says about differences in directness between cultures, why the popular generalisations ("Asians are indirect", "Germans are blunt", "the British never say what they mean") are at best half-true, and how to interpret other speakers without either misunderstanding them or turning them into stereotypes. It builds on the request and refusal strategies of [[pragmatics/requests-refusals]] and the politeness theories of [[pragmatics/politeness-face]].

## Pragmatic failure

::: definition Pragmatic failure {#def-pragmatic-failure}
Jenny Thomas (1983, "Cross-cultural pragmatic failure", *Applied Linguistics*) defined **pragmatic failure** as the inability to understand what is meant by what is said. She distinguished two kinds:

1. **Pragmalinguistic failure** — using the wrong linguistic form for an act: the form carries a different force or degree of politeness in the target language from the one intended. *Please send me the file*, meant as a polite request, may be heard as an instruction ([[modality/modality-politeness]]).
2. **Sociopragmatic failure** — misjudging the social situation: what may be said, to whom, about what, and how much imposition is involved. Asking a new colleague about their salary, or giving direct critical feedback to a senior person in a setting that does not expect it, are sociopragmatic.
:::

Thomas made two observations that are still central. First, pragmatic failure is often **not recognised as a language problem**: listeners forgive grammar mistakes because they hear them as mistakes, but they tend to interpret a pragmatic mismatch as rudeness, coldness or evasiveness. Second, pragmalinguistic failure is easier to teach — it is a matter of forms — while sociopragmatic failure touches values and judgement, and teachers should be careful not to impose their own. The aim is not that learners adopt the values of English speakers, but that they know what their choices will signal.

::: example Which kind of failure? {#ex-failure-type}
Classify each misunderstanding as pragmalinguistic or sociopragmatic, and explain.

(a) Ayşe answers her lecturer's *Would you mind closing the door?* with *Yes, of course* and closes it; the lecturer is briefly confused. (b) Hamid, a new employee, tells his manager in a team meeting that her plan has "a serious mistake". (c) Wei Jun writes to a professor he has never met: *I want to join your research group. Please reply.* (d) Olga is asked by a new neighbour how much her flat cost, and feels uncomfortable.
::: solution
(a) **Pragmalinguistic**, and minor: *Yes* literally answers *Would you mind?* with ‘I do mind’. Ayşe chose the form used to agree to most requests. The action made the meaning clear. (Many English speakers say *Yes, of course* here too.)

(b) Mainly **sociopragmatic**: the issue is whether open criticism of a superior's plan, in front of the team, is appropriate in that workplace. Some teams welcome it; many would expect a private word or heavy softening (*I wonder if there might be a problem with…*). The wording *a serious mistake* adds a pragmalinguistic element: even where criticism is welcome, a hedge would be usual.

(c) **Pragmalinguistic**: the want statement and *please* + imperative are too direct in form for a large request to a stranger with power; *I would be very interested in joining your group, and would be grateful to know whether you might have a place* conveys the same request ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).

(d) **Sociopragmatic** (on the neighbour's side, from Olga's point of view): the question concerns what may be asked of whom. Whether it is a "failure" depends on the norms the neighbour and Olga share; in some communities it is a normal question.
:::
:::

## What the research says

Popular books on business across cultures often rank countries from "direct" to "indirect". Research on actual language use gives a more complicated picture.

**Requests.** The Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project found differences between language groups in how often speakers chose direct strategies: in Blum-Kulka and House's (1989) comparison, speakers of Hebrew and of Argentinian Spanish, for example, chose direct requests more often than speakers of Australian English. But two other findings mattered at least as much. The **situation** — who was asking whom, for what — had a strong effect in every group, and the situations that called for the most and least directness were broadly similar across groups. And in every group **conventional indirectness** (*Could you…?* and its equivalents) was a major strategy. Directness, in other words, is not a single national setting but a choice that every community adjusts to the situation.

**Directness is not rudeness.** Blum-Kulka (1987) showed that speakers did not rate the most indirect requests as the most polite ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals#rule-ccsarp-levels]]). Anna Wierzbicka (1985; *Cross-Cultural Pragmatics*, 1991) argued that the feeling that imperatives are rude is itself a feature of English, tied to a cultural value of personal autonomy, and that in Polish, for example, an imperative softened with a diminutive or a warm address term can be both direct and affectionate. Juliane House and Gabriele Kasper (1981) found that German speakers in their study used more direct complaints and requests, with fewer softeners, than British English speakers — and House later stressed that this reflected different conventions, not lower politeness: what counts as polite is measured against each community's expectations.

**Chinese.** Research on Chinese requests describes a tendency to place reasons and background *before* the request — the "because… therefore" order Andy Kirkpatrick (1991) found in Mandarin letters of request, and Zhang Yanyin (1995) in spoken requests — and English requests that more often state the request first and justify it after. But Kirkpatrick and Xu Zhichang, in *Chinese Rhetoric and Writing* (2012), warned against turning this into the claim that "Chinese is indirect": Chinese rhetoric has long used both orders, much modern Chinese writing puts the main point first, and the order depends on genre and relationship. Lee-Wong Song Mei (2000) found that Mandarin speakers often used direct request forms, especially with people close to them, where directness could express sincerity (诚意) and closeness rather than a lack of politeness. Chinese speakers can be very direct with intimates and very indirect with outsiders — as can English speakers.

::: theorem Directness has several dimensions {#rule-dimensions}
A message can be direct in one way and indirect in another. At least four dimensions should be kept apart:

1. **Strategy** — how the act itself is performed: imperative, conventionally indirect question, or hint ([[pragmatics/requests-refusals#rule-ccsarp-levels]]).
2. **Order** — whether the main point (the request, the refusal, the criticism) comes first or after the reasons and background.
3. **Explicitness** — whether the point is stated or left to inference: *The figures are wrong* or *Did you get a chance to double-check the figures?*
4. **Modification** — how much softening surrounds the act: *Move the conclusion* or *I wonder if it might work better with the conclusion first?*

Lars was direct on all four. Shu Fen was indirect in order and modification, but explicit in strategy: her final sentence was a clear conventional request. The problem was that, in a long message, Lars stopped reading for the point before he reached it.
:::

::: proof
Separating the dimensions explains why the same speaker can be called "direct" by one listener and "indirect" by another, and why the national labels of popular books break down. A speaker who puts reasons first may still make a perfectly explicit request; a speaker who uses *Could you…?* is conventionally indirect in strategy but completely explicit in content; and an imperative with a warm address term may be heavily modified in tone though bald in strategy. It also suggests practical repairs. Shu Fen did not need to change her politeness, only the order: one sentence at the start (*Could you check the figures in your section again? Some of the data has changed — details below*). Lars did not need to become indirect, only to add modification: *I think this paragraph would be stronger with the conclusion first — what do you think?*
:::

::: example Mapping the dimensions {#ex-dimensions}
For each message, describe its directness on the four dimensions of [[#rule-dimensions]].

(a) *Hi Ola — could you send me the budget by Friday? We need it for the board meeting. Thanks!* (b) *As you know, the board meets on Monday, and the finance team have asked for all budgets by the end of the week, since they need time to check them. I have also been asked to prepare a summary. So it would be wonderful if your budget could reach me by Friday.* (c) *Budget by Friday, please.* (d) *I notice the budget's not in yet…*
::: solution
(a) Strategy: conventionally indirect (*could you*). Order: request first, reason after. Explicitness: fully explicit. Modification: light (a greeting, *Thanks!*).

(b) Strategy: conventionally indirect in an impersonal, hypothetical form (*it would be wonderful if… could*). Order: reasons first, request last. Explicitness: explicit — the request is stated. Modification: heavy (*As you know*, *wonderful*, the impersonal passive).

(c) Strategy: direct (an elliptical imperative with *please*). Order: request only. Explicitness: explicit. Modification: minimal. Between close colleagues under time pressure, acceptable; to a stranger, abrupt.

(d) Strategy: a hint (non-conventionally indirect). Order: — . Explicitness: implicit; the request is inferred. Modification: the hint itself is a softening, though it can sound reproachful.

(a) and (b) are equally polite in different ways. For a busy reader, (a) is easier to act on; for a reader who values context, (b) may feel more considerate.
:::
:::

## High context and low context: a theory and its critique

Much popular writing on cultural directness goes back to the anthropologist Edward T. Hall, whose *Beyond Culture* (1976) distinguished **high-context** communication, in which much of the meaning is carried by the shared situation and relationship and little is put into words, from **low-context** communication, in which meaning is made explicit in the message. Hall placed cultures on a scale, with East Asian cultures near the high-context end and Northern European and North American ones near the low-context end. A related tradition, beginning with Geert Hofstede's *Culture's Consequences* (1980), scored countries on dimensions such as individualism and collectivism, using surveys of employees of one multinational company.

These ideas are memorable and have been very influential in business training. Their limitations are well documented. Peter Cardon (2008), reviewing a large body of publications on intercultural business and technical communication, found that Hall's model was widely cited but had rarely been tested, that Hall had not explained clearly how cultures were placed on his scale, and that the empirical studies that did exist gave little support to the rankings. Brendan McSweeney (2002) criticised Hofstede's approach for assuming that a national culture could be read from the answers of employees in one company and then applied to individuals. Both models describe nations, not people, and even at the level of nations the evidence is weaker than their popularity suggests.

## Tendencies, not types

What, then, can be said? Group differences in pragmatic behaviour do exist — the research above finds them — but they are **tendencies**: differences in how *often* members of one group, on average, make a choice in a given situation. The members of every group vary widely, and the ranges overlap.

caption: An **illustrative model**, not data from any study. Each curve shows how a quality — say, how often people choose a direct request in some situation — might be spread among the members of a group, from less direct (left) to more direct (right). The slider *a* moves the two averages apart. Even with a clear difference between the averages, most of the two curves overlap: many members of the "less direct" group are more direct than many members of the "more direct" group. Knowing someone's group tells you very little about what *this* person will do — and real distributions also shift with situation, age, region, profession and relationship.
:::

::: theorem Interpreting other speakers without stereotypes {#rule-interpret}
When another speaker's directness surprises you:

1. **Separate the act from the person.** Ask first what the speaker *did* (made a request, gave feedback) and only then how it was done.
2. **Consider several explanations**: a different convention, a different situation (time pressure, a professional role), a language-learning issue, or the individual's own style — before concluding "rude" or "evasive".
3. **Treat group findings as background, not prediction.** A tendency in a group, even a well-documented one, does not tell you what an individual intends (the overlap in the figure).
4. **Ask, or say what you need.** Talking about the communication itself — *Just to check, do you need this from me?*, *Would you like detailed feedback or just the main points?* — is often the quickest repair.
5. **Describe what people do, not what they are.** *He gave the feedback without softening it* is a description; *He's typical of —* is a stereotype.
:::

::: warning Turning tendencies into stereotypes
✗ *Chinese people never say no.* ✓ *Some research describes a tendency, in some situations, for Chinese speakers to refuse indirectly — but many say no clearly, and so do many people everywhere.*

✗ *English people always mean the opposite of what they say.* ✓ *English speakers sometimes use understatement and polite indirectness, especially in criticism ([[pragmatics/irony-understatement]]).*

✗ *She's from a high-context culture, so she won't tell me what she thinks.* ✓ *I'm not sure what she thinks — I'll ask her.*

A tendency describes an average across many people and situations. Applied to one person, it becomes a prediction that is often wrong and can feel insulting.
:::

::: example Rewriting generalisations {#ex-generalisations}
Rewrite each statement so that it describes a tendency accurately and attributes it where possible.

(a) *Americans are very direct.* (b) *Asians don't like to give their opinions in class.* (c) *British people are polite but never sincere.*
::: solution
(a) In some situations, such as service encounters or requests at work, many American English speakers may state a request or opinion earlier and more explicitly than speakers from some other backgrounds — though conventional indirectness (*Could you…?*) is also central in American English, and individuals vary greatly.

(b) Some students, from many backgrounds, are reluctant to speak in seminars; reasons can include the classroom conventions they grew up with, unfamiliarity with the seminar format, confidence in English, and personality. It is not a fixed feature of any group ([[presentations-and-discussion/seminars-discussion]]).

(c) British English has many conventional politeness forms and uses understatement, which can be hard to interpret for listeners who expect more explicit evaluation. Conventional politeness is not insincerity: it is a shared code, and British speakers vary in how much they use it.

Each version replaces *always/never* with *some* or *many*, names the situation, gives possible reasons, and notes individual variation.
:::
:::

Thomas (1983) would call which of these a **sociopragmatic** failure?

- [x] Asking a new acquaintance how much they paid for their house

::: solution

## Working across differences

In practice, people who work across cultures develop habits that make directness less of a problem. They **accommodate**: speakers adjust their style towards that of the people they are talking to, a process described by communication accommodation theory (Giles, Coupland and Coupland 1991). They **talk about talk**: an international team can agree how it gives feedback, or a new colleague can ask. They **check** before concluding: *When you said it was "interesting", did you mean you liked it?* And they **front-load** written messages for busy readers while keeping courteous framing. Research on English as a lingua franca has found that speakers in international settings often negotiate meaning cooperatively and adapt to each other ([[world-englishes/lingua-franca]]): shared norms are made, not inherited.

::: example Agreeing how to give feedback {#ex-feedback-norms}
After the incident at the start of the chapter, Lars and Shu Fen talk. Write their conversation (6–8 turns) in which they explain their intentions and agree how to work together, using the strategies of [[#rule-interpret]].
::: solution
A model:

**Shu Fen:** *Lars, can I ask you something about the comments on my section? I wasn't sure how to take them.*
**Lars:** *Of course. Did they come across badly? I just meant that the order could be better.*
**Shu Fen:** *I understood the point, but it felt quite strong — like I'd done it all wrong.*
**Lars:** *Ah, sorry — that's just how I write comments, to everyone. I'll soften them a bit. And can I say something too? I missed the request in your message on Wednesday — I only saw it at the end.*
**Shu Fen:** *Oh! I wanted to explain the situation first. But I see — I'll put the question at the top next time.*
**Lars:** *That'd be great. And if a comment seems harsh, just ask me.*
**Shu Fen:** *Deal.*

Each explains intention rather than defending character (*that's just how I write comments*; *I wanted to explain the situation first*); each changes one dimension — Lars adds modification, Shu Fen changes the order — and they agree to ask in future. Neither concludes anything about the other's culture.
:::
:::

::: note English and Chinese
- **Reasons first.** If you tend to give background before a request (因为…所以…), keep the consideration and move the point: in English emails and messages, one sentence stating the request at the start, with the background below, helps busy readers ([[pragmatics/writing-to-people]]).
- **Directness with people close to you.** Both Chinese and English speakers are often direct with family and close friends. Directness between intimates in Chinese (帮我拿一下) is no more rude than *Grab me a plate, would you?* in English.
- **Reading English directness.** Feedback such as *This doesn't work* from an English-speaking colleague may be a habit, a time-saving convention in that workplace, or an individual style; it is not necessarily a judgement of you. Ask if unsure.
- **Reading English indirectness.** Equally, softened feedback (*You might want to look at…*) may be firmer than it sounds ([[modality/modality-politeness]]).
- **Avoid "we Chinese…".** Statements such as 我们中国人都… or *In China we always…*, used to explain your choices, can turn an individual difference into a cultural stereotype in your listener's mind. *I'm used to…* or *Where I worked before, we usually…* describes the same thing more accurately.
:::

::: history
Cross-cultural pragmatics took shape around 1980. Juliane House and Gabriele Kasper's comparison of politeness markers in English and German appeared in Coulmas's *Conversational Routine* (1981), and Jenny Thomas's "Cross-cultural pragmatic failure" in *Applied Linguistics* (1983). Shoshana Blum-Kulka and Elite Olshtain set out the plan of the Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project in 1984, and its results appeared in 1989. Anna Wierzbicka's critique of Anglo-centric pragmatics began with her 1985 paper on Polish and English and continued in *Cross-Cultural Pragmatics* (1991; 2nd edition 2003). Edward T. Hall's *Beyond Culture* (1976) and Geert Hofstede's *Culture's Consequences* (1980) came from anthropology and management studies respectively and were much used in business training; critical reviews such as McSweeney (2002) and Cardon (2008) questioned their evidence. Since the 2000s, research has increasingly studied *intercultural* interaction itself — what happens when people from different backgrounds talk to each other — rather than only comparing groups, for example in Istvan Kecskes's *Intercultural Pragmatics* (2014).
:::

::: summary
- **Pragmatic failure** is a failure to convey or understand the intended meaning; it is **pragmalinguistic** (wrong form) or **sociopragmatic** (wrong social judgement), and is often heard as a fact about character ([[#def-pragmatic-failure]]).
- Research finds group differences in directness, but the **situation** matters in every group, and **directness is not rudeness** (Blum-Kulka 1987; Wierzbicka 1985).
- Chinese requests tend to put reasons first, but this is a tendency that depends on genre and relationship, not a rule (Kirkpatrick 1991; Kirkpatrick & Xu 2012).
- Directness has several **dimensions**: strategy, order, explicitness and modification ([[#rule-dimensions]]).
- Hall's high/low-context model and Hofstede's dimensions are popular but have weak empirical support (Cardon 2008; McSweeney 2002).
- Group differences are **overlapping tendencies**; interpret individuals by asking, checking and describing what they do ([[#rule-interpret]]).
:::

Rewrite Lars's comment from the start of the chapter so that it stays clear but adds modification. Then write a version that is softened too much to be useful, and explain the difference.

::: exercise An intercultural incident {level=3}
Describe a real or invented misunderstanding about directness between two people from different backgrounds (150–250 words). Then analyse it: (a) which dimensions of [[#rule-dimensions]] were involved, (b) whether the failure was pragmalinguistic, sociopragmatic or both, (c) what each person probably concluded about the other, and (d) how it could have been repaired.
::: solution
Answers will vary. A strong answer tells the incident concretely (who, where, exact words); identifies specific dimensions (for example, order and explicitness); classifies the failure with a reason; shows that each person's conclusion was about character (*rude*, *vague*, *cold*) when the cause was a convention or a situation; and proposes a repair that changes a dimension rather than a value — moving the point, adding a softener, or simply asking. It describes the people as individuals and does not present the incident as typical of their nationalities.
:::
