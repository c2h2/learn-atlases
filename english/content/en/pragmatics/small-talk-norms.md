On her first morning at a software company in Toronto, Chen Rui shares the lift with a colleague she met the day before.

**Dev:** *Morning! How's it going?*

**Chen Rui:** *Good morning. Actually, not very well — I slept badly, because my new apartment is very noisy, and I think I have a cold.*

**Dev:** *Oh — sorry to hear that.*

*(silence until the fourth floor)*

At lunch, trying to be friendly, Chen Rui asks another colleague, Karin, a few questions.

**Chen Rui:** *How old are you? Are you married?*

**Karin:** *Ha — old enough! And no, not married.*

**Chen Rui:** *Why not? You should hurry.*

Karin laughed, but changed the subject quickly, and did not sit with Chen Rui the next day.

None of Chen Rui's sentences was a language mistake, and all of them came from a wish to be open and warm. Her answer to *How's it going?* was honest; her questions showed interest; her advice showed care, as a relative's might. But in that office they broke expectations about what small talk is for and what it may touch. This chapter explains those expectations. The basic phrases — starting a conversation, asking follow-up questions, ending politely — are taught in [[everyday-english/small-talk]], and the mechanics of taking turns and back-channelling in [[listening-and-conversation/turn-taking]]; here we look at the norms behind them, why some topics are risky, how to deflect a question you do not want to answer, and which of your own questions may seem too personal.

## What small talk does

::: definition Phatic communion {#def-phatic}
**Phatic communion** is talk whose main purpose is to create or maintain a social bond rather than to exchange information — Malinowski's term (1923) for greetings, remarks about the weather and similar exchanges ([[everyday-english/small-talk]]). Its main function is **relational**: it shows that the speakers are on friendly terms, fills silences that might otherwise feel hostile, and opens and closes encounters smoothly.
:::

John Laver (1975) refined the idea by looking at how phatic remarks are chosen. He noticed that they fall into three kinds, which he called **tokens**:

1. **neutral tokens**, about the shared situation: *Lovely day, isn't it?* *Busy in here today.* *This lift is so slow.*
2. **self-oriented tokens**, about the speaker: *I'm exhausted — I've been on my feet all day.* *I can't wait for the weekend.*
3. **other-oriented tokens**, about the hearer: *How's it going?* *Been anywhere nice on holiday?* *You look busy.*

Laver argued that the choice depends on the relationship. Neutral tokens are safe with anyone. Other-oriented tokens suggest a degree of closeness, and in hierarchical settings may come more naturally from the higher-status person to the lower than the reverse. Self-oriented tokens open the door for the other person to share in return. Modern research, much of it in workplaces, has shown that small talk is not trivial at all. Janet Holmes (2000), recording talk in New Zealand government offices, found that small talk clustered at the boundaries of work — before meetings, at the start of the day, in the kitchen — where it eased people into and out of tasks and did important relational work, and that managers used it both to build collegiality and, at times, to signal when it was time to stop chatting and start working.

::: theorem What small talk expects {#rule-small-talk}
In many English-speaking settings — especially in workplaces, among neighbours and with new acquaintances — small talk tends to follow these expectations:

1. **Positive or neutral tone.** Phatic questions (*How are you?*, *How's it going?*) expect a short, positive or neutral answer and a return question: *Good, thanks — you?* *Not bad — busy week!* Detailed problems are kept for people who ask a second time or know you well.
2. **Easy agreement.** Topics are chosen so that both people can agree or share something: the weather, the journey, the weekend, food, sport, a shared task.
3. **Reciprocity.** Each person gives roughly as much as they receive. A question is followed by a question in return; a small piece of personal information by a similar one.
4. **The other person sets the limit.** People reveal personal matters at their own pace; the listener follows, rather than leads, into private topics.
5. **Short, and easy to leave.** Small talk can end at any moment without offence ([[everyday-english/small-talk#rule-ending]]).
:::

::: proof
Each expectation protects face. A positive answer to *How are you?* keeps the exchange light, so that neither person must offer sympathy or help — an imposition on someone who only meant to greet. Easy agreement serves positive face: in Brown and Levinson's list of positive-politeness strategies, "seek agreement" includes choosing safe topics ([[pragmatics/politeness-face#rule-strategies]]). Reciprocity keeps the relationship balanced, as Leech's maxims and Spencer-Oatey's **equity rights** both suggest. Letting the other person set the limit respects their **association rights** — their sense of how much involvement is appropriate ([[pragmatics/politeness-face#def-rapport]]). None of these is a law: among friends, honesty about a bad day is welcome, and some people and communities treat small talk with strangers as warmer and more personal than others do.
:::

::: example Tokens and their effect {#ex-tokens}
Classify each opening with Laver's tokens, and say in which situations it would fit best.

(a) *Wow, it's freezing out there!* (b) *How was your weekend?* (c) *I've just had the longest meeting of my life.* (d) *That's a nice bike — is it new?* (e) *Have you tried the new coffee machine yet?*
::: solution
(a) **Neutral** (the shared weather). Safe with anyone — a stranger at a bus stop, a manager, a new neighbour.

(b) **Other-oriented**. Natural between colleagues who see each other regularly, especially on a Monday; from a stranger it would be odd.

(c) **Self-oriented**. Invites sympathy and a return story; fits colleagues of similar status in a kitchen or corridor. To a senior person you barely know it may sound like a complaint about the organisation.

(d) **Other-oriented**, with a compliment. Friendly with an acquaintance; fine with a stranger at a bike rack, where the shared situation makes it natural.

(e) **Neutral** (a shared feature of the workplace), with a question. A very safe opener with any colleague.
:::
:::

## Why some questions feel too personal

The everyday course lists the topics often avoided with strangers and new acquaintances in English-speaking settings: salary and prices, age, weight and appearance, religion, politics, marriage and children ([[everyday-english/small-talk#rule-topics]]). It is more useful to understand *why* these topics are risky than to memorise the list, because the reasons tell you when the risk disappears.

::: theorem Four reasons a question may feel intrusive {#rule-intrusive}
A question in small talk is more likely to feel intrusive when it:

1. **measures or ranks** the person — income, rent, the price of their home, age, weight, exam marks — because the answer can be compared with others and judged;
2. **touches private plans or circumstances** — marriage, relationships, children, health, family problems — which the person may have reasons not to share, including painful ones the questioner cannot see;
3. **invites disagreement on values** — politics, religion, controversial news — which threatens the easy agreement that small talk depends on;
4. **questions the person's belonging or identity** — *Where are you really from?*, *Why don't you have an accent?* — which can suggest that the person is not accepted as a member of the community.

The same question is much less risky when the other person has raised the topic, when the relationship is close, or when the setting makes it relevant (a doctor asking about health, a colleague asking about pay in a discussion of fairness at work).
:::

A good way to keep interest without intrusion is to ask **open** questions that let the other person decide how much to reveal. *Do you have kids?* is a closed question about family; *What did you get up to at the weekend?* lets a parent talk about their children and a single person talk about hiking, with no one put on the spot.

::: example From closed to open {#ex-open}
Rewrite each question so that it shows the same interest but lets the hearer decide how much to share. Say which reason in [[#rule-intrusive]] made the original risky.

(a) *How much do you earn?* (b) *Are you married?* (c) *How old are you?* (d) *Which party did you vote for?* (e) *Where are your parents from originally?*
::: solution
(a) Reason 1 (measures). ✓ *How do you like the job?* / *Is it a good place to work?*

(b) Reason 2 (private circumstances). ✓ *What do you like doing outside work?* / *Who do you live with — do you share a flat?* (still personal; use with care) / or simply wait for the topic to come up.

(c) Reason 1 (measures, and in many workplaces age can be connected with discrimination). ✓ *How long have you been working here?* / *Is this your first job after university?* — if you need a rough idea of someone's stage of life, these give it.

(d) Reason 3 (values). ✓ *What did you make of all the election coverage?* is still risky with a stranger; usually avoid the topic until you know the person's views on discussing politics.

(e) Reason 4 (identity), especially if the person has a local accent. ✓ *Did you grow up around here?* — and accept whatever answer they give.
:::
:::

::: warning *Where are you really from?*
✗ *Where are you from? — London. — No, I mean where are you **really** from?*

✓ *Where are you from? — London. — Oh, which part?*

For people whose appearance or name makes others assume they are foreign, the repeated question suggests that their answer is not accepted. Sue et al. (2007), describing what they called racial microaggressions in the United States, gave this question as an example of treating someone as a foreigner in their own country. If someone wants to tell you about their family's history, they will; let them choose.
:::

## When you are asked

Learners are often asked questions they would rather not answer — by English speakers as well as by others, since curiosity is universal. You do not have to answer, and there are gentle ways not to.

::: theorem Deflecting a question politely {#rule-deflect}
To avoid answering without causing offence:

1. **Use light humour**, with a smile: *How old are you? — Old enough to know better!* / *How much did it cost? — Too much!*
2. **Give a vague answer and return a question**: *Are you seeing anyone? — Oh, nothing to report. How about you — how was the wedding?*
3. **Answer a different, easier question**: *How much do you earn? — Enough to pay the rent, just about! It's a good team, though.*
4. **Set a gentle boundary** if the person insists: *I'd rather not talk about that, if you don't mind.* / *That's a bit personal — but tell me about your trip!*

Each strategy keeps the tone warm, gives the other person a way to change topic without losing face, and is clear enough that a second attempt would be rude.
:::

::: example Deflecting {#ex-deflect}
Write a deflection for each question using a different strategy from [[#rule-deflect]].

(a) A new colleague: *Why did you leave your last job?* (b) A neighbour you have just met: *You've put on weight since the summer, haven't you?* (c) A classmate's parent at a party: *Do you have a boyfriend?* (d) A taxi driver: *What do you think of the government in your country?*
::: solution
(a) Answer a different question: *Oh, I fancied a change — and this job looked really interesting. How long have you been here?* (vague answer + return question).

(b) Light humour: *Ha — too much good food on holiday!* (if you are comfortable with that), or a gentle boundary: *Oh, I try not to worry about that! How was your summer?* The second avoids agreeing with the comment.

(c) Vague answer and return: *Oh, I'm concentrating on my studies at the moment! Is your daughter enjoying the course?*

(d) Gentle boundary, framed as a general preference: *Oh, I try to stay out of politics! But I love it here — is this your favourite part of the city?*
:::
:::

::: quiz
At a work party, a colleague you have just met asks you: *So how much rent do you pay?* Which response deflects most gracefully?
- [ ] *That is a private question. In my culture we never ask that.*
- [x] *Ha — far too much! But I love the area. Do you live nearby?*
- [ ] (say nothing and walk away)
- [ ] *Why do you want to know?*
::: solution
Light humour and a return question keep the tone warm and change the topic. The first answer may be true but makes the other person lose face and generalises about cultures; walking away is rude; *Why do you want to know?* sounds suspicious and confrontational.
:::

::: quiz
According to Laver (1975), which opening is a **neutral token**?
- [ ] *You look tired.*
- [x] *The trains are a nightmare today, aren't they?*
- [ ] *I've had such a long day.*
- [ ] *How's your new job going?*
::: solution
A neutral token concerns the shared situation, not the speaker or the hearer. *You look tired* and *How's your new job going?* are other-oriented; *I've had such a long day* is self-oriented.

## Small talk at work

Small talk at work has a particular shape, because it sits next to work and must give way to it. Holmes (2000) showed that it tends to appear at the **edges** of work — arriving, waiting for a meeting to start, making coffee, leaving — and that the person with most authority in the room often decides when it ends, with a marker such as *Right*, *OK, shall we get started?* or *Anyway…*. It is also a place where relationships with people of different status are quietly managed: a manager who asks about a junior colleague's weekend is building rapport, and a junior colleague who answers warmly and asks back is accepting it.

::: theorem Small talk around meetings and tasks {#rule-work-small-talk}
In many English-speaking workplaces:

1. **Arrivals and waiting time** are filled with small talk; arriving and sitting in silence may be read as unfriendly or nervous.
2. **Monday and Friday** questions are routine: *Good weekend?* / *Any plans for the weekend?* A short, specific answer and a return question is enough.
3. **The transition to business** is usually marked by the chair or the most senior person (*Right, let's make a start*); when you hear it, stop the small talk.
4. **Online meetings** compress small talk into the first minute or two (*Can everyone hear me? How's everyone doing?*), and the transition is often explicit ([[listening-and-conversation/calls-online-meetings]]).
5. **Messages and chat** at work often open with a short phatic line (*Hope your week's going well!*) before the request, especially in a first message of the day.
:::

::: example Before the meeting {#ex-meeting}
Read the conversation in a meeting room before a project meeting and answer the questions. (a) Classify the tokens. (b) Where does the transition to business happen, and who makes it? (c) What does Akosua do well?

**Ben (team leader):** *Morning, all. Akosua — how was the half-marathon?*
**Akosua:** *Survived it! Two hours ten — I'm pleased. My legs aren't, though.*
**Tomás:** *Two ten? That's brilliant.*
**Akosua:** *Thanks! Did you get to the match on Saturday, Tomás?*
**Tomás:** *I did — freezing, but we won.*
**Ben:** *Nice. Right — shall we make a start? Has everyone got the agenda?*
::: solution
(a) Ben's *how was the half-marathon?* is **other-oriented**, and shows that he remembers something from Akosua's life — positive politeness from a manager. Akosua's answer contains a **self-oriented** detail (*My legs aren't, though*) told with humour. Tomás pays a compliment, and Akosua's *Did you get to the match…?* is other-oriented, returning the interest.

(b) The transition is Ben's *Nice. Right — shall we make a start?*: *Nice* closes the topic, *Right* marks the boundary, and the inclusive question (*shall we*) turns to business. As team leader, he is the person expected to make it.

(c) Akosua answers briefly and positively with a specific, light detail; accepts the compliment with *Thanks!*; and returns a question to a colleague rather than continuing about herself — reciprocity ([[#rule-small-talk]]). The whole exchange lasts under a minute, which suits its place before a meeting.
:::
:::

## Norms vary — in every direction

It would be easy to conclude that "English speakers" are private and "Chinese speakers" are personal. The evidence does not support so simple a picture. Norms for small talk vary greatly *within* English-speaking countries — between regions, between generations, between urban and rural communities, between workplaces and families — and individuals vary within every group. Some English speakers chat to strangers about their families within minutes; others find even a question about the weekend a little forward. In the same way, many Chinese speakers find questions about salary and marriage intrusive, even from relatives, and say so: the norm is contested in China too.

It also works both ways. English speakers ask questions that some learners find intrusive: about their opinions on their own government, about religion and belief, about why they came and whether they plan to stay, about dating. Some of these are invitations to share; some are curiosity; a few are careless. The strategies of [[#rule-deflect]] work in every direction.

::: note English and Chinese
- **Questions as care.** In many Chinese settings, questions such as 你多大了？, 结婚了吗？, 一个月多少钱？ and comments such as 你胖了 / 你瘦了 can show interest and care, especially from older people and relatives. In English-speaking workplaces and with new acquaintances they are usually avoided, and comments on weight are risky even between friends. Show care in English with open questions (*How are things?*, *How's your family?*) and with offers of help.
- **Greetings as questions.** 吃了吗？ and 去哪儿啊？ are greetings, not requests for information ([[pragmatics/speech-acts]]). *Where are you going?* in English is a real question and, to a colleague you meet in a corridor, may seem nosy; *Hi! Busy day?* does the same job.
- **Advice.** Unrequested advice about personal life (*You should hurry and get married*, *You should eat more*) is a sign of warmth in some families, but between colleagues in English it threatens negative face — it suggests the person is managing their life badly. Offer advice when asked: *Do you want a suggestion?*
- **Silence.** Comfortable silence varies between people and settings in both cultures ([[everyday-english/small-talk]]); in an English-speaking lift or kitchen, a short neutral token (*Busy day?*) is often used to fill it.
- **Answering *How are you?*.** *Fine, thanks — and you?* is not dishonest; it is the expected form of a greeting. If something is really wrong and the person is a friend, you can say so — and if they ask again (*No, really, how are you?*), they want the real answer.
:::

::: history
Bronisław Malinowski's term *phatic communion* (1923) was taken up by linguists in the 1950s and 1960s — Roman Jakobson listed a "phatic function" among the functions of language in 1960 — but small talk was long treated as the least interesting kind of talk. John Laver's "Communicative functions of phatic communion" (1975) and Klaus Schneider's *Small Talk: Analysing Phatic Discourse* (1988) began to describe its structure. The collection *Small Talk*, edited by Justine Coupland (2000), brought together research showing its importance in workplaces, health care and service encounters, including Janet Holmes's study of small talk in New Zealand government offices, part of the Language in the Workplace Project at Victoria University of Wellington. Since then small talk has been studied as a skill of professional life, and as a frequent site of intercultural misunderstanding.
:::

::: summary
- Small talk is **phatic communion**: talk whose main purpose is relational, not informational ([[#def-phatic]]).
- Laver distinguished **neutral**, **self-oriented** and **other-oriented** tokens; neutral ones are safe with anyone.
- Small talk tends to expect a positive tone, easy agreement, **reciprocity**, the other person setting the limit, and an easy exit ([[#rule-small-talk]]).
- Questions feel intrusive when they **measure**, probe **private life**, invite **disagreement on values**, or question **belonging** ([[#rule-intrusive]]).
- **Open questions** show interest and let the other person choose how much to share.
- **Deflect** with humour, a vague answer and a return question, a different answer, or a gentle boundary ([[#rule-deflect]]).
- Norms vary within every community, in both directions; describe what a person does, not what "a culture" does.
:::

Analyse the lunch conversation between Chen Rui and Karin at the start of the chapter using [[#rule-small-talk]], [[#rule-intrusive]] and [[#rule-deflect]]. What did each speaker do, and how could Chen Rui have shown the same friendly interest?

Chen Rui could have asked open questions (*Have you been at the company long? Her warmth would then have come across as it was meant.

With a partner, act out a five-minute conversation at the coffee machine between two new colleagues from different backgrounds. Partner A asks two questions that are slightly too personal; Partner B deflects them politely and steers the conversation to safer ground, then both close the conversation warmly. Afterwards, write down which strategies you used.
::: hint

::: solution
Answers will vary. A model outline:

**A:** *Morning! This machine takes forever, doesn't it?* (neutral token)
**B:** *It really does! I'm Sanjay, by the way — I started on Monday.*
**A:** *Mariana. Welcome! So, are you married, Sanjay?*
**B:** *Ha — not yet, my mum keeps asking too! What about this place — have you been here long?* (humour + return question)
**A:** *Three years. Do they pay you well? What did they offer you?*
**B:** *Oh, I'm not complaining! The team seems really nice. Is it always this busy on Mondays?* (a different answer + a neutral question)
**A:** *Always! Oh — that's mine. Anyway, I'd better get back. Nice to meet you, Sanjay!* (pre-closing and warm close)
**B:** *You too — see you around!*

Strategies: a neutral opening token; two deflections that keep the tone warm; return questions that redirect to safe, shared topics; a pre-closing (*Anyway*) and a warm close.
